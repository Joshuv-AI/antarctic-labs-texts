Peroxiredoxin Ⅲ safeguards cardiac function against doxorubicin by regulating mitochondrial quality control via H2O2 detoxification
Authors: Ji Won Park, Se Yeon Jang, Mi Yeon Kim, Ho Jin Yi, Seong Keun Sonn, Okhee Kweon, Byung-Hoon Lee, Goo Taeg Oh, Hyun Ae Woo, Tong-Shin Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Doxorubicin (DOX) is a widely used anticancer drug associated with severe cardiotoxicity primarily driven by mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a major mitochondrial ROS, significantly contributes to cardiotoxicity, yet its precise mechanistic role in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity remains incompletely understood. Here, we investigated the critical role of mitochondrial H2O2 in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity using rat cardiomyocyte H9c2 cells with stable knockdown or reconstitution of mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme peroxiredoxin Ⅲ (PrxⅢ), which specifically regulates mitochondrial H2O2 levels. Our results demonstrated that severe mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation (>10-fold compared to control) in PrxⅢ-depleted cells exacerbated mitochondrial oxidative stress, evidenced by increased cardiolipin oxidation and mitochondrial membrane potential dissipation. Furthermore, excessive mitochondrial H2O2 impaired mitochondrial fusion by reducing fusion-related protein expression, disrupted autophagic flux via lysosomal dysfunction, and significantly attenuated mitophagy, ultimately leading to enhanced apoptosis. Conversely, moderate mitochondrial H2O2 levels (5- to 8-fold increase compared to control) observed in PrxⅢ-expressing cells promoted mitochondrial elongation, enhanced mitophagy, and preserved autophagic flux, suggesting a protective adaptation against DOX-induced oxidative stress. In vivo experiments using PrxⅢ knockout mice confirmed that loss of PrxⅢ aggravated DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction. Bioinformatic analysis of independent public transcriptome datasets and targeted qPCR validation in PrxⅢ-deficient cardiac tissues further confirmed that the mitochondrial quality control pathways identified in vitro are robustly dysregulated in vivo. Additionally, PrxⅢ deficiency markedly increased mitochondrial structural damage without significantly affecting cardiac fibrosis or hypertrophy. Notably, mitigating the mitochondrial H2O2 burden and protecting the mitochondrial inner membrane using the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant peptide SS-31 successfully rescued this exacerbated cardiac dysfunction. In conclusion, our findings establish mitochondrial H2O2 as a pivotal determinant in DOX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiotoxicity, highlighting PrxⅢ as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating oxidative cardiac injury.

Introduction

Doxorubicin (DOX) is a widely used anthracycline anticancer drug that exerts therapeutic effects by inhibiting topoisomerase Ⅱ, leading to DNA damage, impaired DNA synthesis, and subsequent cell death [1,2]. Specifically, DOX forms complexes with tyrosine residues of topoisomerase Ⅱ, disrupting gene expression and initiating apoptotic pathways [3,4]. DOX has been widely used to treat various cancers and was estimated to have a market share of close to $20 million in 2021 with an increasing compound annual growth rate of 10.7% [5]. Despite its potent anticancer efficacy, the clinical use of DOX is severely limited by its dose-dependent cardiotoxicity, which remains a critical barrier to its broader application [6,7].

A central mechanism underlying DOX-induced cardiotoxicity involves mitochondrial oxidative stress, primarily driven by excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cardiac mitochondria [8,9]. Due to its strong affinity for cardiolipin, a phospholipid abundant in the mitochondrial inner membrane, DOX preferentially accumulates in cardiac mitochondria [10]. This accumulation increases ROS generation through electron leakage from the mitochondrial electron transport chain, particularly at complexes I and III, enhancing the formation of superoxide radicals (O2•-) [11,12]. Superoxide anions produced in the mitochondrial matrix are rapidly converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by superoxide dismutase (MnSOD or SOD2) [13]. Although H2O2 itself is relatively less reactive, it is membrane-permeable and can diffuse throughout cellular compartments, propagating oxidative stress. Furthermore, mitochondrial H2O2 can readily convert to highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH) via the Fenton reaction, catalyzed by iron-sulfur clusters and other divalent metal ions abundant in mitochondria [14,15]. Consequently, the precise regulation of mitochondrial H2O2 levels is essential for mitigating oxidative stress and associated cardiac damage.

Cells have evolved multiple antioxidant systems to counteract mitochondrial ROS toxicity, among which peroxiredoxin Ⅲ (PrxⅢ) plays an especially critical role. PrxⅢ, a mitochondria-specific antioxidant enzyme, effectively detoxifies H2O2 into water with high catalytic efficiency, attributable to its low Km and rapid turnover rate [16,17]. Indeed, previous studies, including our own, have consistently demonstrated the pivotal role of PrxⅢ in protecting various cell types against mitochondrial oxidative stress and associated cellular damage under pathological conditions [[18], [19], [20]]. However, the relationship between mitochondrial H2O2 levels modulated by PrxⅢ activity and alterations in mitochondrial dynamics under DOX-induced cardiotoxicity remains inadequately understood.

Mitochondria are dynamic organelles regulated by mitochondrial quality control (MQC) mechanisms, including mitochondrial biogenesis [21], mitochondrial fusion/fission, and mitophagy [22,23]. Mitophagy selectively eliminates damaged mitochondria via autophagosomal degradation [24], whereas mitochondrial biogenesis replenishes the mitochondrial pool by generating new organelles through transcriptional activation [25]. Mitochondria fusion, mediated by mitofusins (Mfn1 and Mfn2) and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1), dilutes mitochondrial damage by merging impaired mitochondria with functional ones. Conversely, fission, orchestrated by dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), facilitates removal of irreversibly damaged mitochondrial segments [26]. Disruption of the balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission contributes significantly to mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiac injury following DOX exposure [[27], [28], [29]]. Emerging evidence indicates that mitochondrial H2O2 levels critically regulate various MQC processes, suggesting a mechanistic link between oxidative stress and MQC dysregulation.

In this study, we investigated how modulation of mitochondrial H2O2 levels through PrxⅢ alteration affects mitochondrial dynamics and cardiotoxicity following DOX exposure, using in vitro and in vivo models with differential PrxⅢ expression. Our findings highlight the crucial protective role of PrxⅢ in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and suggest that precise control of mitochondrial H2O2 could significantly attenuate DOX-induced cardiac damage, offering a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance the clinical safety of DOX treatment.

Cell culture

Rat cardiomyocyte cell line, H9c2 cells (CRL-1446) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM high glucose (SH30022.01, Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, and 1 μg/ml puromycin. Cells were fed every 2-3 days and maintained at 37 °C with a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

To generate a plasmid expressing a short hairpin RNA specific for rat PrxⅢ, two complementary oligonucleotides targeting the 5′-AAGAGCUGAGUCUCGACGACU-3′ sequence within the open reading frame of rat PrxⅢ mRNA were synthesized (Genotech, Daejeon, Republic of Korea):

5′-GATCCCCGAGCTGAGTCTCGACGACTTTCAAGAGAAGTCGTCGAGACTCAGCTCTTTTTA-3′

5′-AGCTTAAAAAGAGCTGAGTCTCGACGACTTCTCTTGAAAGTCGTCGAGACTCAGCTCGGG-3′ The oligonucleotides were annealed and ligated into the pSUPER-puro vector (VEC-pBS-0008; OligoEngine, Seattle, WA, USA), resulting in the short hairpin RNA-expressing construct pSUPER-siPrxⅢ.

H9c2 cells were transfected with either pSUPER-siPrxⅢ or control pSUPER vectors using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 48 h, cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL), and puromycin-resistant clones were isolated and expanded.

Adenovirus construction and transfection

To overexpress human PrxⅢ, a recombinant adenoviral vector encoding the full-length human PrxⅢ cDNA (Ad-PrxⅢ) was constructed and packaged by VectorBuilder (vector ID: VB220222-1017kpd, Chicago, IL, USA). An adenoviral vector carrying a non-coding stuffer sequence (Ad-Stuffer, vector ID: VB220301-1013hty, VectorBuilder) was used as a control.

For monitoring autophagy, adenoviral vectors encoding GFP-tagged LC3 (Ad-GFP-LC3) or tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3 (Ad-GFP-mCherry-LC3) were used, as described previously [30].

All adenoviral transductions were performed in serum-free medium for 4 h, followed by replacement with complete medium and incubation for 20 h before analysis.

Drugs

DOX stock solution was 2 mg/mL and diluted in DMEM to make a final concentration. DOX was treated in DMEM at indicated concentration for indicated times. BafA1 dissolved in DMSO for culture and treated at 10 nM for 4 h.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. WST-1 reagent (10 μL) was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. Absorbance at 440 nm was measured using a SpectraMax i3x plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Measurement of ROS

To measure intracellular ROS and mitochondrial H2O2, cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they reached approximately 80% confluence. After DOX treatment, cells were stained with 5 μM CM-H2DCFDA or MitoPY-1 [31] for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. A minimum of 2 × 105 cells per sample was used for each measurement. Cells were analyzed using a FACSLyric flow cytometer (BD bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA), and the relative fluorescence intensity was quantified using FlowJo software 10.9 (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

To measure mitochondrial H2O2 with images, cells were grown for 24 h in 12-well plates with poly-l-lysine-coated cover glass. After DOX treatment, cells were stained with 10 μM MitoPY-1 for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark, washed with PBS, and fixed for 15 min with 4% PFA solution. Confocal images were captured using the TCS8 confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Five random fields per sample were selected, and the mean relative fluorescence intensity was calculated using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence analysis

For visualization of the location of FOXO3a, immunostaining of FOXO3a and localization to nucleus was assessed. Seeded cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 15 min. Then, the cells were permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 at RT for 15 min and washed with PBS before being blocked with 3% (w/v) BSA in PBS for 1 h at RT. Subsequently, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against FOXO3a at a 1:300 dilution overnight at 4 °C. After three washes in the blocking solution, the cells were incubated with the secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (A-11001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 2 h at RT and then washed three more times in blocking solution. Finally, the cells were stained with DAPI before being imaged. Fixed cells were examined with the TCS8 confocal microscopy (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Images were analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji software.

Determination of mitochondrial damage

To assess mitochondrial lipid oxidation, cardiolipin oxidation was measured using 10-NAO. After DOX treatment, cells were stained in 5 μM 10-NAO at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min [32]. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was measured using 10 μM Rho-123 under similar conditions. Stained cells were analyzed using a FACSLyric flow cytometer, and the relative fluorescence intensity was quantified using FlowJo software.

ATP assay

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using the CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions with modifications for quantification. Briefly, cells were plated in white opaque 96-well plates and treated with DOX. At the endpoint, CellTiter-Glo reagent was added at 1:1 ratio to culture medium, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min at RT to stabilize luminescence signals. Luminescence was measured using a microplate reader. To determine absolute ATP concentrations, a standard curve was generated in parallel using serial dilutions of ATP prepared in assay buffer, and sample ATP levels were calculated by interpolating luminescence values against the standard curve. ATP concentrations were normalized to protein contents determined from replicate wells using the BSA assay.

Oxygen consumption rate measurement

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using Seahorse XFe 96 or XFp analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) accompanied by an Agilent Seahorse Mito Stress test kit (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer instruction. Cells were treated with oligomycin (1 μM), carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP; 1 μM), and a mixture of rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM) for the measurement of key parameters of mitochondrial respiration. OCR values were normalized to protein contents determined from replicate wells using the BSA assay.

Immunoblot analysis

Cells were lysed with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1% TritonX-100, 10% glycerol, 2 mM EGTA, 1 mM EDTA, 20 mM glycerol 2-phosphate, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 μg/ml aprotinin, 1 μg/ml leupeptin, and 1 mM AEBSF. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,500×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were measured by Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Whatman, Maidstone, UK). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at RT, incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, and probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% skim milk for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using WESTSAVE-up ECL substrate (Ab frontier, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and detected using an Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Band density was quantified with ImageJ/Fiji software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

RNA was prepared from cells using Easy-Blue Total RNA extraction Kit (iNtRON, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and cDNA was synthesized using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The resulting cDNA was amplified by qRT-PCR using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The sequences of primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in TableS1.

Mitochondrial fragmentation measurement

Cells were seeded on poly-l-lysine-coated coverslips in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After treatment, cells were stained with 30 nM MitoTracker Red for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark, then fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Confocal images were taken with the TCS8 confocal microscope, and mitochondrial morphology was quantified using aspect ratio analysis with the Mitochondrial Network Analysis (MiNA) plugin Fiji software [33]. Expression levels of mitochondrial fission and fusion proteins (Drp1, Drp1-phospho-Ser616, Mfn1, Mfn2, and OPA1) were determined by immunoblotting.

Autophagic flux analysis

Autophagic flux was evaluated by Western blotting for LC3B and SQSTM1/p62, and additionally using fluorescence-based LC3 reporters (GFP-LC3 and mCherry-GFP-LC3) as previously described [34]. Cells were transduced with GFP-LC3 adenovirus or GFP-mCherry-LC3 adenovirus for 4 h. It was then changed to full media and incubated for 20 h. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% PFA solution for 15 min. The images were taken with the TCS8 confocal microscopy, and puncta were measured using Image J Fiji software.

Lysosomal blockage analysis

Immunofluorescence analysis was performed as described previously [30]. For measurement of lysosomal acidity or activity, cells were loaded with Lysosensor Green DND-189 or Magic Red reagent in a prewarmed media for 1 h after treatment. Then the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA solution for 15 min. Either, cells were preloaded with 10 μg/ml DQ-BSA Red in prewarmed media for 12 h. They were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA. Additionally, to visualize the lysosome and autophagosome co-localization, fixed cell samples transfected with GFP-LC3 virus were exposed to a blocking solution and incubated at RT for 30 min with antibodies to LAMP1. The cells were washed 3 times with PBS and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at RT. All images were captured with the TCS8 confocal microscope. Fluorescence was quantified using Image J/Fiji software.

Apoptosis assay

Detached cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC (25 mM) and 7-AAD (1 μg/ml) in HEPES-glucose and incubated on ice in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence was analyzed with a FACSLyric flow cytometer, and apoptotic cells were quantified using FlowJo software. In addition, cleaved PARP-1 and cleaved caspase-3 were assessed by Western blotting as described above.

Animals and in vivo treatment protocols

PrxⅢ Wild-type (WT) and knockout (KO) mice were generated by breeding heterozygous mice. 8- to 10-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used for all experiments. Animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ewha Womans University and Seoul National university (EWHA IACUC 21-034-t, SNU IACUC 251224-5). Animals were housed in temperature-controlled conditions (20–22 °C) with a 12-h light/dark cycle.

For the evaluation of acute DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, PrxⅢ WT and KO mice were treated with DOX (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) for 2 weeks. After administration, cardiac function was assessed using echocardiography, and histological samples were collected to evaluate cardiac mitochondrial damage and fibrosis.

To comprehensively evaluate the comparative efficacy of mitochondria-targeted antioxidants and to conduct the definitive rescue study, a 4-week chronic DOX cardiotoxicity model was established. Mice were administered DOX (5 mg/kg once weekly via intraperitoneally (i.p.) injection for 4 weeks) to induce chronic cardiac dysfunction. In the comparative pilot study, WT mice were randomly assigned to receive saline, DOX alone, or DOX combined with daily i.p. injections of the following mitochondria-targeted antioxidants: MitoQ (5 mg/kg), SKQ1 (0.3 mg/kg), or SS-31 (3 mg/kg). Body weights were regularly monitored throughout the experimental period to assess systemic toxicity.

Based on the superior safety and efficacy profile observed in the pilot study, SS-31 was selected for the definitive rescue experiment. WT and PrxⅢ KO mice were subjected to the 4-week chronic DOX regimen with or without daily concurrent administration of SS-31 (3 mg/kg, i.p.). At the experimental endpoint, cardiac function was thoroughly evaluated using echocardiography, and blood samples were collected to measure serum cardiac Troponin I (cTnI) levels for the assessment of structural cardiomyocyte injury.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Cardiac function was assessed by transthoracic echocardiography using the Vevo 2100 system (FUJIFILM Visual Sonics Inc., Toronto, ON, Canada) equipped with a 25–55-MHz linear array transducer at Ewha Fluorescence Core Imaging Center. Mice (10 weeks old) were anesthetized with 1.5–2% isoflurane, and the mice body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a homeothermic heating pad throughout the imaging. Heart rate (HR) was continuously monitored and maintained above 450 bpm to ensure physiological conditions. Left ventricular (LV) parameters, including the ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS), stroke volume (SV), cardiac output (CO), LV end-systolic volume (LVESV), and HR were evaluated in the parasternal long-axis view using Simpson's monoplane method of disks with Vevo Strain software (FUJIFILM Visual Sonics Inc.).

Damaged mitochondria analysis using transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM analysis of mitochondrial ultrastructure, heart tissues were harvested from 10-week-old PrxⅢ WT and KO mice. Sample preparation and TEM analysis were adapted and modified from previously described protocols [35]. Damaged mitochondria were visualized using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1011, JEOL, Japan) operated at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV. The structural integrity and presence of mitochondrial damage, such as disrupted cristae structure and abnormal morphology, were qualitatively assessed and documented.

Cardiac fibrosis analysis

To evaluate cardiac fibrosis, left ventricular (LV) tissues from paraffin-embedded heart samples were sectioned at a thickness of 6 μm and stained with Masson's trichrome stain using a commercially available kit (BBC Biochemical, Mount Vernon, WA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Whole-slide images of the stained sections were obtained using the automated quantitative pathology imaging system (Vectra Polaris, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative analysis of the fibrotic area relative to total LV tissue area (%) was performed using the inForm Advanced Imaging Analysis Software (PerkinElmer).

Serum cardiac troponin Ⅰ (cTnⅠ) analysis

Serum cardiac troponin Ⅰ levels were measured using a mouse cTnⅠ ELISA kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Blood samples were collected from mice at the endpoint of the experiment and allowed to clot at RT. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 1500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Standards and samples were added to antibody-coated wells and incubated with the HRP-conjugated detection antibody. After washing, substrate solution was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Serum cTnⅠ concentrations were calculated from a standard curve generated with known cTnⅠ standards.

Bioinformatic analysis of public transcriptome datasets

To independently validate the signaling pathways examined in our study at the transcriptomic level, we analyzed publicly available gene expression datasets from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. We selected datasets from DOX-induced cardiotoxicity models in mice based on the following criteria: (1) cardiac tissue or cardiomyocyte origin, (2) DOX-treated versus vehicle control comparison, and (3) availability of raw count data or processed expression matrices.

Three datasets meeting these criteria were included in the analysis: GSE233644 (mouse heart, DOX 20 mg/kg single i.p. vs sham, n = 4/group, RNA-seq), GSE236165 (mouse heart, DOX 20 mg/kg single i.p. vs PBS, n = 2/group, RNA-seq), and GSE224157 (mouse heart, DOX 5 mg/kg weekly × 4 weeks vs vehicle, n = 3/group, RNA-seq). Processed count or FPKM data were downloaded directly from GEO.

For RNA-seq count data (GSE233644, GSE224157), raw read counts were normalized to counts per million (CPM) and log2-transformed after adding a pseudocount of 1. Genes with mean expression below 10 counts across all samples were excluded as low-expression filters. For FPKM-based data (GSE236165), log2(FPKM + 1) values were used directly. Differential expression analysis was performed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Genes with adjusted p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

A curated panel of 27 target genes was assembled representing seven functional categories central to our study: mitochondrial antioxidant defense (Prdx3, Prdx5, Sod2, Cat, Gpx4, Txn2), mitochondrial biogenesis (Ppargc1a, Nrf1, Tfam), mitochondrial fusion (Mfn1, Mfn2, Opa1), mitochondrial fission (Dnm1l, Fis1), mitophagy/autophagy (Pink1, Prkn, Bnip3, Map1lc3b, Sqstm1, Lamp1), transcription factor (Foxo3), and apoptosis (Casp3, Parp1, Bbc3, Phlda3, Bax, Bcl2). The expression changes of these target genes were visualized as volcano plots and heatmaps, and cross-dataset consistency was evaluated by comparing log2 fold-change values across independent datasets. All analyses were performed using Python 3.10 with pandas (v1.5), SciPy (v1.10), matplotlib (v3.7), and seaborn (v0.12) libraries.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis of cardiac tissues

Total RNA was extracted from frozen cardiac tissues using TRIzol Reagent according to the manufacturer's protocol. Tissues were homogenized using an electric tissue homogenizer on ice. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ≥ 1.8). cDNA was synthesized using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The resulting cDNA was amplified by qRT-PCR using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The sequences of primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in TableS1.

Western blot analysis of cardiac tissues

Frozen cardiac tissues were lysed in ice-cold 1 × RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors. Tissues were homogenized using a bead homogenizer and incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad) using BSA as a standard. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Whatman, Maidstone, UK). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at RT, incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, and probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% skim milk for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using WESTSAVE-up ECL substrate (Ab frontier, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and detected using an Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Band density was quantified with ImageJ/Fiji software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistics

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad PRISM software (San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Statistical significance between multiple groups was primarily analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test. For comparison of multiple experimental groups against a control group, statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett's post hoc test. For experiments requiring comparisons between two groups only, statistical analysis was performed using Student's t-test (two-tailed). Statistical significance was considered at a threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

DOX increases mitochondrial H2O2 and mitochondrial oxidative damage in rat cardiomyocytes

According to previous studies, DOX induces cell damage due to radicals and ROS generated during the redox process of the mitochondrial electron transport chain [36]. We first assessed the toxicity of DOX in H9c2 rat cardiomyocyte cells. At 24 h after DOX treatment, cell death was significantly increased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). Based on this, the DOX concentration was fixed at 1 μM for all subsequent experiments. Treatment with 1 μM DOX significantly reduced the viability of H9c2 cells in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Intracellular ROS levels were significantly increased 12 h after DOX treatment as assessed using the oxidant-sensitive fluorescent probe CM-H2DCFDA (Fig. 1C). Although CM-H2DCFDA is commonly used to measure cellular ROS, this probe lacks specificity for individual oxidants and the ability to distinguish between mitochondrial ROS and other cellular ROS [37]. Therefore, mitochondrial-specific H2O2 production was specifically assessed using MitoPY-1. Mitochondrial H2O2 levels were significantly elevated after 12 h of DOX exposure (Fig. 1D). Excessive mitochondrial H2O2 exacerbates DOX-induced mitochondrial oxidative damage

PrxⅢ is a mitochondrion-localized peroxidase that serves as the major enzymatic defense against H2O2 within the mitochondrial matrix. Unlike other antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, glutathione peroxidase (Gpx), or SOD2, PrxⅢ directly detoxifies H2O2 generated by mitochondrial metabolism, thereby maintaining intramitochondrial redox balance [16,38]. Given its dominant role in H2O2 removal, we sought to investigate how modulation of PrxⅢ expression—and the resulting differences in mitochondrial H2O2 levels—affects the susceptibility of cardiomyocytes to DOX-induced injury. To this end, we established stable PrxⅢ knockdown rat cardiomyocytes (Fig. S1A) and reconstituted ectopic PrxⅢ in the PrxⅢ knockdown cells to precisely modulate mitochondrial H2O2 levels (Fig. S1C).

Importantly, to determine whether the depletion of PrxⅢ induces compensatory alterations in other cellular antioxidant defense systems, which could confound our observations, we evaluated the basal expression levels of major antioxidant enzymes in pSUPER and pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1B, the expression levels of other key hydrogen peroxide-scavenging enzymes (catalase, GpxIV, and PrxV), the mitochondrial superoxide-scavenging enzyme (SOD2), and the mitochondrial thioredoxin (Trx2) remained unchanged despite the robust knockdown of PrxⅢ. These results indicate that the baseline antioxidant network is not globally altered by PrxⅢ depletion. Therefore, the observed exacerbation of DOX-induced oxidative damage in PrxⅢ-depleted cells is specifically attributable to the loss of PrxⅢ function, highlighting its indispensable and non-redundant role in mitochondrial H2O2 detoxification.

Mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation induced by DOX was first assessed using the mitochondrial-specific fluorescent probe MitoPY-1. After 9 h of DOX treatment, mitochondrial H2O2 levels were significantly increased in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells compared to control (pSUPER) cells, whereas ectopic PrxⅢ reconstitution restored mitochondrial H2O2 levels to baseline (Fig. 2A). To examine how mitochondrial H2O2 levels determine redox signaling, we analyzed FOXO3a localization as a functional readout of redox threshold sensing. FOXO3a, a redox-sensitive transcription factor that mediates antioxidant defense and mitochondrial adaptation [39,40]. In response to DOX, FOXO3a exhibited robust nuclear accumulation in control cells but markedly failed to enter the nucleus in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells with excessive H2O2 accumulation (Fig. 2B). These findings indicate that the difference between moderate and excessive mitochondrial H2O2 is not merely quantitative but represents distinct biological states.

To assess whether excessive mitochondrial H2O2 due to PrxⅢ depletion affects mitochondrial function, we measured parameters reflecting mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetics. Intracellular ATP quantification revealed a strong reduction in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells, even under basal conditions, and a further decrease following DOX exposure, whereas PrxⅢ re-expression restored ATP levels to near control values (Fig. 3C). Excessive mitochondrial H2O2 suppresses DOX-induced mitochondrial biogenesis

To determine whether mitochondrial H2O2 affects mitochondrial biogenesis, we examined multiple canonical markers of mitochondrial after DOX exposure. Mitochondrial biogenesis is a highly coordinated process that integrates nuclear and mitochondrial transcriptional programs to replenish the mitochondrial population and sustain oxidative capacity [47]. This pathway is primarily governed by the transcriptional co-activator PGC-1α, which serves as a master regulator linking metabolic and redox cues to mitochondrial gene expression [48,49]. PGC-1α co-activates the nuclear transcription factor Nrf1, which in turn induces expression of mitochondrial transcription factor A (mtTFA/TFAM) [50]—a key protein required for mitochondrial DNA replication, packaging, and transcription [21]. Together, the PGC-1α–Nrf1–TFAM axis drives the synthesis of new mitochondrial components and maintains the balance between mitochondrial degradation and renewal, ensuring proper MQC under physiological and stress conditions.

In control cells, DOX exposure modestly elevated PGC-1α, whereas its downstream transcription factors Nrf1 and mtTFA were reduced. This pattern suggests a limited, compensatory activation of PGC-1α that fails to propagate through the downstream biogenic cascade. In contrast, pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells exhibited a marked reduction in PGC-1α expression at both mRNA and protein levels, accompanied by a further decline in Nrf1, and TFAM. These changes reflect global suppression of the nuclear-mitochondrial transcriptional program governing organelle renewal. mtTFA is essential for packaging and transcribing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA); its depletion results in impaired mtDNA replication and reduced mitochondrial gene expression [51]. The reduction of mtTFA therefore represents a terminal point of biogenesis failure, translating transcriptional repression into defective organelle replication. To further substantiate the impact on mitochondrial genome expression, we measured the mRNA level of the mtDNA-encoded gene cytochrome c oxidase (COX) as an indicator of mitochondrial transcriptional output (Fig. 4B). COX transcripts were markedly decreased in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells following DOX treatment, consistent with reduced mtTFA expression and mtDNA transcriptional activity.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PrxⅢ deficiency leads to coordinated suppression of the entire mitochondrial biogenesis program, encompassing PGC-1α, Nrf1, mtTFA, and mtDNA-encoded genes. The rescue of these parameters by PrxⅢ re-expression indicates that mitochondrial H2O2 homeostasis is critical not only for degradation pathways such as mitophagy but also for the transcriptional renewal of mitochondria. Thus, PrxⅢ functions as a pivotal regulator that maintains balanced mitochondrial turnover by coupling redox buffering with biogenic signaling under oxidative stress conditions.

Disruption of mitochondrial dynamics can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent cellular damage [26,27]. After 12 h of DOX exposure, mitochondria were notably elongated in pSUPER cells; however, they did not significantly change in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells. Importantly, reconstitution of PrxⅢ in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells restored mitochondrial elongation after DOX treatment (Fig. 5A). We next evaluated the expression levels of protein markers involved in mitochondrial fusion and fission (Fig. 5B). Regarding fusion-related proteins, PrxⅢ depletion alone increased levels of Mfn1, Mfn2, and OPA1 even in the absence of DOX treatment. Upon DOX exposure, the levels of fusion proteins Mfn1, Mfn2, and OPA1 were increased in control pSUPER cells. To investigate mitochondrial fission, we measured the phosphorylation status of Drp1 at serine 616, a post-translational modification known to promote mitochondrial fission [52]. Interestingly, Drp1 phosphorylation levels were not significantly different between pSUPER and pSUPER-siPrxⅢ after DOX treatment. These results indicate that moderate mitochondrial H2O2 levels triggered mitochondrial elongation and fusion, potentially as an adaptive mechanism to mitigate oxidative stress. In contrast, excessive mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation, as observed in DOX-treated PrxⅢ-depleted cells, significantly impaired mitochondrial dynamics, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction.

To further validate autophagic flux, we used BafA1, a lysosomal inhibitor that blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion and thus causes LC3-II accumulation when autophagy is active [55]. BafA1 alone markedly increased LC3-II accumulation in both control and pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells, confirming basal autophagic activity. When combined with DOX, LC3-II levels further increased in control cells compared with BafA1 alone, indicating that DOX-induced autophagy was functionally active. In contrast, pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells showed minimal LC3-II induction upon DOX exposure and LC3-II levels in the DOX + BafA1 group were similar to those of BafA1 alone, suggesting impaired autophagosome formation and reduced autophagic flux (Fig. 6B).

We also assessed autophagic flux using image-based methods. First, GFP-LC3 puncta were quantified to confirm autophagosome formation. After 12 h of DOX treatment, GFP-LC3 puncta significantly increased in control cells but not in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells, indicating defective autophagosome formation under conditions of excessive mitochondrial H2O2. Importantly, reconstitution of PrxⅢ expression in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells restored GFP-LC3 puncta following DOX treatment, reaching levels similar to control cells (Fig. 6C). After autophagosome formation, lysosomes must fuse with autophagosomes to form autolysosomes, facilitating degradation of autophagosomal contents to reduce cellular damage [56,57]. To further analyze autophagic flux, we employed tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3 (mCherry-GFP-LC3) adenovirus, which emits distinct fluorescence signals depending on pH conditions. GFP fluorescence is sensitive to acidic conditions and thus lost in autolysosomes, whereas mCherry fluorescence remains stable. Thus, autophagosomes display combined green and red signals (yellow), whereas autolysosomes exclusively emit red signals [34]. After 12 h of DOX treatment, autophagic flux significantly increased in pSUPER cells but remained impaired in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells. Importantly, re-expression of PrxⅢ in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells restored autophagy to control levels (Fig. 6D).

In succession, we investigated mitophagy, a specialized autophagy process selectively eliminating damaged mitochondria. We specifically evaluated the PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy and BNIP3-mediated mitophagy pathway [56,58]. After 12 h of DOX treatment, protein levels of PINK1 and BNIP3 increased in control cells but were markedly reduced in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, PINK1 and Parkin expression were significantly elevated in PrxⅢ-depleted cells even without DOX exposure. These results suggested that excessive mitochondrial H2O2 levels induced by DOX in the absence of PrxⅢ impair the initiation of mitophagy, leaving damaged mitochondria uncleared. Reconstitution of PrxⅢ in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells effectively restored the levels of PINK1, Parkin, and BNIP3 proteins, indicating successful rescue of mitophagy.

Taken together, these findings demonstrated that moderate mitochondrial H2O2 levels induce mitophagy and autophagy as adaptive mechanisms to protect cardiomyocytes from oxidative stress, whereas excessive mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation impairs autophagic flux and mitophagy, resulting in accumulation of damaged mitochondria.

Next, lysosomal pH was assessed using DQ-BSA Red, a self-quenched fluorescent probe that becomes fluorescent upon proteolytic cleavage in the acidic environment of lysosomes [63], and LysoSensor Green, a pH-sensitive dye whose fluorescence intensity increases in acidic lysosomes [64]. DOX treatment strongly reduced fluorescence intensities of both DQ-BSA Red and LysoSensor Green, with a significantly greater reduction observed in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells compared to control cells. These results indicated an elevated lysosomal pH and impaired lysosomal activity upon PrxⅢ depletion following DOX treatment (Fig. 7B–C). PrxⅢ reconstitution successfully restored lysosome acidity to levels comparable with pSUPER cells.

To further examine if impaired lysosomal fusion contributed to defective autophagic flux, we transfected cells with GFP-LC3 adenovirus and analyzed colocalization of GFP-LC3 with LAMP1, a marker of lysosomes and late endosomes [65]. In control cells, GFP-LC3 puncta showed distinct colocalization with LAMP1-positive lysosomes after DOX treatment, indicative of successful autophagosome-lysosome fusion. In contrast, GFP-LC3 and LAMP1 colocalization was significantly decreased in DOX-treated pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells, strongly suggesting a failure in autophagosome-lysosome fusion (Fig. 7D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrated that lysosome acidity and activity were severely impaired under conditions of excessive mitochondrial H2O2, inhibiting autophagosome-lysosome fusion and consequently blocking autophagic flux. While lysosomal function was moderately impaired at lower mitochondrial H2O2 levels, it was still sufficient to support autophagosome-lysosome fusion and maintain autophagic flux.

Excessive mitochondrial H2O2 leads to apoptotic cell death

Oxidative stress and impaired autophagic flux can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately triggering apoptotic cell death [66,67]. Finally, we evaluated mitochondria-mediated apoptotic cell death induced by DOX treatment. PARP-1 and caspase 3 are cleaved and activated during mitochondria-mediated apoptosis, and these cleavage events can be readily measured by immunoblot analysis [68,69]. After 18 h of DOX exposure, pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells exhibited significantly higher levels of cleaved PARP-1 and cleaved caspase 3 compared to control cells. We further directly measured apoptosis by flow cytometric analysis of cells co-stained with Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD [70]. Consistent with the immunoblot results, apoptotic cell death was significantly increased in pSUPER-siPrxⅢ cells compared to control cells following 18 h of DOX treatment. In summary, under normal conditions, PrxⅢ maintains mitochondrial H2O2 at moderate levels, enabling mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy to proceed effectively, thus preserving cellular homeostasis. Mildly increased mitochondrial H2O2 levels induced by either PrxⅢ knockdown alone or DOX treatments in cells expressing normal PrxⅢ levels activate mitochondrial fusion and mitophagy to alleviate oxidative stress. However, excessive accumulation of mitochondrial H2O2, as observed in PrxⅢ-depleted cells upon DOX exposure, significantly disrupts mitochondrial dynamics and blocks autophagic flux. Consequently, extensive mitochondrial damage and apoptosis ensue. Our findings strongly suggest that PrxⅢ serves as a critical protective factor against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity by tightly regulating mitochondrial H2O2 levels.

SS-31, a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, rescues DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction and mitochondrial injury exacerbated by PrxⅢ deficiency in vivo

To definitively establish the causal link between mitochondrial H2O2 regulation and cardioprotection in vivo, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of mitochondria-targeted antioxidants. While triphenyl phosphonium (TPP+)-conjugated antioxidants, such as MitoQ and SKQ1, are extensively utilized, their mitochondrial accumulation strictly depends on a highly negative ΔΨm [73]. Because DOX treatment severely dissipates ΔΨm, the cellular uptake of these lipophilic cations is intrinsically compromised when they are needed most. Furthermore, under severe oxidative stress, ubiquinone derivatives like MitoQ run the risk of acting as pro-oxidants via redox cycling [74]. In contrast, the mitochondria-targeted peptide, SS-31 selectively localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane and directly stabilizes cardiolipin in a ΔΨm-independent manner [75,76]. By preserving cardiolipin integrity, SS-31 prevents electron leakage from the respiratory chain and inhibits cytochrome c peroxidase activity, thereby effectively suppressing mitochondrial H2O2 generation at its source [42,77]. This dual action of attenuating the H2O2 burden and protecting the inner membrane makes SS-31 a safer and mechanistically sound alternative.

To empirically validate this rationale, we first conducted a comparative in vivo study in WT mice to assess the efficacy and systemic safety of MitoQ, SkQ1, and SS-31 against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. S2). Echocardiographic analysis revealed that SS-31 administration significantly restored cardiac function, evident in improved EF and FS, whereas MitoQ and SkQ1 failed to provide significant cardioprotection (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Moreover, monitoring of body weight to assess systemic toxicity revealed that mice co-treated with MitoQ and SKQ1 exhibited severe body weight loss (average 4–6 g), indicating substantial systemic toxicity. Conversely, the SS-31-treated group maintained a body weight profile similar to the DOX-alone group, highlighting its superior systemic safety (Supplementary Fig. S2B).

Based on these compelling results, we selected SS-31 to investigate whether protecting the mitochondrial inner membrane could rescue cardiac dysfunction in our PrxⅢ KO model (Fig. 11). Echocardiographic assessments demonstrated that DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction was profoundly exacerbated in PrxⅢ KO mice compared to WT mice, as shown by drastic declines in EF, FS, SV, and CO, and alongside LVESV (Fig. 11A). Remarkably, daily SS-31 administration successfully rescued this exacerbated cardiotoxicity in PrxⅢ KO mice, restoring all functional parameters to levels comparable to or better than those of DOX-treated WT mice. Furthermore, we measured serum cTnI levels to evaluate structural cardiomyocyte injury. Consistent with the functional data, the severely elevated cTnI levels observed in DOX-treated PrxⅢ KO mice were markedly attenuated by SS-31 treatment (Fig. 11B).

Although TEM imaging of the rescue cohort could not be performed — as cardiac tissues were snap-frozen at −80 °C to preserve utility for protein- and RNA-based biochemical analyses rather than fixed in glutaraldehyde required for ultrastructural preservation — TEM evidence for DOX-induced mitochondrial ultrastructural damage in PrxIII KO hearts is already documented in Fig. 9C, establishing the structural phenotype from which SS-31-mediated rescue is assessed.

To assess mitochondrial structural integrity at the molecular level, we performed immunoblot analysis on cardiac tissue lysates. OPA1, a key inner mitochondrial membrane fusion protein, was analyzed as L-OPA1/S-OPA1 ratio, which reflects the balance between fusion-competent (long) and cleaved (short) forms. The L-OPA1/S-OPA1 ratio was significantly decreased in DOX-treated PrxⅢ KO hearts compared with control groups, indicating enhanced proteolytic processing of OPA1 under conditions of excessive mitochondrial H2O2. SS-31 co-treatment significantly restored the L-OPA1/S-OPA1 ratio in KO mice, suggesting that cardiolipin stabilization by SS-31 protects OPA1 from stress-induced cleavage (Fig. 11C). TOM20, an outer mitochondrial membrane marker reflecting mitochondrial mass, was decreased in DOX-treated hearts, with a more pronounced reduction in PrxⅢ KO mice. SS-31 treatment partially restored TOM20 levels, indicating preservation of mitochondrial content (Fig. 11C).

Apoptosis markers were also assessed by immunoblot. Bax protein levels were significantly elevated in DOX-treated PrxⅢ KO hearts. SS-31 co-treatment reduced Bax expression. Cleaved caspase-3, the executioner caspase indicative of active apoptosis, was increased in DOX-treated hearts and further elevated in PrxⅢ KO mice. SS-31 treatment attenuated cleaved caspase-3 levels, confirming that mitochondria-targeted antioxidant intervention reduces DOX-induced apoptotic signaling (Fig. 11D).

To further validate the rescue effect of SS-31 at the transcriptional level and to confirm concordance with the public transcriptomic data (Fig. 10), we performed qPCR analysis on cardiac tissues from the same cohort of PrxIII WT and KO mice across the three treatment groups. Bnip3 mRNA was significantly elevated in DOX-treated hearts, with a more pronounced increase in PrxIII KO mice, and this elevation was attenuated by SS-31 co-treatment (Fig. 11E). Similarly, Pink1 and Parkin (Prkn) mRNA levels were increased in DOX-treated PrxIII KO hearts compared with WT controls, and SS-31 partially restored these levels (Fig. 11F and G). Bax mRNA showed a significant increase specifically in DOX-treated PrxIII KO hearts, which was significantly reduced by SS-31 (Fig. 11H). Collectively, these qPCR data demonstrate that PrxIII deficiency exacerbates DOX-induced transcriptional activation of mitophagy and apoptosis pathways in vivo, and that SS-31 intervention rescues these transcriptional alterations, providing convergent molecular validation fully consistent with the public transcriptome data (Fig. 10) and our in vitro mechanistic findings.

These data demonstrate that SS-31 effectively rescues DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction and mitigates molecular markers of mitochondrial structural compromise and apoptosis in PrxⅢ KO hearts. These findings establish that mitochondria-targeted cardiolipin stabilization can functionally compensate for the loss of PrxⅢ-mediated H2O2 detoxification, reinforcing the central role of mitochondrial oxidative stress in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized rat cardiomyocytes with altered expression levels of mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme PrxⅢ to elucidate the specific role of mitochondrial H2O2 levels in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. PrxⅢ is exclusively localized to mitochondria and is primarily responsible for detoxifying mitochondrial H2O2. Unlike catalase or Gpx, which act in other cellular compartments, PrxⅢ directly removes H2O2 within the matrix with high catalytic efficiency and thus defines the physiological redox set-point of mitochondria [17]. Among mitochondrial ROS, H2O2 exhibits high membrane permeability and can diffuse throughout the cell, generating hydroxy radicals via the Fenton reaction and amplifying oxidative damage [15]. Hence, our findings specifically highlighted mitochondrial H2O2 as a critical mediator of DOX-induced cardiac injury.

DOX-induced cardiotoxicity occurs primarily through mitochondrial oxidative stress, initiated by accumulation of DOX in the mitochondrial inner membrane, generating superoxide radicals (O2•−) via electron leakage from the ETC [78]. O2•− is rapidly converted to H2O2 by mitochondrial superoxide dismutase (SOD2), leading to oxidative damage within mitochondria. Increased mitochondrial H2O2 levels induce cardiolipin oxidation, critically damaging the mitochondrial inner membrane. Consistent with this, we observed significantly elevated mitochondrial H2O2 and exacerbated cardiolipin oxidation after DOX treatment in PrxⅢ-depleted cells compared to controls. Cardiolipin plays essential roles in ATP-synthase assembly and the formation of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), the opening of which leads to loss of ΔΨm and initiation of apoptosis [44]. Our data clearly demonstrated greater ΔΨm dissipation in PrxⅢ-depleted cells following DOX treatment, underscoring the importance of PrxⅢ in mitochondrial membrane protection. Beyond morphological changes, we confirmed that excessive mitochondrial H2O2 leads to bioenergetic collapse. PrxⅢ depletion significantly reduced ATP production and OCR, demonstrating that PrxⅢ-mediated redox buffering preserves oxidative phosphorylation and prevents respiratory failure.

Recently, MQC mechanisms, including mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial dynamics (fusion and fission) and mitophagy, have gained considerable attention as critical regulators of mitochondrial function and cellular survival under stress conditions [22,79,80]. We found that PrxⅢ deficiency suppressed the biosynthetic arm of MQC. Expression of PGC-1α, Nrf1, and mtTFA was markedly reduced, accompanied by decreased mtDNA-encoded cytochrome c oxidase transcripts. Interestingly, PGC-1α exhibited a biphasic response to DOX-induced oxidative stress: moderate mitochondrial H2O2 in control cells transiently up-regulated PGC-1α, representing an adaptive transcriptional attempt to reinforce mitochondrial renewal, whereas excessive mitochondrial H2O2 in PrxⅢ-deficient cells led to pronounced decline in both PGC-1α expression and protein stability, accompanied by broad suppression of downstream biogenic genes. This apparent discrepancy reflects the temporal and redox-sensitive nature of the PGC-1α signaling cascade. PGC-1α is rapidly activated through post-translational modifications [48,81,82], but sustained induction of its targets requires stable redox homeostasis and intact nuclear-mitochondrial communication. Moreover, excessive ROS can directly compromise the stability or activity of NRF1 and TFAM proteins via oxidative modification [83,84] or p53-mediated transcriptional repression [85,86], further blocking the biogenic program. Thus, PrxⅢ functions as a pivotal redox buffer that preserves the mitochondrial biogenesis axis.

Mitochondria balance between fusion and fission maintains mitochondrial homeostasis by repairing or removing damaged mitochondria [87]. However, the change of mitochondrial dynamics in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity remains controversial [9,27,88,89]. Here, we clearly demonstrated that PrxⅢ depletion leads to excessive mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation, which subsequently impairs mitochondrial fusion by reducing the expression of fusion proteins (Mfn1, Mfn2, and OPA1). Conversely, moderate mitochondrial H2O2 levels induced by DOX in PrxⅢ-expressing cells enhanced the expression of these fusion proteins. Notably, OPA1 expression exhibited the most prominent change, potentially due to its crucial role in inner mitochondrial membrane fusion, closely interacting with cardiolipin [[90], [91], [92]]. In contrast, mitochondrial fission, mediated by a GTPase called Drp1, facilitates removal of irreversibly damaged mitochondria. Drp1 activity is regulated by post-translational modifications, particularly phosphorylation at Ser616, enhancing Drp1 oligomerization and mitochondrial fission [93,94]. Our study observed reduced Drp1 phosphorylation upon DOX treatment, suggesting impaired mitochondrial fission. However, Drp1 phosphorylation was unaffected by PrxⅢ depletion, indicating mitochondrial fusion may be more sensitive to changes in mitochondrial H2O2 compared to fission under DOX-induced stress conditions.

Autophagy is an indispensable catabolic process that degrades cytoplasmic material and damaged organelles, such as mitochondria or protein aggregates, thus maintaining cell survival [56,95]. Under normal physiological conditions, autophagy proceeds efficiently, removing dysfunctional mitochondria and preventing excessive ROS production [61]. However, when lysosomal function is compromised, autophagic flux is impaired, leading to cellular damage. Even when autophagy is excessive, cell death can increase as important cell components are removed [66,96]. Recent studies showed that excessive ROS impairs autophagy initiation by oxidizing Atg proteins [97] and disrupting lysosomal acidification [61,62]. In our study, we clearly showed that PrxⅢ depletion-induced excessive mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation markedly impaired autophagosome formation and autophagic flux through lysosomal dysfunction, while moderate H2O2 facilitated autophagic flux as an adaptive response. Interestingly, previous reports have shown that DOX affects autophagic flux in a dose-dependent manner. Low doses or early phases of DOX treatment activate autophagy, whereas large doses or late phases of DOX treatment inhibit autophagy, both inducing cellular death [36]. Our current results align with these observations, demonstrating that mitochondrial H2O2 levels can explain this dose-dependent regulation of autophagy by DOX. While we elucidated lysosomal blockage and autophagic flux damage, autophagy initiation and the effects of mitochondrial H2O2 on Atg proteins require further research.

Mitophagy is a specialized form of selective autophagy that specifically targets damaged mitochondria for degradation, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis. Mitophagy initiation involves recognition of damaged mitochondria, followed by their sequestration into autophagosomes and subsequent fusion with lysosomes for degradation. Two major pathways have been widely studied to mediate this selective mitochondrial clearance: the ubiquitin-dependent PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin pathway and the ubiquitin-independent receptor-mediated mitophagy pathway [9,98]. In our study, we specifically investigated mitophagy by analyzing PINK1/Parkin as the representative ubiquitin-dependent pathway and BNIP3 as the representative receptor-mediated pathway to comprehensively understand how mitochondrial H2O2 accumulation under DOX-induced oxidative stress modulates selective mitochondrial removal. BNIP3 has been previously identified as highly responsive to oxidative stress conditions, including those induced by DOX [99,100], and is linked functionally to the PINK1/Parkin pathway through regulation of PINK1 stability and cleavage [101]. Furthermore, transcription control of both the PINK1/parkin and BNIP3 pathways via the FOXO3a transcription factor further underscores their coordinated regulation under oxidative stress conditions [88,93]. Thus, by focusing on these two critical mitophagy parameters –PINK1/Parkin and BNIP3– we provide comprehensive mechanistic insight into MQC under varying mitochondrial H2O2 stress conditions induced by DOX treatment. Our findings clearly demonstrate how differing mitochondrial H2O2 levels distinctly modulate mitophagy pathways, revealing adaptive versus maladaptive mitochondrial responses to DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Our in vivo findings translate the mechanistic framework established in vitro into physiologically relevant cardiac pathology. Critically, the pattern of injury in PrxⅢ KO mice — pronounced systolic dysfunction and mitochondrial ultrastructural damage occurring in the absence of concurrent hypertrophic remodeling or fibrosis — argues that the primary driver of cardiac dysfunction is acute mitochondrial quality failure rather than chronic structural remodeling. This distinction carries important clinical significance: it suggests that the pathological cascade leading to overt heart dysfunction may be reversible at an early stage, before fibrotic changes become irreversible, provided that mitochondrial oxidative stress is appropriately controlled. The temporal concordance between echocardiographic decline and mitochondrial structural deterioration — without accompanying fibrosis — further supports the in vitro finding that excessive mitochondrial H2O2 is sufficient to drive cardiomyocyte dysfunction independently of extracellular matrix pathology.

The transcriptomic data provide an additional layer of interpretive significance beyond mere pathway confirmation. The consistent directionality of mitophagy, mitochondrial fusion, biogenesis, and apoptosis gene dysregulation across three independent public DOX cardiotoxicity datasets — spanning different DOX regimens, time points, and animal cohorts — demonstrates that the pathway alterations identified in PrxⅢ-depleted cardiomyocytes are not artifacts of the in vitro system, but reflect core transcriptional responses to DOX-induced mitochondrial oxidative stress that are conserved across experimental contexts. Furthermore, the amplification of these transcriptional changes in PrxⅢ KO hearts (Fig. 11E–H), together with their attenuation by SS-31, establishes PrxⅢ as a molecular gatekeeper whose enzymatic activity directly determines the amplitude and reversibility of the transcriptional stress response to DOX.

The molecular data from the SS-31 rescue experiment reveal a mechanistically coherent chain of protection that extends beyond simple ROS scavenging. The restoration of the L-OPA1/S-OPA1 ratio by SS-31 is particularly informative: OPA1 cleavage from its fusion-competent long form (L-OPA1) to the short form (S-OPA1) is mediated by the stress-activated inner membrane protease OMA1, which is potently activated by oxidative stress and ΔΨm dissipation [102,103]. Of particular mechanistic relevance, inactivation of PrxⅢ has been shown to directly correlate with OMA1 activation and OPA1 processing [102], consistent with the notion that loss of PrxⅢ-mediated H2O2 detoxification creates a permissive redox environment for OMA1 hyperactivation and consequent inner membrane structural remodeling. By stabilizing cardiolipin directly, SS-31 interrupts this upstream trigger of OMA1 activation, thereby preserving OPA1 in its fusion-competent state rather than simply scavenging H2O2 after oxidative damage has already propagated. This positions cardiolipin integrity as the critical upstream determinant of cristae architecture and mitochondrial fusion competence — consistent with our in vitro finding that OPA1 exhibited the most prominent and DOX-sensitive change among all fusion proteins examined. The partial restoration of TOM20 further suggests that SS-31 not only arrests ongoing mitochondrial loss but may also relieve the excessive mitophagic clearance driven by PINK1/Parkin and BNIP3 pathway activation, allowing partial recovery of mitochondrial mass. The attenuation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 confirms that this structural stabilization propagates downstream to suppress apoptotic commitment.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that targeting the cardiolipin–OPA1 axis at the inner membrane can functionally substitute for the upstream enzymatic H2O2-detoxification capacity of PrxⅢ, even in its complete absence. This mechanistic equivalence has direct translational relevance: elamipretide (SS-31) has already demonstrated cardioprotective potential in multiple preclinical models and has entered clinical evaluation [104], and our findings provide a specific mechanistic rationale for its potential utility in anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity, particularly in patients whose mitochondrial antioxidant capacity may be constitutively reduced by genetic variation, aging, or comorbid conditions.

In summary, our study conclusively demonstrated that precise mitochondrial H2O2 regulation through the mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme PrxⅢ plays a critical protective role against DOX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired MQC, and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes. Importantly, the present study identifies the redox-sensitive transcription factor FOXO3a as a molecular switch that links mitochondrial H2O2 levels to divergent cellular responses [40,105,106]. Moderate increases in mitochondrial H2O2 in PrxⅢ-expressing cells promoted nuclear translocation of FOXO3a [107,108], consistent with activation of antioxidant and mitophagy-related genes that enhance mitochondrial turnover. Conversely, excessive H2O2 accumulation in PrxⅢ-deficient cells disrupted FOXO3a nuclear localization, likely due to irreversible oxidative modification [109] or phosphorylation by stress kinases [110], thereby preventing activation of adaptive programs. Consequently, the cellular response shifts from adaptive to maladaptive, transitioning from redox signaling to oxidative injury. This duality provides a mechanistic explanation for how moderate versus excessive H2O2 levels produce fundamentally different outcomes—adaptive redox signaling versus aggressive oxidative stress—under DOX treatment.

The clear cause-effect relationships established here highlight mitochondrial H2O2 as a central regulatory node controlling mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy. Thus, therapeutic strategies aiming to enhance mitochondrial antioxidant capacity or directly targeting mitochondrial oxidative stress pathways—such as mitochondria-targeted antioxidants (e.g., MitoQ, SkQ1, or SS-31)—may offer promising approaches to mitigate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [36,111]. These findings not only firmly link the preservation of mitochondrial structural integrity to cardioprotection but also provide direct evidence that targeting mitochondrial oxidative stress is a highly efficacious clinical strategy [112]. Further exploration and clinical validation of these mitochondrial-targeted interventions could lead to significantly improved cardioprotective outcomes in patients undergoing chemotherapy with DOX.

Conclusions

Our study provides important insight into how mitochondrial H2O2 critically influences DOX-induced cardiac toxicity through differential regulation of mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy. Specifically, we demonstrate that mitochondrial H2O2 acts as a crucial upstream mediator determining the functional outcomes of the MQC system under DOX-induced stress conditions. Under moderate mitochondrial oxidative stress, mitochondrial fusion and mitophagy processes are activated as adaptive responses, facilitating efficient removal or repair of damaged mitochondria and thereby preserving cardiomyocyte homeostasis. However, severe oxidative stress conditions, as observed in PrxⅢ-depleted cardiomyocytes exposed to DOX, cause substantial decline of mitochondrial biogenesis, impairment of mitochondrial fusion, significantly reduce mitophagy capacity, and disrupt lysosome function, collectively blocking autophagic flux. Consequently, such severe mitochondrial oxidative stress conditions trigger profound mitochondrial damage and eventually apoptotic cell death. Thus, our findings clearly establish a direct cause-effect relationship between mitochondrial H2O2 levels and MQC function, providing critical evidence that maintaining appropriate mitochondrial H2O2 levels through antioxidant systems, particularly PrxⅢ, is essential for cardiomyocyte protection against DOX-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Targeting mitochondrial H2O2 levels and enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant capacity, therefore, represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
