A metacognitive mediation model of sleep quality: the roles of rumination and anxiety sensitivity in a community sample
Authors: Shahram Mohammadkhani, Parya Zamani, Mehdi Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMetacognitive theory suggests that maladaptive beliefs about thinking are associated with the cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS), which is characterized by repetitive negative thinking and heightened threat monitoring. This study examined a dual-mediation model in which rumination and anxiety sensitivity were tested simultaneously as mediators of the relationship between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality in a nonclinical sample.MethodsA total of 346 Iranian adults (18–60 years) completed validated self-report measures of metacognitive beliefs, rumination, anxiety sensitivity, and sleep quality. Structural equation modeling (SEM) with bias-corrected bootstrapping (2,000 resamples) was used to test the hypothesized model.ResultsThe model demonstrated acceptable fit to the data (CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.074). Metacognitive beliefs were not significantly associated with sleep quality after including the mediators. However, significant indirect effects were observed through rumination (β = 0.32, 95% CI [0.20, 0.40]) and anxiety sensitivity (β = 0.28, 95% CI [0.17, 0.37]). The model explained 50% of the variance in sleep quality.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that metacognitive beliefs are statistically associated with poorer sleep quality indirectly through rumination and anxiety sensitivity in a community sample. These results support the value of examining transdiagnostic cognitive–emotional processes in sleep research. Due to the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-report measures, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships and clinical utility of these pathways.

Introduction

Sleep disturbances, including difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep, represent one of the most prevalent health complaints worldwide and constitute a substantial public health burden [14, 16]. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately one third of adults report intermittent sleep problems, and nearly 10% meet criteria for persistent insomnia symptoms associated with daytime impairment [14]. Poor sleep quality has been consistently linked to impairments in cognitive functioning, emotion regulation, and increased risk for psychiatric and cardiometabolic disorders [7, 19]. Recent integrative reviews have highlighted the importance of both sleep-specific and transdiagnostic cognitive–emotional processes in the development and maintenance of sleep disturbance [1, 4].

Cognitive–behavioral models of insomnia have established that maladaptive sleep-related cognitions (e.g., catastrophizing about the consequences of poor sleep) and associated safety behaviors play a central role in perpetuating insomnia symptoms [7, 13]. These models have led to the development of cognitive–behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which remains the first-line treatment with strong empirical support [5, 19]. The strength of CBT-I lies in its specificity to sleep-related thoughts and behaviors, allowing for targeted and parsimonious intervention.

Complementary to these disorder-specific approaches, transdiagnostic frameworks offer additional insight by examining broader cognitive and emotional processes that may operate across psychological conditions, including sleep disturbance. Lifestyle and behavioral factors such as physical activity and circadian patterns have also been linked to sleep quality and related psychological outcomes [8, 10]. Metacognitive theory [24] provides one such transdiagnostic framework. Rather than focusing on the content of thoughts, metacognitive theory emphasizes beliefs about thinking itself—particularly beliefs regarding the uncontrollability, danger, and utility of repetitive negative thinking. According to this model, maladaptive metacognitive beliefs contribute to the activation and persistence of the cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS), characterized by rumination, worry, and heightened threat monitoring. Prolonged engagement in these processes can generate cognitive and physiological arousal that interferes with sleep initiation and maintenance.

Empirical studies have shown associations between maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and sleep disturbance, although findings appear stronger in clinical samples than in nonclinical populations [6, 12, 17]. Two key transdiagnostic processes that may link metacognitive beliefs to sleep quality are rumination and anxiety sensitivity. Rumination involves repetitive and passive focus on distress and its causes and consequences [15] and represents a core feature of the CAS. Anxiety sensitivity, defined as the fear of anxiety-related bodily sensations due to beliefs about their harmful consequences [22], amplifies hyperarousal and vigilance toward somatic sensations at bedtime.

While rumination and anxiety sensitivity have each been independently associated with poorer sleep quality, relatively few studies have examined their simultaneous and joint contribution within a single statistical model. Testing these two theoretically related but distinct pathways (perseverative cognition and fear of arousal) in parallel is important because it allows evaluation of whether they account for unique variance in sleep quality and collectively explain the relationship between metacognitive beliefs and sleep outcomes. The primary aim of the present study was therefore to test a dual-mediation model in which rumination and anxiety sensitivity simultaneously mediate the association between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality in a community sample.

Although previous research has documented individual pathways, integrative latent variable models examining both mediators jointly remain limited, particularly in non-Western community samples. The current study addresses this gap by employing structural equation modeling to examine these transdiagnostic processes in a sample of Iranian adults. We hypothesized that (1) metacognitive beliefs would be positively associated with both rumination and anxiety sensitivity, (2) rumination and anxiety sensitivity would be associated with poorer sleep quality, and (3) the relationship between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality would be primarily indirect, operating through these two parallel mediators.

By situating sleep quality within a broader metacognitive and transdiagnostic framework while acknowledging the established value of sleep-specific models, the present study seeks to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the cognitive–emotional factors associated with individual differences in sleep quality.

Method study design

This study employed a cross-sectional correlational design using structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine the relationships among metacognitive beliefs, rumination, anxiety sensitivity, and sleep quality at the latent variable level. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational research [23].

Participants and procedure

Participants were recruited between March and June 2024 through online advertisements on social media platforms (Instagram, Telegram, and WhatsApp) and community mailing lists. Eligibility criteria included: (a) age between 18 and 60 years, (b) current residence in Iran, (c) fluency in Persian, and (d) provision of informed consent. Individuals who self-reported a history of severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder) or serious neurological conditions known to substantially affect sleep were excluded based on their responses to screening questions.

A total of 367 individuals accessed the survey link. After excluding incomplete responses and ineligible cases, the final analytic sample comprised 346 adults (70.5% women, n = 244). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 55 years (women: M = 29.94, SD = 7.50; men: M = 32.86, SD = 7.36). The majority had university-level education and lived in urban areas.

Data were collected using a secure online survey platform (Google Forms). Participants provided electronic informed consent before completing the questionnaires. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated. Accordingly, the sample should be considered an urban, online convenience sample rather than a representative community sample.

Sample size

The final sample size (N = 346) exceeds commonly recommended thresholds for stable estimation of structural equation models with similar complexity [9, 26]. No formal a priori power analysis or Monte Carlo simulation was conducted prior to data collection. This is acknowledged as a limitation in the “Discussion” section.

Measures

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

Sleep quality during the previous month was assessed with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; [3]. The PSQI consists of 19 items that generate seven component scores. For latent variable modeling, six components (subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction) were used as indicators of the latent sleep quality construct. The habitual sleep efficiency component was excluded due to very high collinearity with sleep duration (r = 0.85), which could destabilize model estimation. The remaining six indicators were retained because they collectively capture the core multidimensional aspects of subjective sleep quality as defined by the PSQI. Internal consistency of the global score in the present sample was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.80).

Metacognitive Beliefs (MCQ-30)

Metacognitive beliefs were measured using the Metacognitions Questionnaire–30 (MCQ-30; [25]. The five subscales (positive beliefs about worry, negative beliefs about uncontrollability and danger, cognitive confidence, need for thought control, and cognitive self-consciousness) served as indicators of a higher-order latent metacognitive beliefs factor. Internal consistency was high (α = 0.91).

Response Style Questionnaire (RSQ) – Rumination Subscale

Rumination was assessed with the 22-item rumination subscale of the Response Styles Questionnaire (RSQ; [15]. In line with the original conceptualization, the brooding and reflection subscales were computed and used as the two indicators of the latent rumination construct. No item parceling was applied. Internal consistency was excellent (α = 0.93).

Anxiety Sensitivity Index–Revised (ASI-R)

Anxiety sensitivity was measured using the 36-item Anxiety Sensitivity Index–Revised (ASI-R; [22]. The four subscales (cardiovascular/gastrointestinal concerns, respiratory concerns, publicly observable anxiety reactions, and cognitive dyscontrol) were specified as indicators of the latent anxiety sensitivity construct. Internal consistency was excellent (α = 0.97).

Data screening and preliminary analyses

Data were screened for missing values, outliers, and normality assumptions. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) and handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML). Univariate normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics (|skewness|< 3, |kurtosis|< 5), and multivariate normality was examined via Mardia’s coefficient. Multivariate outliers were evaluated using Mahalanobis distance (p < 0.001). No extreme outliers were identified that warranted removal.

Statistical analysis

First, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to evaluate the measurement model. Second, the structural model was tested with metacognitive beliefs as the exogenous latent variable, rumination and anxiety sensitivity as parallel mediators, and sleep quality as the endogenous outcome. Model fit was assessed using χ2, χ2/df, RMSEA, SRMR, CFI, and TLI. Acceptable fit was defined as CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA and SRMR ≤ 0.08 [9].

Indirect effects were examined using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 2,000 resamples [18]. Potential covariates (education, socioeconomic status, and self-reported comorbid conditions) were tested but not retained in the final model due to nonsignificant associations.

Measurement model

Prior to testing the structural model, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the measurement model. All observed indicators loaded significantly onto their respective latent constructs (all ps < 0.001). Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.62 to 0.88, indicating adequate convergent validity for all latent variables.

The measurement model provided acceptable fit to the data: χ2(125) = 345.99, p < 0.001; χ2/df = 2.83; RMSEA = 0.073 (90% CI [0.064, 0.082]); SRMR = 0.059; CFI = 0.97; TLI = 0.96. These indices supported the adequacy of the proposed factor structure and allowed progression to the structural model.

Indirect effects

Bias-corrected bootstrap analyses (2,000 resamples) indicated significant indirect associations between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality through both mediators. The indirect effect via rumination was significant (β = 0.32, 95% CI [0.20, 0.40]), and the indirect effect via anxiety sensitivity was also significant (β = 0.28, 95% CI [0.17, 0.37]). Because both specific indirect effects were statistically significant while the direct effect was not, the results are consistent with an indirect-only mediation model [18]. Discussion

Overview of findings

The present study tested a dual-mediation model examining whether rumination and anxiety sensitivity simultaneously mediate the relationship between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality in a community sample of Iranian adults. The results supported the hypothesized model. Metacognitive beliefs were significantly associated with higher levels of rumination and anxiety sensitivity, both of which, in turn, were significantly associated with poorer sleep quality. Once the two mediators were included, the direct association between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality was no longer statistically significant. Bootstrap analyses confirmed significant indirect effects through both rumination and anxiety sensitivity. Overall, the model accounted for 50% of the variance in sleep quality.

Conceptual and theoretical implications

These findings are consistent with metacognitive theory [24], which posits that maladaptive beliefs about thinking contribute to prolonged cognitive and emotional arousal via the cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS). The current results suggest that in nonclinical populations, this process may largely operate through two transdiagnostic pathways: perseverative thinking (rumination) and heightened fear of arousal-related bodily sensations (anxiety sensitivity).

Importantly, the study examined these two mediators simultaneously within a single latent variable model. This joint examination represents a methodological and conceptual advance over previous studies that have typically tested single mediators in isolation [4, 21]. The findings indicate that rumination and anxiety sensitivity each explain unique variance in sleep quality, highlighting the value of considering both cognitive and affective-somatic processes together.

The present results complement rather than compete with established cognitive-behavioral models of insomnia [7, 13]. While cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) focuses on sleep-specific cognitions and behaviors—a clear strength of this approach [19]—transdiagnostic processes such as those examined here may represent more distal vulnerabilities that contribute to sleep disturbance. Because sleep-specific cognitive measures were not included in this study, we cannot determine the relative or incremental contribution of transdiagnostic versus sleep-specific factors.

Community versus clinical samples

The absence of a significant direct effect between metacognitive beliefs and sleep quality in this community sample is consistent with some previous nonclinical studies [6, 20] but differs from findings often reported in clinical insomnia populations [12, 17]. This pattern suggests that metacognitive beliefs may be more indirectly related to sleep quality among individuals with milder complaints, whereas direct associations may become more prominent at higher levels of symptom severity. However, this interpretation remains tentative given the cross-sectional nature of the data.

Clinical and research implications

The current findings suggest that rumination and anxiety sensitivity may be promising targets for preventive interventions aimed at improving sleep quality in nonclinical populations. Emerging evidence indicates that interventions such as metacognitive therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), which target repetitive negative thinking and experiential avoidance, can improve sleep quality [27]. Nevertheless, these implications should be considered preliminary until supported by longitudinal and experimental evidence.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions regarding causality or temporal ordering of the variables [11]. Second, all constructs were assessed via self-report questionnaires, which may have inflated the observed associations due to shared method variance. The substantial amount of variance explained in sleep quality (50%) may partly reflect this shared method variance. Third, the sample was an urban, online convenience sample with a high proportion of women and highly educated participants, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, although potential covariates were examined, the model did not control for depressive symptoms, generalized anxiety symptoms, or psychotropic medication use—variables known to influence metacognition, rumination, anxiety sensitivity, and sleep quality [1, 2]. Fifth, the exclusion of participants with severe psychiatric conditions relied solely on self-report rather than structured clinical assessment. Finally, the absence of sleep-specific cognitive measures (e.g., DBAS) prevents conclusions about their role relative to the transdiagnostic processes examined here. Finally, no a priori power analysis or Monte Carlo simulation was conducted prior to data collection. Although the sample size was adequate for the complexity of the structural equation model, future studies would benefit from formal power estimation procedures to strengthen study design and statistical inference.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study found that metacognitive beliefs are statistically associated with poorer sleep quality indirectly through rumination and anxiety sensitivity in a community sample of Iranian adults. By testing these two theoretically relevant mediators simultaneously within a latent variable framework, the study contributes to a more integrative understanding of cognitive–emotional factors involved in sleep quality. Future longitudinal, experimental, and multi-method studies are needed to clarify the causal status, cultural generalizability, and clinical utility of these pathways.
