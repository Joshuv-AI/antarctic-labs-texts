Toward economically viable green hydrogen production: enhanced catalytic atom-efficiency, dual-functional systems, and non-traditional water sources
Authors: Yuru Liu, Pengpeng Wu, Lin Huang, Junyan Yang, Ningning Song, Jiuyang He, Zhanjun Guo, Minmin Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The light-driven hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) represents a promising renewable energy conversion technology to combat the global energy crisis, particularly as the field shifts toward utilizing abundant water sources including wastewater and seawater rather than freshwater. However, achieving an economically viable levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) requires overcoming several fundamental challenges. First, the leading photocatalytic systems rely heavily on Pt cocatalysts to achieve sufficient surface reaction kinetics, the scarcity and high cost of which severely limit scalability. Second, conventional photocatalytic HER systems consume sacrificial agents (e.g., Na2S, Na2SO3, and TEOA) as hole scavengers, elevating the LCOH through continuous reagent consumption while forfeiting opportunities for value creation. Third, the extensive usage of freshwater in the HER process competes with the freshwater usage for humans and agricultural purposes. In this review, we briefly introduce the fundamentals of the light-driven HER process and establish a set of standardized parameters for evaluating both photocatalytic performance and techno-economic feasibility. Subsequently, we systematically review the recent advances in three key areas: (1) strategies for enhancing the catalytic atom-efficiency, (2) innovative dual-functional photocatalytic systems that productively harness photogenerated holes to synthesize value-added products, including through the utilization of wastewater, and (3) seawater-based feedstocks. By integrating LCOH analysis with technical performance evaluation across diverse strategies and water sources, this review aims to accelerate the transition toward sustainable, green and cost-competitive photocatalytic hydrogen production.

Introduction

With the concerns of rising global energy demand and intensifying environmental pressures from fossil fuels, clean and sustainable energy systems have emerged as a global imperative. While renewable energy sources such as solar, wind, and hydropower are increasingly deployed at scale, they remain constrained by intermittency, geographic limitations, and storage challenges.1 These constraints necessitate complementary energy storage solutions that can decouple energy generation from consumption. Thus, hydrogen, with high energy density and clean combustion profile, has been regarded as one of the key energy sources for achieving carbon neutrality.2–7 Currently, industrial hydrogen production depends mainly on fossil fuel reforming8–10 and water electrolysis.11–13 However, the former process generates significant carbon emissions, whereas the latter is limited by high electrical energy and capital inputs with infrastructural constraints.14–16 Thus, the light-driven hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), a process that directly converts solar energy into chemical energy via a clean and carbon-neutral route, represents a promising sustainable strategy for hydrogen production, as evidenced by the increasing number of publications (Fig. 1a).17–19 However, achieving an economically viable levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) for industrial deployment remains elusive due to several critical challenges.20,21

First, the photocatalytic HER efficiency remains highly impacted by light absorption ranges, photogenerated carrier recombination rates, and surface reaction kinetics.22–24 To improve this situation, researchers developed various modification strategies, including (1) constructing heterojunctions to enhance light absorption via band engineering,25–27 (2) utilizing element doping to modulate the electronic structure and reaction activity,28–31 and (3) loading cocatalysts to introduce active sites to lower the overpotential, thus accelerating surface reactions.32–35 Among these strategies, cocatalyst loading is widely adopted due to its simple structural design, mature preparation methods, and effectivity in achieving high HER performance, with platinum (Pt) as the leading cocatalyst.36–38 However, the high cost and limited natural reserves of Pt significantly increased the LCOH, thus driving research toward the optimization of Pt utilization efficiency or the development of earth-abundant element alternatives (Fig. 1b).39,40 Second, most light-driven HER systems rely on sacrificial agents (e.g., TEOA, Na2S/Na2SO3, and methanol), bypassing the high overpotential requirement for the oxygen evolution reaction, to scavenge photogenerated holes and enhance the HER efficiency (Fig. 1c).41–43 However, many sacrificial agents are expensive, non-recyclable, and potentially polluting, and their oxidative products offer no economic value, thus leading to an increased LCOH, restricting the technology's practical applicability.44,45 Furthermore, it leads to poor comparability of performance and economically feasible data across different studies. In addition, the extensive usage of fresh water for hydrogen generation competes with human usage. Thus, significant efforts have been made to utilize the photo-generated holes for creating value-added products or in wastewater or seawater to lower the LCOH.

Numerous recent reviews have addressed various aspects of photocatalytic hydrogen production, including catalyst design,46–48 noble-metal replacement,47,49 photo reforming/value-added oxidation,50–52 seawater splitting,21 and the costs and industrial scaling of these systems. Unlike previous reviews that discuss individual topics in isolation, this work uniquely integrates these pillars under a unified LCOH-guided framework, aiming to prompt critical reflection on the industrial realization of photocatalytic green hydrogen. In this review, we begin with a brief introduction of the photocatalytic HER process and a set of standardized parameters to evaluate its photocatalytic performance and economic feasibility. Next, we summarize the recent efforts made for developing novel cocatalysts (including single-atom metals, metal oxides, and metal sulfides) to enhance the light-driven HER and lower the LCOH. Subsequently, we review the current efforts focused on utilizing photo-generated holes for value creation, including polymer degradation, organic synthesis and wastewater treatment. Furthermore, we discuss the recent efforts made to replace pure water with abundant seawater, thereby decreasing the LCOH to reach commercial feasibility. Finally, we present a perspective on the remaining challenges and potential future directions for applying nanomaterials for sustainable green hydrogen production from laboratory setting to industrial implementation.

Mechanism of photocatalytic HER processes

A brief introduction to the light-driven HER process

The light-driven HER is a multi-step process (Fig. 2): (1) photo-excitation of electron–hole (e−/h+) pairs upon absorbing photons exceeding the bandgap; (2) migration of carriers to the surface via built-in electric fields; and (3) surface redox reactions at active sites. The typical time-scale for each step is also outlined in Fig. 2. While metal single atoms, metal oxides, and metal sulfides all operate within the overarching three-step macro-framework shown in Fig. 2, subtle distinctions exist in their specific charge transfer pathways and catalytic mechanisms. Specifically, metal single atoms function as localized electron-trapping and reaction active sites. Metal oxides, owing to their deeper (more positive) valence band positions, act predominantly as hole-trapping/extraction centers. Metal sulfides, benefiting from well-matched conduction band levels, high intrinsic electronic conductivity, and near-zero hydrogen adsorption free energy, serve primarily as efficient cocatalysts for electron extraction and conduction. Thermodynamically, the conduction band must be more negative than the H+/H2 reduction potential. Kinetically, rapid carrier recombination must be suppressed.53 To address these challenges, various catalyst modification strategies such as elemental doping, heterojunction construction, and cocatalyst loading have been developed.54–56

Schematic of the light-driven HER process.

Elemental doping creates intermediate energy levels within the lattice to narrow the bandgap and extend light absorption, yet it faces challenges regarding dopant uniformity and limited suppression of carrier recombination.57,58 Heterojunction construction, including type-II, Z-scheme, or S-scheme heterostructures, leverages built-in electric fields for enhanced interfacial charge separation and broader light harvesting. However, its efficacy is constrained by interfacial quality, stringent synthetic requirements, and the mechanistic complexity of multi-phase systems.26,59 Cocatalyst loading deposits metal nanoparticles (e.g. Pt,39,60 Au,61–63 Ag,64–66 and Ni67–70) or single-atom sites onto the catalyst surface as discrete active sites. This approach is highly targeted, substantially reducing the overpotential for specific half-reactions like hydrogen evolution. Cocatalyst loading also offers advantages in structural simplicity, facile preparation, and straightforward mechanistic interpretation. However, the high cost of noble-metal cocatalysts remains a practical limitation. Besides, in light-driven hydrogen evolution reactions, the slow kinetics of water oxidation typically limits the practical efficiency. Consequently, most studies utilize only photogenerated electrons at the reduction site to produce hydrogen, while employing sacrificial agents to scavenge photogenerated holes at the oxidation site, thereby “preserving” the electrons and boosting the hydrogen yield. Commonly used sacrificial agents include methanol,71,72 triethanolamine (TEOA),41,73 and Na2S/Na2SO3,74,75 among others. However, their oxidation products lack commercial value and are sometimes toxic, necessitating research on alternative ways for value-added oxidation pathways. Finally, traditional hydrogen production requires the usage of fresh water as substrates, which competes with human and agricultural usage. Thus, different water sources such as seawater and wastewater have emerged as promising alternatives to alleviate freshwater stress while maintaining or enhancing system economics. We will review the recent advances in the above-mentioned aspects accordingly.

Standardized metrics for photocatalytic HER evaluation

In order to compare between different photocatalytic systems, we recommend that activity reports clearly specify the type, concentration, and pH of the reaction and employ a set of standardized parameters. For the evaluation of photocatalytic performance, we use the following terms: (1) photocatalytic HER rate/efficiency/activity, (2) apparent quantum efficiency (AQE), and (3) solar-to-hydrogen (S.T.H.) conversion efficiency.76–79 Photocatalytic HER rate/efficiency/activity is generally defined as the H2 evolution rate per unit mass of catalyst within the measured time period. However, with variation in reaction pH, temperature, light intensity or usage of scarifying agents, the photocatalytic HER rate tends to change with regard to different systems. Thus, the AQE and S.T.H. conversion efficiency should also be included. The AQE for the photocatalytic HER is defined as the ratio of reacted electrons (2 × H2 molecules) to incident photons at a specific wavelength, which serves as a standardized metric for evaluating its performance independent of light source variations.80 While the apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) evaluates wavelength-specific photon-to-hydrogen conversion, the S.T.H. conversion efficiency, calculated as the ratio of chemical energy stored in evolved H2 (237 kJ mol−1) to incident solar energy under standard AM 1.5 G illumination (100 mW cm−2), represents the most practically relevant metric for assessing photocatalytic water-splitting systems.48 Employing such standardized parameters will facilitate the comparison among different photocatalytic HER systems and spur the development of commercial ready catalysts.

Beyond conventional performance metrics (AQE, STH, and TOF), a comprehensive levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) assessment framework should be implemented to evaluate the photocatalytic HER systems from an economic viability perspective. The LCOH metric, expressed as $ kg−1 H2, integrates all capital expenditures (CAPEX, including photocatalyst synthesis, cocatalyst deposition, and reactor construction), operational expenditures (OPEX, encompassing sacrificial agent consumption, catalyst replacement, water treatment, and gas separation), and system energy inputs over the plant's operational lifetime through discounted cash flow analysis.81–83 This approach encourages researchers to report not only catalytic activity metrics but also techno-economically relevant parameters such as: catalyst-related parameters (concentration, cost, lifetime and others), sacrificial agent consumption rate, and system space-time yield. Presently, no dedicated national or industry standards exist for calculating the photocatalytic LCOH. Because literature assumptions regarding the catalyst lifetime, reactor CAPEX, annual peak sun hours, and discount rates vary widely, the reported values range drastically from $1.89 to over $150 kg−1. Comparing standalone figures is therefore of limited utility without examining the underlying input parameters. To address this, Gunawan et al.48 developed a publicly available evaluation tool for the case-by-case assessment of photocatalytic solar hydrogen production.

Establishing an LCOH-centered evaluation system for large-scale applications will help steer the field from a narrow pursuit of high laboratory activity toward an integrated assessment of economic feasibility and process sustainability, thereby identifying the most promising technology pathways for practical implementation.84,85

Cocatalyst development for the light-driven HER: strategies for Pt replacement and minimization

Cocatalysts play an important role in improving the photocatalytic HER efficiency, including (1) providing active sites to lower reduction overpotentials, (2) facilitating charge separation via Schottky/Mott–Schottky junctions, and (3) mitigating photocorrosion for improved stability.86,87 While Pt remains the benchmark, research has shifted toward transition metal compounds, emphasizing atomic-scale engineering and composite design.57,70,88 This review summarizes the recent progress across three categories: metal single atoms/nanoparticles, metal oxides and metal sulfides.

Metal in single-atom or nanoparticle forms

Metal single-atom/cluster cocatalysts consist of isolated atoms or ultrasmall clusters stabilized on supports via chemical bonds (e.g., M–O, M–N, and M–C) at defects or edges. Their unique coordination geometry and electronic coupling with the support offer several advantages: (1) maximized atom utilization, enabling high activity at low loadings (typically <1 wt%); (2) tunable electronic structures that optimize intermediate adsorption; and (3) high intrinsic activity/selectivity through tailored coordination environments. These features collectively enhance the HER performance and reduce LCOH. Beyond common noble (Au, Ag, and Pt) and transition (Ni and Co) metals, dual- or multi-atom systems further leverage synergistic cooperation between adjacent sites to catalyze sequential reaction steps.

For example, Nguyen et al.39 synthesized Pt/SCN composite photocatalysts with varying Pt loadings on g-C3N4 (SCN) by a solvothermal method (Fig. 3a). The 3 Pt/SCN composite (3 wt% Pt) exhibited the highest photocatalytic HER activity (1.26 mmol g−1 h−1) with triethanolamine (TEOA) as the sacrificial agent under an LED lamp with a light intensity of 100 mW cm−2, which was about 50 times higher than that of the SCN photocatalyst (25.2 µmol g−1 h−1). The enhanced HER performance arose primarily from modulated charge transfer dynamics between the Pt co-catalyst and the SCN support. With insufficient Pt loadings, charge separation and transfer could not be optimized, leading to limited hydrogen evolution activity. Conversely, excess Pt loadings covered the active surface sites, reduced light absorption, and resulted in poor dispersion of Pt nanoparticles, thereby suppressing the photocatalytic performance. Thus, considering the LCOH, the less active Pt/SCN outperformed the slightly more active 3 Pt/SCN.

Additionally, researchers evolved the active site of catalysts from single element to multi-component and multi-functional integrations to utilize the synergistic catalytic system of multiple metal elements while also providing a viable pathway to identify non-precious metal alternatives as cocatalysts to lower the final LCOH. For example, Wang et al.67 synthesized Ni–Pt/TiO2via radiolytic reduction, demonstrating that the synergy between Pt (electron traps) and Ni (donors) creates an efficient pathway for long-lived electrons (Fig. 3b). This bimetallic design achieved Pt-like performance with a significantly reduced noble-metal loading, yielding a HER rate of 1.27 mmol g−1 h−1 (AQY = 78.1%). This represents a 40-fold enhancement over TiO2–P25, highlighting a cost-effective strategy for high-efficiency hydrogen evolution. Jaksani et al.89 developed a novel non-precious-metal catalyst, Co–Ni–CuBTC, by partially substituting Cu ions with Ni and Co ions in the CuBTC MOF (Fig. 3c). Under excitation by a 420 W Xe lamp with 50% methanol as the sacrificial agent, the 15 mol% loading of Co–Ni–CuBTC achieved a HER rate of 23.9 mmol g−1 h−1 (AQE = 57.3% at 420 nm), representing a 6.6-fold enhancement compared to pristine CuBTC. The enhanced HER rate was owing to the synergistic effect induced by the uniform distribution of Co and Ni ions, which not only improves visible-light utilization but also shortens the electron-transfer distance, thereby enhancing charge separation and transfer efficiency. To sum up, single-atom/NP co-catalysts offer a novel pathway to exceed traditional performance limits in photocatalytic hydrogen evolution.

Metal oxides

Although metals in single-atom/NP forms exhibit high atomic utilization and well-defined active sites, they still face several limitations, such as susceptibility to migration, agglomeration, or leaching under strong illumination or prolonged reactions.90,91 Moreover, their synthesis requires precise control over loading and coordination environments, posing challenges for reproducibility and scalability.92–94

Metal oxide cocatalysts (e.g., Ru, Co, Ni, Fe, and Ag) typically possess positive valence band positions, acting as effective hole-trapping centers. These oxides extract photogenerated holes from the semiconductor, while the established interfacial electric field drives directional electron migration. This synergistic mechanism ensures efficient charge separation and significantly suppresses electron–hole recombination. For example, Zhang et al.64 anchored well-defined and highly active AgOx clusters onto the mesoporous TiO2 (ACTs) surface, to improve the stability and efficiency of light-driven HER performance. Experimental and computational studies revealed that the combined effects of electron–hole pair generation in Ag and the rapid charge separation and transfer at the interface endowed the ACTs system with excellent activity and stability (Fig. 3d). Under 300 W Xe-lamp irradiation with 10 vol% methanol as the sacrificial agent, 2 wt% AgOx/TiO2 exhibited optimal photocatalytic hydrogen evolution performance, achieving a rate of 22.8 mmol g−1 h−1, which was comparable to the Pt/TiO2 reference (24.6 mmol g−1 h−1), with an AQE of 2.15% at 350 nm. Furthermore, additional metal oxides (Ni, Co, and Fe) were similarly composited with TiO2 and their HER activities (Co-, Fe-, and Ni-based oxide clusters/TiO2) were significantly lower than that of AgOx/TiO2 evaluated under identical conditions. Additionally, Noureldien et al.69 deposited NiO–Ni(OH)2 onto VS2 nanosheets via a one-step hydrothermal method. Mechanistic studies revealed that the favorable CB potential of NiO–Ni(OH)2 (−0.23 V vs. NHE) relative to that of VS2 (−0.335 V vs. NHE) enabled electron transfer from the CB of VS2 to that of NiO–Ni(OH)2, thereby promoting the spatial separation of electron–hole pairs and enhancing the HER activity (Fig. 3e). Under visible-light irradiation with 10 vol% methanol as the sacrificial agent, the 0.8 mol% NiO–Ni(OH)2/VS2 catalyst achieved a photocatalytic HER rate of 4.16 mmol g−1 h−1 with an AQE of 38.46%, representing an approximately 4-fold enhancement over pristine VS2 (10.63 mmol g−1 h−1; AQE = 9.3%).

Metal sulfides

Unlike metal oxides, sulfides generally possess more negative conduction band positions, which favor electron transfer from semiconductors to the sulfide phase. Additionally, they exhibit near-ideal hydrogen adsorption free energy (ΔG*H ≈ 0), high electronic conductivity (up to ∼103 S m−1), and strong visible-light absorption.95–97 Inspired by that, Wang et al.98 synthesized a novel NiS-modified MnxCd1−xS self-assembled p–n junction via a one-step hydrothermal method (Fig. 4a). After modification with 1.0 wt% NiS, the 0.5MCS sample exhibited the optimal photocatalytic HER rate (53.4 mmol g−1 h−1) under visible-light irradiation using Na2S + Na2SO3 as sacrificial agents, which was 17.6 times that of pristine T-CdS and significantly higher than that of the 1.0 wt% Pt/0.5 MCS catalyst (16.7 mmol g−1 h−1), with an AQE of 22.49% at 420 nm. Yu et al.88 synthesized ZnCdS nanocrystals via a hydrothermal method and successfully anchored MoS2 cocatalysts through electrostatic adsorption (Fig. 4b). The incorporation of MoS2 not only expanded the light-harvesting range but also facilitated charge carrier separation. Under visible-light irradiation, the optimized 8 wt% MoS2/ZnCdS composite achieved a peak photocatalytic hydrogen evolution rate of 42.07 mmol g−1 h−1, which is approximately 5.8 times higher than that of pristine ZnCdS (7.23 mmol g−1 h−1). Similarly, Liu et al.70 employed NiS as a cocatalyst to enhance the light-driven HER activity of ZnCdS (ZCS). In contrast to conventional surface anchoring, the NiS cocatalyst in this research exists as a discrete sub-nanometer cluster dispersed in the reaction solution. Charge transfer was achieved via dynamic collisions between ZCS nanocrystals and NiSx clusters, which facilitated charge separation and suppressed recombination, thereby promoting the hydrogen evolution performance (Fig. 4c). Using Na2S and Na2SO3 as sacrificial agents under visible light irradiation, the 0.03 wt% NiS/ZCS system achieved an optimal hydrogen production rate of 4.46 mmol h−1, corresponding to a 1.7-fold enhancement over pristine ZCS, with an AQE of approximately 64% at 425 nm. The collision mechanism proposed in this work provides a new idea for the action form of cocatalysts and the improvement of photocatalytic activity. Additionally, Fan et al.99 evaluated a series of metal sulfides (CoS2, NiS2, Cu1.8S, SnS2, and MoS2) as cocatalysts for CdS modification. Among the candidates, transition-metal sulfides significantly enhanced the photocatalytic activity in the order of MoS2 > CoS2 > NiS2 (Fig. 4d), with the 1 wt% MoS2/CdS composite exhibiting a 25-fold increase in hydrogen evolution rate compared to pristine CdS.

Beyond single-atom species, bimetallic sulfide systems have been explored to leverage the synergistic catalytic effects. For instance, Wang et al.100 integrated oxygen-doped MoS2/CoS (O–MC) nanosheets onto Zn0.1Cd0.9S nanorods (Fig. 4e), creating a heterostructure capable of full-spectrum solar energy harvesting (up to 1020 nm). The oxygen dopants facilitated near-infrared absorption and photothermal-enhanced charge transfer, resulting in a HER rate of 95.5 mmol g−1 h−1 and a 13.84-fold improvement over the pristine support. Additionally, Li et al.101 synthesized a hydrogenase-mimetic NiCoS cocatalyst deposited on CaTiO3 inspired by the active sites of natural [NiFe]-hydrogenase (Fig. 4f). This biomimetic approach not only accelerated charge separation but also significantly lowered the HER activation energy by facilitating H2O dissociation via a Ni(OH)–Co intermediate. The resulting composite achieved a 60-fold enhancement in activity compared to bare CaTiO3, demonstrating the potential of bio-inspired molecular engineering in designing high-efficiency cocatalysts. In addition, some researchers combined the advantages of metal sulfides and metal single atoms as cocatalysts to improve the photocatalytic HER performance. For example, Xu et al.61 successfully prepared Au-modified CdS nanoparticles and loaded them onto hollow C@MoS2 spherical shells to construct C@MoS2–Au@CdS nanocomposite photocatalysts. Under 300 W Xe-lamp irradiation with 10 vol% methanol as the sacrificial agent, C@MoS2-2 wt% Au@CdS achieved a hydrogen evolution rate of 268.53 mmol g−1 h−1, which is 8950 times higher than that of pristine SiO2@CdS (SCN) (0.03 mmol g−1 h−1) and 6713 times higher than that of hollow C@MoS2NPs (0.04 mmol g−1 h−1). Transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) revealed that hot electrons generated from Au and photogenerated electrons from CdS can be efficiently transferred to the CdS surface, thus significantly enhancing the HER activity with the back-transfer of photogenerated electrons from the CdS shell to the Au core effectively suppressed by sulfur-vacancy defects in the CdS nanostructure, further improving the HER efficiency (Fig. 4g). In situ Raman spectroscopy detected a characteristic signal at 2510 cm−1 corresponding to the S–H intermediate, providing direct evidence that sulfur atoms serve as active sites for the HER catalysis. Moreover, Li et al.62 developed a ternary MASCN photocatalyst by supporting Au-induced 1T-MoS2 on sulfur-doped g-C3N4 (SCN). The introduction of Au nanoparticles not only stabilized the metallic 1T phase of MoS2 through strong Au–S covalent bonding but also enhanced the electrical conductivity and active-site density of the composite (Fig. 4h). Due to accelerated interfacial charge transfer, the optimized 2 wt% MASCN catalyst achieved a hydrogen evolution rate of 4.71 mmol g−1 h−1, representing 87-fold and 25-fold enhancement over pristine SCN and MoS2-modified SCN, respectively. All work elucidated the fundamental mechanism behind the high HER efficiency of plasmonic-semiconductor composite photocatalysts, offering significant insights for the development and mechanistic understanding of future efficient photocatalytic systems. In typical synthesis protocols, Pt, Au, and Ag precursors are commonly supplied as H2PtCl6 6H2O, HAuCl4, and AgNO3 (250 mg quantities at approximately $46.7, $30.02, and $18.7, respectively), corresponding to the calculated molar metal precursor costs of $96 700/mol Pt, $40 800/mol Au, and $12 700/mol Ag. With the incorporation of either Au or Ag, the overall system can deliver significantly enhanced catalytic performance with a lower cost. In conclusion, with the increase in the atomic usage efficiency of Pt by a single-atom strategy or the substitution of Pt with other lower cost metal elements, those strategies showed an increased HER performance in tandem with a lowered LCOH.

Dual-functional photocatalysis: coupling H2 evolution with value-added oxidation

Sacrificial agents significantly influence the HER performance and operational costs. Traditionally treated as mere “hole scavengers,” their underutilized oxidation products often lead to resource wastage and environment burdens. Moreover, inconsistent agent selection complicates performance comparisons across systems. However, leveraging the high oxidation potential of photogenerated holes for value-added transformations enables dual-functional systems that concurrently produce hydrogen and high-value chemicals, enhancing the energy efficiency while reducing the costs. This chapter reviews common sacrificial agents regarding their applications, productions, and limitations. It further explores an emerging hole-driven process, such as polymer degradation, selective organic synthesis, and wastewater treatment, to facilitate sustainable and economically viable hydrogen production.

Common sacrificial agents

In the photocatalytic HER, sacrificial agents enhance the electron utilization efficiency by irreversibly scavenging photogenerated h+via oxidative consumption to bypass the high overpotential oxygen evolution reaction. Their primary functions include: (1) suppressing charge recombination by rapidly trapping and consuming h+, thereby extending the lifetime of e− and enabling their migration to catalytic surfaces for proton (H+) reduction; (2) supplementing reducing equivalents through the oxidation of sacrificial agents, and thus, the released electrons maybe directly or indirectly channeled to active sites on the catalyst to drive proton reduction; and (3) stabilizing the catalyst structure for photocatalysts susceptible to hole-induced oxidation (e.g., CdS and organic semiconductors).

Common sacrificial agents include alcohols (methanol, ethanol, and isopropanol), organic amines (e.g., triethanolamine), sulfides (e.g., Na2S/Na2SO3), and organic acids (e.g., lactic acid). Among these, triethanolamine (TEOA), lactic acid (LA), and Na2S/Na2SO3 are the most frequently employed. We will discuss its common photooxidation products, advantages and disadvantages during the photocatalytic HER process.

TEOA is a classic and widely employed organic amine sacrificial agent in the photocatalytic HER. Its structure, comprising three hydroxyethyl groups and one tertiary amine nitrogen (C6H15NO3), confers high aqueous solubility and strong electron-donating capacity. The lone-pair electrons on tertiary nitrogen enable efficient h+ scavenging, yielding the TEOA+˙ radical cation. TEOA shows a rapid hole-capture kinetics (∼109 M−1 s−1) and an electron-utilization efficiency of 60–80%. With an oxidation potential of approximately +0.7 V vs. NHE (pH = 7), it is readily oxidized by most semiconductor valence bands. Thus, TEOA is regarded as a “gold-standard” sacrificial agent for mechanistic and performance studies. The primary drawbacks of TEOA include its elevated cost, significant viscosity, and strong surface adsorption, which collectively hinder mass transport and attenuate light absorption.102

In comparison to TEOA, lactic acid presents distinct advantages in terms of lower cost and superior fluidity. Its mechanism involves the oxidation of the α-hydroxyl group by photogenerated holes, yielding pyruvic acid (CH3COCOOH). While its decomposition products are environmentally benign, lactic acid demonstrates relatively modest hole-scavenging kinetics (∼107 M−1 s−1), suboptimal electron utilization efficiency (40–60%), and limited protection for certain photocatalysts. Moreover, its effectiveness is generally confined to acidic conditions.103

The Na2S/Na2SO3 system represents a key inorganic sacrificial agent in the photocatalytic HER, typically formulated in molar ratios of 1 : 1 to 1 : 2. Its distinctive sulfur-cycling mechanism and high corrosion resistance make it indispensable in sulfide-based photocatalytic systems, though environmental compatibility and operational safety remain concerns. The S2−/SO32− redox couple achieves exceptional electron-utilization efficiency (>90%) through the following cyclic reactions:S2− + 2h+ → S (or S22−)2S2− + 2h+ → S22−SO32− + h+ + H2O → SO42− + 2H+ + e−

Operational concentrations typically range from 0.1 M to 0.5 M, which shows good compatibility with chalcogenide photocatalysts. The system functions optimally under strongly alkaline conditions; under acidic or neutral pH, however, toxic H2S generation poses significant safety risks.104

Productive hole utilization for value-added products and wastewater treatment

Despite significant advantages that the sacrificial agents offer in the photocatalytic HER process, large-scale implementation will require continuous chemical supply, which inevitably drives up the LCOH. Thus, harnessing photogenerated holes for valuable oxidation reactions has become a key strategy to enhance the economic viability of photocatalytic processes. We will review three main utilization pathways as follows.

Selective organic synthesis

Photogenerated holes can drive the selective oxidation of organic substrates toward high-value chemicals. A representative example is the oxidation of benzyl alcohol to benzaldehyde coupled with hydrogen evolution. This route exhibits high atom economy, avoids hazardous oxidants and harsh reaction conditions, and aligns with green-chemistry principles. For example, Wang et al.105 established a novel paradigm in dual-functional photocatalysis via an atomically engineered ZnIn2S4/ZnCo2O4 (ZIS/ZCO) heterojunction. The composite photocatalyst showed a system's record-breaking performance, achieving 92.7 mmol g−1 h−1 H2 and 72.9 mmol g−1 h−1 benzaldehyde, with a selectivity >93%. Mechanistic characterization showed that the composite is a self-amplified photocatalytic system in which the strengths of each component address the limitations of the other: ZIS provides visible-light absorption and hole-mediated oxidation, while ZCO contributes to broadband light harvesting, thermal activation, and efficient proton reduction (Fig. 5a). This work advances the design of efficient and economically viable photocatalytic systems for hydrogen production, guiding their development toward practical and scalable applications. In addition, Tayyab et al.106 integrated vanadium carbide (VC) as a cocatalyst with CdS nanoparticles to facilitate the selective oxidation of benzyl alcohol (BO) to benzaldehyde (BD) as a sustainable alternative to conventional sacrificial agents. This strategy not only mitigates secondary pollution and reduces the levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH) but also significantly enhances the performance. The optimized CdS/VC-15 catalyst exhibited a hydrogen evolution rate of 20.5 mmol g−1 h−1 with over 99% selectivity for BO oxidation. This enhanced performance is attributed to the synergistic interaction between the high-conductivity VC cocatalyst and CdS, which promotes both rapid charge transfer and efficient organic transformation (Fig. 5b). Following that, Liu et al.107 designed and synthesized a Pt1Au/TiO2 composite photocatalyst that couples ethylene glycol (EG) with ammonia to simultaneously produce formamide (FA) and H2. The system achieves an FA yield of approximately 7.1 mmol g−1 h−1 and a H2 evolution rate of about 22.8 mmol g−1 h−1. Time-resolved transient absorption spectroscopy and in situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed that single-atom Pt sites effectively captured electrons, while Au nanoparticles acted as hole traps, synergistically enhancing charge separation (Fig. 5c). Under illumination, EG undergoes hole-mediated oxidation to form C2 aldehyde intermediates and ˙NH2 radicals. These species subsequently undergo nucleophilic addition, leading to C–C bond cleavage and C–N bond formation. All these works demonstrate a promising photocatalytic route toward large-scale photocatalytic HER implementations with a lower LCOH due to simultaneous hydrogen production and plastic upcycling.

Polymer degradation and resource recovery

Plastic wastes are causing increasing health and environment-related concerns due to its low recycling rates and degradation into microplastics.108–110 Photogenerated holes (h+) play a pivotal role in driving the efficient processing and upcycling of plastic waste. Specifically, photoreforming111–113 utilizes hole-driven oxidative cleavage of polymer chains to selectively convert plastics into value-added fine chemicals (such as small-molecule carboxylic acids, aldehydes, and ketones). Simultaneously, photoexcited electrons (e−) participate in the reduction half-reaction to generate solar fuels,114 with a primary focus in this review on photocatalytic H2 evolution115via water reduction. Crucially, photoreforming represents a sustainable upcycling paradigm distinct from conventional photodegradation, which non-selectively mineralizes polymers into CO2. Operating under mild ambient conditions with highly controllable product selectivity and a minimal environmental footprint, photoreforming uniquely integrates waste remediation with high-value chemical co-production, highlighting its immense potential for future practical deployment. For example, Miao et al.116 prepared Pd–CdS composite photocatalysts by depositing Pd as a cocatalyst onto CdS nanospheres. The Pd–CdS outperformed pure CdS in visible light-driven photo-reforming of pre-treated polylactic acid (PLA), yielding 1.12 mmol H2 and 0.96 mmol pyruvic acid after 12 h of irradiation, with a liquid-product selectivity of 86.8%. In situ EPR spectroscopy revealed that Pd sites effectively activated α-OH bonds and promoted sequential dehydrogenation of lactate, leading to high pyruvate selectivity of Pd–CdS, whereas bare CdS tends to form coupling products and acetic acid. Moreover, Pd–CdS demonstrated excellent photostability and satisfactory activity, enabling direct photo-reforming of commercial PLA plastics without alkaline pretreatment. In addition, Luo et al.117 prepared MoSx supported on Au-modified carbon nitride (CN) for efficient hydrogen evolution coupled with the highly selective upcycling of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) into value-added chemicals. Comprehensive characterizations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm that Au serves as an electron-transfer mediator, accelerating charge migration from CN to MoSx (Fig. 5d). Under photocatalytic conditions, 1 wt% MoSx@Au/CN showed a H2 production rate of 1.73 mmol g−1 h−1, which is 10.5-fold higher than that of bare CN (0.13 mmol g−1 h−1). Notably, the liquid-product selectivity for glycolic acid among C2 compounds reaches 77.9%, representing a threefold enhancement compared with CN alone. The reasons were attributed to the introduction of Au-directed photogenerated holes preferentially toward plastic waste, thus favoring the formation of C2 products over C1 species. Moreover, Ma et al.118 prepared Ni3S4-loaded ZnCdS (N/ZCS-6) via a hydrothermal method for photocatalytic plastic reforming coupled with H2 evolution. Compared with pristine ZCS, the N/ZCS-6 catalyst exhibited significantly enhanced activity, achieving H2 production rates of 27.9 and 17.4 mmol g−1 h−1 in the presence of PLA and PET, respectively. Simultaneously, photogenerated holes oxidized the plastic substrates to yield valuable organic compounds such as formate, acetate, and pyruvate. After 9 h of photo-reforming, PLA and PET generated 1565.6 and 449.5 µmol of organic products, with corresponding selectivity for pyruvic acid and acetic acid reaching 94.2% and 78.3%, respectively. Mechanistic studies suggested that Ni3S4 extracted electrons from ZCS to not only promote H2 evolution but also preserve a high oxidative potential of holes on the ZCS surface, thereby enhancing hole-driven plastic oxidation (Fig. 5e). Wastewater treatment

Photogenerated charge carriers are capable of oxidatively degrading persistent organic pollutants (POPs) in wastewater.119 For example, photocatalytic systems can convert phenolic contaminants into less toxic short-chain organic acids or achieve complete degradation into CO2 and H2O, while co-producing clean H2.120 This integrated approach delivers dual outputs of renewable energy and environmental remediation, providing a sustainable and green pathway for alleviating the usage of fresh water as feedstock and lowering the overall LCOH. For example, Tayyab et al.121 synthesized a ternary dumbbell-shaped photocatalytic system by decorating MoS2 nanoflowers onto the tips of Zn0.1Cd0.9S nanorods (NRs) via a two-step hydrothermal method combined with a soft-template approach. The optimized MTZC-2 photocatalyst demonstrated a remarkable HER rate of 11.01 mmol g−1 h−1 using Na2S/Na2SO3 as sacrificial agents, which is 4.22 times higher than that of pristine Zn0.1Cd0.9S NRs (2.61 mmol g−1 h−1). Furthermore, in the presence of organic wastewater, MTZC-2 exhibited a hydrogen production rate of 525 µmol g−1 h−1 and achieved 99% degradation of 20 ppm ofloxacin (OFX) within 2 hours. Mechanistic studies revealed that when electrons are photoexcited in the Zn0.1Cd0.9S NRs, they traversed the Schottky barrier to reach the tips of flower-like MoS2, thereby facilitating the continuous migration of electrons toward the MoS2 tips, while the photoinduced holes remain confined within the Zn0.1Cd0.9S NRs. Consequently, protons from the organic wastewater accept electrons at the tips of MoS2, enhancing the photocatalytic H2 production. Meanwhile, the photoinduced holes oxidized organic pollutants in the ternary dumbbell structure, decomposing them into harmless substances (Fig. 5f). Chuenangkul, N. et al.122 prepared a series of titanium dioxide (TiO2)-based nanocomposites for the simultaneous production of hydrogen (H2) and the removal of pollutants from biodiesel wastewater. Three different semiconductors, bismuth(iii) oxide (Bi2O3), niobium pentoxide (Nb2O5), and tungsten trioxide (WO3), were added to TiO2 nanoparticles for H2 generation and chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal. The loading of these oxides extended the lifetime of photogenerated carriers. Under UV-visible light irradiation of 5.93 mW cm−2 for 4 hours, the B5/TiO2 nanocomposite (TiO2 containing 5 mol% Bi2O3) showed the highest H2 production (3.79 mmol), and the COD removal rate of the W5/TiO2 nanocomposite material was 29.1%. Wu et al.123 developed a dual-defect heterojunction system (MoS2−x@TiO2-OV) for simultaneous hydrogen evolution and the degradation of recalcitrant organic pollution, including antibiotics and coking wastewater constituents (Fig. 5g). The system utilized wastewater as both a feedstock and a hole scavenger, achieving a maximum HER rate of 41.59 µmol g−1 h−1 with enrofloxacin. The outstanding performance can be attributed to three key factors: (i) oxygen vacancies (OVs) enhancing the surface oxidation capacity; (ii) the hierarchical porous architecture of TiO2 preventing MoS2 restacking and exposing active edge sites; and (iii) the synergy between OVs and sulfur vacancies, which accelerated interfacial charge transfer and optimized H+ adsorption. This work demonstrates a sustainable pathway for concurrent wastewater remediation and green hydrogen production. Those strategies showed an effective way to lower the LCOH through the substitution of sacrificial agents with the usage of photo-generated holes with value-added products and wastewater treatment.

Seawater as a sustainable feedstock for photocatalytic hydrogen production

The main ions in seawater are sodium cations (Na+) and chloride anions (Cl−), with concentrations of approximately 480 mM and 560 mM, respectively.124 These ions have a significant impact on the performance of photocatalytic seawater-splitting processes, and due to the occurrence of many intermediate reactions, the complexity of the system increases. Therefore, to effectively compare with natural seawater, many researchers extensively use 500 mM NaCl or 3.5% NaCl or mixed salts to prepare artificial seawater solutions for studying photocatalytic seawater decomposition. For example, Xia et al.125 successfully synthesized TiO2/rGO/Cu ternary composite materials through a simple NaBH4 reduction method. The TiO2/rGO/Cu composite material promoted electron transfer through two pathways with e− from the TiO2 conduction band transferring to rGO and then to Cu NPs and e− from the TiO2 conduction band directly transferred to Cu NPs. TiO2/rGO/Cu exhibits a simulated hydrogen production rate of 16.08 mmol g−1 h−1 in seawater (3.5 wt% NaCl) under xenon lamp irradiation with methanol as a sacrificial agent, which is 25 times higher than that of TiO2. Moreover, He et al.126 synthesized a core–shell heterojunction (TCP@CZS) by coating a glycerol-derived polymer onto CdxZn1−xS nanorods via an in situ Suzuki reaction for photocatalytic H2 production from water/seawater. The optimal ratio of the TCP@CZS photocatalyst achieves an H2 evolution rate of 93.88 mmol g−1 h−1 in natural seawater containing Na2S/Na2SO3 under simulated sunlight, which is 2.2 times higher than that of pristine Cd0.6Zn0.4S and 1.1 times higher than that in pure water, with an AQE of 22.6% at 420 nm in seawater. The photocatalytic mechanism of TCP@CZS is attributed to synergistic structural and electronic effects. The polymer shell provided a high surface area and porous structure that facilitates H2O activation and promotes H2 desorption, while the intimate contact between TCP and Cd0.6Zn0.4S enhanced the structural stability and effectively suppressed photo-corrosion of the sulfide core in seawater. Additionally, the n–n heterojunction formed between TCP and Cd0.6Zn0.4S improved the light-harvesting efficiency and accelerated the separation of photogenerated charge carriers. These works demonstrate an efficient way to use seawater as feedstocks for the photocatalytic HER. These strategies demonstrate an effective approach to reducing the LCOH by utilizing seawater as the feedstock, thereby eliminating competition with freshwater resources.

Conclusions and prospects

The solar-driven HER provides a critical pathway for converting solar energy into chemical fuels and serves as an essential technological foundation for the transition to clean energy. However, its practical deployment faces several key challenges, including dependence on costly noble-metal co-catalysts, elevated hydrogen production expenses due to sacrificial-agent systems, and the extensive usage of precious freshwater resource. Standardized, comparable evaluation platforms and multi-dimensional performance databases that encompass metrics such as light absorption efficiency, charge separation yield, apparent quantum efficiency, and LCOH have to be established. Multidimensional databases should integrate traditional activity metrics with mechanistic descriptors and techno-economic parameters. Machine learning platforms can leverage such databases to identify structure–property–performance correlations and accelerate discovery. To address the capital costs associated with separating and purifying hydrogen alongside liquid organic by-products, future research should focus on three key priorities: developing precision catalysts for ultra-high selectivity (>90%), integrating green low-energy separation units, and designing continuous-flow photoreactors with in situ separation capabilities. This review systematically examines the mechanisms of photocatalytic hydrogen evolution and catalyst modification strategies, with particular emphasis on performance enhancement routes for co-catalysts, such as noble metals, single-atom sites, and metal oxides/sulfides, along with an analysis of sacrificial agents and productive hole utilizations, like selective organic synthesis, polymer degradation and wastewater treatment, concluding with research using seawater as alternative feedstock to freshwater. To facilitate the transition from laboratory research to industrial applications, we propose establishing a techno-economic assessment framework anchored on the levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH), advocating for the combined use of standardized HER performance parameters and cost analysis in evaluating photocatalytic systems.
