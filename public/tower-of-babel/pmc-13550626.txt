TiO2-facet-dependent reconstruction of Pt nanoparticles during CO oxidation
Authors: Jihun Kim, Yunkyung Kim, Daewon Oh, Seokhyun Choung, Hyeongeon Lee, Hogyun Lee, Eonu Nam, Shinjae Lee, Jeongjin Kim, Sinmyung Yoon, Jeong Woo Han, Kwangjin An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFacet engineering of reducible oxide supports is a powerful strategy for tuning metal–support interactions. We show that this facet dependence extends beyond intrinsic support properties to govern the initial morphology and reaction-induced restructuring behaviour of supported Pt nanoparticles during CO oxidation. For ~1.8 nm Pt nanoparticles on anatase TiO2, microscopy and spectroscopy show that the (001) facet stabilises a wetted Pt structure with expanded interfacial contact, whereas the (101) facet favours compact cuboctahedral nanoparticles with limited interface. Genetic algorithm and graph neural network-based simulations reproduce these facet-dependent structures and support distinct restructuring under CO oxidation. DFT calculations support facile oxygen activation on the (001) facet throughout the MvK pathway, while reaction-order analysis and catalytic cycle tests indicate that the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2(001) interface synergistically couples with oxygen vacancies to enable efficient oxygen replenishment, further lowering the activation barrier by ~25% after the initial run. This work identifies support facets as regulators of dynamic metal–support interfaces governing catalytic performance.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Reaction kinetics and dynamics, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Metal–support interactions (MSIs) have attracted considerable attention in heterogeneous catalysis because they strongly influence the electronic structure and morphology of supported metals, ultimately affecting catalytic performance1–3. From single atoms to nanoparticles (NPs), diverse metal-oxide combinations have been explored for oxidation and hydrogenation reactions4,5. Notably, MSIs enhance the formation of interfacial and surface active sites, facilitate activation of small molecules such as H2 and O26–8, and promote charge transfer across the interface9,10, thereby modulating both the stability of metal NPs and adsorbates behaviour11–13. Among various supports, reducible oxides such as TiO2 and CeO2 have attracted particular interest due to their exceptional redox properties arising from their unique electronic structure14. These oxides can readily accommodate oxygen vacancies (Ov) and support reversible cationic redox transitions beneficial for thermally activated catalytic processes15,16. In particular, the relatively low energy required for Ov formation in TiO2 and CeO2 enables active participation of lattice oxygen in oxidation reactions, typically via the Mars van Krevelen (MvK) mechanism17.

Crystal facet engineering of reducible oxides is a powerful approach for customising the atomic arrangement of surface metals and oxygen species, thereby influencing both redox behaviour and MSI strength18–20. Facet-driven MSIs play a role in various phenomena such as overlayer formation21, intermetallic alloying22,23, electronic modulation24, and metal sintering25,26. However, the relationship between facet-dependent morphological evolution of supported metal NPs and catalytic performance remains insufficiently understood. According to the Wulff–Kaischew theorem27, supported metal NPs initially adopt geometries that minimise surface energy, which are strongly influenced by support crystallography. Recent studies have demonstrated that the morphology of supported NPs can evolve dynamically under reaction conditions28–30. For instance, Lin et al. demonstrated that the size, shape, and interface structure of Au NPs are governed by the exposed surface facets of CeO2 (e.g. (100) vs. (111)) and the surrounding atmosphere (oxidative or reductive). Similarly, Eliasson et al. used scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) to reveal the transition of Pt NPs from 3D morphologies on CeO2(111) to 2D raft-like structures on CeO2(100), highlighting the critical role of support termination in governing NP morphology. Generally, metal NPs can exhibit both 3D and 2D morphologies, with their relative fractions depending on MSI strength and nature. Several approaches for promoting 2D metal morphologies during synthesis have been explored31–33, because 2D structures exhibit enhanced CO oxidation activity by facilitating Ov formation across extended interfacial areas31,34. Despite investigations of phase-dependent reshaping35, growth36, and facet-dependent dispersion of Pt on TiO237, the relationship between the dynamic morphological evolution of supported NPs and their catalytic trajectories remains insufficiently understood.

We investigated how TiO2 crystal facets influence catalytic performance not only through their intrinsic redox properties, but also by governing the morphology and reaction-induced restructuring of supported Pt NPs during CO oxidation. To demonstrate this, well-defined Pt/TiO2 catalysts were prepared using anatase TiO2 supports with distinct morphologies: nanosheets (PTS) predominantly exposing (001) facets and rhombic crystals (PTR) dominated by the (101) facet. Structural transformations of the Pt NPs during reaction were analysed using ex situ extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), STEM, and in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS). Computational simulations were further employed to quantify facet-dependent Pt reconstruction on TiO2. Genetic algorithm (GA) optimisation of Pt201 NPs on TiO2(001) and TiO2(101) revealed distinct facet-dependent equilibrium morphologies, while subsequent CO adsorption induced additional surface roughening consistent with experimental observations. Finally, DFT calculations using Pt28 cluster models evaluated facet-dependent energetics and oxygen activation during CO oxidation. The catalytic consequence of the reconstructed Pt–TiO2(001) interface was further supported by reaction-order analysis and catalytic cycle tests, while its redox-active nature was evidenced by in situ X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and near-ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (NAP-XPS). Together, these results show that TiO2 facets determine not only the initial Pt morphology, but also the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2 interfacial structure that governs facet-dependent catalytic behaviour.

Results

Structural characterisation and CO oxidation performance of Pt/TiO2 catalysts

Pt NPs were deposited onto two anatase TiO2 supports: nanosheets predominantly exposing the (001) facet and rhombic-shaped crystals primarily exposing the (101) facet. The synthetic procedures for these morphology-controlled TiO2 supports are described in Supplementary Note 1. High-angle annular dark field (HAADF)-STEM images of the as-prepared Pt/TiO2 catalysts are shown in Fig. 1a–d and Supplementary Fig. 1. Low-magnification STEM images (Fig. 1a, c) confirm the distinct sheet-like and rhombic morphologies, consistent with TEM images of the pristine TiO2 supports (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the high-magnification STEM image of PTS (Supplementary Fig. 1b), two perpendicular lattice fringes with spacings of 0.19 and 0.38 nm correspond to the TiO2(200) and (010) planes, respectively38. The side-view image of PTS (Fig. 1b) reveals lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.24 nm, assigned to the TiO2(004) plane. Similarly, the STEM image of PTR (Fig. 1d) exhibits lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.35 nm, consistent with the TiO2(101) plane39. Additional STEM images are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1a–e and g–k. The Pt NPs were uniformly distributed, with an average size of approximately 1.8 nm (Supplementary Figs. 1f, l). Both catalysts exhibited similar particle-size distributions, further supported by CO pulse chemisorption (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, the Pt NPs on the TiO2(001) facet adopted a hemispherical morphology with a small contact angle (<90°), whereas those on the (101) facet displayed more faceted geometries with larger contact angles (>90°) (Supplementary Figs. 1d, e, i–k), indicating intrinsically different initial Pt morphologies on both TiO2 facets. In both cases, lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.23 nm correspond to the (111) planes of metallic Pt (Fig. 1b, d). These facet-dependent Pt morphologies are discussed further below.

The crystal and surface structures of the catalysts were characterised by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy. The XRD patterns of the as-synthesised TiO2 and Pt/TiO2 catalysts (Supplementary Figs. 3a, b) show reflections exclusively from the anatase phase (JCPDS card no. 21-1272), with no detectable Pt diffraction peaks due to the small size of the Pt NPs. Raman spectra (Supplementary Figs. 3c, d) exhibit characteristic anatase TiO2 modes at 394, 514, and 636 cm−1, corresponding to the B1g, A1g, and Eg vibrations, respectively40. The A1g mode is more intense in TiO2 nanosheets and PTS, reflecting the abundance of O2c–Ti5c–O2c sites on the (001) facet, whereas the B1g and Eg modes are more prominent in rhombic TiO2 and PTR, consistent with O3c–Ti6c and O2c–Ti5c configurations on the (101) facet41. Both XRD and Raman analyses confirm that the TiO2 morphologies and dominant facet exposures are preserved after Pt deposition.

On Pt-based catalysts, CO oxidation can proceed via Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) and MvK pathways42. In the latter, lattice oxygen from a reducible oxide support participates directly in the reaction. Previous studies on Pt/CeO2 and Au/CeO2 catalysts have demonstrated that catalytic activity depends strongly on oxide crystal facets, particularly under variable CO/O2 environments18,43. Based on these insights, CO oxidation was performed over Pt/TiO2 catalysts using different CO/O2 ratios to elucidate the role of TiO2 facets in reaction behaviour.

PTS consistently outperformed PTR across all examined CO/O2 ratios, exhibiting lower light-off temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 4). Comparison of T90 values (Fig. 1e) shows that under O2-rich conditions (CO/O2 = 0.2 and 0.4), the differences in T90 (ΔT90) remain nearly constant (48 and 47 K), whereas under oxygen-limited conditions (CO/O2 = 1), ΔT90 increases to ~68 K, reflecting a stronger contribution of the TiO2 support when the reaction becomes MvK-dominated44,45. Consistent with this trend, Arrhenius analysis in the kinetically controlled regime (Supplementary Fig. 5) reveals a significantly lower apparent activation energy (Ea) for PTS (58.1 ± 0.3 kJ·mol−1) than for PTR (109.1 ± 1.5 kJ·mol−1) (Fig. 1f), indicating more favourable catalytic behaviour of PTS under MvK-type CO oxidation.

To further elucidate this behaviour, CO temperature-programmed reduction (CO-TPR) experiments were analysed by mass spectrometry (MS)46. Under oxygen-free conditions (top panel of Fig. 1g), the CO2 signal for both catalysts initially increased with temperature and then declined due to depletion of reactive surface oxygen in the absence of gas-phase O2. Notably, the PTS CO2 signal was approximately four times higher than PTR, indicating a significantly larger amount of accessible lattice oxygen on the (001) facet than on the (101) facet. When 1% O2 was introduced during CO-TPR (bottom panel of Fig. 1g), both catalysts exhibited enhanced CO2 evolution, while the onset temperature remained lower for PTS than for PTR. This suggests that although gas-phase O2 increases the oxidation rate, it does not significantly alter the initial onset of the MvK pathway. The superior ability of PTS to utilise surface oxygen under oxygen-deficient conditions is further supported by the CO and O2 MS profiles (Supplementary Fig. 6), and its enhanced performance under CO-rich conditions (CO/O2 = 3 and 5; Supplementary Fig. 4b).

Characterization of TiO2 facet-dependent structural dynamics of Pt NPs

We investigated the origin of the higher activity of PTS by examining structural differences between the Pt NPs supported on the two TiO2 facets and their effects on overall morphology and interface properties. To exclude thermally induced sintering in post-reaction structural analyses, both catalysts were treated at 240 °C for 2 h under identical reaction conditions (CO/O2 = 1) prior to analysis. The rationale for this condition is discussed in Supplementary Note 2.

Ex situ EXAFS studies at the Pt L3-edge were performed on both fresh and spent Pt/TiO2 catalysts. Pt L3-edge k²χ(k) oscillatory functions and corresponding Fourier-transformed EXAFS (FT-EXAFS) spectra are shown in Supplementary Figs. 7, 8 and Fig. 2a. The reference spectra of PtO2 and Pt foil exhibited major features at 1.6 Å (Pt–O) and 2.4/2.9 Å (Pt–Pt), respectively47. All Pt/TiO2 catalysts displayed similar peak positions (Fig. 2a), suggesting coexistence of oxidised and metallic Pt. Notably, Pt–O features were dominant in PTS, whereas Pt–Pt features were more pronounced in PTR for both fresh and spent catalysts.

Least-squares fitting of the FT-EXAFS spectra (Supplementary Table 2) gave Pt–O and Pt–Pt coordination numbers (CNs) of 1.6 ± 0.4 and 6.4 ± 1.2 for fresh PTS, and 1.4 ± 0.8 and 8.1 ± 1.2 for fresh PTR, respectively. For the spent catalysts, the Pt–Pt CN marginally increased to 6.8 ± 1.7 in PTS and to 8.2 ± 1.4 in PTR. Under O2-rich conditions (CO/O2 = 0.2), PTS still retained strong Pt–O features, with the Pt–O CN increasing to 1.9 ± 0.7, whereas that of PTR decreased to 1.2 ± 0.5. Under CO-rich conditions (CO/O2 = 5), both spectral features became more metallic, with the Pt–Pt CNs in PTS and PTR increasing to 8.6 ± 1.6 and 9.8 ± 0.8, respectively. Wavelet transform EXAFS (WT-EXAFS) further corroborated these findings, with fresh PTS showing dominant Pt–O contributions (Fig. 2b, c) and fresh PTR showing pronounced Pt–Pt features (Fig. 2e, f). Similar trends were observed for all spent catalysts (Fig. 2d, g and Supplementary Fig. 9), with enhanced Pt–Pt contributions under CO-rich conditions for both catalysts, consistent with the FT-EXAFS and CN results.

STEM imaging of the spent catalysts (CO/O2 = 1) provided direct visual evidence of Pt NP structural changes (Fig. 2h, j and Supplementary Figs. 10–13). For PTS (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Figs. 12f–g, i–l), STEM images from both side and top views of the TiO2(004) plane revealed clearer Pt(200) and (111) lattice fringes. The particles remained hemispherical but exhibited slightly faceted surfaces compared to the fresh counterpart. FFT patterns (Supplementary Fig. 10) confirmed parallel lattice alignment between Pt(200) and TiO2(004), suggesting a preferential contact relationship where the TiO2(001) surface shares a plane with Pt48. By contrast, PTR (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Figs. 13e, g–i) exhibited more faceted, cuboctahedral Pt NPs with a small radius of curvature, along with lattice mismatch and step structures at the Pt–TiO2(101) interface (Supplementary Fig. 11), indicating weaker MSI and reduced particle stability49.

Owing to the similar Pt size dispersions on both TiO2 supports, the extracted CN values reflect differences in Pt morphology arising from facet-dependent MSI50,51. For both fresh and spent catalysts, higher Pt–O and lower Pt–Pt CNs for PTS correspond to under-coordinated, hemispherical particles with strong Pt–O–Ti interactions and a small contact angle. By contrast, PTR exhibits the opposite trend, indicative of well-coordinated, faceted particles with weaker interfacial bonding and larger contact angles. After the reaction, these trends become more pronounced. PTS largely retains its hemispherical morphology with slightly faceted surfaces, accompanied by modest increases in both Pt–O and Pt–Pt CNs, whereas PTR evolves toward a more ordered cuboctahedral morphology, consistent with decreased Pt–O and marginally increased Pt–Pt CNs, as illustrated in the structural models (Fig. 2i, k).

Further STEM analyses of spent catalysts under various CO/O2 conditions, including harsher thermal treatments (Supplementary Figs. 12–15 and Supplementary Note 2), show that facet-dependent Pt NP morphologies are largely preserved. Under O2-rich conditions, PTS exhibits more dispersed metallic features, including partial redispersion into single atoms, whereas PTR remains largely intact and shows comparable redispersion only under harsher O2-rich conditions. In contrast, under CO-rich conditions, PTS remains sintering-resistant despite increased Pt–Pt CNs indicative of structural ordering, whereas PTR undergoes pronounced sintering, which becomes severe under harsher thermal treatments due to weak interfacial contact with TiO2. The corresponding increase in Pt–Pt CNs for PTR therefore reflects particle growth driven by sintering. These observations indicate that Pt NP evolution under different CO/O2 environments is governed by an interplay between adsorbate-induced restructuring—particularly by strongly adsorbed CO52,53—and facet-dependent redox behaviour at the Pt–TiO2 interface.

CO-DRIFTS analyses under pre-, in situ, and post-reaction conditions reveal the evolution of Pt surface structure54. Both pre- and post-reaction normalised CO-DRIFTS spectra (Fig. 3a, d) exhibit peaks at 2080–2060 cm−1 (Ptstep/edge sites) and 2100–2080 cm−1 (Ptterrace sites)55. After reaction, both catalysts show a slight increase in Ptterrace contributions, while PTS remains dominated by Ptstep/edge sites and PTR exhibits an overall blueshift of the CO adsorption band. Notably, only PTS shows a new feature at 2120–2100 cm−1, assigned to CO adsorbed on Pt interfacial sites (Ptint)56. These observations are consistent with the structural analyses shown in Figs. 1a–d and 2. Fresh PTS, featuring hemispherical Pt NPs, exhibits a higher proportion of under-coordinated Ptstep/edge sites, whereas fresh PTR with more faceted Pt NPs shows stronger contributions from well-coordinated Ptterrace sites. After reaction, the increased contribution from well-coordinated sites in PTS arises from slight faceting of initially hemispherical Pt NPs, whereas spent PTR evolves toward a more extended faceted morphology characteristic of cuboctahedral Pt NPs, leading to a higher fraction of low-curvature terrace sites, resulting in the observed blueshift in the CO adsorption bands57.

a, d Normalised CO-DRIFTS spectra recorded pre- and post-CO oxidation at 25 °C. b, e Contour maps of in situ DRIFTS spectra acquired under CO oxidation. c, f Intensity plots tracking CO adsorbed on Ptstep/edge sites and gas-phase CO2 evolution.

In situ DRIFTS contour maps (Fig. 3b, e) reveal two key features: (1) faster CO desorption and earlier CO2 formation (2400–2300 cm−1) for PTS, indicating catalytic ignition at 160–200 °C55, where rapid CO removal from the surface triggers a sharp increase in CO2 production, consistent with its superior light-off performance; and (2) gradual redshifts accompanied by enhanced CO–Ptstep/edge intensity at elevated temperatures. The temperature-resolved CO2 and CO–Ptstep/edge intensity profiles (Fig. 3c, f) further confirm this behaviour, while PTR maintains CO adsorption up to 240 °C. These spectral changes are accompanied by suppression of the 1850 cm−1 CO–Ptbridge feature, as resolved in the enlarged DRIFTS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 16), together with enhanced CO–Ptstep/edge and CO2 (g) signals, indicative of CO-induced surface roughening prior to catalytic ignition58. Notably, these spectral changes occur more rapidly on PTS than on PTR, suggesting facet-dependent restructuring behaviour.

For previously reported Pt/Al2O3 catalysts55, in situ DRIFTS studies revealed reversible reconstruction, with an increased fraction of under-coordinated Pt sites during CO oxidation due to surface roughening of Pt NPs, followed by recovery of the initial spectral features post-reaction. By contrast, our Pt/TiO2 catalysts exhibited irreversible structural modifications, as evidenced by enhanced CO–Ptterrace intensity and the emergence of CO–Ptint features. Combined with STEM analyses, these results indicate that the irreversible Pt reconstruction is governed by MSI with reducible TiO2 support, unlike Pt on non-reducible Al2O3.

To assess the generality of facet-dependent Pt morphologies across different particle sizes, additional experiments were conducted using size-controlled Pt/TiO2 catalysts with average Pt sizes ranging from 1.5 to 5 nm (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Details are provided in Supplementary Note 3. In summary, high-magnification STEM images (Supplementary Fig. 19) reveal a consistent facet-dependent trend in Pt morphology largely independent of particle size. Post-reaction CO-DRIFTS spectra of the size-controlled catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 20) further corroborate this trend. For example, size-controlled Pt/TiO2(101) catalysts consistently exhibit a stronger CO–Ptterrace feature than (001) catalysts at comparable particle sizes. Notably, a distinct CO–Ptterrace feature is evident for the smallest Pt/TiO2(101) catalyst (1.59 nm), indicating that the tendency toward faceted Pt surfaces on TiO2(101) is preserved even at small sizes. These findings clearly demonstrate that TiO2 facet orientation, rather than initial particle size, is the primary determinant of Pt morphology and its subsequent restructuring path. Separately, electron-beam-induced Pt reconstruction observed during STEM analysis of fresh Pt/TiO2 catalysts provides additional qualitative support for the facet-dependent reconstruction trend (Supplementary Note 4).

Computational insights into facet-dependent Pt reconstructions on TiO2

To simulate experimentally relevant (~1.8 nm) Pt NPs, we optimised Pt201 on anatase TiO2 facets using GA-based optimisation to identify facet-specific equilibrium morphologies (Supplementary Note 5). The GA-optimised results revealed pronounced facet dependence: on anatase TiO2(001), the Pt201 NP spread into a strongly wetted, hemispherical-like morphology, while on TiO2(101) it adopted a more compact, faceted shape (Supplementary Fig. 21). We introduced geometric descriptors to quantitatively evaluate facet-dependent differences in Pt–TiO2 adhesion. The Pt201/TiO2(001) showed pronounced Pt–O and Pt–Ti bonds in radial distribution functions (RDFs) compared to Pt201/TiO2(101) (Supplementary Fig. 22). We further computed the interfacial contact area and an effective contact angle (θ) based on a spherical-cap approximation using the Young-Dupré relation to quantify NP wetting behaviour59. The results show markedly stronger wetting behaviour of Pt on TiO2(001) than on TiO2(101): the effective contact angle is reduced to ~95.7° on TiO2(001) (Fig. 4a), compared to ~118.7° on TiO2(101) (Fig. 4b), indicating enhanced lateral spreading of the NP on the higher-energy (001) surface. These trends are consistent with experimentally observed particle shapes and quantify facet-dependent MSI strength.

We also performed GNN-based molecular dynamics (MD) simulations at 573 K using identical Pt201 Wulff-construction-based NPs supported on TiO2(001) and TiO2(101) (Supplementary Note 6), to assess the dynamic accessibility of these morphologies under thermal excitation relevant to experimental synthesis. The Pt201 NP on TiO2(001) exhibited significantly greater atomic mobility and interfacial disorder than on TiO2(101), as reflected by a larger mean square displacement (MSD) and broader Pt–Pt RDF (FWHM = 0.304 vs. 0.160 Å; Supplementary Fig. 23). These results indicate that Pt201 on TiO2(001) is more fluxional and susceptible to thermal restructuring. Additional GNN–MD simulations at 1500 K for 0.8 ns were conducted to probe structural evolution under extreme thermal conditions (Supplementary Table 3). Although pronounced Pt flattening on TiO2(001) was observed, unphysical slab distortion also occurred, indicating that these conditions are unsuitable for equilibrium structure prediction (Supplementary Fig. 24). Therefore, GA-derived geometries were adopted for subsequent catalytic analysis.

While the Pt201 model captures realistic NP sizes and reproduces experimental trends, its computational cost limits detailed reaction pathway exploration. Therefore, we introduced a smaller-scale, electronically tractable Pt28 rod model to investigate reaction energetics at the DFT level, while retaining key morphological characteristics observed in large-scale simulations60–64. The Pt28 rod geometry preserves the extended perimeter interface observed in Pt201 NPs, while enabling detailed electronic and bonding analysis. Relaxation of an identical hexagonal Pt28 rod (Supplementary Fig. 25) on each facet reproduced the facet-dependent reconstruction trend: Pt28 on TiO2(001) flattens considerably (Fig. 4c), while Pt28 on TiO2(101) remains close to its initial shape (Fig. 4d), with different Pt CNs (Supplementary Table 4). Quantitative analysis confirms that the DFT-level adhesion energy is more favourable on TiO2(001) than on TiO2(101) ( −0.86 eV vs. −0.80 eV per Pt atom), consistent with stronger MSI on the high-energy (001) facet, as also reflected by the normalised Pt binding energy (Supplementary Table 5). The effective contact angle similarly reflects enhanced wetting, with θ = 86.7° on TiO2(001) versus 103.2° on TiO2(101) (Supplementary Fig. 26). This trend is consistent with the Pt201 GA models (95.7° and 118.7°, respectively). Additionally, in line with previous Pt–oxide studies65, the TiO2(001) support shows a calculated work function (5.56 eV) close to that of Pt, resulting in localized interfacial charge redistribution and moderate MSI showing near 90﻿° contact angle noted above (Supplementary Fig. 27).

To further elucidate the experimentally observed CO-induced reconstruction behaviour, additional CO-covered GNN-based MD simulations were performed on Pt201/TiO2(001) and Pt201/TiO2(101) (Supplementary Note 7). A grand-canonical surface phase diagram constructed from an empirical chemical-potential framework identified the experimentally relevant CO coverage matching the in situ DRIFTS condition (T = 513 K, pCO = 0.01 bar) as 39 and 45 CO molecules on Pt201/TiO2(001) and Pt201/TiO2(101), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 28). Under these thermodynamically appropriate CO coverages and reaction-relevant thermal conditions (100 ps at 513 K), CO adsorption perturbed the coordination environment of surface Pt atoms in a facet-dependent manner, as evidenced by broadening of CN distributions (Fig. 4e, f) and Pt–Pt RDFs (Supplementary Fig. 29), particularly on the (101) facet (Supplementary Table 6). Specifically, the mean CN of the CO-adsorbable (CN ≤ 9) surface Pt subpopulation decreased from 7.48 to 7.40 on Pt201/TiO2(001) (ΔCN = 0.08) and from 7.54 to 7.42 on Pt201/TiO2(101) (ΔCN = 0.12), supporting experimental observations of CO-induced Pt roughening.

Facet-dependent Pt structural evolution during CO oxidation

The experimental observations and computational results described above consistently indicate that TiO2 facets regulate the structural evolution of supported Pt NPs under CO oxidation conditions. This behaviour can be understood by considering how the initial facet-dependent Pt morphology responds to CO-induced surface roughening and interfacial reconfiguration.

CO-induced surface roughening and interfacial reconfiguration of supported Pt catalysts, including terrace-to-step transitions and facet rounding, have been reported by microscopy and spectroscopy52,54,66. Vincent et al. reported atomic-scale fluxional behaviour at Pt/CeO2 interfaces driven by CO adsorption, where CO-modified Pt surface energetics and interfacial bonding were coupled with dynamic Ov formation and annihilation at the Pt perimeter, leading to particle reshaping under CO oxidation52. Similarly, CO-induced interfacial reconfiguration has been reported for Pt/TiO2, where the emergence of a DRIFTS feature at 2107 cm−1, assigned to CO adsorbed at interfacial Pt sites (CO–Ptint), was associated with dynamic O2 activation at the Pt perimeter67.

In the second in situ DRIFTS measurements (Supplementary Figs. 30a, b), the CO–Ptint feature was observed exclusively for spent PTS and was absent for spent PTR, although CO-induced surface roughening features were present for both catalysts. By contrast, when the spent catalysts were prepared under harsher conditions (300 °C for 2 h), CO–Ptint features also emerged for PTR (Supplementary Figs. 30c, d). These results indicate that PTR intrinsically exhibits weaker Pt–TiO2 interactions, requiring higher energy for CO adsorbed at perimeter Pt sites to activate interfacial oxygen species. Consequently, Pt fluxional behaviour at the Pt/TiO2 interface differs between the two facets, leading to facet-dependent Pt restructuring.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, facet-dependent MSIs establish distinct initial Pt–TiO2 wetting geometries on TiO2(001) and TiO2(101). On TiO2(001), the larger Pt–TiO2 interfacial contact favours a more wetted Pt configuration, whereas TiO2(101) stabilises a more compact Pt configuration with a confined Pt–TiO2 interface. During reaction, both catalysts undergo CO-induced surface roughening and catalytic ignition, but their reaction-evolved Pt morphologies and Pt–TiO2 interfacial configurations remain distinct. For PTS, the post-reaction STEM and CO-DRIFTS results indicate a more extended Pt–TiO2(001) interfacial configuration with pronounced interfacial and under-coordinated Pt features. By contrast, PTR retains a more faceted Pt morphology and a more confined Pt–TiO2(101) interface after reaction, consistent with less facile interfacial oxygen activation and weaker Pt–TiO2 interfacial reconfiguration. Accordingly, Pt reconstruction is described here not simply as CO-induced surface roughening, but as the facet-dependent reaction-evolved Pt morphology and Pt–TiO2 interfacial configuration retained after CO oxidation. This structural framework suggests that facet-dependent Pt/TiO2 catalysis cannot be understood solely from the intrinsic redox properties of TiO2 facets, but also depends on the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2 interfacial geometry involved in support-oxygen participation.

Reaction mechanism and the origin of superior catalytic activity

DFT calculations were employed to elucidate the influence of reconstructed Pt structures at the Pt–TiO2 interfaces on the catalytic activity. A complete reaction energy diagram was derived for the optimised Pt28/TiO2(001) and (101) models following the MvK pathway (Fig. 6a). The lattice-oxygen activation step exhibits strong facet dependence, with activation barriers of 0.91 eV on Pt28/TiO2(001) and 1.67 eV on Pt28/TiO2(101). The overall rate-determining step is the subsequent coupling of a second CO with the surface oxygen generated by O2 dissociation, for which the barriers are 1.41 eV on Pt28/TiO2(001) and 2.13 eV on Pt28/TiO2(101), in agreement with previous reports that the second CO2 formation is the most energy-demanding step in MvK-type CO oxidation over supported Pt catalysts42. These results indicate that Pt28/TiO2(001) is more reactive than Pt28/TiO2(101) throughout the MvK pathway, exhibiting consistently lower barriers for both lattice-oxygen activation and the subsequent second-CO coupling step. This trend is further supported by microkinetic modeling results, which qualitatively reproduce the facet-dependent reactivity trends (Supplementary Fig. 31 and Supplementary Note 8). Calculated Ov formation energies across multiple surface sites (Supplementary Fig. 32) further support this facet-intrinsic difference in reducibility, revealing the greater intrinsic reactivity of lattice oxygen on the (001) facet.

To identify the electronic-structure origin of this facet-dependent reducibility, projected crystal orbital Hamilton population (pCOHP) analysis was performed on the oxygen atom involved in vacancy formation (Supplementary Fig. 33). The summed -ICOHP (Integrated COHP) values for the target oxygen, accounting for both Pt–O and Ti–O bonds, are lower on TiO2(001) (4.15 eV) than on TiO2(101) (5.13 eV), indicating weaker oxygen bonding at the Pt28/TiO2(001) interface (Fig. 6b). This weakening is further supported by the broader, flatter geometry of the Pt28 cluster on TiO2(001), which increases perimeter contact and yields a larger population of oxygen atoms directly coordinated to Pt. Such interfacial motifs are preferentially involved in lattice oxygen activation during redox catalysis, consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of metal–support interfacial sites18,68. In the present system, this beneficial configuration arises naturally from facet-induced reconstruction on TiO2(001), expanding the metal–support interface and weakening specific oxygen bonds in a manner consistent with the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2(001) interface inferred from the experimental results. We also examined the L–H pathway and found that the Pt28/TiO2(101) model exhibits a substantially higher thermodynamic activation barrier, consistent with strong CO adsorption on Pt (Supplementary Note 9).

During the reaction pathway, although carbonate-like species can form transiently on the catalyst surface, experimental analysis indicates that they do not play a decisive kinetic role in the reactivity difference between the two systems. NAP-XPS under 0.15 mbar CO and 0.1 mbar O2 at increasing temperatures revealed three O 1s peaks: lattice oxygen (Olatt) at ~529.7 eV, adsorbed oxygen (Oads) at ~530.7 eV, and C–O species at ~532 eV (Fig. 6c, d and Supplementary Note 10)69. A notable difference between the catalysts lies in the temperature-dependent behaviour of the carbonate-associated C=O peak (~533.2 eV)70. With increasing temperature, PTS exhibited a 60.4% decrease in C=O peak intensity, coinciding with pronounced CO2 (g) evolution. By contrast, PTR showed only a 30% decrease, with CO2 release delayed until above 227 °C, compared to 177 °C for PTS (Supplementary Fig. 34). This behaviour reflects the consistently lower DFT barriers calculated for Pt28/TiO2(001) than for Pt28/TiO2(101) across the MvK pathway, indicating that the reaction proceeds more readily on PTS during CO oxidation.

To evaluate the intrinsic catalytic behaviour of the reshaped Pt structures, reaction-order analysis of CO oxidation was performed using post-reaction Pt/TiO2 catalysts prepared at 240 °C for 2 h (See Methods). As shown in Fig. 6e, both catalysts exhibit near-zero reaction orders with respect to CO (nCO = 0.07 for PTS and 0.09 for PTR), indicating CO-saturated surfaces. In contrast, the reaction orders with respect to O2 are substantially larger (nO2 = 0.20 for PTS and 0.41 for PTR), and this fractional O2 dependence suggests that lattice oxygen replenishment governs the overall rate, in accordance with an MvK-type mechanism71. Within this framework, the higher O2 dependence of PTR reflects slower reoxidation kinetics and less efficient oxygen transfer across the Pt–TiO2(101) interface, whereas the weaker O2 dependence of PTS indicates more facile supply of lattice oxygen from TiO2(001) to interfacial Pt sites enabled by reconstructed interface geometry. This interpretation is consistent with reports of reverse oxygen spillover, in which lattice oxygen migrates from oxide supports to interfacial metal atoms14, and with mechanistic studies identifying oxygen transfer to interfacial Pt sites as rate-limiting in Pt/TiO2 systems71.

Catalytic cycle tests provide direct kinetic evidence of how this facet-directed restructuring dictates catalytic reactivity. As shown in Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 35, relative to the 1 st run, PTS exhibits a further 22.7–25.6% decrease in Ea over the 2nd to 5th runs, accompanied by enhanced low-temperature activity, whereas PTR shows almost no change in Ea or light-off behaviour. Consistently, Pt particle-size distributions and high-magnification STEM images after the 5th cycle (Supplementary Fig. 36) show no significant change in Pt size, while facet-dependent Pt morphology is maintained without clear evidence of TiO2 surface reconstruction near the Pt–TiO2 interface. These results indicate that reaction-induced restructuring in PTS leads to a more active catalytic state that remains stable over repeated runs.

Beyond the theoretical and kinetic insights into facet-dependent Pt reconstruction and catalytic activity, the intrinsic redox properties of TiO2 facets were examined, focusing on Ov abundance and reactive oxygen species relevant to interfacial redox behaviour (Supplementary Note 11). O 1s XPS analyses under ultra-high-vacuum (UHV) conditions (Supplementary Figs. 37a, b) show that pristine TiO2(001) and PTS exhibit Oads/Olatt ratios—as a descriptor of Ov abundance—that are 1.76 and 1.95 times higher, respectively, than those of their TiO2(101) counterparts. Consistently, O2-TPD and EPR analyses (Supplementary Figs. 38, 39 and Supplementary Note 11) reveal a higher population of reactive oxygen species on the (001) facet, further enhanced at the Pt–TiO2(001) interface.

The redox behaviour of TiO2 under reaction environments was further investigated using in situ Ti K-edge XANES (Supplementary Fig. 40). The Ti K-edge spectra of both pristine TiO2 and Pt/TiO2 catalysts closely matched those of reference anatase TiO2. Upon heating to 225 °C in 5% CO/He, PTS exhibits features characteristic of reduced TiO2 species (Fig. 6g)72, whereas PTR shows negligible changes even up to 275 °C (Fig. 6h). Under CO/O2 co-feed conditions (5% CO + 5% O2/He), no significant changes are observed for either catalyst, implying facile reoxidation of reduced sites in PTS73. These results demonstrate that PTS has a more reducible Pt–TiO2 interface than PTR, owing to favourable surface properties of the (001) facet and the reconstructed interfacial structure under reaction conditions. Additionally, H2-temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) reveals a hydrogen uptake peak at ~350 °C associated with hydrogen spillover and reduction of surface TiO2 near the Pt interface (Supplementary Fig. 41)74. This feature appears only in Pt-containing samples and is more pronounced for PTS than PTR, despite similar Pt loading and dispersion, reflecting a larger Pt–TiO2 interfacial area and stronger MSI for PTS75.

These spectroscopic results indicate that the TiO2(001) facet provides a more efficient oxygen reservoir at the Pt–TiO2 interface under reaction conditions, owing to its higher intrinsic Ov abundance and more reactive oxygen species. Consistently, O 1s XPS analyses under reaction environments (Supplementary Figs. 37c, d and Supplementary Note 11) show that PTS exhibits a pronounced decrease in the Oads/Olatt ratio with increasing temperature (from 0.56 to 0.38), whereas PTR shows only a marginal change. This behaviour suggests that oxygen species associated with Ov sites on TiO2(001) are more efficiently consumed and replenished at the Pt–TiO2(001) interface, facilitating oxygen transfer to interfacial Pt sites and thereby contributing to the enhanced catalytic activity of PTS.

Post-reaction O 1s analyses further show that the initially higher Oads/Olatt ratio of PTS relative to PTR becomes comparable after CO oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 42). When considered together with the catalytic cycle results, in which PTS shows an additional Ea decrease from the 2nd run onward whereas PTR remains nearly unchanged, this result indicates that the enhanced activity of PTS after the first run cannot be described simply by the initial Ov abundance. Instead, it is more closely associated with the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2(001) interfacial structure, which promotes favourable support-oxygen participation during CO oxidation. This interpretation is further supported by reductive pretreatment controls (Supplementary Figs. 43–45 and Supplementary Note 12). H2 pretreatment increases the Ov-related O 1s contribution and enhances the initial activity of both catalysts, but only PTS shows an additional Ea decrease during subsequent CO oxidation runs, reaching the low-Ea state observed in the original catalytic cycling test, while the post-reaction O 1s spectral features of PTS and PTR again become comparable.

To further evaluate whether the facet-dependent Pt structural evolution can be attributed solely to Ov-related effects, we conducted qualitative Pt/Al2O3 (PtA) reference experiments and GA-optimised simulations using Ov-modulated TiO2 models (Supplementary Note 13). PtA was used only as a qualitative irreducible-support reference, not as a direct analogue of Pt/TiO2, because Al2O3 lacks a well-defined facet-controlled surface and exhibits different metal–support interactions from TiO2. The PtA results show reversible CO-induced Pt surface roughening on irreducible Al2O3 without persistent Pt–support interfacial restructuring (Supplementary Figs. 46–49), unlike the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2 interfacial structures observed on reducible TiO2. In parallel, GA-optimised simulations using Ov-modulated TiO2 models show that Ov can modulate local Pt–TiO2 interactions and Pt wetting, while the overall facet-dependent morphology trend remains preserved within the examined Ov range (Supplementary Figs. 50 and 51). These results indicate that Ov-related effects can influence Pt structural evolution, but do not solely determine the facet-dependent Pt morphology. Together with the experimental and computational evidence discussed above, the enhanced reactivity of PTS is consistent with the combined contribution of higher Ov-related redox reactivity and a reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2(001) interfacial configuration derived from the initially wetted Pt morphology.

Discussion

In this work, we employed morphology-controlled Pt/TiO2 catalysts to elucidate how TiO2 crystal facets direct MSI and the resulting dynamic structural evolution and catalytic behaviour of supported Pt NPs during CO oxidation. Combining experimental characterisation with GA optimisation and GNN–MD simulation, we demonstrate that the TiO2(001) facet stabilises more wetted, hemispherical Pt NPs with enhanced interfacial contact, whereas the TiO2(101) facet favours compact cuboctahedral NPs with weaker MSI. Crucially, while CO adsorption induces structural reconstruction on both facets, strong MSI on TiO2(001) prevents severe dewetting and instead drives Pt NPs toward an expanded, reaction-stabilised interfacial state. This facet-directed restructuring creates a dynamic synergy with the intrinsically higher Ov-related redox reactivity of TiO2(001). The catalytic consequence of this synergy is clearly reflected in the markedly lower initial Ea for Pt/TiO2(001) and its further decrease (~25%) upon repeated catalytic runs. Supported by reaction-order analysis and DFT-predicted energetics, this kinetic evolution confirms that the superior performance cannot be attributed solely to the intrinsic support properties of the fresh catalyst, but instead arises from facile oxygen replenishment at the reaction-evolved Pt–TiO2(001) interface via an MvK-type mechanism.

Collectively, these results provide a broader perspective on facet-dependent catalysis on reducible oxides. We establish that the catalytic role of support facets extends far beyond providing static intrinsic properties; rather, they actively direct the evolution of the metal–support interface under reaction conditions. Beyond CO oxidation, this concept of dynamic MSI provides a comprehensive mechanistic framework for other complex metal–metal oxide systems, opening new avenues for designing dynamically evolving and highly efficient heterogeneous catalysts.

Methods

Materials

Tetrabutyl titanate (Ti(OBu)4, 98%), P25 (Titanium(Ⅳ) oxide, 21 nm primary particle size, ≥99.5% trace metal basis), anatase nanopowder (titanium(IV) oxide, anatase form, <25 nm, 99.7% trace metals basis), titanium(Ⅳ) chloride (TiCl4, 99.9% trace metal basis), hydrofluoric acid (HF, 48 wt%), lithium chloride (LiCl, ≥99.98% trace metal basis), ammonium hydroxide solution (NH4OH, 28–30 wt%), isopropanol ((CH3)2CHOH, ≥99.5%), ethylene glycol (HOCH2CH2OH, ≥99%), and chloroplatinic acid hydrate (H2PtCl6 ∙ xH2O, ≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Anatase bulk powder (titanium(IV) oxide, anatase form, 98.5%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH pellets, 98%), absolute ethanol (EtOH, anhydrous, 98%), and hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35.0–37.0%) were purchased from Samchun Chemicals. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of TiO2 supports

Anatase TiO2 Sheets (TiO2-S) with dominant (001) facets were synthesised via a reported method with slight modifications76. Briefly, tetrabutyl titanate (30 mL) and hydrofluoric acid (4.8 mL) were added sequentially to a 200 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, stirred for 20 min, and heated at 180 °C for 24 h. After natural cooling to room temperature (RT, 25 °C), the precipitate was washed with ethanol and deionised (DI) water and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h. To remove fluoride ions, the powder (1 g) was soaked in 0.1 M NaOH (500 mL) for 12 h with stirring at RT, washed with DI water (pH 7–8), and vacuum-dried at 60 °C overnight77. Then, it was treated again in 1 M NaOH (660 mL) at 80 °C for 24 h, washed with DI water (pH 7–8), and dried as described above. Anatase TiO2 rhombic particles (TiO2-R) with predominant (101) facets were synthesised using a modified two-step hydrothermal method21,78. First, commercial TiO2 (2 g) was dispersed in 10 M NaOH (80 mL), stirred for 30 min, transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 24 h. The precipitate was filtered, centrifuged, and washed (pH 10.5). Next, wet Na-titanates (1.5 g) and LiCl (0.1966 g) were added to DI water (150 mL), stirred for 30 min, and hydrothermally treated at 200 °C for 4 h. After cooling, the solids were washed (DI water and ethanol, 3 times each) and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Another rhombic TiO2 material (TiO2-r) with predominant (101) facets was synthesised from TiCl4 following a reported two-step method with modifications79. In the first step, TiCl4 (6.6 mL) was added to 0.43 M HCl (20 mL) in an ice bath, stirred for 20 min, and then added dropwise to 5.5 wt% NH4OH. The pH was adjusted to 6–7. The resulting Ti(OH)4 was centrifuged, washed (pH 7–8), and dried at 70 °C for 12 h. In the second step, Ti(OH)4 (2 g) was dispersed in a 1:1 mixture of DI water and isopropanol (15 mL each), stirred for 30 min, and hydrothermally treated at 180 °C for 24 h. The final solids were washed and vacuum-dried. All TiO2 materials were calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 h (100 mL⋅min−1).

Synthesis of Pt/TiO2 and Pt/Al2O3 catalysts

The Pt/TiO2 catalysts were synthesised via a modified ethylene glycol reduction method80. Typically, TiO2 (0.2 g) and ethylene glycol (50 mL) were sonicated for 20 min in a three-neck flask. Then, a Pt/EG solution (5 mg·mL−1, 0.5 mL) was added, followed by stirring under N₂ for 30 min. Afterwards, 0.25 M NaOH/EG (1.25 mL) was injected, and the mixture was heated to 160 °C for 2 h (10 °C·min−1). After cooling to RT, 0.25 M HCl/EG (2 mL) was added and stirred for 2 h. The product was centrifuged and washed with ethanol and DI water until neutral pH was reached and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight. The Pt/Al2O3 catalyst was synthesised using the same procedure, except that TiO2 was replaced with Al2O3.

Synthesis of size-controlled Pt/TiO2 catalysts

For the size-controlled Pt/TiO2 catalysts, different amounts of NaOH/EG or HCl/EG solutions were added prior to the heating step to create basic or acidic environments that regulate Pt nucleation and growth. The resulting samples were denoted as xPTS (Pt/TiO2 sheet) or xPTR (Pt/TiO2 rhombic), where x corresponds to the fresh Pt particle size determined from TEM (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Typically, for the synthesis of 1.45PTS, TiO2 sheet (0.1 g) and ethylene glycol (25 mL) were sonicated for 20 min in a three-neck flask, followed by addition of a Pt/EG solution (5 mg·mL−1, 0.25 mL) and stirring under N2 for 30 min. Then, 0.25 M NaOH/EG (5 mL) was injected, and the mixture was heated to 160 °C for 2 h (10 °C·min−1). After cooling to room temperature, 0.25 M HCl/EG (1 mL) was added and stirred for 2 h. The product was centrifuged and washed with ethanol and DI water until neutral pH was reached, followed by drying under vacuum at 60 °C overnight. For 1.59PTS, the same procedure was used as for 1.45PTS, except that 0.25 M NaOH/EG (2.5 mL) was employed. For 2.69PTS and 3.76PTS, the same protocol was used as for 1.45PTS, but 0.25 M HCl/EG (0.5 and 2 mL, respectively) was added instead of the NaOH/EG solution. For 1.33PTR, 1.60PTR, and 1.68PTR, the synthesis followed the same procedure as for 1.45PTS, except that TiO2 rhombic was used as the support and 0.25 M NaOH/EG (5, 2.5, and 1.25 mL, respectively) was introduced. For 2.79PTR and 4.75PTR, the synthesis route was the same as for 1.33PTR, except that 0.25 M HCl/EG (0.5 and 2 mL, respectively) was added instead of the NaOH/EG solution.

Catalyst characterization

High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF–STEM) were conducted using JEM-2100F (JEOL) and JEM-ARM300F (JEOL) microscopes operated at 200 and 160 kV, respectively. Powder XRD patterns were recorded using a D/max 2500 diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) equipped with a rotating anode and Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15418 nm). XPS analysis was performed using an Al Kα source (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo-Fisher, USA). Raman spectra were collected using a WITec alpha300R spectrometer with a 532 nm diode laser at a laser power of 1 mW. Spectra were acquired with an exposure time of 10 s and accumulated over 10 scans using a charge-coupled device detector. Specific surface areas were measured with the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method through nitrogen adsorption at −196 °C using a BELSORP-max instrument. Prior to the measurements, the samples were degassed under vacuum at 150 °C for 12 h. EPR spectra were obtained using a Bruker EMXplus (X-band, 9.43 GHz) at −173 °C with 100 kHz field modulation and 4.0 G amplitude.

Pt loading was determined by inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy (ICP–OES, Varian 700-ES) after digestion in aqua regia. CO chemisorption experiments were carried out by pulse injection using a BELCAT II system (MicrotracBEL). In a typical procedure, the catalyst (70 mg) was pretreated at 250 °C for 1 h under 5% O2/Ar (30 mL·min−1), followed by 1 h of Ar flow (30 mL·min−1). After cooling to −40 °C under Ar, a 2% CO/He mixture was pulsed (5 s per pulse), and CO uptake was measured. Pt dispersion was calculated assuming a 1:1 CO-to-Pt atom adsorption stoichiometry.

O2-TPD and H2-TPR experiments were performed using a BELCAT II instrument. For O2-TPD, the catalyst (50 mg) was pretreated at 300 °C for 1 h under He (50 mL·min−1), then cooled to 50 °C. O2 (5% O2/He) was adsorbed for 30 min, followed by purging for another 30 min. TPD measurements were carried out from 50 to 600 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C·min−1 under He. For H2-TPR, the samples were pretreated at 250 °C for 1 h under 10% O2/Ar, cooled to 50 °C, purged with Ar for 30 min, and then heated from 50 to 700 °C under 10% H2/Ar at 10 °C·min−1. CO-TPR was performed using the same system coupled with a mass spectrometer (BEL-MASS, BEL Japan). Catalysts (100 mg) were pretreated at 150 °C for 1 h under Ar (50 mL·min−1), cooled to 50 °C, and then heated to 300 °C (or 350 °C for PTR) at 1 °C·min−1 under either 5% CO/Ar or 5% CO + 1% O2/Ar. The temperature was maintained for 2 h at the final setpoint.

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) measurements were performed using a Nicolet iS10 Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector and a DRIFTS reaction cell (high-temperature reaction chamber; Harrick Scientific) equipped with ZnSe windows. The inlet and outlet gas flows were connected to the DRIFTS cell. For pre-reaction CO-DRIFTS experiments, the sample (20 mg) was loaded into the DRIFTS cell and pretreated at 150 °C for 1 h under a He flow (50 mL·min−1). After cooling to 25 °C, background spectra were collected. CO adsorption was performed until CO saturation under a 1% CO/He flow (50 mL·min−1), followed by desorption under He flow until no further decrease in the CO adsorption spectrum was observed. Spectra were obtained at equal time intervals during CO adsorption and He purging. For in situ DRIFTS experiments under reaction conditions, following pre-reaction CO-DRIFTS experiments, the reactant gas (1% CO + 1% O2/Ar flow, 50 mL·min−1) was sequentially introduced into the DRIFTS cell. After increasing the temperature to 30 °C, in situ DRIFTS spectra were obtained at elevated temperature, with intervals of 20–30 °C, after 5 min stabilisation at each target temperature until catalytic ignition occurred. For post-reaction CO-DRIFTS experiments, following the in situ DRIFTS experiments, the samples were treated at 240 °C for 2 h under a 1% CO + 1% O2/Ar flow (50 mL·min−1). After cooling to 25 °C, spectra were obtained in the same manner as the pre-reaction CO-DRIFTS experiments. To compare the relative CO adsorption features associated with different Pt surface sites, the pre- and post-reaction CO-DRIFTS spectra were baseline-corrected and normalized to the maximum intensity of the linearly adsorbed CO band over 2250–1900 cm−1 region. Figure 3a, d display the enlarged 2150–2000 cm−1 region to highlight the characteristic CO adsorption features discussed in the manuscript. All spectra were collected using 64 accumulated scans with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1.

Ex situ EXAFS spectroscopy at the Pt L3-edge was conducted in the fluorescence mode using a 4-channel Si drift detector at beamline 8C, Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL). In situ Ti K-edge XANES measurements were performed in the transmission mode at beamline 6D (PAL) using a polyimide-tube-based gas flow cell with Be windows. For reducing conditions, 5% CO/He (30 mL·min−1) was used, while for oxidising conditions, 5% O2/He was co-fed. The spectra were recorded 15 min after the target temperature was reached. Data were analysed using ATHENA and ARTEMIS in the IFEFFIT software package. WT-EXAFS analysis was performed using the Morlet wavelet.

NAP-XPS measurements were conducted at beamline 8A2 (PAL) using a PHOIBOS 150 hemispherical analyser (SPECS). Samples were preheated to 130 °C under ultra-high-vacuum and cooled to 25 °C before backfilling with 0.15 mbar CO and 0.1 mbar O2. The photon energy was set at 650 eV. Core-level spectra (O 1s, C 1s, Ti 2p, and Pt 4f) were recorded at each temperature. Deconvolution was performed using CasaXPS with a Lorentzian asymmetric line shape and a Shirley-type background.

Catalytic CO oxidation

The CO oxidation reactions were conducted in a quartz fixed-bed reactor. In a typical run, the catalyst (20 mg) was mixed with quartz sand (480 mg) and loaded between the quartz wool plugs. The catalyst was pretreated at 150 °C for 1 h under Ar (50 mL·min−1). The reaction was conducted using a feed gas containing 1% CO and 1% O2 in Ar (total flow rate: 50 mL·min−1). The CO/O2 ratios were adjusted between 0.2 (O2-rich) and 5 (CO-rich) while keeping the total flow rate constant. This corresponds to a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 150,000 mL·h−1·g−1. The reactor temperature was increased at 5 °C·min−1, and the reaction was stabilised for 10 min at each temperature before performing at least four measurements. The gas products were analysed using a gas chromatograph (YL 6500) equipped with a Carboxen 1000 column (Supelco) and thermal conductivity detector (TCD). CO conversion was calculated by comparing the inlet and outlet CO concentrations. The apparent activation energies (Ea) were determined from Arrhenius plots in the kinetically controlled region at low conversion (~10%) (see Supplementary Fig. 5). All Ea values were averaged over three independent experiments using newly loaded catalysts to ensure reproducibility, and the error bars represent the standard deviation.

Reaction-order analysis of CO oxidation was conducted using post-reaction Pt/TiO2 catalysts treated at 240 °C for 2 h, ensuring the reaction-induced Pt restructuring was preserved. The catalyst (40 mg) was mixed with quartz sand (960 mg), and all measurements were performed at a total flow rate of 100 mL·min−1 with Ar as the balance gas. Reaction rates were measured in the kinetically relevant regime (<5% CO conversion) at 80 °C for post-reaction PTS and 160 °C for post-reaction PTR, corresponding to their respective light-off temperatures. For CO partial-pressure dependence measurements, the CO concentration was varied from 0.5 to 3% while keeping the O2 concentration constant at 5%. For O2 partial-pressure dependence measurements, the O2 concentration was varied from 0.5 to 3% with the CO concentration fixed at 0.5%. Reaction rates were determined under steady-state conditions, and the reaction orders with respect to CO and O2 were obtained from linear fits of log–log plots of the average reaction rate as a function of the corresponding reactant partial pressure.

Computational details

DFT calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP, version 6.2.0) with projector augmented-wave (PAW) potentials81. The spin-polarized-generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was employed for exchange–correlation interactions, and van der Waals interactions were accounted for using Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction scheme82. To accurately describe the electronic structure of Ti atoms, a Hubbard U correction (DFT + U) of 4.2 eV was applied83. For structural modeling, bulk anatase TiO2 was first optimised, and surface slab models with comparable surface areas were constructed. The TiO2(101) surface was modeled as a p(1 × 6) supercell (a = 10.2491 Å, b = 22.9816 Å, c = 30.4871 Å) containing 144 atoms, while the TiO2(001) surface was represented by a p(3 × 6) supercell (a = 11.4908 Å, b = 22.9816 Å, c = 30.8871 Å) comprising 108 atoms83. Platinum nanorods composed of 28 atoms Pt28 were derived from bulk Pt and geometry optimised. Atomic structure visualizations were generated using POV-Ray and VESTA.

Monkhorst–Pack k-point meshes were used for Brillouin zone sampling: 3 × 3 × 1 for TiO2 slabs, 7 × 7 × 1 for bulk Pt, and 1 × 1 × 1 for Pt/TiO2 systems. A vacuum space of ~20 Å was included in the z-direction to avoid inter-slab interactions, and the bottom half of the slab layers was fixed to emulate bulk properties. The Pt28 nanorods were placed on the surfaces with various initial configurations, and the most stable configuration was determined by total energy minimization. The final DFT-optimised models for Pt28/TiO2(001) and Pt28/TiO2(101) are shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 26.
