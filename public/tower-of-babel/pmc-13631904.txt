Variation in vitelline membrane strength during the extended laying cycle in laying hens
Authors: Yu Liu, Jiajie Yang, Biyao Sun, Lin Xuan, Wenbin Zhou, Guoxuan Huang, Yaqi Li, Tongyao Li, Lining Qin, Guiyun Xu, Jiangxia Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The vitelline membrane strength (VMS) of chicken eggs is critical to liquid-egg processing, as a weakened membrane ruptures more easily during yolk and albumen separation, contaminating albumen with yolk. With the adoption of the extended laying cycle, the processing potential of eggs from older hens and stored eggs remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate changes in VMS during the extended laying cycle and the effects of breed, storage time, and temperature, and to assess yolk-contamination risk. At each of 11 time points between 43 and 100 wk, 180 eggs per breed were collected from White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red hens and equally assigned to 0, 7, or 14 d at 25°C. An additional 180 eggs per breed were collected at 53 wk and assigned to 4, 25, or 30°C for 7 d. VMS and egg quality traits were measured using a TA-XT plus texture analyzer and an EMT-5200 egg quality analyzer, and yolk contamination proportion was calculated. Results showed that VMS across hen ages did not decline continuously throughout the laying cycle and remained above 0.86 N in White Leghorn and 0.67 N in Rhode Island Red from 72 to 100 wk. White Leghorn showed higher VMS than Rhode Island Red, with significant differences after 87 wk (P < 0.05). VMS decreased as storage time increased at 25°C. Yolk contamination showed a higher incidence of exceeding 0.2% at 14 d, a threshold relevant to baking quality. Eggs stored at 4°C had significantly higher VMS than those stored at 25°C and 30°C (P < 0.05) at 7 d. Overall, VMS was not associated with albumen height and Haugh unit (P > 0.05). Correlation analysis showed that VMS was positively associated with egg weight, yolk weight, and yolk index (P < 0.05). In conclusion, VMS did not decline continuously throughout the extended laying cycle. Given that VMS decreased with storage time at 25°C, storage at 4°C is recommended for eggs intended for liquid-egg processing.

Introduction

Eggs are an excellent source of high-quality animal protein and nutrients (Sanlier and Üstün, 2021). China is the largest chicken egg producer in the world, with an annual output exceeding 31 million tonnes (FAO, 2026) and a long-standing first place globally (Liu et al., 2024). China is also a major chicken egg-consuming country, where most eggs are sold as table eggs for direct consumption (Yang, 2021; Zhou et al., 2025) and the egg-processing rate is approximately 12% (Liu et al., 2024). In addition, the extended laying cycle has become a common management practice in modern layer production, delivering substantial economic and environmental benefits by lowering the production cost per egg and prolonging the productive cycle of laying hens (Bain et al., 2016). In recent years, the liquid-egg industry in China has developed rapidly. The annual use of liquid egg now exceeds 500,000 tonnes and egg powder production has surpassed 80,000 tonnes (Huang, 2025).

Liquid-egg products include albumen, yolk, and whole egg. Albumen is an excellent foaming agent (Lomakina and Mikova, 2006) and is widely used in baking and the food industry. Albumen contaminated with yolk can alter protein functionality and reduce foaming properties. The effective separation of albumen and egg yolk is a key step in liquid-egg processing and directly affects the quality of the final product, because yolk contamination above the 0.2% threshold can impair batter structure formation and reduce cake quality at higher contamination levels (Li et al., 2022).

The vitelline membrane is an important component of the egg yolk and its integrity is essential to the production of liquid albumen (Marzec et al., 2016). It protects the yolk, maintains its spherical shape, and effectively separates the yolk contents from the albumen (Damaziak et al., 2025). Apart from biological functions, the physical properties of the vitelline membrane, and in particular its strength, are critical in the processing of table eggs. A high vitelline membrane strength (VMS) effectively prevents accidental rupture during separation, thereby ensuring the quality of albumen products (Hervé et al., 2026). VMS, defined as the force required to rupture the membrane and expressed in N, is one of the main physical parameters. Current research on the vitelline membrane has focused mainly on microstructure and proteome (Damaziak et al., 2020a; b; Hervé et al., 2026).

Fresh eggs are usually the main raw material for liquid-egg processing. The processing potential and feasibility of stored eggs and eggs from older hens remain unclear. During storage and transportation, storage conditions and time affect the egg quality, including VMS (Garcia et al., 2026). Egg and yolk weights continued to increase, while eggshell breaking strength decreased markedly by 100 wk (Li et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). However, whether the factors affecting egg quality also influence liquid-egg processing remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the changes in VMS of eggs in White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red during the extended laying cycle, and to evaluate the effects of storage time and temperature. The study also assessed the yolk-contamination risk of processing eggs to evaluate the feasibility of using eggs from the older hens and of stored eggs for liquid-egg processing.

Materials and methods

Egg collection

Eggs were collected from the pure lines of White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red at the Experimental Unit for Poultry Genetic Resources and Breeding of China Agricultural University (Beijing, China). Measurements were taken every 4 to 6 wk starting from 43 wk and continuing until 100 wk. Egg collection was suspended at 48 wk and 78 wk due to management procedures, resulting in a total of 11 time points, with 180 eggs per breed at each time point. These eggs were equally divided into three groups and stored for 0, 7 and 14 d. All eggs were stored at 25°C.

To evaluate the effect of storage temperature, eggs from 53-wk-old White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red hens were stored for 0 and 7 d at 4, 25, and 30°C, with 60 eggs used per breed at each temperature.

Vitelline membrane strength

This procedure for the determination of VMS was adapted from Woodward et al. (Woodward et al., 1987). After each egg was broken, the yolk, separated from albumen with an egg separator, was then carefully placed in a 10-cm-diameter glass container. The bottom of the container was lined with filter paper of the same diameter to prevent the yolk from sliding during measurement. VMS was measured on these isolated yolks using a TA-XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) equipped with a 50-mm cylinder probe (P50). The displacement speed was set at 0.5 mm/s, and the trigger force was 0.200 N. The force required to rupture the vitelline membrane (N) was recorded. Due to the destructive effect of the method, only a single measurement was obtained from each egg.

Egg quality

An EMT-5200 egg quality analyzer (Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure egg quality parameters, including egg weight, albumen height and Haugh unit. The yolk and albumen were then separated with an egg separator, the chalazae were carefully removed, and the yolk was weighed on an electronic balance (Yoke Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The diameter and height of the yolk were measured with the digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Japan), and the yolk index was calculated.

Yolk contamination proportion

Yolk contamination proportion was determined only for eggs in which yolk rupture occurred after egg breaking. After yolk-albumen separation, all yolk remnants and albumen were weighed separately, and yolk contamination proportion was calculated as yolk remnant weight divided by total albumen weight and expressed as a percentage.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with R 4.2.3 and SPSS 27.0. VMS was analyzed by two-way ANOVA to evaluate the effects of breed, hen age, and their interaction. Pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were performed using the emmeans package with Tukey–Kramer adjustment. One-way ANOVA was conducted for storage time and temperature experiments within each breed. Fisher’s LSD test was used when variances were homogeneous; otherwise, Welch’s ANOVA followed by Tamhane’s T2 test was applied. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between VMS and egg quality traits, including egg weight, albumen height, Haugh unit, yolk weight, and yolk index. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Changes in VMS among breeds during the extended laying cycle

In both White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red, VMS did not show a continuous decline across hen ages and remained above 0.86 N in White Leghorn and 0.67 N in Rhode Island Red from 72 to 100 wk. Breed, hen age, and their interaction significantly affected VMS (P < 0.05). VMS was higher in White Leghorn than in Rhode Island Red throughout the laying cycle, with significant differences after 87 wk (P < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 1a. Gulabrai et al. also observed significant strain differences in VMS (P < 0.0001), but with a different pattern: brown strains had higher VMS than white strains, with values of 2.092 g for Hy-Line Brown and 2.045 g for Bovans Brown, compared with 1.856 g for Hy-Line W-36 White and 1.750 g for H&N White hens (Gulabrai et al., 2025). Gulabrai et al. evaluated four cage-free commercial strains using a 1-mm blunt probe, while the present study used two pure lines housed in cages and a 50-mm cylindrical probe (P50) (Woodward et al., 1987). Differences in strain, housing system, and measurement method may have contributed to the different results. Jones et al. found that, with the same cage-free aviary housing and diet, the two white shell strains (DeKalb White and Hy-Line W-36) had higher VMS than the two brown shell strains (Hy-Line Brown and Bovans Brown) at 21 wk but lower VMS at 31, 42, and 60 wk, indicating that strain differences may vary with hen age (Jones et al., 2018a). Wall et al. noted that the 2016 strain showed overall stronger vitelline strength and elasticity than the 1940 strain, suggesting that genetics had a greater impact (Wall et al., 2024). Marzec et al. found that goose egg yolks had the lowest VMS, turkey egg yolks had the highest VMS, and Muscovy duck, guinea fowl, chicken, and Japanese quail showed no apparent differences (Marzec et al., 2016). In domestic geese, Damaziak et al. showed that breed was associated with differences in vitelline membrane microstructure and proteome (Damaziak et al., 2025). In White Leghorn, as shown in Fig. 1b, the VMS of eggs from 43 to 100 wk ranged from 0.76 to 1.61 N. The strength at 100 wk was the highest. As shown in Fig. 1c, VMS in Rhode Island Red fluctuated less with hen age, ranging overall from 0.67 to 1.19 N. A previous study reported that, over 12 production cycles, the VMS of several commercial layer strains decreased from 2.33 to 1.92 g force (Curtis et al., 2005). Marzec et al. reported that VMS showed no clear decline between 45 and 70 wk, with values of 246.94, 254.01, and 237.87 g at 45–46, 55–56, and 69–70 wk, respectively (Marzec et al., 2019). In the present study, VMS did not decrease after 72 wk, with values of 1.61 and 1.04 N at 100 wk in White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red. Correlation analysis further showed that, at 96 wk, VMS was positively correlated with yolk weight in White Leghorn (r = 0.399; P < 0.05) and yolk index in Rhode Island Red (r = 0.315; P < 0.05); at 100 wk, it was positively correlated with egg weight and yolk weight in White Leghorn (r = 0.554 and 0.523; P < 0.05) and with yolk weight in Rhode Island Red (r = 0.388; P < 0.05). Damaziak et al. noted that yolk size, and consequently vitelline membrane surface area, may influence the work required for membrane breaking (Damaziak et al., 2018). These associations suggest that variation in VMS at 96 and 100 wk may be related to egg and yolk characteristics. Therefore, eggs produced during the late laying cycle could be used for liquid-egg processing.

VMS and yolk contamination at different hen ages and storage times at 25°C

As shown in Table 1, VMS decreased with longer storage time in both White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red. Over 14 d, the mean VMS of White Leghorn eggs decreased from 1.12 to 0.82 N, a decline of approximately 26.7%, and that of Rhode Island Red decreased from 0.87 to 0.66 N, a decline of approximately 23.1%. Kirunda and McKee showed that eggs stored for 1 wk at 25°C had lower VMS than fresh eggs, and that the structural integrity of the vitelline membrane was markedly impaired (Kirunda and McKee, 2000). This impairment may make yolk rupture more likely and increase yolk contamination proportion, thereby affecting the quality of albumen products (Berardinelli et al., 2008). During storage, the osmotic-pressure difference between the albumen and the yolk drives water across the semipermeable vitelline membrane into the yolk (Fromm, 1966; Heath, 1976), subjecting the membrane to greater internal tension and lowering its breaking strength. Furthermore, Kido et al. reported that degradation of glycoprotein II, a major structural glycoprotein of the vitelline membrane, was partly responsible for the loss of membrane integrity during storage (Kido et al., 1975). Consistent with the decline in VMS, the yolk contamination proportion tended to increase during storage at 25°C (Figure 2). On 0 d, the vitelline membrane was generally intact, with relatively high VMS. Little yolk rupture occurred across hen ages in either breed, and most yolk contamination proportions remained below 0.2%. On 7 d, as VMS decreased, the yolk contamination proportion ranged from 0 to 0.32%, and exceeded 0.2% at 2 of 11 hen ages in both breeds. On 14 d, the yolk contamination proportion in White Leghorn reached its highest level. Yolk rupture occurred at every age, and 4 of 11 hen ages exceeded 0.2%. In Rhode Island Red, the yolk contamination proportion rose, while 6 of 11 exceeded 0.2%. Different levels of yolk contamination can impair albumen functionality, including foam capacity, foam stability, batter specific density, and cake quality (Wang and Wang, 2009; Macherey et al., 2011; Li et al., 2019, 2022). Although methods such as centrifugation can improve the functionality of yolk-contaminated albumen, the yolk contamination must still be kept below 0.2%. Yolk contamination above 0.2% may impair batter structure formation during heating, and higher contamination levels can reduce overrun and cake specific volume and result in a more compact crumb structure (Macherey et al., 2011; Li et al., 2022). Therefore, 0.2% can be considered a practical threshold for controlling yolk contamination proportion in liquid albumen processing. In the present study, prolonged storage at 25°C reduced VMS and increased the yolk contamination proportion. After 14 d of storage, the decline in VMS and the higher incidence of yolk contamination indicated that these eggs were not suitable for liquid-egg production.Fig. 2Yolk contamination proportion in albumen during storage in White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red eggs. 0 d, 7 d, and 14 d denote storage for 0, 7, and 14 d, respectively.

Effects of storage temperature on VMS and yolk contamination

Fig. 3 shows that, after 7 d of storage at 4, 25 and 30°C, eggs stored at 4°C had higher VMS than those stored at 25°C and significantly higher VMS than those stored at 30°C (P < 0.05). Along with the decrease in VMS, yolk contamination proportion increased. Eggs stored at 4°C had the highest VMS (P < 0.05) and the lowest yolk contamination proportion, indicating that low-temperature storage can effectively maintain VMS. Refrigeration can slow the decline of internal egg quality and vitelline membrane properties during long term storage (Garcia et al., 2026). Jones et al. reported that, over 6 wk of storage, VMS decreased by approximately 18 g force in refrigerated eggs, compared with approximately 85 g force in eggs stored at 22°C (Jones et al., 2018b). Jones and Musgrove also reported that VMS did not change significantly during 10 wk of storage at 4°C, although membrane elasticity decreased as cold storage progressed, a change consistent with decreased yolk viscosity during storage (Jones and Musgrove, 2005). In contrast, higher storage temperature accelerates the increase in albumen pH and aggravates the weakening of the vitelline membrane. After 28 d of storage, albumen pH increased to 8.94 at 4°C and 9.23 at 20°C, while VM breaking strength decreased by 24.6% and 58%, respectively (Hervé et al., 2026). After 7 d of storage, VMS increased slightly at 4°C in White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red by 3.9% and 1.2%, respectively, but decreased by 2.8% and 12.5% at 25°C and by 14.2% and 13.2% at 30°C, respectively. During storage at 25°C, VMS decreased and the yolk contamination proportion increased, indicating an increased risk of yolk contamination during liquid-egg processing. Correlation analysis among VMS and other egg quality traits

As shown in Figure 4, VMS has significant correlations with egg weight, yolk weight, and yolk index (P < 0.05). These correlation coefficients were mostly moderate and predominantly positive, suggesting that stronger vitelline membranes tended to occur in eggs with greater egg or yolk weight and, in some conditions, a higher yolk index. The yolk index, which reflects yolk shape, is commonly used to evaluate internal egg quality and freshness (Marzec et al., 2019). Albumen height and Haugh unit are common indices of egg freshness. Kirunda et al. analyzed fresh eggs and aged eggs together and found a moderate positive correlation between VMS and Haugh unit (r = 0.56) (Kirunda and McKee, 2000). In the present study, when all eggs stored at 25°C for different storage times were analyzed together, the correlation coefficients between VMS and Haugh unit were r = 0.17 and r = 0.18 (P < 0.05) in the two breeds, respectively. When storage time was considered separately, the correlations of VMS with albumen height and Haugh unit were weak and inconsistent, with r values ranging from -0.297 to 0.290 in White Leghorn and from -0.395 to 0.380 in Rhode Island Red. Whether analyzed together or separately, the correlation coefficients remained very low, indicating that VMS had no association with albumen height and Haugh unit. Especially, when the storage conditions of eggs are unknown, albumen height and Haugh unit alone may be insufficient to determine whether eggs are suitable for liquid-egg processing.

The primary limitations are hand separation with an egg separator and the use of pure lines from White Leghorn and Rhode Island Red, which should be considered when applying the findings to industrial processing and commercial hybrids.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that VMS did not decline continuously throughout the extended laying cycle and remained above 0.86 N in White Leghorn and 0.67 N in Rhode Island Red from 72 to 100 wk, suggesting that eggs from older hens may still have potential for liquid-egg processing. Given that eggs stored at 4°C effectively maintained the highest VMS and the lowest yolk contamination proportion, storage at 4°C is recommended for eggs intended for liquid-egg processing.
