Double chambered right ventricle: when unusual defects challenge clinical practice
Authors: Clara Vazquez, Keerby Hernandez, Andres Tavera, Regina de la Mora, Andriana Anagnostopoulou, Giulia Bruno, Martin Altersberger, Luis Antonio Moreno-Ruiz
Published: 2026

Case description

We present an 8-year-old male referred for a systolic murmur. He had central cyanosis, exertional dyspnoea, and oxygen saturation of 80% on room air. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a prominent horizontal muscular band dividing the right ventricle into inflow and apical chambers with a restrictive communication between them and high gradients (Figure A-B). A second communication is identified in the inlet portion, extending towards the right ventricular outflow tract. (Figure C). The pulmonary valve had an annulus of 11 mm (Z-score −1.48), a peak velocity of 3.01 m/s, and gradients of 36 mmHg (maximum) (Figure D). A secundum atrial septal defect with a right-to-left shunt was also present. Transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed the band partially dividing the right ventricle and allowed detailed assessment of the dynamic obstruction of the tortuous intraventricular communication (Figure E-G). Cardiac computed tomography provided 3D interpretation, identifying the band as a flat structure across the cavity and excluding apical agenesis of the right ventricle. A tunnel-like communication between chambers showed dynamic behaviour matching echocardiographic flow data (Figure H-K); the dynamic echocardiographic findings are shown in Supplementary material online, Video S1.

The findings were consistent with double-chambered right ventricle with pseudo-sequestration of the apical chamber [1–3]. The restrictive shunts and preserved inflow-to-outflow communication maintained haemodynamic balance. Cyanosis was attributed to right-to-left atrial shunting driven by elevated right atrial pressure.

Multimodal imaging was indispensable for establishing the diagnosis and characterizing the lesion. After a multidisciplinary team discussion, a conservative approach was adopted for the following reasons. Although cyanosis and exertional dyspnoea were present, these findings reflected a chronically balanced haemodynamic state rather than active deterioration: the right-to-left shunt occurred at the atrial level due to elevated right atrial pressure, while pulmonary blood flow was maintained through two intraventricular pathways—through the trabeculated chamber and through the inlet-to-outlet communication—partially protected by the pulmonary valve gradient. Surgical correction would entail exceptional technical complexity: restoring normal right ventricular architecture in a ventricle structurally remodelled by the anomalous muscular band carries high procedural risk without a reliable expectation of recovering normal biventricular function. Intervention would be reconsidered if objective evidence of reduced pulmonary blood flow, progressive cyanosis, or functional deterioration emerged over time. At six-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, with no evidence of heart failure, arrhythmias, or further decline in oxygen saturation.
