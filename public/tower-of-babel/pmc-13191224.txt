The continuing challenge of Nutcracker syndrome
Authors: Peter Gloviczki
Published: 2026

The management of Nutcracker syndrome (NS) continues to be a major challenge. Compression of the left renal vein (LRV) between the aorta and the superior mesenteric artery is frequently observed in asymptomatic individuals (the so-called Nutcracker phenomenon). In contrast, the presence of left flank pain, hematuria, pelvic pain, or varicocele attributed to renal venous hypertension due to LRV compression (NS) represents a rare but important clinical condition.

There is no single standardized diagnostic test, and trustworthy, evidence-based guidelines for optimal management are lacking. In this international registry from the Vascular Low Frequency Disease Consortium, Suckow et al1 retrospectively reviewed outcomes in 250 patients treated for NS at 17 centers worldwide. The authors should be congratulated for reporting real-world data on the evaluation and treatment of this challenging syndrome, helping to improve our understanding of its pathophysiology and management.

This study provides several important insights into the management of patients with NS. First, a conservative approach—including oral analgesics, nutritional counseling, and expectant management—was successful in 78% of the 116 patients managed nonoperatively. Although the mean follow-up was only 328 days, these findings are important. Given that NS often affects young female patients and that the optimal definitive treatment remains uncertain, these data support recommending initial nonoperative management in most, if not all, patients.

Second, the study reinforces that the diagnosis of NS remains multidisciplinary. Exclusion of other causes of flank, abdominal, or pelvic pain, as well as hematuria or proteinuria, is as important as confirming hemodynamically significant LRV compression using multiple diagnostic modalities. Computed tomography was the most frequently used test (84%), followed by contrast venography (63%) and duplex ultrasound examination (35%). Among patients who underwent venography, pressure measurements confirmed significant renal venous hypertension, with a mean gradient of 3.9 ± 2.4 mm Hg.

Third, distal transposition of the LRV is currently the most performed open surgical procedure for NS, followed by gonadal vein transposition and renal autotransplantation. Nineteen patients (13%) underwent endovascular treatment, with stent migration occurring in 10.5% (2 of 19). Because only eight stented patients had follow-up longer than 1 year and only three had 3-year follow-up, direct comparison between open and endovascular approaches is limited. Nevertheless, the authors' conclusion—that LRV or gonadal/ovarian vein transposition remains the preferred treatment—is reasonable. This aligns with this reviewer's experience2 and with the conclusions of a recent Delphi consensus.3

However, the data also raise important questions. Despite a 96% patency rate at 3 years in the surgical group, 33% of patients experienced recurrence of symptoms. This suggests that either patient selection was suboptimal or hemodynamic improvement alone does not necessarily resolve chronic pain syndromes. In some cases, these may be more effectively managed with renal autotransplantation.

Overall, this article provides valuable insights despite the limitations inherent to its retrospective design, variable follow-up durations, and relatively small number of procedures performed per center over the 24-year study period (mean of nine interventions per institution). Many important questions regarding optimal management remain unanswered.

Nevertheless, significant progress has been made in the diagnosis and treatment of NS over the past two decades. Intravascular ultrasound examination is now available to improve diagnostic accuracy and guide stent selection. Minimally invasive approaches, including robotic and laparoscopic surgery, have reduced the invasiveness of open procedures.4,5 Extravascular stenting (exostenting) may prove effective in the long term,6,7 and selected patients—particularly those with a positive renal hilum block or renal loin pain hematuria syndrome—clearly benefit from renal autotransplantation when renal vein reconstruction fails.8, 9, 10

Outcomes of endovascular procedures in centers of excellence have also improved. In this regard, the recently reported experience from the University of Pittsburgh is noteworthy.11 In 44 patients with NS, stenting resulted in complete or partial symptom resolution in 90.7% at 4 years, with a primary patency of 82.8% and a primary-assisted patency of 100%. Notably, no cases of stent migration or erosion were observed.

Finally, continued advancements in endovascular therapy are anticipated. The development of stents designed to prevent migration is particularly important, as real-world data—such as those presented in this study—still demonstrate a concerning migration rate.
