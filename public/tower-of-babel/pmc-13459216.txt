Associations of allergic diseases with incident hospital-recorded osteoporosis and major osteoporotic fracture in older adults: a prospective cohort study in the UK Biobank
Authors: Cong Peng, Bin Chen, Zhetian Chen, Huanxiong Chen, Zhuguang Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoporosis and fragility fractures are major causes of disability and loss of independence in older adults. Prospective evidence on the associations between allergic diseases and these outcomes in older populations remains limited, particularly regarding coexistence patterns and allergic disease burden. This study therefore evaluated these associations in a large prospective cohort of older adults.MethodsUK Biobank participants aged 60 years or older and free of the corresponding outcome at baseline were included. Baseline allergic rhinitis, atopic dermatitis, and asthma were identified from inpatient records and self-report and were further summarised as coexistence patterns and allergic disease burden. Associations with incident major osteoporotic fracture and hospital-recorded osteoporosis, ascertained from linked inpatient hospital records, were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle, psychosocial, and comorbidity factors. Robustness was assessed through multiple prespecified sensitivity analyses.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 13.8 years, 11,442 major osteoporotic fracture events and 12,096 cases of hospital-recorded osteoporosis occurred among 190,594 and 191,881 participants, respectively. The coexistence of allergic rhinitis and asthma was also associated with both outcomes (major osteoporotic fracture: 1.36, 1.16–1.59; hospital-recorded osteoporosis: 1.46, 1.26–1.69). In addition, allergic disease burden showed a graded association with both outcomes. Background

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterised by low bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture. Owing to its often asymptomatic early course, the disease may remain unrecognised until a fragility fracture occurs, which constitutes its most important clinical consequence [1]. With advancing age, both the prevalence of osteoporosis and the risk of fragility fractures increase substantially [2]. Consequently, fragility fractures have become a major cause of disability, loss of independence, and increased mortality in older adults, while also imposing considerable burdens on healthcare and long-term care systems [3]. In the United Kingdom alone, approximately 549,000 new fragility fractures occur each year, with associated costs exceeding £4.7 billion. Among these, hip fracture is one of the most serious types, with a 12-month mortality rate of approximately 26% [4]. Because osteoporosis often remains clinically silent until fracture occurs and imposes a substantial burden in older adults, characterising factors associated with bone-related outcomes may help clarify their epidemiological correlates in later life.

Current osteoporosis guidelines emphasise that bone-health assessment should consider established clinical factors in addition to bone mineral density (BMD) measurement, particularly in older adults with a substantial burden of comorbidity and medication exposure [4, 5]. Diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic pulmonary disease, systemic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, and long-term glucocorticoid use are commonly recognised contributors to osteoporosis and fragility fracture and are routinely considered in bone-health assessment [5, 6]. In contrast, allergic diseases have received relatively limited consideration in guideline-based bone health risk assessment, although allergic rhinitis (AR), atopic dermatitis (AD), and asthma affect a substantial proportion of older adults and frequently coexist [7–9]. Although these conditions often begin earlier in life, several plausible pathways may link allergic diseases to bone-related outcomes in later life, including long-term chronic inflammatory burden, cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, and lifestyle-related factors [6, 10–12]. In addition, age-related immune alterations and low-grade chronic inflammation may further disrupt skeletal homeostasis [13]. Type 2 immune pathways, including interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 signalling, may also provide biological plausibility for these associations through osteoimmune crosstalk involving bone remodelling [14].

However, the available evidence remains limited and uneven across allergic disease phenotypes. Although asthma has been associated with osteoporosis and fractures, findings remain inconsistent, and many analyses have focused on corticosteroid-related skeletal effects rather than asthma itself [11, 15–17]. In addition, evidence specifically focused on older adults remains limited. For AD, evidence comes mainly from cross-sectional or retrospective studies, with limited prospective validation in older adults [18]. Direct evidence linking AR to adverse bone outcomes is even more limited. More importantly, prospective evidence remains scarce regarding how coexistence patterns across AR, AD, and asthma, as well as allergic disease burden, are associated with incident hospital-recorded osteoporosis and major osteoporotic fracture (MOF) in later life. Using the prospective UK Biobank cohort, this study therefore examined whether AR, AD, and asthma, considered individually, across coexistence patterns, and according to allergic disease burden, were associated with incident hospital-recorded osteoporosis and MOF among participants aged 60 years or older at baseline, a population in which these bone health outcomes are of particular clinical relevance. These associations were further evaluated in prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings to competing mortality, potential reverse causation, and baseline treatment-related factors, with the aim of extending prospective evidence on allergic diseases and bone-related outcomes in older adults.

Methods

Data source and study design

This study was a prospective cohort analysis using data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort that recruited participants between 2006 and 2010 [19]. Baseline exposures and covariates were defined at the baseline assessment visit, and incident outcomes were subsequently ascertained through linked inpatient hospital records during follow-up. Baseline was defined as the date of each participant’s UK Biobank baseline assessment visit. Baseline assessments included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, psychosocial measures, physical measurements, and medical history. For this study, follow-up through linked inpatient records was available until 31 March 2023.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible if they attended the UK Biobank baseline assessment visit, were aged 60 years or older at the baseline assessment date, and had available data on exposures, follow-up time, and covariates included in the primary multivariable models. The study was designed to focus on older adults, among whom osteoporosis and MOF are more clinically relevant. For outcome-specific analyses, participants with prevalent MOF before or at the baseline assessment date were excluded from the incident MOF analysis, and those with prevalent hospital-recorded osteoporosis before or at the baseline assessment date were excluded from the hospital-recorded osteoporosis analysis. Participants with missing information on outcomes, follow-up time, exposures, or covariates included in the primary multivariable models were excluded. Exposure ascertainment

Baseline allergic diseases included AR, AD, and asthma. In the primary analysis, these conditions were ascertained using linked Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) inpatient records and self-reported information recorded in the UK Biobank. Participants were classified as having AR, AD, or asthma if a diagnosis was indicated in either source before or at the baseline assessment date. Because general practitioner (GP) records were available only for a subset of UK Biobank participants, they were not included in the primary exposure definition. This approach allowed the main analyses to retain the full analytic cohort and use a consistent ascertainment framework based on inpatient records and self-report. Broader exposure definitions incorporating GP records in addition to inpatient records and self-report were evaluated in sensitivity analyses restricted to participants with linked GP data. The corresponding diagnostic codes and definitions are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Two composite exposure variables were additionally constructed. First, allergic disease coexistence patterns were defined as five mutually exclusive groups: none or one allergic disease, AR plus AD, AR plus asthma, AD plus asthma, and the allergic triad (AR plus AD plus asthma). Second, allergic disease burden was defined as the number of allergic diseases present (0, 1, or ≥ 2), as a descriptive count-based measure rather than a biological dose. The source breakdown of allergic disease exposures under the primary exposure definition (self-report only, HES only, or both) is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Outcome ascertainment

Outcomes were identified from ICD-10 codes in linked HES inpatient records, with the first recorded diagnosis date after the baseline assessment date taken as the event date. MOF comprised fractures of the hip, humerus, forearm or wrist, and clinically recorded vertebral fractures. The primary osteoporosis outcome was incident hospital-recorded osteoporosis, defined as the first osteoporosis record in linked inpatient hospital data after baseline, with or without pathological fracture. Because osteoporosis is frequently diagnosed or managed outside inpatient settings and may remain unrecognised in routine care, this outcome should be interpreted as an inpatient-recorded endpoint rather than as the full population burden of clinically diagnosed or dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry-defined osteoporosis. In supportive analyses, participants with available dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-derived imaging measures were included in an imaging subgroup. The imaging outcomes comprised femoral neck bone mineral density (BMD), lumbar spine BMD, femoral neck T-score, and lumbar spine T-score, which were obtained at the UK Biobank first imaging visit (instance 2) after baseline. To maintain temporal consistency, measurements from the first imaging visit were used rather than mixing first and repeat imaging assessments. The corresponding diagnostic codes, UK Biobank field IDs, and definitions are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Covariates

All covariates were prespecified and assessed at baseline. These included sociodemographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, the Townsend deprivation index [TDI], and education), lifestyle and anthropometric measures (body mass index [BMI], smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, and sleep pattern), psychosocial factors (loneliness, frequency of depressive symptoms, and strict social isolation), and major comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, cardiovascular disease [CVD], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], CKD, cancer, bronchiectasis, inflammatory arthritis [defined from pre-baseline hospital records and/or self-report], and thyroid disease) [4, 20–24]. Definitions and coding for physical activity and sleep pattern followed previously published UK Biobank studies [25–27]. Additional baseline variables used in sensitivity analyses included history of falls, frailty status, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], and bone-related medications. History of falls at baseline was derived from the relevant UK Biobank questionnaire item. Frailty status was derived using a modified Fried frailty phenotype adapted to available UK Biobank baseline measures and informed by prior studies [28–30], with participants classified as robust, pre-frail, or frail based on five components: weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slow walking pace, and low physical activity. Bone-related medications included anti-osteoporosis medications, sedating antihistamines, inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), systemic glucocorticoids, and vitamin D/calcium supplements; among women, prior hormone replacement therapy (HRT) use was also included [31]. Detailed definitions and coding schemes are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised as mean (standard deviation) for approximately normally distributed continuous variables, median (interquartile range) for skewed continuous variables, and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Follow-up began at the baseline assessment date and ended at the first occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, or 31 March 2023, whichever came first. Kaplan–Meier failure curves were generated to descriptively visualise event patterns across exposure groups and coexistence patterns. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for individual allergic diseases (AR, AD, and asthma), the five mutually exclusive coexistence patterns (reference: none or one allergic disease), and allergic disease burden (0, 1, and ≥ 2), with a test for trend across categories of allergic disease burden. Models were specified sequentially: Model 0 (unadjusted), Model 1 (age, sex, ethnicity, TDI, and education), and Model 2 (Model 1 plus BMI, smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, sleep pattern, loneliness, depressive symptom frequency, strict social isolation, and comorbidities).

The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual-based tests for key exposure variables, the AR plus asthma coexistence pattern, and allergic disease burden. Where evidence of non-proportionality was observed, additional piecewise Cox regression analyses were performed to estimate period-specific associations during the first 5 years and after 5 years of follow-up. Competing-risk analyses were performed using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models with all-cause death as a competing event. Standardised 10-year cumulative incidence and absolute risk differences were additionally estimated from Fine–Gray models to improve clinical interpretability.

In a supportive cross-sectional imaging subgroup analysis, adjusted linear regression models were used to examine associations of allergic diseases, coexistence patterns, and allergic disease burden with femoral neck BMD, lumbar spine BMD, femoral neck T-score, and lumbar spine T-score, with adjustment for the same covariates included in Model 2. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by age (< 65/≥65 years), sex, BMI (< 25/≥25 kg/m²), smoking (current/non-current), physical activity (yes/no), and diabetes (yes/no), with effect modification tested using interaction terms. These analyses were interpreted as exploratory.

Sensitivity analyses included lag-time analyses excluding events occurring within 1–5 years after baseline; additional adjustment for baseline frailty status, history of falls, serum 25(OH)D, and baseline bone-related medications (anti-osteoporosis medications, sedating antihistamines, inhaled corticosteroids, systemic glucocorticoids, and vitamin D/calcium supplements; plus HRT in women). These medication variables reflected baseline medication status only and did not capture cumulative dose, duration, treatment intensity, or time-updated use during follow-up. For baseline medication variables used in medication-related sensitivity analyses, missing or unclassifiable values were treated as uncertain medication status rather than confirmed non-use. Additional restriction analyses were performed after excluding participants using inhaled corticosteroids or systemic glucocorticoids at baseline, and analyses were also conducted in a clean cohort excluding participants with either baseline MOF or hospital-recorded osteoporosis. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). For primary analyses, two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Secondary, subgroup, and supportive analyses were interpreted cautiously.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 190,594 participants contributed to the incident MOF analysis (11,442 events) and 191,881 to the hospital-recorded osteoporosis analysis (12,096 events), with a median follow-up of 13.8 years. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar across the two cohorts. Participants were aged 60–73 years at baseline (mean age, 64.10 years), and 52.0% were women. In the hospital-recorded osteoporosis cohort, AR, AD, and asthma were present in 4.6%, 2.1%, and 11.1% of participants, respectively. Participants with AR generally showed more favourable socioeconomic and cardiometabolic profiles, whereas those with asthma tended to have less favourable profiles, including higher adiposity and a greater burden of respiratory comorbidities. Participants with AD showed a broadly intermediate pattern (Table 1). Baseline characteristics in the MOF cohort were materially similar, with the same general pattern across allergic disease groups (Supplementary Table 3).

Individual allergic diseases and outcome risk

Over follow-up, incidence rates of MOF were 4.81, 4.60, and 5.81 per 1,000 person-years among participants with AR, AD, and asthma, respectively; the corresponding rates for hospital-recorded osteoporosis were 4.97, 5.38, and 7.24 per 1,000 person-years. In the fully adjusted Cox model, asthma showed the most consistent associations with both outcomes, with HR 1.20 (95% CI 1.14–1.27) for MOF and 1.45 (95% CI 1.38–1.52) for hospital-recorded osteoporosis. The estimate for AR in relation to MOF was weaker (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.21), whereas its corresponding estimate for hospital-recorded osteoporosis was closer to the null (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.00–1.18). Similarly, the estimate for AD in relation to hospital-recorded osteoporosis was modest and close to the null (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.01–1.27), whereas the corresponding estimate for MOF was essentially null (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.89–1.14) (Table 2).

Coexistence patterns and allergic disease burden

Across the five coexistence groups, the AR plus asthma group had the highest incidence rates of both MOF and hospital-recorded osteoporosis (6.15 and 7.21 per 1,000 person-years, respectively; Table 3). Descriptive Kaplan–Meier failure curves (1 − S[t]) for the coexistence groups are shown in Fig. 2. In the fully adjusted model, the AR plus asthma pattern was associated with both MOF (HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.16–1.59) and hospital-recorded osteoporosis (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.26–1.69), whereas the estimates for the other coexistence patterns were less precise and generally closer to the null, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Participants with all three allergic diseases were uncommon in both outcome cohorts (MOF: 264 participants with 19 events; hospital-recorded osteoporosis: 261 participants with 21 events), and the corresponding estimates were therefore imprecise, with wide confidence intervals (Table 3). A graded association was also observed for allergic disease burden: compared with no allergic disease, one allergic disease and two or more allergic diseases were associated with MOF (HR 1.15 and 1.25, respectively) and hospital-recorded osteoporosis (HR 1.32 and 1.42, respectively; Supplementary Table 4).

Sensitivity and supportive analyses

The main associations for asthma, the AR plus asthma coexistence pattern, and allergic disease burden were generally similar in direction across sensitivity and supportive analyses for both MOF and hospital-recorded osteoporosis. Fine–Gray competing-risk models yielded results similar in direction and relative magnitude to those of the primary analyses, and the largest standardised 10-year absolute risk differences were observed for asthma and the AR plus asthma coexistence pattern (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Lag-time analyses were broadly consistent with the primary findings, particularly for asthma, the AR plus asthma coexistence pattern, and allergic disease burden (Supplementary Table 8). In particular, the modest association between AD and hospital-recorded osteoporosis became less consistent in the 5-year lag analysis and after additional adjustment for baseline vitamin D (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). Additional adjustment for falls history, frailty status, and baseline medication-related factors, together with restriction analyses excluding baseline inhaled corticosteroid or systemic glucocorticoid users, showed generally similar directions of association, although modest attenuation was observed in selected estimates, particularly for asthma-related associations with hospital-recorded osteoporosis after adjustment for baseline inhaled corticosteroid use (Supplementary Tables 10–13). Analyses using broader exposure definitions incorporating primary care records and analyses in a clean cohort excluding participants with baseline MOF or hospital-recorded osteoporosis yielded directionally consistent results overall (Supplementary Tables 14–16). Schoenfeld residual-based tests suggested some evidence of non-proportionality for asthma and allergic disease burden in the hospital-recorded osteoporosis analysis; in subsequent piecewise Cox models, these associations were stronger during early follow-up and attenuated thereafter, while remaining consistent in direction (Supplementary Tables 17 and 18). In the imaging subgroup, supportive cross-sectional analyses using DXA-derived measures showed generally less favourable BMD and T-score profiles for AR, asthma, the AR plus asthma coexistence pattern, and higher allergic disease burden, providing supportive but non-confirmatory evidence broadly consistent with the direction of the main analyses (Supplementary Table 19).

Discussion

In this prospective analysis of approximately 190,000 UK Biobank participants aged ≥ 60 years at baseline with up to 16 years of follow-up, the most consistent associations were observed for asthma, which was associated with incident MOF and hospital-recorded osteoporosis. Among allergic-disease coexistence patterns, AR plus asthma showed the clearest association with both outcomes, and allergic disease burden showed a graded association with both outcomes, although this count-based measure should be interpreted as a descriptive summary rather than a biological gradient. By contrast, the associations of AR with MOF and of AD with hospital-recorded osteoporosis were weaker and less consistent, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Prospective evidence on the associations between different allergic disease phenotypes and adverse bone outcomes in older adults remains limited, with previous studies focusing mainly on asthma. UK Biobank evidence has suggested that different asthma-onset phenotypes are associated with incident osteoporosis, with a potentially stronger association observed for adult-onset asthma [15]. However, other studies have placed greater emphasis on treatment-related factors, particularly inhaled corticosteroids, and their findings have been inconsistent [32, 33]. In contrast, evidence for AD is largely derived from heterogeneous retrospective studies [18, 34], whereas direct evidence for AR is even more limited. A small number of observational studies have suggested that AR may be associated with an increased risk of low-energy fractures or osteoporosis [35], but genetic evidence has not supported a clear direct causal association [17]. In addition, previous studies have suggested that different combinations of allergic diseases may be associated with distinct long-term health risks [36]. Against this background, the present study complements prior work by evaluating AR, AD, and asthma together in an older population, including their coexistence patterns and allergic disease burden, in relation to incident hospital-recorded osteoporosis and MOF.

The association patterns observed in this study may reflect not only increased skeletal fragility but also broader functional vulnerability. Asthma, the coexistence of AR and asthma, and a higher allergic disease burden may indicate more persistent type 2 immune activation and a greater overall inflammatory burden, which could plausibly disrupt bone remodelling by altering osteoclast and osteoblast activity, although these pathways were not directly tested in the present cohort [14]. The subsequent DXA-derived BMD and T-score findings were broadly consistent with this skeletal interpretation, but these imaging analyses should be regarded as supportive and cross-sectional rather than confirmatory. At the same time, MOF in older adults should be interpreted separately from hospital-recorded osteoporosis, because fractures may also reflect non-skeletal vulnerability, including frailty, falls, functional limitations, medication effects, and differential healthcare contact [37]. Previous studies have also linked late-life asthma to frailty [38], which is consistent with the attenuation of the MOF-related estimates after further adjustment for frailty in the present study. Treatment-related factors, particularly ICS, may also have contributed to the asthma-related signal: additional adjustment for baseline ICS use resulted in greater attenuation of the estimates for hospital-recorded osteoporosis than adjustment for systemic glucocorticoids, suggesting that the asthma-related associations may partly reflect treatment burden and/or underlying disease severity rather than asthma-related inflammation alone [39]. Nevertheless, the overall direction of the associations remained broadly similar in analyses restricted to participants not using ICS at baseline [11, 33]. In contrast, the weaker association between AR and MOF may partly reflect non-skeletal pathways, particularly the fall risk associated with sedating antihistamines [40], which was also consistent with the antihistamine-related analyses. The weaker association between AD and hospital-recorded osteoporosis was no longer stable after further adjustment for baseline serum 25(OH)D, suggesting that vitamin D status may have partly contributed to this signal [41]. Not all findings were fully consistent. The lack of a stable association for the AR + AD + asthma triad may have been related to the small sample size and limited precision in this subgroup, while differences observed across smoking and diabetes subgroups arose from unadjusted interaction tests and may have reflected differences in baseline skeletal risk across strata rather than true biological effect modification [42, 43].

This study has several strengths, including the simultaneous evaluation of AR, AD, and asthma in the same older population, together with their coexistence patterns and allergic disease burden. This framework allowed allergic diseases to be examined both individually and in combination, while allergic disease burden was treated as a descriptive count-based measure. The large prospective cohort, long follow-up, and multiple sensitivity and supportive analyses also strengthened the transparency and consistency of the findings. Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. First, although broader GP/HES/self-report-based definitions were examined in sensitivity analyses, the primary exposure definitions relied on inpatient records and self-report. Because AR and AD are frequently diagnosed and managed in primary care, milder or primary-care-managed allergic disease may have been under-ascertained, and the primary definition may have preferentially captured clinically recognised or relatively more severe phenotypes. Self-reported allergic disease may also have been affected by recall-related error. Second, hospital-recorded osteoporosis and MOF were identified from hospital inpatient records and therefore likely captured more clinically recognised or relatively more severe events. Because osteoporosis is often underdiagnosed in routine practice and may remain clinically silent until fracture or screening, reliance on inpatient records may have further missed milder or outpatient-managed cases. Hospital-recorded osteoporosis should therefore be interpreted as a clinically recognised inpatient-recorded endpoint rather than the full population burden of osteoporosis. In some cases, osteoporosis may also have been coded during fracture-related hospital encounters, and temporal ambiguity, detection bias, surveillance-related bias, and differential healthcare utilisation cannot be fully excluded. Low- and high-energy fractures could not be distinguished within the current MOF definition, and the DXA-based imaging analyses, although supportive, were cross-sectional and did not overcome the limitations of the hospital-recorded osteoporosis outcome. Third, residual confounding may also remain, particularly for the asthma-related findings, because disease severity, healthcare utilisation, and glucocorticoid exposure were not fully captured. Medication variables were available only at baseline and recorded coarsely, precluding assessment of cumulative dose, duration, treatment intensity, and time-varying exposure; baseline non-use of inhaled or systemic corticosteroids did not exclude later initiation or repeated use during follow-up. All covariates were baseline-fixed, and changes during follow-up could not be captured. In addition, functional impairment was not comprehensively measured, and heterogeneity across specific comorbidity subtypes could not be fully examined. Finally, allergic disease burden should not be interpreted as a biological dose–response relationship, because different allergic conditions cannot be assumed to contribute equally or additively to bone-related outcomes. The study also did not assess whether allergic disease profiles improve prediction beyond existing fracture-risk tools; therefore, these findings do not establish direct clinical risk-stratification utility based on allergic disease status alone. The healthy volunteer bias, limited ethnic diversity of the UK Biobank, lack of external validation, and overall modest effect sizes further warrant caution in generalising these findings or inferring substantial clinical impact.

From an epidemiological perspective, these findings add to the understanding of heterogeneity in bone-related outcomes among older adults. More broadly, the coexistence patterns and burden of allergic diseases may reflect not only impaired bone health but also wider vulnerability in later life. In line with the attenuation of the MOF-related estimates after adjustment for frailty, previous studies in older populations with complex comorbidity profiles have suggested that frailty-related markers and validated composite risk scores may provide additional prognostic information beyond traditional risk factors [44, 45]. Future studies should examine the relevance of allergic disease profiles for bone-health risk assessment in independent cohorts using more objective longitudinal skeletal outcomes.

Conclusion

In this large prospective cohort of older UK Biobank participants, asthma, particularly when coexisting with AR, and greater allergic disease burden were observationally associated with incident hospital-recorded osteoporosis and MOF. However, residual confounding related to disease severity and treatment exposure, particularly corticosteroid use, cannot be fully excluded, and further studies are needed to replicate these findings and clarify their interpretation in independent cohorts.
