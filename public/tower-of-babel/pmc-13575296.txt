The Limits of Repetition in the Illusion of Consensus
Authors: Saoirse Connor Desai, Manikya Alister, Keith Ransom, Jemma Martin, Laura Angles, Andrew Perfors, Brett K Hayes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHumans rely on social agreement to assess the credibility of information, but an illusion of consensus can arise through repeated exposure to the same source (dependent consensus), which can make people overestimate credibility. The current work investigated the limits of this illusion across multiple exposures to claims based on either an independent or dependent consensus. In two studies (N = 1015), participants rated their belief in a claim after exposure to an increasing number of consensual claims within a mock social media environment. Initially, when exposed to only a small number of claims, people were equally persuaded by dependent and independent consensus, but independent consensus had a greater impact on beliefs as exposures accumulated. Bayesian nonlinear modeling showed that independent consensus led to a higher asymptotic level of belief and more rapid belief revision than dependent consensus. These effects were robust over variations in claim valence (Experiment 1) and topic (Experiment 2) and were further supported by the free‐text justifications participants provided. Our findings show that the illusion of consensus may be less of a concern than previous research suggested, at least in environments where a high amount of repeated exposure to the same claim is common. They also provide some evidence for a novel mechanism contributing to belief in repeated claims, namely, that repetition of such claims by a secondary source is perceived as a proxy for claim credibility.Keywords: Consensus, Repetition, Social learning, Judgment

Introduction

Our information‐rich environment gives us unprecedented access to the opinions, claims, and recommendations of others. From deciding where to dine or go on holiday to how to vote, we are regularly faced with the challenge of responding to the consensual advice of a virtual crowd. Crucial to this challenge is understanding when to accept consensual claims and when to discount them. Previous work shows that people are more likely to be persuaded by claims that have support from large numbers of individuals (Bond, 2005; Gelpí, Whalen, Griffiths, Xu, & Buchsbaum, 2025; Lewandowsky, Cook, Fay, & Gignac, 2019), tend to trust information from sources perceived as knowledgeable (Oktar & Lombrozo, 2025), and from those believed to have helpful intentions (Shafto, Eaves, Navarro, & Perfors, 2012a). An important issue that remains unresolved, however, concerns the impact of information dependency in determining the value of a consensus—whether it arises from multiple primary sources working independently or whether it is based on repetition of a claim that was generated by a small number of primary sources, or even a single source (see Fig. 1A).

Normative models of judgment and belief revision prescribe that more epistemic weight should be given to a consensus based on independent sources than a dependent consensus (Bovens & Hartmann, 2003; Oktar, Lombrozo, & Griffiths, 2024; Pilditch, Hahn, & Lagnado, 2025; Whalen, Griffiths, & Buchsbaum, 2018), as long as the primary sources are equated for expertise (Pilditch, Hahn, Fenton, & Lagnado, 2020). The common intuition behind these models is that testimony from each independent informant provides additional novel evidence for establishing the truth of a claim, whereas a dependent consensus recycles old evidence.

Empirical support for whether humans reason according to this principle, however, is mixed. Several studies have reported an illusion of consensus, whereby repeated claims from a single primary source are given the same weight as those based on independent consensus (Alister, Ransom, & Perfors, 2022; Foster, Huthwaite, Yesberg, Garry, & Loftus, 2012; Sulik, Bahrami, & Deroy, 2020; Yousif, Aboody, & Keil, 2019). Other work has shown that independent consensus is sometimes accorded more weight than dependent consensus, but only under certain conditions (Connor Desai, Xie, & Hayes, 2022, 2026; Mercier & Morin, 2019; Pfänder, De Courson, & Mercier, 2025) (e.g., when people are aware that dependent informants are rewarded for agreeing with each other). Moreover, individual‐level analysis of the effects of different consensus types showed that only a minority of individuals reliably differentiate between the persuasiveness of independent and dependent consensus (Alister et al., 2025).

Understanding when and why dependent consensus has an oversized impact on people's beliefs is important not only for models of belief revision but because the illusion of consensus can have a pernicious effect on social and political discourse. Oft‐repeated false claims denying climate change (Harvey et al., 2018) or vaccine benefits (van der Linden, 2022), for example, can often be traced back to a small number, or even a single, primary source. A failure to appreciate the dependence between claims shared by multiple sources has also been shown to contribute to belief polarization (Young, Madsen, & De‐Wit, 2025). The likelihood of exposure to claims based on dependent consensus is amplified in social media environments that facilitate claim sharing among users (Lewandowsky et al., 2019).

One explanation for the equivocal findings concerning information dependency is that, to date, the impact of independent and dependent consensus has been studied across a narrow window of evidence. Typically, previous studies have examined belief revision after exposure to no more than four independent or dependent (repeated) claims. However, real‐world information systems (e.g., social media) are likely to present people with claims that are repeated far more often than that.

Further motivation for expanding this evidence window comes from recent findings related to the repetition‐truth effect. An abundant literature has demonstrated that often‐repeated claims are perceived as more likely to be true, regardless of their actual truth status (Henderson, Westwood, & Simons, 2022; Udry & Barber, 2024). Recent work, however, shows that the strength of this effect may diminish as the number of repetitions increase (DiFonzo, Beckstead, Stupak, & Walders, 2016, Fazio, Pillai, & Patel, 2022; Hassan & Barber, 2021). Fazio and colleagues, for example, found that repeated exposure to both true and false statements led to changes in perceived truth that followed a logarithmic function. Initial repetitions produced relatively large changes in perceived truth, with smaller changes observed after 5−10 repetitions.

Although this work did not involve any manipulation of social consensus, it is consistent with a broader principle: reasoners implicitly assess whether each new source contributes genuinely novel evidence, discounting its impact to the extent that evidential overlap with prior sources is perceived as likely. As the number of exposures accumulates, the probability of such overlap increases, producing diminishing returns on belief revision. If so, a dependent consensus—which recycles the same primary source—should show stronger diminishing returns than an independent consensus, where each new source plausibly contributes novel evidence.

Another important reason for comparing independent and dependent consensus over multiple exposures is that this can provide insight into the mechanisms that lead to the inflated impact of repeated claims. Several such mechanisms have been proposed. Many of the proposed mechanisms focus on the way that repetition affects the way a claim is processed. Claim repetition can strengthen its representation in memory, making it feel more familiar and more likely to be retrieved (Begg, Anas, & Farinacci, 1992). A related proposal is that repetition can lead to more fluent processing of claim contents—increasing the subjective ease with which it can be retrieved, which is misinterpreted as evidence of claim veracity. It has been suggested that increases in fluency can arise from either verbatim repetition of a claim (perceptual fluency) or from repetition of its semantic gist (conceptual fluency) (Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009, Unkelbach, Koch, Silva, & Garcia‐Marques, 2019). Repetition may also increase perceptions of semantic coherence—increasing associative links between different component concepts within a claim (Unkelbach & Rom, 2017).

Recently, a different type of explanation has been suggested for the increased persuasiveness of dependent consensus, that focuses on people's inferences about why multiple sources have chosen to repeat a claim (Connor Desai, Fai, Lee, & Hayes, 2026; Jiang, Schwarz, Reynolds, & Newman, 2024). A key assumption of this social sampling account is that people actively interpret why an information source, such as someone posting on social media, has chosen to share a claim. In the absence of other explanations, an information receiver may infer that there was a good reason for intentionally selecting to repeat the claim for sharing (e.g., that the primary source was especially credible; see Fig. 1B). In other words, the intentional repetition of a claim by multiple sources may often function as a proxy for claim credibility.

Consistent with this account, sources who frequently repeat claims (both true and false) are often perceived as more credible than those who present a claim just once (Mattavelli, Brambilla, & Unkelbach, 2026). In a more direct test of the account, Connor Desai et al. (2026) found that the persuasiveness of dependent consensus varied according to the explanation that was provided for claim repetition. When a “positive” explanation was given for why the results of a particular election poll were shared by multiple news sources (e.g., it was the first poll available) or when no explanation was provided, dependent consensus was seen as quite persuasive, approaching the impact of independent consensus. However, when a “negative” explanation for repetition was provided (e.g., news sources were trying to influence public opinion in favor of a candidate), the impact of dependent consensus was reduced.

This social sampling account builds on findings in studies of human judgment and inference that people often consider how an evidential sample was generated when drawing conclusions from that sample (see Hayes, Connor Desai, Ransom, & Kemp, 2023; Shafto, Goodman, & Frank, 2012b for reviews). In property inference tasks, for example, people are presented with a sample of evidence (e.g., category members that share some novel property) and are asked to judge whether the property generalizes to other items. Early research in this area (e.g., Osherson, Smith, Wilkie, Lopez, & Shafir, 1990) emphasized how property generalization was affected by changing the contents of the evidence sample. For example, generalization from a sample to other instances increased with increasing sample size (the number of instances in the sample that shared to the property) and sample diversity (the range of instances within a category that share the property). Notably, however, more recent work has shown that these effects often depend on people's beliefs about how the sample was generated. Robust sample size and diversity effects are observed when people believe that the evidence sample was intentionally generated by a knowledgeable source but not when it was generated via a random process (Hayes, Navarro, Stephens, Ransom, & Dilevski, 2019a; Navarro, Dry, & Lee, 2012; Ransom, Perfors, & Navarro, 2016). Likewise, people's beliefs about why particular instances have been included or excluded from sample shapes their subsequent generalization patterns (Hayes, Banner, Forrester, & Navarro, 2019b; Ransom, Perfors, Hayes, & Connor Desai, 2022).

We do not see the processing and social sampling accounts of the effects of dependent consensus as mutually exclusive. Both types of mechanism may serve to increase the persuasiveness of a claim repeated by multiple sources. However, these different theoretical accounts make divergent predictions about the impact of increasing claim repetitions in dependent consensus. According to processing accounts based on familiarity and/or fluency, additional repetitions should increase claim availability and fluent retrieval, leading to further increases in belief in the claim. In contrast, the social sampling account proposes that multiple exposures to a dependent consensus could lead people to revise their inferences about the reasons for claim repetition. For example, increased repetitions may lead them to become more suspicious of an informant's motives (Ernst, Kühne, & Wirth, 2017). Hence, relative to independent consensus, there should be a diminishing impact of dependent consensus on belief in a claim with additional exposures to the consensual claim. Some have suggested that persistent claim repetition could even lead to a boomerang effect, leading to a net reduction in claim credulity (Ernst et al., 2017; Koch & Zerback, 2013).

The current studies

Our studies examined the persuasiveness of claims based on an independent or dependent consensus over multiple exposures to these claims. Participants in either an independent or dependent consensus condition were presented with multiple iterations of fictional social media posts. We used a mock social media approach because this reflects how many people obtain information about novel social issues and claims (Kim, 2023) and because social media interactions can involve both independent consensus (e.g., receiving claims generated by different users) and dependent consensus (receiving a claim that has been shared or reposted). Notably, previous studies using mock social media tasks have found that people often fail to distinguish between the persuasiveness of claims based on different consensus types (e.g., Alister et al., 2022, 2025; Ransom, Perfors, & Stephens, 2021). In line with findings concerning multiple repetitions of false information (e.g., Fazio et al., 2022) and the social sampling account, we predicted that, as the number of exposures increased, people would be more persuaded by independent as compared to dependent consensus.

An important feature of this work was the novel application of Bayesian nonlinear modeling that allowed us to distinguish between different nuanced versions of this prediction. The social sampling account suggests that the relative impact of a dependent consensus on claim credibility should decline over multiple exposures (i.e., a decelerating function). However, this general prediction could be manifested in a number of different ways when we compare patterns of belief change over multiple exposures to independent or dependent consensus. One possibility is that, over extended exposures, independent and dependent consensus will differ in the rate of belief updating, with larger positive shifts in belief per exposure in the independent case. Another possibility is that the two forms of consensus lead to different asymptotic levels of belief change, with independent consensus leading to a higher maximum level of change. Differences between consensus conditions in both rate of change and asymptote are also possible. Distinguishing between these alternatives is important because they have different implications for the long‐term impact of independent and dependent consensus on people's beliefs. If independent consensus shows a higher asymptote, this would suggest that it should ultimately be more persuasive over time than dependent consensus. If the consensus types differ only in rate of belief change, however, then with sufficient levels of exposure, a dependent consensus could become just as persuasive as independent consensus. Hence, someone who seeks to influence popular opinion on an issue could do so by inundating an information environment with low‐quality repetitions that establish a dependent consensus. We were able to evaluate each of these possibilities by examining the fit of rival Bayesian models that did or did not include parameters corresponding to changes in the rate and asymptote of belief change.

We also examined the extent to which exposure to multiple independent or dependent claims affected participants’ intended social media behavior, by asking about their willingness to share these claims. Assuming that sharing behavior is driven, at least in part, by degree of belief in a claim (Fay et al., 2026), we expected that intention to share would be more likely after someone had received information about a claim from multiple independent than dependent sources.

Additionally, we probed participants’ justifications for their patterns of belief change following exposure to multiple independent or dependent consensual claims, and asked them to consider the impact of the counterfactual type of consensus (i.e., those exposed to dependent consensus were asked how their judgments would change if exposed to an independent consensus, and vice versa). This provided further insight into people's intuitions about the relative value of each type of evidence.

Experiment 1: Effect of multiple exposures to independent or dependent consensus and claim valence

In this study, participants were presented with multiple mock social media posts involving expert claims about genetically modified (GM) foods. Consensual claims were sourced from either independent experts (independent consensus) or the same expert (dependent consensus). Participants repeatedly rated the extent to which they endorsed the consensual claim as successive posts were presented. This allowed us to compare the trajectories of belief change over the course of multiple exposures to independent or dependent consensus. Additionally, we manipulated the valence of the consensual claim (i.e., whether the majority supported or opposed GM foods). This was motivated by the finding that the impact of social media information on beliefs and intentions to share with others can be affected by information valence, with people more likely to share negative than positive posts (Antypas, Preece, & Camacho‐Collados, 2023; Tsugawa & Ohsaki, 2017). Both of our studies focused on claims for which participants did not have strong prior beliefs. This was a deliberate choice because we wanted to model situations where participants are undecided about the truth of a claim and seek online information for guidance. Using claims with weak priors also minimized the chances of ceiling (or floor) effects in belief change.

Methods

Ethics information, preregistration, and availability of materials, data, and analyses

Both studies were approved by the University of New South Wales Human Research Executive Committee (HC Number 220233). Informed consent was obtained from all research participants. Experiment 2 design and hypotheses were preregistered at https://osf.io/6xgav. Data, stimuli, and JASP analyses have been deposited in the Open Science Archive (OSF, 2025), https://osf.io/q379s. Model specification and priors for the nonlinear regression modeling are in the Supplementary Materials, with full code available at: https://github.com/ManikyaAlister/indycon‐repetition.

Participants

A total of 313 participants were recruited via the Prolific crowdsourcing platform. All participants were residents of the USA, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, or Ireland and received £1.50 compensation. They were randomly allocated to one of four conditions formed by the factorial crossing of consensus (independent vs. dependent) and claim valence (positive vs. negative). Data from 37 participants (21 independent, 16 dependent) were excluded because they: (a) showed poor memory for the names of primary sources, or (b) gave highly inaccurate estimates of the number of positive or negative claims (e.g., estimating more negative than positive when the reverse was true). The final samples and demographics were: Independent positive = 64; Dependent positive = 66; Independent negative = 74; Dependent negative = 72; M AGE = 37.57 years, SEM = 0.832; Range = 18–87 years; 142 females, 130 males, 4 nonbinary/other.

Our recruitment strategy aimed to achieve experimental cell sizes comparable to previous studies comparing the effect of independent and dependent consensus using comparable methods (e.g., Connor Desai et al., 2022), allowing for an expected attrition of up to 15% of participants who fail to meet inclusion criteria.

Materials

Experimental stimuli were mock media posts resembling those from the social media platform “X”—see Fig. 2. Participants were told to imagine that an online debate was being held about the use of GM crops and that they would see relevant social media posts (see https://osf.io/q379sfor instruction details). Previous studies have shown that most adults do not have strong positive or negative prior beliefs about this topic or the additional topics in Experiment 2 (Alister et al., 2025; Ransom et al., 2021). We used English‐language names for experts in the mock social media posts and their academic institutions. Each post presented in the independent consensus conditions featured a different expert and institution. Those in the dependent consensus conditions viewed posts about two experts and academic institutions in the baseline phase. One source was repeated in later phases, with posts paraphrased but consistently supporting the claim. Such paraphrased repetitions have been shown to have similar effects to exact repetitions (Pillai & Fazio, 2024). Fig. 2 shows examples of posts in each consensus condition. In the positive conditions, the majority of posts supported the use of GM crops. In the negative conditions, the majority opposed their use. Posts contained no additional sharing or endorsement details, such as “likes” or emojis. Experimental stimuli were produced using TWEETGEN Beta. Media site names were generated using Chat GPT‐3.5, and media site logos were generated using LOGO.com.

Procedure

The experiment was coded and run using JSPsych (De Leeuw, 2015). Participants provided consent and demographic information before reading instructions, followed by a three‐item test of understanding of the experimental procedure. A perfect score was required to proceed; failure returned the participant to the instruction screens. Prior to the main task, participants rated their view of GM foods on a 7‐point scale (1 = definitely oppose, 7 = definitely support, with reverse scoring for negative valence). To minimize the possibility of anchoring effects, the numerical scale differed from that used for subsequent claim endorsement ratings. These ratings confirmed that most participants did not have strong prior positive or negative beliefs about the consensual claim (M = 4.13, SEM = 0.087, see Supplementary Materials for full prior distributions).1

In the main phase of the experiment, participants viewed social media posts in a baseline phase, followed by three separate batches, each containing three posts (see Fig. 3 for the experimental timeline). Posts were attributed to fictional news agencies, with each post citing an expert primary source who either supported or opposed a claim. We first presented all participants with two posts sequentially at baseline in random order; one supporting and one opposing the use of GM crops. Participants had unlimited time to read each post, but 3 s after a post appeared, a button appeared which allowed progress to the next screen. After viewing both baseline posts, they rated their agreement with the claim: “Based on all the claims you have seen so far, to what extent do you agree that genetically modified crops are a good [bad] idea?”, using a 0–100 on screen slider (0 = Strongly disagree, 50 = Neutral, 100 = Strongly agree).

Experimental timeline.

Note. The figure shows the sequencing of batches of social media posts agreeing with a claim (Pro) or opposing the claim (Con) and ratings of agreement. In independent consensus condition, a different expert was cited as the source of each post. In dependent consensus condition, all Pro posts cited the same expert.

In each of the three subsequent batches, participants were shown three social media posts in random order, all reporting a positive/negative view of GM crops. The presentation procedure for each post was the same as in the baseline. After each batch of posts, participants re‐rated their agreement with the claim on the same 100‐point scale. Hence, the proportion of consensual to nonconsensual claims increased from 1:1 at baseline, to 4:1, 7:1, and 10:1, across successive batches. Those in the independent consensus condition viewed a total of 11 posts—one at baseline opposing the claim and 10 with different experts endorsing the claim (one baseline + 3×3 additional posts). Different combinations of expert name and academic institution were used for each post, with a different media outlet responsible for each post. Those in the dependent consensus condition viewed the same total number of posts—one at baseline opposing the claim and 10 repeated endorsements of a claim from a single expert. In the dependent condition, the name of the expert and academic institution was the same for each post, but a different media outlet was presented. Specific expert/institution names used in the dependent condition were randomly selected for each participant from nine possible combinations.

After the final agreement rating, participants completed two memory checks for the post details. They estimated the total number of supporting/opposing claims observed in the main task. They were also presented with a randomly ordered list of 14 expert names, containing the two unique names used in the dependent condition or 11 unique names in the independent condition, respectively. They had to click on all names presented during the task.

They were then asked to type a free‐text response to a question about the effects of consensus on their belief in the claim. This was worded differently for independent and dependent consensus conditions; “Most of the posts you saw cited [different] / [the same] experts. How did this influence your judgment about whether genetically modified crops are a good/bad idea?”. They were also asked to respond to the counterfactual question “How would your judgment have changed (if at all) if all the posts had cited [the same] / [different] experts?” The content of these free‐text responses was coded and analyzed using three large language models (LLMs), with content coding focusing on participants’ beliefs about the relative value of evidence based on independent or dependent consensus. Because the results of these free‐text analyses were similar across the experiments, we defer presentation of the detail until Experiment 2.

Finally, participants were asked to rate their willingness to share one of the posts that expressed the majority positive/negative view of GM foods (1 = “Definitely would not share,” 100 = “Definitely would share”).

Exclusion criteria

In independent consensus, participants were excluded if they selected only one expert name in the recognition memory check, if all selected names were incorrect, or if the difference between their estimates of supporting and opposing claims was equal to or less than zero (direction of this difference reversed in the negative valence condition). In dependent consensus, participants were excluded for selecting more than two different expert names or if all names selected were incorrect, or if the difference between estimates of supporting and opposing claims was negative (a zero difference was acceptable, as this accurately reflects the number of independent posts viewed).

Results

Claim endorsement

Fig. 4 shows ratings of agreement with a claim after the baseline and each additional batch of posts in the independent and dependent consensus conditions. A preliminary 4 (ratings batch) × 2 (claim valence) Bayesian analysis of variance was implemented in JASP v. 0.95.4 (JASP Team, 2025), with conventional Bayes factor interpretation (Lee & Wagenmakers, 2014). This analysis found moderate evidence against an interaction between claim valence and timing of the rating, BF10 = 0.150. This indicates that the pattern of belief change was unaffected by valence. Hence, our data modeling collapsed across this factor (see Supplementary Materials for separate plots for valence conditions).

Note. Points and error bars show means and standard errors of the empirical data. Solid lines show predictions based on the median posterior parameter estimates (lines) of the full Bayesian linear regression model and the 95% credible intervals of those estimates (shaded).

We examined the trajectory of claim endorsement across exposures to baseline and consensual posts by: (a) fitting a Bayesian nonlinear regression model with three key parameters (start point, rate of change, asymptote) to the data (see Fig. 4 for model fits and the Supplementary Materials for parameter estimates), and (b) comparing the fit of models omitting one or more of these parameters. Start is a free parameter indicating the initial level of claim endorsement at baseline, when there is one person agreeing and another disagreeing (no consensus). Asymptote is a free parameter indicating the maximum amount that belief in a claim will grow as a function of consensus size. Rate is a free parameter that controls how quickly belief grows as a function of consensus size.

This approach has several advantages. First, we modeled the relationship between endorsement and level of consensus using a power function, which corresponds to existing theoretical models that have attempted to describe the relationship between conformity and social group size (Bond, 2005; Latané, 1981). Second, this approach has unique predictive value, allowing us to estimate how increasing the number of sources will increase belief in a claim, even if we do not have direct measurements for a given source number (i.e., our study only tests consensus sizes of 4, 7, and 10). Third, the parameters in the model are easily interpretable.

The models were implemented using the R package brms (Bürkner, 2017) version 2.23.0 (see Supplementary Materials and analysis code for the full implementation of different models and their priors, including parameter estimates). Our full model, which assumes that all parameters (start, rate, and asymptote) vary across consensus conditions, provided a good fit of the experimental data. Importantly, it captured the empirical trend whereby belief in the claim increased quickly with initial increases in consensus size before reaching an asymptote. Crucially, Fig. 4 suggests that, over the course of subsequent posts, there was a clear differentiation between the trajectories of claim endorsement based on independent or dependent consensus. To examine the basis for this difference, we compared the fit of eight models which assumed that different combinations of parameters varied across consensus conditions. For example, in the asymptote model, asymptote was the only parameter that could vary across consensus conditions. We also included a full model that assumed all parameters varied across conditions, and a null model that assumed no changes in any parameter across conditions. Model performance was assessed using leave one out cross validation (LOOIC), which balances model complexity with model fit (Gelman, Hwang, & Vehtari, 2014) and change in the difference in expected log point wise predictive density from the best model (ΔELPD). We also calculated average model Weight, which can be interpreted as an approximate posterior probability that each model provides the best prediction.

Table 1 shows the model comparison results. The best performing model was asymptote + rate. This suggests that exposure to multiple independent consensus claims led to higher maximum belief in the claim than exposure to the same number of dependent consensus claims. Additionally, independent consensus led to more rapid increase in belief across posts than dependent consensus. Although consensus type affected both parameters, the evidence was stronger for asymptote. Table 1 shows that the performance of the model with both parameters was nearly identical to a model that included asymptote alone, as shown by the Weight measure and raw LOOIC values. There was little evidence of the effect of consensus on ratings start‐points. To check the robustness of these results, the modeling was repeated without participant exclusions. The obtained model rankings were similar to those in Table 1 (see Supplementary Materials) except that the ranks of the two best models were reversed. In this case, a model with a single asymptote parameter best captured the differences in ratings between independent and dependent consensus.

Recall that previous research has only examined change in belief after exposure to a small number of claims based on independent or dependent consensus (Sulik et al., 2020; Yousif et al., 2019). To compare our findings with this earlier work, we carried out a 2 (consensus condition) × 2 (baseline − consensus 1:1 vs. batch 1 − consensus 4:1) Bayesian analysis of variance on endorsement ratings. The aim of this analysis was to examine whether there was evidence for differential shifts in belief after the first exposure to the first batch of independent or dependent consensus claims. This analysis found extreme evidence for a main effect increase in ratings from baseline to the first batch of posts, BF10 >10,000, but moderate evidence against an interaction between consensus type and ratings change, BF10 = 0.282. In other words, there was a marked increase in belief in response to the first batch of consensual claims, but the magnitude of this change did not differ as a function of consensus type. This pattern replicates the illusion of consensus reported in previous studies with consensus sizes similar to batch 1 (e.g., Yousif et al., 2019). Taken together with the previous analysis containing all trials, this reinforces our key findings that: (1) the illusion of consensus is prevalent when people are exposed to a relatively small number of claims based on independent or dependent consensus, but that (2) the illusion can be dispelled with multiple claims based on independent consensus eventually leading to more belief change.

Willingness to share

Willingness to share a consensus claim was examined using a Bayesian 2 (consensus condition) × 2 (valence) analysis of variance, with default Cauchy priors. The analysis found strong evidence for a main effect of valence with participants more likely to share posts that opposed use of GM crops (M = 42.101, SEM = 2.401) than those that supported use (M = 30.298, SEM = 2.545), BF10 = 24.827. This is consistent with previous findings that negatively valenced social media posts are more likely to be shared (e.g., Tsugawa & Ohsaki, 2017). Although willingness to share tended to be higher following exposure to independent (M = 39.685, SEM = 2.476) than dependent consensus (M = 32.713, SEM = 2.472), there was only anecdotal evidence for this difference, BF10 = 1.223. Likewise, there was anecdotal evidence for an interaction between consensus and valence, BF10 = 1.520.

The increased intention to share a post with a negative compared to a positive valence is consistent with previous studies of information transmission in social networks (Antypas et al., 2023; Tsugawa & Ohsaki, 2017). It is interesting that this same factor had no effect on the strength of people's beliefs in the consensual claim. In this regard, however, previous evidence for a valence effect has been somewhat mixed. For example, positive online product reviews are often seen as less credible than negative reviews (Yin, Bond, & Zhang, 2026). However, other studies have found that the perceived truth of a repeated claim is unaffected by claim valence (Unkelbach, Bayer, Alves, Koch, & Stahl, 2011).

Experiment 2: Effect of multiple exposures to independent or dependent consensus about multiple claim topics

The previous study found evidence that the relative impact of independent as compared to dependent consensus increased as people were exposed to multiple instances of consensual claims. Our Bayesian nonlinear analysis revealed that increasing exposure to independent consensual claims led to a higher maximum level of belief change and a higher rate of belief change than dependent consensus, although the evidence was stronger for the first of these effects.

Experiment 2 aimed to replicate these effects and test whether they extend to claims about different topics (support for clean coal technology and the benefits of medical marijuana for pets). We also manipulated whether the dependent posts cited different secondary sources (different news outlets, as in the previous study) or the same secondary source (the same news outlet). According to the social sampling account, repetition of a claim by the same primary and secondary sources should be less likely to trigger an inference that the primary source is especially credible, leading to a further reduction in impact on claim endorsement and willingness to share. Given that the valence manipulation did not impact how people were affected by different consensus types in the previous study, this manipulation was dropped from Experiment 2. The design and experimental hypotheses were preregistered (https://osf.io/6xgav).

Method

Participants

A total of 702 participants were recruited via the Prolific platform and randomly allocated to one of nine groups formed by the crossing of three consensus conditions (independent, dependent, dependent‐same secondary source) and three claim topics (GM foods, clean coal, medical marijuana). All participants were residents of the USA, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, or Ireland and received £1.50 compensation. Data from 107 participants were excluded based on post‐test checks (22 independent, 44 dependent, 42 dependent same source). Participants who failed an exclusion condition were replaced to keep cell sample sizes approximately equal. The final sample sizes and demographics were: GM foods—independent = 66; GM foods—dependent = 68; GM foods—dependent same‐source = 67; clean coal—independent = 64; clean coal—dependent = 68; clean coal—dependent same‐source = 62; medical pet—independent = 69; medical pet—dependent = 64; medical pet—dependent same‐source = 67; M AGE = 39.27 years, SEM = 0.543, Range = 18–83 years; 276 females, 310 males, 9 nonbinary/other.

Procedure

This was the same as Experiment 1, with two important exceptions. First, participants viewed posts that included positively valenced claims about one of three topics: (i) GM foods (identical to the Experiment 1 positive valence condition); (ii) the benefits of clean coal (e.g., “Expert climate scientist Prof. Noah Down from Liberty College, highlights the advantages of clean coal technology, suggesting we should invest more in this tech”); or (iii) the benefits of medical marijuana for pets (e.g., “Animal physiologist Prof. Noah Down from Liberty College highlights the therapeutic potential of medical marijuana for pets in his latest research”). Second, we added a dependent same‐source consensus condition. The mock posts in this condition were the same as those in the dependent condition, except that a single secondary news source was repeatedly cited in all posts supporting the claim. The news source was randomly selected for each participant from the nine fictional outlets shown in Fig. 2.2

Ratings of prior belief confirmed that most participants did not have strong positive or negative priors about any claim topics (GM goods: M = 4.02, SEM = 0.120; clean coal: M = 4.56, SEM = 0.095; medical marijuana: M = 4.50, SEM = 0.106, see Supplementary Materials for full prior distributions).

Results

Claim endorsement

A Bayesian analysis of variance found inconclusive evidence of a difference in endorsement ratings between the three claim topics (GM foods: M = 57.71, SEM = 1.456; clean coal: M = 60.87, SEM = 1.483; medical marijuana: M = 63.94, SEM = 1.460), BF10 = 1.52, and moderate evidence against an interaction between claim topic and consensus condition, BF10 = 0.214. Hence, to improve the reliability of data modeling in the consensus conditions, we combined data across topics (see Supplementary Materials for separate data for each topic).

Fig. 4 shows endorsement ratings in the independent and the two dependent consensus conditions. The observed trajectories were generally similar to Experiment 1, with a steep rise in belief after the first batch of consensus posts and smaller increases after subsequent batches. As shown, our full Bayesian nonlinear regression model again provided a good fit to the data.

To better understand the impact of different consensus types, we again compared the relative fit of models that omitted one or more parameters. The first of these analyses compared model fits for the independent and dependent conditions (excluding dependent same source). The results, shown in Table 2, were generally similar to Experiment 1. The best fitting model included both asymptote and rate parameters. However, a model containing the asymptote parameter alone achieved nearly identical performance. As in the previous study, multiple exposures to independent consensus claims led to a higher maximum level of belief than equivalent exposure to dependent claims, with some additional evidence that beliefs changed more rapidly in response to independent as compared with dependent consensus. A robustness check where we repeated the modeling without exclusions found a similar pattern of results (see Supplementary Materials), although the ranks of the two best‐fitting models were reversed (i.e., the Asymptote‐only model achieved the best rank).

This interpretation was reinforced by a robustness check where the modeling was repeated without exclusions. This analysis found a similar fit for all eight models, including those that did not include the Start parameter (see Supplementary Materials).

As a comparison with the patterns of belief change reported in previous studies with relatively small levels of claim exposure, we compared changes in belief in the three consensus conditions, from the baseline to ratings following the first batch of consensus claims. A 3 (consensus condition) × 2 (baseline − consensus 1:1 vs. batch 1 − consensus 4:1) Bayesian analysis of variance revealed a similar pattern to that obtained in the previous experiment, with extreme evidence for a main effect increase in ratings from baseline to the first batch of posts, BF10 >10,000, but moderate evidence against an interaction between consensus type and ratings change, BF10 = 0.203. We again found that the magnitude of belief‐change in response to these initial exposures did not differ between consensus conditions.

Willingness to share

Willingness to share a consensus claim was examined using a Bayesian 3 (consensus type) × 3 (topic) analysis of variance, with default Cauchy priors. The analysis found extreme evidence for a main effect of consensus type, BF10 > 10,000. Post‐hoc comparisons found moderate evidence that sharing was more likely for claims based on independent (M = 48.97, SEM = 2.249) than dependent consensus (M = 39.35, SEM = 2.243), BF10 = 7.848. In line with the social sampling account, there was strong evidence that sharing was more likely for dependent consensus claims repeated by different secondary sources than for dependent claims repeated by a single secondary source (M = 29.05, SEM = 2.267), BF10 = 13.800. There was extreme evidence for a topic effect, BF10 = 191.9667. People were more likely to share claims about marijuana for pets (M = 46.28, SEM = 2.243) than claims about clean coal (M = 39.07, SEM = 2.237) or GM foods (M = 32.02, SEM = 2.237), BF10 > 196.16. Notably, there was strong evidence against an interaction between consensus and topic, BF10 = 0.050. Consensus type had a similar effect on willingness to share across topics.

Justifications for ratings

To examine participants’ explicit views about independent versus dependent consensus, we coded and analyzed free‐text responses to post‐test questions about how consensus evidence affected their ratings using three LLMs—Anthropic's “Claude 4.5 Sonnet” (released September 2025), Google's “Gemini 2.5 Pro” (released June 2025), and OpenAI's “GPT 5.1” (released November 2025). The prompts used were specific to each participant and included the topic‐specific claim, the text of the post‐test questions, and each of the participant's responses (see osf.io/q379s for LLM prompts and parameter settings). The outcomes of LLM free‐text analyses were similar for Experiments 1 and 2. For brevity, we only report results for Experiment 2 (Experiment 1 justification results were previously reported in preliminary form in Connor Desai, Ransom, Angles, & Hayes, 2025 and are available in the Supplementary Materials).

Responses to the first question regarding how ratings were influenced by multiple posts citing either different experts (independent condition) or the same expert (dependent condition) were coded according to whether this consensus had a “positive” or “negative” impact on belief in a claim, “none” if it had no effect, or “other positive,” “other negative,” or “unspecified.” Relatively few responses were coded into the last three categories (<10% of responses in each). Responses to a second question, which asked people whether they would have been more convinced by the consensus they saw or the relevant counterfactual, was coded to capture the preference expressed: “dependent,” “independent,” “none” (i.e., no preference), or “unspecified.” Inter‐rater reliability for LLM‐coded responses was assessed using Krippendorf's alpha (Krippendorff, 2018). This revealed moderate reliability for question 1 (α = 0.71 on 702 items with three raters) and good reliability for question 2 (α = 0.88 on 702 items with three raters).

Response proportions for each coding option for each question are shown in Fig. 5. We analyzed the effect of three factors on the generated response codings: consensus condition (dependent vs. dependent source vs. independent), topic (clean coal vs. GMO crops vs. medical marijuana), and LLM (Claude vs. Gemini vs. GPT).3 This analysis revealed two key patterns consistent with our overall findings. First, according to our LLM raters, those in independent consensus conditions more frequently reported that multiple exposures to claims had a positive impact on their belief in the claim than did people in the dependent conditions (odds ratio ≈ 1.75, 95% credible interval [1.31, 2.27], p(odds ratio >1) ≈ 1.00). Likewise, those exposed to independent consensus reported a negative impact less frequently than those exposed to dependent consensus (odds ratio ≈ 0.05, 95% credible interval [0.02, 0.09], p(odds ratio < 1) ≈ 1.00). Second, when asked to consider the counterfactual consensus condition, people showed a strong preference in favor of independent over a dependent consensus (odds ratio ≈ 17.7, 95% credible interval [12.2, 25.7], p(odds ratio > 1) ≈ 1.00). These trends were observed for all claim topics and LLM raters. In sum, after exposure to multiple claims about a range of topics, a majority of participants showed an explicit preference for information based on an independent as compared to a dependent consensus.

General discussion

The illusion of consensus that arises from frequent repetition of a claim is a vehicle for the rapid spread of misinformation and is inconsistent with many normative models of belief revision. As in several previous studies (Alister et al., 2022; Foster et al., 2012; Sulik et al., 2020; Yousif et al., 2019), we found evidence for this illusion after people initially encountered repetitions of a claim made by a single source. Crucially, however, our work reveals the limits of this illusion. Belief in claims based on independent or dependent consensus followed different trajectories of change across multiple exposures. Consensus based on independent primary sources led to a higher maximum level of belief in the truth of a claim than dependent consensus. Independent consensus also led to a more rapid belief change, although the evidence for this effect was weaker (i.e., models including both asymptote and rate of change provided only slightly better fit to the data than those based on asymptote alone). This pattern of results was robust over positively and negatively valenced claims (Experiment 1) and across a range of claim contents (Experiment 2). In free‐text responses, most people reported that they viewed multiple claims based on an independent consensus as more convincing than the same number of claims based on a dependent consensus.

Our finding that belief in a claim reaches a lower asymptote after exposure to multiple repetitions aligns with findings from the illusory truth literature, which has shown that the impact of repetition on perceived truth plateaus with persistent repetition (Fazio et al., 2022; Ransom et al., 2021). Contrary to some accounts (Ernst et al., 2017; Koch & Zerback, 2013), however, we found no evidence of a boomerang effect in our aggregate data, whereby claim endorsement declined from the first to the final batch of repetitions. This was also true at the individual level—across studies, only 6% of those in dependent conditions showed a reduction in endorsement of 10 points or more from the first to the last batch of repeated posts.

Our findings challenge assertions that people are generally insensitive to differences in the epistemic value of independent and dependent consensus (Sulik et al., 2020; Yousif et al., 2019). We have shown that, in the long run, people do follow the prescription to give more weight to evidence based on a consensus arising from multiple independent informants. That said, they suggest some necessary modifications to formal models of how we form and revise beliefs based on social testimony. For example, Bayesian models of belief change based on testimony (Whalen et al., 2018) and rational economic models of belief revision (e.g., Enke & Zimmermann, 2019), often assume a constant discounting function for dependent information. Such models need to be modified to accommodate our findings that discounting is proportional to the amount of exposure to dependent information, and this function is nonlinear (in our case, a power function). An alternative formal approach could involve modification of Bayesian approaches to reasoning that model the impact of a learner's beliefs about how an evidence sample was generated (e.g., Hayes et al., 2019b; Navarro et al., 2012; Ransom et al., 2022). Xie, Navarro, and Hayes (2020) showed that such models can be adapted to explain the different types of inferences that reasoners draw when an experimenter presents new evidence as compared to repeating old evidence. It remains to be seen whether this modeling approach can be extended to capture the differential impact of independent and dependent consensus over multiple claim exposures.

Our findings that independent consensus leads to a higher asymptotic level of belief and, to some extent, faster belief change than dependent consensus, have important, applied implications. The first of these findings is somewhat reassuring. It suggests that even with extended exposure, claims based on dependent consensus will never reach the same level of persuasiveness as those based on extended exposure to an independent consensus. This suggests that there is an inherent limitation to how far people could be swayed by an information (or disinformation) campaign that floods an information environment with claims repeated across multiple secondary sources (e.g., “reposted claims”). The rate of persuasion is also likely to be slower, compared to exposure to consensual claims that are known to originate from independent primary sources. We reiterate, however, that these distinctions between independent and dependent consensus are only likely to be observed over the long‐term, when comparing the impact of substantially more than the handful of claim exposures tested in previous research. Moreover, disinformation campaigns may still succeed if they can successfully disguise the dependence between the messages that are shared across multiple posts (e.g., by creating multiple fake “independent” accounts that share similar claims).

Why is dependent consensus persuasive?

We proposed a novel social sampling mechanism to explain the illusion of consensus. In the absence of alternative explanations, information receivers may interpret repetition of a claim by multiple secondary sources as evidence for the credibility of the primary source. We proposed that this inference would be weakened by multiple exposures to dependent consensus. Across the experiments, we found some support for this account, although the evidence was modest. The declining impact of dependent consensus over successive claim repetitions is consistent with this account. The inclusion of the dependent‐same source condition in Experiment 2 was intended as a more direct test of the social sampling account but yielded mixed findings. Contrary to expectations, we found no differences between the two dependent consensus conditions in the asymptote or rate of belief change in response to successive claims. We did, however, find that willingness to share a dependent consensus claim was reduced when the claim from a single primary source was repeated multiple times by a single secondary source. Moreover, within the subgroup of participants who expressed a positive view of dependent consensus in their free‐text justifications, many explicitly cited the repetition of claims from a single expert as implying high source credibility. The overall picture suggests that some people do perceive the repetition of claims from a single source as boosting that source's credibility, which in turn increases belief in the claim. In this regard, our findings are in line with a growing body of evidence that, across a range of judgment and inference tasks, people's interpretation of the evidence they receive depends on their beliefs about how and why the evidence was generated (Colantonio, Durkin, Caglar, Shafto, & Bonawitz, 2021; Navarro et al., 2012; Ransom et al., 2016, Shafto et al., 2012b).

However, inferring why a secondary source has chosen to repeat a claim may not be the only mechanism that drives people to be swayed by dependent consensus. Although we found that multiple exposures to independently sourced claims led to a higher maximal level of belief than equivalent exposure to dependent claims, it is important to remember that claim repetition nevertheless exerted a consistent positive effect on belief. In all dependent consensus conditions, at all levels of repetition, repetition of a claim led to belief ratings that were above baseline. This positive effect of repetition on claim endorsement may seem surprising given that only a minority of participants expressed a positive view of evidence from dependent consensus in their ratings justifications. However, this disconnect is consistent with the results from other studies of the illusion of consensus (Alister et al., 2025; Strittmatter, Pilditch, & Lagnado, 2024; Yousif et al., 2019) and related studies of belief revision (Xie & Hayes, 2022). These studies have found that although a majority of people show an explicit preference for independent over dependent consensus, they are nevertheless susceptible to the biasing effects of repetition on their beliefs regardless of whether the consensus is independent or not.

This evidence suggests that the biasing effect of repeated information in a dependent consensus is likely to be due to more than one causal mechanism. In addition to inferences about why different informants have chosen to repeat a claim, mere exposure to repeated claims may increase the familiarity or fluent processing of the claim contents and/or increase the coherence of claim contents (Jalbert & Pillai, 2024; Udry & Barber, 2024; Unkelbach et al., 2019). The multicausal nature of the effects of claim repetition is well‐illustrated in work by Mattavelli, Corneille, and Unkelbach (2023). Consistent with an inferential account, they found that just telling people that a particular claim has been repeated many times (e.g., via reposting in social media) can increase confidence in claim veracity, even if the actual repetitions are not experienced. However, belief in the claim was further enhanced when people were given an opportunity to read the repeated claims—suggesting an additional causal role of claim processing. It remains for future research to quantify the contributions of the inferential and processing mechanisms, and how they may interact.

Although our results show when—and, to some extent, why—people discount repeated claims, an important goal for the future is to provide a process‐level account of how discounting occurs. One possibility is that attention to claims from a given source depends on the extent to which the contents of later claims can be predicted from earlier claims (cf. Vlasceanu, Morais, & Coman, 2021). In the dependent case, where there is only a single primary source, predictability of the contents of later claims is likely to be high, leading to reduced attention to and encoding of information across exposures. When there are multiple independent primary sources, however, claim content is likely to be harder to predict, promoting the encoding of subsequent claims.

We intentionally selected claim topics for which people did not show strong or polarized prior beliefs. There is evidence, however, that even strongly held prior beliefs can be shifted after exposure to repetitions of contrary claims (Jiang et al., 2024). It would be interesting, therefore, for future work to compare the relative trajectories of belief in independent and dependent consensus claims over multiple exposures in more hotly contested domains like climate change or mandatory vaccination. On a related note, we acknowledge that our mock social media paradigm necessarily factored out much of the complexity of real social media environments, including the multiple cognitive, social, and motivational factors that influence social media behavior. Caution is needed, therefore, in generalizing the current results to such environments. That said, we note that past empirical work has found a moderate positive correlation between the way people behave (e.g., what posts they choose to share) in mock and real social environments (e.g., Mosleh, Pennycook, & Rand, 2020).

Conclusions

Our work shows that people are sensitive to how an online consensus is formed and become more sensitive to the epistemic implications of different forms of consensus with repeated exposure to claims. These results challenge the idea of general insensitivity to consensus formation. In line with normative accounts, they suggest that, with sufficient exposure, people will give more weight to consensus reached via independent as compared with dependent informants. The findings provide valuable insights for domains like public health or science communication, where perceptions of consensus impact belief, trust, and engagement. Acknowledging that repeated exposure can weaken belief in certain consensus types emphasizes the need for thoughtful messaging in contexts where there is likely to be repeated exposure to a claim.
