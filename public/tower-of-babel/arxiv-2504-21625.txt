Meeseeks: A Feedback-Driven, Iterative Self-Correction Benchmark evaluating LLMs’ Instruction Following Capability
Jiaming Wang, Yunke Zhao, Peng Ding, Jun Kuang, Yibin Shen, Zhe Tang, Yilin Jin, Zongyu Wang, Xiaoyu Li, Xuezhi Cao, Xunliang Cai

Abstract
The capability to precisely adhere to instructions is a cornerstone for Large Language Models (LLMs) to function as dependable agents in real-world scenarios. However, confronted with complex prompts, LLMs frequently encounter difficulties in fulfilling all specified requirements within a single response. Drawing inspiration from recent advancements in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and self-correction methodologies, we introduce Meeseeks 1 1 1 The name is inspired by Mr. Meeseeks from “Rick and Morty,” a character renowned for efficiently accomplishing assigned tasks. See: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr._Meeseeks , a fully automated iterative instruction-following benchmark equipped with an integrated feedback mechanism. Meeseeks identifies erroneous components in model responses and provides corresponding feedback accurately, thereby iteratively guiding the model toward self-correction. The dataset contains over 700 curated instances annotated by 32 distinct capability tags in Chinese and English. Extensive experimental results reveal that different state-of-the-art commercial and open-source LLMs exhibit vastly disparate performance, and even after 20 turns of iterative feedback-driven self-correction, nearly all models demonstrate suboptimal performance. We conducted comprehensive analysis and uncovered numerous common issues prevalent in current state-of-the-art models, as well as several counterintuitive phenomena. Meeseeks has been open-sourced on https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Meeseeks-156A .

While existing self-correction methods are primarily evaluated on mathematical or logical problems, we extend this approach to multi-constraint instruction-following tasks to probe the upper limits of LLMs’ instruction-following capabilities.

Therefore, we introduce Meeseeks, a multi-turn automatic instruction-following benchmark with feedback system. The framework enables Meeseeks to fully automatically identify specific constraints that the LLM fails to meet and explicitly explain the reasons for such failures. The system operates in successive rounds, where Meeseeks evaluates the LLM response, provides feedback, and then assesses the resulting self-correction. Moreover, Meeseeks applies a more challenging dataset embedding 32 capability tags and 3 cognitive evaluation dimensions (intent recognition, granular content validation and output structure validation), examining the entire thought process of the model to establish an integrated instruction-following ability evaluation system. Furthermore, due to the high cost of single to multiple turns evaluation and more challenging data, Meeseeks introduces a code-guided, rule-augmented LLM-based evaluation to reduce cost and raise accuracy of the evaluation workflow.
Meeseeks reveals nuanced patterns in LLMs’ multi-turn self-correction abilities that challenge conventional understanding of instruction following as well as a significant gap between different LLMs’ single-attempt instruction-following performance and their upper-limit instruction-following capability. Even after 20 rounds of self-correction, all prevailing LLMs still fail to provide responses that satisfy all constraints. In conclusion, our main contributions are:

•

We introduce Meeseeks, a challenging multi-turn automatic instruction-following benchmark that operates in successive evaluation-feedback-correction cycles across 32 capability tags and 3 evaluation dimensions. The framework accurately identifies constraint violations, provides targeted feedback, and evaluates LLMs’ self-correction attempts.

•

We present an enhanced automatic evaluation pipeline by introducing a novel code-guided, rule-augmented LLM-based evaluation framework. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed method significantly outperforms existing approaches, delivering substantial improvements in evaluation accuracy, raising accuracy from 78.7% to 98.4%.

•

We conduct experiments across 17 state-of-the-art models and observe that despite implementing 20 iterations of feedback-driven self-correction mechanisms, both leading commercial and open-source LLMs consistently failed to achieve accuracy rates exceeding 91%. Through systematic analysis, we identify both common and distinct limitations among these models, thereby contributing valuable insights and establishing reference benchmarks for future model development and optimization efforts.

3
Meeseeks

Meeseeks implements an iterative self-correction framework comprising response collection, evaluation, and feedback prompting (Figure
1
). More specifically, given questions with multiple constraints, each iteration follows three steps: (1) collecting responses from the model while enforcing all constraints, (2) evaluating the response against each constraint, and (3) providing constraint-specific evaluation results and explanations as feedback to prompt the model for self-correction in the subsequent iteration.

Section

3.1
Evaluation

Due to hallucinations in LLM, directly relying on them to determine whether each constraint is satisfied by the tested model’s response is unreliable
(
Huang et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2023a
;
Zhu et al., 2024
;
Gu et al., 2024
;
Qiu et al., 2024a
;
Chen et al., 2024
;
Hao et al., 2025
;
Bang et al., 2025
;
Yan et al., 2024
)
.
Therefore, our primary method for judging constraints consists of two steps: first, use an LLM evaluator to format the model’s response (typically by converting it into a Python list); second, employ a Python script to assess whether each constraint is correctly satisfied. To facilitate the format section, the framework integrates a dual format mechanism: the coding format, which activates the code-specific pipeline when coding flags are present (flag detail: Appendix
D
; coding format detail: Section
3.3
), and the regenerate format, which formats model responses through regeneration.
The evaluation procedure is bifurcated according to the rule labels associated with each constraint. For constraints with null rule labels (see Appendix
D
), the LLM evaluator assesses the response directly, without any formatting. When specific rule labels are present, corresponding rule-based Python judgment scripts are invoked to verify the validity of the formatted content.

3.2
Feedback Prompting

After evaluation, each constraint is accompanied by a judgment explanation; if a constraint is unmet, the feedback specifies the reason for failure. With the exception of a small portion of feedback generated directly by the LLM evaluator, the majority of feedback is automatically produced by the constraint-specific scripts. These evaluation results and corresponding feedback are integrated into the tested LLM’s chat template for subsequent turns, serving as targeted guidance for its self-correction. The framework monitors improvement after each self-correction iteration.

However, this approach encounters significant challenges in both computational efficiency and accuracy. For long model responses (such as extended essays) and large-scale multi-element generation, the LLM-based format extraction requires regenerating entire clean texts to remove non-essential components, resulting in substantial computational overhead. Furthermore, format accuracy degrades considerably as the complexity of model responses increases, creating a scalability bottleneck for practical applications. To address these issues, we propose a code-guided, rule-augmented LLM-based evaluation. Specifically, we use prompts to guide the LLM to format model response by coding, rather than having the model regenerate all context. By applying the function created by the LLM Evaluator, the necessary information can be formatted efficiently without the regeneration step

4.1
).

3.4
Data collection, Taxonomy and Quality

Meeseeks comprises over 700 meticulously curated instances in Chinese and English, annotated with 32 distinct capability tags, and with a substantially greater difficulty compared to existing datasets in the domain. The dataset is collected from our business log and rigorously formatted to templates (Appendix
F
), wherein all constraints are manually annotated by professional annotators to ensure both precision and contextual relevance. Similar to prevailing instruction following benchmarks, the prompts from our log also contain multiple output constraints, and we use a cognitive-inspired framework that decomposes instruction-following into three fundamental dimensions (Figure
1
): Intent Recognition, Granular Content Validation, and Output Structure Validation. This framework employs hierarchical capability tags across 3 levels and 32 capability tags in total, with lower levels subordinate to higher ones (Appendix
E
). Data instances are systematically generated by varying template parameters and synchronously updating the associated constraint variables, with
NO involvement of LLM at any stage of data generation
. To further enhance the reliability and objectivity of the evaluation, all subjective capability constraints—such as those assessing emotional intelligence in model responses—are deliberately excluded from the dataset. For quality assurance, we recruit three experienced annotators, each holding at least a bachelor’s degree with professional expertise in data annotation, and conducted comprehensive reviews of every data instance and its associated constraints, rigorously verifying their validity and consistency. For instance, they identified and resolved logical contradictions, such as cases where one constraint required the inclusion of an "idea" while another simultaneously prohibited a "great idea," thereby ensuring the integrity and coherence of the synthesized data.

Metrics

Serving as trustworthy agents, LLMs must fulfill all requirements specified in the user’s prompt for the responses to be considered usable.
Utility rate
reflects the ratio of usable responses to total evaluated responses and is essential for evaluating an LLM’s effectiveness as a practical and professional agent. Additionally, to help researchers identify subtle differences in instruction-following abilities between different LLMs, we propose the Meeseeks score, which indicates the overall capability tag accuracy of the LLM (see formula in Appendix
C

4
Experiments

4.1
Experiments on Code-guided, Rule-augmented LLM-based Evaluation

To evaluate the effectiveness of our code-guided, rule-based evaluation, we conduct experiments in two distinct scenarios: long single element generation, where the model is tasked with producing a complete article, and large-scale multi-element response generation, where the model is required to generate a substantial number of comments (These two frequently used scenarios are utilized and modified from Complexbench’s dataset). For long single element scenarios, we choose the question:
A man is performing on stage for the audience, … Please continue and expand this passage with at least 20 sentences.
We vary the required number of sentences from 0 to 500 with an increment of 1, generating synthetic data.
For large-scale multi-element scenarios, we choose the question:
People are … Please write an essay. Requirements: Argumentative essay, objective and neutral language style, no less than 800 characters.
Similarly, we set the required character count from 50 to 5000 (in increments of 100, and rather than use "less than", output length is required in a certain range), generating synthetic data and answers.
The ratio of correct to incorrect answers in both scenarios is set to 1:1, and employed Qwen2.5-32B + Qwen2.5-32B-Coder
(
Qwen et al., 2025
)
as the evaluator (also for main experiments). Subsequently, we conduct a comparative analysis of the accuracy and token consumption patterns exhibited by the code-guided and prevailing formatting methods (Figure
2
).

In large-scale multi-element scenarios, the code-guided formatting approach exhibited substantial performance gains over the prevailing method. It attained an average end-to-end accuracy of 0.994 with an average token usage of 252.94, compared to the prevailing formatting’s accuracy of 0.684 and token usage of 874.65—yielding a 45.32% improvement in accuracy and a 71.08% reduction in token consumption. In scenarios with long single element, code-guided formatting sustained high efficacy, achieving an end-to-end accuracy of 0.998 and average token usage of 98.73, whereas the prevailing approach encountered severe hallucinations and often misidentified core content, resulting in an accuracy drop to the baseline level of 0.5 and token usage of 191.99; this translated to a 97.46% accuracy enhancement for code-guided formatting. The limitations of prevailing formatting primarily arose from its token-saving strategy, which defaulted to outputting merely the "thinking process" and "ALL" without supplementary context ("ALL" indicates that the evaluator considers the model’s response to be free of redundancy). Efforts to address this by employing advanced evaluators such as GPT-4o yielded no discernible benefits, suggesting that the underlying issue resides in the few-shot prompt architecture of the prevailing method. These outcomes highlight the enhanced robustness and resource efficiency of code-guided formatting in varied and demanding contexts.

5
Analysis

5.1
Performance Gap On First and Last Turn

We design the experiment such that models were only required to correct questions they had previously answered incorrectly in subsequent turns. Theoretically, as the pool of questions that can be answered correctly diminishes, the performance gap between different models should gradually narrow. However, our findings indicate the opposite: the disparity between models actually widens with an increasing number of turns. Through this iterative process of correction over multiple turns, we can progressively approach the upper limit of a model’s instruction-following capability. This phenomenon suggests that the difference in the upper limits of instruction-following abilities among various models is substantially greater than the single-turn performance differences might suggest (Figure
3
). Concurrently, we observe that the rankings of the models evolve across turns, and their correlation with the initial ranking in the first turn progressively weakens. We calculate the Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between the model rankings in Turn 1 and those in each subsequent turn. The results reveal that the correlation between a model’s performance in a single attempt and its upper limit of instruction-following ability initially diminishes rapidly and subsequently plateaus as the number of turns increases (Figure
3
). This further reinforces our earlier conclusion: the disparity between a model’s single-turn instruction-following performance and its ultimate capability ceiling is more significant than initially anticipated.

5.2
Performance Gap Across Capability Tags

To further elucidate the underlying reasons for suboptimal model performance, we examine the identified issues through the lens of constraint satisfaction. Specifically, we analyze these challenges from the perspective of our three top-level capability tags along with some additional findings.

Figure 3:
As the turns iterate, the standard deviation of utility rates gradually increases; The correlation between subsequent round rankings and turn 1 rankings shows a declining trend.

Output Structure Validation
When models are tasked with generating multiple contexts (e.g., producing numerous titles or names), their outputs exhibit substantial redundancy, with minimal improvement following feedback interventions. Despite explicit feedback indicating which content elements are duplicated, virtually all evaluated models demonstrate limited capacity to rectify such extensive redundancy issues (Figure
4
). In more demanding scenarios, when models are required to generate content adhering to specific proportional constraints—for instance, producing 20 pieces of copy with a long-form to short-form ratio of 3:2—we observe that most models fail to accurately determine the requisite quantities of long-form versus short-form content. However, reasoning contents in reasoning models help predetermine the quantities of long and short content within their reasoning processes, thereby mitigating such failures.

Figure 4:
Performance trends varied significantly across models and capability tags.

Granular Content Validation
Within this dimension, model deficiencies are predominantly concentrated on word count accuracy. We observe that models fundamentally lack precise word counting capabilities; however, they should theoretically possess a general awareness of output length
(
Hiraoka and Okazaki, 2024
;
Zhao et al., 2025a
;
Kaplan et al., 2025
;
Vieira et al., 2025
)
. Indeed, our experiment shows that models would adjust their output length to align with specified requirements through our feedback mechanism (Figure
4
). Nevertheless, we identify a critical phenomenon: when models receive feedback indicating that their output word count fails to meet requirements—accompanied by the actual word count—they frequently exhibit severe overcorrection behaviors. For instance, when the requirement specifies approximately 400 words (with acceptance criteria of 360-440 words), and the model’s initial output contains 300 words, upon receiving feedback regarding the word count deficiency, most models initially make modest upward adjustments (e.g., to 330 words). However, upon receiving subsequent feedback indicating continued insufficiency, catastrophic overcorrection occurs: outputs may abruptly escalate to 600 words. This adjustment momentum exhibits bidirectional persistence, with subsequent iterations potentially plummeting to 100 words. While we observe that certain models can gradually resolve some questions through sustained iterative feedback, a substantial proportion of models remain unable to address this fundamental issue even after 20 rounds of feedback-guided iterations. To provide more granular analysis, we introduce detailed capability tags spanning: 0-10 words (precise), 10-50 words (precise/range), 50-200 words (range), and above 200 words (range) (Figure
4
). These results demonstrate that models exhibit markedly superior adherence to word count constraints at smaller magnitudes.

Intent Recognition
In this dimension, we implement a particularly challenging task requiring models to modify prompts. Specifically, we delimit prompts using different special notation and instruct models to modify the enclosed prompt content either at the beginning or end of the interaction. We observe that the majority of models fail to comprehend our requirement for prompt modification on the initial attempt. Instead, they erroneously execute the tasks specified within the bracketed prompt content. Even reasoning-capable models demonstrate this misinterpretation during their initial cognitive processes, despite their enhanced reasoning capabilities. Subsequently, we employ feedback mechanisms to explicitly reveal our actual intention and emphasize the prompt modification requirement. However, we find that a substantial proportion of models remain unable to successfully modify the designated prompt content even after clarification. We attribute this limitation to fundamental optimization challenges inherent in multi-turn dialogue systems, suggesting that current model architectures may possess insufficient capacity for maintaining and adapting to task clarification across conversational iterations.

Additional Findings
For regional knowledge requirements, we observe that broad constraints such as rhyming patterns and traditional-simplified Chinese character restrictions pose relatively manageable challenges for models. However, when confronted with more specialized linguistic requirements—such as generating content adhering to specific tonal pattern (pingze) constraints—models encounter substantial difficulties. Given that virtually all evaluated models lack a comprehensive understanding of tonal pattern principles, they demonstrate near-universal failure in completing pingze-related generation tasks. Notably, we identify a particularly intriguing phenomenon: Our feedback mechanism provides models with pingze information corresponding to their generated content. Among all evaluated models, o3-mini (high) emerges as the sole exception capable of leveraging the pingze information provided in feedback to reconstruct content that satisfies the specified tonal pattern requirements. This capability represents a remarkably encouraging discovery, demonstrating the big in-context learning gap among models.

5.3
Reasoning/Non-reasoning Models Gap

Either in single-turn or multi-turn scenarios, most reasoning models generally outperform their non-reasoning counterparts. These findings challenge the prevailing consensus that thinking models are less effective in instruction-following tasks
(
Li et al., 2025b
)
. Our analysis reveals that reasoning models demonstrate consistent improvements over non-reasoning models across five capability tags in the final evaluation round (Generate with certain number of word X; Generate in 50-200 words; Generate in 10-50 words; Generate in X times word number of reference text; Generate Chinese-English-mixed article).
While individual reasoning models exhibit minor performance degradations on specific metrics, these decreases are neither substantial nor systematic across models. Notably, most reasoning models show significant performance gains through feedback-driven self-correction that substantially exceed those of non-reasoning models, even when the reasoning models already demonstrate initial advantages in single-turn evaluations. More specifically, we find reasoning models demonstrate superior sensitivity to word number, whether for Chinese characters or English words. They exhibit greater robustness in both range-based and precise numerical outputs, effectively mitigating the catastrophic adjustment momentum issues previously identified. Moreover, in reasoning contents, reasoning models proactively calculate required content length and proportions for tasks requiring inferential steps, leading to improved task execution. For "Generate with certain number of word X" tasks, reasoning models consistently verify word occurrence within their reasoning context, eliminating the "random guessing" behaviors observed in non-reasoning models.

6
Conclusion

We introduce Meeseeks, a multi-turn instruction-following benchmark that evaluates LLMs’ capability to satisfy multi-constraint instructions through iterative feedback. Facing accuracy drop in complex scenarios, we propose code-guided rule-augmented LLM-based evaluation framework, raising the evaluation accuracy from 78.7% to 98.4%. Through our experiments across 17 popular models, we find even after 20 turns of self-correction, almost all LLMs fail to exceed 90% accuracy, highlighting fundamental limitations in LLMs’ instruction-following capabilities. The substantial performance gaps between single-turn capabilities and multi-turn upper bounds reveal that different LLMs exhibit varying instruction-following ceilings. Consequently, we provide rigorous analysis of the self-correction mechanisms and offer new insights for developing more reliable instruction-following systems in future LLM research.

Limitations

In the comparison between reasoning and non-reasoning models, while adding "Think step by step" prompting to non-reasoning models might potentially improve their performance scores, we deliberately refrained from employing this approach. We did not explicitly restrict models from outputting auxiliary reasoning content, and our rationale for this decision stems from the benchmark’s fundamental purpose of reflecting authentic model capabilities. Considering that users typically lack professional prompting expertise and would experience suboptimal user experience when extracting answers from extensive reasoning contexts, reasoning models prove more suitable for end-users under our experimental conclusions. Consequently, we did not conduct such granular ablation studies.

Instruction-following evaluation is inherently constrained by its design to disregard answer correctness. Even with increased task complexity, establishing a direct correlation between model performance and user experience remains challenging. In our future work, we aspire to bridge the gap between instruction-following capabilities and real-world applications, enabling benchmarks to transcend specific capability boundaries and instead integrate multiple competencies, thereby linking benchmark scores more directly to enhanced user experience.

Meeseeks currently contains a relatively limited dataset, necessitating multiple evaluation runs to obtain stable performance scores. However, this represents a continuously evolving benchmark, and the present work primarily focuses on the novel perspective of multi-turn feedback evaluation. The work has been open-sourced, and we are committed to ongoing updates, with plans to substantially expand the Meeseeks dataset in the near future.

Appendix C
Metric

C.1
Utility Rate

To evaluate LLMs as trustworthy agents, we adopt and generalize IF-Eval
Zhou et al. (2023)
’s strict "is followed" metric, introducing it as Utility Rate - the proportion of responses that fully satisfy all prompt requirements. This intuitive metric measures the ratio of truly usable responses, serving as a crucial indicator of an LLM’s capability to function as a reliable professional agent.

\text{Utility Rate}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}}{n}

U_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if response is usable}\\
0,&\text{if response is not usable}\end{cases}

C.2
Meeseeks Score

To help researchers identify subtle differences in instruction-following abilities between different LLMs, we propose Meeseeks Score, which indicates the overall capability tag accuracy of the LLM.
Meeseeks Score averages the scores of all the level 1 capability tags associated with the user prompt.

\text{Meeseeks Score}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\text{Score}_{\text{tag}_{j}}}{m}

where
m
is the total number of level 1 capability tags associated with the current issue.

The score for each individual level 1 capability tag is the average of the scores of its respective requirements.

\text{Score}_{\text{tag}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Score}_{\text{requirement}_{i}}}{n}

where
n
is the total number of requirements for the capability tag.
