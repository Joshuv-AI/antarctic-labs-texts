DeepSeek-R1 vs. o3-mini: How Well can Reasoning LLMs Evaluate MT and Summarization?
Daniil Larionov †, Sotaro Takeshita †, Ran Zhang †, Yanran Chen ⋄

Abstract
Reasoning-enabled large language models (LLMs) excel in logical tasks, yet their utility for evaluating natural language generation remains unexplored. This study systematically compares reasoning LLMs with non-reasoning counterparts across machine translation and text summarization evaluation tasks. We evaluate eight models spanning state-of-the-art reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI o3), their distilled variants (8B-70B parameters), and equivalent non-reasoning LLMs. Experiments on WMT23 and SummEval benchmarks reveal architecture and task-dependent benefits: OpenAI o3-mini models show improved performance with increased reasoning on MT, while DeepSeek-R1 and generally underperforms compared to its non-reasoning variant except in summarization consistency evaluation. Correlation analysis demonstrates that reasoning token usage correlates with evaluation quality only in specific models, while almost all models generally allocate more reasoning tokens when identifying more quality issues. Distillation maintains reasonable performance up to 32B parameter models but degrades substantially at 8B scale. This work provides the first assessment of reasoning LLMs for NLG evaluation and comparison to non-reasoning models. We share our code to facilitate further research: https://github.com/NL2G/reasoning-eval .

Introduction

Reasoning LLMs have driven recent progress in NLP, often outperforming standard models by leveraging chain-of-thought (CoT) supervision
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
and reinforcement learning
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
to improve multi-step and logical inference
(
Arora and Zanette, 2025
;
Guo et al., 2025
)
.

Reasoning and Non-Reasoning LLMs for Prompt-Based Evaluation.

However, reasoning is not always beneficial—CoT prompting can degrade performance when verbal deliberation introduces unnecessary complexity
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
or the task does not require it
(
Sprague et al., 2025
)
. This shows the need to explore when reasoning is actually advantageous.

A critical but underexplored application is Natural Language Generation (NLG) evaluation. Although LLM-based metrics have become the go-to approach for high-quality machine translation (MT) and text summarization (TS) evaluation
(
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023a
;
Liu et al., 2023
;
Leiter et al., 2023
)
, existing methods are primarily built on non-reasoning LLMs. Reasoning could improve NLG evaluation by enabling nuanced assessment processes that mirror how human experts evaluate translations and summaries, that is, through step-by-step analysis of semantic preservation, identification of subtle contextual mismatches, and systematic weighing of multiple quality dimensions that often requires multi-step thinking rather than holistic pattern matching. This gap raises two key research questions: (1) whether reasoning models do improve NLG evaluation quality, and (2) whether distilled reasoning models can maintain evaluation capabilities while reducing computational costs, given that full-scale reasoning LLMs often have several hundreds of billions parameters.

To address these questions, we systematically test reasoning LLMs and compare them to equivalent non-reasoning models. The overall prompt-based evaluation pipeline is illustrated in Figure
1
. Our framework includes three architectural categories: (1) SOTA reasoning LLMs: DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o3; (2) distilled variants of R1 with 70, 32 and 8 billion parameters; and (3) equivalent non-reasoning LLMs, such as GPT-4o-mini, Qwen-2.5 32B, and Llama-3.1 8B. We test these models using G-Eval
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
for TS and GEMBA-MQM
(
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023a
)
for MT evaluation.

Our results reveal distinct performance patterns across model families:
a)
The OpenAI o3-mini model outperforms non-reasoning baselines on MT evaluation metrics and delivers substantial improvements on ‘coherence’ and ‘fluency’ dimensions in TS evaluation;
b)
DeepSeek-R1 and its distilled versions underperform their non-reasoning counterparts on most MT metrics, with some exceptions in TS ‘consistency’ and ‘fluency’ quality aspects;
c)
Correlation analysis identifies a low-medium correlation between reasoning token count and evaluation quality for the OpenAI o3-mini for MT evaluation and R1-Distill-Qwen-32B for TS evaluation, but very low to no correlation for other models in the study;
d)
Models consistently dedicate more reasoning tokens when assessing texts that they find low-quality compared to high-quality ones, with a strong negative correlation between reasoning length and predicted quality score;
e)
Distillation up to 32B parameters preserves most evaluation quality for reasoning models while 8B models exhibit substantial performance degradation on the studied evaluation tasks.

Our work presents the first systematic comparison of reasoning versus non-reasoning LLMs as NLG evaluators and provides valuable insights into the efficacy of reasoning distillation for practical deployment in NLG evaluation tasks.

2
Related Work

Our work connects to evaluation metrics for MT, summarization and “LLM-as-a-Judge” approaches, efficiency in metrics as well as to reasoning in LLMs.

Traditional Metrics for MT

Early MT evaluation metrics focused on surface-level similarity between system and reference translations. BLEU
(
Papineni et al., 2002
)
and METEOR
(
Banerjee and Lavie, 2005
)
rely on n-gram overlap and edit distance. BERTScore
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
further improved MT evaluation by computing contextualized word embeddings from BERT
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
. To capture semantic shifts more effectively, MoverScore
(
Zhao et al., 2019
)
leverages word embeddings to measure distances between system and reference translations, using Word Mover’s Distance
(
Kusner et al., 2015
)
. Other paradigms popular for evaluating MT include NLI-based metrics
(
Chen and Eger, 2023
)
and trained metrics
(
Rei et al., 2020
;
Guerreiro et al., 2024
)
.

Traditional Metrics for TS

One of the foundational metrics for evaluating TS is ROUGE
(
Lin, 2004
)
, which measures the overlap between system-generated summaries and reference summaries. However, ROUGE struggles with semantic equivalence and factual consistency, particularly in long-form summarization. To address these limitations, content-based metrics such as Pyramid
(
Nenkova and Passonneau, 2004
)
and BE
(
Hovy et al., 2006
)
were introduced and later semantic metrics such as SUPERT
(
Gao et al., 2020
)
. Pyramid aligns important information across multiple references, while BE identifies minimal semantic units in a reference summary. Benchmarking efforts such as SummEval
(
Fabbri et al., 2021
)
have further supported systematic comparisons of TS evaluation metrics.

LLM-based Evaluation

The “LLM-as-a-Judge” paradigm
(
Gu et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2025
;
Huang et al., 2024
)
employs instruction-following LLMs (e.g., GPT-4
(
OpenAI et al., 2024
)
, LLaMA-3
(
Grattafiori et al., 2024
)
) to directly assess system outputs along dimensions such as fluency, coherence, and factual consistency
(
Chia et al., 2023
)
. These methods increasingly correlate with human judgment and offer a scalable alternative to traditional metrics
(
Liu et al., 2023
;
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023a
)
. In MT, GEMBA
(
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023a
;
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023b
)
prompts LLMs to evaluate individual segments in isolation before aggregating scores, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the WMT22 Metrics Benchmark
(
Freitag et al., 2022
)
. AutoMQM
(
Fernandes et al., 2023
)
extends this by asking LLMs to detect and classify translation errors, offering more interpretable and fine-grained feedback. In TS, G-Eval
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
adopts a criteria-based framework using chain-of-thought prompting, while FineSurE
(
Song et al., 2024
)
incorporates key fact extraction and verification by LLMs. Eval4NLP
(
Leiter et al., 2023
)
further benchmarked prompting-based LLM evaluators for summarization in a shared task setting. Despite these advances, existing work leverages only non-reasoning LLMs. The recent release of reasoning-enabled LLMs opens the question of whether reasoning LLMs offer further benefits for the ‘LLM-as-a-Judge’ paradigm.

Efficiency for Evaluation Metrics

With the rising computational cost of LLM-based evaluation, recent work has explored lightweight alternatives to maintain effectiveness under resource constraints. FrugalScore
(
Eddine et al., 2022
)
uses distillation and representation pruning to reduce model size without compromising metric reliability. EffEval
(
Larionov et al., 2023
)
investigates trade-offs between efficiency and quality, showing that adapter-based fine-tuning and compact architectures are viable. Similarly, COMETINHO
(
Rei et al., 2022
)
introduces efficient variants of COMET through optimized inference, while xCOMET-lite
(
Larionov et al., 2024
)
leverages quantization and distillation for real-time applications. Prompt-level optimization methods such as PromptOptMe
(
Larionov and Eger, 2025
)
reduce token overhead while preserving evaluation accuracy. Building on previous efforts in efficient evaluation, we investigate how effective smaller, distilled reasoning models are at NLG evaluation.

Reasoning LLMs

Recent models such as DeepSeek-R1
(
Guo et al., 2025
)
and Open AI o3-mini
1
1

1

https://openai.com/index/openai-o3-mini/
are designed to perform complex step-by-step reasoning using techniques like CoT prompting
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
through Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
. These approaches enable intermediate reasoning supervision and reward shaping
(
Arora and Zanette, 2025
)
, leading to strong performance on mathematically and logically demanding tasks. Further, efficiency and quality gains are achieved through CoT compression
(
Deng et al., 2024
)
, model distillation
(
Guo et al., 2025
)
, and test-time scaling strategies
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
. However, their potential for evaluating NLG remains underexplored, as existing approaches often use heuristic CoT prompting without fully leveraging reasoning LLMs
(
Leiter and Eger, 2024
)
. This motivates our investigation into whether such models can outperform conventional LLMs.

3
Experimental Setup

Our evaluation focuses on two primary NLG tasks: MT evaluation and TS evaluation. We choose MT evaluation as it allows to asses multilingual evaluation capabilities of reasoning LLMs. Summarization, in turn, was chosen as it allows us to evaluation longer-context capabilities of LLMs, because it requires analyzing long original texts and their summaries. For this, the reasoning might potentially be especially helpful. We utilize established prompting-based metrics that have demonstrated strong performance in recent benchmarks.

MT Evaluation

For MT evaluation, we employ GEMBA-MQM
(
Kocmi and Federmann, 2023a
)
, a prompting-based metric that leverages LLMs to identify and rate translation quality errors. GEMBA-MQM instructs LLMs to evaluate MT by detecting errors across dimensions such as accuracy, fluency, and terminology, assigning severity scores to each error. This method has shown state-of-the-art performance on the WMT22 Metrics Shared Task
(
Freitag et al., 2023
)
.

TS Evaluation

For TS evaluation, we implement G-Eval
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
, which prompts LLMs to evaluate summaries along multiple dimensions including coherence, consistency, relevance, and fluency. G-Eval provides explicit criteria for each dimension and asks the model to generate both numerical scores and explanations, making it particularly suitable for testing the impact of reasoning capabilities.

Datasets

For MT we utilize the WMT23 Metrics Shared Task dataset
(
Freitag et al., 2023
)
, which provides human judgments for translations between various language pairs. The dataset includes source texts, reference translations, system translations, and human judgments at segment levels. We use the en-de, he-en, and zh-en language pairs to evaluate across diverse linguistic structures. Each translation is accompanied by span-level error annotations.

For TS evaluation, we use the SummEval dataset
(
Fabbri et al., 2021
)
, which contains human judgments for summaries from 16 different summarization systems on 100 news articles from the CNN/DailyMail dataset. Each summary is evaluated along four dimensions: coherence, consistency, relevance, and fluency, with scores ranging from 1 to 5. Additionally, we employ the Eval4NLP dataset
(
Leiter et al., 2023
)
. The human judgement scores for Eval4NLP are not published online, which allows us to evaluate reasoning and non-reasoning models with no data contamination.

Models

We evaluate three categories of models to systematically investigate our research questions:

SOTA Reasoning LLMs:

DeepSeek R1

(
Guo et al., 2025
)
is a reasoning-enabled variant of DeepSeek V3, fine-tuned using reinforcement learning for explicit generation of reasoning. This 600B parameter model represents the current frontier of reasoning architecture.
OpenAI o3-mini
is a reasoning model with controllable reasoning effort: high, medium, and low. This model allows us to investigate the impact of reasoning intensity on evaluation performance within the same model architecture.

Distilled Variants of DeepSeek R1:

R1 LLaMA 70B
,
R1 Qwen 32B
, and
R1 LLaMA 8B
, representing three different LLM size tiers.

Non-Reasoning LLMs (Control Group):

DeepSeek V3

(
DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025
)
as non-reasoning counterpart to DeepSeek R1;
GPT-4o-mini

(
OpenAI et al., 2024
)
as presumed non-reasoning variant from the GPT-4 family;
Qwen-2.5 32B

(
Qwen et al., 2025
)
as counterpart for R1 Distill Qwen 32B;
LLaMa-3.3 70B

(
Grattafiori et al., 2024
)
as counterpart to R1 Distill Llama 70B and
LLaMa-3.1 8B

(
Grattafiori et al., 2024
)
for R1 LLaMa 8B.

This selection provides a comprehensive cross-section of reasoning vs. non-reasoning models across different parameter scales, allowing us to analyze both capability differences and the impact of distillation.

Evaluation Protocol

All models were evaluated using the same prompting templates to ensure fair comparison. For the GEMBA-MQM metric, we followed the template described in
Kocmi and Federmann (2023a)
,
which instructs the model to identify errors in a translation and then score the translation based on those errors. For G-Eval, we used the prompts detailed in
Liu et al. (2023)
,
which instructs the model to evaluate summaries on multiple dimensions with specific criteria.

For meta-evaluation of MT metrics, we compute segment-level Pearson correlation (
\rho
) between model scores and human judgments, as well as system-level pairwise accuracy
(
Deutsch et al., 2023
)
, which measures how often the metric correctly predicts which of two systems is better according to human judgments. For TS evaluation, we compute segment-level Kendall
\tau
correlation with human judgments across quality aspects.

OpenAI vs. DeepSeek

OpenAI reasoning models show consistent performance irrespective of the reasoning effort setting, with o3-mini-high and o3-mini-low achieving nearly identical SummEval averages (0.337 and 0.335) and the highest overall Eval4NLP scores (0.644 and 0.645). DeepSeek V3 achieves the highest SummEval average score (0.399) among all models tested, with particular strengths in coherence (0.462), relevance (0.446), and fluency (0.356).

Reasoning vs. non-reasoning models

Across model families, we identify a pattern of non-reasoning models outperforming their reasoning counterparts:
1)
DeepSeek V3 (non-reasoning) outperforms DeepSeek R1 in both SummEval average (0.399 vs. 0.351) and Eval4NLP (0.630 vs. 0.583);
2)
LLaMA 3.3 70B (non-reasoning) achieves better scores than R1 LLaMa 70B in both SummEval average (0.375 vs. 0.315) and Eval4NLP (0.624 vs. 0.556);
3)
Qwen 2.5 32B (non-reasoning) outperforms R1 Qwen 32B with higher SummEval average (0.393 vs. 0.355) and Eval4NLP (0.619 vs. 0.564) scores; and
4)
The OpenAI reasoning and non-reasoning models achieve more comparable scores for SummEval average (0.337 vs. 0.346) and Eval4NLP (0.644 vs. 0.634).

However, reasoning models demonstrate specific strengths in evaluation of consistency, coherence, and fluency. DeepSeek R1 excels in consistency metrics (0.565 vs. 0.331, a 70% better correlation), showing substantially better alignment with human judgments as compared to the non-reasoning DeepSeek V3. Similarly, R1 Qwen 32B shows strong consistency performance, outperforming Qwen 2.5 32B by 20% (0.540 vs. 0.449). R1 LLaMa 70B in turn outperforms its non-reasoning counterpart in fluency evaluation by 16% (0.330 vs. 0.284). The OpenAI o3-mini model outperforms the assumed non-reasoning equivalent GPT-4o-mini in coherence by approximately 50% (0.482 vs. 0.321) and in fluency by 25% (0.313 vs. 0.263).

Distilled vs. original models

When comparing the original DeepSeek R1 with its distilled variants, we observe that R1 Qwen 32B matches the original model quality on SummEval average and achieves 97% on Eval4NLP (0.564 vs. 0.583). This distilled model has 21 times fewer parameters than the original one (32B vs. 685B). The distillation process also largely maintains the reasoning model advantage in consistency evaluation over equivalent non-reasoning models.

At the same time, the small distilled model (R1 LLaMa 8B) shows substantially worse performance compared to the original R1 model on the SummEval dataset. We can also see that it loses the advantage in the consistency aspect of TS evaluation.

OpenAI vs. DeepSeek

The OpenAI models demonstrate strong performance across language pairs, with o3-mini-high achieving the highest overall segment-level correlations (0.577 for en-de and 0.568 for zh-en). Within the OpenAI o3-mini variants, we observe that higher reasoning effort settings generally correspond to better performance, particularly for the en-de and zh-en pairs. Interestingly, GPT-4o-mini performs comparatively well on low-resource language pair he-en (0.435), outperforming its reasoning counterparts. For system-level accuracy, the OpenAI models achieve identical scores (0.928 for o3-mini variants and GPT-4o-mini).

Reasoning vs. non-reasoning models

Similarly to our summarization findings, certain non-reasoning models outperform their reasoning counterparts in translation evaluation tasks. Here this pattern holds for DeepSeek models: DeepSeek V3 (non-reasoning) outperforms DeepSeek R1 in both en-de (0.490 vs. 0.364) and zh-en (0.512 vs. 0.441) language pairs, with comparable performance on he-en. R1 LLaMa 70B shows substantial deterioration over LLaMa 3.3 70B across all language pairs, with particularly notable differences in en-de (0.590 vs. 0.421) and he-en (0.420 vs. 0.365). Similarly, Qwen 2.5 32B achieves higher correlations than R1 Qwen 32B in all language pairs, with the largest gap in en-de (0.521 vs. 0.388).

The exception to this pattern is OpenAI o3-mini at varying reasoning efforts, which demonstrate superior performance compared to GPT-4o-mini across two language pairs: in en-de (0.577 vs. 0.410) and zh-en (0.568 vs. 0.487).

For system-level accuracy, the differences between reasoning and non-reasoning models are less pronounced. Qwen 2.5 32B achieves the highest system-level accuracy (0.944), with R1 LLaMa 70B (0.932) performing slightly better than its non-reasoning counterpart (0.924).

The distilled reasoning models show varying levels of quality degradation. R1 LLaMa 70B and R1 Qwen2.5 32B outperform original R1 model on two out of three language pairs (en-de and zh-en), while deteriorating on low-resource he-en. At the same time, the smaller R1 LLaMa 8B shows substantial drop across all 3 language pairs.

Error Correlations

The majority of reasoning models exhibit statistically significant negative correlations between reasoning token count and evaluation error, suggesting that increased reasoning is associated with smaller discrepancies between model-predicted and human judgment scores. The o3-mini variants show strongest overall negative correlations (-0.118/-0.156, -0.110/-0.090, and -0.091/-0.006 for high, medium, and low effort settings respectively) in MT and TS evaluation, while DeepSeek-based models demonstrate weaker correlations (-0.015/-0.054 for DeepSeek R1, -0.042/0.026 for R1 LLaMa 70B, and -0.003/-0.122 for R1 Qwen 32B). Some models in certain settings exhibit either non-significant correlations (R1 Qwen 32B on MT eval and o3-mini-low on TS eval) or weak positive correlation (R1 LLaMa 70B on TS eval).

LLM Score Correlations

We observe substantial differences in how reasoning token count correlates with model-assigned scores across architectures for both MT and TS evaluation. OpenAI models exhibit strong negative
correlations for MT and moderate for TS evaluation, with o3-mini-high showing the strongest relationship in MT (-0.4742), followed by o3-mini-medium (-0.4148) and o3-mini-low (-0.3466). The DeepSeek distillations (R1 LLaMa 70B and R1 Qwen 32B) show moderate correlations across MT and TS evaluation. In contrast, original DeepSeek R1 shows a weak correlation for MT and TS eval (0.0199/-0.010). This indicates that OpenAI models (and to some extend DeepSeek distilled models)
tend to engage in a more extensive thinking when they identify more errors
and assign lower quality scores.

6
Discussion

Our experimental results across MT evaluation, TS evaluation, and reasoning token counts analysis reveal several noteworthy patterns regarding the utility of reasoning capabilities in NLG evaluation.

Figure 3:
Comparison of evaluation quality of reasoning and non-reasoning LLMs in MT and TS evaluation.

Architecture-Specific Performance

The experimental results demonstrate clear performance differences between reasoning model architectures. As shown on Figure
3
, DeepSeek-R1 mostly underperforms compared to its non-reasoning counterpart (DeepSeek V3) in both MT and TS evaluation. In contrast, OpenAI o3-mini models demonstrates superior performance over their non-reasoning equivalent (GPT-4o-mini) in several evaluation scenarios, particularly for MT. This architecture-dependent performance demonstrates that reasoning capabilities alone do not guarantee improved evaluation quality, rather the specific implementation and post-training approach for enhancing reasoning capabilities determines effectiveness.

Our results reveal variable success in distilling reasoning capabilities for evaluation tasks. Task-Specific Reasoning Requirements

The experiment data reveals distinct patterns between tasks. While OpenAI models demonstrate advantages in MT evaluation (40.7% improvement for en-de), their performance advantage diminishes in summarization tasks (3.2% reduction in SummEval average compared to GPT-4o-mini). Correlation analysis further illustrates this task difference, with o3-mini models showing stronger efficacy of increased reasoning token count for MT evaluation than for TS evaluation across all reasoning effort settings. MT evaluation appears to benefit more from the particular reasoning approach employed by o3-mini models.

These findings demonstrate that reasoning capabilities improve evaluation performance very selectively, with efficacy dependent on architectural implementation, task-specific alignment, and reasoning intensity. The superior performance of o3-mini models, particularly in MT evaluation, suggests that they incorporate post-training elements better suited for translation evaluation tasks, potentially including broader multilingual exposure or specific fine-tuning for translation assessment. Future work should investigate targeted tuning to reasoning approaches specifically designed for NLG evaluation contexts, as well as the performance of other reasoning model architectures.

7
Conclusion

This study presents the first systematic comparison of reasoning versus non-reasoning LLMs as NLG evaluators across MT and TS tasks. Our analysis demonstrates that the impact of reasoning capabilities on evaluation quality varies significantly by model architecture, with distinct efficacy patterns emerging across different evaluation dimensions.

The OpenAI o3-mini model consistently outperforms its non-reasoning counterpart on MT evaluation metrics, improving correlation with human judgments by 40.7% for en-de translation. However, DeepSeek-R1 and its distilled versions generally underperform compared to their non-reasoning counterparts across most evaluation tasks, with notable exceptions in TS consistency evaluation, where DeepSeek-R1 shows a 70.7% improvement over DeepSeek V3. These architecture-dependent patterns demonstrate that reasoning capabilities utility for NLG evaluation depends on their specific implementation and integration with model.

Our correlation analysis reveals two important patterns: first, a low-to-medium correlation between reasoning token count and evaluation error (supporting test-time scaling hypothesis) for OpenAI o3-mini on MT evaluation and R1-Distill-Qwen-32B on TS evaluation, with minimal correlation for other models; second, a strong negative correlation between reasoning token usage and model-predicted quality scores across all models, showing that models dedicate substantially more reasoning tokens when they identify quality issues in the evaluated text.

Regarding distillation efficacy, medium-sized models (R1 Qwen 32B) preserve most evaluation capabilities of the original model despite having 21 times fewer parameters. R1 LLaMa 70B shows mixed results across different evaluation dimensions, while smaller 8B parameter models exhibit substantial performance degradation, losing over 50% of evaluation quality. This confirms that effective distillation of evaluation-relevant reasoning requires sufficient model capacity.

These findings highlight that reasoning capabilities can improve NLG evaluation, but their effectiveness depends on model architecture and implementation. Future work should focus on specifically aligning reasoning approaches with evaluation requirements through fine-tuning and architectural modifications that enhance reasoning application to NLG evaluation.

Limitations

There are several limitations associated with the evaluation methodology we used. Future work may address them.

1.

We make an assumption that the GPT-4o-mini model is the most likely non-reasoning equivalent, by capabilities and/or size, to the reasoning model o3-mini. At the same time, there is no public confirmed information about the size of either of these models, thus this cannot be verified.

2.

While the inference API of o3-mini allows specifying the reasoning effort (either ‘low’, ‘medium’ or ‘high’), there is no such option for DeepSeek R1 and its distilled variants. Further studies might examine R1 performance at different reasoning efforts by implementing forced generation of the closing ‘</think>’ token upon generating a pre-determined amount of reasoning tokens.

3.

We have not touched the Hybrid Reasoning LLMs, such as Anthropic Claude 3.7 or most recently released Qwen3. The hybrid approach in those models allows to turn on or off the generation of reasoning trace before providing the result via a special command tokens. Those models could potentially exhibit smaller discrepancy in quality between reasoning/non-reasoning, as the post-training phase of those models is unified.

Potential Risks

Due to reasoning LLMs being a very recent development, little research exists regarding the safety of the reasoning process. Therefore, it is possible (though not observed in our experiment) that reasoning traces of LLMs used in prompt-based evaluation might show certain evidence of misalignment or bias. This could influence generated quality assessments. Further research is necessary to identify and mitigate such risks in reasoning LLMs.
