Picosecond Wireless Synchronization with Entangled Photons via Grid-Based Quantum Coverage in Indoor Optical Systems
Hossein Safi, Member, IEEE, Mohammad Taghi Dabiri, Mazen Hasna, Senior Member, IEEE, Iman Tavakkolnia, Senior Member, IEEE, and Harald Haas, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a novel entanglement-assisted synchronization framework for indoor optical wireless systems based on grid-based beam steering. A central transmitter equipped with a spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) source emits time-energy entangled photon pairs, directing user photons toward spatially defined grids based on estimated user positions, while retaining reference photons for timestamping. The room is partitioned into multiple beam-aligned regions, and photon reception probability is analytically modeled using local coordinate transformations and Gaussian beam optics. To address the inherent sparsity and randomness of photon detection, we develop a robust two-stage synchronization algorithm that performs sparse bit-pattern matching and timestamp averaging to estimate timing offsets. Monte Carlo simulations are then performed to evaluate the impact of synchronization duration, grid resolution, and photon-pair generation rate on synchronization accuracy. Results show that finer grid configurations and optimal photon-pair rates significantly reduce synchronization error, achieving sub- 10 10 ps timing accuracy within 1 ​ ms 1\,\text{ms} of synchronization time. The proposed approach enables scalable, high-precision quantum synchronization in wireless indoor environments, laying the groundwork for future joint quantum communication, sensing, and positioning systems.

Introduction

The convergence of quantum information science and wireless communication is driving the development of a new class of systems that promise secure communication, high-precision sensing, and distributed quantum computing
[
1
,
2
]
. Among these, optical wireless communication (OWC) systems, which use light for high-speed data transfer, have emerged as a promising platform for enabling quantum-enhanced applications, including quantum key distribution (QKD), quantum sensing, and local-area quantum networks
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. A fundamental requirement across nearly all such applications is the ability to distribute and detect entangled or single photons with extremely high temporal precision, often at the picosecond scale
[
7
]
.

This stringent requirement for temporal precision becomes especially critical in protocols such as QKD and entanglement swapping, where synchronization between a central node and multiple users must reach the picosecond level. In these scenarios, the identification of correlated photon detection events depends on extremely narrow coincidence windows. Even minor clock offsets or uncertainties in propagation delay can significantly degrade system performance, increasing error rates and undermining quantum coherence
[
8
,
9
]
. As a result, time synchronization is not merely an engineering challenge but a fundamental physical necessity in quantum communication systems.

Given the critical role of synchronization in quantum systems, the field of quantum clock synchronization (QCS) has evolved along two primary paths. The first focuses on long-haul fiber-optic quantum links, where weak quantum signals must coexist with classical data traffic
[
10
,
11
]
. These systems employ techniques such as wavelength division multiplexing and polarization management to mitigate crosstalk and dispersion effects. Recent advances have further extended this, where quantum-enhanced two-way time transfer leverages the strong temporal correlations in energy-time entangled photons to achieve precise synchronization stability
[
12
,
13
]
. The second path targets long-distance free-space quantum links, including ground-to-satellite communication, where atmospheric turbulence, solar background radiation, and Doppler shifts pose significant challenges. Solutions in this domain typically involve adaptive optics, narrowband filtering, time transfer, and probabilistic error correction
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. While both approaches are technically sophisticated, their assumptions and mitigation strategies are highly specialized and not directly applicable to indoor environments.

While existing QCS approaches have focused on long-haul fiber or free-space links, a parallel line of development in the classical domain has explored scalable indoor OWC architectures. Among these, the Grid-of-Beams (GoB) approach has emerged as a practical solution for supporting mobile users
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. In a GoB system, a dense array of static, narrow laser beams provides full spatial coverage, and user mobility is managed through digital handovers between adjacent grid cells, thereby eliminating the need for continuous beam tracking
[
17
,
19
]
. However, these systems are inherently quantum-agnostic. Each handover alters the optical path length, disrupting prior timing calibration. Moreover, current GoB architectures lack mechanisms to rapidly re-establish the picosecond-level synchronization required by quantum protocols. More broadly, existing QCS schemes assume stable links and long integration times, making them unsuitable for dynamic indoor environments characterized by frequent handovers and ambient lighting noise. This highlights a critical gap at the intersection of quantum synchronization and mobile indoor OWC that must be addressed to enable practical quantum networking in real-world settings.

To bridge this gap, we propose the first integrated framework for entanglement-assisted picosecond synchronization tailored to mobile indoor optical wireless environments. Our approach leverages the temporal correlations of entangled photon pairs in conjunction with a scalable optical delivery system to enable precise synchronization. This method allows for accurate estimation of timing parameters without relying on a shared clock or fixed spatial configuration, making it well-suited for dynamic, mobile scenarios.

To enable practical deployment in dynamic indoor environments, we build upon existing and experimentally validated GoB architectures, originally developed for classical OWC systems to provide full indoor coverage and support user mobility
[
17
,
22
]
. The GoB discretizes space into fixed beams, each targeting a predefined coverage cell, thereby simplifying link acquisition and handover. We extend this architecture by embedding a quantum-native synchronization layer, enabling support for entanglement-based protocols. We complement this architecture with a two-stage synchronization algorithm designed to operate over sparse and noisy single-photon detection streams. The first stage performs coarse alignment using bit-pattern matching, while the second refines synchronization through timestamp averaging. To support system design and performance evaluation, we also develop a comprehensive analytical model that captures photon-pair generation statistics, Gaussian beam propagation, user position uncertainty, and timing jitter from both the source and detectors.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to present and analyze in detail an entanglement-assisted synchronization framework tailored to a grid-based indoor OWC system. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a complete system architecture for high-precision timing in indoor environments, integrating a grid-based optical delivery system with an entanglement-based synchronization protocol. This design directly addresses the challenges of user mobility and line-of-sight link maintenance.

•

We develop a detailed, end-to-end analytical model that captures the key physical phenomena. This includes the Poisson statistics of the quantum unit, Gaussian beam optics for photon propagation, the impact of user positioning errors on reception probability, and a rigorous treatment of both source and detector timing jitter.

•

We design a practical algorithm specifically to handle the sparse and noisy data characteristic of single-photon channels. The algorithm performs sparse bit-pattern matching for coarse slot-level alignment, followed by timestamp averaging over high-confidence matched events to achieve fine-grained, picosecond-level precision.

•

Through Monte Carlo simulations, we evaluate the performance of our framework under realistic conditions. We demonstrate that sub-
10
picosecond synchronization accuracy is achievable within a
1
ms timeframe. Our results quantify the significant impact of spatial grid resolution and reveal the existence of an optimal photon-pair generation rate that balances signal availability against multi-photon ambiguity.

Thus, this work provides a foundational step toward building practical, scalable, and high-precision quantum networks in local indoor settings, bridging quantum timing principles with proven classical networking architectures.

II
System Model

We consider an indoor quantum synchronization system based on a grid-structured optical wireless delivery architecture that utilizes time-energy entangled photon pairs. A central quantum unit, mounted on the ceiling, contains a spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) source that continuously generates entangled photons. In each pair, one photon remains at the central unit as a precise timing reference, while the other is directed toward the user through one of
N_{g}
spatially distinct optical wireless transmission paths. Each transmission path corresponds to a fixed illumination region (grid) on the indoor surface, effectively dividing the room into
N_{g}
non-overlapping zones. In the following subsections, we provide a detailed description of the system model and the parameters considered in this paper. Meanwhile, a summary of the notations used is presented in Table
I
.

The receiver structure includes a circular aperture and SPAD array aligned to detect the photon and record its arrival time for synchronization. Local

II-A

Geometric Configuration and Coordinate Framework

We consider a rectangular indoor environment with length
L
, width
W
, and height
H
. The quantum transmitter unit is mounted at the center of the ceiling, which is chosen as the origin of the global 3D coordinate system. Throughout this work, we denote global positions using the coordinate system
(x,y,z)
, with the transmitter located at
\mathbf{p}_{\text{tx}}=[0,0,0]
.
The room is partitioned into
N_{g}=N_{x}\times N_{y}
non-overlapping grid zones, where
N_{x}
and
N_{y}
are the number of divisions along the
x
and
y
axes, respectively. Each grid is centered at a position
\mathbf{p}_{g}^{(i,j)}
on the horizontal coverage plane
z=-H+h_{\text{cov}}
, where
1\leq i\leq N_{x}
and
1\leq j\leq N_{y}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{g}^{(i,j)}=\left(\left(i-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta x-\tfrac{L}{2},\;\left(j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta y-\tfrac{W}{2},\;-H+h_{\text{cov}}\right),

(1)

with
\Delta x=\frac{L}{N_{x}}
and
\Delta y=\frac{W}{N_{y}}
being the width and height of each grid zone. Here,
h_{\text{cov}}
denotes the vertical distance above the floor at which optical coverage is optimized, i.e., the height where the beam footprint is best aligned with the grid layout. This coverage design follows the 3D optical model and beam divergence analysis discussed in
[
21
]
.

Each optical wireless transmission path is aligned to direct a photon beam from
\mathbf{p}_{\text{tx}}
to the center of a corresponding grid. The direction vector of the beam toward grid
(i,j)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}=\mathbf{p}_{g}^{(i,j)}-\mathbf{p}_{\text{tx}}=\mathbf{p}_{g}^{(i,j)}.

(2)

To ensure that the entire grid at
h_{\text{cov}}
is covered by the optical beam, the divergence angle
\phi_{\text{div}}^{(i,j)}
of each beam is adjusted based on its propagation vector
\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}
.
The divergence angle of each beam is tuned to ensure complete coverage at the receiver plane
z=h_{\text{cov}}
. For further geometric considerations and coverage performance details, refer to
[
21
]
.

Let
\mathbf{p}_{u}=[x_{u},y_{u},z_{u}]
denote the true position of the user, and let
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{u}=[\hat{x}_{u},\hat{y}_{u},\hat{z}_{u}]
represent the estimated position available to the system. The estimation is subject to random errors modeled as independent Gaussian noise in each coordinate. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{u}=x_{u}+n_{x},\quad\hat{y}_{u}=y_{u}+n_{y},\quad\hat{z}_{u}=z_{u}+n_{z},

(3)

where
n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{p}^{2})
are zero-mean Gaussian random variables with identical variance
\sigma_{p}^{2}
. These represent the initial spatial uncertainty in user localization arising from sensing or tracking limitations.
Given
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{u}
, the system activates the optical transmission path corresponding to the grid
(i^{\star},j^{\star})
that minimizes the angular deviation between the beam direction
\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}
and the estimated user vector. Formally, the selected grid is given by

\displaystyle(i^{\star},j^{\star})=\arg\min_{(i,j)}\;\theta_{\text{err}}^{(i,j)}=\arg\min_{(i,j)}\;\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}}{\|\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}\|\|\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}\|}\right),

(4)

where
\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{u}=\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{u}-\mathbf{p}_{\text{tx}}
is the direction vector from the transmitter to the estimated user location. This selection ensures that the beam is directed toward the grid whose nominal axis most closely aligns with the estimated user position in 3D space.

Moreover, to simplify the analysis of photon propagation, we transform all global positions from the original
(x,y,z)
coordinate system to a local coordinate frame
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
aligned with the beam direction
\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}
. In this frame, the
z^{\prime}
-axis is parallel to
\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}
, and the
x^{\prime}
-
y^{\prime}
plane is perpendicular to it.
The user position is then transformed from the global coordinate system to the beam-aligned local frame using

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{u}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}^{(i,j)}\left(\mathbf{p}_{u}-\mathbf{p}_{\text{tx}}\right),

(5)

where
\mathbf{p}_{u}^{\prime}=[x_{u}^{\prime},y_{u}^{\prime},z_{u}^{\prime}]^{\top}
represents the user coordinates in the local
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})
frame, where
[\cdot]^{\top}
denotes the transpose operator. The construction of the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}^{(i,j)}
is provided in Appendix
A
.

II-B

Transmitter Architecture and Photon Pair Emission

The central transmitter unit employs a SPDC source to generate time-energy entangled photon pairs. Each generated pair consists of two photons: one designated as the
reference photon
and the other as the
user photon
. The reference photon is routed to a time-stamping detection module co-located at the transmitter, while the user photon is directed toward a spatially-resolved optical output based on the user’s estimated position, as shown in Fig.
1
.

A SPDC source is widely adopted for quantum communication and synchronization due to its scalability and compatibility with free-space optics
[
23
]
. The system is driven by a periodic train of pump pulses, each with temporal width
\tau_{p}
. We model the photon-pair generation time
t_{g}
as a Gaussian random variable centered at the pump pulse peak
t_{p}
, with standard deviation
\sigma_{t}
, as follows
[
24
]

\displaystyle t_{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(t_{p},\sigma_{t}^{2}).

(6)

This temporal uncertainty arises from the finite spectral bandwidth of the pump and the phase-matching conditions in the nonlinear crystal
[
25
]
. The jitter
\sigma_{t}
represents an intrinsic timing error that limits the accuracy of photon-level synchronization. Typical values of
\sigma_{t}
range from tens to hundreds of femtoseconds, depending on the pump-cavity configuration and spectral filtering
[
26
]
.
To ensure slot-level temporal isolation and mitigate timing ambiguity, each quantum slot is defined to be longer than the pump pulse duration, i.e.,
T_{qs}>\tau_{p}
. Specifically, the slot duration is modeled as

\displaystyle T_{qs}=t_{\text{guard}}^{(1)}+\tau_{p}+t_{\text{guard}}^{(2)},

(7)

where
t_{\text{guard}}^{(1)}
and
t_{\text{guard}}^{(2)}
denote pre- and post-pulse guard intervals, respectively. These intervals are selected to absorb both the temporal jitter
\sigma_{t}
of the SPDC source and the detection-time uncertainty
\sigma_{d}
of the reference detector (e.g., SPAD).

Given the spontaneous nature of SPDC, the number of photon pairs generated within each quantum slot is random. Specifically, the emission statistics follow a Poisson distribution
[
27
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{t}=k)=\frac{\mu_{t}^{k}}{k!}e^{-\mu_{t}},\quad\text{where }\mu_{t}<1.

(8)

Here,
\mu_{t}
denotes the mean number of photon pairs per slot.

Furthermore, each photon in the pair encodes a quantum bit (qubit)
|\psi\rangle
in the polarization basis, defined as

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle,\quad\text{with }|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1,

(9)

where
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
represent the horizontal and vertical polarization states, respectively. The entangled state of the pair can be written as:

\displaystyle|\Psi^{-}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle_{r}|1\rangle_{u}-|1\rangle_{r}|0\rangle_{u}\right)

(10)

where subscripts
r
and
u
denote the reference and user photons, respectively.

To direct the user photon toward the appropriate receiver location, the transmitter utilizes a dynamic beam selection mechanism based on estimated user coordinates
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{u}
. As described in the previous subsection, the system activates the optical beam corresponding to the selected grid, minimizing the angular deviation between the estimated user direction and the grid axis (see (
4
)).

The switching mechanism at the transmitter steers the user photon to the beam path
\mathbf{d}^{(i^{\star},j^{\star})}
via a high-speed optical switch array. Let
\mathcal{S}_{ij}
denote the switch corresponding to path
(i,j)
. At each quantum slot, only one
\mathcal{S}_{ij}
is activated. Simultaneously, the reference photon is routed internally to a time-reference block, where it is detected by a high-resolution single-photon detector (e.g., SPAD). The detection time
t_{r}
is recorded and serves as a synchronization anchor for the corresponding user photon, allowing for precise time-of-arrival (ToA) estimation upon reception.

II-C

Receiver Architecture and Photon Capture

The user-side receiver, shown on the left-hand side of Fig.
1
, is designed to detect entangled photons transmitted via spatially directed beams from the central transmitter. The core component of the receiver is a circular aperture that captures incident photons based on their spatial alignment with the selected transmission beam. Each photon emitted toward the user is received with a probability
P_{\text{recept}}^{(i,j)}
, which depends on the alignment between the beam path
(i,j)
and the user’s actual position. The analytical model for
P_{\text{recept}}^{(i,j)}
is developed in the following section.

After entering the aperture, the photon propagates through a passive optical path that directs it toward a detection module comprising an array of SPADs. These detectors are polarization-resolving and are used to determine the quantum state of the incoming photon based on its polarization encoding
[
28
]
. Each SPAD channel corresponds to a measurement basis (e.g., horizontal or vertical), allowing the receiver to recover the transmitted qubit from the spatially separated entangled photon pair.

Beyond merely detecting the presence of a photon, SPADs employed in this system are capable of precise time-tagging. Upon photon impact, an avalanche current is triggered within the diode, and the timestamp
t_{d}
corresponding to the avalanche peak (or threshold crossing) is recorded.
In practice, SPADs exhibit inherent temporal jitter due to both carrier avalanche buildup and electronic readout delays. This timing uncertainty is typically modeled as a Gaussian random variable
\sigma_{d}

[
29
]
, often referred to as the detector timing jitter

\displaystyle t_{d}=t_{a}+n_{d},\quad n_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{d}^{2}),

(11)

where
t_{a}
is the actual photon arrival time, and
t_{d}
is the recorded detection time. The value of the standard deviation parameter,
\sigma_{d}
, depends on the SPAD’s material and circuit design, and typically ranges from
20
ps to
100
ps in modern systems
[
30
,
31
]
.

III
Spatial Modeling of Photon Reception via Aperture

The photon beam emitted by the transmitter is directed toward the center of the selected grid
(i,j)
, following the predefined transmission vector
\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}
in (
2
).
Although the photon nominally propagates along
\mathbf{d}^{(i,j)}
, quantum diffraction causes its presence to be distributed laterally in the
x^{\prime}
-
y^{\prime}
plane, with the probability density decaying radially from the beam axis
[
32
]
. This spatial uncertainty is captured by modeling the photon’s transverse wavefunction as a Gaussian mode
[
33
]
. Specifically, the spatial probability density in the orthogonal plane at axial distance
z_{u}^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\text{spatial}}^{(i,j)}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\frac{2}{\pi w^{2}(z_{u}^{\prime})}\exp\left(-\frac{2(x^{\prime 2}+y^{\prime 2})}{w^{2}(z_{u}^{\prime})}\right),

(12)

where
w(z_{u}^{\prime})
denotes the beam width at distance
z_{u}^{\prime}
along the propagation axis and is expressed as

\displaystyle w(z_{u}^{\prime})=w_{0}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{z_{u}^{\prime}\lambda}{\pi w_{0}^{2}}\right)^{2}},

(13)

with
w_{0}
being the beam waist (minimum spot size at the focus), and
\lambda
the photon wavelength.

The receiver aperture, centered at the user’s position
\mathbf{p}_{u}^{\prime}=[x_{u}^{\prime},y_{u}^{\prime},z_{u}^{\prime}]^{\top}
in the beam-aligned coordinate frame, is assumed to lie in the plane orthogonal to the beam axis, i.e., the
x^{\prime}
-
y^{\prime}
plane. The aperture captures photons based on their spatial distribution relative to the beam center.
Given the radial symmetry of the beam in the
x^{\prime}
-
y^{\prime}
plane, the photon detection probability depends on the radial displacement of the user from the beam center. This displacement is quantified by the lateral components
x_{u}^{\prime}
and
y_{u}^{\prime}
of the user’s position in the local frame.

Let
\rho_{u}^{\prime}=\sqrt{(x_{u}^{\prime})^{2}+(y_{u}^{\prime})^{2}}
denote the radial offset of the receiver center from the beam axis. The photon detection probability is obtained by integrating the spatial density function (
12
) over the receiver aperture, which is modeled as a circular disk of radius
r_{a}
centered at
(x_{u}^{\prime},y_{u}^{\prime})
in the
x^{\prime}
-
y^{\prime}
plane. The detection probability is thus given by

\displaystyle P_{\text{recept}}^{(i,j)}=\iint_{\mathcal{A}_{u}}P_{\text{spatial}}^{(i,j)}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\,dy^{\prime},

(14)

where
\mathcal{A}_{u}
denotes the circular region defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{u}=\left\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:(x^{\prime}-x_{u}^{\prime})^{2}+(y^{\prime}-y_{u}^{\prime})^{2}\leq r_{a}^{2}\right\}.

(15)

When the beam width at the receiver plane is much larger than the aperture size, i.e.,
w(z_{u}^{\prime})>4r_{a}
, the beam’s intensity doesn’t change much across the tiny area of the aperture
[
34
]
. Thus, the spatial variation of the probability density across the receiver area becomes negligible. In this regime, the integral in (
14
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle P_{\text{recept}}^{(i,j)}\approx P_{\text{spatial}}^{(i,j)}(x_{u}^{\prime},y_{u}^{\prime})\cdot\pi r_{a}^{2},

(16)

where
P_{\text{spatial}}^{(i,j)}(x_{u}^{\prime},y_{u}^{\prime})
is evaluated using (
12
).

IV
Synchronization Model

In order to perform entanglement-based synchronization between the transmitter and the user receiver, we first define a discrete time frame consisting of a sequence of
N_{s}
quantum time slots. Each slot is intended to carry a potential photon-pair emission by the source, with time-stamping performed by the reference unit located at the transmitter side.

IV-A

Slot-Based Reference Sequence Construction

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{1,2,\dots,N_{s}\}
denote the set of all quantum slots, and for each slot
k\in\mathcal{S}
, let
N_{t}[k]
denote the number of photon pairs generated. As described earlier in (
8
), this number follows a Poisson distribution with mean
\mu_{t}<1
.
For synchronization purposes, the reference unit maintains two sequences as follows:

•

A data detection sequence
\mathbf{d}_{r}=[d_{r}[1],d_{r}[2],\dots,d_{r}[N_{s}]]

•

A timestamp sequence
\mathbf{t}_{r}=[t_{r}[1],t_{r}[2],\dots,t_{r}[N_{s}]]

where
d_{r}[k]\in\{0,1,\texttt{fail}\}
indicates the detection status of a valid reference photon in slot
k
, and
t_{r}[k]
is the recorded timestamp when
d_{r}[k]=1
.
A quantum time slot
k
is marked as
fail
under the two following conditions:

•

No photon pair was generated (
N_{t}[k]=0
)

•

More than one photon pair was generated (
N_{t}[k]\geq 2
)

Accordingly, only those slots for which
N_{t}[k]=1
are considered valid and eligible for synchronization. The rest are marked as unreliable. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle d_{r}[k]=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }N_{t}[k]=1\text{ and photon detected},\\
0,&\text{if }N_{t}[k]=1\text{ and no photon detected},\\
\texttt{fail},&\text{if }N_{t}[k]=0\text{ or }N_{t}[k]\geq 2.\end{cases}

(17)

Due to physical limitations of the SPAD detector at the reference unit, the timestamp
t_{r}[k]
is not the exact emission time but includes additive Gaussian noise. Specifically, for each slot
k
with
d_{r}[k]=1
, the recorded time is

\displaystyle t_{r}[k]=t_{g}[k]+n_{r}[k],\quad n_{r}[k]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{d}^{2})

(18)

where
t_{g}[k]
is the true photon generation time for slot
k
, and
n_{r}[k]
models the detection-time jitter at the reference SPAD.
It is important to note that the generation time
t_{g}[k]
itself is also a random variable due to intrinsic temporal uncertainty of the SPDC process, modeled in (
6
). Thus, we have

\displaystyle t_{g}[k]\sim\mathcal{N}(t_{p}[k],\sigma_{t}^{2})

(19)

where
t_{p}[k]
is the center of the pump pulse for slot
k
.
Consequently, the effective timestamp
t_{r}[k]
observed at the reference is subject to a compound noise

\displaystyle t_{r}[k]\sim\mathcal{N}(t_{p}[k],\sigma_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2})

(20)

which combines the SPDC generation jitter and the SPAD detection jitter. In contrast, the detection noise at the user’s SPAD is assumed independent and modeled separately.

IV-B

User-Side Detection Sequence

In each quantum time slot
k
, the number of signal photons
N_{s}[k]
arriving at the aperture of the user receiver follows a Poisson distribution. Its probability mass function is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{s}[k]=n)=\frac{(\mu_{s}^{(i,j)})^{n}}{n!}e^{-\mu_{s}^{(i,j)}},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots

(21)

where the mean value
\mu_{s}^{(i,j)}
depends on three factors: the mean photon-pair generation rate
\mu_{t}
at the transmitter (see (
8
)), the photon reception probability
P_{\text{recept}}^{(i,j)}
determined by spatial alignment (see (
16
)), and the photon detection efficiency
\eta_{d}
of the user’s SPAD detector. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mu_{s}^{(i,j)}=\mu_{t}\cdot P_{\text{recept}}^{(i,j)}\cdot\eta_{d}.

(22)

In addition to signal photons, the receiver also detects background photons that originate from ambient light sources, thermal noise, or detector dark counts. These events are independent of the entangled source and are modeled as a separate Poisson process with mean arrival rate
\mu_{b}
. Because background photons have random polarization states, each such photon has a probability of
1/2
to appear in either polarization-sensitive SPAD channel.
Therefore, the total number of photons impinging on the receiver in slot
k
is the sum of signal and background counts

\displaystyle N_{u}[k]=N_{s}[k]+N_{b}[k],

(23)

where
N_{s}[k]\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu_{s}^{(i,j)})
and
N_{b}[k]\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu_{b})
are independent.

At the user receiver, entangled photons are captured and registered over the same quantum slot structure
\mathcal{S}=\{1,2,\dots,N_{s}\}
as defined at the reference side. The receiver constructs a binary detection sequence
\mathbf{d}_{u}=[d_{u}[1],d_{u}[2],\dots,d_{u}[N_{s}]]
, where each element
d_{u}[k]\in\{0,1,\texttt{fail}\}
reflects the outcome of photon detection and polarization measurement during slot
k
.
Let us define the detection bit
d_{u}[k]
for slot
k
as follows:

•

d_{u}[k]=1
if exactly one signal photon is detected and its polarization is correctly resolved.

•

d_{u}[k]=0
if a signal photon is detected but the measured polarization is incorrect (due to overlap with background).

•

d_{u}[k]=\texttt{fail}
in all other cases: either no photon was detected or multiple photons caused measurement ambiguity.

A slot is most likely to result in a
fail
status due to the absence of any detected photons (i.e.,
N_{u}[k]=0
). However, even if a signal photon is present, a
fail
can also occur if:

1.

A background photon arrives simultaneously with a signal photon (
N_{s}[k]=1
,
N_{b}[k]\geq 1
)

2.

The background photon’s polarization differs from that of the signal, leading to a contradiction in polarization detection.

In quantum slots where
d_{u}[k]\in\{0,1\}
(i.e., photon detection is successful and not marked as
fail
), the user receiver records the photon arrival time
t_{u}[k]
. This timestamp captures three main factors:

•

The photon generation time
t_{g}[k]
at the transmitter (see (
19
))

•

A cumulative synchronization deviation
\tau_{\text{sync}}
, which includes both the physical propagation delay and any initial offset due to system synchronization mismatch

•

The SPAD detector’s timing jitter
n_{u}[k]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{d}^{2})
, modeled as Gaussian noise

Accordingly, for each valid detection slot
k
, the timestamp recorded at the user is given by

\displaystyle t_{u}[k]=t_{g}[k]+\tau_{\text{sync}}+n_{u}[k],\quad n_{u}[k]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{d}^{2}).

(24)

Here,
t_{g}[k]
is itself a Gaussian-distributed variable centered around the pump pulse time
t_{p}[k]
(as described in (
19
)). Thus, the total timing uncertainty at the receiver combines the intrinsic generation jitter
\sigma_{t}
and the detector-side jitter
\sigma_{d}
, resulting in the following distribution

\displaystyle t_{u}[k]\sim\mathcal{N}(t_{p}[k]+\tau_{\text{sync}},\sigma_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}).

(25)

Finally, the complete user-side timestamp sequence is represented as

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}_{u}=[t_{u}[1],t_{u}[2],\dots,t_{u}[N_{s}]],

(26)

where each element
t_{u}[k]
is defined only for slots where
d_{u}[k]\in\{0,1\}
, and marked as
fail
otherwise.

IV-C

Synchronization Recovery at the User

To establish a shared temporal reference between the transmitter and the user receiver, the system utilizes a classical auxiliary synchronization channel in addition to the quantum photon link. This classical link, implemented using technologies such as vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL)-based narrow optical beams, is made feasible by the structured beam-steering setup already present at the transmitter
[
19
]
. Through this channel, the transmitter broadcasts two sequences aligned with the quantum time slots, i.e., the binary detection sequence
\mathbf{d}_{r}=[d_{r}[1],\dots,d_{r}[N_{s}]]
and the associated timestamp sequence
\mathbf{t}_{r}=[t_{r}[1],\dots,t_{r}[N_{s}]]
, which represent detection outcomes and timing information of reference photons, respectively.

At the user side, upon receiving photons through the quantum aperture, the receiver builds its own detection sequence
\mathbf{d}_{u}=[d_{u}[1],\dots,d_{u}[N_{s}]]
and timestamp vector
\mathbf{t}_{u}=[t_{u}[1],\dots,t_{u}[N_{s}]]
, as previously described. However, due to the unknown synchronization offset
\tau_{\text{sync}}
between local and transmitter clocks, the timestamps in
\mathbf{t}_{u}
are shifted relative to those in
\mathbf{t}_{r}
. This alignment is further complicated by the fact that a significant portion of transmitted photons result in
fail
states at the receiver, primarily due to the combined effects of source-side timing jitter, SPAD detector noise, and interference from background photons. As a result, only a small subset of quantum slots provides reliable and temporally matched detection events, making accurate synchronization estimation more challenging under practical conditions.

Due to the random nature of photon detection, the binary sequence
\mathbf{d}_{u}=[d_{u}[1],\dots,d_{u}[N_{s}]]
constructed at the user side includes a large number of
fail
entries, corresponding to undetected or ambiguous photon events. In contrast, the reference sequence
\mathbf{d}_{r}=[d_{r}[1],\dots,d_{r}[N_{s}]]
generated at the transmitter is structurally aligned with the quantum slot framework, although it may also contain
fail
entries due to multi-photon emissions or null generation events. Nevertheless, the fail rate in
\mathbf{d}_{r}
is typically much lower than that observed in the user-side sequence
\mathbf{d}_{u}
. The primary challenge is that the valid bits in
\mathbf{d}_{u}
represent only a sparse and potentially misaligned sample of the original transmission pattern. Additionally, some valid detections at the user may be corrupted due to background-induced bit errors.

Considering the above-mentioned challenges, and to enable robust synchronization, we extract two auxiliary vectors from
\mathbf{d}_{u}
as follows

•

A valid data vector
\mathbf{v}_{u}=[d_{u}[i_{1}],d_{u}[i_{2}],\dots,d_{u}[i_{x}]]
, consisting of all elements where
d_{u}[i_{j}]\in\{0,1\}
,

•

A corresponding index vector
\mathbf{n}_{u}=[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{x}]
, recording the slot positions of these valid entries.

We then perform pattern matching between the observed vector
\mathbf{v}_{u}
and shifted versions of the transmitter-side reference vector
\mathbf{d}_{r}
, evaluating how well
\mathbf{v}_{u}
aligns with subsequences of
\mathbf{d}_{r}
. For each candidate integer shift
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, we compute a matching score as

\displaystyle S[\ell]=\sum_{j=1}^{x}\delta\left(d_{u}[i_{j}],d_{r}[i_{j}-\ell]\right),

(27)

where
\delta(a,b)=1
if
a=b
, and
0
otherwise. The optimal alignment offset is then estimated as

\displaystyle\ell^{\star}=\arg\max_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}S[\ell],

(28)

which identifies the temporal shift that yields the highest agreement between the user and reference detection sequences, ignoring
fail
entries.

Once the optimal shift
\ell^{\star}
is identified, the user constructs a refined index set
\mathcal{J}_{\text{match}}
by filtering those valid entries for which both transmitter and receiver have defined and matching binary values

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\text{match}}=

(29)

\displaystyle\left\{j\in\{1,\dots,x\}\;:\;d_{r}[i_{j}-\ell^{\star}]\in\{0,1\},\;d_{r}[i_{j}-\ell^{\star}]=d_{u}[i_{j}]\right\}.

This filtered subset ensures that the timestamp estimation only uses reliably matched positions, where both the quantum event occurred and the polarization was correctly inferred at the receiver.
The final synchronization offset is then estimated by computing the average temporal difference over the selected indices, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{\text{sync}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{J}_{\text{match}}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\text{match}}}\left(t_{u}[i_{j}]-t_{r}[i_{j}-\ell^{\star}]\right).

(30)

This two-stage matching and refinement process enables robust synchronization even in the presence of asymmetric detection failures, background-induced bit flips, and missing reference values. It ensures that only high-confidence matchings contribute to the final clock alignment.

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we introduced a grid-structured, entanglement-based quantum synchronization architecture for indoor wireless optical systems. The proposed framework leverages a central ceiling-mounted SPDC transmitter that steers time-energy entangled photons toward spatially targeted beams, each corresponding to a predefined spatial grid. By estimating the user’s location and aligning the optical beam accordingly, the system increases the likelihood of photon reception despite the probabilistic nature of quantum propagation and detection. A beam-local coordinate transformation was applied to analytically model the spatial photon reception probability, which plays a key role in determining synchronization reliability.
To overcome sparsity and randomness in photon arrivals, we proposed a robust two-stage synchronization recovery algorithm based on sparse bit-pattern matching and timestamp alignment. This method enables accurate estimation of clock offsets even in the presence of background noise, multiphoton events, and detector jitter.
Simulation results demonstrated the effectiveness of the approach under practical conditions. It was shown that increasing the synchronization sequence duration significantly reduces both synchronization failure probability and mean timing error. Furthermore, increasing grid resolution from
5\times 5
to
15\times 15
notably improves photon alignment and enhances synchronization accuracy, achieving sub-100 ps mean error within
1\,\text{ms}
of allocated time. Additionally, we evaluated the effect of photon-pair generation rate
\mu_{t}
and identified an optimal range that minimizes timing error by balancing between insufficient single-pair events and excess multiphoton emissions.

Future work will focus on extending the system to multi-transmitter environments to enhance spatial coverage, support user mobility, and improve robustness against obstructions. Additionally, a promising direction involves integrating synchronization with quantum communication, sensing, and positioning into a unified framework. Such a system could enable sub-centimeter positioning and sub-picosecond synchronization accuracy, far surpassing the capabilities of conventional classical techniques, and pave the way for secure, high-precision, quantum-enabled indoor wireless infrastructures.
