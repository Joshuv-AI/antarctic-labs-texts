Comparative characterization and anti Helicobacter pylori activity of green synthesized silver nanoparticles from red and yellow fruited Ficus carica L. leaf extracts
Authors: Hamda Al Jabri, Shaik Nyamathulla, Najihah Mohd Hashim, Lip Yong Chung, Saima Farooq, Zakira Naureen, Issa Sulaiman Al Amri, Sobia Ahsan Halim, Ajmal Khan, Mohammad Kamal Hossain, Ahmed Al Harrasi, Lebea N Nthunya, Amal Al Hinai, Zaaima Al Jabri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGreen synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using plant extracts offers an eco-friendly and sustainable approach for developing novel antimicrobial agents. Ficus carica L. leaf extract (FcLE), rich in bioactive phytochemicals, has potential as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. This study aimed to comparatively evaluate AgNPs synthesized from yellow- and red-fruited F. carica L. leaf extracts and assess their antibacterial activity against Helicobacter pylori.ResultsFcLE exhibited strong bioreduction capability, likely mediated by the transformation of phytochemicals (catechin, myricetin, and epigallocatechin gallate) into oxidized derivatives acting as reducing and capping agents. Optimal synthesis conditions were identified at 1% (v/v), 50 °C, and neutral pH, ensuring nanoparticle stability and preventing agglomeration. Morphological characterization revealed distinct differences, with yellow-fruited FcLE producing monodisperse, spherical AgNPs (average diameter 3.87 nm) with a narrow size distribution, whereas red-fruited FcLE generated larger, clustered particles (~ 40–50 nm). X-ray diffraction confirmed a crystalline biphasic silver/silver oxide structure, with higher metallic silver phase purity (60%) in yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs. Zeta potential values (up to − 50 mV) indicated strong colloidal stability due to electrostatic repulsion. Antibacterial assays demonstrated moderate inhibition against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus (11 ± 0.6 mm and 10 ± 0.5 mm, respectively). Against H. pylori, yellow- and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs produced mean inhibition zones of 20.00 ± 0.00 mm and 18.33 ± 0.58 mm, respectively, whereas the crude leaf extract showed no antibacterial activity under the tested conditions. Tetracycline produced a larger inhibition zone (49 mm) and served as the positive control. In silico docking further suggested that FcLE-derived compounds may interact with the urease enzyme.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that FcLE phytochemicals enable efficient synthesis of stable AgNPs at low concentrations and contribute to enhanced antibacterial activity. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibit promising In vitro antibacterial activity against H. pylori and warrant further investigation as plant-mediated nanomaterials for the management of H. pylori-associated gastric infections.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11671-026-05009-3.Keywords: Red and yellow Ficus carica L., Silver nanoparticles, H. pylori, Molecular docking, Urease

Introduction

The escalating prevalence of antimicrobial resistance represents a serious global public health concern, undermining the effectiveness of existing antibiotic therapies and necessitating the development of alternative therapeutic approaches. One particularly resilient pathogen is Helicobacter pylori, a gram-negative, microaerophilic bacterium linked to gastritis, peptic ulcers, and gastric carcinoma [1, 2]. Conventional treatment regimens, which typically combine proton pump inhibitors with antibiotics, are showing declining effectiveness due to increasing antimicrobial resistance and biofilm-associated persistence mechanisms [3–5]. Consequently, these challenges have accelerated the search for natural, effective, safer therapeutic alternatives.

Green synthesis of metallic nanoparticles has gained considerable attention as a viable research avenue. Green synthesis approaches employ plant extracts as environmentally-benign reducing and stabilizing agents, providing a sustainable alternative to conventional fabrication methods that rely on hazardous chemicals or energy-intensive processes [6–8]. The presence of plant-derived bioactive constituents, especially flavonoids and polyphenols, plays a crucial role in nanoparticle formation, thereby improving stability and enhancing biological functionality [9, 10]. Key synthesis parameters, including extract concentration, reaction temperature, and pH, play a critical role in determining nanoparticle size, morphology, crystallinity, and colloidal stability [8–10].

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are of particular interest due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, including effectiveness against antibiotic-resistant microorganisms [11, 12]. This has been attributed to multiple complementary mechanisms, including disruption of bacterial cell membranes, induction of oxidative stress, and interference with enzymatic activity and DNA replication [13, 14]. Previous studies have reported that plant-mediated AgNPs exhibit inhibitory activity against H. pylori, highlighting the potential of nanoparticle-based systems for the management of gastric infections [15–17]. Given the central role of urease in H. pylori survival within the acidic gastric environment, inhibition of this enzyme has been proposed as a key antibacterial mechanism, further supporting the relevance of nanoparticle-based interventions targeting H. pylori [17].

Among the plant species explored for nanoparticle biosynthesis, Ficus carica L. (fig) stands out due to its wide range of medicinal applications. The leaves of this species, traditionally used in both Mediterranean and Asian herbal medicine systems, are rich in bioactive constituents such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, and tannins, with resulting antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and gastroprotective activities [15, 16], including H. pylori inhibition [17]. F. carica L. is commonly cultivated in two major varieties, distinguished by red and yellow-fruited phenotypes which differ in terms of their phytochemical composition and pharmacological properties. However, comparative studies evaluating the performance of these two varieties in the green synthesis of AgNPs remain scarce, particularly in relation to their targeted antibacterial activity against H. pylori. Although numerous studies have reported the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles using plant extracts, comparative investigations examining the influence of intra-species phytochemical variability on nanoparticle formation and biological activity remain limited. In particular, little information is available regarding how different Ficus carica L. varieties affect nanoparticle physicochemical characteristics and antibacterial performance. Therefore, this study provides a comparative assessment of AgNPs synthesized from yellow- and red-fruited Ficus carica L. leaf extracts, contributing new insight into the relationship between plant phytochemical composition, nanoparticle properties, and anti-Helicobacter pylori activity. To address this knowledge gap, the objectives of this study were to: (i) synthesize and comparatively evaluate AgNPs derived from two F. carica L. varieties; (ii) characterize their physicochemical and structural properties; and (iii) assess their in vitro antibacterial activity against H. pylori.

Materials and methods

Materials

Leaves of Ficus carica L. from yellow- and red-fruited varieties were obtained from Muallim Hortigard and Nursery, Perak, Malaysia, and taxonomically authenticated by the Herbarium of Universiti Malaya (KLU), with voucher specimen numbers KLU50204 (yellow-fruited variety) and KLU50203 (red-fruited variety). Silver nitrate (AgNO₃), absolute ethanol (≥ 99.8%), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All chemicals and reagents employed in this study were of analytical grade. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm resistivity) generated using a Milli-Q Gradient purification system (Millipore, USA) was used throughout the experimental procedures. For antibacterial susceptibility testing, normal saline (Fisher Chemical, UK), Mueller–Hinton agar (Oxoid™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK), Columbia blood agar supplemented with 5% defibrinated blood, and sterile antibiotic discs (bioMérieux, France, and Liofilchem, Germany) were utilized. Bacterial inocula were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard using a CrystalSpec nephelometer (BD Diagnostics, USA). All experimental procedures were performed under aseptic conditions in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommendations [18].

Extract preparation

Dried leaves were finely ground into a uniform powder and accurately weighed. For aqueous extraction, 10 g of the powdered leaves were suspended in 100 mL of purified water and heated at 70–80 °C under continuous magnetic stirring for 45 min. This thermal extraction process was chosen because it promotes the release of key phytochemical constituents, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and tannins, which function as natural reducing and capping agents during AgNPs biosynthesis [19]. Following extraction, the mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature and sealed with parafilm to prevent contamination and solvent loss. The extract was then clarified by vacuum filtration to remove particulate matter, yielding a clear aqueous solution suitable for subsequent nanoparticle synthesis.

Nanoparticle synthesis and optimization of parameters

The AgNPs were synthesized through the dropwise addition of 10 mL of F. carica L. leaf extract (FcLE) to 90 mL of a 1 mM silver nitrate solution with continuous stirring. The reaction mixture was maintained at 50 °C and neutral pH for 2 h. Nanoparticle formation was initially indicated by a visible color change [8] and subsequently verified using ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry. Following completion of the reaction, the suspension was centrifuged at 40,000 rpm for 5 min using a Rotofix 32 A centrifuge (HettichLab, Germany) to separate the nanoparticles. Collected particles were washed four times with purified water and once with diluted ethanol to remove residual impurities. The purified nanoparticles were then dried in a VPH-6050 vacuum oven (CATEC Group, United Arab Emirates) at 60 °C for 6 h and stored for further characterization. To optimize synthesis conditions, three parameters were systematically varied: extract concentration (10%, 5%, and 1% v/v), reaction temperature (25 °C and 50 °C), and pH (7 and 9). These variables were selected based on their reported influence on nanoparticle size, morphology, and colloidal stability [8, 20].

Morphological characterization

UV–Vis spectroscopy was performed using a UV/VIS-Lambda 365 spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) over a wavelength range of 200–800 nm to monitor nanoparticle formation and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) characteristics.

The crystalline structure of AgNPs was analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (D8-ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Dried nanoparticle powders were mounted on a glass sample holder and scanned over a 2θ range of 10–100°.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed using a Bomem MB-100 spectrometer (ABB Bomem, Canada). Dried samples were mixed with spectroscopic-grade KBr, compressed into pellets, and analyzed over the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.

Zeta potential measurements were conducted using a Zetasizer ZS-90 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) at 25 °C. Samples were dispersed in deionized water prior to analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using a Carl Zeiss LIBRA® 120 microscope (Zeiss, Germany) operated at 120 kV. A drop of diluted nanoparticle suspension was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to air-dry before imaging.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was carried out using a JSM-6380LA microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Dried nanoparticle samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold prior to imaging.

LC–MS analysis was performed using a 6530 Accurate-Mass Q-TOF LC/MS system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in positive ion mode over an m/z range of 50–1000.

Assessment of antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of synthesized AgNPs against Escherichia coli, H. pylori, and Staphylococcus aureus was evaluated using an agar well diffusion assay technique adapted from previously reported methods [21, 22]. Clinical isolates of E. coli, S. aureus and H. pylori were cultured under microaerophilic conditions and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (≈ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) using sterile saline. The bacterial suspension was uniformly spread onto Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with 5% defibrinated blood to ensure confluent lawn growth. Wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically punched into the agar, and 100 µL of the synthesized AgNPs suspension (0.1 mg/mL) was added to each well.

Tetracycline (30 mg/mL) was used as positive control, while the negative control contained an equivalent concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide. Although clarithromycin or metronidazole are standard antibiotics for H. pylori susceptibility testing, tetracycline was used as the positive control in this study because it was available in the laboratory during experimentation. While not considered a first-line drug, tetracycline is a broad-spectrum antibiotic with inhibitory potency against H. pylori, thereby providing a valid benchmark for comparative assessment. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for E. coli and S. aureus for 18–24 h under aerobic conditions, and at 35–37 °C for H. pylori for 48–72 h under microaerophilic conditions. Zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters using a digital caliper. The antibacterial activity against H. pylori was evaluated in three independent biological experiments (n = 3) using freshly prepared bacterial cultures to ensure reproducibility. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was used as a computational approach to explore possible interactions between the proposed phytochemical compounds and the urease enzyme of H. pylori. The crystal structure of urease was taken from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (entry 4GOA) and imported into Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software, version 2024.06 (Chemical Computing Group, Canada). The QuickPrep MOE module was used to construct missing residues in urease, add hydrogen atoms, and perform partial charge calculations using the Optimized Potentials for Liquid Simulations All-Atom (OPLSAA) forcefield. Nickel atoms in the active site were charged as + 2 (Ni2+) with default parameters (mass = 58.6930, q = 2.0, R = 1.4170, eps = 0.1225, m = 12, n = 6). Compound structures were generated in ChemDraw™ (PerkinElmer) in MOL format and converted to three dimensions in MOE via an energy minimization process with a root mean square gradient set to 0.5 kcal/mol/Å and AM1-BCC charges. Afterwards, the MOE Triangle Matcher placement method was employed with the London dG scoring function, and 100 conformations were set for each ligand for docking. The best-docked conformation was selected based on the docking score and molecular interactions within the binding site.

Statistical analysis

Antibacterial activity measurements against H. pylori were performed in three independent biological experiments (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The average inhibition zone diameters were calculated from three independent biological experiments. An independent-samples Student’s t-test was used to compare the inhibition zone diameters between red- and yellow-derived AgNPs, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Nanoparticle synthesis and optimization of parameters

After the addition of leaf extracts to the AgNO3 solution, synthesis of AgNPs from yellow and red-fruited FcLE was visually confirmed by a distinct color change from light yellow to reddish-brown (Fig. 1a, b). Biosynthesis was time-dependent; the appearance of a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak in the 420–431 nm range confirmed nanoparticle formation, while the systematic increase in absorbance indicated progressive formation over time. The yellow-fruited variety exhibited higher absorbance compared to red-fruited FcLE (Fig. 1c, d), with yellow-fruited FcLE reaching a higher final absorbance after 120 min (1.1536 vs. 1.0717). Red-fruited FcLE displayed a slight red-shift to 430 nm, whereas yellow-fruited FcLE peaked at 421 nm. Both varieties exhibited rapid formation kinetics within the first 60 min, after which the rate of formation decreased, indicating near-completion of the reduction process.

(a, b) Colour change during AgNP formation after 120 min. (c, d) Time-dependent UV–Vis spectra of yFcLE-AgNPs and rFcLE-AgNPs, respectively. Insets show the spectra at 120 min, highlighting the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peaks at 420 nm and 431 nm

Optimal experimental conditions were determined. At a concentration of 10% v/v, both yellow and red-fruited FcLE-derived AgNPs exhibited nearly identical wide SPR peaks at around 430–435 nm with high absorption values (~ 1.0), indicating agglomerated and irregular sized particle formation. At 5% dilution, red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs exhibited an SPR peak at 436 nm, while yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs showed a blueshift to 412 nm. At 1% dilution, SPR peaks shifted to 417 nm and 407 nm for red and yellow-fruited FcLE, respectively, indicating the formation of smaller, monodisperse, spherical NPs. In terms of temperature, both extract varieties failed to provide sufficient activation energy for silver ion reduction at 25 °C, resulting in no peak formation. Reduction was successfully achieved at 50 °C. A pH of 9 resulted in higher absorbance; however, it produced a broad SPR peak, indicating large and agglomerated particles. As such, further experiments were conducted using 1% v/v concentration, neutral pH, and at 50 °C.

Morphological characterization

Using XRD, distinct diffraction peaks were observed at 2θ values of ~ 38.1°, 44.3°, 64.4°, and 77.5° (Fig. 2a, b), corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of the face-centered cubic structure of metallic silver. Semi-quantitative analysis using the reference intensity ratio method revealed that yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs were primarily composed of metallic silver (60%), whereas red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs show higher phase contribution from silver oxide (~ 57%) (Fig. 2c, d). Surface morphology was determined using SEM. Micrographs at ×5,000 and ×30,000 magnification showed a substrate embedded with nanoparticle clusters and irregularly shaped agglomerates, suggesting the NPs tend to coalesce rather than remain evenly dispersed (Fig. 3a, b). A zoomed view of the selected area of interest (Fig. 3c) indicated individual and aggregated AgNPs with diameters of approximately 4–5 nm. The aggregated nanoparticles showed strong inter-particle interactions, along with considerable variations in dimension and height (Fig. 3i, inset).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy showed distinct stretching vibrations corresponding to O–H (~ 3,400 cm-1), C-H/aldehyde (2,968 cm-1), C-N (1,388 cm-1), and carbohydrate/C-O (~ 1,045 cm-1) functional groups (Fig. 6a, b). There was a noticeable decrease in the intensity of these characteristic peaks due to nanoparticle formation (Fig. 6c, d), indicating that the corresponding functional groups were actively involved in the reduction of silver ions and subsequently interacted with the nanoparticle surface. Additional peaks were observed at 2,329 and ~ 670 cm-1, which correspond to the artifact and to metal oxide bond formation (Ag-O), respectively.

In terms of surface charge, both yellow and red-fruited FcLE-derived AgNPs demonstrated a single sharp peak of electrophoretic mobility distribution, centered near − 2 and − 3 μm·cm/Vs, respectively. Additionally, phase plots confirmed consistent electrophoretic behavior, with sharp, narrow peaks in mobility distributions suggesting a monodisperse surface charge population, particularly in the yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs formulation. Corresponding zeta potential values ranged from − 30 to -50 mV and − 25 to -45 mV for yellow and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs, respectively.

Based on the LCMS analysis, various phytochemicals were proposed to aid in the reduction and stabilization of yellow and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs (Tables 1 and 2), including flavonoids (i.e., myricetin, kaempferol), tannins (i.e., ellagitannin, proanthocyanidins), and phenolic acids (i.e. caffeic acid, gallic acid).

Assessment of antibacterial activity

At a concentration of 100 mg/mL, both yellow- and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs exhibited zones of inhibition of approximately 11 mm and 10 mm against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, respectively. In contrast, the antibacterial activity against Helicobacter pylori was evaluated in three independent biological experiments (n = 3). No inhibition was observed for the 5% DMSO negative control or the crude FcLE (100 mg/mL). However, AgNPs synthesized from red- and yellow-fruited F. carica leaf extracts (100 mg/mL) produced mean inhibition zones of 18.33 ± 0.58 mm and 20.00 ± 0.00 mm, respectively (Table 3). Representative photographs of the agar well diffusion assay are shown in Fig. 7. Tetracycline (30 mg/mL) produced a larger inhibition zone than the synthesized AgNPs and was used as the positive control for comparison.

Molecular docking

Proposed compounds were docked into the urease binding site to assess their interactions with the enzyme, with docking scores ranging from ˗8.76 to ˗2.66 kcal/mol (Table 4). Proanthocyanidin trimer and ellagitannin (Fig. 8a, b) emerged as the most effective binders, while caffeine yielded the lowest score, likely due to its smaller size. Compounds such as proanthocyanidin trimer, ellagitannin, procyanidin dimer, proanthocyanidin dimer, kaempferol glycoside, epigallocatechin gallate, and myricetin formed hydrogen bonds with urease residues. These molecules remained at the active site entrance, potentially blocking the access of the substrate, hindering urease function. Smaller molecules like pyroglutamic acid and caffeine penetrated more deeply, interacting with Ni2+ ions through metallic bonds.

Discussion

This study explored how phytochemical differences among F. carica L. varieties influenced both nanoparticle synthesis and the resulting biological activity, advancing environmentally sustainable nanomedicine strategies. By combining phytochemical variability with nanotechnological approaches, this research introduces a plant-based antibacterial platform that may be useful to further efforts to harness natural resources to manage antibiotic-resistant gastric pathogens. Collectively, these findings provide a scientific basis for the rational development of plant-mediated nanomaterials aimed at gastric infection control.

To begin, biosynthesis of AgNPs was confirmed via color change after the addition of leaf extracts to the AgNO3 solution. This transformation indicates the reduction of silver ions (Ag⁺) to elemental silver (Ag⁰), with the characteristic absorption arising from the excitation of SPR in the newly formed nanoparticles [23]. The appearance of prominent SPR peaks at 420 nm and 431 nm for yellow and red-fruited FcLE, respectively, further confirmed the formation of nanoparticles, which aligns with previously reported findings [23–25]. The yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs showed a higher apparent reducing capacity than the red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs, which may be associated with differences in phytochemical composition, particularly phenolic constituents, although further investigation would be required to confirm the exact mechanism. Above 120 min, yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs exhibited higher final absorbance, indicating a higher concentration of nanoparticles [8]. Red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs displayed a slight red-shift to 430 nm, which may be associated with larger particle size or increased aggregation, whereas yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs exhibited an SPR peak at 421 nm [25]. These differences in SPR peak position suggest variations in nanoparticle optical properties and formation behaviour. However, particle size distribution, morphology, and aggregation state were subsequently confirmed through TEM and SEM analyses. Both of the extract varieties exhibited rapid initial kinetics, with the majority of nanoparticle formation occurring within the first hour. The subsequent decline in the reaction rate suggests that the reduction of silver ions was nearing completion. This observation is consistent with the typical kinetics observed in plant-mediated synthesis of AgNPs [23, 24].

The leaves of F. carica L. are known to contain high levels of phenolic and flavonoid compounds, with caffeoylmalic acid, rutin, and quercetin reported as the predominant constituents [26–30]. It has been reported that these phenolic compounds exhibit a strong reducing ability, rapidly converting metal ions to their metallic form, whereas flavonoids act as capping and stabilizing agents for the reduced metals [27, 28]. In contrast to the red-fruiting variety, yellow-fruiting F. carica L. leaves contain higher concentrations of polyphenols and fewer flavonoids resulting in faster nucleation and nanoparticle formation [25]. The continuous increase in absorbance without significant peak broadening indicates controlled, stable formation of nanoparticles, demonstrating the effectiveness of FcLE for green AgNPs synthesis. In the present study, optimal experimental conditions were noted at 1% v/v at 50 °C and neutral pH for AgNPs stabilization while preventing agglomeration. The 1% (v/v) extract concentration indicates that even a low concentration of FcLE was sufficient to efficiently synthesize AgNPs. Temperature also played a critical role in the formation of elemental silver [8–10]. Similar observations regarding the influence of extract concentration, reaction temperature, and pH on nanoparticle formation, morphology, and colloidal stability have been reported in other plant-mediated AgNP synthesis systems, highlighting the importance of optimizing these parameters to obtain stable and uniformly dispersed nanoparticles [31]. Because alkaline pH typically favors the reduction process, rapid nucleation and growth likely led to a rapid reaction and subsequent particle agglomeration [32].

Crystallinity and phase purity of synthesized AgNPs were assessed using XRD which indicated distinct diffraction peaks. These peaks are in good agreement with the standard reference pattern for metallic silver (International Centre for Diffraction Data card #04-0783). These characteristic peaks confirm the formation of crystalline AgNPs [32]. The appearance of peaks around ~ 27°, ~ 32°, and 45.1° corresponds to the formation of silver oxide (AgO). The secondary phase formation can be attributed to partial surface oxidation, driven by the high surface reactivity of nanoparticles and the presence of oxygen-rich functional groups in the plant extracts, which promote silver oxide formation. Similar findings have been reported by other groups for the plant-based synthesis of AgNPs [33]. Furthermore, the reference intensity ratio method revealed that metallic silver was the major phase in yellow-fruited FcLE-AgNPs (60%), while red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs contained a comparatively greater fraction of silver oxide (~ 57%). This variation in the proportion of phase contributions can be attributed to differences in the phytochemical composition and the reducing abilities of the leaf extract constituents, which govern the extent of silver ion reduction [25].

Morphological analysis also revealed clear differences between the nanoparticles produced by the two leaf extract varieties. The AgNPs synthesized using yellow-fruited FcLE were predominantly small, spherical, with a narrow size distribution and an average diameter of ~ 3.87 nm, as confirmed by Gaussian fitting (R² ≈ 0.999). The consistent morphology, uniform size, and limited aggregation suggest effective control over nucleation and growth, resulting in high-quality, monodisperse nanoparticles suitable for optical, catalytic, or biomedical applications. In contrast, AgNPs from red-fruited FcLE were larger, more heterogeneous, and tended to aggregate, with diameters of 40–50 nm. Micrographs indicated close particle proximity, likely due to interparticle interactions such as van der Waals force, while internal contrast variation reflected differences in crystallinity or particle thickness. The aggregation observed in the micrographs likely arises from interparticle interactions due to incomplete surface passivation or solvent effects during synthesis.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy suggested the presence of phenolic compounds, carbonyl functionalities, and carbohydrate moieties, which are well known for their ability to reduce metal ions and stabilize the resulting nanoparticles [34–36]. These findings were also confirmed by the LCMS analysis. The detection of phytochemicals bound to the nanoparticle surface indicates added therapeutic potential, particularly through their inherent antioxidant and antibacterial activities. This enhances the suitability of the synthesized AgNPs as promising agents for biomedical applications [35, 36], including antiulcer treatment and targeted inhibition of H. pylori [37].

The surface charge of the synthesized AgNPs was also measured to evaluate colloidal stability. Both varieties exhibited a single, sharp peak in the electrophoretic mobility distribution, indicating effective electrostatic repulsion that prevents particle aggregation and promotes monodispersed and stable colloidal particles. The negative surface charge also suggests capping of the nanoparticles by adsorbed phytochemicals [38]. Corresponding zeta potential results were also consistent with earlier reports showing that values exceeding − 30 mV are characteristic of well-dispersed nanoparticles with long-term stability in aqueous environments [39]. Additionally, the stabilization effect attributed to phytochemicals corresponds with the findings of Ahmed et al. [7], who demonstrated that plant-derived reducing agents can provide both electrostatic and steric stabilization, thereby improving nanoparticle dispersion and enhancing their biological compatibility. Such properties are beneficial for in vitro studies, where nanoparticle stability is essential for reliable assessment of biological activity.

The LCMS analysis identified flavonoids (myricetin, kaempferol), tannins (ellagitannin, proanthocyanidins), and phenolic acids (caffeic acid, gallic acid) in both yellow and red-fruited FcLE. These phytochemicals participate in the reduction and stabilization of AgNPs, as evidenced by their transformation into oxidized products in the LCMS spectra. Initially, Ag+ ions are chelated, forming complexes with polyphenols, flavonoids and tannins. Typically, catechin and epigallocatechin gallate chelated Ag+ ions through -OH and C = O groups [40]. Oxidation of these functional groups generates Ag0 nuclei, which grow into nanoparticles through coalescence or Ostwald ripening. Phytochemicals such as tannin and flavonoids act as reducing and capping agents, surrounding the nanoparticle surface to prevent aggregation and modulate the release of AgNPs, thereby contributing to long-term stability and antibacterial activity. The formation of AgNPs in plant-mediated systems is a complex process involving multiple classes of phytochemicals. Based on the LC–MS and FTIR findings, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, tannins, and other bioactive metabolites may collectively contribute to silver ion reduction and nanoparticle stabilization. Both yellow- and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs exhibited mild antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus at 100 mg/mL, producing inhibition zones of approximately 11 mm and 10 mm, respectively. In contrast, FcLE-AgNPs demonstrated greater antibacterial activity against Helicobacter pylori. In three independent biological experiments, yellow- and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs produced mean inhibition zones of 20.00 ± 0.00 mm and 18.33 ± 0.58 mm, respectively, whereas no inhibition was observed for the crude leaf extract or the 5% DMSO negative control. Although tetracycline produced a larger inhibition zone, the synthesized AgNPs exhibited appreciable antibacterial activity against H. pylori under the experimental conditions. The greater susceptibility of H. pylori compared with E. coli and S. aureus may be attributed to differences in bacterial cell envelope structure and the increased sensitivity of H. pylori to oxidative stress and membrane disruption induced by silver nanoparticles.

Both yellow and red-fruited FcLE-AgNPs exhibited a similar range of antibacterial activities, which may be attributed to their smaller particle size and uniform morphology, as shown by TEM analysis. These properties likely enhance interaction with and penetration of bacterial membranes. Previous studies have reported similar antibacterial effects for green tea extract-derived AgNPs against H. pylori [20]. Various nanoparticle properties, including size, phytochemical content, and colloidal stability, are known to play a substantial role in determining antimicrobial efficacy [41]. AgNPs exert antimicrobial effects primarily through silver ion release and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress, membrane damage, protein dysfunction, and interference with nucleic acid synthesis in bacterial cells [13, 14, 35]. The pronounced activity observed against H. pylori may additionally be influenced by the small particle size and phytochemical-capped surface of the synthesized nanoparticles, which can enhance nanoparticle–bacteria interactions and facilitate penetration through the bacterial cell envelope. Furthermore, the phytochemicals identified by LC–MS may contribute synergistically to antibacterial activity through their intrinsic bioactive properties and their role in nanoparticle stabilization. The molecular docking analysis further suggests that selected FcLE-derived phytochemicals may interact with the urease enzyme of H. pylori, a key virulence factor responsible for bacterial survival in the acidic gastric environment [17]. This proposed mechanism is further supported by a recent study demonstrating that green-synthesized AgNPs exhibited significant urease inhibitory activity and may represent a promising strategy for targeting H. pylori-associated pathogenesis [42]. Although these findings provide mechanistic insight into the observed antibacterial activity, further experimental investigations, including membrane integrity, ROS generation, Ag⁺ release, and urease inhibition assays, are required to confirm the exact antibacterial mechanism. However, molecular docking provides only predictive insight into ligand–protein interactions; therefore, further experimental validation, such as urease inhibition assays, is required to confirm these findings. Despite these promising results, the present study is limited by the absence of in vivo validation, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination, and experimental confirmation of urease inhibition. Future studies should address these limitations to further validate the therapeutic potential and clinical applicability of the synthesized nanoparticles. In addition, microscopic evaluation of untreated and AgNP-treated bacterial cells using SEM or TEM would provide valuable insight into nanoparticle-induced morphological alterations and should be considered in future mechanistic investigations. Furthermore, assessment of bacterial surface charge following nanoparticle exposure through zeta potential measurements would contribute to a better understanding of nanoparticle–membrane interactions and possible alterations in bacterial membrane properties and should be considered in future studies. Additionally, quantification of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) following AgNP exposure would provide direct experimental evidence of oxidative stress-mediated antibacterial activity and should be considered in future mechanistic studies. In addition, the antibacterial evaluation was based on agar well diffusion assays performed in three independent biological experiments. Future studies should incorporate broth microdilution methods, multiple clinical isolates, and comprehensive dose–response analyses to further strengthen the evaluation of antibacterial efficacy.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the successful green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using leaf extracts from yellow- and red-fruited varieties of Ficus carica L. and highlighted the influence of varietal phytochemical composition on nanoparticle formation, physicochemical characteristics, and antibacterial activity. LC–MS and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy suggested that phytochemical constituents participated in the reduction and stabilization of AgNPs, while morphological characterization revealed that yellow-fruited FcLE produced smaller and more uniformly distributed nanoparticles than the red-fruited variety. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited good colloidal stability and significant antibacterial activity against Helicobacter pylori, with molecular docking analysis suggesting that selected phytochemicals may contribute to antibacterial effects through interactions with urease-related pathways. These findings provide new comparative insight into the role of phytochemical variability in plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis and support the potential application of FcLE-derived AgNPs as sustainable antibacterial nanomaterials for H. pylori-targeted research. Future studies should focus on mechanistic validation, cytotoxicity evaluation, long-term stability assessment, physicochemical optimization, and in vivo investigations to further explore their biomedical potential.
