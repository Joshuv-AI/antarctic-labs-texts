Introduction

The fetal period is the foundation of human life and has been widely addressed in law, medicine, ethics, and religious studies. Across many legal systems, personhood is recognized from early stages of fetal development, granting rights such as life, health, and protection [1]. In Islamic jurisprudence, the fetus is considered a respected being with both material and spiritual rights, including the right to life, lineage, and competent parents [2]. Neglecting these rights can result in adverse demographic and health consequences, such as illegal abortions and social harm [3]. In Iran’s legal framework, several laws address fetal protection, such as postponement of punishment for pregnant women, fasting exemptions, and restrictions on divorce during pregnancy [2, 4]. Additionally, the Constitution emphasizes dignity and protection of the fetus (Article 20). However, challenges such as illegal abortions, unnecessary cesarean sections, and inadequate awareness of fetal rights persist [5]. Previous studies have addressed fetal rights from religious, ethical, and legal perspectives; however, most of these investigations remain fragmented and lack an integrated framework. Although some international conventions such as the Convention on the Rights of the Child emphasize the protection of the child before and after birth, no comprehensive charter specifically dedicated to fetal rights has yet been developed [6]. This gap underscores the necessity of conducting a systematic review to identify fetal rights in both Iran and the global context, thereby providing a structured foundation for policymaking. The present study aims to systematically identify these rights and propose pathways for developing a national fetal rights charter.

Methods

This study was conducted using a scoping review method based on the five-stage framework of Arksey and O’Malley [7]. A systematic search was performed in domestic and international databases, including IranMedex, Magiran, SID, Scopus, ISI, PubMed, and Google Scholar, using relevant keywords. Inclusion criteria included Persian and English articles published between 2000 and 2024. After screening, 24 studies were selected for final analysis. Data were analyzed using MAXQDA software (version 10) and Braun and Clarke’s six-stage thematic analysis method [8].

Additionally, gray literature, such as reports from the Iranian Parliament Research Center, the Ministry of Health, affiliated universities, the United Nations, and the World Bank, was reviewed. All documents, including guidelines, regulations, circulars, laws, conference proceedings, books, articles, and research related to fetal rights in various countries, were included. Inclusion criteria comprised Persian and English articles published between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2024 (1380–1404 in the Persian calendar) addressing fetal rights. Exclusion criteria included studies in languages other than Persian or English, studies published after December 31, 2024, books, theses, and conference proceedings. The search was conducted by one team member, with article screening performed independently by two researchers, followed by comparison and discussion to resolve discrepancies.

A data extraction form was used, capturing author details, journal name, publication year, study location, study objective, study year, research type, data collection method, fetal rights, and policies surrounding fetal rights globally. Qualitative data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-stage thematic analysis method, including familiarization with data, initial coding, theme searching, theme review, theme definition, and report preparation [8]. The study aimed to identify fetal rights and related policies in Iran and globally through a scoping review, with all relevant policies extracted and coded from quantitative and qualitative studies.

Results

A total of 24 studies published between 2000 and 2024 met the inclusion criteria and addressed fetal rights and related policies. The initial database search yielded 186 records. After removing 62 duplicates and articles without full text, 124 remained for screening. In the second stage, 72 articles were excluded based on irrelevance to the topic after abstract review, and an additional 28 were removed because they did not pertain to fetal health. Ultimately, 24 studies were included in the final analysis (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to identify fetal rights in Iran and globally to inform the development of a fetal rights charter. A total of 59 fetal rights were identified, categorized into three domains: medical, ethical, and economic. Medical rights include the right to life, physical and mental health, appropriate medical care, growth, healthy nutrition, and protection from external harm. Ethical rights encompass dignity, respect, identity, a decent life, a balance between maternal and fetal rights, and sanctity. Economic rights include inheritance and maintenance. As this charter focuses on fetal health, the medical and ethical domains are discussed in detail.

The right to life is a fundamental human right [13]. Afshar noted that the fetus, as an entity capable of becoming a complete human, possesses inherent value and the right to life as a fundamental right [11]. International documents, such as the Convention on the Rights of the Child, explicitly call for protecting children’s rights “before and after birth,” emphasizing the recognition of the fetal right to life [6]. Pezeshki’s review also highlighted the fetus’s right to life, a decent life, and physical and mental health post-conception [2]. Beyond these descriptive findings, a broader synthesis shows that while the right to life is universally acknowledged, the extent of legal recognition and its enforcement vary considerably across contexts. Despite shared human rights principles, fetal rights laws vary significantly across legal systems due to cultural, religious, and legal differences. For instance, Onuegbulam’s 2021 study reported that the American Convention on Human Rights supports the fetus’s right to life and physical/mental integrity, while Nigerian criminal laws recognize the unborn as a living entity but do not grant independent legal personhood. In the UK, the fetus is considered part of the mother with no separate rights. In India, while laws do not explicitly protect fetal rights, supportive policies for pregnant women and fetuses exist through health and social programs [14]. These variations underscore the need to consider cultural and social contexts in developing fetal rights laws. Thus, one of the key findings of this review is that global discrepancies reflect not only legal traditions but also differing balances between maternal autonomy and fetal protection.

A key aspect of fetal health rights is mental health, which depends on the mother’s mental well-being during pregnancy [15]. Najafi’s study showed that adverse womb conditions can have lasting negative effects on fetal development and future child health [12]. Similarly, Rostami et al. found that the lived experiences of near-miss mothers reflect the emotional burden and psychological dimensions of maternal health during pregnancy [33]. Factors such as maternal substance use during pregnancy, despite its harmful consequences, lack criminal penalties in Iran’s legal system [12], whereas some legal systems criminalize such behavior [13]. The fetus also has a right to independent, appropriate medical care that considers its unique needs while aligning with maternal health. In cases of medical negligence, the fetus has the right to legal recourse, exercisable by parents or legal representatives [12]. The right to growth and development is also recognized in international and national laws [34]. Taleghan Ghaffari noted that optimal growth is a fetal right, and premature cesarean sections for non-medical reasons (e.g., to align with a specific date) deprive the fetus of this right [15].

These examples highlight how maternal health behaviors and medical interventions are tightly interlinked with fetal rights, illustrating the central tension between protecting the fetus and safeguarding maternal autonomy. Proper maternal nutrition during pregnancy is another critical fetal right. Research from Harvard’s Institute of Medical Law (2021) indicates that maternal malnutrition violates fetal rights, leading to long-term cognitive impairments. Germany’s Embryo Protection Act mandates that parents and the state ensure essential nutrient provision, with judicial intervention permitted in cases of severe neglect [35]. Asadi also highlighted proper maternal nutrition as a fetal right [4]. Protection from external harm, such as environmental pollution or physical violence, is enshrined in the “precautionary principle” of international environmental law and national criminal laws. The World Health Organization (2023) identified air pollutants as a threat to fetal rights, advocating for stringent regulations [36]. Mustapha’s study in Nigeria recognized protection from external harm as a fetal right [31]. Meshkat’s review highlighted fetal harm as a neglected health issue in Iran [24]. Hantoushzadeh emphasized ethical considerations and protection from external harm, noting that the fetus feels pain, and procedures like induced abortions may require pain reduction methods [17]. Taken together, these findings suggest recurring global patterns nutrition, environmental health, and medical interventions are consistently framed as fetal rights, although the degree of enforcement varies.

In the ethical domain, fetal rights include dignity, respect, identity, sanctity, and a balance between maternal and fetal rights. As humans are worthy of respect due to their inherent rationality and dignity, the fetus, as a developing entity, also possesses this dignity [16]. Mashayekhi recognized the fetus’s right to dignity and respect [26]. Premature cesareans for non-medical reasons, common in some developing countries like Iran, violate fetal dignity [26, 37]. Rafiei, Abbassi, and Ghaffari identified appropriate delivery methods as a fetal right, with unnecessary or premature cesareans violating fetal rights [15, 21, 22] Such procedures, especially in the final days of pregnancy, disregard fetal health and civil rights, implicating both medical professionals and parents [15]. Neglecting fetal dignity also constitutes fetal violence. Obstetric violence varies globally, with unnecessary cesareans and episiotomies more prevalent in developing countries, while developed countries view childbirth as a natural process, and such interventions are considered obstetric violence [38]. Vedam noted that loss of maternal autonomy in childbirth decisions and pressure for unwanted medical interventions violate fetal rights, with improved healthcare conditions indirectly enhancing fetal rights [27]. Zampas emphasized respectful, non-discriminatory, and non-coercive maternal healthcare as a right for both mother and fetus [5]. The WHO also recognizes safe, high-quality care during pregnancy as a maternal and fetal right [39]. Cultural and political factors influence fetal rights, as Mousavi noted that religion and policy impact decisions regarding fetal life and health [25]. Preiksaitis highlighted laws as a key factor affecting fetal rights [20]. In line with these findings, Alimohammadi et al. demonstrated that global gender equality is significantly associated with maternal and neonatal health outcomes [40] This synthesis highlights both convergence (shared emphasis on dignity and respect) and conflict (variations in maternal autonomy), reaffirming that any fetal rights charter must carefully balance ethical principles with maternal rights.

Overall, the findings of this review demonstrate that, while there is a global consensus on the fundamental right to life and health of the fetus, significant discrepancies remain in the recognition and enforcement of broader fetal rights. These differences largely stem from cultural, religious, and legal traditions, which shape how the balance between maternal autonomy and fetal protection is defined. In Islamic jurisprudence, the fetus is considered a dignified being with both spiritual and material rights, whereas in many Western legal systems, priority is given to maternal autonomy. This tension reveals that any effort to design a comprehensive fetal rights charter must not only integrate medical and ethical dimensions but also reconcile these diverse perspectives. Such an integrative approach can provide a balanced framework that both respects maternal rights and ensures the protection of the fetus.

In Iran, the lack of a cohesive fetal rights framework can lead to increased intentional abortions, unnecessary childbirth interventions, and threats to future generations’ health. Therefore, developing a comprehensive fetal rights charter informed by both Islamic jurisprudence and international human rights frameworks would not only safeguard the fetus but also strengthen maternal rights, family health, and demographic stability.

Conclusion

Based on the reviewed literature, the fetus possesses ethical dignity and elements of legal personhood, making respect for its rights a moral and legal obligation. Neglecting these rights contributes to intentional abortions, unnecessary medical interventions, and long-term threats to maternal and child health. Delivery methods themselves reflect fetal rights, as unnecessary cesareans can undermine both dignity and health. While the right to life is widely recognized, significant cross-national discrepancies remain: countries such as the UK, Nigeria, and India stop short of granting independent legal personhood [15, 36]. These differences largely reflect cultural, religious, and policy contexts, underscoring the absence of a uniform global framework.

The findings of this review highlight not only areas of convergence but also critical tensions between protecting fetal rights and safeguarding maternal autonomy. Developing a fetal rights charter that draws upon both Islamic jurisprudence and international human rights frameworks could help reconcile these tensions [20, 25]. Such a charter would provide clearer legal and ethical guidance, enhance maternal and neonatal healthcare quality, and strengthen demographic and population policies. Beyond health, it could also promote social justice by ensuring that both mothers and fetuses are protected within a balanced rights-based framework.

Limitations and future directions

This review contains several limitations which must be discussed. To begin with, while a thorough search strategy was implemented both internationally and domestically, only English and Persian sources were included which might have excluded relevant studies published in other languages. Furthermore, some unpublished documents, policy papers, and other grey literature might have been overlooked which would have expanded the findings. Lastly, the scope of the review emphasizes breadth as opposed to depth. For example, while we identified and categorized 59 fetal rights from medical, ethical, and economic perspectives, the weight and practical application of these rights in various legal frameworks were not critically evaluated.

Therefore, future research needs to adopt a comparative legal and empirical approach to evaluate the practical application of fetal rights, especially in situations where there are conflicts between fetal protection and maternal autonomy. In addition, other research could assess the efficacy of cross-cultural perspectives, gender equality, the role of health system structures in policy-making, and guidelines to shape fetal rights. Such research would not only enhance the evidence gap but would also create a stronger charter tailor-made to the needs of the people.
