Prognostic impact of primary tumor resection and nomogram development for survival prediction in colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma patients with liver metastasis: a SEER database-based study
Authors: Jingxiong Chen, Haizhou Diao, Kai Qian, Kaiguang Zhang, Tengyue Zhang, Jizhong Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the prognostic impact of primary tumor resection in colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma (NECs) patients with liver metastasis, compare survival outcomes between left- and right-sided tumors, and develop a nomogram for predicting overall survival (OS) in this population.MethodsData from 449 colonic NECs patients with liver metastasis (2010–2021) were extracted from the SEER database. Patients were stratified by primary tumor location (left vs. right colon) and surgical resection status. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance the groups and subsequently investigate survival differences between them. Survival differences were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox regression. A nomogram integrating independent prognostic factors was developed using LASSO regression and validated for 6-, 12-, and 24-month OS prediction. Model performance was assessed via ROC curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsRight-sided colonic NECs predominated (85.1%, n = 382) and exhibited better OS than left-sided tumors (before PSM: 7.0 m vs. 6.0 m, p = 0.038; after PSM: 8.0 m vs. 6.0 m, p = 0.031). Primary tumor resection independently improved survival in both left colon (9.0 m vs. 5.0 m for no surgery, p = 0.012) and right colon (9.0 m vs. 6.0 m for no surgery, p < 0.001) groups. The nomogram incorporated age, tumor size, and resection status, demonstrating moderate-to-strong predictive accuracy (AUC: 0.623–0.755 in training cohort; 0.691–0.760 in validation cohort). Calibration curves and DCA confirmed clinical utility.ConclusionRight-sided colonic NECs with liver metastasis have superior survival compared to left-sided tumors. Primary tumor resection is associated with prolonged OS, regardless of tumor location. The validated nomogram provides a practical tool for individualized prognosis prediction, supporting clinical decision-making in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03952-8.Keywords: Colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma, Liver metastasis, Primary tumor location, Prognosis

Introduction

Neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) of the colon are malignancies derived from neuroendocrine cells, with approximately two-thirds being found in the gastrointestinal system [1–4]. Colonic NECs, while less common among gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors, represent the second most prevalent malignancy of the colon. In the United States, the estimated incidence is 0.3 cases per 100,000 population [5–9]. Worldwide, colorectal tumors are more commonly observed in Western populations, while colonic neuroendocrine cancers are rarer in Asia. However, current research has noted an increase in the incidence of colonic neuroendocrine tumors globally [10]. Among gastrointestinal NECs, over 40% are located in the colon, and more than half of these patients present with distant metastases, most commonly to the liver, with a median survival typically under one year [8, 11]. While surgical resection remains the cornerstone of NEC treatment, NANETS and ENETS guidelines do not recommend removal of the primary tumor in patients with metastatic colonic NECs, except when necessary due to local symptoms [11–13].

Embryologically, the colon is classified into left and right segments, derived from the hindgut and midgut, respectively, with the distal transverse colon acting as the demarcation line. Emerging research highlights distinctions in clinical presentation, pathological biology, and molecular profiles between left- and right-sided colonic adenocarcinomas, providing essential guidance for personalized colorectal cancer treatment [14–17]. However, research on the clinical and pathophysiological differences between left- and right-sided colonic NECs is limited, with most studies being small-scale and conducted in single centers.

The nomogram, a visualized multivariate statistical model, has been widely used for disease screening, treatment guidance, risk stratification, and the prediction of prognosis [18]. Although various cancer nomograms have been developed, models specifically designed for colonic NEC patients with liver metastasis have not yet been established. This study aims to analyze data from the SEER database on colonic NECs to examine the clinical characteristics of patients with hepatic metastases from different primary sites, assess the impact of primary tumor resection on survival outcomes, and develop an overall survival (OS) nomogram for this patient group.

Data and methods

Data source and study population

This study utilized data retrieved from the SEER database, which compiles demographic, tumor, treatment, and survival outcome data from cancer patients across various U.S. regions. This information is publicly accessible in accordance with relevant guidelines. The study analyzed SEER data spanning 17 registries, representing approximately 34% of the U.S. population. Data extraction from the database was conducted using SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.3; NCI, Bethesda, MD, USA). The study included 449 cases of primary colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma diagnosed between 2010 and 2021, excluding the appendix due to the distinct biological behavior of neuroendocrine neoplasms at this site compared to other colorectal locations [2, 19]. Neuroendocrine carcinoma classification adhered to the criteria outlined in the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) [20], which defines large cell NEC (code 8013), small cell NEC (code 8041), and neuroendocrine carcinoma, not otherwise specified (code 8246). Only patients with high-grade (poorly differentiated or undifferentiated) NECs were included in the analysis. Inclusion criteria comprised individuals aged between 18 and 80 years at the time of diagnosis with a confirmed pathological diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included diagnoses based solely on autopsy or death certificate, non-primary malignancies, and missing or incomplete key variables such as age, race, survival status, metastasis, treatment, and surgical data.

Variable definitions

The SEER database provided data on colonic NECs, including demographics such as diagnosis year, age, gender, and race, as well as tumor details like location, size, grade, and metastasis. Treatment approaches were coded based on the American College of Surgeons Oncology Registry [21], and survival outcomes were also included. Tumors size were categorized into three groups: < 2 cm, 2–5 cm, and > 5 cm. Based on ICD-O-3 criteria, the left colon includes the proximal colon up to the transverse colon (C18.0, C18.2, C18.3, C18.4), while the right colon encompasses the splenic flexure to the distal colon (C18.5, C18.6, C18.7) [20]. The primary outcome measure was OS, calculated from the time of diagnosis to death or censoring. This research is retrospective in design and did not necessitate formal consent. All protocols involving human participants in this study followed the ethical principles set forth by the appropriate institutional and/or national research ethics committees, in line with the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its subsequent revisions, or similar ethical guidelines. The study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines [22].

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance the groups and subsequently investigate survival differences between them. The dataset collected from the SEER database was randomly divided into training and validation cohorts at a ratio of 7:3, and the variables were compared. Continuous variables were reported as either mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on the normality of the data. Statistical differences were assessed using t-tests or two-sided unpaired Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%), and group differences were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves, with group survival differences assessed using log-rank tests. Univariate and multivariate survival analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards models to identify independent prognostic variables. In the training cohort, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) cox regression analysis was used for multivariate analysis to screen the independent risk factors and build a prediction nomogram for survival. The performance of the model was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calibration curve, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) ranging from 0.5 (no discriminant) to 1 (complete discriminant). A decision curve analysis (DCA) was also performed to determine the net benefit threshold of prediction. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation). A significance level of p < 0.05 in two-sided tests was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

Using the SEER database, this study identified 1598 patients diagnosed with colonic NECs via pathology from 2010 to 2021. After screening, 449 cases of colonic NECs with liver metastases were included and categorized into left colon (n = 67) and right colon (n = 382) groups (Fig. 1). The median follow-up duration was 7 months (interquartile range: 3–14 months). Table 1 summarizes the general clinical data of the two groups. There were no statistically significant differences in sex, marital status, ethnicity, primary site surgeries proportion or tumor size between the groups (p > 0.05 for all). Patients in the left colon group had a median age of 58.00 years, while those in the right colon group had a median age of 60.00 years (p = 0.003). The left colon group comprised of 62.69% males and 37.31% females, whereas the right colon group had 50.52% males and 49.48% females. Marital status and race distribution were similar between the groups. Regarding treatment, there were no significant differences between the groups in the proportion of chemotherapy use (left colon 74.63% vs. right colon 67.02%; p = 0.217).

Prognostic effects of primary tumor site and surgery

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that among colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma patients with liver metastases, individuals with right-sided primary tumors had better overall survival (7.0 months, 95%CI 6.0–8.0) compared to those with left-sided tumors (6.0 months, 95%CI 5.0–8.0) (Fig. 2A). Further PSM analysis, adjusted for age, primary site surgery, and sex, was performed with a 1:1 matching ratio and a caliper value of 0.05. A total of 134 patients were matched, with 67 from the left side of the colon and 67 from the right side. Subsequent Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients with right-sided tumors still had a significantly better prognosis (8.0 m vs. 6.0 m for left side, p = 0.031) (Table S1 and Figure S1). A further comparison of baseline characteristics was conducted for patients with left- and right-sided colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma with liver metastases, categorized by whether primary site surgery was performed (Table 2). Among the left colon group, there were no significant differences in age, gender, marital status, or ethnicity between those who underwent primary site surgery and those who did not (all p > 0.05). However, patients who underwent surgery were less likely to receive chemotherapy (57.69% vs. 85.37%; p = 0.011) and had larger tumor sizes (p = 0.004). In the right colon group, patients who underwent primary site surgery had lower rates of chemotherapy use (55.84% vs. 78.92%; p < 0.001) and larger tumor sizes (p < 0.001), while age, gender, marital status, and race showed no significant differences between the subgroups (all p > 0.05). Survival analysis of colonic neuroendocrine carcinoma patients with liver metastases, stratified by primary site surgery, showed that the surgery group consistently had better cumulative survival rates than the non-surgery group, regardless of the tumor’s location (Table S2). Specifically, for left-sided tumors, the median overall survival without surgery was 5.0 months (95% CI: 4.0–7.0), compared to 9.0 months (95% CI: 7.0–13.0) with surgery. For right-sided tumors, the mOS without surgery was 6.0 months (95% CI: 5.0–7.0), while with surgery it was 9.0 months (95% CI: 7.0–13.0) (Fig. 2B and C).

In the right colon group, age, sex, tumor size, and primary tumor surgery were identified as independent prognostic factors for patients with neuroendocrine carcinoma and liver metastases. Patients who underwent primary tumor surgery experienced a significant improvement in prognosis compared to those who did not (HR 0.62; 95% CI, 0.47 ~ 0.81; p < 0.001). Conversely, male (HR 1.30; 95% CI, 1.04 ~ 1.62; p = 0.020), older patients (HR 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01 ~ 1.03; p = 0.002), as well as those with tumor diameters of 2–5 cm (HR 1.83; 95% CI, 1.03 ~ 3.27; p = 0.041) or > 5 cm (HR 3.13; 95% CI, 1.73 ~ 5.65; p < 0.001) had poorer prognoses (Fig. 3B).

Predictive model and validation

To further establish a prognostic prediction model for NEC liver metastasis patients, 70% (314 patients) were randomly assigned to the training set, and 30% (135 patients) to the validation set. No significant differences were observed in demographic or clinical characteristics between the training and validation groups (p > 0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

NECs are a rare form of malignancy, characterized by high heterogeneity, which is widely acknowledged. This heterogeneity is largely related to the primary site and exhibits distinct patterns of metastasis [23]. A major clinical question remains whether right- and left-sided colonic NECs, given their distinct embryological origins, exhibit biological differences that may impact treatment decisions for colonic NECs. Consequently, using data from the SEER database, we investigated the clinical features and prognostic disparities between left- and right-sided colonic NECs with liver metastases and assessed the effect of primary tumor resection on patient outcomes. Additionally, we developed a nomogram for prognostic prediction in this patient population.

Extensive literature on colonic adenocarcinoma widely supports that biological differences exist between left- and right-sided colon cancers, significantly affecting therapeutic outcomes and patient survival. In a cohort study by Helvaci et al. involving 1,725 colonic adenocarcinoma patients, right-sided adenocarcinoma was found to be more prevalent, with 57.6% of patients aged over 65 at diagnosis, and the median survival was significantly lower than that of left-sided adenocarcinoma (43 months vs. 62 months; p < 0.05) [24]. A meta-analysis by Yahagi et al. summarizing 15 studies revealed that right-sided colonic adenocarcinoma had significantly worse overall survival compared to left-sided colonic adenocarcinoma (HR 1.14; 95% CI, 1.06 ~ 1.22; p < 0.01) [25]. Our study demonstrated that colonic NECs are mainly located in the right colon, with right-sided patients exhibiting a 3-year survival rate (16.5% vs. 3.51%; p < 0.05) significantly better than left-sided patients, which is in stark contrast to the survival patterns of colonic adenocarcinoma. This suggests that NECs' distinct pathological features underlie their unique biological characteristics [26, 27]. Mao et al. conducted a study on non-metastatic colorectal NECs and found that treatment responses to chemotherapy differed depending on the primary site. Specifically, left-sided tumors showed a better response (HR = 0.55) than right-sided tumors (HR = 0.89) [28]. However, it is important to note that this study included the rectum as a part of the right-sided tumors, rather than focusing solely on left and right colonic comparisons, and further research is needed to clarify these findings. Through transcriptomic comparative analysis of NEC and adenocarcinoma tissues, Lee et al. discovered that colorectal NECs displayed a higher prevalence of BRAF mutations (23%), of which 71% were p.V600E mutations, and RB1 alterations (47%), with 57% being deletion mutations. The mutation rates of other genes (e.g., KRAS, APC, SMAD4, and PIK3CA) and the proportion of microsatellite instability were comparable to those observed in adenocarcinoma [29]. Kasajima et al. identified that TP53 mutations play a pivotal role in driving the transformation of low-grade neuroendocrine tumors into high -grade neuroendocrine carcinomas, thereby contributing to rapid tumor progression [30]. Ali et al. reported that approximately 45% of colonic NEC patients harbor TP53 mutations. These mutations were shown to influence the prognosis of colorectal NECs with distant metastases. Specifically, patients with TP53 mutations had a poorer progression-free survival (PFS) (HR 2.5; 95% CI: 1.1 ~ 5.8; p = 0.04) and OS (HR 3.0; 95% CI: 1.3 ~ 6.9; p < 0.01) [31]. Such genetic distinctions highlight the unique biological properties of NECs and may account for their differing clinical characteristics.

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines for metastatic colonic adenocarcinoma treatment emphasize tailored therapeutic strategies based on the primary tumor location within the colon [32]. Chen et al. conducted a study on 7,386 colorectal cancer patients and found that resection of the primary tumor was correlated with better overall survival across all tumor locations (right colon HR 0.41, left colon HR 0.57; p < 0.001) [33]. Arnold et al. performed a meta-analysis of six randomized controlled trials to evaluate the impact and predictive role of primary tumor location in unresectable RAS wild-type metastatic colonic adenocarcinoma. They compared outcomes of chemotherapy + anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody (experimental group) with chemotherapy or chemotherapy + bevacizumab (control group). The study showed that RAS wild-type mCRC patients with right-sided tumors had poorer overall survival, progression-free survival, and objective response rates compared to left-sided tumors. Primary tumor location was an independent prognostic factor, and anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies were most effective in left-sided tumors (Odds ratio 2.12; 95% CI, 1.77 ~ 2.55; p < 0.05) [34].

In contrast, limited research has been conducted on colonic NECs in this context. Our study revealed that primary tumor resection significantly benefited colonic NEC patients with liver metastases, irrespective of tumor location: left colon (HR 0.32; 95% CI, 0.11 ~ 0.88; p = 0.027) and right colon (HR 0.65; 95% CI, 0.44 ~ 0.97; p = 0.044). However, a multicenter retrospective study conducted by Yu et al., which included 68 patients with metastatic colorectal neuroendocrine tumors, reported that palliative primary tumor resection did not provide a survival benefit. The median PFS was identical between groups (4 months vs. 4 months, p = 0.613), and the median OS was also comparable (24 months vs. 31 months, p = 0.983) [35]. Similarly, Wu et al. observed that for elderly patients with stage IV colorectal NEC, surgery did not offer significant survival benefits. No significant differences were found between the surgical and non-surgical groups in cancer-specific survival (p = 0.317) or OS (p = 0.385) [36].

On the other hand, Fields et al. found that high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma patients of the colon and rectum, treated with chemotherapy and primary tumor resection, exhibited significantly better overall survival in the U.S. National Cancer Database compared to those without resection (HR 0.54; 95% CI, 0.44 ~ 0.66; p < 0.001). Their study included separate analyses for patients with initial localized and metastatic disease. Among those with localized disease, the 5-year survival rates were 0% for those not receiving chemotherapy or surgery, 15.9% for those receiving chemotherapy only, 31.7% for those receiving surgery only, and 37.0% for those receiving both treatments (p < 0.001). In patients with metastatic disease, 5-year survival was 0% for those not receiving chemotherapy or surgery, 1.6% for those receiving chemotherapy only, 3.7% for those receiving surgery only, and 6.1% for those receiving both treatments. However, this retrospective study may have inherent selection bias, as patients chosen for surgery could have had better overall health or fewer disease-related complications. Additionally, the observed survival benefit might be influenced by factors such as more favorable patient characteristics, including performance status and disease burden, which may make them more likely to undergo surgery, a pattern also seen in other cancers like metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma [37]. Alese et al., in a cohort of patients with high-grade gastrointestinal NEC, identified through multivariate Cox regression analysis that the absence of surgery on the primary tumor was associated with significantly worse overall survival (HR 1.92; 95% CI: 1.61–2.29, p < 0.001). However, their study combined all disease stages in the analysis, which may have skewed the results, as surgery likely served as a marker for localized disease with a better prognosis [38]. Although these studies had large sample sizes, they lacked stratified analyses of primary sites, making it difficult to discern clinical benefit variations between right colon, left colon, and rectum. Our stratified analysis indicates that primary tumor resection in colonic NEC patients with liver metastases is more than a palliative approach and contributes to prolonging overall survival.

A growing body of research indicates that approximately half of colonic NECs patients experience distant metastases, with the liver being the predominant metastatic site [23, 39]. The resection of metastatic lesions, however, remains a subject of debate [40, 41]. Likewise, some studies have explored the outcomes of liver metastasis resection in NECs. Ammann et al. found that although patients with grade 3 neuroendocrine tumor liver metastases (NETLM G3) and neuroendocrine carcinoma liver metastases had significantly worse prognosis after cytoreductive liver resection compared to patients with grade 1/2 NETLM, some patients may still benefit from surgery. Survival in NETLM G3 patients was associated with lesion size and number, while no clear survival predictors were identified for NECs, indicating the need for the development of new stratification criteria to select candidates for surgery [42]. Galleberg et al. demonstrated that in a rigorously selected group of metastatic gastroenteropancreatic NEC patients, liver metastasis resection or radiofrequency ablation RFA with curative intent significantly improved survival (median OS 35.9 months, 5-year OS rate 43%), particularly in patients with Ki-67 < 55%. These results challenge the current guidelines, which do not recommend surgery, suggesting that surgical treatments may have clinical value in specific subgroups, such as those with relatively low Ki-67 [43]. Presently, there is no evidence to support that combined metastatic resection offers a survival advantage over primary tumor resection alone. Accordingly, our study recommends prioritizing primary tumor resection in colonic NECs, given the higher difficulty of hepatic metastasis resection, the multifocal nature of liver metastases, and their high recurrence rate post-surgery.

When contextualized within existing literature, our nomogram addresses a critical gap in NEC-specific prognostic tools. While Iasonos et al. established the fundamental principles for constructing oncology nomograms, their work primarily focused on adenocarcinomas [18]. Similarly, recent advances in the prognosis model of neuroendocrine tumors, such as the gastrointestinal pancreatic NET model by Yang et al., have excluded specific considerations for high-grade NECs and liver metastasis [44]. The nomogram developed in this study represents a significant advancement in prognostic stratification for colonic NECs patients with liver metastasis, integrating three clinically actionable variables (age, primary tumor resection, and tumor size) into an intuitive visual tool. The 24-month OS prediction yielded an AUC of 0.755 in the training set and 0.760 in the validation set, demonstrating strong predictive performance. Sorbye et al. conducted the NORDIC NEC2 study, which found that age and gender were strongly correlated with overall survival in patients with digestive neuroendocrine carcinoma [45]. In our study, age was also included as a variable in the nomogram. Similarly, the incorporation of primary tumor resection status as a modifiable prognostic factor aligns with recent surgical oncology paradigms emphasizing the therapeutic value of cytoreduction in metastatic NECs [37]. The calibration curve shows a close alignment between the predicted and observed survival probabilities at time intervals of 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months. This further validates the clinical reliability of the model, especially among patients with colonic NECs patients with liver metastasis. These patients have a poor prognosis, and the first two years after diagnosis are critical, as most treatment decisions are made during this period.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the number of patients with left-sided and right-sided colon tumors is not balanced. Although PSM and sensitivity analyses were conducted, the retrospective cohort design based on the SEER database may introduce biases in data collection, inclusion criteria, analysis methods, and statistical procedures. These biases must be carefully addressed to ensure the conclusions are accurate and reliable. Second, the SEER database lacks detailed information, such as genetic data (RAS/BRAF/TP53), surgical margin status, mitotic index, lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, Ki-67, and surgical and treatment details. This limits the scope for deeper analysis. Additionally, the SEER database does not include variables that could influence surgery selection, such as the purpose of surgery or patient performance status, which may introduce selection bias. Furthermore, this study focuses specifically on the prognosis of colonic NEC patients with liver metastasis, so the nomogram may not accurately reflect the prognosis of other colonic neoplasm subtypes. Finally, with advances in treatment and new drugs, patient survival rates may improve, reducing the predictive power of the model over time. Therefore, the model requires continuous refinement.

Conclusion

Colonic NECs are typically right-sided and often metastasize to the liver. Notably, the survival rate of right-sided colonic NECs with liver metastases is higher than that of left-sided colonic NECs. In patients with colonic NECs and liver metastases, primary tumor resection is associated with improved survival outcomes. However, there is a critical gap in the availability of NEC-specific prognostic tools. This study developed a nomogram for predicting the prognosis of colonic NECs with liver metastasis, which was internally validated. This tool could be highly valuable in the shared decision-making process.
