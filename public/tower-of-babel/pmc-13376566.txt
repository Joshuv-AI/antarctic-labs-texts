Realistic benchmark RBD360 dataset for quality assessment of random user generated 360° videos
Authors: Manav Arun Mehta, Pramit Mazumdar, Kalyan Chatterjee, Sweta Dey, Mudassir Khan, Raja Shekar Kadurka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUser-generated 360° video content is becoming increasingly popular due to the availability of low-cost acquisition devices and its inherently immersive nature. To facilitate research and development in this area, we introduce RBD-360, a dataset designed explicitly for quality assessment of user-generated 360° videos that includes various distortions typical of non-professional settings. RBD-360 features a collection of randomly captured source videos across multiple categories, recorded in three different environments: indoor, outdoor during the day, and outdoor at night. The impaired video sequences are created using three standard codecs—H.264, H.265, and VP9 at varying bitrates and quantization parameters. To evaluate subjective video quality, we employed an HMD-based Modified Absolute Category Rating protocol, along with assessments from the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire. This helps capture time-dependent viewer discomfort according to ITU-T P.919 recommendations. We implement benchmark quality assessment metrics on the RBD-360 dataset and report the results. The dataset is publicly available for testing quality assessment models tailored for user-generated videos at https://github.com/manav2701/RBD-360.Keywords: Video Quality Assessment (VQA), 360° videos, User Generated Content (UGC), Modified ACR, Quality of Experience (QoE)Subject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Head-mounted displays (HMDs) and affordable 360° capture have made virtual reality (VR) increasingly widespread, shifting immersive content creation from professional studios to everyday users1–3. As a consequence, large volumes of 360° media are now produced outside controlled settings, bringing practical demands for perceptual quality assessment under real-world constraints.

In this context, user-generated content (UGC) denotes 360° videos recorded by end users with consumer-grade devices in unconstrained, in-the-wild environments. Typical UGC features handheld operation, spontaneous or rapid camera motion, incidental occlusions, heterogeneous exposure and illumination, and ad hoc scene composition—characteristics that contrast with professionally produced material captured under stable lighting, staging, and stabilization4. Beyond capture, the appearance of UGC as distributed is shaped by platform encode–decode pipelines and transcoding constraints4, which motivates dataset designs that reflect both capture conditions and delivery-side codec/rate settings5,6.

Assessing the perceptual quality of 360° UGC is challenging for multiple reasons. Handheld recordings introduce motion instability, which degrades comfort and can induce simulator sickness during HMD viewing7–9. Unstructured shooting leads to harmful occlusions and misalignment that disturb depth cues and presence7. Substantial illumination variability across indoor, outdoor-day, and outdoor-night scenes alters contrast and texture statistics, complicating both compression and saliency modelling10,11. On the compression side, codec and rate-control choices (e.g., H.264/H.265/VP9) interact with spatial detail and motion to produce distinct artifact profiles that are non-trivial to predict in head-tracked viewing in the future5,6,12,13. Standardized guidance for VR quality studies emphasizes presentation style, sickness tracking, and participant numbers for HMD-based tests8,9,14, yet these elements are not uniformly integrated across prior works15.

A growing body of resources surveys 360° streaming and QoE and contributes datasets or protocols from complementary perspectives and UGC-focused efforts2,3,16–24. Nevertheless, much existing data emphasises single-codec pipelines, curated capture, or laboratory conditions, which can limit generalisation to truly in-the-wild UGC scenarios. Recent in-the-wild VR/360° quality studies highlight the difficulty of extrapolating from lab-style content to UGC with unstable motion and illumination16,23. Learning-based methods tailored to 360° images/videos continue to advance12,13,24, yet their behaviour on user-generated capture remains underexplored.

Random user-generated (RUG) content defined here as video recorded by everyday users under completely unconstrained real-world conditions without deliberate scene selection, staging, or environmental control, takes UGC a step further by embracing the true unpredictability of everyday recording25. Instead of carefully selected or staged scenes, RUG captures footage in any setting users happen to record—homes, streets, events—using a variety of consumer cameras. This approach surfaces a full spectrum of real-world variations: sudden camera shakes, improvised framing, mixed lighting conditions, and unplanned occlusions, mirroring the diversity of content found online23,26. By reflecting genuine user behavior without pre-selection, RUG datasets expose quality assessment algorithms to the kinds of irregular distortions and dynamic content transitions they must handle in practical applications. This distinguishes RUG from traditional UGC, where user intent and platform processing establish an implicit quality baseline. In RUG content, distortions are irregular, temporally inconsistent, and structurally unpredictable, posing challenges that extend beyond those addressed by conventional benchmarks27.

The aim here is to establish a dataset and protocol that (i) reflect everyday user-generated capture conditions, (ii) isolate codec–rate interactions across H.264/H.265/VP9, and (iii) enable reproducible benchmarking of 360° VQA for head-mounted viewing. To characterise scene complexity and motion that drive codec behaviour and perceived quality, sequence-level spatial/temporal information (SI/TI) statistics are reported, aligning with motion-tuned and spatio-temporal quality findings in prior work28. Complementary resources that log head motion and investigate edit/alignment effects further reinforce the need for protocols aligned with actual exploration patterns20,22.

This work presents RBD-360, a real-world 360° UGC video quality dataset designed to capture the interaction between authentic capture artifacts (handheld motion, occlusions, illumination variation) and synthetic compression distortions across multiple codecs and operating points (H.264/H.265/VP9). The study adopts a Modified-ACR presentation with grey-screen intervals, HMD playback, and pre/mid/post Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) to capture time-dependent discomfort, consistent with recommendations for VR evaluations8,9,15,29, thereby addressing gaps observed in recent datasets that employ single-codec pipelines or laboratory-controlled capture conditions30,31. The dataset composition and benchmarking protocol are intended to facilitate reproducible evaluation and to stress-test contemporary 360° VQA methods under genuinely in-the-wild conditions23,26,28. Data collection followed standard consent and privacy practices for public spaces; sequences avoid personally identifiable close-ups and are released for research use under a non-commercial licence.

Limitations in codec diversity

Analyzing human behavior with 360° video is essential for developing effective quality metrics. An early and valuable study by Li et al.21 utilized the VQA-ODV dataset to understand subjective quality in the context of compression. However, their research focused exclusively on H.265 encoding, which restricts the applicability of its findings to other prominent codec standards. Similarly, the study by Mahmoud et al.32 does not provide subjective quality assessments across different codecs, resulting in gaps in the comprehensive evaluation of user experience. As observed in Table 1, this limitation persists across several subsequent studies, which have not included multiple modern codec standards in their design.

Distortion types and capture environments

The types of distortion present in displayed videos play a critical role in shaping a user’s experience23,33. These distortions can be broadly categorized into two types: (i) Authentic distortions, which are naturally occurring artifacts from real-world capturing and processing, such as lighting variations or motion blur, and (ii) Synthetic distortions, which are artificially added impairments in controlled settings, like compression artifacts or added blur, often for research purposes16.

Fang et al.16 noted that prior datasets focused primarily on synthetic distortions and developed a dataset emphasizing authentic ones. However, their work did not integrate both authentic and synthetic distortions, constraining the dataset’s capacity to evaluate user perception under more representative real-world conditions. Furthermore, as established by Seshadrinathan et al.28, camera motion dynamics significantly influence perceived video quality. Yet, in the context of 360° video, most studies assess quality using professionally captured static camera footage or in controlled lab environments. This contrasts sharply with user-generated or “in-the-wild” videos, which feature handheld camera movements that introduce unsteady motion and varying lighting conditions, giving rise to complex authentic distortions.

Recent large-scale efforts such as Leader360V and STAV360 have made notable contributions toward advancing video quality assessment30,31. However, these datasets rely exclusively on H.265 encoding and do not jointly address multi-codec compression alongside real-world capture variability. Furthermore, the integration of time-dependent simulator sickness tracking across pre-, mid-, and post-session evaluations remains largely unaddressed in the existing literature. Broader surveys on perceptual image and video quality assessment further contextualise the challenges of developing robust metrics for complex distortion scenarios34,35.

Advancements in subjective QoE assessment

In addition to dataset characteristics, subjective testing methodologies for assessing simulator sickness are also evolving. The work by Elwardy et al.17 in subjective tests follows the standard practice of a pre- and post-evaluation using the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ). Building on this, RBD-360 integrates a mid-test SSQ, following methodologies seen in large-scale studies such as Gutierrez et al.8. This addition enables a more nuanced analysis of the impact of VR exposure duration on participant health, contributing to a more comprehensive assessment of QoE in immersive environments.

To mitigate the aforementioned constraints, the proposed dataset RBD-360 emphasizes videos captured with authentic distortions, which are then subjected to synthetic distortion via compression. It includes variations in lighting along with unstable camera motion and is encoded using multiple codecs36 across different quantization parameters (QP) and bitrates. The dataset effectively addresses the shortcomings of previous work by combining a well-balanced blend of both authentic and synthetic distortions, thereby more accurately simulating real-world deployment scenarios.

The videos used were recorded using the GoPro MAX 360° Action Camera37, and the recordings were captured at 60 fps with a resolution of 3 K for a time duration of 30 seconds each. There were nine original 360° videos that were then processed using multiple video formats and compression levels to generate a comprehensive dataset using synthetic distortions for further analysis. The selection of nine source videos was designed to ensure a balanced representation across three environmental categories, with the SI/TI distribution illustrated in Figure 2 confirming a meaningful spread of spatial and temporal complexity across the chosen sequences, in line with the scale of several well-regarded benchmark datasets in the literature. While the SI/TI and luminance statistics provide a quantitative basis for characterising authentic distortion content across sequences, more granular per-sequence distortion annotation remains a valuable direction for future work.

Encoding procedure

Video conversion tool

The conversion and compression of each video is automated using a custom script utilizing the FFmpeg tool36. For this dataset, FFmpeg36 was used to encode the original 360° videos into the three chosen codecs, with the required Bitrates & QP values.

The converted videos have a resolution of 2272 726, reflecting a reduction in visual fidelity. This intentional downscaling aligns with the objective of simulating user-generated 360° (RBD-360) content following a synthetic distortion, which reflects the practical constraints of real-time video streaming, where bandwidth limitations and device compatibility considerations commonly necessitate operating below the native capture resolution.

Video encoding pipeline

This study introduces the RBD-360 dataset, comprising 81 Random User-Generated impaired video sequences transcoded from 9 original 360° videos. The transcoding process, involves encoding with three quantization parameters (QPs) and transcoding using three widely adopted codecs. Each video in a given codec is assigned a unique bitrate to prevent redundancy and keep the dataset size manageable, ensuring ease of use for future research. The pipeline is illustrated in Figure 5.

Illustration of the User content generation process for the dataset, which contains 9 reference and 81 transcoded videos in total sequences.

The 9 original video sequences are initially encoded by regularizing the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) coefficients38, enabling compression with varying QPs: 22, 32, and 42. These QP values represent different compression levels, where QP 22 denotes high-quality encoding, QP 32 indicates moderate compression, and QP 42 results in lower-quality encoding. This step generates 27 videos per QP (9 original videos 3 QPs).

The dataset is then categorized into three environmental subgroups, each containing three reference videos: i) Indoor (V1, V2, V3), ii) Outdoor (Daytime) (V4, V5, V6), and iii) Outdoor (Nighttime) (V7, V8, V9)

Each subgroup undergoes transcoding, resulting in 27 videos per environment, totaling 81 videos across all environments.

A unique feature of this design is that for every video and QP level, a distinct combination of codec and bitrate is assigned. We employed three widely used video codecs—H.264, H.265, and VP9. Implemented using FFmpeg v4.4.1 with standard presets: x264 (slow), x265 (slow), and libvpx (good)36. The full encoding commands and reproduction instructions are provided at https://github.com/manav2701/RBD-360. For example, video V1 with QP 22 & QP 32 & QP 42 is transcoded as: i) H.264: 500 Kbps, ii) H.265: 3000 Kbps, and iii) VP9: 1500 Kbps. This structured assignment ensures that each bitrate is represented across all three codecs within every environment. A uniform bitrate design would have reduced the diversity of compression conditions and limited the scope of comparative observations39.

The transcoding design follows a structured sequence where the videos in each environment iterate through different quantization parameters (QPs) and codecs. This approach ensures full coverage of all QP settings and bitrates without unnecessary repetition, while also keeping the process user-friendly. Each video in a given codec was assigned a unique bitrate, following a balanced Latin-square design40 for maintaining a standard dataset size, reduce subject fatigue during the HMD sessions8 and prevent potential rating bias that could arise from presenting the same bitrate repeatedly across sequences.

As shown in Table 3, each new iteration introduces a change in QP, bitrate, and video content. Furthermore, the codec changes every three iterations, thereby minimizing contextual overlap and ensuring a balanced distribution across encoding configurations.

Dataset structure

The RBD-360 dataset is organized into three main folders, each corresponding to a different environment: Indoor, Outdoor_Daytime, and Outdoor_NightTime. Each folder contains 27 encoded 360° video sequences resulting from the controlled transcoding procedure described earlier. This structure ensures every sequence encompasses variations across three codecs, their designated bitrates, and quantization parameters (QPs), arranged in the order detailed in Table 3.

Videos are stored in .mp4 format, following a consistent naming convention: <VideoName>_<Codec>_<Bitrate>_<QP>.mp4. Alongside the video files, the dataset includes a CSV file listing Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) collected during subjective video quality assessments.

Subjective test setup

Software, hardware, and sequences

Quality of experience (QoE) was assessed in a controlled laboratory setting using a Meta Quest 3 head-mounted display (HMD) to ensure immersive playback. Ratings were recorded with AVTrack36020, and videos were presented using the Whirligig player. The HMD provides a per-eye resolution of 2064 2208 pixels and an approximate horizontal field of view of 110, offering high-fidelity rendering during evaluation. Stimuli were rendered from a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX A5000 GPU, a 13-generation Intel Core i9-13900K CPU, and a WD Green SN350 2 TB SSD. The proposed RBD-360 dataset was the basis for subjective video quality assessment (VQA) and subsequent metric evaluation14.

Test methodology and procedure

Subjective assessment followed the Modified Absolute Category Rating (M-ACR) methodology15. Each 360° video was presented once per session with a 5 s gray screen between stimuli to minimize temporal context effects8,17. Observers rated quality on a five-level categorical scale: (5) Excellent, (4) Good, (3) Fair, (2) Poor, (1) Bad. Figure 7 depicts the session structure.

Session structure for the M-ACR procedure, split into two blocks (S1 and S2) with a mid-session SSQ and rest.

Prior to viewing, participants received a standardized briefing and completed a pre-test Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ)8. The 27 stimuli for a given environment (indoor, outdoor-day, outdoor-night) were then shown in the fixed order defined in Table 3, with the starting index cyclically offset by +1 for each successive participant to counterbalance order and fatigue effects across the participants. To reduce adaptation to a single operating point, the codec was switched after every three sequences, while bitrate and quantization parameter (QP) varied across subsequent items41. At the midpoint of the session, a mid-test SSQ was administered, followed by a 2 min rest. A post-test SSQ concluded the session, enabling analysis of time-dependent discomfort in line with prior VR studies8,17.

Participants and assignment

Eighty-one university students participated (37.9% women, 62.10% men; age 17–24, mean 19.82). Prior VR experience was reported by 34.14% of participants; 65.86% had no previous HMD use. All experiments were conducted according to the relevant guidelines and regulations of the host Institution, Indian Institute of Information Technology Vadodara. The test involved only non-clinical experiments. The participants were involved in judging the perceptual quality of the recorded videos. The age range of participants corresponds to the demographic most actively engaged with consumer-grade immersive technologies, and their perceptual judgments carry strong relevance for real-world quality-of-experience scenarios in present and in the future42. Consent were taken from all participants before involving them in the subjective test. For each of the three environments, 27 observers viewed 27 impaired sequences (single presentation per sequence). To discourage information exchange and mitigate position effects, the starting index in the presentation order was offset by for each successive participant. This systematic offset ensures that subjects position is effectively decorrelated from content, helping control for position effects without compromising the structured codec–bitrate–QP coverage required by the experimental design. Thus, no two participants experienced the same video/encoding configuration at the same sequence position until the cycle repeats. For example, while the first observer viewed V1 at 500 kbps with QP 22 as the first stimulus, the second observer viewed V1 at 500 kbps with QP 32 in that position.

Quality control and data processing

Ratings were screened and aggregated using standard practices. First, obvious logging errors and incomplete entries were removed. Second, per-subject z-normalization was considered to mitigate scale-use bias prior to averaging, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for MOS were estimated via non-parametric bootstrap resampling (10,000 draws)9,43.

To assess rating consistency, subject–panel agreement was examined using rank correlation between each subject’s scores and the corresponding panel MOS (leave-one-out). Subjects with persistently low agreement across stimuli (threshold set a priori) were flagged for review; no removals were performed unless a clear protocol violation was identified. These steps align with common recommendations for subjective quality testing in immersive media8,43.

Objective quality metric analysis

To examine alignment between objective predictions and human judgments on RBD-360, several video quality assessment (VQA) methods—ranging from classical spherical PSNR variants to recent learning-based models—were evaluated against MOS using criteria recommended by the Video Quality Experts Group (VQEG)43. Conclusion & future work

This work introduced RBD-360, a comprehensive subjective quality assessment dataset designed to reflect the challenges of real-world, user-generated 360° videos. Unlike existing databases that primarily focus on controlled laboratory settings or synthetic impairments, RBD-360 incorporates both authentic and synthetic distortions arising from practical capture conditions, including camera motion, illumination variability, compression artifacts, and environmental dynamics across indoor, outdoor-daytime, and outdoor-night scenarios. The dataset covers multiple modern codecs and encoding configurations, and whilst the codec-wise observations are descriptive rather than causally definitive, the results surface important research questions regarding compression behaviour under genuine in-the-wild capture conditions. By addressing the gap between laboratory-centric benchmarks and in-the-wild content, RBD-360 provides a valuable resource for the development, training, and evaluation of robust 360° video quality assessment models. The dataset is expected to facilitate future research on perceptual modeling, codec-aware quality prediction, and immersive media optimization, thereby contributing to the advancement of reliable quality assessment for next-generation virtual reality and immersive multimedia applications.

Several directions can be explored to further extend the scope and impact of RBD-360. First, the dataset can be expanded to include higher frame-rate content, additional capture devices, and emerging codecs, enabling broader generalization of learning-based VQA models. Second, incorporating head and gaze tracking data, would allow investigation of viewport-dependent perceptual quality and attention-aware quality modelling, and represents a central design feature planned for the next iteration of this dataset. Third, future versions of RBD-360 may integrate longer video sequences and temporal quality annotations to address temporal quality variation and memory effects that 30-second stimuli, chosen here to minimise HMD-induced fatigue and SSQ confounds, do not fully capture. Finally, benchmarking deep learning and foundation-model-based VQA approaches, as well as exploring cross-dataset generalization and domain adaptation, represents a promising avenue for advancing robust and scalable 360° video quality assessment.
