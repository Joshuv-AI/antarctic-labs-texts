Impact of knee geometry on joint contact mechanics after meniscectomy
Authors: Ruoqi Deng, Sabri Uzuner, L. P. Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Finite element modeling has served as a cornerstone in understanding knee joint mechanics post-meniscectomy, yet the influence of varying knee geometries remains unknown. The present study aimed to fill that gap by employing statistical shape modeling to generate knee models from MRI data of 31 human knees, capturing the population’s knee size and shape variations. Finite element simulations were conducted to replicate intact, partial, and total medial meniscectomy conditions during standing. The results revealed a substantial shift in load distribution from the medial to lateral compartment following medial meniscectomy with its magnitude depending on knee geometry. Cartilages experienced variable degrees of pressure changes at different sites, which could also be different for fluid and contact pressures. While changes in joint size led to somewhat predictable alterations in contact pressure, variations in joint shape resulted in unexpected changes in contact and fluid pressures, emphasizing the need for computational simulations. The average knee geometry exhibited the lowest contact and fluid pressures under the given loading and boundary conditions, in contrast to knees with shapes deviating from the average. This study highlights the significance of individual knee shape in the biomechanical outcome of meniscectomy, potentially explaining the variability in clinical outcomes observed post-surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79662-y.

Introduction

The human knee joint is a complex anatomical structure responsible for providing load support, stability and mobility in various activities. Central to this joint’s functionality are the menisci, C-shaped fibrocartilage structures that distribute stresses across the knee during loading and aid in joint lubrication1–3. However, the menisci are susceptible to injury due to a combination of axial and rotational loadings which produces shearing in the meniscus4,5. Tears, predominantly occurring from traumatic sports-related incidents or degenerative changes, can lead to pain, swelling, and limitations in joint motion6.

Meniscectomy is a procedure commonly performed for degenerative or traumatic meniscal tears as a surgical treatment for pain relief, yet its impact on the mechanical behavior of the knee joint remains a subject of ongoing research and debate7. While experimental studies on meniscectomy remains challenging, computer simulations are of great potentials in predicting surgical outcomes, and guiding postsurgical rehabilitation8. Finite element models have been used to simulate total meniscectomy and various partial meniscectomy scenarios, to elucidate changes in contact pressures, and stress patterns in the knee joint9–11.

Knee joint geometry and alignment are key factors in influencing knee joint contact mechanics. For example, the contact pressure was found to be sensitive to the size and shape of menisci or meniscal implant12,13. Generally speaking, deviations in knee joint geometry, particularly in the coronal and transverse planes, have been linked to altered load distribution over the articular cartilage, affecting ligament strains and load sharing between the lateral and medial compartments14,15. Geometrical variations in cartilage and meniscus substantially impact knee joint mechanics, with mathematical models of simplified anatomy showing similar trends but differing in detailed contact pressure distribution compared to image-based models16. Malalignment in the coronal, sagittal, and transverse planes can increase the stress and contact pressure on the tibial insert, emphasizing the importance of proper alignment tolerance during knee arthroplasty17,18. Finite element modeling has indicated the importance of accurate geometrical modeling for predicting joint contact pressures and load sharing during weightbearing activities19,20.

While previous studies have elucidated the biomechanics of meniscectomy, discrepancy in their results underscores the intricate nature of knee joint biomechanics and the susceptibility of outcomes to specific parameters. These differences in results may be attributed to the unique knee joint characteristics of the subjects studied. Several model studies have applied statistical shape modeling (SSM) to the meniscus only, in order to understand the impact of meniscal or implant geometry on cartilage degeneration and knee joint mechanics13,21,22. On the other hand, although having not yet been used to study the mechanics of meniscectomy, SSM has been implemented at the knee joint level in different applications to ensure the consistency of other articular geometrical features23–25.

SSM examines all shape variations of organs across a population using principal component analysis, which characterizes shape variations with several principal components. A small number of top-ranked principal components would effectively capture the most morphological distinctions present within the population23,26,27. Therefore, SSM based finite element knee models may depict the big picture of compromised mechanical function of meniscectomized knees for a population and potentially improve the reproducibility of the numerical results, a major concern over computational modeling of the knee joint28.

One advantage of SSM is the capacity of producing a range of possible outcomes for a given scenario that are representative of the population, while a patient-specific model can only provide results that are specific to the individual29. Of particular interests to this research were acceptable ranges and patterns of contact and fluid pressures, and stress distribution across the joint. The combined SSM and finite element modeling approach may increase the reliability and applicability of the modeling results in medical decision making. The objective of the present study was to investigate the impact of various geometrical characteristics of the knee joint on its mechanical behavior following medial meniscectomy.

Results

Results are presented for the maximum contact and fluid pressures at the end of ramp compression, load sharing between the lateral and medial compartments, contact and fluid pressure contours during creep. We investigated the impact of meniscectomy on load distribution for five different knee models: the average, and two for each of the two principal modes considered. Each model involved three different degrees of meniscal resection: no resection or intact knee, partial resection and total medial meniscectomy. Note that the same loading was used for all 15 cases for the purpose of comparison. More results are shown for the lateral tibial cartilage due to its higher contact pressures than the medial tibial and femoral cartilages.

The peak contact and fluid pressures for the femoral and lateral tibial cartilages were summarized for all 15 cases (Figs. 1 and 2). Meniscectomy consistently increased the contact and fluid pressures across all models. For instance, the peak contact pressure in the femoral cartilage of the average knee was 2.41 MPa with intact menisci, which increased to 2.58 (or 7% ↑) and 3.06 MPa (27% ↑), respectively, following a partial and total meniscectomy (Fig. 1a). The pressure increase was of different degrees for different tissues and different knees. For the femoral cartilage, the peak contact pressure for the + 3SD model of principal mode 2 exhibited a dramatic rise from 3.61 to 5.19 MPa (Fig. 1 & S7: intact vs. total, 44% ↑). For the lateral tibial cartilage, however, the largest increase was observed in the − 3SD model of the same principal mode (13% ↑). Meniscectomy caused greater changes in both contact and fluid pressures in mode 2 than mode 1 knee models (Figs. 1 and 2; Modes 1 and 2 reflect, respectively, predominantly the joint size and shape variations).

The lateral compartment normally bears more vertical loading than the medial one under knee extension with the given loading, which is referred to as load sharing asymmetry in this paper (Fig. 3). The load asymmetry escalated with the extent of meniscectomy, with the largest increase observed in the − 3SD model of mode 2. Surprisingly, a medial meniscectomy seemed to cause the least change in load sharing in the average knee model (Fig. 3): the contact force difference was 99 N for the intact knee, and 113 N or 13% increase following total meniscectomy. For all cases considered, the medial meniscectomy caused the greatest change in the − 3SD knee model of mode 2: the difference was 109 N for the intact knee, and 162 N or 49% increase after total meniscectomy. The total vertical force, 400 N, was correctly determined to be shared by the two compartments (Fig. S1, Supplementary Data), indicating good overall numerical results. Nevertheless, their maximum contact pressures were enormously different (Fig. S2).

A more uniform distribution of contact pressure across the lateral tibial cartilage was observed in the intact knee joint (Fig. 4 for mode 1; Fig. S3 for mode 2). The contact pressure became more concentrated and unevenly distributed with the increased degree of meniscectomy. The maximum contact pressures increased by 10–17% after a total medial meniscectomy as compared to the corresponding intact joint. On the other hand, a change in joint shape by three standard deviations, in either positive or negative direction (Average ± 3SD, mode 2), would cause a 30–60% increase in the maximum contact pressures with respect to the average knee (Fig. S3).

The fluid pressure in an inside layer of the lateral tibial cartilage also exhibited a more heterogeneous distribution, or more pronounced regions of elevated fluid pressure, with the degree of meniscectomy on the medial side (Fig. 5 for mode 1; Fig. S4 for mode 2). Note that the pore fluid pressure influences substantially the contact pressure at a fast knee compression. The patterns of contact and fluid pressures were, however, slightly different from each other due to nonuniform stresses in the solid matrix, and different pressurization in a different layer (Fig. 5 vs. 4; Figs. S4 vs. S3). The maximum fluid pressures shown at 1 s increased by 10–19% after a total removal of the medial meniscus as compared to the corresponding intact joint for both modes 1 and 2. However, an increase in joint size (Average + 3SD, mode 1), would lower the maximum contact pressure by approximately 20% (Fig. 4, 1st vs. 2nd column) but reduce the maximum fluid pressure by no more than 6% with respect to the average knee (Fig. 5, 1st vs. 2nd column). In contrast to principal mode 1, a different pattern of variation was observed for mode 2, where both deviation models (Average ± 3SD, mode 2) had much greater contact and fluid pressures than the average knee: approximately 60% and 90% increases, respectively, in the contact and fluid pressures for the + 3SD model (= Average + 3SD), and 30% and 150%, respectively, in the contact and fluid pressures for the − 3SD model (Figs. S3 & S4, using 2nd columns as reference).

The evolution of contact pressure during creep (the period of constant external loading) showed a quick pressure redistribution in the first 60 s when highly localized pressure concentration zones diminished (Fig. 6 for the average knee). As creep progressed, the pressure maps indicated a shift of loading between the two compartments. On the other hand, the intact knee approached a stable pressure distribution faster than the meniscectomized knees, as clearly evidenced by its lower and more uniform fluid pressures at 60–600 s (Fig. S5).

The lateral side consistently showed greater contact and fluid pressures (Figs. 6 and 7, S6-S9). The lateral tibial cartilage also sustained a greater contact pressure than the lateral femoral cartilage (2nd vs. 1st columns of the same figure, Figs. S6 & S7), or the lateral meniscus reduced the contact pressure in the femoral cartilage. The lateral meniscus reduced the maximum contact pressure in the lateral femoral cartilage of the + 3SD models of both principal modes by 30–50%, as compared to that of the lateral tibial cartilage (Figs. S6 & S7). Although a similar role of the lateral meniscus in the fluid pressurization was seen in principal mode 1 (Fig. S8; 20% ↓), it was negligible for the fluid pressurization in mode 2 (Fig. S9; 2–3% ↓).

The intact knee displayed a more uniform contact pressure in both compartments (Fig. 7). The partial meniscectomized knee showed a shift in pressure distribution, with an increased pressure in the lateral compartment and reduced pressure in the medial compartment. The total meniscectomized knee further exacerbated this asymmetry, with pronounced high-pressure areas in the lateral compartment and diminished pressures in the medial compartment.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate the effect of knee geometry on the compromised joint mechanics after partial and total medial meniscectomy. The study demonstrated how SSM depicted all possible outcomes of meniscectomy across different knee geometries. Unlike patient-specific models, SSM can capture the variability across a population. Key findings of the present study include: (1) Intact knee joints, regardless of their size and shape, exhibited the lowest contact and fluid pressures under a given loading, as well as the lowest load sharing asymmetry between the two compartments, as compared to the same joints with partial or total medial meniscectomy (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7, & S2-S9). Moreover, a total medial meniscectomy would elevate the contact and fluid pressures in the vicinity of the contact center more than a partial meniscectomy. The effect of meniscectomy on joint mechanics, however, diminished at long-term creep or equilibrium. (2) Variations in joint shape showed unforeseen results that could not be speculated without computer simulations (Figs. 1, 2 and 3, S1-S4, S7 & S9), although both joint size and shape influenced joint contact mechanics, such as contact areas, contact and fluid pressures. (3) The average knee seemed to experience the lowest contact and fluid pressures as compared to those with a deviation in the knee joint shape from the average shape (Figs. 1 and 2, & S2-S4), indicating a reduced contact area with a change in knee joint shape for the population modeled under the given loading and boundary conditions. These findings are elaborated below.

Any resection of the medial meniscus would increase the maximum contact and fluid pressures within the knee joint (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 & S2-S9), indicating the protective role of the medial meniscus in distributing load. This finding is consistent with previous studies, which have shown that the meniscus plays a critical role in load distribution in the knee joint and impaired or resected menisci may exhibit a contact pressure increase by up to 235%2,30,31. Specifically, our modeling results indicated elevated contact pressures with the total meniscectomy. This increase in contact pressures can accelerate cartilage wear and contribute to the development of osteoarthritis32. On the other hand, the variability observed across the SSM models highlights the significance of knee joint geometry on contact pressure distributions. The range of pressures observed in the ± 3SD models suggests that individuals with different knee geometries may experience varying degrees of biomechanical impact following meniscectomy. This underscores the importance of personalized approaches in clinical decision-making and rehabilitation strategies.

Fluid pressures inside cartilages revealed trends that were similar to, but sometimes distinct from, the contact pressure (Fig. 5, S4, S5, S8 & S9). The intact knee consistently exhibited the lowest fluid pressures (Figs. 2 and 5, S4, S5, S8 & S9), indicating effective fluid pressurization towards a more uniform distribution in its tissues. In contrast, meniscectomy conditions, particularly total meniscectomy, produced elevated fluid pressures in the vicinity of contact centers, which may impair normal nutrient transport in cartilage and increase the risk of cartilage degeneration33. Published studies have indicated altered cartilage mechanics and reduced joint function with intensified interstitial fluid pressures34. The present study quantified the fluid pressure for a range of knee geometries, providing a comprehensive picture of the biomechanical changes associated with meniscectomy. Interestingly, an increase in joint size by three standard deviations (+ 3SD) from the average knee would barely reduce the peak fluid pressure (2–6% ↓), but a decrease in joint size by the same deviation (-3SD) would increase the peak fluid pressure by approximately 60% (Fig. 5; principal mode 1; for the site compared). This asymmetry was not expected before performing finite element analyses.

The load sharing asymmetry indicated a substantial shift in joint load support from the medial to the lateral compartment following medial meniscectomy. This shift was most pronounced in the total meniscectomy, where the lateral compartment bore a disproportionately higher load (Fig. 3). This finding supports previous research that has shown meniscectomy leads to altered load distribution patterns, which can result in lateral compartment overloading and increased risk of cartilage damage35,36. Our computer simulations suggested that the extent of load sharing asymmetry correlated with the degree of meniscectomy. This relationship highlights the compensatory mechanism that the knee joint employs to maintain mechanical function despite structural alterations. However, these compensatory changes may have detrimental effects in the long term, such as accelerated wear and tear in the lateral compartment and the development of osteoarthritis37.

Substantial alterations were observed in the contact pressure of the knee joint with joint size changes. Specifically, the larger + 3SD model of principal mode 1 exhibited a reduction in the maximum contact pressure, indicative of load dispersion across an extended surface area, in contrast to loading concentration over a diminished area in the − 3SD model. These results were expected and consistent with findings in published studies that have shown the sensitivity of cartilage contact stresses to the contact area between the cartilaginous tissues38,39. The size of the knee joint may further affect how the contact pressure is distributed across the articular surface40,41, which was also confirmed in the present study (Fig. 4). Note that principal mode 1 may also include a secondary shape change.

The raised contact and fluid pressures observed in the mode 2 models relative to the average knee could not be simply explained by the joint size (Figs. 1 and 2, S3 & S4). Rather, it indicated the influence of the bone shape and relative positions of the lateral and medial compartments. In the meantime, the contact area in the medial side decreased with the meniscal resection, while the contact area in the lateral side barely changed. These changes explain the increased contact and fluid pressures observed in both compartments after medial meniscectomy (Fig. 6 & S5-S9). These findings underscore the importance of inclusive modeling of various knee geometries in developing therapeutic interventions for a population that aim to counteract the negative consequences of meniscectomy and maintain joint mechanical functions.

A total medial meniscectomy in some knee models caused a greater relative increase in the contact pressure in the lateral femoral cartilage than the mating tibial cartilage (Fig. S7; +3SD model of mode 2), because of a severe compromised congruence in the medial compartment. Menisci experience a smaller contact pressure in the upper than lower surfaces consistent with their shapes. Therefore, a lower contact pressure was observed in the lateral femoral than tibial cartilage for both principal modes 1 and 2 (Figs. 6 and 7). On the other hand, a meniscus might or might not produce a substantial fluid pressure difference between the two mating cartilage layers depending on the knee geometry (mode 1 in Fig. S8 vs. mode 2 in Fig. S9), which is another indication of the significance of finite element analysis based on inclusive knee geometries.

A substantial load redistribution in the joint occurred during the first minute of creep when the maximum contact pressure was substantially reduced (by over 26% for the average knee; Fig. 6) and loading became more uniformly distributed in the contact region (given a constant load such as bodyweight), while the maximum fluid pressure was reduced by more than 50% (Fig. S5). The effect of meniscectomy on the contact mechanics was diminishing at long-term creep (Fig. 6 and S5). These findings support the use of slowly applied loadings during postsurgical rehabilitations to avoid overloading knee cartilage and meniscus, because a slowly applied loading would produce a stress state similar to that of long-term response.

The findings of this study have several important clinical implications. First, agreeing with previously published studies with one or a few individual knees, our results underscore the importance of preserving as much of the meniscus as possible during surgical interventions for a broader population. Partial meniscectomy, while still disruptive, results in less severe biomechanical changes compared to total meniscectomy. These results advocate for the use of meniscal repair techniques whenever feasible to maintain knee joint function and prevent long-term degenerative changes42. Second, the variability observed in the present study indicated the necessity of considering population diversity and recognize various changes including atypical alterations in the load response of compromised knee joints that may not have been reported in the literature. Consequently, individualized treatment plans based on patient-specific knee geometry may be necessary to improve the outcomes of meniscectomy or postoperative rehabilitation. Personalized rehabilitation programs and biomechanical assessments could help mitigate the adverse effects of meniscectomy by addressing the unique needs of each patient43, for example, reduce the load sharing imbalance between the lateral and medial compartments (Fig. 3).

This study has several limitations that must be understood when interpreting the results. The synthetic models were produced from 31 right knees of white men, which may not sufficiently represent a broader population. Yet only the first two principal modes of knee geometry variations were investigated with finite element analyses, which means some contact and fluid pressure and load sharing patterns remain to be determined for the rest of the population. Nevertheless, it is undoubtedly the simplest and most effective way to demonstrate the significance of the methodology using the first and second principal modes, because five additional modes accounted for only additional 21% of the geometry variations (Fig. S10). Another limitation was lack of various meniscectomy cases considered in the present study. The effect of meniscectomy may vary depending on the exact amount and location of meniscal resection, but the present study only modeled two scenarios of medial meniscectomy, possibly missing some important joint mechanical alterations pertinent to additional partial meniscectomy cases. Note that some studies have shown less ideal postoperative outcomes and more elevated contact pressures in the lateral than medial meniscectomy44,45. Lastly, a relatively small loading was used to test the methodology and demonstrate the concept in order to avoid slow convergencies with larger deformation. The trend of mechanical alterations with meniscectomy should remain but the magnitude of alterations would elevate should larger loadings be used due to tissue nonlinearity.

In summary, the novelty of the present study was to determine a more complete pattern of the biomechanical alterations of medial meniscectomy for different knee geometries using statistical shape modeling, as compared to previous studies with individual knees. By leveraging SSM to model knee joint geometry with principal modes, we are able to explore typical or possible variations in knee joint behavior post-meniscectomy with no need to simulate the mechanics for all individual knees. This approach facilitates a more complete description of various joint mechanical alterations with meniscectomy that are applicable to a large population - There is no limitation on the number of knees that can be used for generating synthetic models; the additional time for using a larger population will be mostly in joint reconstructions with MRI – While some results were expected, e.g., the contact pressure increased with decreased joint size, other results could not be speculated without performing finite element analyses (findings 2 and 3). The modeling results highlight the critical role of the meniscus in minimizing contact and fluid pressures and stipulating normal load distributions in the knee joint. These findings emphasize the importance of meniscal preservation and personalized treatment strategies to prevent postoperative joint degeneration. By integrating advanced modeling techniques with clinical practice, we may enhance our understanding of knee joint biomechanics and potentially improve clinical outcomes of meniscal repair.

Methods

Subject-specific knee geometry was independently reconstructed for each right knee joint using its MRI. Synthetic knee models were produced with SSM based on 31 individually reconstructed knees. All tissues were simultaneously processed to ensure consistent interfaces in the synthetic models, e.g., cartilage matched its underlying bone. Fifteen finite element simulations were performed with five synthetic knee models subject to three knee conditions: the intact, partial and total medial meniscectomy. Cartilage and menisci were considered fibril-reinforced poromechanical to model the creep response of the knee joint.

Meniscectomy models

To prepare for shape modeling, geometries of 31 right knee joints were reconstructed individually using MRI data from Caucasian male subjects (55 ± 7.6 years, 80.7 ± 7.7 kg, 176.0 ± 6.9 cm, BMI: 26.1 ± 3.0 kg/m2). The MRI data were sourced from the Osteoarthritis Initiative database (https://nda.nih.gov/oai/). These sequences had dimensions of 384 × 384 × 160 voxels and a sagittal plane resolution of 0.37 × 0.37 × 0.70 mm. The automated MRI reconstruction followed a structured methodology outlined in a published study46.

Statistical shape modelling was then conducted on the 31 subject-specific models to mathematically produce synthetic knee models. The process followed an established SSM protocol for bones47 and adapted it to include soft tissue structures48. It involved establishing nodal correspondence and registration using custom code in MATLAB (R2021b, Mathworks, MA, USA). Principal component analysis was applied to identify principal modes of variation in the knee geometry (Fig. S10), each principal component determined one mode of variation. Synthetic knee models were generated based on the average model of the population by deviating with a number of standard deviations (SD) corresponding to a particular principal mode (Fig. S11). Only the first and second principal modes were considered in the present study, which represented 59% of the total variability in knee geometry of the population (Fig. S10). Mode 1 mainly represented overall knee size variation, while mode 2 predominantly characterized knee shape variation (Fig. S11).

The effect of knee geometry was investigated using five representative synthetic knees: the average, and two deviation models (Average ± 3SD) for each principal mode (5 knee models = 1 average model + 2 models per principal mode × 2 principal modes considered). For each model, the lateral meniscus was intact, while three scenarios were considered for the medial meniscus: the intact condition, partial, and total meniscectomy. Therefore, a total of 15 cases were simulated computationally (= 5 knees × 3 cases/knee). The knee models with both intact menisci served as the baseline for comparison. The partial meniscectomy represented approximately 50% circumferential resection of the inner portion of the medial meniscus including a complete white-white zone and some red-white zone, which was implemented in the finite element model by removing the corresponding part of structured mesh (Fig. S12). The total meniscectomy modeled a complete removal of the medial meniscus.

Finite element modeling

The finite element meshes for all models were generated using IA-FEMesh (University of Iowa, USA). The meshes were structured similarly to those in subject-specific finite element models, which are recognized for their efficacy in pore fluid pressure modeling. To optimize for quicker convergence in fluid pressure-related contact analysis, the cartilaginous tissues were meshed with four layers of 8-node hexahedral elements, instead of tetrahedral elements or 20-node hexahedral elements, as suggested by previous studies11,49. The finite element models of the knee joint varied in their element counts, ranging from 54,236 to 55,800 per joint, contingent on the individual geometry of each model. The mesh was chosen based on a mesh sensitivity analysis of the intact average model (Table S1, & Fig. S13). The finite element simulations were conducted using ABAQUS (Simulia, France), where different elements were employed for various tissues: femur and tibia were meshed using R3D4 elements, ligaments with C3D8, and cartilages and menisci with C3D8P elements. They are, respectively, the elements of 3D 4-node rigid, 3D 8-node solid and 3D 8-node solid with pore pressure. The selection of element types was attributed to the inherent rigidity of bony structures which surpasses that of soft tissues, and the negligible capacity of fluid in tension within the ligaments50. However, the fluid pressurization in the articular cartilage was necessarily considered to model the creep response of knee joint in compression.

The finite element model of knee joint has been previously developed and validated51,52. In these models, bone structures were treated as rigid bodies, while ligaments were depicted as solids reinforced with fibers. The cartilaginous tissues must be considered fibril-reinforced poromechanical to capture the nonlinear behavior boosted by the interplay between fluid pressurization and collagen fibril reinforcement53 or fluid pressure driven fibril reinforcement54. Additionally, the fibrillar property and permeability of these cartilaginous tissues were characterized as being orthotropic. The properties of soft tissues were established by correlating them with values reported in existing literature49. The tissue properties used in the present study were summarized in Table S2.

Boundary and loading conditions

To facilitate understanding the results and avoid the complexity of multiple variables, the knee in full extension (Fig. S12) was simulated to demonstrate the impact of knee geometry on the joint mechanics. The femur was restricted in all rotations and permitted to translate along the lateral-medial, anterior-posterior, and vertical directions, with these translations constrained by the four ligaments. The tibia was fixed in all six degrees of freedom. To model the anatomy of the knee joint while all tissues were individually produced in SSM, TIE constraints were utilized to link the femoral and tibial cartilages, respectively, to their underlying bones, anchor ligaments to bones, and attach the meniscal horns to the tibial plateau. Pore fluid pressures on free surfaces were set to zero, thereby permitting fluid exudation from the tissue.

The soil consolidation procedure in ABAQUS was used in finite element simulations because of the math analogy of creep behavior between fluid-saturated cartilaginous tissues and saturated soil in compression55. To ensure proper contact between the cartilages and menisci, a preload of 30 N was applied at 3 N/s and then maintained for 4000 s48. Subsequently, the load was increased in 1s by 400 N in the vertical direction, 80 N in anterior-posterior direction and 60 N in lateral-medial direction and then remained constant for 2 h (standard creep loading protocol). Multi-direction loading was applied to simulate the forces of the knee joint during daily activities. One purpose of using this type of loading was to help understand the role of menisci in load transmission and stability of the knee joint50.
