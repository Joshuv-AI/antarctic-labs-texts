Transcriptome signature for host directed antiviral reprogramming by Nimba & Triphala in macrophage Dengue virus infection models
Authors: Debayani Chakraborty, R. Namitha, Rahul Roy, K. Lavanya Devi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue virus (DENV) is responsible for hundreds of millions of infections per year, but no clinically approved antiviral therapy exists, and direct-acting candidates are limited by toxicity and viral genetic diversity. Traditional medicinal preparations such as Nimba/Neem (Azadirachta indica) and polyherbal Ayurvedic preparation Triphala (a combination of Terminalia chebula, Terminalia bellirica and Phyllanthus emblica) contain multiple metabolites with antiviral and antioxidant properties but their mechanisms of action are not fully characterised. Here, we combined cell-based antiviral assays with transcriptome profiling to compare how Nimba and Triphala shape host responses during DENV infection in RAW264.7 macrophages, a key innate immune target. Nimba and Triphala demonstrated low cytotoxicity in the concentrations of our assays, which supports a favourable therapeutic window. The RNA-seq analysis showed that Nimba induced broader transcriptional reprogramming in DENV-infected macrophages than Triphala, with 4,182 differentially expressed genes in the NB + DV versus DV comparison, indicating a stronger host transcriptional impact during infection. It increased interferon-stimulated gene (Isg15) programs and attenuated inflammatory signalling (e.g., Cxcl10), oxidative stress (Txnip, Aox4, Phlda3), and endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways (Trib3) upon infection. Triphala, in contrast, caused relatively restrained baseline transcriptional reprogramming and a more selective infection-context response. Docking analysis suggested that Triphala-associated metabolites may engage multiple DENV proteins. In line with this, molecular docking of nine proteins of DENV indicated multi-target binding of Triphala metabolites, with corilagin showing favorable predicted binding affinities (ΔG −13.9 kcal/mol), in certain proteins, superior to that of reference compounds (Remdesivir and NITD008). Together, these data support broader host-response modulation by Nimba and a more selective host response with a possible virus-directed contribution for Triphala during DENV infection and lay the foundational research for synthesis of anti-viral herbal formulations using Nimba and Triphala ingredients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53729-4.

Introduction

Natural products remain as a major source of bioactive chemical diversity and continue to inspire antiviral discovery, particularly where conventional single-target approaches strive for translation into broadly effective therapies. Dengue virus (DENV) is highly dependent on host membranes, redox balance, and immunometabolic state, and infection of innate immune cells can induce a trade-off between antiviral defence and immunopathology. This provides a compelling basis for investigating plant-derived secondary metabolites and polyherbal formulations as sources of direct-acting antiviral scaffolds and host-directed modulators that “pre-condition” cells toward an antiviral, cytoprotective state.

Here, we highlight Nimba (Azadirachta indica A. Juss.) and Triphala (polyherbal formulation of Terminalia chebula Retz., Terminalia bellirica (Gaertn.) Roxb., and Phyllanthus emblica L.) as classical phytochemical interventions1,2 through transcriptome analysis using RAW264.7 macrophages to understand how these treatments reprogram DENV-driven host responses.

Nimba (A. indica) contains numerous flavonoids, terpenoids along with other secondary metabolites and multiple studies have reported activity against DENV targets3 screened against the DENV NS2B-NS3 protease with measurable inhibition of DENV-2 infectivity. In-silico studies have also pointed out that neem triterpenoids as potential binders of NS2B-NS3 protease3. Triphala is a renowned classic Rasayana formulation4(Charaka Samhita, Chikitsa Sthana, 1.3.41-47) and is often discussed in the context of antioxidant and immunomodulatory activities, which are mechanistically relevant to dengue immunopathogenesis (redox stress, cytokine amplification, and stress-induced cell death5. In modern pharmacological literature, Triphala and its constituent fruits are reported to exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory activities, and are frequently studied in cellular systems for gastrointestinal and metabolic indications5,6. Triphala has been experimentally assessed for anti-dengue activity in human cell models where T. bellirica extract and Triphala decreased virus production & reduced inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CXCL-10, with gallic acid implicated as the major constituent and a putative interactor of DENV NS5 in docking analyses7. Polyphenols/phenolic acids contribute to antioxidant and inflammation-modulating effects and possible interplay with viral replication machinery (NS5) in-silico7. DENV replicates on ER-derived membranous structures, triggering the ER stress (intersecting autophagy contributing antiviral control8, activating the unfolded protein response (UPR) through PERK and IRE1 pathways, with context-dependent ATF6 involvement and showing that perturbation of UPR signaling can influence DENV replication9.

The present study uses RNA-sequence based transcriptome profiling in RAW264.7 macrophages to define infection driven transcriptional programs, to determine pretreatment impact with Triphala or Nimba, and identify coordinated immune, oxidative-stress, ER-stress, autophagy, and metabolic pathways that plausibly support antiviral and anti-inflammatory effects. We also used molecular docking as a rapid, structure guided approach to predict if any key constituent can directly engage dengue viral proteins that govern entry and replication. This can be considered as a foundational study that can lead to in-depth inventions to understand the mechanisms of complex chemical set up of natural extracts, which interact collectively against the pathogens.

Materials and methods

Here, ‘Nimba/NB’ indicates a neem extract with their phyto-constituents, and ‘Triphala/TP’ denotes polyherbal preparation rich in the respective phyto-constituents (Phyllanthus emblica, Terminalia chebula and Terminalia bellirica). Dengue virus infected samples are referred to as the Dengue Virus infected (DV), while the Negative Control (NC) represents untreated, uninfected samples.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The Vero E6 (ATCC CRL-1586) and RAW 264.7 (ATCC TIB-71) cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, as previously described. Cells were passaged at 70–80% confluency and routinely confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

Nimba and Triphala extracts

Nimba (NB; a natural extract from Azadirachta indica leaves) and the polyherbal formulation Triphala (TP; comprising Terminalia chebula, Terminalia bellirica and Phyllanthus emblica) were procured as commercial product from I-AIM, TDU, and subjected to ethanol extraction at a 1:20 (w/v) ratio in 70% ethanol under the conditions described previously3,7. Batch and lot information was recorded for reproducibility. Stock solutions were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), filter-sterilized, aliquoted and stored at -20 °C. Immediately before use, stocks were diluted in culture medium; the final DMSO concentration in all treatments, including vehicle controls, was ≤ 0.1% (v/v).

Dengue virus production and titration

Dengue virus serotype 2 (DENV-2; strain 16681) stocks were generated from the pFK-DVs infectious cDNA clone by in vitro transcription followed by transfection into BHK-21 cells, as previously described10. The culture supernatants were collected at 24 h post-transfection, clarified by low-speed centrifugation, aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C. Viral titres were determined either by focus-forming assay on Vero E6 cells or by quantifying genomic RNA copy number via qRT-PCR using a plasmid based standard curve corresponding to the DENV-2 target region.

Cytotoxicity and selection of working concentrations

The cytotoxicity of NB and TP towards Vero E6 and RAW 264.7 cells was assessed by an MTT-based viability assay, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Mosmann, 1983) and our previous protocol10. Dose–response viability data were plotted as cell viability (%) versus concentration and fitted by nonlinear sigmoidal regression using a four-parameter logistic model. For all functional and transcriptomic experiments, we used 12.5 µg/mL NB or TP, a concentration substantially below the CC₅₀ values determined in RAW 264.7 cells.

Antiviral assays in Vero E6 and RAW 264.7 cells

Antiviral activity was assessed in Vero E6 and RAW264.7 cells. Vero E6 cells (0.05 × 10⁶ cells/well, 24-well plates) were treated with NB, TP, individual TP constituents (12.5 µg/mL), corilagin (Merck, G0424) at the indicated concentrations, or vehicle, and antiviral activity was evaluated under both pre-treatment and post-treatment conditions. For pre-treatment assays, cells were incubated with the compounds for 4 h before infection with DENV-2 (MOI 0.05). For post-treatment assays, compounds were added after viral adsorption. Culture supernatants were collected at 48 h post-infection (hpi) for viral RNA quantification by qRT-PCR. RAW264.7 cells were treated for 4 h with increasing concentrations of NB or TP, including 12.5 µg/mL, prior to DENV-2 infection (MOI 0.05), and supernatants were harvested at 48 hpi for qRT-PCR-based viral genome copy number analysis. Dose–response curves were generated from ≥ 2 independent experiments (Table 1).

RNA isolation and quantitative qRT–PCR

Viral RNA quantification

Viral RNA from culture supernatants was extracted using TRIzol, reverse-transcribed with random hexamers (SuperScript IV), and quantified by SYBR Green qRT-PCR using DENV2-specific primers as previously described10, absolute genome copy numbers were calculated from a plasmid standard curve.

Host gene expression analysis

For host transcript quantification, total RNA was isolated from cell lysates using TRIzol reagent, and 1 µg RNA was reverse transcribed with oligo(dT) and random primers. Gene-specific primers for Trib3, Phlda3, Tnfrsf10b, Ifi44, Aox4 and the reference gene β-actin (Table 2) were designed using Primer-BLAST and validated for amplification efficiency. Relative mRNA levels were calculated by the 2–ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the endogenous control. Each sample was run in three biological replicates.

RNA-sequencing experiments

Sample preparation and library construction

For transcriptomic profiling, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at 0.1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates 24 h before treatment. Cells were left untreated (NC), treated with NB or TP alone, infected with DENV-2 alone (DV) (infection control), or pre-treated for 4 h with NB or TP (12.5 µg/mL) before DENV-2 infection, with the corresponding compound maintained throughout infection. Total RNA was harvested 24 hpi (hours post infection) using TRIzol. The mRNA libraries were prepared from poly(A)-selected RNA using a strand-specific protocol, indexed libraries were PCR-enriched, purified, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X plus platform to generate 150 bp paired-end (PE) reads.

Read processing, alignment and quantification

Adapters and low-quality bases (Phred score < 20) were trimmed with Trim Galore11, and read quality was assessed using fastp (v0.23.2), a FastQC12 pre-processor. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using STAR (v2.7.11b)13 with default parameters. Gene-level counts were obtained with feature Counts14 in paired-end (PE) mode, excluding multi-mapping reads.

Differential expression and pathway enrichment

Differential expression was assessed using PyDESeq2, a Python implementation of the DESeq2 framework15,16. Genes were considered differentially expressed when they met the thresholds of |log2 fold change| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05. For heatmap visualization, normalized expression values were transformed as log2(count + 1) and scaled as gene-wise Z-scores across samples and conditions. Gene Ontology (GO) over-representation analyses were carried out using PantherDB, with all expressed genes as background and false-discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing17. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for GO biological process terms was performed using the GSEApy package with pre-ranked gene lists18. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39/mm39) using STAR with default parameters19. KEGG pathway enrichment and visualization were performed using KEGG/KEGG Mapper, and KEGG resources were cited as recommended by Kanehisa Laboratories20–23.

Validation of RNA-Seq results through qRT-PCR

To validate key transcriptomic findings, qRT-PCR was performed on five representative genes spanning ER-stress, apoptotic, and antiviral pathways: Trib3, Phlda3, Tnfrsf10b, Ifi44, and Aox4. RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR were performed as described above, and fold changes were calculated relative to the NC group using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for virological and qRT-PCR assays were performed in GraphPad Prism Ver. 11.043. Pairwise comparisons were carried out using two-tailed unpaired Welch’s t-test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Differential expression analysis was performed using PyDESeq2, a Python implementation of the DESeq2 framework, with a multifactor design, and significance was defined as adjusted p < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. The GO, Reactome and KEGG enrichment was performed using over-representation and GSEA with FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 as significant.

Molecular docking for significant target identification

To investigate a potential direct-acting molecular basis for dengue inhibition, given the comparatively limited transcriptome modulation observed, we performed molecular docking of multiple Triphala-derived metabolites curated from our previous study2 and from PubChem44, against a panel of Dengue virus proteins. This workflow comprised retrieval of the 3D structures of proteins and ligands, preparation of both molecules for docking, and analysis of predicted amino acid interactions. Using nine distinct proteins from Dengue virus including different strains such as Envelope (E) glycoprotein (DENV-2 ectodomain PDBID 1OAN)45, Envelope (E) glycoprotein (DENV-3 ectodomain PDBID 1UZG), NS2B-NS3 protease/helicase (DENV-4 full-length NS3, PDBID 2VBC)46, NS2B-NS3 protease domain (DENV-2 NS2B-NS3 complex, PDBID 6MO0), NS3 helicase with RNA (DENV-2 Helicase-RNA complex, PDBID 5XC6)47, NS3 helicase without RNA (DENV-2 Helicase-RNA complex, PDBID 5XC7), NS5 (DENV-2 Full-length NS5, PDBID 5ZQK), NS5 (DENV-3 Full-length NS5 PDBID 5CCV)48, NS5 RdRp domain (DENV-3 Polymerase domain only PDBID 2J7W)49.

The molecular docking was performed by downloading proteins from the RCSB PDB database (rcsb.org) in PDB format50. Further, the three-dimensional structure from all metabolites of Triphala2,44 and standard drugs were selected for Dengue virus such as Remdesivir51 and NITD00852. These were downloaded from the PubChem database44 in the SDF format, converted to PDB format using Open Babel v.2.4.153. The preparation of Protein and ligand was carried out using AutoDock v.4.2.654 removing water molecules, and heteroatoms and by adding polar hydrogen atoms, the charges were applied using the Gasteiger and Kollman method. The molecular docking was performed using AutoDock vina v.1.2.0.55 where the best metabolite common to all the three constituents with high affinity score and standard drug torsions were fixed to the protein with the grid box parameters 50 × 50 × 50 with 5 Å were set around the active sites. The docking resulted in binding affinity values which are expressed in the negative Gibbs free energy form (delta G, kcal/mol). The molecular interactions of the best docking pose were visualized using PyMoL -v.2.556. The metabolites included in the docking analysis were selected based on our previously published metabolome profiling studies and relevant literature, but were not independently quantified in the exact extract batches used in the present work2,57. This molecular docking was studied as an additional supportive example through in-silico for further exploration of many other significant molecules which can be used as promised dengue viral inhibitors in the future studies.

In vitro bulk fusion assay using DENV-2 prM–Env VLPs

A cell-free bulk fusion assay was performed using DENV-2 prM–Env VLPs and calcein-loaded liposomes to assess whether corilagin affects low-pH-triggered membrane fusion. Liposomes were prepared with POPC: POPG: SM: CH in a 1:1:1:1 molar ratio. Corilagin was added to the calcein-filled vesicle preparation at the indicated concentrations before initiating fusion. Fusion was triggered by acidic pH and quantified as percentage fusion relative to the untreated condition, following the previously described bulk fusion workflow10.

Results

The goal of the present study is to investigate the antiviral potential and host modulatory effects of polyherbal combinations through virological assays and transcriptome profiling. The host responses to Dengue virus (DENV − 2) infection and phytochemical treatments on RAW264.7 mouse macrophages at diverse experimental conditions such as untreated controls (NC), virus-infected cells (DV), and cells treated with Nimba (NB) or Triphala (TP) in the presence or absence of infection were investigated using RNA-Seq analysis. As a prerequisite, infectious virus generated from a full-length cDNA system was validated through qRT-PCR and focus-forming assays prior to downstream analyses. Sub-cytotoxic working concentrations of NB and TP were established using dose–response viability assays in RAW264.7 macrophages, ensuring that subsequent antiviral and transcriptomic observations were not confounded by cytotoxic effects.

Differential expression analyses and downstream pathway analyses were used to identify the key modulations in biological processes such as antiviral, inflammatory, stress-response and immune related pathways in response to TP and NB treatments. Considering the TP results, a molecular docking study was performed for few metabolites supporting their efficiency as antiviral molecules which lays foundation for an explorative future study.

Nimba and Triphala are non-cytotoxic at antiviral working concentrations

To validate the generation and propagation of dengue virus (DENV) generated from the full-length cDNA system (a gift from Ralf Bartenschlager), transfected supernatants were used to infect Vero cells. Infectious virus production was first confirmed by quantitative PCR comparing virus load from the supernatant with the standard curve built and also focus-forming assay using anti-envelope (E) antibody-based detection as described previously10.

The sub cytotoxic working concentration of Nimba (NB) and Triphala (TP) was selected from dose response viability curves generated in RAW264.7 cells, in which cell viability (%) was plotted against extract concentration and fitted by nonlinear regression to estimate the concentration causing 50% loss of viability (CC₅₀). This analysis showed a concentration-dependent reduction in viability for both cases, with CC₅₀ values of 164 µg/mL and 231 µg/mL, respectively, therefore, 12.5 µg/mL, a concentration well below the CC₅₀ for both extracts, was used for all subsequent experiments (Figure S1A).

Vero cells were used for antiviral assays and viral load quantification at 48 h post infection (hpi), whereas RAW264.7 macrophages were used for transcriptomic analysis at 24 hpi (hours post infection) to capture early host immune-response reprogramming in a relevant innate immune cell type. At 48 h post infection, viral RNA in the culture supernatant was determined and reported as normalized viral copy number in comparison to the infected DV control (= 1). In treatment groups, a decrease less than the DV is a sign of the inhibition of infection with the treatment regimen; an analysis of the differences between the effects at the beginning of the treatment (pre-treatment before infection) and at the end of the treatment (post-treatment after infection) is a preliminary indicator of whether the treatment acts on early stages (entry or establishment) or later phases (post-entry replication or assembly).

Nimba and Triphala exhibit antiviral activity against DENV-2 in a pre- and post-treatment-dependent manner

A pre-treatment or post-treatment impact of NB and TP on Vero E6 cells was assessed to understand the effectiveness of the metabolite on which phase of virus lifecycle. For NB, this revealed the ~ 25-fold decrease in the viral load relative to the DV control (quantified by qRT-PCR - Fig. 1A) suggesting the restricted infection. The NB post treatment viral RNA reduction was noteworthy ~ 160-fold, suggesting the anti-viral activity after the virus entry, targeting mostly the post-entry stages of virus infection or infection-amplified host processes (Fig. 1B). Further, a post-infection dose response assay in RAW 264.7 cells demonstrated a clear reduction in viral copy number with increasing NB concentrations (~ 6-fold in 100 µg/ml), consistent with progressive suppression of viral replication in macrophage model (Figure S1B).

Similarly, another polyherbal formulation Triphala (TP), we evaluated pre-treatment and post-infection effects in Vero E6 cells, testing the complete formulation, its individual constituents (Phyllanthus emblica (PE), Terminalia chebula (TC) and Terminalia bellirica (TB)), and the TP + NB combination. In the pre-treatment setting, TP produced a modest (~ 40 fold) and more variable reduction in viral RNA relative to DV Control (Fig. 1 C). In contrast, post-infection TP treatment yielded a lesser decrease (~ 2 fold) in viral copy number, consistent with predominant activity at pre-entry stages (Fig. 1D). The TP + NB combination showed the strongest suppression (Fig. 1E, F). In RAW 264.7 macrophages, a pre-infection dose–response further supported TP activity, with viral copy number declining progressively with increasing TP concentration (~ 8 fold in 100 µg/ml) (Figure S1C). A similar kind of suppression of Dengue viral load was reported in the A129 mice using the pine extract58. In another study, medicinal plant extracts induced post treatment in vitro dose dependent reduction of Dengue and Chikungunya viral load was reported59. A similar viral load reduction in Vero cells was reported with three flavonoids silymarin, baicalein, and baicalin60 boosting the flavonoid rich Triphala as the antiviral agent in the present study.

RNA-seq quality metrics confirm high-confidence transcriptomic data across all experimental groups

The RNA-seq dataset comprised 12 samples across 6 experimental conditions with 2 biological replicates each, generating a total of 243.7 million raw reads. Unique mapping rates to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) ranged from 77.77% to 84.05% across all samples, with a mean mapping efficiency of 81.3% ± 1.9% (Table S1), indicating high-quality library preparation and sequencing. The analysis of RNA-seq sample-to-sample similarity revealed excellent concordance between biological replicates within each condition and distinct separation between experimental groups (Fig. 2A). Pearson correlation coefficients between biological replicates ranged from R² = 0.94 to R² = 0.98, confirming reproducible transcriptional profiles. Principal component analysis (PCA) clearly separated samples by treatment and infection status (Fig. 2B), with PC1 explaining 60% of the total variance and PC2 explaining 12% of the total variance. The first principal component distinguished infected from uninfected conditions, while the second component separated treatment groups, demonstrating that both DENV-2 infection and phytochemical treatments drive substantial and reproducible changes in the macrophage transcriptome.

DENV-2 infection induces acute-phase inflammatory response, ER-stress, and cell death pathways in RAW264.7 macrophages

DENV-2 infection in naïve RAW 264.7 macrophages induced robust transcriptional activation of acute-phase response genes (Lcn2, Saa3, Mmp9, Slpi), pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (Il1a, Ccl2, Ccl22, Cxcl2, Osm), and ER-stress and apoptosis markers (Ddit3, Trib3, Phlda3, Tnfrsf10b), with 1,212 genes significantly altered compared to uninfected controls (Figure S6 A &C). This transcriptional signature aligns with previous reports of DENV-induced pathogenesis involving acute-phase responses, ER-stress, and immune modulation32.

Nimba induces broad transcriptional reprogramming while Triphala exhibits selective baseline modulation in uninfected macrophages

To assess baseline transcriptional effects independent of viral infection, we compared Nimba and Triphala treatments to untreated controls using thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05, which identified 412 DEGs for Nimba versus NC and 44 DEGs for Triphala versus NC, indicating a nearly 10-fold difference in baseline transcriptional perturbation. A comparison of treated and untreated macrophages to distinguish the treatment effects independent of infection proved the occurrences of many of the strongest induced transcripts by Nimba were host-defence and inflammatory mediators (Lcn2, Saa3, Slpi, Mmp9) (Figure S3 A). A clear antiviral-priming signature (Fig. 3A) (including type-I interferon, antiviral-priming signature), like upstream sensors of transcriptional regulators and antiviral, indicating that NB can pre-condition macrophages toward an antiviral state even without viral challenge. NB appears to rewire how the cell handles both inflammation and metabolism. It increased complement and immune effector genes (C3, Cxcl10) and induced immunometabolic regulators such as Acod1, suggesting broader rewiring of inflammatory metabolic coupling at baseline in naive macrophages. Consistent with its reported antioxidant property, NB upregulated multiple redox buffering and detoxification genes, while downregulating stress-associated regulators such as Txnip. The increase in the immune effector genes such as C3, Cxcl10 previously reported as marker of immunopathology32& Txnip gene indicating cytoprotective state41. Our GO-BP enrichment analysis (Figure S3C) reflects these findings. It shows that NB activates innate immunity and antiviral defences while simultaneously strengthening the ability of the cells to handle oxidative stress.

The genes activated by TP involve specifically acute-phase immune mediators (Saa3), interferon and antiviral effectors, and complement components (C3), together with macrophage activation and antimicrobial immune-regulatory markers (Figure S5 A & B). Beyond immunity, TP also modulated immunometabolic and stress-adaptive nodes, including lipid remodelling and iron transport genes and the integrated stress response marker Trib3. On the other hand, TP reduced stress redox-linked and signalling transcripts such as Rasd2. This is consistent with a baseline shift toward controlled redox/inflammatory tone. Our GO-BP enrichment analysis (Figure S5 C) confirms this trend (interferon-linked and complement-associated biology) alongside metabolic and redox-supportive processes.

Nimba pre-treatment extensively reprograms macrophage antiviral responses and attenuates DENV-2-induced pathology

When we infected macrophages already primed with NB, we observed extensive transcriptional reprogramming with 4,182 significantly altered genes (NB + DV vs. DV, |log2FC| > 1, padj < 0.05), reflecting a comprehensive rewiring of the cell’s antiviral response (Figs. 3B and S4A). Antiviral interferon-stimulated genes and complement factors were dramatically amplified, including Ifi44, Ifi44l, Ifi27, Isg15, Irf7, Rsad2, Ifit2, Ifit3, Ifitm3, Oasl1, Oasl2, Oas1q, Oas1g, Oas2, Oas3, Mx2, and C3/C1q subunits, along with the transcription factor Stat1. This is consistent with a robust interferon-driven antiviral state and enhanced capacity for viral RNA sensing and degradation. NB pretreatment markedly attenuated virus-induced cell death and ER stress, expression of death-receptor and p53-dependent apoptotic genes (Tnfrsf10b, Tnfrsf11a, Casp8ap2, Bcl2l11, Trp53inp1, Trp53bp1, Trp53bp2, Phlda3)34,35,37 was reduced in NB + DV relative to DV alone, whereas anti-apoptotic Bcl2a1a, Bcl2a1b and Bcl2a1d37 were upregulated. Classical ER-stress markers associated with the PERK, eIF2α, REDD1 Sesn2 axis (Eif2ak3, Ddit4, Sesn2, Hspa4l) showed reduced induction in NB + DV, consistent with dampening of UPR signalling. Additional decreases in ERCC5, Abl2, Setd5, Stard9 and Tmem170b imply a reduction of virus-induced DNA damage responses, transcriptional instability, vesicular transport disruption and ER dysfunction. The direct comparison of NB + DV vs. NB revealed 4,823 DEGs, demonstrating that Nimba-primed cells elicit a massive antiviral and immunometabolic response specifically upon viral challenge, while NB alone minimally perturbs basal transcription patterns (Figures S7A, B). The relative expression patterns of selected genes across experimental conditions are summarized (Fig. 3C), highlighting distinct transcriptional profiles associated with dengue infection and phytochemical treatment. Nimba also downregulates NFE2L2 (NRF2), a master stress response transcription factor, thus rebalancing cell homeostasis disrupted by virus infection.

Triphala pre-treatment selectively enhances antiviral defence while attenuating inflammatory and endocytic pathways

We next compared TP + DV versus DV, revealing 261 significantly altered genes (|log2FC| > 1, padj < 0.05), indicating that Triphala priming selectively reshapes the DENV-driven transcriptional programme with markedly more focused effects than Nimba (Fig. 4A). DV-induced inflammatory mediators and ER-stress and apoptosis genes were broadly attenuated, whereas innate defence genes were selectively reinforced, with increased expression of ISGs and complement components including Ifi27, Ifi44, Isg15, Oas1a/Oas1b, Oas2/Oas3, Oasl2, Rsad2, Mx1, Rtp4, Irf7 and C3/C125,28 (Figure S8 C). In parallel, reduced expression of endocytic and vesicular trafficking markers (Dnm1, Baiap2, Acap2) is consistent with diminished virus entry via endocytosis (Figure S8 C). The comparison of TP + DV vs. TP showed 1,650 DEGs, demonstrating substantial transcriptional activation upon viral challenge in Triphala-primed cells. GO enrichment for TP + DV vs. DV (Fig. 4B) highlights defence response to virus, type I interferon production, negative regulation of viral genome replication life cycle, glutathione-linked redox processes, and vesicle endocytosis, consistent with a model in which Triphala may promote a combined antiviral and redox-protective state while inhibiting virus infection. Consistent with improved homeostasis, TP further strengthens antioxidant programmes (e.g., Aox4) and reduces stress-associated signatures, including the NRF2, Nfe2l2 axis.

qPCR validation of key ER-stress, apoptosis, and antiviral gene expression across treatment conditions

To validate and expand upon the RNA-seq data, we performed quantitative PCR (qPCR) on five representative genes spanning key biological pathways: Trib3 (ER-stress adaptor), Phlda3 (p53-responsive pro-apoptotic gene), Tnfrsf10b (DR5; extrinsic apoptosis mediator), Ifi44 (interferon-stimulated gene), and Aox4 (aldehyde oxidase involved in detoxification and reactive oxygen species regulation)38(Figs. 3 and 4). In untreated uninfected cells (NC), Trib3 transcript levels were low, but DENV infection triggered a robust ~ 50-fold upregulation. This induction was significantly attenuated by NB or TP pretreatment, resulting in only a modest ~ two-fold increase compared to the negative control (Fig. 3D). Similar trends were observed for Phlda3 and Tnfrsf10b, with highest level in virus-only conditions (several tens-fold for Phlda3 and nearly tenfold for Tnfrsf10b), while their expression was markedly reduced in NB + DV and TP + DV groups, which indicated suppression of p53- and death-receptor–mediated apoptotic pathways (Fig. 4D, F).

On the other hand, Ifi44 expression remained low in NC, NB, and TP samples but was highly upregulated (~ 30 to 40-fold) following DENV infection. Most importantly, its expression remained at high levels in NB-pretreated infected cells and therefore indicating that NB preserves or enhances interferon-driven antiviral responses (Fig. 3E). Finally, Aox4 induction was highest in NB + DV samples, with moderate upregulation in TP + DV, underscoring the idea that NB and to a lesser extent TP potentiates aldehyde and ROS detoxification mechanisms during infection (Fig. 4E).

Integrated model of Nimba and Triphala-mediated host reprogramming during dengue virus infection

KEGG pathway analysis indicated that Nimba treatment modulates key inflammatory signalling pathways during dengue virus infection, including NF-κB, chemokine, TNF, and NOD-like receptor pathways (Fig. 5A). These combined effects are represented in the category-level module heatmap (Fig. 5B). A striking feature of the integrated analysis is the scale of host reprogramming elicited by Nimba compared with Triphala during infection. Whereas NB + DV vs. DV is accompanied by a broad transcriptome-wide shift, thousands of significantly altered genes, for example modules corresponding to pathways such as Antiviral interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), Complement and opsonisation and Immunometabolism (TCA, one-carbon, eicosanoids) exhibited strong positive Z-scores compared with DV, reflecting reinforced antiviral signalling, complement activation and metabolic support for host defence. In contrast, categories within Cytokines/Chemokines (inflammation), Inflammasome and pattern recognition receptors, and ER-stress / apoptosis regulation show attenuated induction. TP + DV vs. DV produces a more selective set of changes, despite showing a similar directional pattern at the module level (enhanced ISG/complement activity with relative suppression of cytokine/chemokine, inflammasome and ER-stress/apoptosis programs). This quantitative contrast indicates that Nimba drives broader transcriptome-level changes across multiple pathway layers compared to the more selective modulation observed with Triphala during infection. Accordingly, Triphala-associated changes were largely confined to dampened inflammatory and stress-injury pathway scores alongside sustained antiviral pathway activity. In line with this, TP + DV also shows coordinated downregulation of endocytosis/vesicle-trafficking markers, with Dnm1 significantly reduced and directional decreases in Baiap2, Acap2, Rabgef1, and Vamp1, consistent with reduced efficiency of productive internalisation and endosomal routing during infection. The integrated antiviral phenotype with transcriptomic and pathway level changes are reflected in the schematic model summarising how Nimba (NB) and Triphala (TP) modulate host responses during dengue virus infection in macrophages. KEGG pathway mapping indicates that Nimba re-organizes early pathogen-sensing and inflammatory circuits in early dengue infection, limiting maladaptive chemokine secretion as well as NF-kB–mediated cytokine amplification while preserving host defense signaling. In comparison, Triphala-responsive genes in infected macrophages are mostly localized to viral RNA-sensing and interferon-associated signaling nodes (Fig. 5D, S11-14).

Comparative docking against reference antivirals indicated strong predicted binding of metabolites of Triphala & significance of Corilagin

In support of anti-viral activity of Triphala, a molecular docking study was performed on the metabolites reported in the previous study2. The comparatively modest transcriptome-wide reprogramming induced by Triphala suggests that its antiviral activity is not driven solely by broad host-state rewiring and is consistent with an additional possible virus-directed contribution.

From molecular docking studies, the favorable predicted binding affinities were observed for most of the Triphala metabolites, as they exhibited highest binding affinity in comparison with the standard drugs (Remdesivir and NITD008). The multiple docking was performed for all the nine viral proteins viz., 2VBC, 2J7W, 5CCV, 1UZG, 1OAN, 5XC6, 5XC7, 5ZQK, 6MO0 against the Triphala metabolites and their binding affinity values indicated (supplementary Data, Table S2).

One of the top Triphala-associated metabolites, corilagin, exhibited the highest affinity against all nine viral proteins with docking scores (ΔG = − 10.2 to − 13.9 kcal/mol), exceeding those of the standard molecules Remdesivir (ΔG = − 7.2 to − 9.9 kcal/mol) and NITD008 (ΔG = − 6.8 to − 9.8 kcal/mol), with significant amino acid interactions with respect to the standard drugs (Supplementary File 2; Fig. 6B, C and Figure S10). The consistently favorable docking scores across structurally distinct DENV targets identify corilagin as a candidate metabolite for further investigation. Representative ligand-protein complexes for 1OAN and 5xC7 as presented in the main Fig. 6B, C, where corilagin (yellow) clusters within the same canonical cleft as Remdesivir (red) and NITD008 (green) and the remaining seven complexes (5XC6, 1UZG, 2VBC, 6MO0, 2J7W, 5CCV, 5ZQK) are shown in Figure S10A-G.

For the protein 5CCV (Figure S10F), the binding affinity was stronger with a stable ligand-protein interaction complex than the standard drug, identifying corilagin as a candidate metabolite with favorable predicted binding. The binding sites of 5XC6 and 6MO0 relied on the same site as that of corilagin for the standard drugs (Figure S10A, D). This overlap with reference-compound binding regions is consistent with engagement of potentially relevant ligand-binding sites, strengthening the mechanistic plausibility of the in-silico hits.

These docking results placed corilagin among the top candidate metabolites in this analysis for acting against viral activity, along with a few other metabolites from Triphala. Notably, metabolites from Phyllanthus emblica (PE), including caulophyllogenin, lupenone, friedelin, bacosine, anemosapogenin, and norbuprenorphine, exhibited greater binding affinity than corilagin. Although a few molecules showed higher docking affinity, corilagin emerged as a stable and consistent molecule, supporting its relevance as a candidate metabolite for further study as a key bioactive constituent in Triphala through the present in silico analysis. As docking ranks alone are insufficient evidence of activity, we prioritised corilagin, which showed consistent predicted binding across all nine targets, for further validation.

To further examine whether a Triphala-associated metabolite could contribute to the antiviral phenotype, we evaluated corilagin in Vero E6 cells. Corilagin reduced viral RNA output under both pre-treatment and post-treatment conditions, with stronger inhibition observed at 10 µM in both settings and a clearer effect of 5 µM in the pre-treatment condition (Fig. 6D, E). Activity under pre-treatment exposure indicates that corilagin constrains replication after viral entry, and is therefore not restricted to an entry-blocking mode. Dose response fitting yielded IC₅₀ values of approximately 3.77 µM and 7.58 µM for the pre-treatment and post-treatment conditions, respectively, while the estimated CC₅₀ in Vero E6 cells was ~ 13.38 µM (Fig. 6A and Figure S1 D). These values indicate antiviral activity at concentrations below the estimated cytotoxic range in Vero E6 cells. In addition, in vitro bulk fusion assays using DENV-2 prM-Env VLPs showed a concentration-dependent reduction in low-pH-triggered fusion in the presence of corilagin (Fig. 6F). This biophysical readout is consistent with an effect on an early envelope-mediated fusion-associated step and converges with its favourable docking against envelope-associated structures (Fig. 6B; Figure S10B) and replication-machinery targets (Fig. 6C, Figure S10A, C-G). These observations provide experimental support for a Triphala-associated antiviral component and are consistent with a possible contribution at an early entry or fusion-associated step. Together, the docking, cell-based antiviral, and bulk-fusion data support corilagin as a Triphala-associated antiviral candidate for further study.

Discussion

Dengue virus (DENV) remains a major global health problem. It is estimated that there are approximately 390 million infections annually, with a significant proportion of them developing clinically apparent disease61. Despite decades of work, a specific antiviral drug against dengue has not yet been widely approved and clinical treatment remains mostly supportive62,63. A major challenge for direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) is a small treatment window, the necessity to have activity against a wide range of serotypes, and is often combined with the problem of cytotoxicity, pharmacokinetics, and viral resistance62. These realities make a strong case in favor of host-directed approaches that inhibit viral replication as well as mitigating host inflammatory injury that results in severe disease64.

A central feature of dengue pathogenesis is that DENV infection of innate immune cells, particularly macrophages may result in imbalance in that DENV infection induces antiviral signaling, whereas the presence of excessive cytokines and chemokines, stress pathways, and cell-death programs that drive vascular leakage and severe dengue65. In our study we used RAW 264.7 cells to evaluate dengue virus response. In parallel, DENV infection is closely associated with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) as it replicates on ER-derived membranes, thus when infected, ER stress, unfolded protein response (UPR) signaling, and autophagy-related pathways are commonly activated, thereby contributing to the replication efficiency as well as inflammatory damage9. Another common signature of dengue infection is oxidative stress and the NRF2 redox network has also been demonstrated to influence antiviral, inflammatory and survival responses to infection66.

Within this framework, our study supports Nimba (Azadirachta indica) and Triphala (Phyllanthus emblica, Terminalia chebula and Terminalia bellirica) as host-modulating antiviral candidates in macrophages. At sub-cytotoxic concentrations, both extracts reduced DENV RNA when used either before infection or after viral entry, consistent with prior reports that these traditional formulations can show antiviral activity in cell systems3,7. Significantly, our transcriptome data showed that the extracts do not act by simply shutting down cellular transcription. Instead, they selectively rearrange the infection response in a selective manner where antiviral programs (notably interferon-stimulated genes, ISGs) remain active, while inflammatory and stress-injury pathways are suppressed. Nimba and Triphala were used at much lower doses than the cytotoxic levels in order to minimize cellular cytotoxicity and not to disrupt important cellular functions.

Severe dengue is strongly linked to immunopathology, rather than just viral load. Clinical and mechanistic reports of Dengue fever include high expressions of inflammatory mediators (i.e. IL-6, TNF-related signals, and chemokines like CXCL10)65 and dysregulated innate immune activation are also observed in our transcriptome response. Our data suggest that Nimba and Triphala drive macrophages to a state that is still antiviral but less prone to cytokine response, less ER-stress, and less apoptotic-priming, which is a favorable host-directed dengue therapy.

Nimba showed the most pronounced interaction with infection, building a host response in infected macrophages that was clearly different from its effect alone. It was previously reported in silico that Nimbin, a metabolite from Nimba, inhibits Envelope protein3. In our study, we found that in macrophages the prevalent signature is one of host reprogramming by increased production of antiviral modules and decreased production of inflammatory and ER-stress responses and activation of the antiviral immune response, especially the interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs).

Triphala showed a different mode of action. In our study, Triphala induced relatively minimal baseline transcriptional change, yet upon infection induced a shift in response towards the antiviral defence program and diminished inflammatory stress programs. This is compatible with prior experimental studies in human cell models (Huh-7) demonstrating that Triphala formulations (or constituent components) have the potential to reduce dengue infection and decrease inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and7. Additionally, in our data, there is a decrease in endocytosis or vesicle-trafficking-associated signatures with Triphala during infection that is conceptually compatible with inhibition by early viral lifecycle stages (entry and trafficking) or by decreased productive infection. This also further explains that the calibre of Triphala to disrupt dengue entry or early replication stages such as early virus life cycle.

A key implication of the combined findings is the fact that such natural products, Nimba and Triphala can decouple direct virus inhibition to host mediated response in macrophages. DENV commonly results in adverse conditions in which innate sensing leads to chemokine/cytokine amplification and stress signaling that injures host tissues65. Our results suggest a pronounced shift in macrophages toward a more self-protective antiviral cell state. ISG and innate defence modules remain active, while ER-stress and apoptosis-linked markers decrease.

Finally, it is important to view Nimba and Triphala as multi-component interventions. Natural extracts typically contain diverse metabolites with partially overlapping functions, some may weakly inhibit viral proteins, while others modulate inflammation, redox balance, or protein-folding stress62. For neem, nimbin has been suggested to interact with envelope protein, and neem bioflavonoids have been examined against NS2B–NS3 protease, consistent with a possible virus-directed contribution67. For Triphala, experimental work involves the constituent chemistry in Huh-7 cell (e.g., gallic acid-rich fractions) with post-entry inhibition and NS5 docking, which is compatible with a possible virus-directed component7.

From a chemical perspective, the literature indicates that all these extracts have a range of metabolites that can not only block viral proteins (such as NS2B-NS3 or NS5), but also deal with inflammation. Therefore, this antiviral effect is probably a multi-target, multi-component effect instead of one major mechanism3. Nimbin is an active metabolite that has been reported to react with the dengue virus envelope protein3, whereas corilagin, a compound present in Triphala formulation, has been reported to prevent various phases of dengue infection, with a predominant effect on viral replication. Corilagin predicted favourable binding across multiple DENV targets (Fig. 6), with the most pronounced engagement observed for the NS3 helicase RNA complex (5XC7, 5XC6) and additional predicted binding to NS5 and NS2B-NS3 proteins, collectively supporting a plausible mechanism by which Triphala may contribute to restriction of viral entry or replication-associated processes even in the absence of extensive host transcriptome remodelling. Predicted interactions with the envelope (E) glycoprotein further indicate the possibility of a role in the initial stages of infection (entry and fusion), which is consistent with the decreased endocytosis or vesicle-trafficking signature found between the TP + DV and DV. Corilagin was the most consistent Triphala metabolite in our docking analysis, with favorable predicted binding across all nine DENV targets and, in several cases, occupying the same sites as the reference drugs. Docking alone does not prove antiviral activity, but our cellular and bulk-fusion data strengthen this case. Corilagin reduced viral RNA in both pre- and post-treatment conditions and inhibited DENV-2 prM–Env fusion in a host-free assay, suggesting that at least part of its activity targets the early entry/fusion step, although post-entry effects cannot be ruled out. Together, these findings suggest that Triphala may combine selective host modulation with a metabolite-associated virus-directed component against dengue.

Together, these findings support a dual-action framework in which Triphala couples selective host-protective modulation (dampened inflammatory and stress-injury programs with preserved antiviral competence) with a plausible multi-target, metabolite-driven constraint on viral entry and replication, thereby limiting viral burden while reducing host cell injury.

Notably, corilagin also exhibits anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity68 which supports its utility as a therapeutically active antiviral ingredient against dengue. This is conceptually aligned with the host-directed framework that targeting convergent vulnerabilities such as membrane-associated replication, stress adaptation, and innate immune setpoints can lead to robust restriction while lowering the likelihood of rapid viral escape. Nimba and Triphala used in the present study were based on formulations that had been previously profiled by LC-MS/HPLC, which provided a rationale for selecting metabolites for the docking analysis. Accordingly, the docking results are presented here as supportive, literature-guided evidence to complement the biological findings, while not serving as direct batch-specific quantification of metabolite abundance in the tested extracts. In addition, the transcriptomic associations remain correlative, and targeted perturbation experiments, such as ISG knockdown, UPR pathway inhibition, and endocytosis perturbation, will be required to establish causality. The study was also performed in a single macrophage cell line and with one DENV serotype, and broader validation will be required in additional experimental systems. Because transcriptomic profiling was not performed for the NB + TP combination, the stronger antiviral effect observed for the combined treatment could not be mechanistically resolved in the present study and remains an important direction for future work. Overall, the research together forms a platform to the comprehension of the molecular Host-defence processes of the multi-component systems e.g., Ayurveda or any other conventional polyherbal formulations. In the case of dengue virus, when Nimba and Triphala are taken into consideration, the anti-viral effect with the differential expression of host defence associated genes upregulation and immuno-pathology associated genes downregulation and reports the same transcripts evidence here is the classical pilot attempt. This will help in the future in designing the drugs for early and later stages of dengue infection with in-depth understanding of the chemistry and chemical networking of significant groups of metabolites.
