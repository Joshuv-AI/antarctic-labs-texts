Need-standardised comparisons are required before inferring sex and gender bias in major trauma care
Authors: Pieter Fouche
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

We read with interest the scoping review by Ghika-Nanchen and colleagues on sex and gender bias in major trauma care [1]. The review brings together an important literature and identifies plausible equity concerns in trauma systems, particularly around trauma-centre access, triage, transfer pathways, tranexamic acid use and haemorrhage care. These issues deserve serious attention.

However, some of the review’s language moves further than much of the included evidence can support. The abstract states that women were more likely to be “under-triaged and under-treated”, and the conclusion argues that observed disparities “are not fully explained by differences in injury mechanism or severity” and appear to reflect modifiable features of trauma systems [1]. These claims may be true, but they require stronger need-standardised evidence than many of the included studies provide.

The central issue is comparability. A health-care disparity is not simply any observed treatment difference between groups. It is a treatment difference that remains after patients are made comparable on legitimate clinical need, treatment preference and appropriateness of intervention [2, 3]. In major trauma, comparability requires more than adjustment for age, mechanism, Injury Severity Score (ISS) or Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Trauma decisions depend on the information available at the decision point, including suspected bleeding risk, physiology, injury pattern, entrapment, anticoagulation, frailty, comorbidity, pregnancy-related issues, prehospital course, local capability, transport distance and protocol thresholds. If these domains are absent, poorly measured or measured after the decision, residual sex/gender coefficients are difficult to interpret as bias or undertreatment.

The review itself gives reasons for caution. It is a scoping review, no formal critical appraisal was undertaken, and the evidence base was largely observational and retrospective [1]. The authors also note high risk of selection bias, confounding, generalisation bias, overfitting, collider bias and sex misclassification across the observational literature [1]. At the same time, the review documents substantial differences between men and women in age, mechanism, injury pattern and anatomical injury distribution. Men were more commonly injured in high-energy mechanisms, assault, penetrating trauma, motorcycle crashes and motor vehicle crashes, while women were older and more commonly injured in ground-level falls, self-inflicted injury, pelvic injury and spinal cord injury [1]. These are not minor nuisance variables. They are core determinants of triage, transfer and early treatment.

Nor does statistical adjustment automatically solve this problem. ISS is a retrospective anatomical severity measure and is too coarse to standardise the actual clinical presentation that paramedics, emergency clinicians or trauma teams faced. GCS captures neurological status, not overall trauma need. Composite prognostic scores, including TRISS-type measures, may incorporate variables measured after early triage, transfer or resuscitation decisions, depending on the dataset and timing. Causal interpretation of regression coefficients depends critically on temporal ordering, the adjustment set and the estimand being targeted [4, 5]. Penalised regression or Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator methods may reduce overfitting, but they cannot determine whether a variable is a legitimate clinical-need variable, mediator, collider or consequence of prior care. That decision requires clinical and causal reasoning, not variable selection alone [3–5].

This is also relevant to the trial evidence cited in the review. CRASH-2, CRASH-3 and CRYOSTAT-2 were randomised trials of trauma interventions, not randomised trials of sex or gender. Randomisation protects the treatment-versus-control comparison within those trials. It does not make men and women exchangeable. Their sex-disaggregated analyses can inform whether the randomised treatment effects appeared similar in men and women, but comparisons between men and women remain observational and do not by themselves establish sex/gender bias in trauma care. For tranexamic acid, the trial evidence helps show that benefit appears broadly similar by sex, while the disparity question mainly arises from routine-care registry evidence about who receives treatment.

This distinction matters for the review’s recommendations. We agree that future studies should be prospective and that trauma systems should audit sex/gender differences in access and time-critical treatment. We also agree that sex and gender should be measured and reported with greater precision, rather than relying uncritically on binary administrative categories [6]. But audits and future studies should be framed around need-standardised comparisons. They should define the decision point, specify the clinical information available at that time, measure pretreatment need and treatment eligibility, avoid inappropriate downstream adjustment, and account for clustering by site, clinician and trauma system.

The strongest current signals appear to be trauma-centre access and routine-care tranexamic acid use, particularly where studies attempted to account for injury severity, bleeding risk or treatment eligibility. Even there, the most defensible conclusion is that women may experience need-standardised disparities in some trauma-system processes, not that the literature has proven sexism or gender bias as the cause. For transfusion, fluid resuscitation, surgery and analgesia, the evidential standard should be even higher because indications depend on body size, bleeding source, physiology, contraindications, treatment opportunity, survival time and local resources.

The review’s contribution would be strengthened by a clearer separation between crude sex/gender differences, need-standardised disparities, and discrimination or bias as a mechanism. The first is well demonstrated. The second is suggested in some areas but not consistently established. The third remains plausible and important, but is rarely proven by routine-care observational studies. Precision here is not semantic caution. It is necessary if trauma systems are to identify the right modifiable failures and avoid building policy on comparisons that have not made men and women clinically comparable.
