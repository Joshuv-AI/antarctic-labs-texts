Disulfiram Prevents Blood-Brain Barrier Disruption in Epileptic Mice by Inhibiting Astrocyte Pyroptosis to Improve Seizure Activity
Authors: Mulan Zhang, Linqian Zhao, Shihao Chen, Wenting Huang, Xing Jin, Huiqin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy is a prevalent neurological disorder whose pathogenesis is closely associated with blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption. Emerging evidence suggests that astrocytic pyroptosis may play a pivotal role in BBB impairment. Based on this, we propose two central hypotheses: (1) seizure activity triggers astrocytic pyroptosis and exacerbates BBB damage; (2) pharmacological inhibition of pyroptosis can restore BBB integrity and alleviate epileptic symptoms. This study established a kainic acid-induced mouse model of epilepsy and an in vitro BBB model. Cell pyroptosis was induced using LPS and ATP. The expression of BBB and pyroptosis-related proteins was assessed through Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and RT-PCR. Additionally, a pyroptosis inhibitor, disulfiram (DSF), was used to inhibit pyroptosis, and the therapeutic effects on epilepsy were evaluated through behavioral experiments, electroencephalography, and electron microscopy. BBB damage and pyroptosis were observed in the hippocampal tissue of mice following seizures, with immunofluorescence results indicating the occurrence of pyroptosis in astrocytes. In vitro experiments demonstrated that astrocyte pyroptosis leads to the downregulation of tight junction proteins in endothelial cells. Inhibition of pyroptosis by DSF improved the integrity of the BBB and resulted in reduced severity of seizure activity and improved cognitive function in mice. This study reveals the role of astrocyte pyroptosis in seizure activity and the consequent BBB damage. Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the five major neuropsychiatric disorders prioritized for prevention and treatment by the World Health Organization, affecting over 70 million individuals worldwide [1, 2]. Frequent and severe epileptic seizures can result in brain injury, which in turn may lead to cognitive impairment and various psychiatric and behavioral abnormalities [3]. Historically, it was believed that the pathogenesis of epilepsy was primarily attributed to ion channel dysfunction; however, current research has shifted its focus towards exploring the progression of epilepsy, including neurogenesis, neuroinflammation, glial proliferation, and the disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [4]. Among these, the BBB has garnered particular attention due to its critical role as a protective barrier within the central nervous system [5].

The BBB is formed by tight junctions between endothelial cells in the brain [6], accompanied by astrocytes, pericytes, and neurons in the periphery [7]. This structure effectively maintains the chemical composition and peripheral environment essential for normal neural function [8, 9]. Animal studies have indicated that various induced epilepsy models, such as those created using kainic acid (KA), piracetam, or electrical stimulation, exhibit BBB disruption that can persist for several hours to days. Additionally, BBB damage has also been observed in brain tissue excised from patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. Conversely, structural or functional impairments of the BBB may also promote the progression of epilepsy and accelerate seizure activity [10]. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the mechanisms underlying BBB disruption during the onset and progression of epilepsy.

In recent years, pyroptosis has emerged as a novel form of programmed cell death, characterized by inflammatory responses and the formation of pores in the cell membrane, leading to the release of cellular contents, a process mediated by the pore-forming protein GSDMD [11, 12]. Research has shown that when pyroptosis occurs, the activation of inflammatory responses can compromise the integrity of the BBB, establishing a significant association between pyroptosis and BBB disruption [13–15].

Consequently, this study proposes a central hypothesis: GSDMD-mediated astrocytic pyroptosis is a key pathological mechanism underlying blood-brain barrier disruption in epilepsy. To test this hypothesis, we employed in vivo and in vitro experimental systems to systematically investigate the following scientific questions: (1) whether seizure-induced astrocytic pyroptosis directly mediates BBB dysfunction in epilepsy, and (2) if pharmacological inhibition of pyroptosis can effectively preserve BBB integrity and reduce epileptogenesis in both in vivo and in vitro models.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The experimental animals were healthy male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 weeks and weighing 25 ± 2 g. The mice were provided by the Experimental Animal Center of Wenzhou Medical University and were housed under standard conditions, with a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50–60%, and a light/dark cycle of 12/12 h. They had free access to food and water and were allowed to acclimatize to the environment for two weeks prior to the commencement of the study. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (Permit Number: WYYY-AEC-YS-2023-0144) and were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Establishment of KA-Induced Epilepsy Model

The KA-induced epilepsy model in mice exhibits pathological characteristics similar to those observed in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) [16]. Initially, the mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a 4% pentobarbital sodium at a dosage of 10 mL/kg, and subsequently secured in a stereotactic frame. The coordinates for the injection site relative to the bregma were as follows: AP: −2.0 mm, ML: −1.8 mm, DV: −1.8 mm. The injection site was targeted at the hippocampal CA3 subregion in mice, an established approach that has been widely adopted in current research practice [17, 18]. A total of 2 µL of KA solution (400 ng/µL) was injected at a rate of 1 µL/min, followed by a five-minute retention of the needle to prevent reflux of the drug along the injection track. Mice in the sham surgery group received an injection of 2 µL of saline solution. This model requires only a single KA injection to establish the epileptic phenotype.

The severity of the seizures was assessed using the Racine classification scale, which categorizes seizures into five levels: (1) rhythmic movements of the mouth and facial muscles; (2) head nodding; (3) unilateral limb clonus; (4) generalized clonus or tonic–clonic seizures; (5) loss of postural control with generalized tonic–clonic seizures, characterized by repeated standing and falling episodes. Successful modeling was defined as the occurrence of seizures rated at or above level 4 on the Racine scale; these mice were included in subsequent studies.

Primary Astrocyte Culture

As previously described, primary cortical astrocytes were cultured and purified [19]. In brief, astrocytes were harvested from the brains of neonatal mice within 24 h after birth. The bilateral cerebral cortices were collected in ice-cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Gibco, USA), and the meninges and blood vessels were carefully dissected under a microscope, with the hippocampus being isolated subsequently. The hippocampal tissue was then minced and digested with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco) at 37 °C for 20 min. Following digestion, DMEM complete culture medium was added to terminate the enzymatic process. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm filter and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to obtain a cell pellet, which was resuspended in 12 mL of complete medium (DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin). This suspension was then cultured overnight in T75 cell culture flasks pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (PDL, Sigma, USA). After 24 h, the medium was changed to remove non-adherent cells. Thereafter, the medium was replaced every 3 days until the cell density reached approximately 90%. To harvest the purified astrocytes, the cells were transferred to a 37 °C incubator on a shaking platform, where they were shaken at 250 rpm to remove contaminating cells. After 24 h, floating cells were washed away using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Adherent cells were then digested at room temperature with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA for 3 min. The resuspended astrocytes were seeded in cell culture dishes for subsequent experiments.

Establishment of Astrocytic Pyroptosis Model

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and ATP are classical reagents used to induce pyroptosis [20]. LPS, as a bacterial lipopolysaccharide, can activate immune cells, while ATP functions as an extracellular signaling molecule that further activates the inflammasome, leading to the occurrence of pyroptosis. When primary astrocytes reached approximately 80% confluence, they were randomly divided into Control, LPS, and LPS+ATP groups. In the LPS and LPS+ATP groups, 500 ng/mL LPS was added and incubated for 12 h, while the LPS+ATP group received an additional treatment of 5 mM ATP for 30 min. For our in vitro experiments, each trial was conducted as an independent biological replicate.

In Vitro Simulation of the BBB Microenvironment

Mouse brain microvascular endothelial cells (Bend3) were obtained from Shanghai Fantai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. In the previously established astrocytic pyroptosis model, after completing the induction, the stimulation medium was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Residual PBS was aspirated, and fresh complete medium was added for continued culture for 24 h. The resulting supernatant was designated as the conditioned medium from astrocytic pyroptosis. This conditioned medium was then added to an appropriate density of Bend3 cells for culture, and after 24 h, the cells were collected for subsequent experiments.

Western Blot Analysis

Mouse hippocampal tissue or cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF and protease inhibitors (1:1:100). An appropriate volume of loading buffer was added, and the samples were heated at 100 °C for 10 min to prepare protein samples. These protein samples were subsequently separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking with 5% BSA, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Following this, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added, and incubation was performed at room temperature for 1.5 h. The blots were visualized using an ECL western blotting detection kit and analyzed with Image J software. The primary antibodies included NLRP3 antibody (1:500, Novus Biologicals), GSDMD antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), Caspase-1 antibody (1:2000, Proteintech), ZO-1 antibody (1:5000, Proteintech), Claudin5 antibody (1:1000, Abcam), and Occludin antibody (1:5000, Proteintech). The secondary antibodies consisted of Goat anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP (1:10000, Zen-Bioscience) and Goat anti-Mouse IgG-HRP (1:10000, Zen-Bioscience). All target protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software for grayscale analysis and normalized to the β-actin loading control to correct for loading variations. For intergroup comparisons, the data were ultimately expressed as standardized values relative to the normalized levels of the control group.

Immunofluorescent Staining of Frozen Sections

Following standard PBS perfusion, the mice were subjected to cardiac perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were then immersed in 30% sucrose for 3 days and embedded in OCT. Subsequently, sections were cut at 25 µm using a cryostat. The slices were blocked at room temperature with 5% BSA for 1 h. The tissue sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA. On the following day, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 1 h in the dark with secondary antibodies. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Image acquisition was performed using a Leica D8IM microscope (Leica, Germany), with the light source used as the excitation source to ensure clear fluorescence signals. The images were captured at a magnification of 20× to clearly present the hippocampal tissue structure. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software, with all analyses conducted under the same settings to ensure data consistency. For each animal, 3 hippocampal slices were obtained, with the selection criterion based on the CA3 region of the hippocampus. One region of interest (ROI) was selected from each image, chosen based on its representative location in the brain area. The ROI size was 640 × 640 µm to ensure sufficient cell coverage for reliable analysis. These technical details have been described in detail in the Methods section, and we strictly followed standardized protocols in the experiments to ensure data accuracy and reproducibility. The primary antibodies included GFAP antibody (1:400, Cell Signaling Technology), NeuN antibody (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology), CD31 antibody (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology), GSDMD antibody (1:800, Cell Signaling Technology), Claudin5 antibody (1:200, Abcam), and Occludin antibody (1:200, Proteintech). The secondary antibodies used were Donkey anti-Rabbit AlexaFluor488 (1:800, Abcam) and Donkey anti-Goat AlexaFluor647 (1:800, Abcam).

DSF Administration

Disulfiram (DSF), a small-molecule pyroptosis inhibitor, effectively suppresses GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis [21]. In cell-related experiments, primary astrocytes were randomly divided into Control, LPS+ATP, and LPS+ATP+DSF groups. In the LPS+ATP+DSF group, DSF was administered (prepared in DMSO at a concentration of 100 ng/mL) following the completion of the pyroptosis model induction until the conditioned medium was collected.

In animal-related experiments, mice were randomly assigned to the Sham, KA, and KA+DSF groups. After the completion of the modeling surgery but before spontaneous epileptic seizures, mice in both the KA+DSF and KA groups received intraperitoneal injections of either DSF (dose: 50 mg/kg, prepared at 12.5 mg/mL concentration in sesame oil) or an equivalent volume of vehicle, respectively. The treatment regimen continued for three consecutive days, after which the animals were euthanized for subsequent experimental analyses.

Electrode Implantation and Local Field Potential Recording

Electrode implantation was conducted following previously published protocols [22]. We did not perform EEG analysis on all animals, but rather conducted experiments with six mice per group (Sham, KA, and KA+DSF groups). During the establishment of the KA epilepsy model, three cortical electrodes were implanted in the mouse skull at the following coordinates relative to the bregma: anterior 1.37 mm and right lateral 1.23 mm; posterior 2.15 mm and right lateral 1.98 mm; and lateral 6.14 mm and right lateral 0.85 mm. The implanted electrodes were secured using dental cement. Due to the 7-day baseline stabilization period required for newly implanted cortical electrodes, EEG recordings in epileptic mice began on day 7 post-KA injection. Continuous EEG monitoring was conducted for 2 weeks (from day 7 onward) to capture chronic-phase seizure activity. In contrast, video monitoring using night-vision high-definition digital cameras commenced on day 1 post-KA injection to behaviorally assess seizure progression. Throughout the recording period, mice were allowed free movement in the monitoring cage with ad libitum access to food and water. EEG signals were acquired using a specialized small-animal EEG system (ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia), with signal conditioning including bandpass filtering (0.1–100 Hz), 1000× amplification, and digitization at 1 kHz sampling frequency. EEG data were analyzed and quantified using LabChart 8 software. Spontaneous seizures were defined as evolving spike-wave discharges with a frequency > 2 Hz, amplitudes at least three times baseline, and a duration of ≥15 s [23], with seizure counts performed by experimenters who were double-blind to the group assignments.

Behavioral Experiments

Behavioral experiments were conducted referencing previous work [24]. Briefly, the Morris water maze test was performed in a circular pool with a radius of 50 cm, containing a platform with a radius of 6 cm. The testing consisted of two phases: the acquisition phase (also known as the orientation navigation test) and the probe trial (spatial exploration test), lasting for 5 days. The orientation navigation test spanned 4 days, with one test conducted daily, comprising four trials per session, and the starting position randomized among four predetermined points. On the fifth day, the probe trial was conducted with the platform removed. The swimming trajectories of the mice were recorded using the DigBehv-MWM Morris computer tracking system (Jilian Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). During the orientation navigation test, swimming speed and escape latency were recorded to assess the navigational performance of the mice. In the probe trial, the number of crossings over the platform location within 1 min was recorded to measure spatial cognitive function.

Electron Microscopy

Subcellular events associated with pyroptosis include the formation of pores in the plasma membrane and nuclear membrane, as well as swelling and rupture of mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles [25, 26]. Therefore, transmission electron microscopy was employed to investigate the ultrastructural damage of astrocytes in the hippocampal region. In brief, fresh hippocampal tissue was cut into small cubes (1 mm3) and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for over 4 h. Subsequently, samples were fixed in 1% OsO4 in phosphate-buffered saline for over 2 h, dehydrated through a gradient of ethanol and acetone, and embedded in Epon 816 (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA). Specimens were sectioned using an RMC Boeckeler PowerTome XL ultramicrotome, and the sections were stained with uranyl acetate or lead citrate for 5 to 10 min. TEM images were captured using a Hitachi transmission electron microscope (model H-7550, Tokyo, Japan).

Evans Blue Staining

Evans Blue (EB) extravasation was used to evaluate BBB permeability [27]. Seventy-two hours after KA injection, mice were intravenously administered EB solution (4 ml/kg, 2% in saline, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai) via the tail vein and allowed to circulate for 2 h. Under deep anesthesia, intravascular dye was cleared by transcardial perfusion with ice-cold PBS. Whole-brain images of EB staining were captured for each group, after which the ipsilateral hemisphere (excluding the olfactory bulb and cerebellum) was dissected and weighed. Tissue samples were homogenized and incubated in formamide at 60 °C for 72 h. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged (15,000 rpm, 30 min), and the supernatant absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometric fluorometer at excitation/emission wavelengths of 610 nm and 680 nm, respectively. EB concentration was quantified based on a linear standard curve and expressed as micrograms per 100 mg of brain tissue.

Statistical Analysis

Normality tests were conducted using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests were utilized when both normal distribution and homogeneity of variance assumptions were satisfied. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was applied if both normality and homogeneity of variance were met; otherwise, Welch’s ANOVA was employed if only normality was achieved. In cases where the data did not conform to normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized. Results are presented as the mean ± SEM, with p-values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Kainic Acid-Induced Epileptic Phenotypes and Experimental Design in Mice

A total of 110 C57BL/6 J mice were used to establish the KA-induced epilepsy model, with seizure severity assessed using the Racine scale. Consistent with previous studies [28, 29], the first epileptic seizures occurred 50–100 s after KA injection. Eight mice that showed no response or failed to meet the Racine scale criteria were excluded from subsequent experiments. To evaluate post-KA mortality, we plotted a survival curve. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, mortality was primarily concentrated within the first 1 day after KA administration. Excluding those utilized for experimental purposes, a total of 12 mice were excluded from the study due to seizure-related mortality. Surviving mice exhibiting severe seizures (Racine stages 4–5) were randomly allocated for further experiments. The exact number of mice used in each experimental group is detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Disruption of the BBB and Pyroptosis in Mice Following KA-Induced Seizures

To ascertain the relationship between pyroptosis and seizures, we first examined the temporal expression of GSDMD, a key executor of pyroptosis. The results demonstrated a notable upregulation of the active form of GSDMD-N protein in the hippocampal region on days 3 to 21 post-KA induction, while no significant changes were observed in GSDMD-FL (Fig. 1A, B). As GSDMD-N is activated by Caspase-1 recruited by NLRP3, we concurrently assessed the expression levels of both proteins. It was evident that the expression levels of NLRP3 and Caspase-1 in the KA-treated hippocampal region were significantly higher compared to the sham group during the same timeframe (Fig. 1A, B). These results indicate that pyroptosis occurs in the hippocampal tissue of mice following seizures.

Tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and Occludin have been utilized in previous literature to assess the extent of BBB disruption [30, 31]. Following KA-induced seizures in mice, we employed Western blotting to determine whether the BBB was compromised. Compared to the sham group, a significant downregulation of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin was observed in the hippocampal region on days 3 to 21 post-status epilepticus (Fig. 1C, D). These findings suggest that BBB disruption occurs in the hippocampal tissue of mice following seizures. Given that significant BBB damage was evident by day 3, we selected this time point for subsequent experiments.

Pyroptosis of Astrocytes in the Hippocampus Following KA-Induced Seizures

To identify the cell types undergoing pyroptosis, we performed immunofluorescent co-staining using NeuN (neurons), GFAP (astrocytes), and CD31 (endothelial cells) as cell-specific markers.In the seizure group, immunofluorescence revealed neuronal loss in the hippocampal CA3 region compared to the sham group. Notably, GSDMD-positive cells did not co-localize with NeuN (Fig. 2A, D), suggesting that pyroptosis was not observed in neurons under these conditions. In contrast, GFAP-positive astrocytes exhibited both increased expression and significant co-localization with GSDMD in the seizure model (Fig. 2B, D). Notably, endothelial cells (CD31 +) showed no evidence of pyroptosis post-seizure (Fig. 2C, D). Quantitative analysis further confirmed a marked increase in GSDMD/GFAP co-localized cells 3 days after KA induction (Fig. 2E). These findings indicate that astrocytes, but not neurons or endothelial cells, undergo pyroptosis in the hippocampal CA3 region following seizures.

Astrocyte Pyroptosis Can Lead to Endothelial Cell Damage

Astrocytic end-feet establish direct contact interfaces between glial cells and endothelial cells, which play a crucial role in maintaining the barrier function of brain microvascular endothelial cells and regulating cerebral blood flow [32]. To investigate whether astrocyte pyroptosis contributes to BBB disruption, we created an in vitro model using primary mouse astrocytes and Bend3 mouse brain vascular endothelial cells to simulate the in vivo BBB. Following the induction of astrocyte pyroptosis by adding LPS and ATP to the culture medium, we subsequently administered the conditioned medium to the endothelial cells for culture followed by Western blot analysis.

Initially, we successfully established an astrocyte pyroptosis model, as evidenced by significant upregulation of key pyroptotic markers including NLRP3, caspase-1, and active GSDMD fragments (cleaved GSDMD) as detected by Western blotting (Fig. 3A, B). To investigate the potential impact of astrocyte pyroptosis on endothelial function, we collected conditioned medium from these pyroptotic astrocytes and applied it to Bend3 endothelial cells. Subsequent analysis revealed marked downregulation of critical tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin in the endothelial cells (Fig. 3C, D). These findings demonstrate that: (1) our model effectively recapitulates the molecular signature of astrocyte pyroptosis through activation of the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, and (2) pyroptotic astrocytes release factors that can compromise endothelial barrier integrity by disrupting tight junction protein expression. Together, these results strongly suggest that astrocyte pyroptosis may contribute to cerebrovascular dysfunction through paracrine-mediated endothelial damage.

DSF Significantly Ameliorates Endothelial Cell Damage Induced by Astrocyte Pyroptosis

DSF has been previously reported to inhibit pyroptosis and the release of inflammatory cytokines [33]. To further validate whether the inhibition of astrocyte pyroptosis can prevent endothelial cell damage, we treated astrocytes with DSF in vitro. Compared to the ATP group, the levels of NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD precursor, and activated GSDMD were significantly downregulated in the DSF-treated group (Fig. 4A, B). This indicates that DSF effectively suppresses the occurrence of pyroptosis. Furthermore, upon treating endothelial cells with conditioned medium from DSF-treated pyroptotic astrocytes, we observed a significant increase in the levels of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin5 compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 4C, D), suggesting that DSF can ameliorate endothelial cell damage caused by astrocyte pyroptosis.

DSF Improves Cognitive Dysfunction and Seizure Activity in Epileptic Mice by Inhibiting BBB Disruption

To determine whether DSF exhibits similar effects in in vivo experiments, we administered DSF or an equivalent volume of vehicle via intraperitoneal injection following KA modeling. Subsequently, behavioral tests were performed at the time points specified in Fig. 5A. Western blot analysis revealed that DSF significantly reduced the levels of cleaved GSDMD, NLRP3 and Caspase-1 (Fig. 5B, C). Electron microscopy results indicated that astrocytes in the sham group exhibited intact plasma and nuclear membranes, with normal and abundant mitochondria. In contrast, KA-treated cells showed swelling, vacuolization of organelles, ruptured mitochondria, and disrupted plasma and nuclear membranes, starkly contrasting with the sham group. However, astrocytes from DSF-treated mice displayed intact plasma and nuclear membranes, with clearly visible mitochondria, and the specific features have been marked with color-coded arrows (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results indicate that DSF can inhibit astrocyte pyroptosis following seizure induction in mice. We also assessed the expression levels of tight junction proteins in the hippocampal tissue, revealing that DSF treatment significantly upregulated ZO-1 and Occludin, which were downregulated due to seizures (Fig. 5E, F). These findings suggest that inhibiting astrocyte pyroptosis following seizures can significantly ameliorate BBB disruption.

To explore whether DSF can improve the prognosis of chronic seizure episodes by inhibiting BBB disruption during the latent period, we analyzed the EEG of the mice. The EEG manifestations of seizures in mice are characterized by the emergence of sharp waves and polyspike activities [34]. We assessed the severity of seizures by synchronizing video monitoring with EEG signals. Compared to the sham group, the baseline in the SE group was significantly elevated, and after DSF administration, both the frequency and amplitude of seizure-associated spikes were reduced (Fig. 6A). Additionally, statistical analyses of seizure frequency, incidence, and duration revealed a significant reduction in seizure scores following DSF treatment (Fig. 6B), with a decrease in the duration of each epileptic seizure(Fig. 6C) and total seizure counts (Fig. 6D). Thus, we conclude that the pyroptosis inhibitor DSF can significantly ameliorate the severity of chronic seizures in mice. Furthermore, we evaluated cognitive function changes in mice treated with DSF using the water maze experiment. The results from the spatial exploration test indicated that DSF administration improved the trajectory changes in KA-induced epileptic mice, with a significant increase in the number of platform crossings within 60 s (Fig. 6E, F). The results during the training period also showed that after administering DSF, the epileptic mice were able to locate the platform more easily, particularly on days 3–4(Fig. 6G). Finally, we evaluated BBB integrity using Evans blue extravasation assays in the epileptic hemispheres of mice across all three experimental groups. Our findings revealed that epileptic induction caused significant BBB disruption, as demonstrated by enhanced Evans blue leakage. Importantly, DSF treatment effectively mitigated this BBB damage by suppressing astrocytic pyroptosis, resulting in substantially reduced dye extravasation compared to untreated epileptic controls (Fig. 6H, I). Meanwhile, the hippocampal tissue stained with Evans Blue showed visible blue injection needle holes, indicating that our modeling was successful. In addition, a DAPI stained image shows that the injection trajectory entered the CA3 area of the hippocampus (Supplemental Figure S1B,C). In summary, these experimental findings suggest that DSF can mitigate cognitive dysfunction and seizure activity in mice by alleviating BBB damage.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate whether GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis is involved in the pathogenesis of TLE. Our results demonstrate the presence of GSDMD-mediated astrocyte pyroptosis in mice subjected to KA-induced seizures, suggesting it may be one of the mechanisms underlying BBB disruption. Furthermore, we found that DSF can alleviate BBB damage and improve seizure activity by inhibiting GSDMD-mediated astrocyte pyroptosis, indicating its potential as a novel therapeutic agent for epilepsy.

Current research indicates a vicious cycle exists between seizure activity and BBB dysfunction. On one hand, seizures are accompanied by varying degrees of BBB impairment, which can manifest as morphological alterations, vascular leakage, and abnormalities in neurovascular structures in the epileptogenic regions of epilepsy patients [10]. Astrocytes and endothelial cells, as critical components of the BBB, work together to establish and maintain its integrity while also contributing to its disruption during central nervous system inflammation and infection [35]. On the other hand, BBB disruption can accelerate the occurrence and progression of seizures [36]. Animal studies have shown a positive correlation between BBB permeability and the frequency of seizures in chronic epilepsy models; artificially inducing BBB openings using mannitol leads to increased seizure frequency [37]. This suggests that BBB leakage occurs during the chronic stage of epilepsy and contributes to its progression. In human epilepsy patients, BBB impairment or dysfunction can accelerate the onset of seizures, promote seizure activity, and result in recurrent seizures [5]. Moreover, the latest research suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis may also affect the gut–brain axis by regulating immune responses, leading to neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier damage, and thus participating in the occurrence and progression of epilepsy [38–40]. Our study observed a downregulation of tight junction proteins following SE induction, indicating BBB damage. Additionally, pyroptosis was noted three days after KA modeling. Pyroptosis is a novel form of cell death, and our findings show that the pore-forming protein GSDMD-N plays a crucial role in this process [41]. Although we observed a significant increase in the N-terminal level of the pyroptosis-related protein GSDMD three days after KA modeling, the full-length GSDMD did not exhibit significant changes. This does not imply that the full-length GSDMD level did not increase; instead, it may reflect a dynamic equilibrium where GSDMD-FL, increased post-pyroptosis, is cleaved by Caspase-1 into its active form.

Our results indicated that pyroptosis mediated by GSDMD occurred selectively in astrocytes. As one of the most abundant glial cell types in the central nervous system, astrocytes help maintain BBB integrity by secreting various cytokines and signaling molecules that regulate tight junctions between endothelial cells. Conversely, the retraction or separation of astrocytic end-feet can increase BBB permeability [32]. Research by Lyu et al. indicated that astrocyte pyroptosis induced by brain ischemia/reperfusion may lead to dysfunction in the lymphatic system of the BBB [42]. Furthermore, the occurrence of pyroptosis in glial cells is quite prevalent, significantly exacerbating BBB damage and further amplifying the degree of brain injury [43].

Specifically, several potential mechanisms may explain how pyroptotic astrocytes contribute to BBB damage. First, the overexpression of multidrug transporters and multidrug resistance proteins may enhance the ability of substances to cross the BBB, thereby compromising its integrity. Second, reduced expression of glutamate transporters may hinder glutamate buffering, adversely affecting BBB stability. Additionally, the reduction of perivascular AQP4 channels may impair K+ buffering, further compromising BBB function. A decrease in the expression of inwardly rectifying potassium channels may also exacerbate K+ buffering deficiencies. Lastly, impaired gap junctions between astrocytes could further magnify the K+ buffering insufficiency, leading to the loss of BBB function [36, 44, 45]. Collectively, these factors contribute to the enhanced damage that pyroptotic astrocytes inflict on the BBB.

Previous studies have confirmed that inhibiting GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis can alleviate the severity of seizures and astrocyte injury in mice [46]. As an already approved clinical drug, DSF has a favorable safety profile and minimal side effects compared to other pyroptosis inhibitors; a study demonstrated the safety of DSF in vivo treatment by monitoring body weight and measuring the function of major organs in mice [47]. On one hand, it can reduce the release of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor and IL-1β via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, thereby mitigating inflammatory responses and inhibiting pyroptosis [33]. On the other hand, a key process in pyroptosis is lysosomal rupture; instability of the lysosomal membrane can lead to the release of tissue cathepsin B, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and inducing pyroptosis [48]. DSF can protect lysosomal membranes and reduce the release of cathepsin B, thus alleviating inflammatory responses [49]. In summary, DSF can inhibit the occurrence of pyroptosis through multiple pathways. Overall, our study is the first to demonstrate that astrocyte pyroptosis contributes to BBB damage following KA-induced SE in the hippocampus. We also provide the first evidence that inhibiting astrocyte pyroptosis can effectively ameliorate BBB damage, with DSF showing potential as a therapeutic agent that can prevent further BBB disruption and reduce the severity of seizures.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the phenomenon of astrocytes undergoing pyroptosis while simultaneously being activated and proliferating remains difficult to explain. Secondly, the in vitro model simulating the blood-brain barrier is overly simplified and does not accurately reflect the material exchange processes in the microenvironment. Finally, this study has not elucidated the specific pathways through which astrocytic pyroptosis causes blood-brain barrier damage, and it overlooks the bidirectional communication mechanisms between seizure activity, endothelial cell, and glial cell activation—current research primarily focuses on the impact of pyroptotic glial cells on the blood-brain barrier.

Based on the findings of this study, future work will focus on addressing the following key scientific questions: At the mechanistic level, first, it is necessary to validate the causal and essential role of GSDMD-mediated astrocytic pyroptosis in blood-brain barrier disruption by constructing cell-specific knockout models. Secondly, the direct mechanisms by which seizure activity impacts endothelial integrity should be systematically explored, and the feedback regulatory effects of changes in the vascular microenvironment on neuroglial function should be elucidated. Finally, proteomics and other techniques should be employed to identify characteristic effector molecules released during pyroptosis, revealing their specific downstream pathways that disrupt the tight junctions of the vascular endothelium. At the translational level, the characteristic effector molecules identified in this study not only provide candidate biomarkers for the precise diagnosis of epilepsy, but also point to pyroptosis-targeting therapy as a novel therapeutic strategy for drug-resistant epilepsy.

Conclusion

After a seizure, the blood-brain barrier is disrupted, and astrocytic pyroptosis is a target affecting the destruction of the blood-brain barrier. DSF inhibits astrocytic pyroptosis through the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, thereby improving the destruction of the blood-brain barrier. At the same time, through this mechanism, DSF can alleviate the severity of seizures and cognitive dysfunction. In summary, DSF may be a potential anti-epileptic drug.
