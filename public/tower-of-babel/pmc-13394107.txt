Pylorus‐Preserving Gastrectomy for Gastric Cancer: Current Evidence and Future Directions
Authors: Sa‐Hong Kim, Hyuk‐Joon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPylorus‐preserving gastrectomy (PPG) is a function‐preserving surgery for clinically T1N0M0 (cT1N0M0) early gastric cancer (EGC) located in the middle third of the stomach, at least 4 cm proximal from the pylorus. By preserving the peri‐pyloric tissues, including the hepatic branch of the vagus nerve and the infrapyloric vessels, PPG maintains physiological pyloric function without compromising oncologic safety. Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated the functional advantages of PPG. In particular, the Korean multicenter RCT (KLASS‐04) reported a lower incidence of gallstone formation, bile reflux, and postoperative nutritional deficiencies. While PPG is technically demanding and associated with concerns regarding delayed gastric emptying (DGE), recent advances in minimally invasive surgery and intraoperative assessment tools, including augmented imaging technology using indocyanine green (ICG), have facilitated its safe implementation. Although PPG is currently indicated for cT1N0M0 EGC, emerging evidence suggests that patients pathologically confirmed as advanced disease postoperatively may still achieve favorable outcomes with standard adjuvant therapy without requiring additional surgery, suggesting potential oncological acceptability of PPG in carefully selected patients with advanced disease. The integration of robotic platforms and navigation‐guided visualization with augmented imaging technology may further enhance oncologic safety and technical feasibility. This review aims to outline the current evidence and future directions of PPG, focusing on the feasibility of extending its application beyond its traditional indications.

Introduction

Nationwide gastric cancer screening programs in Japan and Korea have led to early diagnosis, resulting in improved clinical outcomes [1, 2, 3]. Given the favorable prognosis of early gastric cancer (EGC), pylorus‐preserving gastrectomy (PPG) offers a balance between oncologic radicality and the preservation of physiological function [4].

PPG was first performed for gastric ulcer treatment [5], and was noted to reduce the frequency of postoperative complications associated with conventional distal or total gastrectomy, such as dumping syndrome, bile reflux esophagitis, and nutritional deficiencies [4]. These advantages are attributed to the preservation of the hepatic branch of the vagus nerve and the maintenance of infrapyloric blood supply to the pylorus and antral cuff [6, 7]. Consequently, PPG has been recommended as a standard procedure for cT1N0M0 EGC patients who meet specific criteria, with its oncologic safety and functional advantages more objectively validated by several randomized controlled trials (RCTs), particularly from East Asian countries, including South Korea [6, 8, 9].

Meanwhile, recent emerging evidence suggests that patients confirmed as having advanced gastric cancer (AGC) postoperatively may still achieve a favorable prognosis when treated with standard adjuvant chemotherapy without requiring completion surgery [10]. The growing use of minimally invasive surgical techniques, including laparoscopic and robotic surgery [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16] combined with advances in intraoperative assessment modalities such as gastroscopy [17] and indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence‐navigated imaging, a near‐infrared fluorescence technique used to visualize blood flow or lymphatic drainage during surgery [18], has further enhanced the oncologic safety and technical feasibility of PPG, and is expected to broaden its future indications and clinical applicability of PPG.

While previous studies have addressed specific aspects of PPG, comprehensive reviews incorporating recent evidence and emerging technologies remain limited. This review provides a comprehensive overview of PPG, focusing on preoperative considerations, surgical techniques, and functional and oncologic outcomes. In addition, it further explores the potential expansion of PPG indications beyond EGC to selected AGC cases, supported by robotic platforms and navigation‐guided visualization with augmented imaging technology.

Preoperative Considerations for PPG: Indications

According to the Korean and Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines, PPG is indicated for cT1N0M0 EGC located in the middle third of the stomach, provided that the distal end of the tumor is at least 4 cm away from the pylorus to ensure both function preservation and oncological safety [19, 20].

Firstly, in terms of function preservation, minimizing manipulation around the pylorus is crucial. The right gastric artery, which supplies the suprapyloric area (lymph node station number 5; LN#5), runs alongside the pyloric branch of the vagus nerve. Earlier studies demonstrated that shorter antral cuffs are associated with higher rates of delayed gastric emptying (DGE) [21], while preserving an approximately 2‐cm suprapyloric buffer zone without direct manipulation helps prevent nerve injury and maintain pyloric function.

Secondly, in terms of oncologic safety, PPG requires balancing meticulous infrapyloric lymph node dissection (LN#6) with the preservation of the infrapyloric vessels. LN#5 dissection was typically omitted in PPG, supported by evidence showing a low therapeutic index in middle third gastric cancer (LN#5: 0.8 vs. LN#6: 3.9) [22], in which the therapeutic index represents the product of metastatic frequency and the survival benefit of lymph node dissection, and a low incidence of lymphatic spread to LN#5 in appropriately selected patients (LN#5: 0% vs. LN#6: 0.7%, for cT1‐3 N0/1 M0) [23]. Unlike LN#5, meticulous LN#6 dissection remains mandatory [24], but preservation of the infrapyloric vessels inevitably raises concerns about incomplete lymph node dissection. Therefore, PPG is only to be indicated for cT1N0M0 to ensure limited lymphatic spread to LN#5 and LN#6.

Preoperative Evaluation for Optimizing the Surgical Plan

Japanese criteria recommended a minimum distance of 4 cm from the pylorus to the distal end of the tumor [15], and subsequent evidence in Korea suggested a refined criterion [24].

Preoperative gastroscopy is essential for the accurate assessment of tumor location and extent [25], and for marking the tumor margins with metal clips. This procedure facilitates optimal surgical planning, while intraoperative gastroscopy further provides real‐time confirmation of the tumor margins and the gastric transection line [17].

Contrast‐enhanced CT is a fundamental preoperative study for gastric cancer patients, providing information on tumor extent, regional nodal status, and distant metastasis [26]. Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) is helpful for assessing the depth of invasion and perigastric nodal involvement [27], though its diagnostic accuracy varies across studies, with reported understaging rates of approximately 7.4%–10% [10, 28]. Therefore, the combined use of CT and EUS is recommended to improve overall preoperative staging accuracy.

Surgical Techniques for PPG: Preservation of the Pylorus

PPG starts with an omentectomy, followed by lymph node dissection with ligation of the left and right gastroepiploic vessels (LN#4sb and LN#4d). Careful dissection is mandatory to preserve the infrapyloric vessels at the LN#6 area. A Japanese anatomical study reported that the infrapyloric artery (IPA) arises from one of three sources: The gastroduodenal artery (proximal type, 12.7%), the anterior superior pancreatoduodenal artery (distal type, 64.2%), or the right gastroepiploic artery (caudal type, 23.1%) [29]. A Chinese observational study reported IPA origins as proximal (36.8%), distal (31.0%), and caudal (27.2%) [30]. In a post hoc analysis of a Korean multicenter RCT, the distribution was proximal (38.0%), distal (23.4%), and caudal (38.5%) [31]. Therefore, the ligation point of the right gastroepiploic artery root depends on the origin of the IPA.

The suprapyloric area (LN#5) should be handled cautiously to avoid injuries to the hepatic branch of the vagus nerve; therefore, the right gastric vessels are ligated after branching off the first or second arcade. LN#7, #8a, #9, and optionally #11p, are dissected with ligation of the coronary vein and the left gastric artery. Since the hepatic branch of the vagus nerve usually originates above the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ), the right paracardial LN (LN#1) can be adequately dissected at or below the GEJ level without sacrificing this nerve branch [32], followed by dissection of the lesser curvature LNs (LN#3a and 3b).

Adequate antral cuff and resection margins from the tumor are confirmed by intraoperative frozen biopsy [33] to optimize sufficient remnant stomach volume [34]. Extracorporeal gastro‐gastrostomy is typically performed with a hand‐sewn, end‐to‐end anastomosis, which re‐establishes continuity between the proximal and distal gastric remnants [35]. Similar to an intracorporeal delta‐shaped gastroduodenostomy in laparoscopic DG, a triangular stapled anastomosis technique widely used in minimally invasive gastrectomy [36], an intracorporeal delta‐shaped gastro‐gastrostomy [37, 38], and another intracorporeal variation known as the ‘piercing method’ have been adopted in clinical practice [39].

Surgical Advantages

By preserving the pyloric sphincter, PPG enables a more gradual and regulated passage of food, reducing rapid transit symptoms often observed in other types of gastrectomy [4]. The oncologic feasibility of PPG has been supported by a meta‐analysis [40] and a Korean multicenter prospective RCT [8], both demonstrating no significant differences in complication or recurrence rates compared to DG. Key advantages of PPG include reduced postoperative complications, improved gastrointestinal function, favorable nutritional outcomes, and enhanced quality of life. These benefits are closely interrelated and mutually reinforcing, warranting a comprehensive assessment with an integrated perspective.

Postoperative Complications

Overall postoperative outcomes after PPG are favorable compared with DG, although DGE remains an important functional concern.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that PPG provides better functional outcomes, including less body weight loss, reduced bile reflux, lower incidence of anastomosis leakage [40, 41], lower rates of dumping syndrome [42], and less gallstone formation [8] compared to DG.

DGE is often diagnosed based on clinical manifestations such as postprandial epigastric fullness, dyspepsia, or early satiety, supported by imaging modalities including a plain radiography or an upper gastrointestinal series (UGIS). During the early experience with PPG, the reported incidence of DGE was as high as 40% [43]. More recent series of papers have shown substantially lower rates ranging from 5.6% to 21.6% [44, 45, 46, 47].

In a Japanese retrospective study, early postoperative gastric stasis (E‐stasis) was associated with age, diabetes mellitus, and postoperative intra‐abdominal infection. Late postoperative food residue (L‐residue) at 1 year postoperatively was associated with male sex, age, diabetes mellitus, body mass index (BMI), and antecedent E‐stasis [48]. In addition to patient factors, several surgical contributors to DGE after PPG have been reported, including injuries to the hepatic branch of the vagus nerve, the infrapyloric artery [44], the infrapyloric vein [49], and a short antral cuff length [21]. In a Korean RCT, these surgical contributors were effectively minimized through procedure standardization and surgeon qualification, yet DGE still occurred more frequently after PPG than DG (16.3% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.001) [8]. In a secondary analysis of the same trial, a prior history of abdominal surgery was the only factor associated with a reduced risk of DGE (p = 0.026), suggesting that less manipulation during surgery may contribute to better postoperative gastric motility [50]. Another analysis from the same cohort demonstrated that IPA anatomy itself was not associated with DGE [31].

As a preventive measure, gentle intraoperative manual dilation of the pylorus has been associated with a lower rate of early stasis [51], and several therapeutic interventions have been reported for pyloric dysfunction after PPG. Bae et al. [52] demonstrated significant improvements in symptom scores and in the pyloric canal‐to‐vertebral height ratio after initial balloon dilatation (BD) (p < 0.001, retrospectively). In another retrospective study, first‐line BD showed a success rate of 41.7% [53]. Botulinum toxin injection into the pylorus has been reported to be an effective method for symptom relief [54]. Gastric per‐oral endoscopic myotomy (G‐POEM) has recently emerged as a minimally invasive alternative, showing comparable outcomes to surgical pyloroplasty [55]. In addition, in an investigational porcine model, Park et al. evaluated the role of temporary biodegradable pyloric stents for maintaining temporary pyloric patency after PPG [56] (Table 1).

Nutrition

Functional preservation after PPG is reflected in improved postoperative nutritional status.

By preserving gastric acid and gastrin secretion and maintaining the physiological duodenal passage, PPG supports efficient digestion and nutrient absorption, particularly for iron. These physiological advantages of PPG are reflected in superior postoperative nutritional outcomes, including better preserved hemoglobin and serum protein [8], a higher serum albumin level [40], greater postoperative body weight [57], and preservation of skeletal muscle mass and overall nutritional status [58] (Table 2).

Quality of Life (QoL)

Several studies suggest improved postoperative quality of life after PPG, although this effect may be influenced by DGE.

Questionnaire‐based assessments of postoperative QoL after PPG indicate meaningful benefits in various functional and symptom‐related domains. In a large‐scale investigation of 1 153 patients undergoing various types of gastrectomy, PPG demonstrated the lowest incidence of dumping syndrome [59]. A nationwide survey of 2 368 patients from 52 institutions using the Postgastrectomy Syndrome Assessment Scale‐45 (PGSAS‐45), a validated questionnaire assessing post‐gastrectomy symptoms and quality of life, demonstrated that PPG was associated with fewer dumping symptoms and less diarrhea compared with DG [57]. Consistently, PPG showed significantly better global, functional, and symptom scales than DG based on the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30 (EORTC QLQ‐C30) and Stomach Module 22 (EORTC QLQ‐STO22) [60]. In contrast, a recent observational study has reported no significant differences in postoperative body weight changes or overall QoL between PPG and DG [61], while a secondary analysis of a Korean RCT demonstrated that DGE, which occurs more frequently after PPG, adversely affects QoL [50] (Table 3).

Oncologic Outcomes of PPG: Feasibility in Advanced Gastric Cancer

A systematic review encompassing 21 studies confirmed that PPG achieves comparable long‐term survival and recurrence rates to DG, despite its inherently limited lymphadenectomy, possibly owing to the omission of LN#5 dissection [40]. A Korean retrospective study of 701 patients undergoing curative TG or DG evaluated the oncological feasibility of middle‐segmental gastrectomy. After excluding cN2/N3 carcinomas, LN#5 metastasis was absent in all cT1–3 N0/1M0 cases, and LN#6 metastasis occurred in only 0.7% (4 out of 612) of patients, predominantly in those with advanced or large tumors. Based on these findings, middle‐segmental gastrectomy was safely suggested for carefully selected clinical EGC [23]. In addition, a Korean multicenter RCT, which included a small number of advanced cases (pT3/4a: PPG 2, DG 4; pN2/3: PPG 4, DG 6), showed no significant differences in overall survival (OS) or disease‐free survival (DFS) (p = 0.16 and 0.98, respectively), with only two recurrences [8]. Notably, a recent retrospective study evaluating the natural course of patients with pathologically confirmed LN#6 metastasis after PPG demonstrated that even patients with advanced disease, who were preoperatively understaged, achieved excellent DFS with standard adjuvant chemotherapy alone, without requiring completion gastrectomy or additional lymphadenectomy [10].

While the current evidence remains limited, accumulating data suggest the potential oncologic acceptability of PPG in carefully selected patients with more advanced disease, warranting cautious interpretation and further prospective validation.

The earliest prospective multicenter RCT conducted in Japan randomized 81 patients with cT1N0M0 EGC to undergo either PPG or DG with Billroth I reconstruction. Early surgical outcomes, including operative time, blood loss, postoperative morbidity, and mortality, were comparable between the two groups. In terms of long‐term outcomes, the incidence of dumping syndrome was significantly lower after PPG (p = 0.037) [6].

The Korean multicenter prospective RCT (KLASS‐04) enrolled 256 patients with cT1N0M0 EGC to undergo either laparoscopic PPG or laparoscopic DG. The primary endpoint, dumping symptoms at one year, showed no significant difference between the two groups (13.2 vs. 15.8%). PPG was associated with significantly less gallstone formation (p = 0.026) and better preservation of hemoglobin and total protein levels compared to DG (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002, respectively). However, grade IV DGE and reflux esophagitis were more frequent after PPG (p = 0.005 and p = 0.001, respectively) [8].

The most recent prospective single‐center RCT conducted in China randomized 88 patients with clinical and pathological T1N0M0 EGC to undergo either laparoscopic‐assisted PPG or laparoscopic‐assisted DG. DFS and OS were equivalent at 5 years (p = 0.46 and 0.64). In terms of functional outcomes, the gallbladder emptying rate was significantly higher after PPG (p = 0.015). Although the incidence of DGE was not significantly different (p = 0.398), gastric emptying scintigraphy after PPG demonstrated a longer time for half gastric emptying (p = 0.033) and greater gastric retention at 120 min (p = 0.048), indicating delayed yet functionally preserved gastric emptying [9].

Collectively, these RCTs demonstrated that PPG provides comparable oncologic outcomes to DG with favorable functional and nutritional benefits.

Integration of Robotic and Navigation‐Guided Surgery in PPG

The technical capabilities of robotic platforms, including three‐dimensional magnified visualization, articulated instruments with enhanced dexterity, and tremor‐control technology, can facilitate greater precision in nerve and vessel preservation, along with oncologic thoroughness in PPG.

A retrospective propensity score–matched analysis comparing robot‐assisted PPG and laparoscopy‐assisted PPG demonstrated no significant differences in postoperative complication rates or the number of retrieved lymph nodes [62]. Despite accumulating experience with robotic PPG [63], most comparative studies have focused on robotic vs. laparoscopic gastrectomy in general [64], and a direct comparison between robotic and laparoscopic PPG remains scarce.

A Korean multicenter RCT comparing laparoscopic standard gastrectomy (LSG) with laparoscopic sentinel node navigation surgery (LSNNS), a technique that identifies sentinel lymph nodes to guide selective lymphadenectomy and facilitate function‐preserving surgery [65], demonstrated that LSNNS achieved comparable short‐ and long‐term outcomes, including 30‐day complications, 5‐year DFS, disease‐specific survival (DSS), and OS [66], along with superior QoL and nutritional results [67]. Notably, in the per‐protocol analysis, segmental gastrectomy cases included within the LSNNS cohort demonstrated acceptable oncologic safety (17 out of 198 patients). Given the morphological similarity of PPG to segmental gastrectomy, these findings support the potential applicability of navigation‐guided visualization to PPG, enabling tailored lymphadenectomy while maintaining oncologic safety [68]. Integration of robotic precision and navigation‐guided surgery may redefine the technical boundaries of PPG, potentially broadening its application beyond traditional indications.

Conclusion

Given accumulating evidence from randomized and observational studies in East Asia, PPG has been widely adopted as a standard curative surgical option for patients with cT1N0M0 EGC, with favorable postoperative outcomes compared with DG. These advantages include reduced postoperative symptoms, improved nutritional preservation, and better quality of life, although delayed gastric emptying remains an important functional concern. Accumulating data further suggests that PPG may be oncologically acceptable for carefully selected patients with advanced diseases. Advances in minimally invasive robotic platforms and navigation‐guided surgery enable precise and tailored surgery, thereby broadening the application of PPG beyond its traditional indications. As most of the existing evidence originates from Eastern populations [69], the generalizability of current findings remains limited. Future investigations involving geographically and demographically diverse cohorts are required to clarify the broader applicability of PPG.
