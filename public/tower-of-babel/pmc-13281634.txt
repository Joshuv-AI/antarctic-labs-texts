Effect of Ringer’s Lactate versus normal saline irrigation on postoperative morbidity after surgical removal of mandibular impacted third molars: a randomized split-mouth clinical trial
Authors: Mehdi Abrishami, Mohammad Reza Abrishami, Sarina Sadeghi, AmirAli Abrishami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNormal saline remains the most commonly used irrigant during third molar surgery despite evidence that balanced crystalloids may reduce inflammation and acidosis in other surgical fields. This study compared the effect of intraoperative irrigation with lactated Ringer’s solution versus normal saline on postoperative pain, trismus, and facial swelling after bilateral impacted mandibular third molar removal.MethodsA prospective, double-blind, split-mouth randomized clinical trial was conducted in 28 healthy patients. Each patient received 10 mL of normal saline on one side and 10 mL of lactated Ringer’s solution on the contralateral side immediately after extraction. Pain (VAS 0–10), maximum mouth opening, and facial swelling (three linear measurements) were assessed on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 14. Inter-side differences were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for pain scores and paired t-tests for mouth opening and facial swelling measurements, with data analysed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).ResultsTwenty-eight healthy patients completed the study. Pain intensity was significantly lower on the lactated Ringer’s side at all time points (day 1: 4.36 ± 2.71 vs. 6.71 ± 1.59; all P < 0.05), with large effect sizes (r = 0.57–0.69). Maximum mouth opening on day 14 was significantly greater on the lactated Ringer’s side (46.1 ± 7.9 mm vs. 43.3 ± 8.5 mm; P = 0.016; Cohen’s d = 0.744). No significant differences were found in facial swelling at any time point (all P > 0.05). No infections or adverse events occurred.ConclusionIn this small randomized trial, intraoperative irrigation with lactated Ringer’s solution was associated with lower postoperative pain at all assessed time points and improved late trismus recovery compared with normal saline, without differences in swelling. These preliminary findings suggest that lactated Ringer’s may be a promising alternative irrigant for third molar surgery, but larger studies are needed to confirm these effects.Trial registrationThis clinical trial was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) under the registration number IRCT20130812014333N258. Background

Surgical removal of impacted mandibular third molars is the most commonly performed procedure in oral and maxillofacial surgery, with a global prevalence of mandibular third molar impaction ranging from 58.7% to 73.0% in young adults [1, 2]. Despite advancements in surgical technique, the procedure is consistently associated with considerable postoperative morbidity, including moderate-to-severe pain in up to 75% of patients, facial swelling in 60–80%, trismus in 45–65%, and surgical site infection or alveolar osteitis in 1.7–25% of cases, depending on risk factors [3–5].

The occurrence and severity of these postoperative complications (pain, swelling, and trismus) are significantly influenced by the difficulty of extraction, which depends on factors such as impaction depth, angulation, root morphology, bone density, and spatial relationship to adjacent structures like the mandibular canal and second molar. Recent classification systems, often incorporating three-dimensional imaging (e.g., cone-beam computed tomography [CBCT]), provide more accurate preoperative assessment of surgical complexity compared to traditional two-dimensional methods, enabling better prediction of morbidity and improved treatment planning [6, 7]. Studies have shown that higher surgical difficulty levels (e.g., deeper impaction or horizontal angulation) correlate with increased operative trauma, longer surgery duration, greater bone removal, and subsequently elevated postoperative inflammatory responses and complications [6].

Intraoperative irrigation of the surgical site is a fundamental step intended to remove bone debris, blood clots, and bacteria, thereby reducing inflammatory load and promoting healing [8, 9]. Normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) has been the standard irrigant for decades due to its availability, low cost, and isotonicity. However, its supraphysiologic chloride concentration (154 mmol/L) can induce hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired immune response when used in large volumes [10–12]. In contrast, lactated Ringer’s solution is a balanced crystalloid with near-physiological electrolyte composition and lactate as a buffer. Multiple high-quality trials and meta-analyses in general surgery, trauma, and critical care have demonstrated that lactated Ringer’s solution significantly reduces postoperative complications, acute kidney injury, and major adverse kidney events compared with normal saline [13–16].

In oral and maxillofacial surgery, evidence regarding the optimal irrigation fluid remains limited and contradictory. While several studies have investigated antimicrobial irrigants (chlorhexidine, metronidazole, or povidone-iodine), no study has compared normal saline with balanced solutions in third molar surgery, with a split-mouth randomized design with strict standardization of volume, temperature, and delivery pressure [17]. The split-mouth model is compelling in bilateral third molar surgery because it eliminates inter-individual variability in age, bone density, impaction pattern, surgical trauma, and healing capacity, thereby increasing statistical efficiency and ethical acceptability [18, 19].

Given the physiological advantages of lactated Ringer’s solution demonstrated in other surgical fields and the absence of high-level evidence in third molar surgery, there is a clear need for a rigorously designed trial. The present randomized clinical trial was therefore conducted to compare the effect of intraoperative irrigation with lactated Ringer’s solution versus normal saline on postoperative pain, facial swelling, trismus, and infection rates following surgical removal of bilateral symmetrical impacted mandibular third molars.

Methods

Study design and setting

This randomized, double-blind, split-mouth clinical trial was conducted between March and September 2025 at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, School of Dentistry, Islamic Azad University of Khorasgan, Isfahan, Iran. The study protocol was approved by the university ethics committee (approval no. IR.IAU.KHUISF.REC.1404.279) and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20130812014333N262). The trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and reported according to the CONSORT 2025 guidelines and SPIRIT statement(see Fig. 1 for the CONSORT flow diagram illustrating participant flow through the trial).

CONSORT flow diagram

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software based on a 2 × 4 mixed-design ANOVA (two irrigants × four time points) for the primary outcome (VAS pain score). Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, power = 80%, and intra-subject correlation of 0.5, a minimum of 24 patients was required. Allowing for a 15% dropout rate, 28 patients were recruited.

Eligibility criteria

Patients aged 18–35 years presenting with bilateral symmetrical impacted mandibular third molars (Pell–Gregory Class II or III, position B or C; mesioangular, horizontal, or vertical according to Winter’s classification) requiring surgical removal under local anaesthesia were included provided they met all of the following criteria: ASA physical status I or II; no systemic disease affecting wound healing; no use of NSAIDs, corticosteroids, antibiotics, or anticoagulants in the preceding two weeks; no active infection, cyst, or tumour associated with the third molars; no known hypersensitivity to normal saline or lactated Ringer’s solution; signed informed consent; and willingness to attend all follow-up visits.

Patients were excluded if any of the following occurred: intraoperative complications (mandibular fracture, severe haemorrhage, nerve injury); need for additional (unplanned) therapeutic prescription of antibiotics or NSAIDs beyond the standardized postoperative regimen; pregnancy or lactation; failure to attend scheduled follow-up appointments; severe allergic reaction to the irrigants; heavy smoking (> 10 cigarettes/day) or regular alcohol consumption; or voluntary withdrawal at any stage.

Randomization and blinding

Block randomization (block sizes 4 and 6) was generated by an independent statistician using SPSS version 27. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Each patient was randomly assigned to receive normal saline on one side and lactated Ringer’s solution on the contralateral side. Participants were blinded to the irrigant used on each side (the irrigants are visually indistinguishable clear solutions, and no identifying information was provided to patients). The outcome assessor (who performed all postoperative measurements) and the statistical analyst were also blinded to group allocation. The operating surgeon was not blinded, as they prepared and delivered the allocated irrigant during surgery.

Surgical procedure and outcome assessment

All surgeries were performed in a single session(i.e., both mandibular third molars were extracted during the same appointment/visit) by one experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeon (> 10 years’ experience) following an identical standardized protocol [20–22]. Inferior alveolar nerve block and buccal infiltration anaesthesia were achieved with 2% lidocaine with 1:80,000 epinephrine (mean 2–4 cartridges). A standard envelope flap was raised (sulcular incision from tooth 6 to 8 with a distal relieving incision along the midline of the ramus). Buccal and distal bone removal was performed with a round bur under continuous sterile saline cooling; tooth sectioning with a fissure bur was performed when required. After complete tooth delivery and gentle socket curettage, the alveolar socket was irrigated with exactly 10 mL of the allocated solution (normal saline or lactated Ringer’s solution, room temperature 23–25 °C) using a 10-mL syringe with gentle pressure (flow rate ≈ 30 mL/min). Primary intention closure was achieved in all cases with 3 − 0 polyglactin 910 (Vicryl®, Ethicon) interrupted sutures by fully repositioning the flap without intentional openings for secondary healing or drainage. The surgeon performed the procedure sequentially on one side first, followed immediately by the contralateral side.

Postoperative regimen was standardized: amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 h for 5 days, paracetamol 500 mg as required (maximum 3 g/day), 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthrinse twice daily, and extraoral ice packs for the first 48 h. Patients were instructed to take paracetamol only when needed for pain and to record the number of tablets taken daily in a provided diary; however, quantitative analgesic consumption data were not systematically collected or analyzed in this study.

Outcome assessment, including baseline (preoperative) measurements, was performed by a single trained and blinded examiner. Preoperative baseline values for facial swelling and trismus were obtained prior to anesthesia and surgery to establish individual reference points. Assessments were repeated on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 14 using the following validated methods:

Pain intensity: 100-mm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 100 = worst imaginable pain) [23].

Pain intensity: 100-mm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 100 = worst imaginable pain) [23].

Facial swelling: mean of three linear measurements (mm) using a non-stretchable flexible tape measure: tragus to soft-tissue pogonion, tragus to mouth corner, lateral canthus to gonion [24, 25]. Preoperative measurements served as baseline.

The examiner underwent training in the standardized measurement protocols prior to the study. However, formal calibration exercises (e.g., repeated measurements on pilot patients or volunteers) and intra-examiner reliability testing (e.g., intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]) were not performed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 27. Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences between the two sides were compared using a paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Time-course changes were evaluated with the Friedman test followed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Binary outcomes were analysed with McNemar’s test. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 28 patients (17 males and 11 females) with a mean age of 27.7 ± 4.3 years completed the study.

Postoperative pain

On both sides, the highest pain intensity was recorded on the first postoperative day and gradually decreased to nearly zero by day 14. However, pain intensity was consistently and markedly lower on the Ringer’s lactate-irrigated side at all time points.

In summary, irrigation with Ringer’s lactate solution, compared with normal saline, was associated with significantly lower postoperative pain at all time points, faster and more complete recovery of maximum mouth opening by day 14, and no difference in facial swelling after surgical removal of impacted mandibular third molars.

Discussion

Surgical extraction of impacted mandibular third molars is among the most common procedures in oral and maxillofacial surgery, with postoperative pain, trismus, and facial swelling affecting up to 70% of patients and contributing to reduced quality of life and increased healthcare costs [4, 29]. The current double-blind, split-mouth randomized clinical trial (n = 28) demonstrates that intraoperative socket irrigation with 10 mL lactated Ringer’s solution, compared with equivalent normal saline, significantly reduces pain intensity at all postoperative time points and enhances maximum mouth opening recovery by day 14, without differences in facial swelling or infectious complications. These findings highlight the potential of balanced crystalloids to modulate local wound healing dynamics in a low-volume irrigation context.

The observed pain reduction aligns with emerging evidence on balanced crystalloids’ anti-inflammatory properties. A 2024 systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis of 34,685 critically ill adults (BEST-Living trial) reported that balanced crystalloids may modestly reduce 90-day mortality (RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.91–1.01) and acute kidney injury (RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.89–1.02) compared to saline, attributing benefits to mitigation of hyperchloremic acidosis [30]. Similarly, our large effect sizes for pain reduction may be conceptually linked to observations from perioperative trials (e.g., SOLAR), though those involved larger volumes and systemic administration in different patient populations [31].While these systemic findings provide a plausible mechanistic rationale (e.g., reduced acidosis and inflammation), direct extrapolation to localized, low-volume irrigation in oral surgery should be made cautiously due to differences in context, volume, and patient physiology.

In oral surgery-specific contexts, a 2023 randomized trial (n = 112) comparing chlorhexidine, povidone-iodine, metronidazole, and saline irrigation after third molar extraction found saline yielded the highest VAS scores [32]. This observation supports lactated Ringer’s superior buffering capacity, as saline’s high chloride may exacerbate local acidosis and nociceptor activation, while lactate may promote fibroblast proliferation and cytokine modulation [10, 33]. However, an earlier meta-analysis of irrigation solutions in non-renal surgery (n = 871) reported no significant pain benefit from balanced crystalloids over saline (MD -0.8 VAS; 95% CI -1.6 to 0.0), potentially due to higher volumes and heterogeneous endpoints; our focused low-volume, split-mouth design may help address this discrepancy by isolating local effects [34]. Nevertheless, differences in surgical setting and irrigation volumes limit direct comparisons.

For trismus, the late superiority of lactated Ringer’s suggests a sustained anti-inflammatory effect on masticatory muscles. This is analogous to the 2023 triple-blind trial (n = 60) of ozonated water versus saline irrigation, where ozonated solution improved mouth opening by 3.1 mm at day 14, attributed to reduced oxidative stress—analogous to lactate’s antioxidant signaling [35]. Secondary analyses from similar perioperative studies suggest balanced crystalloids may better attenuate certain inflammatory markers via pH stabilization, though again in systemic contexts distinct from our localized application [31].

In contrast, a 2024 prospective study (n = 100) of hydrogen peroxide versus saline found no trismus benefit (mean difference 1.2 mm), highlighting that not all irrigants equally address acidosis; our large effect appears more pronounced, possibly due to the split-mouth minimization of confounders like surgical trauma [36]. Discrepancies with earlier reviews (e.g., 2023 meta-analysis showing no trismus reduction from antimicrobials; RR 0.92; 95% CI 0.84–1.01) underscore balanced crystalloids’ unique metabolic advantages over bactericidal agents [37].

Facial swelling showed no inter-group difference, consistent with a 2024 randomized trial (n = 80) comparing povidone-iodine and saline, where swelling changes were equivalent (MD 0.4 mm; 95% CI -1.2 to 2.0), as edema is primarily trauma-driven rather than irrigant-dependent [38]. This null result aligns with broader evidence showing no found no clear reduction from balanced crystalloids (RR 0.98; 95% CI 0.92–1.05), but diverges from a 2025 pilot study (n = 40) of dexamethasone-saline irrigation showing 1.5 mm less swelling ; the latter’s pharmacologic additive likely explains the inconsistency, as our physiologic approach targets inflammation without steroids [39, 40].

Although facial swelling was measured using linear tape measurements, which is a simple and widely accessible method, this approach has inherent limitations in objectivity and reproducibility. More advanced automated three-dimensional workflows for both linear and volumetric assessment of postoperative facial swelling have been described in the recent literature and could provide greater precision in future studies [41].

Limitations and recommendations

Despite the strong internal validity conferred by the split-mouth design, which effectively controlled inter-individual confounding factors (age, bone density, healing capacity, and surgical difficulty), several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single academic center by one experienced surgeon; therefore, the generalizability of the findings to surgeons with varying levels of expertise or to different flap and ostectomy techniques remains to be determined. Second, the relatively young cohort (mean age 27.7 ± 5.1 years) and excellent general health status (ASA I–II) may have underestimated the potential benefit of lactated Ringer’s in older patients or those with comorbidities, in whom chloride-induced acidosis is more pronounced. Third, pain assessment relied on subjective VAS reporting, and facial swelling was evaluated using simple linear measurements; more objective methods such as three-dimensional optical scanning or stereophotogrammetry could have increased measurement precision. Fourth, although adequately powered for the primary outcome (pain), the sample size limited the detection of rare complications such as alveolar osteitis or infection.

Fifth, despite the split-mouth design minimizing inter-individual variability in extraction difficulty, the relatively small sample size precluded stratification of outcomes by difficulty level (e.g., using three-dimensional classification systems incorporating cone-beam computed tomography [CBCT] for more accurate assessment of impaction depth, angulation, root morphology, and proximity to the mandibular canal). This may have influenced the interpretation of postoperative morbidity outcomes, as extraction difficulty is a well-established predictor of increased pain, swelling, trismus, and complications.

Sixth, postoperative analgesic consumption (paracetamol taken as required) was not quantitatively recorded or statistically compared between groups. Although paracetamol acts systemically and would affect both sides equally in this split-mouth design, potentially reducing the risk of differential confounding, variable intake could still influence subjective VAS pain scores (e.g., patients experiencing more pain on one side might have taken more paracetamol). This represents a potential limitation in interpreting the magnitude of pain reduction attributable solely to the irrigant.

Seventh, although the outcome assessor was trained in the standardized protocols, no formal calibration process or assessment of intra-examiner reliability (e.g., ICC for repeated measurements of swelling or trismus) was conducted. This may introduce minor measurement variability or error, particularly for linear facial measurements and trismus, though the blinded design and use of validated methods mitigate this risk to some extent.Finally, long-term outcomes beyond postoperative day 14 (e.g., temporomandibular joint function or chronic pain) were not assessed.

Given the promising results, future studies should be multicentre, involve multiple surgeons and a broader age range (particularly patients > 40 years); measure serum and local inflammatory biomarkers (IL-6, TNF-α, CRP) and socket-fluid pH; perform dose–response comparisons (5 mL, 10 mL, and 20 mL) of lactated Ringer’s versus normal saline; compare lactated Ringer’s with other balanced crystalloids (e.g., Plasma-Lyte or Hartmann’s solution) and with cooled normal saline; evaluate efficacy in more complex cases (deep impactions, elderly patients, medically compromised individuals) and, with larger sample sizes, allow stratification of patients according to extraction difficulty based on three-dimensional classification systems (e.g., incorporating CBCT beyond traditional Pell-Gregory and Winter classifications) to further minimize the influence of surgical complexity on postoperative morbidity and enhance generalizability; and conduct cost-effectiveness and quality-of-life analyses (OHIP-14, return-to-work days) to provide sufficient evidence for updating clinical guidelines and recommending the replacement of normal saline with lactated Ringer’s as the standard intraoperative irrigant in oral and maxillofacial surgery.

These limitations do not undermine the internal validity of the present findings but clearly delineate important directions for future research to enhance generalizability and strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

In conclusion, lactated Ringer’s irrigation emerges as a superior, low-cost alternative to saline for reducing pain and optimizing trismus recovery after third molar surgery, bridging gaps in oral-specific evidence from broader perioperative trials. These results advocate protocol updates and warrant dose-optimization research.
