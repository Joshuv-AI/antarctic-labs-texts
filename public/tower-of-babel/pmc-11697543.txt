Protocol for purifying and reconstituting a vacuole membrane transporter Ypq1 into proteoliposomes
Authors: Felichi Mae Arines, Aleksander Wielenga, Randy B Stockbridge, Ming Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryStudying the biochemical function of membrane transporters is important in understanding the biology of transporter-laden organelles such as lysosomes and vacuoles. We present a protocol for overexpressing, purifying, and reconstituting a vacuole membrane transporter Ypq1 into proteoliposomes and describe steps to measure transport activity using radioactive substrates. The protocols established here can be used to study other vacuolar or lysosomal membrane transporters.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Arines et al.1Subject areas: Cell Membrane, Protein Biochemistry, Protein expression and purification

Before you begin

This protocol describes the purification of the yeast lysosomal lysine transporter Ypq1, its reconstitution into vacuole (yeast lysosome)-mimicking liposomes, and the measurement of its transport activity using a radioactive substrate assay. Ypq1 belongs to the conserved PQ-loop membrane transporter family.2,3,4,5,6 We have previously published several papers investigating Ypq1.1,7,8,9,10 Over the years, we have discovered that: 1) Ypq1 is ubiquitinated and degraded when cytoplasmic lysine concentration is low; 2) this ubiquitination depends on a vacuole membrane-anchored E3 ubiquitin ligase complex comprising Ssh4 and Rsp5; and 3) after ubiquitination, Ypq1 is internalized into the vacuole lumen by the ESCRT machinery for degradation.7,9,10 Confusingly, Ypq1 was previously proposed to be a lysine exporter on the vacuole.3 This raised the question of why yeast cells would degrade a lysine exporter when cytoplasmic lysine levels are low. The yeast vacuole is a storage site for excess amino acids, including lysine. When cytosolic lysine is low, it seems counterintuitive to degrade a lysine exporter like Ypq1. To resolve this controversy, we developed techniques to overexpress and affinity-purify Ypq1 and reconstitute it into proteoliposomes. Our in vitro transporter assays indicate that Ypq1 is actually a lysine importer that uses the proton gradient to transport lysine into the vacuole for storage, not an exporter.1 Our methods can be readily adapted for the study of other yeast vacuolar and mammalian lysosomal membrane transport proteins such as zinc transporters Zrc1, Zrt3 and cystine exporter cystinosin.6,11

For protein purification, we have previously prepared a diploid yeast strain (FAY146) stably overexpressing four copies of Ypq1 driven by the strong constitutive TEF1 promoter (see key resources table).1

Limitations

The conditions mentioned in this protocol have been optimized for the purification, reconstitution, and transport analysis of the yeast lysine transporter Ypq1. In principle, this protocol can be adapted for membrane transporting proteins, but some conditions may need to be empirically derived such as: presence and concentration of transporting substrates in the buffers (i.e., Ypq1 requires Lysine for stability, other proteins may require other substrates), detergent type and concentration during the membrane solubilization screen.11,16

Troubleshooting

Problem 1: Purification cultures grow slowly or do not grow to optimal density

To ensure maximum protein yield, dense cultures are necessary. There could be multiple reasons for slow growing yeast. However, do not increase the culture temperature, as this causes lower Ypq1 yield in our experience.

Potential solutions

•Increase the amount of yeast cells inoculated in the starter culture (Step 1) by combining multiple colonies. You may need to streak out the yeast strain into a fresh plate to further increase the amount of inoculant.

Problem 2: Low spheroplasting efficiency

If the efficiency calculated using the formula under step 11 is below 90%, the spheroplasting is incomplete. Some of the cells will not be completely lysed and this can significantly reduce protein yield.

Potential solution

•Extend the zymolase incubation time until the efficiency is above 90% (Step 11).

Problem 3: Protein purification yield is low

Different membrane proteins have different purification needs, and multiple steps need to be optimized. However, general guidelines can be followed to maximize membrane protein purification success.

Potential solution

•Ensure that all protein purification steps are done on ice or in the cold room, unless stated otherwise.

Ensure that all protein purification steps are done on ice or in the cold room, unless stated otherwise.

•Lysis buffers must have protease inhibitors, EDTA, and PMSF (Step 12).

Lysis buffers must have protease inhibitors, EDTA, and PMSF (Step 12).

•Ypq1 relies on the presence of its substrate lysine to be stable. Ensure that lysine is incorporated in the purification buffers. Note that lysine is a positively charged amino acid which affects the buffer pH. Thus, re-adjust the buffer pH after mixing in lysine. Other proteins may require the addition of their substrate or co-factor for stability.

Problem 4: Protein is unstable or crashes out of the solution

Protein stability is important for transport assays and other functional studies. An indication that your purified protein is unstable is when it forms insoluble aggregates in solution.

Potential solution

•Perform a stability check by running a small amount (∼25 μg) of protein through an FPLC. If the protein is stable, it should run as a single peak at the same volume as the purification. If the protein is unstable, the FPLC profile starts forming a shoulder or the peak volume shifts to the left, indicating that the protein is starting to form larger molecular weight aggregates (Figure 8). If the protein appears unstable, double-check the lysine content and pH of the purification buffers.

Problem 5: Low transport activity

The transport activity of Ypq1 should be above 10% using the conditions described above. However, sometimes transport activity can fall much lower due to low protein level or low proteoliposome level.

Potential solution

•Perform the reconstitution steps, especially the ones performed with potential for lipid oxidation, quickly and carefully to prevent loss of lipid. Additionally, ensure that the tubes containing lipids are completely sealed whenever they are not actively being worked with.

Perform the reconstitution steps, especially the ones performed with potential for lipid oxidation, quickly and carefully to prevent loss of lipid. Additionally, ensure that the tubes containing lipids are completely sealed whenever they are not actively being worked with.

•Proceed immediately from protein purification to reconstitution, membrane protein that is stored can aggregate and have a significantly reduced activity.

Proceed immediately from protein purification to reconstitution, membrane protein that is stored can aggregate and have a significantly reduced activity.

•Check for protein stability after reconstitution by running a small amount of proteoliposome in an SDS-PAGE gel and staining with SYPRO Ruby. The protein should run at the same molecular weight as the purification. If the protein runs as a high molecular-weight smear, the protein could be aggregating (Figure 9). If so, optimize the protein purification and storage conditions.
