SGMHA: semantic graph reconstruction with multi-head attention for gene regulatory network inference
Authors: Xujian Zhang, Wenhao Li, Yuliang Pan, Xupeng Wang, Jihong Guan, Zhiwei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inferring gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data is fundamentally challenged by severe data sparsity, where pervasive dropout events obscure true regulatory signals and compromise the reliability of downstream inference. Existing supervised methods, while leveraging prior network structures, remain highly susceptible to this noise due to their end-to-end learning paradigm. To address this bottleneck, we propose SGMHA, a novel two-stage framework that decouples representation learning from link prediction. Specifically, SGMHA first employs a self-supervised graph masked autoencoder (GraphMAE) to learn robust gene representations by reconstructing randomly masked expression values, thereby mitigating sparsity-induced distortions. Subsequently, an MHA (multi-head attention)-based fine-tuning module integrates these pre-trained representations with raw expression data to accurately infer directed regulatory links. Extensive benchmarking across seven scRNA-seq datasets demonstrates that SGMHA consistently outperforms eight state-of-the-art methods in both area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Applying SGMHA to breast cancer metastasis revealed context-specific GRNs and identified 26 high-confidence candidate drivers. Among these, six (NDUFAF4, ENY2, CCT5, PGK1, DCTPP1, and H2AFZ) were validated as prognostic biomarkers, with their mechanistic roles in metastatic adaptation detailed through multi-omics integration. Collectively, SGMHA provides an accurate, scalable, and biologically interpretable tool for GRN inference, holding strong promise for biomarker discovery in complex diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13022-0.

Introduction

GRNs represent complex systems of molecular interactions where transcription factors (TFs) coordinate the expression of their target genes, thereby governing fundamental biological processes such as cell differentiation, functional maintenance, and response to environmental stimuli [1–3]. Deciphering GRNs is thus crucial for understanding the underlying mechanisms of complex human diseases, with direct implications for prevention, diagnosis, and the identification of novel therapeutic targets [4].

The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized our ability to profile gene expression at an unprecedented resolution [5], capturing cellular heterogeneity that was previously obscured by bulk sequencing techniques. This technological leap provides a unique opportunity to reconstruct context-specific GRNs [6], driving the development of numerous computational methods for inference from scRNA-seq data [3]. These methods can be broadly categorized into unsupervised [7] and supervised [8] learning paradigms.

Unsupervised learning methods infer regulatory relationships directly from gene expression data without relying on known interactions. To capture these latent regulatory patterns, a variety of computational strategies have been developed over the years. Among these, information theory-based methods [9], such as Mutual Information (MI) [10] and Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) [11], infer relationships by measuring statistical dependence between gene expression profiles. While computationally efficient, they cannot distinguish direct from indirect interactions and typically produce undirected networks lacking regulatory directionality. Alternatively, model-based approaches [12], including LogBTF [13] and MetaSEM [14], attempt to fit gene expression data to predefined mathematical frameworks like Boolean networks or structural equation models. These flexible approaches allow tailored model selection but remain sensitive to data sparsity and noise, with their performance depending heavily on how well the model assumptions match true regulatory dynamics. Furthermore, machine learning-based methods, such as GENIE3 [15], use random forests with multiple transcription factors as inputs to predict target gene expression, utilizing feature importance scores to indicate regulatory strengths. Although capable of modeling complex relationships without prior knowledge, these methods face scalability issues and extensive computational time when applied to large datasets. In summary, while the independence from known labels makes unsupervised methods conceptually appealing, they collectively struggle to overcome the fundamental barriers of scRNA-seq data—namely, its inherent noise, high dimensionality, and pronounced sparsity [16]—which ultimately restricts their scalability and reliability in large-scale GRN reconstruction.

In contrast, supervised learning approaches utilize known regulatory interactions (e.g., from chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data) to train predictive models [17], generally achieving higher accuracy. Within this paradigm, methodologies have progressively evolved to better capture the complex architecture of gene interactions. Initially, conventional deep learning models [18], such as CNNC [19] and 3DCEMA [20], attempted to tackle this by transforming gene expression data into engineered inputs like histograms or 3D tensors for convolutional neural network (CNN)-based classification. However, they often suffer from high computational costs and may lose nuanced semantic expression information during such rigid feature transformation. To overcome these representational limitations, more recent strategies have shifted towards graph-based methods [21], including GNNLink [22] and GENELink [23], which frame GRN inference naturally as a link prediction task. While graph neural networks (GNNs) effectively incorporate topological information, many existing models overemphasize the graph structure itself and underutilize gene-level features from scRNA-seq data, limiting their ability to capture nuanced regulatory semantics. Furthermore, specific methods like GRGNN [24] depend on initial networks built from traditional correlation measures, which can inadvertently introduce noise into the graph structure.

To address these critical limitations, we propose SGMHA (Semantic Graph Reconstruction with Multi-Head Attention), a novel supervised framework that synergizes self-supervised graph representation learning with attentive supervised fine-tuning for accurate GRN inference [25]. SGMHA tackles the dual challenge of data sparsity and topological integration through a decoupled two-stage strategy. By explicitly separating general gene feature learning (self-supervised) from specific regulatory edge prediction (supervised), SGMHA substantially reduces the model’s sensitivity to the quality of the prior network. This decoupling provides a crucial buffer against inherent false-positive edges in the reference graph. Subsequently, during the supervised fine-tuning phase, a multi-head attention (MHA) mechanism dynamically integrates these pre-trained embeddings with the original graph features and raw expression data. This deep fusion enables the model to jointly capture rich semantic information and topological context for precise link prediction.

Extensive evaluations on seven scRNA-seq benchmark datasets show that SGMHA achieves superior performance compared to eight state-of-the-art methods, attaining leading or comparable results in both area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) across multiple ground-truth networks. Crucially, SGMHA extends beyond theoretical network inference to provide an end-to-end translational validation workflow. Applied to breast cancer metastasis data, it successfully reconstructs context-specific GRNs for primary and lung-metastatic tumors. Integration with differential expression analysis then identifies 26 high-confidence candidate drivers. To assess the biological fidelity of this computational pipeline, we further analyzed a subset of six prioritized regulators (NDUFAF4, ENY2, CCT5, PGK1, DCTPP1, and H2AFZ), revealing their convergent roles in metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming. These six genes were subsequently validated as prognostic biomarkers in independent clinical cohorts. Together, these results demonstrate that SGMHA effectively closes the loop from algorithm development to clinical discovery, underscoring its strong potential for uncovering key regulatory drivers in complex diseases.

Materials and methods

Link prediction addresses the inference of unknown or potential interactions in biological networks by leveraging known topological structure and node features. In recent years, GNNs have emerged as a powerful framework for this task [26], capable of jointly encoding graph structure and node attributes into low-dimensional representations. Within this context, GRN reconstruction is formulated as a link prediction task. This paradigm leverages graph representation learning to extract latent embeddings from biological networks—a strategy proven robust in related domains such as drug-disease and miRNA-disease association prediction [27]. Supervised models then learn a mapping function to preserve both topological and semantic information, generating node embeddings for precise link scoring.

The SGMHA framework

SGMHA is a GNN-based architecture for GRN inference. The model integrates scRNA-seq data, a structured reference GRN, and transcription factor (TF)–target annotations. In the resulting gene relational graph, nodes represent TFs or ordinary genes, while edges correspond to known or potential regulatory interactions. Specifically, regarding the scope of inference, SGMHA is designed to infer directed and unsigned regulatory interactions. That is, the model predicts the probability of a directed edge existing from a specific TF to a target gene, but does not explicitly classify the specific regulatory type (i.e., up-regulation or down-regulation) in the final output. Node features are derived from scRNA-seq expression profiles. A key innovation of the framework is its two-phase training strategy. In pre-training, a semantic graph reconstruction module based on GraphMAE is used self-supervised to reconstruct masked node features, capturing structural and semantic patterns. This produces low-dimensional node embeddings integrating gene expression and graph information. In fine-tuning, an MHA mechanism dynamically combines pre-trained embeddings with original graph features. A supervised link prediction model then scores regulatory relationships accurately. Pre-training

Our self-supervised pre-training employs a semantic graph reconstruction architecture based on GraphMAE that jointly models node features and graph topology to learn semantically rich gene representations. The model uses a GNN-based encoder–decoder structure handling graph-structured data. Through neighborhood aggregation, it captures topological dependencies among nodes, learning expressive node embeddings. For GRN inference from scRNA-seq data, genes are treated as nodes and regulatory interactions as edges. During self-supervised training, the decoder reconstructs only features of masked nodes, reinforcing consistency between expression patterns and topological context.

In the pre-training phase, SGMHA learns generalizable low-dimensional representations of gene nodes from scRNA-seq data. This is achieved via a Graph Attention Network (GAT)-based GraphMAE, which leverages a prior gene interaction graph to guide representation learning through the reconstruction of masked node features. Intuitively, this “fill-in-the-blank” reconstruction forces the model to rely strictly on unmasked neighbors and graph topology, thereby preventing noise memorization. Consequently, it extracts robust co-expression patterns and mitigates the inherent data sparsity of scRNA-seq data—an architectural advantage of graph autoencoders recently validated in other complex biological networks [29].

Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning uses pre-trained gene embeddings for supervised TF-target link prediction. A fusion architecture (LinkModel) employs multi-head self-attention to model gene pair interactions.

The LeakyReLU activation function is employed to mitigate the “dying neuron” issue associated with the standard ReLU by providing a small gradient (with a slope set to 0.2) for negative inputs, thereby ensuring training stability. Furthermore, it is crucial for preserving biologically meaningful negative regulatory signals in gene expression data. Although SGMHA’s final output strictly focuses on predicting the probability of directed regulatory links (i.e., directed unsigned link prediction), distinguishing the semantic features of activation and inhibition within the internal latent representations provides the model with richer regulatory context, thereby effectively enhancing its accuracy in determining the existence of regulatory relationships.

To model global interactions among nodes and capture the complex, multi-dimensional nature of gene regulation, we employ an MHA mechanism—a robust strategy whose capacity for extracting interpretable, high-dimensional features has been extensively validated across diverse biochemical applications [30, 31]. In this framework, each attention head acts as an independent analytical channel, focusing on distinct semantic aspects of the TF-target relationship. The mechanism then dynamically assigns weights to the most relevant features, enabling the model to capture diverse regulatory dependencies rather than treating all inputs equally. Through this two-stage strategy, SGMHA captures generalizable gene representations from sparse scRNA-seq data via self-supervised pre-training, and accurately infers TF-target links via attentive fine-tuning.

Datasets and data pre-processing

Despite the significant advances made by the aforementioned baselines in GRN inference, each category still suffers from inherent limitations. Traditional methods (PCC, GENIE3) rely on statistical or ensemble tree approaches and fail to capture higher-order topological dependencies. Early deep learning models (CNNC, GNE) introduce non-linear feature extraction but largely treat gene pairs in isolation, neglecting global graph structures. Recent GNN-based methods (GNNLink, GENELink, GATCL, GRANet) explicitly model network topology through message passing. However, their purely supervised, end-to-end paradigm renders representation learning highly susceptible to the severe sparsity and high dropout rates typical of scRNA-seq data. In contrast, SGMHA adopts a decoupled two-stage architecture: self-supervised pre-training followed by attention-based fine-tuning. This design effectively mitigates data sparsity during pre-training and dynamically integrates structural and expression features during fine-tuning, thereby achieving superior predictive accuracy.

Evaluation strategies

We designed two evaluation strategies based on benchmark networks.

For the STRING and non-specific ChIP-seq networks, all annotated TF-gene pairs were used as positives, with a 2:1 train-test split. A balanced set of negatives was constructed via uniform random sampling, supplemented by hard negative sampling during training. Test negatives were strictly held-out to prevent information leakage.

For the cell-type-specific ChIP-seq networks, a TF-wise split was adopted. For each transcription factor, its targets were divided into training and test sets, preserving the original class ratio, with 10% of the training data used for validation.

All models were trained and tested under identical data splits. Implementation details

To ensure full reproducibility of the experimental results presented in this study, we detail the specific hyperparameter configurations and training environments used for SGMHA.

Pre-training configurations: The self-supervised GraphMAE module was constructed using a 2-layer GAT architecture. The model was configured with a hidden dimension of 256 and an output embedding dimension of 16. During the masking process, a random masking rate of 25% was applied to the gene nodes. To prevent overfitting and ensure stable feature extraction, we applied a dropout rate of 0.6 and utilized the LeakyReLU activation function with a negative slope (α) of 0.2. The pre-training objective minimized the MSE loss between the original and reconstructed node features.

To ensure the robustness and reproducibility of SGMHA, we conducted hyperparameter optimization for the fine-tuning stage. The initial learning rate was tuned within the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-3}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-5}$$\end{document}, and the final output dimension of the LinkModel was evaluated at both 16 and 32. Based on the empirical performance across the benchmark datasets, the final configurations were set as follows. The LinkModel was configured with an input dimension of 256, a hidden dimension of 64, and an optimal output dimension of 32. The MHA mechanism employed 8 parallel attention heads to capture diverse regulatory dependencies. The model was optimized using the Adam optimizer [42] with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-4}$$\end{document}, which demonstrated the most stable convergence across the majority of cell types. A StepLR learning rate scheduler [43] was applied with a step size of 1 and a gamma decay factor of 0.99.

The fine-tuning stage was trained for a maximum of 1000 epochs using a batch size of 128. To address the inherent class imbalance between positive and negative regulatory links, we utilized a weighted Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits Loss, assigning a positive class weight of 1.5. Additionally, gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0 was applied to maintain training stability.

Baseline reproduction: For all comparative baseline models evaluated in Fig. 2, algorithms were executed using the exact same pre-processed scRNA-seq datasets, transcription factor sets, and unified training-test splits. To maintain a fair and unbiased comparison, all baseline methods were run utilizing the default hyperparameter configurations recommended by their respective authors.

Results

Benchmark evaluation

Model performance

To systematically evaluate the performance of SGMHA, we used the benchmark datasets summarized in Table 1 and assessed model performance using both AUROC and AUPRC. We compared against eight state-of-the-art baseline methods for GRN inference—GRANet, GATCL, GNNLink, GENELink, GNE, CNNC, PCC, and GENIE3—all trained and tested under identical data splits to ensure a fair comparison.

SGMHA demonstrated strong and consistent performance across all benchmark categories. On STRING-derived networks, it matched or exceeded the best baseline in 64.3% (9/14) of datasets by AUROC and led in 50% (7/14) by AUPRC. Its performance was notably stronger on ChIP-seq benchmarks, achieving perfect AUROC scores (14/14, 100%) on non-specific networks while also attaining top AUPRC results in 57.1% (8/14) of cases. Similarly, on cell-type-specific ChIP-seq data, SGMHA achieved leading or comparable AUROC values in 92.9% (13/14) of datasets and the best AUPRC in 78.6% (11/14).

Overall, SGMHA matched or exceeded the best baseline in 85.7% (36/42) AUROC and 61.9% (26/42) AUPRC evaluations. In the few cases where it did not rank first, SGMHA consistently placed second in AUROC and within the top three in AUPRC. The result is shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S1.

Furthermore, experimental results demonstrate that GNN-based methods significantly outperform traditional machine learning models and other deep learning architectures on key evaluation metrics. Compared to conventional approaches, GNNs provide a more powerful modeling paradigm for analyzing complex interactions between genes. Particularly noteworthy is that GNNs and their main variants—such as GATs and Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)—can simultaneously capture both local neighborhood structural features and global dependencies in GRNs through their distinctive message-passing mechanisms. This inherent capability to model the multi-level topological characteristics of biological networks enables GNN-based methods to demonstrate distinct advantages in uncovering complex regulatory logic.

We further investigated the generalization ability of SGMHA under limited training samples. Using the cell type-specific ChIP-seq benchmark, we conducted experiments on human embryonic stem cells (hESC) and mouse dendritic cells (mDC) with varying proportions of training data (10% to 50%). A fixed 10% of target genes were held out as the validation set, and the remainder were used for testing.

Results show that model performance improved with increasing training data, but began to stabilize once the training proportion reached 40%. Specifically, increasing training data from 20% to 40% resulted in only marginal improvements—1.5% in AUROC and 3.3% in AUPRC. These results demonstrate that SGMHA maintains strong inference capability even with limited samples, highlighting its data efficiency and robustness. Performance trends are detailed in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Ablation study

To further validate the impact of different modules on the overall performance of SGMHA, we conducted a series of ablation experiments by progressively removing or replacing key components of the model and analyzing their effects on performance. Specifically, we constructed four variants of SGMHA (as shown in Supplementary Table S2): (i) SGMHA w/o M: SGMHA without the masking module, which simplifies the model into a standard self-supervised graph feature extractor with a GAT encoder/decoder architecture. (ii) SGMHA w/o R: SGMHA without raw data input, where the branch for raw gene expression data and its associated fusion structure are removed during fine-tuning. (iii) SGMHA w/o A: SGMHA without the MHA mechanism, replacing the attentive feature integration with a naive element-wise addition to fuse the pre-trained semantic embeddings and the graph topological features. (iv) SGMHA w/o P: SGMHA without the self-supervised pre-training stage, relying solely on randomly initialized node representations that are trained entirely from scratch during the supervised fine-tuning phase.

To evaluate the contributions of individual modules to the performance of SGMHA, we systematically compared the full model against its ablated variants. All variants were trained using identical fine-tuning configurations as the full SGMHA model and evaluated on the same seven benchmark scRNA-seq datasets.

The results demonstrate that incorporating the graph masking mechanism provides a consistent performance advantage. Specifically, the full model outperformed the masking-ablated variant (SGMHA w/o M) in 21 out of the 28 evaluation metric comparisons (spanning 14 cell-type-specific dataset configurations across both AUROC and AUPRC) (Supplementary Fig. S3, Supplementary Fig. S4). This finding indicates that the graph masking mechanism facilitates the learning of more robust feature representations from single-cell RNA-seq data during pre-training.

During the fine-tuning stage, SGMHA integrates raw gene expression data with the pre-trained representations to enhance inference capability, albeit at the cost of increased computational complexity. To validate the necessity of this design, we constructed a controlled model, SGMHA w/o R, which utilizes only the pre-trained representations during fine-tuning. The full model demonstrated superior performance in 25 out of the 28 evaluations (Supplementary Fig. S5, Supplementary Fig. S6). This performance gap confirms that although incorporating raw data increases computational overhead, it provides the model with essential supplementary information, thereby substantially improving the accuracy of GRN inference. Therefore, the integration of raw data during fine-tuning represents an effective and necessary design choice for enhancing inference performance.

Furthermore, to evaluate the effectiveness of the MHA mechanism during the fine-tuning stage, we compared SGMHA against SGMHA w/o A. In this variant, the attentive feature integration is replaced with a naive element-wise addition. The full model demonstrated superior performance in 23 out of the 28 evaluations (Supplementary Fig. S7, Supplementary Fig. S8). This consistent performance gap suggests that simply aggregating graph topological features and pre-trained semantic embeddings is insufficient. The MHA mechanism is crucial for dynamically weighting and capturing complex regulatory logic, thereby effectively enhancing the accuracy of GRN inference.

Finally, we assessed the contribution of the self-supervised pre-training stage by evaluating SGMHA w/o P, a variant that relies on randomly initialized node representations trained entirely from scratch. The full model outperformed this pre-training-ablated variant in 22 out of the 28 evaluations (Supplementary Fig. S9, Supplementary Fig. S10). This performance gap confirms that self-supervised pre-training equips the model with essential prior semantic and structural knowledge. It enables SGMHA to learn more generalizable and resilient representations, effectively mitigating the challenges posed by the inherent sparsity and noise of scRNA-seq data.

Sensitivity to prior network quality

To evaluate the robustness of SGMHA and assess its dependency on the quality of the prior network, we conducted a comprehensive sensitivity analysis. Specifically, we simulated scenarios of incomplete prior knowledge by randomly dropping 10% to 40% of the established regulatory edges in the cell-type-specific ChIP-seq networks for both hESC and mESC datasets. To ensure a rigorous evaluation, for each specific edge drop rate, the SGMHA model was completely re-trained from scratch, encompassing both the self-supervised pre-training and the attentive fine-tuning stages.

As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S11, SGMHA exhibited remarkable stability against the degradation of prior structural information. For instance, in the mESC dataset (Top 500 genes), removing up to 40% of the prior edges resulted in only a moderate decrease in AUROC (from 0.93 to 0.88), while the AUPRC shifted from 0.85 to 0.77. Similar robust trends were observed across the hESC datasets.

These findings underscore SGMHA’s capacity to maintain robust performance regardless of the completeness of the initial topological structure. By effectively mitigating, rather than strictly relying on, the structural quality of the prior network, the framework exhibits strong resilience to potential structural noise or missing regulatory information.

Computational efficiency

To assess practical usability, we evaluated SGMHA on its most demanding configuration—the mESC dataset with TFs and the top 1000 most-varying genes using the cell-type-specific ChIP-seq network—on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The training process for 1,000 epochs completed in 419.71 seconds with a peak GPU memory footprint of only 0.53 GB, while inference on the full test set took merely 0.0020 seconds. These quantitative results confirm that despite its sophisticated MHA design, SGMHA imposes no prohibitive computational burden and remains highly practical for standard GRN inference tasks. This efficiency stems fundamentally from its decoupled learning paradigm: the self-supervised GraphMAE pre-training is performed once as a reusable investment, while the optimized attention-based fine-tuning enables extremely rapid inference.

In contrast, less aggressive tumors show reduced MAPK or PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway activity and may activate apoptotic pathways, which not only eliminate tumor cells but also promote immune cell infiltration via damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). We next extracted state-specific GRN components to investigate mechanisms exclusive to metastasis. Enrichment analysis of the metastasis-specific TF-target interactions revealed significant association with multiple metastasis-related signaling pathways and functions, a pattern not observed in the primary tumor-specific network (Supplementary Table S3). By integrating RNA-seq data from 52 primary and 6 lung-metastatic breast cancer samples, we identified 663 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| \log _2 \textrm{FC}\right|$$\end{document} > 0.5 and p< 0.05) (325 downregulated, 338 upregulated; Fig. 3b). Intersection with the metastasis-specific GRN yielded 26 high-confidence candidate genes (6 down, 20 up; Fig 3c, d). These genes were enriched in key metastatic pathways including ErbB, HIF-1, VEGF, mTOR, PI3K-Akt, and Notch signaling, as well as growth hormone signaling and ERK1/ERK2 cascades (Supplementary Table S4). These pathways form an adaptive signaling network facilitating metastatic colonization.

VEGF is a key driver of breast cancer metastasis, primarily through promoting angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis to supply nutrients and dissemination routes. Its pathway overexpression correlates with poor survival [44, 45]. Tumor microenvironment-derived VEGF downregulates E-cadherin and upregulates N-cadherin via the PI3K/AKT/Snail axis, enhancing cell migration and basement membrane degradation [46]. HRAS-mutant breast cancers exhibit elevated VEGF expression and vascularization, likely associated with enhanced MAPK signaling [47]. Src-PTK2 signaling, essential for the β5 integrin-mediated malignant phenotype in triple-negative breast cancer [48], requires upstream HRAS and PTK2 activation for optimal VEGF function. PI3K family genes and downstream AKT signaling amplify VEGF activity; PIK3CA activating mutations increase PIP3 levels, promoting AKT2 membrane recruitment and phosphorylation by PDK1/mTORC2, thereby prolonging VEGF half-life [49]. AKT phosphorylation also relieves HIF-1α transcriptional repression, upregulating VEGF-A/C and VEGFR expression and establishing a positive feedback loop [50]. The MAPK pathway directly regulates VEGF expression in response to extracellular stimuli and functions downstream of VEGF to facilitate metastasis [51]. In ERBB2 signaling, PI3K-AKT-mTOR acts as the primary downstream effector. ERBB2 activation triggers PI3K-mediated PIP3 production, initiating the AKT-mTOR cascade essential for biosynthesis, proliferation, and distant metastasis [52–54]. EGF binding to ERBB2/EGFR dimers recruits SOS1/SOS2, activating HRAS/KRAS and the RAF family to initiate the MAPK cascade. This leads to nuclear translocation of downstream effectors such as MYC and JUN, which promote expression of cell cycle genes (e.g., Cyclin D) and tumor factors including VEGF, thereby enhancing invasiveness and drug resistance [55–57]. The regulatory relationships among these key genes in our predicted lung metastasis GRN are illustrated in Fig. 3e.

Survival analysis identified NDUFAF4, ENY2, CCT5, PGK1, DCTPP1, and H2AFZ as significantly associated with poor prognosis (HR > 1; Supplementary Table S5, Fig. 3f). Among these, NDUFAF4 and ENY2 were also dysregulated in breast cancer patients with bone metastasis (Fig. 3g), suggesting potential commonalities between lung and bone metastasis mechanisms and highlighting their promise as broad metastatic predictors/therapeutic targets. High expression of either NDUFAF4 or ENY2 correlated with reduced overall survival (Fig. 3h). A detailed biological interpretation of these six candidate driver genes, including their specific mechanistic contributions to metastatic adaptation and translational implications for targeted combination therapies across multiple cancer types, is provided in Supplementary Note 1. Limitations in dataset scale necessitate further experimental validation of the SGMHA-predicted GRN.

In summary, our study demonstrates the utility of the SGMHA model in reconstructing context-specific GRNs and identifying key drivers of breast cancer lung metastasis, offering a valuable approach for discovering therapeutic targets and predicting clinical outcomes.

Discussion

The inference of GRNs from single-cell RNA-seq data remains challenging due to data sparsity, high dimensionality, and technical noise. Existing methods face limitations: unsupervised approaches often achieve limited accuracy in complex biological contexts, while supervised methods typically depend on manual feature engineering or fail to adequately capture gene-level semantics. To address these issues, we propose SGMHA, a two-stage framework that integrates self-supervised representation learning with attentive supervised fine-tuning.

In the first stage, SGMHA employs GraphMAE to pre-train gene representations by reconstructing masked node features, capturing both expression patterns and topological dependencies without labeled regulatory links. The second stage uses an MHA mechanism to dynamically integrate these pre-trained embeddings with the original graph structure and raw expression data. Unlike standard feature concatenation, SGMHA employs an asymmetric attention design: GCN-extracted topological features serve as the Query, while pre-trained semantic representations act as the Key and Value. Biologically, this allows the physical network architecture to actively guide the dynamic extraction of expression signals, forming comprehensive representations that combine high-level semantics with detailed features.

Evaluations against eight state-of-the-art methods demonstrate SGMHA’s superior performance. It achieved perfect AUROC scores on all non-specific ChIP-seq networks and matched or exceeded the best baseline in over 85% of AUROC evaluations. This robust performance confirms its architectural advantages: it surpasses unsupervised methods in accuracy, exceeds feature-engineered models in efficiency, and outperforms other GNN-based approaches through its pre-training and fusion strategy. Beyond performance metrics, the core strength of SGMHA lies in its ability to bridge the gap between static structural knowledge and biological heterogeneity. While the input prior provides a universal blueprint of potential interactions, SGMHA leverages state-specific scRNA-seq expression matrices to explicitly prune inactive prior edges and accurately weight contextually active regulatory links. This refinement process allows the model to transform a generic reference into a highly context-specific GRN, successfully capturing the unique regulatory landscapes of different disease states, such as primary and metastatic tumors. Furthermore, SGMHA demonstrates excellent scalability and tolerance for varying dataset sizes. Even when applied to high-throughput scRNA-seq datasets involving massive cell populations and extensive gene sets, the framework efficiently manages the computational demands without encountering significant memory bottlenecks, highlighting its practical utility for large-scale atlas-level network inference.

Biologically, SGMHA reconstructed context-specific GRNs for breast cancer primary and metastatic tumors. Functional analysis revealed enhanced activation of key metastatic pathways—including VEGF, ERBB, and PI3K-Akt signaling—in the metastatic network. By integrating the metastasis-specific GRN with differential expression analysis, SGMHA identified 26 high-confidence candidate driver genes. Six of these (NDUFAF4, ENY2, CCT5, PGK1, DCTPP1, and H2AFZ) were further characterized as key mediators of metastatic adaptation and validated as prognostic biomarkers. While our current approach successfully identifies metastasis drivers by intersecting state-specific GRNs with differential expression analysis, the comparative methodology for cross-condition networks could be further systematized. Advanced graph representation learning frameworks designed specifically for network comparison, such as iHerd, provide a compelling future direction [58]. By employing hierarchical graph alignment, such methods can quantitatively measure the topological rewiring of gene communities across different biological conditions, offering a more native graph-learning approach to directly prioritize disease-specific risk genes.

Despite its significant advantages, certain limitations of the current SGMHA framework require consideration. First, as demonstrated by our sensitivity analysis, while SGMHA successfully reduces dependency on the prior network, achieving optimal fine-tuning performance still benefits from a baseline level of reference graph quality. Second, while the model accurately predicts the existence of directed regulatory links, it currently does not explicitly classify the specific nature of the regulation (i.e., distinguishing between activation and repression). Finally, the current framework relies exclusively on transcriptomic data and operates in a static manner, meaning it does not explicitly model dynamic regulatory interactions over time or capture epigenetic factors (such as chromatin accessibility) that also fundamentally govern gene regulation.

To address these limitations and further advance the field, our future research will focus on several key directions. First, to further minimize the remaining dependency on prior networks and move towards zero-shot inference, we will explore advanced transfer learning strategies. By adapting techniques from other successful bioinformatics applications, such as protein crystallization prediction [59, 60], or leveraging pre-trained single-cell foundation models, SGMHA could achieve robust zero-shot network inference. Second, we plan to enhance the model’s output by introducing an edge-classification module capable of inferring the excitatory or inhibitory nature of the identified regulatory links. Third, to shift from static snapshot modeling to capturing the inherently dynamic nature of gene regulation, we aim to incorporate temporal modeling. By integrating pseudo-time trajectory or RNA velocity data, the framework could infer time-varying regulatory networks that track disease progression. Finally, to move towards more comprehensive regulatory modeling, we will pursue the integration of multi-omics data (such as scATAC-seq) and spatial transcriptomics. This will open new avenues for reconstructing spatially resolved, multi-layered regulatory and cell-cell communication networks within complex microenvironments.

In summary, SGMHA significantly advances GRN inference by effectively combining self-supervised learning with attentive supervised prediction, achieving state-of-the-art performance while generating biologically interpretable and clinically relevant insights.

Conclusions

This study introduces SGMHA, a computational framework that advances GRN inference from scRNA-seq data by innovatively integrating self-supervised graph reconstruction with MHA mechanisms. The model’s two-phase architecture effectively addresses the persistent challenges of data sparsity and high dimensionality, establishing a new paradigm for accurate network inference.

Comprehensive evaluations across seven scRNA-seq benchma- rks demonstrate SGMHA’s superior performance, outperforming eight state-of-the-art methods in 85.7% of AUROC and 61.9% of AUPRC assessments. The framework achieves perfect AUROC scores on all non-specific ChIP-seq networks, with ablation studies confirming the essential roles of both the masking mechanism and raw data integration. Beyond technical merits, SGMHA reconstructs distinct GRNs for primary and metastatic breast tumors, identifying 26 high-confidence candidate drivers. Notably, six candidate drivers—NDUFAF4, ENY2, CCT5, PGK1, DCTPP1, and H2AFZ—emerge as validated prognostic biomarkers that exhibit coordinated roles in sustaining metastatic demands and significant correlation with poor patient survival.

Beyond its technical achievements, SGMHA represents a conceptual advance in computational biology methodology. The strategic integration of graph-based self-supervision with attention mechanisms creates a powerful approach for extracting meaningful biological insights from complex single-cell data. This architectural innovation provides a scalable foundation for future research in network biology.

The framework’s modular design naturally extends to several promising directions, including dynamic network modeling and multi-omics integration. For instance, if SGMHA’s inferred GRNs are integrated into spatial transcriptomics models, it could provide critical intracellular context for reconstructing macroscopic cell-cell communication networks [61]. As a robust tool for uncovering context-specific regulatory mechanisms, SGMHA enables deeper exploration of gene regulation across diverse biological contexts and disease states, opening new avenues for both computational and experimental investigation.
