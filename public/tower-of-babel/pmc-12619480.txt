Analgesic effects of bilateral ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block versus patient controlled intravenous analgesia in correction surgery for idiopathic scoliosis: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chenxi Liao, Jie Zhong, Xingning Lai, Ren Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScoliosis correction surgery involves surgical wounds, intense intraoperative stimulation, and severe postoperative pain, often necessitating the use of potent-opioids-based patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA), which have obvious side effects. An effective pain management strategy with both ensuring sufficient analgesia and minimizing opioid-related adverse effects is an urgent need in clinical practice. The ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block (ESPB) has been reported to provide effective and safe analgesia in many surgical types. Building on this evidence, we hypothesize that ESPB can significantly reduce postoperative pain scores, decrease perioperative opioid consumption, and mitigate opioid-related side effects when compared to PCIA.MethodsThis study is a randomized controlled trial designed to test the superiority of ESPB in providing postoperative analgesia compared with PCIA. Forty participants undergoing idiopathic scoliosis surgery will be enrolled and randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio into either the ESPB group or the PCIA group. The primary outcome is the resting numerical rating scale (NRS) pain score within 2 h postoperatively. The secondary outcomes include perioperative opioid consumption, the incidence of opioid-related adverse effects, and postoperative NRS pain scores on movement.DiscussionThe study will provide evidence for ESPB as an innovative and effective method for postoperative analgesia in scoliosis correction surgery. The results of this trial will contribute to the development of multimodal analgesia regime for managing spinal surgery.Trial registrationThe National Institutes of Health Clinical Trials Registry, NCT06728046. Registered on December 6, 2024.Keywords: Erector spinae plane block, Patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, Idiopathic scoliosis correction surgery, Randomized controlled trial

Background

Scoliosis correction surgery involves significant surgical wounds spanning multiple vertebrae, extensive muscle dissection and intense intraoperative simulation. Excruciating postoperative pain after scoliosis correction surgery could be severely due to multiple factors such as surgical trauma, inflammation, prolonged reflexive muscle spasms and neuropathic mechanisms (Abu Elyazed et al. 2019). In a multicenter cohort study involving 115,775 patients across 179 types of surgical procedures, spinal fusion was associated with the most severe postoperative pain, ranking second and third in pain severity (Adhikary 2018). Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with intravenous potent opioids, such as sufentanil, fentanyl, or morphine, remains the cornerstone for managing postoperative pain. However, this conventional opioids-based analgesia model frequently carries notable side effects, including postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), pruritus, dizziness and potential respiratory depression, which often limit its tolerability and efficacy for achieving adequate analgesia in many clinical settings and negatively impact patients’ quality of life and postoperative recovery. Consequently, alternative techniques of postoperative analgesia for scoliosis correction surgery would be a critical clinical requirement.

In recent decades, Enhanced Recovery after Surgery (ERAS) strategy has emerged as the benchmark for promoting postoperative recovery (Akesen et al. 2022). Multimodal analgesic (MMA) regimen, a fundamental element of ERAS, incorporating various drugs and techniques, has demonstrated superior efficacy in managing postoperative pain while minimizing opioid-related side effects (Canbolat 2022). A key component of MMA is regional anesthesia, which plays a pivotal role in supporting recovery and alleviating postoperative pain by targeting specific nerve regions. Among the emerging regional anesthesia, the erector spinae plane block (ESPB) has garnered increasing attention in recent years. First described by Forero et al. in (2016), ESPB is a novel type of paravertebral interfascial plane block designed for treating neuropathic pain, such as thoracic and dorsal shingles (Chen et al. 2021). The technique involves ultrasound-guided injection of local anesthetic (LA) into fascial plane between the erector spinae muscle and the transverse process of the vertebra, allowing the anesthetic to diffuse anteriorly into paravertebral and epidural spaces and laterally into the intercostal spaces across several levels. By targeting both the ventral and dorsal ramus of the spinal nerves, it achieves effective blockade of the thoracic, abdominal, and dorsal region (Chin 2021; Chin and Lewis 2019). The diffusion of LA into the paravertebral space via the costotransverse foramina and intertransverse complex provides a combination of visceral and somatic pain relief (Chin et al. 2020). ESPB, being relatively simple to perform, accommodating lower coagulation function, and offering a puncture site positioned safely away from the spinal cord, minimizes the risk of spinal cord injury and other complications. ESPB has since been widely applied in perioperative pain management for thoracic surgery, shoulder arthroscopy, breast surgery, abdominal surgery, cesarean sections, and urological procedures, as well as for acute and chronic pain conditions such as rib fractures, postherpetic neuralgia, burns, and cancer pain (Canbolat 2022; Cirenei et al. 2021; Coppens et al. 2023; Desai et al. 2024; Duan et al. 2022; Elsharkawy et al. 2019; Eochagain 2022; Forero et al. 2016; Fu et al. 2023; Gerbershagen et al. 2013; Gillies et al. 1999; Goel et al. 2021). Studies have demonstrated that ESPB effectively reduces postoperative pain scores, decreases opioids consumption, delays the need for rescue analgesics, reduces the number of patients requiring rescue medication, and lowers the incidence of nausea and vomiting in spinal surgeries (Gürkan et al. 2018; Harbell et al. 2020; He et al. 2020; Kamel et al. 2020; Kanna et al. 2023; Kot et al. 2019; Mathiesen et al. 2013; Melvin 2018). As demonstrated in the case report by Desai et al., a multi-modal approach incorporating opioid-obviating analgesia significantly reduced opioid consumption and facilitated rapid recovery in adolescent spinal fusion surgery (Mitra et al. 2018). However, most existing studies on scoliosis correction surgery are limited to case reports and small-sample retrospective analyses, with randomized controlled trials being scarce and narrowly focused. Based on the analgesic mechanism of ESPB and previous findings, we hypothesize that ESPB provides superior postoperative pain control compared to PCIA, significantly reducing postoperative pain scores, perioperative opioid consumption, and associated adverse effects.

Methods/design

Objectives/hypothesis

This study aims to evaluate the safety and efficacy of bilateral ESPB compared to PCIA for postoperative analgesia following scoliosis correction surgery and provide new insights and evidence to enhance clinical practice in multimodal analgesia.

Study design

This single-center, prospective, exploratory randomized controlled trial that test the superiority of bilateral ultrasound-guided ESPB in relief of the postoperative pain was approved by the Biological-Medical Ethical Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (WCH20241806). This study was prospectively registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06728046) on December 6, 2024, and it will be conducted under the regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent will be obtained from patients or their legal guardians before they participate in the study. The study design will be strictly accordance with the e Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 statement and adhere to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (http://www.consort-statement.org/). Patient recruitment

The investigator will screen all patients scheduled for idiopathic scoliosis correction surgery one day before surgery and provide detailed explanation of the study to the eligible participants. Research assistants will then inform the participants that they have the right to withdraw their consent at any time and obtain the informed consent form. The study is anticipated to start on January 1, 2025, and expected to be completed on May 31, 2027. The current status of the trial is preparing for recruitment of participants.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria are (1) patient aged 14 years old or older, regardless of gender, (2) scheduled for scoliosis correction surgery, (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, (4) and signed informed consent from the participant or their legal guardian.

Exclusion criteria are (1) allergy to local anesthetics such as ropivacaine, (2) family history of malignant hyperthermia, (3) severe systemic diseases affecting the heart, lungs, liver, or kidneys, (4) neurological or psychiatric disorders, (5) contraindication of ESPB: coagulation abnormality, bleeding disorders, and infection at the puncture site, (6) communication difficulties, such as hearing impairment or speech disorders, (7) concurrent participation in other clinical studies, (7) and other conditions deemed unsuitable for participation by the investigators.

Dropout criteria are (1) participant withdrawal from the study and (2) investigators terminating the involvement of participants who meet the exclusion criteria or whose safety is jeopardized. The detailed reason for withdrawal will be recorded and reported by research assistants.

Randomization and blinding

Participants will be randomly assigned to either the ESPB group or PCIA group in a 1:1 ratio based on a computer-generated random number table, with the randomization procedure carried out by an independent third-party statistician who is not involved in the trial. This study is designed as an open-label trial, meaning that the investigators, participants, and clinical staff will be aware of treatment allocation. The differences in clinical management such as the additional costs and the label of the controlled opioids like sufentanil on PCIA pump complicating blinding workflow. However, to minimize the risk of bias, follow-up researchers and statisticians responsible for data analysis will remain blinded to the group assignments. Thus, while the trial is open-label, the outcome assessment and statistical analysis will be conducted under blinded conditions.

Sample size calculation

The study hypothesizes that the application of ESPB will result in a reduction in pain scores 2 h postoperatively compared to PCIA. The effect of postoperative analgesia is considered as the primary outcome and compared between groups with a superiority design. Based on previous clinical experience, the numerical rating scale (NRS) pain score at 2 h postoperatively with PCIA is estimated to be 5 ± 2. It is assumed that ESPB can decrease NRS to 3. Using a significance level of α = 0.05, a power of 1-β = 0.8, and a superiority margin of 1, 16 participants are required in each group. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, 20 participants per group will be recruited, for a total of 40 participants.

Study procedures

General anesthesia and perioperative management

Patients will undergo standard monitoring including blood pressure, electrocardiogram, pulse oximetry, and end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) after entering the operating room. General anesthesia induction with sufentanil 0.3–0.5 μg/kg, cisatracurium 0.15 mg/kg, and propofol 1.5–2.5 mg/kg will be applied followed by sevoflurane 1 ~ 1.5 MAC for anesthesia maintenance. Additional remifentanil 0.1 ~ 0.3 μg/kg/min (to be discontinued 20 min before surgery ends), sedatives, and vasoactive drugs could be administered according to the anesthesiologists to maintain hemodynamic parameters alteration within 20% compared to the baseline values. After extubating in the operating room, patients will be transported to the post-anesthesia care unit and monitored for at least 30 min.

Trial groups and intervention

ESPB group

Participants will receive bilateral bilevel ultrasound-guided ESPB in the prone position before intubation. The affected spinal segment will be divided into three equal parts, with puncture sites selected at the trisection points on each side. Using a linear array high-frequency ultrasound probe and a 22-gauge block needle to access the plane between the transverse process and the deep surface of the erector spinae muscle. Once the needle tip contacts the bony surface of the transverse process, 1–2 mL of saline will be injected to confirm the correct location. After confirming no return of blood or cerebrospinal fluid upon aspiration, 10 mL of 0.3% ropivacaine (maximum of 3 mg/kg) will be injected at each site. A total of 40 mL of ropivacaine will be administered, with 20 mL injected on each side.

PCIA group

Participants will receive postoperative PCIA with a formulation of 200 µg sufentanil diluted to 100 ml. The PCIA settings will include a background dose of 3 ml/h, a lockout time of 15 min, and a bolus dose of 2 ml.

Acute pain rescue measures

Sulfentanil or fentanyl will be administered to participants in the PACU when their NRS pain score exceeds 4. In the ward, if a participant's resting NRS pain score exceeds 4, an intravenous dose of 5 mg of morphine may be administered. If the pain score remains ≥ 5 after 30 min, an additional 5 mg of morphine may be given. The total daily morphine dose will be capped at 30 mg.

Safety assurance

The whole team will adhere strictly to the established protocol to ensure procedural consistency and patient safety. The ESPB will be performed exclusively by experienced anesthesiologists under close monitoring of vital signs aiming to minimize the risk of adverse effects including spinal nerve injury, pneumothorax, local anesthetic systemic toxicity and infection. Furthermore, the lead investigator is responsible for deciding the trial status of patients with severe adverse events and following up their postoperative recovery. To monitor long-term outcomes, patients who experience significant postoperative complications, persistent pain, or impaired functional recovery will be followed up for 30 days postoperatively via outpatient visits or telephone interviews. During the follow-up, pain intensity, functional status, and the presence of any delayed adverse events will be assessed.

Data collection

Baseline data including demographic characteristics, ASA classification, comorbidities, and surgical vertebral span range will be collected by research assistants. Intraoperative data such as anesthesia duration, surgical duration, and total anesthetics amount will be extracted from the anesthesia record sheet. And follow-up data collection will be conducted by researchers blinded to the intervention to ensure unbiased results. Follow-up data like NRS scores and postoperative opioid usage will be documented by researchers who are unaware of the study intervention.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The resting NRS pain scores at 2 h postoperatively.

Secondary outcomes

Mortality and the incidence of severe complications categorized into five grades based on severity: Grade I involves issues resolved with temporary treatment (e.g., postoperative nausea and vomiting); Grade II requires a prolonged hospital stay (e.g., pneumonia needing antibiotics); Grade III includes life-threatening events recoverable during hospitalization (e.g., deep vein thrombosis); Grade IV signifies significant harm lasting 30 days or more with a marked decrease in quality of life (e.g., postoperative cognitive dysfunction, delirium); and Grade V represents death within 30 days postoperatively. This system is an original classification developed for this study.

Data management

The implementation of the study, as well as the completeness and accuracy of the data, will be supervised by the Department of Anesthesiology and the Office of Scientific Research at West China Hospital. Dr. Ren Liao, acting as the alert personnel, will be responsible for managing serious complications and making the final decision regarding the termination of the trial. A Data Safety and Monitoring Board, consisting of an anesthesiologist, orthopedic surgeon, ethicist, statistician, and an internal physician relevant to the trial, will oversee the study throughout its duration. The board will periodically review all investigational data for accuracy and completeness to ensure compliance with the protocol. No preliminary analysis will be conducted prior to the completion of the study. All personnel will adhere to strict confidentiality guidelines, and there are no conflicts of interest. Prior to the commencement of the trial, all study team members will undergo specialized training relevant to the research.

Statistical analysis

All outcomes will be analyzed by a modified intention-to-treat-principle (ITT) principle. The resting NRS pain score at 2 h postoperatively, day 1, 2, and 3, as well as NRS pain scores on movement from day 1 to day 3 after operation will be analyzed using the two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution will be presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using the Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables will be expressed as median (interquartile range, P25–P75) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Group comparison of categorical variables described as percentages (%) will use chi-square test (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 indicates statistically significant.

Discussion

Correction of idiopathic scoliosis deformation is a major spine surgery with extensive surgical incision and multiple tissue trauma. The majority of patients undergoing this type of surgery are adolescents or younger children, with a global prevalence ranging from 0.47% to 5.20%, accounting for approximately 90% of all idiopathic scoliosis cases (Peng et al. 2020; Restrepo-Garces et al. 2017). Studies have reported that, compared to adults, pediatric patients exhibit heightened sensitivity to pain stimuli, making severe postoperative pain a common and challenging clinical issue (Sadhasivam et al. 2009; Shanthanna et al. 2022). Poorly controlled postoperative pain can significantly impair patients’ mobility, delay functional recovery, and eventually elevate the risk of complications such as deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary infections, thereby leading to prolonged hospitalization and increased healthcare cost (Singh et al. 2020). PCIA using opioids including morphine, sufentanil, or fentanyl, often in combination with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or sedatives such as dexmedetomidine or benzodiazepines is the most commonly modality for postoperative pain treatment. Although PCIA had been shown to be safe and effective in patients, its broader clinical application is restrained by the high incidence of opioids-related complications, particularly when a continuous background infusion is employed. Additionally, pain arises from a complex interplay of mechanisms including nociceptive stimulation, inflammatory responses, and central sensitization (Taenzer and Clark 2010). Previously considered gold standard PCIA is now deemed insufficient for postoperative analgesia. So, integrating multimodal analgesia with regional blocks is crucial for optimizing pain control and minimizing opioid-related side effects following scoliosis correction surgery.

With the introduction of ultrasound in regional anesthesia, plane blocks have become increasingly popular in anesthetic practice due to their ease of administration and the ability to reduce technique-related complications. Previous studies have demonstrated that, for patients undergoing spine surgery such as posterior lumbar surgery, lumbar internal fixation, and lumbar spinal decompression surgery, preoperative ultrasound-guided ESPB with either ropivacaine or bupivacaine can provide significantly decreased postoperative pain scores, reduce opioid consumption, facilitate earlier mobilization and promote faster recovery (Harbell et al. 2020; He et al. 2020; Kanna et al. 2023; Melvin 2018; Taenzer and Clark 2010; Tulgar 2019; Urits et al. 2019; Varela et al. 2024; Vidal et al. 2018; Wittayapairoj et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2021). Moreover, researchers have noted that the combination of local anesthetic with dexmedetomidine, ketamine or dexamethasone in ESPB provides satisfactory analgesic effect evidenced by lower NRS scores and prolongs the duration of pain relief in patients undergoing posterior spinal fusion (Gürkan et al. 2018; Harbell et al. 2020). Based on these findings, we speculate that ultrasound-guided ESPB could serve as a safe and effective technique for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing idiopathic scoliosis correction surgery, and provide superior postoperative pain control compared to PCIA, while also reducing perioperative opioid consumption and associated adverse effects.

Although the clinical application of ESPB is increasingly widespread, its underlying mechanism of action, the extent of drug diffusion, and the optimal dosing regimen remain controversial. Based on current evidence, the plausible mechanism of ESPB is attributed to the direct diffusion of local anesthetics and their action on neural targets (Yayik et al. 2019). Furthermore, differences in spinal nerve courses, muscle anatomy, and individual variability across spinal segments result in variations in both the extent and direction of local anesthetic diffusion during ESPB (Yuan et al. 2022; Zhang 2020; Zhang et al. 2021, 2023). In particular, body weight may influence drug distribution, duration of action, and the potential for systemic absorption. The involvement of different spinal levels can also affect the consistency of analgesic coverage. These factors highlight the need for a personalized analgesia strategy rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. So, we plan to incorporate baseline variables such as body weight, height, surgical duration and the spinal segment locations (thoracic versus lumbar) to conduct the subgroup analyses. Despite the relatively small sample size, these preliminary analyses will help identify trends and inform hypotheses for future large-scale studies. Future research should aim to build predictive models or apply stratified analyses to refine ESPB protocols based on patient-specific anatomical and procedural variables. Incorporating imaging parameters (e.g., ultrasound depth, muscle layer thickness) may further enhance the precision and efficacy of ESPB across diverse populations undergoing spinal surgery.

This study has several inevitable limitations. First, open-label design may lead to selection bias, allocation bias, or measurement bias, potentially influencing patient inclusion, exclusion, and outcome analysis phases. Second, our single-center study, conducted with only 40 patients, cannot adequately represent populations from other regions. Expanding the sample size and increasing the number of centers involved would be necessary to enhance the reliability and generalizability of the trial results.

In conclusion, the further development of ESPB should emphasize individualized and precision-based protocol to align with the principle of ERAS. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial based on clinical practice to evaluate the application of ultrasound-guided ESPB in adolescents and adults undergoing scoliosis correction surgery. The findings of this study will provide evidence for improving pain management in this type of surgery with severe postoperative pain, enhance healthcare quality, and improve the overall patient experience.
