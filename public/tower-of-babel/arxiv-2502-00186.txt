Formalising Propositional Information via Implication Hypergraphs
Vibhu Dalal

Abstract
This work introduces a framework for quantifying the information content of logical propositions through the use of implication hypergraphs. We posit that a proposition’s informativeness is primarily determined by its relationships with other propositions; specifically, the extent to which it implies or derives other propositions. To formalize this notion, we develop a framework based on implication hypergraphs, that seeks to capture these relationships. Within this framework, we define propositional information, derive some key properties, and illustrate the concept through examples. While the approach is broadly applicable, mathematical propositions emerge as an ideal domain for its application due to their inherently rich and interconnected structure. We provide several examples to illustrate this and subsequently discuss the limitations of the framework, along with suggestions for potential refinements.



1.  Introduction

Information theory provides a mathematical definition of the ’information’ of an event, expressing it as a function of the event’s probability. This formalism was first introduced in Claude Shannon’s seminal work,
A mathematical theory of communication

[
5
]
. In this context, we extend this concept to logical propositions: how can we quantify the information content of a proposition? An information-theoretic perspective might suggest treating logical propositions as events and then calculating their Shannon information. However, a key challenge arises, as there is no straightforward probability distribution that can be assigned to these ’events’ (logical propositions).

One potential solution involves heuristically estimating the probability of a proposition’s ’occurrence’, perhaps by analyzing its frequency within a relevant corpus or body of literature. While intriguing, this approach is both complex and, more critically, misaligned with our intuitive understanding of what makes a statement informative. In this framework, a proposition with a low probability (or low frequency) would inherently be assigned a higher information value. However, from an intuitive standpoint, it is unclear why rarity alone should determine the informativeness of a proposition.

Several studies have explored methods for representing or quantifying logical propositions in various forms. For example,
[
7
]
introduces a framework where propositions are modeled as vectors in a logical space, while
[
2
]
proposes the concept of a proposition space with associated logical transformations. In
[
3
]
, the focus is on exploring ’degrees’ of information and contradiction within a propositional framework, whereas
[
1
]
uses implication hypergraphs for the representation and simplification of propositional formulae. However, to the best of our knowledge, none of the works explicitly addresses the task of numerically quantifying the information content of a logical proposition.

We propose that the informativeness of a proposition is intrinsically linked to its relationships with other propositions. More precisely, a proposition’s informativeness can be quantified by the number of other propositions derivable from it, that is, the extent of its implications. This perspective aligns with the conceptual notion of an informative statement: one that establishes numerous meaningful connections or implications.

To formalize this idea, we use implication hypergraphs, which provide a flexible and strctured framework to represent these relationships. Within this framework, we define propositional information, derive some key results and properties, and illustrate the concept through examples. Prior to this, we introduce some preliminary definitions and terminology that will be used throughout the text.

While the framework we present is general, its primary focus is on mathematical propositions. Mathematics presents a vast and intricate web of interconnected propositions, where each connection stems from a previously established theorem or result. This inherent structure makes it an ideal domain for using implication hypergraphs to compute propositional information. As a simple example, stating that a set is ’compact’ is usually far more informative than stating it is ’closed’. This is because, firstly, compactness implies closure (in a Hausdorff space), and secondly, compactness plays a central role in numerous theorems and results in analysis. The tools developed in this work not only enable such comparisons of informativeness but also provide a method to quantify it. In Section 6, we present a couple of examples illustrating this idea.

2.  Preliminary Definitions

Definition 2.1

(Hypergraph)
.

A hypergraph generalizes a graph by allowing a hyperedge to connect any number of nodes. It is represented as
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
, where
\displaystyle V
is the set of vertices, and
\displaystyle E
is the set of hyperedges.

For a directed hypergraph, a hyperedge is an ordered pair
\displaystyle e=(T,H)
with
\displaystyle T,H\subseteq V
as the
tail
(source nodes) and
head
(target nodes), respectively. We denote
\displaystyle t:E\to P(V)
and
\displaystyle h:E\to P(V)
such that
\displaystyle t(e)=T
and
\displaystyle h(e)=H
. Additionally,
\displaystyle L(H)
represents the set of leaf nodes of
\displaystyle H
.

As a simple example of a directed hypergraph, consider the hypergraph
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
, where
\displaystyle V=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
is the set of vertices, and
\displaystyle E=\{(\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\{v_{3}\})\}
is the set of directed hyperedges. The directed hyperedge
\displaystyle(\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\{v_{3}\})
consists of the
tail

\displaystyle\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, which represents the source vertices, and the
head

\displaystyle\{v_{3}\}
, which represents the target vertex.

Definition 2.2

(Implication Hypergraph)
.

An
implication hypergraph
is a specialized directed hypergraph used to represent implication relationships between logical propositions. Formally, it is a pair:

H=(V,E),

where:

•

\displaystyle V
is the set of vertices, each representing a logical proposition,

•

\displaystyle E
is the set of directed hyperedges, where each hyperedge
\displaystyle e=(T,H)
represents the relation:

\bigwedge_{P\in T}P\implies\bigwedge_{Q\in H}Q

Directed hyperedges in implication hypergraphs represent logical relationships, where the set of propositions in the tail collectively imply the proposition(s) in the head. Figure
1
shows a simple example of an implication hypergraph.

.

Definition 2.3

(Strict Implication Hypergraph)
.

A
strict
implication hypergraph is an implication hypergraph
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
that satisfies the following property:

\forall v,w\in V,(v,w)\in E\implies(w,v)\notin E.

Implication hypergraphs have been previously employed to represent logical propositions (

minimal

Definition 2.4

(Minimal Implication Hypergraph)
.

A
minimal
implication hypergraph is an implication hypergraph
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
that satisfies the following properties:

(i)

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,(v,v)\notin E
.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle v\in V
and
\displaystyle u
is a descendant of
\displaystyle v
but not an immediate neighbor, then
\displaystyle(v,u)\notin E.

(iii)

If
\displaystyle e=(T,H)\in E
, then
\displaystyle e^{\prime}=(T^{\prime},H)\notin E
where
\displaystyle T\subset T^{\prime}
.

3.  Propositional Information

In our model of propositional information, we are guided by the following principles:

(i)

If
\displaystyle P\implies Q
, then
\displaystyle P
encompasses all the information contained in
\displaystyle Q
.

(ii)

For a leaf node (proposition)
\displaystyle Q
, the information it contains is a fixed, strictly positive value, denoted by
\displaystyle\nu
.

(iii)

If
\displaystyle P\implies Q
but
\displaystyle Q\not\implies P
, then
\displaystyle P
contains strictly more information than
\displaystyle Q
. This additional information is in part quantified by a strictly positive value, denoted by
\displaystyle\epsilon
.

(iv)

If
\displaystyle P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{n}
collectively imply
\displaystyle Q
, then each
\displaystyle P_{i}
contributes an equal share of the information leading to
\displaystyle Q
, specifically
\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}
of the total information derived from
\displaystyle Q
.

The final point generalizes the first principle to scenarios involving hyperedges, where multiple propositions collectively imply a single proposition.

Before defining propositional information, we first define the adjacency matrix of an implication hypergraph.

Definition 3.1

(Adjacency Matrix)
.

Let
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
be an implication hypergraph. We denote its vertices by
\displaystyle V=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}
.
Let
\displaystyle E_{i}=\{e\in E\mid p_{i}\in h(e)\}
. Then the adjacency matrix of
\displaystyle H
,
\displaystyle A
, is defined by:

A_{ij}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|h(e)|}\mathbf{1}_{p_{j}\in t(e)}

for
\displaystyle 1\leq i,j\leq n
.

As an example, the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A
corresponding to the implication hypergraph shown in Figure
1
is:

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0\\
0&0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&1\\
0&0&0&1\\
0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

Definition 3.2

(Propositional Information)
.

Let
\displaystyle H\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\displaystyle\nu,\epsilon>0
. Let
\displaystyle A
denote the adjacency matrix of
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
, where
\displaystyle V=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
. For
\displaystyle p_{i}\in V
, we define its propositional information as follows:

\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{j})+\epsilon),&\text{if }p_{i}\notin L(H),\\
\nu,&\text{if }p_{i}\in L(H).\end{cases}

The definition of propositional information is implicit by nature, requiring the resolution of a system of equations where
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{n})
are the unknowns. Notably, this definition neither ensures the existence of a solution nor guarantees that the solutions, if they exist, are strictly positive. The proposition below addresses the issue of existence.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle H\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\displaystyle\nu,\epsilon>0
. Let
\displaystyle A
denote the adjacency matrix of
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
, where
\displaystyle V=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
. Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
be the vector of
\displaystyle n
ones, and
\displaystyle l
be a vector where the
\displaystyle i
-th entry is 1 if
\displaystyle p_{i}
is a leaf node, and 0 otherwise. If
\displaystyle A-I
is invertible, or equivalently if 1 is not an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle A
, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}=(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{n}))
is well-defined, and

\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}=(I-A)^{-1}\left(\epsilon A\mathbf{1}+\nu l\right).

Considering once again the implication hypergraph in Figure
1
, we observe that
\displaystyle A-I
is invertible. Consequently,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}=(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{4}))
is well-defined. Here,
\displaystyle l=(0,0,0,1)
. Applying the formula, we obtain:

\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}\nu+\epsilon\\
\tfrac{3}{2}\nu+2\epsilon\\
\nu+\epsilon\\
\nu\end{bmatrix}

Since we associate the value of information to be positive, we would ideally aim to obtain all positive values through Definition
3.2
. The following definition specifies those implication hypergraphs that satisfy this condition exactly.

Definition 3.3

(Configured Implication Hypergraph)
.

Let
\displaystyle H=(V,E)\in\mathcal{H}
and let
\displaystyle\nu,\epsilon>0
. The vertices of
\displaystyle H
are denoted by
\displaystyle V=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}
. The hypergraph
\displaystyle H
is called a
configured
implication hypergraph if
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})>0
for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})
is as defined in Definition
3.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle H\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\displaystyle A
denote its adjacency matrix. If
\displaystyle H
is
configured
, then for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

0\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}A_{ji}<1.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle H=(V,E)
, where
\displaystyle V=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}
. Since all values of
\displaystyle A
are positive by definition, the left inequality follows immediately. We will now consider the right inequality. Let
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
\displaystyle p_{k}\in L(H)
, then for all
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\displaystyle A_{jk}=0
, and hence the inequality is easily verified. Conversely, suppose that
\displaystyle p_{k}\notin L(H)
. Then, by Definition
3.2
, we know that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{k})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{kj}(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{j})+\epsilon)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{kj}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}A_{jl}(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{l})+\epsilon)+\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{kj}A_{jk}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{k})\>+\>C,

where
\displaystyle C>0
, since for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})>0
, as
\displaystyle H
is
configured
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{k})\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{kj}A_{jk}\right)=C.

Finally, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{k})>0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{kj}A_{jk}<1.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

The necessary condition in Theorem
3.1
is not sufficient. Consider the following example.

Its adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A
is given by:

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&0\\
1&0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&0\\
1&0&0&0\\
0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&0\end{bmatrix}

and it satisfies the following condition:

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\;0\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}A_{ji}<1.

Since
\displaystyle p_{1}
is not a leaf node:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{2})+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon+\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{3})+\frac{1}{3}\epsilon

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})+\frac{1}{6}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{3})+\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})+\frac{1}{6}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})+\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})+C,

where
\displaystyle C>0
. Thus we finally obtain
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})=\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1})+C
, which is impossible. Thus
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}
is not well-defined, and hence the implication hypergraph is not representable.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle H=(V,E)\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\displaystyle V=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}
. Let
\displaystyle A
denote its adjacency matrix. If for all
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the diagonal of
\displaystyle A^{k}
is zero, i.e.,
\displaystyle\text{diag}(A^{k})=\mathbf{0}
, then
\displaystyle H
is
configured
.

Proof.

The case
\displaystyle n=1
is straightforward, as the graph consists of a single node, which is necessarily a leaf node. Consequently, its information value is
\displaystyle\nu>0
. Now, let us consider the case where
\displaystyle n\geq 2
.

We prove by contradiction that
\displaystyle 1
cannot be an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle A
. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
\displaystyle 1
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle A
. Since the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A^{2}
are the squares of the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A
, this would imply that
\displaystyle\text{trace}(A^{2})>0
. However, by our hypothesis, we have
\displaystyle\text{diag}(A^{2})=\mathbf{0}
, leading to a contradiction. Therefore,
\displaystyle 1
cannot be an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle A
. Consequently, by Proposition
3.1
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}=(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{n}))
is well-defined.

Let
\displaystyle p_{i}\in V
. If
\displaystyle p_{i}\in L(H)
, then
\displaystyle I_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})=\nu>0
. Suppose
\displaystyle p_{i}\notin L(H)
. To show that
\displaystyle I_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})>0
, we proceed by contradiction. Suppose that
\displaystyle I_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})\leq 0
. By Definition
3.2
:

I_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{j})+\epsilon).

For all
\displaystyle i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\displaystyle A_{ij}\geq 0
. Let
\displaystyle S\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the set of indices
\displaystyle s
such that
\displaystyle A_{is}>0
for all
\displaystyle s\in S
. Since
\displaystyle p_{i}\notin L(H)
, we have
\displaystyle S\neq\emptyset
. Therefore, there exists
\displaystyle i_{1}\in S
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i_{1}})\leq 0
. Note that there exists a hyperedge
\displaystyle e\in E
such that
\displaystyle i\in H(e)
and
\displaystyle i_{1}\in T(e)
. By repeatedly applying this reasoning, we construct a sequence

\displaystyle Q=(p_{i_{1}},p_{i_{2}},\ldots)
of vertices such that:

1.

For each pair of consecutive vertices
\displaystyle p_{i_{k}},p_{i_{k+1}}
in
\displaystyle Q
, there exists a hyperedge connecting them.

2.

\displaystyle\forall p_{i_{k}}\in Q
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i_{k}})\leq 0
.

3.

Q terminates at
\displaystyle p_{i_{k}}
if and only if
\displaystyle p_{i_{k}}\in L(H)
.

Since
\displaystyle\operatorname{diag}(A^{k})=\mathbf{0}
for all
\displaystyle k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\displaystyle H
is acyclic. Hence, the sequence
\displaystyle Q
must be finite. Let
\displaystyle Q=(p_{i_{1}},p_{i_{2}},\ldots,p_{i_{m}}),

where
\displaystyle m<n
, and consequently
\displaystyle p_{i_{m}}\in L(H)
. However, if
\displaystyle p_{i_{m}}\in L(H)
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i_{m}})=\nu>0,

which contradicts our earlier assumption that
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i_{k}})\leq 0
for all
\displaystyle p_{i_{k}}\in Q
.

Thus,
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}(p_{i})>0
holds for all
\displaystyle p_{i}\notin L(H)
, completing the proof.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

The sufficient condition in Theorem
3.2
is not necessary. Consider the following example.

Its adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A
is given by:

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0\\

and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\nu,\epsilon}
can easily found to be equal to
\displaystyle(2\epsilon,3\epsilon,2\epsilon)

4.  Applications

The extensive corpus of mathematical theorems and results that underline the intricate interconnectedness of mathematical ideas serves as an ideal domain for applying the concepts developed in the preceding sections. Our framework enables the distillation of a set of mathematical results into a single implication hypergraph, allowing us to quantify the information content of each mathematical entity. However, obtaining the final numerical values requires specifying the parameters
\displaystyle\nu
and
\displaystyle\epsilon
.

4.1.  The role of
\displaystyle\epsilon
and
\displaystyle\nu

In a strict implication hypergraph, if a proposition
\displaystyle p_{1}
implies another proposition
\displaystyle p_{2}
, the information content of
\displaystyle p_{1}
must be strictly greater than that of
\displaystyle p_{2}
, and
\displaystyle\epsilon
ensures this. However, if
\displaystyle p_{1}
implies additional propositions beyond
\displaystyle p_{2}
, the cumulative implications naturally ensure that
\displaystyle p_{1}
’s information content remains strictly greater than those it implies. This raises the question: why is
\displaystyle\epsilon
necessary?

The key idea is that if the hypergraph is not ’complete’, which it may never be, or if certain relationships are omitted, the computed information content may underestimate the ’true’ value. The parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon
addresses this incompleteness by introducing a buffer, attempting to bring the calculated information content closer to the true value. Consequently, the choice of
\displaystyle\epsilon
reflects our belief in the completeness of the hypergraph: a relatively small
\displaystyle\epsilon
((compared to
\displaystyle\nu
) indicates high confidence in its completeness, while a larger
\displaystyle\epsilon
compensates for greater uncertainty or perceived omissions.

\displaystyle\nu
, on the other hand, represents a unit of information content. All leaf nodes (propositions) are assigned the same information content, as they are, in a sense, the fundamental ’atoms’ of information. While the exact value of
\displaystyle\nu
is not particularly significant, its relative magnitude compared to
\displaystyle\epsilon
is important, as discussed in the previous paragraph.

5.  Discussion

It may perhaps be theoretically possible to condense most, if not all, of mathematics into an implication hypergraph similar to the ones presented in the previous section. Achieving this would require identifying a comprehensive set of mathematical propositions, simple yet versatile, that can serve as building blocks for representing the vast corpus of mathematical theorems and results. These propositions would serve as the nodes of this envisioned hypergraph. An intriguing reference for such an endeavor could be Bertrand Russell’s
Principia Mathematica

[
4
]
, which systematically formalizes and constructs several fields of mathematics from the ground up. Such a hypergraph, if realized, could provide a striking visual representation of the interconnectedness of mathematical ideas. Furthermore, the concepts developed in this work could then quantify the information content of each of these fundamental mathematical propositions.

This work introduces a straightforward method for quantifying propositional information using implication hypergraphs, while leaving ample room for further exploration and refinement. Potential avenues for future research can include:

•

Analysing the roles of
\displaystyle\nu
and
\displaystyle\epsilon
in depth, and establishing criteria for determining their optimal values.

•

Defining the information of the conjunction of two logical propositions, and eventually generalising it.

•

Revisiting the assumption that, when a group of propositions implies another proposition, each proposition in the group contributes equally to the implication. This oversimplification warrants further refinement.

•

Incorporating additional nuances into the current framework to more effectively capture the factors that contribute to a proposition’s informativeness.

6.  Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a method for quantifying the information content of a logical proposition using implication hypergraphs. We proposed that the information content of a proposition is closely tied to its implication relationships with other propositions; specifically, the more propositions it implies, the more informative it is. Building on this premise, we formally defined propositional information and derived several key properties and results. We then discussed how mathematical propositions provide an ideal domain for applying this framework, and subsequently illustrated this with two examples. Finally, we examined the limitations of the current framework and outlined potential avenues for refinement.
