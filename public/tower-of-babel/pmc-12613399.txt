Reflections on the impact of the Lived Experience Group in the James Lind Alliance Palliative and End of Life Care Priority Setting Partnership Refresh
Authors: Jessica Bush, Jodie Crooks, Anna Dawson, Rashmi Kumar, Alexandra Reece, Diana Robinson, Sabine Best, Briony Hudson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn 2025, Marie Curie and other partners launched the results of a refreshed Priority Setting Partnership with the James Lind Alliance, to identify priorities for palliative and end of life care research, based on the views of people affected by serious life limiting illness. To our knowledge, this was the first Priority Setting Partnership to have a dedicated group of people with lived experience as equal partners in the research. This paper provides reflections for other researchers considering embedding a Lived Experience Group into future Priority Setting Partnerships and provides evidence of the positive impact that this can bring.Main textData on involvement impact and experience was collected using the Public Involvement in Research Toolkit (PIRIT), semi-structured interviews with Lived Experience Group and Steering Group members, and a short online survey for Lived Experience Group participants. The PIRIT tracker recorded 66 impacts throughout the project, which included shaping the project, refining data collection tools, influencing language, encouraging diverse responses, and aiding dissemination.ConclusionsForming a Lived Experience Group to support the refresh of the Palliative and End of Life Care Priority Setting Partnership ensured that people with lived experience of dying, death and bereavement had a clear route for involvement in all stages of the project. This regular and consistent engagement required considerable resource but shaped the project in several ways and ultimately improved its quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-025-00774-4.

Background

Across the UK, and internationally, the number of people who need palliative care is increasing steeply [1, 2]. About 90% of people who die are estimated to need palliative care [3]. Despite this, In the UK in 2022, only 0.23% of health-related research project and programme funding was allocated to this topic [4]. Given the small amount of research funding allocated to this field, it is imperative that research that is funded, aligns with the priorities of people affected by dying, death and bereavement. The James Lind Alliance (JLA) supports and facilitates Priority Setting Partnerships (PSP) that enable patients and people around them, including family members, informal carers and health and social care professionals to identify and prioritise evidence uncertainties that could be answered by research [5]. In 2015, Marie Curie (in partnership with other organisations) launched a Palliative and End of Life Care Priority Setting Partnership, which generated a list of priorities for palliative and end of life care research. Over 1400 people with life limiting illnesses, their families and health and social care professionals contributed their views to this process [6]. Since publication, much progress has been made in addressing these priorities through funded research, targeted grant funding calls, and works of individual researchers [7], but gaps in knowledge relating to these priorities remain.

As a decade has passed since the identification of these initial research priorities, in 2025 Marie Curie, in partnership with the James Lind Alliance, with co-funding from the Motor Neurone Disease Association and the UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) published a refresh of this Priority Setting Partnership.

The UK standards for public involvement outline a framework describing public involvement in research. They are intended to promote reflection and learning around public involvement in research and form the framework for this paper [8].

Underlying project: a refresh of the James Lind Alliance Palliative and End of Life Care Priority Setting Partnership

Published in 2025, the Palliative and End of Life Care Priority Setting Partnership refresh [9] aimed to: (1) Identify the unanswered questions about palliative and end of life care from patient, carer and health and social care professionals, and (2) Prioritise the identified questions for future research. Forming the Lived Experience Group

A Lived Experience Group was established to support and inform all stages of the project. Various channels were used for recruitment to the Lived Experience Group including the Marie Curie Research Voices Group, newsletters from UK-wide and nation-specific organisations and networks and blog posts [10].

Each prospective member discussed their experiences, motivations, and the level of involvement that they would like to have with Marie Curie’s Public Involvement and Engagement Manager (JB) prior to joining the group.

How were the Lived Experience Group involved in the project?

Members of the Lived Experience Group attended nine online meetings between May 2023 to February 2025, when the final report was launched [9]. Prior to each meeting, an agenda and accompanying documents were circulated. A summary and action points were circulated after meetings. Members were also encouraged to contribute via email if they were unable to attend the meetings.

Within the project, there were different subgroups that members could join, the Steering Group and the Data Subgroup. Six members of the group also joined the Steering Group, alongside health and social care professionals, representatives from charities and members of Marie Curie’s research team. Eight members joined the Data Subgroup which met online, six times for a total of twelve hours to discuss and refine the categorisations of data generated from the project’s initial survey. There were also different types of activities that people could opt into or out of. For example, independent desk-based activities, such as reviewing project documents, or more interactive activities such as piloting surveys or joining group discussions. This enabled people to participate in aspects of the project which they felt best aligned with their skills or interests.

Support, reward & recognition

Support for members of the group came via multiple avenues including regular check-in calls and emails with JB and access to free telephone support lines delivered by the lead organisation. A safeguarding plan was created which outlined a process should any safeguarding concerns be raised. This was created in partnership with the safeguarding lead for the host organisation.

All members of the group were offered renumeration for their involvement, as well as reimbursement of expenses such as carer costs to enable involvement. Reimbursement rates were in line with National Institute for Health and Care Research Guidelines [11]. Previous research has noted how remuneration can be seen as a display of acknowledgement for people’s time and input [12].

Aim

This paper reports reflections on the impact and experience of the Lived Experience Group within the 2025 refresh of the James Lind Alliance Palliative and End of Life Care Priority Setting Partnership.

The Public Involvement in Research Toolkit (PIRIT)

The PIRIT tracker [14] is a spreadsheet for recording public contributions in a research project, the difference they make, and how each contribution corresponds to the UK Standards for Public Involvement. After each Lived Experience Group meeting JB entered information into PIRIT relating to (anonymous) contributions of members, including direct quotes, the impact of each contribution (i.e., what was achieved or influenced) and which Standard [8] each contribution related to.

Interviews

At the end of the project, all Lived Experience Group Members and Steering Group members were invited to take part in an online interview with a researcher (JC), who was not known to them. The interviews encouraged reflection on their involvement, feedback on the process, experience, and impact of the Lived Experience Group and any recommendations for future research (appendix 1).

Online feedback survey

Concurrent to the semi-structured interviews, an online survey was circulated, in case any members preferred to share their experiences and reflections via a survey.

The survey explored views on communication and support from the project team, experiences of involvement and views about the impact of the Lived Experience Group. Informed consent was gained at the beginning of the survey. The survey was developed in MS Forms and took approximately 10-min to complete. The questions from the survey are available in appendix 1.

Data analysis

Interviews were recorded, written out word-for-word, made anonymous and then uploaded into a software program called NVivo for analysis. The written interviews were reviewed and coded according to the UK standards. All the information from PIRIT, interviews, and surveys was organized into tables for comparison. Analysis of data from all sources followed a Framework Analysis. Quotes from surveys, PIRIT entries, and interviews were matched with the standards when appropriate. If something didn’t fit into the existing standards, new labels were created. In addition, the types of impact recorded in the PIRIT tracker, were grouped and summarised to give an overview of the impact of the Lived Experience Group on the project.

Governance

UK standard: Involve the public in research management, regulation, leadership and decision making

At the outset of the project, terms of reference and ways of working documents were co-created with both the Lived Experience Group and the Steering Group. Lived Experience Group members were kept up to date with the project’s progress and milestones via regular online meetings and they were consulted on key decisions throughout the project.

The ordering, timing and purpose of each groups’ meetings were carefully considered. Based on feedback from the group, the ordering of the meetings varied, depending on agenda for the meeting, to enable the views of both groups to be heard and considered by the other.

“We need to do a bit of both, don't we? Because if we meet before then we are putting information in. But what comes out as a result of that discussion, we may need to talk about” (PIRIT).

Six Lived Experience Group members joined the project Steering Group, and eight joined the Data Subgroup. Through these channels, members had direct routes to influence decisions made throughout the project.

Conclusions

Perhaps the most significant impact of the Lived Experience Group was the adaptations made to the language used in promotional information and data collection tools. Discussions within Lived Experience Group meetings highlighted that palliative care, and many terms associated with are often not well understood by non-practitioners. Input from the group enabled clear explanations of who was eligible to take part in the surveys to be formulated, and made the language used throughout more accessible to a lay audience.

The final section of this paper contains key reflections on involvement that other groups embarking on a Priority Setting Partnership may wish to consider, with regards to involvement.

Consider the resource available to support involvement

The project team drew upon an existing full-time role within the lead organisation, which focused on enabling engagement (JB). Thus, existing resource and expertise could be utilised to establish and maintain the Lived Experience Group. In addition to recruiting group members, facilitating meetings and completing impact logs, JB provided support to group members as required. The time needed for this can be hard to predict but should not be underestimated [15, 16].

Define roles clearly

Establishing clearly defined roles and a clear purpose is key to successful involvement [17, 18]. In this project we collectively reviewed the terms and reference and ways of working for both the Lived Experience Group and the project as a whole. Defining these roles and expectations early was well received.

Recognise the importance of consistent relationships

Consistent involvement of the Lived Experience Group was beneficial as it enabled relationships to develop and reciprocal learning to occur. The importance of developing strong rapport between project team members overtime has been identified as a key attribute of successful involvement in palliative care research [15, 16]. Several members of the group have chosen to continue wider involvement with the host organisation which suggests that involvement was percieved as a good use of their time.

The current project spanned almost two years. The duration of a project is another factor to consider. People may choose to stop their involvement or become less engaged for a variety of reasons including personal circumstances, competing demands, deteriorating health or the sense that a project is no longer relevant or practical for them. Maintaining regular communication helped to maintain engagement of the group.

Support involvement from a diverse group

Lived Experience Group members were able to draw upon existing connections to share the surveys from the project with community groups and organisations and made amendments to the language used to make the project more accessible to a wider audience.

JB held initial calls with each member to explore whether there were any aspects of the project in which members may require additional support. We explored whether adaptations could be made to the project to accommodate different needs and circumstances of group members. Specific training relating to involvement has been recommended in previous guidance, [17, 19] but was not provided in this project. Instead, the current stage of the project and associated methods were discussed and reflected upon in Lived Experience Group meetings. A framework for Equity, Equality, Diversity and Inclusion (EEDI) in PPI for palliative care research has been developed which can inform future work [16].

Incorporate flexibility

Flexibility has been highlighted as important in involvement in palliative care research [15, 20]. Flexibility was incorporated into this project via opportunities for people to be involved in different aspects of the project. We were also able to change the way that we undertook the data analysis, to facilitate involvement from the Lived Experience Group.

Recognise and celebrate the impact of involvement

How to recognise and promote the contribution of public involvement is another important reflection to carry forward into future work [18, 21]. In this project, all Lived Experience Group members were invited to be co-authors on the final report and other publications based on the project. One member wrote the foreword to the final report, and another presented at the launch event. We also endeavoured to celebrate and note the impact of the group throughout the project by highlighting changes made and together reviewing impacts collected through the PIRIT.

Strengths and limitations

This paper has drawn upon different sources of data to reflect on the impact of the first Lived Experience Group within a Priority Setting Partnership. The Lived Experience Group itself was made up of twenty people affected by serious life limiting illness, who largely remained engaged over a project spanning almost two years. We acknowledge that certain groups or types of experience were under-represented within the group. In addition, only a small number of members completed the survey reported in this paper, however we have also drawn on the PIRIT tracker which was maintained throughout the course of the project.

In conclusion, the Lived Experience Group made sustained and impactful changes to many elements of the project. We are grateful and inspired by the passion, commitment, and generosity of the group in sharing their time and experiences to shape the project.
