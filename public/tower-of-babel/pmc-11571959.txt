Aberrant Mitochondrial tRNA Genes Appear Frequently in Animal Evolution
Authors: Iuliia Ozerova, Jörg Fallmann, Mario Mörl, Matthias Bernt, Sonja J Prohaska, Peter F Stadler, Selene Fernandez-Valverde
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial tRNAs have acquired a diverse portfolio of aberrant structures throughout metazoan evolution. With the availability of more than 12,500 mitogenome sequences, it is essential to compile a comprehensive overview of the pattern changes with regard to mitochondrial tRNA repertoire and structural variations. This, of course, requires reanalysis of the sequence data of more than 250,000 mitochondrial tRNAs with a uniform workflow. Here, we report our results on the complete reannotation of all mitogenomes available in the RefSeq database by September 2022 using mitos2. Based on the individual cases of mitochondrial tRNA variants reported throughout the literature, our data pinpoint the respective hotspots of change, i.e. Acanthocephala (Lophotrochozoa), Nematoda, Acariformes, and Araneae (Arthropoda). Less dramatic deviations of mitochondrial tRNAs from the norm are observed throughout many other clades. Loss of arms in animal mitochondrial tRNA clearly is a phenomenon that occurred independently many times, not limited to a small number of specific clades. The summary data here provide a starting point for systematic investigations into the detailed evolutionary processes of structural reduction and loss of mitochondrial tRNAs as well as a resource for further improvements of annotation workflows for mitochondrial tRNA annotation.Keywords: tRNA secondary structure, mitochondrial tRNAs, truncated tRNAs, armless tRNAs, tRNA loss, tRNA annotation

Significance

Several metazoan lineages exhibit mitochondrial tRNAs with truncated structures. A comprehensive computational reevaluation of the currently available data shows that such structural variations have appeared independently in many different clades, but also confirms well-known hotspots of structural aberrations and loss of mitogenomic tRNAs.

Introduction

Most metazoan mitogenomes encode 22 tRNA genes that typically conform to a cloverleaf shaped secondary structure, see e.g. Bernt et al. (2013) and Salinas-Giegé et al. (2015) for overviews. While nuclear tRNAs are extremely well conserved in both sequence and structure, there is extensive variation on the tRNA complement as well as variability of sequence and structure in the mitochondrial counterparts in metazoa (Kuhle et al. 2020). Missing mitochondrial tRNAs (mt-tRNAs) are compensated by importing the corresponding cytosolic tRNAs (Schneider 2011; Salinas-Giegé et al. 2015). In some cases, such as mt-tRNA-Lys in marsupials, only a single mt-tRNA is affected (Dörner et al. 2001), while the overwhelming majority of—and in some cases such as Nematostella vectensis all—mt-tRNAs have been lost from cnidarian and sponge mitogenomes (Wang and Lavrov 2008; Haen et al. 2010). An absence of mt-tRNAs has been observed also in Ctenophora (Arafat et al. 2018). In some cases, the loss of an mt-tRNA gene is compensated by duplication and remolding of another mt-tRNA (Cantatore et al. 1987; Higgs et al. 2003; Rawlings et al. 2003). Although particularly prevalent for the two mt-tRNA-Leu paralogs, there is evidence for a much broader array of remolding events (Sahyoun et al. 2015; Jiang et al. 2016; Romanova et al. 2020).

Besides tRNA import and remolding, editing reactions restore missing tRNAs in mitochondria, completing the mt-tRNA set (Schürer et al. 2001; Schuster and Mörl 2004). In marsupials, such an editing event changes the identity of an mt-tRNA, compensating for the loss of the corresponding gene (Börner et al. 1996). Other editing events rebuild the base-paired acceptor stem in mt-tRNA transcripts that result from overlapping or truncated mt-tRNA genes (Yokobori and Pääbo 1995b; Tomita et al. 1996; Reichert et al. 1998; Lavrov et al. 2000b; Schürer et al. 2001). Hence, several strategies exist that compensate for the apparent lack of certain mt-tRNAs in metazoan mitochondrial genomes.

Although most metazoan mt-tRNAs fold into a canonical cloverleaf structure, different shapes can also be found in phylogenetically diverse clades (Krahn et al. 2020). The evolutionary fragility that resulted in such structures appears to have arisen in Bilateria evolution (Kuhle et al. 2020). The most common aberration is the loss of the otherwise highly conserved D-loop, which affects almost all mt-tRNA-Ser(AGY) genes (Arcari and Brownlee 1980; Jühling et al. 2009; Watanabe et al. 2014), mt-tRNA-Ser(UGA) in Chromadorea (Okimoto et al. 1992), and mt-tRNA-Cys in some vertebrates (Seutin et al. 1994). Deletions of the T-arm were first identified in nematodes (Wolstenholme et al. 1987), and later also found in Arthropoda (Masta 2000). Even smaller “armless” tRNAs lacking both D- and T-arms appeared in at least two independent evolutionary events, once in mermithids such as Romanomermis (Jühling et al. 2012b; Wende et al. 2014; Jühling et al. 2018) and once in Dysderidae, a group of spiders (Pons et al. 2019). Despite the interest in aberrant mt-tRNAs and the rapid accumulation of mitogenomic sequences, a comprehensive search for and assessment of structural variations has not been done yet. In this contribution, we report on the reannotation and analysis of the mt-tRNAs in animals.

Results

Global Distribution of Missing and Noncloverleaf mt-tRNAs

The reannotation of the 13,387 metazoan mitogenomes available in RefSeq by September 2022 with mitos2 resulted in an initial annotation of 287,415 mt-tRNA genes. About 95% (274,975) of these exhibit the canonical cloverleaf shape. But note that around half of the mitogenomes (6,288 genomes reported for 6,073 of 13,030 distinct species) harbor at least one mt-tRNA with a structure that is predicted to be aberrant. The lengths of the predicted genes are centered around 70 bases, as expected for a canonical mt-tRNA, see Fig. 1. However, the distribution is unexpectedly broad. On the one hand, mt-tRNAs are not known to harbor a variable loop and are not expected to be longer than 90 bases. On the other hand, one of the shortest “armless” tRNAs in Romanomermis is only 45 nt long (Jühling et al. 2009). Therefore, extremely long and or short outliers are most likely inaccuracies in the annotation process. We observe, furthermore, that short tRNAs preferentially appear in shorter mitogenomes, see supplementary figure S1, Supplementary Material online.

Length distribution of mt-tRNA genes predicted by mitos2 for the annotated mitogenomes. Overall, the overwhelming majority has a length around 70, corresponding to the canonical cloverleaf structure (shown in blue). Aberrant mt-tRNAs (orange) are typically shorter. Outliers are most likely incorrect predictions.

Only 308 mitogenomes contain predicted mt-tRNAs longer than 80 bases, and only 458 tRNAs have a length of more than 80 bases. We expect that these few extreme outliers are incorrect predictions since many of these are also predicted to have aberrant structures.

Figure 2 summarizes the global result of this survey and highlights many of the previously described major changes in mt-tRNA complement. For instance, the wide-spread loss of mt-tRNAs in Cnidaria, Ctenophora (Haen et al. 2010), and some of Porifera (Wang and Lavrov 2008; Lavrov et al. 2024) is clearly visible. The well-known examples of aberrant mt-tRNA structures in nematodes (Wolstenholme et al. 1987; Jühling et al. 2018) and arachnids (Masta 2000; Pons et al. 2019) are also well reflected in the data. We also observe both losses and abundant structural reduction in Acanthocephala, in line with a recent detailed study of this clade (Gao et al. 2023) which also reported that the protein-coding genes in the mitogenomes of Acanthocephala were difficult to annotate. Our data also confirm that aberrant structures are by far most common for mt-tRNA-Ser, affecting 4,339 of the mitogenomes (4,173 individual species).

We observe, furthermore, that both mt-tRNA losses and aberrant mt-tRNA structures appear in clusters conforming to taxonomic groups. This suggests that structurally deviating mt-tRNAs persist over long evolutionary times. Due to the fast rate of neutral evolution in mitogenomes (Hoser et al. 2020) and the strong selective pressure on their size, one can expect pseudogenes to be quickly cleared from the mitogenomes. This effect has been documented e.g. for tRNA genes in the wake of genome rearrangements caused by tandem-duplication random loss events (Boore 2000; Al Arab et al. 2017). Thus, aberrant mt-tRNAs that are observed over large evolutionary time-scales almost certainly have retained their function.

In the following sections, we consider clades with substantial changes in their mt-tRNAs in more detail. To this end, we enhance the results of mitos2 annotation to obtain a more stringent classification of the structure. In the graphical summaries, we distinguish canonical cloverleaf-shapes (blue), the loss of one arm (orange), and the loss of both arms (red). In species with variant genetic codes, an mt-tRNA-X is annotated for anticodons corresponding to stop codons in the standard genetic code. White entries report an apparent loss of mt-tRNA. Black entries denote two tRNAs with different shapes for the same isoacceptor class in the same species.

Mt-tRNA Loss in Cnidaria, Ctenophora, and Acanthocephala

Figure 3 shows the well-known reduction of the mt-tRNA complement in Cnidaria. With the exception of Octocorallia, which lost mt-tRNA-Trp, the mitogenomes of Cnidaria harbor canonically shaped mt-tRNA-Met and mt-tRNA-Trp (Feng et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2023), a pattern that is broken only by the mitogenomes from the genus Kudoa (class of Myxozoa) as reported by Takeuchi et al. (2015). In addition, we observe clade-specific presence of some structurally reduced mt-tRNAs, such as mt-tRNA-Val in the family of Merulinidae (order of Scleractinia) or mt-tRNA-Ala and mt-tRNA-Phe in some genera dispersed across Cnidaria.

Unexpectedly, cloverleaf mt-tRNA-Tyr appears in most species of the genus Sinularia (order of Malacalcyonacea). A closer inspection shows that this is an artifact as it has a very low score and is located in the ORF of the mtMutS gene, which exceptionally has been gained in octocorals (Muthye and Lavrov 2021). Low-scoring mt-tRNA hits are accepted by mitos2 in this location because the MutS insertion is not a part of its annotation model, i.e. the location inside a bona fide coding region is not recognized and the mt-tRNA is not excluded.

Concerning Ctenophora, our data show that mt-tRNAs are completely absent, consistent with previous reports of loss of both mitogenomic proteins and mt-tRNAs during gene rearrangements events (Arafat et al. 2018).

While the reduction of the mitogenomic mt-tRNAs is complete in Ctenophora and has progressed very far in Cnidaria, it appears to be in progress in Acanthocephala (Lophotrochozoa). A detailed graphical representation can be found in supplementary figure S2, Supplementary Material online, an overview is included in Fig. 4. All annotated mitogenomes of Acanthocephala lost mt-tRNA-Asn. While Oligacanthorhynchidae from Archiacanthocephala have retained a (nearly) complete set of mt-tRNAs, there are several consistent losses in Eoacanthocephala (mt-tRNA-Asp, -His, -Lys, -Thr, -Val, and -Tyr), and Pomphorhynchus from Echinorhynchida (mt-tRNA-Cys, -Asp, -Ile, -Gln, -Arg, and -Tyr). Moreover, in Palaeacanthocephala, most of the retained genes have lost one arm. The notable exception is mt-tRNA-Trp, similar to Cnidaria, it is present throughout the Acanthocephala and has retained the cloverleaf shape.

Moderate Changes in Lophotrochozoa

With the exception of the Acanthocephala, Lophotrochozoa have largely preserved the typical structure of animal mt-tRNAs, see Fig. 4. The simultaneous presence of mt-tRNA-Ser1 and mt-tRNA-X suggests that the anticodon of the mt-tRNA-Ser1 may have been changed into one of the stop codons (i.e. “UAA”, “UAG”, “UGA”) (Abascal et al. 2006, 2012). In this case, the tRNA, more similar in structure to other members of the mt-tRNA-Ser1 family, would be designated as mt-tRNA-X by mitos2 (innermost track in the figures). As expected, mt-tRNA-Ser1 or mt-tRNA-X lacks one arm in most cases, for example, in most Annelida and Bivalvia except Mytilidae. Notable exceptions, with cloverleaf-shaped mt-tRNA-Ser1 are Caenogastropoda and some other clades within Gastropoda and almost all cephalopods.

In addition to the frequent loss of an arm in mt-tRNA-Ser1, we observe that mt-tRNA-Ser2 has a similar reduced structure. Documented examples include annelid families, such as Nereididae (Perinereis aibuhitensis (Kim et al. 2015) and Nereis species (Kim et al. 2017)) in Polychaeta clade, as well as some platyhelminthes families in Monogenea (Diplozoidae (Zhang et al. 2018)) and Trematoda (Digenea) (Echinostoma miyagawai (Li et al. 2019), Fasciola hepatica L. (Le et al. 2001), Paragonimus ohirai (Le et al. 2019), Metorchis orientalis (Na et al. 2016), and Opisthorchis felineus (Mordvinov et al. 2009)). Also, in mollusks, mt-tRNA-Ser2 is commonly lacking the D-arm.

A notable pattern is the loss of an arm in mt-tRNA-Arg and mt-tRNA-Cys. It appears that this has occurred in several independent evolutionary events. The loss of mt-tRNA-Arg has been reported e.g. for the order Polychaeta (Annelida), in Phyllodocida (Kim et al. 2015, 2017), as well as several lophotrochozoan orders of the class Cestoda (subclass Eucestoda), such as Phyllobothriidea, Onchoproteocephalidea II, Tetraphyllidea and Trypanorhyncha (Trevisan et al. 2021), and Caryophyllidea (Nakao 2002; Li et al. 2017).

Cases of reduced mt-tRNA-Cys structures have previously been reported in classes of Monogenea (Gyrodactylidea (Bachmann et al. 2016), Paradiplozoon yarkandense and Paradiplozoon homoion (Hao et al. 2022)), Digenea (Echinostoma miyagawai and Paragonimus ohirai (Li et al. 2019), Fasciola hepatica L. (Le et al. 2001), Metorchis orientalis (Na et al. 2016), Opisthorchis felineus, (Mordvinov et al. 2009), and Schistosoma mansoni (Blair et al. 1999)), and several families of Eucestoda (Nakao 2002; Jia et al. 2010; Li et al. 2017; Trevisan et al. 2021).

Loss of an arm has been described for mt-tRNA-Thr in Schistosoma mansoni (Blair et al. 1999), and can also be observed in some species of Mytilidae (Mollusca: Bivalvia). In addition, we observe a prevalence of mt-tRNA-His genes that lack one arm in Digenea and Eucestoda, and a tendency toward reduced tRNA shapes in Eupulmonata (Mollusca: Gastropoda), in particular among the land snails (Stylommatophora).

Drastic Changes in Ecdysozoa

The available data for Ecdysozoa are mainly concentrated on Nematoda and Arthropoda. In general, we observe that mt-tRNA-Ser1 is frequently reported as missing while, at the same time, an mt-tRNA-X is annotated. As in Lophotrochozoa, we assume that this corresponds to a change in anticodon associated with a variant genetic code.

Nematoda are well-known for their aberrant mt-tRNAs (Sakurai et al. 2006). In fact, truncated mt-tRNAs are the rule rather than the exception, see Fig. 5. The most conservative picture is given by Trichinellida which have retained cloverleaf structures for seven mt-tRNAs, i.e. mt-tRNA-Ile, -Lys, -Leu1, -Leu2, -Met, -Arg, and -Trp. On the other hand, Mermithida show extensive losses and indications for tRNAs missing one arm. In contrast to the mitos2 data, Jühling et al. (2012b) report extremely reduced mt-tRNAs that have lost both the T- and D-arms. These predictions were based on a detailed analysis of sequence alignments and later shown to be indeed expressed (Wende et al. 2014) and functional as tRNAs (Jühling et al. 2018). It appears that mitos2 lacks sensitivity for such extreme cases and, at the same time, tends to extend predictions into the surroundings. We shall return to this issue in the ‘Classification of structurally reduced mt-tRNAs’ section.

Rhabditina and Ascaridomorpha, from the order Rhabditida, show a very consistent pattern, suggesting that mt-tRNA-Met and mt-tRNA-Lys have retained a full cloverleaf shape, while most other mt-tRNAs have lost one arm. In contrast, Tylenchoidea (order Rhabditida) shows a conspicuous pattern of about eight consistent losses, which also includes mt-tRNA-Lys.

Limulus polyphemus is a species from the earliest branching group of Arthropoda, the order of Xiphosura. According to Lavrov et al. (2000a), the mitochondrial genome encodes all 22 canonical mt-tRNAs. The proposed secondary structures suggest that all mt-tRNAs have a cloverleaf structure, with the possible exception of mt-tRNA-Ser1, which may not form a proper D-arm. However, arthropod diversity is also reflected in the mt-tRNA complement.

Aberrant mt-tRNAs in Arthropoda, summarized in supplementary figures S3 and S4, Supplementary Material online, are concentrated in the Arachnida (Fig. 6). With the exception of the Parasitiformes (ticks) and other less represented orders (for example, Ricinulei and Opiliones), Arachnida show a very complex pattern of both mt-tRNA gene losses and structural reductions. The details of these changes are clearly clade-specific. The largest number of structurally aberrant mt-tRNAs are annotated in Acariformes (mites) and Araneae (spiders) (Fig. 6).

In Sarcoptiformes (order within Acariformes), almost all mt-tRNAs are either annotated as structurally aberrant or were not identified by mitos2 at all. Although the annotation is not completely consistent, most mt-tRNAs are aberrant, with the exception of mt-tRNA-Lys. Trombidiformes (an order within Acariformes) have retained some mt-tRNAs with cloverleaf shape, in particular mt-tRNA-Lys and mt-tRNA-Leu2. For mt-tRNA-Met and mt-tRNA-Asn, aberrant shapes are the exception. Nevertheless, the annotation of mt-tRNAs in this group remains a challenge: mt-tRNA-Val and -Arg are almost completely absent in Trombidiformes. In Tetranychus even more mt-tRNAs ‘ for example, mt-tRNA-Ile, -Ala, -Asp, -Cys, -Pro, -Ser2, and -Thr). For Tetranychus urticae, it was shown in Warren and Sloan (2021) that mt-tRNA are dramatically reduced, but still exist and can be functional.

Large variations in the annotation are observed, in particular, in the order of Araneae (Domes et al. 2008; Pons et al. 2019; Zhan et al. 2021). Almost all mt-tRNA families are predicted to have both cloverleaf-shaped and aberrant members. The cloverleaf shape is more prevalent but not ubiquitous for mt-tRNA-Met, -Gln, and -Leu2.

Other subgroups of the Arachnida show mostly cloverleaf shapes, except for mt-tRNA-Ser1. Within Ixodoidea, mt-tRNA-Cys lacks an arm with the exception of Ixodes, and mt-tRNA-Phe is also predicted with aberrant shape in many mitogenomes of Parasitiformes. Aberrant structures are also annotated for several mt-tRNAs in Scorpiones. For several other clades, mitogenomes are available only for a small number of species. This makes it difficult to infer general patterns. The Pseudoscorpiones show large structural changes, consistent with the observation that almost all mt-tRNAs have reduced structures in Pseudogarypus banksi and Paratemnoides elongatus (Ovchinnikov and Masta 2012).

In comparison to Arachnida, the changes to the canonical mt-tRNA complement are moderate in other Arthropoda. Generally, we observe a full set of cloverleaf-shaped mt-tRNAs with the exception of mt-tRNA-Ser1. The latter is usually one-armed and is frequently subjected to codon mutation as suggested by the simultaneous presence of mt-tRNA-Ser1 and mt-tRNA-X (see ‘Moderate changes in Lophotrochozoa’). While this is the case for Myriapoda and Pancrustacea (including Insecta), mt-tRNA-X is either one-armed or cloverleaf-shaped in Pleocyemata, a clade of the order Decapoda, and in Coleoptera and some Hymenoptera, both insect orders. Otherwise, only a few clades can be found that deviate from the general pattern in arthropods. We could find some clades in Myriapoda, Oligostraca, and Copepoda that show a notable amount of losses and reductions of mt-tRNAs (supplementary figure S3, Supplementary Material online).

The Peracarida (including Amphipoda and Isopoda) also harbor some aberrant mt-tRNAs. In Amphipoda, most often mt-tRNA-Gln, -Arg, -Ser1, -Ser2, and -Val are aberrant. Isopoda, however, show additional truncated mt-tRNAs, i.e. mt-tRNA-Ala and mt-tRNA-Cys, and the absence of mt-tRNA-Ile in the mitos2 annotation.

Concerning changes in Insecta, there is a tendency of some Hemiptera for arm-loss in mt-tRNA-Cys, mt-tRNA-Glu, mt-tRNA-His, mt-tRNA-Thr, and mt-tRNA-Val. Hymenoptera also seems to be amenable to occasional arm-losses in mt-tRNAs, most prevalently in mt-tRNA-Cys and mt-tRNA-Glu. The pattern of the mt-tRNA shapes in Psocodea is also not regular.

Conserved Cloverleaf-shaped mt-tRNAs in Chordata

Aberrant tRNAs are rare among vertebrates. Mammalia (supplementary figure S5, Supplementary Material online) and many Sauropsida (supplementary figure S6, Supplementary Material online) share the truncated mt-tRNA-Ser1 (Helm et al. 2000), while most Aves, however, exhibit a cloverleaf shape for this mt-tRNA. We also observe the loss of mt-tRNA-Asp and mt-tRNA-Lys in most Metatheria. Interestingly, in the Tasmanian tiger Thylacinus cynocephalus, a full complement of mt-tRNAs is present (Miller et al. 2009), but we observe the loss of mt-tRNA-Asp and mt-tRNA-Lys as in whole Metatheria. For Cingulata, we see the reduced mt-tRNA-Cys previously reported for Euphractus sexcinctus (Paviotti-Fischer et al. 2016).

Truncated mt-tRNA-Cys is also observed in several subgroups of the Lepidosauria. Some propensity for truncated mt-tRNAs and maybe also mt-tRNA loss was found in Siluriformes. Interestingly, other Actinopteri tend to have a full complement of cloverleaf mt-tRNAs (supplementary figure S7, Supplementary Material online).

The Ascidiacea (see supplementary figure S8, Supplementary Material online) clade shows the most diverse pattern of mt-tRNA arm losses among the Chordata. It remains unclear whether this is correlated with the use of a nonstandard genetic code (Pichon et al. 2019) and/or the appearance of unusual chemical modifications (Kondow et al. 1998). Moreover, there is a loss of mt-tRNA-Cys in Phlebobranchia and possibly some Aplousobranchia.

Echinodermata (see supplementary figure S9, Supplementary Material online) and Enteropneusta (supplementary figure S10, Supplementary Material online) show a clade-specific pattern of arm-loss in mt-tRNA-Ser1, also observed in the Chordata. In addition, we observe a few scattered arm-loss events that do not seem to follow a particular pattern.

Duplicated mt-tRNAs

While in general the mt-tRNA labeled X in the figures is the missing mt-tRNA-Ser1 with a different anticodon, there are some clades in which X is present in addition to the usual 22 mt-tRNA genes. These can be identified as duplicated mt-tRNAs, often with divergent anticodons. Examples are particularly frequent in tunicates. The genus Clavelina features paralogs of mt-tRNA-Met and sometimes additional genes, as reported previously by Stach et al. (2010). Duplicated mt-tRNAs are also present in Ciona. For instance, two paralogs of mt-tRNA-Gly and mt-tRNA-Met were reported for Ciona (Yokobori et al. 2003) and Herdmania (Singh et al. 2009). The mitos2 annotation lists a duplicated mt-tRNA-Thr and a missing mt-tRNA-Arg in Herdmania. We speculate that tRNA-remolding takes place in this case. Similar patterns are observed in all major subclades of the tunicates.

In some cases, such as mt-tRNA-Leu2 in Microcebus (order Primates), we find both a cloverleaf-shaped and an aberrant mt-tRNA to coexist in some species, while other members of the same genus have either one or the other. This may be an interesting example shedding light on the emergence of aberrant tRNA shapes from a duplication with subsequent structural changes in one of the two copies. In Microcebus, the two structurally different copies of mt-tRNA-Leu are also syntenically conserved.

Classification of Structurally Reduced mt-tRNAs

A more detailed comparison of the mitos2 annotations with the mt-tRNA genes predicted (e.g. in spiders (Pons et al. 2019; Li et al. 2021, 2022; Liu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023), crustaceans (Kilpert et al. 2012; Pons et al. 2014), mites and ticks (Shao et al. 2005; Jeyaprakash and Hoy 2007; Domes et al. 2008; Xue et al. 2016; Schäffer et al. 2018; Warren and Sloan 2021), and in some Lophotrochozoa (Gao et al. 2023)) and in part experimentally validated (e.g. in Armadillidium vulgare [Arthropoda] (Doublet et al. 2015), Trichinella species (Arita et al. 2006), or Romanomermis culicivorax) (Wende et al. 2014; Jühling et al. 2018) suggests that mitos2 misses some of the extremely reduced, armless mt-tRNAs and on occasion tends to predict more regular mt-tRNA structures in such cases. It is worth mentioning that most of the structures reported in the literature are based on comparative sequence analysis only. Thus, uncertainty remains in particular as far as the exact gene boundaries are concerned, which in turn could substantially affect the structure predictions. In order to assess such discrepancies in more detail, we tested whether more accurate annotation of aberrant mt-tRNA genes can be obtained by employing covariance models (CM) providing template structures that lack one or both arms.

Semiglobal alignments of the CMs for the four different possible shapes were evaluated by bit-score as described in detail in the ‘Materials and Methods’ section. Figure 7 summarizes the results for the case of Romanomermis, where the most complete experimental data are available. We observe that the custom CMs in general show an increased sensitivity compared to mitos2. However, manual checks and conflict resolution are still required for unusual cases. mt-tRNA shapes obtained with custom truncated CMs complement the results predicted by mitos2 (mostly with T-arm loss) with armless structures, while shapes with D-arm loss are still not detected. Somewhat disappointingly, the custom CMs are not sufficiently sensitive to recognize all mt-tRNAs. In contrast, CMs constructed from hand-curated alignments of Mermithidae mt-tRNAs from Jühling et al. (2012b) retrieve an almost complete set of candidate mt-tRNAs. This is not surprising, of course, since these CMs are constructed from closely related sequences.

Romanomermis genus: mt-tRNA shapes obtained from different sources. mt-tRNA shapes were collected from mitos2 pipeline, shortened CM set (custom CMs) with further locus-specific filtration and CM set, constructed with published Enoplea alignment (Jühling et al. 2012b). All candidates were manually checked, and the final distinction between D- and T-armless mt-tRNA is presented.

Unfortunately, these data suggest that mitos2 does not provide a complete annotation of highly aberrant structures. In conjunction with annotations of closely related groups, mosaic patterns of apparent losses and aberrant structures can be taken as an indication of the possible presence of highly aberrant mt-tRNAs. A very detailed, alignment-based analysis of sufficiently narrow clades would be required to gain more conclusive evidence.

Discussion

The purpose of this contribution is to provide a comprehensive overview of the evolution of structural variations in metazoan mt-tRNAs. Encompassing over 12,500 mitogenomes and more than 250,000 individual mt-tRNAs, it is not feasible to review the pertinent primary publications. Instead, we have resorted entirely to a re-analysis of the available mitogenomic sequences. In order to ensure comparability of the data, the entire dataset was reannotated with mitos2. A birds-eye view of the data, Fig. 2 clearly identified the main hotspots of aberrant mt-tRNAs in animal phylogeny, in particular: the systematic loss of mt-tRNAs in Cnidaria and Ctenophora, the systematic reduction of mt-tRNAs in Acanthocephala, Nematoda, and several clades within Arachnida. Complementarily, we observed the frequent reduction of mt-tRNA-Ser1, as well as a propensity for structural changes in several other mt-tRNAs that occur independently in multiple unrelated clades.

An interesting quantitative observation is the moderate negative correlation (r=−0,34, P-value<2.2e−16, Spearman’s rank correlation) between size of the mitogenome and the abundance of truncated mt-tRNAs. This supports the hypothesis that strong selection to minimize genome size is the cause of tRNA truncation (Masta and Boore 2004).

While mitos2 produces near-perfect annotations of canonical mt-tRNAs and in general identifies mt-tRNAs that have lost either the T-arm or the D-arm, it seems to struggle with the annotation of highly aberrant structures, in particular armless mt-tRNAs. In these cases, we encounter either false negatives, i.e. aberrant mt-tRNAs that are not detected at all or an overprediction of structure that typically includes one false positive arm. We investigated whether the detection and classification performance could be substantially improved by extending the MitFi mt-tRNA models with additional CM obtained by artificially removing arms. We found, however, that in species with armless mt-tRNAs such as Romanomermis the sequences of the mt-tRNA genes have diverged from canonical sequences and simple arm-truncated CMs do not improve detection sensitivity to the fullest extent. The most accurate prediction was achieved with clade-specific models constructed from hand-curated alignments. While such an enterprise is beyond the scope of this contribution, our data give clear indications of which clades are interesting candidates for an in-depth analysis.

Primary candidates for highly aberrant mt-tRNAs are exactly those for which a significant fraction of the mitos2 predictions is reported to be aberrant, the set of mitos2 predictions is incomplete, and the predicted losses are inconsistent between nearby clades. Interestingly, there are rare cases that match these conditions but are not connected with truncated mt-tRNAs, i.e. in marsupials, where instead of aberrantly structured mt-tRNAs, the missing mt-tRNAs are replaced by the import of nuclear tRNA-Lys (Dörner et al. 2001), and the editing of mt-tRNA-Gly in the anticodon to obtain mt-tRNA-Asp (Börner et al. 1996).

In addition to Mermithida (Nematoda), clades that are most promising to harbor mt-tRNAs with structural deviations such as loss of T- or D-arm are the Meloidogyne and Onchocercidae among the Nematoda, Sarcoptiformes, Trombidiformes, and Araneae among the Arachnida, the Acanthocephala and possibly also the Trematoda (Platyhelminthes). Snails (Heterobranchia), and among them Stylommatophora (air-breathing land snails and slugs) also exhibit some highly aberrant mt-tRNAs, although the majority of the genes seems to preserve cloverleaf-shaped mt-tRNAs.

Currently, structural deviations in aberrant mt-tRNAs are almost exclusively the result of mt-DNA sequencing followed by the computational, homology-based annotation of the putative mt-tRNA transcripts, from which secondary structures are predicted. While canonical, cloverleaf-shaped mt-tRNAs can be identified with very high accuracy, other predictions are sometimes erroneous and result in misleading annotations. The first completely armless mt-tRNAs and their corresponding genes were predicted in the nematode Romanomermis culicivorax (Jühling et al. 2012a). Although the computation annotations were rather accurate in this case, biochemical analysis of several encoded mt-tRNAs revealed that their 5’ and 3’-ends differ in some cases, resulting in shorter acceptor stems than predicted (Wende et al. 2014). In contrast to the conventional mt-tRNAs, the armless versions do not follow the rule of 7 bp in the acceptor stem but can have only 5 or 6 bp. However, a correct acceptor stem is essential for the functionality of an mt-tRNA. The resulting interdependence of transcript ends and secondary structure makes it at least very difficult to obtain accurate models without detailed tRNA-seq data.

The difficulty in predicting aberrant mt-tRNA genes and structures exclusively from DNA sequence data is exemplified by the mitogenome of the oribatid mite Steganacarus magnus. According to Domes et al. (2008), only six mt-tRNA genes were identified. Candidates for several of the remaining 16 genes (which were described as lost by Domes et al. (2008)) were proposed in the following years, resulting in several conflicting, highly controversial annotations in terms of anticodon/mt-tRNA identity, mt-tRNA size/structure, and genome position (Domes et al. 2008; Klimov and OConnor 2009; Edwards et al. 2011; Schäffer et al. 2018; Xue et al. 2018; Zhan et al. 2021).

The situation is further complicated in cases where the mt-tRNA gene is transcribed as a nonfunctional molecule that is converted into a functional mt-tRNA by editing events. A prime example is the mitogenome of marsupials such as Didelphis virginiana (Janke and Pääbo 1993; Mörl et al. 1995; Börner et al. 1996). They lack a gene encoding an mt-tRNA-Asp with the expected anticodon GUC (outer white segment in supplementary figure S5, Supplementary Material online) and instead harbor two different genes for mt-tRNA-Gly. Both genes are transcribed and processed into mature and functional glycyl-mt-tRNAs decoding all four glycine codons. About half of the mt-tRNA-Gly-GCC are edited by deamination of C35, resulting in the anticodon GUC, which acts as a replacement for the missing mt-tRNA-Asp. In general, such cases are impossible to distinguish from genuine losses on the basis of DNA data only. In some cases, however, they may explain the ambiguous structure annotations shown as black segments in the presence/absence diagrams.

A different class of editing events converts mt-tRNA transcripts with aberrant structures into typical mt-tRNAs. In the Japanese land snail Euhadra herklotsi, some mt-tRNA genes overlap with adjacent downstream located protein-coding genes such as coxI or other mt-tRNA genes (Yokobori and Pääbo 1995a). The 4 to 6 positions in the overlapping region belong to the 5’-part of the downstream tRNA or mRNA and do not form base pairs in the acceptor stem of the upstream tRNA. These are released as 3’-truncated transcripts that are subsequently completed by an unidentified polymerase activity. The most extreme insertional mt-tRNA editing is described in the velvet worm Oroperipatus sp., where 14 mt-tRNAs are completed by a 3’-terminal addition of up to 34 nucleotides, restoring not only the acceptor stem, but also the T-loop, the variable arm, and parts of the anticodon arm (Segovia et al. 2011). Whether this nucleotide addition occurs in a template-dependent or -independent way, or a combination of both is not clear. Some of the mt-tRNAs in the crustacean Armadillidium vulgare are fully integrated within mRNAs (Doublet et al. 2015); these transcripts are again restored by insertional editing at the 3’-end. Computationally, these cases appear as extreme truncations that may not be recognized as functional genes (Braband et al. 2010).

The genomic position of the missing mt-tRNA-Lys in marsupials (inner white segment in supplementary figure S5, Supplementary Material online) encodes for tRNA-like transcripts with aberrant structures in which the D-arm is replaced by a loop region and alternative sequences replace the UUU anticodon. The degenerated mt-tRNA-Lys structures are not edited into a functional tRNA but are probably required for the maturation of the huge mitochondrial primary transcript (Ojala et al. 1981). Instead, the nuclear mt-tRNA-Lys is imported (Dörner et al. 2001).

Taken together, while structure prediction of mt-tRNAs at the genome level gives fascinating insights into tRNA evolution and an unexpected structural variety, experimental evidence is required to verify these aberrant transcripts and to identify post-transcriptional corrections of anticodons, mismatches, or missing parts of a tRNA structure. However, one must keep in mind that even highly bizarre mt-tRNAs can represent functional molecules participating in mitochondrial protein synthesis. In such cases, one can expect a fascinating co-evolution and specific adaptations of the involved mitochondrial protein components, such as CCA-adding enzymes (Hennig et al. 2020), elongation factors (Sakurai et al. 2006; Ohtsuki and Watanabe 2007; Sato et al. 2017), or ribosomes (Watanabe et al. 2014).

The data reported here serve as a starting point for in-depth investigations into mt-tRNA evolution within narrow clades, highlighting the species and mt-tRNA classes with potentially interesting behavior. Our overview also provides the groundwork for the future improvement of annotation pipelines by indicating the clades where mitos2 likely suffers from false negatives, i.e. those where scattered (apparent) losses are reported. Clade-specific mt-tRNA models accommodating possible structural changes, in particular arm-losses, appear to be a promising approach.

Materials and Methods

We downloaded all metazoan mitogenomes available by September 2022 (a total of 13,387) from the RefSeq database (O’Leary et al. 2016). For species having more than one mitogenome sequence all of them were downloaded. The mitos2 pipeline (ver. 2.0.8) (Donath et al. 2019) was used to obtain a consistent reannotation of all sequences. In brief, mitos2 uses MITFI (Jühling et al. 2012a) to annotate mt-tRNAs, which uses specific CM for each of the 22 mt-tRNA families, as well as a conflict resolution step mitigating discrepancies between initial tRNA, protein, and rRNA annotations. mitos2 allows a 50 bp intersection between the mt-tRNAs by default. mitos2 annotation was based on the reference data for Metazoa derived from RefSeq63 (Bernt et al. 2020). The Vertebrate genetic code was used as a standard.

The tRNAs annotated by mitos2 were then classified into three groups according to their secondary structure. mt-tRNAs exhibiting three hairpin loops that conform, at least approximately, to the standard cloverleaf shape were classified as cloverleaf. tRNAs with two loops were classified as one-arm-truncated lacking the D- or T-loop. If there is only one loop in the annotation, the tRNA was considered as two-arms-truncated, lacking both D- and T-loops.

For the initial analysis, a number of cloverleaf and truncated mt-tRNAs were averaged over families to obtain an overview of the data. More fine-grained analysis was conducted for selected groups of organisms. The taxonomic trees were retrieved from the NCBI Taxonomy Browser in June 2024 and include unranked taxa (Schoch et al. 2020). iTOL was used for tree visualization (Letunic and Bork 2024); some taxonomic clades have been generalized to parental groups to simplify data presentation.

Aiming at a more refined classification of the truncated mt-tRNAs, the genus Romanomermis was investigated in more detail. To this end, structurally aberrant mt-tRNAs were grouped according to the loss or drastic reduction of the main structural elements. Since functional tRNAs require the anticodon stem-loop and the acceptor stem with a 3’-terminal CCA-end, one can distinguish four shapes: cloverleaf structures (), constituting the ancestral state, loss of the D-arm (⊢), loss of the T-arm (⊣), and armless tRNAs (), having lost both, the D- and the T-arms.

Distinguishing these cases using only the mitogenomic sequence is difficult as mitos2 uses only cloverleaf-shaped models (-models) for annotation. The classification of tRNA shapes presented here and the subsequent analysis is the result of an extensive manual curation process based on the initial tRNA annotation produced by mitos2.

mitos2 preference for the -models may lead to partially incorrect alignments that incorporate sequences outside of the true mt-tRNA gene. To counteract this effect, we constructed truncated ⊢, ⊣, and CM. These were obtained starting from the alignments underlying the mitos2 mt-tRNA models (Bernt et al. 2020) by deleting only the loops of the T- and/or D-arms as well as the entire T- and/or D-arms. These truncated models are intended to avoid scoring costly deletions or structurally inconsistent matches to -models, and thus yield a better total score. In addition, Enoplea-specific models were prepared using the alignments that were presented in the article by Jühling et al. (2012b). Based on these alignments, we constructed CMs with cmbuild and calibrated them with cmcalibrate (both parts of the Infernal software (Nawrocki and Eddy 2013)).

Mitogenomes were reannotated with these CM using cmsearch, also a part of the Infernal suite, using the global (-g) alignment mode. To increase sensitivity, all HMM filters were turned off using the --max option. Only matches with the correct anticodon sequence that left the anticodon unpaired were considered. The classification of mt-tRNA shapes was then curated based on the number of loops identified in the secondary structure annotation derived from either cmsearch for specific models or mitos2 in the initial survey. Similar to the rules established in MitFi, anticodon loops were identified as follows: in the case of short models, they were determined by their central position in the structure, and in the case of long models, they were furthermore expected to be embedded between at least 7 or 9 unpaired bases. We allow overlapping tRNAs but assume that overlapping anticodon loop positions are less likely. The annotated candidates were grouped according to the anticodon loop locus (coordinates, and strand). That allows the annotation of the mt-tRNAs on the same locus coordinates but on different strands in case this happens. Among competing annotations for the same locus, the match with the highest bit-score was selected as the final candidate (without threshold), duplicates in the prediction are allowed if candidates have comparably high bit-scores. Results obtained by automatic filtering were manually checked, and annotation results were updated if needed.
