Retinal microstructural disorganization in persistent macular edema secondary to branch retinal vein occlusion: an adaptive optics OCT study
Authors: Naomi Nishigori, Yuki Muraoka, Takahiro Kogo, Yuki Akiyama, Yuki Hama, Masaharu Ishikura, Shin Kadomoto, Tomoaki Murakami, Sotaro Ooto, Akitaka Tsujikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persistent macular edema (ME) secondary to branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) may show a limited anatomical response to anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy despite apparent control of vascular leakage. In this non-interventional observational study, we examined whether retinal microstructural alterations beneath foveal cystoid spaces are associated with anatomical characteristics of persistent macular edema following standard anti–vascular endothelial growth factor therapy. Thirty eyes of 30 patients with persistent BRVO-related ME (≥ 6 months after onset) underwent adaptive optics optical coherence tomography (AO-OCT). Retinal microstructural organization and ellipsoid zone (EZ) integrity beneath the foveal cystoid space were evaluated, and anatomical response was assessed by the reduction in central subfield thickness (CST) after the final anti-VEGF injection. Eyes with retinal microstructural disorganization showed significantly smaller CST reductions than those with preserved retinal alignment (P = 0.019). Microstructural disorganization was also associated with greater EZ disruption (P < 0.001) and a lower density of high-intensity dots in the outer nuclear layer (P = 0.009). In contrast, macular vessel density reduction assessed by OCT angiography was not significantly associated with CST reduction. These findings suggest that retinal microstructural integrity beneath foveal cystoid spaces is associated with the anatomical response to anti-VEGF therapy in persistent BRVO-related ME.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50806-6.

Introduction

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) is the second most common retinal vascular disorder after diabetic retinopathy and primarily affects middle-aged and older adults1–3. It is frequently complicated by macular edema (ME), a major cause of visual impairment4. The advent of anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy has significantly improved outcomes, particularly during the acute phase of BRVO5–7. Nevertheless, some patients experience persistent ME in the chronic phase, limiting full visual recovery8.

ME arises when the balance between vascular leakage and fluid clearance mechanisms is disrupted9. While fluorescein angiography (FA) and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) have provided valuable insights into the vascular components of ME10–12, the mechanisms underlying fluid removal from retinal tissue remain insufficiently characterized. In particular, the pathophysiology of ME that persists despite treatment is still poorly understood.

Müller glial cells span the entire retinal thickness and serve as essential mediators of homeostasis and retinal fluid regulation13,14 through various channels, including aquaporins and potassium ion channels15,16, yet their precise role in treatment-resistant ME associated with BRVO is not fully defined.

Recent advancements in imaging technologies have improved our ability to visualize the retinal microstructure in vivo. While conventional spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT) is widely used, its axial and lateral resolution may be insufficient for evaluating fine structural changes in Müller cells. Adaptive optics (AO) technology, originally developed for astronomy, has been adapted for ophthalmic use, allowing high-resolution retinal imaging. When combined with OCT, AO enhances both lateral and axial resolution and enables detailed visualization of individual cellular structures such as cone photoreceptors and Müller cells17–19.

In this study, we employed AO-OCT to investigate microstructural alterations in eyes with persistent ME secondary to BRVO. Specifically, we aimed to explore the association between retinal microstructural organization beneath foveal cystoid spaces and the anatomical response to anti-VEGF therapy. A clearer understanding of the relationship between structural retinal alterations and therapeutic response may help elucidate the mechanisms of persistent ME and support the development of more effective management strategies.

Methods

Patients

This non-interventional observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine (approval number: 1272). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We included patients with persistent ME secondary to BRVO (> 6 months after onset) who underwent AO-OCT imaging at Kyoto University Hospital between August 2021 and November 2023. All participants were treated according to standard clinical practice, and no treatment decisions or patient management strategies were influenced by the study protocol.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eyes with persistent ME, defined as a central subfield thickness (CST) ≥ 250 μm and the presence of foveal cystoid spaces measuring ≥ 250 μm in horizontal width, were enrolled, in accordance with previous studies20,21. CST was defined as the mean retinal thickness within the central 1-mm ETDRS subfield on the macular thickness map, after manual verification of segmentation. These criteria ensured that the analysis was focused on cases with substantial cystoid ME, minimizing variability due to small or transient cystic changes.

The exclusion criteria included multiple retinal vein occlusions, BRVO located nasal to the optic disc, central retinal vein occlusion, and coexisting ocular diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, hypertensive retinopathy, retinal arterial occlusion, retinal arterial macroaneurysm, glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, and age-related macular degeneration. Patients with high myopia (>−6.0 D), high astigmatism (> ± 3.0 D), or poor-quality AO-OCT images due to eye movement or media opacities were also excluded.

Treatment history prior to AO-OCT imaging

To accurately assess the clinical course of BRVO, only patients who had been treated at our institution from the acute phase onward were included in this study. At the initial presentation, all patients exhibited ME, serous retinal detachment, and/or retinal or subretinal hemorrhages involving the fovea. Initial treatment consisted of a single intravitreal injection of ranibizumab (0.5 mg/0.05 mL; Lucentis, Novartis Pharma AG, Basel, Switzerland). No additional treatments, such as scatter or grid laser photocoagulation, steroid therapy, surgical intervention, or anti-VEGF agents other than ranibizumab, were administered during the observation period. Following the initial injection, a pro re nata (PRN) protocol was implemented. Reinjections were performed only when SD-OCT confirmed persistent or recurrent ME or serous retinal detachment involving the fovea and the patient provided informed consent. However, reinjections were not performed in cases where prior injections resulted in minimal subjective visual improvement and the patient was reluctant to continue treatment, or where the treating physician determined that further reinjections would be of limited benefit despite anatomical indications, in accordance with a previous study22.

Routine ophthalmic examinations included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement using a Landolt chart, intraocular pressure assessment, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, SD-OCT, and OCTA.

AO-OCT imaging was performed in eyes with persistent ME at least 6 months after BRVO onset and at least 1 month after the final ranibizumab injection, when the effect of the treatment was considered to have diminished and additional treatment was being considered23, in order to evaluate retinal morphology (Supplementary Fig. 1). The final anti-VEGF injection was defined as the last injection administered as part of routine clinical care before AO-OCT imaging.

Evaluation of treatment response using SD-OCT

Therapeutic response was retrospectively assessed based on the maximum reduction in CST measured using SD-OCT. This was defined as the difference between the CST immediately before the final anti-VEGF injection and the lower of the CST values measured at 2 weeks and 1 month after the injection. By defining post-treatment CST in this manner, this approach allowed us to capture both cases in which macular edema gradually improved after anti-VEGF treatment and those in which edema initially improved but recurred shortly thereafter.

Assessment of macular vessel density using OCTA

Swept-source OCTA (3 × 3 mm, Plex Elite 9000) was acquired at 300 × 300 A-scans and upsampled to 1024 × 1024 pixels. All volume scans were reviewed for segmentation accuracy. When the automated slab boundaries were inaccurate, manual correction was performed by trained graders before analysis. Macular vessel density was quantified for both the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) and deep capillary plexus (DCP) using the Phansalkar local thresholding method in ImageJ. A 3-mm ring centered on the fovea was divided into superior and inferior halves. Following previous studies12,24, the hemifield corresponding to the occluded vein was defined as the affected region, and the opposite hemifield was defined as the unaffected region. Assessment of retinal microstructure using AO-OCT imaging

AO-OCT imaging was performed using a custom-built system developed by Canon Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), which incorporated wavefront aberration correction to achieve high-resolution imaging, with axial and lateral resolutions of 3.4 μm and 3.0 μm, respectively. Each scan captured a 2.5° × 2.5° area (equivalent to 728 μm) centered on the fovea, acquired over a 15-second period at a rate of 45 frames per second. To enhance image quality and reduce noise, following previously described methods17–19, approximately 1000 consecutive B-scans were acquired with real-time fundus tracking. From these, 100 B-scans most closely corresponding to the scan passing through the fovea were automatically selected using built-in software and averaged to generate the final image.

In normal eyes, structures consistent with Müller cell bodies have been visualized on AO-OCT as slender, vertically oriented low-reflectivity elements extending from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the external limiting membrane (ELM) (Supplementary Fig. 3), with photoreceptor nuclei aligned along these structures17. In the present study, the retinal tissue below the cystoid spaces, where AO-OCT provided stable delineation of the outer retinal layers across eyes, was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively (Fig. 1) to ensure consistent focus and image quality.

For qualitative assessment, microstructural organization beneath the cystoid space was classified into two patterns: an aligned pattern, characterized by orderly arrangement of photoreceptor nuclei and visible Müller cell–like structures, similar to normal eyes, and a non-aligned pattern, in which such organization was disrupted.

For quantitative assessment, following previously reported methods17, the density of photoreceptor nuclei was evaluated. High-intensity dots were defined as fine, punctate reflective signals arising from cone photoreceptor nuclei within the outer nuclear layer (ONL) on AO-OCT. Gaussian filtering was employed to suppress background noise, followed by application of the Find Maxima function in ImageJ to identify discrete reflective spots. The density of high-intensity dots was calculated by dividing the total count by the ONL area within the central area of 400 μm. These high-intensity dots are distinct from the hyperreflective foci described in vascular or inflammatory diseases25, as they represent reflective signals from the cone nuclei visualized on AO-OCT.

Image quality–related variability may influence the visibility of cellular structures, and reduced image quality is often associated with decreased signal intensity in the corresponding region. To account for this potential confounding factor, we also evaluated the local reflectivity of the ONL area beneath the cystoid space, based on previously described protocols26. The original grayscale intensity values of the retinal tissue beneath the cystoid space were measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). The vitreous cavity was defined as the baseline reference (0), and the reflectivity of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) was set at 100, allowing normalization of intensity values across images26–28. Ellipsoid zone (EZ) disruption was quantified as the horizontal extent of signal loss, excluding areas where the EZ remained faintly visible or continuous beneath the foveal cystoid region29.

To compensate for individual variations in axial length, all spatial measurements were calibrated using the Bennett-modified Littmann formula30.

All AO-OCT image analyses, including qualitative classifications and quantitative measurements, were independently performed by two experienced retinal specialists (N.N. and Y.M.) who were blinded to the clinical data. Inter-grader reproducibility for quantitative parameters was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (two-way random-effects model), and agreement in qualitative classification was assessed using Cohen’s κ.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were performed using JMP version 16 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). BCVA values were converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) units. Sex, the presence of hypertension, and microstructural organization pattern were treated as categorical variables, while all other variables were treated as continuous variables. Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations.

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationships between clinical or imaging parameters and CST reduction. Comparisons between eyes with and without preserved retinal microstructural alignment on AO-OCT were conducted using unpaired t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value < 0.05.

Results

Persistent ME secondary to BRVO was identified in 30 eyes from 30 eligible patients (7 men and 23 women), which were subsequently analyzed (Table 1). At the initial visit, the mean CST was 508.9 ± 177.7 μm, and the mean BCVA was 0.19 ± 0.20 logMAR (Snellen equivalent: 20/100–20/15). At the time of AO-OCT examination, performed 55.2 ± 34.8 months after disease onset, the BCVA had improved to 0.05 ± 0.17 logMAR (Snellen equivalent: 20/63–20/12), and the CST had decreased to 420.7 ± 122.4 μm. Both BCVA and CST showed significant improvements compared with values at the initial visit (P = 0.001 and P = 0.049, respectively). However, mild retinal thickening persisted in the macular area, indicating residual ME. The mean CST immediately before the final anti-VEGF injection was 474.3 ± 126.7 μm. Within 1 month after the final anti-VEGF injection, the minimum CST was observed at 2 weeks in 6 eyes and at 1 month in 24 eyes. The mean minimum CST within 1 month after the final anti-VEGF injection was 340.6 ± 129.0 μm, corresponding to a mean CST reduction of 133.7 ± 98.7 μm. The mean interval between the final anti-VEGF injection and AO-OCT imaging was 22.1 ± 22.2 months.

AO-OCT imaging identified preserved retinal microstructure in 23 eyes (77%), characterized by a sharply defined hyperreflective ELM, distinct high-intensity dots in the outer nuclear layer (ONL), and aligned hyporeflective thin structures within the outer retina. In contrast, seven eyes (23%) with disorganized retinal tissue lacked these features. All quantitative AO-OCT parameters demonstrated excellent reproducibility (all ICC > 0.94), and qualitative classification of microstructural alignment showed complete agreement between graders (κ = 1.0).

Eyes with retinal structural disruption exhibited significantly smaller CST reductions after the final treatment compared with those with preserved retinal microstructural alignment (mean difference: 97.5 μm, P = 0.019; Fig. 2). Among quantitative AO-OCT parameters, EZ disruption showed the strongest association with CST reduction (R = − 0.424, P = 0.019; Table 2). In contrast, the density of high-intensity dots in the outer retina and the reflectivity of the outer retina beneath the foveal cystoid space were not significantly associated with CST reduction (R = 0.297, P = 0.110, and R = − 0.048, P = 0.802, respectively).

Retinal microstructural disorganization was associated with long duration from onset to AO-OCT examination (P = 0.035; Table 3). Among AO-OCT parameters, retinal microstructural disorganization was also associated with greater EZ disruption (P < 0.001; mean difference: 257.0 μm) and a lower density of high-intensity dots in the ONL (P = 0.009; mean difference: 929 dots/unit area; Table 3; Figs. 3 and 4). All seven eyes with disorganized retinal microstructure had not received anti-VEGF injections within the 6 months preceding AO-OCT imaging, whereas eyes with preserved microstructure had received 0.68 ± 0.88 injections during the same period. Retinal reflectivity beneath the cystoid spaces on AO-OCT did not differ significantly between eyes with disorganized and preserved retinal microstructure. Furthermore, macular vessel density reduction in either the SCP or DCP was not significantly correlated with CST reduction.

Discussion

Persistent ME secondary to BRVO remains a significant therapeutic challenge, as it can limit visual recovery even after anti–VEGF treatment. Prior investigations utilizing FA and OCTA have demonstrated that elevated parafoveal vascular density may reflect venous congestion and increased exudation from abnormal retinal vessels, thereby contributing to prolonged ME11,12. Conversely, severe macular ischemia has been associated with spontaneous resolution of ME, presumably due to decreased leakage from the compromised capillary network31. Nevertheless, clinical experience suggests that ME can occasionally resolve even in eyes with relatively high vascular density, implying that factors beyond vascular leakage may play a role in its persistence. These observations underscore the importance of exploring alternative mechanisms—particularly those related to intraretinal fluid homeostasis.

Müller glial cells are essential for retinal fluid regulation, functioning in both fluid absorption and metabolic maintenance. Histologic studies have identified signs of Müller cell degeneration in the vicinity of cystoid changes, indicating a possible contribution to ME pathogenesis13,14. However, most in vivo imaging studies to date have emphasized vascular characteristics such as perfusion and permeability, while less attention has been given to the role of the retinal tissue in fluid clearance. With the advent of AO-OCT, detailed visualization of glial structures has become feasible17. Given its high spatial resolution, AO-OCT is well suited to detect subtle glial alterations, which may provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying persistent ME. Importantly, in our cohort, eyes with microstructural disorganization consistently showed reduced CST improvement after anti-VEGF therapy.

In this study, eyes with retinal structural alterations identified on AO-OCT showed significantly smaller CST reductions after anti-VEGF treatment compared to eyes with preserved retinal alignment. These microstructural alterations were also associated with a greater extent of EZ disruption and a lower density of high-intensity dots in the ONL. These findings suggest that retinal microstructural organization is associated with anatomical outcomes in persistent ME. It is conceivable that the observed structural alterations may be related to changes in glial architecture, although further histological or longitudinal investigations are needed to substantiate this hypothesis. Notably, a previous study using en face OCT imaging interpreted retinal integrity in ME as reflecting the presence or absence of tissue columns potentially composed of Müller cells between the outer plexiform layers32. This interpretation is consistent with the structural features observed in the present study.

In addition, none of the eyes exhibiting disorganized microstructure had received anti-VEGF injections for at least 6 months before AO-OCT imaging, supporting the interpretation that these changes may represent chronic remodeling rather than transient edema. This finding suggests that sustained anti-VEGF therapy may be associated with preservation of retinal structure in chronic BRVO-associated ME, consistent with previous studies33.

Previous studies have suggested that hyperreflective walls delineating the boundaries of cystoid spaces on SD-OCT are associated with treatment resistance in patients with diabetic ME21. The structural alterations observed on AO-OCT in the present study align with this hypothesis. Hyperreflective walls delineating the boundaries of cystoid spaces likely represent localized glial or fibrotic remodeling around chronic cystoid cavities rather than transient fluid accumulation.

Interestingly, retinal reflectivity beneath the foveal cystoid spaces on AO-OCT was not significantly correlated with CST reduction. This finding may reflect the superior resolution of AO-OCT in detecting subtle microstructural changes such as photoreceptor nuclei and glial remodeling, compared with conventional SD-OCT. Our findings did not demonstrate a significant correlation between macular vessel density reduction and the treatment response, suggesting that ME persistence may not be solely dependent on vascular permeability changes but could also be associated with failure of glial-mediated fluid transport. While previous studies11,12 have primarily attributed ME to excessive vascular exudation, our results indicate that retinal structural organization may contribute to fluid clearance and that features suggestive of glial involvement may be associated with prolonged edema.

In the present study, eyes with BRVO exhibited extensive EZ disruption and a low density of high-intensity dots in the ONL, consistent with findings from a previous study using AO-scanning laser ophthalmoscopy34. Müller cells provide metabolic support and regulate ion homeostasis in the retina16. Their dysfunction leads to metabolic stress in photoreceptors, potentially exacerbating EZ disruption and photoreceptor degeneration. Chronic ME may, in turn, contribute to further retinal structural deterioration, creating a cycle of persistent retinal edema.

Although the disorganization identified in this study conceptually relates to the “disorganization of the retinal inner layers (DRIL)” described in diabetic ME20, the two phenomena are possibly mechanistically distinct. DRIL primarily reflects ischemic injury and loss of laminar boundaries within the inner retina, whereas the disorganization observed in our study occurred in the outer retina adjacent to the cystoid spaces. This pattern likely represents glial remodeling and impaired Müller cell-mediated water transport rather than ischemic thinning and resembles cystoid macular degeneration (CMD)35, a chronic form of Müller cell dysfunction.

Given the potential role of Müller cells in retinal fluid regulation9, their dysfunction may affect the long-term visual prognosis of eyes with BRVO. In some cases of persistent ME, interventions such as cystectomy36 and steroid therapy37,38 have been considered, particularly when CMD-like features are observed on AO-OCT. However, the efficacy of these approaches remains uncertain, and further studies are needed to evaluate their potential role in combination with anti-VEGF therapy.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, requiring validation of the findings in larger cohorts. Second, FA was not routinely performed due to its invasive nature; therefore, vascular leakage could not be directly evaluated in this study. Third, the cross-sectional design precluded the assessment of longitudinal changes in Müller cell integrity. In addition, although this study demonstrated an association between microstructural changes and treatment resistance, causality cannot be inferred. Fourth, AO-OCT imaging is technically demanding, with image quality potentially affected by media opacity and eye movement. Fifth, the response to the final anti-VEGF injection was assessed retrospectively, and no additional anti-VEGF treatment was administered after AO-OCT imaging. Sixth, although ranibizumab was generally administered using a 1 + PRN regimen, in the chronic phase, some eyes did not receive additional injections despite the presence of ME, as further treatment benefit was considered limited, reflecting real-world clinical practice rather than a standardized treatment protocol. Finally, qualitative evaluation of the retinal microstructure was confined to the region below the cystoid spaces. This reflects the inherently narrow depth of focus resulting from the enlarged beam diameter used to achieve high lateral resolution on AO-OCT. Future studies employing extended-focus or volumetric reconstruction techniques, refined imaging protocols, and larger datasets could improve reproducibility and facilitate comprehensive assessment of both the superior and inferior aspects of cystoid cavities.

Despite the limitations of this cross-sectional study, AO-OCT enabled high-resolution visualization of retinal microstructural changes in eyes with persistent ME secondary to BRVO. Retinal disorganization was associated with a limited anatomical response to anti-VEGF therapy and was observed in eyes without recent treatment, suggesting that these changes reflect chronic remodeling. These findings may reflect glial architectural alterations and impaired retinal fluid clearance, and further highlight the potential value of sustained anti-VEGF therapy in preserving retinal structure. However, direct histopathological correlates of these findings remain unavailable, and further studies are needed to support this interpretation. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine whether AO-OCT-derived indicators of glial remodeling can serve as prognostic biomarkers or guide individualized therapeutic strategies.
