The rising burden of non-motor vehicle crashes: An emerging challenge for road traffic injury prevention in China, 1997–2023
Authors: Ruibing Deng, Shuxian Yu, Jiexuan Lin, Zhaojing Yang, Wanbao Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate preventive achievements and identify emerging trends and challenges regarding road traffic injuries (RTIs) in China from 1997 to 2023.Study designEcological trend study.MethodsData on road traffic crashes, injuries, and deaths were extracted from China's National Bureau of Statistics. Joinpoint regression analysis was employed to identify significant temporal trends and compute the annual percentage change (APC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC). All outcome variables were log-transformed prior to analysis due to skewed distributions, as determined by normality assessment.ResultsOver the 27-year period, the number of road traffic crashes and associated casualties showed three distinct phases: a rapid increase until 2001, a significant decline until around 2011, and a subsequent stabilization or slight rebound. While motor vehicle-related incidents initially drove these trends, injuries (APC2010-2023 = 12.88, P < 0.01; AAPC = 6.54, P < 0.01) and deaths (APC2011-2019 = 13.57, P < 0.01; APC2019-2023 = 5.06, P < 0.01) involving non-motor vehicles have risen sharply since around 2010. This surge has partially offset earlier gains in overall RTI prevention and now poses a substantial threat to national road safety. Notably, deaths exhibited a strong resurgence post-2011, exceeding 5000 in 2023.ConclusionsDespite commendable gains in reducing motor vehicle-related RTIs, China now faces the acute challenge of a rising injury burden involving non-motor vehicles. Consequently, future road safety initiatives must be formulated on a holistic approach that integrates enhanced vehicle safety, road infrastructure optimized for vulnerable users, and precise behavioral interventions targeting high-risk populations. The findings provide an evidence base for prioritizing non-motor vehicle safety in China's next phase of road safety strategy and offer methodological insights for similar trend analyses in other low- and middle-income countries experiencing rapid e-bike adoption.Keywords: Road safety, Traffic injury, Non-motor vehicle, Trend analysis, Public health

Introduction

Road traffic crashes (RTCs) claim approximately 1.19 million lives annually worldwide, constituting a major public health crisis according to the World Health Organization (WHO). Ninety-two percent of these deaths occurred in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Road traffic injuries (RTIs) are the leading cause of death among children and young adults aged 5–29 years and have resulted in a high number of victims for many years. Pedestrians, motorcyclists, cyclists, and e-bike riders are vulnerable road users and account for nearly half of all road traffic fatalities [1]. Although RTIs are commonplace, tackling such a long-standing problem remains challenging due to a persistent lack of commitment and investment in most countries and regions. Advanced analytical tools, such as road incident maps that geolocate collisions between motor vehicles and vulnerable users (e.g., cyclists and pedestrians), have proven useful for local safety analyses [2]. In 1990, RTIs ranked as the ninth leading cause of the global burden of disease and were projected to become the fifth leading cause of death globally by 2030 [3]. Despite a downward trend in the global burden of RTIs in the past two decades, they remain a major global public health challenge [4]. Target 3.6 of the Sustainable Development Goals, which aimed to halve the number of global road traffic deaths and injuries by 2020 was not achieved, with only an approximately 5% reduction in deaths [1]. This outcome suggests that actions have lagged behind the calls for RTI prevention.

As the world's largest developing country, China has experienced complex and fluctuating trends in RTIs. In the 1980s, China possessed the largest bicycle fleet globally, earning the designation as the “Kingdom of Bicycles” [5]. With sustained economic development, motorcycles and private cars have progressively become common in millions of households [6]. The road environment has grown more complex due to the increasing number and diversity of road users [7]. Beginning in the 1990s, more than 150 Chinese cities imposed bans on motorcycles in their central urban areas to address the issue of collisions and serious injuries between two-wheeled and four-wheeled vehicles. Although this policy has led to significant improvements in road safety, it has also exacerbated traffic congestion and sparked persistent controversy over inequitable road usage [8]. Driven by the policy and parallel improvements in electric drive technology, e-bikes grew into a primary alternative, filling the mobility gap between traditional bicycles and banned motorcycles [9]. In most regions, the absence or recurrent occupation of dedicated non-motorized lanes forces cyclists and e-bike riders to share roads with motor vehicles, resulting in a heightened vulnerability to traffic crashes [10]. The adoption of e-bikes accelerated markedly around 2010. This trend was further fueled by the introduction of shared bicycle and e-bike services in 2016, leading to a boom in non-motorized transportation. Although the shared mobility service later scaled back, the established convenience of e-bikes had solidified the habit for many. This shift solidified into a lasting trend of private e-bike ownership, which continues to the present day.

China has been undergoing a rapid demographic transition toward an aging society since the beginning of the 21st century. As of 2024, individuals aged 60 years and older accounted for approximately 22% of the total population [11]. Against this backdrop, the decline in conventional public transport, combined with the proliferation of app-based ride-hailing services and the expansion of shared mobility modes, has reshaped the urban transportation landscape. These changes have further constrained travel options for vulnerable road users, particularly older adults and minors. As a result, two-wheeled vehicles have emerged as one of the limited mobility alternatives available to these groups. However, due to their generally weaker physical function and reduced ability to avoid hazards, they often face a higher risk of RTIs. The importance of population protection as a foundation for labor supply and societal consumption has been emphasized in recent injury prevention literature [12]. This study extends prior work by extending the analysis to 2023 to specifically quantify the post-2010 surge in non-motor vehicle-related injuries and deaths—a period marked by rapid e-bike proliferation—and explicitly examining the erosion of earlier prevention gains [13]. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the data sources, variable definitions, and the Joinpoint regression analytical procedure. Section 3 presents the temporal trends in road traffic crashes, injuries, and deaths, with a focus on the contrasting patterns between motor vehicle and non-motor vehicle incidents. Section 4 interprets the findings, discusses the implications for road safety policy. Section 5 acknowledges the study's strengths and limitations.

Methods

The data for this study were extracted from the database (data.stats.gov.cn) maintained by China's National Bureau of Statistics (NBS). These data were collected and compiled by the public security departments and the NBS in accordance with relevant laws and regulations on traffic safety and statistics. According to the NBS classification, “non-motor vehicles” include traditional bicycles, tricycles, animal-drawn vehicles, and electric bicycles (e-bikes). Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0) and the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.6.0.0) developed by the National Cancer Institute (https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/).

The analytical procedure involved three steps: (1) assessing data normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; (2) specifying either a linear or log-transformation Joinpoint model based on the normality results; and (3) determining the optimal number and locations of joinpoints via a Monte Carlo permutation test, with a maximum of five joinpoints allowed and a significance level of P < 0.05. The annual percentage change (APC), average annual percentage change (AAPC), t-values, and P-values were computed for each segment and the overall period [14].

Results

The trends in the number of traffic crashes

From 1997 to 2023, the numbers of RTCs, motor vehicle crashes (MVCs), and non-motor vehicle crashes (NMVCs) in China exhibited three distinct phases (Fig. 1). The number of RTCs rose rapidly from 1997 to 2001 (APC = 32.75, P < 0.01), then declined annually by 13.72% from 2001 to 2011 (APC = −13.72, P < 0.01). Although it rebounded significantly after 2011 (APC = 2.97, P < 0.01), the figure remained well below its peak. Overall, the number of RTCs showed no significant net change across the entire period (AAPC = 0.04, P > 0.05). MVCs followed a similar pattern, with a sharp increase until 2001 (APC = 32.23, P < 0.01), a decline to the initial level through 2011 (APC = −13.37, P < 0.01), and a gradual recovery thereafter (APC = 1.86, P < 0.05), resulting in a non-significant overall decrease (AAPC = 0.37, P > 0.05). In contrast, NMVCs demonstrated a strong upward trend from 1997 to 2023 (AAPC = 4.60, P < 0.01). While it also went through ascending (APC = 18.22, P < 0.01) and descending (APC = −10.59, P < 0.01) phases, the fluctuations were more moderate. However, the number rose rapidly after 2010, reaching new highs (APC2010-2021 = 13.74, P < 0.01; APC2021-2023 = 4.68, P > 0.05).

The trends in the numbers of crash injuries

As shown in Fig. 2, the total number of injuries fluctuated in four segments between 1997 and 2023. Increases occurred during 1997–2001 (APC = 34.28, P < 0.01) and 2012–2023 (APC = 2.68, P < 0.01), while declines were observed from 2001 to 2015 (APC2001-2005 = −4.19, P > 0.05; APC2005-2012 = -11.17, P < 0.01). The trend for MVC injuries closely mirrored the overall injury trend, indicating that MVCs are the primary driver of total injuries. In contrast, NMVC injuries showed a marked upward trend over the entire period (AAPC = 6.54, P < 0.01). A decline between 2001 and 2010 (APC = −7.27, P < 0.01) was far outweighed by a substantial increase after 2010 (APC = 12.88, P < 0.01).

The trends in the numbers of crash deaths

The total number of road traffic deaths showed a slight non-significant downward trend from 1997 to 2023 (AAPC = −0.65, P > 0.05; Fig. 3). After a two-segment increase until 2004 (APC1997-2001 = 10.22, P < 0.01; APC2001-2004 = 0.73, P > 0.05), deaths followed a two-segment continuous decline through 2014 (APC2004-2009 = −8.93, P < 0.01; APC2009-2014 = −3.27, P < 0.01). In the most recent decade, the death toll stabilized at around 60,000 annually, following a slight non-significant increase (APC2014-2017 = 3.98, P > 0.05) and a subsequent slight decrease (APC2017-2023 = −1.06, P < 0.05). Deaths from MVCs were consistent with this overall pattern. Meanwhile, deaths from NMVCs exhibited a V-shaped trend (AAPC = 0.70, P > 0.05). A slight rise from 1997 to 2002 (APC = 1.93, P < 0.01) was followed by a continuous decrease through 2011 (APC2002-2005 = −23.16, P < 0.01; APC2005-2011 = -5.52, P < 0.01). After 2011, deaths rose continuously (APC2011-2019 = 13.57, P < 0.01; APC2019-2023 = 5.06, P < 0.01), surpassing initial levels and exceeding 5000 in 2023.

Discussion

The findings from this study reveal two overarching patterns. Firstly, RTIs in China have gone through three phases of increasing, decreasing and stabilizing over the past nearly 30 years, suggesting that China has successfully curbed the deterioration of traffic injuries caused by the excessive motorization. Second, MVCs caused the majority of RTIs. However, the number of non-motor vehicle involved crashes, injuries, and deaths increased after around 2010, resulting in increasingly greater absolute value and proportion of RTIs and eroding the achievements of motor vehicle-related RTI prevention.

Rapid motorization was the predominant driver behind the initial sharp increase in RTIs. Throughout the research period, China witnessed a greater than 39-fold increase in its motor vehicle population, equating to an average annual growth of around 15% [15]. The motorcycling boom, characterized by an estimated 8% annual growth since 2001, has been primarily spurred by the vehicles' affordability, convenience, and fuel efficiency [16]. Inadequate road infrastructure development and governance, in relative to motorization, have resulted in more frequent and severe conflicts between motorists and vulnerable road users, particularly riders of two-wheeled vehicles [17]. In areas where motorbikes are a dominant mode of transport, they account for 70% to 80% of all traffic crashes, with some regions seeing even higher proportions [18]. Surging demands for transport, commuting, and tourism have increased total travel mileage, which heightens the collective exposure and consequently the risk of RTI [19]. A downward trend in crash, injury, and death figures commenced in 2001, coinciding with the SARS outbreak. While some scholars link this decline to reduced traffic exposure due to home isolation, they argue that this specific, temporary factor is not broadly applicable [20]. The 2011 Amendment to the Criminal Law, which came into effect on May 1, criminalized drunk driving, stipulating criminal detention and fines and thereby providing a powerful deterrent [13]. The subsequent plateau in RTCs and RTIs after 2015 potentially reflects a combination of factors, including a maturation of the vehicle fleet and traffic management systems, alongside the integration of smart internet and high-tech industries. In the past decades, the lack of transformative safety measures on the scale of seatbelts or airbags has hindered further breakthroughs in RTI prevention.

Although motor vehicle-related crashes, injuries, and deaths have experienced a slow decline since 2010, non-motorized traffic incidents have exhibited rapid growth, with annual increases exceeding 10%. This divergent trend indicates that the growing burden of non-motorized RTIs is counteracting the progress made in overall RTI prevention. This shift is considered to be linked to the rapid proliferation of e-bikes and shared bicycles [21]. The widespread popularity of non-motor vehicles has coincided with a decline in public transport ridership, evidenced by bus route cancellations and reduced metro services [22,23]. This deterioration of public options may, in turn, create a feedback loop that further increases reliance on non-motorized travel [24]. The legacy of road infrastructure, designed primarily for motorized transport, continues to marginalize non-motor vehicles. This is evidenced by a pervasive lack of dedicated lanes, and where provided, their fragmented nature or frequent obstruction. Without targeted interventions, this systemic oversight is likely to sustain elevated levels of RTI risk indefinitely.

In 2018, China's new national standard for electric bicycles mandated a maximum speed of 25 km/h. However, this speed limit has proven inadequate for some users. Consequently, many have begun bypassing the speed restrictors, leading to a proliferation of non-compliant, motorized e-bikes on the roads. This is a critical safety concern because vehicle speed directly influences both crash risk and the severity of resulting injuries [1,25]. A meta-analysis on e-bike injuries estimated a mortality rate of 9.0%, underscoring the grave consequences of high-speed collisions [26]. Children under the age of 16 represent the largest group among e-bike injury cases, while individuals over 40 constitute another high-risk demographic [27,28].

These two demographics present deficits in attention, traffic perception, real-time avoidance, and traffic rule comprehension compared to adults under 40, with their smaller physical stature reducing visibility and thereby increasing risk [29]. Among senior adults, these vulnerabilities are further amplified by natural sensory and reactive declines, predisposing them to severe trauma like fractures in accidents [30]. Despite being less likely to engage in vigorous cycling, the elderly exhibit poor helmet compliance—a serious problem they share with children and other groups [31]. Given their flexibility, point-to-point capability, and use of clean energy, non-motor vehicles like e-bikes are indispensable for many, especially vulnerable groups such as the elderly and teenagers. Consequently, urban policy should not focus on restriction but on guaranteeing equitable road access. RTI prevention must be guided by a systemic approach that integrates safer vehicle standards, protective road design, and educated user behavior.

Strengths and limitations

This study leverages a long-term (1997–2023) and consistently sourced dataset from China's NBS to systematically quantify the sharp surge after 2010 in non-motor vehicle-related injuries and deaths. It provides empirical evidence that the rapid proliferation of e-bikes has begun to erode previous road safety gains. The findings offer timely and policy-relevant insights for China and other low- and middle-income countries experiencing similar e-bike adoption trends, highlighting the need to reorient road safety strategies toward vulnerable road users. A noteworthy limitation of this study is its reliance on data from public security departments rather than cause-of-death surveillance, which is widely regarded as a more dependable source for assessing the true burden of road traffic injuries and enabling international comparisons [32,33]. However, misclassification bias may occur in cause-of-death data, which is particularly influenced by the diagnostic and coding practices of healthcare institutions [33,34]. Moreover, cause-of-death surveillance data suffer from substantial reporting delays, with the most recent data currently available only up to 2021, which limits their utility to some extent. In contrast, data from the public security department systematically underestimate the number of injuries and fatalities, but exhibit consistent bias over time [35]. Given that traffic incidents typically involve on-site investigations, there is reason to believe that these data remain valuable for studying trends in injury burden. Another concern is that this study relies entirely on absolute counts rather than rate-based measures (e.g., per registered vehicle or per vehicle-kilometer traveled). Over the 27-year study period, China's population changed modestly, and our aim was to assess the population health threat of road traffic injuries, not individual exposure risk. Even if per-user risk had remained constant, the growth in e-bike ownership since 2010 would still predict an increase in absolute NMV-related injury. From a public health perspective, what matters is the total number of preventable deaths and injuries rather than personal exposure risk. Therefore, while the absence of denominator data precludes conclusions about individual risk trends, the rising absolute burden remains policy-relevant. It signals that current prevention efforts are insufficient to counter the population-level impact of widespread e-bike adoption. To argue otherwise would be to mistake a methodological limitation for a substantive justification for inaction. Nonetheless, the findings still offer meaningful insights into temporal changes despite such limitations.

In conclusion, China has successfully reduced road traffic injuries over the past three decades, yet this progress has stalled since 2011. While motor vehicle-related incidents have continued to decline, the sharp rise in non-motor vehicle crashes, particularly among vulnerable road users, has begun to erode previous gains. This dual trend reveals a critical shift in the nature of road safety challenges, namely that the burden is moving from motorized to non-motorized transport, a shift often overlooked in conventional policy frameworks. Consequently, future interventions must move beyond vehicle-focused measures and adopt a holistic strategy that integrates protective infrastructure, safer vehicle standards, and targeted behavioral programs for high-risk groups such as children and the elderly. Without such systemic reorientation, China's hard-won achievements in road safety risk being further undermined.
