Reliability of muscle architecture assessment using clinical ultrasound in post-stroke patients: a systematic review
Authors: Yu-Ying Li, Wei Liu, Chang-Liang Luo, Shi-Fu Feng, Hui-Dong Wu, Lu Li, Shao-Chun Chen, Christina Zong-Hao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlterations in skeletal muscle architecture following stroke-induced neural injury serve as a major contributor to post-stroke motor impairment. Ultrasound has demonstrated promising potential for muscle assessment, however, its reliability in evaluating muscle in post-stroke patients was not systematically investigated. This systematic review aimed to synthesize available data on the reliability of ultrasound-based measurements of muscle architecture in post-stroke patients.MethodsLiterature searches were conducted across PubMed, Medline, CNKI, Embase, and Wed of Science using keywords of ultrasound (ultrasonic/echotomography) and stroke. Studies were included if they focused on the reliability of ultrasound-based muscle measurements in post-stroke patients. Data on reliability was extracted. Methodological quality was evaluated using a checklist proposed by Pretorius and Keating. The overall level of evidence was also determined.ResultsOf the 130,484 initial records identified, 13 studies were included. Among these, 12 were rated as high quality and 1 as low quality. Ultrasound demonstrated moderate-to-excellent reliability for measuring both muscle thickness and pennation angle in the gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles, with intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) values ranging from 0.69 to 0.99, supported by strong evidence. Furthermore, it exhibited good-to-excellent reliability in assessing both cross-sectional area (ICC = 0.76–0.97 in gastrocnemius, biceps brachii, and flexor digitorum superficialis/profundus) and muscle length (ICC = 0.89–0.98 in TA, gastrocnemius, and biceps brachii), supported by moderate evidence. Similarly, moderate evidence also supported excellent reliability of ultrasound in assessing muscle volume in the TA (ICC > 0.90).ConclusionsUltrasound generally demonstrated moderate-to-excellent reliability for measuring muscle architecture parameters, including muscle thickness, pennation angle, cross-sectional area, muscle volume, and length, across various muscles in post-stroke patients, with supporting evidence rating from moderate to strong.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09294-4.Keywords: Stroke, Ultrasound, Muscle assessment, Reliability

Background

Stroke is a neurological condition characterized by localized or widespread brain tissue damage caused by various factors [1]. The China Stroke Prevention Report 2021 highlights high morbidity, disability, recurrence, and mortality rates associated with stroke [2]. Globally, over 12.2 million new cases are reported annually, with 16% of patients aged 15–49 and 62% under 70 years old [3]. Beyond its life-threatening implications, stroke often leads to significant motor dysfunction [4, 5]. Previous studies reported that there were approximately 70% of post-stroke patients experiencing different degrees of motor dysfunction [6, 7].

Neurological damage from a stroke often induces abnormal muscle tone and spasticity, contributing to motor dysfunction. These changes subsequently promote structural changes in muscle (i.e., atrophy and fibrosis), which further exacerbate functional impairments Consequently, post-stroke patients may experience balance impairments (49%), upper limbs dysfunction (55.5%), manual dexterity deficits (52%), lower limbs dysfunction (44%), and gait abnormalities (54%) [8–11]. Such pathological transformations are important contributors to long-term disability, characterized by disuse atrophy, an imbalance between protein synthesis and degradation, and a slow-to-fast fibre-type transition [12, 13]. These changes weaken muscle and disrupt the physiological synergy between agonists and antagonists, leading to abnormal motor control and hemiplegic movement patterns [10, 12, 14–16]. Spasticity and fibrosis further distort muscle architecture [17], while continuous reflex-driven activity shortens fascicles, increases pennation angle and triggers, and promotes intramuscular connective-tissue proliferation, these changes in muscle architecture begin within weeks after stroke and most of them are irreversible, limiting rehabilitation efficacy and delay recovery [16]. Therefore, assessing muscle architecture alterations is important for developing rehabilitation strategies.

Several modalities are available for assessing muscle architecture. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is widely used for muscle assessment, providing high-resolution and accurate measurements without radiation exposure. However, it is costly and time-consuming [18, 19]. Computed tomography (CT) is helpful for bone evaluation but less suitable for muscle assessment and involves radiation exposure. Skinfold thickness measured using calipers estimates body fat percentage and lean mass, but its reliability remains further improvement [18, 19]. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry utilizes two distinct X-ray energy levels to quantitatively differentiate bone mineral content, adipose tissue, and lean body mass. While coeffective for body composition analysis, its clinical utility is limited by high costs and radiation exposure [18, 20]. Ultrasound serves as a useful modality for muscle assessment, characterized by being non-invasive, radiation-free, affordable and real-time [20, 21].

Ultrasound has been demonstrated as a reliable method for assessing muscle architecture, including thickness, cross-sectional area, pennation angle, and stiffness, across various populations such as healthy individuals aged 7–77 [22–24] as well as patients with osteoarthritis [24] and cerebral palsy [25]. In post-stroke population, muscle assessment via ultrasound has been frequently employed, and its reliability has been examined in different individual studies [24, 26, 27]. However, no systematic review has yet critically evaluated its overall reliability for this specific population. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically review and synthesize the published data on the reliability of ultrasound-based muscle measurements in post-stroke patients, providing evidence for its clinical application.

Methods

This review was registered in PROSPERO (Ref.: CRD42024614870) and conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [28].

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed in the following databases on August 30, 2025: PubMed, Medline, CNKI, Embase, and Wed of Science. Key words of “ultrasound” (or Ultrasonic or Echotomography) and “stroke”, combined with “AND” or “OR”, were used.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) involvement of post-stroke patients; (2) availability of valid data on reliability of ultrasound-based muscle architecture measurements in a skeletal muscle; and (3) availability of full-text articles in English. Exclusion criteria included expert opinions, case reports, abstract-only publications, and studies involving patients with other muscular disorders.

Study selection and data extraction

Following the identification of initial records, titles and abstracts were screened, and followed by a full text review for eligibility. Reference lists of selected studies were manually retrieved and assessed. Data extracted from the eligible studies included: (1) study characteristics (first author, year, country/region, study sample, and ultrasound system); (2) ultrasound-based assessments details, including muscle evaluated, posture, measured parameters, and reliability data. The measured parameters included muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, muscle volume, muscle length, and pennation angle. The reliability data contained intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), correlation coefficient (r), mean deviation, measurement standard error (SEM), and minimum detectable change at the 95% confidence interval (MDC). Two assessors (LYY, LL) selected studies and extracted data independently. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a senior assessor (LW).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using a checklist proposed by Pretorius and Keating [29]. The checklist comprises 10 items and each item scores one point, totaling a maximum of 10 points. A greater score indicates higher study quality. Studies with scores of 6 or above were considered high quality, while those with scores of below 6 were deemed low quality.

Data analysis

The reliability was interpreted as excellent (ICC ≥ 0.90), good (0.75 ≤ ICC < 0.90), moderate (0.50 ≤ ICC < 0.75), or poor (ICC < 0.50) [30]. Meta-analysis was not conducted because of heterogeneity among studies and limited number of studies. As presented in Table 1, relevant data were qualitatively analyzed and synthesized [31].

Results

A total of 130,484 records were identified. After screening titles and abstracts and removing duplicates, 44 studies underwent full-text review, with 12 deemed eligible for analysis. Additionally, 1 eligible study was identified through manual references screening. Thus, 13 studies were included for the final analysis. Reliability of ultrasound-based measurements

As summarized in Table 4, ultrasound demonstrated excellent intra- and inter-rater reliability (ICC >0.90) in assessing MT of the MG [32, 42], and moderate-to-excellent intra- (ICC = 0.74–0.92 [40]) and inter-rater (ICC = 0.69–0.96 [43]) reliability in measuring the MT of TA. Additionally, good-to-excellent inter-rater reliability was also observed in assessing MT of the deltoid (ICC = 0.84–0.93) [34], supraspinatus (ICC >0.98) [35], and abdominal muscles (ICC >0.92) [41].

Discussion

This study systematically reviewed published data on the reliability of ultrasound-based muscle assessments in post-stroke patients, providing evidence to support the clinical application of ultrasound. Key findings included: (1) ultrasound exhibited moderate-to-excellent reliability in assessing MT and PA, supported by strong evidence; (2) ultrasound demonstrated good-to-excellent reliability in measuring CSA and ML, supported by moderate evidence; (3) ultrasound showed excellent reliability in evaluating MV, supported by moderate evidence.

Reliability of ultrasound-based measurements

In general, ultrasound demonstrated moderate-to-excellent overall reliability for assessing muscle architecture parameters, including MT, PA, CSA, MV, and ML, across various muscles in post-stroke patients. Its reliability was similar to those of MRI for measuring lower extremity muscles in healthy adults [23], and of ultrasound-based muscle assessment in both healthy population [23, 48] and children with cerebral palsy [25]. However, its reliability can be varied by muscle state and joint angle. The ICC values for PA were lower at rest (0.70–0.96) than in contraction of the TA (0.94–0.96), and ICC values for MT of the deltoid were higher when shoulder joint angle at 0º (0.93) and 30º (0.91) while decreased at 60º (0.84). Notably, dynamic ultrasound assessments were also performed [32, 49]. The dynamic ultrasound showed good-to-excellent reliability in measuring MT of the gastrocnemius and TA in post-stroke patients (ICC = 0.79–0.96) [32], which was similar to that for gastrocnemius and rectus femoris assessment in children with cerebral palsy (ICC = 0.94–0.99) [50]. Furthermore, other statistical estimators of reliability like ESM [33, 38, 40, 42] and MDC [40] were only provided by some of selected studies. The SEM ranges were 0.02–1.87 and 0.50–5.00.50.00 for MT and ML of the TA in post-stroke patients, respectively. The former was relatively narrower in comparison to children with cerebral palsy, whereas the latter fell within a similar range (0.90–5.33 mm) [51]. Future studies are encouraged to provide a more comprehensive set of reliability data, including, but not limited to, ICC, SEM and MDC, to enable more thorough analysis and synthesis.

Clinical application

Post-stroke spasticity and muscle fibrosis are two major factors affecting patients’ functional recovery. They can lead to significant motor impairments and a decrease in quality of life. Spasticity increases muscle tone, affecting the range of joint motion, while fibrosis alters muscle architecture and function [32, 37, 44]. Therefore, accurate assessment of these changes is crucial for developing effective rehabilitation plans. The application of ultrasound can promptly evaluate post-stroke muscle changes, which is essential for formulating personalized treatment plans, helping to prevent further functional impairments.

Synthesizing the included studies, this study concluded several recommendations for clinical practice. First, the subject should be placed in the standard measurement posture. Select the appropriate measurement position based on the target muscle’s anatomical location and physiological state, utilizing stabilization techniques when necessary. For example, during tibialis anterior measurements at rest, maintain the ankle at 90°dorsiflexion using an ankle-foot orthosis (AFO). When measuring the upper limb muscles, the patient takes the sitting position and fixes the upper limb according to the required angle. Second, the accuracy of the ultrasound probe must be ensured. The probe should be perpendicular to the skin surface at a specific angle from the long muscle axis (e.g. 90) and minimize pressure to avoid muscle deformation. Skin positions were marked at the time of measurement to ensure a consistent location for each measurement. Third, during image acquisition, ensure that the image is clear and complete, covering the measurement area. For the analysis, dedicated software was utilized for quantitative measurements including echo intensity calculation and muscle thickness assessment to minimize measurement error. Fourth, multiple evaluation modalities should be integrated by combining ultrasound with standardized clinical scales (i.e., Modified Ashworth Scale, Fugl-Meyer Assessment). Fifth, comprehensive operator training programs must be implemented to ensure proficiency in standardized measurement protocols and analytical methodologies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting the findings of this study. Firstly, inclusion of only English-language, full-text publications may have resulted in the omission of relevant non-English literature. Secondly, due to the limited number of available studies and significant heterogeneity among selected studies, a meta-analysis was not feasible; thus, the synthesis was limited to a qualitative analysis. Thirdly, the absence of validity data highlights the need for further investigation in future studies. Finally, participants across the selected studies showed a relatively wide age range (48 to 80.5 years). Caution should be warranted when applying the findings to patients near the extremes of this range, due to potential age-related variations in muscle architecture. Nevertheless, this systematic review synthesized the available evidence on the clinical application of ultrasound in post-stroke patients and outlines its utility in this population.

Conclusions

Ultrasound demonstrated moderate-to-excellent reliability for assessing muscle thickness and pennation angle, good-to-excellent reliability for measuring cross-sectional area and muscle length, and excellent reliability in evaluating muscle volume in post-stroke patients, supported by strong or moderate evidence. These findings provided evidence for the clinical application of ultrasound in post-stroke patients.
