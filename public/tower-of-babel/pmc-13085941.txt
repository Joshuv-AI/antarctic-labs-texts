Phosphorylation of a novel STK substrate SstP1 links SUF Fe-S cluster biogenesis to oxidative stress resistance and virulence in Streptococcus suis
Authors: Shuying Ren, Hang Yin, Yiming Wang, Ru Yan, Xiaoyu Jia, Zhiwei Li, Yuqing Li, Yutong Tian, Lianci Peng, Juan Li, Rendong Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters are ancient prosthetic groups that are incorporated into apoproteins to generate fully functional Fe-S proteins, representing a conserved regulatory pathway in both Eukarya and Prokaryotes. In bacteria, the sulfur utilization factor (SUF) system mediates Fe-S cluster biosynthesis under stress conditions, thereby supporting essential metabolic processes to resist oxidative stress. In this study, we identify a novel substrate of serine/threonine kinase (STK), the protein with a DUF1831 domain (named as SstP1) in Streptococcus suis. GST pull-down and enzymatic activity assays demonstrated that STK-dependent phosphorylation of SstP1 at residue threonine 35 is required to sustain SUF-dependent Fe-S cluster biosynthesis under oxidative stress through direct interaction with the scaffold protein SufB. Functional analyses revealed that both deletion of sstP1 (ΔsstP1) or introduction of a phospho-ablative T35A mutant impaired bacterial growth and markedly reduced survival under oxidative stress and iron limitation conditions. In a mouse infection model, both the ΔsstP1 and T35A mutants exhibited significantly reduced bacterial burdens in the blood, lung, spleen, and brain, accompanied by attenuated virulence compared to the WT, phospho-mimetic T35E, and sstP1 complementary (CΔsstP1) mutants. Collectively, these findings reveal that STK-mediated phosphorylation of SstP1 at Thr35 is essential for oxidative stress resistance and virulence in S. suis, uncovering a novel mechanism that links phosphorylation signaling to SUF-dependent Fe-S cluster biosynthesis in bacterial pathogenesis.KEYWORDS: Streptococcus suis, STK, Fe-S cluster biogenesis, oxidative stress resistance, virulence

Introduction

Streptococcus suis is a zoonotic pathogen that causes meningitis, septicemia, and arthritis in both pigs and humans, posing serious economic and public health threats [1–3]. Over thousands of human infection cases have been reported from over 30 countries so far, with the highest disease burden reported in Southeast Asia, particularly in China [4–6]. While clinical cases are sporadic, the associated high mortality necessitates further investigation into the mechanisms of pathogenicity [7,8]. S. suis typically initiates infection by colonizing the upper respiratory tract (e.g. nasopharynx and tonsils) as a commensal [9], and transitions to an invasive pathogen that disseminates through the bloodstream to distal organs (such as the brain, spleen, and joints), leading to severe inflammatory responses and tissue damage in hosts [10]. During these processes, the bacterium is constantly challenged by oxidative stress arising from host immune defenses and fluctuating environmental conditions [11]. A comprehensive understanding of the antioxidative systems of S. suis is therefore essential for developing effective prevention and control strategies.

Iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters are conserved and ubiquitous prosthetic groups essential for fundamental cellular processes [12,13], many of which underpin redox homeostasis and oxidative stress tolerance in bacteria. The biosynthesis of Fe-S clusters relies on a conserved core machinery made of cysteine desulfurase, which serves as a sulfur donor, and scaffold proteins, which act as sulfur and iron acceptors [14,15]. Three distinct Fe-S cluster assembly systems have been identified in bacteria: NiF (nitrogen fixation), ISC (iron-sulfur cluster), and SUF (sulfur mobilization) systems [16–20]. The SUF system functions as a core component for bacterial stress adaptation under oxidative stress or iron starvation [21]. Knockdown of suf transcripts in Staphylococcus aureus disrupted the Fe-S cluster biogenesis, resulting in decreased bacterial viability [22]. Reduced fitness and pathogenicity have been observed in the suf expression-suppressed Mycobacterium tuberculosis mutant [19]. Fe-S cluster biosynthesis in the SUF system can thus be considered a bacterial virulence determinant during host infection [21,23].

Serine/threonine kinases (STKs) are enzymes that catalyze reversible phosphorylation of target proteins at serine or threonine residues to modulate their function, localization, or stability [24–27]. Although once considered exclusive to eukaryotes, STKs in bacteria also regulate diverse substrates and contribute to their viability, virulence, and pathogenicity [28–30]. In S. suis, an abnormal cell division has been observed in the stk deletion mutant (Δstk) due to the disrupted STK-mediated phosphorylation on the division protein DivIVA [31,32]. The attenuated pathogenicity of S. suis Δstk is characterized by impaired adherence to HEp-2 cells, a decreased survival rate in whole blood, and reduced clinical manifestations in animal infection models [29]. Furthermore, a recent study elucidates that STK regulates capsular polysaccharide synthesis (CPS) in S. suis by phosphorylating the substrate Ccps, which functions to activate the CpsBCD pathway required for CPS production [24]. On the contrary, STK-phosphorylated GntR decreased the transcription of NADH oxidase, leading to impaired bacterial oxidative resistance and virulence [30]. Extensive studies in S. suis highlight the intricate functional importance of STK-mediated phosphorylation across a variety of substates that contribute to bacterial growth, virulence, and stress resistance [26,29,33]. In eukaryotic mitochondria, phosphorylation of cysteine desulfurase enhanced its enzymatic activity, thereby boosting the metabolic supply of sulfur and Fe-S clusters with improved oxidative resistance as a result [34,35]. Nevertheless, PTM (post-transcriptional modification)-mediated regulatory mechanisms relevant to Fe-S cluster biogenesis in S. suis or in bacteria in general remain largely uncharacterized.

To investigate the STK-dependent regulatory mechanisms, we constructed a Δstk mutant using a wild-type (WT) S. suis SC19 strain and performed phosphoproteomic analysis in comparison with the WT. The results revealed that the phosphorylation level of a protein with a DUF1831 domain encoded by the B9H01_05215 gene was significantly reduced in the Δstk. Previous studies in S. suis show that the B9H01_05215 protein is a novel member of the pfam08866 family with a full-length DUF1831 domain [29,30], with computational analyses suggesting its potential functional association with cysteine desulfurase activity in S. suis [30]. The protein shares 34.51% sequence identity, and a 77.8% maximum score similarity with the LP2179 protein in Lactobacillus plantarum, which also harbors a DUF1831 domain. X-ray crystallography revealed that the LP2179 adopts a TATA-binding protein (TBP)-like fold [31]. Therefore, we name the B9H01_05215 in S. suis as Streptococcus suis TBP-like fold protein 1 (abbreviated as SstP1), given the conservation of the pfam08866 family and the TBP-like fold structure. Building on this discovery, we constructed the sstP1 deletion mutant (ΔsstP1) and site-directed mutant strains. We aimed (1) to identify phosphorylation site(s) of SstP1 using in vitro kinase assay and site-directed mutagenesis; (2) to characterize the interactions of SstP1 with proteins involved in Fe-S cluster biosynthesis, particularly within the SUF (sulfur mobilization) pathway; and (3) to determine the functions of SstP1 phosphorylation in oxidative stress resistance and bacterial virulence using a mouse infection model.

Materials and methods

Comparative phosphoproteomic assay

To explore the phosphorylation landscape regulated by the serine/threonine kinase (STK), we constructed gene deletion (Δstk) and complementary (CΔstk) mutants using the thermal suicide vector pSET4s. Gene-specific primers (Table 1) were used to verify our successful gene deletion and complementation (Fig. S1 A-F), followed by a qPCR confirming the adjacent genes were unaffected (Fig. S1 G). Δstk mutant and wild-type (WT) SC19 were cultured in biological triplicates within Todd-Hewitt Broth (THB) medium at 37 °C. Upon reaching mid-log growth phase, bacterial cultures were harvested and homogenized with SDS lysis buffer. After boiling for 3 min and sonicating for 2 min, the homogenants were centrifuged at 16,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to extract fragmented peptides. Phosphopeptides enrichment was performed afterwards using a Fe-NTA Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by vacuum-concentration and phosphopeptides reconstitution in 0.1% formic acid. Phosphoproteomic analysis was conducted based upon the enriched phosphopeptides using capillary liquid chromatography-nanoelectrospray tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) with an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer, coupled with a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system to record the signals over a 240-min gradient. We processed MS data using Spectronaut software, and determined a protein encoded by the B9H01_05215 gene with putative cysteine desulfarase property as a candidate substrate of STK.

Cloning, expression, and purification of recombinant proteins

Genomic DNA of the WT strain was isolated using the OMEGA Bacteria DNA Extraction kit (OMEGA Bio-tek, US, D3350-02). The coding sequences of sstP1 and stk were PCR-amplified with specific primers (Table 1), digested with restriction endonucleases XhoI and BamHI, and ligated into the pGEX vector to generate GST-tagged fusion constructs (pGEX-sstP1 and pGEX-stk). After sequence verification, constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG (isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside), followed by purification using Glutathione magnetic agarose beads (Beyotime, CN, P2258). Protein purity was verified by SDS-PAGE, Coomassie Blue staining, and Western blot with anti-GST antibody (Proteintech Group, USA, 80,006–1-RR), and concentrations were determined using a Bradford assay (Epizyme, CN, ZJ101L).

Production of polyclonal antibodies against SstP1

New Zealand White rabbits were immunized subcutaneously with 600 μg of purified SstP1 emulsified in Freund’s complete adjuvant (FCA; Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany, F5881). Two subsequent boosters were administered with Freund’s incomplete adjuvant (FIA, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany, AR002) at 2-week intervals. Indirect ELISA was performed on pre- and post-immunized sera to evaluate antibody titers. Briefly, 96-well plates were pre-coated with recombinant SstP1 (10 μg/well), and serum reactivity was quantified using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, CN, P0205M). OD values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader with cutoff values of the negative control’s mean OD + 2 SD. A serum dilution was positive if its OD exceeded this cutoff and was ≥ 2.1-fold the negative control’s mean OD. Antibody titer was defined as the reciprocal of the highest positive serum dilution.

In vivo phosphorylation assay

We harvested WT and Δstk strains at mid-log growth phase and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, BBI, CN, E607008) containing 1 mg/mL lysozyme (Beyotime, CN, ST206) and phosphatase inhibitors (Epizyme, CN, GRF102). The suspensions were sonicated with five cycles of sequential pulses for 30 seconds and cooling for 30 seconds, followed by centrifuging at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. Cleared lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C using anti-SstP1 antibodies bound to Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime, CN, P2089). After triple PBS washings, samples were eluted by boiling in SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 10 min. Immunoprecipitated proteins were resolved by 12% SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blot using both anti-SstP1 and anti-phospho-(Ser/Thr) antibodies (Abcam, USA, ab117253).

Western blot

The total proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE (12% for IP, 10% for aconitase assay, and GST-pull down), and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Vazyme Biotech, CN, E801-01) using ColorMixed Protein Marker 180 (ABClon, KR, RM19001) as a ladder. The membrane was blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat dry milk and incubated for 1.5 h. After washing with 1xPBST (Phosphate-Buffered saline pH 7.5 with 0.01% Tween-20), membranes were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000 dilution) for 1 h. Phosphorylation signals were visualized using an ECL Luminescent Substrate (Abbkine, CN, BMU102).

In vitro phosphorylation assay

To determine whether SstP1 is a substrate of STK, 5 μg purified SstP1 was incubated with 15 μg STK in phosphorylation buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 25 mM NaCl, 100 mM ATP, 1 mM DTT, pH 8.0) (Beyotime, CN, MgCl2-ST269, ATP-ST041, DTT-D7378) at 37°C for 2 h. Reactions with STK and SstP1 alone were individually used as controls. After incubation, 5x protein loading buffer (Beyotime, CN, P0280) was added, and the samples were boiled for 5 min. The phosphorylation modification and the phosphorylated site(s) in SstP1 were confirmed using mass spectrometry.

Verification of the phosphorylation sites

The mutant protein SstP1-T35A was cloned, expressed, and purified as described above, followed by an in vitro phosphorylation assay in comparison with the WT SstP1. The phosphorylations at Thr35 were confirmed by resolving the resultant mixture in 10% SDS-PAGE and proceeding to Western blot using anti-GST-tag (Proteintech Group, USA, 80,006–1-RR) and anti-phospho-(Ser/Thr) antibodies (Abcam, USA, ab117253).

GST pull-down assay

Purified GST-SstP1 was immobilized on glutathione-agarose beads (Beyotime, CN, P2258) by gentle agitation at 4 °C for 1 h. The resultant products were then incubated with the S. suis lysate under moderate agitation at 4 °C for 2 h to allow protein–protein interactions to occur. The beads were then subjected to triple PBS washings to remove unbound proteins and boiled for 10 min with loading buffer to elute the proteins. The eluents were collected and visualized on 10% SDS-PAGE, followed by silver staining using Fast Silver Stain Kit (Beyotime, CN, P0017S). Differential bands were excised and subjected to LC-MS/MS (liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry) analysis.

Purification of SufB and in vitro immunoprecipitation

The sufB gene was amplified and digested using restriction endonucleases HindIII and XhoI. The purified fragment was cloned into pET-28a (+) and transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) after sequence confirmation, and induced with 0.5 mM IPTG. His-tagged SufB was purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (Vazyme Biotech, CN, HP201-01) and validated with SDS-PAGE and Western blot (anti-His antibody, PTM BIO, CN, PTM-5614). In vitro immunoprecipitation was performed by incubating His-tagged SufB with GST-SstP1 (also with SstP1, which was subjected to in vitro phosphorylation by STK or control proteins) immobilized on glutathione-agarose beads (Beyotime, CN, P2258) for 2 h at 4 °C. Afterward, the beads were collected by centrifugating at 3000 rpm and subjected to triple PBS washings. The complexes were eluted by boiling in 2×SDS for 10 min, and separated by 10% SDS–PAGE, followed by Western blot using anti-His and anti-GST antibodies.

Construction of gene knockout, site-directed, and complementary mutants

The ΔsstP1 mutant was constructed by homologous recombination using the pSET4s thermosensitive suicide vector [36,37]. Upstream and downstream flanking regions of sstP1 were fused by overlap PCR and cloned into pSET4s. We electroporated the vector into the WT strain and selected double-crossover mutants at 37°C that were verified by PCR using flanking primers (Table 1). SstP1-T35A and SstP1-T35E strains were generated by constructing the recombinant shuttle vector pSET2. We amplified the sstP1 ORF to fuse with the previously reported impdh promoter, which was then ligated into a pSET2. Then pSET2-sstP1 and pSET2-sstP1-T35A/E (generated via site-directed mutagenesis by Sangon Biotech) were introduced into the ΔsstP1 mutants to generate site-directed and complemented mutants [36,37]. We used sstP1-targeted primers (Table 1) to verify our successful introductions (Fig S1(D-F)), followed by a qPCR confirming that adjacent genes were unaffected (Fig. S1 H). Site-directed mutants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. S1 I).

RNA extraction, RT-PCR, and qPCR

We isolated total RNA using the Bacterial RNA Isolation Kit (Omega, USA) and reverse transcribed it to cDNA with HiScript Q Select RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, qPCR was performed to verify the successful gene deletion or site-directed mutagenesis in respective strains using the 16S rRNA gene as a control to amplify in triplicates (Fig. S1 G and H). The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCT) method. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments.

Aconitase assay

Aconitase assays were performed by mixing 50 μg of bacterial lysate isolated from respective strains with 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, followed by adding 0.15 mM cis-aconitate (Macklin, CN, A903135) to initiate reactions at 25°C. Aconitase activity was determined by spectrophotometrically monitoring cis-aconitate depletion at 240 nm using a UV/visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Biomat 3S, USA) every 15 s for 30 min [38]. The absorbance values were plotted against reaction time to calculate aconitase activity based upon the linear portion of the curve in the initial 5 min. An extinction coefficient of 3,500 M−1cm−1 was used to calculate the rates.

Detection of growth characterizations

The overnight bacterial cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.6 and inoculated into fresh THB supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). In parallel, an iron limitation condition was induced by inoculating diluted strains into medium with 2,2’-Dipyridyl (DIP) (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany, D216305) at a final concentration of 5 mM. Bacterial cultures were then incubated at 37°C and 160 rpm for 10 h, along with hourly OD600 recording to characterize their growth profiles. Growth curves were plotted by GraphPad Prism 9 (La Jolla, CA, USA).

Environmental stress survival assays

Environmental stress survival assays were performed by challenging targeted strains, respectively, with hydrogen peroxide (10 mM H2O2, 30 min), nitric oxide (5 mM DETA NONOate, 30 min, MCE, HY-136278), acidic pH (5.0, 1 h), or heat stress (42°C, 1 h). After exposure, cultures were serially diluted and plated on THB agar to incubate in a 5% CO2 aerobic atmosphere at 37 °C for 12 h. CFUs were counted to assess their survival rates. Each treatment was performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Phagocytosis assay

Targeted strains were cultured to mid-log growth phase (OD600 of 0.6) and were harvested for phagocytosis assay. The assay was performed by infecting RAW 264.7 macrophages with harvested strains at a bacteria-to-cell ratio of 100:1 at 37 °C for 1 h. After triple PBS washings, the infected cells were continually incubated for 2 h in DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11,965,092) supplemented with gentamicin (100 μg/ml) mixed with penicillin (5 μg/ml). Subsequently, the cells were lysed and twofold-diluted with sterile deionized water to plate on THB agar in triplicates.

Mice infection experiment

We used female C57BL/6 mice (Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China) as animal infection models. To minimize external interferences, mice were kept in noise-control conditions, and one-third of the soiled bedding was retained when changing cages. Mice at the age of 7–8 weeks and with a weight of 18–20 g were selected and randomly assigned to each experiment (A total of 75 mice were included, 25 per experiment). Only the personnel responsible for data analysis were aware of the group allocations. Survival assay was conducted by inoculating with bacteria at a 2.5 × 108 colony-forming units (CFUs) dose. Clinical manifestations were recorded every 8 h until the completion of the infection experiments or upon the death of the individual mouse. Bacterial burden assay was performed by inoculating with 5 × 107 CFUs of bacteria. Mice were sacrificed with isoflurane at 24 h post-infection (hpi) for tissue collections. To quantify the bacterial burden in each organ, tissues were homogenized with sterile PBS and serially diluted to plate onto THB agar for CFUs counting. Pathological analysis was performed using the spleen by fixing in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, CN, P0099) for sectioning and staining with hematoxylin–eosin (HE). Microscopic lesion assessments were performed by evaluating five fields from each tissue section randomly and recorded as 0 (no lesions), 1 (minimal), 2 (mild), 3 (moderate), or 4 (severe) depending on the pathological conditions. The experimental animal protocol was reviewed and approved by the Southwest University Animal Ethics Committee (Approval Number: IACUC-20231215-02).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed on GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (La Jolla, CA, USA). Results are plotted as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three biological replicates. Inter-group differences were statistically compared using an unpaired t-test, repeated-measures ANOVA, log-rank t-test, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. The relative intensity of each band was normalized against the loading control for statistical analysis.

Results

STK phosphorylated SstP1 in S. suis

Our phosphoproteomics analysis [39] revealed a reduced phosphopeptide signal for SstP1 in the Δstk mutant compared to the wild-type (WT) S. suis (Figure 1(A)), which was confirmed by in vivo immunoprecipitation (IP) assay (Figure 1(D)). We then determined the phosphorylation at the Thr35 using mass spectrometry by incubating recombinant protein SstP1 with STK in phosphate buffer (Figure 1(B)). Western blot analysis revealed that the phosphorylation level of the phospho-ablative T35A mutant (SstP1-T35A) was substantially reduced compared to the WT SstP1 in vitro (Figure 1(C)), confirming that the Thr35 in SstP1 is a functional target site of STK-mediated phosphorylation.

SstP1 phosphorylated at Thr35 functions in the SUF pathway Fe-S cluster biosynthesis by interacting with SufB

Guided by the putative cysteine desulfurase property of SstP1, we identified SufB as a potential interactor via GST pull-down assay (Figure 2(A)), given that it is a core scaffold protein responsible for Fe-S cluster biogenesis through the SUF pathway. AlphaFold-Multimer simulation predicted a robust interface between the two, stabilized by hydrogen bonds at E433, E444, E453 of SufB and K23, R28, R44 of SstP1 (Figure 2(B)). The interaction was biochemically confirmed by an in vitro co-immunoprecipitation assay using His-tagged SufB (Figure 2(C)). To investigate whether this interaction is modulated by STK-mediated phosphorylation at Thr35, we performed a comparative binding assay. Our results showed that SstP1 phosphorylation significantly enhanced the affinity between SstP1 and SufB, indicated by phosphorylated SstP1 capturing a higher fraction of SufB compared to the unphosphorylated protein or the phospho-ablative T35A mutant (Figure 2(D)).

Considering that aconitase serves as a proximal indicator of Fe-S cluster assembly efficiency, we measured aconitase activity of different strains to assess the physiological importance of this interaction [40,41]. Since the SUF machinery is preferentially recruited for Fe-S cluster restoration under stress, our assays were conducted at the recovery phase (after 8 h DETA-NO stress exposure) [42–44]. Our results demonstrated that aconitase activity in the Δstk, ΔsstP1, and phospho-ablative T35A mutants was significantly decreased at 8 h (DETA-NO) and failed to recover by 24 h (REC) compared to untreated (UT) (Figure 2(E)). Conversely, phosphor-mimetic T35E mutant sustained robust enzymatic activity, mirroring the WT phenotype (Figure 2(E)). The overall enzymatic defects directly correlate with the impaired recovery kinetics observed in the mutant strains (Figure 2(E) and 3(B-D)). Notably, Western blot confirmed that aconitase protein abundance remained constant across different strains throughout the treatment, indicating that the observed loss of enzymatic activity was due to dysfunction of Fe-S cluster assembly rather than the altered protein expression (Figure 2(E)). Collectively, our results suggested that STK-mediated phosphorylation of SstP1 at Thr35 is essential for the functional integrity of the SUF-dependent Fe-S cluster biosynthetic pathway.

Phosphorylation of SstP1 enhances antioxidant defense

To elucidate the biological significance of STK-mediated SstP1 phosphorylation at Thr35 in S. suis, we characterized the growth profiles, stress tolerance, and phagocytosis evasion capacity. Under standard conditions, the ΔsstP1 and T35A mutants exhibited moderate growth defects (Figure 3(A)). However, these defects were significantly exacerbated under iron-depleted conditions, where both strains displayed severe growth inhibition compared to the WT, T35E, and CΔsstP1 strains (Figure 3(B)).

We further challenged strains with environmental stressors. Our results showed that ΔsstP1 and T35A mutants exhibited significantly reduced survival specifically under oxidative (H2O2) and nitrosative (DETA NONOate) stress (Figure 3(C,D)). In contrast, no significant differences were observed under thermal or acidic stress (Fig. S2 A, B), suggesting a specific role in antioxidant resistance.

Finally, we assessed bacterial capacity to evade macrophage-mediated phagocytosis. The intracellular survival assay in RAW 264.7 cells revealed significantly higher bacterial loads for the ΔsstP1 and T35A mutants compared to the WT, T35E, and CΔsstP1 mutants (Figure 3(E)). The results demonstrate that STK-dependent phosphorylation of SstP1 is a critical determinant of bacterial growth, environmental fitness, and immune evasion in S. suis.

The phosphorylation of SstP1 enhances S. suis virulence

To evaluate the biological contribution of STK-mediated phosphorylation of SstP1 in bacterial virulence, a mouse infection model was employed. Mice infected with the T35A or ΔsstP1 mutants exhibited significantly increased survival rates compared to those infected with the WT, T35E, and CΔsstP1 strains, which resulted in 100% mortality (Figure 4(A)). Bacterial burden analysis across different organs demonstrated that the bacterial loads in mice infected with the WT strain were significantly higher than those in mice infected with either the T35A or ΔsstP1 strains, indicating that the disruption of SstP1 phosphorylation enhances bacterial clearance from host tissues (Figure 4(B-F)). Histopathological examination further supported these findings. Spleens from WT-infected mice displayed marked pathological alterations, indicative of severe white pulp atrophy and pronounced extramedullary hematopoiesis (Figure 4H). Significant pathological changes were also observed in the spleens of SstP1-T35E-infected mice. In contrast, spleen tissues from mice infected with the SstP1-T35A or ΔsstP1 strains exhibited only mild histological changes (Figure 4(G,H)). Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrated that phosphorylation of SstP1 was critical for the full virulence of S. suis, contributing to tissue damage during bacterial infections.

Discussion

Fe-S clusters are ancient cofactors that are assembled and inserted into apoproteins to form fully functional Fe-S proteins. Given that they are highly sensitive to oxidants (e.g. O2−, H2O2) [45], the clusters once incorporated are generally protected by coordination with the polypeptide to sustain enzymatic activity in the proteins that depend on them [46]. However, under excessive oxidative exposure, the ISC system that serves as the main system to generate Fe-S clusters is impaired by disrupting the nascent clusters on the scaffold protein IscU [42,43], which in turn activates the SUF machinery to sustain Fe – S biogenesis under stress conditions [44]. Therefore, understanding SUF Fe-S synthesis machinery in pathogenic bacteria, which are often exposed to excessive oxidative stress during infection, may reveal novel therapeutic and preventive targets. In this study, we demonstrated that the SstP1 of S. suis is a substrate of STK and serves as a functional partner that directly interacts with the scaffold protein SufB to regulate the Fe-S cluster assembly through the SUF pathway. The fully functional activation of SstP1 requires STK-mediated phosphorylation specifically at the site of threonine 35 (Figure 1, 2).

The SUF Fe – S cluster biosynthesis requires cysteine desulfurase SufS and the SufBCD scaffold complex. Mobilized sulfur by SufS is transferred by sulfur carrier SufU to the SufBCD complex [18,47]. SstP1 has previously been reported to harbor a DUF1831 domain, with computational analyses suggesting a potential functional association with cysteine desulfurase activity in S. suis [48]. In the current study, GST pull-down and in vitro immunoprecipitation assay have demonstrated that SstP1 directly interacts with SufB (Figure 2(A,C)). A protein complex is formed with three hydrogen bonds at E433, E444, E453 of SufB with K23, R28, R44 of SstP1, which crossed the phosphorylation site at Thr35 of SstP1 (Figure 2(B)). To assess the consequence of this interaction, we measured the activity of 4Fe-4S dependent enzyme aconitase after 8 h exposure to nitric oxide and following 24 h recovery. A significant decrease in aconitase activity was observed in the WT strain and also in the ΔsstP1, T35A, and T35E mutants (Figure 2(E)). However, recovery of the enzymatic activity after 24 h was only detected in the WT and phospho-mimetic T35E strains (Figure 2(E)), suggesting phosphorylation of SstP1 supports SUF Fe-S cluster biogenesis through its interaction with SufB. Our results suggest that SufB requires STK-phosphorylated SstP1 as a partner protein prior to forming a fully functional SufBCD complex, indicating serving as a support of the SUF Fe-S cluster biogenesis in S. suis.

STK contributes to S. suis pathogenesis through phosphorylation of diverse substrates. The stk-deletion strains display reduced tolerance to environmental stressors such as thermal, acidic, and oxidative stresses, and show attenuated virulence [29,49]. More specifically, phosphorylation of GlmM at Ser101 is required for peptidoglycan synthesis [49]. The transcriptional regulator GntR, if phosphorylated at Ser41, acts as a nox expression repressor, leading to reduced clearance capacity of intracellular NADH and thus increased oxidative stress susceptibility [30]. In this study, deletion of sstP1 or disruption of its phosphorylation at Thr35 markedly impaired bacterial growth and cell infection (Figure 3(A and E)), and reduced their resistance to iron-limited conditions (Figure 3(B)) and oxidative stresses (Figure 3(C and D)). In contrast, no survival differences have been observed in any of the strains when challenged with heat or acid (Fig. S2 A, B). In a mouse infection model, both the ΔsstP1 and phospho-ablative T35A strains exhibited improved host survival and a lower bacterial burden across multiple host organs compared to the WT phosphomimetic T35E and CΔsstP1 strains (Figure 4(A and B)). Histopathology analysis of the spleen demonstrated profound tissue damage, characterized by white pulp atrophy (indicated by black arrow) in the WT, phosphomimetic T35E, and CΔsstP1 strains, suggesting an enhanced bacterial virulence (Figure 4(G and H)).

Collectively, our findings identify SstP1 as a novel STK substrate that plays a pivotal role in oxidative stress defense and virulence in S. suis. SstP1 with STK-mediated phosphorylation at Thr35 directly interacts with scaffold protein SufB, resulting in a hydrogen-bonded precursor complex. The protein interaction prior to the SufBCD formation ensures the Fe-S cluster biosynthesis through the SUF system, allowing the bacterium to withstand host-derived and environmental oxidative challenges. Loss of this modification significantly compromises oxidative stress resistance and virulence. Our findings link STK signaling to the SUF pathway, revealing a newly characterized mechanism by which STK mediates SUF-dependent Fe-S cluster biogenesis via SstP1. This STK-dependent pathway may serve as a potential target for antimicrobial prevention.
