Prevalence, barriers and determinants of exclusive breastfeeding among Palestinian mothers: a multicenter study
Authors: Sana’ Al-Aqqad, Lama Abu-Alrob, Lama Zakarneh, Anwar Eqelan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe World Health Organization recommends exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) for the first six months of an infant’s life to ensure optimal growth and health. Despite its well-established benefits, many mothers discontinue EBF early due to various barriers. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of EBF among Palestinian mothers, identify EBF barriers, and determine factors associated with maintaining EBF during the first six months.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in four Ministry of Health primary care clinics across the West Bank, Palestine. Mothers who visited the selected clinics to vaccinate their infants aged 6–12 months between April 15, 2023, and May 15, 2023, were interviewed face-to-face using a structured questionnaire. Logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with EBF. A p-value ≤ 0.05 is considered statistically significant.ResultsOf the 495 participating mothers, 38.2% reported exclusively breastfeeding for six months. The most common self-reported barrier was the perception of insufficient breast milk. Mothers with prior EBF experience were more than twice as likely to maintain EBF compared to those without such experience (AOR = 2.081; 95% CI: 1.094, 2.405). Working mothers had nearly 39% lower odds of maintaining EBF compared to non-working mothers (AOR = 0.610, 95% CI: 0.386–0.965), and pacifier use was associated with a 59.8% reduction in the odds of maintaining EBF (AOR = 0.402, 95% CI: 0.275–0.588).ConclusionThis study indicates a relatively low prevalence of EBF among Palestinian mothers. Employment and pacifier use were negatively associated with EBF practices, while prior experience significantly supports the continuation of EBF for up to six months. It is essential to emphasize the importance of breastfeeding education and support, especially for new mothers.Keywords: Barriers, Breastfeeding, Exclusive breastfeeding, Determinants, Prevalence

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF) strongly recommend that infants should be exclusively breastfed for the first 6 months of life [1]. Exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) offers numerous benefits to infants, including support for healthy brain development, improved eating habits, favorable weight gain, lower body mass index, reduced adiposity, and better cognitive development [2]. Moreover, strong evidence supports the protective effects of EBF in lowering the risk of gastrointestinal and respiratory infections [3]. Despite these benefits, global adherence to EBF remains below the WHO recommendations. In 2023, only 48% of infants worldwide were exclusively breastfed during the first six months, far behind the global target of 70% by 2030 [4].

Regional variations are noticeable in the Middle East, where the average EBF rate is around 20.5%, much lower than the global average [5]. EBF prevalence varies significantly across this area, ranging from 2% in Kuwait to 56.4% in Iran [5]. Studies have identified a declining trend in EBF rates over the last 30 years in Iraq, Libya, and Palestine [6]. Recent studies revealed that the prevalence of EBF among Jordanian mothers was 24% [7], while it was 29.9% among Syrian refugee mothers living in the Jordan community [8].

In the Palestinian context, UNICEF reported that while 96% of Palestinian mothers initially breastfeed their babies exclusively from birth, this percentage declines sharply over the following months. By the time infants reach six months of age, only 40% are still exclusively breastfed [9]. However, it’s important to note that population-based data on EBF practices in Palestine are lacking.

Previous studies in comparable socioeconomic settings identified several factors associated with successful EBF. These factors include place of residence (city or village), mother’s age and education, employment status, mode of delivery, number of children, night feeding, breastfeeding initiation, and maternal knowledge of EBF [5, 7, 8].

EBF barriers commonly cited across Middle Eastern countries include resuming work, perception of insufficient mother’s milk, cesarean section delivery, breast-related issues (such as cracked or sore nipples), lack of knowledge about EBF benefits, inadequate communication or recommendations from healthcare providers, lack of family support, negative societal influences, traditional practices, and lack of privacy in public and private spaces [5, 7, 8, 10–12].

To date, no studies have comprehensively examined the determinants of EBF and the barriers to its maintenance during the first six months of infant life in Palestine. Moreover, current data on EBF prevalence in the Palestinian context remain limited. This research gap is particularly significant given the unique cultural, social, political, and economic factors that may influence breastfeeding practices in the region.

The study aims to investigate the current prevalence of EBF among Palestinian mothers during the first six months of infant life, determine the sociodemographic and healthcare-related factors associated with EBF continuation, and identify self-reported barriers that prevent Palestinian mothers from continuing EBF for the recommended duration.

Subjects and methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across four primary healthcare clinics managed by the Ministry of Health (MOH) in the West Bank, Palestine, specifically in Jenin, Nablus, Hebron, and Ramallah. These clinics were selected strategically to ensure representation from the entire West Bank, including the northern, central, and southern regions.

Sampling and sample size calculation

The study employed convenient sampling, including all eligible mother-infant pairs who visited the clinics during the study period. The study targeted mothers attending the targeted clinics to vaccinate their infants, aged between six months and one year. Recruitment and interviews were conducted from April 15, 2023, to May 15, 2023.

The sample size was calculated using Epi Info™ software based on a 40% projected EBF prevalence with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, resulting in 369 participants. To increase power and account for probable non-response or missing data, the target sample size was increased by 10%, yielding a final sample of 406 mothers.

Data collection

Data was collected from mothers through face-to-face interviews conducted by uniformly trained midwives using a specially designed structured questionnaire. The midwives were trained through a standardized protocol covering interview techniques, informed consent, confidentiality, and bias reduction. The questionnaire comprised four sections and was developed based on previous literature to ensure the collection of all necessary information. The first section of the questionnaire collected demographic and socioeconomic data on the participating mothers and their infants (mothers’ age and education level, employment status, place of residence, infant weight and gender, time to initiate breastfeeding after delivery, and use of pacifiers during the first six months). The second section addressed the obstetrical and gynecological history of the mothers, including parity, antenatal follow-up, mode of delivery, number of children, antenatal care counseling, and prenatal care counseling. The third section focused on EBF knowledge and practice, including infants’ feeding patterns, covering topics such as duration of EBF (for one, three, and six months), knowledge or information about EBF, sources of information about EBF, support from husbands toward EBF, and feeding patterns following the cessation of EBF. The mother will also be asked if she has previous experience with EBF (defined as the mother’s history of having exclusively breastfed a child in the past, no matter how long the duration lasted). The final section of the interview gathered information on the barriers to implementing and maintaining EBF for six months. Mothers who did not practice or maintain successful EBF were provided with a checklist of 20 suggested EBF barriers, of which the majority have already been mentioned in previous literature. The face and content validity of the questionnaire were evaluated by five maternal and child health specialists, including midwives. The questionnaire’s reliability (for the barriers section) was verified by Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.806.

EBF was defined as no other food or drink, minerals, or water, except breast milk (including milk expressed or from a wet nurse) for the first 6 months of life, except rehydration solution, drops, and syrups such as vitamins, minerals, and medicines [1].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Mothers with infants six months to one year of age, giving birth to a single healthy full-term newborn infant via vaginal delivery or cesarean section, and mothers who agree to participate in the study are included and interviewed. The current study excluded mothers who were < 18 years old, with preterm babies, had multiple gestations, contraindications to breastfeeding (e.g.: taking medications that interfere with breastfeeding or affect body weight), and mothers having babies with major birth defects (e.g., congenital or genetic disorders, chronic medical disorders, or physical malformations) that may interfere with feeding patterns. Additionally, mothers whose infants were admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit after birth were excluded from the study.

Ethical issues

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Nablus University for Vocational and Technical Education approved this study (Approval ID: ISC/MID.APRIL.2023/2). The Palestinian MOH granted permission to visit the specified primary health clinics. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and all participating mothers signed an informed consent form before their involvement. The participant’s information and data were kept confidential and utilized exclusively for scientific and research purposes.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 28. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants and the prevalence of EBF. The categorical data was in the form of frequency distributions and percentages. The dependent variable was EBF for six months (yes/no). To assess associations between demographic variables and EBF for up to six months, the chi-square test (χ²) was employed. Every variable linked to EBF in the univariate analyses was included in the logistic regression. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to account for potential confounding variables. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our findings by re-categorizing key variables, excluding mothers under 20 years, and performing subgroup analyses. Alternative model specifications were also tested. The associations between key predictors and exclusive breastfeeding remained consistent, supporting the stability and reliability of the main findings.

EBF knowledge and feeding practice after EBF discontinuation

Table 2 reveals that only 61.4% of mothers indicated that they had learned about EBF while pregnant. The primary source of this information was their private doctors (27.7%), followed by mothers reading for themselves (14.1%) and prenatal clinics (13.1%). After childbirth, 81.2% received additional information, with family and friends as the main source (29.1%).

Following the discontinuation of EBF, the most common feeding method was formula only, adopted by 40.4% of mothers. This was followed by a combination of formula and breast milk, as reported among 35.4% of mothers, and then 9.9% reported a combination of breast milk and soft food (Table 2).

The multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 5) identified several significant determinants of EBF up to 6 months. Working mothers had 39% lower odds of maintaining EBF compared to non-working mothers (AOR = 0.610, 95% CI: 0.386–0.965), indicating that employment may present a substantial obstacle to continued breastfeeding. There was a significant negative association (p < 0.01) between pacifier usage and EBF, with mothers who used pacifiers having 59.8% lower odds of maintaining EBF (AOR = 0.402, 95% CI: 0.275–0.588). Notably, among mothers who had previously practiced EBF, the likelihood of continuing EBF for six months was substantially higher (2.081 times) than for mothers who had never practiced EBF AOR = 2.081; 95% CI: 1.094–2.405; p < 0.001), suggesting a strong positive influence of previous EBF experience on current practices. However, the mode of delivery did not show any significant variation in maintaining EBF in the multivariate analysis (p = 0.328, AOR = 1.660, 95% CI: 0.600-4.619).

Discussion

The prevalence of EBF for six months postpartum in Palestine was relatively low (38.2%). Despite this low level, the prevalence was higher than in other Arab countries. For example, in Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of mothers practicing EBF was 27.6% in 2019 [13] and 22% in 2021 [14]. In Lebanon, it was reported as 12.3% [15], while in Qatar it was 24.3% [16]. In Jordan, the rates were 33% in 2021 [17] but declined to 24% in 2024. In the United Arab Emirates, the prevalence was 24.4% [18]. Additionally, the EBF rate observed in our study was higher than the regional average of 30.9%, as reported in a review of the Eastern Mediterranean countries [6].

The present study identified the main barriers to EBF in Palestine. The primary barrier, reported by two-thirds of mothers, was the perception that their infants were not receiving sufficient breast milk. This finding aligns with previous studies from Qatar [16], Jordan [8, 17], Oman [19], and Saudi Arabia [14, 20]. Due to this belief, the mothers began supplementing or bottle-feeding their infants before the sixth month to meet their perceived needs. Mothers often interpret infant behaviors such as frequent crying, fussiness, feeding difficulties, or insufficient weight gain as signs of inadequate milk supply [21–23]. Insufficient maternal knowledge and breastfeeding education may exacerbate these perceptions [24], highlighting the need for robust antenatal and postnatal breastfeeding counseling.

The second barrier identified in this study was frequent infant crying, which was a common reason for early cessation of EBF. Similarly, constant infant crying was commonly cited as a reason for early EBF cessation in Qatar [19] and Jordan [17]. Mothers may incorrectly attribute infant crying to hunger or poor milk quality, which leads to premature supplementation. This misconception underlines the critical importance of educating mothers about normal infant behaviors during breastfeeding.

The multivariate analysis identified three main determinants associated with maintaining EBF: maternal employment, previous EBF experience, and pacifier use. The working mothers were significantly less likely to maintain EBF. This is consistent with the findings of earlier studies in Jerusalem Governorate (under Palestinian Authority) [25], Jordan [17], Qatar [16], Saudi Arabia [20], Egypt [26], Iran [27], and Malaysia [28]. A recent study in the Jerusalem Governorate found that stay-at-home mothers were 2.3 times more likely to achieve successful EBF than mothers with full- or part-time jobs [25]. Working mothers often abandon EBF due to a variety of reasons, including work policies on breastfeeding [20], maternal fatigue, weaning in preparation for returning to work, and the difficulty of balancing the demands of employment and breastfeeding [29]. It is important to note that, according to Palestinian labor law, working mothers are given only 90 days of paid maternity leave. In addition, facilitating breastfeeding at the workplace is typically neither flexible nor available.

A mother’s previous experience with EBF in earlier children was associated with greater success in maintaining EBF in subsequent children. This finding is consistent with several studies conducted on Jordanian mothers [17], Syrian refugee mothers living in Jordan [8], Indonesia [30], and Saudi Arabia [31], which have shown that prior experience with EBF helps maintain the practice in subsequent children. It was previously reported that a mother’s likelihood of continuing EBF increased by 3.3 times if she had previously experienced EBF [30]. This could happen when mothers feel more capable of handling past challenges or are motivated to continue EBF in the future. Additionally, prior experience with EBF may boost a mother’s confidence and attitude toward breastfeeding, as they are more knowledgeable and familiar with the process [32].

In the current study, the infants who used pacifiers were less likely to be exclusively breastfed compared to those who did not use pacifiers. The use of pacifiers has been reported to have a negative association with EBF in countries such as Saudi Arabia [33] and Brazil [34]. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that, in observational studies, there was a positive correlation between pacifier use and the disruption of EBF; however, this correlation was not observed in randomized controlled trials [35]. Pacifier use can disrupt effective breastfeeding by causing infant fatigue, reducing nursing frequency, and ultimately disrupting EBF [36]. This emphasizes the need for targeted education for mothers on the potential risks of early pacifier introduction during the critical breastfeeding period.

Although vaginal delivery was associated with higher EBF rates in a univariate analysis, this association was not significant in a multivariate analysis. Mothers undergoing cesarean sections often experience a longer recovery period and surgery-related complications (e.g., pain and discomfort), which delays the initiation of breastfeeding during the early postpartum days [37]. As a result, bottle feeding may be introduced more readily in these cases, impacting the establishment of EBF. However, this result contrasts with the findings of other studies where vaginal delivery facilitated early breastfeeding initiation and successful EBF maintenance [11, 12, 37]. Differences in hospital policies, postpartum support, and sample characteristics across different settings may explain this discrepancy and warrant further investigation.

The current study has some limitations that are worth mentioning here. First, the reliance on self-reported data could introduce potential recall bias. To mitigate this limitation, the recall period was limited to 6–12 months postpartum. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Third, some important potential confounding factors, such as mental health status, postpartum depression, and cultural practices influencing infant feeding decisions, were not assessed. Fourth, information on breastfeeding techniques among the participating mothers was not evaluated. Finally, as the sample was taken from selected Ministry of Health clinics, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Palestinian mothers, particularly those accessing private or refugee healthcare services.

Conclusion

Compared to the global average of 48%, Palestinian mothers demonstrated a relatively low prevalence of EBF. Among the factors examined, maternal employment status, previous experience with EBF, and pacifier use were significantly associated with EBF practices. The most commonly reported barrier to maintaining EBF was the perception of insufficient breast milk supply, leading many mothers to introduce supplementary feeding before six months. Strengthening laws, regulations, and policies that promote and support EBF through education, health promotion, and workplace accommodations are essential. Special attention should be given to supporting working mothers and educating caregivers on the potential impact of early pacifier use on breastfeeding continuation.

Implications for practice

The results of our study provide crucial, up-to-date information on EBF practices among Palestinian mothers. The findings will contribute to a better understanding of the challenges faced by Palestinian mothers in maintaining EBF. They highlight the need for targeted interventions addressing perceived milk insufficiency, workplace barriers, and pacifier use. They may inform targeted interventions and policies to support and promote EBF in the Palestinian context.

The findings of this study provide crucial, up-to-date insights into exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) practices among Palestinian mothers. They highlight the need for targeted interventions that strengthen breastfeeding education during antenatal and postnatal care to address misconceptions around milk insufficiency. Additionally, policies that extend maternity leave beyond 90 days and mandate workplace support measures, such as providing lactation rooms and flexible return-to-work arrangements, are urgently needed to support working mothers in maintaining EBF for six months. Public health interventions should also focus on discouraging early pacifier use and supporting first-time mothers through targeted education and peer mentorship programs.
