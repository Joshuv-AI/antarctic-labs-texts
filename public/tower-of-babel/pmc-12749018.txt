Water-induced damage mechanism and dual-radius grouting control of weakly cemented argillaceous roadways in Western China
Authors: Zaijiang Yu, Zhijiang Lun, Zizheng Zhang, Weiguang Zhang, Shuai Wang, Leilei Zhao, Shuaigang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe weakly cemented argillaceous surrounding rock (WSR) presents significant challenges in underground roadways in Western China, characterized by water-induced softening, large deformations, and difficult stability control. This study systematically investigates the mechanism of water-induced deterioration and the corresponding control technology for roadways with WSR through laboratory experiments, numerical simulations, and field monitoring. Experimental results highlight the critical role of moisture content and confining pressure in dictating the strength and deformation response of WSR. UDEC Trigon simulations elucidate the evolution of fracture propagation and plastic zones under hydro-mechanical coupling, showing that the total damage degree in the roof increases dramatically from 48% to 97% as the water content rises from 2% to 12%, with a transition from a symmetrical to an asymmetrical failure pattern. To address this, a control technology using combined deep and shallow grouting reinforcement is proposed. COMSOL-based simulations optimize the grouting parameters, determining that the optimal grouting pressure is 1–2 MPa for shallow holes and 3–4 MPa for deep holes, with injection times of 6–8 min yielding diffusion radii of 1.5–2.0 m and 2.5–3.0 m, respectively. Field applications at the tailgate of panel 1801 in Dananhu No. 5 Mine confirm that this method successfully mitigates roadway deformation and secures prolonged stability. The findings provide both theoretical insights and a practical methodology for supporting roadways with WSR under challenging hydrogeological conditions.Keywords: Weakly cemented argillaceous surrounding rocks (WSR), Water-induced deterioration, Stability mechanism, Control technologySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With the gradual depletion of coal resources in eastern China, coal mining activities are increasingly concentrated in the western region, which is endowed with abundant coal reserves. Influenced by unique lithological conditions, geological structures, and sedimentary history, this area features extensive weakly cemented coal-bearing strata from the Mesozoic Jurassic and Cretaceous periods, classified mainly into argillaceous, calcareous, and siliceous cementation types1,2. Among these, weakly cemented clayey rock masses are highly sensitive to water. The cementing material contains clay minerals such as kaolinite, illite, montmorillonite, and mixed-layer structures. They easily swell after absorbing water, and once in contact with moisture, their physical structure changes significantly, leading to disintegration, a sharp decrease in mechanical strength, low residual strength, and noticeable deformation. Weakly cemented rock masses bonded by clay-based materials exhibit notable characteristics such as strength degradation and structural disintegration. Roadway excavation in weakly cemented muddy surrounding rock have poor self-supporting capacity, large deformations, long-lasting deformation periods, and severe roadway deformation damage. This poses a significant challenge to maintaining tunnel stability and directly threatens the safety and production goals of coal mines in western China3–7.

Roadways excavated in soft rock formations are highly prone to deformation and failure both during and after construction8–10, with underlying mechanisms being complex due to multiple influencing factors11. To improve prediction of squeezing ground conditions in soft rocks, Akbariforouz et al. [6] established predictive models through both binary and multi-class regression analysis. Li et al. 12focusing on roadways in water-rich strata, uncovered their failure mechanisms and consequently advocated for a synergistic support system comprising grouting, anchor cables, and shotcrete. To evaluate the behavior of soft rock under humid conditions, Wang et al13. implemented a coupled numerical simulation that integrates moisture transport with mechanical damage, underscoring water content as a pivotal factor governing stability. Meanwhile, Meng et al14. provided insights into the deformation processes of deep soft rock roadways subjected to dynamic pressures, forming a theoretical foundation for support design in such settings. Zhao et al15. identified the detrimental impact of water saturation on weakly cemented sandstone, noting that it compromises particle bonding, elevates pore pressure, and deteriorates the microstructure. These effects collectively result in a marked reduction in both shear strength and the deformation modulus, thereby promoting instability during excavation. Furthermore, Cao et al16. systematically explored and analyzed the diffusion patterns of grouting materials under different working conditions. Liu et al17. highlighted the significant role of principal stress orientation on the mechanical response. Their findings indicate that varying the direction angle from 0° to 45° leads to an increase in the major principal strain, a decrease in the intermediate and minor principal strains, a lower peak stress, and the initiation of shear cracks. They also achieved a successful modification of the Lade–Duncan criterion to account for the effects of both stress direction and magnitude.

Numerical modeling serves as an indispensable tool for elucidating the failure behavior of weakly cemented soft rock roadways, while also offering a scientific foundation for the optimization and performance assessment of support schemes18–20. Using ABAQUS-based numerical simulation, Kang et al21. explored a phased control method for support structures in aquiferous weakly cemented sandstone formations. By modeling bolt yield behavior through adjustments in material properties, they achieved notable reductions in surrounding rock deformation (by 16.1%) and principal stresses (by up to 88.9%) compared with conventional anchor-mesh support systems. Through coupled FLAC3D and COMSOL Multiphysics simulations, Li et al. 22 analyzed the stress state of coal pillar areas under different collapse conditions after the upper layer of the mined-out area was excavated and established a mechanical model of coal pillars within sections of the working face. Tian et al. 23analyzed weakly cemented argillaceous surrounding rock in roadways located in a strongly weathered oxidation zone, identifying pronounced deformation and widespread plastic zone expansion. Fan et al24. conducted FLAC simulations to assess the effect of coal pillar dimensions on plastic zone extent and stress evolution in such roadways, determining that a 40-m pillar width effectively alleviates asymmetric deformation and limits mining-induced disturbance.

To investigate the underlying causes of deformation and failure in weakly cemented soft rock roadways, Li et al25. attributed the severe instability to the synergistic influence of rock softening and swelling. They validated an integrated support approach involving grouting, anchor cables with mesh, and an inverted arch structure, which resulted in a displacement control efficiency of 77.1% to 92.0% and a shrinkage in the extent of the plastic region by 18.7% to 72.4%. Gao et al26. analyzed how water seepage influences the deformation process, indicating that excavation and moisture ingress cause a decline in pre-tension, material weakening, and considerable convergence. Sun et al27. systematically revealed the evolution pattern of underlying stratum fractures under repeated mining controlled by multiple key strata, clarified the key controlling role of cumulative damage in the overlying strata during close-distance repeated coal seam mining, and the superimposed effect of mining-induced stress fields on fracture morphology. Cao et al28. analyzed the relationship between energy accumulation, dissipation, and the energy balance equation by establishing a dual-body mechanical model composed of a thick hard roof and cracked coal slabs. Furthermore, the dynamic instability mechanism of the cracked coal slabs was explored using the fold catastrophe theory. Zhang et al29. introduced an elastic-softening-fracturing constitutive model incorporating both strength loss and shear dilation. Their simulations indicated that higher initial cohesion and internal friction angle contribute to a nonlinear contraction of the plastic zone. Based on these findings, a composite support method combining anchored mesh, cables, and grouting was put forward, demonstrating clear benefits in maintaining the integrity of deep soft rock excavations under weak cementation.

In summary, significant research efforts have been devoted to the distinctive engineering geological conditions presented by weakly cemented strata30–32. Previous work has extensively explored the fundamental characteristics, failure modes, and stabilization approaches relevant to such rock masses, providing an important knowledge base for understanding the behavior and control of roadways in argillaceous weakly cemented environments. Despite these advances, critical research gaps remain. In response to the practical challenges associated with controlling large-scale convergence in roadways excavated in clay-rich weakly cemented formations, this study takes such rock as its focus. Through an integrated methodology incorporating laboratory experiments, numerical modeling, and field validation, we investigate the coupled hydro-mechanical response of the rock and the associated evolution of roadway damage. A coordinated deep and shallow grouting technique is proposed to enhance the stability of these roadways. The specific research objectives are as follows:

Strength and deformation characteristics of WSR

Specimen Preparation and hydro-mechanical testing scheme

The WSR samples utilized in this study were obtained from the tailgate of the 1801 working face at Dananhu No. 5 Mine, operated by Xuzhou Coal Mining Group Hami Energy Co., Ltd., located within the widely distributed Jurassic weakly cemented formation. To minimize sample disturbance and limit moisture uptake during coring, a two-phase sampling methodology was employed. This involved first extracting large-diameter core samples on-site, which were then transported to the laboratory and precision-machined into cylindrical specimens of the prescribed geometric specification. All specimen end faces were finely ground and polished to meet standardized testing requirements.

A series of uniaxial and triaxial compression tests were conducted to systematically evaluate the mechanical behavior of the WSR specimens. The experiments were carried out on an electro-hydraulic servo-controlled testing system designed for rock mechanics applications. To investigate the influence of moisture, natural specimens were initially oven-dried and then conditioned to target water contents (2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, 10%, and 12%) through a controlled free-immersion process. During saturation, each sample was wrapped with perforated plastic film to prevent structural disintegration. Triaxial tests were performed under confining pressures set at 2 MPa, 4 MPa, and 6 MPa to replicate representative in-situ stress states. All tests employed a displacement-controlled loading scheme in the axial direction. The acquired experimental data encompassed the complete stress-strain response, key strength characteristics, deformation parameters, and the progression of specimen failure.

Experimental results clearly indicate that water content plays a critical role in governing the compressive strength of WSR. At 2% water content, the material reaches its maximum compressive strength of 14.13 MPa. However, further hydration leads to a monotonic reduction in strength: values decrease to 12.09 MPa at 4%, 9.97 MPa at 6%, 8.06 MPa at 8%, and 3.62 MPa at 10%, ultimately reaching the lowest value of 2.71 MPa at 12% moisture content. These findings establish a definitive pattern of mechanical strength deterioration with increasing hydration levels in WSR.

Examination of the triaxial compression tests reveals distinct mechanical responses under hydrous conditions:

(1) Under constant confining pressure, the mechanical strength of WSR, including both its peak and residual values—systematically decreases with increasing water content. The elastic modulus is also moisture-sensitive, exhibiting a continuous decline, whereas the axial strain at peak strength rises. These observations verify that water ingress deteriorates both the load-bearing capacity and deformation resistance of the rock, while also amplifying irreversible deformation. At a fixed confining pressure, elevated moisture levels induce an earlier yield transition, marked by a lower elastic limit and more pronounced plastic deformation, though the residual strength eventually converges to a low constant value.

(2) With rising water content, the dominant failure mechanism evolves from localized brittle fracturing to distributed ductile response, accompanied by a more protracted failure process. Measured strength data illustrate a continuous reduction: the compressive strength falls from 19.53 MPa at 2% water content to 18.32 MPa (4%), 15.21 MPa (6%), and 13.42 MPa (8%), before a pronounced decline to 6.51 MPa at 10%, and finally a minimum of 5.73 MPa at 12% saturation. This progressive loss of strength provides clear evidence of the pronounced hydro-mechanical weakening in weakly cemented rock under confining stress.

At this moisture level, a higher confining stress leads to a notable enhancement in both the load-bearing capacity and material stiffness, as evidenced by the elevated peak stress and steeper initial slope of the curves. Comprehensive analysis of influencing factors

Based on the experimental results presented in Sect. 2.2 to 2.4, the following key observations are summarized regarding the mechanical behavior of WSR under varying moisture contents and confining pressures:

(1) Under conditions of low moisture content and low confining pressure, the mechanical response of WSR specimens during loading progresses through four characteristic phases: Initial compaction, marked by the gradual closure of internal fractures and pores under axial load, producing initial nonlinear deformation; Elastic deformation phase, exhibiting a predominantly linear stress-strain relationship; Yield and failure stage, where microcrack initiation and propagation lead to mixed elastic-plastic deformation. The flattening curve slope indicates decelerating stress growth accompanied by rapid strain accumulation until peak strength is attained; Post-peak phase, during which the bearing capacity diminishes abruptly, yet a certain level of post-failure strength is retained due to frictional resistance between fractured rock fragments. With continuing deformation, the stress reduction rate gradually decreases until stabilizing at the residual strength level.

(2) With simultaneous increases in both confining pressure and moisture content, WSR specimens show a transition in failure mechanism from brittle fracture to ductile failure, accompanied by increasingly evident time-dependent deformation characteristics.

(3) When the water content is held constant, an increase in confining stress results in a significant enhancement of the peak strength and a steeper initial slope in the stress–strain relationship, indicating improved resistance to deformation and a higher elastic modulus. The mechanical response also transitions toward more evident strain-hardening behavior and a greater capacity to retain load-bearing capacity after the peak stress is reached.

(4) Under fixed confining pressure, the introduction of moisture causes a consistent reduction in compressive strength, post-failure load retention, and structural stiffness of the WSR. Concurrently, the axial deformation necessary to attain peak stress shows a progressive increase.

These phenomena confirm that water saturation not only weakens the rock’s strength and stiffness but also promotes more considerable permanent deformation. At elevated moisture levels, the transition from elastic to plastic behavior occurs earlier, manifested through lowered elastic limit strength and dramatically increased plastic flow, even as the residual strength eventually stabilizes despite significant stress deterioration.

Numerical model configuration

A two-dimensional simulation utilizing the Universal Distinct Element Code (UDEC) was developed to analyze the hydro-mechanical response and damage evolution of WSR roadways (Fig. 5). Compared with general continuum mechanics methods such as FLAC/FLAC3D, UDEC describes the two fundamental components of rock mass, rock blocks and joints, using continuum mechanics laws and contact laws, respectively. An individual continuum can be treated as an independent object during mechanical analysis and interact with other continua through contacts, allowing a more realistic representation of the stress and deformation of roadways. The Trigon method uses the Voronoi polygon division technique to divide the region into multiple triangular blocks, better reflecting the failure characteristics of coal and rock masses. The modeled domain measures 240 m in width and 80 m in height. To optimize computational performance, the region of interest was discretized using the Trigon approach in UDEC, which subdivides the medium into triangular blocks averaging 0.2 m per edge. Coal seams were represented by polygonal Trigon blocks of about 0.5 m, while the outer coal-bearing strata were modeled using progressively larger blocks, with typical sizes of 1.0 m and 2.0 m, respectively. Displacement constraints were applied to the bottom (vertical) and two side (horizontal) boundaries. The overlying strata at a burial depth of 220 m were represented by a vertical stress of 5.5 MPa applied to the upper boundary, with the horizontal stress set at 1.2 times the vertical stress. The input mechanical parameters for the surrounding rock mass were determined from the uniaxial compression tests summarized in the preceding section.

Evolution of plastic zones in surrounding rock under hydrological conditions

The failure pattern observed in WSR surrounded roadways represents an external manifestation of the internal stress state within the rock mass, which itself fundamentally drives roadway instability. WSR exhibits complex elastoplastic mechanical behavior. In its original state, the transition of the rock mass from elastic response to ultimate failure is invariably preceded by a critical stage of plastic deformation. It should be emphasized that although plastic yielding does not equate to immediate failure, the yielded rock mass continues to deform under sustained high stress. This ongoing deformation involves the separation and sliding of structural planes, the propagation of existing fractures, and the initiation of new cracks. These mechanisms collectively lead to the pronounced deformation and failure characteristic of WSR roadways. Therefore, the plastic state acts as a crucial intermediary linking the stress condition of the rock mass to its eventual failure mode. The expansion and evolution of plastic zones provide key insights into the failure mechanisms that ultimately develop in the roadway.

From the theoretical framework of plastic zone analysis, further investigation into roadway failure mechanisms reveals that the deformation and failure processes of WSR are intrinsically connected to the formation, development, and mechanical response of rock masses within these plastic regions. The evolution of roadway failure modes correlates strongly with dynamic changes in the plastic zones of WSR, indicating a fundamental interdependence between these phenomena. Figure 8 presents numerical simulation results illustrating the evolutionary distribution characteristics of plastic zones in WSR under hydrological conditions.

Evolution of plastic zones in WSR roadway under different moisture contents.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, as the water content of the WSR rises from 2% to 6%, the plastic zone surrounding the roadway progressively enlarges, showing marked increases in both depth and area. The most prominent expansion occurs in the roof section. Specifically, at 2% water content, the plastic zone depth in the roof measures 1.8 m; when moisture levels reach 4% and 8%, this depth extends to 3–4 m. Under 6% water content conditions, plastic deformation propagates into the overlying rock strata above the WSR layer. As these superior WSR layers undergo plastic failure, a detachment layer forms at their interface. Coupled with moisture-induced weakening of the rock mass, this mechanism can trigger large-scale collapse of the overlying strata above the roadway roof, creating conditions conducive to roof collapse incidents.

The increase in water content not only enlarges the plastic zone depth on both sides of the roadway from 2.7 m to 4.5 m but also leads to a significant expansion of the plastic range at the corner zones. Furthermore, it fundamentally alters the failure symmetry. At a low water content of 2%, the plastic zones in the roof and floor are comparable in size and roughly symmetrical. As hydration progresses, however, the plastic zone develops more prominently in the roof, resulting in a distinctly asymmetrical pattern that intensifies with increasing moisture levels.

Grouting reinforcement technology for shallow and deep boreholes in roadway with WSR

Principle of grouting reinforcement technology for deep and shallow holes

The efficacy of rock bolt and cable support systems in roadway engineering is largely governed by the geological and mechanical characteristics of WSR. The abundant joints and fractures in such rock masses severely undermine anchorage reliability. When subjected to substantial surrounding rock convergence, the load-bearing performance of bolts and cables degrades markedly, often resulting in support failure and accelerated deformation38. To address these challenges, a combined deep and shallow grouting strategy has been developed, tailored to the distinct mechanical and structural responses of rock at different depths. By optimizing grouting parameters and deploying boreholes in both deep and shallow rock layers, this approach leverages the rapid setting and strong adhesion of low-pressure shallow grouting, along with the penetrative capability of high-pressure grouting in deeper formations. As a result, the grouting process enhances the overall mechanical properties of the rock mass, establishes reliable anchoring foundations for shallow bolts and deep cables, reinforces active support capacity, improves the structural bearing performance of the support system, and ultimately achieves stability control in WSR roadways. The underlying mechanism of the combined deep and shallow grouting approach is described as follows:

(1) Shallow low-pressure permeation grouting.

This method enables grout to effectively penetrate and seal near-surface fractures via compaction and permeation mechanisms, thereby improving the structural coherence of the shallow rock mass. As the slurry solidifies, it binds the fragmented near-field rock into a continuous, high-strength composite. This process not only restores the bearing capability of the superficial zone but also strengthens the fractured region, allowing it to withstand the elevated pressures required for subsequent deep grouting operations. Due to the relatively large cracks near the surface, there is a problem of slurry leakage. The low-pressure grouting method can control the spread of the slurry, thereby reducing the amount of leakage.

(2) Deep high-pressure fracture grouting.

For the consolidation of deeper rock formations, a high-pressure fracture grouting technique is applied. The process propels grout over greater distances into the rock matrix, mitigating fracture concentration and establishing a consolidated, high-strength reinforcement body. This is achieved through a combination of mechanical keying and physicochemical bonding between the grout and the rock fractures. The fractures in the deep surrounding rock are relatively small, and increasing the grouting pressure through high-pressure grouting can enhance the diffusion radius of the grout.

Determination of grouting parameters for roadway with WSR

The core design parameters for grouting include: selection of grouting materials, injection pressure control, grout volume, prediction of diffusion radius, assessment of the consolidated rock mass’s mechanical behavior, and layout of grouting holes. The mechanical performance after grout solidification is intrinsically linked to the materials chosen for reinforcing the roadway surrounding rock. To define the key technical parameters for the grouting procedure, COMSOL Multiphysics was utilized to investigate the flow and diffusion process of grout within WSR roadways. The following assumptions were applied in the COMSOL simulation setup:

(1) The grout initially enters the annular gap between the grouting pipe and the borehole wall through the designated outlet. Infiltration and diffusion into the surrounding rock mass commence only after this annular space is fully occupied.

(2) The selected grouting slurry is modeled as a Newtonian fluid, and its flow and migration through the surrounding rock are assumed to strictly adhere to Darcy’s law.

(3) Throughout the simulation, only the borehole wall is considered a permeable boundary, while the weakly cemented rock mass itself and the outer model boundaries are defined as impermeable.

(4) The fracture distribution within the WSR is presumed to be relatively uniform, allowing the rock mass to be approximated as a quasi-continuous or continuous medium.

(1) Grouting materials.

High water speed setting materials are composed of two primary components: Component A is made by mixing sulfoaluminate cement clinker, retarder, and an appropriate amount of suspension agent; Component B consists of gypsum, lime, suspension agent, and rapid-setting early-strength agent. These materials feature rapid setting, a high water-cement ratio, excellent flowability and permeability, and ensure a 100% stone formation rate and no dewatering phenomenon in high water-cement ratio environments. They also possess good plasticity, allowing them to adapt to the deformation of surrounding rock, and exhibit slight expansion, ensuring effective filling. When the water-cement ratio is set at 1.5, the solidification strength of the high water speed setting material can reach 9.5 ~ 14 MPa, and it maintains the characteristic of no dewatering under these conditions, achieving a 100% stone formation effect41–44. In grouting applications in roadway with WSR, these materials prevent issues such as mudification or expansion, making them ideal for grouting.

(2) Grouting pressure.

Grouting pressure serves as the primary driving force for slurry propagation through fractures in the surrounding rock, with its magnitude being affected by multiple variables including grout properties, grouting methodology, and rock mass porosity. Insufficient grouting pressure results in a restricted effective diffusion radius due to progressive pressure dissipation during permeation, thereby compromising the overall grouting efficacy. Conversely, excessively high pressure risks surpassing the shear and tensile strength thresholds of the surrounding rock, potentially initiating new fractures and damaging the inherent rock structure.

The efficiency of slurry penetration and diffusion is intrinsically linked to the applied grouting pressure. To optimize grout propagation within the fracture network of the surrounding rock, this study examines slurry diffusion patterns in the 1801 tailgate of the Dananhu No. 5 Mine under grouting pressures of 1 MPa, 2 MPa, 3 MPa, 4 MPa, and 5 MPa. Using a constant grouting duration of 10 min, the spatial distribution of slurry pressure within the rock mass serves as an indicator of diffusion extent. The grouting process is deemed to have terminated when the slurry pressure decays to match the surrounding rock’s hydrostatic pressure. To quantitatively assess the specific influence of grouting pressure on slurry propagation, this research measures and compares the areas of slurry diffusion zones under different pressure conditions, thereby establishing the relationship between grouting pressure and diffusion radius. The corresponding results are presented in Fig. 9.

Grouting pressure versus slurry diffusion radius in a WSR roadway.

The grouting pressure serves as a key factor governing the grout diffusion radius, demonstrating a positive correlation between the applied pressure and the extent of slurry spread. As pressure is applied, slurry propagation terminates at a distance of 0.98 m from the borehole wall, corresponding to a diffusion radius of 0.98 m. This represents a pronounced expansion compared to the case under zero grouting pressure, with a growth rate of 97.8%. When the grouting pressure is increased to 2 MPa, the diffusion radius rises markedly to 1.35 m, reflecting a 38.1% increase over the value observed at 1 MPa. As the pressure is elevated further from 2 MPa to 3 MPa, the growth of the diffusion radius shows clear deceleration, increasing only from 1.35 m to 1.51 m, which corresponds to a growth rate of 11.8%—a reduction of 68.9% compared to the previous rapid-increase phase. With continued pressure elevation, the rate of radius expansion continues to diminish. Across subsequent pressure increments, the inter-stage growth rates drop to 8.8% and 6.5%, respectively, underscoring a significant slowdown in the diffusion progress. Accordingly, the grouting pressure for shallow grouting holes should be maintained within 1–2 MPa, while for deep grouting holes, it is recommended to be controlled between 3 and 4 MPa.

(3) Grouting time.

Grouting duration plays a critical role in governing the diffusion behavior of grout within the surrounding rock. Insufficient grouting time prevents complete permeation of the rock mass, as premature pressure dissipation may leave certain areas untreated, thereby failing to deliver the intended reinforcement. Conversely, excessively prolonged grouting allows the slurry to rapidly fill all available fractures, beyond which further extension of grouting duration yields no improvement in effect and may only result in unnecessary resource consumption. Thus, precise control of grouting time is essential to achieve optimal diffusion and reinforcement performance.

This study examines the slurry diffusion behavior in the surrounding rock of the 1801 tailgate in the Dananhu No. 5 Mine under a constant injection pressure of 4 MPa and varying grouting durations of 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min. When the grouting time is extended to 4 min, the radius of the slurry’s penetration and diffusion increases by 37%, reaching 1.08 m. However, after the grouting time exceeds 6 min, although the slurry’s diffusion radius continues to expand with increased grouting time, its growth rate begins to decrease. The analysis reveals that the grouting process is subject to a law of diminishing returns. Beyond a certain duration, the marginal gain in slurry diffusion radius from extending the grouting time decreases significantly. Therefore, an optimal grouting duration of 6 to 8 min is recommended to maximize operational efficiency.

Field application and stability evaluation of grouting reinforcement in WSR roadway

The 1801 working face serves as the primary extraction panel, featuring a mudstone immediate roof with a thickness of 12.30 m. Influenced by its geological age and depositional environment, the rock exhibits low strength, poor cementation, and a tendency to disintegrate upon contact with water. The tailgate of the 1801 working face is driven along the coal seam roof, with a rectangular cross-section measuring 5.0 m in width and 4.0 m in height. Following excavation, the roadway is supported using a rock bolt-mesh system. The roof is reinforced with Φ20 mm diameter, L2200 mm left-handed thread rock bolts without longitudinal rebar, spaced at 900 × 900 mm intervals. Supplementary support is provided by mine anchor cables of Φ17.8 mm diameter and L6300 mm length, with three cables per row and a row spacing of 1800 × 1800 mm. The rib sides are supported by left-handed thread rock bolts of Φ20 mm diameter and L2200 mm length, installed at 900 × 900 mm intervals.

Conclusions

(1) Compression test results indicate that increasing water content significantly reduces the strength and stiffness of WSR, When the moisture content rises from 2% to 12%, the rock compressive strength decreases from 14.13 MPa to 2.71 MPa; an increase in confining pressure enhances the rock compressive strength and suppresses brittle failure. When the confining pressure rises from 0 MPa to 6 MPa, the rock compressive strength increases from 8.02 MPa to 16.11 MPa.

(2) Numerical simulation of roadway damage shows that with the rise in water content across the range of 2% to 12%, the total damage in the roof area rises from 70% to 90%, and the plastic zone expands notably. An increase in water content leads to a deeper plastic zone on both sides of the roadway.

(3) Based on grouting diffusion simulations, it was determined that the optimal grouting pressure is 1 ~ 2 MPa for shallow holes and 3 ~ 4 MPa for deep holes, with a recommended grouting duration of 6 ~ 8 min. The estimated grout diffusion radii are 1.5 ~ 2.0 m for shallow grouting and 2.5 ~ 3.0 m for deep grouting.

(4) Field applications confirmed that the proposed deep-shallow grouting control method successfully contains roadway convergence and maintains the long-term integrity of the surrounding rock mass.

(5) Although this research addressed the coupled effect of static loading and water seepage, subsequent studies should examine how dynamic disturbances, such as those induced by mining activities or seismic events, affect the long-term serviceability of WSR-supported structures.
