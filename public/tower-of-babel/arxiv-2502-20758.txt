Collective Reasoning Among LLMs: A Framework for Answer Validation Without Ground Truth
Seyed Pouyan Mousavi Davoudi, Alireza Shafiee Fard, Alireza Amiri-Margavi

Abstract
We present a collaborative framework where multiple large language models, namely GPT-4-0125-preview, Meta-LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct, Claude-3-Opus, and Gemini-1.5-Flash, work together to generate and respond to complex PhD-level probability questions in the absence of definitive ground truth. This study explores how inter-model consensus enhances response reliability and serves as a proxy for assessing the quality of generated questions. To quantify agreement and consistency, we employ statistical methods including chi-square tests, Fleiss’ Kappa, and confidence interval analysis, measuring both response precision and question clarity. Our findings highlight that Claude and Gemini generate well-structured and less ambiguous questions, leading to higher inter-model agreement. This is reflected in their narrower confidence intervals and stronger alignment with answering models. Conversely, LLaMA demonstrates increased variability and lower reliability in question formulation, as indicated by broader confidence intervals and reduced consensus rates. These results suggest that multi-model collaboration not only enhances the reliability of responses but also provides a valuable framework for assessing and improving question quality in the absence of explicit ground truth. This research offers meaningful insights into optimizing AI-driven reasoning through collaborative large-language model interactions.

Large Language Models, Collaborative Intelligence, Answer Validation, Game Theory, Statistical Analysis

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing and artificial intelligence by enabling machines to handle complex language tasks with a level of proficiency that was once unimaginable. Models such as OpenAI’s GPT-4, Meta’s LLaMA series, Anthropic’s Claude, Google’s Gemini, and DeepSeek have shown impressive skills in generating human-like text, understanding context, and even reasoning through complex problems
Guo et al. 2025
;
Mann et al. 2020
;
Touvron et al. 2023
. These breakthroughs have opened up exciting new possibilities for automated knowledge generation and validation in specialized fields
Kojima et al. 2022
.

Despite these advancements, a significant challenge persists: ensuring the accuracy of model-generated responses in the absence of a definitive correct answer while also evaluating the quality of the questions produced by these models. This issue is especially pronounced in complex domains such as advanced statistics and probability, where the intricacies of the subject matter make manual verification both resource-intensive and costly
Jiao et al. 2023
;
Amiri-Margavi et al. 2024
. In such cases, conventional validation techniques that rely on direct comparisons to predefined answers prove inadequate
Hendrycks et al. 2020
.

One promising solution is to utilize the collective power of multiple LLMs. By combining the strengths of different models, we can approximate correctness and identify consensus even in the absence of a ground truth
He et al. 2022
. This idea draws inspiration from ensemble learning techniques in machine learning
Dietterich 2000
and the wisdom-of-crowds phenomenon observed in human decision-making
Mennis 2006
.

Recent work has shown that different LLMs often bring complementary strengths and weaknesses to their ability to reason
Taylor et al. 2022
. This suggests that a collaborative framework, where multiple models contribute their unique insights, might overcome individual limitations and produce more reliable answers. However, the dynamics of such collaboration—especially in the realm of probabilistic reasoning—remain largely unexplored.

Our research builds on three key theoretical frameworks: collective intelligence, distributed cognition, and consensus formation. These frameworks help explain how collaboration among LLMs can lead to better problem-solving.

Collective Intelligence Theory
Lévy 1997
tells us that groups of agents can outperform individuals by combining their diverse perspectives. In our context, different LLM architectures (such as GPT-4, Claude, LLaMA, and Gemini) offer various approaches to problem solving, which, when combined, can result in more robust solutions
Hong and Page 2004
. This approach supports our idea of merging individual outputs through structured consensus formation
Woolley et al. 2010
while maintaining diversity in responses to reduce cascading errors
Vercammen et al. 2019
.

The Distributed Cognition Framework
Hutchins 1995
further reinforces our approach by showing how cognitive tasks can be shared among multiple agents. Since different models might capture different aspects of probability knowledge, the distribution of the task can lead to a more comprehensive and accurate overall solution
Du et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
. This framework also helps us to understand how information flows between models during collaborative problem solving
Naik 2024
.

Furthermore, mathematical models of consensus formation help us understand how agreement emerges among different decision makers
Friedkin 1990
. These models focus on how consensus develops, the influence each agent has
Olfati-Saber et al. 2007
, and the conditions needed for stable agreement
Baronchelli 2018
. Recent studies have successfully applied these ideas to multiagent systems, offering valuable insight into how collaborative AI can function effectively
Bahrami et al. 2010
.

By integrating collective intelligence, distributed cognition, and consensus formation, we create a comprehensive framework for analyzing collaborative AI systems
Hutchins 1995
;
Woolley et al. 2010
. We can express this integration as:

R=f(\text{CI},\text{DC},\text{CF})

(1)

where

. This unified perspective allows us to better understand how multiple AI models can work together to produce reliable solutions for complex reasoning tasks.

Our analytical framework combines quantitative and qualitative methods drawn from these theoretical perspectives
Klein et al. 2006
. On the quantitative side, we employ metrics inspired by consensus formation models to measure agreement and convergence in multi-agent systems
Mennis 2006
. Our validation approach emphasizes the value of multiple points of view when evaluating the results of the model
Kittur and Kraut 2008
, ultimately helping us to evaluate the reliability and robustness of collaborative AI solutions
Malone and Bernstein 2022
. This solid theoretical foundation not only grounds our study in established principles but also offers new insights into the challenges of AI collaboration in statistical and probabilistic reasoning

.

Although previous research has largely focused on the performance of individual LLMs
Ahn et al. 2024
or basic ensemble methods
Huang et al. 2024
, there is a clear gap in our understanding of how multiple state-of-the-art LLMs can collaborate to validate complex knowledge when there is no ground truth. This gap is especially significant in specialized fields where:

•

Questions demand sophisticated and nuanced reasoning.

•

Manual validation by experts is prohibitively time-consuming and costly.

•

Traditional automated validation methods fall short.

•

The constantly evolving nature of knowledge makes it difficult to maintain up-to-date ground truths.

To address these challenges, our study explores the collaborative dynamics between different LLMs in probabilistic reasoning tasks. We aim to investigate how the answers from various models align or differ when tackling complex probability questions and to identify patterns in their interactions. Our goal is to develop a framework for evaluating the reliability of these collaborative outcomes without relying on predefined correct answers, using statistical techniques such as chi-square tests and Fleiss’ Kappa to measure consensus and inter-model agreement.

This research makes several important contributions:

The implications of our findings extend across educational technology, automated assessment systems, and research validation in specialized academic fields. By deepening our understanding of how artificial systems can work together, we hope to pave the way for more reliable, scalable, and efficient AI-powered knowledge systems.

Given the absence of ground-truth answers, we employed several mechanisms to validate the models’ answers based on inter-model consensus.

Majority Vote, Reliability, and Confidence Interval

To determine the consensus answer
A_{i}^{\text{cons}}
for a given question
Q_{i}
, we utilized a majority voting mechanism over the responses from multiple LLMs. Formally, the consensus answer is defined as:

A_{i}^{\text{cons}}=\arg\max_{k\in\{A,B,C,D\}}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\delta(a_{ij},k),

(4)

where
a_{ij}
denotes the response provided by the
j
-th LLM for question
Q_{i}
, and
\delta(a_{ij},k)
is the Kronecker delta function:

\delta(a_{ij},k)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }a_{ij}=k,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This formulation assigns a frequency-based score to each answer option
k
, with the majority-selected response designated as the consensus answer. Both partial and complete agreements are considered valid, forming the basis for subsequent analyses.

In the absence of ground truth labels, we introduced a reliability metric to assess the trustworthiness of the consensus answers. Reliability measures the alignment between the consensus response and the answer provided by the LLM that generated the question. Let
A_{i}^{\text{LLM-q}}
denote the querying LLM’s response for question
Q_{i}
. The reliability score
R_{i}
is computed as:

R_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }A_{i}^{\text{cons}}=A_{i}^{\text{LLM-q}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5)

A higher reliability score indicates a more substantial alignment between the consensus answer and the querying LLM’s response, increasing confidence in the consensus’s validity. This measure is precious in settings where ground truth data is unavailable.

To evaluate the robustness of consensus rates across LLMs, we calculated confidence intervals (CIs) using a bootstrap resampling approach. Confidence intervals provide a probabilistic range for the actual consensus rate, capturing variability in the observed data. Narrow CIs indicate high reliability, while wider CIs reflect more significant uncertainty.

Bootstrap resampling enables CI estimation without assumptions about the underlying data distribution. Specifically,
B
bootstrap samples are generated by sampling the original dataset with replacement. The mean agreement rate is computed for each sample, constructing a bootstrap distribution of the consensus rate. The
\alpha/2
-th and
1-\alpha/2
-th percentiles of this distribution define the lower and upper CI bounds, respectively. For a 95% CI, these correspond to the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

By incorporating confidence intervals, we mitigate uncertainty in agreement rates and establish a statistical basis for comparing LLMs. Overlapping CIs suggest no significant differences between models, whereas non-overlapping intervals indicate statistically significant differences. This methodology ensures a robust and interpretable evaluation of model reliability, mainly when ground truth labels are unavailable.

To further validate reliability and assess statistical significance, we employed the following additional statistical measures:

Chi-Square Test of Independence

A chi-square test was used to evaluate whether the distribution of selected answers deviated significantly from random chance. The test statistic was calculated as:

\chi^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(O_{k}-E_{k})^{2}}{E_{k}},

(6)

Where:

•

O_{k}
is the observed frequency of answer choice
k
across all models and questions.

•

E_{k}
is the expected frequency of answer choice
k
, assuming uniform random selection,
E_{k}=\frac{N\times n_{j}}{K}
.

•

K=4
is the number of answer choices.

•

N
is the total number of questions.

•

n_{j}=3
is the number of answering models.

To determine statistical significance, the computed
\chi^{2}
value was compared against the chi-square distribution with
K-1
degrees of freedom.

Fleiss’ Kappa Coefficient

Fleiss’ kappa
\kappa
was employed to quantify the agreement among the models beyond chance. It is defined as:

\kappa=\frac{\overline{P}-\overline{P_{e}}}{1-\overline{P_{e}}},

(7)

Where:

•

\overline{P}
is the mean observed agreement among the models.

•

\overline{P_{e}}
is the mean agreement expected by chance.

The value of
\kappa
ranges from
-1
(perfect disagreement) to
1
(perfect agreement), with
0
indicating no agreement beyond chance.

These mechanisms collectively ensured a robust framework for validating model responses and assessing the reliability of the generated consensus.

Conclusion

This research explores the collaborative dynamics among multiple LLMs in the generation and validation of complex probabilistic questions in the absence of ground truth. By systematically analyzing inter-model agreement, reliability, and consensus formation, we provide empirical insights into how different LLMs align or diverge when reasoning through probabilistic concepts.

Our findings reveal that Claude and GPT-4 exhibit the highest levels of reliability and consistency between models, achieving strong agreement rates and narrow confidence intervals (Claude:

Statistical analyzes using Chi-square tests and Fleiss Kappa further validate the robustness of inter-model agreement, confirming that consensus among models is not due to random chance. However, the weaker performance of LLaMA, with the lowest reliability percentage (
66.7\%
) and the highest rate of disagreement, emphasizes the need for further optimization. Our results also emphasize the significant influence of the question-generating model on overall consensus, highlighting that some models may formulate questions in ways that are inherently more interpretable by others.

Beyond evaluating model agreement, this research highlights the potential for LLM collaboration as a validation mechanism in scenarios where human-labeled ground truth is unavailable. Majority voting and confidence interval analysis prove to be effective strategies for approximating reliability, offering a scalable approach to knowledge verification in automated systems.

Future research should explore the incorporation of human expert evaluations to benchmark model reliability against established ground truth standards. Additionally, investigating bias propagation within inter-model agreement structures is crucial to preventing the reinforcement of systematic errors. By addressing these challenges, we can improve the robustness, scalability, and trustworthiness of LLM-based consensus mechanisms, paving the way for more reliable AI-driven reasoning systems.
