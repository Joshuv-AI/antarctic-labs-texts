Community health worker implementation of transdiagnostic evidence-based treatment in Spanish: protocol for refinement, implementation, and a feasibility pilot RCT

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLatine people in the U.S. face unequal access to mental healthcare, particularly families and parents with limited English proficiency. Community health workers (CHWs) are well-positioned to increase access to care if integrated into mental health services. The Common Elements Treatment Approach (CETA) is a transdiagnostic evidence-based treatment (EBT) designed to be delivered by lay providers with little to no prior mental health training (i.e., CHWs). CHWs have demonstrated effectiveness in delivering CETA and other EBTs globally; however, there is limited evidence to guide domestic implementation of these models. This study aims to evaluate the implementation of CHW-delivered CETA with Spanish-speaking Latine parents to establish initial feasibility and proof of concept prior to expanding the study of CHW EBT models across other populations and settings.MethodsThis protocol outlines a three phase study. Background

Mental healthcare access among latine families

Innovative mental health service models are urgently needed to address enduring disparities in access to care for those with unmet needs, particularly for Spanish-speaking Latine parents. The Latine community is the largest and one of the fastest-growing ethnic groups in the United States (U.S.), comprising 20% of families with a child under 18 [1, 2]. Yet, Latine adults and children are approximately half as likely to receive mental health services compared to their non-Latine White counterparts – and Latines with limited English proficiency have even lower odds of service use [3–6]. Further, when Latine people do access services, they are less likely to receive guideline-congruent (i.e., evidence-based) care [7]. Thus, Spanish-speaking Latine parents stand at the nexus of multiple mental healthcare inequities, with the odds triply stacked against them: with reduced likelihood of accessing services for themselves and their children, and reduced likelihood of receiving quality services when they do access care.

From an ecological perspective, the mental health of youth and parents (defined as primary caregivers of youth) must be considered in tandem. In their report on the youth mental health crisis, the Surgeon General’s first recommendation on how caregivers can support youth mental health is to take care of their own mental health [8]. Indeed, there is a robust link between the two. Parental mental illness is associated with poorer child outcomes, including worse attachment, emotion regulation, lower cognitive functioning, more behavioral problems, poorer school achievement, increased likelihood of psychopathology, and greater distress in adulthood [9–12]. These effects are even stronger for low-income and minoritized families [9]. However, research on interventions targeting adult mental health often fails to consider that many of these adults are parents. As such, the potential benefits for their children and family are overlooked [13]. Therefore, when examining ways to improve mental health supports for the Latine community, it is imperative to evaluate not only individual-level outcomes for parents but also child and family outcomes, given the protective benefits that may be conferred onto the family system.

Mental health inequity for Latine families is an issue of access. Systematic reviews evaluating evidence-based treatments (EBTs) across racial/ethnic groups find that therapy offers similar benefits across groups [14]. It is in treatment access (i.e., participation, retention) where significant disparities are observed [15, 16]. Thus, there is a paradox of having effective treatments, but inaccessible service models. Barriers to care include a range of attitudinal (e.g., stigma) and structural (e.g., economic precarity, availability of services in the community) factors [17, 18]. In particular, the U.S.’s shortage of Spanish-speaking mental health providers poses a major barrier to care for Spanish-speaking Latines. From 2014 to 2019, the proportion of U.S. facilities offering Spanish services declined by 17.8%, while the number of Spanish-speaking Latines continued to rise [19, 20].

Growing roles for community health workers: implementation & ethical considerations

Community health workers (CHWs) are trusted members of the community who often have shared lived experiences with those served. They do not have specialized degrees but, with targeted training, can increase access to health care through a variety of roles in outreach and education, service navigation, as well as program delivery for health promotion [21, 22]. CHWs’ positionality as members from the community served makes them well-suited to provide linguistically and culturally appropriate services, reduce stigma, and facilitate client trust, while increasing and diversifying the health workforce within community settings [23]. CHWs have been widely and successfully deployed in health promotion, patient education, and care navigation across a range of health conditions (e.g., nutrition, vaccination, diabetes), particularly in underserved communities [21, 22]. However, these roles are distinct from the delivery of mental health interventions, which involve unique clinical, ethical, and systems-level considerations. This type of health care delivery model, i.e., CHW-delivered EBTs, is in line with a stepped care, task-shifting approach, in which lower-intensity treatments (e.g., brief intervention with non-specialists) are scalably delivered to large portions of a population with less intensive needs, allowing those with more severe symptomology to step-up to higher intervention care (e.g., therapy with licensed providers, psychopharmacology) [23]. A growing evidence base supports the feasibility and effectiveness of training lay providers in time-limited EBT delivery; however, most research on CHW-delivered EBTs has been conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs; [24, 25]). Within the U.S. mental health system, CHWs primarily occupy a navigation or auxiliary role (e.g., supporting engagement, adherence), but rarely are they the primary interventionists delivering an EBT [23]. Outside of mental health, CHWs have shown high engagement rates with U.S. Latines in the context of health promotion interventions [26]. Hence, there is great potential in CHW-led mental health service models as an effective means to increase access to care for U.S. Latines.

Because CHWs are rarely in a mental health interventionist role in the U.S., there is little evidence to guide domestic implementation of these models. Filling this gap requires considerations from both an implementation science (IS) lens and an ethics lens. Compared to LMIC, where the majority of CHW-delivered EBTs have been examined, the U.S. presents a distinct implementation landscape in terms of policy, population, healthcare infrastructure, and workforce. Here, an IS lens is necessary for understanding how to effectively translate EBTs into new contexts and populations by identifying and addressing the contextual factors (e.g., organization-, provider-, patient-level factors) most relevant for successful implementation [27]. There is a lack of existing literature on implementing CHW models to address gaps in mental healthcare, which is a necessary consideration for effective and sustainable deployment of these models. An ethics lens is also necessary, given that CHWs are providers with their own unique responsibilities and scope of practice. Further, CHWs serve marginalized groups, practice in low-resource settings, and are often members of the communities served. The intersection of CHWs’ positionality with the role of EBT provider raises ethical questions, such as: How can we leverage the strengths and capacities of CHWs while not overextending their role so as not to put patients and CHWs at risk? Are there unique needs that CHWs might have in a mental health provider role, given that CHWs face many of the same stressors and inequities as their patients? How do we ensure the implementation of CHW mental health services is high-quality so as not to perpetuate disparities in care for marginalized communities? Thus, an ethical analysis is necessary to minimize risks, optimize benefits, and weigh the tradeoffs while centering the needs of marginalized communities and ensuring fairness in the implementation process. Consistent with community-engaged research approaches, addressing these implementation and ethical considerations for CHW-delivered EBT requires input from multiple key partners (i.e., CHWs, supervisors, clinic leaders, Latine parents) whose perspectives can inform and guide intervention feasibility, organizational fit, cultural relevance, and client engagement.

Common elements treatment approach: transdiagnostic EBT for CHW delivery

The Common Elements Treatment Approach (CETA) is an ideal intervention model for advancing our understanding of CHW-delivered mental health services in the U.S. CETA is a transdiagnostic EBT designed for implementation in low-resource settings by lay providers with little to no prior mental health training (i.e., CHWs). CETA consists of 11 modules addressing the most common mental health disorders, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress [28]. Modules are based on common elements of EBTs and include topics such as psychoeducation, behavioral activation, relaxation, cognitive restructuring, exposure therapy, safety planning, and substance use reduction [29]. As a transdiagnostic treatment, each individual’s course of CETA is tailored to their presenting problems, and providers are trained in the selection, ordering, and dosage of CETA modules. Thus, the number of sessions for a course of CETA may vary; a standard course of CETA consists of approximately 12–14 weekly sessions, with 7–8 sessions being the minimum dosage for a full course of the intervention [29, 30]. CETA materials follow a simple, concrete format, with a 1–5 page “manual” section and a 1–2 page “steps sheet” for each module that includes goals, prompts, and guidance for in-session use [29]. CETA’s training model favors active, experiential learning strategies in addition to didactic teaching, and includes ongoing supervision and consultation while trainees deliver CETA [31]. CETA can be delivered in-person or via telehealth, and in individual or group formats. These features make CETA uniquely well-positioned for CHW use.

CETA has demonstrated effectiveness in addressing depression, anxiety, trauma, and intimate partner violence in randomized controlled trials across multiple cultural contexts, including Thailand, Iraq, Zambia, and Colombia [30, 32–34]. Yet, to date, only one study has examined the implementation of CETA in the U.S., and this study was implemented by English-speaking masters-level community mental health providers rather than CHWs [35]. Thus, we need to advance our understanding of how to implement CETA with CHW providers in the U.S. We also need to refine CETA to optimize fit for Spanish-speaking Latine parents in U.S. settings given linguistic and cultural differences from implementation of the curriculum in Spanish in Colombia.

CETA is well-suited for supporting Latine mental health. First, Latine people often prefer seeking help from trusted, known individuals within their community before using formal behavioral therapy services [36]. Thus, CETA’s design for use by CHWs aligns with their help-seeking preferences, increasing the likelihood of engagement. Second, CETA addresses multiple presenting problems as a modularized transdiagnostic model, making it well-suited for supporting mental health in the context of pervasive comorbidities [37]. Third, CETA explicitly encourages key partner-driven refinement to best situate the intervention within the local context, such as aligning intervention techniques with cultural practices (e.g., mapping relaxation strategies onto religious practice of meditation) or modifying language to accommodate cultural norms (e.g., referring to the intervention as a “program” to reduce stigma associated with “therapy”) [29, 31]. This allows for tailoring to increase the cultural and contextual relevance for Latine parents.

Objectives

This study will examine the implementation of CHW-delivered CETA in the U.S., focusing on fit and feasibility for delivery in Spanish with Latine parents. This study will address the following aims:

Aim 1

Collaborate with key partners to optimize CETA fit for the local context and population. Adaptation will follow ADAPT-ITT, a framework developed by Wingwood and colleagues to sequentially guide EBT refinement. We will form a community advisory board (CAB) with key partners (CHWs, supervisors, clinic leaders, and Latine parents) to lead refinement around areas of relevance and engagement (e.g., language, alignment with norms and values) [38].

Aim 2

Identify preliminary contextual determinants and develop an implementation plan. Guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), we will use mixed methods to assess multilevel contextual determinants (intervention-, key partner-, and organization-level factors) over the course of the CETA training period, which includes implementation with patients [11]. Findings will be shared with the CAB to inform implementation planning for the feasibility pilot. Particular attention will be given to identifying ethical and professional dilemmas that may arise in delivering CETA and supporting CHWs and organizations to optimally address these issues (e.g., staying within scope of practice, ensuring appropriate cases, providing necessary supports), with the goal of optimizing provider and organizational readiness for implementation.

Aim 3

Conduct a randomized feasibility pilot of CHW-delivered CETA in Spanish for Latine parents living in a household with a child 18 years or younger. Spanish-speaking Latine parents with depressive, anxious, and/or traumatic stress symptoms in the moderate range will be randomized to either immediate intervention (CHW-delivered CETA) or a no-intervention 5-month delay (after which they will have the option to receive CHW-delivered CETA). This delay period is commensurate with existing waitlist times in the setting’s behavioral health department. We will collect administrative data (e.g., patient attendance, retention), patient-reported data (e.g., symptom measures), and provider-reported data (e.g., fidelity) to evaluate feasibility. Aim 3a (primary) will determine the feasibility of recruitment, randomization, and measurement procedures, and the feasibility of intervention implementation and engagement. It is hypothesized that the intervention and evaluation plan will be feasible to implement for CHWs and for parents to engage with. Aim 3b (secondary) will compare differences between study conditions in mental health outcomes for parents’ symptoms, their children’s functioning, and family functioning. It is hypothesized that parents who receive CETA will have improved outcomes in their own mental health symptoms, as well as child and family functioning.

Methods

This project consists of three phases, each aligned with the respective specific aim as detailed below.

Setting

This project is conducted in partnership with Sinai Chicago (Sinai), a safety net hospital on Chicago’s West Side with a service area that is 46% Latine, including neighborhoods that are predominantly Latine [39, 40]. Sinai’s Urban Health Institute (SUHI), Sinai’s community engaged research center, has over 25 years of experience in training and integrating CHWs into health systems and community settings across a variety of health domains. With growing local mental health needs, Sinai’s patient population identified mental health as the highest priority in a recent community needs assessment, and SUHI’s CHW trainees have expressed a growing interest in mental health training [39]. Thus, this project responds to the priorities of local key partners by examining the feasibility of a CHW-delivered EBT.

Aim 1

Use the ADAPT-ITT model to collaborate with key partners by establishing a CAB to refine the fit of CETA for Spanish-speaking Latine parents.

Aim 1 Overview. The ADAPT-ITT model offers a systematic, 8-step approach for refining EBTs. Phases 1 and 2 have been completed via identifying the community’s interest in addressing mental health needs (Phase 1) and selecting CETA as the intervention for use (Phase 2). Phases 3–8 comprise the current study (see Table 1) [38]. Tailoring is a standard process to prepare for CETA implementation, as CETA developers explicitly encourage key partner-driven refinement to contextualize the intervention within the local community [27, 29]. CETA has been translated and delivered in Spanish in Colombia [33], but because CETA has never been delivered to Spanish-speaking Latine people in the U.S., we will need to optimize materials for engagement and contextual relevance, including refining areas of language (e.g., matching local Spanish dialect of Latines in Chicago, terms used to discuss mental health), alignment with local norms and values (e.g., familismo, respeto, personalismo), and intervention delivery preferences (e.g., individual vs. group, in-person vs. telehealth formats). CAB work will be guided by community-engaged research principles that center community involvement throughout all stages of research [41].

Participants. Potential CAB members will be identified through referrals made by West Side community partners. Aim 1 procedures do not involve human subjects research as CAB members will serve in a consultation role providing guidance on study materials, procedures, and design. CAB members will be recruited to represent the following groups: CHWs with experience working with Latine parents, licensed mental health clinicians with experience working with Latine parents, leadership at Sinai Chicago with CHW programming experience, and Spanish-speaking Latine parents with experience with mental health services.

Procedures. The CAB will meet twice monthly for 6 months to focus on theater testing and initial refining for each of the 11 CETA modules (ADAPT-ITT Phases 3 & 4), and then shift to meeting every other month for the next 6 months (ADAPT-ITT Phases 5 & 6). Theater testing is a methodology for optimizing an intervention that involves the active demonstration of key elements of the intervention to key partners (i.e., the CAB), followed by a facilitated discussion based on key partners’ feedback to inform adaptations that can improve the fit of the intervention for the target population (Spanish-speaking Latine parents) [38]. This will result in an initial draft of modifications (Phase 4), which will be reviewed with topical experts (i.e., CETA developers) to further refine the materials (Phase 5). Modifications will be integrated and reviewed by the CAB to finalize the CETA manual (Phase 6). The CAB will continue to meet to monitor and provide feedback during the CETA training (Aim 2) and the feasibility pilot (Aim 3).

Analyses. Across all phases of ADAPT-ITT, modifications to CETA informed by CAB feedback will be documented using the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Interventions (FRAME) [42]. FRAME is a systematic approach for coding modifications made to EBTs, based on what, when, how, and why a modification was made [42]. Analyses will include descriptive statistics about the nature (content, context), process (at what stage it was done, who contributed to the decision), and the goals for the modification (cultural adaptation, feasibility). Aim 1 will result in (1) CETA materials that are optimized for local Spanish-speaking CHWs and Latine parents and (2) the identification of patterns in the adaptation process to support and guide the adaptation of CETA for implementation in other contexts.

Aim 2

Use a mixed-methods approach to identify contextual determinants for delivery of CETA during the initial training period and use this data to inform the development of an implementation plan.

Aim 2 Overview. Aim 2 activities follow an observational design. Because CETA has never been delivered by CHWs in the U.S., it is necessary to understand what factors will impact implementation in the local context, and the ethical considerations involved in having CHWs in a mental health interventionist role. The CETA training period involves a didactic workshop followed by supervised delivery with initial patients, thus providing an opportunity to examine determinants and ethical questions during initial implementation. We will use the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) – a framework that highlights the importance of multi-level factors (provider-, patient-, organizational-, and intervention-level factors) important for implementation – to guide mixed-methods data collection with key partners (CHWs, supervisors, and patients) [43]. Qualitative interviews and questionnaires will identify factors impacting CETA acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility during training and delivery with training cases. Findings will be shared with the CAB to inform the development of an implementation plan.

Participants. Six CHWs and three advanced supervisory staff (senior CHW supervisors and/or licensed mental health clinicians), henceforth described collectively as “providers”, will be recruited through Sinai to receive training in CETA by the intervention developers and provide feedback on initial CETA delivery to patients. CHWs will be recruited based on the following criteria: (1) being Spanish-speaking and (2) completing the CHW core competencies certification program from Sinai or having 2 or more years of working experience as a CHW.

An estimated 27 patients will be recruited (2–3 patients per provider) from across Sinai’s clinics and local Latine-serving organizations. Potential patient participants will be screened for the following inclusion criteria: (1) 18 years old or older, (2) identify as Latine, (3) Spanish-speaking, (4) caregiver to a child younger than 18 years living in the same household, and 4) have moderate symptoms of depression, anxiety, and/or post-traumatic stress, as measured by the Computerized Adaptive Tests for Mental Health (CAT-MH) [44]. Patients will be excluded if: (1) they have symptom scores higher than the moderate range in any of the CAT-MH modules; (2) they have CAT-MH symptom scores of mild or higher for mania, psychosis, or suicidal ideation; or (3) they are currently receiving psychotherapy services.

Providers (n = 9) and patients (n = 13) will be invited to participate in CFIR qualitative interviews, consistent with qualitative sample size guidelines for reaching saturation [45].

Procedures. Providers will receive training in CETA from the intervention developers. Standard CETA training includes an approximately 80-hour didactic workshop followed by trainee delivery of CETA to patients while receiving supervision from CETA trainers. As part of an apprenticeship model, advanced supervisory staff (senior CHW supervisors and/or licensed clinicians) will additionally be trained on how to supervise the implementation of CETA [31]. CETA developers will monitor trainee progress and determine when a provider has met CETA competency.

Via zoom or telephone, study staff will screen potential training case patient participants for the inclusion/exclusion criteria. During screening, potential training case participants will be sent a REDCap link to complete the CAT-MH to screen for mental health symptom severity in the past 30 days across domains of: depression, anxiety, mania/hypomania, substance use, suicide, PTSD, and psychosis. CAT-MH scores will be immediately seen and reviewed by the trained research staff member. Should any safety concerns be endorsed, a safety protocol will be enacted, including immediate contact of the study PI for safety evaluation and planning. Eligible participants will then be asked to provide informed consent.

During CETA implementation, providers and patient participants will complete quantitative and qualitative assessments on contextual determinants to identify barriers and facilitators for the implementation of CETA. The data will be used by the CAB to inform the selection of implementation strategies for Aim 3, with emphasis on addressing contextual barriers for delivery [46].

Measures. Table 2 summarizes Aim 2 measures. All assessments will be available in English and Spanish. Established IS measures were selected to assess key contextual determinants, including provider attitudes (EBPAS), provider and patient reports of intervention feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness (mhIST), and organizational factors (ORIC, mhIST) [47–49]. Although some models (i.e [50], frame feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness as implementation outcomes in themselves, here we align with Damschroder et al.’s conceptualization of these constructs as antecedent assessments that are predictors of implementation outcomes such as adoption and implementation [51]. A CFIR-guided qualitative interview will examine provider and patient accounts of contextual determinants, including the intersection of ethical dilemmas and implementation (e.g., support needs unique to CHWs). Measures will also be collected as part of standard CETA implementation with patients, including assessments for symptoms (CAT-MH, administered to patients at baseline and monthly until CETA completion) and fidelity monitoring (CETA Fidelity Monitoring System, completed by providers after every CETA session).

Aim 2 measures

Questionnaires will be administered via REDCap, and qualitative interviews will be conducted via a Zoom audio/video call or in-person, depending on participant preference. Quantitative questionnaires assessing contextual determinants will be completed by patients at the end of CETA, and by providers after completing the didactic portion of the training and upon completion of their first training case or six months into the training period, whichever occurs first. Qualitative interviews exploring contextual determinants will be conducted with clients at the end of CETA, and by providers upon completion of their first training case or six months into the training period, whichever occurs first.

Analyses. A QUAN+QUAL mixed-methods design will be used, with quantitative and qualitative data collected simultaneously throughout the CETA training, and both data types weighted equally [52]. The design function is one of complementarity– using quantitative and qualitative data to elaborate upon one another and increase the understanding of factors that impact implementation potential [53]. We will conduct descriptive statistics to analyze quantitative data and use rapid qualitative analysis (RQA) to evaluate the qualitative data [54, 55]. During RQA, we will code for any ethical challenges emerging in participants’ interviews, focused on potential harms, benefits, inequities, or threats to patient autonomy, as well as potential CHW moral distress or conflicts in CHW professional obligations. From Aim 2 analyses, we expect to: (a) identify the determinants relevant to CETA implementation; and (b) develop an implementation plan that addresses barriers to CETA implementation, to guide the Aim 3 pilot. Specifically, we will present a summary of CFIR-coded barriers and facilitators to the CAB to get their feedback on the highest priority determinants. The Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change compilation (ERIC), a compilation of implementation strategies, will be used to select strategies that address identified determinant barriers [56]. These ERIC implementation strategies will then be incorporated in the Aim 3 trial.

Aim 3

Conduct a randomized pilot study to test the feasibility of CHW-delivered CETA programming for Spanish-speaking Latine parents.

Aim 3 Overview. Aim 3 uses a two-arm, parallel randomization design to evaluate the feasibility of CHW-delivered CETA with Spanish-speaking Latine parents over the course of a 24-month pilot conducted at Sinai (aligned with Phase 8 of ADAPT-ITT). As a feasibility pilot, the primary goal is to evaluate the feasibility of CETA implementation and research methods; assessing CETA effectiveness is a secondary, exploratory goal. Toward this end, patients will be randomized to either immediate or delayed CETA, with a focus on evaluating the feasibility of: (a) CETA implementation and engagement, and (b) randomization and data collection during a no-intervention period (in the delayed condition) for this population. We hypothesize that the intervention and evaluation plan will be feasible to implement for CHWs and feasible for parents to engage with. Group differences in symptoms between immediate and delayed conditions will be assessed as a secondary aim. We hypothesize that parents who receive CETA will show improvements in their own mental health symptoms, as well as child and family functioning.

Participants. From providers who met CETA competency in Aim 2, three CHW providers will serve as interventionists delivering CETA, and two advanced supervisory staff (senior CHW supervisors and/or licensed clinicians) will oversee supervision for CHW providers. Approximately 52 patient participants will be recruited from across Sinai clinics and local Latine-serving organizations. Allowing for a 20% attrition rate, we anticipate a final analytical sample of 42 patients. Patient participant inclusion and exclusion criteria and screening will be the same as for training case patient participants outlined in Aim 2. Following intervention completion or dropout, we will interview approximately half of the patient sample (n = 21), consistent with qualitative sample size guidelines for reaching saturation [45]. We will also invite providers (n = 4) and SUHI clinic and organization leaders (n = 5) to participate in qualitative feedback interviews.

Procedures. Figure 1 displays the CONSORT flow diagram. Patients will be randomized into one of two parallel conditions: immediate CETA or the delayed condition. The allocation sequence will be generated by a statistician not involved in enrollment, using random number generation with a 1:1 allocation ratio using the randomizeR package designed for clinical trials randomization in R (CRAN). Assignments will be provided to study enrollment personnel via REDCap after eligibility is confirmed and baseline assessments are completed.

Across all data collection, questionnaire and quantitative data will be collected via REDCap, and qualitative interviews will be conducted via a Zoom audio/video call or in-person, depending on participant preference. Data will be routinely reviewed to identify missing information and errors.

Analyses. Feasibility of patient participation in CETA will be evaluated based on whether at least 60% of patients complete seven or more sessions of CETA. This is based on examination of active dosage for CETA indicating that seven sessions is the lower limit for large clinical improvement [61]. Further, across therapies internationally, the modal number of therapy sessions people receive is one; thus, if the majority of the sample engages in any number of multiple sessions, this would demonstrate an improvement in participation compared to current engagement rates [62]. Participation rates will be examined alongside patient-reported scores of feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness on the mhIST, in addition to feedback from patient qualitative analyses. The feasibility of CHW implementation will be evaluated based on whether CHW providers achieve 75% on the CETA fidelity monitoring system. These data will be analyzed alongside provider-reported scores of feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness on the mhIST, and feedback from provider/leadership qualitative analyses.

Feasibility of methods will be evaluated based on descriptive statistics of measure completion rates and participant retention rates across conditions, in addition to qualitative feedback from patients and providers. Furthermore, ethical questions regarding feasibility will be evaluated via qualitative thematic analyses that examine patient perception of quality of care from CHW providers and CHWs’ experience as mental health providers [63].

To explore CETA effectiveness, linear and generalized linear mixed-effects models (LMMs/GLMMs) will be used to examine changes in patient and family mental health outcomes between the immediate intervention and delay arms. Missing data will be handled using LMMs/GLMMs, which use maximum likelihood estimation to accommodate missing outcome data over time under a missing-at-random assumption. Thus, all available outcome data will be utilized in analysis after assessing patterns of missingness. Main effects of the immediate arm will provide information about overall differences in outcomes across timepoints. Intervention type by time interactions will indicate differences in change over time based on arm allocations. Based on previous CETA trials, 21 participants per condition should be sufficient as a pilot to detect the equivalent of endpoint differences between groups representing medium effect size endpoint differences (d = 0.5), or even small effect sizes (d = 0.30) based on established methods to assess pilot sample size and power such as the 80% one-sided confidence interval approach or general pilot power recommendations [30, 32, 33, 64–66].

Discussion

This protocol outlines three phases to refine, implement, and evaluate a transdiagnostic EBT delivered by CHWs with Spanish-speaking Latine parents. This study addresses a critical public health crisis in the current mental health landscape – namely, significant mental health workforce shortages and unmet mental health needs. With 122 million Americans living in a mental health provider shortage area, there is an immense need for expanding the mental health workforce and increasing access to quality service models, particularly for communities that are less likely to access care [67]. CHW mental health models offer a strengths-based, community-driven approach to care by partnering with trusted community members who have local expertise and equipping them with the tools to expand access to treatment. By examining the feasibility of an innovative mental health model, i.e., CHW-delivered CETA, with one population (Spanish-speaking Latine parents), this project will establish initial viability and proof of concept of implementation and methodology to be used in a future clinical trial, with the ultimate goal of advancing the science of implementation and sustained integration of CHWs in mental healthcare for the benefit of diverse populations and settings across the US.

To our knowledge, this would be the first study of a transdiagnostic EBT delivered by Spanish-speaking CHWs in the U.S. While there is a growing evidence base of CHW-delivered EBTs globally, there is a gap in domestic research to inform local implementation and policy [24, 25]. Given that CHWs have yet to be integrated into U.S. mental health services, particularly in the delivery of EBTs, this research is a novel and necessary step forward in innovating domestic mental health models. Additionally, this study is well-timed given recent legislative changes expanding CHW coverage and services [68, 69]. To date, limitations in coverage have posed a barrier to scaling CHW-delivered services. However, this is changing. For example, Illinois recently passed legislation opening a reimbursement pathway for services provided by CHWs through Medicaid [69]. Findings from this study will provide preliminary evidence on the feasibility and acceptability of CHW-delivered CETA, contributing foundational implementation data that can inform future discussions of CHW scope of practice in mental health and the domestic adoption of CHW-delivered EBTs. An additional strength of this study is its two-generation outcome assessment, which addresses a common limitation in adult mental health research that often fails to consider the potential benefit treatment of adult parents may have on the family system [13]. Two-generation outcome assessment is particularly relevant for research with the Latine community as familismo, or the dedication and commitment to family, is a central cultural value, thus making the assessment of intervention outcomes for both parents and children all the more important [70]. Overall, this study contributes to improving the capacity, accessibility, and equity of mental health services. Data will lay the groundwork for a future clinical trial evaluating CHW-delivered CETA across multiple populations and settings, with the long-term aim of building a body of robust empirical evidence to inform the implementation of CHW EBT models.
