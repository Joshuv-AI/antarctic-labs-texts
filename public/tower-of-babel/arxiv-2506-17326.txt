CopulaSMOTE: A Copula-Based Oversampling Approach for Imbalanced Classification in Diabetes Prediction
Agnideep Aich, Md Monzur Murshed, Sameera Hewage and Amanda Mayeaux

Abstract
Diabetes mellitus poses a significant health risk, as nearly 1 in 9 people are affected by it. Early detection can significantly lower this risk. Despite significant advancements in machine learning for identifying diabetic cases, results can still be influenced by the imbalanced nature of the data. To address this challenge, our study considered copula-based data augmentation, which preserves the dependency structure when generating data for the minority class and integrates it with machine learning (ML) techniques. We selected the Pima Indian dataset and generated data using A2 copula, then applied four machine learning algorithms: logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, and extreme gradient boosting. Our findings indicate that XGBoost combined with A2 copula oversampling achieved the best performance improving accuracy by 4.6%, precision by 15.6%, recall by 20.4%, F1-score by 18.2% and AUC by 25.5% compared to the standard SMOTE method. Furthermore, we statistically validated our results using the McNemar test. This research represents the first known use of A2 copulas for data augmentation and serves as an alternative to the SMOTE technique, highlighting the efficacy of copulas as a statistical method in machine learning applications.

1.  Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus has become a significant global health issue, affecting not only individuals but also their families and entire communities. According to the latest International Diabetes Federation (IDF)
[
31
]
Diabetes Atlas (2025), 11.1% of adults aged 20 to 79, equivalent to 1 in 9, are living with diabetes, and more than 40% of these individuals are unaware of their condition. By 2050, the number of people with diabetes is expected to increase by 46%. Additionally, over 90% of diabetes cases are classified as type 2. The common factors contributing to type 2 diabetes include urbanization, lack of exercise, and unhealthy eating habits. Shockingly, a significant proportion of people are unaware that they are diabetic, which poses a major risk to their health as diabetes can damage the kidneys over time and is also linked to hypertension. With early detection and preventive measures, we can mitigate these risks. Recent advancements in the medical field enable us to detect the onset of diabetes at an early stage. Combining these medical advancements with machine learning techniques can significantly enhance the identification process of diabetic versus non-diabetic individuals.

Over the last decade, numerous researchers have adopted machine learning techniques, as traditional statistical methods can offer valuable insights but often struggle to identify the complex structure of nonlinear relationships among variables. Machine learning techniques like random forests, support vector machines, artificial neural networks, and gradient boosting have become popular for classifying and predicting Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). Despite being a remarkable development in the field of ML classification, it still suffers from errors. The clinical dataset used to train the model may incorporate bias, particularly if there is a class imbalance with more diabetic instances than non-diabetic ones. When this occurs, it can affect the learning of the ML models, leading to misleading findings. To address this issue, a method called SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) (Chawla et al.
[
8
]
) has been introduced, which generates synthetic samples from the minority class using the nearest-neighbor approach. Several extensions of this method are also available, including Borderline-SMOTE
[
20
]
, SMOTE-Tomek Links
[
5
]
, and ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Approach for Imbalanced Learning)
[
22
]
. These methods mitigate the problem of overfitting by producing high-quality samples. However, these methods often overlook multivariate dependencies, which can lead to less realistic samples. In this context, copulas can serve as an alternative method for data generation, maintaining the dependency structure while producing new data. Copulas are widely used in finance, hydrology, and medical research to model complex dependencies and variables. We can apply this concept in machine learning to generate synthetic data that retains the original data’s structure and helps produce data for minority classes.

Sklar introduced copula theory
[
47
]
in 1959, and with further developments by Nelsen
[
40
]
in 2006. Copulas can effectively distinguish marginal distributions from dependency structures. This allows for the analysis of non-normal relationships. Various copulas are available, including Gaussian, Clayton, Gumbel, t, and Vina copulas. Recently, Aich et al.
[
2
]
presented two new Archimedean copula, initially termed the A and B copula and subsequently referred to as the A1 and A2 copula, aimed at modeling dependency. In our study, we will utilize the A2 copula to determine if we can produce quality data from the minority class that enhances the accuracy of machine learning models. These copulas have also been applied in deep learning contexts
[
3
,
4
]
.

In this study, we have used copula theory alongside machine learning (ML) techniques to classify T2DM incidents. We have selected the PIMA Indian dataset from Kaggle
[
51
]
due to its public availability; numerous papers have utilized this dataset for ML classification, and it presents an imbalanced distribution (with only 268 diabetic individuals). Initially, we applied three feature selection technique to identify the most significant features, followed by the application of copula theory to generate synthetic data from the minority class. We then implement several machine learning (ML) classification methods and compute metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the curve (AUC). Finally, we compare these results with those obtained using the traditional SMOTE method, and additionally, we use the McNemar test to statistically validate the findings. Our study revealed that synthetic data generated by copulas improves performance over the SMOTE method, with XGBoost combined with A2 copula oversampling achieving the best results, showing a 4.6% increase in accuracy, 15.6% in precision, 20.4% in recall, 18.2% in F1-score, and 25.5% in AUC.

2.  Related Work

Creating synthetic data for the minority class helps to balance the training set, reducing bias and enhancing machine learning techniques. The widely used approach for generating this synthetic data is SMOTE, introduced by Chawla et al.
[
8
]
. This method works by taking a minority instance and merging it with its nearest neighbor to create new data points. However, a limitation of this technique is its tendency to produce less diverse data points, which can sometimes lead to mislabeled data.
Incorporating data cleaning techniques with SMOTE methods like SMOTE-ENN (edited nearest neighbor) (Batista et al.
[
5
]
) and the SMOTE-Tomek link (Tomek
[
50
]
) has also been employed to eliminate borderline examples that are typically noisy. As the borderline cases are often more susceptible to misclassification. To address this issue, Han et al.
[
20
]
implemented Borderline-SMOTE, which focuses on instances close to the class boundary. An extension of SMOTE was proposed by He et al.
[
22
]
, who utilized ADASYN to adaptively create more synthetic data in areas where minority class responses are more difficult to learn. A hybrid approach, such as the K-Means-SMOTE technique proposed by Douzas and Bacao
[
14
]
, first clusters the minority samples before applying SMOTE within each cluster. This method has demonstrated enhanced classification performance. With the recent developments in computational methods and generative models, several new powerful data augmentation strategies, such as Conditional Variational Autoencoders (CVAEs)
[
34
]
, Conditional Tabular Generative Adversarial Network (CTGAN)
[
53
]
, and a GAN-based model, showed promising results. Notably, the VAE approach is capable of producing synthetic data that is tailored to specific class levels, which is advantageous for imbalanced classification (Kingma and Welling
[
34
]
). Meanwhile, CTGAN and GAN-based models effectively maintain the dependency structures of features while creating synthetic data (Xu et al.
[
53
]
).

In addition to those generative models, copula-based models have also been used to model the complex structure of features while preserving their marginal distribution. Meyer et al.
[
38
]
demonstrate that in geophysical sciences, where understanding dependency structures is crucial, a copula-based synthetic data augmentation framework can enhance model performance. Restrepo et al.
[
44
]
proposed a nonparametric copula-based method to generate features. Their research showed that the copula-based approach yields better results than the SMOTE method for imbalanced data. Houssou et al.
[
29

developed a comprehensive copula-based framework to generate and simulate synthetic datasets. Their study has shown that synthetic data generated by copula can preserve the statistical properties of the original data. Wan et al.
[
52
]
developed a framework called SynC that uses Gaussian copulas to create synthetic populations. Unlike SMOTE, which relies on simple interpolation, SynC can capture both linear and some non-linear relationships between variables. Jutras-Dubé et al.
[
33
]
presented a copula-based model capable of generating synthetic data by simulating categorical and numerical data. This approach is particularly beneficial in areas like healthcare and social sciences, as it can result in more realistic and interpretable synthetic data. Aich et al.
[
1

recently demonstrated the use of the A2 copula for feature selection. This study lays the groundwork for our current research, in which we propose a novel application of the A2 copula to create synthetic minority classes and assess the resulting improvement in accuracy and AUC. 3.2
Feature Selection Techniques

The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), proposed by Robert Tibshirani
[
49
]
in 1996, is a regression technique that applies L1 regularization. This method penalizes the absolute values of the regression coefficients, enabling feature selection by shrinking some coefficients exactly to zero. Formally, LASSO minimizes the objective function:

\hat{\beta}=\arg\min_{\beta}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-X_{i}^{\top}\beta)^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_{j}|\right\},

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a tuning parameter controlling the strength of the penalty.

Another popular optimization technique is Genetic Algorithms (GA), originally developed by Holland
[
27
]
in 1975. Inspired by the process of natural selection, GAs iteratively evolve subsets of features through mechanisms such as selection, crossover, and mutation. These algorithms are particularly effective for high-dimensional feature selection problems where exhaustive search is computationally infeasible (Goldberg, 1989).

Mutual Information (MI), introduced by Shannon
[
46
]
in 1948, measures the non-linear dependence between two variables by quantifying the reduction in uncertainty of one variable given knowledge of the other. For feature selection, MI is computed as

I(X;Y)=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in Y}p(x,y)\log\left(\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\right),

where
p(x,y)
is the joint distribution of variables
X
and
Y
, and
p(x),p(y)
are their marginal distributions. MI is widely used in feature selection because it can capture complex dependencies that go beyond simple linear correlations (Cover et al.
[
12
]
).

3.3
Copulas and Dependency Modeling

Copulas are statistical tools that model dependency structures between random variables, independent of their marginal distributions. Introduced by Sklar
[
47
]
(1959), they provide a unified approach to capturing joint dependencies. Archimedean copulas
[
18
]
, known for their simplicity and flexibility, are defined using a generator function, making them particularly effective for modeling bivariate and multivariate dependencies.

Like all Archimedean copulas, the novel A2 copula is defined through generator functions
\phi(t)
that are continuous, strictly decreasing, and convex on
[0,1]
, with
\phi(1)=0
. The A2 copula extend the Archimedean copula framework to capture both upper and lower tail dependencies more effectively. In general, an Archimedean copula is given by:

C(u,v)=\phi^{-1}\bigl(\phi(u)+\phi(v)\bigr).

(1)

For the A2 copula, the generator and its inverse are defined as:

\displaystyle\phi_{A2}(t;\theta)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{1-t}{t}\Bigr)^{\theta}(1-t)^{\theta},\quad\theta\geq 1,

(2)

\displaystyle\phi_{A2}^{-1}(t;\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{1/\theta}+2-\sqrt{\Bigl(t^{1/\theta}+2\Bigr)^{2}-4}}{2},\quad\theta\geq 1.

(3)

Copula parameterized by
\theta\geq 1
, which governs the strength and nature of the dependency. The flexibility in capturing dual tail dependencies makes them attractive for modeling complex real-world phenomena.

3.4
Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)

To address the imbalance between classes in the dataset, SMOTE is a widely used method developed by Chawla et al.
[
8
]
. Unlike simple duplication, which can lead to overfitting, SMOTE generates entirely new synthetic data for the minority class by examining the relationships between existing data points.

For each sample in the minority class, the algorithm identifies its
k
nearest neighbors (typically
k=5
). It then picks one of those neighbors at random and creates a new synthetic point between the original sample and that neighbor. The position of the new point is determined by a random factor, ensuring some variation. Mathematically, the new sample is calculated as:

x_{\text{new}}=x_{i}+\delta\cdot(x_{zi}-x_{i})

where
x_{i}
is the original minority instance,
x_{zi}
is a randomly selected nearest neighbor, and
\delta
is a random number between 0 and 1. This process allows to generate more diverse and realistic examples of the minority class. The number of synthetic samples generated can be controlled by specifying the oversampling rate (e.g., 100% to double the size of the minority class).

3.5
Machine Learning Classification Algorithms

Several machine learning algorithms are widely used in feature selection due to their effectiveness in handling high-dimensional data and offering insight into variable importance. Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble method that builds multiple decision trees using bootstrap samples and considers random subsets of features at each split. This strategy enhances robustness and reduces overfitting, while also providing built-in feature importance metrics, making RF suitable for noisy datasets
[
6
]
. Gradient Boosting (GB), another popular ensemble method, constructs models sequentially by fitting new trees to the residuals of previous ones. It minimizes a differentiable loss function using gradient descent and can deliver high accuracy, though it typically requires careful tuning to prevent overfitting
[
17
]
.

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), a refined implementation of GB, was originally developed by Chen and Guestrin
[
10
]
. It introduces regularization techniques to reduce overfitting, supports parallel computation for faster training, and efficiently handles missing or sparse data. These improvements have made XGBoost a leading choice for classification tasks, especially in imbalanced datasets. On the other hand, Logistic Regression (LR) is a simple and interpretable model that estimates class probabilities by modeling the log-odds as a linear combination of predictors. While it lacks the flexibility to model complex nonlinear patterns, LR remains a widely used baseline in binary classification tasks due to its speed, ease of implementation, and transparency
[
30
]
.

3.6
McNemar’s Test

McNemar’s test
[
37
]
is a non-parametric statistical method commonly used for comparing the performance of two classification models on the same dataset, particularly when the response variable is nominal. Unlike standard accuracy comparisons, McNemar’s test focuses on the disagreements between paired predictions, making it suitable for evaluating whether two classifiers have significantly different error rates on the same instances.

The test is based on a
2\times 2
contingency table summarizing the number of instances where the models agree or disagree in their predictions:

•

n_{11}
: Number of instances correctly classified by both models.

•

n_{00}
: Number of instances misclassified by both models.

•

n_{10}
: Instances correctly classified by Model A but misclassified by Model B.

•

n_{01}
: Instances correctly classified by Model B but misclassified by Model A.

The null hypothesis of McNemar’s test states that the two classifiers have equal performance, which translates to
n_{01}=n_{10}
. The test statistic is given by:

\chi^{2}=\frac{(|n_{01}-n_{10}|-1)^{2}}{n_{01}+n_{10}},

which approximately follows a chi-squared distribution with 1 degree of freedom. A small
p
-value (e.g., below 0.05) indicates a statistically significant difference in model performance.

This test is particularly useful in classification studies where the same data samples are evaluated under two different models, as it accounts for within-subject variability and emphasizes the cases where model predictions diverge.

4.  Methodology

In this section, we present the proposed methodology. Our objective is to introduce an alternative to SMOTE based on the A2 copula for diabetes risk prediction. Accordingly, we will first present the feature selection step, followed by the data balancing procedure based on the A2 copula.

4.1
Feature Selection

We used three complementary methods on the standardized training set to identify the most informative features for diabetes prediction.

A2 copula is a bivariate copula. In order to apply data augmentation using the A2 copula, our first step is to identify the top two most informative features. To identify the most informative features for classification, we employed three different feature selection techniques, each selecting the top two features. All methods were implemented in
Python
using the
scikit-learn
library, with additional support from
NumPy
and
pandas
.

•

L1-Regularized Logistic Regression (Lasso): We used
LogisticRegressionCV
from
scikit-learn
with an L1 penalty and the
liblinear
solver. This method performs 5-fold cross-validation to tune the regularization strength. The two features with the largest absolute coefficients were selected.

•

Mutual Information (MI): Using
mutual_info_classif
from
scikit-learn
, we computed mutual information scores between each feature and the binary outcome. The top two features with the highest MI scores were selected.

•

Genetic Algorithm (GA): A simple genetic algorithm was implemented using
NumPy
,
random
, and
scikit-learn
. The algorithm evolved 2-feature subsets over 40 generations. Each subset’s fitness was evaluated using the 5-fold cross-validated accuracy of a logistic regression classifier. Selection, crossover, and mutation operators were used to iteratively improve the population. The best-performing subset from the final generation was selected.

We then proceed with the selected features in the next step, where we performed oversampling of the minority class. New samples were generated based on the novel A2 copula.

4.2
Oversampling Based on Copula

We employ the A2 Archimedean copula to generate synthetic minority-class samples as follows:

Step 1: Generate Copula Samples

As an initial step, the copula parameter
\theta
is fixed at a value from the set
\{2,5,10\}
, reflecting different levels of dependence. Given this parameter, we generate
n=232
synthetic pairs
(u_{i},v_{i})\in(0,1)^{2}
by sampling from the A2 Archimedean copula using Algorithm
1
proposed by Genest and Rivest (1993), which ensures that the dependence structure between the variables is appropriately modeled.

Algorithm 1

(Genest & Rivest, 1993)

1.

Generate independent
s,t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
.

2.

Compute

w=K^{-1}(t),\quad K(x)=x-\frac{\phi_{A2}(x;\theta)}{\phi_{A2}^{\prime}(x;\theta)},

where
K^{-1}(t)=\sup\{x:K(x)\leq t\}
.

3.

Set

u=\phi_{A2}^{-1}(s\,\phi_{A2}(w;\theta);\theta),\quad v=\phi_{A2}^{-1}((1-s)\,\phi_{A2}(w;\theta);\theta).

Step 2: Map Copula Samples to Original Scale

Once the
n=232
pairs
(u_{i},v_{i})
are generated in the unit square via the copula sampler, we transform them to realistic clinical measurements. Specifically, we apply the inverse empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs) of each selected feature computed from the minority class subset (size
N_{1}=268
).

Specifically, the empirical CDFs
F_{1,\mathrm{emp}}
and
F_{2,\mathrm{emp}}
for the two selected features are used to map

\tilde{X}_{i}^{(1)}=F_{1,\mathrm{emp}}^{-1}(u_{i}),\quad\tilde{X}_{i}^{(2)}=F_{2,\mathrm{emp}}^{-1}(v_{i}),

where
\tilde{X}_{i}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{X}_{i}^{(2)}
denote the transformed values for the first and second selected features, respectively. In practice, this corresponds to selecting the
\lceil u_{i}(N_{1}+1)\rceil
-th smallest value for feature 1 and the
\lceil v_{i}(N_{1}+1)\rceil
-th smallest value for feature 2 in the minority class.

For the remaining features, their values for each synthetic sample
i
are drawn with replacement from the original minority class data by sampling an index
j_{i}
uniformly from
\{1,\dots,N_{1}\}
and assigning

(\tilde{X}_{i}^{(3)},\dots,\tilde{X}_{i}^{(d)})=(X_{j_{i}}^{(3)},\dots,X_{j_{i}}^{(d)}),

where
d
is the total number of features. This bootstrap procedure preserves the empirical marginal distributions of all features aside from the two selected ones, which are generated via the copula.

Finally, the synthetic records
\big(\tilde{X}_{i}^{(1)},\tilde{X}_{i}^{(2)},\tilde{X}_{i}^{(3)},\dots,\tilde{X}_{i}^{(d)},\text{Outcome}=1\big)
are appended to the original minority class data, resulting in a balanced dataset. Before model training, the combined dataset is shuffled to randomize the record order.

This approach ensures that (i) the synthetic pairs of the two selected features respect the dependence structure induced by the copula while exactly matching their marginal distributions, and (ii) the other features retain their original marginal distributions through bootstrap sampling.

Classification Performance

The classification algorithms included in our evaluation are: Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), XGBoost (XGB), and Logistic Regression (LR). All models were trained using their default hyperparameters with Logistic Regression using up to 1000 iterations. The models were evaluated on the same 30% stratified held-out test set. We report accuracy, precision, recall,
F_{1}
-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to comprehensively assess performance. We report accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to comprehensively assess performance. The evaluation metrics are computed from the confusion matrix components: true positives
TP
, true negatives
TN
, false positives
FP
, and false negatives
FN
. These metrics are defined as follows:

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN};

\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP};

\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN};

\text{F1-score}=2\times\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}};

The AUC is defined as the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve, which plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate at various classification thresholds. It can be expressed mathematically as

\text{AUC}=\int_{0}^{1}TPR(FPR)\,dFPR

where
TPR=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}
is the true positive rate and
FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}
is the false positive rate.

Across nearly all settings, A2-based synthetic data led to superior classification outcomes compared to SMOTE, particularly when paired with more flexible and nonlinear models such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost. For example, the A2 method with
\theta=10
combined with XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy of 77.3%, precision of 78.1%, and
F_{1}
-score of 77.0%, along with a strong AUC of 0.851. Similarly, Gradient Boosting and Random Forest showed improved recall and balanced performance across metrics when trained on A2-generated data, especially for
\theta=5
and
\theta=10
.
However, in the case of Logistic Regression, SMOTE slightly outperformed A2-based augmentation. For example, SMOTE combined with Logistic Regression achieved an accuracy of 76.2% and an AUC of 0.839, whereas A2-based Logistic Regression models showed consistently lower accuracy around 70.3 to 70.6% and AUC approximately 0.788 to 0.791 across all
\theta
values. This suggests that the dependence-preserving structure of the A2 copula may not align as well with the linear decision boundary assumptions of Logistic Regression.

According to Figure
5
, the A2 copula-based oversampling method consistently outperforms SMOTE in balancing precision and recall across all four classifiers. Average Precision (AP) scores improve substantially with A2, increasing from 0.717 to 0.863 for Random Forest and from 0.629 to 0.856 for XGBoost. Gradient Boosting and Logistic Regression similarly show notable gains.

In summary, the A2 copula-based method, especially at higher
\theta
values, proved to be a highly effective oversampling strategy. It improved overall classification metrics and demonstrated a consistent advantage over SMOTE in most scenarios, offering a powerful alternative for handling class imbalance in complex modeling tasks.

6.  Conclusion and Future Work

In this study, we explored a copula-based data generation method for the minority class as a principled alternative to the widely used SMOTE algorithm. Copulas can preserve the dependency structure, which makes the generated data more realistic than that produced by the traditional SMOTE method. To this end, we investigated the use of the recently introduced Archimedean copula A2, applying it to the imbalanced PIMA Indian diabetes dataset.

Our empirical results demonstrated that the A2 copula-based oversampling method led to superior classification performance compared to SMOTE, particularly when used in conjunction with advanced machine learning models such as Random Forest,
Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost. The improvement was most pronounced in accuracy, precision,
F_{1}
-score, AUC and was further supported by McNemar’s test, confirming that the observed differences were statistically significant and not due to random variation. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first application of the A2 copula in the context of data augmentation for imbalanced classification tasks.
These results highlight the potential of copula-based approaches to capture and utilize the intrinsic joint structure of minority class observations, offering a promising alternative in scenarios where SMOTE may fall short. Future work can expand this framework in several directions.

Our study focused solely on implementing data augmentation for the minority class. However, this approach could be applied to the entire training dataset. Recent studies have explored various data augmentation strategies, including generating data at rates of 2x, 5x, and 10x, and their impact on classification accuracy. Furthermore, our future objective is to compare different copula-based data augmentation techniques to identify which one is most effective in capturing the dependency structure, ultimately enhancing the diabetic diagnostic process.

One limitation of the study is that the proposed A2 copula-based method captures only bivariate dependency structures. Feature selection must be applied prior to prediction, which may cause the machine learning classification models to overlook important higher-order relationships that are important for accurate classification. As a future direction, we aim to extend the proposed oversampling approach to accommodate multivariate dependencies beyond the bivariate case, potentially enhancing predictive performance. Furthermore, applying the proposed method to diverse real-world domains with severe imbalance, such as medical diagnostics, fraud detection, or rare event prediction, would help validate its broader applicability and effectiveness.

Another future direction would be integrating the proposed copula method with the recently introduced categorical Gini correlation (CGC) for feature selection
[
13
,
23
]
. This novel dependence measure utilizes Gini’s mean difference as a robust measure of variability
[
24
,
25
]
. CGC has been applied in feature selection for classification, especially for imbalanced classes, where it has demonstrated superior performance over existing methods. The combined procedure may potentially improve classification performance on imbalanced datasets.

By following these steps, we can improve early detection of diabetes incidence and assist clinicians in their diagnoses. This will ultimately benefit not only the clinicians but also the patients in enhancing their quality of life.
