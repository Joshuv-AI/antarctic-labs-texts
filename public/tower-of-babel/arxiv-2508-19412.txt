Gabriel Acosta, Eugenia Belén, Francisco M. Bersetche, Juan Pablo Borthagaray

Abstract
. We propose a deep learning approach to the obstacle problem inspired by the first-order system least-squares (FOSLS) framework. This method reformulates the problem as a convex minimization task; by simultaneously approximating the solution, gradient, and Lagrange multiplier, our approach provides a flexible, mesh-free alternative that scales efficiently to high-dimensional settings. Key theoretical contributions include the coercivity and local Lipschitz continuity of the proposed least-squares functional, along with convergence guarantees via Γ \Gamma -convergence theory under mild regularity assumptions. Numerical experiments in dimensions up to 20 demonstrate the method’s robustness and scalability, even on non-Lipschitz domains.



1.
Introduction

Variational inequalities arise in the modeling of several nonlinear phenomena such as contact, friction, and plasticity
[
9
,
23
,
31
]
.
A classic example is the equilibrium position of an elastic membrane constrained to remain above a given obstacle. These problems are inherently challenging for numerical approaches due to the low regularity of solutions and the a priori unknown contact boundary. While extensive work has been done on numerical methods for such problems, including finite element approaches (see, for example,
[
10
,
17
,
34
]
), the exploration of least-squares formulations remains relatively limited, particularly in contexts beyond finite element methods.
We may mention
[
16
]
as one of the first works to formalize least-squares formulations for the obstacle problem. The first-order system least-squares (FOSLS) approach proposed in that reference reformulates the obstacle problem by introducing auxiliary variables, such as the Lagrange multiplier and the gradient of the solution. This transforms the obstacle problem into a convex minimization problem subject to linear constraints. The FOSLS framework has demonstrated strong potential for providing stable approximations, facilitating error estimation, and enabling adaptivity.

In parallel, deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool for approximating solutions to partial differential equations (PDEs) by minimizing loss functionals that enforce the governing equations and boundary conditions
[
15
,
18
,
26
,
27
,
30
,
32
,
36
]
. While not always competitive with classical methods in low-dimensional settings, neural networks show promise in high-dimensional contexts and for problems with complex geometries
[
14
,
35
]
. Importantly, neural network formulations based on first-order systems
[
6
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
circumvent the need for second-order derivatives in the cost functional, streamlining computation and enabling the approximation of weak solutions.

This paper bridges the gap between the two aforementioned frameworks by proposing a deep learning approach based on the FOSLS methodology for the obstacle problem. Specifically, we adapt the Deep FOSLS framework
[
6
]
to the first-order system formulation introduced in
[
16
]
. This approach provides a mesh-free alternative to traditional discretization methods that leverages the flexibility and scalability of neural networks. By simultaneously approximating the solution, the gradient, and the Lagrange multiplier, we demonstrate the efficiency and adaptability of this methodology in solving obstacle problems in high-dimensional instances. Moreover, the formulation within the Deep FOSLS framework enables the derivation of convergence results under appropriate regularity assumptions.

1.1.
Related work

Recent advancements in the literature have explored the approximation of solutions to the obstacle problem through machine learning techniques.
In
[
8
]
, the obstacle problem is reformulated as a penalized minimization problem whose solutions converge to those of the original problem as the penalty parameter approaches infinity. The authors employ neural networks to approximate these minimizers, discretizing the cost functional through Monte Carlo integration. By decomposing the total error into three components—(i) an approximation error (dependent on network depth and width), (ii) a statistical error (governed by sample size), and (iii) an optimization error (related to the empirical loss minimization)—they establish non-asymptotic convergence rates under regularity assumptions on the penalized problem’s solutions.
In
[
38
]
, the authors propose two distinct approaches for solving the obstacle problem in one and two dimensions. The first method strictly enforces the obstacle constraint through direct energy minimization, while the second employs a penalized formulation that reduces to solving a linear Dirichlet problem when the constraint is inactive. Both approaches conduct the minimization within a function space parameterized by shallow neural networks. The convergence analysis leverages the universal approximation properties of neural networks under the
L^{\infty}
-norm.
Reference
[
2
]
approaches the obstacle problem within a Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) framework, minimizing the
L^{2}
-norm of the PDE residual while strongly enforcing boundary conditions. Similarly,
[
32
]
employs an analogous residual-based loss function but incorporates boundary conditions through penalization; this approach is subsequently applied to parabolic obstacle problems arising in American options pricing.
Additionally,
[
12
]
formulates the obstacle problem as a quadratic programming problem using finite element methods, subsequently solved with a neural network model.

2.
Variational Formulation and First-Order Reformulation

This section offers a brief theoretical background on the obstacle problem and discusses the theoretical foundation for reformulating it as a first-order system, which a critical step in developing our deep learning methodology. Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be an open, bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary,
g\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. The obstacle problem consists in finding

(2.1)

u_{0}:=\arg\min_{v\in K}I(v),

where
I
is the classical Dirichlet energy functional

I\colon H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R},\quad I(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}-\int_{\Omega}fv,

and
K
the convex set

K:=\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,\colon\,v\geq g\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\}.

It is well-known that this energy-minimization problem admits a unique solution
u_{0}\in K
, and that such a solution satisfies the variational inequality

(2.2)

(\nabla u_{0},\nabla(v-u_{0}))\geq(f,v-u_{0})\quad\forall v\in K,

where the parentheses stand for the duality pairing in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Furthermore, in the following we shall denote the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm by
\|\cdot\|
.

In the space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have the well-known Poincaré inequality

(2.3)

\|v\|\leq C_{P}\|\nabla v\|\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

The constant
C_{P}
only depends on the diameter of
\Omega
. This results allows one to obtain the following simple stability bound for solutions to the obstacle problem.

Lemma 2.1

(
H^{1}
-stability)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
g\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and
u_{0}\in K
satisfy (
2.2
). Then,

(2.4)

\|\nabla u_{0}\|\leq C\left(\|f\|+\|\nabla g\|\right),

with a constant
C=C(\Omega)
.

Proof.

We use (
2.2
) with
v=g
:

\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}\leq(\nabla u_{0},\nabla g)+(f,u_{0}-g)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla g\|^{2}+\|f\|\|g\|+\|f\|\|u_{0}\|.

For some
\epsilon>0
to be chosen, we apply Young’s and Poincaré’s inequalities to obtain

\|f\|\|u_{0}\|\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{4\epsilon}+\epsilon\|u_{0}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{4\epsilon}+\epsilon C_{P}^{2}\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}.

Therefore, we deduce

\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon C_{P}^{2}\right)\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla g\|^{2}+\|f\|\|g\|+\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{4\epsilon}.

To conclude, it suffices to fix
\epsilon
such that the term in parenthesis in the left hand side is positive and apply the Poincaré’s inequality to
g\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
∎

Under additional regularity hypotheses (cf.
[
23
]
), the energy minimizer
u_{0}\in K
satisfies the
complementarity-form
equation

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \min\{-\Delta u_{0}-f,\ u_{0}-g\}=0&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u_{0}=0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

In this work, following
[
16
]
, we consider an equivalent reformulation of the obstacle problem as a first-order least squares system. For that purpose, we introduce two auxiliary variables: the flux density

{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0}:=\nabla u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and the Lagrange multiplier

\lambda_{0}:=-\Delta u_{0}-f\in H^{-1}(\Omega):=[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)]^{\prime}.

In the following, we employ angle brackets to denote the
H^{-1}(\Omega)
-
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
duality pairing.
We consider the space

\mathcal{V}:=\{{{\bm{v}}}=(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\lambda)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega;\,\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{-1}(\Omega)\,\colon\,\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}+\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},

with norm

\|(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\lambda)\|_{\mathcal{V}}:=\left(\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|{{\bm{\phi}}}\|^{2}+\|\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}+\lambda\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

introduce a least-squares functional
\mathcal{J}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(2.6)

\mathcal{J}({{\bm{q}}};f,g):=\|\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}+\lambda+f\|^{2}+\|\nabla u-{{\bm{\phi}}}\|^{2}+\langle\lambda,u-g\rangle,

and the convex set

K^{\mathcal{V}}:=\{{{\bm{v}}}=(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\lambda)\in\mathcal{V}\,\colon\,u\geq g,\ \lambda\geq 0\},

and study the minimization problem

(2.7)

{{\bm{q}}}_{0}=\arg\min_{{{\bm{v}}}\in K^{\mathcal{V}}}\mathcal{J}({{\bm{v}}};f,g).

According to
[
16
, Theorem 1]
, problems (
2.1
) and (
2.7
) are equivalent: the unique solution of (
2.7
) can be characterized as
{{\bm{q}}}_{0}=(u_{0},\nabla u_{0},-\Delta u_{0}-f)
, where
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is the unique solution to (
2.1
).

Even though the functional
\mathcal{J}
is suitable for the analysis we pursue in this paper,
our approach makes use of Monte Carlo integration; we therefore prefer to avoid the duality pairing in
\mathcal{J}
and replace it with suitable inner products in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. For the sake of rewriting
\mathcal{J}
, let us consider a triple
{{\bm{v}}}=(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\lambda)\in\mathcal{V}
. Because
\gamma:=\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}+\lambda\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we can integrate by parts to rewrite the last term in (
2.6
) as

\langle\lambda,u-g\rangle=\langle\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}},u-g\rangle=(\gamma,u-g)+({{\bm{\phi}}},\nabla(u-g)).

Therefore, we introduce the space

\mathcal{W}:=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega;\,\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\Omega)

furnished with the product norm

\|(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)\|_{\mathcal{W}}:=\left(\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|{{\bm{\phi}}}\|^{2}+\|\gamma\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

the convex set

(2.8)

K^{\mathcal{W}}:=\{{{\bm{w}}}=(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)\in\mathcal{W}\,\colon\,u\geq g,\ \gamma\geq\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}\},

and the functional
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}

(2.9)

\mathcal{L}({{\bm{w}}};f,g):=\|\gamma+f\|^{2}+\|\nabla u-{{\bm{\phi}}}\|^{2}+(\gamma,u-g)+({{\bm{\phi}}},\nabla(u-g)).

We are interested in the minimization problem

(2.10)

{{\bm{p}}}_{0}=\arg\min_{{{\bm{w}}}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}}\mathcal{L}({{\bm{w}}};f,g).

It is clear that problems (
2.7
) and (
2.10
) are equivalent. Indeed, we can define the bijective, norm-preserving mapping

(2.11)

\mathcal{V}\ni(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\lambda)\mapsto(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}+\lambda)\in\mathcal{W};

we point out that the restriction of this map to
K^{\mathcal{V}}
is onto
K^{\mathcal{W}}
. The equality

\mathcal{J}(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\lambda)=\mathcal{L}(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}+\lambda)
follows immediately from the definitions of both functionals.
Thus,
\mathcal{L}
has a unique minimizer in
K^{\mathcal{W}}
and it is given by
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}:=(u_{0},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0},\gamma_{0}),
where
{{\bm{q}}}_{0}=(u_{0},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0},\lambda_{0})
is the minimizer of
\mathcal{J}
in
K^{\mathcal{V}}
, and
\gamma_{0}:=\lambda_{0}+\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0}
. Recalling that
{{\bm{q}}}_{0}=(u_{0},\nabla u_{0},-\Delta u_{0}-f)
, we obtain
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}=(u_{0},\nabla u_{0},-f)
.

The following theorem yields the coercivity of
\mathcal{L}
over
K^{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proposition 2.1

(coercivity)
.

Let
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
satisfy (
2.10
). Then, there exists some
C=C(\Omega)>0
such that

\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}};f,g)\geq C\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}\quad\forall{{\bm{p}}}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}.

Proof.

This is a rewriting of
[
16
, Theorem 1]
in terms of the admissibility class
K^{\mathcal{W}}
. Indeed, such a result states that if
{{\bm{q}}}_{0}\in K^{\mathcal{V}}
solves (
2.7
), then

\mathcal{J}({{\bm{q}}};f,g)\geq C\|{{\bm{q}}}-{{\bm{q}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}\quad\forall{{\bm{q}}}\in K^{\mathcal{V}}.

Given
{{\bm{p}}}=(v,{{\bm{\tau}}},\xi)\in K^{\mathcal{W}},
we use (
2.11
) to introduce
{{\bm{q}}}=(v,{{\bm{\tau}}},\xi-\Div{{\bm{\tau}}})\in K^{\mathcal{V}}
and deduce

\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}};f,g)=\mathcal{J}({{\bm{q}}};f,g)\geq C\|{{\bm{q}}}-{{\bm{q}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}=C\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}.

∎

We next prove the local Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{L}
around the minimizer
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}
.

Proposition 2.2

(local Lipschitz continuity)
.

Let
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
satisfy (
2.10
). Then, there exists some
C=C(\Omega,f,g)>0
such that

\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}};f,g)\leq C(1+\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}})\ \|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}\quad\forall{{\bm{p}}}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}.

Proof.

Let
{{\bm{p}}}=(v,{{\bm{\tau}}},\xi)\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
. Then,

\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}};f,g)=\|\xi+f\|^{2}+\|\nabla v-{{\bm{\tau}}}\|^{2}+(\xi,v-g)+({{\bm{\tau}}},\nabla(v-g)).

The unique minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
in
K^{\mathcal{W}}
is

{{\bm{p}}}_{0}:=(u_{0},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0},\gamma_{0})=(u_{0},\nabla u_{0},-f),

where
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is the solution of the obstacle problem, and therefore

(-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0}+\gamma_{0})(u_{0}-g)=0\quad\mbox{a.e. in }\Omega.

Using these properties, we can write

(2.12)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}};f,g)=&\|\xi-\gamma_{0}\|^{2}+\|\nabla(v-u_{0})-({{\bm{\tau}}}-{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0})\|^{2}\\
&+(\xi,v-g)-(\gamma_{0},u_{0}-g)+(\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0},u_{0}-g)+({{\bm{\tau}}},\nabla(v-g)).\end{split}

On the one hand, it is clear that

\|\xi-\gamma_{0}\|^{2}+\|\nabla(v-u_{0})-({{\bm{\tau}}}-{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0})\|^{2}\leq C\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}.

On the other hand, we can bound

\begin{split}|(\xi,v-g)-(\gamma_{0},u_{0}-g)|&=|(\xi,v-u_{0})+(\xi-\gamma_{0},u_{0}-g)|\leq\|\xi\|\|v-u_{0}\|+\|\xi-\gamma_{0}\|\|u_{0}-g\|\\
&\leq\|\xi-\gamma_{0}\|\|v-u_{0}\|+\|\gamma_{0}\|\|v-u_{0}\|+\|\xi-\gamma_{0}\|\|u_{0}-g\|\\
&\leq\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}+(\|\gamma_{0}\|+\|u_{0}-g\|)\,\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}},\end{split}

and similarly

\begin{split}|(\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0},u_{0}-g)+({{\bm{\tau}}},\nabla(v-g))|\leq\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}+(\|{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0}\|+\|u_{0}-g\|)\,\|{{\bm{p}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}.\end{split}

We recall
\gamma_{0}=-f
and
{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0}=-\nabla u_{0}
. Because
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we can collect the estimates above and apply the Poincaré inequality together with (
2.4
) to conclude the desired result.
∎

3.
Description of the method

We propose a method to approximate the unique minimizer
(u_{0},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{0},\gamma_{0})
of the functional in (
2.9
) by using neural networks. A natural, direct approach involves seeking a set of parameters
{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\mathcal{L}(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{0}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{0}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{0}})=\min_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathcal{L}(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}),

with functions
(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}})
belonging to a suitable neural network space.
However, a critical challenge arises: the neural network approximations
(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}})
are not guaranteed a priori to satisfy the admissibility constraints
u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}\geq g
,
\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}\geq\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}
and the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}
.
It is a common practice in neural network approaches to weakly impose such constraints through penalty terms added to the loss functional.
In contrast, our method enforces these conditions strongly by design, ensuring strict adherence throughout the optimization process. For boundary conditions, we adopt the strategy proposed in
[
5
]
, where pre-training the network to explicitly satisfy boundary data reduces the number of iterations needed for convergence.

As discussed in Section
3.1
below, we initially create certain auxiliary functions to ensure
(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}})\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
for all
{{\bm{\Theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The optimization procedure involves sampling
N
points
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset\Omega
and approximating

\mathcal{L}(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}})\approx L_{N}(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}),

at each step of a gradient descent algorithm, where
L_{N}
is defined as

(3.1)

\begin{split}L_{N}(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma):=\frac{|\Omega|}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ &\big(\gamma+f(x_{k})\big)^{2}+\big|\nabla u(x_{k})-{{\bm{\phi}}}(x_{k})\big|^{2}\\
&+\gamma(x_{k})\big(u(x_{k})-g(x_{k})\big)+{{\bm{\phi}}}(x_{k})\cdot\big(\nabla u(x_{k})-\nabla g(x_{k})\big).\end{split}

We expose the details below.

3.1.
Strong imposition of boundary and admissibility conditions

We follow ideas from
[
5
,
6
,
33
]
about the imposition of the boundary conditions and restrictions. Instead of trying to directly compute either
u
or
\gamma
and incorporate boundary conditions or restrictions by a penalization term, we shall enforce them in the construction of the neural network approximations. For that purpose, we make use of the following notion.

Definition 3.1

(surrogate distance function)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\overline{\partial\Omega}
be a closed set. We say that a Lipschitz continuous function
d_{\Gamma}\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is a surrogate distance function if there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

c_{1}d_{\Gamma}(x)\leq dist(x,\Gamma)\leq c_{2}d_{\Gamma}(x)
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
.

Notice that the function
dist(x,\Gamma)
is itself a surrogate distance function. However, since an explicit expression for
dist(x,\Gamma)
is seldom available, we prefer to state our results in terms of a computable substitute
d_{\Gamma}(x)
.
Keeping that in mind, we briefly comment on the use of surrogate distance functions in the strong imposition of admissibility and Dirichlet boundary conditions at a continuous level. Consider a Lipschitz continuous function
a\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
such that
a(t)=0
if
t=0
. In the computation of
u
in either (
2.6
) or (
2.9
), we restrict the class of functions to be

(3.2)

u(x):=g(x)+d_{\partial\Omega}(x)\,a(v(x)),

where the unknown is the function
v\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
g
is the obstacle, and
d_{\partial\Omega}
is a surrogate distance function to
\partial\Omega
.
In a similar fashion, we can incorporate the condition
\lambda\geq 0
by considering

(3.3)

\gamma(x):=\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}(x)+a(\eta(x)),

and the new unknown is the function
\eta\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that, in this setting,
a(\eta)
plays the role of the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
.
Therefore, we can seek
{{\bm{y}}}=(v,{{\bm{\psi}}},\eta)
such that the corresponding tuple
(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)
, with
u
given by (
3.2
),
{{\bm{\psi}}}={{\bm{\phi}}}
, and
\gamma
according to (
3.3
), minimizes the loss function
\mathcal{L}
. The distinction in notation for the flux variable –employing
{{\bm{\psi}}}
rather than
{{\bm{\phi}}}
– serves to differentiate its role within two distinct contexts. Specifically,
{{\bm{\psi}}}
denotes a component of the triple
(v,{{\bm{\psi}}},\eta)
, which belongs to an unconstrained function space, whereas the notation
(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)
is reserved for admissible triples satisfying suitable constraints.

3.2.
Computational aspects

In the following, we assume the user has provided a surrogate distance function as in Definition
3.1
. For a comprehensive analysis on the construction of such functions, even with higher differentiability properties than those required in this work, we refer to
[
33
]
. The surrogate distance can also be approximated by a neural network, as described in
[
6
]
. We consider a set of random points
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset\Omega
and aim to minimize the cost functional
L_{N}
introduced in (
3.1
). The trainable parameters
{{\bm{\Theta}}}
emerge in the computation of the auxiliary functions
v
and
\eta
from the construction of
u
and
\gamma
(see (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)).

In broad terms, the method we propose can be summarized as follows.
Until some stop criterion is reached, do:

•

Sample random points
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subset\Omega
.

•

Update parameters:

{{\bm{\Theta}}}\leftarrow{{\bm{\Theta}}}-\ell\nabla_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}L_{N}(u_{{\bm{\Theta}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{\bm{\Theta}}},\gamma_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}).

•

Adjust learning rate
\ell
.

The computation of
L_{N}(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\eta)
requires evaluating the derivatives of
u
and
{{\bm{\phi}}}
with respect to the input variables at
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
.
Since all the involved functions are smooth, these derivatives can be efficiently computed by using back propagation.

For the numerical examples, we implemented our algorithm within the PyTorch library. We utilized one- to three-layer neural networks with a SoftPlus activation function for the main variables
v
and
{{\bm{\psi}}}
. A piecewise constant neural network was used for the variable
\eta
and a three-layer network with a ReLU activation function was employed for high-dimensional examples. The ADAM
[
24
]
optimization algorithm demonstrated good results in numerical experiments. Further details about the implementation of the method can be found in Section
5
.

4.
Convergence analysis

We consider a set of unconstrained neural network functions

(4.1)

\begin{split}C_{m}:=\Big\{(v_{{\bm{\Theta}}},{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{\bm{\Theta}}},\eta_{{\bm{\Theta}}}):\ &v_{{\bm{\Theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ {{\bm{\psi}}}_{{\bm{\Theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\ \eta_{{\bm{\Theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\Big\},\end{split}

where we collect all the parameters in a vector
{{\bm{\Theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The choice of the neural network architecture is intentionally left ambiguous at this point. In what follows, we will assume certain approximation properties of this set and later focus on a specific instance of
C_{m}
and a variant from it.

Assuming that we are able to construct a surrogate auxiliary function
d_{\partial\Omega}
as in Definition
3.1
, and considering a suitable choice of
a:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
, we define the set of discrete admissible functions

(4.2)

\begin{split}W_{m}:=\Big\{{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}=(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{\bm{\Theta}}}):\ &u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}=g+d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}),\ {{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}={{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\\
&\gamma_{{\bm{\Theta}}}=\Div{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}+a(\eta_{{\bm{\Theta}}}),\quad(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\eta_{{\bm{\Theta}}})\in C_{m}\Big\},\end{split}

which we regard as a subset of
\mathcal{W}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega;\,\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and therefore furnish with the norm
\|(u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\gamma_{{\bm{\Theta}}})\|_{\mathcal{W}}=(\|\nabla u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}\|^{2}+\|{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}\|^{2}+\|\gamma_{{\bm{\Theta}}}\|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
We remark that the fulfillment of the boundary and admissibility conditions is guaranteed within the set
W_{m}
, in the sense that
u_{{\bm{\Theta}}}=0
whenever
d_{\partial\Omega}=0
, and moreover
u_{{\bm{\Theta}}}\geq g
, and
\gamma_{{\bm{\Theta}}}-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}_{{\bm{\Theta}}}\geq 0
.

4.1.
\Gamma
-convergence

We seek to prove the convergence of the neural network approximations obtained through our method to the minimizers of the least-squares functional
\mathcal{L}
in (
2.9
). To this end, we employ
\Gamma
-convergence theory, which guarantees that if a sequence of functionals
\Gamma
-converges and their minimizers converge, then solutions to the limit problem exist, along with the convergence of both minimum values and minimizers. Below, we provide a brief review of
\Gamma
-convergence and some key results relevant to this work. We refer to
[
7
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the subject.

Definition 4.1

(sequential
\Gamma
-convergence)
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
F_{n}
,
F:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=[-\infty,+\infty]
. We say that
F_{n}

\Gamma
-converges to
F
(and write
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}F
) if, for every
x\in X
we have

•

(lim-inf inequality)
for every sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
converging to
x
,

F(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x_{n});

•

(lim-sup inequality)
there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
x
such that

F(x)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x_{n}).

Definition 4.2

(equi-coercivity)
.

Let
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of functions
F_{n}:X\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. We say that
\{F_{n}\}
is equi-coercive if for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
there
exists a compact set
K_{t}\subset X
such that
\{F_{n}\leq t\}\subset K_{t}
.

Our primary motivation for employing
\Gamma
-convergence theory stems from the following result, which states that if a sequence of functionals is equi-coercive and
\Gamma
-converges, then any corresponding sequence of minimizers converges to a minimizer of the

\Gamma
-limit functional. In essence,
\Gamma
-convergence, together with an appropriate compactness condition, ensures the convergence of minimizers.

Theorem 4.1

(fundamental theorem of
\Gamma
-convergence)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an equi-coercive sequence of functions on
X
, and
F
be such that
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}F
. Then,

\exists\min_{X}F=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{X}F_{n}.

Moreover, if
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a precompact sequence in
X
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{X}F_{n}
, then every
limit of a subsequence of
\{x_{n}\}
is a minimum point for
F
.

Regularization plays an instrumental role in neural network approximations: one trades a small decrease in training accuracy for better generalization, thus reducing overfitting.
In the context of numerical PDEs, regularization avoids convergence towards non-physical (in our setting, non-variational), almost everywhere solutions

On the other hand, we recall that in the admissible class

We split the proof of convergence of our method into several steps.
We start by proving the following auxiliary lemma, that shows certain boundedness properties of the functions in (

Lemma 4.1

(boundedness)
.

Let

be defined as in (

4.2
), and fix

according to (

Then, under Assumption
4.1
, it holds that
G_{3},G_{4}\in L^{\infty}(B(0,R)\times\Omega)
. Moreover, there exist

for all
(\mathbf{\Theta},x)\in\overline{B(0,R)}\times\Omega

Proof.

Consider a triple
(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}},\eta_{{\bm{\Theta}}})\in C_{m}
, where

is the set of unconstrained neural network functions, cf. (

). We begin by considering the map

.
Under Assumption
4.1
, namely that

possesses Lipschitz continuous activation functions, it follows that
\mathscr{F}
itself is Lipschitz continuous. Consequently,
\mathscr{F}
is bounded on
{B(0,R)}\times\Omega

\mathscr{G}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
via
\mathscr{G}({{\bm{\Theta}}},x):={{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}(x)
, we deduce that
\mathscr{G}
exhibits the same boundedness properties, with essentially bounded derivatives over
{B(0,R)}\times\Omega
. Furthermore, considering

yields its essential boundedness on

The function

can be constructed using

and

is a surrogate distance function and

. Similarly, because

over the same domain follows.

Next, the relation

yields the estimate

G_{1}({{\bm{\Theta}}},x)\leq 2|\mathscr{H}({{\bm{\Theta}}},x)|^{2}+2|f(x)|^{2}\leq 2M+2|f(x)|^{2}=:s_{1}(x),

for some constant
M>0
.
Thus, the inclusion
s_{1}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
follows from our assumption

. A similar argument allows us to conclude that there exists
s_{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying
G_{2}({{\bm{\Theta}}},x)\leq s_{2}(x)
for all
({{\bm{\Theta}}},x)\in{B(0,R)}\times\Omega
.
∎

Our goal is to approximate the minimizer
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}
of
\mathcal{L}
in

, which corresponds to the solution of the obstacle problem, using minimizers of the same functional over the set of admissible neural network functions

. However, in practice, the exact evaluation of

is unattainable due to the reliance on numerical integration. Consequently, instead of approximating

through the minimizers of

in

, the following lemma targets accounting for the error introduced in the computation of the neural network minimizers.

Lemma 4.2

(approximation with
W_{m}
)
.

For every

, we define the set of neural network quasi-minimizers

\mathcal{I}_{m}:=\{{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}\in W_{m}:\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}})\leq\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}})+1/m\quad\forall{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}\in W_{m}\}.

Then, under Assumption
4.1
, if
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
is the unique minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
in

, we have

\sup_{{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}\in\mathcal{I}_{m}}\|{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }m\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}\in W_{m}
be arbitrary and
{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
be the minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
.
From Propositions
2.1
and
2.2

(4.6)

\alpha\|{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}};f,g)\leq\beta(1+\|{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}})\ \|{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}

for some positive constants

.

Let

. By assumption (

), we consider

such that
d({{\bm{p}}}_{0},W_{m})<\varepsilon
and
1/m<\varepsilon
for all

\begin{split}\alpha\|{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}-{{\bm{p}}}_{0}\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}&\leq\mathcal{L}({{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}};f,g)\\

∎

The previous result is of theoretical interest, as it involves the continuous functional

Lemma 4.3

(almost sure pointwise convergence)

Let

Because the parameter

According to Definition

(convergence)

4.3.
Approximation properties of neural networks

The convergence results from the previous section are based on the four conditions from Assumption
4.1

In this section, we show certain constructions of the set

that satisfy the structural condition 2) from Assumption

and, for them, we prove that hypotheses (

) and (

) hold.

4.3.1.
A first example

For the sake of clarity, we shall restrict our analysis to fully-connected, feedforward neural networks having one hidden layer with

Accordingly, we consider the following set of unconstrained neural network functions,

C_{m}:=\Big\{(v_{{\bm{\Theta}}},{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{\bm{\Theta}}},\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}):v_{{\bm{\Theta}}}=B_{v}\sigma(A_{v}x+c_{v}),\,{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{\bm{\Theta}}}=B_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}\sigma(A_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}x+c_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}),\,\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}}=B_{\eta}\sigma(A_{\eta}x+c_{\eta})\Big\}.

Above
A_{v},A_{{{\bm{\psi}}}},A_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
c_{v},c_{{{\bm{\psi}}}},c_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
,
B_{v},B_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
,
B_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, and
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\sigma
a Lipschitz continuous, non-polynomial activation function, applied coordinate-wise. We gather all parameters in
{{\bm{\Theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m=n(4d+5)
. Note that, in this setting, stating
m\to\infty
is equivalent to expressing that the number of neurons
n
increases without bound. Furthermore, in this section we shall operate either under the choice

(4.11)

a(t):=\text{ReLU}(t)=\max\{0,t\}.

The approximation properties of our spaces are proved in Proposition

below. Before addressing this issue, we note that the strong imposition of the homogenous boundary condition in (
3.2
), calls for an appropriate understanding of the space to which the function
\frac{u-g}{d_{\Omega}}
belongs. This is the purpose of the following definition and the subsequent two lemmas; we point out that the Lipschitz condition on
\Omega
introduced in Assumption
4.1
is instrumental for the validity of our discussion.

Definition 4.3

(weighted
L^{2}
space)
.

Let
d_{\partial\Omega}
be a surrogate distance function (Definition
3.1
). For
\alpha\geq 0
, we introduce the weighted
L^{2}
space

L^{2}(\Omega,\alpha):=\{w\colon\ d_{\partial\Omega}^{\alpha}w\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},

equipped with the norm
\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\alpha)}:=\|d_{\partial\Omega}^{\alpha}w\|

and its Sobolev extension

H^{1}(\Omega,\alpha,\beta):=\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega,\alpha)\colon\ \nabla w\in L^{2}(\Omega,\beta)\},

with the induced norm
\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,\alpha,\beta)}:=\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\alpha)}+\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega,\beta)}
.

It is clear that the definition above is not dependent on the particular choice of
d_{\partial\Omega}
, as all resulting spaces turn out to be equivalent.
The following result is implicitly given in
[
25
]
but not stated in the form needed in this article. For completeness, we outline the proof.

Lemma 4.4

(density)
.

The set
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is dense in
H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)
.

Proof.

Taking
k=1
,
\varepsilon=2
and
p=2
, in
[
25
, Proposition 9.6]
, we first note that
H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)=H^{1}(\Omega,1,1)
. Combining this with the identity
\overline{C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}^{\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,1,1)}}=H^{1}(\Omega,1,1)
shown in
[
25
, Theorem 7.2]
, we deduce the claimed density.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(embedding)
.

For all
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, it holds that
w/d_{\partial\Omega}\in H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)
and

\|w/d_{\partial\Omega}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)}\leq C\|w\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

with a constant depending on
\Omega
and the choice of
d_{\partial\Omega}
.

Proof.

Let
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. On the one hand, Hardy’s inequality readily gives

(4.12)

\|w/d_{\partial\Omega}\|^{2}\leq C(\Omega)\|\nabla w\|^{2}.

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\|d_{\partial\Omega}\nabla\big(w/d_{\partial\Omega}\big)\|^{2}&=\int_{\Omega}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2}\left(\frac{\nabla w}{d_{\partial\Omega}}-\frac{w\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}}{d_{\partial\Omega}^{2}}\right)^{2}\\
&\leq 2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}+\frac{w^{2}|\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}|^{2}}{d_{\partial\Omega}^{2}}\\
&\leq C(\Omega,\|\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)})\|\nabla w\|^{2},\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from (
4.12
) and the uniform boundedness of
|\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}|
.
∎

Our choice of
a
in (
4.11
) gives rise to the following well-known result. While its proof is standard, we include it here for completeness.

Lemma 4.6

(convergence of composition)
.

Let
a
be given by (
4.11
) and let
\beta\geq 0
. If
w_{m}\to w
in
H^{1}(\Omega,0,\beta)
, then
a(w_{m})\to a(w)
in
H^{1}(\Omega,0,\beta)
.

Proof.

By the Lipschitz continuity of
a
, it is easy to observe that
a(w_{m})\to a(w)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
:

\|a(w_{m})-a(w)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|w_{m}-w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

To prove the convergence of the gradients, we note
\nabla(a(w))=\chi_{\{w>0\}}\nabla w
and write

(4.13)

\nabla(a(w_{m}))-\nabla(a(w))=\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}\nabla(w_{m}-w)+\left(\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}-\chi_{\{w>0\}}\right)\nabla w.

It is clear that the first term tends to
0
in
L^{2}(\Omega,\beta)
:

\int_{\Omega}|\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}\nabla(w_{m}-w)|^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2\beta}\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(w_{m}-w)|^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2\beta}\to 0.

For the second term in (
4.13
), since
\nabla w=0
a.e. on the set
\{w=0\}
, we can write

\int_{\Omega}\left|\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}-\chi_{\{w>0\}}\right|\,|\nabla w|^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2\beta}=\int_{\{w\neq 0\}}\left|\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}-\chi_{\{w>0\}}\right|\,|\nabla w|^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2\beta}.

Over the set
\{w\neq 0\}
and up to a subsequence, we have
w_{m}\to w
a.e., and by the continuity of
a^{\prime}(t)=\chi_{\{t>0\}}
for
t\neq 0
, we deduce
\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}\to\chi_{\{w>0\}}
a.e. on
\{w\neq 0\}
. Since the integrand is bounded by
|\nabla w|^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2\beta}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, we can therefore apply the dominated convergence theorem to conclude that

\int_{\Omega}\left|\chi_{\{w_{m}>0\}}-\chi_{\{w>0\}}\right|\,|\nabla w|^{2}d_{\partial\Omega}^{2\beta}\to 0.

∎

We have collected all the ingredients to show that the choice (

)–(

).

Proposition 4.1

(approximation)
.

Let the structural condition 1) in Assumption
4.1
hold, and the neural network classes be constructed according to (
4.2
) by using (
4.10
) and (
4.11
). Then, for all
{{\bm{p}}}\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
, we have
d({{\bm{p}}},W_{m})\to 0
as
m\to\infty
; in particular, (
H1
) holds.

Proof.

First, we recall that the desired approximation property is expressed with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{W}}
, given by
\|(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)\|_{\mathcal{W}}=\left(\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|{{\bm{\phi}}}\|^{2}+\|\gamma\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.

Let
{{\bm{p}}}=(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
.
Since
u-g\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, Lemma
4.5
yields
(u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}\in H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)
. Thanks to Lemma
4.4
, using classical results on
H^{1}
approximation
[
29
, Section 6]
, along with the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)}\leq C(\Omega)\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, we can ensure the existence of a sequence of weights
\{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\|(u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)}\to 0\quad\text{as }m\to\infty.

By Lemma
4.6
, we deduce
a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\to a\left((u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}\right)
in
H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)
. But, as
(u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}\geq 0
it follows that
a(w_{m})\to(u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}
in
H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)
.
Thus, we can guarantee the existence of a sequence
\{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(4.14)

\|(u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)}\to 0\quad\text{as }m\to\infty.

We now aim to prove that
\|\nabla\left(u-g-d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\right)\|\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. To this end, we write

\nabla\left(u-g-d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\right)=\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}\left((u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\right)+d_{\partial\Omega}\nabla\left((u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\right),

so that

\begin{split}\|\nabla\big(u-g-d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\big)\|&\leq\|\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}\big((u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\big)\|+\|d_{\partial\Omega}\nabla\big((u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\big)\|\\
&\leq C\|(u-g)/d_{\partial\Omega}-a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}^{m}_{v}})\|_{H^{1}(\Omega,0,1)},\end{split}

with a constant
C>0
depending on
\|\nabla d_{\partial\Omega}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. Using (
4.14
), we deduce that

\|\nabla\left(u-g-d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{v}^{m}})\right)\|\to 0\ \mbox{as }m\to\infty,

which, naming
u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{v}^{m}}:=g+d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{v}^{m}})
according to (
4.2
), yields
u_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{v}^{m}}\to u
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Following similar but simpler arguments, we construct the sequences
\{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}}\to{{\bm{\phi}}}
in
H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}}\to\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Clearly, the former yields
\|{{\bm{\phi}}}-{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}}\|\to 0
and
\|\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}-\Div{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}}\|\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. Furthermore, we can estimate

(4.15)

\|\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}}-a(\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}})\|\leq\|\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}-a(\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}})\|+\|\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}-\Div{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}}\|.

Since
{{\bm{p}}}=(u,{{\bm{\phi}}},\gamma)\in K^{\mathcal{W}}
, we have
\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}\geq 0
. Additionally, as
a(t)=\max\{0,t\}
, and
\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}}\to\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, we deduce that
\|\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\phi}}}-a(\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}})\|\to 0
as
m\to\infty
. Combining this with estimate (
4.15
), we conclude that
\|\gamma-\Div{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{{\bm{\psi}}}}^{m}}-a(\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}})\|\to 0
.

By taking
\{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}=\{({{\bm{\Theta}}}_{v}^{m},{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{\bm{\psi}}}^{m},{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m})\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
and defining

{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}:=\left(g+d_{\partial\Omega}a(v_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{v}^{m}}),{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{\bm{\psi}}}^{m}},\Div{{\bm{\psi}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{{\bm{\psi}}}^{m}}+\eta_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{\eta}^{m}}\right),

we find that
{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}\in W^{m}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
{{\bm{p}}}_{{{\bm{\Theta}}}_{m}}\to{{\bm{p}}}
as
m\to\infty
in
\mathcal{W}
.
∎

4.3.2.
A second example.

In proving Proposition
4.1

We now focus on the specific construction of the neural network to compute the function

in the least squares functional (

) through the relation

. More precisely,
the remainder of this section provides an analysis of

approximation properties and stability –in the sense of hypotheses (

Regarding the approximation property (
H1

L^{2}
approximation property we need. There are several by now classical results

[
11
,
21
,
3
]

regarding the approximation properties of
neural networks, although without the
incorporation of boundary conditions.
We additionally point out to
[
13
,
19
,
20
,
37
]
for recent results concerning upper and lower bounds for the expressive power of deep ReLU networks in the context of approximation in Sobolev spaces.
