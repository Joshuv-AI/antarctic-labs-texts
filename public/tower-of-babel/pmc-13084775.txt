How effective coverage has been used to measure quality in maternal and child health services in sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Ferguson Saapiire, Richard N. Dogoli, Francis Agbokey, Kwaku Poku Asante, Frank Baiden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate how effective coverage has been used to measure healthcare quality of maternal and child health services in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).DesignScoping review.Data sourcesWe systematically searched five indexed databases and grey literature for studies published from 1 January 2000 to 30 April 2025.Eligibility criteriaAll articles on global policy and those conducted in SSA were considered. All original articles that reported measuring effective coverage based on the WHO framework for maternal, newborn and child health were included in the review. Abstracts presented at meetings and conferences were eligible for inclusion. Other studies on ‘Health care quality’ were also considered.Data extraction and synthesisWe charted study characteristics, Maternal and Child Health (MCH) services, routine use and quality domains and compared crude service coverage with effective coverage. A descriptive synthesis and thematic analysis were conducted.Results21 studies met the inclusion criteria; 66.7% (n=14) were from East Africa, indicating poor and disproportionate use of effective coverage across SSA. Most studies on effective coverage used cross-sectional (n=19, 90.5%) and ex-post (n=12, 57.1%) data. Effective coverage lagged behind CC, thus revealing quality gaps. Despite the failures of health systems to adopt routine measurement of effective coverage, few studies (n=4) relied on data from national health registries to explore effective coverage. The review also noted mixed inequities in effective coverage with women from wealthier groups often benefiting the most in institutional deliveries as compared with their poor counterparts. This varied in the uptake in the case of antenatal and postnatal care services among users. The review has also revealed variability in the quality measures used to compute effective coverage.ConclusionsEffective coverage is critical for evaluating MCH service quality in SSA. Its underuse signals the need for stronger data systems, integrated quality assessment frameworks and standardised quality measures.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Effective coverage is a proportion of mothers and children in need of a service that had a positive health outcome from the service. It is a key indicator for measuring health system performance. Most studies in low- and middle-income countries assess input and quality-adjusted coverage. Few studies measure all steps of the WHO effective coverage cascade or assess equity. To the best of our knowledge, no evidence synthesis on how effective coverage has been used to measure quality in Sub-Saharan Africa, where unacceptable quality gaps exist.

Background

Maternal and Child Health (MCH) impact indicators such as maternal and child mortalities remain unacceptably high, despite significant progress in global and regional health initiatives.1 Globally, the maternal mortality ratio has decreased by 40% between 2000 and 2023.2 Yet, disparities remain stark with Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) amid the gains, accounting for 70% of global maternal deaths3 and 58% of global child deaths.4 This slow progress indicates that improved access to care alone has not translated into better health outcomes. The region is projected to fall short of achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.1, which targets fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births by 2030.2 These persistent challenges call for urgent and targeted investments to ensure that all women have access to the care, thus quality and effective care they need to accelerate progress towards global MCH targets.

Traditionally, service coverage has guided the implementation of MCH services, which primarily focus on determining the proportion of the population who have access to health interventions without accounting for service quality.5 Although service coverage disaggregates coverage into five cascade steps: availability, accessibility, acceptability, contact and quality to identify where service drop-offs occur,6 existing indicators (antenatal and postnatal care (PNC) visits, skilled birth attendance (SBA), child immunisation coverage, family planning (FP) coverage, prevention of mother-to-child transmission coverage, care-seeking for pneumonia in children under 5, oral rehydration therapy use for diarrhoea in children under 5, malaria prevention through insecticide-treated nets or intermittent preventive therapy, etc) lack the quality index needed to better monitor the quality of services.7

Effective coverage, in contrast, goes beyond measuring service contact or utilisation (service or crude coverage (cc)) to assess whether the care provided is of sufficient quality to produce the intended health outcomes.8 It integrates the dimensions of need, use and quality, providing a more comprehensive measure of health system performance.5 Thus, contact-based indicators alone often fail to capture the effectiveness of MCH services.9 As a result, this lack of quality index metrics may have made it more challenging to focus on and earmark resources for MCH care activities in SSA.5 10 Notably, in recent years, maternal and newborn deaths are often more associated with poor quality of care than insufficient access.11

In the era where access to services was extremely limited, service coverage was quite an important indicator12 and was key to monitoring the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) 4&5 initiatives.13 The idea was to reach many people with access to health services. To a large extent, in many parts of SSA, access to health services has been achieved through many community-based approaches, including the introduction of Community-based Planning and Health Services, National Health Insurance Schemes and free maternal policy. These approaches have positively impacted the reduction of adverse MCH outcomes in many settings in SSA.14 However, a further push is needed to bring these key MCH indicators (eg, maternal, newborn and child mortalities) down to more acceptable levels, which will necessitate a more in-depth assessment of the capacity of health systems to deliver effective services. It will also require a much closer look into the quality of care, even when the client makes the expected contacts (eg, eight or more antenatal care (ANC) contacts) with the service. Therefore, focusing solely on service coverage is insufficient, with experts proposing effective coverage concept adoption. In doing so, this will enable health systems to understand operational bottlenecks and guide quality improvements. The recommendations are more centred on measurement frameworks that incorporate input-adjusted, quality-adjusted, user-adherence adjusted and outcome-adjusted metrics.7

A review by the WHO/UNICEF Effective Coverage Think Tank Group8 in 2019 defined an approach to include seven (7) cascade steps, representing the proportion of a population in need and using a service that yields a positive outcome.15 These cascade steps include: defining a definite population in need, service contact coverage, intervention coverage, input-adjusted coverage, quality-adjusted coverage, user-adherence adjusted coverage and outcome-adjusted coverage. Effective coverage evaluates not only service availability and use, but also the quality and impact of health services.16 It also assesses how well health interventions translate into real benefits by combining needs, use and quality as a single indicator.17

Effective coverage has been well-integrated into most developed healthcare systems.8 It has been a key indicator of the Mexican Institute of Social Security for assessing health system performance.18 This has also been used often by the United States Agency for International Development and others to evaluate MCH services in SSA.19 20

While effective coverage has been explored widely by stakeholders, little can be said about how it has been used routinely to monitor the quality of MCH services. It is also evident that nationally representative data (ex-post) from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), Multi-Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) and other facility and household surveys have been largely used to explore effective coverage.21 In contrast, information on the use of routine monitoring data and others like Service Availability and Readiness Assessment (SARA), Service Provision Assessment (SPA) (ex-ante) to explore this framework is scanty.

Whereas the approach was first applied in 2005, it remains underused in SSA with numerous systemic challenges.22 As a result, its application in monitoring and evaluating MCH services in SSA is not well understood. This scoping review aims to map the existing evidence on how effective coverage has been used in SSA.

Materials and methods

The review was conducted and reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).23

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Google Scholar, Cochrane Library, Ebscohost and CINAHL. The published literature was retrieved between 1 January 2000 and 30 April 2025. The initial search strategy was built in PubMed and remapped to other databases (online supplemental file 1). The initial search was performed on 5 May 2023 and was subsequently updated regularly based on prompts from databases. The search terms were written in English. Also, a manual search of references and grey literature was conducted. These include materials from WHO, World Bank, Ministries of Health and other agencies. Basic searches were performed on the Google search engine, Google Scholar and the Maternal Health Task Force platform. Snowballing of the retrieved articles was also performed to further expand the search.

A search strategy was developed and tested in collaboration with an experienced medical information specialist. This was then peer-reviewed by a second information specialist (librarian), resulting in one suggested revision. Context, effective coverage, measuring, MCH services and text related to the search terms were used to build the search strategy.

Key concepts, context and medical subject headings were used to build the search strategy. The search terms were combined with Boolean operators, whereas the other terms were truncated.

Eligibility criteria

The titles and abstracts were examined to determine whether the articles met the inclusion or exclusion criteria. All original studies that measured effective coverage based on the WHO framework14 for maternal, newborn and child health were included. Abstracts presented at meetings and conferences, as well as studies related to healthcare quality, were also eligible. Only articles written in English were considered.

In contrast, review articles, commentaries on MCH interventions, conference proceedings, editorials, study protocols, news pieces and opinion papers were excluded. Articles outside the scope of maternal, newborn and child health, as well as those that assessed effective coverage but reported only CC, were also omitted.

Selection of sources of evidence

The selection process was conducted in three stages. First, the principal investigator screened titles, removed duplicates and exported eligible studies to EndNote 20. In the second and third stages, two independent reviewers applied the eligibility criteria to screen the abstracts and full-text articles. When papers were inaccessible, the investigators contacted the authors. To ensure reliability, a training exercise was conducted before formal screening. Conflicting decisions were resolved through consensus between the two reviewers, and any unresolved disagreements were addressed by a third reviewer during a final meeting.

Data charting and charting

For consistency, a customised data charting table (online supplemental file 2) was created and piloted by two independent investigators using a random sample of eight included studies. The table was adjusted based on feedback and was continuously updated. Comparisons were performed after data extraction. The approach was iterative, entailing piloting of the tool, discussion of the outcomes and updating the form or tool.

Data extraction of the included papers was conducted individually and in duplicate (20%). Information was extracted using definitions and options to describe the characteristics of the articles (methodology, data sources, study settings (eg, country of origin, urban/rural setting), title, author, year of publication, study aims, interventions), applicability in MCH services, evaluation of the quality domains and measures explored to measure effective coverage comparison of cruse service and effective coverage, and health systems’ willingness and capacity to adopt effective coverage.

Quality appraisal of sources of evidence

The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist was used to evaluate the methodological quality of the included literature.24 The tool consists of nine items that assess the internal and external validity of the studies included in the qualitative analysis. Articles that received a minimum score of six out of nine were classified as having adequate quality. For each eligible article, information about the author(s), study setting, study period, aims, target population, intervention assessed and measurement strategy used to determine quality was extracted.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment is not necessary for this scoping review.

Data synthesis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to characterise the included papers (SSA regions, countries, study design and data source). The investigators conducted a thematic analysis to summarise the data, particularly for quality measures, crude and effective coverage. A descriptive synthesis was provided for the applicability of effective coverage and quality domains.

Results

A total of 676 articles were identified across several databases: PubMed (233), Google Scholar (370), EBSCOHOST (53), CINAHL (18) and Cochrane Library (2). An additional 10 articles were found through basic searches and reference snowballing. This resulted in 686 articles in the initial search, of which 90 duplicates and cited materials were removed. Next, 513 studies were removed during title and abstract screening because they did not meet the eligibility criteria for inclusion, and 50 were removed during the entire article screening stage. Ultimately, 21 articles were included in the review. The steps used to select the studies for the review are provided in the PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1).

Geographic and methodological characteristics

Study location and type

14 out of the 21 articles were based on research conducted in Uganda (n=3) Ethiopia (n=5), Tanzania (n=1), Malawi (n=2), Kenya (n=1), Zambia (n=1) and Rwanda (n=1). Another nine (n=6, 28.6%) were based on research conducted in Senegal (n=1), Nigeria (n=1), Ghana (n=1), Burkina Faso (n=1), Côte d'Ivoire (n=1) and Sierra Leone (n=1). In Central Africa, only one study was retrieved from Cameroon (n=1).

Study designs

19 (90.5%) and 2 (9.5%) were cross-sectional and longitudinal studies, respectively. The two longitudinal studies were quasi-experimental and retrospective.25 26

Data sources explored to measure effective coverage

The various data sources relied on for effective coverage estimations by numerous studies are: DHS, MICS, SPA, SARA, DHIMS 2, Supportive Supervision (SS), and Surveillance, Facility and Community assessment and Project data. One study used both surveillance and health facility assessment data to estimate effective coverage for obstetric care,27 whereas two studies used only DHS data in the effective coverage.10 28 Another eight studies combined DHS and MICS with SPA and SARA for a comprehensive analysis of the effective coverage for MCH services.2936 Likewise, another two studies also combined SPA and DHIMS data to estimate effective coverage.16 25 Additionally, two more studies also combined supportive supervision with routine data.37 38 Furthermore, five studies used project-specific data to estimate effective coverage.3944

Due to the limitations of existing data sources, such as data insufficiency, inaccuracy and limited accessibility to independently measure effective coverage, linking household surveys with health facility data was adopted to address these barriers and enhance data completeness and reliability.

Critical appraisal within sources of evidence

No critical appraisal was conducted in this scoping review, as the objective was to map available evidence rather than assess methodological quality

Applicability of effective coverage in MCH services

While no studies have reported the use of effective coverage for routine monitoring and evaluation of MCH services, limited available studies have explored data from national health registries (eg, District Health Information Systems (DHIS) 2) to estimate effective coverage for MCH services in SSA.16 38

For instance, a study in Rwanda examined the institutionalisation of effective coverage for routine use by assessing the health system’s efforts to adopt effective coverage metrics for measuring quality of care in pursuit of MDGs 4&5.28 However, its adoption was not feasible due to the challenges with the routine data system in relation to the requisite metrics for effective coverage computation in MCH services. Similarly, the exploration of effective coverage for antenatal, postnatal, delivery care and FP services has been reported in 17 studies.1625 28 29 32 33 3840 43 Also, eight articles reported on effective coverage for newborn and other under-five interventions,1027 3032 35 36 39 41 while two studies reported on maternal nutrition45 and HIV testing and counselling (HTC).34 In all these studies, effective coverage was generally lower than crude service coverage.

Measuring effective coverage cascade steps

No published study has assessed all the standardised steps of the 2019 WHO Effective Coverage Think Tank Group framework for measuring effective coverage (service contact coverage, intervention coverage; input-adjusted; intervention; quality-adjusted; user-adherence; and outcome-adjusted). Inequity in effective coverage

Five articles examined equity in effective coverage.11 28 32 40 42 These studies reported varying socio-economic impacts on effective coverage. For instance, a study in Uganda found that effective PNC coverage was significantly higher among rural women and those with the poorest socioeconomic status, whereas institutional delivery had higher coverage among wealthier women.42 This indicates that the relationship between wealth and effective coverage varied across, suggesting that socio-economic status did not consistently favour women from wealthier backgrounds.

Domains of quality measures for exploring effective coverage

The studies reported in the articles initially assessed the structural, process and outcome quality as the basis for estimating effective coverage. The structural quality of MCH services was assessed by measuring the service availability and readiness index (availability of inputs).25 27 28 30 33 35 38 40 43 44 Service utilisation was evaluated for process quality, as several studies examined whether services were provided according to guidelines or evidence-based practice.1016 29 3134 36 39 42 45 Other studies evaluated process quality based on user experiences of care, although data on outcome quality measurements for key MCH interventions were limited.39

Quality measures, CC and effective coverage

A composite quality index was created from five tracer indicators for measuring the effective coverage in a study conducted in the Pwani Region, Tanzania. These were the availability of equipment, supplies and medicines, health worker knowledge and competence, facility infrastructure and provision of routine obstetric care, including emergency obstetric and newborn services.40 A facility was considered a ‘high’ threshold for each dimension of care if all tracer indicators were near complete (≥90%). In the case of an examination threshold for adequate performance or the knowledge and skill dimension, the average health worker score was 80%. A wide gap was observed between a CC of 81.6% and the effective coverage of SBA at 25%.

In a similar study in 2019 in Ethiopia, investigators used the WHO decision-making tools for FP providers and national guidelines for ANC to measure the content of care.29 On average, while 35.8% of women received quality care during FP visits, about 86% of women received <50% of the recommended clinical actions. Also, whereas 34% received quality ANC, about 81% received <50% of the recommended ANC clinical actions. The CC was 61% for FP and 62% for ANC, while effective coverage was 22% and 22%, respectively.

A study reported linking the 2016 Ethiopian DHS and the 2021–2022 Ethiopian Service Provision Assessment (ESPA) using administrative boundaries.34 They applied the ESPA framework—comprising 38 structural and 23 process indicators—to compute a weighted quality-of-care score. While the average overall service quality was 45.2%, the crude and effective coverage for HTC were 17.2% and 10.8%, respectively, during ANC visits.

An Ethiopian study in 2023 measured both crude and quality-adjusted coverage of newborn PNC. This was defined as postnatal checks received by newborns within 48 hours, followed by six or more specific care components to estimate effective PNC coverage.10 The crude PNC visits and effective care coverage were 13.2% and 9%, respectively.

A study in Eastern Uganda reported how many providers strictly adhered to protocols using six ANC tracer indicators,42 post-delivery tracer indicators and neonatal immunisations (oral polio vaccine and Bacille Calmette-Gue’rin (BCG) to measure effective coverage. The findings indicated a high CC of 88% for ANC, 54% for SBA and 76% for PNC. Effective ANC was lower than effective PNC (38% vs 76%, respectively). The coverage of SKB and institutional delivery was high (88%), while the coverage of postnatal visits was low (51%).

In Rwanda, Hetegaka et al conducted a study to calculate the effective coverage using statistical techniques and biomarkers. Quality measures included adherence to WHO recommendations for ANC and the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) guidelines.28 The content of care for newborns was measured using the WHO safe childbirth checklist. The study also evaluated facilities for the availability of equipment, infrastructure, medicines and record-keeping. The average effective coverage was 33.2% across all five MCH services, 30.1% for maternal health services (average of ANC and delivery) and 35.3% for sick child care. Conversely, the CC which averaged was 56.5% across all five MCH services, 67.3% for maternal health services and 49.2% for sick child care.

In Côte d'Ivoire, one study used the WHO/UNICEF guidelines to assess ANC quality by evaluating service availability, availability of drugs, diagnostics and other consumables, training and supervision received.30 Based on aggregate and geo-linking, the CC was 93%, with effective coverage ranging from 13% to 63%.

A study in Senegal applied repeated evaluations of the structural and operational qualities of ANC, delivery and child health services in health institutions.33 For curative services and consultations for children under the age of five, data were gathered through interviews, facility audits of service preparedness and availability and direct observations. Health posts emerged as the main service points in the study for the periods covered in the DHS surveys, where they received 71% of ANC 1 users, 66% of ANC 4 users, 56% of deliveries and 80% of sick child visits. Quality-adjusted coverage for child health services showed more enormous variations than input-adjusted coverage, the median relative difference reached 19.6%, while the input-adjusted coverage was 2.7%, but linear differences were higher at 2.4% for quality-adjusted coverage and 0.7% for input-adjusted coverage.

In Ghana, a study estimated effective coverage based on tracer indicators for routine deliveries, Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care signal functions and non-medical quality. The study focused on 9 out of the 11 functions . Three extra functions (weighing the newborn, blood pressure monitoring and the application of eye ointment after delivery) were included. The study also assessed the availability of essential medications, equipment and qualified health staff.27 The CC for facility delivery was 68%, while effective coverage was 18%.

In 2024, Abdissa et al applied the WHO/UNICEF effective-coverage cascade to ANC in Ethiopia by calculating the proportion of women who attended ≥4 ANC visits at facilities with essential inputs and received iron–folate supplementation plus two tetanus vaccine doses.43 Overall, 40% of the women met the four-visit threshold, with coverage ranging from 3% in Afar to 74% in Addis Ababa. Facility readiness averaged 70%, vaccination+supplementation coverage 26% and ANC process quality 64%. Consequently, input-adjusted, intervention-adjusted and quality-adjusted ANC coverage were 28%, 18% and 12%, respectively. However, user-adherence adjusted and outcome-adjusted coverage could not be assessed.

Bertoncello et al estimated effective coverage by assessing the quality of care in ANC, institutional deliveries and PNC in Uganda. It used quality measures from checklists compiled during supportive supervision to evaluate the readiness of facilities and their likelihood of providing quality of care. A composite quality index based on facility readiness and capacity38 was created. The readiness indices were 0.81 for ANC, 0.82 for SBA and 0.88 for PNC. This study reported gaps between crude and effective coverage of 30% for 4+ANC visits, 18% for institutional deliveries and 23% for PNC. Effective coverage was at 40% for ANC 4+visits, 48% for institutional deliveries and 77% for PNC care. The gap between crude and EC was higher for ANC4 (−30%) than for institutional deliveries (−18%) and PNC (−23%).

A study in Cameroon estimated the quality-adjusted coverage of ANC and intra- and postpartum care. Inequalities were assessed using absolute and relative inequality measures.35 Contact coverage for ANC services was 86.3%, but only 14.4% of the women had the opportunity to receive comprehensive ANC of optimal quality. Similarly, contact coverage for intra- and postpartum care was 68.5%, but only 31.4% of the women had the opportunity to receive optimal quality intra- and postpartum care. The missed opportunity for health bed nets was greater for intra- and postpartum care than ANC (20.0% vs 10.9%).

A study in Mayuge District, Uganda in 2018, explored the use of both individual and ecological linking methods to estimate effective coverage. Linking at the individual level showed better agreement when assessed using Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient compared with the ecological linking method.44 Data linkage is a simple and innovative approach for estimating care quality at the population level by linking data from households and facilities, particularly in situations in which routine Health Management Information System (HMIS) data are unreliable. The 55% CC for SBA and 10% effective coverage due to the quality index developed based on providers’ compliance with guidelines and readiness of facilities to provide intrapartum care.

The quality measures in the study in Nigeria assessed quality by measuring access to care, availability of inputs and compliance with guidelines.16 The quality measures for service readiness were the availability of infrastructure, staffing and drugs and commodities. In this study, three sources of data were combined using the ecological linking method. DHS and Project data were linked. CC for childbirth by SBA was 30%. Effective coverage was low: 2% based on project data and less than 1% using routine data (DHIS2).

In 2018, a study in Malawi reported measuring the quality of nutrition interventions by conducting direct observations of ANC and assessing care processes.45 This measured the provision of iron-folic acid supplements, nutritional counselling during pregnancy and support for early breastfeeding during ANC. Observations included breastfeeding within an hour of delivery and skin-to-skin contact after birth. In that study, the prevalence of low birth weight decreased from 13.7% to 10.8%, while immediate breastfeeding increased from 75.9% to 86.0%

One study assessed the quality of services in rural Burkina Faso by evaluating four process indicators for health status evaluation and five for health status review. Indicators included ‘weight check’, ‘temperature check’ and the availability of essential inputs. The study also assessed providers’ knowledge of first-line management for specific conditions and evaluated structural factors that include electricity, water and sanitation. Additionally, content care for Integrated Management of Childhood Iliness (IMCI) was assessed based on the IMCI guidelines.41 Consequently, CC for IMCI=69.5% and effective coverage was low (5.3% for high quality and 44.6% for intermediate quality).

In a pilot study in Southern Province, Zambia, various techniques were evaluated to assess effective coverage for the management of childhood illnesses.36 The competencies of service providers were assessed using case scenarios. The study also conducted a SARA to evaluate the health abilities of care providers to handle children’s illnesses and the adequacy of inputs to provide care. CC of sick child care was 76% in rural areas and 62% in urban areas. The effective coverage was 60% in rural areas and 49% in urban areas.

In a Kenyan study, quality was assessed based on the availability of functional weighing scales and thermometers. Quality was measured using the guidelines for sick children, routine weighing, recording temperatures, checking immunisation status and maintaining patient records.32 The results indicated that CC for FP, ANC and SBA improved significantly, whereas effective coverage showed less progress. The CC for FP in 2003, 2008 and 2014 showed 53.7%, 62%, 67.8%, while effective coverage was 31.9%, 36.6% and 40.7%, respectively. Similarly, for ANC, the CC in 2003, 2008 and 2014 was 52.3%, 47.1% and 58.2%, respectively. While the effective coverage was 31.7%, 31.6% and 44.6%, respectively. As in the case of SBA, CC in 2003, 2008 and 2014 was 41.6%, 43.8% and 61.8%, respectively. While the effective coverage was 32.6%, 36.7% and 51.3%, respectively.

The study in Tanzania in 2023 evaluated three weighting strategies using the Tanzania Service Provision Assessment and health information system data from 2014 to 2015 to measure facility readiness.25 They used caseload-weighted (ideal), facility-weighted and observation-weighted methods. The findings indicated that 58% of facility-weighted estimates and 64% of observation-weighted estimates passed the equivalence test against the caseload-weighted benchmark, while facility-weighted readiness measurements were systematically lost to the ideal by a minor level.

Synthesis of results

Overall, the included studies demonstrated that effective coverage of MCH services was consistently lower than CC across SSA. Although service contact was generally high, substantial reductions occurred after adjusting for service readiness, quality of care and content of care.

Most studies relied on linking household survey data (DHS, MICS) with facility assessments (SPA, SARA) or routine systems such as DHIS2. Data linkage was frequently adopted to address limitations in routine data, including incompleteness and inaccuracy. Project-specific and supportive supervision data were also used to strengthen quality measurement.

Across countries, gaps between crude and effective coverage were wide. For example, SKB, ANC, PNC, FP and child health services often showed moderate to high contact coverage but markedly lower quality-adjusted estimates. In several settings, effective coverage dropped to below 20% after quality adjustment.

No study assessed all steps of the 2019 WHO Effective Coverage Think Tank cascade. Most focused on input- or quality-adjusted coverage, with no assessment of user adherence or outcome-adjusted coverage. Equity analyses were limited and showed inconsistent socio-economic patterns.

Collectively, the evidence indicates that although methods for estimating effective coverage exist, routine systems in SSA remain insufficient for comprehensive and standardised measurement of effective coverage in MCH services

Discussion

This scoping review has provided evidence of how effective coverage has been applied to measure MCH services. A clear issue is the multiplicity of approaches and lack of clarity in standards and consistency in sustaining what effective coverage ought to be. Quality assessments varied, focusing on adherence to clinical guidelines, content of care, provider competence and service readiness.

The lack of defined quality metrics challenges the WHO/UNICEF Think Tank Group’s standardisation of the effective coverage cascade framework in 2019. The variation in quality assessments focusing on adherence to clinical guidelines, provider competence and service readiness reflects the multifaceted nature of healthcare quality. Consequently, improving monitoring and evaluation practices is urgently required, as the lack of effective coverage metrics hampers quality assessment. Notably, there is a need for standardisation of prospective quality measures to make universally accepted domains available for measuring effective coverage. This would enhance the validity, reliability and comparability of the approach. Otherwise, when effective coverage is measured, studies rely on their judgement, convenience and description to select the quality measures.

Several studies emphasised the need to standardise quality measures and methodological approaches, as the heterogeneity in constructing quality measures affects the estimation and comparability of effective coverage.20 21 To enhance reliability, characterising uncertainty in these estimates should become standard practice to support their integration into national and global health monitoring.46 47 Within the scope of the reviewed literature, no health system in SSA has institutionalised effective coverage for routine use. Only a few studies used national registry data, revealing underuse of routine data due to weaknesses in health information systems, challenges in data collection and reporting issues within DHIS2.47 Despite these challenges, routine data remain vital for informed decision-making and identifying care gaps. Improving data quality and transitioning to electronic systems could strengthen monitoring, as shown by progress in Ethiopia, Rwanda and Tanzania.16 38

The combination of ex-ante and ex-post measures provides the most comprehensive definition of effective coverage, whether at the individual, intervention or health system level.17 Many of the reviewed articles employed an ex-post approach to estimate effective coverage. However, many countries in SSA do not regularly conduct ex-post measures like DHS. Estimating quality-adjusted coverage was only possible in studies that used ex-post measures for data collection. The limited nature of available data hinders the estimation of user-adherence-adjusted coverage and outcome-adjusted coverage.48 The absence of these measures has important implications. It prevents a full understanding of whether services received translate into improved health outcomes and whether clients follow through with prescribed treatments or care plans. Without accounting for adherence and outcomes, policymakers risk overestimating the performance of MCH services, limiting opportunities to design interventions that improve continuity and effectiveness of care. As health management information systems continue to develop, periodic health facility surveys like the SPA provide information to monitor progress and target interventions. However, these surveys are complex, and consensus around key indicators or summary measures is lacking.48 Robust vital registries and health information systems are urgently needed to advance performance assessment.

This review also revealed significant geographic disparities in the effective coverage of MCH services, with variations observed across various zones in SSA. These differences reflected unequal distribution of health resources, variations in service quality and disparities in access to skilled care. Zones with stronger health system capacity and better infrastructure tended to achieve higher effective coverage compared with underserved areas. Geographically, it was also observed that most of the articles reviewed were from studies conducted in East and West Africa. The review further found that effective coverage almost always yielded measures substantially lower than crude service coverage, which reflects only service contact or utilisation. This difference indicated major quality gaps in health service delivery, particularly in MCH services. Evidence from 27 studies conducted across 49 low- and middle-income countries showed that effective coverage was significantly lower than crude service coverage.49 The gap between crude and effective coverage reached as high as 86%, indicating poor quality of care. These differences were attributed to healthcare providers’ non-adherence to standard treatment protocols and inadequate facility preparedness, including equipment shortages and insufficient qualified personnel. The findings suggest that access to healthcare services continues to expand; however, the quality of delivered care remains a major concern.40

This review was limited by the inclusion of literature published only in English, which may have excluded relevant studies in other languages. It covered a wide range of studies that used varying methodologies and definitions of effective coverage, making direct comparisons and synthesis of findings challenging. In addition, considerable variability existed in how quality was measured across studies, further complicating the aggregation and comparison of effective coverage estimates. Nonetheless, the search process was conducted systematically and comprehensively, and the findings should be interpreted within the scope of the studies that were identified.

Conclusion

This review was conducted to assess how effective coverage has been used in SSA, focusing on the use, progress and challenges associated with measuring effective coverage of MCH services. Therefore, the findings revealed that effective coverage consistently remained lower than CC across most MCH services, indicating that access to care does not always translate into quality or effectiveness. Additionally, the review found wide variations in the methods and indicators used to measure effective coverage, limiting comparability across studies and contexts.

The growing interest in measuring effective coverage for MCH services holds the potential to not only enhance quality but also accelerate progress towards achieving SDG 3.8.1. These findings emphasise the need to standardise measurement approaches and improve data quality and availability. Institutionalising effective coverage measurement within national health information systems would strengthen decision-making and resource allocation, ensuring that progress toward universal health coverage (SDG 3.8.1) reflects both access and quality of care.

Given the compelling evidence supporting effective coverage as a core indicator, health systems should prioritise its implementation. Innovation and advancement at the administrative level are needed to improve the functionality of existing surveillance and information systems in SSA, including the collection of demographic, epidemiological and routine health service data.

Future work should focus on strengthening existing WHO frameworks by addressing data limitations, harmonising methodologies and enhancing routine monitoring systems to facilitate consistent and meaningful assessment of MCH service performance across all levels of the health system.
