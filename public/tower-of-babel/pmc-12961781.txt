The impact of adding selenium-enriched spirulina platensis on the quality and microbial community of alfalfa silage
Authors: Yuxuan Wang, Xiangdong Liu, Mingjian Liu, Gentu Ge, Yushan Jia, Zhijun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo improve the quality and extend the preservation of forage, natural functional additives are increasingly explored for silage preparation. This study aims to investigate the effects of Selenium-enriched Spirulina platensis (Se-Spirulina) as a functional additive on the nutritional quality, fermentation characteristics, and microbial community of alfalfa silage. Four treatments were established: a control and treatments supplemented with 1%, 3% and 5% Se-Spirulina, designated CK, FXZ1, FXZ3 and FXZ5, respectively. After 60 days of ensiling, a systematic analysis was conducted on the nutritional components, fermentation quality, and bacterial community structure. The results showed that the addition of Se-Spirulina significantly increased the dry matter(DM) and crude protein(CP) content, reduced the neutral detergent fiber(NDF) and acid detergent fiber(ADF) levels, with the 5% addition showing the optimal effect. Se-Spirulina promoted lactic acid(LA) accumulation, accelerated pH decline, and significantly inhibited the production of propionic acid (PA), butyric acid (BA), and ammonia nitrogen(NH3-N). Microbial analysis revealed that Se-Spirulina enhanced the abundance of dominant LAB such as Lactiplantibacillus and inhibited the growth of potential spoilage bacteria. Functional prediction results indicated that it could enhance the activity of the silage microbial community in pathways such as carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, improving the fermentation microenvironment. In summary, Se-Spirulina is a highly effective natural additive that combines nutritional regulation with fermentation optimization, demonstrating promising application prospects.Keywords: Alfalfa, Fermentation quality, Microbial community, Selenium-enriched Spirulina platensis, Silage

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a perennial leguminous plant, is widely cultivated across the globe—including in Asia, the Americas, and Europe—due to its strong tolerance to cold, drought, and adaptability to diverse climatic conditions. Furthermore, alfalfa is known as the “King of Forage” owing to its high nutritional and economic value, making it one of the most important forage crops worldwide [1, 2]. Ensiling, a common and effective method for forage preservation, plays a crucial role in mitigating seasonal feed shortages and ensuring a stable nutritional supply for ruminants [3]. Due to its excellent nutrient profile and high crude protein content, alfalfa serves as a valuable resource for silage production [4]. However, its high buffering capacity and complex chemical composition [5–7] often lead to challenges such as inadequate fermentation, acid spoilage, and mold formation during the ensiling process, thereby compromising the nutritional quality and palatability of the final product [8]. Therefore, optimizing the fermentation process and improving silage quality has become a key focus of current research.

Se-Spirulina is a functional microalga that transforms inorganic selenium (Se) into its organic forms through a bioaccumulation process. Its core value lies in the combination of Spirulina’s high protein content (60–70%) and the unique biological activity of Se [9]. As an essential trace element, Se possesses various biological functions, including antioxidant activity, immune enhancement, and modulation of microbial communities [10]. Studies have shown that Se enrichment can significantly increase the CP and organic Se content in Medicago sativa silage, reduce NDF and ADF contents, promoting the growth of LAB, and inhibit the proliferation of harmful microorganisms, thereby improving fermentation quality [11]. Spirulina platensis, a blue-green alga, is rich in proteins, polysaccharides, β-carotene, and other bioactive compounds, and is known for its high nutritional value and bio-regulatory functions [12]. Through biotransformation, Se-Spirulina significantly enhances the bioavailability and safety of selenium [13, 14]. In addition, research on its application in animal nutrition and food processing has been steadily increasing. Several studies have confirmed its potential to enhance antioxidant capacity, immune response, and overall health in animals [15, 16]. However, its application in silage production remains in the exploratory stage.

At present, research on the application of selenium Se-Spirulina in the ensiling of Medicago sativa is relatively limited, and systematic studies on its effects on physicochemical parameters and microbial community structure during the fermentation process are lacking. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of Se-Spirulina as a functional additive in alfalfa silage, and to analyze its impact on fermentation quality and microbial community composition, thereby providing a theoretical basis and technical support for the development of high-quality alfalfa silage.

Materials and methods

Silage preparation

Alfalfa (cv. WL319) was harvested at the Inner Mongolia Agricultural University farm, Tumed Left Banner, Inner Mongolia (111°36′E, 40°68′N; 1,016 m a.s.l.; mean annual temperature 12.8 ℃).The site has an average annual temperature of 12.8 °C and an altitude of 1,016 m. On July 30, 2023, M. sativa at the early flowering stage was harvested and chopped into segments of 15–20 mm in length for ensiling. The experimental design included a CK and three treatment groups with additions of 1%, 3%, and 5% selenium Se-Spirulina, respectively. For each treatment, 400 g of chopped alfalfa was thoroughly mixed with the designated percentage of Se-Spirulina, packed into polyethylene bags, and sealed using a small-scale vacuum plastic sealer. The sealed bags were stored indoors at room temperature (20–25 °C), under vacuum conditions, and kept in a dark, dry environment to avoid direct sunlight exposure for 60 days. Each treatment was replicated four times. The Se-Spirulina powder used in this study was purchased from Inner Mongolia Yike Biotechnology.

Chemical composition and fermentation profile

Approximately 250 g of silage sample was dried in a forced-air oven at 65 °C to determine DM content [17]. After drying, the samples were ground using a laboratory mill and passed through 0.42 mm and 0.15 mm sieves for chemical analysis. CP was determined using an automatic Dumas nitrogen analyser [18]. NDF and ADF were measured using the Van Soest method with an ANKOM A2000i fiber analyzer (ANKOM Technology) [19]. Water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) were quantified using the anthrone colorimetric method [20]. Total Se content was determined using hydride generation atomic fluorescence spectrometry (HG-AFS) [21].

For pH and fermentation product analysis, 10 g of silage sample was placed in a vacuum-sealed bag with 90 mL of sterile water, shaken thoroughly, and homogenized in a stomacher for 3 min. The homogenate was filtered to obtain silage extract. The pH of the extract was measured using a calibrated glass-electrode pH meter (STARTER 100/B, OHAUS, Shanghai, China). LA, acetic acid (AA), PA, and BA concentrations were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) according to the method of [22]. NH₃-N content was measured by the phenol–hypochlorite colorimetric method [23].

Bacterial community sequencing analysis

Total DNA was extracted from fresh Medicago sativa and silage samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the extracted DNA was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and its concentration and purity were determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (NanoDrop™ 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) [24].

The hypervariable V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer pair 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) on a PCR thermal cycler (GeneAmp™ 9700, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). PCR amplification was performed using the following thermal cycling conditions. PCR products were extracted from 2% agarose gels and purified using a DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration of purified DNA was quantified using a Quantus™ fluorometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Bioinformatics analysis

The purified amplicons were used to construct a paired-end (PE) 2 × 300 library following the standard protocol of the Illumina MiSeq platform. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., China). Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using the QIIME pipeline (version 1.9.1), where low-quality reads were filtered and trimmed to obtain high-quality clean reads. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (version 1.2.11). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered with a 97% similarity threshold from the clean sequences [25].

All bioinformatics processing and analyses were conducted on the Majorbio I-Sanger Cloud Platform (www.i-sanger.com). Alpha diversity indices were calculated using QIIME (v1.9.1), and beta diversity was assessed via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the weighted UniFrac distance matrix. To identify significantly different bacterial taxa, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied as proposed by [26]. Spearman correlation heatmaps were generated using R software to visualize the relationships between bacterial communities and fermentation parameters [27]. Microbial functional pathways were predicted using PICRUSt2 based on the KEGG database [28]. Bar plots were created using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM, n = 3). Prior to analysis, data were checked for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). When significant differences were detected (P < 0.05), Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. Values in tables are presented in the same format. Tables were prepared using Microsoft Excel 2019, and figures were generated with Origin 2024 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) and R version 4.4.0.

Microbial community structure

In this study, a Venn diagram was constructed to display the shared and unique OTUs across groups (Fig. 1A). A total of 38 OTUs were detected in all groups, while each treatment group also exhibited varying degrees of unique microbial composition. The FM group contained 75 unique OTUs, which was considerably higher than those in other groups, indicating that the ensiling process may have led to the loss of some initial microbial populations. In comparison, the FXZ1 and FXZ3 groups contained 24 and 11 unique OTUs, respectively, while the CK group and FXZ5 group contained 9 and 7 unique OTUs, respectively.

B PCoA illustrating structural differences in bacterial communities of FM and silage subjected to various treatments during ensiling

Additionally, the β-diversity of microbial communities was analyzed at the ASV (Amplicon Sequence Variant) level using PCoA (Fig. 1B). The results showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 57.88% and 11.61% of the variance, respectively, accounting for a total of 69.49% of the differences in community structure, indicating that the main community variation could be represented by these two axes. According to the PCoA results, the FM group was clearly separated from the other groups in the PCoA space, suggesting that its community structure underwent significant changes during the ensiling process. In contrast, samples from the CK group and the groups supplemented with different concentrations of Se-Spirulina were more clustered in the PCoA space, indicating a higher similarity in microbial community structure among these treatment groups.

To assess the impact of different treatments on bacterial community structure during ensiling, we evaluated the relative abundance of ASVs at both the phylum and genus levels (Fig. 2A and B).

At the phylum level (Fig. 2A), Bacillota dominated the CK group, accounting for nearly 90% of the community. In the FXZ1 group, Bacillota declined markedly relative to CK, whereas Pseudomonadota increased, indicating that low-dose (1%) Se-Spirulina supplementation perturbed the bacterial community. With higher supplementation levels (FXZ3 and FXZ5), Bacillota gradually recovered its dominance, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in Pseudomonadota. At the genus level (Fig. 2B), Lactiplantibacillus was the dominant genus in CK. Its abundance dropped sharply in FXZ1, concomitant with increases in Weissella and Pediococcus. As Se-Spirulina supplementation rose (FXZ3 and FXZ5), Lactiplantibacillus regained dominance, while the relative abundances of Weissella and Pediococcus declined substantially.

Functional prediction of bacterial communities

To investigate the influence of Se-Spirulina on the microbial functional potential during the ensiling of alfalfa, bacterial communities were profiled at different functional levels based on the PICRUSt2 functional-prediction results.

At Level 1 (Fig. 4A), Metabolism was the dominant function in all treatments, with a relative abundance significantly higher than that of any other functional category. Environmental Information Processing and Genetic Information Processing ranked second, whereas Cellular Processes, Human Diseases and Organismal Systems were present at low abundances and did not differ among groups. At Level 2 (Fig. 4B), bacterial functions were primarily enriched in pathways such as global and overview maps, carbohydrate metabolism, amino-acid metabolism and membrane transport. The relative abundances of global and overview maps and carbohydrate metabolism in the FXZ5 group were significantly higher than those in the CK group (p < 0.05). Other pathways—including energy metabolism, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, and xenobiotics biodegradation and metabolism—did not differ significantly among treatments. Membrane transport, the main function within Environmental Information Processing, was similar across all groups. Cell motility was significantly lower in every silage treatment than in the FM group (p < 0.05). At Level 3 (Fig. 4C), bacterial functions were concentrated in carbohydrate metabolism, amino-acid metabolism and energy metabolism. Several metabolic pathways—such as Fatty acid metabolism, Carbon metabolism, Pyruvate metabolism and Microbial metabolism in diverse environments—were more abundant in the FXZ5 group. In addition, functions related to signal transduction and cellular motility, including Two-component system and Flagellar assembly, also varied among treatments.

Discussion

Influence of Se-Spirulina on nutritional composition

The chemical composition of forage during ensiling not only determines its ultimate nutritional value but also serves as an indicator of fermentation quality [29]. To improve the ensiling performance of alfalfa, this study employed Se-Spirulina at varying inclusion levels as an additive and systematically evaluated its effects on the principal chemical constituents. The results demonstrate that Se-Spirulina exerted positive effects on multiple parameters, with the magnitude of these benefits displaying a discernible dose-dependency.

The DM content in all Se-Spirulina treatments was significantly higher than in the control, with the FXZ5 group reaching 360.65 g/kg DM (p < 0.05). Supplementation with Se-Spirulina curbed the activity of spoilage microorganisms during ensiling, thereby reducing their consumption of substrate. This inhibitory effect is likely linked to the antioxidant properties of selenium, which improve the fermentation quality of alfalfa silage by favoring the growth of beneficial microbes such as LAB, ultimately enhancing the nutritional value and stability of the silage [11].

Crude protein (CP) content is a key indicator of feed nutritional value. The present study found that CP was significantly elevated in all Se-Spirulina-supplemented groups, peaking at 246.18 g/kg DM in the FXZ5 treatment. Se-Spirulina itself is rich in high-quality proteins, selenoproteins, and free amino acids, which directly contribute to the observed increase in CP content [30]. More importantly, Se-Spirulina possesses pronounced antioxidant and antibacterial properties that can suppress the activity of proteolytic enzymes and spoilage bacteria during ensiling, thereby curtailing the conversion of true protein into non-protein nitrogen such as NH₃-N and markedly improving protein preservation efficiency [31].Furthermore, the phenolic compounds and other bioactive substances contained therein can inhibit the protease activity of spoilage microorganisms (e.g., fungi), thereby reducing the degradation of plant-derived crude protein and better preserving the nutritional value [32].

Both NDF and ADF were significantly reduced in the Se-Spirulina treatments, with the lowest values observed in the FXZ5 group. The reductions in NDF and ADF may be partially attributed to enhanced fibrolytic activity. Our sequencing results showed higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus plantarum and L. buchneri in Se-Spirulina groups, taxa that have been reported to produce ferulic acid esterases and contribute to cell-wall degradation [33, 34]. Selenium supplied by Se-Spirulina further promotes the dominance of these fibrolytic taxa by modulating the microbial community structure, thereby improving fibre-degradation efficiency [11]. WSC serve as the primary fermentable substrate for LAB during the early ensiling phase; their concentration directly governs acidification rate and the speed at which pH declines [35]. In the present study, WSC content was higher in all Se-Spirulina treatments, with the FXZ5 group significantly exceeding the control. In the Se-Spirulina groups, the elevated WSC levels can be primarily attributed to two mechanisms: (i) the antioxidant activity of Spirulina, which protected native WSC and suppressed competing microbes, and (ii) the release of additional fermentable sugars through partial hydrolysis of cell-wall polysaccharides. These processes, together with Spirulina-derived polysaccharides serving as extra substrates for LAB, accelerated fermentation while reducing undesirable microbial consumption of WSC [36–38].

Selenium quantification in the silage samples revealed significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05). The FXZ1 group exhibited the lowest selenium concentration, whereas FXZ5 achieved the highest level, demonstrating that Se-Spirulina as an additive progressively elevates selenium enrichment in silage in a dose-dependent manner. Consistent with these findings, Wang et al. reported that foliar Se supplementation not only markedly improved the fermentation quality of alfalfa silage but also expanded the diversity and functional expression of bacteria linked to energy metabolism [39]. Malyugina et al. and Ebeid et al. further emphasized that organic Se forms—particularly selenomethionine—are more stable in fermentative environments and more readily absorbed by biological systems than inorganic Se, ultimately enhancing selenium deposition and antioxidant capacity in animals. As a natural organic Se carrier rich in selenomethionine and selenocysteine [40, 41], Se-Spirulina has been shown to possess excellent biological stability and synergistic efficacy within silage matrices [42].

Influence of Se-Spirulina on fermentation characteristics

The quality of fermentation during ensiling directly governs both the shelf-life and palatability of silage; an optimal fermentation environment suppresses spoilage microbes while promoting the accumulation of desirable fermentation products. The present study found that supplementing alfalfa with Se-Spirulina markedly enhanced silage fermentation quality.

During ensiling, a rapid drop in pH is essential for suppressing the proliferation of anaerobic spoilage bacteria and for maintaining system stability; this is achieved primarily through the rapid accumulation of LA. Homo-fermentation dominated by LA can quickly lower pH and inhibit undesirable microbes [43].AA, usually produced during hetero-fermentation, may reduce LA but improves aerobic stability [44]. The LA/AA ratio is a key indicator of silage quality. A higher ratio reflects dominant homofermentation, leading to rapid LA accumulation, fast pH decline, and suppression of spoilage microbes. However, excessively high ratios may reduce aerobic stability, whereas moderate AA levels inhibit yeasts and molds. Therefore, a balanced LA/AA ratio ensures both efficient fermentation and improved aerobic stability [45–47].

In the present study, the FXZ5 group exhibited the lowest pH, significantly lower than CK and the other treatments, and also had the highest LA concentration (p < 0.05). This is attributable to the abundant soluble sugars, polypeptides, and vitamins in Se-Spirulina, which serve as readily available carbon sources and growth factors for lactic-acid bacteria [48]. Conversely, the FXZ1 group showed the lowest LA content (p < 0.05), indicating that a 1% inclusion of Se-Spirulina inhibited the activity of LAB, resulting in slow accumulation of both LA and AA and a higher pH. A possible explanation is that Se-Spirulina itself has a slightly alkaline pH (initial pH 6.02 vs. 5.81 in fresh alfalfa); although it only accounted for 1% of the mixture, this small proportion was enough to slightly raise the initial pH and thereby impede the rapid initiation of lactic-acid fermentation [49]. Consistent with these results, previous studies have shown that Spirulina exerts concentration-dependent effects on LAB: at 2% inclusion, the survival rate of Lactobacillus significantly increased [50], whereas a 0.5% inclusion reduced both Lactobacillus survival and the antioxidant properties of yogurt [51]. Although these findings originate from yogurt fermentation, they provide supportive evidence that Spirulina concentration may exert comparable effects on LAB activity across different fermentation systems.

During ensiling, the accumulation of PA and BA is generally regarded as a negative indicator, because elevated levels are closely associated with poor fermentation and extensive protein degradation [52]. In the present study, both PA and BA were lower in all Se-Spirulina treatments than in the CK, demonstrating that the additive effectively suppressed the metabolic activity of spoilage bacteria. This inhibitory effect is attributed to the combined action of Spirulina-derived bioactive compounds—including polysaccharides, polyphenols, carotenoids, and selenium—which can disrupt bacterial membrane integrity, inhibit enzyme activities, and modulate intracellular redox balance, thereby interfering with the metabolism of anaerobic pathogens [53]. Specifically, selenium acts as an antioxidant, not only by directly scavenging free radicals but also by strengthening the competitive advantage of beneficial microbes such as LAB, indirectly curbing the proliferation of spoilage organisms [54].

Microbial community and its relationship with silage quality

Microbial community structure and function are the decisive factors in the success or failure of silage fermentation, with distinct microorganisms performing critical roles in substrate conversion, pH regulation and nutrient conservation. Using high-throughput sequencing, the present study systematically analysed the microbial community structure and functional shifts in alfalfa silage across treatments, revealing that Se-Spirulina exerts a pronounced modulatory effect on the assembly of the silage microbiota.

PCoA revealed that the microbial communities in Se-Spirulina treatments were clearly separated from those of the control and formed tighter clusters, indicating that the additive stabilizes and homogenizes community structure. Venn analysis further showed that the number of unique OTUs declined after Se-Spirulina supplementation, reflecting a convergent trajectory of microbial diversity. Such convergence in silage ecosystems is generally linked to community optimization and functional specialization, favoring the colonization of dominant fermentative taxa and enhancing metabolic efficiency [55]. At the phylum level, Bacillota dominated the CK group, but its relative abundance transiently declined in FXZ1, whereas Pseudomonadota markedly increased—suggesting that the low inclusion rate did not fully suppress potential spoilage bacteria. As the inclusion level rose, Bacillota rapidly recovered and became significantly enriched, while Pseudomonadota was further suppressed. This pattern may be attributed to the antimicrobial bioactive compounds in Se-Spirulina, such as peptides, selenium species, and polyphenols, which could inhibit undesirable microbes and indirectly favor beneficial taxa [53, 56].

At the genus level, early-stage facultative heterofermentative LAB, including Weissella and Pediococcus, increased in FXZ1 but were subsequently outcompeted by acid-tolerant Lactiplantibacillus as pH declined [57–59]. Thus, the low dose of Se-Spirulina appears to perturb the competitive hierarchy among genera and fails to consolidate the dominance of desirable LAB. As the inclusion level rose, Lactiplantibacillus rapidly rebounded and became the overwhelming genus in FXZ3 and especially FXZ5. Owing to its robust acid-producing capacity and high environmental adaptability, Lactiplantibacillus is pivotal for driving pH decline and suppressing undesirable microbes [7]. Its dominance in FXZ5 aligns with the highest lactic acid accumulation and the lowest pH observed in this treatment. Concomitantly, spoilage-related genera such as Pseudomonas and Pantoea were markedly reduced in all Se-Spirulina treatments, thereby lowering the risk of deamination, gas production, and protein degradation [60]. Correlation analyses further corroborated the synergistic relationships between the microbiota and both chemical indices and fermentation quality. Lactiplantibacillus exhibited strong positive correlations with crude-protein and lactic-acid contents, while displaying significant negative correlations with pH and acid-detergent fibre (ADF). These findings align closely with the observations of Si and Xiao [61, 62].

Collectively, these results indicate that Se-Spirulina reshapes the microbial community, fosters beneficial fermentative bacteria, suppresses spoilage organisms, and improves silage fermentation quality and nutritional value. Such microbial and chemical improvements may also enhance rumen fermentation and animal health when the silage is used as feed.

Functional prediction of bacterial communities

Leveraging PICRUSt2 functional profiling, this study evaluated how Se-Spirulina supplementation altered the functional potential of the bacterial community during alfalfa ensiling. At the top functional level (Level 1), “Metabolism” dominated in every treatment, underscoring that substrate degradation and energy conversion are the primary activities of the bacterial consortium under the anaerobic silage environment.This is consistent with the findings of Wang et al. [39], who reported that bacterial community functions in Se-supplemented alfalfa silage were overwhelmingly concentrated in metabolic pathways, far exceeding information-processing or signal-transduction categories.

At Levels 2 and 3, high-dose Spirulina (FXZ5) significantly up-regulated key metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, and fatty-acid metabolism. These changes suggest enhanced microbial carbon-utilization efficiency and energy-conversion capacity, which likely contribute to accelerated acidification and improved nutrient preservation in silage [42]. Similar effects of Se supplementation on bacterial metabolism have been reported, supporting the notion that Se can modulate microbial functional potential by influencing enzymatic and redox processes [11].

Although some signal-transduction and motility functions (e.g., Two-component system, Flagellar assembly) also showed treatment-responsive changes, their direct significance in silage remains less clear. Nevertheless, these functional shifts indicate that Se-Spirulina can modulate microbial community activity and ecological interactions, potentially supporting fermentation efficiency and nutrient conservation.Importantly, functional predictions based on 16 S rRNA sequencing have inherent limitations, as they infer potential activity rather than directly measuring gene expression or metabolic fluxes.

Overall, these results suggest that Se-Spirulina enhances the metabolic capacity of the silage microbiome, which may underpin the observed improvements in fermentation quality and nutritional value, providing a mechanistic basis for its use as a functional silage additive.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that adding Se-Spirulina to alfalfa silage significantly improves its nutritional composition, fermentation quality, and microbial community structure in a dose-dependent manner. Higher levels of Se-Spirulina enhanced DM and CP contents, reduced fiber fractions including NDF and ADF, and increased WSC, contributing to better nutrient preservation. Moreover, Se-Spirulina promoted LA production, lowered pH, and suppressed undesirable fermentation products such as PA and BA, reflecting improved fermentation stability. Microbial analyses confirmed that Se-Spirulina reshaped the silage bacterial community by enriching beneficial LAB and reducing spoilage microorganisms, while functional predictions revealed enhanced metabolic pathways critical for efficient fermentation. Overall, Se-Spirulina shows strong potential as a functional additive for improving the quality and safety of alfalfa silage.

From an industrial perspective, Se-Spirulina may be considered a promising additive for silage production, as it can improve feed quality while simultaneously providing a source of organic selenium. Further studies under large-scale production conditions are still needed to confirm its practical feasibility and economic efficiency.
