ProxSTORM—A Stochastic Trust-Region Algorithm for Nonsmooth Optimization
Robert J. Baraldi, Aurya Javeed, Drew P. Kouri, Katya Scheinberg

Abstract
We develop a stochastic trust-region algorithm for minimizing the sum of a possibly nonconvex Lipschitz-smooth function that can only be evaluated stochastically and a nonsmooth, deterministic, convex function. This algorithm, which we call ProxSTORM, generalizes STORM Chen et al. 2018 ; Blanchet et al. 2019 —a stochastic trust-region algorithm for the unconstrained optimization of smooth functions—and the inexact deterministic proximal trust-region algorithm in Baraldi and Kouri 2022 . We generalize and, in some cases, simplify problem assumptions so that they reduce to more succinct version of assumptions on STORM when the convex term is zero. Our analysis follows the STORM framework by employing martingales, but again simplifies certain steps and proving global convergence and an expected complexity bound in the more general setting of a possibly nonsmooth term. To demonstrate that the method is numerically viable, we apply the algorithm to ℓ 1 \ell^{1} -regularized neural network training and also to topology optimization.



keywords
nonsmooth optimization, stochastic optimization, trust-region algorithm, proximal mapping, convex constraints

Introduction

We present a new stochastic trust-region algorithm for composite optimization problems.
Our algorithm—which we call ProxSTORM—solves

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;\left\{\tilde{f}(x):=f(x)+\varphi(x)\right\},

(1)

where
\varphi
is convex but generally nonsmooth and
f
is smooth but typically nonconvex.
We require that
f
and
\varphi
satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

-

1.

The function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is proper, closed, and convex.

2.

The function
f
is
L
-smooth on
\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi:=\{x:\varphi(x)<\infty\}
, i.e.,
f
is differentiable on an open set
U\supseteq\mathrm{dom}\,{\varphi}
and has
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradient:

\text{there exists}\quad L>0\quad\text{such that}\quad\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|\leq L\|y-x\|\quad\text{for all}\quad x,\,y\in U.

3.

The objective function
f+\varphi
is bounded below on
\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi
.

ProxSTORM addresses the paradigm of a
\varphi
that can be evaluated exactly and whose proximal mapping,

\mathrm{prox}_{r\varphi}(x):=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{2r}\|y-x\|^{2}\right\}

(2)

can also be evaluated exactly.
For
f
, we allow stochastic models and estimates that can be arbitrarily inaccurate, provided that:

1.

these quantities are sufficiently accurate, sufficiently often, and

2.

the objective function
f+\varphi
does not increase too much when estimates of
f
are inaccurate.

We precisely state these assumptions later but note for now that this paradigm is a practical one.
For instance, it corresponds to the case that
f
is an expected loss with respect to a data distribution and
\varphi
is a sparsifying regularizer like the
\ell^{1}
norm or is an indicator function for convex constraints.

ProxSTORM combines STORM (a trust-region framework for
st
ochastic
o
ptimization with
r
andom
m
odels)
Chen et al. 2018
;
Blanchet et al. 2019
with the proximal trust-region framework in
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
.
The latter framework imposes the same exactness conditions on
\varphi
and its proximal mapping as we do and permits
deterministic
inexactness concerning
f
.
The analysis in
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
stipulates that models and estimates of
f
always satisfy error bounds decreasing to zero as the algorithm progresses.
In many practical applications, however, inexactness is
stochastic
, resulting from sampled approximations of expectations or sporadic failures of subroutines used to evaluate
f
that arise from sophisticated simulations
Chen et al. 2018
.
To accommodate stochastic inexactness in the context of (
1
), we generalize STORM.
In doing so, we use the norm of the
f+\varphi
proximal gradient as our stationarity measure, in keeping with
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
; that is, we use the norm of

h(x)\coloneqq\frac{x-\mathrm{prox}_{r\varphi}\left(x-r\nabla f(x)\right)}{r},

(3)

where
r
is a (user-specified) parameter on which our analysis does not depend.
A stationary point
x^{*}
of
f+\varphi
indeed satisfies
h(x^{*})=0
with
h
reducing to
\nabla f
when
\varphi\equiv 0
.
The contribution of our work is to accommodate
\varphi\not\equiv 0
while also recovering theory no worse than the theory for STORM when
\varphi\equiv 0
.
We accomplish this in part through the condition that
f+\varphi
does not increase too much stated above and through the nonexpansivity of the proximal mapping, namely
(
Bauschke and Combettes 2017
, Proposition 12.27)
:

\|\mathrm{prox}_{r\varphi}(y)-\mathrm{prox}_{r\varphi}(x)\|\leq\|y-x\|\quad\text{for all}\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(4)

In motivating ProxSTORM, we noted that
f+\varphi
can be a stochastically approximated objective function with deterministic convex constraints for the optimization variable
x
.
Several recent papers propose optimization algorithms for stochastic objective function inexactness and deterministic constraints.
These methods, however, do not involve nonsmooth functions and are based on constrained optimization techniques, such as sequential quadratic programming
Berahas et al. 2021
;
Curtis et al. 2024
and interior point methods
Curtis et al. 2023
;
Curtis et al. 2024
.
Our ProxSTORM algorithm only addresses convex constraints but our approach and the assumptions of our analysis are significantly simpler and easier to enforce than those for general stochastic constrained optimization.
The STORM algorithm we generalize is not the only stochastic trust-region method.
Other methods include those introduced in
Cao et al. 2024
;
Gratton et al. 2017
.
We find, however, that the framework of STORM is the most suitable for our purposes since it is the most general and also yields expected complexity bounds.

In Section

ProxSTORM Algorithm

Given a (user-specified) starting point

\underset{||s||\leq\delta_{k}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;\{m_{k}(x_{k}+s)+\varphi(x_{k}+s)\}.

For

(S1)

(S2)

Following

(5)

Given

(corresponding to a successful or unsuccessful iteration, respectively) by comparing

with an approximation of the actual reduction,

{\rm ared}_{k}\coloneqq f(x_{k})+\varphi(x_{k})-f(x_{k}+s_{k})-\varphi(x_{k}+s_{k}).

A standard step acceptance condition for trust-region algorithms is

\frac{{\rm ared}_{k}}{{\rm pred}_{k}}\geq\eta_{1}.

In ProxSTORM, however,
{\rm ared}_{k}
is not computable and is replaced by a computed reduction,
{\rm cred}_{k}
, that approximates
{\rm ared}_{k}

ProxSTORM uses the same step acceptance criteria as STORM, which—in addition to using
{\rm cred}_{k}

Specifically, to accept a step, ProxSTORM stipulates that for fixed (user-specified) parameters
\eta_{1}\in(0,1)
and
\eta_{2}>0

\frac{{\rm cred}_{k}}{{\rm pred}_{k}}\geq\eta_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|h_{k}\|\geq\eta_{2}\delta_{k}.

(6)

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+s_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta_{k+1}=\min\{\gamma\delta_{k},\delta_{\text{max}}\}.

(7)

Here,
\delta_{\text{max}}:=\gamma^{\ell}\delta_{0}

,

, and
\delta_{0}>0

When (

x_{k+1}=x_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta_{k+1}=\gamma^{-1}\delta_{k}.\hskip 33.00008pt

(8)

2.1
Computing the Trial Step
s_{k}

There are several algorithms for generating trial steps that satisfy (S1) and (S2).
See
Baraldi and Kouri 2025
.
Each of the methods in
Baraldi and Kouri 2025
employs a quadratic model

m_{k}(s+x_{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}s^{\top}Q_{k}s+g_{k}^{\top}s,

where
g_{k}=\nabla m_{k}(x_{k})\approx\nabla f(x_{k})
and
Q_{k}
is a symmetric
n\times n
matrix that characterizes the curvature of
f
around
x_{k}
; for instance,
Q_{k}
can be the Hessian of
f
when it exists or an approximation thereof.
The methods in
Baraldi and Kouri 2025
first determine a Cauchy point.
There are two Cauchy point definitions in
Baraldi and Kouri 2025
, but we only discuss the original Cauchy point introduced in
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
.
Let
p_{k}(r)
denote the Cauchy arc

p_{k}(r)\coloneqq\mathrm{prox}_{r\varphi}(x_{k}-r\nabla m_{k}(x_{k})).

To determine a Cauchy point, one can employ the bi-directional proximal search presented as
(
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
, Algorithm 2)
.
The search produces a step length
r_{k}>0
that satisfies
(
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
, (25))
, in which case
the Cauchy step defined by

s^{c}_{k}\coloneqq p_{k}(r_{k})-x_{k},

satisfies (S1) and (S2).
The methods in
Baraldi and Kouri 2025
improve upon the Cauchy step
s^{c}_{k}
with iterative procedures such as spectral proximal gradient, proximal nonlinear conjugate gradient, or semismooth Newton.
These methods produce descending model values at each iteration while enforcing the trust-region constraint, so the computed trial step
s_{k}
continues to satisfy (S1) and (S2).
For our numerical results, we use
(
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
, Algorithm 5)
—a spectral proximal gradient method.

3
Stochastic Formalism

In the preceding section, we introduced ProxSTORM in terms of a realization of the stochastic algorithm, which we interpret as a point
\omega
in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Each
\omega
encapsulates
all
the randomness when running the algorithm.
The realizations are elements of the set
\Omega
,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{\Omega}
is a
\sigma
-algebra, and
\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
is a probability measure.
We use the formalism here to analyze ProxSTORM in Section
4
.
Randomness enters the algorithm through the models
m_{k}
of
f
and through the computed reductions
{\rm cred}_{k}
.
These quantities are in fact
random variables
, i.e., measurable functions on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Entities in the algorithm that depend on them are thus random variables as well.

We generally use capital letters for random variables and lowercase text for their realizations.
Concretely, for a realization
\omega
of ProxSTORM at iteration
k
, the model and the computed reduction are

m_{k}=M_{k}(\omega,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm cred}_{k}=\mathcal{C}_{k}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

Similarly,

x_{k}=X_{k}(\omega),\quad\delta_{k}=\Delta_{k}(\omega),\quad h_{k}=H_{k}(\omega),\\
\quad s_{k}=S_{k}(\omega),\quad{\rm pred}_{k}=\mathcal{P}_{k}(\omega),\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm ared}_{k}=\mathcal{A}_{k}(\omega).

The analysis of ProxSTORM relies on assumptions about
M_{k}
,
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{k}
.
The assumptions and the analysis both involve
events
—subsets of
\Omega
belonging to
\mathcal{F}
.
We use the following standard conventions for events.

(N1)

Specifications of events omit elements of
\Omega
; for instance, the event

\mathcal{S}_{k}:=\{\text{ProxSTORM accepts the trial step }S_{k}\}

(10)

is understood to mean

\{\omega\in\Omega:\text{ProxSTORM accepts the trial step }S_{k}(\omega)\}.

(N2)

An unqualified condition involving random variables is a probability one event.

(N3)

An overline represents the complement of an event, i.e.,
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{k}}:=\Omega\backslash\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

Formally, ProxSTORM is a
stochastic process
adapted to a filtration

\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\frac{1}{2}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\frac{3}{2}}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{F}.

(11)

Each
\mathcal{F}_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{M_{\ell},\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\leq k}
.
This generating is the source of randomness through iteration
k
.
Informally,
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is the
information
created through iteration
k

Durrett 2019
.
Each half iterate,
\mathcal{F}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}
, is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
M_{k+1}
together, i.e., the information created in iteration
k+1
, up to but not including
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
.
The random variables
S_{k}
,
X_{k+1}
and
\Delta_{k+1}
are adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, and
M_{k+1}
is adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}
.

Much of the analysis is a matter of partitioning the set of outcomes
\Omega

4.1
Assumptions

We begin with assumptions about
M_{k}
, the models of
f
.

Assumption 2

(Bounded Model Curvature)
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the trust-region subproblem models,
M_{k}
, have uniformly bounded curvature, i.e., there exists a number
\kappa_{\rm{bmh}}>0
independent of
k
and
\omega
so that

1+b_{k}\leq\kappa_{\rm{bmh}}

for every
\omega\in\Omega
, where
b_{k}
is defined in (
5
).

We also make an assumption about the accuracy of the models.
We formulate this assumption through the event

I_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\|\nabla M_{k}(X_{k})-\nabla f(X_{k})\|\leq\kappa_{\rm{grad}}\Delta_{k}\right\},

(12)

where
\kappa_{\rm{grad}}>0
is an arbitrary constant independent of
k
and
\omega
.

Assumption 3

(Model Accuracy)
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the trust-region subproblem models,
M_{k}
, are sufficiently accurate, sufficiently often, meaning

\mathbb{P}(I_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1})\geq\alpha.

Here,
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a number sufficiently close to one that we set later.

The definition of
I_{k}
stipulates that
\nabla M_{k}
is close to
\nabla f
at
X_{k}
.
This definition of accuracy is adequate because the analysis of ProxSTORM requires only the pointwise (i.e., per
\omega
) bound of
\mathcal{P}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{k}
provided by the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(Accurate Model Gradient
\Rightarrow
Accurate Predicted Reduction)
.

Let

\kappa_{\rm{val}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{4}(L+\kappa_{\rm{bmh}}+2\kappa_{\rm{grad}}).

(13)

If Assumption
2
holds, then

f(x_{k}+s)-m_{k}(x_{k}+s)-f(x_{k})+m_{k}(x_{k})\leq 2\kappa_{\rm{val}}\delta_{k}^{2}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|s\|\leq\delta_{k}
.

Proof.

This result is a consequence of the descent lemma
(
Bauschke and Combettes 2017
, Lemma 2.64)
applied to the difference between the smooth functions
f
and
m
.
Indeed,

\Big(f(x_{k}+s)-m_{k}(x_{k}+s)\Big)-\Big(f(x_{k})-m_{k}(x_{k})\Big)\\
\leq\Big(\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla m_{k}(x_{k})\Big)^{\top}(x_{k}+s-x_{k})+\frac{L+\kappa_{\rm{bmh}}}{2}\left\|x_{k}+s-x_{k}\right\|^{2},

(14)

where
L+\kappa_{\rm{bmh}}
is an upper bound for the Lipschitz constant for the gradient of
f-m_{k}
.
We then apply Cauchy-Schwarz to the first term on the right-hand side, noting

\|\nabla m_{k}(x_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\kappa_{\rm{grad}}\delta_{k}
since
\omega\in I_{k}
.
∎

Assumption
2
imposes bounded model curvature always, while Assumption
3
formulates
M_{k}
as a probabilistic oracle whose gradient is accurate with sufficiently high probability and otherwise can be arbitrarily inaccurate.
We formulate the accuracy of the computed reductions in a probabilistic manner as well.
In particular, for a positive constant
\eta\in\left(0,\min\{\eta_{1},1-\eta_{1}\}\right)
independent of

\omega
, we define the event

J_{k}:=\left\{\left|\mathcal{A}_{k}-\mathcal{C}_{k}\right|\leq\eta\mathcal{P}_{k}\right\}

and make the following assumption.

Assumption 4

(Computed Reduction Accuracy)
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the computed reductions,
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, are sufficiently accurate, sufficiently often, meaning

\mathbb{P}(J_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-\frac{1}{2}})\geq\beta.

Here,
\beta\in(0,1)
is a number sufficiently close to one that we set later.

Assumption
4
streamlines its STORM counterpart in a manner analogous to Assumption

\mathcal{A}_{k}

We note that Assumptions

and of objective function estimates, as one would expect.
A second aspect of Assumption

that can work for STORM is the quantification of accuracy in terms of
\mathcal{P}_{k}
instead of
\Delta_{k}^{2}
.
Our use of

is consistent with conventional objective function inexactness conditions for deterministic trust-region algorithms

;

.
In the stochastic setting of ProxSTORM, our use of

has two advantages.
First, it decouples the definition of

Second it can be less restrictive since (S2) ensures

\mathcal{P}_{k}\geq\kappa_{\rm{fcd}}\|H_{k}\|\min\left\{\frac{\|H_{k}\|}{\kappa_{\rm{bmh}}},\Delta_{k}\right\}.

which is at least

(When

Our final assumption pertains to the actual reduction,

Assumption 5
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}

,

\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathcal{A}_{k}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\overline{J_{k}})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right]\leq c_{1}\Delta_{k}^{2}+c_{2}\|h(X_{k})\|\Delta_{k}.

Proposition 2

(Applicability of Assumption
5
)
.

•

If the objective function is smooth, i.e.,
\varphi\equiv 0

•

Assumption

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{C}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{k}|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right]\leq c_{3}\Delta_{k}^{2},

(16)

Proof.

When
\varphi\equiv 0

\nabla f(X_{k})

5
follows from a pointwise application of the descent lemma to

f(X_{k}(\omega)+S_{k}(\omega))-f(X_{k}(\omega)),\quad\omega\in\overline{J_{k}}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(f(X_{k}(\omega)+S_{k}(\omega))-f(X_{k}(\omega))\Big)\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\overline{J}_{k})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\|\nabla f(X_{k})\|\Delta_{k}+\frac{L}{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}\right)\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\overline{J}_{k})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right]\leq(1-\beta)\Big(\frac{L}{2}\Delta_{k}^{2}+\|\nabla f(X_{k})\|\Delta_{k}\Big).

To show Assumption
5
is implied by (

), observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathcal{A}_{k}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\overline{J_{k}})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(|\mathcal{C}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{k}|-\mathcal{C}_{k}\right)\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\overline{J_{k}})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right].

On
\mathcal{S}_{k}
,
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is positive since
\mathcal{C}_{k}\geq\eta_{1}\mathcal{P}_{k}
is a step acceptance condition and
\mathcal{P}_{k}>0
due to (S2).
By monotonicity of the expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathcal{A}_{k}\mathbbm{1}(\mathcal{S}_{k}\cap\overline{J_{k}})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{C}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{k}|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1/2}\right],

so (
16
) ensures Assumption
5
with
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
as claimed.
∎

The inexact objective function condition used by the deterministic algorithm
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
first appeared in
Kouri et al. 2014
.
The condition is that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a number
\kappa_{\rm{obj}}>0
independent of
k
for which

|{\rm ared}_{k}-{\rm cred}_{k}|\leq\kappa_{\rm{obj}}\left(\eta\min\left\{{\rm pred}_{k},\theta_{k}\right\}\right)^{\mu}.

(17)

Here,
\mu
and
\theta_{k}
are (user-specified) nonnegative numbers with

\mu>1\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\theta_{k}=0,

while for ProxSTORM’s trust region updates,
\eta\in(0,\min\{\eta_{1},1-\eta_{1}\})
as in Assumption
4
.
This condition has more parameters than the traditional inexactness condition

|{\rm ared}_{k}-{\rm cred}_{k}|\leq\eta{\rm pred}_{k}

(18)

(compare with (
15
)) and hence can be more flexible in practice.
For instance,
\kappa_{\rm{obj}}
need not be specified, which enables the use of error indicators that may depend on uncomputable constants, to bound the left-hand side of (
17
).
When
k
is sufficiently large, however, (
17
) implies (
18
) since
\theta_{k}\to 0

(
Kouri et al. 2014
, Lemma A.1)
.
Similar to our decision to forgo a more flexible trust-region update, we note that the definition of
J_{k}
in Assumption
4
could be modified according to (
17
) without meaningfully altering the convergence analysis of ProxSTORM.
We refrain from doing so, however, in the interest of clarity.
We merely note that it suffices for our assumptions to hold only when
k
is large enough.

4.2
Expected Decrease

The following result concerns the expected decrease of the Lyapunov function

\Psi_{k}:=\Psi_{k}(X_{k},\Delta_{k})=\nu(f(X_{k})+\varphi(X_{k}))+(1-\nu)\Delta_{k}^{2},

where
\nu\in(0,1)

Theorem 3

(Expected
\Psi
Decrease)
.

If Assumptions

–

More importantly, Theorem

The primary mechanism is proving that the trust-region radius

The proof follows the analysis of STORM and differs from the standard deterministic trust-region analysis.
This is because the trust-region radius serves a dual purpose in the stochastic setting: it is the size of the ball in which the step is computed as well as the measure of model accuracy—see (

We begin the proof of Theorem

To begin, that for

4.3
First-Order Global Convergence

We begin with an overview of the proof of global convergence.
On a realization of the algorithm
\omega
for which
\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta_{k}(\omega)=0
but
\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|h(X_{k}(\omega))\|\neq 0
, the quantities
\|h(X_{k}(\omega))\|
and
\Delta_{k}(\omega)
will always be separated once
k
is sufficiently large.
More precisely, for any constant
c
, there exists a number
K
dependent on
c
such that

\|h(X_{k}(\omega))\|\geq c\Delta_{k}(\omega)\quad\text{for all}\quad k>K.

For
c
sufficiently large and
k
larger than the corresponding
K
,
\omega\in I_{k}\cap J_{k}
implies acceptance of the step
S_{k}(\omega)

(see Lemma
9
).
Acceptance of the step, however, increases the trust-region radius:

\Delta_{k+1}(\omega)=\gamma\Delta_{k}(\omega).

Per the results in Section
4.2
,
\omega\in I_{k}\cap J_{k}
for enough
k
that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta_{k}(\omega)\neq 0
; that is,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\|h(X_{k})\|\neq 0\right\}\cap\left\{\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta_{k}=0\right\}\right)=0.

(42)

Our next result is that the second event in (
42
) has probability one, so if indeed
(
42
) holds, the first event (a lack of global convergence) will have probability zero.

Corollary 14

(Trust-Region Radius Converges to Zero)
.

If the assumptions of Theorem
3
hold, then the ProxSTORM trust-region radius,
\Delta_{k}
, satisfies

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta_{k}=0.

Proof.

Taking the (unconditional) expectation of (
21
) and summing over

gives

\Theta\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{k}^{2}\right]\leq\Psi_{0}-\mathbb{E}[\Psi_{n+1}],

and since both
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k}
and
\{\Delta_{k}\}_{k}
are uniformly bounded from below, the monotone convergence theorem implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{k}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k}^{2}\right]<\infty.

As a result,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k}^{2}<\infty
with probability one, completing the proof.
∎

(49)

6
Conclusion

We developed a new method, ProxSTORM (Algorithm
1
) for
objective functions that are bounded from below but include a proper, closed, and convex term
\varphi
that is in general nonsmooth.
When
\varphi\equiv 0
, ProxSTORM reduces to STORM.
Our more general ProxSTORM algorithm inherits the key properties of first-order STORM; specifically, a limit-type global convergence guarantee (Theorem
18
) and an
\epsilon^{-2}
complexity bound (Theorem
19
).

A difference between ProxSTORM and deterministic trust-region methods
Conn et al. 2000
is that the latter do not typically require the trust-region radii to decrease to zero.
This is intuitive: close to an optimal solution, deterministic algorithms should take unencumbered Newton-like steps for fast convergence.
In contrast, the trust-region radii of the stochastic ProxSTORM and STORM methods satisfy the Robbins-and-Monro-like conditions
Robbins and Monro 1951

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k}^{2}<\infty\quad\text{and possibly}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{k}=\infty.

In particular,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\Delta_{k}=0
(Corollary
14
), meaning these stochastic trust-region methods will reject steps infinitely often, as that is the only mechanism for reducing
\Delta_{k}
.
For deterministic settings, inexactness conditions exist that do not drive the trust-region radii to zero.
Consider, for example, the inexactness condition from
Baraldi and Kouri 2022
for gradients of models.
In our notation, it reads,

\left\|\nabla M_{k}(X_{k})-\nabla f(X_{k})\right\|\leq c\min\{\|H_{k}\|,\Delta_{k}\}\quad\text{for some constant }c.

The proximal gradient of the the model appears on the right-hand side of this inequality, so the inequality is implemented using an iteration (Algorithm 4 of that paper).
Unfortunately, such iterations are ill-suited for stochastic settings like ours.
When an inequality does not hold with full probability, iterations like that for the condition above amplify the chances of an erroneous exit in a manner that is impractical to control.
Better understanding nuances like this in order to design rigorous yet practical stochastic algorithms is an interesting topic for future research.
