Combining fruquintinib with TAS-102 as a promising strategy: antitumor activity in preclinical colorectal and gastric cancer xenograft models
Authors: Takahito Hara, Megumi Morimoto, Masaaki Kakehi, Momoko Kamedani, Miyu Nakayama, Syunsuke Yamamoto, Tsuyoshi Osaka, Junpei Soeda, Hideaki Bando, Takayuki Yoshino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe combination of bevacizumab and TAS-102 has emerged as a treatment option for metastatic colorectal cancer (CRC). Bevacizumab is an anti-VEGF-A antibody, and TAS-102 is a cytotoxic nucleotide analog. Recently, fruquintinib, an oral, potent and highly selective inhibitor of all three vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs), has been approved for the treatment of chemorefractory CRC. The purpose of this study was to investigate the combined antitumor activity of fruquintinib and TAS-102 in CRC and gastric cancer (GC) models and to reveal possible mechanisms to explain the combined effects.MethodsIn vitro angiogenic and lymphangiogenic activities were studied by tube formation assays using HUVECs and HDLECs, respectively. Protein expression was measured by Western blot analysis. Intratumor drug concentrations were determined by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis. Antitumor activity was studied in xenograft models.ResultsFruquintinib inhibited tube formation in both HUVECs and HDLECs. Trifluridine, an anti-neoplastic component of TAS-102, potentiated the inhibition of tube formation by fruquintinib in HUVECs but not in HDLECs. Suppression of VEGFR downstream signaling, together with induction of DNA damage response, was observed in HUVECs treated with the fruquintinib plus trifluridine combination. In an HT-29 CRC xenograft model, the intratumor levels of trifluridine and its active metabolites were higher following the fruquintinib plus TAS-102 combination treatment than following TAS-102 monotherapy. Antitumor studies demonstrated that the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 suppressed tumor growth more potently than individual agents in HT-29 and COLO-205 CRC xenograft models. The combined effect was comparable to that of bevacizumab plus TAS-102 in CRC models and was also observed in SC-2-JCK and MKN45 GC xenograft models. The addition of fruquintinib did not increase body weight loss caused by TAS-102 alone.ConclusionsThe combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 improved antitumor activity in CRC and GC xenograft models, with in vitro evidence of enhanced anti-angiogenic activity of fruquintinib by trifluridine and ex vivo evidence of an increased amount of intratumor trifluridine with fruquintinib. Our data support testing the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 in clinical studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16153-5.Keywords: Fruquintinib, TAS-102, Colorectal cancer, Gastric cancer, VEGF, VEGFR, Xenograft model, Angiogenesis

Background

The family members of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) tyrosine kinases, VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2 and VEGFR-3, play critical roles in angiogenesis and/or lymphangiogenesis [1, 2]. The vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family consists of VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C, VEGF-D and placental growth factor (PlGF) [1, 2]. VEGFR-1 is a receptor of VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and PlGF [2]. VEGFR-1 signaling is critical for tumor growth and metastasis as well as the activation of monocytes and macrophages [2]. VEGFR-2 binds to VEGF-A, VEGF-C, and VEGF-D and plays a critical role in angiogenesis [1, 2]. VEGFR-3 binds to VEGF-C and VEGF-D and is involved in lymphangiogenesis [2]. Angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis not only contribute to tumor growth through the supply of nutrients and oxygen to tumor cells, but also to hematogenous and lymphatic metastasis [3, 4]. Anti-angiogenic drugs such as anti-VEGF-A bevacizumab and VEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have been widely used for cancer treatment [5]. It has also been reported that anti-angiogenic agents might normalize poorly organized imperfect blood vessels in tumors to decrease interstitial pressure, resulting in increased blood supply and accumulation of combined cytotoxic drugs in tumors [6]. Anti-angiogenic drugs are included in the central part of drug treatments for colorectal cancer (CRC). Generally, FOLFOX/FOLFIRI plus bevacizumab or anti-epidermal growth factor receptor is used for the first- and second-line treatment of CRC, followed by TAS-102, a cytotoxic nucleotide analog, or regorafenib, a VEGFR TKI, as a third-line treatment or later. Recently, the phase III SUNLIGHT trial in chemo-refractory metastatic CRC showed that the combination of bevacizumab plus TAS-102 resulted in significantly longer overall survival and progression-free survival, and better disease control than TAS-102 monotherapy [7]. Also, recently, fruquintinib, an oral, potent, and highly selective TKI against VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2, and VEGFR-3, has been approved for the treatment of chemo-refractory metastatic CRC. Fruquintinib demonstrated anti-angiogenic activity with VEGFR-2 inhibition in nonclinical models [8]. The purpose of this study is to reveal the combined antitumor activity of fruquintinib and TAS-102 in CRC and gastric cancer (GC) models and to elucidate possible mechanisms to explain the combined effects. We first investigated the combined effects of fruquintinib and trifluridine (FTD), an anti-neoplastic component of TAS-102, on angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis in vitro. Next, we investigated the intratumor concentrations of FTD and its active metabolites following the combination treatment with fruquintinib and TAS-102. Finally, we investigated the antitumor activity of the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 in CRC and GC xenograft models.

Methods

Reagents

For in vitro studies, fruquintinib, bevacizumab, FTD and regorafenib were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). F3dTMP, F3dTDP, and F3dTTP were synthesized by the Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). For the in vivo studies, fruquintinib was suspended in 0.5% carboxymethylcellulose-Na. Bevacizumab was purchased from Chugai Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) and was dissolved in saline. TAS-102 (FTD-tipiracil hydrochloride mixture) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals LLC (Houston, TX, USA)(RRID-SCR_003823) and suspended in 0.5% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (Shin-Etsu Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). [13C,15N2]-FTD, purchased from Alsachim (Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France), was used as the internal standard for bioanalysis.

Cells

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (RRID-CVCL_2959) were purchased from Lonza (Basel, Switzerland)(RRID-SCR_000377) and maintained in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2) (Lonza). Human dermal lymphatic endothelial cells (HDLECs, adult donors) were purchased from PromoCell (Heidelberg, Germany)(RRID-SCR_023579) and maintained in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium MV2 (PromoCell).

The human colon cancer cell line HT-29 (RRID-CVCL_0320) was purchased from the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Porton Down, UK)(RRID-SCR_010617) and maintained in McCoy’s 5 A (modified) medium (Sigma-Aldrich, US)(RRID-SCR_008988) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)(Nichirei Bioscience Inc. Japan). The human colorectal cancer cell line COLO-205 (RRID-CVCL_0218) was purchased from the RIKEN BioResource Research Center (Ibaraki, Japan)(RRID-SCR_003250). Human GC cell line MKN45 (RRID-CVCL_0434) was purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Biosources Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan) (RRID-SCR_023187). COLO-205 and MKN45 cells were maintained in RPMI1640 medium (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Japan) supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were cultured at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. The GC patient-derived tumor xenograft model SC-2-JCK was purchased from the Central Institute for Experimental Medicine and Life Science (Kanagawa, Japan).

Tube formation assays

HUVECs and HDLECs were cultured overnight in the assay medium (EBM-2 for HUVECs and Endothelial Cell Basal Medium MV (Takara Bio, Japan)(RRID-SCR_021372) for HDLECs in the absence of FBS). On the following day, they were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/35 µL/well in the assay medium into the µ-Plate 96 Well 3D (ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany) coated with 10 µL of the Geltrex LDEV-Free Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Matrix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The cultures were then treated with VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C and VEGF-D (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) (For HUVECs, VEGF-A: 3000 ng/mL, VEGF-B: 10 ng/mL, VEGF-C: 300 ng/mL, and VEGF-D: 300 ng/mL. For HDLECs, VEGF-A: 3000 ng/mL, VEGF-B: 0.3 ng/mL, VEGF-C: 100 ng/mL, and VEGF-D: 300 ng/mL) and/or test drugs in 35 µL of the assay medium. Following 16–24 h of incubation, the cultures were treated with 1 µM Calcein AM (Nacalai Tesque) for 30 min. Fluorescent imaging was performed using Operetta CLS (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA)(RRID-SCR_018810) and the tube length was quantified using Harmony software (PerkinElmer)(RRID-SCR_018809). All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least twice to confirm reproducibility.

Simple western

VEGFR downstream signaling and DNA damage response (DDR) signaling were evaluated by the Simple Western automated western blot system using Simple Western Jess (ProteinSimple, Tokyo, Japan) (RRID-SCR_025095). HUVECs or HDLECs were cultured overnight in the assay medium as tube formation assays. On the following day, they were seeded with 1 × 105 cells/0.25 mL/well in the assay medium into a 24-well plate coated with 100 µL of the Geltrex LDEV-Free Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Matrix and treated with test drugs in 0.25 mL of the assay medium for 4 h. The cells were subsequently treated with 10 × VEGF-mix in 50 µL of the assay medium for 10 min. Cultures were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Antibodies against extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) (RRID-AB_330744), phosphorylated ERK (pERK; Thr202/Tyr204) (RRID-AB_331646), protein kinase B (Akt) (RRID-AB_329827), phosphorylated Akt (pAkt; Ser473) (RRID-AB_329825), checkpoint kinase 1 (Chk1) (RRID-AB_2080320), phosphorylated Chk1 (pChk1, Ser345) (RRID- AB_331212), checkpoint kinase 2 (Chk2) (RRID-AB_11178526), phosphorylated Chk2 (pChk2, Thr68) (RRID-AB_2080501) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (Danvers, MA, USA) (RRID-AB_476977). The area under the curve (AUC) of the chemiluminescence signal peak was analyzed using Compass for SW software (version 4.1.0, ProteinSimple).

Animals

Six-week-old female BALB/cSlc-nu-nu mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan) and used for all HT-29 xenograft studies. Six-week-old female BALB/cAJcl-nu/nu mice were purchased from CLEA Japan, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan)(RRID-SCR_026053) and used for the MKN45 and SC-2-JCK xenograft studies. Six-week-old female CAnN.Cg-Foxn1nu/CrlCrlj mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory Japan (Kanagawa, Japan) and used for the COLO-205 xenograft studies. The animals were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle at a constant temperature of 23 ± 2 °C while food and water were provided ad libitum. Euthanasia was performed by inhalant anesthetic overdose followed by exsanguination. Briefly, using V-1 Tabletop Laboratory Animal Anesthesia System (VetEquip Inc.), 2 to 5% isoflurane was administered and continued until respiratory arrest occurred. The animal was removed, and rapid exsanguination was performed by incision of abdominal aorta to assure euthanasia. All other in vivo procedures were conducted in compliance with the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments of Japan, and at facilities approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Quantification of FTD and its phosphorylated forms in tumors

Intratumor FTD and its three phosphorylated forms (F3dTMP, F3dTDP, and F3dTTP) were determined by liquid chromatography (Nexera Ultra, High Performance Liquid Chromatograph, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) (RRID-SCR_026529) coupled with a Triple Quad 6500 + System mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, MA, USA)(RRID-SCR_025627). Nude mice bearing HT-29 xenograft tumors were treated with TAS-102 monotherapy 300 mg/kg/day BID orally (days 1–4), a combination of TAS-102 (days 1–4) plus fruquintinib 2 mg/kg/day QD orally (days 1–4) or a combination of TAS-102 (days 1–4) plus bevacizumab 10 mg/kg intraperitoneally (day 1). Tumors were collected and frozen quickly in liquid nitrogen 2 h after the last TAS-102 administration. For extraction of FTD and its metabolite, the tumors were homogenized in 0.4 N perchloric acid solution (FUJI FILM Wako Pure Chmical, Osaka, Japan) with a Multi‑Beads Shocker (Taitec Corporation, Saitama, Japan) and centrifuged at 3200 rpm for 2 min at 4˚C. Two hundred µL of the homogenate spiked with internal standard was extracted with 300 µL of tri‑n‑octylamine/dichloromethane (10:35, v/v), and the organic solvent was removed after centrifugation at 20 000 × g for 3 min at 4˚C. The mixture was centrifuged at 20 000 × g for 5 min at 4˚C, and the supernatant was transferred to another tube. The same volume of mobile phase A (5 mM dibutylammonium acetate containing 5 µM methylenediphosphonic acid) was added to the tube, and 7.5 µL of sample was subjected to liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry coupled with C18 column (2.1 mm I.D. × 50 mM, 1.9 μm particle size; YMC, Kyoto, Japan). Mobile phase B consisted of methanol with 5 µM methylenediphosphonic acid and the flow rate was 0.75 mL/min. Mobile phase B was started from 5% of total flow and increased to 35% at 2.7 min. The ionization mode was negative and each precursor and product ion pair transition (m/z 295–115.9 for FTD, m/z 374.9–181.9 for F3dTMP, F3dTDP, and F3dTTP ) was chosen for multiple reaction monitoring mode. Samples from xenografts that were not administered TAS-102 were used as blanks.

Antitumor activity

HT-29, COLO-205 and MKN45 cancer cells were transplanted subcutaneously into one flank of each mouse at densities of 5 × 106, 3 × 106 and 1 × 107 cells/mouse in 50% Matrigel/phosphate-buffered saline (without Matrigel for COLO-205), respectively. SC-2-JCK tumors were transplanted by tumor pieces. When the mean tumor volumes (TVs) reached approximately 100–200 mm3, the animals were randomized. Fruquintinib was administered orally from day 1 to the end of the treatment period (day 28, day 18, day 28, and day 18 in HT-29, COLO-205, SC-2-JCK, and MKN45, respectively), once daily (QD). TAS-102 was administered twice daily (BID) orally from day 1 to 14. The TAS-102 dose was caluculated based on the FTD amount. Bevacizumab was administered intraperitoneally twice a week. In the control group, the vehicle was administered in a similar manner. The tumor diameter and body weight (BW) were measured twice per week. The TV was estimated as 0.5 × length × width2. The tumor growth inhibition ratio (TGI) was calculated using the following formula: TGI (%) = (1 − TV of the treated group/TV of the control group) × 100 (%). Additionally, the MKN45 tumors were collected on the last day of the study to prepare hematoxylin and eosin-stained tissue sections. The necrotic area, determined by the area of low nuclear density, was calculated using the open-source software QuPath (RRID-SCR_018257).

3D structural analysis of vessel architecture

Xenograft tumors were collected on the last day of the SC-2-JCK antitumor efficacy study. Before tumor sampling, mice were administered Alexa Fluor 594-labeled anti-cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31) antibodies (clone MEC13.3 and clone 390, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA)(RRID-AB_2563319) and heparin intravenously followed by perfusion with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde. Post-fixation and clearing of tumors were conducted as previously described [9]. Briefly, samples were dehydrated in methanol and bleached with hydrogen peroxide. After rehydration, antigen retrieval and immunostaining were conducted to stain α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)+ vessels with a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled anti-α-SMA antibody (clone 1A4, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). The samples were then dehydrated, transferred to dichloromethane, and optically cleared using dibenzyl ether. The cleared specimens were scanned with an UltraMicroscope Blaze (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany)(RRID-SCR_008984). Imaging analysis was performed using the Imaris (version 9.7.0, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK) (RRID-SCR_007370). The morphologies of αSMA + and CD31 + vessels were extracted using the filament tracer module, and the structural parameters were calculated.

Statistical analysis

In tube formation assays, differences between the control and treatment groups were analyzed using Dunnett’s test, and differences between each treatment group in combination studies were analyzed using the Tukey-Kramer test. In studies measuring intratumor drug levels, differences between the TAS-102 monotherapy group and combination treatment groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test with Holm’s correction. In antitumor studies, differences between the control and treatment groups were analyzed using Dunnett’s test, and differences between the combination and each single agent group were analyzed using Student’s t-test with Holm’s correction. In the 3D imaging analysis, the differences between the control and treatment groups were analyzed using Dunnett’s test. For all experiments, statistical analyses were conducted after evaluating data normality and variance.

Results

Enhanced in vitro anti-angiogenic activity of fruquintinib by FTD

First, we studied the effects of the combination of fruquintinib with FTD, an anti-neoplastic component of TAS-102, on angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis in vitro. It has been reported that tumors in patients with CRC or GC express VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C or VEGF-D at high levels [10, 11]. Therefore, to mimic the VEGF status in CRC and GC tumors in patients, we performed tube formation assays using HUVECs and HDLECs stimulated with a mixture of VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C and VEGF-D. In the presence of the VEGF mixture, we investigated the activity of fruquintinib, bevacizumab, and FTD, together with regorafenib.

Fruquintinib and regorafenib suppressed HUVEC tube formation in a concentration-dependent manner to basal levels, whereas bevacizumab did not suppress it to basal levels except at 1000 µg/mL (Fig. 1A). Similar responses were observed for the HDLECs (Fig. 1B). In HUVECs, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of fruquintinib, bevacizumab, and regorafenib were 0.3 nM, 8.8 µg/mL, and 2.0 nM, respectively, while those in HDLECs were 1.4 nM, 230 µg/mL, and 9.0 nM, respectively. FTD did not suppress HUVEC or HDLEC tube formation (Fig. 1A and B).

We then investigated the effect of the combination treatment of fruquintinib or bevacizumab plus FTD on tube formation at the concentrations near the IC50 values for fruquintinib and bevacizumab, and those of clinical relevance for FTD. In HUVECs, the tube formation inhibitory activity of 0.3 nM fruquintinib was potentiated by the addition of 0.3 µM and 1 µM FTD (Fig. 1C, E). A similar trend was observed with 10 µg/mL bevacizumab and 0.3 µM and 1 µM FTD, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 1C, E). In contrast, in HDLECs, the same concentrations of FTD did not potentiate the inhibition of tube formation by 1 nM fruquintinib or 100 µg/mL bevacizumab (Fig. 1D, E).

Molecular signaling in HUVECs and HDLECs by combination of fruquintinib plus FTD

To reveal the molecular signaling underlying the effect of the combination of fruquintinib and FTD on HUVEC tube formation, we investigated the phosphorylation of ERK and Akt as downstream signaling effects of VEGFR activation in the culture conditions of tube formation assays. First, we confirmed that the VEGFR TKIs fruquintinib and regorafenib, at concentrations of 0.3–30 nM, suppressed the phosphorylation of ERK and Akt in a concentration-dependent manner to basal levels (Fig. 2A, B, C, Fig.S1), while bevacizumab at 10 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL did not suppress them to basal levels in HUVECs (Fig. 2A, B, C, Fig.S1). FTD at 0.3 µM and 1 µM did not affect ERK or Akt phosphorylation (Fig. 2A, B, C, Fig.S1). Similar responses were observed in the HDLEC (Fig. 2A, D, E, Fig. S1). Next, since we reasoned that FTD would potentiate ERK and Akt signal suppression by fruquintinib, we examined the effects of the combination of fruquintinib and FTD on ERK and Akt signaling. Contrary to expectations, adding 0.3 µM and 1 µM FTD did not potentiate either pERK/ERK or pAkt/Akt inhibition by 0.3 nM fruquintinib in HUVECs (Fig. 2F, G, H, Fig. S2). Similar responses were observed in HDLECs, where the addition of FTD at 0.3 µM and 1 µM did not potentiate pERK/ERK or pAkt/Akt inhibition by 1 nM fruquintinib (Fig. 2F, I, J, Fig. S2).

To further investigate molecular signaling for the potentiation of the anti-angiogenic effects of fruquintinib by FTD, we examined the phosphorylation of Chk1/2 as a DNA damage response signal [12] because FTD is a DNA-targeting agent. In HUVECs, 0.3 µM and 1 µM FTD clearly induced the phosphorylation of Chk1 (Fig. 2F, K, Fig. S2). Fruquintinib (0.3 nM) did not induce the phosphorylation of Chk1 (Fig. 2F, K, Fig. S2). The extent of pChk1/Chk1 induction by FTD did not increase when combined with 0.3 nM fruquintinib (Fig. 2F, K, Fig. S2). Unlike in HUVECs, the induction of Chk1 phosphorylation in HDLECs was not clearly detected in the FTD-containing treatments (Fig. 2F, L, Fig. S2). pChk2 was not detected in HUVECs or HDLECs.

Increased intratumor levels of FTD and its phosphates by fruquintinib

Since it has been reported that anti-angiogenic agents might normalize imperfect tumor vasculature to increase blood flow and accumulate combined cytotoxic drugs in tumors [6], we measured the concentrations of FTD and its phosphorylated forms (F3dTMP, F3dTDP, and F3dTTP) in the HT-29 xenograft tumors after the mice had been treated with TAS-102 monotherapy, combination of fruquintinib plus TAS-102, and combination of bevacizumab plus TAS-102 for 4 days. In the TAS-102 monotherapy group, the amount gradually decreased from FTD towards the serially proceeded phosphorylated forms (FTD > F3dTMP > F3dTDP > F3dTTP), with an FTD concentration of 6.22 nmol/g (Fig. 3). In the fruquintinib plus TAS-102 combination group, the FTD, F3dTMP, and F3dTDP levels were significantly higher than those in the TAS-102 monotherapy group, with an FTD concentration of 20.0 nmol/g (Fig. 3). In the bevacizumab plus TAS-102 combination group, the FTD, F3dTMP, and F3dTDP levels were significantly higher, but the F3dTTP level was significantly lower than that of TAS-102 monotherapy, with an FTD concentration of 12.8 nmol/g (Fig. 3). The differences in FTD, F3dTMP, and F3dTDP levels between the fruquintinib plus TAS-102 group and the bevacizumab plus TAS-102 group were not statistically significant (Fig. 3).

Enhanced antitumor activity with the combination of fruquintinib plus TAS-102

Next, we studied the antitumor activity of a combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 in two CRC and two GC xenograft models. In the CRC xenograft models, the combination of bevacizumab and TAS-102, one of the current therapies for metastatic CRC, was evaluated simultaneously in the same experiments. In the HT-29 CRC xenograft model, the combination of fruquintinib (2 mg/kg/day QD orally) and TAS-102 (300 mg/kg/day BID orally) suppressed tumor growth more potently than treatment with each agent alone (Fig. 4A). The combination of bevacizumab (10 mg/kg twice per week intraperitoneally) and TAS-102 (300 mg/kg/day BID orally) also suppressed tumor growth more potently than each single agent (Fig. 4B). The TGIs of fruquintinib, TAS-102, bevacizumab, fruquintinib plus TAS-102, and bevacizumab plus TAS-102 were 56.2%, 38.5%, 45.1%, 68.4%, and 70.3%, respectively (Fig. 4A and B). The change in BW in each treatment group was moderate (Fig. 4C).

In the COLO-205 CRC xenograft model, the combination of fruquintinib (1 mg/kg/day QD orally) and TAS-102 (150 mg/kg/day BID orally) suppressed tumor growth more potently than treatment with each agent alone (Fig. 4D). The combined effect of fruquintinib and TAS-102 was comparable to that of bevacizumab (1 mg/kg twice per week intraperitoneally) and TAS-102 (150 mg/kg/day BID orally) (Fig. 4E). The TGIs of fruquintinib, TAS-102, bevacizumab, fruquintinib plus TAS-102, and bevacizumab plus TAS-102 were 36.4%, 41.2%, 43.4%, 59.6%, and 67.3%, respectively (Fig. 4D and E). BW loss in the vehicle group was severe, and one mouse was euthanized on day 15 owing to more than 20% BW loss from day 0 (baseline) in accordance with animal ethics. The change in BW in the other treatment groups was moderate (Fig. 4F).

In the SC-2-JCK GC xenograft model, the combination of fruquintinib (5 mg/kg/day QD orally) and TAS-102 (150 mg/kg/day BID orally) suppressed tumor growth more potently than treatment with each agent alone (Fig. 5A). The TGIs of fruquintinib, TAS-102, and fruquintinib plus TAS-102 were 62.4%, 54.2%, and 76.2%, respectively (Fig. 5A). The change in BW in each treatment group was moderate (Fig. 5B). 3D vasculature structure analysis revealed that the vessel length and branch number of CD31 + vessels, as well as the vessel length of αSMA+ vessels, were significantly reduced in the TAS-102, fruquintinib, and fruquintinib plus TAS-102 treatment groups (Fig. 5C and D, Fig. S3). The branch number of αSMA+ vessels was also significantly reduced in the fruquintinib plus TAS-102 treatment group (Fig. 5C and D). All these parameters were reduced to the greatest extent by the combination treatment (Fig. 5C and D).

In the MKN45 GC xenograft model, the combination of fruquintinib (2 mg/kg/day QD orally) and TAS-102 (150 mg/kg/day BID orally) suppressed tumor growth more potently than treatment with each agent alone (Fig. 5E). The TGIs of fruquintinib, TAS-102, and fruquintinib plus TAS-102 were 48.2%, 29.5%, and 77.9%, respectively (Fig. 5E). In this study, TAS-102 resulted in comparatively severe BW loss; five mice in the TAS-102 monotherapy group were euthanized, and one mouse in the combination group was euthanized, all on day 14, owing to more than 20% BW loss from day 0 (baseline), in accordance with animal ethics. The change in BW in the other treatment groups was moderate (Fig. 5F). Image analysis of the tumor tissue sections revealed that the combination treatment induced significant intratumor necrosis (Fig. 5G and H).

Taken together, the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 potentiated antitumor activity in CRC and GC xenograft models.

Discussion

We demonstrated that (1) the anti-angiogenic activity of fruquintinib was enhanced by FTD in vitro, (2) the intratumor levels of FTD and its active metabolites were increased by cotreatment with fruquintinib, and (3) the antitumor activity was improved by the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 in CRC and GC xenograft models.

In the in vitro tube formation assays in the presence of a mixture of VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C and VEGF-D, fruquintinib and regorafenib completely inhibited tube formation in both HUVECs and HDLECs at clinically relevant concentrations. These results reflect the fact that these drugs are potent inhibitors against VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2, and VEGFR-3. In contrast, bevacizumab showed moderate inhibition of tube formation, in parallel with moderate VEGFR downstream signal inhibition under the experimental conditions, possibly due to the presence of VEGF-B, VEGF-C, and VEGF-D. Consistently, it has been reported that VEGF-D tumor expression may be a potential biomarker for bevacizumab resistance in CRC [13]. Together with the inhibition of tumor growth by fruquintinib in the bevacizumab-insensitive HT-29 model [14], our findings may partially explain why fruquintinib shows benefit for patients previously treated with VEGF antibodies [15, 16].

With respect to the combined effect of fruquintinib and FTD on angiogenesis, we found a possible mechanism responsible for the improved antitumor activity of this combination. FTD single-agent treatment did not inhibit tube formation in HUVECs; however, interestingly, 0.3 µM FTD and 1 µM FTD potentiated the inhibition of tube formation by fruquintinib. The intratumor FTD concentrations in the HT-29 xenograft tumors were 6.22 nmol/g and 20.0 nmol/g with TAS-102 monotherapy and the fruquintinib plus TAS-102 combination, respectively, which approximately corresponded to 0.19 µM and 0.60 µM of the unbound FTD concentrations, respectively. Taken together, these data suggest that increased intratumor FTD levels by the combination treatment might potentiate the anti-angiogenic effect of fruquintinib.

We investigated intracellular signaling by comparing HUVECs, in which tube formation was sensitive to the fruquintinib and FTD combination, with HDLECs, in which a combination effect was not observed. The combination of fruquintinib and FTD suppressed ERK and Akt signaling in both cell lines; however, we observed pChk1 induction by FTD only in HUVECs. The coordination of intracellular signaling caused by both VEGFR inhibition and DDR activation may contribute to the potentiation of anti-angiogenic activity by the combination of fruquintinib and FTD. However, no direct causal link has been demonstrated between pChk1 activation and enhanced anti‑angiogenic effects, and the underlying mechanism remains hypothesis‑generating at this stage. Further studies are necessary to fully elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the enhanced anti-angiogenic activity of the fruquintinib plus FTD combination.

It has been reported that anti-angiogenic agents might normalize imperfect blood vessels in tumors to accumulate combined cytotoxic drugs in tumors [6]; indeed, bevacizumab, when combined with TAS-102, reportedly increases the level of sum-of-FTD phosphates in SW48 CRC xenograft tumors [17]. In this study, we demonstrated that not only bevacizumab, an antibody for VEGF-A, but also fruquintinib, a VEGFR TKI, accumulated cytotoxic FTD and its active metabolites in tumors. We quantified the intratumor concentrations of FTD and its phosphorylated forms (F3dTMP, F3dTDP, and F3dTTP) separately. F3dTMP potently inhibits thymidylate synthase, and F3dTTP is incorporated into DNA, leading to DNA strand breaks [18]. Our data demonstrated that the intratumor levels of FTD, F3dTMP, and F3dTDP in the HT-29 xenografts were increased to a similar extent by the fruquintinib plus TAS-102 combination and the bevacizumab plus TAS-102 combination, compared with TAS-102 monotherapy. At present, the reason why combination treatment with fruquintinib plus TAS-102 or bevacizumab plus TAS-102 did not result in an increased intratumoral concentration of F3dTTP remains unclear. One possible explanation is that these treatments may, through as-yet-undefined mechanisms, suppress the conversion of F3dTDP to F3dTTP. Alternatively, it is conceivable that the incorporation of F3dTTP into DNA is enhanced under these conditions. In support of the latter hypothesis, it has been reported that, given the rapid utilization of F3dTTP for DNA incorporation, its intracellular steady-state level may remain low [18]. Because F3dTTP represents the terminal phosphorylated metabolite and may not accumulate intracellularly but instead be rapidly incorporated into DNA to exert its cytotoxic effects, this possibility may, at least in part, account for the lack of an observable increase in F3dTTP levels. Taken together, the antitumor effect of the combination of fruquintinib plus TAS-102 may partially stem from the cytotoxic effect of FTD and its active metabolites, which are accumulated by fruquintinib, on tumor cells.

The combinatorial effects of fruquintinib or bevacizumab with FTD in the HUVEC angiogenesis inhibition assay were reproducible. However, while the combination effect of fruquintinib plus FTD was more pronounced than that of bevacizumab plus FTD in the HUVEC assay, the antitumor effects of fruquintinib plus TAS-102 and bevacizumab plus TAS-102 were nearly comparable in the in vivo xenograft studies. Taken together, these observations suggest that the increased intratumoral FTD concentration may contribute more substantially to the combinatorial antitumor effects observed in vivo.

For the antitumor xenograft studies, the doses showing suboptimal antitumor activity were set based on preliminary dose-response studies or previously reported findings. The dose of fruquintinib used for the combination studies was 1–5 mg/kg/day. As the dose of fruquintinib of 5 mg/body/day in a clinical setting is equivalent to approximately 4 mg/kg for mice based on the AUC level [19], the doses of fruquintinib used in this study are clinically relevant. For the TAS-102 dose, 150 mg/kg/day or 300 mg/kg/day was used for the combination in this study, which is approximately within the range used in nonclinical models reported previously [20, 21].

It has been reported that tumors in patients with CRC or GC express VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C or VEGF-D at high levels [10, 11]. Using immunohistochemistry, we confirmed that VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C, and VEGF-D were expressed in all tumor models used in this study under untreated conditions, although the expression levels varied among models. However, the expression levels of these VEGF family members following treatment were not evaluated for each treatment group. Comprehensive evaluation of post-treatment expression profiles of VEGF family members may provide novel insights that could contribute to the development of new therapeutic strategies and therefore warrants further investigation.

The mice inoculated with MKN45 xenografts exhibited cancer cachexia, and some mice exhibited more than 20% BW loss with the TAS-102 single-agent treatment at 150 mg/kg/day; however, the addition of fruquintinib did not exacerbate BW loss or rather alleviated it, possibly due to a reduced tumor burden. Careful monitoring of side effects is necessary when evaluating the efficacy of the combination therapies in clinical studies.

In this study, the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 showed enhanced antitumor activity in the HT-29 and COLO-205 CRC xenograft models. The HT-29 and COLO-205 cell lines harbor the BRAF V600E mutation [22], and HT-29 is a bevacizumab-resistant CRC model [14]. The potentiated antitumor activity of fruquintinib plus TAS-102 has also been reported in KRAS-mutant HCT116 and KRAS wild-type SW48 CRC xenograft models [20]. The combination therapy may be beneficial for patients with CRC regardless of their mutation status. In addition, unlike previous studies, we compared directly the combined effects of fruquintinib plus TAS-102 with those of bevacizumab plus TAS-102 in the same experiment. Both combination treatments resulted in similar antitumor activities, suggesting that the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 might be beneficial in a clinical setting. Furthermore, we demonstrated improved antitumor activity with fruquintinib plus TAS-102, compared with TAS-102 monotherapy, in GC xenograft models. Since TAS-102 is currently used for the treatment of GC, this combination therapy may also improve GC therapy.

Although the antitumor efficacy of fruquintinib plus TAS‑102 appeared comparable to that of bevacizumab plus TAS‑102 in the present xenograft models, fruquintinib‑based combination therapy may offer potential clinical advantages. Both fruquintinib and TAS‑102 are orally administered agents, enabling an entirely oral treatment regimen. This may reduce the treatment‑related time burden associated with intravenous administration of bevacizumab, even when delivered in an outpatient setting. In addition, differences in the adverse event profiles of fruquintinib and bevacizumab may allow for more flexible treatment selection tailored to individual patient characteristics and preferences. Taken together, these considerations suggest that fruquintinib plus TAS‑102 could represent an alternative therapeutic option from a patient‑centered perspective.

The current study has several limitations. First, although, we demonstrated improved antitumor activity by the combination of fruquintinib with TAS-102 in four xenograft models, we performed measurement of intratumor FTD concentrations, 3D vasculature analysis, and analysis of intratumor necrosis area only in HT-29, SC-2-JCK, and MKN45 models, respectively. Further study is required to validate findings across multiple models to avoid potential bias. Second, the intratumor FTD concentrations and 3D vasculature parameters were measured only at a single time point. In addition, the sample size of the 3D vasculature analysis was relatively small because tissue clearing and subsequent quantitative parameter analyses are highly time‑ and labor‑intensive procedures, which limited the number of samples available for analysis. Sequential analyses at several time points with larger sample size might bolster the reliability of the data. Third, we demonstrated, for the first time, the inhibition of lymphangiogenesis by fruquintinib using HDLECs in vitro, making it important to confirm the anti-lymphangiogenic effect of fruquintinib or the combination with TAS-102 in vivo.

Conclusions

We demonstrated that the combination of fruquintinib and TAS-102 improved antitumor activity in CRC and GC xenograft models, with in vitro evidence of enhanced anti-angiogenic activity of fruquintinib by FTD and ex vivo evidence of an increased amount of intratumor FTD and its active metabolites with fruquintinib. The combination therapy may be a promising option for treating CRC and GC.
