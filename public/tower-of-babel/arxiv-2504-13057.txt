Covariate balancing estimation and model selection for difference-in-differences approach
Takamichi Baba

Abstract
Remarkable progress has been made in difference-in-differences (DID) approaches to causal inference that estimate the average effect of a treatment on the treated (ATT). Of these, the semiparametric DID (SDID) approach incorporates a propensity score analysis into the DID setup. Supposing that the ATT is a function of covariates, we estimate it by weighting the inverse of the propensity score. In this study, as one way to make the estimation robust to the propensity score modeling, we incorporate covariate balancing. Then, by attentively constructing the moment conditions used in the covariate balancing, we show that the proposed estimator is doubly robust. In addition to the estimation, we also address model selection. In practice, covariate selection is an essential task in statistical analysis, but even in the basic setting of the SDID approach, there are no reasonable information criteria. Here, we derive a model selection criterion as an asymptotically bias-corrected estimator of risk based on the loss function used in the SDID estimation. We show that a penalty term can be derived that is considerably different from almost twice the number of parameters that often appears in AIC-type information criteria. Keywords : Average effect of treatment on the treated, Doubly robustness, Information criterion, Moment condition, Propensity score analysis, Semiparametric difference-in-differences, Statistical asymptotic theory, Weighted mean squared risk



1
Introduction

The difference-in-differences (DID) approach is one of the most common methods used in fields such as economics and epidemiology to assess the effect of a particular intervention or treatment. In the basic setting, there are two groups: a treatment group receiving a specific treatment and a control group receiving no treatment or other treatment. The pre- and post-treatment outcomes of each group are measured and the change in the outcome for the control group is subtracted from the change in the outcome for the treatment group to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). Under the parallel trend assumption, an estimation in the DID approach is valid. Recently,
Abadie (2005)
dealt with inference under a more natural assumption that involves using information from the covariates, specifically a parallel trend assumption conditioned on the covariates. They further proposed a semiparametric DID (SDID) approach that does not necessarily require modeling of the covariates to the outcome and that estimates the ATT by weighting the inverse of the propensity score.

With or without conditionality, the parallel trend assumption is key to the DID approach. This assumption is one where the mean changes over time in the potential outcomes of the treatment group and the control group would have been similar if there had been no treatment. On the other hand, especially in epidemiology, the ignorable treatment assignment assumption has usually been invoked in causal inference (e.g.
Robins et al. 1994
).
This is the assumption that the potential outcome variable and the treatment assignment variable, which represents the received treatment, become independent conditioned on the covariates. Although neither assumption is verifiable, to evaluate the validity of the parallel trend assumption, visual inspection is useful when observations over many time points are available. Moreover, the parallel trend assumption naturally holds if it can be supposed that there is no change over time in the expectation of outcomes without treatment. Needless to say, the parallel trend assumption is considerably weaker than the ignorable treatment assignment assumption, and estimating the ATT under such a desirable assumption is the advantage that the DID approach gains in exchange for collecting pre- and post-treatment outcomes.

The methodological development of the DID approach has been growing because of the above-mentioned advantages and the increasing amount of available observational study data (see
Roth et al. 2023
for a comprehensive review of recent studies on the DID approach). However, for example,
Abadie (2005)
assumes that the model for the propensity score is correctly specified. However, the estimator will have a bias if the model is misspecified.
As an extension of
Abadie (2005)
,
Sant’Anna and Zhao (2020)
assumes not only a regression model for the treatment assignment variable used to obtain the propensity score, but also a regression model for the outcome variable, and proposes an estimator for the ATT derived from the two models. Their estimator is consistent if either the treatment assignment model or the outcome regression model is correctly specified, that is, the estimator is doubly robust.

In this article, as with
Abadie (2005)
, we suppose that the ATT is a function of covariates and address the semiparametric estimation problem for that function.
From the estimated function, the heterogeneity of the ATT, that is, the conditional ATT can be evaluated.
Two regression models as above are considered, and in order to give double robustness to the estimation, we incorporate covariate balancing (e.g.
Egel et al. 2008
,
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
and
Fan et al. 2022
), a method using propensity scores such that a certain moment condition is satisfied. This method actually estimates the treatment assignment model, but only supposes an outcome model and does not estimate it. Nevertheless, the SDID estimator derived by weighting the inverse of the propensity score, which is obtained by the covariate balancing, will be consistent if either of the regression models is correctly specified. One point to note is that, in order for the SDID approach to have this favorable asymptotic property, the moment condition has to be constructed in a different way from the usual one, which is due to considering the conditional ATT. Also, the incorporation of covariate balancing leads to an estimation via weights that always balances the moments of the covariates across groups, which is also preferable from an interpretive point of view (
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
). We call the proposed method covariate balancing for DID (CBD).

Obviously, model selection is also an essential task in using the DID approach in a series of data-analysis tasks.
Specifically, by selecting the function of covariates for the ATT addressed in this paper, it becomes possible to evaluate the heterogeneity of the ATT. There have been various important studies conducted in this area.
For example, in a study estimating the effect of union membership on wages,
Houngbedji (2016)
treated the workers’ demographics with a linear model for causal effects and explored factors affecting the causal effect on the basis of the values of the regression coefficients and its
p
-value. In other words, they addressed the issue of covariate selection. However, there is no satisfactory information criterion, a basic tool for model selection, even for the basic setting of SDID. To remedy this situation, we will derive an information criterion for a basic SDID approach and the CBD method that is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of the risk based on the loss function used in the estimation. Traditional information criteria such as the generalized information criterion (GIC) (
Konishi and Kitagawa 1996
) cannot be applied because the loss function includes weights based on the propensity score, which are random variables. Since the SDID approach places importance on being based on the conditional parallel trend assumption, it does not rely on the ignorable treatment assignment assumption when deriving the information criterion, which causes some difficulties. As a result, we will derive a penalty term that is considerably different from twice the number of parameters, which often appears in AIC-type information criteria including the GIC.

In summary, the aim of this paper is to develop a new doubly robust estimation method with the advantage of covariate balancing in the SDID approach and to derive a model selection criterion for the SDID approach, for which no reasonable model selection criteria exist yet. Its organization is as follows. Section
2
describes the model and the estimation target and introduces the SDID approach that was proposed in
Abadie (2005)
. In Section
3
, we propose the CBD method and give a theorem showing its double robustness. Numerical experiments indicate that the estimation is robust against model misspecification. In Section
4
, we derive a model selection criterion for when the propensity score is known as a bias-corrected estimator of the risk that is naturally defined from the loss function used in the SDID estimation. Then, we also prove a theorem that suggests that the penalty term of the model selection criterion is asymptotically valid. As a comparison, we derive a model selection criterion that naturally extends the idea of QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
in
Platt et al. (2013)
to the SDID approach. Furthermore, through numerical experiments, we verify the approximation accuracy of the penalty term of the proposed criterion from the perspective of risk estimation and show its superior performance to the QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
from the viewpoint of risk minimization. Section
5
summarizes the results for the CBD method in the same way as Section
4
. In other words, a model selection criterion is derived from the naturally defined risk, a theorem supports the validity of its penalty term, and its performance is evaluated through numerical experiments. Section
6
reports the results of the estimation and model selection for the real data in
LaLonde (1986)
. Finally, Section
7
discusses future developments of the proposed approach.

2
Preparation

2.1
Model and Assumptions

Suppose that there are two groups, a treatment group and a control group, and that outcomes are collected before and after the possible treatment time point, respectively. We consider the counterfactual setting, where
y^{[1]}(t)
denotes the outcome variable of the treatment group,
y^{[0]}(t)
the outcome variable of the control group,
t=0
before treatment and
t=1
after treatment. In practice, only one of the outcomes of the treatment group or control group can be obtained. By preparing treatment assignment variables such that
d^{[1]}=1\ (d^{[0]}=0)
if the sample is assigned to the treatment group and
d^{[0]}=1\ (d^{[1]}=0)
if the sample is assigned to the control group, the observed outcome variable collected at each time point is expressed as

\displaystyle y(t)=d^{[1]}y^{[1]}(t)+d^{[0]}y^{[0]}(t).

Denoting the change in the outcome variables at two time points in the treatment group as
\Delta^{[1]}=y^{[1]}(1)-y^{[1]}(0)
and the change in the control group as
\Delta^{[0]}=y^{[0]}(1)-y^{[0]}(0)
, the observed change is expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta=d^{[1]}\Delta^{[1]}+d^{[0]}\Delta^{[0]}.

Here, for the ATT conditioned on the covariate
x\ (\in\mathbb{R}^{p})
,

\displaystyle a(x)\equiv{\rm E}[y^{[1]}(1)-y^{[0]}(1)\mid x,d^{[1]}=1],

(1)

we suppose
g(x;\theta)

(\theta\in\theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p})
as a working model to approximate it, and for simplicity, set the working model to be a linear one
x^{{\rm T}}\theta
. Then, we estimate
\theta
and give the ATT by taking the expectation with respect to
x
in the treatment group.

Let us define the optimal value of
\theta
as

\displaystyle\theta^{*}\equiv\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}{\rm E}[\{{\rm E}[y^{[1]}(1)-y^{[0]}(1)\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]-x^{{\rm T}}\theta\}^{2}\mid d^{[1]}=1].

(2)

Abadie (2005)
shows that
\theta^{*}
can be consistently estimated with the SDID approach by using propensity scores
e^{[1]}(x)\equiv{\rm pr}(d^{[1]}=1\mid x)
and
e^{[0]}(x)\equiv{\rm pr}(d^{[0]}=1\mid x)
under several assumptions, and therefore the ATT can be consistently estimated. The assumptions are as follows.

Assumption 1

(Conditional parallel trend)
.

It holds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}[y^{[0]}(1)-y^{[0]}(0)\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]={\rm E}[y^{[0]}(1)-y^{[0]}(0)\mid x,d^{[0]}=1],

which means that, under the expectation conditioned on
x
, the change in outcomes without treatment is the same regardless of whether it is the treatment group or the control group.

Assumption 2

(No-anticipation)
.

It holds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}[y^{[1]}(0)-y^{[0]}(0)\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]=0,

which means that, on average, there is no treatment effect prior to its implementation.

Assumption 3

(Strong positivity)
.

For some
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\varepsilon<{\rm pr}(d^{[1]}=1\mid x)<1-\varepsilon.

Abadie (2005)
showed that, by letting
d=(d^{[0]},d^{[1]})
and using weights
\rho(d,x)\equiv d^{[1]}/e^{[1]}(x)-d^{[0]}/e^{[0]}(x)
,

\displaystyle{\rm E}[\rho(d,x)\Delta\mid x]={\rm E}[\Delta^{[1]}\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]-{\rm E}[\Delta^{[0]}\mid x,d^{[0]}=1]

\displaystyle={\rm E}[\Delta^{[1]}-\Delta^{[0]}\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]={\rm E}[y^{[1]}(1)-y^{[0]}(1)\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]=a(x)

(3)

holds. Assumption
1
is used in the second equality and Assumption
2
is used in the third equality. This equation means that, by multiplying the observed values of change with weights based on propensity scores, the conditional ATT can be estimated via the DID approach. Then, from (
2
) and (
3
), the optimal solution of
\theta
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\theta^{*}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)\{\rho(d,x)\Delta-x^{{\rm T}}\theta\}^{2}].

(4)

Moreover, from this equation,

\displaystyle{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)x\{\rho(d,x)\Delta-x^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}]

\displaystyle={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)x\{{\rm E}[\rho(d,x)\Delta\mid x]-x^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}]={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)x\{a(x)-x^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}]=0

(5)

is satisfied, and therefore,

\displaystyle{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xa(x)]={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]\theta^{*}

(6)

is obtained.

2.2
Semiparametric estimation

Here, for an independently and identically distributed set of samples of size
n
, the
i
-th sample will be denoted by the subscript
i
.
Abadie (2005)
proposed

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}\rho(d_{i},x_{i})\Delta_{i}

(7)

as a natural estimator of
\theta^{*}
by solving (
4
). When the observed values of change are multiplied by weights based on propensity scores, a term corresponding to the difference-in-differences appears; thus, this method is called a semiparametric DID (SDID) estimator. The following regularity conditions are assumed.

Assumption 4
.

(\mathrm{i})

\theta^{*}
is in the interior of the compact set
\theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

(\mathrm{ii})

{\rm E}[y(t)^{2}]<\infty
for all
t
.

(\mathrm{iii})

{\rm E}[x]
is bounded and
{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]
is non-singular.

From Assumption
4
and (
6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}=\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}\rho(d_{i},x_{i})\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)x\rho(d,x)\Delta]

\displaystyle={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xa(x)]={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]\theta^{*}=\theta^{*},

and therefore
\hat{\theta}
is a consistent estimator of
\theta^{*}
. In practice, the propensity scores are often unknown. In such cases, the propensity scores can be modeled as
e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)={\rm logit}(x^{{\rm T}}\alpha)
; for example, the parameter
\alpha
can be estimated as
\hat{\alpha}
by maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), and the propensity score in (
7
) can be replaced by
e^{[1]}(x;\hat{\alpha})
to obtain the SDID estimator. If the propensity score model is correctly specified, i.e., it can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm pr}(d^{[1]}=1\mid x)={\rm logit}(x^{{\rm T}}\alpha^{*}),

and if
\hat{\alpha}
converges to
\alpha^{*}
under some further assumptions, then
\hat{\theta}
is still a consistent estimator of
\theta^{*}
.

3
Covariate balancing for difference-in-differences approach

3.1
Estimation method and its statistical properties

Propensity scores are often estimated using MLE, but if the model for the propensity scores is misspecified, the estimator of the ATT will be biased. Here, we propose a robust method that can deal with model misspecification. While a doubly robust estimation known as augmented inverse-probability-weighted estimation, which uses estimators from an outcome regression model, has been addressed in
Sant’Anna and Zhao (2020)
and
Ning et al. (2020a)
, here we construct a doubly robust estimation by incorporating covariate balancing (e.g.
Egel et al. 2008
,
Imai and Ratkovic 2014
and
Fan et al. 2022
). Let
{\rm O}
be a zero matrix and let us define

\displaystyle H^{[1]}(d,x;\alpha)\equiv e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)\bigg\{\frac{d^{[1]}}{e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)}-1\bigg\}xx^{{\rm T}},

\displaystyle H^{[0]}(d,x;\alpha)\equiv e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)\bigg\{\frac{d^{[0]}}{e^{[0]}(x;\alpha)}-1\bigg\}xx^{{\rm T}},

and consider the moment conditions,
{\rm E}[H^{[1]}(d,x;\alpha)]={\rm O}
and
{\rm E}[H^{[0]}(d,x;\alpha)]={\rm O}
.
From these conditions is obtained
{\rm E}[d^{[1]}xx^{{\rm T}}]={\rm E}[\{e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)/e^{[0]}(x;\alpha)\}d^{[0]}xx^{{\rm T}}]
, which means that the expectation of
xx^{{\rm T}}
in the treatment group is adjusted to match the expectation of
xx^{{\rm T}}
in the control group through weighting based on the propensity scores. In the original covariate balancing, the first-order moments of the covariates are balanced; that is,
xx^{{\rm T}}
in the expectation is naturally set to
x
. It should be noted, however, that the converse does not necessarily hold. In this paper, the second-order moments of the covariates are balanced.

The semi-vectorization of the upper triangular part of the matrix is written as
{\rm vech}(\cdot)
. Since
xx^{{\rm T}}
is a symmetric matrix, by letting

\displaystyle h(d,x;\alpha)\equiv({\rm vech}\{H^{[1]}(d,x;\alpha)\}^{{\rm T}},{\rm vech}\{H^{[0]}(d,x;\alpha)\}^{{\rm T}})^{{\rm T}},

the moment conditions can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[h(d,x;\alpha)]=0.

(8)

We will denote an
\alpha
satisfying
(
8
)
as
\alpha^{\dagger}
. Since the propensity score model may be misspecified,
\alpha^{\dagger}
is not necessarily the true value
\alpha^{*}
. In practice, the SDID approach uses a
\hat{\alpha}
satisfying the empirical version of the moment conditions,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha})=0.

(9)

If the moment conditions are just-identified, that is, the dimension of the moment conditions (the number of constraints) is the same as the dimension of the parameter
\alpha
, then
\alpha
that satisfies
(
9
)
exists. On the other hand, if they are over-identified, that is, the dimension of the moment conditions is larger than the dimension of
\alpha
, then an
\hat{\alpha}
that satisfies (
9
) does not necessarily exist. In that case, an
\hat{\alpha}
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha})\approx 0

is used. Specifically,
\hat{\alpha}
is obtained from the generalized method of moments (
Hansen 1982
) or the generalized empirical likelihood method (
Qin and Lawless 1994
).

Here, we will use the generalized method of moments and propose
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}\equiv\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\alpha}\{h_{n}(\alpha)^{{\rm T}}\allowbreak W_{n}\allowbreak h_{n}(\alpha)\}
as the estimator of
\alpha
by using the covariate balancing (CB) method, where
h_{n}(\alpha)\equiv n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)
and
W_{n}
is an arbitrary semi-positive definite matrix. Upon defining
G_{n}(\alpha)\equiv\partial h_{n}(\alpha)/\partial\alpha^{{\rm T}}
, one sees that the estimator
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}
satisfies

\displaystyle G_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})^{{\rm T}}W_{n}h_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})=0.

(10)

Its simplest candidate is the identity matrix, but the optimal matrix,
\{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\allowbreak h(d_{i},\allowbreak x_{i};\alpha)^{{\rm T}}\}^{-1}
, provides the most efficient estimator of
\alpha
. However, this optimal matrix is not necessarily practical since it may be too close to being a degenerate matrix for some data, causing considerable instability in the estimation of
\alpha
and consequently
\theta
.

Remark 1
.

The idea of using constraints that approximately satisfy the empirical version of the moment conditions to estimate the parameters of the propensity scores has also been examined. In
Ning et al. (2020b)
a doubly robust estimation method is proposed for dealing with the case of high-dimensional covariates. This method combines a semiparametric estimation of the ATT with sparse estimation. It is assumed that the outcome can be expressed by a linear model of covariates, and to speed up the convergence of the Horvitz-Thompson estimator, the weak covariate balancing property
is included as a constraint in estimating the parameter of the propensity scores.

Here, we state the regularity conditions of the generalized method of moments (
Newey and McFadden 1994
) that are necessary to derive the properties of the proposed estimation method.

Assumption 5

(Consistency)
.

(\mathrm{i})

W_{n}
converges in probability to
W
, which is a semi-positive definite matrix. Also,
\alpha=\alpha^{\dagger}
if and only if
W{\rm E}[h(d,x;\alpha)]=0
.
(\mathrm{ii})

\alpha^{\dagger}
is in the interior of the compact set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
(\mathrm{iii})

h(d,x;\alpha)
is continuous; that is,
e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)
is continuous at each
\alpha\in A
with probability one.
(\mathrm{iv})

{\rm E}[\sup_{\alpha\in A}\|h(d,x;\alpha)\|]<\infty
.

Assumption 6

(Asymptotic normality)
.

(\mathrm{i})

h(d,x;\alpha)
is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood
\Gamma
of
\alpha^{\dagger}
with probability approaching one.
(\mathrm{ii})

{\rm E}[h(d,x;\alpha^{\dagger})]=0
and
{\rm E}[\|h(d,x;\alpha^{\dagger})\|^{2}]\allowbreak<\infty
.
(\mathrm{iii})

{\rm E}[\sup_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\allowbreak\|\partial h(d,x;\alpha)/\partial\alpha^{{\rm T}}\|_{\rm F}]<\infty
.
(\mathrm{iv})

G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}W\allowbreak G(\alpha^{\dagger})
is non-singular for
G(\alpha)\equiv{\rm E}[\partial h(d,x;\alpha)/\partial\alpha^{{\rm T}}]
.

Under these regularity conditions, the proposed method can be shown to be doubly robust, as follows. Here, we name the estimator obtained by replacing the propensity scores with
e^{[1]}(x;\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})
in (
7
) the covariate balancing for DID (CBD) estimator and denote it as
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}
.

Theorem 1
.

If the propensity score model
e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)
is correctly specified, or if the change in outcomes follows a model in covariates of the form

\displaystyle{\rm E}[\Delta^{[k]}\mid x,d^{[k]}=1]=x^{{\rm T}}\beta^{[k]*}+\kappa(x),

(11)

where
\kappa(x)
is an unspecified function, then under Assumptions
1
-
6
,
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}
converges in probability to
\theta^{*}
. That is, the proposed estimator has double robustness.

Proof.

From Assumption
5
,
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}
is a consistent estimator of
\alpha^{\dagger}
. By expanding
h_{n}(\hat{\alpha})
around
\alpha^{\dagger}
and applying Assumption
6
and (
10
), it is shown to be

\displaystyle\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}-\alpha^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=-\{G_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})^{{\rm T}}W_{n}G_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\}^{-1}G_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})^{{\rm T}}W_{n}\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}h_{n}(\alpha^{\dagger})+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)

(12)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{\to}}{\rm N}(0,\{G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}WG(\alpha^{\dagger})\}^{-1}G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}W\Omega(\alpha^{\dagger})WG(\alpha^{\dagger})\{G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}WG(\alpha^{\dagger})\}^{-1}),

where
\Omega(\alpha)\equiv{\rm E}[h(d,x;\alpha)h(d,x;\alpha)^{{\rm T}}]
. From the consistency of
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}
, we also obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}-\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})x_{i}\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}

(13)

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})x_{i}\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1).

(14)

If the propensity score model is correctly specified and
\alpha^{\dagger}=\alpha^{*}
is satisfied, that is, if
e^{[1]}(x)=e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{\dagger})
and
e^{[0]}(x)=e^{[0]}(x;\alpha^{\dagger})
hold, then from (
5
),
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}
is a consistent estimator of
\theta^{*}
. On the other hand, even if the propensity score model is misspecified, when the change in outcomes follows a linear model of covariates, that is, when (
11
) holds, we obtain

\displaystyle a(x)={\rm E}[\Delta^{[1]}\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]-{\rm E}[\Delta^{[0]}\mid x,d^{[0]}=1]=x^{{\rm T}}(\beta^{[1]*}-\beta^{[0]*})

from (
3
). Then, it follows from (
6
) that

\displaystyle\theta^{*}={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xa(x)]=\beta^{[1]*}-\beta^{[0]*}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{\dagger})x\{\rho(d,x;\alpha^{\dagger})\Delta-x^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}]

\displaystyle={\rm E}\bigg[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})x_{i}\bigg\{\frac{d^{[1]}_{i}}{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})}{\rm E}[\Delta_{i}^{[1]}\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]

\displaystyle\phantom{={\rm E}\bigg[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})x_{i}\bigg\{}-\frac{d^{[0]}_{i}}{e^{[0]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})}{\rm E}[\Delta_{i}^{[0]}\mid x_{i},d^{[0]}_{i}=1]-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\bigg\}\bigg]

\displaystyle={\rm E}\bigg[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})x_{i}\bigg[\bigg\{\frac{d^{[1]}_{i}}{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})}-1\bigg\}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\beta^{[1]*}-\bigg\{\frac{d^{[0]}_{i}}{e^{[0]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})}-1\bigg\}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\beta^{[0]*}\bigg]\bigg]

\displaystyle={\rm E}[h_{1}(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\beta^{[1]*}-h_{0}(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\beta^{[0]*}]=0.

Here, the last equality holds from the moment conditions in (
8
). From the above, when the propensity score model is correctly specified, or when the change in outcomes follows a linear model of covariates, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})x_{i}\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}={\rm O}_{\rm P}{(\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n})}

in (
14
), and therefore
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}
is consistent.

Remark 2
.

Let us discuss the relationship between our proposed method and the method in
Sant’Anna and Zhao (2020)
.
Unlike our setting, which estimates the conditional ATT, the latter directly estimates the ATT using a doubly robust approach.
In their approach as well, the propensity scores are estimated so that the first-order moments of the distribution for the covariates are balanced.
They also suppose a parametric model of the covariates for the change in the control group’s outcomes and estimate it, using these estimators to estimate the ATT.
In contrast, we place importance on evaluating the heterogeneity of the ATT, including variable selection, and then set a function of the covariates for the ATT.
We suppose a parametric model of the covariates for the change in outcomes as well, although not estimating it.
Then, if the propensity scores are estimated so that not the first-order but the second-order moments of the distribution for the covariates are balanced, it is easily, yet somewhat unexpectedly, shown that the conditional ATT estimator is doubly robust.

Remark 3
.

After the initial posting of our preprint, a related manuscript by
Li and Matsushita (2025)
became publicly available.
That work studies the semiparametric local efficiency of estimating the unconditional ATT via covariate balancing in the SDID approach, building on
Fan et al. (2022)
.
The emphasis there is primarily theoretical and provides valuable insights into efficiency considerations.
In contrast, Theorem 1 above highlights a methodological contribution: it shows that doubly robust estimation of the conditional ATT necessitates balancing second-order moments of the covariate distribution.
A detailed investigation of efficiency properties is beyond the scope of the present paper.
While the efficiency in the case where (
11
) holds can be derived following
Fan et al. (2022)
, efficiency analysis when (
11
) does not hold would require more advanced semiparametric tools, such as those developed in
Graham and de Xavier Pinto (2022)
.

3.2
Numerical experiments

Let us examine the robustness of the proposed estimation against model misspecification through simulation studies. The setting for data generation follows

\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2}\sim{\rm Uniform}(0,2),\quad d^{[1]}\sim{\rm Bernoulli}({\rm logit}(-x_{1}+\alpha^{*}x_{2})),

\displaystyle y^{[0]}(0)=y^{[1]}(0)\sim{\rm N}(0,1),\quad\epsilon^{[0]},\epsilon^{[1]}\sim{\rm N}(0,1),

\displaystyle y^{[0]}(1)=y^{[0]}(0)+\epsilon^{[0]},\quad y^{[1]}(1)=y^{[1]}(0)+\beta^{*}x_{1}+\epsilon^{[1]}.

Throughout this paper, we will suppose that all random numbers are generated independently. The parameter
\beta^{*}
represents the magnitude of the contribution from the covariate
x_{1}
to the causal effect. The propensity score is estimated using only
x_{1}
; that is, the parameter
\alpha^{*}
represents the magnitude of the contribution from
x_{2}
to the true propensity scores, which is not included in the model for estimating the propensity scores. When
\alpha^{*}\neq 0
,
x_{2}
should also be included in the model, and we are doing a model misspecification because it was not included. Anyway, since (
11
) holds, the proposed estimation is theoretically guaranteed to be consistent. In this setting, we will compare the proposed method with a naive method estimating the propensity score by the MLE and check whether the proposed method is robust to misspecification of the propensity score model. Each of the numerical experiments described below was repeated 3,000 times.

The true values of the ATT, the mean estimates, and their empirical confidence intervals are listed in Table
1
for several
\alpha^{*}
,
\beta^{*}
and sample sizes
n
. With increasing
\alpha^{*}
, which represents the magnitude of the misspecification of the propensity score model, the bias of the naive method becomes larger, and the true value is eventually no longer included in the confidence interval. Regarding the proposed method, the approximation accuracy is considerably high when the identity matrix is used as the weighting matrix in the generalized method of moments. On the other hand, when the optimal matrix is used as the weighting matrix, although the accuracy is often comparable to that of using the identity matrix, in some cases, it is similar to that of the naive method. Since there are settings in simulation studies where the optimal matrix is close to being degenerate, those cases in which the accuracy deteriorates to that of the naïve method may be attributed to the fact that the estimation of
\theta
is not stable. Regardless of whether (
11
) holds, if the propensity score model is estimated correctly, both the proposed method and the naive method can estimate the ATT without bias.

4
Model selection criterion for semiparametric difference-in-differences when propensity scores are known

4.1
Risk function and its asymptotic evaluation in semiparametric difference-in-differences

As mentioned in Section
1
, there are no reasonable model selection criteria even for the basic setting treated in
Abadie (2005)
. To remedy this situation, in this section, we will first derive a model selection criterion for when the propensity scores are known and then develop a model selection criterion for when the proposed CBD method is used. Specifically, by using the ideas of
Baba et al. (2017)
and
Baba and Ninomiya (2025)
, we will derive the model selection criteria as asymptotically unbiased estimators of the natural risk corresponding to the loss function used to obtain SDID estimators. Using these criteria, the dimension of
\theta
is determined and the component of
x
is selected. A natural choice is to define a weighted risk based on (
4
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}\}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}\}^{2}]

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\Delta_{i}-{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]\}^{2}]

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\Delta_{i}-{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]\}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[}\times\{x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}-{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]\}].

(15)

Following the derivation of the conventional C
{}_{\text{{P}}}
criterion, we will remove the expectation in the first term, ignore the second term which is independent of the models, and asymptotically evaluate the third term. For simplicity, we will refer to the third term as a bias when the risk is simply estimated. If we define
\varepsilon_{i}\equiv\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\Delta_{i}-a(x_{i})
using
a(x_{i})
in (
1
), the third term can be written as

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\Delta_{i}-a(x_{i})\}\{x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}-a(x_{i})\}]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}-a(x_{i})\}]=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)x_{i}^{{\rm T}}(\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*})].

(16)

The second equality is satisfied by
{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)a(x_{i})]=0
and
{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\allowbreak x^{{\rm T}}_{i}\theta^{*}]=0
since it follows from (
3
) that
{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}\mid x_{i}]=0
. Here, we have used the same
e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)
as that was used to construct
\hat{\theta}
. Then, letting
c^{\rm limit}
be the weak limit of the third term with the expectation removed, we consider
{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]
as the asymptotic evaluation of the third term of (
15
). While we consider a risk based on the loss function in deriving the SDID estimator, it is also possible to consider a simple squared error.

4.2
Derivation of model selection criterion

In the case of known propensity scores, we will denote
e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})
as
e^{[1]}(x_{i})
and
\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{*})
as
\rho(d_{i},x_{i})
for simplicity. The main term of the error of the SDID estimator can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}-\theta^{*}={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}\{1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\}

\displaystyle={\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}\{\varepsilon_{i}+a(x_{i})-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}\{1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\}.

Accordingly, (
16
) is evaluated as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm E}\bigg[\varepsilon_{i}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}^{{\rm T}}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j})x_{j}\{\varepsilon_{j}+a(x_{j})-x_{j}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}\bigg].

This expectation for the case of
i\neq j
is the product of the expectations for
i
and
j
, from the independence among the samples. The expectation for
i
can be calculated as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}^{{\rm T}}]={\rm E}_{x_{i}}[{\rm E}[\varepsilon_{i}\mid x_{i}]e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}^{{\rm T}}]=0.

(17)

Therefore, we only have to consider the case of
i=j
. Since
a(x_{j})-x_{j}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}
contains only
x_{j}
as a random variable, this term disappears from (
17
), and the following theorem is obtained.

Theorem 2
.

When the propensity scores are known, it holds that

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=2{\rm E}[\varepsilon e^{[1]}(x)x^{{\rm T}}{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}e^{[1]}(x)x\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=2{\rm tr}\{{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x)xx^{{\rm T}}]^{-1}{\rm E}[\sigma^{2}(x)e^{[1]}(x)^{2}xx^{{\rm T}}]\},

where

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(x)\equiv{\rm E}[\varepsilon^{2}\mid x]

\displaystyle={\rm E}[\{\rho(d,x)\Delta\}^{2}\mid x]-{\rm E}[\rho(d,x)\Delta\mid x]^{2}.

(18)

Although one may think that an empirical evaluation of
{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]
is easy, we must consider how to deal with the term
{\rm E}[{\rm E}[\rho(d,x)\Delta\mid x]^{2}e^{[1]}(x)^{2}xx^{{\rm T}}]
. This cannot be empirically evaluated as
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(d,x)^{2}\allowbreak\Delta^{2}e^{[1]}(x)^{2}xx^{{\rm T}}
. Here, we will substitute
x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}
into
{\rm E}[\rho(d_{i},x_{i})\Delta\mid x_{i}]
. As a result, when the propensity scores are known, we propose

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}\}^{2}

\displaystyle+2{\rm tr}\biggl(\biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\biggr\}^{-1}\biggl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i})^{2}\Delta^{2}-(x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta})^{2}\}e^{[1]}(x_{i})^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\biggr]\biggr)

(19)

as the model selection criterion for the basic SDID approach.

Through simulation studies, we demonstrate that the second term in (
19
), which serves as the penalty term of the proposed criterion, accurately approximates the bias in (
16
) that we originally wanted to evaluate.
In contrast, the penalty term of an intuitive model selection criterion obtained by extending QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
(
Platt et al. 2013
) to the present setting substantially underestimates this bias.
We further compare the two criteria using an empirical estimate of the risk as the primary performance measure. The results show that the proposed criterion outperforms QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
in almost all settings.
Details on these numerical experiments, including the extension of QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
, are provided in the Supplementary Material.

5
Model selection criterion for covariate balancing difference-in-differences

5.1
Derivation of model selection criterion

As we did for the case of known propensity scores in Section
4.2
, here, we will derive a validated model selection criterion for when the CBD method is used. Let us evaluate

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}2{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{i}-{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}-y^{[0]}_{i}\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]\}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}(\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}-\theta^{*})],

which is the third term of the risk in (
16
). Substituting
x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}
, which is a working model for conditional ATT, into
{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}-y^{[0]}_{i}\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]
first and then substituting the right-hand side of (
13
) into
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}-\theta^{*}
yields

\displaystyle 2{\rm E}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})x_{j}x_{j}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\}^{-1}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{{\rm E}\bigg[}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\{\rho(d_{j},x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{j}-x_{j}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{j}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\bigg].

Since
n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})x_{j}x_{j}^{{\rm T}}
converges in probability to

\displaystyle L(\alpha^{\dagger})\equiv{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{\dagger})xx^{{\rm T}}],

(20)

by letting
u
be the Gaussian random vector that is the weak limit of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i},

(21)

we can express the weak limit of (
16
) with expectation removed as

\displaystyle c^{\rm limit}=2u^{{\rm T}}L(\alpha^{\dagger})^{-1}u=2{\rm tr}\{L(\alpha^{\dagger})^{-1}uu^{{\rm T}}\}.

When
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}
is a consistent estimator of
\theta^{*}
, it holds that
{\rm E}[uu^{{\rm T}}]={\rm var}[u]
since the expectation of
u
becomes
0
. Accordingly, the asymptotic evaluation of (
16
), which is to be used in the information criterion, can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=2{\rm tr}\{L(\alpha^{\dagger})^{-1}{\rm var}[u]\}.

Now let us derive a specific expression for
{\rm var}[u]
. Since the error of the estimator
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}
in the propensity scores is written as (
12
), we can expand (
21
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\bigg\{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\bigg\{}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha^{{\rm T}}}\bigg(e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\bigg[\frac{d^{[1]}_{i}-e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})}{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\{1-e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\}}\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\bigg]\bigg)(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}}-\alpha^{\dagger})\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}\times\{1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[}-M(\alpha^{\dagger},\theta^{*})\{G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}WG(\alpha^{\dagger})\}^{-1}G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}Wh(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{\dagger})]\{1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\},

where

\displaystyle M(\alpha,\theta)={\rm E}\biggl[\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)\biggl[\frac{d^{[1]}-1}{\{1-e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)\}^{2}}\Delta-x^{{\rm T}}\theta\biggr]x^{{\rm T}}\biggr].

Then, by defining

\displaystyle V(\alpha^{\dagger},\theta^{*})\equiv{\rm var}

\displaystyle[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{\dagger})\{\rho(d,x;\alpha^{\dagger})\Delta-x^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x

\displaystyle\;-M(\alpha^{\dagger},\theta^{*})\{G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}WG(\alpha^{\dagger})\}^{-1}G(\alpha^{\dagger})^{{\rm T}}Wh(d,x;\alpha^{\dagger})],

(22)

it can be shown that
{\rm var}[u]=V(\alpha^{\dagger},\theta^{*})
. Thus, the following theorem is obtained.

Theorem 3
.

When the propensity scores are estimated using the CBD method, the third term in (
15
) is evaluated as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=2{\rm tr}\{L(\alpha^{\dagger})^{-1}V(\alpha^{\dagger},\theta^{*})\},

where
L(\cdot)
and
V(\cdot,\cdot)
are defined in (
20
) and (
22
), respectively.

Since the penalty term in this theorem depends on the true values of the parameters, we can use empirical estimates in practice.
Specifically, letting

\displaystyle M_{n}(\alpha,\theta)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\bigg[\frac{d^{[1]}_{i}-1}{\{1-e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\}^{2}}\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta\bigg]x_{i}^{{\rm T}}

(23)

and

\displaystyle V_{i}(\alpha,\theta)\equiv\

\displaystyle e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta\}x_{i}

\displaystyle-M_{n}(\alpha,\theta)\{G_{n}(\alpha)^{{\rm T}}W_{n}G_{n}(\alpha)\}^{-1}G_{n}(\alpha)^{{\rm T}}W_{n}h(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha)],

we will replace
L(\alpha^{\dagger})
by
L_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\equiv n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\allowbreak e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}
and
V(\alpha^{\dagger},\theta^{*})
by
V_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}})\equiv n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}})V_{i}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}})^{{\rm T}}
.
As a result, for when the propensity scores are estimated with the CBD method, we propose

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}}\}^{2}+2{\rm tr}\{L_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}})^{-1}V_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{CB}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{CBD}})\}

(24)

as the model selection criterion, which is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of the risk in (
15
). Here, the propensity scores are estimated using covariate balancing, but the information criterion can be derived in the same way when the MLE is used (see the Supplementary Material).

5.2
Numerical experiments

The simulation studies presented in this section examine the accuracy of the approximation for the penalty term and the performance of the model selection criterion in (
24
) for the proposed CBD method. On the basis of the results in Section
3.2
, we will use the identity matrix as the weighting matrix in the generalized method of moments and, even when the optimal matrix is used as the weighting matrix, the accuracy of the approximation for the penalty term and the performance of the model selection with comparable to those obtained with the identity matrix (see the Supplementary Material).

In Table
2
, the data generation settings include Case 1-1,

\displaystyle x_{1}\sim{\rm Uniform}(0,2),\quad d^{[1]}\sim{\rm Bernoulli}({\rm logit}(-x_{1})),\quad y^{[0]}(0)=y^{[1]}(0)\sim{\rm N}(0,1),

7
Discussion

Our proposal, the CBD method, is an SDID approach incorporating covariate balancing. In order to ensure double robustness, i.e. to ensure that the ATT is consistently estimated when the model for the propensity score is correctly specified or the model for the changes in the outcomes over time is correctly supposed, we have shown that the second-order moments of the distribution for the covariates should be balanced between the treatment and control groups with the former as the target population.
Since covariate balancing usually balances the first-order moments, the value for the fact that the moment is second-order rather than first-order lies more in having been discovered due to its unexpectedness than in how to demonstrate its validity.
Moreover, our model selection criterion for the CBD method is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of risk based on the loss function used for the estimation. Specifically, we derived a model selection criterion using just a DID-specific assumption called the conditional parallel trend assumption, i.e., without relying on the ignorable treatment assignment condition, which is often assumed in causal inference. The penalty term has been shown to be considerably different from twice the number of parameters that often appears in the AIC-type model selection criteria. Since no model selection criteria existed for even the basic SDID approach, we developed a model selection criterion not only for the CBD method, but also for when the propensity scores are known or estimated by MLE. Numerical experiments show that the proposed method estimates the ATT more robustly than the method that estimates propensity scores by using MLE and that the proposed criterion clearly reduces the risk targeted by the SDID approach compared with the intuitive generalization of the existing information criterion to the SDID approach. In addition, analyses of real data confirm that there is a large difference between the results of the proposed method and those of the existing method.

Although this paper deals with a simple linear model of covariates as a working model for the outcome, its results can be easily extended to situations where a nonlinear model of covariates should be used, for example, when using the kernel method. In fact,
Hazlett (2020)
has treated the function
\phi(x):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space where
q
may be larger than
p
or
n
. They assumed that the outcome could be represented by a linear model of
\phi(x)
, which in our setting can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[y^{[1]}(1)-y^{[0]}(1)\mid x,d^{[1]}=1]=\phi(x)^{{\rm T}}\theta.

(25)

Then, by using

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg\{\frac{d^{[1]}_{i}\phi(x_{i})}{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)}-\frac{d^{[0]}_{i}\phi(x_{i})}{e^{[0]}(x_{i};\alpha)}\bigg\}=0

as the moment conditions for estimating the propensity scores, they propose the kernel balancing method, which can flexibly estimate causal effects even if the relationship between the covariate and the outcome is nonlinear. For making a doubly robust estimation using the kernel balancing in the setting of this paper, together with the assumption in (
25
), let us additionally suppose that the change in the outcomes follows

\displaystyle{\rm E}[\Delta^{[k]}\mid x,d^{[k]}=1]=\phi(x)^{{\rm T}}\beta^{[k]*}.

Let
k(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the kernel function corresponding to
\phi(x)
, and let
K
be a
p\times p
semi-definite symmetric matrix whose
(l,m)
-component is
k(x_{l},x_{m})
. If we estimate the parameter
\alpha
that satisfies

\displaystyle H^{[1]}(d,x;\alpha)={\rm E}\bigg[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)\bigg\{\frac{d^{[1]}}{e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)}-1\bigg\}K\bigg]={\rm O},

\displaystyle H^{[0]}(d,x;\alpha)={\rm E}\bigg[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha)\bigg\{\frac{d^{[0]}}{e^{[0]}(x;\alpha)}-1\bigg\}K\bigg]={\rm O},

and then estimate
\theta
and derive a model selection criterion based on it, it would be a natural extension of the proposed method.

There are other valuable directions of study. For example,
Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021)
and
Goodman-Bacon (2021)
proposed a DID approach in a more flexible setting than the original one, where there are multiple time points and treatment can be initiated at any of them. Moreover,
De Chaisemartin and D’haultfœuille (2023)
developed a DID approach for when there are more than two groups, and
Richardson et al. (2023)
proposed one to estimate causal effects under alternative assumptions to the parallel trend assumption. While the outcome in the basic DID approach is continuous,
Tchetgen et al. (2024)
dealt with the case where the outcome follows a generalized linear model. In this way, the DID approach has been theoretically developed in accordance with actual problems, so we believe that extending the proposed method to enable it to deal with those problems would thus be a potentially profitable goal for the near future. In econometrics, synthetic control (
Abadie et al. 2010
) has also become standard in causal inference, and a combination of the two has been developed as synthetic DID (
Arkhangelsky et al. 2021
). Here, because the objective of the information criterion is what to synthesize and with what weights, the proposed method cannot necessarily be used; however, an investigation along the lines presented in this paper might prove fruitful. Furthermore, propensity score analysis with high-dimensional covariates is also an important topic (e.g.,
Belloni et al. 2017
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
,
Ning et al. 2020b
), wherein selecting covariates that truly have impact on causal effects and robustly estimating these effects is an essential task to ensure the validity of the analysis. Even when the covariates are high-dimensional, asymptotic properties of the estimator of causal effects tend to be obtained as well, so it is surely possible to extend our method.

Appendix A
Performance of model selection criterion for semiparametric difference-in-differences when propensity scores are known

A.1
Extension of QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}

As a comparator for the proposed model selection criterion, we will extend QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
(
Platt et al. 2013
), which is an intuitive model selection criterion for weighted estimation using propensity scores, to the setting of this paper. First, we construct the goodness-of-fit term using the same idea as for QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
. To do so, the log-likelihood function for full data is differentiated, a weighted potential outcome variable is substituted into the one whose expectation is the same as the outcome variable, the differentiation is reverted, and the estimator is substituted into the parameter. The number of parameters is used as the penalty term. Within this setting, the log-likelihood function for full data is

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\{\mbox{$y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)$ when $d_{i}^{[1]}=1$}\}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta]^{2}.

If we take
\rho_{i}\Delta_{i}
as the weighted potential outcome variable, its conditional expectation can be shown to be the same as the conditional expectation of the outcome variable from (
3
). We will call the sum of the goodness-of-fit term and the penalty term multiplied by a constant
2\sigma^{2}
, that is,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}\}^{2}+2\sigma^{2}p,

the QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
for the SDID approach. In fact, if
\sigma^{2}(x)
is a constant, then our proposed criterion is equivalent to the QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
. The problem is to estimate the variance
\sigma^{2}
of the difference,
y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)
when
d_{i}^{[1]}=1
. This estimate cannot be obtained from the data using only the present assumptions. Since it follows from
y^{[0]}(0)=y^{[1]}(0)
that
y^{[1]}_{i}(1)-y^{[0]}_{i}(1)=\Delta_{i}^{[1]}-\Delta_{i}^{[0]}
, and from Assumption
1
, the expectation of
\Delta_{i}^{[1]}-\Delta_{i}^{[0]}
in
d^{[1]}_{i}=1
is the same as the expectation of
\Delta_{i}^{[1]}
in
d^{[0]}_{i}=0
minus
\Delta_{i}^{[1]}-\Delta_{i}^{[0]}
in
d^{[1]}_{i}=1
. However, in order to evaluate the variance, we will assume that
\Delta_{i}^{[1]}
and
\Delta_{i}^{[0]}
are independent conditioned on
d^{[1]}=1
and
d^{[0]}=1
, from which we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}={\rm var}[\Delta^{[1]}\mid d^{[1]}=1]+{\rm var}[\Delta^{[0]}\mid d^{[0]}=1]

\displaystyle={\rm E}[\Delta^{2}\mid d^{[1]}=1]-{\rm E}[\Delta\mid d^{[1]}=1]^{2}+{\rm E}[\Delta^{2}\mid d^{[0]}=1]-{\rm E}[\Delta\mid d^{[0]}=1]^{2}.

Moreover, defining
n_{1}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{[1]}_{i}
and
n_{0}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{[0]}_{i}
, we will use

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{[1]}_{i}\Delta_{i}^{2}-\bigg(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{[1]}_{i}\Delta_{i}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{[0]}_{i}\Delta_{i}^{2}-\bigg(\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{[0]}_{i}\Delta_{i}\bigg)^{2}

in the present study.

Remark 4
.

Let us compare
\sigma^{2}(x)
in the proposed criterion with
\sigma^{2}
in the QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
. In (
18
), if all expectations do not actually depend on
x
, then

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(x)=\

\displaystyle{\rm E}\bigg[\frac{\Delta^{[1]2}}{e^{[1]}}\ \bigg|\ d^{[1]}=1\bigg]-{\rm E}[\Delta^{[1]}\mid d^{[1]}=1]^{2}

\displaystyle+{\rm E}\bigg[\frac{\Delta^{[0]2}}{e^{[0]}}\ \bigg|\ d^{[0]}=1\bigg]-{\rm E}[\Delta^{[0]}\mid d^{[0]}=1]^{2}+2{\rm E}[\Delta^{[1]}\mid d^{[1]}=1]{\rm E}[\Delta^{[0]}\mid d^{[0]}=1].

Since
\Delta^{[1]}
and
\Delta^{[0]}
include fluctuations over time, it is natural that
\Delta^{[1]}\Delta^{[0]}
should be non-negative. In other words, basically
\sigma^{2}(x)>\sigma^{2}
, and the penalty term of the proposed criterion is larger than that of the QIC
{}_{\text{{W}}}
.

Appendix B
Model selection criterion for semiparametric difference-in-differences via maximum likelihood estimation

B.1
Derivation of model selection criterion

For the parameter
\alpha
in the propensity scores, the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}}
is obtained by maximizing
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{d_{i}^{[0]}\log e^{[0]}(x_{i};\alpha)+d_{i}^{[1]}\log e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha)\}
.
Denoting the Fisher information matrix based on this likelihood as
I(\alpha)
, the error of
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}}
multiplied by
\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}}-\alpha^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}I(\alpha^{*})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\biggl\{d_{i}^{[0]}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[0]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})+d_{i}^{[1]}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\biggr\}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1).

(26)

Let us derive the model selection criterion when the propensity scores are estimated by the MLE, using the same approach as the model selection criterion for the covariate balancing in the difference-in-differences (CBD) method.
In its derivation, we need to evaluate the third term of the risk in (
16
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}2{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{i}-{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}-y^{[0]}_{i}\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]\}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}(\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}}-\theta^{*})].

Substituting
x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}
into
{\rm E}[y^{[1]}_{i}-y^{[0]}_{i}\mid x_{i},d^{[1]}_{i}=1]
first, and then substituting the term corresponding to the right-hand side of (
13
) into
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}}-\theta^{*}
, yields

\displaystyle 2{\rm E}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})x_{j}x_{j}^{{\rm T}}\bigg\}^{-1}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{{\rm E}\bigg[}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{j},x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{j}-x_{j}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{j}+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\bigg].

Since
n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{j};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})x_{j}x_{j}^{{\rm T}}
converges in probability to

\displaystyle L(\alpha^{*})\equiv{\rm E}[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{*})xx^{{\rm T}}],

by letting
v
be the Gaussian random vector that is the weak limit of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i},

(27)

we can express the weak limit of (
16
) with expectation removed as

\displaystyle c^{\rm limit}=2v^{{\rm T}}L(\alpha^{*})^{-1}v=2{\rm tr}\{L(\alpha^{*})^{-1}vv^{{\rm T}}\}.

When
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}}
is a consistent estimator of
\theta^{*}
, it holds that
{\rm E}[vv^{{\rm T}}]={\rm var}[v]
since the expectation of
v
becomes
0
.
Then, the asymptotic evaluation of (
16
), which is to be used in the information criterion, is written as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=2{\rm tr}\{L(\alpha^{*})^{-1}{\rm var}[v]\}.

Now let us derive a specific expression for
{\rm var}[v]
.
Since the error of the parameter
\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}}
in the propensity scores is written as (
26
), we can expand (
27
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\bigg\{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{*})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha^{{\rm T}}}\bigg(e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\bigg[\frac{d^{[1]}_{i}-e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})}{e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\{1-e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\}}\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\bigg]\bigg)(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}}-\alpha^{*})\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}\times\{1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\alpha^{*})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x_{i}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vphantom{x}}{n}}}-M(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})I(\alpha^{*})^{-1}\Big\{d_{i}^{[0]}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[0]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})+d_{i}^{[1]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[1]}(x_{i};\alpha^{*})\Big\}\Big]\{1+{\rm o}_{\rm P}(1)\}.

Then, by defining

\displaystyle V^{\rm{ML}}(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm var}\Big[e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{*})\{\rho(d,x;\alpha^{*})\Delta-x^{{\rm T}}\theta^{*}\}x

\displaystyle\ \phantom{\equiv{\rm var}[}-M(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})I(\alpha^{*})^{-1}\Big\{d^{[0]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[0]}(x;\alpha^{*})+d^{[1]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[1]}(x;\alpha^{*})\Big\}\Big],

(28)

it can be shown that
{\rm var}[v]=V^{\rm{ML}}(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})
.
Thus, the following theorem is obtained.

Theorem 4
.

When the propensity scores are estimated using the MLE, the third term in (
15
) is evaluated as

\displaystyle{\rm E}[c^{\rm limit}]=2{\rm tr}\{L(\alpha^{*})^{-1}V^{\rm{ML}}(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})\},

where
L(\cdot)
and
V^{\rm{ML}}(\cdot,\cdot)
are defined in (
20
) and (
28
), respectively.

Since the penalty term in this theorem depends on the true values of the parameters, we can use empirical estimates in practice.
Specifically, we will replace
L(\alpha^{*})
by
L_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\equiv n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\allowbreak\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})x_{i}x_{i}^{{\rm T}}
and
V^{\rm{ML}}(\alpha^{*},\theta^{*})
by

\displaystyle V_{n}^{\rm{ML}}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}}\}x_{i}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}}-M_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}})I(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})^{-1}\Big\{d_{i}^{[0]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[0]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})+d_{i}^{[1]}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\log e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Big\}\Big],

where
M_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
23
).
As a result, for when the propensity scores are estimated with the MLE, we propose

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{[1]}(x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\{\rho(d_{i},x_{i};\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})\Delta_{i}-x_{i}^{{\rm T}}\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}}\}^{2}+2{\rm tr}\{L_{n}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}})^{-1}V_{n}^{\rm{ML}}(\hat{\alpha}^{\rm{ML}},\hat{\theta}^{\rm{ML}})\}

(29)

as the model selection criterion, which is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of the risk in (
15
).
