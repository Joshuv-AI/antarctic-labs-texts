Macrolides usage as anti-inflammatory agents in the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia and ventilator-associated pneumonia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ramona Nasr, Abir Abdel Rahman, Nisrine ElHadi, Pascale Salameh, Hanine Mansour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) require effective treatment strategies. In addition to their antimicrobial role, macrolides may have a potential anti-inflammatory role in CAP and VAP. The purpose of this systematic review is to explore the use of macrolides as anti-inflammatory drugs in CAP and VAP and to assess their impact on mortality and hospital length of stay while adhering to treatment guidelines.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted. Inclusion criteria were determined based on the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design) framework. The PICOS framework was used to define study eligibility and was not applied as a weighting or scoring system. Only observational studies, non-randomized studies, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) meeting these criteria were included. A standardized eligibility screening form was employed by three independent reviewers, adhering to the established Cochrane guidelines and utilizing specialized software for analysis.ResultsAmong 11,960 identified records, 13 studies met the inclusion criteria and underwent qualitative synthesis. Nine studies underwent meta-analysis. Of these, 25% exhibited a low risk of bias, and approximately 50% presented a moderate risk of bias. Meta-analysis showed that macrolide’s use was associated with a significant reduction in in-hospital mortality (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.25–0.71) and post-discharge mortality (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.31–0.87). A non-significant reduction in length of hospital stay was also observed (MD − 0.68, 95% CI − 1.67 to 0.32). However, the limited number of RCTs reduces the overall quality of the available evidence.ConclusionsMacrolides demonstrate potential anti-inflammatory benefits in CAP and VAP, leading to improved clinical outcomes. However, further well-designed RCTs are needed to strengthen the evidence and confirm these findings.Systematic review registrationThis review was registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42018088203).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03190-1.Keywords: Community-acquired pneumonia, Ventilator-associated pneumonia, Macrolide, Mortality, Length of stay, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

Macrolides are bacteriostatic antibiotics that are widely used to treat several infections including those related to the respiratory tract and also possess potent immunomodulatory properties. This dual functionality raises the possibility of addressing immune dysregulation in critically ill patients without compromising crucial antimicrobial defenses [1]. A 2007 study by Healy et al. [2] demonstrated that, beyond their anti-infective activity, macrolides exhibit immunomodulatory effects, including reduction of elastase activity, inhibition of neutrophil oxidative bursts, suppression of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and decreased production of proinflammatory cytokines like interleukin (IL)−1, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).

Pneumonia is an infection of the lung parenchyma that represents a major cause of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [3, 4]. It is one of the leading causes of hospital admission, with 5–15% of hospitalized patients requiring admission to an intensive care unit (ICU) [5], largely due to complications such as shock or respiratory failure. Pneumonia can be categorized as community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) [6]. CAP is defined as pneumonia occurring outside healthcare settings, whereas HAP develops 48 h or more after hospital admission, and VAP arises 48–72 h after endotracheal intubation [7, 8]. Together HAP and VAP are among the most common hospital-acquired infections, accounting for 21.8% of cases, according to a point prevalence survey conducted across 10 states in the USA [9].

According to current updated guidelines, the treatment of CAP involves the use of fluoroquinolones alone or in combination with a β-lactam (BLM) and a macrolide for non-severe in-patients with CAP. For severe inpatient pneumonia, a beta-lactam with a macrolide or a beta-lactam with a fluoroquinolone is recommended [10]. For ICU patients, combination therapy including macrolides is frequently recommended, particularly in the presence of risk factors for resistant pathogens[11]. While these recommendations primarily reflect the antimicrobial spectrum of macrolides, their potential additional clinical benefits remain an area of ongoing investigation.

Inflammation is vital in the pathophysiology of pneumonia. Notably, nearly two-thirds of pneumonia-related deaths occur within the first week of hospitalization despite appropriate antibiotic therapy [12]. This observation emphasizes the role of an exaggerated immune response to disease development and death, implying that additional immunomodulation may be effective in improving outcomes [12, 13]. Accordingly, several anti-inflammatory agents, including macrolides, have been proposed as potential adjunctive agents due to their dual antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties.

However, the therapeutic advantages of using antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory treatments in combination remain inconsistent and controversial in patients with CAP and VAP. While some studies suggest improved outcomes, others report no significant benefit, highlighting ongoing uncertainty in the field.

Several studies have reported positive results following macrolide therapy. For instance, Restrepo et al. [14] reported a significant reduction in both 30- and 90-day mortality associated with macrolide use in severe pneumonia cases. Moreover, Ceccato et al. [15] observed significantly lower 30-day mortality rates in individuals who received therapy using β-lactams and macrolides compared to fluoroquinolones. This was especially the case for pneumococcal CAP with an inflammatory response. In addition, according to the meta-analysis conducted by Sligl et al. (2014) [16] of over 10,000 critically ill patients with CAP, the inclusion of macrolides resulted in an 18% relative decrease in mortality.

Conversely, other studies have reported no additional clinical benefit associated with macrolide therapy. Arnold et al. [17] reported no statistically significant difference between cytokine levels and neutrophil activity among CAP patients prescribed a macrolide-containing regimen. Subsequently, in 2020, Gilbert et al. [18] conducted a study on 171 patients diagnosed with CAP to assess if there was a difference in 30-day readmission rates among patients treated with β-lactam plus macrolide combination therapy compared with patients treated with fluoroquinolone monotherapy. They demonstrated that the combination therapy with the antibiotic β-lactams and the macrolides was not better than the use of fluoroquinolone monotherapy in these patients.

Despite this ongoing controversy, many physicians continue to prescribe macrolides alongside antibiotics to treat the different classes of pneumonia. To ensure optimal patient care, it is crucial to evaluate the actual implementation of macrolides as anti-inflammatory agents in the treatment of CAP and VAP. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to assess the impact of macrolide-containing regimens on mortality outcomes in patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). Secondary objectives include evaluating the impact of macrolides on hospital length of stay and other clinical outcomes, such as clinical stability and recurrence. In the context of the review, the term “anti-inflammatory role” refers to clinical effects that are potentially related to the immunomodulatory effects of macrolides, based on patient outcomes rather than direct measurement of anti-inflammatory effects. By synthesizing data from various study designs, this study seeks to clarify the clinical utility of macrolides as immunomodulatory agents in pneumonia management and determine whether their use is justified based on existing guidelines.

Table 1 presents an overview of recent publications investigating the immunomodulatory effects of macrolides in patients with pneumonia. While several studies suggest potential clinical benefits, particularly in reducing mortality, the evidence remains inconsistent due to heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and outcome measures. Specifically, it should be mentioned that most of the data collected so far are based on observations, and there has been relatively little attention paid to randomized controlled studies in the field. Additionally, studies of the immunomodulatory action of macrolides in CAP and VAP patients have usually been conducted independently, with separate results reported.

Methods

This systematic review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, ensuring transparent reporting of the study process [19, 20]. In this study, the PICOS framework was used exclusively for study selection purposes and not for parameter weighting or pairwise comparison. In addition, this study is based on published clinical data extracted from eligible studies; therefore, no primary datasets, spatial resolution parameters, or preprocessing steps were applicable. The study protocol was created and registered in February 2018 with the registration number (CRD42018088203) [21].

Eligibility criteria

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), controlled trials, and non-randomized studies (NRS) with or without concurrent controls and pre/post-design studies are included in this review. The focus is on evaluating the efficacy of macrolides as anti-inflammatory agents in the treatment of CAP and VAP. Studies must focus on CAP and VAP diagnosed with moderate to severe pneumonia in adults over 18 years old, including in-patients, hospitalized patients, and ICU patients. HAP was not included in this review due to its distinct etiological factors, pathogen profiles, and clinical management protocols, which differ significantly from those of CAP and VAP. The selection of criteria required that the control group follow non-macrolide treatment regimens. These regimens primarily included fluoroquinolones, which are established comparators to macrolides in the management of CAP and VAP, along with mono- or combination therapies using beta-lactam antibiotics. Studies were excluded if they involved patients not admitted to a hospital setting, those not diagnosed with CAP or VAP, and those not receiving macrolides in their treatment protocols.

In line with the study objective, the primary outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis was the effect of macrolides on mortality in patients with CAP and VAP. More specifically, we assessed mortality during hospitalization and within 30 days post-discharge. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay, pneumonia recurrence within 90 days, clinical stability, ICU admission, and ICU length of stay. In this review, the anti-inflammatory effect of macrolides was indirectly evaluated by clinical outcomes such as mortality, length of stay, and clinical stability, based on previous reports, rather than directly by measuring inflammatory markers. Where available, biomarker data (e.g., cytokine levels such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) were described narratively to support the potential immunomodulatory effects of macrolides.

Search strategy

A thorough literature search was conducted to find studies evaluating the effectiveness of macrolides as anti-inflammatory drugs in the treatment of VAP and CAP. The search was restricted to English-language articles only. The initial search was conducted by two reviewers (AA and HM) between June 2019 and January 2020. To ensure the inclusion of the most recent literature, a search update was performed between January 2023 and November 2024 by the two reviewers (AA and RN). From their inception to November 2024, a thorough examination of the following databases, including CINAHL Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (accessed via EBSCO), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, EMBASE, and MEDLINE, was carried out. Additionally, Google Scholar searches were conducted by the same three reviewers (AA, RN, and HM), and the reference lists of the finalized articles were examined for any additional relevant sources. The search was structured around Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms) and relevant keywords. Search terms included variations of “macrolides,” “anti-inflammatory agents,” “community-acquired pneumonia,” “ventilator-associated pneumonia,” and related terms. (See Additional file 2 for search terms and search strategy performed in each database). Also, the retrieved citations were organized using the RefWorks program.

Data extraction and analysis

Selection of studies

Two reviewers (AA and RN) independently screened all retrieved titles and abstracts to determine their eligibility. The full text of any selected article was obtained if a reviewer deemed it eligible. Using a standardized data extraction and screening form, each reviewer read the full text of all included studies. In cases of disagreement between the two reviewers (AA and RN), a consensus meeting was held with a third author (HM) to resolve any conflict. The agreement between the two reviewers was assessed for study inclusion using the Kappa statistic [22]. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: 0 to 0.20 represented slight agreement, 0.21 to 0.40 fair agreement, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial agreement, and greater than 0.80 almost perfect agreement.

Data extraction

The extraction of data from each study included in the analysis was independently conducted by two reviewers (AA and RN). The collected data encompassed various aspects, including (1) study identification (first author’s last name and year of first full report publication), (2) study design (RCTs, clinically controlled trials, or pre/post evaluation), (3) study setting (hospital services, adult medical patients), (4) measurement/intervention details (guidelines followed, risk assessment tools used, and other interventions applied to enhance adherence to macrolide usage guidelines), (5) outcome measures (proportion of patients assessed for clinical stability, hospital length of stay, pneumonia recurrence within 90 days, ICU admission and ICU length of stay), and (6) an overall conclusion (see Additional file 3 for data extraction screening form).

Risk of bias

The risk of bias was assessed at the study level, and the checklists chosen were relevant to the study designs. MINORS was chosen as the methodological index for non-randomized studies [23, 24] which assesses eight domains: clarity of objectives, consecutive patient inclusion, prospective data collection, objective outcome assessment, and adequate follow-up. The risk of bias percentages reported (e.g., low and moderate risk) reflect methodological quality assessments using validated tools and are not derived from biological or clinical parameter thresholds. Each domain was rated on a scale of 0 (not reported), 1 (reported but inadequate), or 2 (reported and adequate). Each study received a total score; studies with a score of 16 or above were deemed to have a low risk of bias, while studies with a score of 16 or below were considered to have a moderate to high risk of bias (see Additional file 6 for MINORS). The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool was used for the included RCTs, focusing on seven domains: random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attribution bias), and freedom from selective reporting (reporting bias) (see Additional file 7 for Cochrane). The two reviewers (AA and RN) independently assessed the methodological quality of each included study and resolved disagreements through discussion with the third author (HM).

Data synthesis

This review includes a forest plot of study findings as well as a narrative summary of the features of the included studies. To account for heterogeneity between studies, a random-effects model was selected for pooling the results, and I2 was calculated to measure the degree of heterogeneity. When feasible, data from several studies were combined using a random effects model to incorporate anticipated clinical and methodological variation between studies. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) or mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The percentage of variability in study results not attributable to chance (I2 test) along with a visual inspection of forest plots, was used to assess the degree of heterogeneity. If substantial heterogeneity is detected, an investigation into the possible causes for this variation is warranted [25]. Publication bias was planned to be assessed using funnel plots when at least 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis.

Results

Description of studies

Results of the search

Thirteen studies [11, 15, 17, 26–35] were included from the 11,960 records originally identified through database searching and other sources (Fig. 1). Following the removal of 6343 duplicate records, 5575 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 5534 records were excluded based on predefined eligibility criteria, primarily due to irrelevance to the study objectives, inappropriate study design, or lack of relevant outcomes. A total of 41 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 23 were excluded for reasons including non-relevant interventions, inappropriate study design, or absence of outcomes of interest. Consequently, 13 studies met all inclusion criteria and were retained for qualitative synthesis. Among these, only 9 studies [11, 17, 26, 27, 29–32, 34] provided sufficient and comparable data to be included in the quantitative meta-analysis. Agreement between reviewers during the full-text screening phase was high, with a kappa coefficient of 0.78, indicating substantial inter-rater reliability.

Study characteristics

The included studies involved 11,747 patients with CAP or VAP; the median sample size was 903 patients, and the length of the study periods ranged from 1 year [26] to 20 years [15]. All selected studies were published in English (see Additional file 4 for the characteristics of included studies). Of the thirteen selected studies, seven were conducted in Europe (3 in Spain, 1 in Germany, 1 in Portugal, 1 in many European countries, and 1 in Greece) [11, 15, 26–30], one in Asia (Malaysia) [31], three in the USA [17, 32, 35], one in Iran [33], and one multinational study [34] (Table 2). Patients were recruited from hospitals with 300 beds [32], and six studies included a group of hospitals [11, 17, 26–28, 34]. Most of the included studies were prospective and observational designs [11, 15, 17, 26, 27, 29–32, 35]. However, one study [33] employed a prospective parallel clinical trial, another [28] used a double-blind, randomized, multicenter trial and one study [34] adopted the Prospective and TREAT study - Observational and Interventional approach. All studies exclusively assessed hospitalized patients.

Clinical practice guidelines implemented in the studies

The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA)/American Thoracic Society (ATS) consensus guidelines were used by four studies [11, 15, 17, 33, 35]. In Stahl et al. [32], a hospital-specific guideline for CAP treatment was followed at the Department of Medicine, New England Medical Center. Two studies [27, 29] audited compliance with the Spanish Society of Pulmonology and Thoracic Surgery guidelines (SEPAR). Another study [34] implemented multiple guidelines, including the British Thoracic Society Standards of Care Committee (BTS), IDSA/ATS, Canadian Infectious Disease Society, the Canadian Thoracic Society, and the Swedish Society of Infectious Diseases guidelines. Two studies [26, 30] followed the recommendations of both the British Thoracic Society Standards of Care Committee (BTS) and IDSA/ATS guidelines, whereas one of these [30] also adhered to German guidelines on ambulant-acquired pneumonia and deep airway infections. One study [31] applied the BTS, Canadian guidelines, and the Infectious Diseases Society of the Republic of China guidelines. Finally, one study [28] adopted the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines for the management of severe sepsis and septic shock.

Interventions favoring macrolides

The studies reviewed various interventions that assessed the anti-inflammatory effects of macrolides on patient outcomes with CAP (see Additional file 4 for characteristics of included studies). Macrolide-containing regimens frequently showed favorable outcomes, especially among patients with specific risk factors or severe illness. In a prospective longitudinal study by Lorenzo et al. (2015) [29] involving 52 patients with poor responses after 72 h of antibiotic treatment, β-lactam/macrolide (BLM) therapy was linked to a shorter hospitalization compared to those not receiving macrolides. Similarly, Stahl et al. (1999) [32] reported that the hospital stay was reduced to 2.8 days in patients receiving macrolides (n = 12) within the 24 h of admission at the New England Medical Center, compared to 5.3 days for those not receiving macrolides (n = 64). Three prospective observational studies investigated macrolide use in CAP. Tessmer et al. (2009) [30] found that 51% of 1844 patients with CAP received BLM therapy.

Martin-Loeches et al. (2010) [11] reported that 175 out of 218 patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation due to CAP received a macrolide-containing regimen across 27 ICUs in 9 countries. Ceccato et al. (2019) [15] revealed that 932 out of 1715 patients admitted to the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona between 1996 and 2016 received BLM therapy. These studies consistently found that the combination of β-lactam and macrolide was associated with lower mortality rates, particularly in patients with higher CRB56 risk classes [30] and severe inflammatory responses [11]. A prospective observational multicenter study by Periera et al. (2018) [26] reported that 502 patients were admitted across 14 Portuguese hospitals within 1 year, and 76% received combination therapy—primary β-lactam and macrolide (80%). The combination was linked to both a reduced length of stay and a lower 6-month mortality rate.

Another prospective observational study by Aspa et al. (2006) [27] analyzed 638 patients from 35 Spanish hospitals, divided into treatment groups: β-lactam monotherapy (n = 251), macrolide monotherapy (n = 37), β-lactam plus macrolide (n = 198), levofloxacin alone/combination (n = 48), and other combinations (n = 104). The results indicated that the initial antibiotic regimen independently influenced the 30-day mortality rate, especially among patients with a high pneumonia severity index. In Arnold et al. (2016) [17], a prospective study involving 40 participants found that only 14 received macrolides while the others did not. This study, the first to evaluate the effect of macrolides on innate immunity, reported no fatalities, with a 2-day shorter length of stay and a half-day faster clinical stabilization in the macrolide group. Levels of certain cytokines (IL-6, IL-8) also approached significantly lower levels in the macrolide group. Finally, a double-blind, randomized, multicenter trial by Giamarellos-Bourboulis et al. (2008) [28] assessed the impact of clarithromycin in patients with sepsis and VAP. Among 100 patients receiving clarithromycin between June 2004 and November 2005 in Greek hospitals, the length of hospital stay and mortality were significantly lower than in the placebo group (n = 100). These results suggest that clarithromycin has a positive impact on patient outcomes.

Interventions not favoring macrolides

In hospitalized adults, the addition of a macrolide to treatment did not yield additional clinical advantages. Loh et al. (2005) [31] conducted an observational study involving prospective patients admitted to an urban-based hospital in Malaysia across two recruitment periods, from March 2003 to July 2004. Macrolide treatment began on the first day of admission. After 16 months, data from 141 patients (of the 226 initially recruited) were analyzed; 45% received a macrolide and 55% received a single broad-spectrum antibiotic. The study found that adding macrolides to broad-spectrum antibiotics did not provide any additional clinical benefit for patients with either severe or no severe pneumonia. No differences were observed in mortality rate, length of hospital stay, or the need for intubation and assisted ventilation.

Paul et al. (2007) [34] conducted the TREAT trial across three teaching-based institutions in three countries. The study enrolled 611 patients, of whom 74% received a β-lactam, while 26% received empirical antibiotic treatment with an added macrolide. The 30-day mortality rate was 7% in the combination therapy group versus 22% in the β-lactam group. However, the mean length of hospital stay was comparable between the two groups. The authors noted significant differences between the two patient groups that may not be fully captured, suggesting variations in patient characteristics that could have influenced mortality outcomes.

In a prospective clinical trial, Yadegarynia et al. (2022) [33] recruited patients from a hospital in Tehran and randomly assigned them to two treatment groups. Participants were matched for gender, age, and sex. The first group received azithromycin and ceftriaxone, while the second group received levofloxacin. The study concluded that hospitalized CAP patients may benefit more from fluoroquinolone treatment than from the combination of β-lactam and macrolide, although no significant difference in mortality was observed between the two treatment groups. Finally, Greco Kinney et al. (2023) [35] conducted a prospective observational cohort study from March 2020 to July 2022. The study involving 149 patients with severe CAP was admitted to the medical ICU (MICU) of an academic quaternary medical center in the USA, where 63 patients received azithromycin as treatment and 86 patients received doxycycline. As results, no statistically significant difference in mortality rate or length of hospital stay was found between patients treated with β-lactams plus doxycycline versus azithromycin.

Excluded studies

Twenty-three studies were excluded from the review. The reasons for exclusion are summarized in the Additional file 5 (See additional file 5 for reasons for the excluded studies).

Risk of bias in included studies

The review assessed the quality of the thirteen studies. Three of them were found to have randomized controlled trial (RCT) designs [28, 33, 34]. The quality of evidence of these studies was individually evaluated using the Cochrane tool for risk of bias. Giamarellos-Bourboulis et al. (2008) [28] scored “low risk” in the domains related to selection bias, performance bias which included blinding of participants and personnel, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other risks of bias. In addition, the risk of bias was unclear about detection bias. As for Paul et al. (2007) [34], it scored “low risk” in the domains related to attrition bias, performance bias which included blinding of participants and personnel, reporting bias, and other risks of bias. In addition, the risk of bias was unclear about selection bias, and detection bias. Yadegarynia et al. (2022) [33] scored “low risk” in the domain related to selection bias (Random Sequence Generation), attrition bias, reporting bias, and other risks of bias. It scored high in the domain related to performance bias, which included blinding of participants and personnel. In addition, the risk of bias was unclear about selection bias (Allocation Concealment), and detection bias.

Upon applying the MINORS methodological index for grading the nine included non-comparative articles, the resulting scores ranged from 17/24 to 23/24 for the eight domains included in the adopted tool for non-comparative studies. The mean and median scores were 19.9 and 20, respectively. Four studies [11, 15, 26, 35] had a MINORS score of 21/24 or higher and were considered to have a low risk of bias while the other studies’ scores ranged from 17/24 to 20/24 and were reported with moderate risk of bias. In brief, all studies assessed with MINORS had clearly stated the aim, inclusion of consecutive patients, and prospective collection of data. Quality assessment was conducted independently by two reviewers (AA and RN) and achieved a uniform bias assessment. Detailed risk of bias assessment using MINORS for included studies in this review is presented in Additional file 6 for Summary of Risk of Bias-MINORS.

Effects of admission of macrolides

Results were pooled separately for RCTs and non-RCTs to ensure the robustness of the findings. This separation is essential because, in contrast to RCTs, non-randomized research could bring different biases. A meta-analysis for randomized controlled trials (RCT) [28, 33, 34] was not possible in this review due to differences in interventions, patients’ populations, and outcomes; the study’s findings are instead summarized narratively. This approach guarantees that the findings are still included in the review while preventing the inclusion of biases that could occur from trying to combine data from a single study. In addition, the control group in the selected studies comprised patients receiving treatment with non-macrolide alternative antibiotics. According to the treatment guidelines, these therapies specifically included fluoroquinolones, beta-lactam monotherapy, and additional medications including doxycycline. Studies were included if they offered a clear comparison between the macrolide-treated intervention group and the control group receiving any of these other therapies were included in the analysis. This specificity was necessary to evaluate the changes in treatment regimens and their potential impact on results across the studies.

Mortality rate during hospitalization

Mortality NRS (acute up to 7 days or during hospitalization)

A meta-analysis of five studies [11, 17, 26, 31, 32] involving 738 patients, compared clinical outcomes—specifically, the mortality rate between two patient groups: one receiving the standard regimen and the other receiving a combination of antibiotics, including a macrolide. As shown in Fig. 2, the administration of a macrolide had a statistically significant impact (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.25 to 0.71) indicating that adding a macrolide could reduce the mortality rate within the first 7 days of hospitalization. The I2 value of 18% suggests that only a low percentage of the variation in effect sizes across studies was due to heterogeneity, meaning most variation could be attributed to sampling error (chance). However, the certainty of evidence supporting macrolide administration was low, due to the observational study design and the high risk of bias in most individual studies.

Macrolide therapy and in-hospital mortality in CAP patients (N = 5)

Mortality rate post-discharge

Mortality NRS (short-term more than 7 days up to 30 days post-discharge)

A meta-analysis of five studies [17, 27, 30, 32, 34] comprising 3059 patients compared the mortality rate up to 30 days post-discharge. The inclusion of macrolides in the treatment of hospitalized patients showed a statistically significant effect (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.31–0.87), indicating that adding macrolides could reduce mortality rates up to 30 days post-discharge. The I2 value of 65% suggests that a high percentage of the variability in effect estimates was due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error (chance) (Fig. 3). However, the certainty of evidence for macrolide administration was low, owing to the observational study design and the high risk of bias in most individual studies.

Effect of macrolide therapy on post-discharge mortality in CAP and VAP (N = 5)

Length of hospitalization

Length of hospital stay NRS

A meta-analysis of six studies [17, 26, 29, 31, 32, 34] involving 1146 patients, compared the duration of hospital stays between individuals receiving standard treatment and those receiving macrolide therapy in addition to standard care. The analysis showed a statistically significant effect (mean difference − 0.68, 95% CI − 1.67 to 0.32), indicating that administering may reduce the length of hospital stay. The I2 value of 20% suggests that only a small portion of the variability in effect estimates was due to heterogeneity, with most of the variability attributed to sampling error (chance) (Fig. 4). However, the certainty of the evidence supporting macrolide administration was relatively low, as most individual studies were observational and carried a high risk of bias.

Other outcomes

In a prospective observational study by Lorenzo et al. (2015) [29], the cytokine profiles, particularly tumor necrosis factors (TNF), were analyzed as clinical outcomes in patients with CAP. Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most frequent etiological micro-organism identified in CAP cases. The results showed that IL-6 and TNF-α levels in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), as well as the level of IL-8 in blood, were lower in patients treated with a macrolide regimen. However, there were no significant differences in overall blood cytokine levels between patients treated with a macrolide and those who were not. Another prospective study by Arnold et al. (2016) [17] assessed the neutrophil function and cytokine and chemokine levels. Although no statistically significant differences were found between the treatment groups for most cytokine levels, the levels of IL-6, IL-8, and 12p40 were lower in the macrolide group.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the available data regarding the use of macrolides as immunomodulators in the treatment of CAP and VAP and to evaluate the effectiveness of this intervention on several outcomes, including mortality rate, length of stay, recurrence of pneumonia within 90 days, and clinical stability. In nine out of the 13 studies, the use of macrolides as anti-inflammatory agents in the treatment of CAP and VAP was found to be effective in reducing mortality rate and length of stay as primary outcomes among adult patients, compared to standard treatment.

The meta-analyses of included studies confirmed these findings, showing a consistent reduction in mortality rates during hospitalization when patients received a β-lactam and macrolide combination, compared with monotherapy or fluoroquinolone + β-lactam combination. The pooled OR of 0.42 (95% CI 0.25–0.71) indicates that patients receiving macrolides were 58% less likely to die during hospitalization than those who did not (p = 0.001). The low heterogeneity (I2 = 18%) suggests that the effect of macrolides on mortality is generally consistent across studies and patient demographics. These findings aligned with the results of Asadi et al. (2012) [36], which reported that macrolide use was associated with a 22% decrease in mortality in hospitalized CAP patients compared to non-macrolide regimens.

The reduction highlighted suggests that the use of macrolides in the treatment of patients with CAP decreases the likelihood of death during hospitalization compared with those who received standard treatment. This effect is mostly noticeable in the study by Periera et al. (2018) [26], which had the largest sample size and highest weight in the analysis. However, no significant difference was found in multiple individual studies [31, 32], highlighting inconsistencies in the results. In particular, Arnold et al. (2016) [17] had a relatively small sample size (40 patients) and a very low incidence rate (0 events in the macrolide group). Due to the observational nature of the study, selection bias may exist, and the estimated uncertainty is reflected by the large confidence interval (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.01 to 4.81). Moreover, the small sample size in the study by Stahl et al. (1999) [32] (76 patients) reduces the ability to detect differences among groups. With a non-significant result (OR 1.85, 95% CI 0.11 to 30.74) and a very wide confidence interval, the study's findings indicate that small sample size has a significant influence, raising uncertainty and decreasing reliability [32].

In the post-discharge period, the meta-analysis results showed that patients treated with macrolides were 48% less likely to die within 30 days post-discharge compared to those who did not receive macrolides (pooled OR of 0.52, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.25–0.71). However, the moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 65%) suggests variation in the effect of macrolides on mortality reduction across different trials and patient populations. This suggests that post-discharge mortality may significantly decrease with the use of macrolides, offering a sustained protective impact that could help stabilize patients as they transition back to outpatient care. This effect, most pronounced in the studies by Paul et al. (2007) [34] and Tessmer et al. (2009) [30], shows a statistically significant decrease in the mortality rate. This finding is particularly significant as it demonstrates how macrolides might improve long-term outcomes and lower readmission rates. When comparing our study’s findings to those of other studies, the main problem is that no observational study has shown that combined therapy is beneficial in lowering 30-day mortality. Moreover, Baddour et al. (2004) [37] confirmed that combination therapy including macrolides was associated with lower 14-day mortality among critically ill patients.

The results of the meta-analysis examining the effect of macrolides on the length of hospital stay indicate a potential effect of macrolide administration, but this effect is not statistically significant. The strongest evidence comes from the study by Lorenzo et al. (2015) [29], which shows a significant reduction in hospital length of stay. Although this finding is statistically insignificant, the overall pooled mean difference of − 0.68 indicates a trend towards a shorter length of stay for patients receiving macrolides. The low heterogeneity (I2 = 20%) suggests consistency across studies, but further research is needed to confirm these findings. No meta-analysis was found in the literature search to support or compare these findings.

The clinical benefits observed with the use of macrolides in the management of CAP can be related to several factors. Macrolides are used mainly for their antimicrobial effects, but their immunomodulating effects could also be related to the clinical benefits, which include the prevention of complications like acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and sepsis, which are major causes of death in hospitalized patients [1, 38]. This is particularly relevant in pneumococcal CAP and in patients with a high level of inflammation, i.e., CRP levels higher than 15 mg/dL, as shown in the study by Baddour et al. (2004) [37]. Furthermore, macrolides are active against a wide range of pathogens, including atypical bacteria, not always covered by conventional antibiotics, and may be helpful in the management of co-infection and secondary infection, hence reducing the risk of mortality [39]. Therefore, the observed associations between macrolide use and improved outcomes should be interpreted in the context of both their antimicrobial effects and potential immunomodulatory properties, without inferring direct causality.

Moreover, the timing of macrolide administration appears crucial. In patients with severe CAP, Periera et al. (2018) [26] found that the macrolide intervention significantly improved both 6-month and in-hospital mortality rates, especially in patients who received treatment within the first 6 h after diagnosis. Moreover, Stahl et al. (1999) [32] reported that the hospital length of stay was shorter for patients receiving macrolides within 24 h of admission compared with those not receiving macrolides. Administering a macrolide early during hospital admission could ensure that anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects occur at a critical point in the patient’s illness trajectory, preventing further complications [40]. To improve patient outcomes, future research should focus on determining the optimal time for macrolide administration.

However, it is important to highlight the limitations of the study. First, the majority of the included studies were observational, which limits the ability to control for confounders such as age, underlying health conditions, and concurrent treatment, in addition to unmeasured confounding that could still arise despite the use of multivariable analyses. Second, there is a low level of certainty in the evidence across the included studies, mainly because many of the individual studies encompassed in the analysis had a significant risk of bias, mostly caused by observational study designs. Third, language bias may also be present as only English-language publications were included which potentially excludes other relevant studies published in different languages. Fourth, the variability in the types of macrolides used in the treatment of CAP, including azithromycin, clarithromycin, and erythromycin, affects their effectiveness in reducing mortality and hospital length of stay due to the unique pharmacokinetics and spectrum of activity of each [41].

Furthermore, the variety of macrolide dosages used, the inconsistency in the duration of therapy, the different times of administration, and the combination with other antibiotics reported can affect the mortality rate and hospital length of stay as primary outcomes in this study. Additionally, the presence of macrolide-resistant pathogens, as reported in some included studies, may influence treatment effectiveness and clinical outcomes, thereby introducing further variability in the observed results. Furthermore, Publication bias could not be formally assessed due to the limited number of included studies (< 10 per meta-analysis), which may affect the robustness of the findings. Finally, the small sample sizes in some studies may not adequately represent the larger population and could influence the generalizability of findings.

These results emphasize the need for larger, well-designed randomized controlled trials to support these findings and better understand the efficacy of macrolide usage in clinical practice. Future research should also aim to better distinguish between the antimicrobial and potential immunomodulatory effects of macrolides, particularly in well-defined patient subgroups.

Conclusions

Findings from this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that adding macrolides to standard antibiotic regimens for CAP and VAP may reduce in-hospital and post-discharge mortality, as well as hospital length of stay. However, several limitations, including the observational nature of the study designs, small sample sizes, variations in the types of macrolides used, and a high risk of bias, highlight the need for more reliable randomized controlled trials to confirm our findings.
