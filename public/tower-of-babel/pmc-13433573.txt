Safety and Efficacy of Allogeneic Integrin α10β1-Selected Mesenchymal Stem Cells in the Treatment of Knee Osteoarthritis: A First-In-Human Phase I/IIa Dose Escalation Study
Authors: Lucienne A. Vonk, Camilla Wennersten, Kristina Uvebrant, Kavitha Siva, Liselotte Theorell, Marc Russo, Stephen Hall, Evy Lundgren-Åkerlund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSafety and efficacy of allogeneic integrin α10β1-selected MSCs (integrin α10-MSCs) have been shown in two post-traumatic osteoarthritis (OA) equine models. The aim of this study is to evaluate safety, tolerability and preliminary efficacy of allogeneic integrin α10-MSCs in human symptomatic knee OA.MethodsIn a first-in-human prospective multicentre phase I/IIa dose escalation study, 24 patients (41-75 years, Kellgren-Lawrence grade II-III knee OA) were treated with a single intra-articular injection of 4, 8 or 16 million integrin α10-MSCs. Safety and tolerability were assessed by adverse events, electrocardiography, vital signs, and physical and laboratory examinations. Clinical outcome was measured by visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain and Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). Structural outcomes were assessed by X-ray and MRI. The total follow-up period was 24 months for the highest dose and 18 months for the lower doses.ResultsThe most frequently reported study drug-related adverse events were injection site pain, joint swelling and injection site movement impairment. All doses showed sustained improvements in pain and function, whereas the 16 million dose cohort showed earlier effects. The 16 million dose cohort also showed a reduction in bone marrow lesions, signs of an improved cartilage quality by T2* relaxation time, and a stable medial minimum joint space width.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated safety and efficacy of intra-articular injection of integrin α10-MSCs for the treatment of mild to moderate knee OA. At a dose of 16 million integrin α10-MSCs, a potentially quick and long-lasting therapeutic effect, supportive of disease-modification, was evident.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic, progressive, degenerative joint disease that represents a leading cause of pain, disability and reduced quality of life. 1 Treatment relies mostly on symptom management as there is currently no disease-modifying treatment available. 2 Although knee arthroplasty is an established and effective intervention for end-stage knee OA, it is not desirable in younger patients. 3 Orthobiologic injections have emerged as an increasingly popular treatment for OA. Amongst the spectrum of orthobiologics, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are of special interest due to their potential regenerative, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. 4

A growing body of peer-reviewed publications have reported on intra-articular injections of culture-expanded MSCs to treat knee OA.5,6 Although study quality is heterogenous and bias has been detected in many of these publications, 7 the collective findings suggest a favourable safety profile and improvements in pain and function. 6 In addition, a few studies report some signs of disease modification. Favourable results have been published on both autologous and allogeneic MSCs, coming from a wide variety of tissue sources. When it comes to culture-expanded MSCs, only allogeneic products can offer an “off-the-shelf” treatment, with scalable manufacturing processes potentially leading to improvement in production costs and accessibility. Allogeneic MSCs are regarded safe for clinical use in unmatched patients and are considered immune evasive as undifferentiated MSCs are very low in the expression of immune-relevant surface markers and possess immunomodulatory properties. 8

Heterogeneity in the cellular composition of MSC preparations is an issue.9,10 MSC preparations, regardless of the origin, are usually not pure populations of MSCs and contain varying proportions of contaminating cells, such as hematopoietic cells, endothelial progenitors, immune cells, pericytes and fibroblasts. Despite this, MSC preparations often adhere to the minimal criteria for defining multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells, positioned by the International Society for Cell and Gene Therapy (ISCT). 11 The cellular heterogeneity in MSC preparations can influence both safety and efficacy outcomes and are a regulatory concern. Selection of MSCs from heterogenous preparations provides more homogenous and consistent MSC preparations. In this study, MSCs were selected based on cell surface expression of the collagen receptor integrin α10β1. Allogeneic equine integrin α10β1-selected MSCs (integrin α10-MSCs) have shown safety and efficacy in two equine models of post-traumatic OA.12,13 Moreover, human integrin α10-MSCs have demonstrated homing capacity and the ability to differentiate to chondrocytes (or chondrocyte-like cells) and to produce articular cartilage matrix proteins in a rabbit chondral defect model. 14 The present study reports the effects of human integrin α10-MSCs in the treatment of symptomatic knee OA. The primary objectives of the study were to assess the safety and tolerability of a single intra-articular injection of integrin α10-MSCs in patients with symptomatic knee OA of Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade II - III. Secondary objectives were to assess preliminary efficacy in terms of clinical (pain and knee function) and structural (by X-ray and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) assessments) changes compared to baseline.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This trial was a first-in-human, prospective, multicentre, uncontrolled, non-randomised, open-label phase I/IIa study (https://ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05344157). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines on Good Clinical Practice. The protocol was approved by Human Research Ethics Committee, Australia, and the study was performed at two investigational sites in Australia.

Eligible patients were aged 40-75 years with a body mass index (BMI) <35 kg/m2, and with mild to moderate symptomatic knee OA (KL grade II-III and visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain score ≥30) in the study knee. Patients with malalignment (valgus or varus) >5 degrees in the study knee were excluded from the study. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table 1. Informed consents were signed at or prior to the screening visit, when also a bilateral X-ray for eligibility was carried out. The study was designed as a dose escalation trial. Twenty-four patients were enrolled in three consecutive cohorts and eight patients in each of the cohorts received 4, 8 or 16 million integrin α10-MSCs (Figure 1). The study was subject to oversight by a Safety Review Committee (SRC). Each cohort commenced with dosing of 1 sentinel patient. If no safety concerns were observed in the sentinel patient at Visit 2 (1 week after dosing), the remainder of the cohort was dosed. The SRC reviewed all available safety, tolerability and preliminary efficacy data up to at least Visit 3 (4 weeks after dosing) of all patients in each cohort. In absence of any safety concerns (including meeting any of the pre-defined halting criteria), the SRC decided to allow recruitment and dosing of the next dose level cohort. An additional review by the SRC was performed when all patients in the study had completed at least their Visit 4 (3 months after dosing). Follow-up visits were carried out at 1 week, 4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and 18 months after dosing. For the highest dose cohort, an additional follow-up visit at 24 months was added.Figure 1.Participant flow chart

The sample size of this study was determined based on practical, logistical, and medical considerations and was not based on statistical power regarding hypothesis testing, or precision regarding parameter estimation.

Manufacturing, Preparation, and Administration of Integrin α10-MSCs

Manufacturing was performed at the GMP-authorized facility of Xintela AB (Lund, Sweden). A lipoaspirate from a single donor (female, 37 years old, signed informed consent) was used as starting material. Donation, testing and procurement of the tissue was performed compliant to the tissue establishment license (authorized by the Swedish Medical Products Agency (MPA)). Lipoaspirate was processed into the stromal vascular fraction and subsequently plated into sterile HYPERFlasks (Corning, Glendale AZ, USA). Cells were cultured in MSC-Brew GMP Medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) at 37°C, 4% O2 and 5% CO2, and expanded in HYPERFlasks and Xpansion bioreactors (Pall Life Sciences, Hoegaarden, Belgium). A selection step of MSCs based on expression of integrin α10β1 using CliniMACS Plus (Miltenyi Biotec) and specific integrin α10 antibody (Xintela AB), was performed after three passages. At Population Doubling Level 14.9, cells were cryopreserved in CryoStor CS10 (Biolife solutions, Bothel, WA, USA). The cells were tested for appearance, endotoxins, sterility including mycoplasma, cell count and viability, immunophenotype (CD73 ≥95%, CD105 ≥95%, CD90 ≥95%, and CD45 ≤2%, CD34 ≤2%, CD11b ≤2%, CD19 ≤2% and HLA-DR ≤2%), trilineage (adipogenic, chondrogenic and osteogenic) differentiation capacity, selected viruses, genetic instabilities and chromosomal aberrations.

Prior to administration, frozen vials with cell suspension were thawed in a water bath and diluted with CryoStor CS10 cryomedia to a total volume of 5 mL.

A subcutaneous local injection of anaesthetics (containing 2% lidocaine) was administered prior to the administration of integrin α10-MSCs. A designated physician administered a single dose (5 mL) as an intra-articular, ultrasound-guided injection into the study knee. All injections were via the suprapatellar route.

Following injection of the integrin α10-MSCs, patients were asked to lay in bed or sit on a recovery chair for at least 30 minutes, and the patients remained at site under observation for at least 4 hours post treatment.

Outcome Measures

Primary Outcomes

Safety and tolerability were assessed by adverse events (AEs) up to 24 months after dosing, and 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), vital signs, physical examination, and laboratory tests up to 18 months after dosing. The AEs were evaluated according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5 and coded by MedDRA version 24.1. Study drug-related and study procedure-related AEs were defined as treatment-emergent AEs where the relationship to study drug or study procedure was reported as ‘Possible’, ‘Probable/Likely’, ‘Certain’ or missing.

Other Outcomes

VAS for pain and Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Sscore (KOOS) were assessed at baseline and at 1 week (VAS for pain only), 4 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months and 18 months after dosing for all cohorts and at 24 months for the 16 million dose cohort. For KOOS, responder rates were evaluated for each cohort using a threshold of 10 points change from baseline as the minimal clinically important difference. 15

X-ray and MRI were conducted at baseline and at 6, 12 and 18 months after dosing for all cohorts and at 24 months for the 16 million dose cohort. For X-ray analyses, the X-rays taken at the screening visit to confirm eligibility, were used as baseline. Fixed flexion view radiographs were obtained using a positioning frame. Minimum joint space width (min JSW) was measured in the medial and lateral femorotibial compartments using a computer-aided integrated functionality of Dynamika (Image Analysis Group, London, UK), with a review and correction of the landmarks if required by an experienced independent radiologist.

All MRI scans were performed on 1.5-Tesla clinical MRI scanners (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). A standard protocol was used which included proton density turbo spin echo fat saturated sequences in three planes, sagittal 3D gradient echo fat-suppressed or water excitation T1-weighted sequences, and sagittal 2D T2* sequences.

For T2* relaxation times as a measure of cartilage quality, the cartilage was semiautomatically outlined by a region of interest method, comprising the regions patella, medial femur, trochlea, lateral femur, medial tibia and lateral tibia. The T2* relaxation time values were extracted from the T2* mapping sequence.

Whole knee health was assessed using MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS), 16 performed by an independent and trained reader.

Statistics

All data were analysed as collected and missing values were not imputed or replaced. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 or higher (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). A paired t-test was used to compare the values for VAS for pain, KOOS and its subscales, min JSW, and T2* relaxation time at each follow-up visit against the baseline value. Level of significance was set at P < 0.05. For MOAKS, frequency counts and percentages were generated for patients who had not changed, improved or got worse at follow-up compared to baseline.

Additionally, one patient in the 16 million dose cohort withdrew after the 18-month visit.

The cohorts were well balanced regarding demography, except for mean VAS for pain at baseline, which was higher for the 16 million dose cohort.

Safety and Tolerability

Treatment-emergent AEs were reported by 22 patients (91.7%) and 7 of these patients reported study drug-related AEs. The most frequently reported study drug-related AEs were injection site pain, joint swelling and injection site movement impairment (Table 3). Ten patients (41.7%) reported study procedure-related AEs, the most frequently reported were injection site pain, joint swelling, injection site movement impairment, and presyncope. Important to note is that the majority (84.6%) of the study drug-related AEs were also reported to be related to study procedure (due to difficulties for Principal Investigators to determine if related to the study drug or study procedure). Grade 3 AEs occurred in 4 patients (16.7%). No Grade 4 or Grade 5 AEs were reported. One patient in the 4 million dose cohort experienced a study drug-related Grade 3 AE. This event was reported as transient severe pain at the injection site (started immediately post-injection of integrin α10-MSCs and settled within 15 minutes) and were reported by the Principal Investigator to be related to both study drug and study procedure. Three patients experienced serious AEs after dosing; reported as fractured neck of femur, prostate cancer and squamous cell carcinoma, which were all considered unrelated to both study drug and study procedure by the Principal Investigator. No patients discontinued the study due to AEs. The clinical laboratory safety results did not show any clinically meaningful post-dose changes from baseline, and no patients had abnormal clinically significant changes in vital signs or ECG parameters. Discussion

The most important finding of this study was the demonstration of safety and preliminary efficacy of intra-articular injection of integrin α10-MSCs for the treatment of mild to moderate knee OA. It showed that a single intra-articular injection of allogeneic integrin α10-MSCs at doses of 4, 8, and 16 million was safe and well tolerated. The evaluation of preliminary efficacy in this study showed sustained improvements on knee pain and knee function up to 18 months following treatment with integrin α10-MSCs at all dose levels tested. For the 16 million dose cohort, improvements in knee pain and knee function were evident from 4 weeks and sustained up to 24 months after injection, suggesting both a quick and long-lasting therapeutic effect. The 16 million dose cohort also showed signs of improvements in joint structure with a stable medial min JSW, a significantly decreased T2* relaxation time and a reduction in BMLs, all of which is supportive of a disease-modifying effect.

For study drug- and procedure related AEs, there was no indication of dose relationship to incidence nor severity. Most AEs reported as related to study drug were also reported as AEs related to study procedure, as it was not possible to make a discrimination. The study drug-related AEs, injection site pain, joint swelling and injection site movement impairment, have been observed in other clinical studies evaluating intra-articular injection of allogeneic adipose tissue (AT)-derived MSCs17-20 and also in relation to other kinds of intra-articular injections such as corticosteroids, hyaluronic acid, and platelet-rich plasma (PRP),21-23 suggesting they are quite common in knee OA treatment.

In the current study, no statistical analyses between dose cohorts were performed due to the relatively low number of patients in the study. For the patient reported outcomes, other than earlier therapeutic effects on VAS for pain, KOOS pain, and KOOS ADL of the 16 million dose, no trend of a dose-response effect could be observed. This is in line with the other clinical studies on allogeneic AT- MSCs, where no clear dose-response was observed on subjective efficacy outcomes.18-20,24 On the other hand, we could observe a dose-effect trend on structural outcomes. A possible explanation for the difference in observed dose-response effects between subjective and objective structural outcomes could be the complex and multifactorial mechanism of action of MSC therapy, whereby certain therapeutic effects may have a different minimum effective dose and biological targets may reach saturation at different levels than other therapeutic mechanisms. The current study showed advantages of the highest 16 million dose on structural outcomes. A dose-effect trend can be observed for the min JSW results. Structural changes are sparsely reported in clinical studies injecting MSCs 25 and no robust evidence of disease-modifying effects has been reported in studies investigating allogeneic AT-MSCs. Freitag et al have reported a non-significant mean percentage improvement in total knee cartilage volume at 12-month follow-up with their allogeneic AT-MSC product dosed at 10 and 100 million cells. 19 They also reported a non-significant improvement of cartilage quality by T2-mapping values of the 100 million MSC dose at 12-month follow-up. Lu et al noted non-significant improvements in cartilage volume for their low-dose group of 10 million cells at 48-week follow-up. 17 A significant increase in cartilage volume compared to placebo was only shown by Kuah et al, on the lateral tibia, 12 months after injection of 3.9 million allogeneic AT-MSCs together with conditioned medium. 20 The higher 6.7 million dose showed a non-significant increase of cartilage volume on the lateral tibia. In the current study, we have reported a stable medial min JSW for the 16 million dose cohort up to 24 months, while the lower dose cohorts showed a non-significant progression of joint space narrowing over 18 months on X-ray. Also, a trend towards improved cartilage tissue quality as assessed by T2* relaxation time was observed for the 8 and 16 million dose cohorts, up to 18 and 24 months, respectively, reaching statistical significance at 12 months in the 8 million dose cohort and at 24 months in the 16 million dose cohort. T2* mapping is a sensitive MRI technique that evaluates macromolecule organization and water mobility in the tissue.26,27 It has a short acquisition time and provides high image resolution, but it is also sensitive to magic angle effects and susceptible to artifacts. Currently, it is unknown what its prognostic value is in relation to both cartilage degeneration and regeneration, therefore it should not be used standalone as a surrogate marker for disease modification. In the current study, T2* supported the findings on the min JSW with improved relaxation times in the cohorts where there was less joint space narrowing or a stable JSW. One other study, evaluating various quantitative MRI techniques as outcome after injection of three different doses of allogeneic AT-mesenchymal progenitor cells, has reported on T2* relaxation times. 28 That study also reported statistically significant reductions in T2* after cell injection and improvements in patient reported outcomes. For T2 mapping, only superficial medial cartilage T2 was associated with relevant disease progression, 29 whereas in the current study, changes in T2* were observed in all the zones of the cartilage, and trending to be most pronounced in the superficial zone. Whether this reflects a difference between disease progression and regeneration remains to be investigated. Overall, heterogeneity in imaging methods and imaging-related analyses techniques make it difficult to compare structural outcomes between studies. One other structural change that stood out in the current study was the improvement of BMLs, especially in the 16 million dose cohort. BMLs represent an important feature of OA. The presence and severity of BMLs are associated with OA-related pain, structural worsening and an increased risk of conversion to total knee arthroplasty.30-32 Specifically, improvement of BMLs was also observed in a study comparing the effects of three monthly intra-articular injections with autologous adipose tissue-derived stromal cells with PRP. 33 In a more recent study, the authors showed improvement of BMLs associated with knee OA after combining intra-osseous and intra-articular injections of autologous adipose tissue-derived stromal cells in athletes. 34 Subchondral bone improvements were also observed in an equine post-traumatic OA study with allogeneic equine integrin α10-MSCs. 12

In comparison to other studies evaluating intra-articular injection of culture-expanded MSCs, the current study used relatively low numbers of MSCs. Most other studies used ranges between 20 and 100 million MSCs, with up to date a maximum of 150 million.35,36 In clinical studies using allogeneic AT-MSCs, the number of MSCs have ranged between 10 – 100 million, 19 16 – 64 million, 18 and 10 – 50 million. 17 Only Kuah et al used lower numbers of MSCs (3.9 and 6.7 million), but these were combined with cell culture supernatants, which also contain active components. 20 Given the MSC selection for integrin α10β1, the current study used a homogenous population of MSCs. This together with safety and efficacy results of two equine studies using 20 million equine integrin α10-MSCs,12,13 were reasons to start the clinical development with relatively low cell numbers. The benefits observed with the highest 16 million dose of integrin α10-MSCs compared to the lower doses, does raise the question whether the effects would improve further with a higher dose. Based on especially safety results from the other MSC studies, there is sufficient room to go higher in dosing with integrin α10-MSCs. Nevertheless, all doses tested in the current study provided relevant and significant improvements in the patient reported outcomes, which were still present 18 and 24 months after dosing.

A limitation of the current study is the lack of a placebo, and placebo effects following intra-articular injections are substantial. 37 However, the effects still observed at 18 and 24 months after dosing of integrin α10-MSCs, are unlikely to be a result of a placebo effect. The effects are longer than therapeutic effects observed for instance for corticosteroids and hyaluronic acid.22,37,38 A possible explanation of the long-term effects could be an increased retention of the cells through the expression of the collagen receptor integrin α10β1. The integrin α10-MSCs have shown improved attachment to articular cartilage and subchondral bone compared to non-selected MSC preparations, ex vivo. 39 In a rabbit osteochondral defect model, homing, engraftment in the neocartilage and production of type II collagen and aggrecan by integrin α10-MSCs suggesting chondrogenic differentiation, have been observed. 14 In addition, the allogeneic integrin α10-MSCs could be traced back in the osteochondral unit in an equine post-traumatic OA model 52 days after their intra-articular injection. 13 In line with this, a recent publication has shown anoikis resistance, increased intra-articular retention and improved therapeutic efficacy in an OA mouse model of MSCs with artificially constructed collagen-targeting receptors. 40

In the present study, the 16 million dose cohort started with a higher VAS for pain score compared to the lower dose cohorts. The strong decrease in VAS for pain at 4 weeks compared to baseline for the 16 million dose cohort was not just a result of higher baseline values as the actual mean VAS for pain of this cohort at 4 weeks was 12 – 14 points lower than that of the other cohorts. A rapid improvement at 4 weeks compared to baseline for the 16 million dose cohort was also observed for KOOS pain, KOOS ADL, and KOOS5. For these scores, the 16 million dose cohort did not start at lower mean values compared to the other cohorts. For all cohorts, there was a reduction in the decreased VAS for pain compared to baseline at 12 months. This could potentially be caused by a return to more normal or increased levels of activity and sports, which could have caused some increase in pain. It was observed throughout all three cohorts and the 12-month follow-up visits were between August 2023 and July 2024, covering all seasons. Additionally, the visits took place at two sites, without major clear changes, in for instance personnel having contact with the study participants. Thus, seasonal influences or changes in people taking the visits can be ruled out. It should be noted that no such dip was observed for all three cohorts in KOOS pain or other KOOS subscales.

Besides the lack of a placebo group as addressed above, the small sample size is a limitation. The limited number of participants reduces statistical power, and results should be confirmed in larger studies. Another key limitation of this study is that participants were permitted to use concomitant pain medication. They were only instructed not to take any pain medication in the morning prior to attending a planned study visit and pain medication usage has not been collected in sufficient detail to investigate any changes over time. Use of analgesics may have confounded the observed improvements in pain and function. However, pain medications cannot have influenced the structural improvements observed in mainly the 16 million dose cohort. Another limitation is bilateral OA. All participants had OA, of variable severity, in the contralateral (non-treated) knee. Differences in structural involvement and symptom burden in the non-treated knee may have influenced pain and function outcomes of the study knee. Additionally, the heterogeneity in the severity may have introduced additional variability into the outcome assessments and potentially diluted the observed treatment effect specific to the study knee.

Conclusions

A single intra-articular injection of allogeneic integrin α10-MSCs at doses of 4, 8, and 16 million was safe and well tolerated in patients with symptomatic mild to moderate knee OA. The evaluation of preliminary efficacy in this study showed sustained improvements on knee pain and knee function up to 18 and 24 months following treatment with allogeneic integrin α10-MSCs. For the highest dose of 16 million integrin α10-MSCs, improvements in knee pain and function were evident from 4 weeks and sustained up to 24 months after treatment, which together with improvements in cartilage and bone structure, suggests a quick and long-lasting therapeutic effect, supportive of a disease-modifying effect.
