Quantifying Patient Positioning Errors and Radiation Dose Variation in Cardiac Computed Tomography Angiography
Authors: Yasser H Hadi, Hassan B Hawsawi, Niamh Moore, Helle Precht, Andrew England, Mark McEntee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAccurate patient centring at the computed tomography (CT) scanner isocentre is essential for optimising radiation dose. This study aimed to quantify positioning errors, assess their relationship with radiation dose, and estimate the potential dose reduction achievable with optimal centring.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent electrocardiography (ECG)-gated cardiac CT angiography between January 2023 and July 2025 at a tertiary hospital. Scans were performed using Siemens SOMATOM Force dual-source CT systems. Vertical and lateral deviations from the scanner isocentre were calculated from scout images using integrated analytics within the institutional dose monitoring platform (Qaelum NV, Leuven, Belgium). Radiation dose indices, including the volume-weighted CT dose index (CTDIvol) and dose length product (DLP), were extracted from DICOM dose reports. Statistical analyses comprised descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, multivariable regression, and simulation of potential dose reductions with ideal centring.ResultsData from 399 patients were evaluated. Significant off-centring (>10 mm) occurred in 83% of scans in the anteroposterior (AP) direction and 50% laterally. Severe AP off-centring (>30 mm) was present in 39% of cases. Offcentring showed no independent association with CTDIvol or DLP after adjustment for body mass index (BMI), age, and sex. Male patients demonstrated significantly greater off-centring in both the AP (p = 0.048) and lateral (p = 0.037) directions, and BMI category was significantly associated with off-centring magnitude in the AP (p = 0.031) and lateral (p = 0.022) directions. Literature-based scenario modelling suggested that ideal centring could hypothetically reduce mean CTDIvol by approximately 12%, with a similar reduction in DLP.ConclusionPatient off-centring was highly prevalent in cardiac CT examinations. No independent association between off-centring and radiation dose was observed when adjusting for BMI, age, and sex. Literature-based scenario modelling suggests that meaningful radiation dose reductions may be achievable with optimum centring, although this requires prospective confirmation.Keywords: Cardiac CT, CT, Patient positioning, Radiation dose, Off-centring

1. Introduction

Computed tomography (CT) has become an indispensable medical imaging modality due to its ability to provide detailed cross-sectional images of the body [1,2]. Cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA), in particular, plays a vital role in the diagnosis of coronary artery disease [3]. While CT offers significant diagnostic benefits, it is also a notable source of ionising radiation exposure, contributing substantially to the overall population dose from diagnostic radiology [4,5]. Therefore, optimising radiation dose while maintaining high diagnostic image quality is a critical concern in CT imaging, including CCTA [6,7]. Achieving this balance often involves the use of dose optimisation tools such as automatic tube current modulation (ATCM) and appropriate patient positioning [8,9].

Accurate patient positioning and centring within the CT scanner gantry are fundamental for optimal scanner operation, which directly impacts radiation dose and image quality [10–13]. Patient positioning refers to aligning the patient relative to the scanner’s isocentre, the central point of the gantry’s aperture. Patient mis-centring, or off-centring, occurs when the patient’s cross-sectional midpoint deviates from the isocentre [14]. Studies show that patient mis-centring is a common issue in routine clinical practice [15]. Observations from large datasets indicate that a significant majority of CT examinations of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis are performed with patients positioned off the iso-centre [16]. This deviation can occur in the vertical (superior/inferior) and lateral (left/right) axes, although proper vertical positioning is particularly important, especially when ATCM and bowtie filters are employed [17–20].

The consequences of mis-centring are well-documented and include adverse effects on both radiation dose and image quality. In terms of radiation dose, mis-centring can significantly affect the radiation dose delivered to the patient [21]. When a patient is positioned closer to the X-ray tube, the ATCM system may perceive the patient as being larger on the localiser image, leading to an increase in radiation output as it compensates for the apparent size. Conversely, positioning the patient further away can result in insufficient radiation exposure. Even small vertical deviations from the isocentre can cause substantial changes in radiation exposure when using ATCM [18]. Phantom and clinical studies have demonstrated that mis-centring leads to increased surface dose and affects dose metrics, including CT Dose Index (CTDIvol) and Dose-Length Product (DLP) [19,22]. For example, a mis-centring of 6 cm has been reported to increase surface dose by up to 41% in one study [10], and a 6 cm vertical offset resulted in a 27% dose increase in another [23].

Beyond radiation dose, mis-centring also degrades image quality [24]. The bowtie filter, designed to provide uniform signal detection and dose distribution when the patient is correctly centred, performs suboptimally when the patient is off-centre. This can result in increased image noise, reduced contrast, artefacts, and non-uniformity [25–27]. Quantitative metrics such as the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and Contrast-to-Noise Ratio (CNR) have been shown to decrease with increased off-centring. Inaccurate CT numbers, which can also result from miscentring, may even lead to potential misdiagnosis [28].

For CCTA, maintaining both high image quality and low radiation dose is particularly critical. Accurate diagnosis of coronary artery disease relies on clear visualisation of anatomical structures and pathology, requiring optimal image quality. Given that CT contributes significantly to medical radiation exposure, dose reduction efforts are essential [6]. Manual patient positioning, traditionally performed by radiographers using laser guidance, can be time-consuming, practitioner-dependent, inconsistent, and prone to errors. This variability contributes to inconsistent dose and image quality across examinations [29,30]. While automated patient positioning systems, for example, 3D cameras, are being developed and evaluated to improve positioning accuracy and potentially reduce dose compared to manual methods, patient mis-centring remains a challenge in routine practice [12]. Contemporary cardiac CT recommendations emphasise placing the heart at the gantry isocentre to optimise bow-tie filtration and tube current modulation, thereby improving dose efficiency and image quality [31]. During the study period, however, patients imaged within the study institution were positioned using mid-thoracic anatomical landmarking, reflecting a protocol designed to ensure full thoracic coverage when cardiac assessment is performed alongside evaluation of potential incidental or non-cardiac findings [32,33].

Despite the accepted limitations of mis-centring, quantifying the degree of these positioning errors in specific CT applications such as CCTA and establishing a direct correlation with patient-specific dose metrics, such as CTDIvol and DLP, using clinical data is important for understanding real-world implications and identifying areas for optimisation. This study aims to evaluate the degree of patient positioning errors in a cohort of patients undergoing CCTA by quantifying their deviation from the scanner isocentre. Furthermore, this work seeks to correlate the magnitude and direction of these positioning errors with corresponding patient-specific radiation dose metrics, specifically CTDIvol and DLP, recorded for each examination.

2. Methods

2.1. Research design

This study employed a retrospective observational design to systematically evaluate the accuracy of patient positioning in CCTA and its association with individual radiation dose metrics. The investigation was conducted at the Department of Medical Imaging at King Abdullah Medical City (KAMC). The primary focus was to quantify deviations from the scanner iso-centre in the anteroposterior (AP; table height/vertical) and lateral (LAT) directions, using localizer images, and to assess whether these deviations are statistically associated with patient-specific CTDIvol and DLP values as recorded in the scan protocol metadata. No modifications to clinical workflows were introduced, and all data were acquired during routine diagnostic imaging procedures. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research [34].

2.2. Population and sampling technique

The target population comprised adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent clinically indicated CCTA at KAMC over a 2.5-year period (January 2023 to July 2025). Eligible cases were retrospectively identified through the Radiology Information System (RIS) and the hospital’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), specifically AGFA HealthCare Enterprise Imaging version 8.1.2 SP5.7. A purposive, consecutive sampling strategy was employed to include all examinations performed on a single scanner platform using standardised ECG-gated protocols. This approach ensured consistency in acquisition parameters and reduced technical variability. The final sample size was determined by the total number of cases meeting the inclusion criteria within the defined study window.

2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible cases were defined as CCTA examinations with complete imaging datasets, including localizer images and corresponding axial image series, performed on adult patients using routine clinical protocols. Examinations were included only if they employed ECG-gating and had complete DICOM metadata with documented CTDIvol and DLP values. Exclusion criteria consisted of examinations with corrupted image data, incomplete scout or axial series, and significant motion artefacts that affected landmark recognition.

2.4. Data collection process

Patient positioning accuracy was assessed using localiser (scout) images from clinically acquired CCTA examinations performed on two Siemens SOMATOM Force dual-source CT scanners (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). Scout images were reviewed using the institutional Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS; AGFA HealthCare Enterprise Imaging version 8.1.2 SP5.7) and the integrated radiation dose monitoring platform (Qaelum NV, Leuven, Belgium), which supports quality control by providing structured dose analytics and image positioning data.

For each case, AP and LAT deviations from the scanner iso-centre were measured in millimetres using anatomical landmarks. Specifically, the midline alignment of the vertebral column and the sternum served as a reference point. These deviations were calculated as the absolute distance between the patient’s midline and the scanner’s reference centre in both the AP and LAT directions, derived from coronal and sagittal localiser images. Within the study institution, radiographers positioned patients using standard mid-thoracic landmarking (sternum and vertebral column). The local protocol intentionally required mid-thoracic centring since many of the cardiac CT referrals also required assessment of the lungs, mediastinum, or broader thoracic pathologies. Accordingly, the vertical and lateral offsets measured in this study represent deviations from the isocentre under a thorax-centred rather than heart-centred positioning approach. All measurements were performed by automated centring analytics provided by Qaelum’s dose management tools (Fig. 1). Significant off-centring was defined a priori as a displacement of greater than 10 mm from the scanner isocentre in either the AP or LAT direction, based on previous literature demonstrating that deviations of ~10 mm or more are prevalent and clinically relevant [22,35].

Automated patient-centring analysis using Qaelum’s dose management tools, showing measured anteroposterior (AP; table height) and lateral (LAT) offsets between the patient’s position (orange) and the scanner isocentre (blue).

Radiation dose parameters, including CTDIvol and DLP, were extracted from the scanner’s embedded DICOM dose reports and cross-validated through Qaelum NV’s automated audit logs. This software provides a harmonised, vendor-neutral environment for evaluating scanner output and radiation dose distribution at the individual examination level. Demographic information, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), was obtained from the RIS and linked to the imaging dataset. All data were fully anonymised prior to analysis, and each case was assigned a unique study identifier to ensure patient confidentiality.

2.5. Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JASP version 0.19.2.0 (JASP Team, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate. Primary predictors were AP and LAT centring deviations, and outcomes were CTDIvol and DLP.

Correlations between off-centring magnitude and dose metrics were tested using Pearson or Spearman coefficients. Group comparisons were performed using t-tests, one-way ANOVA, or chi-square tests, as applicable. Multivariable linear regression was used to assess independent predictors of radiation dose, adjusting for sex, age, and BMI, with model assumptions verified. Regression coefficients are reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to facilitate interpretation of effect size and precision. An interaction between BMI and sex was also examined.

Intra-patient analyses assessed variability in positioning and dose among patients with repeated scans. A dose reduction simulation was performed to estimate potential savings achievable with ideal patient centring. The applied reduction factor was derived from previously published phantom and clinical studies and was not calculated from the regression analyses performed in the present study. This simulation therefore represents a literature-based scenario rather than an empirical causal estimate. Sensitivity analyses excluding outliers (>2 SD from the mean) confirmed robustness of results.

2.6. Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at KAMC, under IRB number 25–1422. As the study involved secondary analysis of de-identified clinical imaging data with no patient contact or intervention, a waiver of informed consent was granted. Data handling and analysis were conducted in accordance with institutional policies. All datasets were anonymised and stored in encrypted folders on secure institutional servers accessible only to the research team.

3. Results

3.1. Sample characteristics

A total of 399 adult patients who underwent CCTA were included in this analysis. The cohort consisted of 220 males (55%) and 179 females (45%), with a mean age of 53.8 years (standard deviation [SD]: 13.2 years; range: 22–102 years). The mean body mass index (BMI) across the sample was 29.7 kg/m2 (SD: 7.1; range: 13.3–65.1 kg/m2), indicating a predominance of overweight and obese individuals. All examinations were performed using standard institutional protocols for coronary artery evaluation, and all scans were acquired on the same multi-detector CT system (Table 1).

3.2. Patient positioning errors

Analysis of patient positioning revealed considerable variation in alignment with the scanner iso-centre. In this study, off-centring is defined as the deviation from the scanner isocentre under the thorax-centred landmarking protocol used at the study institution, and does not necessarily represent technologist positioning error. Because the local protocol centres on the mid-thorax rather than the heart, some degree of deviation from the scanner isocentre is an expected consequence of the protocol design rather than an indicator of suboptimal positioning performance. The mean (AP; table height/vertical) offset was −3.4 mm (SD: 33.9 mm), with values ranging from −90 mm (above iso-centre) to +100 mm (below iso-centre). Negative AP values denote positioning above the isocentre, and positive values denote positioning below; for LAT offsets, negative values indicate leftward displacement and positive values indicate rightward displacement. The mean LAT offset was +7.7 mm (SD: 17.3 mm), ranging from −37 mm to +66 mm (Fig. 2). Notably, the distribution of AP offsets was negatively skewed, suggesting that a substantial subset of patients were positioned above the scanner’s iso-centre in the AP (table height) direction. In contrast, LAT offsets were more frequently positive, reflecting a trend towards right-sided positioning errors (Fig. 3).

Significant off-centring, defined as a displacement greater than 10 mm from the scanner iso-centre, was highly prevalent across the cohort. In the AP direction, 83% of patients were displaced more than 10 mm from the isocentre, and 62% by more than 20 mm. Severe AP off-centring, defined as an absolute error greater than 30 mm, was observed in nearly 39% of the sample. In the LAT dimension, half of the cohort (50%) had a displacement exceeding 10 mm, and one in four (24%) were displaced by more than 20 mm. Extreme LAT misalignment exceeding 30 mm occurred in 11.5% of cases (Table 2).

3.3. Correlation between off-centring and radiation dose

The relationship between patient positioning errors and radiation dose metrics was systematically evaluated. No statistically significant correlation was identified between the magnitude of AP or LAT off-centring and either the CTDIvol or DLP. Both Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were close to zero for all combinations of offsets and dose parameters (all p-values >0.25). This indicates that, in this clinical dataset, the degree of off-centring alone did not predict radiation dose.

3.4. Patterns of off-centring by sex and BMI

Subgroup analysis highlighted notable differences in off-centring patterns by sex and BMI category. Male patients exhibited greater mean AP off-centring than females (30.0 mm ± 17.6 vs 26.5 mm ± 19.9, p = 0.042). Furthermore, males were more likely to experience significant (>20 mm) positioning errors, both in the AP direction (67.7% for males vs. 54.2% for females) and in the LAT direction (30.5% vs. 16.2%) (Table 3). This suggests a possible sex-related difference in patient setup or body habitus that warrants further investigation.

3.6. Simulation of potential dose reduction

To estimate the clinical impact of optimal patient positioning, a dose-reduction simulation was performed for all patients with significant off-centring (defined as an AP or LAT offset greater than 20 mm, comprising 269 individuals). Based on prior phantom and clinical work showing that correcting patient centring reduces CTDIvol by ~10–15% through improved bowtie filtration and tube current modulation, an illustrative 12% reduction factor (approximate midpoint of the reported range) was applied as a scenario simulation to estimate the hypothetical dose improvement with ideal centring [19,36]. For this group, mean CTDIvol was 25.0 ± 18.4 mGy and mean DLP was 1024 ± 810 mGy cm. The simulation predicted that, if all patients were centred based on the mid-thorax in both the AP and lateral planes, the mean CTDIvol across the entire cohort would decrease from 20.7 ± 16.9 mGy to 18.2 ± 14.6 mGy, and the mean DLP would decrease from 845 ± 720 mGy cm to 745 ± 610 mGy cm [36]. Under this illustrative scenario, the estimated cumulative reduction in total CTDIvol of nearly 1000 mGy, and in total DLP of approximately 40,000 mGy cm, corresponding to a projected relative dose reduction of 12% (Table 6). This illustrative scenario, using an approximate midpoint within the reported 10–15% range, provides a hypothetical estimate of the potential benefit achievable through precise manual or automated patient-centring systems; actual dose savings may vary depending on scanner model, protocol, and patient factors. These estimated reductions are not patient-specific and should not be extrapolated to individual dose savings; they serve only to illustrate the potential scale of benefit suggested by prior controlled studies.

3.7. Patient factors associated with radiation dose

A multivariable regression analysis was conducted to determine whether patient factors confounded the relationship between off-centring and radiation dose. BMI emerged as the strongest predictor, with each unit increase in BMI associated with a 1.0 mGy increase in CTDIvol (p < 0.001). Male sex (β = 3.10, p = 0.049) and advancing age (β = 0.14, p = 0.018) were also statistically significant predictors of increased radiation dose. In contrast, neither AP nor LAT off-centring was independently associated with CTDIvol after adjusting for BMI, sex, and age. The overall model explained 19% of the variance in CTDIvol (R2 = 0.19; adjusted R2 = 0.18), as detailed in Table 7 and illustrated in Fig. 4.

3.8. Quality improvement: outlier identification

For quality assurance purposes, the dataset was screened for cases with extreme off-centring (absolute AP or LAT offset ≥75 mm) or radiation doses above the 95th percentile for CTDIvol or DLP. Ten cases meeting these criteria were identified, including patients with AP offsets up to 100 mm and CTDIvol values up to 39.1 mGy (Table 8). These outliers are visually highlighted in Fig. 5 and warrant further review of the workflow, as they may reflect unique positioning challenges, difficulties with patient cooperation, or rare technical issues.

4. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive real-world assessment of patient positioning errors during cardiac CT and their impact on radiation dose in a large, contemporary cohort. Our findings reveal that significant off-centring remains highly prevalent in clinical practice, with over 80% of patients positioned more than 10 mm from the scanner isocentre. Severe vertical AP and LAT misalignments were frequent and, in some cases, extreme, echoing prior reports that patient offcentring persists despite advances in scanner technology and radiographer training. It is important to note that the local protocol used midthoracic landmarking rather than heart-centred positioning. Current SCCT guidelines recommend aligning the heart at the gantry isocentre to maximise the effectiveness of the bow-tie filter and improve dose efficiency [31]. As a result, the observed off-centring values in this study are partly systematic and protocol-driven, reflecting anatomical displacement of the heart relative to the scanner isocentre rather than inconsistent or erroneous technologist performance. Consequently, the high prevalence of off-centring reported should not be interpreted solely as suboptimal practice, but rather as a predictable outcome of a thorax-centred positioning strategy designed to ensure broader thoracic coverage. This distinction is essential to avoid misattributing protocol-defined positioning choices to operator error and to correctly contextualise centring performance within the clinical objectives of the scan.

While previous phantom and simulation studies consistently report a strong relationship between off-centring and dose increase [19,37], our clinical data did not demonstrate a direct, statistically significant correlation between the magnitude of off-centring and radiation dose metrics (CTDIvol, DLP). Several factors likely explain this finding. First, thorax-based landmarking introduces systematic heart mis-centring that is protocol-driven rather than due to technologist error; meaning measured offsets may not reflect true cardiac positioning [31]. Second, modern CT systems use advanced automatic tube current modulation and bow-tie filtration that can partially compensate for mild centring deviations, reducing their measurable impact on dose metrics such as CTDIvol [38]. Third, BMI-driven tube voltage and current selection exert a stronger influence on CTDIvol than centring alone, consistent with BMI emerging as the dominant predictor in our regression model [39]. Finally, real-world clinical variability in patient habitus, anatomy, and protocol factors may dilute the clearer dose–centring relationships observed in controlled phantom studies [40,41]. Although the regression analysis showed no independent association with radiation dose, scenario modelling based on existing literature suggests that accurately aligning patients (mid-thorax) to the scanner’s isocentre could hypothetically lead to a projected reduction in CTDIvol and DLP of about 12%. This estimate reflects a modelled scenario informed by prior studies and should not be interpreted as an empirically observed causal effect within this dataset [12,42].

Several recent investigations have demonstrated that automated and camera-based positioning systems significantly improve centring accuracy and reduce dose variability compared to manual positioning. Manava et al. found that 3D camera guidance improved isocentre accuracy and achieved measurable dose reduction in a large clinical cohort, reducing DLP and CTDIvol by 6–8% relative to laser-guided methods [29]. Saltybaeva et al. (2018) reported that automated 3D camera positioning reduced mean vertical offset from 19 ± 10 mm (manual) to 5 ± 3 mm (automatic), and consistently prevented severe offcentring [42] A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that automatic centring systems typically reduced vertical offsets to under 7mmand were associated with radiation dose reductions of 5–31% compared to manual positioning, although improvements in image quality were generally modest [12]. Specific to cardiac CT, recent phantom studies confirm that automatic 3D camera systems enhance centring precision, lower radiation dose, and improve image quality across a range of patient sizes and positioning scenarios [28].

The high prevalence of mis-centring observed in our cohort is consistent with global estimates. Aly et al. reported off-centring in 76% of body CT examinations, with larger patients being at greatest risk of misalignment, and a notable influence of operator experience and workflow factors [43]. Our subgroup analysis similarly identified greater off-centring in males and in patients with higher BMI, corroborating previous work indicating a need for targeted training and workflow optimisation in these groups.

Notably, although off-centring alone was not an independent predictor of increased CTDIvol in our multivariable model, higher BMI and male sex were significantly associated with greater radiation dose, reflecting well-documented challenges in optimising protocols for larger patients. The observed BMI–dose relationship aligns with prior literature highlighting the need for BMI-specific protocol adaptation to maintain consistent dose management [39].

A quality improvement analysis identified a small group of patients who exhibited significant miscentring or high radiation doses, underscoring the need for ongoing quality assurance. These cases may arise from clinical challenges such as limited mobility or emergency situations and warrant further examination. To enhance centring, implementing heart-centred positioning in accordance with SCCT guidelines [31] can help minimise miscentring. Structured feedback through dose-monitoring dashboards can improve staff awareness and education. Automatic patient-centring systems may further reduce positioning variability. Finally, integrating centring metrics into routine CT quality assurance programmes will support continuous workflow optimisation.

Our exploratory intra-patient analysis (n = 8) suggested reasonable within-patient consistency in positioning and dose across repeated scans, although the small sample size limits firm conclusions. These findings support continued integration of automated centring systems, regular staff education, and robust QA protocols to further reduce variability and outlier events. Given current international guidelines emphasising heart-centred positioning to maximise bow-tie filtration efficiency and dose utilisation, the findings support aligning local practice with SCCT standards or adopting automated positioning systems to reduce systematic mis-centring.

5. Limitations and recommendations

This study has several limitations. First, it is retrospective and single-centre in nature, which may limit generalisability to other clinical environments with different scanner models, radiographers’ experience, and patient populations. Second, radiographer positioning protocols within the study institution differed from contemporary SCCT recommendations, which advise centring the heart itself at the scanner isocentre to optimise beam filtration and image quality [31]. As such, the study did not include direct comparison with heart-centred positioning, which limits full evaluation of guideline-recommended centring benefits. Third, dose metrics (CTDIvol, DLP) are surrogate measures and may not fully reflect individual organ doses or effective dose, though they remain the standard in CT dose reporting. Off-centring can affect the dose delivered to radiosensitive organs, such as the heart, breast, and lungs. Therefore, the absence of an association with CTDIvol/DLP does not mean there is no clinical impact; in particular, it does not exclude changes in breast, heart, or lung dose distribution, nor potential image noise non-uniformity across the scan field. Future studies using organ-dose modelling, size-specific dose estimates, or Monte Carlo simulation may better capture centring-related dose effects and should be considered the proper endpoints for evaluating the dosimetric consequences of off-centring. Lastly, our analysis did not directly assess image quality or diagnostic outcomes; previous work suggests these can be variably affected by off-centring, and further research should integrate objective and subjective image quality assessments. Accordingly, although image quality implications are discussed, the present study cannot determine whether off-centring influenced diagnostic image quality or clinical interpretability.

6. Conclusion

Off-centring was highly prevalent in cardiac CT performed using mid-thorax landmarking and was associated with sex and BMI. Off-centring did not independently predict radiation dose in this cohort after adjustment for BMI, age, and sex. Scenario modelling based on published literature suggests that meaningful reductions in radiation dose may be achievable with ideal patient centring, although this remains to be confirmed in prospective studies. Given that current guidelines favour heart-centred positioning to maximise dose efficiency, optimising the positioning approach warrants further evaluation. Ongoing education, protocol optimisation, and integration of advanced positioning technologies should be prioritised to advance patient safety and imaging quality in CT practice.
