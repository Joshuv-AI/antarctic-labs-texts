Urban land use optimization prediction considering carbon neutral development goals: a case study of Taihu Bay Core area in China
Authors: Mingfang Tang, Yuejing Rong, Lifu Zheng, Yue Luo, Kai Li, Xin Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the increasing commitment of numerous nations to achieving future carbon neutrality, urban development planning that integrating carbon storage considerations plays a crucial role in enhancing urban carbon efficiency and promoting regional sustainable development. Previous studies have indicated that optimizing land use structure and quality is essential for regional carbon storage management. Taking the core area of Taihu Bay as study area, this study innovatively combined high-precision urban 3D data to account for the whole urban carbon pools of buildings, vegetation, soils, water. Then, multi-objective linear programming model and PLUS (Patch-generating Land Use Simulation) model were applied at patch scale to assess and compare carbon storage in various scenarios, considering both carbon storage maximization and urban development requirements.ResultsThe results were presented as follows. (1) Urban woodland carbon pool accounts for only a fraction of total carbon pool, and the role of soil and building carbon pools cannot be ignored. (2) Compared with the current situation, the carbon-growth optimized scenario will lead to the increase of total carbon storage by 38,568.31 tons. (3) Carbon-growth optimized scenario has reduced carbon storage in Woodland, Cropland, Village, Water compared to the Natural growth scenario, but has increased carbon storage in Garden plots, Street, Urban district, Town and other areas.ConclusionsTherefore, we find that for fast-growing cities, rationally planning built-up areas and woodland areas can achieve the twin goals of economic development and maximizing regional carbon storage. Furthermore, the implementation of new energy policies and projects such as green roofs can help to achieve regional carbon neutrality. The study provides new insights into the accounting of carbon pools within cities and the simulation of fine-grained land use planning based on the dual objectives of carbon stock maximization and urban development.

Background

In the past decades, terrestrial ecosystems have acted as huge carbon storage pools in mitigating global climate change [1, 2]. At the same time, the expansion of cities greatly influence the regional vegetation and soil carbon storage. Containing the most complex land use types and having most intense land use changes, cities are the critical areas to achieve carbon neutrality [3–5]. Though projects of upgrading high-emission industries and promoting energy-retrofit have been launched to reduce carbon emissions in urban areas [6, 7], it is difficult to raise the total carbon storage cap within cities. Therefore, there is a conflict between space for urban development and carbon storage. It is urgent to allocate the carbon storage ability of vegetation, soil and other carbon pools rationally to achieving goals of economic development and carbon storage increase through land use planning [8, 9].

Theoretically, vegetation has been usually considered a major carbon sink pool, while other urban land uses such as industrial, traffic, commercial and residential areas are contributors to carbon emission. However, there is a lack of theoretical research on the carbon storage effects of land use planning, especially within cities. Numerous researches have suggested that regional vegetation coverage should be expanded to increase carbon storage [10, 11]. This may be applicable to large-scale watershed, whereas it is too rough in cities. Due to the lack of precise land use data, existing research has not considered the significance of whole carbon pools (e.g., buildings, soil, vegetation, and water) [12, 13]. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the whole carbon pools within cities to maximize total urban carbon storage.

Furthermore, how to allocate future land use in space is of great significance. A rational future map is capable of guiding the land use expansion or shrinkage in accordance with the paths with the maximum social, economic and environmental effects [14]. Existing studies usually use Markov model, CLUE model, etc. for future land use monitoring [15, 16]. These models can effectively simulate future land use area and spatial distribution in large-scale areas. However, they lack the ability to simulate spatial and temporal land use changes at the patch scale, and cannot simulate detailed land use changes in the inner space of cities [17]. Recent research has proposed a useful patch-generating land use simulation (PLUS) model to predict spatial distribution of land use in the future [18]. Compared with existing prediction models, the PLUS model uses random forest algorithm to calculate the respective land use type’s development potential more precisely and applies multi-type random patch seeds (CARS) to model multiple land-use types changing at a fine-scale resolution. The simulation result of PLUS model can be verified using the confusion matrix and the kappa coefficient [19].

In this study, a newly established Economic Development Zone, Taihu Bay Core Area (TCA) is selected as the research area. The total carbon accumulation of the TCA in 2020 is analyzed to set the carbon accumulation maximization objective function under the background of the TCA’s 2035 land planning. The area of various land uses in 2035 is calculated using the linear programming method, and the spatial distribution is predicted using the PLUS model. Compared with existing research, this study refines the theoretical analysis framework for the carbon storage pools within cities and proposes an innovative way to balance social development and carbon neutrality in small-scale cities based on integrated carbon storage ability analysis. The aim of this study is to solve two vital questions as follows: (1) What are the ecosystems that enable carbon storage enhancement in rapidly growing cities? (2) How to achieve the dual goals of economic development and maximum carbon storage through spatial land use planning in TCA?

Methods

Research design

First, the study analyzes the carbon storage of vegetation, buildings, soil and water bodies in TCA in 2020 and calculates the carbon storage coefficients of different types of land use in combination with high spatial resolution land use data. Subsequently, the study used Markov chains combined with 2015 and 2020 land use data to predict the land use area for the natural growth scenario in 2035, while a multi-objective linear planning model was developed to calculate the land use area for the carbon-growth optimized scenario, based on the objectives of maximizing carbon storage and the needs of urban development in TCA. Finally, the study uses the PLUS model to simulate and compare spatial land use in the TCA in 2035 under the natural growth scenario and the carbon optimization scenario. Research framework and technical roadmap

Study area

Taihu Bay Core Area (TCA), adjacent to Taihu Lake, is located in the core area of Taihu New Town and in the southeast of Wuxi city. The core area of Taihu Bay is one of the most rapidly developing areas in the Taihu Lake Basin in the Yangtze River Delta of China. In 2020, the total area of TCA is 56.60 km2, of which urban district and town account for the largest, 40.32% and the area of streets accounts for 15.76%. Water accounts for 10.28% and cropland accounts for 10.18%. Woodland and garden plots account for the least area with 7.39% and 5.53% respectively. Spatially, urban district and town are concentrated in the north, cropland is mainly in the southwest, and water bodies are distributed in the northwest and southeast.

TCA is positioned as a pioneer area for ecological protection, scientific and technological innovation, modern industries and high-end talents in the context of the readjustment of the TCA’s management system and Wuxi city’s development plan. The TCA faces the trade-off between land uses for social development and carbon storage enhancement due to the limitation of land space (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Geographical location of study area

Data source

In this study, precise 2015 and 2020 land use vector data are acquired from the Wuxi Municipal Natural Resources and Planning Bureau. They are converted into 5 m resolution raster land use data in ArcGIS10.2. Soil and water carbon pools are calculated based on raster land use data. Vegetation carbon pool is calculated using 2020 Annual Net Primary Production (NPP) data, MOD17A3HGF version 6, originating from EARTHDATA (https://earthdata.nasa.gov) combined with land use data [20]. Vector building data in 2020 with height information originates from Baidu Map [21] (https://lbsyun.baidu.com) and is converted to the raster data with 5 m resolution. Then, building carbon pool is calculated based on building raster data.

Annual rainfall and temperature raster data with 1 km spatial resolution from 2015 to 2020, 2020 GDP data and population per square kilometer can be acquired from Resource and Environment Science and Data Center, Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, CAS [22] (https://www.resdc.cn/). 30 m GDEMV3 DEM data are acquired from Geospatial Data Cloud (https://www.gscloud.cn/search). Besides, trunk, railway, primary road, secondary road, tertiary road, and motorway data in 2020 originate from Open Street Map [23] (https://www.openstreetmap.org/#map=13/31.4859/120.3042). Government POIs in 2020 are originated from Baidu Map [21] (https://lbsyun.baidu.com). All the data mentioned above are used in PLUS model to calculating the driving factors of land uses.

When calculating carbon pools and predicting future land use space, all the grid data used are reclassified to 5 m, consistent with the resolution of the land use data. Such spatial resolution is compatible for the area of the study area.

Methodology

Urban carbon storage calculation model

The vegetation carbon pool, the soil carbon pool, the building carbon pool, and the water carbon pool are calculated in this study to obtain carbon accumulation ability, the comprehensive carbon storage coefficient of the respective land use type in the TCA in 2020. For vegetation carbon pool, although tree species own different carbon storage capacity, this paper only considers the carbon storage discrepancy from land use perspectives, and ignore the vegetation difference within the same land use type. Due to the small regional area and relatively homogeneous geographical conditions, the original soil quality is almost same in the TCA and soil’s carbon storage capacity is mainly affected by land uses. We also consider that buildings in TCA have the same carbon storage capacity because building materials in urban regions are similar. Other carbon pools (e.g., the garbage carbon pool) are not considered due to data accessibility.

Markov chain model

Two types of land use growth scenarios are compared in this study. One is Carbon-growth optimized scenario, another one is natural growth scenario. Carbon-growth optimized scenario considers both TCA’s 2035 planning requirement and the largest urban carbon storage. The natural growth scenario considers the land use in 2035 under the trend of land use change from 2015 to 2020. It represents the future land use scenario based on the current change trend without making regional development plans and maximizing carbon storage. Land use data of the TCA in 2015, 2020 are inputted in Markov chain model to simulate land use area of natural growth scenario in 2035.

Multi-objective linear programming model

Compared with natural growth scenario, carbon-growth optimized scenario plans land use change under social development and carbon neutrality goals. Different expected area requirements for each land use are set based on the territorial spatial planning of the Wuxi Economic and Development Zone (2021–2035), and the optimal areas of different land uses in 2035 are calculated using a multi-objective linear planning model with the objective of maximizing carbon storage.

Transforms of land use will lead to changes in the carbon pools of the corresponding land ecosystem vegetation, soils and surface buildings, and water bodies, thus affecting the carbon storage capacity of TCA. Land use spatial prediction model (PLUS model)

The PLUS model is a patch-based model for simulating land use, and by applying new analytical strategies, it can better explore the inducement of various types of land use changes. At the same time, the PLUS model contains a new multi-accumulation seed growth mechanism, which can better simulate changes in multiple types of land use at the patch level. Coupled with a multi-objective optimization algorithm, the results of the PLUS model can better support planning policies to achieve sustainable development [34–36]. Therefore, PLUS is suitable for accurate simulation of future land use within cities.

To simulate land use space, the PLUS model first analyzes land uses’ expanding potentials. The random forest algorithm is adopted to mine the driving factors and obtain the development probability of various land uses one by one by extracting various land use changes between two periods and sampling from the increased part:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{i,k}^{d}(x)=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{M}I({h}_{n}\left(x\right)=d)}{M}$$\end{document}Pi,kd(x)=∑n=1MI(hnx=d)Mwhere \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{i,k}^{d}(x)$$\end{document}Pi,kd(x) is the growth probability of land use type \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document}k at cell \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document}i. Based on analyzed driving factors and the constraint of development probability for different land use types, the PLUS model then uses random seeds production and threshold decreasing mechanism dynamically simulate the automatic generation of land use patches in time and space.

In this study, the PLUS model is applied to predict land use spaces of the carbon-growth optimized scenario and natural growth scenario in 2035. Gonghu Bay Wetland Park and Dongli Lake water area, permanent basic farmland, and urban district are set as the immutable restricted areas. Distance from government POI, railway, motorway, trunk, primary road, secondary road and tertiary road, DEM, slope, average annual rainfall, and temperature from 2015 to 2020, 1 km2 GDP and population data in 2020 serve as the potential driving factors of land use change in the TCA. Combined with the 5 m resolution 2015–2020 land expansion grid data processed in ArcGIS, the potential growth probability and driving factors of various land types are analyzed [37, 38]. Subsequently, based on the land use of the carbon-growth optimized scenario, the future land use distribution is predicted. Lastly, paper uses the land use data in 2015 to simulate the spatial distribution of land of the TCA in 2020 and through the confusion matrix, difference between the simulated land use and the actual land use is analyzed to verify the simulation accuracy of PLUS model in the TCA.

Spatial prediction results of two growth scenarios

As revealed by the simulation results of the carbon-growth optimized scenario, urban district, town accounts for the 43.41% of the TCA in 2035, and it is continuously distributed, which is the main land type of the TCA. Woodland accounts for 15.60% of the TCA, which is scattered in the area and continuously distributed close to Jinkui Park, Shangxianhe Wetland Park, Shangxianhe Rose Garden, Gonghuwan Wetland Park, Jinchengwan Park, Huaqing Avenue and Zhouxin East Road in the northeast. Gardens, cropland, and villages account for 14.38% of the TCA, primarily distributed in the south and northeast. The spatial distribution of the three types of land shows a close relationship. Mining, scenic spots, and special land are dotted and distributed in the north of the TCA. Water surface accounts for 10.28% of the TCA, which is interconnected to form a dense water network.

Discussions

A theoretical framework for urban land use and carbon storage potential is developed

Existing researches on urban carbon storage have usually focused on the carbon storage of urban vegetation [39, 40]. For example, study in Nador City estimates the role of urban greening in carbon storage enhancement and prove that extensive green roof projects will improve the total carbon storage by 45.36% [41]. However, they ignore the carbon storage of vegetation in built-up areas, sides of roads, gardens, as well as other main carbon pools due to the low accuracy of land use data. Since TCA is characterized by its small area, clear development orientation, rapid urbanization, and industrial renewal, it is necessary to formulate the future land optimization plan to maximize the regional carbon storage [42, 43]. Therefore, thorough consideration to main urban carbon pools is needed to develop the theoretical framework for urban land use and carbon storage potential.

In this study, the four parts of vegetation carbon pool, soil carbon pool, building carbon pool and water carbon pool in Wuxi TCA are analyzed based on the high-precision land use data and 3D buildings. Then, in the process of calculating the comprehensive carbon storage coefficient of land uses, the accounting of the carbon density of vegetation, soil and buildings refers to the average value of several literatures whose study area is close to the environmental conditions of TCA. Compared with previous studies, this paper constructs the theoretical relationship between urban land uses and carbon storage potential. Results suggest that the comprehensive carbon storage capacity of different land uses changes significantly, and the carbon storage capacity of vegetation accounts for only a part of the comprehensive carbon storage capacity [44]. Accordingly, the effect of changes in all carbon pools should be comprehensively considered for formulating the goal of maximizing regional carbon storage from the land use planning perspective.

Explore visualizing land use space under carbon neutrality-social development goals

Previous urban development scenario studies usually ignore the goal of social development while paying attention to the goal of maximizing carbon storage. Land use scenarios formulated in this orientation are not related to the needs of actual social development and even runs counter to the development direction [45]. Moreover, existing research has lacked effective methods in the spatial visualization of land use optimization, so the scenario takes on a poor significance in guiding the future urban renewal. In order to solve these problems, it is necessary to combine a multi-objective linear planning model and a land use prediction model to spatially simulate land use in urban areas based on carbon targets and urban development goals. These models hold significant value for land use planning in other cities globally, especially in achieving carbon neutrality targets.

In this study, a linear optimization model with dual objectives of carbon storage maximization and social development is built under the demand background of land planning and social development of the TCA in 2035. The spatial distribution of land use in the future is predicted in accordance with the optimization results coupled with the PLUS model. The value ranges of the woodland and cropland areas in the linear optimization model are mainly derived from the Wuxi Economic and Development Zone (2021–2035). At the same time, the value range of the area of urban district, town, street, garden plots and other land uses are set with reference to the development speed of the same type of cities. In general, the value range of parameters is reasonable to meet the development needs of TCA. Compared with existing research, the PLUS model is capable of better excavating the incentives of various land use changes and simulating the changes of multiple land use patches. The simulation results will more effectively support the planning policies to achieve city’s sustainable development for achieving the goal of maximizing carbon storage on the premise of meeting the needs of social development. Furthermore, spatial visualization significantly enhances the enforceability of the carbon-growth optimized scenario.

4.3 Proper land use planning combined with government management can achieve the twin goals of urban economic development and carbon storage neutrality

Eco-first scenarios that maximize the area of vegetation are usually the most favored in existing studies. The case of Chongqing shows that among natural development, high urbanization, farmland protection and eco-development scenarios, only the eco-development scenario improves the future regional carbon storage capacity [46]. Similarly, the case of the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei urban agglomeration demonstrates that the scenario that prioritizes woodlands and grasslands minimizes the reduction in regional carbon storage capacity [47]. However, the eco-first model does not mean that there is no cost. For cities, the built-up area and the rate of economic development will be limited. At the same time, excessive forested land can also increase regional water consumption, leading to the potential problem of ecosystem service trade-offs [48]. This study demonstrates that within cities, biggest woodland areas does not guarantee the maximum carbon storage and the expansion of towns and woodland areas can be rationally planned to satisfy economic development needs while maximizing future carbon storage capacity. On this basis, city managers can implement policies to reduce the carbon emission intensity of cities and towns through energy saving, emission reduction, and the application of new energy technologies. At the same time, they can improve green infrastructure, such as enriching the vegetation types of urban green spaces, strengthening the planting of good tree species with high carbon sequestration capacity, and implementing green space projects, green roofs, green buildings and other projects to help achieving the regional carbon neutrality goal.

Outlook and limitation

Future scenarios for carbon emissions are not precise enough since it is difficult to accurately predict the effect of the development of energy-saving technologies and the promotion of energy-saving measures on land carbon emissions, carbon sinks in the future [49, 50]. Thus, the optimization scenario of carbon emissions minimization, carbon sink maximization and land use area change from the perspective of industrial emission inventory is not analyzed in this study. In the future, novel multi-objective land optimization schemes should be investigated to simultaneously minimize industrial carbon emissions and maximize ecosystem carbon storage within cities. Furthermore, the social development objectives of the TCA lack the support of effective socio-economic quantitative indicators, which only refers to the social development plan of the TCA and the regular urban development pattern of other cities to set limitation for land use area. In the future, we will further quantify the socio-economic indicators and the goal of carbon neutrality for more sophisticate multi-objectives analysis.

Conclusion

Urban land-use planning is crucial for regional economic development and carbon storage capacity. Existing studies have focused more on the carbon storage role of woodland in large-scale regions, while neglecting the carbon sequestration potential of various types of land use and buildings within cities. Taking TCA as study area, this paper analyzes the future carbon storage capacity and land use under the natural growth scenario and carbon-growth optimized scenario using fine-grained land use data combined with the PLUS and multi-objective linear programming model. The results show that: (1) The important components involved in the TCA carbon pool and the land use layout for carbon storage maximization were clarified, and it was found that the soil carbon pool accounted for 90.30% of the total carbon pool in the TCA, while the woodland carbon pool accounted for only 6.72% and the building carbon pool accounted for 2.93%. This provides a planning direction for the optimal allocation of land use under the goal of urban carbon neutrality in the future; (2) The trend of carbon storage changes under different development scenarios was verified, and compared with the natural growth scenario, the carbon-growth optimized scenario considers the dual objectives of carbon storage increase and built-up area development, resulting in the predicted results of the optimal allocation of land use types, and we found that reasonable built-up area size, forest land and cropland area planning can achieve economic development while taking into account the goal of carbon–neutral development; (3) Furthermore, it is recommended that city managers introduce energy-saving and emission reduction policies, encourage the application of new energy technologies to further reduce the carbon emission intensity of cities and towns, and at the same time enhance the carbon sequestration capacity of sites through the layout of green infrastructure such as green roofs, so as to help achieve the goal of regional carbon neutrality.

This study provides a practical urban land use modelling case considering both economic development needs and carbon storage objectives for emerging green cities around the world. Compared with previous studies, the research develops a theoretical framework for urban land use and carbon storage potential. Results testify that urban planning can balance the twin goals of economic development and carbon storage maximization and highlights the crucial role of greening strategies in land use planning towards achieving carbon neutrality, particularly emphasizing the carbon storage growth potential of soils and buildings. Overall, this study builds on previous research to offer new perspectives and methods for integrating land use planning with carbon–neutral policies, providing valuable insights for the sustainable development of future cities.
