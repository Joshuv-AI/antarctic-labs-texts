Exploring the relationship between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care among nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Majid Tavakol, Nahid Tavakol, Shohreh Javadpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompassionate care is a fundamental ethical component of nursing that significantly influences patient outcomes, satisfaction, and healthcare provider well-being. Spiritual sensitivity, reflecting a nurse’s ability to recognize and respond empathetically to patients’ spiritual needs, is increasingly recognized as a key driver of compassionate care. Despite its importance, research on the interplay between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care, particularly within non-Western cultural contexts, remains limited.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care among nurses and to examine the influence of demographic variables on these constructs.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted from March to May 2024 among 132 nurses employed in three hospitals affiliated with Jahrom University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Validated Persian versions of the Spiritual Sensitivity Questionnaire and Compassionate Care Questionnaire were used for data collection. Statistical analyses included Independent T-tests, One-Way ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and regression analyses.ResultsFindings revealed that nurses displayed moderate levels of spiritual sensitivity and good levels of compassionate care. A significant moderate positive correlation was found between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care (r = 0.453, p < 0.001), with both professional and internal spiritual sensitivity dimensions significantly associated with compassionate care. Demographic factors such as gender, education level, and marital status were not significantly related to either construct. However, compassionate care increased significantly with age and work experience. Variations in spiritual sensitivity were observed across different hospital wards.ConclusionsThe study highlights the pivotal role of spiritual sensitivity in augmenting compassionate care among nurses. Targeted educational and organizational interventions to strengthen spiritual sensitivity may improve the quality of compassionate nursing care and patient outcomes. Healthcare policymakers and managers should foster supportive, spiritually nurturing work environments to enhance nurse resilience and reduce burnout, ultimately benefiting healthcare delivery.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

In the current technology-driven era, where intelligent systems and evidence-based care are redefining healthcare processes, a fundamental question arises: Can machines exhibit compassion in the same way humans do? The answer to this question highlights the increasing importance of compassionate care in nursing. Compassionate care is not only a moral nursing base but also a fundamental component in improving patient outcomes, patient satisfaction, and reducing job-related stress for healthcare professionals [1]. The International Council of Nurses (ICN), on the other hand, considers it a basic skill essential for all nurses [2]. Compassionate care, stemming from common nursing practice, is that nursing practice which nurtures a humane, moral relationship with patient care, providing the nurse with the insight to “see, understand, and act to alleviate patient suffering” [3]. Although it has been considered fundamental, providing compassionate care in a real-world healthcare setup, with high patient volume, a dominant need for technology, and a shortage of healthcare workforce, is a huge challenge [4]. The reported instance of diminished compassionate practice leads to a significant question regarding what factors might retain such a paramount skill in a challenging situation.

The theoretical basis of this research is based on Gardner’s conceptualization of spiritual intelligence as a separate dimension of human intelligence to logical, emotional and inter personal intelligences [5]. This conceptualization of spiritual intelligence as a measurable ability for addressing existential concerns and finding meaning and transcending physical adversity is indispensable to providing compassionate care. In this model of care, Spiritual Sensitivity is the perceptual aspect of spiritual intelligence and Compassionate Care represents the practical response to those perceived needs. Consequently, we posit a direct connection in that an increased level of Spiritual Intelligence results in greater Spiritual Sensitivity and thus higher quality Compassionate Care [6, 7].

In light of this consideration, researchers have assessed the antecedents that affect the quality of care. The current literature indicates that caring practices are the result of a rich blend of personal, ethical, and spiritual components [8, 9]. An essential component that has been identified in contemporary research is the level of spirituality exhibited by the nursing professional [10]. The importance of spiritual sensitivity, which is more than religiosity, is considered a way to assess the capacity to identify and empathetically respond to patients’ spirituality [11, 12]. Previous research has proven that nurses with high spiritual well-being are best capable of providing complete care, in addition to effectively handling job-related stresses [13]. For instance, research carried out by Sadeghian et al. [14]supports that spirituality helps nursing professionals overcome ethics-related challenges, apart from delivering personalized patient care [15].

Apart from spiritual considerations, the demographics of nurses (age, gender, educational level, work experience, etc.) might affect the potential for compassionate nursing practice [16]. In a literature scan of previous research, though, it has been found that there are sometimes inconclusive findings on this topic. For instance, certain studies support that increased age and work experience are linked to increased compassion, while others find that there is no significant relationship, even a negative relationship, for senior nursing staff experiencing burnout [17, 18]. Most previous research, further, has been completed in a Western setting [2]. The presence of spirituality, because of its close association with cultural and religious norms, means that the lack of research in the geographical area of IRAN represents a significant research gap.

The necesesity of this study is based on the identified gaps. The relationship between dimensions of demographical, spiritual, and nursing compassion, specifically within the cultural setting, has not been extensively investigated. Therefore, this research intends to fill the identified gaps by assessing the factors that predict compassionate nursing care with a special focus on the relationship between spiritual sensitivity and demographics caracteriction. The findings of this research may provide a framework for designing targeted interventions to enhance nurse resilience and improve care quality.

Methods

Study design

In this research, a cross-sectional descriptive design was used. This design is most appropriate for providing a snapshot of the extent or characteristics of variables (spiritual intelligence, spiritual sensitivity, and compassionate care) in a particular population at one point in time [19, 20]. A self-administered questionnaire was distributed between March and May 2024.

Setting and sample size

The study was conducted in three hospitals affiliated with Jahrom University of Medical Sciences in Fars Province, southern Iran. The total nursing population across these hospitals was approximately 700, and 132 nurses participated in the study. The required sample size was calculated by a statistical consultant using the formula for estimating correlation coefficients. Drawing upon findings from Tehranineshat et al. [21], a minimum expected correlation coefficient (r) of 0.3 was considered. Given an αlevel of 0.05, a two-sided test, and a desired statistical power of 90% (1-β = 0.9), the minimum required sample size was determined to be 120 participants. Accounting for a 10% potential attrition, the final sample size was set at 132, and all participants completed the questionnaires.

Participants

The participants were educational hospital nurses. Demographic characteristics, such as age, gender, education level, marital status, ward type, employment status and work experiences (years) were recorded. These variables were introduced to describe the sample, assess its representativeness and explore their possible relationship with spiritual intelligence, spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care considering that they have been found in previous studies to play a mediating or confounding role in nursing research on spirituality and compassion [17, 18]. This also increases clarity on how participant recruitment relates to the focus of the study with practicing nurses working in direct patient care.

Recruitment and sampling procedure

Following ethical approval from Jahrom University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee, a Stratified Simple Random Sampling method with proportional allocation was employed to select participants from the target population.

The study setting included three affiliated hospitals under the university’s management. The total nursing population of approximately 700 served as the sampling frame.

The sampling procedure involved the following steps: 1- Stratification: The total population was divided into three non-overlapping strata based on their affiliated hospital to ensure representativeness from each site. 2- Proportional Allocation: The required sample size of n = 132 was proportionally allocated across the three hospitals based on the size of the nursing population in each hospital (ensuring the sample reflected the actual distribution of nurses). 3- Selection: A comprehensive, numbered list of all eligible nurses in each stratum (hospital) was obtained from the Human Resources Department. Participants were then randomly selected from these lists using a random number table until the quota for each stratum was fulfilled.

Inclusion criteria were having at least a bachelor’s degree in nursing, a minimum of six months of work experience, and no history of psychiatric disorders or medication use.

The selected participants were contacted privately, informed about the study objectives and confidentiality guarantees, and invited to participate (the initial ‘public call’ was an invitation to the already selected individuals). Written informed consent was obtained, and the completed questionnaires were collected within one week.

Survey tools

Spiritual sensitivity questionnaire

This scale developed and validated specifically for Iranian nurses by Omolbanin Akbari, Mahlagha Dehghan, and Batool Tirgari (2023) in Iran [8]. This 20-item scale is developed and tested based on the eight stages suggested by DeVellis (2016). The concept of spiritual sensitivity was generated using a process of qualitative content analysis (carried out on 22 nursing interviews) and successive refinement through literature reviews, which provided an initial list of 71 items. A 5-point Likert scale was utilized (0 = Never to 4 = Always). There was a positive CVR, and high I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave values, which ensured the content validity, and there was also a high level of face validity with a positive quantitative and qualitative index of item impact, which was greater than 1.5. In the pilot study conducted among 50 nurses and utilizing item analysis, 7 items were deleted, and the remaining items were 41 (Cronbach alpha = 0.929). Using a main survey of 412 nurses, two factors were discovered through exploratory factor analysis: professional spiritual sensitivity (12 items) and internal spiritual sensitivity (8 items), explaining 57.62% variance. Convergent validity was confirmed using the King’s Spiritual Intelligence Scale with a value of r = 0.66, while the internal consistency and reliability measures were very high with a Cronbach alpha value of 0.927 and omega value of 0.923 This 20-item scale ( scores range: 0–80, with higher scores indicative of high sensitivity) has acceptable psychometric properties suitable for measuring the spiritual sensitivity among nurses.

Compassionate care questionnaire

This 28-item scale was developed as an indigenous tool for Iranian nurses by Banafsheh Tehranineshat, Mahnaz Rakhshan, Camellia Torabizadeh, Mohammad Fararouei, and Mark Gillespie (2021) [21]. Its development was a two-phase (qualitative and quantitative) methodological study. The qualitative phase used conventional content analysis through interviews with Iranian nurses, patients, and family caregivers to define the concept. The quantitative phase confirmed its validity through face validity, content validity (CVR and CVI), and construct validity (Exploratory and Confirmatory Factor Analysis on a sample of 420 nurses). Reliability was confirmed through internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89) and stability (ICC = 0.94). It has four subscales: professional performance, continuous follow-up, patient-centered performance, and empathetic communication. Items use a five-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 28 to 140, categorizing levels as poor (28–65), moderate (66–103), and good (104–140)20. In the current study, the internal consistency was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85.

Validity and reliability in the current study

Prior to data collection, a mini-evaluation was performed: Face Validity of both scales was checked by 10 nurses in the study setting to ensure the clarity and comprehensibility of the items within the local context. Internal consistency (Reliability) was then calculated on the current study sample, yielding excellent Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for both the Spiritual Sensitivity Scale and the Compassionate Care Questionnaire, confirming their reliability for use in this specific population.

Statistical tests

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze demographic variables. Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA compared mean scores across demographic groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient and linear regression analysis assessed relationships between study variables.

Ethics

Prior to the study, ethics approval was obtained from Jahrom University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (code IR.JUMS.REC.1402.123). Participants provided informed consent after being informed about the study’s purpose and confidentiality assurances.

Results

A total of 132 nurses participated, including 41 (31.1%) males and 92 (68.9%) females, with a mean age of 30.98 ± 5.46 years. Demographic distributions and mean comparisons for spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care are presented in Tables 1 and 2.

Independent t-tests revealed no significant association between gender, education level, or marital status and spiritual sensitivity or compassionate care (Table 1).

One-way ANOVA showed a significant difference in compassionate care among different age groups (20–29, 30–39, 40–49 years) (p = 0.010), but no significant difference in spiritual sensitivity across age groups (p = 0.314) (Table 2).

Significant differences were found between hospital wards concerning spiritual sensitivity (p = 0.024), but not in compassionate care (p = 0.077). Employment status showed no significant effect on either variable (p > 0.5).

Descriptive analysis indicated a mean spiritual sensitivity score of 53.73 ± 16.80, representing moderate sensitivity. The professional and internal subdimensions had means of 32.00 ± 10.46 and 21.73 ± 7.33, respectively (Table 3).

Mean compassionate care score was 111.49 ± 22.99, indicating a good level. Approximately 4.5% (n = 6) were at a poor level, 28% (n = 37) moderate, and 67.4% (n = 89) good (Table 3).

Subdimension means for compassionate care were: professional performance 59.8 ± 37.24, continuous follow-up 23.85 ± 5.51, guardianship 27.35 ± 5.33, and empathetic communication 23.36 ± 5.32 (Table 3).

Pearson correlation analysis showed a significant moderate positive correlation between total spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care (r = 0.453, p < 0.001). Professional spiritual sensitivity correlated strongly with compassionate care (r = 0.445, p < 0.001), as did internal spiritual sensitivity (r = 0.404, p < 0.001). Subscales within compassionate care and spiritual sensitivity showed high internal consistency with correlations ranging from 0.892 to 0.961, supporting good scale reliability (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the relationship between spiritual sensitivity (SS) and compassionate care (CC) among nurses affiliated with Jahrom University of Medical Sciences. Grounded in theoretical frameworks related to spiritual care and compassion in nursing, spiritual sensitivity is recognized as a key factor enhancing compassionate care, closely linked to the humanistic and spiritual dimensions of patient care.

The findings showed that the average compassionate care score was at a good level, with more than two-thirds of participants reporting high levels, indicating a strong commitment among nurses to their patients. Correlation analysis revealed a significant moderate positive relationship between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care, with both dimensions of spiritual sensitivity (professional and internal) showing strong positive correlations with compassionate care. These results align with recent nursing research confirming the association between compassionate performance and spiritual awareness. For example, a study on nursing students in Turkey found that higher compassionate abilities correlated with greater spiritual perception and spiritual care [17]. According to this finding, Altundal Duru et al. [22] stated that promoting nurses’ resilience and capacity for compassion may positively impact enhancing their spirituality and, in turn, offering spiritual care. Furthermore, a review by Ausar et al. (2021) [23] confirmed that Nurses with higher spiritual intelligence are more likely to provide comprehensive, high-quality, and compassionate care. Spiritual intelligence acts as a key factor in increasing nurses’ empathy, compassion, moral sensitivity, and professional ethics. Similarly, Polat & Özdemir (2022) [24] demonstrated that Increasing the level of nurses’ compassion was associated with an increased frequency of spiritual care therapeutic provided for patients. This suggests that spiritual sensitivity can serve as an important driver for improving the quality of compassionate care. Similarly, Hassan Doost et al. [25] reported that spiritual sensitivity encourages nurses to adopt a more holistic and humane approach to patients, emphasizing their psychological and spiritual needs, ultimately improving compassionate care. Also Rakhshan et al. (2024) [4] revealed that threre was a significant, relevant relationship between spiritual health, resiliency, and the provision of compassionate care by nurses. The importance of these findings lies in highlighting the role of spiritual skills education and support for nurses to enhance care quality.

The essential argument in this study is that Spiritual Sensitivity is far more than a correlate of Compassionate Care: it is a cognitive and moral precursor, an excellent resource base for it. While researchers point stressed out and burn out issues hindering care, our findings indicate that spiritual sensitivity is more of an empowering resource that promotes the delivery of compassionate care. Specifically, the “internal spiritual sensitivity” dimension allows nurses to more effectively cope with difficult situations by allowing enough resilience and self awareness to remain compassionate despite difficult, trying conditions. This is consistent with Koenig’s framework (2021) [26], which sposits that a strong spiritual foundation mitigates the moral injury associated with caring for suffering patients, enabling sustainable compassionate action. In other words, the spiritual sensitivity of nurses is key to improving the state of compassionate care without necessarily looking for external motivations to enhance their practice.

The positive correlation between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care observed in this study signifies that educational and clinical interventions aimed at strengthening nurses’ spiritual dimensions can substantially improve the quality of compassionate care and overall patient experience. Recent studies, such as Ünlügedik et al. (2023) [27], have confirmed the impact of spiritual health on reducing burnout and increasing compassionate care among intensive care nurses.

In examining demographic variables, this study found no significant effects of gender, education level, or marital status on spiritual sensitivity or compassionate care. These results are consistent with prior studies, such as that by Borhani et al. [28], which indicated that gender and education level do not significantly affect nurses’ spiritual care attitudes or practices. However, Kondaguli et al. (2023) [29] reported that female nurses often demonstrate higher levels of compassion, potentially linked to socialization patterns and caregiving role expectations. This underscores that spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care in nurses are complex phenomena influenced by psychological factors, individual beliefs, and work environments, and cannot solely be explained by demographic variables.

This study also revealed that compassionate care significantly increases with nurses’ age, while spiritual sensitivity showed no significant differences across age groups. These findings are consistent with Hassan Doost et al. [25], who observed that professional experience and maturity correlate with enhanced emotional and compassionate care, whereas spiritual sensitivity remains a more stable dimension less dependent on age. The increase in compassionate care with age and experience may be due to repeated practice, deeper understanding of patients’ conditions, and the development of empathy and active participation in care, as supported by other research showing that psychological and cognitive maturity enhances compassionate care abilities [30].

On the other hand, significant differences in spiritual sensitivity across hospital wards were observed, indicating that work environment and task nature in different departments (emergency, intensive care, etc.) influence nurses’ spiritual sensitivity. Purnamasari et al. [31] reported that nurses working in critical care settings during the COVID-19 pandemic showed very high spirituality levels (85.5%), which correlated with lower burnout levels, suggesting that spiritual sensitivity may enhance resilience in stressful environments. Conversely, the finding that no significant differences in compassionate care levels were found across wards, indicating that compassionate care is largely maintained across departments through nursing education and organizational culture. Employment status had no significant effect on spiritual sensitivity or compassionate care, reflecting uniformity in educational and managerial processes across the studied healthcare centers.

Limitations and future directions

The implications of this study’s results must be considered with respect to certain limitations of this study. First, because this study has a cross-sectional design, it is not possible to test causal hypotheses about relationships between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care. Even though there are certain theories that explain how spiritual sensitivity leads to compassionate care, it would be important to conduct a longitudinal study to test this particular hypothesis.

Second, quantitative data are gathered through a self-report questionnaire for both independent and dependent variables. In doing so, it is likely prone to common method bias and social desirability bias where respondents tend to rate their compassionate traits higher because of their professionalism or moral standards.

Third, the investigation only targeted teaching hospitals that were affiliated with the Jahrom University of Medical Sciences. This means that the outcome of the study is specifically influenced by the culture of the aforementioned region within Iran. Therefore, findings from the study may not be generalizable to a setting where nurses are employed by private hospitals. Furthermore, the study failed to control its findings by excluding potential confounding factors. These factors may have included the immediate state of the nurses’ psychological condition.

Overall, these findings confirm that increasing nurses’ spiritual sensitivity leads to greater compassionate care. Therefore, it is recommended that educational and healthcare centers plan and implement spirituality and compassion training programs to promote these important indicators.

Innovative and practical aspects

This study offers an innovative contribution by focusing specifically on the relationship between spiritual sensitivity and compassionate care among nurses. Unlike many previous studies that predominantly examined general nursing attitudes and performances, this research uniquely identifies spiritual sensitivity as a measurable and improvable driver of compassionate care.

Practically, the findings lay the groundwork for designing and implementing targeted educational programs aimed at enhancing nurses’ spiritual and emotional skills. Such interventions might include meditation exercises, spiritual emotional intelligence training, and the cultivation of spiritual self-awareness, ultimately leading to higher quality care and greater patient satisfaction.

Moreover, these findings can assist policymakers and healthcare managers in creating spiritually supportive and nurturing work environments that bolster nurse resilience and reduce burnout. This approach is especially crucial in high-stress units such as emergency and intensive care. Ultimately, emphasizing nurses’ spiritual sensitivity promotes the development of a compassion-centered organizational culture, which not only improves working relationships and job satisfaction but also enriches patient experiences and outcomes.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that spiritual sensitivity plays a vital role in enhancing nurses’ compassionate care, and education in spiritual skills can improve care quality. Demographic factors showed no decisive impact, reflecting the complexity and interplay of psychological and work environment influences. Nurses’ experience and professional maturity strengthen compassionate care. Creating supportive and spiritual work environments facilitates resilience and reduces burnout. It is recommended that healthcare centers implement targeted training and cultural programs to foster nurses’ spiritual sensitivity and compassion. These findings can guide more effective policy-making and nursing service quality improvement. Future studies are advised to investigate psychological and organizational factors in greater depth to provide comprehensive strategies for enhancing compassionate care.
