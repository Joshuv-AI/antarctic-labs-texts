Design and performance study of biomimetic bone scaffolds based on the fusion of triply periodic minimal surfaces
Authors: Yadi Sun, Yan Wang, Chunhui Ji, Yiyang Li, Jianxiong Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt present, the treatment of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) with bone scaffolds has become a research hotspot. However, comparative studies on the performance of different fusion bone scaffolds are relatively scarce. This study adopted a method with a constant porosity of 70%. By adjusting the TPMS function equation, the three basic structures of D, G, and P were fused in pairs to form six fusion structures: D-G, G-D, D-P, P-D, G-P, and P-G. The simulations were performed using Ti6Al4V as the scaffold material, and through quasi-static compression experiments, we investigated the mechanical properties of these fused TPMS bone scaffolds. At the same time, we also used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) technology to study the effects of permeability, pressure, and flow rate on the fusion TPMS bone scaffolds. The results show that the fusion structure P-D has a low elastic modulus and a yield strength similar to that of natural bone tissue (153.6 MPa). The average wall shear stress of the P-D structure is 0.0037 Pa, the area suitable for cell growth accounts for as high as 98.9%, and it has the highest permeability (5.7245 × 10− 8m2).The design of the fusion porous scaffold meets the implantation requirements of human bone tissue.Keywords: ONFH, Biomimetic, Bone scaffolds, TPMS

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is an orthopedic disease caused by insufficient blood supply, which leads to bone necrosis and subsequent hip pain and dysfunction [1]. Studies have shown that femoral head collapse is a direct consequence of decreased mechanical properties and, if not treated early, most patients will collapse within three years [2]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop effective early ONFH treatment strategies to maintain hip function, prevent femoral head collapse, and reduce patients’ dependence on long-term treatment [3]. The current treatment methods are mainly divided into non-surgical treatment and surgical treatment. Non-surgical treatment mostly adopts conservative treatment methods, which can relieve pain and delay the course of the disease, but cannot prevent femoral head collapse [4]. Surgical treatments include total hip replacement and core decompression, but these operations are complex, traumatic, and have many complications [5]. Therefore, conservative treatment that can preserve the contour and mechanical properties of the femoral head, prevent joint collapse, and avoid arthroplasty is particularly important [6].

In recent years, studies have shown that implanting bone scaffolds into necrotic bone tissue is an effective treatment method. Bone scaffolds can accelerate and stabilize bone repair by providing rapid, robust, and durable mechanical support, thereby reducing the need for repeated interventions and significantly slowing the progression of early-stage ONFH [7]. The use of a bone scaffold with a porous structure significantly reduces the elastic modulus of the implant, avoids the stress shielding effect, and can provide good mechanical support for the femoral head [8, 9]. Furthermore, the interconnected pore network facilitates cell adhesion, proliferation, and tissue ingrowth, while enabling efficient nutrient and metabolite exchange with the surrounding tissues, thereby improving long-term biostability [10]. The structural diversity of bone scaffolds leads to heterogeneity in their mechanical and biological properties, which in turn show different responses in the interaction with the host bone after implantation. Therefore, how to construct bone scaffolds that meet the requirements is one of the research focuses.

At present, in order to imitate the structure of natural bones, computer-aided design (CAD) technology is usually used, including regular structures, irregular structures and triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS), to design bone scaffolds that better meet performance requirements [11]. Although scaffolds with regular geometries are easy to fabricate, their sharp edges hinder integration with host bone tissue. In contrast, irregular structures offer greater geometric freedom but are difficult to precisely control in terms of pore morphology and uniformity [12]. Among them, the structure of TPMS is highly similar to the topological characteristics of human trabecular bone and can be described as a periodic infinite framework with zero average curvature at any point along three independent axes. Its superior biomimetic properties make it a popular choice for simulating natural bone structure [13, 14]. Compared with other grid-based lattice structures, TPMS is a periodic geometric structure with a lower level of stress concentration [15]. Structural diversity within TPMS scaffolds can be achieved by varying their implicit mathematical functions. Among them, the Primitive (P), Gyroid (G), and Diamond (D) types are the most extensively studied and widely applied [16, 17]. Most researchers tend to compare the performance of these homogeneous TPMS structures, but by fusing them into a dual-layer structure that mimics human bone, their performance can be optimized and different structures can be used in different areas [18–20]. Despite this, there is still a relative lack of systematic research on the structure of fused TPMS [21].

Natural bone tissue is a double-layer mixed structure, which contains two parts: a dense outer structure of cortical bone and a sparse inner structure of cancellous bone [11]. Bone exhibits multiscale structural diversity. At the macroscopic level, cortical bone provides high mechanical strength for load bearing, whereas cancellous bone features a porous architecture that supports metabolic exchange and shock absorption. At the microscopic level, cancellous bone consists of a trabecular network formed by interconnected struts, which ensures both mechanical adaptability and biological functionality. The structural characteristics of natural bone itself provide a template for constructing artificial bone scaffolds. The double-layer bone scaffolds designed based on biomimetic structures inspired by natural structures have better performance than homogeneous bone scaffolds [22]. Currently, uniform bone scaffolds cannot correctly mimic the human bone structure to assume different functions. After being implanted into the human body, under load conditions, they may cause excessive mechanical properties that cannot meet the performance requirements of the bone [23]. The double-layer scaffold design mimics the double-layer structure of natural bone, matching its mechanical and biological properties with the surrounding tissue at the implantation site. The cortical layer of the scaffold provides load-bearing strength, whereas the cancellous layer, with its porous network, facilitates nutrient exchange, supports cell proliferation and tissue ingrowth, and satisfies metabolic demands [24]. By adjusting the parameters of the double-layer structure, the internal lattice structure configuration can be adjusted to change the mechanical and biological properties of the bone scaffold [25].

Therefore, this study deeply explored the combined effects of different TPMS bone scaffolds on effective elastic modulus, yield strength, permeability, and wall shear stress through comprehensive numerical analysis. A hybrid TPMS design strategy was employed to replicate the dual-layer composite structure of cortical and cancellous bone. Six different double-layer hybrid TPMS bone scaffolds were designed by mixing three TPMS structures—P, G, and D—in pairs. Using numerical simulation technology, we compared the performance differences between these bone scaffolds to provide a scientific reference for the design of bone scaffolds.

Mechanical performance simulation

In this study, the commercial finite element analysis software ABAQUS was used to simulate the compression of bone scaffolds. A controlled load with a displacement of 30% (9 mm) was applied to the top surface to simulate physiological loading conditions in the human body, while the bottom surface was fixed. Contact conditions between scaffold surfaces were defined as frictionless to ensure stability during simulation, and the boundary conditions were shown in Fig. 4. The scaffold was meshed using 3D linear hexahedral elements (C3D8R) with an average element size of 0.3 mm, ensuring sufficient resolution for capturing stress and strain distributions. The material properties were configured based on Ti6Al4V: density of 4.41 g/cm³, yield strength of 944 MPa, elastic modulus of 110 GPa, and Poisson’s ratio of 0.3 [33]. Ti6Al4V was chosen as the scaffold material due to its excellent biocompatibility, high strength-to-weight ratio, and well-established use in orthopedic implants. During the simulation, the analysis steps were dynamic and explicit. The stress contour plot of the bone scaffold under 1% compressive strain was recorded. The force-displacement curve of the scaffold during compression was obtained through the Abaqus process output and converted into a stress-strain curve to obtain the effective elastic modulus and yield strength.

Computational fluid dynamics simulation

Permeability affects the transport of nutrients in the scaffold, which is essential for cell growth, while wall shear stress provides mechanical stimulation that can be sensed by cells, leading to better differentiation and proliferation [34, 35]. CFD modeling was approximated by single-phase and creeping flow models. Cell differentiation analysis

In order to further study the differences in bone tissue differentiation among six double-layer fusion bone scaffolds, this paper conducted a computational analysis of the tissue differentiation of the bone scaffold wall under in vitro perfusion system culture conditions. In the in vitro culture environment, tissues differentiate along the inner wall of the bone scaffold, and this process is mainly affected by the load stimulation at the fluid-solid interface. Therefore, this study focuses on the tissue differentiation at the fluid-solid interface in order to reveal the mechanism of the influence of different bone scaffold structures on tissue differentiation. The mechanical stimulation of BMSC differentiation includes OSS and WSS. In order to analyze the different morphological scaffolds under the dual mechanical stimulation of fluid flow and strain, the total mechanical stimulation (SS) of different morphological bone scaffolds was quantitatively analyzed by FSI [41].

Results

Mechanical properties of fusion bone scaffolds

In the field of modern orthopedic repair, the design and application of bone scaffolds are of vital importance, especially in providing mechanical support and promoting bone tissue regeneration. This study shows in detail the stress distribution of six double-layer hybrid TPMS bone scaffolds with different designs under constant deformation pressure through Fig. 6. In the experimental setup, we used a precisely controlled constant deformation pressure to simulate the mechanical environment that bone scaffolds may encounter in actual applications. The six bone scaffolds, including P-G, G-P, P-D, D-P, G-D and D-G, all showed their own unique stress response patterns.

Stress contour plot of bone scaffold

As shown in Fig. 7, during the compression process, for the P-G and G-P bone scaffolds, the stress concentration phenomenon was significantly larger than the other four structures, and the stress concentration mainly appeared on the P structure, and these stress concentration areas were perpendicular to the direction of the applied external force. For the P-D and D-P bone scaffolds, although they also showed similar stress distribution trends, it is worth noting that their maximum stress values ​​were significantly lower than those of the P-G and G-P structures. This may mean that these bone scaffolds have greater safety and stability when subjected to the same pressure. Further observing the G-D and D-G bone scaffolds, we found that stress concentration areas mainly appeared on the G structure, and these areas were parallel to the direction of external force. This stress distribution characteristic is highly consistent with the force behavior of bones, indicating that the design of G-D and D-G bone scaffolds is more in line with the biomechanical properties of human bones. That is, the compression behavior of G-D and D-G bone scaffolds is more consistent with the natural stress pattern of human bones. This bionic design not only provides better mechanical support, but also helps promote the natural growth and repair of bone tissue. Therefore, these findings are of great significance in guiding clinicians to select appropriate bone scaffolds and provide patients with more effective treatment options.

Stress contour plot on the front of the bone scaffold

Elastic modulus and yield strength are important indicators for evaluating the mechanical properties of porous structures, which can measure the ability of the structure to resist elastic deformation and withstand external loads. As a load-bearing part that replaces the natural bones of the human body, the porous structure must have a certain strength to ensure that it is not damaged under the action of external loads. Figure 8 shows the comparison of the elastic modulus and yield strength of 6 porous structures. Among the 6 porous structures: the elastic modulus (19.906GPa) and yield strength (178.8 MPa) of the DG structure are the largest, and the elastic modulus (12.474GPa) and yield strength (142.3 MPa) of PG are the smallest. The elastic modulus and yield strength (142.3–178.8.3.8 MPa) of the six structures are within the requirements of implantable prostheses for the human body. The elastic modulus of natural cortical bone is reported to be between 7 GPa and 30 GPa, while its yield strength is typically in the range of 100 MPa to 200 MPa [44]. These mechanical properties ensure adequate load-bearing capacity and compatibility with surrounding bone tissue, minimizing the risks of implant failure or stress shielding effects.

Permeability of fusion bone scaffold

Nutrients such as blood, oxygen, and cells in the human body will circulate in the internal channels of the scaffold, and new bone tissue will also grow from the outside of the scaffold to the inside of the scaffold along the channels of the scaffold. Therefore, for better growth of bone tissue, it is necessary to ensure adequate transmission of nutrients and cells within the porous channels. Therefore, it is very helpful to explore the flow pattern of nutrients in the scaffold pores and the permeability of the designed structure for bone tissue regeneration.

Figure 9 shows the fluid velocity contour plots of six types of axially fused porous scaffolds. In the six structures, the flow rate of the flow channel near the fusion point of the scaffold is significantly higher than that of the surrounding area, which also ensures that nutrients can be transported into deeper pores and avoids the difficulty of new bone tissue growing into the interior of the scaffold. problem, it also confirms that the axial fusion design is conducive to the flow of nutrients and cell migration; while the flow velocity in the peripheral area is smaller, this is mainly due to the greater frictional contact between the fluid and the hole wall in the peripheral area, and the smaller flow velocity and larger Friction can give cells more time to adhere to the surface of the porous structure, thereby increasing the cell adhesion rate. In addition, the fluid velocity of the D-G and G-D structures is higher, and the flow velocity of the porous structure is evenly distributed. This is mainly attributed to the regular distribution of pore channels and small pore diameters in the G and D structures; the G-P and P-G structures have a large number of large pores due to the P structure and the pores are distributed axially, causing the fluid flow rate to be small and the flow rate to accumulate at the P structure.

Wall shear stress of fusion bone scaffold

Another important feature of the fluid response of the scaffold is the fluid wall shear stress (WSS). The liquid-induced FSS will provide mechanical pulses, which are sensed by cells and lead to better cell proliferation and differentiation. When the fluid wall shear stress is between 0.05 and 57 mPa, it is conducive to cell growth; when the shear stress is less than 0.05 mPa or greater than 57 mPa, cell growth is inhibited [37]. The WSS distribution contour plot on the inner wall of the scaffold is shown in Fig. 11. For all models, the calculated average WSS varies between 3.08 and 9.65 mPa, which is similar to the research results of Derya Karaman et al. [46]. Combined with Fig. 10, it is found that the average wall shear stress structure of the G-D structure is due to its large specific surface area, which is mainly due to the high degree of tortuosity of the G structure wall, the flow velocity rotation is accelerated, and a large wall shear stress is formed. The average wall shear stress of the two fusion structures of the D and P structures is not much different, and is within the design requirements of the wall shear stress. After calculation, the average wall shear stress of the six axial fusion structures was in the range of 0.1–10 mPa, and the results were similar to those of Kumar and Tang et al. [47] Fig. 12 shows the proportion of suitable cell growth area. Among them, the proportion of P-D suitable for cell growth area is 98.2%, which is much larger than that of other scaffolds. From this point of view, the fusion scaffold P-D has a better wall shear stress distribution and is more suitable for the attachment of bone cells after implantation.

Cell differentiation

Due to the changes in the bone scaffold structure and the restriction of fluid flow in different regions, tissue differentiation in the bone scaffold is uneven. Computational models have been applied to predict the mechanical stimulation experienced in the bone scaffold under in vitro flow perfusion or mechanical compression. As can be seen from the SS distribution cloud map in Fig. 13, the stimulation intensity of bone scaffolds with different structures shows significant differences under the same strain and inlet flow rate conditions. In all bone scaffolds, there are regions with high and low SS values, which are closely related to the distribution of wall shear stress and strain. High SS regions usually correspond to high WSS and high strain regions, which are often concentrated in areas with more active fluid flow. These regions have high WSS and strain values, which are conducive to cell proliferation and differentiation. Low SS regions correspond to low WSS and low strain regions. Low SS regions often appear in areas with limited fluid flow or denser structures. These regions have low WSS and strain values, and cell differentiation may be inhibited. The SS distribution of G-P and P-G structures is relatively more uniform, and the high SS region is more widely distributed. This is mainly due to the relatively tortuous internal shape of the G structure, which has a significant dispersion effect on fluid flow, making the WSS distribution more uniform. This uniform WSS distribution indicates that the G-P and P-G structures have obvious advantages in mechanical stimulus transmission, which makes the differentiation in osteocytes and chondrocytes more uniform. Compared with other structures, the SS distribution of P-D and D-P structures is more concentrated, and the high SS area is mainly concentrated in the transition zone of the two structures. This phenomenon may be due to the existence of a direct current channel inside the P structure, which causes the fluid to directly impact the transition zone, thus forming a local high SS value. Although this concentrated distribution may affect the uniform differentiation of cells, high SS values ​​in specific areas contribute to the differentiation of specific types of cells, such as osteocytes. Among the six bone scaffolds, the P-D and D-P structures have the highest degree of osteocyte differentiation. The SS distribution of D-G and G-D structures is between the above two situations, showing both a certain uniformity and a certain concentration. This may be because the internal shapes of the D and G structures are relatively tortuous, which has a dispersive effect on the fluid flow, making the WSS distribution relatively uniform. In general, there are significant differences in the SS distribution of bone scaffolds with different structures. These differences are mainly attributed to the structural design of the bone scaffold and the restriction of fluid flow, which provides an important theoretical basis for the design of bone scaffolds.

Discussion

This study deeply explored the design of bionic bone scaffolds based on TPMS to provide a more effective treatment strategy for ONFH. Six double-layer hybrid TPMS bone scaffold models were constructed by fusing three TPMS structures (P, G, and D) in pairs, and their mechanical properties, permeability, and wall shear stress were comprehensively evaluated.

This study employed a uniform overall porosity (70%) across all six fusion structural designs. This setting ensures that performance differences stem primarily from structural morphology rather than porosity variations. Although the porosity of natural cortical bone is typically less than 25%, introducing a significant porosity change directly into a two-layer scaffold can lead to poor transitions and stress concentration at the junction of the upper and lower layers, disrupting continuous mechanical transmission. To avoid interference from these localized stress peaks in structural comparisons, this study chose to conduct analyses under uniform porosity conditions. Future designs should incorporate more realistic simulations of the cortical-to-cancellous transition.

As shown in Fig. 8, the effective elastic modulus of the six fusion scaffolds ranged from 12.47 to 19.91 GPa, and the yield strength from 142.3 to 178.8 MPa. The P–D and D–P structures achieved the best balance between stiffness and strength, closely matching natural bone and effectively reducing stress shielding. In contrast, the D–G and G–D scaffolds showed higher stiffness, which may lead to stress concentration at the bone–implant interface, while the P–G and G–P scaffolds exhibited lower stiffness but weaker strength. Overall, the P–D structure demonstrates the most favorable mechanical performance, providing sufficient load-bearing capacity with minimal stiffness mismatch.When exploring the mechanical properties of bionic bone scaffolds, we found that the P-D structure exhibited excellent mechanical properties due to its uniform stress distribution, low elastic modulus similar to natural bone tissue, and moderate yield strength, which helped to reduce stress shielding effects and promote bone integration [48, 49]. In addition, uniform stress distribution helps to avoid bone resorption and implant loosening, while moderate yield strength provides the necessary stability and durability for the bone scaffold [50]. Although other structures in this study, such as P-G and G-P, also show certain mechanical adaptability, their stress concentration is more significant, which may affect long-term biological stability [51]. In contrast, the G-D and D-G structures may be more suitable for areas subject to higher loads due to their consistency with the mechanical behavior of bones [52]. These findings provide us with a deep understanding of bone scaffold design and performance assessment and highlight the importance of considering mechanical properties during the design process. In terms of permeability, Santos et al. [53] studied three uniform TPMS geometries: D, G and P, and targeted these geometries respectively. Two porosity levels of 60% and 70% were implemented. They used CFD models to evaluate the permeability inside these TPMS scaffold geometries. The study found that under the condition of 70% porosity, the permeability of the D stent was 1.9 × 10− 9mm2, the permeability of the P stent was 3.5 × 10− 9 mm2, and the permeability of the G stent was The permeability is 3.2 × 10− 9mm2. In addition, Pires et al. [54] also obtained the results of bone scaffold permeability in the same study, with values ​​ranging from 7.2 × 10− 9 mm2 to 1.2 × 10− 8 mm2. These results indicate that bone scaffolds with different TPMS geometries have differences in permeability, and changes in porosity also have a significant impact on permeability. These findings have important implications for understanding the fluid transport properties of bone scaffolds and their potential impact on cell growth and tissue regeneration. In contrast, the axial fusion biomimetic porous structure in this study has a larger permeability range and better permeability controllability, which has guiding significance for the design and optimization of bone scaffolds. Furthermore, the influence of WSS distribution on cell behavior cannot be ignored. WSS is unevenly distributed throughout the structure. Literature has confirmed that fluid wall shear stress has a great impact on cell proliferation and differentiation. Mahammod et al. [55] proposed using average wall shear stress to characterize cells on the surface of the scaffold. Proliferation: For example, the average wall shear stress of osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) is between 0.1-10mPa. Zhang et al. [56] pointed out that the proportion of wall shear stress area suitable for cell growth can be used to judge the proliferation of cells on the scaffold surface. This article uses the mid-surface analysis function of Comsol to find the total area of ​​the wall, and then extracts the surface with wall shear stress between 0.05 and 57 mPa to obtain the wall proportion suitable for cell growth.

This study used Ti6Al4V as the material for numerical simulations. This alloy has extensive experience in clinical implantation. However, its high elastic modulus may still lead to long-term stress shielding. Although increasing the porosity to 70% significantly reduces the equivalent elastic modulus, to further match bone tissue and reduce stress shielding, future experiments may consider using materials with lower modulus, such as β-titanium alloy. Although this study provides valuable insights into the design of TPMS bone scaffolds, further studies are needed to verify the preclinical effects and long-term stability of these bone scaffolds. Future work will focus on in vitro and in vivo testing of these bone scaffolds, including implantation studies and long-term follow-up in animal models to evaluate their biocompatibility, mechanical stability, and ability to promote bone integration. Previous studies have demonstrated that density-gradient TPMS scaffolds can effectively replicate the porosity variations of cancellous bone, thereby improving the load transfer behavior of the implant and enhancing its osteogenesis [9, 16]. This study differs from density-gradient designs by integrating different types of TPMS structures into the same scaffold, achieving biomimetic simulation of the structural characteristics of different regions. Therefore, this design concept provides a new approach for constructing more biomimetic bone scaffolds.

In summary, this study not only provides a scientific basis for the design of bone scaffolds, but also provides a potential more effective treatment option for ONFH patients. By deeply understanding the mechanical properties, permeability, and wall shear stress of bone scaffolds, we can better design bone scaffolds that meet clinical needs to improve patients’ quality of life and reduce dependence on long-term treatment.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel bone scaffold design, integrating TPMS structures into a bilayer structure that emulates the natural bone’s bilayer architecture, where the upper layer corresponds to cortical bone and the lower layer to cancellous bone. Unlike previous studies focusing on single-TPMS structures, this research assesses the performance of six different bilayer hybrid TPMS scaffolds. The axial fusion design of the TPMS structures is aimed at creating a biomechanical gradient akin to that of human bone. Six double-layer hybrid TPMS bone scaffold models were constructed by fusing three different TPMS structures (P, G, and D) in pairs, aiming to simulate the structure and performance of human cortical bone and cancellous bone. The mechanical properties, permeability, and wall shear stress of these bone scaffolds were comprehensively evaluated through quasi-static compression tests and CFD analysis.

The results showed that the axial fusion structure (P-D) exhibited a low elastic modulus and yield strength (153.6 MPa) similar to those of natural bone tissue, while having high permeability (5.7245 × 10− 8 m2) and suitable wall shear stress (0.0037 Pa), and the area suitable for cell growth accounted for as high as 98.9%. The stress distribution characteristics of this structure effectively reduced the stress and strain differences between cortical bone and cancellous bone, avoided stress concentration, and reduced the risk of bone resorption. In addition, the design of the double-layer bone scaffold mimics the double-layer structure of natural bone, and by adjusting the internal lattice structure configuration, it achieves matching the mechanical and biological properties of the bone tissue at the implantation site. This study provides a theoretical basis for the design of bilayer TPMS bone scaffolds through numerical simulation; However, the current work has not yet addressed long-term cytocompatibility at the cell–material interface or in vivo osseointegration validation, which will be the focus of subsequent investigations to comprehensively evaluate the scaffold’s performance.
