Structure-based classification of bacterial chitooligosaccharide-specific outer membrane porins (ChiPs)
Authors: Rawiporn Amornloetwattana, Piyakamon Bunkum, H Sasimali M Soysa, Arnaud Baslé, Anuwat Aunkham, Robert C Robinson, Bert van den Berg, Wipa Suginta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChitoporins (ChiPs) are chitooligosaccharide-specific outer membrane porins found in various Gram-negative bacteria and are critical for marine species that use chitin as a carbon and nitrogen source. Vibrio spp. ChiPs, exemplified by VhChiP from Vibrio harveyi, are trimeric OmpC-like 16-stranded β-barrels with a long pore-confining loop L3 that interacts exclusively with long chain chitooligosaccharides. Here, using crystallography and electrophysiology, we show that two atypical ChiPs, Escherichia coli ChiP (EcChiP) and Serratia marcescens ChiP (SmChiP), are monomeric 18-stranded β-barrels with shorter L3 loops, which display weaker and less selective substrate interactions compared with their Vibrio counterparts. Structure-based phylogenetic analysis of bacterial ChiPs resolves three clades: monomeric ChiPs; clade A, trimeric ChiPs with an N-plug; and clade B, trimeric ChiPs lacking the characterized helical N-plug. These architectural differences likely reflect environmental selection. Marine Vibrio spp., living in chitin-rich environments, possess trimeric ChiPs that enable rapid chitin uptake, whereas soil-borne S. marcescens and multihabitat E. coli encode monomeric ChiPs that we speculate are more suited to variable substrate availability.Keywords: chitin metabolism, chitoporin, chitooligosaccharides, sugar-specific porins, structure-based phylogenetic analysis

The outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria contains both general and sugar-specific porins that conduct small nutrients into the periplasm (1). Among these, LamB was the first sugar-specific porin to be structurally and functionally characterized (2): initially identified as a bacteriophage receptor, it was later recognized as a maltodextrin-specific channel (maltoporin) (3, 4). Other sugar-specific porins include ScrY (sucrose) (5, 6, 7), CymA (cyclodextrins) (8, 9), and OprB (glucose) (10).

Chitin is the most abundant polysaccharide in marine ecosystems and is efficiently degraded by marine Vibrio bacteria, the primary chitin recyclers in oceans. Chitin turnover by these bacteria is central to global carbon and nitrogen cycling. Vibrio spp. are facultatively anaerobic and can grow by respiratory or fermentative metabolism depending on environmental conditions (11). They also possess a highly efficient, regulated system for chitin utilization. They secrete specialized enzymes that degrade the abundant chitin polymer into soluble chitooligosaccharides and monomeric sugars, which are imported and metabolized. The chitin catabolic pathway is highly dynamic and involves the coordinated action of chitin-degrading enzymes (12, 13, 14), chitin-binding proteins (15, 16) and sugar-transport systems (17, 18, 19). The resulting chitin-derived sugars, such as N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and chitooligosaccharides, serve as important carbon and energy sources that enter central metabolic pathways to support bacterial growth (20). This chitin-processing machinery works cooperatively under the control of a histidine kinase chitin sensor (ChiS) (13, 21). In the soil bacterium Serratia marcescens, uptake of chitooligosaccharides through its chitooligosaccharide-specific porin (SmChiP) is a rate-limiting step for growth on chitin-derived sugars (22), highlighting the critical role of outer membrane transport in the utilization of chitin-derived nutrients. ChiPs were initially identified in Vibro furnissii (23) and later in Vibrio cholerae and Shewanella spp. (20, 24, 25), but their function in chitin transport remained unclear. We recently elucidated the X-ray crystal structure of VhChiP from Vibrio harveyi (now Vibrio campbellii), which is a trimeric assembly, with the N-terminal 19-residues of each pore acting as an intersubunit pore plug to cause the opening/closing of a neighboring channel within the trimer (19), analogous to the N-plug in CymA, which is specific for transporting cyclodextrins (9).

By contrast, Escherichia coli is a nonchitinolytic bacterium that usually grows on glucose-rich substrates. Genes encoding the enzymes and protein machinery in the chitin catabolic pathway in E. coli are normally silent and are only induced by high concentrations of chitobiose (26, 27, 28). The E. coli chiP gene, formally named ybfM, is repressed by ChiX sRNA (29, 30). A homologous system exists in S. marcescens, where the chiPQ-ctb (formerly ybfMN-ctp) cluster is similarly regulated by ChiX sRNA (22, 31, 32). Gene deletion studies showed that the expression of the chiP gene is controlled by the noncoding ChiX sRNA, which binds to the gene’s 5′-untranslated region (UTR), preceding the chiPQ-ctb cluster (22, 31, 32). A S. marcescens mutant strain lacking ChiP (SmΔChiP) exhibited drastically impaired growth on chitobiose and chitotriose (22). Despite EcChiP and SmChiP showing selectivity for the same chitin oligosaccharide substrates as VhChiP (19, 33, 34, 35, 36), they share <15% sequence identity, raising the question as to what extent their structures and functions are related.

Here, we used X-ray crystallography to show that EcChiP and SmChiP are monomeric porins with 18-stranded β-barrels, in contrast to the trimeric Vibrio ChiPs. Comparison of the ChiP cocrystal structure with chitohexaose, together with electrophysiology and isothermal titration calorimetry, reveals that the trimeric channel binds chitohexaose with substantially higher affinity, mediated by an extensive network of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts. These striking results prompted us to construct a structure-based phylogenetic classification of bacterial ChiPs. Integrating experimental structures with AlphaFold3 predictions (37), we define two major ChiP clades (monomeric/trimeric) and two subclasses within the trimeric group distinguished by the presence or absence of the characterized helical N-terminal plug. Overall, our work provides a molecular framework for understanding how divergent ChiP architectures shape the efficiency of chitin uptake in bacteria occupying distinct ecological niches.

Results and discussion

Structure determination of EcChiP and SmChiP

No ChiP structure from a nonchitinolytic bacterium has previously been described. We initially determined the X-ray crystal structure of E. coli ChiP (EcChiP). Native, full-length, tag-free EcChiP was expressed in the outer membrane of an Omp-deficient E. coli BL21 (DE3) Omp8 Rosetta strain. Following extraction with 3% (v/v) octyl POE, purification via anion exchange and size-exclusion chromatography yielded 1 to 2 mg of protein per liter culture. The purified EcChiP was concentrated and readily crystallized under standard sitting-drop vapor diffusion conditions. Since this work predated AlphaFold, no suitable molecular replacement (MR) model was available; phases were therefore obtained by heavy-atom soaking. A single crystal soaked with potassium tetrachloroplatinate (K2[PtCl4]) diffracted to 2.10 Å resolution, enabling structure determination by single-wavelength anomalous diffraction phasing (Table 1). This structure was subsequently used as a MR template for the determination of apo-EcChiP and chitohexaose-bound EcChiP at 1.85 and 1.90 Å resolution, respectively (Table 1).

Given that SmChiP shares 75% sequence identity with EcChiP but only 16% with VhChiP, for comparison we also solved the structures of S. marcescens ChiP (SmChiP) in apo and chitohexaose-bound forms at 1.85 and 2.70 Å resolution using the same MR approach (Table 1). SmChiP was expressed in the E. coli BL21 (DE3) Omp8 Rosetta strain and purified using the same detergent extraction and chromatographic workflow as EcChiP, yielding comparable protein amounts suitable for crystallization. Crystals were obtained under similar vapor-diffusion conditions, and the EcChiP structure served as the molecular-replacement template for phasing. Both EcChiP and SmChiP adopt monomeric 18-stranded β-barrel architectures, resembling Occ-family channels, a class of substrate-specific outer membrane porins that transport small carboxylate-containing molecules (38), in sharp contrast to the trimeric 16-stranded β-barrel architecture of previously reported VhChiP (19) (Fig. 1). Structure homology searches indicate that VhChiP is more similar to other trimeric channels, such as OmpC, rather than monomeric Occ-family channels, possibly indicating functional convergence in transporting chitooligosaccharides. These structural data reveal that ChiPs can be classified into at least two distinct groups with different oligomeric states, which may reflect differences in their functional properties.

Monomeric ChiPs differ substantially from the trimeric Vibrio channel

To evaluate the structural differences between monomeric and trimeric ChiPs, we compared the ligand-free and ligand-bound forms of EcChiP and SmChiP with the previously reported trimeric VhChiP (19) (Fig. 1). EcChiP and SmChiP share highly similar structural features and secondary structure organization (Fig. S1). In contrast, comparison with the trimeric VhChiP reveals notable differences in several structural elements, particularly in the arrangement of β-strands and extracellular loops. These differences are consistent with the structural-based sequence alignment shown in Fig. S1, which highlights the distinct structural organization of monomeric ChiP compared with the trimeric VhChiP. In addition, superimposition of a chain A VhChiP promoter with EcChiP yielded poor alignment of Cα atoms (r.m.s.d. of 3.8 Å computed by DALI (39)), but they still share structural similarity (z-score of 15.6) despite differences in the number of beta-barrels. (Fig. S2). In the monomeric ChiPs, three surface-exposed loops L2, L7, and L9 are unusually long and fold inward, constricting the outer surface of the channel (Fig. 1, A and B). In contrast, VhChiP L2 loops mediate intersubunit contacts that stabilize its trimeric assembly (Fig. 1C) (19). The pore-constricting loop L3 of VhChiP (33 residues) is nearly twice the length of L3 in EcChiP and SmChiP (18 residues). Furthermore, VhChiP loop L8 folds inward into the pore and may assist substrate binding to the L3 constriction region, functionally corresponding to L9 in the monomeric channels.

Electrostatic surface potential representations show that all ChiPs possess highly polar interiors (Fig. S3), with uneven distributions of positively and negatively charged side chains lining the pore walls, creating an asymmetric electrostatic environment within the channel lumen. EcChiP and SmChiP exhibit similar electrostatic profiles and are less negatively charged than VhChiP. Consistent with this difference, previous electrophysiological studies reported reduced cation-selective ion permeability for EcChiP and SmChiP (PK+/PCl− = 2.8) (33) compared to VhChiP (PK+/PCl− = 3.2) (40). These differences likely reflect variations in the electrostatic environment within the pore lumen. However, as chitooligosaccharides are neutral, their transport is unlikely to involve ion-coupled cotransport.

Comparison of chitooligosaccharide-binding modes between monomeric and trimeric ChiPs

In the EcChiP-chitohexaose complex, electron density was observed for only five N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) units, suggesting partial occupancy or disorder at the reducing end of the sugar chain. By contrast, the SmChiP complex exhibited well-resolved electron density for all six GlcNAc units, enabling complete modeling of the ligand within the channel. To facilitate comparison between species, the binding site for each GlcNAc residue was designated a subsite, numbered according to the position of the corresponding GlcNAc unit along the oligosaccharide chain. The chitooligosaccharide chain in EcChiP occupies the upper part of the constriction zone, binding to affinity sites 2 to 6, whereas in SmChiP it spans sites 1 to 6, with the reducing end sugar unit located between subsites 1 and 2 (Fig. S4). This SmChiP binding mode closely resembles that observed in VhChiP, where chitohexaose extends fully across the pore from the extracellular to the periplasmic side, occupying all six affinity sites.

In monomeric ChiPs, loops L2, L7, and L9 interact with the GlcNAc units near the channel entrance, whereas loop L3 contacts the reducing end at the pore constriction (Fig. 2, A and B). In the previously reported trimeric VhChiP structure (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 5MDR) (Fig. 2C), the chitohexaose chain is extended inside the protein pore, with the entry-side sugars positioned close to loop L8 and central units forming more extensive contacts with L3, and fewer contacts with L2.

To further define these interactions, we analyzed protein-ligand interactions using LigPlot+ (41), focusing on affinity sites 1 to 6 (Fig. 3). The sugar chain adopts a consistent orientation, with the reducing end (GlcNAc-1) facing the periplasmic side and the nonreducing end (GlcNAc-6) toward the extracellular side. In EcChiP (Fig. 3A), no electron density was observed for GlcNAc-1, but GlcNAc-2 to GlcNAc-6 were well-defined and could be confidently modeled. Loops L1, L2, L3, L7, and L9 lie within 3.5 Å of these sugars, placing them in range for direct interaction with most contacts mediated by residues in loop L7. Overall, six H-bonds are involved in sugar binding: these are (L3)Ser133/C2-N-acetamido of GlcNAc-2; (β1)Arg18/C6-CH2OH of GlcNAc-3; (L2)Glu78/C2-N-acetamido and (L7)Asp316/C6-CH2OH of GlcNAc-4; and (L7)Arg295/C2-O acetamido and (L9)Asn417/C2-N-acetamido of GlcNAc-5 (Table S1). No hydrogen bonds were observed for GlcNAc-6 at the extracellular entrance of the channel. In addition to polar interactions, several aromatic residues mediate hydrophobic or π–π stacking interactions with the sugar rings. GlcNAc-2 is sandwiched by (β1) Trp16 and (L3) Phe134; GlcNAc-3 interacts with (L7) Trp310; and GlcNAc-6 stacks against (L2) Trp92. These interactions likely contribute to both ligand stabilization and directional guidance of the oligosaccharide within the pore.

These interactions closely mirror those observed in EcChiP but with subtle differences in residue identity and positioning.

In the previously reported trimeric VhChiP (PDB ID:5MDR) (Fig. 3C), hydrogen bonds are primarily clustered within the central pore constriction zone (affinity sites 2–4). Key interactions include (L8) Tyr349 with the C6-CH2OH group of GlcNAc-1; (L8) Glu347 (C2-O-acetamido), (L3) Arg312 (C2-N-acetamido), and (L3) Asn127 (C2-acetamido) with GlcNAc-2; and multiple contacts to GlcNAc-3 from (L1) Arg94 (C2-acetamido), (L3) Asp122 (C6-CH2OH), (L3) Arg148 (C2-O-acetamido), and (L3) Tyr118 (C3-OH). A final H-bond to GlcNAc-4 is also mediated by (L3) Tyr118 (C3-OH). Hydrophobic interactions in VhChiP involve (L3) Trp123 with GlcNAc-1, (L1) Phe84 with GlcNAc-6, and (L8) Trp331 with GlcNAc-6. Notably, loop L3 in VhChiP is the primary structural element mediating sugar binding, in contrast to EcChiP and SmChiP, where loop L7 dominates ligand interaction.

Collectively (Table S1), comparison of the key binding residues among ChiPs highlights notable differences in the distribution of aromatic residues and hydrogen-bonding interactions along the substrate-binding pathway. EcChiP lacks residues equivalent to VhChiP’s Trp123 (subsite 1), Trp136 (subsite 4), and Phe84 (subsite 6). Similarly, SmChiP lacks equivalents of Trp123 and Trp132 (subsite 2), Trp136 (subsite 4), and Phe84 (subsite 6). In contrast, VhChiP contains a greater number of centrally positioned aromatic residues together with a more concentrated hydrogen-bonding network, particularly around subsite 3. These structural differences suggest that substrate interactions in EcChiP and SmChiP may involve fewer key binding contacts compared with VhChiP.

Monomeric ChiPs exhibit lower sugar-binding affinity than trimeric ChiPs

To relate chitin-binding efficiency to the sugar interactions observed in the crystal structures, we performed single-channel electrophysiology EcChiP, SmChiP, and VhChiP using their preferred substrate, chitohexaose. All three proteins inserted readily into artificial phospholipid membranes and formed stable channels in 1 M KCl. All channels responded more strongly to applied negative potentials than to positive potentials.

For EcChiP (Fig. 4A), the average conductance was 0.5 ± 0.03 nS at −100 mV for the fully open state. Titration with chitohexaose produced frequent blocking events, with histogram analysis indicating that the sugar molecule fully blocked the channel, as the ion current dropped from the open-channel level to zero (Fig. 4A). Kinetic analysis yielded a binding constant K = 64,000 ± 3000 M−1. SmChiP behaved similarly with comparable conductance (0.5 ± 0.05 nS) but fewer blocking events and a ∼4-fold lower affinity (K = 16,000 ± 4000 M−1) (Fig. 4B).

By contrast, trimeric VhChiP (Fig. 4C) displayed a larger average conductance (2.0 ± 0.2 nS), and the highest affinity for chitohexaose (K = 270,000 ± 43,000 M−1), ∼4-fold higher than EcChiP and ∼16-fold higher than SmChiP (Table 2). This enhancement results from both elevated on-rates (kon) and off-rates. These single-channel kinetics indicate that trimeric VhChiP’s captures chitooligosaccharides (represented by kon) more rapidly than the monomeric channels, suggesting stronger substrate interactions in VhChiP compared with EcChiP and SmChiP. This aligns with previous studies on stochastic current fluctuations, indicating that each subunit of VhChiP exhibits memory effects. Trimerization may enhance substrate capture by creating a bowl-like architecture, thereby increasing the likelihood of repeated sugar-binding events and contributing to the high kon value (42).

Comparison of binding thermodynamics

To quantify binding energetics, we performed isothermal microcalorimetry (ITC) on detergent-solubilized ChiPs titrated with chitohexaose. For EcChiP (Fig. 5A) and SmChiP (Fig. 5B), binding is driven by favorable entropy changes, whereas the binding of chitohexaose to VhChip (Fig. 5C) was enthalpy driven (Table S2). The ΔG values for the monomeric ChiPs were ∼1.8 to 2.6 kcal/mol less favorable than for VhChiP, consistent with weaker ligand binding and suggesting that trimerization stabilizes protein–ligand interactions.

Trimeric ChiP shows higher bulk sugar permeability

Next, to examine the bulk sugar permeation into ChiP-reconstituted liposomes, we carried out liposome swelling experiments (Fig. 6). This assay enables evaluation of the permeability of chitooligosaccharides through the reconstituted ChiP channels by monitoring osmotic swelling of proteoliposomes as the permeable solutes diffuse through the pore (43). Liposome swelling assays provide experimental evidence for substrate permeation/uptake through ChiP channels. These experiments show that all ChiPs are permeable to several chitooligosaccharides (GlcNAc1–GlcNAc6), with VhChiP exhibiting the highest permeation rates among the channels tested.

For all ChiPs, permeability increased with sugar oligomer chain length (chitohexaose > chitotetraose > chitotriose > chitobiose). However, the single sugar subunit (D-GlcNAc) showed a higher permeability rate than chitobiose and chitotriose, consistent with its smaller molecular size and the lower steric barrier for translocation through ChiP. This apparent difference reflects the contribution of two factors influencing permeation through ChiP channels: molecular size and specific interactions with the pore. Although smaller sugars such as D-GlcNAc can diffuse more readily because of reduced steric hindrance, longer chitooligosaccharides can establish more extensive interactions with residues lining the ChiP pore, facilitating their translocation through the channel. A control experiment using free liposomes (without ChiP) confirmed that none of the tested chitosugars exhibited detectable permeability, indicating that sugar transport was entirely dependent on ChiPs. Thus, the relatively high permeation rate of D-GlcNAc can be attributed to its small size, whereas the enhanced permeation of longer oligomers likely arises from their more favorable interactions with the ChiP binding sites. Overall, permeation of these sugars was size-dependent for oligomers, and EcChiP displayed lower permeation rates compared to SmChiP and VhChiP.

Structure-based phylogenetic classification of ChiPs

To explore how sequence variation correlates with oligomeric state and function, we compiled a nonredundant set of full-length bacterial porin sequences (≤90% sequence identity) identified by Fold-seek and Blastp searches using the sequences and structures of three characterized chitoporins as queries: VhChiP to identify trimeric homologs and EcChiP, and SmChiP to identify monomeric homologs. A phylogenetic tree constructed from the resulting multiple sequence alignment (Fig. 7) resolved two major porin lineages corresponding to the known structural classes: monomeric including the OprD/OccD1 family (44) and trimeric including the OmpC family (45, 46).

The monomeric ChiPs cluster with the OprD family, adjacent to UidC porins. Members of this lineage adopt 18-stranded β-barrels, and available crystal structures confirm their monomeric architecture (PDB ID: 2ODJ and 7VU0). In contrast, trimeric ChiPs cluster with the OmpC lineage, which separates into two principal subclades: one containing the general porins OmpC and OmpU, and the other containing specialized porins ChiP and PorB. Within the trimeric ChiP lineage, two distinct clades are apparent. Clade A, which includes VhChiP (PDB ID: 5MDQ), is characterized by a conserved 9-residue N-plug helix that regulates channel gating in VhChiP (19) and contributes to sugar translocation in V. cambellii ChiP (47). Clade B comprises Vibrio species whose ChiPs possess divergent largely unstructured N-terminal extensions (Fig. 8). Functionally characterized representatives of this clade include VfChiP from V. furrnissii (23) and VcChiP from V. cholerae (20, 25).

AlphaFold3 (AF3) structure predictions support these assignments (37). Monomeric ChiPs proteins consistently are predicted to be monomeric 18-stranded β-barrels, whereas trimeric proteins are predicted to form 16-stranded barrels that assemble into plausible trimers (Figs. S5 and S6). Thus, clade membership of ChiPs correlates strongly with oligomeric state and the number of strands in the barrel architecture.

Focusing on the three experimentally characterized ChiPs (EcChiP, SmChiP, and VhChiP), we aligned their N-terminal sequences using residue numbering that starts at the mature protein following signal peptide cleavage (Fig. 8A). Monomeric ChiPs share ∼80% identity in the first two β-strands (β1–β2) immediately following an invariant “AGF” motif. These β-strands integrate into the barrel wall. By contrast, Trimeric class A members possess the highly conserved nine-residue N-plug helix (DGANSDAAK), whose first amino acid residue (Asp1) makes extensive contacts with the channel pore (LigPlot + analysis; Table S3).

Clade B chitoporin N-termini lack this characteristic helical N-plug helix. Their N-termini are shorter, unstructured, and are predicted by AlphaFold3 (AF3) to insert into the neighboring pore in an extended conformation, forming far fewer contacts, leaving the pore mostly open (Fig. S8). Removal of the N-termini from the sequences before AF3 prediction in Clades A and B did not affect trimeric formation in either Clade (Fig. S9). Superposition of AF3 models and available crystal structures confirms that each clade adopts a characteristic fold (r.m.s.d ∼0.5 Å over 300 Cα atoms) packing into distinct quaternary architectures (Fig. 8B). These structure-sequence correlations support the conclusion that ChiPs from the two families have evolved distinct pore architectures. Monomeric chitoporins are found predominantly in Enterobacteriaceae, whereas trimeric ChiPs are characteristic of marine Vibrionaceae. These contrasting architectures are consistent with adaptation to different ecological niches. Trimeric Vibrionaceae ChiPs assemble into functional trimers in which the N-terminal region contributes to channel gating and substrate recognition, facilitating nutrient uptake in chitin-rich marine environments (19, 25, 48, 49, 50). These features optimize permeability and selectivity for growth under variable conditions. In contrast, monomeric ChiPs from Enterobacteriaceae possess a structurally distinct pore architecture and exhibit different gating and flux characteristics, likely suited for their roles in terrestrial and host environments (51, 52). Together, these findings suggest that chitin-specific porins evolved independently from distinct ancestral porin families, converging on a common function in the uptake of chitin-derived oligosaccharides, illustrating how adaptation to different ecological niches can drive convergent evolution of substrate specificity.

Notably, these two clades share specificity for chitooligosaccharides despite adopting distinct β-barrel architectures and oligomeric states. Monomeric ChiPs belong to the 18-stranded OprD/Occ-family, whereas trimeric ChiPs are members of the 16-stranded OmpC family, both of which encompass porins with diverse substrate specificities. These observations indicate that chitooligosaccharide specificity is not dictated by overall barrel architecture. Nevertheless, both families share architectural features, including the inward positioning of loop L3 (Fig. 1), suggesting that similar structural principles underlie substrate recognition despite their distinct evolutionary origins. Although 16- and 18-stranded β-barrels may themselves have evolved from a common ancestral outer membrane β-barrel through strand-pair gain or loss, our phylogenetic analysis indicates that chitin-specific transport arose independently within the OprD- and OmpC-derived lineages. Thus, the shared specificity of the two ChiP families is most consistent with convergent evolution toward efficient transport of chitooligosaccharides.

Concluding remarks

ChiPs are chitooligosaccharide-specific channels whose oligomeric state and functional properties reflect ecological niche. Structural, electrophysiological, ITC, and permeation assays indicate that trimeric VhChiP exhibits stronger binding and distinct transport properties for chitohexaose compared with monomeric ChiPs. Structure-guided phylogeny further classifies ChiPs into monomeric OprD/OccD1-like and trimeric OmpC-like groups, with trimeric proteins subdivided by N-terminal plug architecture. Together, these findings exemplify how porin function can evolve independently, with divergent assemblies converging on a shared solution for chitooligosaccharide transport. This structure–function–evolution framework provides a basis to understand how oligomerization and N-terminal motifs may modulate ChiP performance in relation to substrate availability and environmental context.

Experimental procedures

Recombinant expression and purification of EcChiP and SmChiP

Expression and purification of the recombinant EcChiP and SmChiP were carried out as described previously (18, 33). Briefly, the expression vector pET23a(+) harboring the full-length chiP gene was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) Omp8 Rosetta strain. Cells were grown at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin and 25 μg mL−1 kanamycin. During the exponential growth phase (A600 ∼0.6–0.8), ChiP expression was induced with isopropyl thio-β-D-galactoside (IPTG, 0.5 mM final concentration). After 6 h of further incubation at 37 °C, the cell pellet was harvested by centrifugation at 2948 g for 25 min at 4 °C.

For protein purification, the cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 2.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1 mM CaCl2), containing 10 μg mL−1 RNase A and 10 μg mL−1 DNase I. Cells were disrupted with a high-speed ultrasonic homogenizer with a 6-mm diameter probe (EmulsiFlex-C3, Avestin Europe) for 10 min. After this, 20% (w/v) SDS solution was added to obtain a final concentration of 2% to facilitate membrane solubilization and extraction of outer membrane from the peptidoglycan layer. The suspension was then incubated at 50 °C for 60 min with 300 rpm shaking, to ensure complete lysis. Cell wall components were collected by centrifugation at 100,000 g at 4 °C for 1 h. The pellet, containing recombinant protein, was extracted twice with 2.5% (v/v) Octyl-POE (n-octylpolyoxyethylene; ALEXIS Biochemicals) in 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4 and centrifuged again. The supernatant was then dialyzed thoroughly against 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, containing 0.05% (v/v) LDAO (lauryldimethylamine oxide; Sigma-Aldrich Pte. Ltd). The solubilized ChiP was subjected to ion-exchange chromatography using a Hitrap Q HP prepacked column (5 × 1 ml) connected to an ÄKTA Prime plus FPLC system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Life Sciences Instruments, ITS Co. Ltd). Bound proteins were eluted with a linear gradient of 0 to 1 M KCl in 20 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, containing 0.05% (v/v) LDAO. The purity of the eluted fractions was confirmed by SDS-PAGE. To obtain protein of high purity, fractions containing ChiP were pooled and subjected to size exclusion chromatography (sec) using a HiPrep 16/60 Sephacryl S-200 High Resolution column. The purity of the ChiP fractions obtained after the size exclusion step was verified by SDS-PAGE before they were pooled and the protein concentration of the freshly prepared ChiP was estimated using the Novagen BCA protein assay kit (EMD Chemicals Inc).

Crystal screening and ChiP crystallization

Crystallization trials of apo-EcChiP and apo-SmChiP were carried out by the sitting drop technique in Swissci 96-Well 2-Drop MRC Crystallization Plates (Molecular Dimensions) using a Mosquito crystallization robot (TTP Labtech). The purified proteins were exchanged into the crystallization buffer containing 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.4% (v/v) tetraethylene glycol monooctyl ether (C8E4) and concentrated to 15 mg mL−1 for EcChiP and 9 mg mL−1 for SmChiP. Two screening kits; MemGold1 and MemGold2 (Molecular Dimensions Limited) were used with protein solution: mother liquor ratios of 200 nl:200 nl in the top wells and 200 nl:150 nl in the bottom wells. The screening plates were incubated at 16 °C. Single crystals of EcChiP were observed in MemGold1 under the condition MG1_G8: 33% (v/v) PEG400, 4% (w/v) ethyl β-D-thioglucoside, 0.1 M NaCl, 0.1 M Mes, pH 6.5, whereas single crystals of SmChiP were observed in MemGold2 under the condition MG2_C10: 22% (w/v) PEG8000, 0.1 M calcium acetate, 0.1 M Mes, pH 6.0. Single crystals were further optimized in 24-well plates using the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method. In each well, two drops with three different proteins: precipitant volume ratios, 1.0 μl:1.0 μl and 1.5 μl:1.0 μl, 1.0 μl:1.5 μl were set up on an 18 mm cover slip. For the single-wavelength anomalous diffraction experiment, single crystals of EcChiP were soaked with 0.2 μl of 50 mM K2[PtCl4] solution. For EcChiP and SmChiP in complex with chitohexaose, the freshly prepared protein dissolved in 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.4% (v/v) C8E4 were mixed with 10 mM chitohexaose before crystallization. The single crystals from the optimized condition were harvested under a liquid-nitrogen steam for X-ray data collection.

Data collection and processing

X-ray diffraction data from the EcChiP crystals soaked with K2[PtCl4] and complexed with chitohexaose were collected at beamline I04-1 (Diamond Light Source). The datasets were processed with Dials (53) and scaled with Aimless (54). The structure of EcChiP-Pt was solved by multiwavelength anomalous diffractionphasing and was used as a template for MR of apo-EcChiP and EcChiP complexed with (GlcNAc)6. Both multiwavelength anomalous diffraction and MR phasing techniques were carried out by PHASER in CCP4i program suite v. 7.0 (55, 56). Model building was performed by Coot (57), and the structures were refined by Phenix (58). For apo-SmChiP, the X-ray diffraction data were processed and scaled as described for EcChiP, and the structure was solved by MR technique using apo-EcChiP as a structure template. For SmChiP in complex with (GlcNAc)6, the X-ray diffraction experiments were performed at the beamline, TPS-05A, in NSRRC (National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center). The X-ray data were processed by HKL2000 (59). The complex structure was solved by MR using apo-SmChiP as the structure template. The statistics of data collection and structural refinement are shown in Table 2. The structures were displayed with the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System v. 2.5.0. The atomic coordinates of the refined structures were deposited in the PDB database.

Single-channel electrophysiology

Solvent-free black lipid membrane reconstitution was carried out following the Montal-Mueller technique (57, 60) using 1,2-diphytanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylcholine (DPhPC, 5 mg mL−1) (Avanti Polar Lipids). First, a 25-mm-thick Teflon film was sandwiched between the two chambers of a cuvette with an aperture of 50 to 100 nm, which was prepainted with a few microliters of 1% (v/v) hexadecane in n-hexane. The chambers were filled with 1 M KCl in 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, and black lipid membrane experiments were carried out at 25 °C. A planar bilayer was formed across the aperture by lowering and raising the liquid level. Ionic currents were detected using Ag/AgCl electrodes, where the reference electrode was connected to the cis side of the membrane (ground) and the working electrode connected to the head-stage of an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Axon Instruments). Single channel measurements were performed in the voltage clamp mode and digitized using the Axon Digidata 1550 digitizer, and the data acquisition was performed using Clampex software (Axon Instruments). Using a low-pass Bessel filter with a sampling frequency of 50 kHz, the traces obtained were filtered at 10 kHz. Single-channel analyses were performed using Clampfit software (all from Molecular Devices). Single protein channels were reconstituted in lipids, and fully open channels of the three ChiPs were titrated with chitooligosaccharides (0–20 μM for EcChiP/SmChiP, 0–5 μM for VhChiP)on the cis or trans side of the chamber. Fluctuations of ion flow produced by sugar binding and diffusion through the inserted channel were usually recorded for 2 min at different transmembrane potentials of ±100 mV. However, only the data acquired under the most active external potential (−100 mV) are shown as representative ion traces.

Direct binding studies by ITC

ITC measurements were performed using a Microcal PEAQ-ITC (Malvern Instruments LimitedWR141XZ) thermostated at 25 °C with the three ChiPs prepared in 20 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.4 containing 0.05% (v/v) LDAO. EcChiP and SmChiP (60 μM) were added to a 300-μl sample cell and then titrated with 40 μl of 1 mM ligand (prepared in the dialysis buffer). SmChiP (60 μM) was in the sample cell with 40 μl of the ligand (1–2.5 mM in dialysis buffer) in the syringe, depending on its affinity for SmChiP. In the case of VhChiP, 5 μM of the protein was titrated with 0.3 mM chitohexaose. Control experiments were performed by injecting ligand solution into the buffer, buffer solution into the cell material (protein), and buffer into the buffer in an identical manner and the resulting heat changes were subtracted from the measured heat of binding using the manufacturer’s Microcal PEAQ-ITC analysis software with a “one set of sites” fitting model. The integrated peaks allowed the stoichiometry (n), the equilibrium binding association constant (Ka), the enthalpy change (ΔH°), the Gibb’s free energy of binding (ΔG°binding), and the entropy change (ΔS°) of binding to be estimated.

Liposome swelling assays

The ChiP-reconstituted proteoliposomes were prepared as described elsewhere (3). Soybean L-α-phosphatidylcholine (20 mg mL−1, Sigma-Aldrich) freshly prepared in diethyl ether was used to form multilamellar liposomes. For the preparation of proteoliposomes, 200 ng of ChiP (EcChiP, SmChiP, and VhChiP) was reconstituted into 200 μl of the liposome suspension by sonication, and then 17% (w/v) dextran (40,000 Da) was entrapped in the proteoliposomes. The isotonic solute solution with a concentration of 70 mM was established using D-Raffinose (504 Da) and served as a reference for adjusting the isotonic concentration of other solutes. To carry out a liposome-swelling assay, 25 μl of the proteoliposome suspension was added to 600 μl of sugar solution, and changes in absorbance at 500 nm were monitored immediately. The absorbance change over the first 60 s was used to estimate the swelling rate (s−1) using the equation ϕ = (1/Ai)dAi/dt in which ϕ is the swelling rate, Ai is the initial absorbance and dAi/dt is the rate of absorbance change during the first 60 s. The swelling rate of L-arabinose (150 Da) was measured in six independent experiments, and the mean value was used as a reference and set to 100%. The swelling rates of other sugars were then normalized relative to this reference value. The values presented are averages from six independent biological replicates. Protein-free liposomes and proteoliposomes without sugars were used as negative controls. The sugars tested were 70 mM D-melezitose (522 Da), and 1 mM of D-GlcNAc (221 Da), (GlcNAc)2 (424 Da), (GlcNAc)3 (627 Da), (GlcNAc)4 (830 Da), and (GlcNAc)6 (1237 Da), mixed with 69 mM D-Raffinose. Statistical analysis was performed using One-way ANOVA. Significant differences are shown by asterisks (∗) and set at 0.001 < p < 0.05.

Construction of a sequence-based phylogenetic tree

Putative bacterial ChiP sequences were retrieved from BLASTP searches (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?PAGE=Proteins) using three functional ChiPs as query sequences: EcChiP from E. coli (UniProt entry id: sp_P75733) (33), SmChiP from S. marcescens (UniProt entry id: tr_A0A0P0QBS3) (36) and VhChiP from V. harveyi (UniProt entry id: tr_L0RVU0) (19). The putative ChiP sequences were retrieved with an e-value cutoff of 1 × 10−7 and a query coverage of 70 to 100. The other porin sequences were retrieved from Foldseek (61) using the crystal structures of SmChiP (PDB ID: 7VU2) and VhChiP (PDB ID: 5MDQ) as templates, with an e-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 from two databases: SwissProt and the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Redundant sequences were removed at 90% identity using CD-HIT in MAFFT, a multiple alignment program (62). The final number of sequences was 150. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the pipeline within phylogeny v2.0 (63) using the ClipKIT module to clean the sequence alignment (64) followed by IQ-Tree (65).
