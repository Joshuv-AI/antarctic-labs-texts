Quantifying partial pathological response rate in prostate cancer patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy using a novel morphometric approach
Authors: Wei Huang, Huihua Li, Philipos Tsourkas, Sean Mcilwain, Irene Ong, Christos E. Kyriakopoulos, Brian Johnson, Steve Y. Cho, Shane A. Wells, Alejandro Roldan Alzate, David F. Jarrard, Erika Heninger, Joshua M. Lang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate assessment of partial pathological response rate (ppRR) to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAT) is critical for assessing the efficacy of therapy and for optimal clinical management. Because of a lack of accurate estimation of baseline cancer burden, assessment of ppRR has never been attempted in prostate histologically. We presented a novel morphometric approach assessing ppRR in patients who underwent NAT and then correlated the ppRR with patients' outcomes. A control cohort consisted of 39 NAT-naïve Caucasian patients who had high-risk PCa (defined as Gleason Grade Group >2) and an adequate biopsy sample (defined as the size of the biopsy PCa area, including PCa epithelium and stroma >2 mm2). A study cohort included 26 patients with high-risk PCa (defined as clinical stage T3a or higher, serum PSA >20 ng/mL, or GGG of 4–5, or with oligometastatic disease) who underwent androgen deprivation therapy plus docetaxel. Using the PCa epithelial to stromal ratio (E/S) as a metric, surrogate BCB for the study cohort was predicted from the pre-treatment biopsy samples, and ppRR was calculated. Correlation analysis of patients' ppRR with progression-free survival was performed using ppRR >80% as a cut-off.Nine of the 26 patients from the study cohort experienced a significant response to NAT (ppRR > 80%) using the PCa E/S-based approach, and these patients had significantly better progression-free survival (p = 0.006). ppRR to NAT can be reliably assessed using PCa E/S as a surrogate metric from biopsy and RP samples, and ppRR can be used to predict patients' outcomes.

Background

Neoadjuvant hormonal or chemotherapy (NAT) is an area of increased interest in the management of high-risk prostate cancer (PCa).1, 2, 3 Pathological response is considered an important metric for evaluating treatment efficacy. Parameters to assess treatment response include pathological complete response (pCR, no residual cancer), residual cancer burden (RCB), and minimal residual disease (MRD).3,4 Studies have shown that prostate cancer volume (PCaV) correlates with patient outcomes.5, 6, 7 In practice, PCaV is often manually estimated as the cubic volume including all PCa epithelium and the intervening stroma. However, due to the limited sampling and different methods used for PCaV estimation in prostate biopsy samples, there is a relatively poor correlation between PCaV in biopsy and RP specimens.8,9 Therefore, PCaV estimated from biopsy samples cannot reliably predict patients' baseline cancer burden (BCB) for patients undergoing NAT therapy.

Due to the lack of an accurate estimation of BCB, quantifying partial pathological response rates (ppRR) is challenging in patients with clinically significant PCa (>0.5 cm3), as defined by Stamey et al.10 PCa has morphological changes after NAT or radiation therapies, most significantly in PCa epithelium, including vanishing, shrinking in size, and other cytological alterations,11, 12, 13, 14 whereas the PCa stroma remains relatively unchanged. As a result, the PCa epithelial to stromal ratio (PCa E/S) in post-NAT sample may be reversed compared to that in the pre-treatment biopsy sample in some patients with good treatment response, whereas post-NAT PCaV or RCB (areas marked by PCa cells) may remain large if only looked at the epithelial component.

Biopsy is commonly used to establish a diagnosis of PCa. Theoretically, if adequately sampled, the PCa epithelial and stromal composition should be the same or at least highly correlated in location and Gleason Grade Group (GGG)-matched PCa areas in the patient's biopsy and RP samples. Currently available digital pathology and artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled advanced morphometric analysis of cancer morphology and composition, making tissue and cell segmentation and measurement relatively easy tasks.15, 16, 17 We hypothesize that the PCa E/S can be used as a surrogate metric for assessing cancer burdens (BCB and RCB), and thereby ppRR. In this study, we assessed ppRR in patients who underwent NAT and correlated the ppRR with patient outcomes using an AI-enabled digital pathology approach.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (2017-0606).

Control cohort

Thirty-nine NAT-naïve Caucasian patients who had high-risk PCa (defined as Gleason Grade Group (GGG) > 2) and an adequate biopsy sample (defined as the size of the biopsy PCa area, including PCa epithelium and stroma >2 mm2 ) were included in this study. Biopsy PCaV was manually estimated based on positive core length and total core length from the entire biopsy samples, including benign cores. RP PCaV was estimated based on PCa area and total prostate tissue section areas and was extracted from the original pathology reports. Case information is summarized in Supplemental Table 1. This control cohort was used to study the PCa E/S correlation between biopsy and RP samples as follows:

One hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slide containing the highest GGG PCa from the biopsy and the following RP samples were selected from each patient. The slides were de-identified and scanned with Aperio AT2 (Leica, IL) at 40× to generate whole-slide images (WSIs). The PCa area(s) were defined by manually drawing a line along the outward borders of PCa to encircle the PCa by a genitourinary pathologist. PCa annotations, epithelial and stromal segmentation, and measurement were performed using AI-enabled Halo software (Halo v3.3.2541.345, Indica, Albuquerque, NM). Entrapped benign acini within the annotated PCa foci were excluded from analysis using the software cutting tool. Halo software includes multiple modules. For this version (Halo v3.3.2541.345, Indica, Albuquerque, NM), we utilized the Halo drawing toolbar to outline the areas of PCa in the peripheral and transitional zones. Then, we used the AI-enabled Tissue Classifier module for tissue segmentation and quantification. The module allowed us to draw a few examples of cancer stroma areas and the AI learned from the examples to segment the epithelium from the stroma. By repeating this process a few times, high accuracy in tissue segmentation was achieved. The epithelial and stromal areas were masked with different colors and the sizes of the segmented areas were tabulated immediately (Fig. 1). The PCa epithelial and stromal areas were measured in mm2 and the E/S ratio was calculated. Pearson's correlation of the paired (biopsy vs. RP) PCa E/S was analyzed and an equation was established with IBM SPSS Statistics 27. Study cohort

Twenty-six patients with high-risk PCa (defined as clinical stage at T3a or higher, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) >20 ng/mL, or GGG of 4–5, or with oligometastatic disease) underwent androgen deprivation therapy plus docetaxel and were included in this study. The patient's pertinent clinical information and manually estimated PCaV are listed in Supplemental Table 2. One H&E-stained biopsy slide and one RP whole-mount slide containing the PCa with the highest GGG from each patient were selected and scanned to generate WSI. The PCa in the biopsy WSI was annotated and the PCa E/S in biopsy and RP slides were measured as described above. For the RP whole-mount WSI, PCa was annotated by zonal distribution such as peripheral (P), transitional (T), central (C), and anterior (A), if possible (Fig. 2). Using the equation established from the control cohort, surrogate BCB (sBCB) for patients who underwent NAT was predicted from the biopsy PCa E/S. sRCB%=ESrpsBCBx100, where ESrp represents E/S ratio measured from patient's RP sample.

ppRR calculation and outcome correlation

ppRR was calculated as:ppRR%=1−sRCB.

An analysis of the correlation between patients' ppRR and progression-free survival was performed. The assessment of disease progression included PSA levels, radiographical findings (both bone and soft tissue) and other clinically relevant events. Survival analysis was performed using a Cox regression model with ppRR as the covariate, employing the survival analysis package in R statistical software. Appropriateness of the Cox fit was tested using the Schoenfeld test. A cut-off value of 80% was used to generate survival curves and test for significance based on bootstrapping the 26 patients over 10,000 iterations, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and plot of precision, sensitivity, and specificity (Supplemental Figs. 2–4).

Results

We found a superior correlation in the E/S ratios between the biopsy and RP sample pairs compared to the correlation in the PCaV between the same sample pairs. Using the E/S ratio from the pre-treatment biopsy samples, we were able to predict patients' BCB and assess patients' ppRR from the post-treatment RP samples.

E/S and PCaV correlation in biopsy and RP samples

Correlation analysis from 38 NAT naïve cases (one outlier #37, see supplemental Table 1, was removed from the analysis) showed strong positive correlation (R2 linear = 0.695, y = 0.71 + 0.57*x, p < 0.001 in E/S ratio between the paired biopsy and RP samples (Fig. 3a). This equation will be used in the study cohort to predict the BCB before the NAT treatment. ppRR and outcome prediction

None of the 26 patients from the study cohort had achieved pCR. Based on conventional assessment, 25 of the 26 patients (96%) had significant RCB (PCaV >5%, equivalent to PCa > 0.5 cc, Supplemental Table 2). Using the PCa E/S-based approach, we found that 9 of the 26 patients had a significant response to NAT (ppRR >80%, Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

Past studies have demonstrated that the correlation between PCaV in biopsy and RP samples was moderate to poor due to sampling bias and the various manual methods used by pathologists,8,18,19 when morphometric quantification was not yet widely available in pathology practice. Currently, RCB (PCaV) estimation is performed by measuring tissue area marked by recognizable PCa cells, regardless of the PCa morphology, which does not reflect whether or not NAT had an effect on PCa.

In this manuscript, we utilized an AI-enabled digital pathology approach to assess ppRR in patients who underwent NAT without achieving pCR. Because ppRR was measured based on pre- and post-treatment PCa E/S, it captures treatment effects, such as PCa shrinkage and partial dropout, which appear to be superior to PCaV estimation (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

Whereas significant RCB was found in 25 of 26 patients (96%) by PCaV estimation manually, a significant treatment response, measured by ppRR (>80%), was found in 9 of 26 patients (35%), who had significantly better progression-free survival. A similar approach was reported by others in assessing treatment response in rectal and breast carcinomas.20,21

Albeit promising as a novel method for assessing treatment response, this PCa E/S-based approach has its limitations. A few factors could affect the accuracy of predicting PCa sBCB from biopsy samples, thereby affecting ppRR measurement. First, this method requires adequate biopsy samples. Our data showed that the PCa composition, measured by PCa E/S in biopsy and RP samples, correlated strongly only when the biopsy PCa sample sizes were adequate (>2 mm2). Second, due to PCa heterogeneity in histological phenotypes, even in PCa with the same Gleason pattern 4 or 5,22 biopsy PCa sampling bias and the difference in biopsy and RP sample sizes included for analysis might lead to artificially lower sBCB predictions from the biopsy samples, hence overestimating ppRR. Therefore, an adequate threshold for ppRR needs to be established to assess patients' response to NAT and prognosis. We found that patients with a positive-predictive ratio (ppRR) greater than 80% were associated with significantly better clinical outcomes.

The prostate is divided into four zones anatomically: peripheral, transitional, anterior, and central. Peripheral zone cancer accounts for the great majority of PCa, index cancer in the transitional zone accounts for less than 25%, and index cancer in the central and anterior zones accounts for less than 5% of PCa.23,24 In this study, we lumped the peripheral, anterior, and central zone cancers into one group (peripheral). Because we used whole-mount prostate section images, it was relatively easy for the pathologist to visualize and differentiate the zonal distribution of the cancer and to annotate accordingly. It has been well recognized that there are PCa zonal differences in histology, molecular profile, and clinical behavior. Some published data have shown that TZ PCa is associated with a lower histological grade and overall better clinical outcomes.23, 24, 25 Based on their findings, some researchers have proposed the development of zonal PCa-specific management as an alternative to the current zonal-agnostic treatment approach.23,24 In the present study, we found that high-risk PCa in TZ was less responsive to NAT compared to high-risk PCa in the PZ. Similar findings have been reported by Teloken et al., who found that high-grade PCa in TZ was associated with higher serum PSA levels, larger PCa volume, and positive margins, resulting in a higher rate of biochemical recurrence. Salerno et al. reported higher PCa recurrence rates in TZ in patients following low-dose-rate brachytherapy,26 whereas O'Neil et al. reported that adjuvant radiotherapy appears to be associated with adverse outcomes for TZ PCa.27 These findings on PCa zonal origins and their clinical behaviors are intriguing; further large cohort studies are warranted.

In summary, this study demonstrates that the ppRR to NAT can be reliably assessed using the PCa E/S ratio from biopsy and RP samples, and ppRR can be used to predict patients' outcomes.
