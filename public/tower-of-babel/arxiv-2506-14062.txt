An Exact and Efficient Sampler for Dynamic Discrete Distributions
Lilith Orion Hafner, Adriano Meligrana

Abstract
Sampling from a dynamic discrete distribution involves sampling from a dynamic set of weighted elements, where elements can be added or removed at any stage of the sampling process. Although efficient for static sets, the Alias method [ 12 ] becomes impractical in dynamic settings due to the need to reconstruct the sampler after each update, which incurs a computational cost proportional to the size of the distribution, making it unsuitable for applications requiring frequent insertions, deletions, or weight adjustments. To address this limitation, different approaches have been studied, such as the Forest of Trees method [ 10 ] and the BUcket Sampling (BUS) method [ 15 ] . However, all previous methods suffered from numerical issues which can bias the sampling process. In this paper, we describe EBUS (Exact BUcket Sampling), the first exact algorithm with O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) sampling and update cost. The sampler can be updated by base- b b numbers with bounded precision and exponent, and sample the distribution of its elements exactly and efficiently. We provide also a state of the art implementation 1 1 1 the method’s implementation can be found at https://github.com/LilithHafner/WeightVectors.jl . of the method using IEEE 64-bit floating point numbers which we empirically show to be more efficient than several implementations of previous inexact methods.

Introduction

Sampling over a discrete distribution is a well-studied problem because of its many applications and can be formulated straightforwardly: Given a set of indices
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{N}
with weights
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{N}
the task is to sample an index
a_{i}
with probability
w_{i}/\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}
. Importantly, the set of indices and their corresponding weights can be fixed throughout the sampling or can change over time.

In this work, we provide a novel sampling methodology for sampling exactly and efficiently from a set of weights which can change dynamically over time. To the best of the authors knowledge, this is the first algorithm which is both fully correct when working with finite precision weights as well as requiring only
O(1)
instructions for all the supported operations (
sample
,
add
&

).

2
Related Work

When the set of indices and their weights is known and fixed for all the sampling process, the classical solution provided by the Alias Method
[
12
]
can be employed to solve the problem efficiently, with
O(1)
time for random draw. The method falls short when the weights have to be updated frequently, since the sampler needs to be re-initialized after each update, incurring an
O(n)
operation for each of them. Another classical solution often employed is to use a Binary Search Tree to record the sum of the weight in the constituent sub-trees
[
13
]
, but this still requires
O(\log(n))
sampling and update time. For this reason, previous works looked for efficient methodologies which could deal with this limitation. In
[
10
]
, the author developed a data structure to deal with the case where the number of elements of the distribution is fixed but the weights could vary over time achieving expected constant time both in sampling and update operations, and they noted that a slight modification of their approach could be used to deal also with the case where also the number of elements in the sampler varies over time. The basic technique at the core of their method is exponential bucketing, namely, to group elements in levels such that level
i
contains all elements with weights in the range
[2^{i},2^{i+1}]
so that rejection sampling in a level can be done in amortized constant time. In
[
15
]
, the same approach in taken, but differently from
[
10
]
, the authors used linear search for finding the level from which sampling an index and, by tracking the total weight of all the elements in the sampler, they were able to search into an interval of length of max
\log_{2}(n)
size. This was done in the general setting where elements can have weights belonging to
\mathbb{R}
. While that method can be considered
O(1)
in sampling and update if restricted to the finite precision setting, it still suffers strong numerical issues since it works on the floating point representation both for inter-level and intra-level sampling.

3
Our Exact Algorithm

As in
[
15
]
, weights are divided into levels according to their exponents, and we first sample a level through linear search and then sample an element within that level through rejection sampling. Differently from that paper, we assume to work in finite-precision arithmetic, and throughout the text, we will further assume to work with non-negative 64-bit floating point for simplicity but the results are easily extended to numbers with different finite precision and basis. To sample exactly, both phases cannot operate on the weights with the standard floating-point operations, but, instead, need to work with their more low-level bit representation, extracting mantissas and exponents.

The method performs a weighted random selection where each group of numbers is assigned a weight based on the precise bit-level representation of their floating‑point values. It first computes a high‑precision weight by extracting and summing the transformed significands, then scales these sums according to the numbers’ exponents and a global shift to obtain an approximated weight. The selection process involves drawing a random integer within the total of these weights and then determining the corresponding group by a linear scan of the level weights, employing rejection sampling in specific conditions to refine the process in order to ensure that the probability of selecting any group remains exactly proportional to its true weight, eliminating bias from rounding errors. After having sampled a level exactly, we need to be able to sample exactly within that level. This can be done by using the same intra-level rejection technique described in
[
4
,
11
,
15
]
, but, to do it exactly, instead of using the finite precision representation of the weights, we use their significands. We now go into the details of each phase.

The key result which proves that algorithm 1 is an exact linear search algorithm for sampling, can be expressed by considering that at each repetition of the sampling procedure we have that there is a probability of
\frac{SS_{L_{i}}}{\sum_{i}\widetilde{SS}_{L_{i}}}

\frac{SS_{L_{i}}}{\sum_{i}SS_{L_{i}}}

The refinement process works by progressively incorporating additional bits of precision that were not included in the initial 64‑bit approximation of the weights. When the random sample exactly matches the boundary value determined by the approximate weight, the algorithm recognizes that the decision is ambiguous because the fractional part of the weight was lost during the initial conversion. It then ”zooms in” on this fractional part by drawing a new random 64‑bit value and increasing the bit shift.

3.3
Global Shift Adjustments

The true weight of a level
L_{i}
is the sum of the weights of its elements, which could be represented accurately as
SS_{L_{i}}\ll L_{i}
using arbitrary precision arithmetic. However, as already discussed, for fast level sampling the algorithm can use an approximate weight for each level that fits into a 64-bit integer. To do so, we use a global shift
G
to scale the true level weights down into a 64-bit range, so that we then use
\widetilde{SS}_{L_{i}}
for most of the sampling. There are two main situation in which we need to adjust the approximate level weights:

Out-of-Range Approximate Levels Sum

To not overflow a 64-bit integer to store the levels approximate sum, we need to evaluate at each update if the sum is going to overflow a 64-bit integer with the current global shift
G
and reduce it if this is going to happen.

Single Approximate Level Overflow

To adjust the sum when it overflows, we need to be able to add the new weight to the appropriate approximate level weight, however, this can in itself cause the overflow of the level weight, so a new smaller target global shift needs to be computed also in this case.

Bounding Rejection Probability

To keep the level sampling rejection probability
P_{r}
low, we need to keep the sum of the approximate level weights minimum at a sufficiently high value, since, for a total number of levels equal to
N
, we have in the worst case that
P_{r}=\frac{N}{\sum_{i}\widetilde{SS}_{L_{i}}-N}
. We can deal with this by only increasing
G
when the algorithm hits the ”zooming in” phase during sampling and the approximate sum has fallen below the chosen threshold, which we set in the implementation at
2^{32}
.

Crucially, in all three cases we need to recalculate all existing approximate level weights using the new global shift and update the total approximate sum. Note though that this operation is infrequent in non-pathological cases and still
O(1)
for finite precision arithmetic since the exponent range is bounded, e.g. in the case of 64-bit floating point number, there are at most 2098 levels.

4
Miscellaneous Optimizations

To improve the constant factors of our method, we applied several key optimizations to its implementation. These changes aim to make the algorithm more efficient in practice, even if the asymptotic complexity remains unchanged.

First, instead of searching the level with the maximum exponent as in
[
15
]
, we track it after each addition or deletion so that we can start the sampling from that level without incurring any searching cost. We use a bit-set data structure for tracking non-empty levels so that if the current maximum level becomes empty, finding the new one is efficient. Another important optimization concerns the global shift adjustment. When the global shift changes, rather than recomputing the entire set of approximate level weights, we limit the update to a smaller range. This is possible because many level weights are already 0 or 1 and will remain unchanged after the shift.

Memory-wise, we use a single vector to store all the different weight significands used in Algorithm 2 for sampling, this requires a delicate manual memory management implementation, but has the advantage that we can re-compact the levels which become empty due to deletion of elements to save memory in respect of using a vector for each level, which, in practice, would have required in the worst case
2098\cdot 2n
integers for
n
stored weights and indices. This could be the case for instance if the
n
indices had all weights to be stored in the first level, then due to updates, all the weights need to be moved to the second level, then to third, and so on. Many memory allocators do not reclaim memory automatically after removals, and, even if that is the case, allocating and deallocating frequently is costly. Our allocator implements a custom compaction mechanism to deal with this so that we don’t need to allocate/deallocate when weights are updated. With our approach, we require
7n
integers to store indices and weights not to worsen our amortized
O(1)
time per update. A single 64-bit integer for each index is used to store its level location together with the corresponding exponent to save space. Thus, the memory consumption of our sampler grows as
O(8n)
.

Finally, when sampling many indices at once we implemented a specialized method which constructs an exact multinomial distribution for level sampling based on a simplified version of the algorithm described in
[
5
]
, here we use arbitrary arithmetic, but the size of the maximum integer is still bounded by
(2098+128+1)
bits, since the maximum sum of levels for double-precision numbers when they are represented in arbitrary arithmetic is bounded by
2^{2098+128+1}
.

5.1
Numerical Comparison

To compare the numerical efficacy of our sampler with respect to other methods, we designed a simple experiment: we take a fixed set of
n
items and allow the initial weights to decay gradually over
t
iterations. At each decay step we draw a large number of samples to see how closely the empirical frequencies match the exact distribution.

Concretely, for each index
i=1,\dots,100
, define the initial weights
w_{i}^{(0)}\;=\;\Bigl(2+\tfrac{1}{10000}\,i\Bigr)^{1000}
. This gives us a distribution of weights which are large enough to admit a large number of decay steps. For each
t=1,2,\dots,100
, we simulate a “decaying” effect by dividing each weight by the same base factor once per iteration. In closed form, one obtains
w_{i}^{(t)}\;=\;w_{i}^{(t-1)}\,\Big/\Bigl(2+\tfrac{1}{10000}\,i\Bigr)
so that
w_{i}^{(t)}=\Bigl(2+\tfrac{1}{10000}\,i\Bigr)^{1000-t}
. As
t
increases, the distribution gradually flattens, moving from being highly skewed at
t=0
toward a more uniform‐like shape by the time
t\approx 1000
.

In practice, we only take
t
up to 100 (since the inexact sampler stopped working already at
t\approx 50
) so the distribution remains somewhat non‐uniform but still less peaked than the initial one. At each
t\in\{1,\dots,100\}
, we perform
10^{6}
independent draws from the dynamic discrete distribution
\{\,w_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{100}
to construct the frequencies of the empirical distribution.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we presented a new algorithm, which we called EBUS (Exact BUcket Sampling), which is the first algorithm capable of maintaining exactness and performance in sampling from a weighted dynamic distribution in any finite precision setting. This required carefully designed rejection sampling procedures which we believe could be also adapted to other algorithms in the sampling realm. The numerical experiments revealed that inexact samplers can suffer from big numerical errors, which could completely bias the sampling process, or even halt it because the propagation of those errors can break some internal state of those samplers. Furthermore, we have shown how our state of the art implementation can compete with the most efficient inexact implementations of previous methods we could find. This novel method could be useful in cases where biasing the sampling could give spurious results, and in cases where sampling efficiency is important as in
[
2
]
. Finally, this could be used as a benchmark for exact methods for specific distribution subclasses given its generality.
