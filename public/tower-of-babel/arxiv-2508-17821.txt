Limitations of Normalization in Attention Mechanism
Timur Mudarisov, Mikhail Burtsev, Tatiana Petrova, Radu State

Abstract
This paper investigates the limitations of the normalization in attention mechanisms. We begin with a theoretical framework that enables the identification of the model’s selective ability and the geometric separation involved in token selection. Our analysis includes explicit bounds on distances and separation criteria for token vectors under softmax scaling. Through experiments with pre-trained GPT-2 model, we empirically validate our theoretical results and analyze key behaviors of the attention mechanism. Notably, we demonstrate that as the number of selected tokens increases, the model’s ability to distinguish informative tokens declines, often converging toward a uniform selection pattern. We also show that gradient sensitivity under softmax normalization presents challenges during training, especially at low temperature settings. These findings advance current understanding of softmax-based attention mechanism and motivate the need for more robust normalization and selection strategies in future attention architectures.

Theoretical Analysis

Consider a sequence of token embeddings
\displaystyle\mathbb{X}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
, where each embedding
\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
\displaystyle d
-dimensional vector. We begin by reviewing the classical self-attention mechanism introduced by Vaswani et al.
vaswani2017attention
:

\mathbf{q}_{m}=f_{q}(x_{m},m),\quad\mathbf{k}_{n}=f_{k}(x_{n},n),\quad\mathbf{v}_{n}=f_{v}(x_{n},n),

(1)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{m}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{n}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{n}
denote the query, key, and value vectors, respectively. The attention weights are computed using these query and key vectors as follows:

a_{m,n}=\exp\left(\mathbf{q}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{n}/T\right)/\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp\left(\mathbf{q}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{j}/T\right),

(2)

where the parameter
\displaystyle T
is known as the temperature, typically set to
\displaystyle T=\sqrt{d}
(as recommended in
vaswani2017attention
). To extend the scope of our analysis beyond the standard softmax normalization, we introduce a more general normalization framework:

a_{m,n}=F(\mathbf{q}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{n},\theta)/\sum_{j=1}^{L}F(\mathbf{q}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{j},\theta),

(3)

where
\displaystyle F:\mathbb{R}^{1+c}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth positive function parameterized by
\displaystyle\theta
, which can include parameters such as temperature or the number of tokens. For convenience, we denote the inner product
\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{n}
as
\displaystyle l_{m,n}
, referring to it as the
logit
associated with the token pair
\displaystyle(m,n)
.

In this work, we provide a detailed theoretical examination of the general normalization framework introduced in Equation (
3
). We focus on several critical aspects: (1) general limitations associated with softmax-type normalization; (2) the influence of normalization on token separation; (3) geometric insights into token separation; (4) connections between normalization and training dynamics.

We begin by addressing the general limitations of softmax normalization. As highlighted by
veličković2024softmaxforsharpoutofdistribution
, one critical limitation is the phenomenon of
vanishing attention weights
. Intuitively, when the number of tokens
\displaystyle L
increases, the normalization procedure (e.g., softmax) distributes attention weight across many tokens, causing the weights for individual tokens to become extremely small. This issue hampers the model’s ability to clearly differentiate between relevant and irrelevant tokens.

Formally, consider a set of logits
\displaystyle\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{L}\}
and their corresponding attention weights
\displaystyle\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{L}\}
, computed from the logits using a normalization function. We present the following general results regarding these normalized attention weights (see Appendix
for full proofs):

Lemma 1
.

Consider the normalization scheme defined by Equation (
3
) with a smooth function
\displaystyle F(l_{i},\theta)
that does not explicitly depend on the number of tokens
\displaystyle L
(i.e.,
\displaystyle L\not\in\theta
). Assume also that the logits are bounded,
\displaystyle l_{i}\in[-a,a]
. Then, the normalized attention weights satisfy:

\frac{C_{1}}{L}\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{L},

(4)

where the constants
\displaystyle C_{1}
and
\displaystyle C_{2}
do not depend on
\displaystyle L
.

This lemma implies that for any normalization of function independent of token count, the attention weights inevitably become uniformly small (on the order of
\displaystyle 1/L
) as the context size grows. As a result, the mechanism loses the ability to effectively highlight specific important tokens when processing long sequences. The proof relies on the boundedness and continuity of the function
\displaystyle F(l_{i},\theta)
on the compact interval
\displaystyle[-a,a]
. Due to these properties, both the numerator and denominator are bounded by constants independent of
\displaystyle L
. Hence, when normalized by the summation over all
\displaystyle L
tokens, each weight naturally scales as
\displaystyle 1/L
. Full mathematical details are provided in Appendix
.

Corollary 1
.

In the special case of softmax normalization (Equation (
2
)), with temperature parameter
\displaystyle T
, the attention weights satisfy:

\frac{1}{L}\exp\left(-\frac{2\Delta}{T}\right)\leq\alpha_{i}\leq\frac{1}{L}\exp\left(\frac{2\Delta}{T}\right),

(5)

where
\displaystyle\Delta=\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{k}\|_{2}
.

This corollary highlights how the magnitude of the query and key vectors directly impacts the distribution of attention weights. Specifically, the bounds show that unless the vectors have sufficiently large magnitudes (relative to temperature
\displaystyle T
), the attention weights remain close to uniform. Thus, increasing vector magnitudes can partially mitigate—but not eliminate—the vanishing attention problem.

These results demonstrate a fundamental limitation of softmax-type normalization: as the sequence length
\displaystyle L
grows, each attention weight
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
shrinks toward
\displaystyle O(1/L)
. In turn, the model struggles to assign appreciably larger weights to genuinely informative tokens, blurring the distinction between relevant and irrelevant embeddings. This loss of focus impairs the model’s ability to exploit salient information within long input sequences and ultimately hampers effective learning.

2.1
Distance analysis

We next study how normalization influences the
total deviation
—the discrepancy between a learned representation and the underlying conditional distribution. Yang et al.
yang2018breakingsoftmaxbottleneckhighrank
describe the
softmax bottleneck
: the low rank of the logit matrix limits the model’s ability to represent the true conditional distribution, thereby increasing total deviation. This deviation is not a consequence of insufficient model capacity or sub-optimal optimization, but rather of restrictions imposed by the final softmax projection layer.

To quantify the role of normalization in separating informative from non-informative tokens, let

\displaystyle\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{L}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{L}\},\;x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

denote the attention weights and their corresponding token embeddings, respectively.
We focus on the top-
\displaystyle N
tokens with the largest weights; their indices are collected in

\displaystyle I_{N}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\}
.
The
context vector
derived from these tokens is

s\;=\;\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\alpha_{i}\,x_{i}.

(6)

Our quantity of interest is the cumulative distance between this context vector
\displaystyle s
and all
non-selected
embeddings:

\tilde{d}\;=\;d\!\bigl(\mathbb{X}\setminus\mathbb{X}_{I_{N}},\,s\bigr)\;=\;\sum_{i\in I\setminus I_{N}}\bigl\|\alpha_{i}\,x_{i}-s\bigr\|_{2},

(7)

where
\displaystyle I=\{1,\ldots,L\}
,
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidean norm, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{I_{N}}=\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{N}}\}
is the set of selected embeddings.
Intuitively, a smaller value of
\displaystyle\tilde{d}
indicates that many low-weight tokens lie close to the high-weight aggregate
\displaystyle s
, signalling reduced separability and a greater likelihood of attention “dilution.”

Next, we distinguish two settings for the weight vector
\displaystyle\{\alpha_{i}\}
.
In the first, the weights are treated as
fixed
; in the second, we analyse a
random-selection
scenario in which the index set

\displaystyle I_{N}
is drawn uniformly at random from all subsets of size
\displaystyle N
:
\displaystyle I_{N}\;\sim\;U(I,N)
,
\displaystyle\qquad I\;=\;\{1,\ldots,L\}.

In this random case, the attention weights on the non-selected tokens vary with the draw of
\displaystyle I_{N}
.
We seek the expected cumulative distance

E\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{I_{N}}\bigl[\tilde{d}\bigr],

(8)

where the expectation is taken over the uniform choice of
\displaystyle I_{N}
.

We now adopt a classical metric-learning perspective: the above quantities quantify the Euclidean “separation margin” between selected and non-selected token embeddings. The next theorem formalises these observations by providing explicit upper bounds on the distance
\displaystyle\tilde{d}
in both the fixed and random top-
\displaystyle N
settings.

Theorem 1
.

For the representation distance defined in (
7
) the following bounds hold.

1.

Fixed top-
\displaystyle N
set.
If
\displaystyle I_{N}
is fixed,

\tilde{d}\;\leq\;(1-\bar{\alpha}_{N})\,d_{1}\;+\;\max_{j\in I_{N}}\|x_{j}\|_{2}\,\bigl[\bar{\alpha}_{N}(L-N)-(1-\bar{\alpha}_{N})\bigr],

where

\displaystyle d_{1}=\max_{i\notin I_{N},\;j\in I_{N}}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}

and

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{N}=\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\alpha_{i}
.

2.

Uniformly random top-
\displaystyle N
set.

When
\displaystyle I_{N}
is sampled uniformly from all
\displaystyle\binom{L}{N}
subsets of size
\displaystyle N
,

E\;=\;\frac{L-N}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\Bigl\|\bigl(\alpha_{i}+\tfrac{N}{L-1}\bigr)x_{i}-\bar{x}\Bigr\|_{2}\;+\;\varepsilon,

where
\displaystyle\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\alpha_{i}x_{i}
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{N}{L}\Bigr)\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{N}{L-1}\,\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}^{2}\|x_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\bigl\|\alpha_{i}x_{i}-\tfrac{N}{L-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\alpha_{j}x_{j}\bigr\|_{2}}.

Intuitively, for a
fixed
top-
\displaystyle N
set, the first term scales the largest out-of-set distance
\displaystyle d_{1}
by the total weight
\displaystyle(1-\bar{\alpha}_{N})
carried by the remaining
\displaystyle L-N
tokens, while the second term accounts for how much those low-weight tokens can still perturb the context vector, proportional to the norm of the largest selected embedding.
When the top-
\displaystyle N
indices are chosen
uniformly at random
, each token is excluded with probability
\displaystyle(L-N)/L
. Replacing the random indicator variables by their expectations yields the main sum; the residual
\displaystyle\varepsilon
captures the Jensen gap and is small whenever the individual weights
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
are not too concentrated. Full derivations are provided in Appendix
.

Theorem
1
relates the distance
\displaystyle\tilde{d}
to key parameters (
\displaystyle N
,
\displaystyle L
, and the weights
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
).
To see how the bound behaves in two extreme regimes, we state the following corollary.

Corollary 2
.

(i)

Fixed
\displaystyle N\ll L
.
When
\displaystyle N
is held constant and
\displaystyle L
grows,

E\;\approx\;\sum_{i=1}^{L}\|\alpha_{i}x_{i}\|_{2}.

(ii)

Fixed
\displaystyle L
,
\displaystyle N\to L
.

If
\displaystyle L
is fixed and the top set expands to the full sequence, i.e.
\displaystyle N\to L
,

E\;\longrightarrow\;0.

(9)

When
\displaystyle N
is small relative to the sequence length, most tokens are excluded and the expected distance is dominated by the individual contributions
\displaystyle\|\alpha_{i}x_{i}\|_{2}
of those low-weight tokens.
In contrast, as
\displaystyle N
approaches
\displaystyle L
, the context vector
\displaystyle s
eventually incorporates
all
embeddings, so the distance between
\displaystyle s
and the (now empty) set of non-selected tokens vanishes.

2.2
Geometrical interpretation

We now recast the analysis in geometric terms, focusing on the spatial arrangement of the token embeddings
\displaystyle x_{i}
.
When these vectors lie close together, the model struggles to separate informative from non-informative tokens, hindering training.
To quantify how many tokens can be reliably distinguished, we work under two standard geometric assumptions:

Assumption 1 (Uniform spherical distribution).

Each embedding lies uniformly on a
\displaystyle\,d
-dimensional sphere of radius
\displaystyle M
:

x_{i}\;\sim\;U\!\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(M)\bigr),\qquad i=1,\ldots,L,

where
\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(M)
denotes the
\displaystyle(d-1)
-sphere of radius
\displaystyle M
.
In practice, we
normalize
embeddings so that they lie on this sphere.

Assumption 2 (Minimum pairwise separation).

There exists a fixed lower bound on the distance between any two embeddings:

\min_{i\neq j}\;\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}\;=\;\delta\;>\;0,

ensuring that no two tokens collapse onto one another.
In experiments, we set
\displaystyle\delta
to the empirical minimum pairwise distance.

Under these assumptions, we can bound the number of embeddings that fall within a specified neighbourhood of the context vector, thereby quantifying the model’s effective resolution.

Let
\displaystyle I_{N}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\}
denote the indices of the
\displaystyle N
selected tokens and recall the context vector

s\;=\;\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\alpha_{i}x_{i}.

Fix a tolerance radius
\displaystyle r>0
and consider the closed Euclidean ball
\displaystyle B_{r}(s)
centred at
\displaystyle s
.
We say that a selected embedding
\displaystyle x_{i}
is
geometrically distinguishable
if its rescaled version
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}x_{i}
remains within
\displaystyle B_{r}(s)
while every non-selected embedding satisfies
\displaystyle\alpha_{j}x_{j}\notin B_{r}(s)
for
\displaystyle j\notin I_{N}
.
The count of such distinguishable embeddings is

N_{s}\;=\;\#\bigl\{\,i\in I_{N}\;:\;\|\alpha_{i}x_{i}-s\|_{2}\leq r\bigr\},

(10)

and the ratio
\displaystyle N_{s}/N
measures the fraction of “non-noise” vectors the model can reliably separate (see Fig.
1
for an illustration).

Our objective is to bound the expectation
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{s}]
and hence provide explicit upper and lower limits on the model’s effective resolution as a function of
\displaystyle r
,
\displaystyle N
, and
\displaystyle L
under Assumptions 1–2.

In the previous section, we examined separation in terms of Euclidean distances. The geometric approach introduced here, by contrast, focuses on directional separability and reframes the problem as one of metric learning. Now, we present the main result (for the proof, see Appendix
):

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions 1–2, the fraction of geometrically distinguishable embeddings satisfies

1\;-\;\frac{1}{rN}\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\xi_{i}\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{s}]}{N}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in I_{N}}\!\exp\!\Bigl[-\tfrac{(r-\xi_{i})^{2}}{16M^{2}}\Bigr],

(11)

where

\xi_{i}^{2}\;=\;M^{2}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I_{N}\\[2.0pt]
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\alpha_{j}^{2}\;+\;\Bigl(M^{2}-\tfrac{\delta^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\in I_{N}\\[2.0pt]
j\neq k\neq i\end{subarray}}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}.

(12)

The quantity
\displaystyle\xi_{i}
measures how widely the other
\displaystyle N-1
selected embeddings, rescaled by their weights, are spread around
\displaystyle x_{i}
.
If
\displaystyle\xi_{i}
is small, most selected points cluster near the context vector
\displaystyle s
, so many of them fall inside the ball
\displaystyle B_{r}(s)
and become distinguishable.
The lower bound in (
11
) subtracts from one a penalty proportional to the cumulative spread
\displaystyle\sum_{i}\xi_{i}
; the tighter the cluster (smaller
\displaystyle\xi_{i}
), the closer the ratio
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{s}]/N
is to
\displaystyle 1
.
Conversely, the upper bound shows that once
\displaystyle r
is smaller than
\displaystyle\xi_{i}
for many
\displaystyle i
, the exponential term decays rapidly, implying very few embeddings remain separable under the chosen radius.

2.3
Gradient Sensitivity of Attention

The results above show that a language model must sharply distinguish informative from non-informative tokens; in other words, the attention weight distribution should be as selective as possible. Refining that distribution, however, exposes a second difficulty:
gradient sensitivity
during training.

Consider two nearly identical logit vectors

\boldsymbol{\ell}^{(1)}=(0,\ldots,0,\,a,\,a+\varepsilon),\qquad\boldsymbol{\ell}^{(2)}=(0,\ldots,0,\,a+2\varepsilon,\,a),

so that their Euclidean distance satisfies

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\ell}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\ell}^{(2)}\|_{2}=\sqrt{5}\,\varepsilon
.
Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(2)}
be the corresponding softmax weights
(Equation (
2
)) and denote by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\ell}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
the Jacobian of the softmax map.
A first-order expansion gives

\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(2)}\bigr\|_{2}\;\approx\;\bigl\|\nabla_{\!\ell}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(1)}\,(\boldsymbol{\ell}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\ell}^{(2)})\bigr\|_{2}\;\sim\;\sqrt{2}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{T},

because the largest two logits swap order and the associated softmax gradient scales as
\displaystyle 1/T
.
Hence, even though the logits differ by only
\displaystyle O(\varepsilon)
, the output distribution can change by

\displaystyle O(\varepsilon/T)
.
For a sufficiently small temperature
\displaystyle T
(a common tactic for sharpening attention) this factor becomes large, making the gradient step volatile and potentially destabilising optimisation.

This example highlights a fundamental trade-off: stronger normalisation (smaller
\displaystyle T
, or any variant that steepens the softmax) improves token separability but simultaneously amplifies gradient variance, complicating training of deep transformers.

Let have a closer look at how normalisation affects gradient descent.
For the general scheme in (
3
), let
\displaystyle\{l_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
be the logits and
\displaystyle\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
the resulting attention weights.
We characterise the magnitude of the Jacobian
\displaystyle\nabla_{l}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Lemma 2
.

With the notation above,

\|\nabla_{l}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}\leq\min\left\{\|F^{\prime}\|_{2}\left(\frac{1}{L\min_{j}F(l_{j},\theta)}+\frac{\|F\|_{2}}{L^{2}\min_{j}F^{2}(l_{j},\theta)}\right),\sqrt{2}\right\}

(13)

where
\displaystyle\|F\|_{2}=\max_{i}|F(l_{i},\theta)|
and

\displaystyle\|F^{\prime}\|_{2}=\max_{i}|F^{\prime}(l_{i},\theta)|
.

The upper bound grows when any logit
\displaystyle l_{j}
pushes
\displaystyle F(l_{j},\theta)
toward zero, because both terms inside the parentheses scale inversely with
\displaystyle\min_{j}F(l_{j},\theta)
.
Thus, a sharply peaked normalisation—softmax with a small temperature, for instance, induces large Jacobian norms, signalling high gradient sensitivity during training.

Corollary 3
.

For the softmax normalisation in (
2
),

\bigl\|\nabla_{l}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;\min\!\left\{\frac{1}{4T},\;\sqrt{2}\right\}.

A smaller temperature
\displaystyle T
sharpens the softmax but simultaneously inflates the Jacobian norm (scaling as
\displaystyle 1/T
), making the attention distribution highly sensitive to even tiny logit perturbations and thus harder to train stably.

Thus—even with idealised spherical embeddings—softmax attention cannot cleanly separate more than about four-fifths of the tokens it selects; adding further tokens mainly dilutes the representation without improving geometric resolution.

4
Discussion

Softmax remains the default normalisation in modern Transformers because it
is simple, differentiable and lends a probabilistic interpretation to the
weights
(
vaswani2017attention
)
. Its limitations, however, are now well
established: it produces dense distributions, its peak value drops as the
context grows (
vanishing–attention
effect), and its Jacobian explodes
when the temperature is driven low, yielding unstable gradients.
Three main research lines have emerged to address these issues.

1.

Sparsity-inducing rules
such as Sparsemax and

\displaystyle\alpha
–Entmax replace the softmax exponential with
projections onto a simplex, producing exact zeros and lowering
entropy
(
martins2016softmaxsparsemaxsparsemodel
)
.

2.

Length-aware rescales
such as
Scalable–Softmax scale logits by
\displaystyle\log L
so that the maximum
attention weight stays roughly constant as the sequence grows,
alleviating the vanishing–attention problem
(
nakanishi2025scalablesoftmaxsuperiorattention
)
.

3.

Gradient-controlled variants
(e.g. Self-Adjusted
Softmax) re-weight logits according to the layer-wise dynamic
range, keeping the Jacobian spectrum in a healthy
band
(
zheng2025selfadjustsoftmax
)
.

Our work complements these efforts by
explaining, in
closed form, why the above modifications are necessary
.
Where previous papers introduced a new rule and showed empirical gains, we
derived non-asymptotic bounds (Theorems
1
–
2
) and
gradient limits (Lemma
2
) that
apply to
any

normalisation function

\displaystyle F(\cdot,\theta)
. In particular:

•

The
distance bound

shows that when
\displaystyle N
grows proportionally to
\displaystyle L
,
the representation distance
\displaystyle\tilde{d}
necessarily collapses to
zero, formalising the empirical softmax bottleneck noted by

yang2018breakingsoftmaxbottleneckhighrank
.
Our GPT-2 experiment (Fig.
2
, middle) confirms the
predicted plateau and justifies why sparse or length-aware rules
can improve long-context performance.

•

The
geometric bound

states that even under optimistic spherical assumptions no more
than
\displaystyle\approx 80\%
of the top-
\displaystyle N
tokens can stay inside the
selective ball (Fig.
3
). This explains why empirical
studies report that multiple heads are required to cover distinct
parts of the context: a single head cannot make every “important”
token simultaneously salient. The reason behind this idea is pretty simple. Assuming the independence of the heads as attention mechanism parts, we can conclude that for given separability level
\displaystyle p
,
\displaystyle H
heads can cover up to
\displaystyle 1-(1-p)^{H}
of the top-N tokens. Under our analysis, we see that for
\displaystyle p=0.8
it is needed approximatelly
\displaystyle H=3
to cover up the 99% of information.

•

The
gradient bound

recovers the familiar
\displaystyle 1/(4T)
law but also
shows how any normaliser with a vanishing minimum value
will inherit the same instability.
Figure
4
demonstrates that GPT-2 operates close to the
theoretical limit when
\displaystyle T<10^{-1}
, validating the usefulness of
gradient-controlled variants such as SA-Softmax.

The combined theory–experiment picture suggests three practical
guidelines.

1.

Keep the active set small.

The critical-
\displaystyle N
curve in Fig.
2
grows roughly
like
\displaystyle 0.06L
; selecting more tokens yields vanishing returns
and erodes separability. Top-
\displaystyle k
or sparse attention should be
preferred when
\displaystyle L\!\gg\!k
.

2.

Monitor attention entropy.

A rising entropy or a drop in the empirical

\displaystyle N_{s}/N
ratio is an early sign that a head has saturated its
geometric capacity; adding additional heads or switching to a
length-aware normaliser can restore separability.

3.

Avoid overly sharp softmax.

Lowering
\displaystyle T
below
\displaystyle 10^{-1}
increases Jacobian norms without
increasing separability (Figs.
3
,
4
).
Practitioners should instead use normalisers that decouple
selectivity from gradient health (e.g. Sparsemax,
SS-Max, or SA-Softmax).

Our analysis assumes embeddings are
a priori
L2-normalised and
roughly isotropic. Real models may violate these assumptions, and
future work should extend the geometric bound to non-spherical
distributions. Another promising direction is to design a
length-adaptive, gradient-controlled normaliser that inherits the best
properties of Sparsemax (sparsity), SS-Max (length awareness) and
SA-Softmax (stable Jacobians) while admitting proofs analogous to
Theorems
1
–
2
.

5
Conclusions

This work provides theoretical and empirical
analysis of normalisation in attention mechanisms beyond the classical
softmax. We derived two non-asymptotic bounds (Theorems
1
and

2
) that link token separability to sequence length
\displaystyle L
,
selection size
\displaystyle N
, and the embedding geometry, and we established a
general Jacobian bound (Lemma
2
) that explains the
well-known trade-off between sharpness and gradient stability.
Experiments on GPT-2 confirmed all three predictions:

•

the representation distance collapses once
\displaystyle N
grows
proportionally to
\displaystyle L
;

•

no more than
\displaystyle\approx 80\%
of the selected tokens can be
geometrically distinguished, even under ideal spherical
embeddings;

•

the empirical Jacobian norm tracks the theoretical

\displaystyle 1/(4T)
law, saturating at low temperature.

Taken together, these results recast softmax attention as a
selective
but capacity-limited aggregator
: it discriminates well only while the
active set is a small fraction of the context. The analysis also
clarifies why recently proposed normalisers—Sparsemax, Scalable-Softmax,
and Self-Adjusted Softmax—offer complementary benefits: they relax one
or more of the intrinsic limits quantified here.

Practical takeaways.

(1) limit the top-
\displaystyle k
set to a sub-linear function of the context
length; (2) monitor attention entropy or the
\displaystyle N_{s}/N
ratio during
training; and (3) prefer length-aware or sparsity-inducing normalisers
over aggressive temperature scaling.

Overall, our experiments shows several limitations providing the practical diagnostic idea:

1.

When the level of geometrical separability drops to
\displaystyle 70-80
%, this signifies that the head has saturated its geometric capacity.

2.

The temperature limitations shows that making the distribution sharp makes the Jacobian norm exploding. The teoretical and practical analysis shows that it’s better to avoid using the
\displaystyle T\leq 0.1
.

Future directions.

Immediate extensions include (i) relaxing the spherical-embedding
assumption, (ii) analysing multi-query and multi-head interactions, and
(iii) designing a single normalisation rule that is simultaneously
length-adaptive, sparse, and gradient-stable. We hope the quantitative
framework introduced here will serve as a benchmark for such
developments and, more broadly, for principled improvements to long-
context transformers.

Overall, the present study provides theoretical footing for the growing
body of work that modifies softmax, and furnishes quantitative tools for
diagnosing when a given attention head has reached its intrinsic limit.
