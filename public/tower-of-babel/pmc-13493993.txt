Soybean (Glycine max) germination response to static magnetic field treatment
Authors: Sai Tharuna Sree Gajula Lakshmipathi, Katarzyna Niemczyk, Izabela Michalak, Sylwia Lewandowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe focus of the present research is to evaluate the influence of a static magnetic field (SMF) on the germination and early growth of four soybean cultivars (Adelfia, Adessa, Pamela, and Abelina). Seeds were exposed to magnetic induction of 250 mT and 500 mT for 3 and 12 min. The results demonstrate that SMF treatment significantly enhanced seed vigour and germination kinetics, particularly in cultivars with lower initial quality (Adelfia, Pamela). The most pronounced improvement in the Seed Vigour Index (SVI) was observed in cv. Adessa (77.9% increase) and cv. Adelfia (74.4% increase) under 500 mT for 3 min compared to the control (p < 0.05), without exposure to the SMF. While the highest absolute vigour values were achieved by cv. Abelina under 250 mT and 500 mT for 3 min. Therefore, the study highlights that shorter exposure times (3 min) are more effective across all genotypes. These findings suggest that SMF can serve as an efficient, non-chemical tool for seed invigoration in sustainable soybean production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54735-2.Keywords: Soybean genotypes, Seed treatment, Magnetic field, Exposure time, Germination percentage, Chlorophyll contentSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Soybean is one of the legume plants which is highly adaptable, nutritious and crucial in commercial usage1. Native to East Asia, soybean has become one of the crops that have most significantly shaped modern global agriculture2. What is more, it has been an important nutritional part of the diet of many people living in China, Japan, Korea, the Philippines, and Indonesia, among others, through various types of products such as milk, tofu, and cheese. Soybeans provide all nine essential amino acids due to their unique kind of complete plant-based protein. Beyond food, soybean is also contributing to the worlds edible oil production (25%) and biggest contributor of about two-thirds of protein used in animal feed worldwide3.

It is worth mentioning that this plant has developed into the fourth most important and fastest growing crop in the world, after rice, wheat, and corn, in terms of global production. According to the estimation of the International Grains Council-IGC 2025, it can be observed that the global soybean cultivation, which reached 396 million tons in the 2023/24 season, has gained 22 million tons in the 2024/25 season, giving a total of 418 million tons3. It was speculated that this trend will carry over until the 2025–2026 season, when the production is expected to be superior to current levels. To satisfy the demand for soybean growth in the future, high-quality seeds are needed to achieve maximum germination rates and to continue an increase in plant production4. According to Lewandowska et al.5, soybean cultivation for many farmers is considered as “godsend” for correcting imbalanced crop rotations, which typically consist of cereals, maize, and rapeseed, but also a crop with some fascinating hidden attributes. This plant has another amazing feature that can fix atmospheric nitrogen by symbiotic association with soil bacteria (Bradyrhizobium japonicum), reducing the need for nitrogen mineral fertilizers and promoting soil health. In addition, soybean is a nutritious crop and also provides food for humans and feed for farm animals.

Nowadays one of the major challenges in the seed production sector is to eliminate the use of chemicals in agriculture to protect nature but also to get higher yields and better germination percentage. Still one of the major environmental problems that the world is facing at present is the increasing chemicalization of agriculture that through long-term consumption in food may lead to dangerous health problems6,7. Such practices not only harm the soil responsible for being incapable of producing nutritious crops, but also contaminate food, gradually poisoning both humans and animals with harmful chemicals. In the 21st century, modern agriculture is facing the challenge of using natural resources wisely in the process of finding safe ways to improve crop quality. One of the key factors to boost crop yields is the preparation of seed material by using safe conditioning methods. This helps to improve plant germination and makes seedlings stronger, more efficient for growing. Nowadays soybean seeds are often treated with insecticides, pesticides, beneficial bacterial to improve nitrogen fixation and protect against pathogens. However, Sartori’s8 recent study, provided that agrochemical seed treatments might disturb the rhizobial survival, nodulation process, and limits consequently plant health and yield8.

The most important step in establishing healthy seedlings for successful crop production is the process of seed germination4. Seed quality plays a significant role in crop growth affected by environmental factors during germination, plant growth, maturation, and storage after harvest. Germination is a complex physiological process that starts when seeds absorb water and ends with the radicle emergence9. Increasing the germination rate is at present a challenge for modern farming. As a result, seed companies are constantly looking for new solutions to improve seed germination, vigour, seedling strength, and crop yield. This has led to more studies of different seed improvement methods.

Since soybean is very popular, researchers continue to explore new techniques for promoting and stimulating its growth. One of the ways to enhance the seed production sector could be the use of physical stimulation factors. Physical methods used for improving seed quality include exposure to electromagnetic induction (such as infrared, ionizing, laser, or ultraviolet) or the application of various types of fields including magnetic, electric, or electromagnetic10.

The main goal of this research was to look for alternative and safe solutions for soybean growth stimulation that will not cause negative effects on nature or significantly reduce them. According to Kataria et al.11 treating the seeds with a magnetic field before sowing is considered as eco-friendly technology that is healthy for both the environment and plants. Analysing various types of magnetic field, the static magnetic field is constant and unchanging in nature. What is more, SMF can stimulate seed germination by affecting only the seeds, hence emergency dynamics are enhanced and protecting seedling growth. The outcomes of such treatments include greater plant vigor, emergence and higher yield11,12. Also, in Radhakrishnan’s research pointed out the interest in magnetic field (MF) applications particularly in agricultural science, where studies have examined MF’s influence on seed germination, biochemical and hormonal changes, plant development, and final crop yield. Still more research is needed to clarify the underlying cellular and tissue-level mechanisms of magnetic action13. However, examples from many scientists demonstrate the positive impact of static magnetic fields on soybean seeds and other legume species (Table 1).

The aim of this research was to examine the effect of static magnetic field treatment on seed germination, early growth and development of four cultivars of soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merrill) – Adelfia, Adessa, Pamela and Abelina coming from growing season 2023. It was hypothesized that the physical factor, which is SMF would significantly improve soybean germination and early growth parameters under laboratory conditions. The magnetic field intensities (250 and 500 mT) and exposure times (3 and 12 min) were selected based on previous studies indicating that moderate static magnetic fields within this range may stimulate seed germination and early seedling growth, while avoiding potential inhibitory effects associated with excessive exposure. The chosen exposure times represent short and prolonged treatments commonly applied in laboratory studies on magnetic seed stimulation, enabling evaluation of both intensity- and time-dependent responses. The proposed approach may not only increase soybean yields in the future, but also reduce the use of chemicals in agriculture.

Materials and methods

Soybean seeds

Soybean seeds (Glycine max (L.) Merrill), cv. Adelfia, Adessa, Pamela, and Abelina (not genetically modified) of uniform size were used in the present study. The seeds were obtained in 2023 from the SAATBAU Polska plant breeding station located in Poland. They were certified. Different genotypes were chosen of varied maturity groups: Adelfia — according to breeders — is a medium late variety, rich in protein and oil content, with high growth potential in low-yielding plants. Adessa is a very early and early variety. It is distinguished by its high fat and protein content, very high early vigor, tolerance to cold weather and resistance to lodging. It also has very good resistance to pod cracking. Pamela is an early to mid-early cultivar, so it is recommended for cultivation throughout the country - Poland with a guarantee of ripening at the optimum time. Its high yield potential, high-set of the first pod, and excellent health (as per the manufacturer’s information) have been successfully confirmed in official and breeding experiments. The last evaluated cultivar - Abelina belongs to the group of early varieties and is distinguished by its high yield potential and exceptional percentage over years. It is a purple flowering variety with a dark stigma that reaches very early maturity. Due to its very intensive early vigour, Abelina obtains a medium to high density of plants and is regarded the best yielding genotype in relation to the length of the vegetation period. In terms of quality, it is a variety with a very high-fat content and a high protein yield. The experimental seed material was from the 2023 growing season that were kept under the standard seed storage conditions. Seeds were obtained from a single commercial lot and visually inspected for uniformity before experimentation. A preliminary germination test was performed prior to the main experiment to verify the uniformity of the seed lot used in the study.

Exposure of soybean seeds to a static magnetic field

Before stimulation with SMF, soybean seeds were grouped by 100 seeds. The next stimulation with a MF was carried out using SMF with an induction of 250 mT and 500 mT. Permanent NdFeB magnets were used in the experiment. During the stimulation process, a special container was used, which allowed to ensure a constant value of the magnetic field vector on the seeds during the magnetization process. Exposure times of 3 and 12 min were used for seed stimulation. The selection of the time of exposure of seeds to the magnetic field was made based on literature reports in which the best positive effects were noted17,23–26,59. Treatment of SMF was done at room temperature (22 ± 1 °C) under laboratory conditions without further humidity control. The seeds were exposed to the magnetic field and sown the next day. Then the germination tests were subsequently performed in a growth chamber at 25 °C according to ISTA (International Seed Testing Association) methodology. Preliminary test of soybean seeds

In order to check the general germination percentage of raw soybean seeds (without treatment), preliminary tests were conducted before the main experiment. 50 seeds of each soybean variety were sown using two different methods of filter paper substrate – between paper (BP) and pleated paper (PP) to choose the best option. The substrate in both methods was soaked in the same volume of distillated water. The preliminary test showed visible germination and an effective seedling growth in the pleated paper method over between paper (Fig. 1). Figure 1 presents the germination process based on the selected variety-Abelina. Germination tests

These tests were performed under laboratory conditions in a growth chamber (FITO, Biogenet, Poland) at a constant temperature of 25 °C. A pleated paper substrate was used to conduct the experiment which was carried out in 4 replications for all soybean variety. Each treated cultivar was compared with control. The untreated seeds (control) for each cultivar were maintained under the same experimental conditions. Each replication contained 100 soybean seeds. According to ISTA, the first counting (energy of seedlings) was done after 5 days and the second one (germination of seedlings) after 8 days. It is important to add that the day of setting up the experiment has not been taken into account only the next day. Based on ISTA rules, after 8 days the seeds were segregated into normal and abnormal seedlings (Fig. 2).

Segregation of soybean into (a) normal seedlings and (b) abnormal seedlings based on the main experiment (Photo: T. Sree).

The soybean seedling is regarded as normal (healthy), when the primary root is undamaged or has minor damage in the form of discoloration, cracks, spots or splits. Besides, a seedling can be classified as normal even when the primary root is damaged or missing, but secondary roots are developed enough. Usually the differences between well-developed and abnormal seedlings are connected with dwarfism, rotten due to primary infection, lack of chlorophyll (albinotic seedling), an irregular hypocotyl shape or its different defects. Tiny defects are accepted. The cotyledons are undamaged or have small defects (according to the current 50% rule, no more than 50% of the total surface might be damaged). Normally there are two cotyledons but sometimes there may be even three of them or only one, provided that it is healthy and not damaged. What is more, the first leaves are undamaged or have small defects (in accordance with the current 50% rule, no more than 50% of the total surface may be damaged), there might be three leaves or only one normal leaf provided that it is not damaged. It is worth adding that the top bud should not be infected. A seedling is defined as abnormal (with defects) if is broken, deformed, de-structured, white or yellow, glassy, or rotten as a result of primary infection. Abnormal seedlings are always rejected for biometrical analysis and in natural conditions will never develop in a well-structured plant.

Seed germination was calculated and expressed as percentage. The minimum germination limit for soybean is 80% (in accordance with ISTA regulation). Soybean germination is considered as epigeal because cotyledons are pulled above the soil. The hypocotyl (part of the stem below the cotyledon) elongates while the length of the epicotyl (part of the stem above the cotyledon) remains the same. The following parameters were analysed in germination tests based on 30 seedlings selected randomly from each repetition: root length, hypocotyl and epicotyl length, including also chlorophyll content (SPAD). Chlorophyll content was measured in soybean leaves by using non-destructive method - the handheld chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502 m, Konica Minolta, Japan). Measurements were taken on fully developed first true leaves of soybean seedlings on the 8th day of the germination test. Chlorophyll content was measured in 40 randomly selected soybean seedlings from each of the four replicates within a given treatment group, and the results were averaged. The SPAD index was calculated as a relative indicator of chlorophyll content rather than converted into absolute chlorophyll concentration.

The main purpose of this research was to check whether seeds exposure to a SMF (1) enhances soybean germination and seedling growth compared to untreated seeds, (2) stimulates better the soybean estimated parameters (3) and which varieties from tested plant species are showing the best biometric parameters.

Seed Vigour Index (SVI)

Seed vigour was calculated (from the mean values) using the formula described by Abdul-Baki and Anderson27.

SVI = germination percentage (%) x total seedling length (root length + shoot length).

Statistical analysis

Statistica version 13.0 (TIBCO Software Inc., Tulsa, Ok, USA) was used to elaborate the obtained results statistically. For germination parameters (GP and GS), basic statistical analysis (mean and standard deviation) of all experimental groups was carried out. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyse the differences between groups and cultivars separately with 4 replicates, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. For morphological parameters (root length, shoot length and chlorophyll content), data are presented as median with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3). Differences among treatments were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant differences were detected, multiple comparisons between groups were performed using Dunn’s test. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the measured parameters.

Results and discussion

The present research focused on exposure of soybean seeds to static magnetic fields to increase germination percentage, growth parameters (root and shoot length), and chlorophyll content in leaves. In crop production, seed germination is a crucial phase; however, its efficiency and uniformity are often limited by low seed vigour, which slows down the establishment of the plantation26,28,29. Seed vigour and germination speed may be enhanced by traditional chemical methods, however, their repeated use harms soil, microorganisms, the environment, and human health30–33. According to Hu et al.34, Pawelek et al.7 and Sarraf et al.35, stated that SMF is one of the physical factors, which gives promising results on plant seedling growth and development34,35. In recent years, physical methods such as the use of static magnetic field have become a safe and environmentally friendly alternative30,36. SMF may affects seeds structurally, genetically, and biochemically, depending on parameters like intensity, duration, and plant type13,30,36. SMF treatments have been shown to improve germination, seedling growth, and chlorophyll content in many crops such as barley, soybean, cucumber, wheat, and maize4,9,11,12,37,38.

Static magnetic field (SMF) treatment influenced the germination percentage (GP) in all tested soybean cultivars (Fig. 4). In most experimental groups (250 mT + 3 min, 250 mT + 12 min, 500 mT + 3 min, 500 mT + 12 min), GP was higher than in the control, with the exception of Abelina exposed to 250 mT for 12 min. Among the cultivars, the germination percentage for Abelina was higher in most of the treatments compared to control and then followed by Adessa, Pamela, and Adelfia. In the control group, Adelfia had the lowest GP (55%), however the greatest relative improvement was achieved after SMF treatment, especially at 500 mT for 3 min, where GP increased to 79%. Also based on breeder information, cv. Adelfia has performed lower GP in the field but tend to produce better yield. Fortunately, in our research, the use of SMF treatment led to positive improvement in cv. Adelfia. The relatively lower germination percentage observed in the control group of cv. Adelfia can be attributed to cultivar-specific seed lot variability rather than to the presence of seed dormancy. Across both levels of magnetic induction, the shorter exposure time (3 min) generally resulted in a higher GP of soybean seeds compared to the 12 min treatment. This effect was more pronounced at 500 mT, where the seeds of cultivars exhibited significantly improved germination percentage after the 3 min exposure. In contrast, at the lower induction of 250 mT, the duration of seeds exposure to SMF had a less significant impact, with the exception of cv. Abelina. For this cultivar, the 3 min treatment was notably more effective than the control. Overall, in most varieties, the 250 mT induction tended to yield a higher GP than the 500 mT treatment when longer exposure times were applied. The findings suggest that SMF stimulation was strongly genotype-dependent.

The effect of exposure time on soybean seed germination for a constant magnetic induction value of 250 mT

Comparing the seed exposure time of 3 and 12 min to the magnetic field (250 mT), the germination percentage of the seeds of the Adelfia, Adessa and Pamela varieties was similar, whereas for the cv. Abelina a significantly higher GP was obtained for the exposure time of 3 min than for 12 min.

The effect of exposure time on soybean seed germination for a constant magnetic induction value of 500 mT

Differences in the percentage of germinated seeds depending on the exposure time – 3 and 12 min for a constant magnetic induction value of 500 mT were more visible than for an induction of 250 mT. Higher GP values were obtained for the 3-minute exposure time compared to the 12-minute time for all evaluated varieties. Generally, it was observed that a shorter seed stimulation time with a magnetic field of a given induction (250 or 500 mT) determines higher GP values.

The effect of the magnetic field induction value on soybean seed germination for an exposure time of 3 min

With a shorter exposure time of seeds, the magnetic field induction value does not significantly affect GP. For the cv. Adessa, Pamela, and Abelina, the inductions of 250 and 500 mT are comparable. Only for the Adelfia variety, higher results were obtained for the 500 mT SMF.

The effect of the magnetic field induction value on soybean seed germination for an exposure time of 12 min

For a 12-minute exposure time of soybean seeds to a magnetic field, the GP was higher for 250 mT magnetic induction than for 500 mT for three evaluated varieties, except for Abelina.

Based on the conducted research, the cv. Adelfia can be recommended for further study and pre-sowing seed stimulation with a magnetic field induction of 500 mT for 3 min. Similarly, in the work of Dziergowska et al.12 the influence of SMF (250 mT + 3 min) on the process of soybean seeds germination showed significantly better germination percentage (90%) compared to control (85%)12. The same changes were also observed by Lewandowska et al.17 In her research a magnetic field induction (400 mT) and exposure time (3 min) along with macroalgal extract applied to soybean seeds showed that GP was increased in comparison with control. Talking about another legume plant - chickpea, pre-treatment of seeds with SMF (100 mT for 1 h) significantly enhanced GP and growth parameters (root and shoot length, and vigor indices) under different levels of salinity39. Chickpea seeds also showed improvement in germination rate and seedling growth after being treated with SMF with induction of 100 mT for 1 h21. Not only in legume plants but also in cereals, static magnetic field was applied. For example, Katsenios et al.40 treated durum wheat seeds with MF (12.5 mT for 45 min), Luo et al.41 treated brown rice seeds with SMF (10 mT for 60 min) and for both plants, an increase in GP was observed in comparison with control. Few researchers also proved that the speed of germination and germination percentage increased in crops like barley42 and wheat43 after using magnetic field for seed treatment. Moreover, vegetable seeds, e.g., tomatoes, exposed to a MF (250 mT for 20 min) showed positive germination results (95%) compared to unexposed seeds44.

Effect of SMF on soybean germination speed (GS)

The application of SMF on soybean seeds has increased the germination speed in all four varieties compared to the control group (Supplementary Materials - Fig. 1). GS was found to be high especially at lower exposure time − 3 min for both inductions (250 mT and 500 mT). These results align with the existing literature where magnetic field treatment has the potential to improve seed germination by enhancing water uptake, enzyme activation (e.g., α-amylase), and metabolic rate during initial imbibition45. Among the examined varieties, Abelina and Adessa had a consistently higher germination speed across treatments, whereas cv. Adelfia showed not only a higher germination speed but also the greatest relative improvement compared to its control.

Effect of SMF on seedling growth parameters: root length (RL), shoot length (SL) and chlorophyll content in leaves

The effect of static magnetic field treatment on root length (RL), shoot length (SL), and chlorophyll content differed in cultivar-specific ways (Table 3). In general, most of the treated groups had a higher root length than the control. The best results in RL were observed at 3 min exposure of seeds to SMF. Nonetheless, responses varied among cultivars suggesting genotype-specific sensitivity to SMF. Treatment of seeds with SMF also had a positive impact on shoot length especially at shorter duration (3 min), suggesting that longer exposure (12 min) may not further enhance early growth. The content of chlorophyll (SPAD index) tended to be higher in treated seedlings than in the control. Unlike growth parameters, a higher chlorophyll content with increasing exposure time (12 min) to SMF was observed, particularly at 500 mT. Interestingly in Adelfia cultivar, the highest chlorophyll content was found in the group 500 mT + 12 min, although there was no increase in root length. This implies that chlorophyll accumulation and root elongation could be independently responsive to SMF treatment in early development of seedlings. Overall, the interaction of the cultivar - SMF parameters suggests that optimization of treatment should consider genotype-specific responses.

The effect of exposure time on soybean root length, shoot length, and chlorophyll content for a constant magnetic induction value of 250 mT

The root length (RL) for seedlings of Adessa, Pamela and Abelina varieties exposed to 250 mT SMF for 3 and 12 min was similar, but for cv. Adelfia was slightly higher in the group treated with 12 min of exposure than to 3 min. In terms of SL, the maximum parameter was found for 3 min exposure time for Adessa, Pamela and Abelina than 12 min. However, the Adelfia variety showed almost the same results for both exposure times (3 and 12 min). The chlorophyll content in the leaves of Adessa, Pamela and Abelina was significantly higher in the group with a longer exposure time (12 min), whereas cv. Adelfia had almost the same chlorophyll content in both groups (SMF 250 mT + 3 min and SMF 250 mT + 12 min). But it was higher for shorter exposure time − 3 min.

The effect of exposure time on soybean root length, shoot length, and chlorophyll content for a constant magnetic induction value of 500 mT

For the application of 500 mT SMF, the results of RL were almost the same for both seed exposure times to SMF but were higher for Adessa, and Abelina varieties in a shorter duration (3 min). In the case of cv. Pamela and Adelfia the length of the root was higher in the group in which the seeds were exposed for 12 min than for 3 min. Regarding SL, the results were very clear, the dominated exposure time (the highest value of SL) was 3 min than 12 min for all the varieties especially in Adessa (statistically significant differences were observed). The application of SMF with the induction of 500 mT for 12 min directly to seeds increased the chlorophyll content in seedlings in all tested soybean varieties in comparison with the exposure time for 3 min. The longer exposure time (12 min) of the seeds to SMF resulted in a significantly higher chlorophyll content in the seedlings than for a shorter time (3 min). Probably it might be due to its potential to trigger hormonal changes that promote chloroplast development46, upregulate chlorophyll biosynthesis genes47, and enhance photosynthetic efficiency47.

Root length varied among treatments and differed from the control, with no consistent improvement under SMF exposure. In several cases, particularly under 500 mT treatments, RL was comparable to or lower than the control. In contrast, chlorophyll content tended to be higher under the same conditions, especially at 500 mT for 12 min. This inverse or non-parallel response suggests that chlorophyll accumulation and root elongation may be regulated by partially independent physiological mechanisms.

The effect of the magnetic field induction value on the soybean root length, shoot length and chlorophyll content for an exposure time of 3 min

In general, comparing the results of RL of soybean in treated with 250 and 500 mT SMF for 3 min, were almost parallel to each other in the experimental groups. Within the examined varieties, cv. Adelfia and Abelina showed longer roots at high induction (500 mT), whereas Pamela and Adessa at 250 mT. For SL, no significant differences were observed between two exposure times but SL of Adelfia and Adessa was higher at 500 mT than at 250 mT. For cv. Pamela and Abelina, SL increased in the group treated with SMF at 250 mT. The dependencies for chlorophyll content were the same as for RL between the tested varieties. For Adelfia and Abelina varieties, higher chlorophyll content was found in the groups treated with higher SMF induction (500 mT), whereas for Pamela and Adessa in the groups with lower induction (250 mT). Notably, statistically significant differences for a given parameter were between two groups (500 mT + 3 min) in Adelfia followed by (250 mT + 3 min) in Adessa.

The effect of the magnetic field induction value on the soybean root length, shoot length and chlorophyll content for an exposure time of 12 min

In case of longer exposure time of soybean seeds to the magnetic field, the length of roots was higher in the group treated with lower induction (250 mT) than in the case of higher induction of 500 mT for three evaluated varieties, with the exception of Abelina for which the results were opposite. For SL, Adelfia and Adessa varieties responded well in the groups with 250 mT in comparison with 500 mT but for Pamela and Abelina it was reverse. The chlorophyll content in soybean leaves was higher in the groups with a higher induction (500 mT) than lower (250 mT) among all the varieties.

In conclusion, based on the conducted experiment, pre-sowing seed stimulation with a magnetic induction of 500 mT for 3 min can be recommended to increase seedling growth and chlorophyll content.

Seedling growth and morphometric response

The application of a static magnetic field induced significant genotype-specific alterations in the early developmental stages of the studied cultivars. Seedling measurements were rounded to 0.1 cm to reflect the precision of the manual recording instruments.

Shoot Elongation: A pronounced stimulatory effect on shoot length (the sum of hypocotyl and epicotyl) was observed in Adessa, Pamela, and Abelina. The most substantial increase in SL was recorded for Abelina cultivated in the group 250 mT + 3 min (from 10.0 cm in control to 14.0 cm) and for Adessa grown in the group 500 mT + 3 min, reaching a peak of 15.1 cm.

Root System: In contrast to the shoot, the root system exhibited higher sensitivity to increased magnetic flux density. In the Pamela cultivar, seed exposure to 500 mT for 3 and 12 min resulted in a significant reduction in primary root length (9.0–9.5 cm) compared to the control (11.0 cm).

Cultivar Stability: Adelfia demonstrated the highest morphometric stability, with significant changes limited primarily to the hypocotyl and epicotyl regions, while root length remained largely unaffected across most treatments (remaining at approximately 8.0–9.0 cm).

Chlorophyll content

Analysis of chlorophyll levels in soybean seedlings revealed a complex physiological response to magnetic induction.

Growth-Pigment Trade-off: In the Adessa cultivar, the maximum shoot length (500 mT + 3 min) was inversely correlated with chlorophyll content, which dropped to its minimum value of 24.50 compared to 29.90 in the control.

Exposure Duration Effects: For Adessa and Pamela, extending the exposure time to 12 min favored chlorophyll accumulation, suggesting an adaptive physiological mechanism to prolonged SMF presence.

Cultivar-Specific Vulnerability: Abelina was identified as the most sensitive genotype regarding photosynthetic apparatus stability, showing a drastic decline in chlorophyll content in soybean seedlings grown in the group the 250 mT + 3 min treatment (from 28.90 to 19. 90).

Stimulatory Response in Adelfia: Conversely, Adelfia exhibited a positive response to high - induction of SMF (500 mT + 12 min), with chlorophyll content increasing significantly from 37.50 to 54.00.

Effect of SMF on seed vigour index

The analysis of the Seed Vigour Index (SVI) revealed a pronounced genotype-dependent response to SMF treatment (Supplementary Materials - Table 1). The most significant enhancement in seed vigour was observed in cv. Adessa, where seed exposure to 500 mT for 3 min resulted in the highest relative increase of 77.9% (from 1401 to 2492) compared to the control. Although cv. Abelina reached the highest absolute SVI values (2681), its relative improvement was lower (58.2%). Interestingly, for cv. Adelfia, which characterized the lowest initial quality (control SVI of 847), the SMF treatment of seeds at 500 mT for 3 min provided a substantial invigoration effect, increasing its vigour by 74.4%. These findings suggest that while SMF effectively boosts high-quality seed lots, its potential is most transformative for genotypes with lower or moderate initial vigour, such as Adessa and Adelfia.

Correlation analysis between measured parameters

Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed treatment-dependent relationships between the tested parameters (GP, RL, SL and Chlorophyll content) for the examined soybean cultivars (Supplementary Materials - Table 2).

Firstly regarding cv. Adelfia, under control group, shoot length was positively correlated with germination percentage (r = 0.99, p = 0.01), while at 250 mT for both exposure durations (3 and 12 min) none of the measured parameters showed statistically significant correlations (p < 0.05). Root length was found to be negatively correlated with shoot length (r = − 0.98, p = 0.02) under 500 mT for 3 min. However, at 500 mT for 12 min, germination percentage was positively correlated with chlorophyll content (r = 0.98, p = 0.02). Secondly regarding cv. Adessa, in the control group, a strong positive correlation was observed between shoot length and chlorophyll content (r = 0.98, p = 0.02). Unfortunately, there were no statistically significant correlations between the parameters tested (250 mT for 3 min, 250 mT for 12 min, 500 mT for 3 min, and 500 mT for 12 min) for root length, shoot length, germination percentage, and chlorophyll content in the experimental groups (p > 0.05). Regarding cv. Pamela, in the group 250 mT for 12 min, a strong negative correlation was found between root length and shoot length (r = − 0.96, p = 0.04). However in the other groups, no significant correlations were observed. In case of cv. Abelina, under control group, a very strong negative correlation was observed between shoot length and germination percentage (r = − 0.99, p = 0.01). There were no statistically significant correlations among the parameters tested in other groups.

Overall, the results indicate considerable variability among cultivars, suggesting that responses to static magnetic field are genotype-dependent and not uniform. Strong correlations between parameters were observed only for specific cultivars under particular magnetic inductions and exposure times. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as they cannot be generalized. Nevertheless, SMF may influence plant growth, although its effectiveness depends on the treatment conditions and genotype. Further studies, particularly under field conditions, are required to obtain more consistent and agronomically relevant evidence.

Plant seeds could respond differently to SMF, depending on plant variety and experimental conditions, including induction level and duration of SMF exposure. Generally, many studies have conducted experiments on magnetic fields and reported that magnetic seed treatment can explain how electromagnetism affects plants by influencing light, heat, and chemical processes48–50. Seeds stimulated by electric or magnetic fields can either help or harm their growth. Electric fields cause the movement of water and nutrients in plants, thereby enhancing growth. Similarly, magnetic fields can improve seed sprouting and seedling length by affecting particles, such as ions: Ca, K, H, and Cl, as well as electrons and free radicals, which in turn influence metabolism, water movement, and growth processes51 in plant cells30. But, excessive exposure to magnetic fields can induce stress to plants and inhibit seedling growth as mentioned in the research of Morillo-Coronado30. Consistent with this, Morillo-Coronado et al.30 demonstrated that low magnetic exposure (21 mT and 10 mT for 1 h) improved onion seeds germination and seedling growth with statistically significant differences compared to the control30.

Having longer roots, especially those with well-developed lateral roots, enhance nutrient uptake efficiency from the soil50. Studies have suggested that SMF can significantly improve root length, potentially enhancing plant resistance to environmental stressors. A deeper and more extensive root system enables water uptake capacity which is quite beneficial especially in a drought situation52,53. Previous studies have proved that the application of SMF (200 mT for 1 h) applied to soybean seeds significantly improved root growth36. Likewise, studies on maize (Zea mays L.)54and sun flower (Helianthus annus)55 showed that there was a significant improvement in root length compared to untreated seeds (control) under laboratory conditions54,55. According to the author54, RL root length of maize was increased doubl after pre-sowing seed exposure to magnetic field (200 mT for 1 h) relative to the control. Similar results were obtained for maize (Zea mays L.) and barley (Hordeum vulgare L.), where SMF (200 mT for 1 h) in maize and (125 mT) in barley caused the significant increase in the length of root system, root diameter and root dry weight56,57. The observed enhancement in root length following SMF treatment is consistent with findings in other species, such as soybean17 and lentils58.

Research results indicate that seed treatment with a magnetic field affects shoot length. Magnetically stimulated seeds with a shorter exposure time (3 min) had better results in terms of the shoot length in soybean seedlings compared to the control12. Corresponding results were observed in the current study, where short-duration SMF exposure (3 min) enhanced shoot growth in soybean seedlings. Comparable findings have been reported in chickpea seeds, where varying SMF intensities and shorter exposure times significantly affected shoot length20. In contrast, high exposure magnetic fields can slow down shoot growth by disrupting key cellular processes like cell division and cell elongation59. Seeds stimulated by strong MF inductions can cause oxidative stress, which leads to an increase in harmful molecules called reactive oxygen species58,60. These molecules act against growth and plant cells. The exact mechanism of growth changes in response to static magnetic fields remains unclear; however, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and their scavengers might play an important role in plant responses to magnetic treatment41,58,60. ROS are oxygen-containing, highly reactive molecules that can interact with cellular components. In fact, excess ROS accumulation leads to damaging cells and oxidative stress. However, when maintained at controlled levels, it functions as an essential signaling role in plant growth and stress responses58. Additionally, strong magnetic fields can disrupt the distribution of auxins (the hormones that help plants growth), which leads to stunted shoot development35. Moreover, longer duration of seed exposure to SMF may also disrupt the movement of nutrients and water within plant tissues, further contributing to growth inhibition30,35,59.

Chlorophyll is necessary for the development of seedlings growth and health. It directly influences plant growth by determining photosynthetic efficiency and energy capture. Moreover, increased chlorophyll content generally support better growth and structure of the plant. The effect of SMF on the chlorophyll content has been widely studied due to its importance in plant physiology61. Ercan et al.57 investigated the effects of magnetic fields of 20, 45, 125, 250 mT on the chlorophyll content in barley (Hordeum vulgare) and reported that 20 mT significantly increased this parameter in comparison with untreated seeds. In this case, the highest chlorophyll content in leaves was observed in the group with low magnetic induction (20 mT), whereas the lowest content for the highest MF induction – 250 mT 57. However, in few publications, including present study, it was observed that the chlorophyll content was higher in species such as soybean (Glycine max)12,15, maize (Zea mays)55, sunflower (Helianthus annus)55, sugar beet (Beta vulgaris L.)62, lupin (Lupinus angustifolius)63 under higher magnetic inductions in comparison to control. It might be connected with the fact that, higher magnetic inductions are responsible for enhancing metabolic activities, then they lead to increase chlorophyll synthesis in the leaves46,47.

The application of a static magnetic field induced significant, albeit cultivar-specific, physiological and morphometric shifts in soybean seedling development. Our findings indicate that SMF treatment can serve as an effective physical seed-conditioning agent, though its efficacy is heavily dictated by the genetic background. A notable observation is the biphasic response, characteristic of hormesis, where a lower magnetic flux density (250 mT) combined with short-term exposure (3 min) acted as a potent bio-stimulant for shoot elongation, particularly in cultivars Adessa and Abelina59.

A critical phenomenon identified in this study is the resource allocation trade-off. Under high-intensity exposure (500 mT), cultivars Adessa and Pamela exhibited a “shade avoidance-like” syndrome, characterized by rapid shoot elongation at the expense of chlorophyll biosynthesis. This suggests that elevated SMF levels may be perceived as an environmental stressor, triggering an emergency escape mechanism that prioritizes vertical growth while downregulating energy-expensive metabolic pathways, such as pigment production64,65.

In contrast, the root system demonstrated a higher sensitivity to the magnetic threshold. The significant reduction in root length observed in Pamela at 500 mT highlights that the root apical meristem (RAM) might be a primary site for magnetic perception. Excessive flux density likely disrupts normal gravitropism or cell division within this region. The inconsistency in the correlations between germination, root growth, and chlorophyll content in Adelfia, Adessa, Pamela, and Abelina confirms that the genotype is the decisive factor in magnetoreception, determining whether SMF acts as a growth enhancer or a physiological constraint59,65.

The positive response suggests that SMF treatment can be feasibly integrated into existing seed processing systems through conveyor-based magnetic exposure units. As a non-chemical approach, it offers advantages such as low recurring cost and suitability for organic farming. However, further studies are needed to standardize treatment protocols across crops and evaluate large-scale economic feasibility.

Hence, the current experiment presents that different level of SMF inductions (high and low) clearly show that not every genotype × treatment interaction has a positive effect, sometimes it the effect can be negative. This could potentially indicate the ineffectiveness of using too strong a magnetic field or inappropriate laboratory conditions, which could lead to inhibition of germination, seedling growth and leaf chlorophyll content in delicate genotypes66.

The mechanism of action of the magnetic field on seeds

Exposure of seeds to MF can cause biological effects depending on the application. Based on data from the literature, Fig. 5 presents that MF triggers several interrelated processes that ultimately increase germination percentage of the seeds. The physiological mechanisms underlying the observed effects of the SMF treatment of seeds were not directly investigated in the present study. Therefore, the proposed explanations are based on previously published literature and should be interpreted with caution.

Flow chart of mechanistic pathways linking magnetic field induction to seed germination. The diagram represents a conceptual model based on the published literature.

Magnetic intensities can penetrate directly into the plant tissues and influence several cellular structures and physiological processes. The energy needed for seed germination and cellular growth is supplied primarily through mitochondrial respiration and ATP production67. The cell membrane may also be affected, as magnetic field can modify ion transport and membrane permeability, thereby influencing important ions such as potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and hydrogen (H)51,68.

Moreover, MF applied to seeds can affect various metabolic reactions, leading to changes in proteins and enzymes (amylase)35,69. Furthermore, magnetic fields can induce variations in DNA transcription or replication activity70, which leads to the potentiality of gene expression patterns that result into increased growth and enhanced stress tolerance in plants70.

Seeds treated with MF can induce significant physiological and biological changes in plants71. These physiological and biochemical modifications may enhance seed metabolism, improve water uptake through increased hydrophilicity during imbibition, and support hormonal regulation involving auxins and gibberellins, which are essential for cell division and elongation71–73.

As a result of these processes (physiological and biological), the germination percentage of seeds sown in the substrate (e.g., soil) may be increased. Then they need less time for sprouting and germinate faster, which ensures the successful development of seedlings72. Moreover, the developed seedlings are more likely to be more vigorous, as they tend to have stronger root and shoot systems, which makes them more resilient and productive74. Therefore, the effectiveness of MF depends on factors like intensity, exposure time, and field orientation, as insufficient intensity may produce negligible effects, whereas excessive exposure can induce stress responses and inhibit plant growth.

The mechanistic explanations proposed in this study are based on published literature. The transport of ions, membrane permeability, balance of reactive oxygen species, enzymatic activity, and phytohormonal regulation have been proposed to be affected by static magnetic fields. These processes, however, are not directly measured in the present experiment and therefore must be considered as potential mechanisms but not as experimentally proven pathways. Importantly, the present results were obtained under controlled laboratory conditions. Even though SMF has potential to be used as a non-chemical seed conditioning method, further studies are being conducted under pot experiment and field conditions to evaluate its agronomic relevance.

Conclusion

The treatment of soybean seeds with the static magnetic field affected the germination percentage and the soybean seedling growth in the early stage of development in controlled laboratory conditions. Differences were observed in response to cultivar, value of magnetic induction, and exposure time. Short-term (3 min) exposure produced more significant effects on germination and seedling development than longer exposures. The findings show that SMF treatment can be a physical seed-conditioning agent, but its efficacy seems to be genotype variable. The inconsistency in the correlations between germination, root growth and chlorophyll content indicates that magnetic stimulation might influence various physiological parameters during the initial growth of plants. The agronomic significance of SMF seed treatment requires further confirmation in real-world greenhouse and field conditions. Further studies are recommended in the area of physiological and biochemical pathways using magnetic stimulation and the optimum parameters of treatment in various soybean varieties.
