Feature Attribution from First Principles
Magamed Taimeskhanov, Damien Garreau

Abstract
Feature attribution methods are a popular approach to explain the behavior of machine learning models. They assign importance scores to each input feature, quantifying their influence on the model’s prediction. However, evaluating these methods empirically remains a significant challenge. To bypass this shortcoming, several prior works have proposed axiomatic frameworks that any feature attribution method should satisfy. In this work, we argue that such axioms are often too restrictive, and propose in response a new feature attribution framework, built from the ground up. Rather than imposing axioms, we start by defining attributions for the simplest possible models, i.e. , indicator functions, and use these as building blocks for more complex models. We then show that one recovers several existing attribution methods, depending on the choice of atomic attribution. Subsequently, we derive closed-form expressions for attribution of deep ReLU networks, and take a step toward the optimization of evaluation metrics with respect to feature attributions.



1
Introduction

As machine learning models continue to improve, they are increasingly deployed in critical domains such as healthcare, self-driving, etc.
Despite their impressive performances, deep learning models remain notoriously opaque due to their vast parameter spaces, complex designs, and reliance on a succession of non-linear transformations.
These characteristics hinder human understanding of how specific outputs are produced, earning such models the label of “black boxes”
(
6
)
.
In such settings, lack of transparency can undermine trust, hinder error diagnosis, and raise ethical or legal concerns.
In response, the field of eXplainable AI (XAI) has emerged, aiming to bridge this gap by making model behavior more transparent and interpretable.

A wide range of interpretability methods have emerged in recent years, such as LIME
(
36
)
, KernelSHAP
(
30
)
, Integrated Gradients
(
41
)
, Grad-CAM
(
39
)
and LRP
(
5
)
.
These approaches typically fall under the classical feature attribution paradigm, which seeks to assign importance scores to input features based on their contribution to a model’s prediction.
In computer vision, such feature attributions are often referred to as saliency maps.
Despite their popularity, these methods produce inconsistent attributions for the same model and input point to explain
(
24
)
, and no widely accepted theoretical foundation has yet emerged.
While some efforts
(
41
,
19
,
10
)
have attempted to formalize attribution through axiomatic frameworks, a consensus on what constitutes a “correct” explanation remains elusive.

This work builds on the classical result that functions can be approximated by linear combinations of weighted indicator functions.
We propose to begin by assigning atomic attributions to these indicator functions and then extend the attribution to more complex models through summation.
Under mild regularity conditions, this construction yields a well-defined attribution method.
This perspective leads naturally to a measure-theoretic framework for feature attribution, where explanations are represented as integrals.
Our
main contributions
are threefold:

•

We analyze limitations of existing attribution axioms (Th.
2.1
).

•

We propose a constructivist, measure-theoretic framework for feature attribution (Th.
3.2
).

•

Within this framework, we derive closed-form attributions for deep ReLU networks (Cor.
4.1
), recover existing methods (Table
1
), and show how to optimize evaluation metrics as functions of attributions (Th.
4.1
).

Our implementation for computing these explanations is available
online
. All proofs can be found in Appendix
D
.

Limitations of existing axioms

In this section, we explore the landscape of existing axioms for feature attribution methods and show formally that they tend to produce similar attributions, as observed by
(
4
)
.
The main difference with
(
4
)
is that our result is valid for any feature attribution satisfying the axioms introduced in
(
41
)
.

2.1
Existing axioms

The problem at hand is to explain the predictions of a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\mathcal{X}
the input space, and
\mathcal{F}
is a vector space of functions to be explained.
One common approach is to use a feature attribution method
\phi
, defined as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Feature attribution
(
8
)
)
.

A feature attribution method is any mapping
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\phi(\mathbf{x},f)_{j}
represents the attribution of the
j
-th feature of the input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
in explaining the output
f(\mathbf{x})
, with
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket\vcentcolon=\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Intuitively, if
\phi(\mathbf{x},f)_{j}\gg 0
, then the feature
\mathbf{x}_{j}
is important for the model’s prediction
f(\mathbf{x})
.
We now list a handful of properties/axioms, found in the literature
(
41
)
, which are often taken as desirable for any attribution method
\phi
.

Definition 2.2

(Completeness
(
41
)
)
.

Given a baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say that a feature attribution method
\phi
is
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
-
complete
if:

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\phi(\mathbf{x},f)_{j}\,.

That is,
\phi
splits the model’s output for any given input into contributions from each individual feature.
In the literature
(
8
,
41
)
,
Completeness
is often defined so that it holds for every baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We do not consider this setting here.

Definition 2.3

(Sensitivity
(
41
)
)
.

Define the perturbed vector
\mathbf{x}^{(x^{\prime},j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the vector obtained by replacing the
j
-th coordinate of
\mathbf{x}
with
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.
,
\mathbf{x}^{(x^{\prime},j)}_{j}\vcentcolon=x^{\prime}
.
A feature attribution
\phi
is
sensitive
if:

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\quad\left(\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\forall x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\,f(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}^{(x^{\prime},j)})\implies\forall\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\phi(\bm{\xi},f)_{j}=0\right)\,.

In other words, if feature
j
has no impact on the model’s output, then
\phi_{j}=0
for any input.

Definition 2.4

(Linearity
(
41
)
)
.

A feature attribution
\phi
is
linear
if:

\displaystyle\forall f,g\in\mathcal{F},\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\phi(\mathbf{x},f+\lambda g)=\phi(\mathbf{x},f)+\lambda\phi(\mathbf{x},g)\,.

We offer a more exhaustive review of existing axioms in Appendix
A.1
.

2.2
Feature attribution derived from the previous axioms

In this section, our goal is to show that any feature attribution method satisfying the previous axioms yields attributions similar to those of
Gradient
\times
Input

(
40
)
.
Given a differentiable model
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and input to be explained
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is defined as

\phi(\mathbf{x},f)\vcentcolon=\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\odot\mathbf{x}\,,

where
\odot
denotes the element-wise product.
Intuitively, it captures both how large the feature is and how sensitive the prediction is.
In the spirit of
(
4
)
, we now show that any feature attribution
\phi
which satisfies
Completeness
,
Sensitivity
, and
Linearity
is a perturbation of the feature attribution method
Gradient
\times
Input
, provided that the model is smooth enough.
The magnitude of the perturbation is bounded by the “degree of linearity” (
i.e.
, norm of the Hessian) of the explained model
f
.
First, we show that the
Completeness
and
Sensitivity
properties are very strong as they can completely determine the attribution of some models.

Proposition 2.1

(Completeness
+
Sensitivity)
.

Define
\pi_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
the projection of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
on its
j
-th coordinate as
\pi_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}_{j}
.
Given a baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a
sensitive
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
-
complete
feature attribution
\phi
satisfies:

\displaystyle\forall f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\forall k,j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f\circ\pi_{j}\right)_{k}=\mathds{1}_{k=j}\left(f(\mathbf{x}_{j})-f(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\prime})\right)\,.

By adding the
Linearity
property into the previous proposition, one can show that any such feature attribution methods is equal to
Gradient
\times
Input
plus a remainder.

Lemma 2.1

(Completeness
+
Sensitivity
+
Linearity
+
smoothness)
.

Let
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the space of twice continuously differentiable function from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Given a vector
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a
sensitive
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
-
complete
,
linear
feature attribution
\phi
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\forall f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\phi(\mathbf{x},f)=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{0})\odot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+\phi(\mathbf{x},R_{\mathbf{x}_{0}})\,,

with
R_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}(\mathbf{z})\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_{0})^{\top}\nabla^{2}f\left(\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{z}}\right)(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_{0})
for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, given
\mathbf{z}
, there exists
\tau\in[0,1]
such that
\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{z}}\vcentcolon=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\tau(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}_{0})
.

The remainder term
R_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}
comes from the Taylor expansion of
f
. Since it is quadratic, the explanation of the remainder term
\phi(\mathbf{x},R_{\mathbf{x}_{0}})
may be large.
We show how to control it under the assumptions that
\phi
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to its second argument, and that the input space
\mathcal{X}
is restricted to
[0,1]^{d}
—this is a standard assumption in practice.
Before proceeding, we specify Lipschitz continuity in our context.

Definition 2.5

(Lipschitz continuity)
.

Set
B([0,1]^{d})
the space of bounded function from
[0,1]^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
We say that
\phi:[0,1]^{d}\times B([0,1]^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is Lipschitz continuous (in its second argument) if:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\exists L_{\mathbf{x}}\geq 0,\forall f,g\in B([0,1]^{d}),\qquad\left\lVert\phi(\mathbf{x},f)-\phi(\mathbf{x},g)\right\rVert_{2}\leq L_{\mathbf{x}}\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{\infty}\,,

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
(resp.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
) denotes the Euclidean (resp. sup) norm.

Theorem 2.1

(Bound on remainder)
.

Define
M\vcentcolon=\max_{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
.
Given a baseline
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d}
and a
sensitive
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
-
complete
,
linear
, Lipschitz continuous feature attribution
\phi
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\forall f\in C^{2}([0,1]^{d}),\forall\mathbf{x}_{0}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\qquad\left\lVert\phi(\mathbf{x},R_{\mathbf{x}_{0}})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{L_{\mathbf{x}}d}{2}M\,,

where
R_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}
is as in Lemma
2.1
, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
is the operator norm of a matrix.

Intuitively,
M
measures the linearity of
f
on
[0,1]^{d}
.
If
f
is linear, then
M=0
and we get no remainder, meaning that our attribution method
\phi
on this model is exactly
Gradient
\times
Input
.

(
4
)
and
(
22
)
show, both theoretically and experimentally, that for deep ReLU networks, common attribution methods (Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, etc.) behave like
Gradient
\times
Input
.

Th.
2.1
generalizes this observation to arbitrary feature attribution methods checking the previous axioms.

This theorem can be extended to deep ReLU networks by approximating them via convolution using a sequence of mollifier functions.
More precisely, given a sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of mollifiers functions, the smoothed network
\varphi_{n}\star f
converges to the original network
f
as
n\to+\infty

(
31
)
, where
\star
denotes convolution.
Since
\varphi_{n}\star f
is smooth, it satisfies the regularity conditions required by Th.
2.1
.
Moreover, under mild regularity assumptions on the attribution method
\phi
, the attribution
\phi(\mathbf{x},\varphi_{n}\star f)
converges to
\phi(\mathbf{x},f)
for any input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This connection may allow the issues identified with
Gradient
\times
Input

(
1
)
, such as its tendency to behave like an edge detector, to be transferred to any such attribution methods.

Thus,
Completeness
and
Sensitivity
, while intuitive, are overly restrictive.
Introducing any new element or even reinforcing/weakening existing axioms seems to inevitably undermine the axiomatic system’s coherency.
For instance, in Appendix
A.2
, we show that enforcing
Completeness
without a baseline leads to a contradiction.
Similarly, formulating a local version of
Sensitivity
seems challenging, as naive formulations also lead to contradiction.
Therefore, we propose an alternative approach, aiming to escape these pitfalls.
Among the previous axioms, we will only keep
Linearity
, as it is a reasonable property for any attribution method to satisfy.

3
Our framework: a constructivist approach

In our framework, we start with a simple model for which we can be confident in the explanation.
A natural candidate for such a model is the indicator function.
This choice is justified by a classical approximation result: any continuous function defined on a compact set can be approximated arbitrarily well by a finite sum of indicator functions (see Appendix
B.1
).
Building on this, we derive attributions for the full model by aggregating the explanations of its indicator function components.
From now on, we restrict ourselves to models which are continuous on

\mathcal{X}\vcentcolon=[0,1]^{d}
.

3.1
Warm-up:
2
-dimensional example of our framework

In order to give a good intuition of our framework, let us carry out the informal derivation in the case where the input dimension is
d=2
.

First step (Attribution of indicator functions).

Let
\mathds{1}_{R}:[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\}
be the indicator function of a rectangle
R\vcentcolon=(a,b]\times(c,d]\subset[0,1]^{2}
defined as
\mathds{1}_{R}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=1
if
\mathbf{x}\in R
, else
0
.
A possible definition for the
elementary (atomic) attribution

\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{R}\right)
is

\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{(a,b]\times(c,d]}\right)\vcentcolon=\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in(a,b]},\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{2}\in(c,d]}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,.

We argue that this definition is natural, since
a feature is considered positively important for the prediction of the model if it lies within the region where the indicator function is active.

Specifically, if
\mathbf{x}_{1}
falls in the interval
(a,b]
, we give to feature
1
a score of
1
.
The same reasoning applies to
\mathbf{x}_{2}
with the interval
(c,d]
.
To avoid binary attributions lacking a notion of magnitude, such as simply assigning
0
or
1
, we scale each attribution by the length of the remaining intervals:

\displaystyle\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{(a,b]\times(c,d]}\right)\vcentcolon=\left((d-c)\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in(a,b]},(b-a)\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{2}\in(c,d]}\right)^{\top}\,.

The previous display can be interpreted as follows: if
(c,d]
is significantly larger than
(a,b]
, then most of the attribution magnitude is assigned to the first feature.
This makes sense because when
(c,d]
covers a large range of the second coordinate, the condition
\mathbf{x}_{1}\in(a,b]
is much more important as an explanation to the prediction
\mathds{1}_{(a,b]\times(c,d]}
than the condition

\mathbf{x}_{2}\in(c,d]
, which is always satisfied as
(c,d]
covers a large portion of the second feature.

Second step (Approximation of the model).

We approximate the function
f
using a sequence of piecewise-constant functions
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
defined as:

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star},\qquad f_{n}\vcentcolon=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=0}^{n-1}f\left(a^{(n)}_{i_{1}},a^{(n)}_{i_{2}}\right)\mathds{1}_{R_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{(n)}}\,,

(1)

with
\left(a^{(n)}_{i}\right)_{i\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket}
a partition (
n+1
elements) of
[0,1]
, such that
a^{(n)}_{0}=0<a^{(n)}_{1}<\cdots<a^{(n)}_{n}=1
, and the rectangles

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star},\forall i,j\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket,\qquad R_{i,j}^{(n)}\vcentcolon=\left(a^{(n)}_{i},a^{(n)}_{i+1}\right]\times\left(a^{(n)}_{j},a^{(n)}_{j+1}\right]\,.

Standard arguments show that the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a good approximation of the model
f
(see Appendix
B.1
for details).
We depict this approximation in Figure
1
.

Third step (Attribution of sum of indicator functions).

Given
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{2}
, if
\phi
is
Linear
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f_{n}\right)_{1}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=0}^{n-1}f\left(a^{(n)}_{i_{1}},a^{(n)}_{i_{2}}\right)\left(a^{(n)}_{i_{2}+1}-a^{(n)}_{i_{2}}\right)\left(\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\leq a^{(n)}_{i_{1}+1}}-\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\leq a^{(n)}_{i_{1}}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(2)

Last step (Attribution of the whole model
f
).

Under mild assumptions, the previous display converges to the following double integral:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\forall\mathbf{x}\in(0,1]^{2},\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}(\mathds{1}_{x\geq\mathbf{x}_{1}})\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{1}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,.\end{split}

(3)

The same argument applies to the second coordinate of the attribution.
Finally, assuming minimal regularity assumptions on
\phi
, the sequence
\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f_{n}\right)
converges to
\phi(\mathbf{x},f)
as
n\to+\infty
.
Combining this convergence with Equation (
3
), we obtain the feature attribution for the whole model
f
:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in(0,1]^{2},\qquad\phi(\mathbf{x},f)=\left(\int_{0}^{1}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\int_{0}^{1}f(x,\mathbf{x}_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\top}\,.

(4)

Intuitively, this means that each feature is explained by marginalizing the effect of the remaining feature,
i.e.
, by integrating over it.
As it turns out, this coincides with the Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) of
(
15
)
under the uniform distribution, meaning no prior over the input space is assumed and all points are weighted equally.

3.2
Motivation for our construction from a functional analysis perspective

We can recover the previous integral form of feature attribution using a classical result from functional analysis, allowing us to bypass the more involved derivations presented earlier.
The construction from Section
3.1
relies on the following regularity property of
\phi
:

Definition 3.1

(Functional Supremum Continuity)
.

A feature attribution
\phi
is
functionally supremum continuous
(
FSC
) if for all sequences
(f_{n})_{n\geq 0}
(
f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
) and all
f\in\mathcal{F}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left\lVert f_{n}-f\right\rVert_{\infty}=0\implies\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lim_{n\to+\infty}\phi(\mathbf{x},f_{n})=\phi(\mathbf{x},f)\,.

In other words, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
,
\phi(\mathbf{x},\cdot)_{j}
is continuous where
\mathcal{F}
is endowed with the topology of uniform convergence.

FSC
is also similar to the

property
(
13
)
(see Appendix
A.1
).
However, the difference is that this property provides stability to the attribution
\phi
with respect to perturbations of the model
f\in\mathcal{F}
, rather than the explained point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
This new property enables us to construct a framework that offers greater flexibility than the axiomatic framework of
(
41
)
(Section
3.1
), without sacrificing a controlled structure for feature attribution.

Theorem 3.1

(Riesz–Markov in our setting

Let

.
Or more succinctly,
\mu_{1,\mathbf{x}}\vcentcolon=\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{L}
and
\mu_{2,\mathbf{x}}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{L}\otimes\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{2}}
with
\mathcal{L}
the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

Remark 3.1
.

If the feature attribution
\phi
is assumed to be positive, the
FSC
property is automatically satisfied and the representing measure
\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}
is positive.

FSC
can seem very strong but can be interpreted as follows:
two models which have
\varepsilon>0
close attribution (in term of
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
) should be
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
distant to each other (in term of
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
)
.
This is a weak version of the
Model Invariance
property
(
41
)
(Appendix
A.1
).
Another argument in favor of
FSC
is given in Remark
3.1
, as it is naturally checked by
Linear
and positive feature attribution methods.

Remark 3.2
.

All the theorems in the following sections can be formulated with
FPC
(see Appendix
B.2
) instead of
FSC
. Moreover, the topology induced by
FPC
is weaker than the one generated by
FSC
.
One should note that Th.

does not apply with

.
For simplicity, we state all the results with

.

3.3
General case:
d
-dimensional feature attribution

In this section, we extend the construction from Section
3.1
to the
d
-dimensional input space.
In doing so, we also specify the measures in Th.
3.1
by defining the attribution assigned to indicator functions
\mathds{1}_{R}
, where
R\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a hyperrectangle.
Our main result is the following:

Theorem 3.2

(From atomic to general attribution)
.

Let
\phi
be a
Linear
and
FSC
attribution method.
Let
\{\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}\}_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}

be a family of finite signed Borel
measures
on
[0,1]^{d}
.
Assume that the atomic attributions of indicator functions are given by:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{R}\right)_{j}\vcentcolon=\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}\left(R\right)\in\mathbb{R}\,,

where
R\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a hyperrectangle.
Then, the attribution of a continuous model
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the following Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f\right)_{j}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})\,.

One should read Th.
3.2
as follows:
first, decide on an attribution for the indicator function
\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{R}\right)
, then, get the attribution for the whole model
\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f\right)
in the form of an integral.

The intuition behind the attribution
\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{R}\right)_{j}\vcentcolon=\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(R)
is clear: it expresses the attribution of
\mathds{1}_{R}
in terms of the measure of the region
R
.
This reflects how important feature
j
is for activating
\mathds{1}_{R}
, where the notion of importance is determined by the measure chosen for that feature.
In other words:
in our framework, doing feature attribution is equivalent to choosing a family of measures over the input space
\mathcal{X}
.

To simplify the selection of the measures, we can restrict our attention to measures
\mu
that are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
, meaning they can be written as
\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})=h(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
with
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
a density function.
This restriction enhances intuition because the density
h
can then be interpreted as a weight function over the input space
[0,1]^{d}
.
This setting is presented in Appendix
B.3
.

In practice, the implementation of our framework is done in two manners: either Monte-Carlo with a sampler for high dimensional models or a classical Riemann sum approximation of the integral which is vectorized on GPU.
It is compatible with any PyTorch model and allows the use of arbitrary integrands/measures to explore various instantiations of our feature attribution framework.

4
Applications of our framework

Now that our feature attribution framework is defined, we illustrate its applications through several examples, which span through theory and practice.

4.1
Feature attribution for piecewise affine functions

We derive a closed-form representation of the feature attribution for the class of piecewise affine continuous functions
\mathcal{F}
, defined as:

Definition 4.1

(Piecewise affine continuous function)
.

We say that a continuous function
f:\penalty\ [0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is piecewise affine continuous if it can be written as

\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\qquad f(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})}\left(\mathbf{a}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+b_{P}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathbf{x}\in P}\,,

where
\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})
is a partition of
[0,1]^{d}
into
d
-polytopes (
i.e.
, any shape in
[0,1]^{d}
formed by gluing together finitely many convex
d
-dimensional pieces along their faces), and
\mathbf{a}_{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
b_{P}\in\mathbb{R}
are the coefficients of the local linear model in region
P
.

Interestingly,
this class of functions corresponds exactly to the deep ReLU networks

(
35
)
as defined in Appendix
C.1
.
The following result, direct consequence of Th.
3.2
, provides a representation of feature attributions for piecewise affine continuous functions, making it directly applicable to deep ReLU networks.

Corollary 4.1

(Feature attribution of piecewise affine continuous functions)
.

Let
\phi
be a
Linear
and
FSC
attribution method.
Let
\{\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}\}_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}

be a family of finite positive Borel
measures
on
[0,1]^{d}
.
Assume that the atomic attributions of indicator functions are given by:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{R}\right)_{j}\vcentcolon=\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(R)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,,

where
R\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a hyperrectangle.
Then, the attribution of a piecewise affine continuous function
f
is given by:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f\right)_{j}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})}\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(P)\left(\mathbf{a}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{(P,j,\mathbf{x})}+b_{P}\right)\,,

where
\mathbf{m}^{(P,j,\mathbf{x})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
center of mass
of
P
defined as:

\forall i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\mathbf{m}^{(P,j,\mathbf{x})}_{i}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}(\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(P))^{-1}\int_{P}\mathbf{y}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})&\text{if $\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(P)\neq 0$}\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Informally, for this class of models, feature attribution is computed as a weighted sum of the model’s output evaluated at the center of mass of each
P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})
.
The sum is taken over all elements of the input space partition
\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})
, with the weight of each element given by its corresponding explanation measure
\mu(P)
.
Notably,
this corollary also extends to CART trees and random forests
by relying only on
Linearity
and the atomic attribution of indicator functions.

FSC
is not needed as CART-trees create a
fixed
partition of
\mathcal{X}
with axis-aligned hyperrectangle, and the sole purpose of
FSC
is to take the limit of the partition.

Remark 4.1
.

For ReLU networks, the coefficients
(\mathbf{a}_{P},b_{P})
of the linear regions

have closed-form expressions in terms of the network’s weights.
See Appendix
C.1
for details.

Definition 4.2

(Recall)
.

Given a threshold
\beta>0
and a linear model

, with weights
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the index partition
D_{1,\mathbf{w}}\sqcup D_{0,\mathbf{w}}=\llbracket d\rrbracket
, where
D_{0,\mathbf{w}}\vcentcolon=\{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:\left\lvert\mathbf{w}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\beta\}
and
D_{1,\mathbf{w}}\vcentcolon=D_{0,\mathbf{w}}^{\complement}
(called the
golden set
).
Also, let
\phi
be a global feature attribution method which depends on a vector of measures
\mu\vcentcolon=(\mu_{j})_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
.
We define the
Recall
metric, with a threshold
\alpha>0
, as

\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mu)\vcentcolon=\frac{\sum_{j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert\geq\alpha}}{\sum_{j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert\geq\alpha}+\sum_{j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert<\alpha}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert\geq\alpha}}{\left\lvert D_{1,\mathbf{w}}\right\rvert}\,,

where
\mathcal{P}^{d}
is the space of probability measures on
[0,1]^{d}
.

Informally,
Recall
measures the proportion of features retrieved by
\phi
that are actually used by the model
f_{\mathbf{w}}
.
Thus, ideally, we would like to solve

\Argmax_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{d}}\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mu)\,.

(5)

This can be casted into an optimization problem in
[0,1]^{d}
(see Appendix
C.3
) as

\Argmax_{\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)})\,.

(6)

The measures solving Problem (
5
) are generally model-specific,
i.e.
, they depend on the particular choice of
\mathbf{w}
and are denoted by
\mu_{\mathbf{w}}
.
To identify measures that perform well across a class of models, we can intersect the sets of optimal solutions obtained for individual models, provided the intersection is non-empty.
That is why, defining the evaluation metric on specific models is natural, since finding a measure that is optimal for all possible unconstrained models is, in general, not feasible.
Using geometric arguments, one can show that the measures
\mu
which optimize
\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}
are projected on vectors that lie above a given hyperplane as follows

Theorem 4.1

(Optimal projections for
\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}
)
.

For each
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\geq\alpha\right\}\cup\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\leq-\alpha\right\}\text{ if $j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}$}\,,\\
[0,1]^{d}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, the solutions of (
6
) are
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}
for all
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
.

This theorem is illustrated in Figure
2
.
It should be interpreted as a sanity check for the optimality of a given measure and can thus aid in designing good measures.
More precisely, to check if a measure
\mu
is optimal, we compute its center of mass (see Appendix
C.3
) and see if it lies inside of a given solution set
\mathcal{S}_{j}
.
Also, it is possible to construct trivial or degenerate measures that project to a given vector, but these are typically model-dependent.

There are two main limitations to this result.
First, it relies on the availability of a ground-truth for the features, which is not always the case.
Second, it does not provide a direct characterization of the optima in the space of measures.
Instead, it characterizes the optima in
[0,1]^{d}
after projecting the measures.
Since this projection is not bijective, it seems that there is no straightforward way to recover all possible measures that map to a given vector in
[0,1]^{d}
.
Additional analysis related to the
Precision
metric, a natural counterpart to
Recall
, and the optimization of feature attribution for ReLU networks can be found in Appendix
C.3
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we showed that any attribution method satisfying the axioms introduced in
(
41
)
can produce attributions similar to
Gradient
\times
Input
.
Based on this observation, we propose a new framework for feature attribution.
By defining attributions for indicator functions and making mild assumptions on the feature attribution method, one can reconstruct the attribution for the entire model.
We then express several existing XAI methods within our framework and derive closed-form attributions for deep ReLU networks.
An implementation of this framework is available online.
Finally, we use our framework to introduce a sanity check for feature attribution methods and take a first step toward theoretically optimizing feature attribution with respect to a given evaluation metric.

As future work, we want to explore the optimization of feature attribution, as introduced in Section
4.3
, along two main directions.
First, we aim to better characterize the properties of attributions that optimize a given evaluation metric, and, if possible, derive closed-form solutions.
Second, on the empirical side, we plan to apply the gradient ascent algorithm of
(
33
)
in the context of feature attribution, providing a way of empirically constructing optimal feature attribution methods.

Before introducing the first property, we need an auxiliary definition:

Definition A.1

(Action of a partition on vector)
.

Given
S
a partition of
\llbracket d\rrbracket
and a vector
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
\bm{\xi}^{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the unique vector verifying:

\forall i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\exists!\,S^{\prime}\in S,\qquad\left(i\in S^{\prime}\implies\bm{\xi}_{i}^{S}=\bm{\xi}_{\min{S^{\prime}}}\right)\,.

Informally,
\bm{\xi}^{S}

\bm{\xi}

\bm{\xi}
at the smallest index in

We illustrate this definition as follows: given

\bm{\xi}^{S}=(5,-2,5)^{\top}

Definition A.2

(Weak symmetry
[
41
]
)
.

A feature attribution method
\phi
is

if:

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\forall\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d},\qquad\left(\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,f(\mathbf{x})=f(g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}))\implies\forall\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\phi(\bm{\xi},f)=g_{\sigma}(\phi(\bm{\xi}^{\llbracket d\rrbracket\small/\sigma},f))\right)\,,

where
\bm{\xi}^{\llbracket d\rrbracket\small/\sigma}
is defined as in Definition
A.1
, using the partition
\llbracket d\rrbracket\small/\sigma

\llbracket d\rrbracket

, two elements

\sigma^{n}(x)=y
).

For example, if we take the permutation
\sigma=(12)(35)
(with

), then
\llbracket d\rrbracket\big/\sigma=\left\{\{1,2\},\{3,5\},\{4\}\right\}
.
We introduces a stronger version of

and leads to a more natural mathematical formulation.

Definition A.3

(Strong symmetry)
.

A feature attribution method
\phi
is
strongly-symmetric
if:

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\forall\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d},\qquad\left(\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,f(\mathbf{x})=f(g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}))\implies\forall\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\phi(\bm{\xi},f)=g_{\sigma}(\phi(\bm{\xi},f))\right)\,,

where
g_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\left(\mathbf{x}_{\sigma(1)},\mathbf{x}_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\sigma(d)}\right)
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
is the permutation group of
\llbracket d\rrbracket
.

To illustrate the difference between
Weak symmetry
and

, consider the simple model
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x,y)\vcentcolon=x+y
, and the permutation
\sigma=(1\,2)
that swaps the two input features.
Since

for all

, the function

is invariant under
\sigma
.
A feature attribution method that satisfies

is only required to produce equal attributions when the input itself is invariant under
\sigma
.
In other words:

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\phi((x,x),f)_{1}=\phi((x,x),f)_{2}\,.

(7)

By contrast, a

attribution method must produce equal attributions for all inputs whenever the function is invariant under
\sigma
, regardless of the specific input values:

\forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\phi((x,y),f)_{1}=\phi((x,y),f)_{2}\,.

(8)

This stricter requirement ensures that the symmetry of the function is fully reflected in the attributions across all inputs.

Definition A.4

(Model invariance
[
41
]
)
.

A feature attribution method
\phi
is

if:

\displaystyle\forall f_{0},f_{1}\in\mathcal{F},\qquad\left(\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,f_{0}(\mathbf{x})=f_{1}(\mathbf{x})\implies\forall\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\phi(\bm{\xi},f_{0})=\phi(\bm{\xi},f_{1})\right)\,.

In other words, if two models yield the same outputs for the same inputs, their attributions should be identical as well.

Definition A.5

(Robustness
[
13
]
)
.

A feature attribution method
\phi
is

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\qquad\phi(\cdot,f)\text{ is continuous}\,.

Robustness

\mathbf{x}
.

(Agreeing with linear models

A

\forall\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\forall j\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\phi(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\times(\cdot))_{j}=\mathbf{w}_{j}^{2}\,.

In other words, this axiom treats linear models as a sanity check, since they provide a ground-truth attribution by design.

B.3.3
Measure-based feature attribution

One might ask why we don’t directly use general measure-based attributions as in Th.
3.2
. The answer is that this approach does not directly fit within the approximation result of Th.
B.1
(which is a classical construction for the Riemann integral). In a Lebesgue-type integral, one partitions the range (image space) of the function and then takes the preimages of these partitions. However, these preimages do not necessarily form hyperrectangles (which are very simple objects), unlike the straightforward case where the partition is applied directly to the input space.
Moreover, the Stieltjes integrand-based framework is more general, as it works with
BV
integrands
g
rather than requiring
BHK
and right-continuity, the latter being a stronger condition than
BV
.

Remark B.3
.

Although, one can derive the same framework using the Lebesgue integral construction, doing so requires additional work. In particular, it involves rewriting the preimages of the range partition elements as unions of hyperrectangles.

Finally, we clarify the statement made at the end of Section
3.3
by formalizing the density-based feature attribution framework.

Corollary B.3

(Radon-Nikodym feature attribution)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
and
\{\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}\}_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}
be the family of finite signed Borel measures which are absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure with respective Radon-Nikodym derivatives
\{h_{j,\mathbf{x}}\}_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}
, meaning

\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\forall A\in\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d}),\qquad\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(A)=\int_{A}h_{j,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,.

Let
\phi
be
Linear
and
FPC
(or
FSC
) attribution method.
Assume that the atomic attributions of the indicator functions are given by:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},\mathds{1}_{R}\right)_{j}\vcentcolon=\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}\left(R\right)\in\mathbb{R}\,,

where
R\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a hyperrectangle.
Then, the attribution of a continuous model
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the following Lebesgue-Stieltjes integral:

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\forall j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,\qquad\phi\left(\mathbf{x},f\right)_{j}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}f(\mathbf{y})h_{j,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\,.

Proof.

It is a direct consequence of Theorem

Definition C.1

(Deep ReLU network)

We define a

, we present the following representation theorem for deep ReLU networks:

Theorem C.1

(Linear representation of Deep ReLU networks,

In the following section, we explain how the infinite-dimensional optimization Problem (

Proposition C.1

(Simple surjection of measure into vectors)
.

Set
\mathcal{P}
as the space of probability measures on
[0,1]^{d}
.
There exists a simple surjection from
\mathcal{P}
to
[0,1]^{d}
defined as

\mathcal{P}\twoheadrightarrow[0,1]^{d}:\mu\mapsto\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\vcentcolon=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{y})\right)_{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\,,

where
\mathbf{m}^{\mu}
is the center of mass of
[0,1]^{d}
w.r.t.
\mu
.

Proof.

Given
\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}
, take the measures
\mu
on
[0,1]^{d}
defined as

\forall A\in\mathcal{B}([0,1]^{d}),\qquad\mu(A)\vcentcolon=\bigotimes_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\delta_{\mathbf{m}_{j}}\,.

The center of mass of
[0,1]^{d}
w.r.t.
\mu
is exactly
\mathbf{m}
.
∎

Thanks to the previous proposition, we can reformulate Problem (
5
) as Problem (
6
) by surjection with the center of mass. As noted in the main paper, this reformulation involves a loss of information.
The following technical result guarantees that the
center of mass stays inside
[0,1]^{d}
.

Proposition C.2

(Center of mass stays in convex compact of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Given a convex, closed and Borel measurable subset
\mathcal{S}\subset[0,1]^{d}
and a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
. If
\mu(\mathcal{S})\neq 0
, then the center of mass of
\mathcal{S}
checks
\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

The proof is done by contradiction. Let us assume that
\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\notin\mathbf{S}
. By applying the Hahn–Banach separation theorem (with the variant where both sets are closed and at least one of them is compact) on
\{\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\}
and
\mathcal{S}
, as they do not intersect, there exists a nonzero vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two real numbers
c_{1}>c_{2}
s.t.

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\left\langle\mathbf{x}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle>c_{1}\text{ and }\left\langle\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle<c_{2}\,.

Which implies (by
c_{1}>c_{2}
)

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S},\qquad\left\langle\mathbf{x}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle>c_{2}>\left\langle\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,.

(13)

We rewrite the last term of the Inequality
13
as follows

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\left(\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{y}_{1}\,d\mu(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{y}_{d}\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})\right)\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})\,.

Now, the contradiction arise by looking at the following integral

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})>\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\int_{\mathcal{S}}c_{2}\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})=c_{2}\times 1>\left\langle\mathbf{m}^{\mu}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})\,,

where the first and last inequality comes from
13
. To sum up the contradiction is

\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})>\frac{1}{\mu(\mathcal{S})}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}\;,\;\mathbf{v}\right\rangle\,d\mu(\mathbf{y})\,.

∎

C.3.2
Additional results on Precision and ReLU networks

We introduce the counterpart to
Recall
, called
Precision
.

Definition C.2

(Precision)
.

Given a threshold
\beta>0
and a linear model
f_{\mathbf{w}}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, with weights
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the index partition
D_{1,\mathbf{w}}\sqcup D_{0,\mathbf{w}}=\llbracket d\rrbracket
, where
D_{0,\mathbf{w}}\vcentcolon=\{i\in\llbracket d\rrbracket:\left\lvert\mathbf{w}_{i}\right\rvert\leq\beta\}
and
D_{1,\mathbf{w}}\vcentcolon=D_{0,\mathbf{w}}^{\complement}
(called the
golden set
).
Also, let
\phi
be a global feature attribution method which depends on a vector of measures
\mu\vcentcolon=(\mu_{j})_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
.
We define the
Precision
metric, with a threshold
\alpha>0
, as

\mathrm{Precision}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mu)\vcentcolon=\frac{\sum_{j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert\geq\alpha}}{\sum_{j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert\geq\alpha}+\sum_{j\in D_{0,\mathbf{w}}}\mathds{1}_{\left\lvert\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}\right\rvert\geq\alpha}}\,.

where
\mathcal{P}
is the space of probability measures on
[0,1]^{d}
.

In general, there is a trade-off between
Precision
and
Recall
.
One easy solution is to prioritize one metric over the other, the choice should depend on whether false positives or false negatives carry a higher cost. Ultimately, this decision depends on the specific requirements of your task, whether you are doing recourse, or generating counterfactual explanations.
We optimize the
Precision
metric using the finite-dimensional formulation

\Argmax_{\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{Precision}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)})\,.

(14)

Theorem C.2

(Optimal projections for
\mathrm{Precision}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}
)
.

For each
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\left\lvert\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\right\rvert\geq\alpha\right\}\text{ if $j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}$}\,,\\
\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\left\lvert\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\right\rvert<\alpha\right\}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then, the solutions of (
14
) are
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}
for all
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
.

Proof.

The goal is to maximize the precision metric
\mathrm{Precision}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}
in the finite-dimensional case.
To do this, we:

•

maximize the numerator:
ensure that
|\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}|\geq\alpha
for all
j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}
.

•

minimize the denominator:
ensure that
|\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}|<\alpha
for all
j\in D_{0,\mathbf{w}}
, but keeping the fact that
|\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}|\geq\alpha
for all
j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}
.

Since the optimization problem involves disjoint index sets (
D_{1,\mathbf{w}}\cap D_{0,\mathbf{w}}=\varnothing
), the optimization decouples across features
j
.
Note that
\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
, and the indicator
\mathds{1}_{|\phi(f_{\mathbf{w}})_{j}|\geq\alpha}
depends only on
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
. Thus, for each
j
:

•

If
j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}
, we want
|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}|\geq\alpha
.

•

If
j\in D_{0,\mathbf{w}}
, we want
|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}|<\alpha
.

Therefore, for each
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
, the optimal
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
must lie in the set
\mathcal{S}_{j}
defined above.
∎

This result is illustrated in Figure
2
, where the white area represent the solutions
S_{j}
when
j\in D_{0,\mathbf{w}}
.
Now, we show that maximizing
Precision
implies maximizing the
Recall
.

Lemma C.1

(Maximizing Precision
\implies
maximizing Recall)
.

In the previous notations, we have

\displaystyle\Argmax_{\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\mathbf{m}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{Precision}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\mathbf{m}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)})

\displaystyle\subset\Argmax_{\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\mathbf{m}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\mathbf{m}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)})\,.

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem
C.2
. For all
j\in D_{1,\mathbf{w}}
, both optimization problems (for
Precision
and
Recall
) require that
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}=\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}|\geq\alpha\}
.
However, for
j\in D_{0,\mathbf{w}}
, the recall objective imposes no constraint on
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
, while the precision objective requires
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}=\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:|\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{m}|<\alpha\}
.
Thus, any solution that maximizes
Precision
also maximizes
Recall
, implying the inclusion.
∎

Since the local linear models of a ReLU network are explicitly known, one may consider them as a form of ground-truth for feature attribution. This motivates the following definitions of
Recall
and
Precision
tailored to deep ReLU networks.

Definition C.3

(ReLU Recall and Precision)
.

Let
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a deep ReLU network and
\phi
be a global feature attribution method which depends on a vector of measures
\mu\vcentcolon=(\mu_{j})_{j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket}\in\mathcal{P}^{d}
.
We define the ReLU-Recall metric, with a threshold
\alpha>0
, as

\mathrm{RRecall}_{\alpha,f}(\mu)\vcentcolon=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})}\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{a}_{P}}(\mu)\,,

and ReLU-Precision as

\mathrm{RPrecision}_{\alpha,f}(\mu)\vcentcolon=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})}\mathrm{Precision}_{\alpha,\mathbf{a}_{P}}(\mu)\,.

For each region
P
, the corresponding local linear model has coefficients
(\mathbf{a}_{P},b_{P})
(see Property
C.1
).

This definition extends
Recall
and
Precision
, from Definition
4.2
, to ReLU networks.
Again, we optimize the ReLU-
Recall
metric using the finite-dimensional formulation

\Argmax_{\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{RRecall}_{\alpha,f}(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)})\,.

(15)

Theorem C.3

(Optimal projections for
\mathrm{RRecall}_{\alpha,f}
)
.

For each
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j}\vcentcolon=\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})\text{ s.t. }j\in D_{1,\mathbf{a}_{P}}}\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\left\lvert\mathbf{a}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\right\rvert\geq\alpha\right\}\,.

Then, for Problem (
15
), a subset of solutions is given by choosing
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}
for all
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
.

Proof.

We want to maximize the objective
\mathrm{RRecall}_{\alpha,f}
, which aggregates recall values over all regions
P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})
.
A sufficient condition for maximizing
\mathrm{RRecall}_{\alpha,f}
is to simultaneously maximize each individual
\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{a}_{P}}
.
By Theorem
4.1
, for each
P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})
and each
j\in D_{1,\mathbf{a}_{P}}
, recall is maximized when
\left\lvert\mathbf{a}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right\rvert\geq\alpha
. Since the same feature
j
may belong to
D_{1,\mathbf{a}_{P}}
for multiple
P
, we intersect the corresponding solutions sets to find a common region of optimality.
Therefore, for each
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
, the set of
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
that simultaneously maximizes all relevant
\mathrm{Recall}_{\alpha,\mathbf{a}_{P}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j}\vcentcolon=\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})\text{ s.t. }j\in D_{1,\mathbf{a}_{P}}}\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\left\lvert\mathbf{a}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\right\rvert\geq\alpha\right\}\,.

Note that this characterization may not capture all maximizers, as the constraints are not disjoint and the intersection may be empty.
∎

We obtain a similar result for the optimization of ReLU-

\Argmax_{\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)}\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathrm{RPrecision}_{\alpha,f}(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(d)})\,.

(16)

.

For each

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j}\vcentcolon=\bigcap_{P\in\mathcal{R}([0,1]^{d})}\mathcal{S}_{j,P}\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{j,P}\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\left\{\mathbf{m}\in[0,1]^{d}:\left\lvert\mathbf{a}_{P}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\right\rvert\geq\alpha\right\}\text{ if $j\in D_{1,\mathbf{a}_{P}}$}\,,\\

Then, for Problem (

\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{S}_{j}
for all
j\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
.

Proof.

This is exactly the same proof as for Theorem
C.3
, but we use Theorem
C.2
(instead of Theorem
4.1
) as we are maximizing

.
