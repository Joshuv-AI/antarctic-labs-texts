Explicit Families of Spinor Representations
Jesus Sanchez Jr

Abstract
We provide a recipe for building explicit representations of the real Clifford algebras once an explicit family is given in dimensions 1 1 through 4 4 . We further give an explicit construction of spin coordinate systems for a given real spinor module and use it to explicitly compute the parallel transport of spinor fields. We further highlight some novelties such as the relationship with the spectrum of the spinor Dirac operator and the Hodge de Rham operator when a parallel spinor field exists and a brief discussion of spinors along a hypersurface in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} . Lastly, we extend our construction to arbitrary signature quadratic forms thus providing a complete and explicit family of spinor representations for all mixed signature Clifford algberas. We show that in all cases the spinor representations can be expressed as tensor products of multi-vectors over the fields ℝ \mathbb{R} , ℂ \mathbb{C} , and ℍ \mathbb{H} .



1
Introduction

Given a real quadratic vector space
(V,g)
, the representation theory of both the real and complexified Clifford algebra is well understood and completely classified by realizing the corresponding Clifford algebra as a matrix algebra or direct sum of two matrix algebras. The definition of spinor module
S
then becomes that of an irreducible module over the Clifford algebra, real or complex depending on the choice of real or complexified Clifford algebra. In the case of the complexified Clifford algebra
\mathbb{C}\ell(V,g)
, there is a natural choice of spinor representation where one takes
S:=\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}(V,J)
for a chosen complex structure
J
on
V
when
V
is even dimensional and
S:=\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}(V\oplus\mathbb{R},J)
when
V
is odd dimensional. This representation can be used to show that any complex manifold is
Spin^{c}
and is important in the discussion of the
Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch
theorem in complex geometry. If one restricts to the case of Euclidean Clifford algebras then there is a satisfactory answer which involves the use of octonions as in
[
4
]
and
[
2
]
. However, the real side suffers in that there are is no know unified treatment for the families of irreducible representations of
C\ell(V,g)
which are explicit and geometric as in the case of the complexified Clifford algebras
and
valid over all possible signatures.

There has been recent interest in understanding the geometry of spinors as well as generalizing the concept to new domains. The work of
[
3
]
presents a new take by defining the notion of
d
-blades with a view towards understanding the role of spinors in quantum field theory, whereas the work of
[
6
]
presents an approach to spinor fields on the infinite dimensional loop space of a Riemmanian manifold
(M,g)
. The interest in understanding spinors derives from the application of the
Atiyah-Singer index theorem
in spin geometry, which states that on a closed, oriented, spin Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, the
\hat{A}
-genus is related to the
spinor Dirac operator
\not{D}
via

\int_{M}\widehat{\textbf{\emph{A}}}(M)=\textbf{\emph{dim}}\text{Ker}\not{D}^{+}-\textbf{\emph{dim}}\text{Ker}\not{D}^{-}.

As the above expression is only valid on spin Riemannian manifolds, having an explicit and geometric construction of the spinor bundle as well as the corresponding spin frame bundle which double covers the oriented orthonormal frame bundle of
(M,g)
quickly becomes highly desirable.

Organization

In this paper we will provide a recipe for cooking up explicit families of real spinor representations for the real Euclidean Clifford algebras, which only needs explicit representations in dimensions
1
-
4
. The key idea will be to use mixed field tensor products where the tensor products are allowed to vary from
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, and
\mathbb{H}
. From here we construct an explicit family of Euclidean spinors modeled on quaternionic multi-vectors as a well a family based on square-roots of space. Once a choice of spinor module is given, we discuss the notion of spin frames from this perspective as needed for passage to the spin vector bundle setting. For completeness, we include a section on the construction of spinor bundles which is rather standard but does also mention the spin orientation
\mathbb{R}
-gerbe from our angle. We further include novel low dimensional phenomena including a discussion of the spin Levi-Civita connection of an oriented surface
\Sigma
, the treatment of spinors along hypersurfaces embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and a discussion of products of spinors giving a local Stoke’s theorem for the Dirac operator. We conclude the paper with the generalization of our spinor modules to the setting of pseudo-Euclidean vector spaces, thus giving a complete collection of explicit geometric irreducible representations of real (pseudo)-Euclidean Clifford algebras.

2
Preliminaries

We begin by collecting a few standard definitions and structural results concerning Clifford algebras. The standard references for these can be found in
[
1
]
or
[
5
]
. Given a finite dimensional real Euclidean vector space
(V,g)
, the
Clifford Algebra

C\ell(V,g)
of
(V,g)
is the universal unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra generated by vectors
v\in V
subject to the relations

v\cdot w+w\cdot v=-2g(v,w),\quad\text{for all }v,w\in V.

Using the
\mathbb{R}
-tensor algebra of
V

T_{\mathbb{R}}(V):=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}V^{\otimes^{j}_{\mathbb{R}}}

an explicit construction of
C\ell(V,g)
can be given as follows: take the two sided ideal of the tensor algebra
T_{\mathbb{R}}(V)
of
V

I_{g}:=\langle\bigl\{v\otimes w+w\otimes v+2g(v,w)\big|v,w\in V\bigr\}\rangle

and define

C\ell(V,g):=T_{\mathbb{R}}(V)/I_{g}.

A key property of
C\ell(V,g)
can be obtained from this explicit construction. The
transpose automorphism
on the tensor algebra is defined as

\displaystyle\left(\cdot\right)^{t}:T_{\mathbb{R}}(V)

\displaystyle\longmapsto T_{\mathbb{R}}(V)^{op}

\displaystyle v_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}v_{2}\cdots\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}v_{k}

\displaystyle\longmapsto v_{k}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\cdots v_{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}v_{1}

and preserves the ideal
I_{g}
and thus descends to an algebra automorphism

\left(\cdot\right)^{t}:C\ell(V,g)\xrightarrow{\sim}C\ell(V,g)^{op}.

Given any unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbb{R}
-linear map
\varphi:V\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, if
\varphi
satisfies the
Clifford condition

\varphi(v)\varphi(w)+\varphi(w)\varphi(v)=-2g(v,w),\quad\text{for all }v,w\in V

then
\varphi
lifts to a unique unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra morphism

\varphi:C\ell(V,g)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}.

Note that if
(V,g)\simeq(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n})
is isometric to the standard Euclidean vector space of dimension
n
then

C\ell(V,g)\simeq C\ell(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n}),

and we will denote
C\ell(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n})=C\ell_{n}
. What this effectively means is that we are free to work with one particular family of Clifford algebras when discussing algebraic properties of Clifford algebras and the structure of modules.

One of the key features that the real Clifford algebras
C\ell_{n}
possess is the
8
-fold algebraic Bott periodicity which states that

C\ell_{n+8}\simeq C\ell_{n}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}C\ell_{8},

where the
\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}
is the tensor product of
\mathbb{R}
-algebras. This isomorphism effectively means that to determine the algebraic structure of
C\ell_{n}
one need only determine the algebraic structure of
C\ell_{1}
through
C\ell_{8}
. This is very well known and can be done explicitly as seen in
[
1
]
or
[
5
]

\displaystyle C\ell_{1}

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{C},\quad C\ell_{2}\simeq\mathbb{H},\quad C\ell_{3}\simeq\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H},\quad C\ell_{4}\simeq M_{2}(\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle C\ell_{5}\simeq M_{4}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle,\quad C\ell_{6}\simeq M_{8}(\mathbb{R}),\quad C\ell_{7}\simeq M_{8}(\mathbb{R})\oplus M_{8}(\mathbb{R}),\quad C\ell_{8}\simeq M_{16}(\mathbb{R}).

In particular this shows that the Clifford algebras are either simple
\mathbb{R}
-algebras or direct sums of simple
\mathbb{R}
-algebras. This is particularly convenient for determining the structure of their modules. When the Clifford algebra
C\ell_{n}
is isomorphic to a real matrix algebra
M_{j}(\mathbb{K})
for
\mathbb{K}
a division algebra over
\mathbb{R}
, as is the case when
n\neq 3\text{ (mod $4$)}
, there is up to isomorphism one unique irreducible module given as

S=\mathbb{K}^{j}

and the action is by the usual left action of matrices on vectors. When the Clifford algebra
C\ell_{n}
is isomorphic to two copies of the same matrix algebra
M_{j}(\mathbb{K})\oplus M_{j}(\mathbb{K})
, as is the case when
n=3\text{ (mod $4$)}
, then there are two distinct irreducible modules up to isomorphism given by

S=\mathbb{K}^{j}

where either the first factor acts by matrix-vector multiplication and the second factor acts trivially or the first factor acts trivially and the second factor acts by matrix vector multiplication.

The real Clifford algebras come equipped with a real
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading automorphism
\varphi:C\ell(V,g)\rightarrow C\ell(V,g)
defined on the generators by

\varphi(v)=-v\quad\text{for all }v\in V.

The even and odd components of the Clifford algebras are defined to be the
\pm 1
-eigenspaces of
\varphi

C\ell^{0}(V,g)=\bigl\{\omega\in C\ell_{n}\big|\quad\varphi(\omega)=\omega\bigr\},\quad C\ell^{1}(V,g)=\bigl\{\omega\in C\ell_{n}\big|\quad\varphi(\omega)=-\omega\bigr\}

Furthermore, in dimensions
n=0(\text{ mod }4)
, the
real volume element

\bm{\nu}=e_{1}\cdots e_{n}\in C\ell_{n}
squares to
1

\bm{\nu}\cdot\bm{\nu}=1.

This implies that if we are given a module
C\ell_{n}\circlearrowright M
, then this module becomes
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded by defining

M^{\pm}:=\left(1\pm\bm{\nu}\right)\cdot M.

In the case when
M
is irreducible, this
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading is compatible with the right action of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
. If we are given two a right
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
\mathbb{K}
-module
M
and a left
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
\mathbb{K}
-module
N
, then we can form the tensor product becomes
M\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}N
becomes
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded by defining

\displaystyle\left(M\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}N\right)^{+}

\displaystyle=M^{+}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}N^{+}\oplus M^{-}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}N^{-}

\displaystyle\left(M\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}N\right)^{-}

\displaystyle=M^{+}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}N^{-}\oplus M^{-}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}N^{+}

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading on
M
induces a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading on
\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}}M
by defining
T
to be even if it preserves the splitting
M=M^{+}\oplus M^{-}
and odd if it swaps the splitting.
If
T\in\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}}M
and
S\in\textnormal{End}^{L}_{\mathbb{K}}N
have a well defined grading and
m\otimes n\in M\hat{\otimes}N
with
m\in M^{\pm}
, one defines

T\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}}S\left(m\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}n\right):=(-1)^{\text{deg}S\cdot\text{deg}m}Tm\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}Sn.

For general
T\in\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}}M
and
S\in\textnormal{End}^{L}_{\mathbb{K}}N
one extends the above definition bi-linearly on the graded components of
T
and
S
.

The groups
Pin(V,g)
and
Spin(V,g)
are defined as

Pin(V,g):=\langle\bigl\{v\in V\big|g(v,v)=1\bigr\}\rangle\quad Spin(n):=Pin(V,g)\cap C\ell^{0}(V,g).

If
C\ell^{\times}(V,g)
denotes the collection of invertible elements of
C\ell(V,g)
, then
Pin(V,g)
is a subgroup of
C\ell^{\times}(V,g)
and thus inherits the
twisted adjoint representation

\widetilde{Ad}:C\ell^{\times}(V,g)\rightarrow GL(C\ell(V,g))

given by

\widetilde{Ad}(\omega)(\tau):=\omega\cdot\tau\cdot\varphi(\omega)^{-1}.

Note that if
\tau=v\in V
and
\omega=w\in V
, then

\widetilde{Ad}(w)(v)=v-2\frac{g(v,w)}{|w|^{2}}w,

i.e.
\widetilde{Ad}(w)
is the reflection through the plane perpendicular to
w\in V
. Thus this induces a
2
-to
1
representation

\widetilde{Ad}:Pin(V,g)\rightarrow O(V,g)

which when restricted to
Spin(V,g)
gives a double cover of
SO(V,g)

\widetilde{Ad}:Spin(V,g)\rightarrow SO(V,g).

If we are given an irreducible left real module
S
for
C\ell(V,g)
then the
S
-spin representation
is defined to be the restriction of

\pi_{S}:C\ell(V,g)\rightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(S)

to
Spin(V,g)

\pi_{S}:Spin(V,g)\rightarrow GL(S,\mathbb{R}).

For a given irreducible left module
S
of
C\ell(V,g)
the
intertwiner algebra

\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S):=\bigl\{T\in\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(S)\big|\quad T(c(\omega)s)=c(\omega)T(s)\text{ for all }\omega\in C\ell(V,g),\text{ and }s\in S\bigr\}

is isomorphic to a
\mathbb{R}
-division algebra. Moreover, since all
\mathbb{R}
-division algebras are isomorphic to their opposite algebra, we have that there is an isomorphism

\chi:\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S)\xrightarrow{\sim}\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S)^{op}.

Thus, we can consider the left
C\ell(V,g)
-module as a
C\ell(V,g)
-
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S)
-bimodule

C\ell(V,g)\circlearrowright S\circlearrowleft\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S).

Note that in the case of
C\ell_{n}
there is an isomorphism of algebras
\psi:C\ell_{n-1}\xrightarrow{\sim}C\ell^{0}_{n}
induced by

\psi(v):=v\cdot e_{n},\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

This has an interesting consequence when discussing the intertwiners of irreducible modules of
C\ell_{n}
. For example, consider
C\ell_{5}
and its irreducible action on
\mathbb{R}^{8}
which has an algebra of intertwiners isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
. If we restrict this action to
C\ell^{0}_{5}\simeq C\ell_{4}
, then the algebra of intertwiners grows to an algebra isomorophic to
\mathbb{H}
. What this entails is that if
S
is an irreducible
C\ell(V,g)
-module with intertwiner algebra isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}_{n}
, when one restricts this representation to
Spin(V,g)
, the intertwiner algebra may grow to a larger algebra of intertwiners. For this reason we denote by
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
the intertwiner algebra for the the restriction of an irreducible action of
C\ell_{n}
to
C\ell^{0}_{n}
. These algebras are written up to isomorphism below where
n
is taken
(\text{mod }8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{1}^{0}\simeq

\displaystyle M_{2}\left(\mathbb{R}\right),\quad\mathbb{K}^{0}_{2}\simeq M_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right),\quad\mathbb{K}^{0}_{3}\simeq\mathbb{H}\quad\mathbb{K}^{0}_{4}\simeq\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{0}_{5}

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{H},\quad\mathbb{K}^{0}_{6}\simeq\mathbb{C},\quad\mathbb{K}^{0}_{7}\simeq\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbb{K}^{0}_{8}\simeq\mathbb{R}

whereas

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{1}\simeq\mathbb{C},\quad\mathbb{K}_{2}\simeq\mathbb{H},\quad\mathbb{K}_{3}\simeq\mathbb{H},\quad\mathbb{K}_{4}\simeq\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}_{5}\simeq\mathbb{C},\quad\mathbb{K}_{6}\simeq\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbb{K}_{7}\simeq\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbb{K}_{8}\simeq\mathbb{R}.

Note that
\mathbb{K}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
and the
intersection
of the centers of
\mathbb{K}_{n}
and
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
is always naturally isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
. This has the following consequence: If
S_{n}
is an irreducible module of
C\ell_{n}
, then the real vector space of left-
C\ell_{n}
-right-
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
linear maps
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}}\left(S_{n}\right)
is one dimensional.

The main idea we will use to construct explicit modules for the Clifford algebras goes as follows: By the above classification, if we can find a module for a given
C\ell_{n}
which has the same dimension over
\mathbb{K}_{n}
as the irreducible modules for
C\ell_{n}
, then by uniqueness this must be an irreducible module for
C\ell_{n}
. The catch of course is to find these explicit representations!

3
The General Recipe

Suppose
S_{i}
are irreducible real representations of
C\ell_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 4
. We denote by

c_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{i}\rightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(S_{i})

the
\mathbb{R}
-linear map which induces the representation
C\ell_{i}\circlearrowright S_{i}
. Note that in the case when
i=3
, if we compose
c_{3}
with
-1
, we obtain

-c_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(S\right)

which one can check generates a distinct irreducible representation of
C\ell_{3}
which we denote by
-S_{3}
. Using the fact that the intertwiner algebra is symmetric
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell_{i}}(S_{i})\simeq\textnormal{End}_{C\ell_{i}}(S_{i})^{op}
and the Clifford algebras are also symmetric via the transpose automorphism, we can treat any of our irreducible modules as
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S_{i})-C\ell_{i}
bimodules

\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S_{i})\circlearrowright S_{i}\circlearrowleft C\ell_{i}.

In effect, what we have done is show that there are two versions of a given irreducible representation
S_{i}
, one for which the Clifford action is left-
\mathbb{K}_{i}
-linear or right-
\mathbb{K}_{i}
-linear. This distinction is a superfluous when
\mathbb{K}_{i}\simeq\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{K}_{i}\simeq\mathbb{C}
, but is necessary when
\mathbb{K}_{i}\simeq\mathbb{H}
. We will label the Clifford map by
L
or
R
to distinguish these actions

c^{L}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{i}\rightarrow\textnormal{End}^{L}_{\mathbb{K}_{i}}(S_{i}),\quad c^{R}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{i}\rightarrow\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{i}}(S_{i}).

We further remark that we already know that we can take
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S_{i})\simeq\mathbb{H}
for
2\leq i\leq 4
and
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(S_{1})\simeq\mathbb{C}
.

We are now in a position to construct all irreducible representations for
C\ell_{n}
for
any
n
using the known representations
S_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq 4
. We first construct the representation
S_{8}
. Given
\left(S_{4},c^{R}_{r}\right)
and
\left(S_{4},c^{L}_{4}\right)
, and recalling the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading by the volume element, we can take the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
\mathbb{H}
-tensor product of these modules obtaining

S_{8}:=S_{4}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}S_{4}.

We now obtain a
\mathbb{R}
-linear map

\displaystyle c_{8}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{4}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(S_{4}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}S_{4}\right)

\displaystyle\left(u,v\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto c^{R}_{4}(u)\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}1+1\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}c^{L}_{4}(v)

which satisfies the Clifford condition and thus lifts to a representation

C\ell_{8}\circlearrowright S_{4}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}S_{4}

which is irreducible by dimension comparison. Furthermore note that this module is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded over
\mathbb{R}
since
8=0(\text{ mod $4$})
. Moreover, note that we can cook up the corresponding irreducible modules for
C\ell_{8k}
by simply taking
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded
\mathbb{R}
-tensor products of the above module with itself

S_{8k}:=\left(S_{8}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}

and defining

\displaystyle c_{8k}:\left(\mathbb{R}^{8}\right)^{\oplus^{k}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(S_{8k}\right)

\displaystyle\left(u_{1},\cdots,u_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^{k}I^{\hat{\otimes}^{j-1}_{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{R}}c_{8}(u_{j})\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{R}}I^{\hat{\otimes}^{k-j}_{\mathbb{R}}}

Now consider the general case of
n=8k+r
with
1\leq r\leq 7
. If
1\leq r\leq 4
we take

S_{8k+r}:=\left(S_{8}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}S_{r}.

We make an important point about the graded tensor products seen above: Although
S_{8k}:=\left(S_{8}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}
is perfectly well defined, the tensor product with
S_{r}
will be taken as graded when
r=4
and ungraded otherwise. However, when taking the tensor product of
I_{S_{8k}}
with
c^{R}_{r}
, we will still take the standard
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded tensor product of operators

\displaystyle\left(I_{S_{8k}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{R}}c^{R}_{1}\right)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{S_{8k}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}c^{R}_{1}(x)&\quad\text{on }S^{+}_{8k}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}S_{r},\\
-I_{S_{8k}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}c^{R}_{1}(x)&\quad\text{on }S^{-}_{8k}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}S_{r}\end{array}\right.

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
. We now take

\displaystyle c_{8k+r}:\left(\mathbb{R}^{8}\right)^{\oplus^{k}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{r}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(S_{8k+r}\right)

\displaystyle\left(u,v\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto c_{8k}(u)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}I_{S_{r}}+I_{S_{8k}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{R}}c^{R}_{r}(v)

and observe that this satisfies the Clifford condition so lifts to an irreducible representation of
C\ell_{8k+r}
by dimension comparison. When
5\leq r\leq 7
, first note

S_{8k+4}=\left(S_{8}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{R}}S_{4}

is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded and note that the grading and
c_{8k+4}
are compatible with the right
\mathbb{H}
-action. We now take

S_{8k+r}:=S_{8k+4}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}_{r-4}}S_{r-4}

with

\displaystyle c_{8k+r}:\left(\left(\mathbb{R}^{8}\right)^{\oplus^{k}}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)\oplus\mathbb{R}^{r-4}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(S_{8k+r}\right)

\displaystyle\left(u,v\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto c^{R}_{8k+4}(u)\otimes_{\mathbb{K}_{r-4}}I_{S_{r-4}}+I\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{K}_{r-4}}c^{L}_{r-4}(v).

The above map satisfies the Clifford condition and thus lifts to an irreducible representation of
C\ell_{8k+r}
by dimension comparison. In the case when
r=3\text{ (mod $4$)}
there is still one more irreducible representation floating around which can be accounted for by replacing
S_{3}
by
-S_{3}
in the above construction. We remark that in general, to construct an irreducible module for
C\ell_{n}
out of the four
S_{i}
one typically needs tensor products not only over
\mathbb{R}
but also over
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{H}
. This may explain the internal complexity of the representations and why it took some time for this construction to come to light.

3.1
The First Explicit Family

We construct here a family of modules for
C\ell_{n}
modeled on the multi-vectors of
\mathbb{H}
. To begin with, note that when
n=1
, if we consider the inclusion

\displaystyle c_{1}:\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{Im}\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto ix

then since

c_{1}(x)c_{1}(y)+c_{1}(y)c_{1}(x)=-2x\cdot y,\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R},

we see that
c_{1}
lifts to a unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra morphism

c_{1}:C\ell_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}.

One can check by dimension comparison that this is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
-algebras. In particular we can take as our spinor module in dimension
1
to be
\mathbb{C}
with the left action coming from
c_{1}
.

In dimension
2
, if we consider the standard
\mathbb{R}
-linear inclusion

c_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}

and then treat
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the imaginary part of
\mathbb{H}
, then we obtain an inclusion

c_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\text{Im}\mathbb{H}

which satisfies

c_{2}(x)c_{2}(y)+c_{2}(y)c_{2}(x)=-2g_{2}(x,y)\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Thus
c_{2}
lifts to a unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra morphism

c_{2}:C\ell_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}

which is an isomorphism by dimension comparison. In particular we can take our spinor module in dimension
2
to be
\mathbb{H}
with the left action coming from
c_{2}
.

In dimension
3
, we can use the same observation in
n=2
and identify

c_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\text{Im}\mathbb{H},

in which case
c_{3}
satisfies

c_{3}(x)c_{3}(y)+c_{3}(y)c_{3}(x)=-2g_{3}(x,y),\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

This will give us an induced unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra morphism

c_{3}:C\ell_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}.

Note however that this will
not
be an isomorphism since the
\mathbb{R}
dimension of
C\ell_{3}
is
8
while that of
\mathbb{H}
is 4. Note however that
c_{3}
does produce a spinor module via

\displaystyle C\ell_{3}\times\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle(x,q)

\displaystyle\mapsto c_{3}(x)\cdot q.

This module is irreducible via the classification
C\ell_{3}\simeq\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}
. To obtain the other spinor module, simply compose
c_{3}
with multiplication by
-1
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e.

\displaystyle-c_{3}:\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{Im}\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto-c_{3}(x).

One has that

c_{3}(x)c_{3}(y)+c_{3}(y)c_{3}(x)=-2g_{3}(x,y)\quad\text{for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

and thus
-c_{3}
lifts to a unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra morphism

-c_{3}:C\ell_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}.

Thus we have obtained another irreducible spinor module for
C\ell_{3}
. To see that the modules given by
c_{3}
and
-c_{3}
are distinct, note that for the element
\omega=e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}\in C\ell_{3}
, for
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, one has

c_{3}(\omega)=-1,\quad-c_{3}(\omega)=1.

Thus there can be no intertwining map between the modules determined by
c_{3}
and
-c_{3}
. We take these to be our spinor modules in dimension
3
. Note that
c_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 3
are naturally right
\mathbb{K}_{i}
-linear.

Things take an interesting turn in dimension
4
. Firstly, we no longer can employ the trick of embedding the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{4}
into the imaginary part of a larger
associative

\mathbb{R}
-division algebra for which the Clifford relations will be satisfied. Rather here we will treat
\mathbb{R}^{4}
as
\mathbb{H}
and take as our spinor module
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
. We first briefly discuss the
\mathbb{H}
-bimodule
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
. Note that if
\mathcal{A}
is a unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra, and
M
is a
bimodule
over
\mathcal{A}
, then the
\mathcal{A}
-tensor algebra is

T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M}):=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}T^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})

where for
k>0

T^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M}):=\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}\otimes_{\mathcal{A}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}

where there are
k
-tensor factors appearing in the above tensor product. For
k=0
we take the convention

T^{0}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})=\mathcal{A}.

Note that
T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
remains a
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule in a natural manner. Thus the
\mathcal{A}
-tensor algebra
T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})
is an
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{A}
-algebra. If we take
I_{\wedge}
to be the two sided ideal

I_{\wedge}:=\langle\bigl\{m\otimes_{\mathcal{A}}m\big|m\in\mathcal{M}\bigr\}\rangle,

then the
\mathcal{A}
-exterior algebra of
\mathcal{M}
is defined to be

\wedge_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}:=T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M})/I_{\wedge}.

Note that
\wedge_{\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{M}
remains a
\mathcal{A}
-
\mathcal{A}
-algebra. We are interested in employing this construction when
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{H}
. In this case we obtain the quaternic exterior algebra

\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}.

Note that we have
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\simeq\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}
as
\mathbb{H}
-bimodules. If we further note that
\mathbb{H}
has a right quaternic metric

g_{\mathbb{H}}^{R}:\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}

given via

g^{R}_{\mathbb{H}}(q,w):=\overline{q}\cdot w,

then for any
q\in\mathbb{H}
we obtain a left contraction operator
\iota^{L}_{q}:\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
. If we write
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}=\wedge^{0}_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\oplus\wedge^{1}_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
then in matrix form the operator
\iota^{L}_{q}
is

\iota^{L}_{q}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\overline{q}\cdot\\
0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

where
\overline{q}\cdot
denotes
left multiplication
by
\overline{q}
. In particular
\iota^{L}_{q}
is right-
\mathbb{H}
-linear. We also have the left wedge operator defined
\varepsilon^{L}_{q}:\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
for any
q\in\mathbb{H}
where in matrix form it is given as

\varepsilon^{L}_{q}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
q\cdot&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

here again
q\cdot
denotes left multiplication. Note also that
\varepsilon^{L}_{q}
is right-
\mathbb{H}
-linear for any
q\in\mathbb{H}
. We now define

\displaystyle c^{R}_{4}:\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{H}}(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\mapsto\varepsilon^{L}_{q}-\iota^{L}_{q}.

First note that
c^{R}_{4}(q)
is
only

\mathbb{R}
-linear in
q
. Secondly, note that

c^{R}_{4}(q)c^{R}_{4}(w)+c^{R}_{4}(w)c^{R}_{4}(q)=-2g_{4}(q,w)\quad\text{for all }q,w\in\mathbb{H}.

Thus
c^{R}_{4}
lifts to unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra morphism

c^{R}_{4}:C\ell_{4}\rightarrow\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{H}}(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}).

By dimension comparison this will be an isomorphism and we have thus obtained an explicit model for real spinors in dimension
4
using quaternionic multi-vectors. Furthermore, in this case the spinor module is naturally
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded by taking the degree of the multivector to be the choice of grading.

By the general recipe given above, we see that for
n=8k+r
,
k\geq 0
we can take

\displaystyle S_{8k+r}

\displaystyle:=\left(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C},\quad r=1

\displaystyle S_{8k+r}

\displaystyle:=\left(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H},\quad r=2,3

\displaystyle S_{8k+r}

\displaystyle:=\left(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H},\quad r=4

\displaystyle S_{8k+r}

\displaystyle:=\left(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C},\quad r=5

\displaystyle S_{8k+r}

\displaystyle:=\left(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k}_{\mathbb{R}}}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\otimes_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H},\quad r=6,7

\displaystyle S_{8k+r}

\displaystyle:=\left(\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\hat{\otimes}_{\mathbb{H}}\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}\right)^{\hat{\otimes}^{k+1}_{\mathbb{R}}},\quad\quad r=8

The astute reader may have noticed that tensoring by
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{H}
in dimensions
8k+r
,
5\leq r\leq 7
is redundant within these models for
S_{8k+r}
. One could simply take
S_{8k+r}
to be some appropriate tensoring of
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
with itself
k+1
-times, and then define the remaining action by the
r
-variables on the right by multiplication by
i
,
j
, or
k
. However, as we shall see below, there are different models for
S_{r}
for which a canonical identification of
S_{r}
with
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{H}
, or
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
is not available.

4
The Notion of Spin Coordinate System

Recall that for
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
, a real, oriented Euclidean vector space of
\mathbb{R}
-dimension
n
, one can consider the set of oriented orthogonal frames
P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
defined as the set of lists of vectors
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
such that the elements of the list are unit length, pair-wise orthogonal, and the full list forms a basis of
V
which is compatible with the orientation on
V
. An alternative perspective is to view elements of
P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
as linear isometries

\phi:(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n})\rightarrow(V,g)

which preserves the orientation (here
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n})
is given the canonical orientation). From the latter perspective we can consider
P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
as the “moduli space of oriented orthonormal coordinate systems” for
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
. The two interpretations of
P_{SO}(v,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
are clearly equivalent and we will freely interchange between the two when convenient. For us, the latter perspective on frames will be best when describing the spin coordinate systems of
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Consider
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
along with an irreducible left module
\mathscr{S}_{V}
for
C\ell(V,g)
. We further require that
\mathscr{S}_{V}
admits the following extra
bells and whistles

(I)

A right action of the division algebra
\mathbb{K}_{n}
for which the Clifford algebra is a matrix algebra over in such a way that
C\ell(V,g)\circlearrowright\mathscr{S}_{V}\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}_{n}
becomes a
C\ell_{n}-\mathbb{K}_{n}
-bimodule. This is effectively a choice of unital
\mathbb{R}
-algebra isomorphism
\textnormal{End}_{Cl}\left(\mathscr{S}_{V}\right)\simeq\mathbb{K}_{n}
. For
\mathbb{K}_{n}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{K}_{n}=\mathbb{C}
the choice is unique, but for
\mathbb{K}_{n}=\mathbb{H}
there is a moduli of choices.

(II)

A
real
inner product
g_{\mathscr{S}}
such that for any given
v\in V
and
a\in\text{Im}\mathbb{K}_{n}
the actions
c^{L}(v)
and
a_{R}
are skew-adjoint

(III)

A right action by the algebra
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
for which
C\ell^{0}_{n}\circlearrowright\mathscr{S}_{V}\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
is a
C\ell^{0}_{n}-\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
bimodule and moreover for which the right actions by
\mathbb{K}_{n}
and
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
are compatible with respect to the natural inclusion
\mathbb{K}_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
. Once again, this is equivalent to a choice of untial
\mathbb{R}
-algebra isomorphism
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell^{0}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{V}\right)\simeq\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
which extends the chosen isomorphism
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell}(\mathscr{S}_{V})\simeq\mathbb{K}_{n}
in (I). In general, there may be a moduli of possible choices.

The existence of such structures on
any
given module
\mathscr{S}_{V}
is given in
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
. A rather important remark with regards to such metrics
g_{\mathscr{S}}
is that not only do they exist, but they are also determined up to a positive constant, i.e. if
g^{1}_{\mathscr{S}}
and
g^{2}_{\mathscr{S}}
both satisfy condition (II), then there exists
\lambda>0
such that

g^{2}_{\mathscr{S}}=\lambda g^{1}_{\mathscr{S}}.

On the quaternic multivector model, there is a natural choice of metric
g_{\mathscr{S}}
which we briefly describe. On
\mathbb{C}
, we let
g_{S_{1}}
be the real part of the standard Hermitian metric
g_{\mathbb{C}}(z,w)=\overline{z}w
on
\mathbb{C}
. On
\mathbb{H}
, we take
g_{S_{2}}
and
g_{S_{3}}
to be the real part of the standard quaternic metric on
\mathbb{H}

g_{\mathbb{H}}\left(q,w\right)=\overline{q}w
. On
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
we take
g_{S_{4}}
to be the real part of the quaternic exterior power of the standard quaternic metric on
\mathbb{H}
,
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}g_{\mathbb{H}}\left(\left(q_{1},q_{2}\right),\left(w_{1},w_{2}\right)\right)=\overline{q_{1}}w_{1}+\overline{q}_{2}w_{2}
. For a general
S_{8k+r}
,
0\leq r\leq 8
, we take
g_{S_{r}}
to be tensor products of the corresponding metrics on
S_{i}
defined above.

Fix an irreducible spinor module
S_{n}
for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
along with a
spin metric

g_{S_{n}}
satisfying (II) above. If we are given a module with such structure, denoted as

C\ell^{\bullet}(V,g)\circlearrowright(\mathscr{S}_{V},g_{\mathscr{S}})\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}_{n},

then we define the
spin coordinate systems of
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
as the set of isometries
\Phi:(S_{n},g_{S_{n}})\rightarrow(\mathscr{S}_{V},g_{\mathscr{S}})
which are right
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
-linear and such that there exists a
unique

\phi\in P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
for which given any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
s\in S_{n}
one has

\Phi(c(v)\cdot s)=c(\phi(v))\cdot\Phi(s).

That this set is non-empty follows from the classification of Clifford modules. We denote the set of spin coordinate systems of
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
as
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
. When no confusion can arise, we will omit the extra details from our notation of
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
. Note that for any given
g\in Spin(n)
, one has that
\Phi\circ g\cdot\in P_{Spin}(V)
and thus
P_{Spin}(V)
admits a natural
free right action
of
Spin(n)

\displaystyle P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})\times Spin(n)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})

\displaystyle(\Phi,g)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\Phi\circ g\cdot.

Furthermore, note that
P_{Spin}(V,g)
admits a natural map

\displaystyle p:P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle\longmapsto\phi

which is compatible with the double covering
Ad:Spin(n)\rightarrow SO(n)

p(\Phi\circ g)=p(\Phi)\circ Ad(g),\quad\text{for all }\Phi\in P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})\text{ and all }g\in Spin(n).

To see that the map
p
is
2
-to-
1
suppose that
\Phi,\Psi\in P_{Spin}(V,g)
are such that
p(\Phi)=p(\Psi)
. Then one has that

\Psi^{-1}\Phi:(S_{n},g_{S_{n}})\rightarrow(S_{n},g_{S_{n}})

such that for all
v\in C\ell_{n}
,
s\in S_{n}
, and
a\in\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
, one has

\Psi^{-1}\Phi(c(v)\cdot s)=c(v)\cdot\Psi^{-1}\Phi(s),\quad\Psi^{-1}\Phi(s\cdot a)=\Psi^{-1}\Phi(s)\cdot a.

The only such transformations of
(S_{n},g_{S_{n}})
which satisfy the above relations are
\pm I_{S_{n}}
, for
I_{S_{n}}
the identity operator of
S_{n}
. Thus any given preimage
p^{-1}(\phi)
is a
2
-point set. To see that
p
is
surjective
consider a fixed
\phi\in P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
and a fixed
\Psi\in P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
.Then
p(\Psi)=\psi
and there exists a unique
R\in SO(n)
such that
\psi\circ R=\phi
. Then if
g\in Spin(n)
is chosen so that
Ad(g)=R
, then
p(\Psi\circ g)=p(\Psi)\circ Ad(g)=\phi\circ R=\psi
. Thus the map
p
is indeed
2
-to-
1
.

What the above computation has shown is that if we were to consider the spin coordinate systems for
S_{n}
then we have

P_{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},S_{n})=Spin(n),

i.e. a linear isometry of
(S_{n},g_{S_{n}})
which commutes with the right action of
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
and commutes with the left action of
C\ell_{n}

up to an oriented isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
must be a left action by an element
g\in Spin(n)
. From a certain perspective this is a geometric characterization of spin transformations in the sense that special orthogonal transformations are those which are linear isometries of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n})
which preserve the orientation.

We have yet to check that the action of
Spin(n)
on
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
is
transitive
. Moreover, we have also not considered a topology and smooth structure for
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
and shown that the action of
Spin(n)
is
smooth
. The choice of smooth structure is the natural choice: if we fix
\Phi\in P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
, then the map

\displaystyle F_{\Phi}:P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})

\displaystyle\rightarrow P_{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},S_{n})

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle\mapsto\Psi^{-1}\Phi

is a set bijection which is equivariant with respect to the right action of
Spin(n)
. Since
Spin(n)
act transitively on
P_{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},S_{n})
, we have that it also acts transitively on
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
. We use the map
F_{\Phi}
to equip
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
with the smooth structure of
P_{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},S_{n})
. If we were to choose a different
\Psi\in P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S})
then there exists a unique
g\in Spin(n)
such that

F_{\Psi}\circ F_{\Phi}^{-1}(h)=g\cdot h\quad\text{for }h\in P_{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^{n},g_{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},S_{n})

and which implies smooth structure given by
F_{\Psi}
and
F_{\Phi}
agree. We see from the defining smooth structure that the map

p:P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})\rightarrow P_{SO}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})

is smooth. Thus we have obtained our model of spin coordinate systems
P_{Spin}(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
.

4.1
Spin Structures on Smooth Vector Bundles

We now wish to extend the notion of spin coordinate systems of
(V,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{V})
to that of a real smooth vector bundle
E\rightarrow M
over a smooth manifold. We will assume that
E\rightarrow M
is equipped with a Euclidean fiber metric
g_{E}
and has a global orientation
\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Recall that
(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})\rightarrow M

admits a spin structure
if there exists a principal
Spin(n)
-bundle
P\circlearrowleft Spin(n)\rightarrow M
and a fibered double covering

p:P\rightarrow P_{SO}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})

such that for all
g\in Spin(n)
one has
p(\Phi\cdot g)=p(\Phi)\cdot Ad(g)
. It is known that in the
Spin^{c}
setting that a
Spin^{c}
-structure is equivalent to a bundle of complex Hermitian vector spaces
(\mathscr{S},h)\rightarrow M
which admits an irreducible left action by the bundle of complexified Clifford algebras
\mathbb{C}\ell(E,g_{E})\rightarrow M
(Question 4.36 in
[
9
]
). In this case, the analogue in the real setting is given below. We let
\underline{\mathbb{K}_{n}}
denote the trivial bundle of division algebras over
M
.

Theorem 4.1
.

An oriented Euclidean vector bundle
(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})\rightarrow M
of rank
n
admits a spin structure if and only if there is a real Euclidean vector bundle
(\mathscr{S}_{E},g_{\mathscr{S}})\rightarrow M
which is a bundle of
C\ell(E,g_{E})-\underline{\mathbb{K}_{n}}
bimodules as well as a bundle of
C\ell^{0}(E,g_{E})-\underline{\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}}
-bimodules for which the Clifford action is irreducible and the Clifford action by vectors in
E
and right multiplication by
a\in\emph{Im}\underline{\mathbb{K}_{n}}
is skew-adjoint.

Proof.

Suppose that
(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})\rightarrow M
admits a spin lift
P\circlearrowleft Spin(n)\rightarrow P_{SO}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})\circlearrowleft SO(n)
. If
S_{n}
denotes the real irreducible module constructed for
C\ell_{n}
above, note that the spin representation
\rho:Spin(n)\rightarrow GL(S_{n},\mathbb{R})
is given by restriction of the action
C\ell_{n}\circlearrowright S_{n}
to
Spin(n)
. In particular, the spin representation inherits all of the extra bells and whistles that the Clifford action has, i.e. commutativity with the right action by
\mathbb{K}_{n}
and the real metric
g_{S_{n}}
for which the left action by vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and left action by elements in
\text{Im}\mathbb{K}_{n}
is skew-adjoint. Moreover, note that
Spin(n)\subseteq C\ell^{0}_{n}
, hence if
C\ell^{0}_{n}
has a larger right intertwiner algebra
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
, then the action
Spin(n)\circlearrowright S_{n}
will be compatible with the right action by
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}
as well. Since the right action
C\ell_{n}\circlearrowright S_{n}
is compatible with the respective spin representations, this action descends to an action

P\times_{Ad\rho}C\ell_{n}\circlearrowright(P\times_{\rho}S_{n},g_{S_{n}})\circlearrowleft\underline{\mathbb{K}_{n}},\quad P\times_{Ad\rho}C\ell^{0}_{n}\circlearrowright P\times_{\rho}S_{n}\circlearrowleft\underline{\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}}

Because
C\ell(E,g_{E})\simeq P\times_{Ad\rho}C\ell_{n}
as a bundle of algebras, we have the asserted spinor module.

Conversely, suppose we are given such a spinor module

C\ell(E,g_{E})\circlearrowright(\mathscr{S}_{E},g_{\mathscr{S}})\circlearrowleft\underline{\mathbb{K}_{n}},\quad C\ell^{0}(E,g_{E})\circlearrowright\mathscr{S}_{E}\circlearrowright\underline{\mathbb{K}^{0}_{n}}

We define the
principal bundle of spin coordinate systems for
(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{E})
to be the disjiont union

P_{Spin}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{E}):=\bigsqcup_{m\in M}P_{Spin}(E_{m},g_{E_{m}},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{E_{m}}).

The smooth structure is the natural choice and this fibration
P_{Spin}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{E})\rightarrow M
admits a right free and fibered transitive action of
Spin(n)
such that the natural map

p:P_{Spin}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{E})\rightarrow P_{SO}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})

is a smooth, fibered double covering which is compatible with the double covering
Ad:Spin(n)\rightarrow SO(n)
. Thus, we have obtained the desired spin structure
P_{Spin}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},\mathscr{S}_{E})
.
∎

Given a spin lift
P\circlearrowleft Spin(n)\rightarrow P_{SO}\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)
, one can check that the map

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\longrightarrow P_{Spin}\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}},P\times_{\rho}S_{n}\right)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(s\rightarrow[(p,s)]\right)

where
[p,s]
denotes the equivalence class in the quotient
P\times_{\rho}S_{n}
, gives an isomorphism of principal
Spin(n)
-bundles. Thus, what we have really shown is that
every principal
Spin
lift of
P_{SO}\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)
is, up to isomorphism, of the type we have described. i.e. a bundle of spin coordinate systems for a Euclidean bundle of real spinors
\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},g_{\mathscr{S}}\right)\rightarrow M
.

4.2
Constructing Spinor Bundles

Suppose that
M=\cup_{\alpha\in A}U_{\alpha}
where all finite intersections of the
U_{\alpha}
are contractible, and we are also given explicit sections
e_{\alpha}\in\Gamma\left(P_{SO}(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})\big|_{U_{\alpha}}\right)
. The
e_{\alpha}
generate an oriented Euclidean bundle isomorphism

\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\big|_{U_{\alpha}}\simeq_{e_{\alpha}}U_{\alpha}\times\left(\mathbb{R}^{r},g_{r},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)

which induce a spinor bundle for
C\ell\left(E,g_{E}\right)
along
U_{\alpha}

\left(C\ell^{\bullet}\left(E,g_{E}\right)\right)\big|_{U_{\alpha}}\circlearrowright_{e_{\alpha}}U_{\alpha}\times\left(S_{r},g_{S_{r}}\right)\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}_{r}.

The transition functions
g_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\rightarrow SO(r)
have
two possible lifts

\pm\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\rightarrow Spin(r).

Once a choice of lift has been made, one can use the
\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}
as transition data for
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\times\left(S_{r},g_{S_{r}}\right)
. In general, what one will find is that the transition data
almost
satisfy the cocylce condition

\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}\tilde{g}_{\beta\gamma}=\pm\tilde{g}_{\alpha\gamma}.

What one then obtains is
Čech
2
-cochain
with coefficients in
\{\pm 1\}
,

w_{2}\left(E,\{U_{\alpha}\}\right)\in C^{2}\left(\{U_{\alpha}\},\{\pm 1\}\right)
defined by

w_{2}\left(E,\{U_{\alpha}\}\right)\left(U_{\beta\gamma\delta}\right):=\tilde{g}_{\beta\gamma}\tilde{g}_{\gamma\delta}\tilde{g}_{\beta\delta}^{-1}.

One can check that this is in fact a
2
-cocycle. In general, the Čech cohomology class of this cocylce is the
second Steifel-Whitney class
of the Euclidean vector bundle
(E,g_{E})
and is non-trivial in general. In the case when
w_{2}\left(E,\{U_{\alpha}\}\right)
is
exact
, i.e. when
w_{2}\left(E,\{U_{\alpha}\}\right)=\delta\tau
for
\tau\in C^{1}\left(\{U_{\alpha}\},\{\pm 1\}\right)
, then this allows us to
adjust
our choice of patching data for
U_{\alpha}\times\left(S_{r},g_{S_{r}}\right)
along
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}
by assigning

\tau\left(U_{\alpha\beta}\right)\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\rightarrow Spin(r)

as the new patching data. One now checks that
w_{2}\left(E,\{U_{\alpha}\}\right)=\delta\tau
ensures that the adjusted patching data satisfies the cocylce condition.

An alternative perspective to the gluing of local spinor modules can be achieved through the use of
\mathbb{R}
-gerbes. As above, each local oriented Euclidean framing
e_{\alpha}
of
P_{SO}\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)
gives us an associated spinor module

C\ell^{\bullet}\left(E,g_{E}\right)\big|_{U_{\alpha}}\circlearrowright_{e_{\alpha}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{e_{\alpha}},g_{\mathscr{S}}\right)\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}_{r}.

On a given intersection
U_{\alpha\beta}
, we obtain a real line bundle

L_{\alpha\beta}:=\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{e_{\alpha}},\mathscr{S}_{e_{\beta}}\right)

and on a three fold intersection
U_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
, composition of linear maps gives an isomorphism

c_{\alpha\beta\gamma}:L_{\alpha\beta}\big|_{U_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}L_{\alpha\beta}\big|_{U_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}\xlongrightarrow{\sim}L_{\alpha\gamma}\big|_{U_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}.

We denote this family of
\mathbb{R}
-line bundles by

\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}_{r}^{0}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\cdot},\mathscr{S}_{\cdot}\right)\rightarrow U_{\cdot,\cdot}

and call this the
spin orientation
\mathbb{R}
-gerbe associated to the trivializing cover
\{U_{\alpha}\}
. An
\mathbb{R}
-gerbe
L_{\cdot,\cdot}\rightarrow U_{\cdot,\cdot}
admits a
global non-vanishing section
if each
L_{\alpha\beta}\rightarrow U_{\alpha\beta}
admits a non-vanishing section
s_{\alpha\beta}
such that

c_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\left(s_{\alpha\beta}\otimes s_{\beta\gamma}\right)=s_{\beta\gamma}.

A choice of non-vanishing section
s_{\alpha\beta}\in\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\alpha},\mathscr{S}_{\beta}\right)
is equivalent, upon rescalling if necessary, to a choice of lifting
\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha\beta}\rightarrow Spin(r)
of the transition date
g_{\alpha\beta}:U_{\alpha\beta}\rightarrow SO(r)
. Thus, we see that
the oriented Euclidean bundle
(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}})
admits a spin structure if and only if the spin orientation
\mathbb{R}
-gerbe associated to the trivializing cover
\{U_{\alpha}\}
admits a global non-vanishing section
. One should compare this viewpoint on the existence of spin structures to the fact that a vector bundle
E\rightarrow M
admits an orientation if and only if the
\mathbb{R}
-orientation line bundle
\wedge^{top}_{\mathbb{R}}E\rightarrow M
admits a global section.

There are instances when one is working with a cover
\{U_{\alpha}\}
which is trivializing but whose intersections may not all be contractible. We can still construct the
\mathbb{R}
-gerbe
\textnormal{End}_{Cl,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\cdot},\mathscr{S}_{\cdot}\right)\rightarrow U_{\cdot,\cdot}
however what can occur now that is that for a given
U_{\alpha,\beta}
, the line bundle
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\alpha},\mathscr{S}_{\beta}\right)\rightarrow U_{\alpha\beta}
may not admit a global section. Suppose however that we know in advance that our bundle
\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\rightarrow M
admits a global spinor bundle

C\ell^{\bullet}\left(E,g_{E}\right)\circlearrowright\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},g_{\mathscr{S}}\right)\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}_{r}

and that each
U_{\alpha}
is contractible. Then on each
U_{\alpha}
, the line bundle

\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E}\big|_{U_{\alpha}},\mathscr{S}_{\alpha}\right)\rightarrow U_{\alpha}

is trivial. Moreover along a given intersection
U_{\alpha\beta}
we have an isomorphism of line bundles

\displaystyle\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\alpha},\mathscr{S}_{E}\big|_{U_{\alpha}}\right)\big|_{U_{\alpha\beta}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E}\big|_{U_{\beta}},\mathscr{S}_{\beta}\right)\big|_{U_{\alpha\beta}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\alpha},\mathscr{S}_{\beta}\right).

Thus, we have the following observation:
Suppose
\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\rightarrow M
is a spin oriented Euclidean bundle and
\{U_{\alpha}\}
is a trivializing open cover with each
U_{\alpha}
contractible. Then for any choice of local spinor module
\mathscr{S}_{\alpha}\rightarrow U_{\alpha}
, the line bundles
\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}^{0}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{\alpha},\mathscr{S}_{\beta}\right)\rightarrow U_{\alpha\beta}
must be trivial along
U_{\alpha\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta
. This produces a useful criteria for determining when an oriented Euclidean vector bundle
does not
admit a global bundle of spinors.

4.3
Spinors and Real Line Bundles

Recall that for a given orientable Euclidean vector bundle
\left(E,g_{E}\right)\rightarrow M
, the set of orientations is acted on freely and transitively by the
zeroth cohomology
of
M
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-coefficients
H^{0}\left(M,\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)
. Similarly, there is typically not a
unique
choice of spin structure for an oriented Euclidean bundle
\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\rightarrow M
, instead the isomorphism classes of possible spin structures are acted on freely and transitively by the
first cohomology
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-coefficients
H^{1}\left(M,\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)
when
M
is a finite CW complex. We give a brief treatment of this fact from the perspective of spinor bundles. Suppose that
\mathscr{S}_{E}\rightarrow M
is a fixed real spinor bundle with all of the bells and whistles. Suppose that
\left(L,g_{L}\right)\rightarrow M
is a Euclidean line bundle over
M
. Then we can
twist
\mathscr{S}_{E}
by
L

\left(L,g_{L}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},g_{\mathscr{S}}\right)\rightarrow M.

While there are instances when this bundle is isomorphic to the original
\mathscr{S}_{E}
at the level of
\mathbb{R}
-vector bundles, if we consider the action of
C\ell(E,g_{E})
on
L\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{S}_{E}
defined by

c(v)\cdot a\otimes s:=a\otimes c(v)\cdot s

then this gives us a new real spinor bundle. Moreover, one has

L\simeq\textnormal{End}_{C\ell,\mathbb{K}_{n}^{0}}\left(L\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{S}_{E},\mathscr{S}_{E}\right).

Thus there will be a global identification of
L\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{S}_{E}
with
\mathscr{S}_{E}
as bundles of
C\ell^{\bullet}(E,g_{E})-\underline{\mathbb{K}_{n}^{\bullet}}
bimodules if and only if
L
is trivial. Under the natural identification of the
real Picard group
with
H^{1}\left(M,\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)
via the first Steifel-Whitney class

w_{1}:\left(\text{Vect}^{1}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(M\right),\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^{1}\left(M,\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)

we have recovered the action of the real Picard group on the set of spin structures of
\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)
. We leave it to the reader to check that this action is free and transitive.

5
Novelties

We now collect a few novel low dimensional constructions from our angle and include a discussion on the tensor product of spinor fields.

5.1
The Spin Geometry of a Surface

In the same way that much of the intuition for the
Levi-Civita connection
is derived from studying surfaces
\Sigma
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we will use our explicit spinor representations to unravel the spin geometry of an oriented
\left(\Sigma,\bm{\circlearrowleft}\right)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus the vector bundle in consideration will be
T\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma
and the metric will be the
first fundamental form
of
\Sigma
, denoted
g_{\Sigma}
. Firstly, note that the orientation of
\Sigma
gives a global normal vector field
\nu:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
which gives a global trivialization of

\displaystyle\wedge^{1}T\Sigma\oplus\wedge^{2}T\Sigma

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\sim}\underline{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

\displaystyle\left(\vec{v},a\bm{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\vec{v}+a\nu.

Note that we may treat
\mathbb{R}^{3}\simeq\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
. Given that the metric
g_{\Sigma}
is simply the restriction of
g_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
to
T\Sigma
, the above map gives an identification of bundles of algebras

C\ell\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma}\right)\simeq\underline{\mathbb{H}}.

Thus if we take
\mathscr{S}_{\Sigma}:=\underline{\mathbb{H}}
with the standard quaternic metric

g_{\mathbb{H}}\left(q,w\right):=\overline{q}\cdot w,

then we have almost obtained a real spinor module

C\ell\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma}\right)\circlearrowright\left(\underline{\mathbb{H}},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)\circlearrowleft\mathbb{H}.

In dimension
n=2
, the intertwiner algebra
\mathbb{K}^{0}_{2}\simeq M_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
, thus there should be a natural action of
M_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
on
\underline{\mathbb{H}}
which commutes with the right action of
C\ell^{0}(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma})
. Given a point
p\in\Sigma
, note that

C\ell^{0}(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma})_{p}\simeq\text{Span}\{1,\nu_{p}\}\simeq\mathbb{C}.

Because the action of
A\in M_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{H}
is of the form

A\cdot q=k\cdot q\cdot w+q\cdot w^{\prime},\quad w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H},

we see that the action of
A\in M_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
on
\underline{\mathbb{H}}_{p}
is

A\cdot_{p}q:=\nu_{p}\cdot q\cdot w+q\cdot w^{\prime}.

This gives us a spinor bundle with the desired properties

C\ell^{\bullet}\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma}\right)\circlearrowright\left(\underline{\mathbb{H}},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}_{2}.

We obtain an induced principal
Spin(2)
lift of the special orthogonal frame bundle

P_{Spin}\left(\underline{\mathbb{H}},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)\circlearrowleft Spin(2)\rightarrow P_{SO}\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\circlearrowleft SO(2).

Suppose that
x\in\Sigma
is fixed and we are given an oriented orthonormal frame
e_{1},e_{2}\in T_{x}\Sigma
. Then there is a unique
R\in SO(3)
such that

R\cdot[i\hskip 2.84544ptj\hskip 2.84544ptk]=[e_{1}\hskip 2.84544pte_{2}\hskip 2.84544pt\nu_{x}],

where
i,j,k
are the standard quaternic basis for
\mathbb{R}^{3}=\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
. Let
g\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
be viewed as a unit quaterion such that for every
q\in\text{Im}\mathbb{H}
one has

g\cdot q\cdot g^{-1}=Rq.

Then the
spin coordinate system corresponding to
g
is the isometry

\displaystyle\Phi_{g}:\left(\mathbb{H},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\left(\mathbb{H},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\longmapsto g\cdot q.

Given
v=ai+bj
, we have

\Phi_{g}\left(v\cdot q\right)=g\cdot v\cdot q=g\cdot v\cdot g^{-1}\cdot g\cdot q=\left(ae_{1}+be_{2}\right)\Phi_{g}(v)

and for
w\in\mathbb{H}
and
A\in M_{2}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
expressed as
A=k_{L}a_{R}+b_{R}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

\Phi_{g}(q\cdot w)=\Phi_{g}(q)w,\quad\Phi_{g}\left(\left(k_{L}a_{R}+b_{R}\right)w\right)=\left(\nu_{L}a_{R}+b_{R}\right)\Phi_{g}\left(w\right)

which shows that
\Phi_{g}
is indeed a spin coordinate system. Note that the other choice of spin coordinate system covering the frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
would be
\Phi_{-g}
.

5.2
Parallel Translation of Spinors

We briefly recall E. Cartan’s
moving frame
approach to the Levi-Civita connection. Suppose
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow\Sigma
is a smooth curve and
t_{0}\in(a,b)
. Then if
V_{t}\in\Gamma\left(\gamma^{*}T\Sigma\right)
, the
covariant derivative of
V_{t}
along
\gamma
is defined as

D_{t}V_{t}:=P^{T\Sigma}\frac{d}{dt}V_{t}.

In the above, we are treating
V_{t}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
to take
\frac{d}{dt}
in the ordinary sense, and
P^{T\Sigma}
is the projection of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to
T\Sigma
. We say that
V_{t}
is
parallel along
\gamma
if
D_{t}V_{t}\equiv 0
. Given
t_{0}\in(a,b)
and
v\in T_{\gamma(t_{0})}\Sigma
, ODE theory grants the existence and uniqueness of a
parallel transport
of
v
along
\gamma
, i.e.
V_{t}\in\Gamma\left(\gamma^{*}T\Sigma\right)
with

D_{t}V_{t}\equiv 0,\quad V_{t_{0}}=v.

For a given oriented orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
in
T_{\gamma(t_{0})}\Sigma
, the Levi-Civita connection of
\left(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma}\right)
instructs us on how to parallel translate the initial frame along
\gamma
giving us a parallel section
\{e_{1}(t),e_{2}(t)\}
of
P_{SO}\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\circlearrowright_{\mathbb{R}}\right)
. If we view
\{e_{1}(t),e_{2}(t)\}
as a curve in
P_{SO}\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\circlearrowleft\right)
, then this curve defines the
horizontal lift of
\gamma
through the frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
and we will denote this as
\gamma^{H}_{\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}:[a,b]\rightarrow P_{SO}\left(T\Sigma,g_{\Sigma},\circlearrowleft\right)
.

As above, let
R_{\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}:[a,b]\rightarrow SO(3)
be be the unique smooth map which satisfies

R_{\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}(t)[i\hskip 2.84544ptj\hskip 2.84544ptk]=[e_{1}(t)\hskip 2.84544pte_{2}(t)\hskip 2.84544pt\nu_{\gamma(t)}]

Then by the
path lifting property
of the double covering
\mathbb{S}^{3}\rightarrow SO(3)
, we have two possible smooth choices of path lifting
\pm g_{t}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{3}
such that for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

R_{\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}(t)(v)=\pm g_{t}\cdot v\cdot\left(\pm g_{t}\right)^{-1}.

Making a choice of
\pm
, gives a
parallel spin coordinate system along
\gamma
,
\Phi_{g(t)}:[a,b]\rightarrow P_{Spin}\left(\underline{\mathbb{H}},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)
. If we are given a fixed spinor
q\in\underline{\mathbb{H}}_{\gamma(t_{0})}
, then there is a unique
q_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
such that

\Phi_{g(t_{0})}\left(q_{0}\right)=q.

The
parallel transport of the spinor
v
along
\gamma
is then defined to be

q(t):=\Phi_{g(t)}\left(q_{0}\right).

If we view the parallel propagation of spin coordinate systems as a method for lifting paths from
\Sigma
to
P_{Spin}\left(\underline{\mathbb{H}},\text{Re}g_{\mathbb{H}}\right)
, then this defines the
spin Levi-Civita connection
on spinor fields.

Example
.

Consider the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\bigl\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\big|\quad x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1\bigr\}
. We consider the geodesic
\gamma(t)=\left(\sin(2\pi t),0,\cos(2\pi t)\right)
with initial framing
\{e_{1}(0),e_{2}(0)\}=\{i,j\}
. Then for
\gamma^{H}_{\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}(t)=\{e_{1}(t),e_{2}(t)\}
we have

R_{\{e_{1},e_{2}\}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2\pi t)&0&\sin(2\pi t)\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin(2\pi t)&0&\cos(2\pi t)\end{bmatrix}

and we can choose as our lift
g:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{3}
as

g(t)=\exp\left(\pi jt\right).

In this case, if we are given an initial spinor
q\in\underline{\mathbb{H}}_{\gamma(0)}
and
q=g_{0}\cdot q_{0}
, then the parallel propagation of
q
along
\gamma
will be

q(t)=\exp(\pi jt)\cdot q_{0}.

One rather interesting consequence of this, is that if we treat a tangent vector, such as
i
at
\gamma(0)
as a spinor, then the spin parallel transport will be

i(t)=\exp(\pi jt)\cdot i=\cos(\pi t)i-\sin(\pi t)k

which does
not
remain tangent to the surface.∎

The above calculation demonstrates that on a general Riemannian
2
-fold
\left(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma}\right)
, if we take the spinor bundle to be
\mathscr{S}_{\Sigma}=\wedge T\Sigma\simeq\underline{\mathbb{H}}
, then a
constant spinor

q:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
,
q(x)=q
for
x\in\Sigma
, is not typically going to be
parallel with respect to the spin Levi-Civita connection
. Even more drastic, the volume form
\nu
, although parallel with respect to the Levi-Civita connection, will in general not be parallel with respect to the spin Levi-Civita connection.

If the surface
\Sigma
has non-trivial
H^{1}\left(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)
, then the above spinor module will not be the only possible choice. Although
\Sigma
may not be compact, we may still use

H^{1}\left(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)\simeq\left(\text{Vect}^{1}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\Sigma\right),\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\right).

The latter is parameterized by the set of homotopy classes of maps
[\Sigma,\mathbb{R}P^{\infty}]
, which by Sard’s theorem may be reduced to the set of homotopy classes of maps
[\Sigma,\mathbb{R}P^{3}]\simeq[\Sigma,SO(3)]
. Given a map
R:\Sigma\rightarrow SO(3)
, we may define a new action of
C\ell(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma})
on
\underline{\mathbb{H}}
by

\displaystyle C\ell\left(\Sigma,g_{\Sigma}\right)\times\underline{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\underline{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle(v,q)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(Rv\right)\cdot q.

5.3
Products of Spinors

It is well known that the existence of non-trivial parallel spinors give rise to interesting geometric structures such as the existence of Ricci-flat Kähler manifolds, and holnomoy reductions of the tangent bundle to
G_{2}
and
Spin(7)
(see Chapter
4
of
[
5
]
for survey of results in this area). One of the main techniques in this direction is the “squaring” operation on spinors. We discuss this operation now.

For a general rank
r
oriented Euclidean vector bundle
\left(E,g_{E},\circlearrowleft\right)\rightarrow M
equipped with a spinor bundle

C\ell^{\bullet}\left(E,g_{E}\right)\circlearrowright\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},g_{\mathscr{S}}\right)\circlearrowleft\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}_{r}

we have that

C\ell\left(E,g_{E}\right)\simeq\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E}\right),(\text{ $r\neq 3$ mod 4}),\quad C\ell^{0}\left(E,g_{E}\right)\simeq\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E}\right),(\text{ $r=3$ mod 4}).

Note furthermore that in each various dimension, the spinor metric
g_{\mathscr{S}}
can be extended to a fiber Hermitian, or fiber hyper-Hermitian metric when
\mathbb{K}_{r}\simeq\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{K}_{r}\simeq\mathbb{H}
, denoted
g^{\mathbb{K}_{r}}_{\mathscr{S}}
. In this case, if
s\in\Gamma\left(\mathscr{S}_{E}\right)
then we let

\overline{s}:=g^{\mathbb{K}_{r}}_{\mathscr{S}}\left(s,\cdot\right)\in\Gamma\left(\textnormal{Hom}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},\mathbb{K}_{r}\right)\right)

denote the
\mathbb{K}_{r}
-dual of
s
. Observe that
\textnormal{Hom}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},\mathbb{K}_{r}\right)
is naturally a left
\mathbb{K}_{r}
-module. In this case, if we are given two spinor fields
s_{1},s_{2}
, we may take their
tensor product
and obtain

s_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\overline{s}_{2}\in\textnormal{End}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E}\right)

and thus there exists a unique mixed degree differential form
\omega\in\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}E
such that

c(\omega)=s_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\overline{s}_{2}.

If
\left(E,g_{E},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\rightarrow M
comes equipped with a metric compatible connection
\nabla^{E}
, then we let
\nabla^{\mathscr{S}_{E}}
denote the associated spin connection on
\mathscr{S}_{E}
. The bundle
\textnormal{Hom}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},\mathbb{K}^{r}\right)
has a canonical connection induced by
\nabla^{\mathscr{S}_{E}}
, which we denote by
\nabla^{\textnormal{Hom}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},\mathbb{K}_{r}\right)}
. Then we have

\nabla^{\wedge E}\left(s_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\overline{s}_{2}\right)=\nabla^{\mathscr{S}_{E}}s_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\overline{s}_{2}+s_{1}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}^{r}}\nabla^{\textnormal{Hom}^{R}_{\mathbb{K}_{r}}\left(\mathscr{S}_{E},\mathbb{K}_{r}\right)}\overline{s}_{2}.

Suppose now that
(M,g)
is an oriented Riemannian manifold which admits a global family of spinors

In this case, if

given above, along with the fact that the operators

\int^{\mathcal{F}}_{\left(S,\bm{\circlearrowleft}\right)}d^{*}\omega=(-1)^{k}\int^{\mathcal{F}}_{\left(\partial S,\partial\bm{\circlearrowleft}\right)}\omega,\quad\omega\in\Omega^{n-k+1}M.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(M,g,\bm{\circlearrowleft})

6
An Alternative Set of Representations

At this point, one may inquire on whether or not one should consider thinking of spinors geometrically as quaternic multivectors in dimensions
=0
(mod
4
), and some geometric cousin of these objects in other dimensions. The generality of our construction of spinor modules provides a method for probing this question; in particular we can ask if there is a distinct set of spinor modules which has a different
geometric flavor
than the one presented with quaternions we have given above. We give an alternative construction of spinor modules in dimensions
1,2,3,\text{ and }4
which is based on decomposing the canonical representation of the Clifford algebra
C\ell_{n}
on the exterior algebra
\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

6.1
An Alternative Set of Spinor Modules

For
n=1
, we take as our irreducible module the Clifford algebra acting on the exterior algebra

C\ell_{1}\circlearrowright\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}.

In dimension
2
, we also take as our irreducible module the exterior algebra

C\ell_{2}\circlearrowright\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In dimension
3
, the action
C\ell_{3}\circlearrowright\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{3}
is too large. We can slice this action in half by using the
Hodge star operator
\star
to identify
\wedge^{0}\mathbb{R}^{3}\simeq_{\star}\wedge^{3}\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\wedge^{1}\mathbb{R}^{3}\simeq_{\star}\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In which case this gives us the action

c_{3}(v)\left(\lambda,w\right):=\left(-\langle v,w\rangle,\lambda v+\star v\wedge w\right)

where
v,w\in\wedge^{1}\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\lambda\in\wedge^{0}\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In dimension
4
, we can still identify
\wedge^{0}\mathbb{R}^{4}\simeq_{\star}\wedge^{4}\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
\wedge^{1}\mathbb{R}^{4}\simeq_{\star}\wedge^{3}\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Since the splitting
\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{4}=\wedge^{+}\mathbb{R}^{4}\oplus\wedge^{-}\mathbb{R}^{4}
is invariant under
\star
, we have to make a choice of summand in order to slice
\wedge\mathbb{R}^{4}
in half. Either choice will do, but we will choose the anti-self dual component
\wedge^{-}\mathbb{R}^{4}
and let
P_{\wedge^{-}}
denote the projection onto the anti-self dual component. In this case

c_{4}(v)\left(\lambda,w,\tau\right):=\left(-\langle v,w\rangle,\lambda v+\star v\wedge\tau-\iota_{v}\tau,P_{\wedge^{-}}v\wedge w\right)

where
\lambda\in\wedge^{0}\mathbb{R}^{4}
,
v,w\in\wedge^{1}\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and
\tau\in\wedge^{-}\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

If we are given a general
k
-multivector
\omega\in\wedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Hodge star satisfies

\omega\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\star\omega=|\omega|^{2}\bm{\nu}

where
|\omega|^{2}
is the
k
-volume of
\omega
squared. From this perspective it seems that identifying a form with it’s Hodge star is equivalent to taking a square root of the volume form. For this reason, we will call these representations the
Square Roots of Space
model for spinors.

This method of constructing the irreducible representations of
C\ell_{n}
by slicing
\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{n}
in half using the hodge star operator clearly fails beyond dimension
4
, since

\left(\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)/2>\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}S_{n}

for
n>4
. However, our general recipe tells us that the better way to approach this method of constructing the square roots of space irreducible modules for
C\ell_{n}
should be to first choose an oriented factoring

\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)=\left(V_{1},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\oplus\cdots\left(V_{k},\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)\oplus\left(W,\bm{\circlearrowleft}_{\mathbb{R}}\right)

where
n=4k+r
,
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}V_{j}=4
and
\text{dim}_{\mathbb{R}}W=r
. From here one slices the forms within
\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}V_{j}
and
\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}W
using
\star
, and then takes appropriate tensor products of these square root representations. We will denote these representations by
\sqrt{\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, and note that the choice of square root depends on the choice of square root necessarily depends on the choice of oriented splitting of
\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{\circlearrowleft}\right)
given above.

These representations can be used as follows: Suppose that
\left(E,g_{E},\circlearrowleft\right)\rightarrow M
has rank
r\leq 4
. Then because in the above construction we only used the Hodge star operator
\star
on multivectors, we can form the vector bundle
\sqrt{\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}E}\rightarrow M
which comes with an irreducible action of
C\ell(E,g_{E})

C\ell(E,g_{E})\circlearrowright\sqrt{\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}E}.

However, this
does not imply
that the oriented Euclidean bundle
(E,g_{E},\circlearrowleft)\rightarrow M
is spin. The problem in this case can be traced back to
apriori
absence of the bells and whistles that a true bundle of spinors must have. To give an example, let
L\rightarrow\mathbb{R}P^{2}
denote the universal
\mathbb{R}
-line bundle over
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
. Then if we take
E:=L\oplus L
and equip
E
with an arbitrary metric
g_{E}
, then
E
is indeed orientable since

\wedge^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}E\simeq L\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}L\simeq\underline{\mathbb{R}}.

However, the bundle of intertwiners of
\sqrt{\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}E}
is given by

\textnormal{End}_{Cl}\left(\sqrt{\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}E}\right)\simeq C\ell\left(E,g_{E}\right)\simeq\wedge_{\mathbb{R}}E\simeq\underline{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\oplus E

which cannot be globally trivialized to
\underline{\mathbb{H}}
since this would imply
E
is stably trivial, which it isn’t.

6.2
Yet Another Set of Representations

For completeness, we include another set of spinor representations modeled on the Octonions. One can find these in
[
5
]
,
[
4
]
, and in
[
2
]
. The first three representations we constructed

C\ell_{1}\circlearrowright\mathbb{C},\quad C\ell_{2}\circlearrowright\mathbb{H},\quad C\ell_{3}\circlearrowright\mathbb{H},

remain unchanged. If one loosens the definition of
\mathbb{R}
-algebra and allow for non-associative product structures, then the next division algebra after the quaternions is the
Octonions
, which are defined as

\mathbb{O}:=\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}

with product and conjugation structure defined as

\left(a,b\right)\cdot\left(c,d\right):=\left(ac-\overline{d}b,da+b\overline{c}\right),\quad\overline{\left(a,b\right)}:=\left(\overline{a},-b\right).

The
real axis
and
imaginary axis
of
\mathbb{O}
are the subspaces

\text{Re}\mathbb{O}:=\bigl\{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathbb{O}\big|\text{ $a\in\mathbb{R}$ and $b=0$}\bigr\},\quad\text{Im}\mathbb{O}:=\bigl\{\left(a,b\right)\in\mathbb{O}\big|\text{ $a\in\text{Im}\mathbb{H}$ and $b\in\mathbb{H}$}\bigr\}.

Note that we can form the embedding

\displaystyle c:\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\text{Im}\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(0,q\right)

which induces a map into operators on
\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle R_{c}:\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\left(a,b\right)\mapsto\left(a,b\right)\cdot\left(0,q\right)\right).

Note then
R_{c}
satisfies the Clifford condition

R_{c}\left(q\right)^{2}=-|q|^{2}

and
R_{c}
commutes with the natural right action of
\mathbb{H}
on
\mathbb{O}

\left(a,b\right)\cdot q:=\left(a\cdot q,b\cdot q\right).

Thus the image of
R_{c}
lies within the smaller algebra of quaternionic linear maps on
\mathbb{O}

R_{c}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{O}.

The above maps induces an isomorphism of the Clifford algebra in dimension
4

R_{c}:C\ell_{r}\xrightarrow{\sim}\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{O}.

The careful reader may have noted that the above construction is really just a different way of book keeping the
C\ell_{4}
representation on
\wedge_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{H}
, after all that is what it means to have isomorphic representations! The interesting thing is that the above trick of embedding
\mathbb{R}^{4}
into the imaginary part of
\mathbb{O}
extends through to
\mathbb{R}^{7}

c_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\text{Im}\mathbb{O},\quad 4\leq k\leq 7

and gives an irreducible spinor module

C\ell_{k}\circlearrowright\mathbb{O},\quad 4\leq k\leq 7.

One can see that if we want to extend this to
\mathbb{R}^{8}
, we would need to embed into the imaginary part of some
\mathbb{R}
-division algebra. One could perhaps turn to the
Sedonions
, but at this point the non-associativity, as well as not possessing the property of being alternative encourages a different take. We can double the octonions
\mathbb{O}\oplus\mathbb{O}
and define the map

\displaystyle c_{8}:\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{O}\oplus\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\left(u,v\right)\mapsto\left(-\overline{x}v,xu\right)\right).

Although the Octonions are not associative, they are alternative which implies that the above map satisfies

c_{8}\left(x\right)^{2}=-|x|^{2}.

Thus we have obtained an irreducible spinor representation

C\ell_{8}\circlearrowright\mathbb{O}\oplus\mathbb{O}.
