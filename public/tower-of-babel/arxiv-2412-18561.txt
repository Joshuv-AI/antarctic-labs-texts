Fano physics behind the N-resonance in graphene
R.O. Kuzian, D.V. Efremov, E.E. Krasovskii

Abstract
Bound states and scattering resonances in the unoccupied continuum of a two-dimensional crystal predicted in [Phys.Rev. B 87 , 041405(R) (2013)] are considered within an exactly solvable model. A close connection of the observed resonances with those arising in the Fano theory is revealed. The resonance occurs when the lateral scattering couples the layer-perpendicular incident electron wave to a strictly bound state. The coupling strength determines the location of the pole in the scattering amplitude in the complex energy plane, which is analytically shown to lead to a characteristic Fano-lineshape of the energy dependence of the electron transmissivity through the crystal. The implications for the timing of the resonance scattering are discussed. The analytical results are illustrated by ab initio calculations for a graphene monolayer.



I
Introduction

The interest in the electronic properties of low-dimensional systems
has been growing in the last decades owing to the plethora of unique
quantum phenomena impossible in the three-dimensional (3D) extended
systems
Giamarchi 2003
;
Phaedon Avouris 2017
;
Mario Rocca 2020
. A striking example are
the bound states and scattering resonances in the unoccupied continuum of
two-dimensional (2D) crystals
Nazarov et al. 2013
. In an infinite 3D crystal
bound states cannot exist at any energy, whereas in a stand-alone 2D layer
the electrons below the vacuum level
E_{\rm vac}
are bound to the layer and
those above
E_{\rm vac}
can escape to infinity. However, as was first shown in
Ref.
Nazarov et al. 2013
in atomically thin layers solutions of the Schrödinger
equation may exist above the vacuum level that are bound to the layer, thereafter
referred to as N-states. Such solutions may occur both at complex and at real
energies, the former giving rise to scattering resonances and the latter being
true stationary states—a rare example of a bound state in continuum (BIC)
Hsu et al. 2016
.
In contrast to von Neumann and Wigner BIC

von Neumann and Wigner 1993
;
Stillinger and Herrick 1975
;
Herrick 1976
;
Stillinger 1976
in a 1D continuum and to those
arising from the interaction of Feshbach’s resonances in the scattering on a spherically
symmetric potential
Fonda and Newton 1960
;
Friedrich and Wintgen 1985a
;
Friedrich and Wintgen 1985b
, the N-states are extended
along the crystal plane and bound in the perpendicular direction. In
Ref.
Nazarov et al. 2013
, scattering resonances related to complex-energy N-states were
predicted to exist in graphene, where they were soon observed
experimentally
Pisarra et al. 2014
;
Wicki et al. 2016
;
Krivenkov et al. 2017
;
Matta et al. 2022
;
de Jong et al. 2018
;
Da et al. 2020
;
Neu et al. 2021
;
Ewert et al. 2021
;
Hong et al. 2023
;
Corso et al. 2023
and
further studied
theoretically
Pisarra et al. 2014
;
Kogan et al. 2014
;
Kogan and Silkin 2017
;
Ueda et al. 2018
;
Grozdanov and Solov’ev 2022
;
Grozdanov and Solov’ev 2024
.

Conventional band structure or low energy electron diffraction (LEED) calculations are
performed for real energies, so the parameters of the resonance cannot be directly inferred.
This calls for the development of an analytical model that would provide a functional relation
between the complex energy of the resonance and the observables. In the original theory, the
resonances appeared as a result of sophisticated analytical computations for a specific model or
as an
ab initio
numerical outcome, and although a Fano-like lineshape of the electron transmission
spectrum was observed in Ref.
Nazarov et al. 2013
both in the analytical model and in graphene,
no explanation for this shape has been suggested and its relation to the properties of the
N-states has not been considered. Here, we fill this gap by considering a simple model
that elucidates a close connection of the N-resonance with that in the Fano model. We
analytically
show that the transmission amplitude near the resonances has a Fano character
Fano 1961

t(E)=t_{0}\frac{E-E_{0}}{E-E_{\textsc{p}}+i\Gamma/2},

(1)

where
E_{\textsc{p}}-i\Gamma/2
is the pole location in the complex energy plane, and
E_{0}
is the
point of total reflection, the maximum transmissivity being unity. Real graphene will be shown
to closely follow this formula.

Apart from the transmission probability
T(E)
, an important observable is the
transmission timing. With the recent development of ultrashort laser pulses, the dynamics
of Fano resonances in atoms was studied in real
time
Ott et al. 2014
;
Deshmukh et al. 2018
;
Banerjee et al. 2019
;
Deshmukh et al. 2021
. It is characterized by
the wave-packet group delay introduced by Bohm, Wigner, Eisenbud, and Smith

Bohm 1951
;
Wigner 1955
;
Smith 1960
(Wigner time delay), which can be obtained as the energy
derivative of the scattering phase
\eta=\arg(t)
,

\Delta\tau=d\eta/dE\equiv\dot{\eta}.

(2)

Hartree atomic units
\hbar=e=m_{e}=1
are used throughout the paper, so
\Delta\tau
is
measured in the units of
\hbar/1{\rm Ha}=\hbar^{3}/m_{e}e^{4}=24.19
as. Similar to the
atomic case
Deshmukh et al. 2018
, the resonant
part of the delay is a Lorentzian function with the maximum at
E_{\textsc{p}}
and width
\Gamma
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we introduce a 2D model for
electron scattering on a 1D crystal
(a thin wire) characterized by a sharp attractive potential in the
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
direction
and a weak corrugation along
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
.
We present the
solution of the model in a general form, which is analyzed
in the following sections. Section
III
discusses the system without
corrugation in terms of eigenstates extended and localized in the
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
direction. In
Sec.
IV
, the corrugation is shown to couple the
extended states with the localized ones
of the same parity, which leads to Fano scattering resonances.
The timing of the wave packet scattering
is considered in Sec.
V
. In Sec.
VI
, the formalism
is generalized to two identical
wires. Sec.
VII
summarizes the results. The details of the
calculations are given in
Appendices
A
–
E
.

Infinitely thin wire

To understand the physics of the resonance states

,

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{p}_{x}^{2}+\hat{p}_{y}^{2}}{2}+\hat{V},

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{V}

\displaystyle=[2\Omega\cos(Kx)-\kappa]\delta(y)

potential

corrugation

along
\hat{\mathbf{x}}

Similar to our previous work

(5)

\displaystyle g=Kn,\quad n=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\exp(ik_{y}y)+\delta\Psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\delta\Psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\iint\!\!d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\;G_{0}\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime};E\right)\hat{V}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\Psi(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\int\!\!dx^{\prime}G_{0}\left(x-x^{\prime},y;E\right)\left[2\Omega\cos(Kx^{\prime})-\kappa\right]\Psi(x^{\prime},0),

(8)

After the integration

\Psi(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{g}\phi_{g}(y)\exp(igx)=\exp(ik_{y}y)\,+\sum_{g}\exp(igx)G_{\rm 1D}\left(y,E-g^{2}/2\right)\left[\Omega\left(\phi_{g-K}+\phi_{g+K}\right)-\kappa\phi_{g}\right],

(9)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\exp(-k_{0}|y|)}{k_{0}},&E<0,\\[10.0pt]

(10)

\displaystyle k_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{2\left|E\right|}.

(11)

It follows from Eq. (
10

scattering function
\Psi(\mathbf{r})
of Eq. (
9
) contains only propagating waves
with the positive energetic argument

. Thus, in the asymptotic region, the sum
over

contains only finite number of terms with
g^{2}<2E=k_{y}^{2}
. In particular, for

k_{y}<K
, i.e., for

,

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\exp(ik_{y}y)\,

\displaystyle+G_{\rm 1D}(y,E)\left[\Omega\left(\phi_{-K}+\phi_{K}\right)-\kappa\phi_{0}\right].

(12)

Above

, secondary beams emerge, with
g>0
.
In other words, the

9
) become propagating waves.
We equate the coefficients

9
) for

:

\displaystyle\phi_{g}(1+\kappa F_{g})

\displaystyle=\delta_{g,0}+\Omega F_{g}\left(\phi_{g-K}+\phi_{g+K}\right),

(13)

\displaystyle F_{g}

\displaystyle\equiv\zeta_{g}^{-1}\equiv G_{\rm 1D}\left(0,E-g^{2}/2\right).

(14)

The solution of the infinite three-term recurrence relation (
13
)
has the form of a continued fraction

;
Viswanath and M uller 1994
:

\displaystyle\phi_{K}

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\Omega\phi_{0}}{\zeta_{K}+\kappa-\Omega^{2}\theta_{2}},

(15)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}

\displaystyle=\cfrac{\zeta_{0}}{\zeta_{0}+\kappa-\cfrac{2\Omega^{2}}{\zeta_{K}+\kappa-\Omega^{2}\theta_{2}}},

(16)

where

\theta_{2}=\cfrac{1}{\zeta_{2K}+\kappa-\cfrac{\Omega^{2}}{\zeta_{3K}+\kappa-\cfrac{\Omega^{2}}{\ddots}}},

(17)

see the derivation in Appendix
B
. Due to the symmetry of the

, so the

9
) satisfy the relation

. They can be calculated

\phi_{n_{0}K}=0
at some

n_{0}

\phi_{nK}
for

.

To obtain the functions

10

14

For propagating

and for

The wave function for the central beam is

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\exp(ik_{y}y)\,+r\exp(ik_{y}|y|)

(20)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\exp(ik_{y}y)+r\exp(-ik_{y}y),&y\rightarrow-\infty,\\
t\exp(ik_{y}y),&y\rightarrow+\infty,\end{cases}

(21)

where

,
t\equiv|t|\exp(i\eta_{\textsc{t}})

the reflection and transition complex amplitudes. From Eq. (
12

The energy dependence of the scattering phase shifts

and
\eta_{\textsc{t}}
determines

and transmitted

2

III
Bound states above vacuum level

Besides the propagating states, which are expressed by
Eq. (
9
), the Schrödinger equation has solutions bound in the
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
direction.
These states can be found from the Lippmann-Schwinger equation (
6
)
without the incident wave
\Psi(\mathbf{r})=\delta\Psi(\mathbf{r})

with
\delta\Psi(\mathbf{r})
given by Eq. (
7
).

Let us first neglect the corrugation, i.e., set

\Omega=0
in Eq. (
4
). In this case the recurrence relation
(
13
) reduces to
\phi_{g}(1+\kappa F_{g})=0
and has nontrivial solutions

\phi_{\textsc{b}}\neq 0
for energies
E_{\textsc{b}}(g)=(g^{2}-\kappa^{2})/2
at which
1+\kappa F_{g}=0

\phi_{\textsc{b}}(y)=-\kappa G_{\rm 1D}\left(y,E_{\textsc{b}}(g)-\frac{g^{2}}{2}\right)\phi_{\textsc{b}}=\sqrt{\kappa}e^{-\kappa\left|y\right|},

(23)

g^{2}>\kappa^{2}

\Omega=0

\hat{H}_{y}=\frac{\hat{p}_{y}^{2}}{2}-\kappa\delta(y).

(24)

The eigenstates include one bound state
\phi_{\textsc{b}}(y)

(25)

For a particular case of

E_{\textsc{b}}(g)=(g^{2}-\kappa^{2})/2

Lippmann-Schwinger equation. For a general

E_{\textsc{b}}(k_{x})=(k_{x}^{2}-\kappa^{2})/2

Apart from the bound state

. The

The functions

\varphi^{+}

\hat{p}_{x}^{2}/2+\hat{H}_{y}

energies
E(\mathbf{k})=k^{2}/2
,

where

IV
Coupling by Umklapp processes

The corrugation

The Wigner time delay
\Delta\tau
is defined as the difference in
the time of arrival of a free particle and a scattered one in a region
far from the scatterer. For a spectrally narrow wave packet the delay
equals the energy derivative of the scattering phase
Bohm 1951
;
Wigner 1955
;
Smith 1960
,
see Eq. (
2
). In our model, the transmission amplitude is the product
of the amplitude
t_{0}
in the absence of the corrugation and the resonant

and (

is the sum

\displaystyle\eta_{\textsc{t}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\textsc{1D}}+\eta_{r},

\displaystyle\tan\eta_{r}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\Omega^{2}k}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})\left[\zeta_{K}+\kappa-\Omega^{2}\theta_{2}\right]-2\Omega^{2}\kappa}

(41)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\Gamma}{2(E-E_{\textsc{p}})}.

(42)

The approximate equality (

and is valid in the vicinity of the resonance. Hence, the delay is

, and the resonant part

E_{\textsc{p}}
:

\dot{\eta_{r}}\approx\frac{\Gamma}{2\left[(E-E_{\textsc{p}})^{2}+(\Gamma/2)^{2}\right]}.

(43)

The exact general expression for

VII
Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the N-resonances in electron scattering on
atomically thin layers
occur due to the same mechanism as classical the Fano resonance, namely the
coupling between the bound and extended states. It is thus not surprising that the
transmissivity curve has the Fano shape. We have presented the
mathematical proof and derived analytical expressions for the Fano parameters
for the specific case of corrugated wires, which establish a functional relation
between the complex energy of the resonance and the observed transmissivity. The
ab initio
calculations for graphene
Nazarov et al. 2013
have turned out to be fully consistent with this
theory: We explained and quantitatively reproduced the results of Ref.
Nazarov et al. 2013
,
in other words, we have traced the observed scattering resonance to the existence of a
strictly bound stationary state in an uncorrugated reference layer.

Furthermore, we have shown that the scattering phase exhibits dramatic variation with energy
near the resonance, which results in a large Wigner time delay
\Delta\tau
. The
ab initio
calculation of
\Delta\tau(E)
for graphene has been found to closely follow the analytical
theory and agree with the resonance shape inferred from the scattering spectral function.

The theory has been generalized to several wires (layers in the 3D case) arranged in a
superstructure, whereby the bound states of the individual wires interact and the N-resonance
splits. In this case the scattering amplitude has the Fano shape near each resonance, and the
line-shape parameters depend in a rather complex way on the distance between the individual
scatterers.

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V}_{F},

(61)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\left|\varphi\right\rangle E_{\varphi}\left\langle\varphi\right|+\int\limits_{E_{\varphi}-D}^{E_{\varphi}+D}\left|\psi_{E}\right\rangle E\left\langle\psi_{E}\right|dE,

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{F}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{E_{\varphi}-D}^{E_{\varphi}+D}\left(\left|\varphi\right\rangle V_{E}^{*}\left\langle\psi_{E}\right|+\left|\psi_{E}\right\rangle V_{E}\left\langle\varphi\right|\right)dE,

is
the eigenfunction of its discrete energy level

The perturbation
\hat{V}
couples these states. The eigenstates

of
\hat{H}_{F}

Substituting

\hat{G}_{0}(\omega)\hat{V}=\int\limits_{E_{\varphi}-D}^{E_{\varphi}+D}\left(\frac{\left|\varphi\right\rangle V_{E}^{*}\left\langle\psi_{E}\right|}{\omega-E_{\varphi}}+\frac{\left|\psi_{E}\right\rangle V_{E}\left\langle\varphi\right|}{\omega-E}\right)dE

64

\displaystyle\left|\varPhi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\psi_{E}\right\rangle+\int\limits_{E_{\varphi}-D}^{E_{\varphi}+D}\left(\frac{\left|\varphi\right\rangle V_{E^{\prime}}^{*}\left\langle\psi_{E^{\prime}}|\varPhi\right\rangle}{E+i0-E_{\varphi}}\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\left|\psi_{E^{\prime}}\right\rangle V_{E^{\prime}}\left\langle\varphi|\varPhi\right\rangle}{E+i0-E^{\prime}}\right)dE^{\prime}.

(65)

(66)

and the multiplication by

\left\langle\psi_{E^{\prime\prime}}|\varPhi\right\rangle=\delta\left(E-E^{\prime\prime}\right)+\frac{V_{E^{\prime\prime}}\left\langle\varphi|\varPhi\right\rangle}{E+i0-E^{\prime\prime}}.

(67)

Upon substituting Eq. (
67

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi|\varPhi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{E}^{*}}{E+i0-E_{\varphi}-F(E)},

(68)

\displaystyle F(E)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\limits_{E_{\varphi}-D}^{E_{\varphi}+D}\frac{\left|V_{E^{\prime}}\right|^{2}}{E+i0-E^{\prime}}dE^{\prime}.

This gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{E^{\prime\prime}}|\varPhi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta\left(E-E^{\prime\prime}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{V_{E^{\prime\prime}}}{E+i0-E^{\prime\prime}}\frac{V_{E}^{*}}{E+i0-E_{\varphi}-F(E)}.

(69)

Finally, we substitute Eqs. (

69

(70)

It is easy to verify that
\hat{H}_{F}\left|\varPhi\right\rangle=E\left|\varPhi\right\rangle
.

Let us consider the
r\rightarrow\infty
asymptotic of
\left\langle r|\varPhi\right\rangle
.
The eigenfunction of the discrete energy level is localized, and
\left\langle r|\varphi\right\rangle\rightarrow 0
.
For the eigenstates that belong to the continuous spectrum, one have

\left\langle r|\psi_{E^{\prime}}\right\rangle\propto\sin[k(E^{\prime})r+\delta]
,
where
k(E^{\prime})=\sqrt{2E^{\prime}}
,
\delta
is a phase shift
Fano 1961
;
Landau and Lifshitz 1981

It is reasonable to assume that the matrix element between the localized
and extended functions
V_{E^{\prime}}
decreases rapidly as a function
of
|E^{\prime}-E_{\varphi}|
. Then, passing to the integration over
k
,
we may extend the limits of integration to
\pm\infty
and apply
the residue theorem for the calculation of the integral
Fano 1961

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{V(k^{2}/2)\sin[k(E^{\prime})+\delta]}{E\pm i0-k^{2}/2}kdk=-\pi V_{E}\cos[k(E)r+\delta].

Then

\displaystyle\left\langle r|\varPhi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\propto\sin[k(E)r+\delta]-\frac{\pi|V_{E}|^{2}\cos[k(E)r+\delta]}{E+i0-E_{\varphi}-F(E)}

\displaystyle\propto\sin[k(E)r+\delta+\Delta_{r}],

where the resonance part of the phase shift is (cf. Eq. (15) of Ref.

Fano 1961
)

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}

\displaystyle=-\arctan\frac{\pi|V_{E}|^{2}}{E-E_{\varphi}-F(E)}-\arctan\frac{\Gamma}{2(E-E_{\textsc{p}})},

where
\Gamma\equiv 2\pi|V_{E}|^{2}
and
E_{\textsc{p}}\equiv E_{\varphi}+F(E)
.
The sharp variation of the phase shift as
E
passes through the
resonance energy
E_{\textsc{p}}
leads to a large Wigner time delay in a
wave packet scattering, see Eq. (
43

Appendix C
Analysis of Fano resonance curve

Here we show how to infer the real and imaginary parts of the pole,
E_{\textsc{p}}

and
\Gamma/2
, from the energies of the minimum (zero) of the experimental
curve
E_{0}
, its maximum
E_{\rm max}
, and the point
E_{\rm mid}
, where
the transmissivity is half of its maximum.
Near a Fano resonance, the transmission amplitude has the form
given by Eq. (
1
). The transmissivity reads

\displaystyle T(E)=|t|^{2}=T_{0}\frac{(E-E_{0})^{2}}{(E-E_{\textsc{p}})^{2}+(\Gamma/2)^{2}}.

(80)

From Eq. (
1
) we see that the amplitude has a pole at

z=E_{\textsc{p}}-i\Gamma/2
.
Strictly speaking, the pole occurs in the analitic continuation of the
amplitude to the lower complex-energy half-plane of the unphysical sheet
of the Riemann surface (through the branch cut on the real axis for E ¿ 0).

If one obtains the curve
T(E)
from a (numerical) experiment, the
parameters
E_{\textsc{p}}
and
\Gamma
are not known. Below, we show how
they may be obtained from three characteristic features of the curve.
Namely, the minimum (zero) of the experimental curve
E_{0}
, its
maximum,
E_{\rm max}
and the point
E_{\rm mid}
, where
T(E_{\rm mid})=T(E_{\rm max})/2

The expression (
80
) is usually written as a function
of the reduced variables
q_{\textsc{F}}
(
39
) and
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle T(\epsilon,q_{\textsc{F}})

\displaystyle=T_{0}\frac{(\epsilon+q_{\textsc{F}})^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}+1},

(81)

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\equiv(E-E_{\textsc{p}})/(\Gamma/2),

(82)

Figure
7
shows the reduced transmission probability
T/T_{0}
for
q_{\textsc{F}}=1
.
The curve is described by the three
characteristic points: the minimum
T(\epsilon_{0},q_{\textsc{F}})=0
at
\epsilon_{0}=-q_{\textsc{F}}
,
the maximum
T(\epsilon_{\rm max},q_{\textsc{F}})=T_{0}(q_{\textsc{F}}^{2}+1)
at
\epsilon_{\rm max}=1/q_{\textsc{F}}
, and the midpoint

T(\epsilon_{\rm mid},q_{\textsc{F}})=T(\epsilon_{\rm max},q_{\textsc{F}})/2

at
\epsilon_{\rm mid}=(1-q_{\textsc{F}})/(1+q_{\textsc{F}})
. Using the definitions (
82
) and (
39
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{m}

\displaystyle=E_{\rm max}-E_{0}=\frac{\Gamma(q_{\textsc{F}}^{2}+1)}{2q_{\textsc{F}}},

(83)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm mid}-\epsilon_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{\textsc{F}}^{2}+1}{q_{\textsc{F}}+1},

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=E_{\rm mid}-E_{0}=\frac{\Gamma(q_{\textsc{F}}^{2}+1)}{2(q_{\textsc{F}}+1)}=\frac{q_{\textsc{F}}\Delta_{m}}{q_{\textsc{F}}+1},

T(E_{\rm mid})=T(E_{\rm max})/2

E_{0}
and maximum at

we have used the relation between

\Gamma

83

Thus, we may find the parameter

(84)

which allows us to obtain the imaginary part of the pole position from Eq. (

\Gamma=\frac{2q_{\textsc{F}}\Delta_{m}}{q_{\textsc{F}}^{2}+1},

(85)

and the real part
