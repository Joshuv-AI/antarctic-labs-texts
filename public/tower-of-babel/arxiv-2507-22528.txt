Supersymmetric Schur polynomials have saturated Newton polytopes
Dang Tuan Hiep, Khai-Hoan Nguyen-Dang

Abstract
. We prove that every supersymmetric Schur polynomial has a saturated Newton polytope (SNP). Our approach begins with a tableau-theoretic description of the support, which we encode as a polyhedron with a totally unimodular constraint matrix. The integrality of this polyhedron follows from the Hoffman–Kruskal criterion, thereby establishing the SNP property. To our knowledge, this is the first application of total unimodularity to the SNP problem.

1.
Introduction

For a multivariate polynomial
f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}c_{\alpha}\,{\mathbf{z}}^{\alpha}
its
Newton polytope
is the convex hull
\Newton(f):=\Conv\{\alpha\mid c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.
We say that
f
has a
saturated Newton polytope
(SNP for short) if every lattice point of
\Newton(f)
already occurs in the support, i.e.,
\Newton(f)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\Supp(f)
, where
\Supp(f):=\{\alpha\mid c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}.
Equivalently, the Newton polytope can be seen as the
integer hull
of its support. In recent years, the saturation property of Newton polytopes has emerged as a powerful tool, underpinning advances in commutative algebra (e.g.
[
9
,
7
,
17
]
), algebraic geometry (e.g.
[
13
,
11
]
), representation theory (e.g.
[
14
,
24
,
4
,
3
]
), and manifold theory (e.g.
[
8
]
). Moreover, SNP plays a pivotal role in establishing a Lorentzian polynomial
[
6
]
, thereby unlocking a host of applications across diverse areas (e.g.
[
20
,
23
,
1
,
22
]
).

Rado’s permutahedron theorem (1952) (e.g.
[
30
]
) shows that every Schur polynomial
s_{\lambda}
has SNP because
\Newton(s_{\lambda})
equals the permutahedron
P_{\lambda}
, the convex hull of the
S_{d}
-orbit of
\lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The dominance order arguments force all interior weights to appear (see
[
27
]
). The authors in
[
27
]
also compiled a systematic list of families known (or conjectured) to have SNP (e.g., skew Schur, Stanley, Hall–Littlewood, certain resultants, discriminants,
\dots
) and crucially framed the question in a polyhedral language powerful enough to accommodate Schubert‐type bases. Since the work, a sequence of breakthroughs has confirmed SNP for most outstanding cases:
symmetric Grothendieck polynomials

[
12
]
,
key polynomials and Schubert polynomials

[
14
]
,
Grothendieck and dual characters

[
20
]
,
double Schubert polynomials

[
10
]
,
good class of symmetric polynomials

[
28
]
,
chromatic symmetric functions
[
23
]
,
Demazure characters

[
4
]
, …

Classical symmetric function theory is built on one alphabet of one set of variables and governs the characters of the general linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
. When the representation theory is upgraded from
algebras
to
super
–algebras, the natural ring is the polynomial algebra in
two disjoint sets of variables.

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\qquad y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell}),

and the correct symmetry notion is
supersymmetry
: simultaneous invariance under permutations of the
x
’s
and
of the
y
’s together with the
cancellation property

f(x_{k}=t,\;y_{\ell}=-t)=f
.

For example, supersymmetric Schur functions
S_{\lambda}(x,y)
which can be seen as characters of
\mathfrak{gl}(k\,|\,\ell)
introduced by Berele–Regev
[
2
]
, sit at the crossroads of Lie super-algebras and algebraic combinatorics. Yet,
nothing
was known about their Newton polytopes or support geometry. In this paper, we show that they also admit saturated Newton polytopes (see theorem
8
). We further conjecture that every supersymmetric Schur function is Lorentzian
[
6
]
.

1.1.
Beyond Rado’s technique

One can find an expansion of
S_{\lambda}(x,y)
in terms of classical Schur polynomials, that is

(1)

S_{\lambda}(x,y)=\sum_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}s_{\mu}(x)s_{(\lambda/\mu)^{\prime}}(y)

where
\mu:=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots)\subseteq\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
if
\sum_{1}^{n}\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{1}^{n}\lambda_{i}
for all
n\geq 1
. For any partition
\mu=(\mu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{k}\geq 0)
define its

permutahedron

\Pi(\mu)=\Bigl\{\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\,k}\;\Big|\;|\alpha|=|\mu|,\;\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{i}\leq\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{i}\;(1\leq i<k)\Bigr\},

By Rado’s theorem (see
[
27
]
)

(2)

\displaystyle\Newton\bigl(s_{\mu}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\bigr)=\Pi(\mu),\qquad\Pi(\mu)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k}=\Supp\bigl(s_{\mu}\bigr)

so every ordinary Schur polynomial has SNP. From that, it is not hard to prove that each summand in
1
is SNP. We set

(3)

P_{\mu}:=\Pi(\mu)\times\Pi\bigl((\lambda/\mu)^{\prime}\bigr)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell}.

Then we have

(4)

\Supp\bigl(s_{\mu}(x)s_{(\lambda/\mu)^{\prime}}(y)\bigr)=P_{\mu}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k+\ell}.

By (
1
) and the non-negativity of all coefficients of the supersymmetric Schur polynomial, we have

(5)

\Supp(S_{\lambda})=\bigcup_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}\Supp\bigl(s_{\mu}(x)s_{(\lambda/\mu)^{\prime}}(y)\bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:supp_prod}}}{{=}}\bigcup_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}(P_{\mu}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k+\ell}).

Consequently, the Newton polytope is

(6)

\Newton(S_{\lambda})=\Conv\!\left(\bigcup_{\mu\subseteq\lambda}P_{\mu}\right).

Unlike the classical situation, the product-polytopes
P_{\mu}=\Pi(\mu)\times\Pi\!\bigl((\lambda/\mu)^{\prime}\bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell}
are not ordered by inclusion when we vary
\mu\subseteq\lambda
. Hence a direct analogue of Rado’s theorem (
\mu\subseteq\lambda
iff
P_{\mu}\subseteq P_{\lambda}
”) can fail.

1.2.
Our method

Our strategy is to recast the tableau-theoretic definition of a supersymmetric polynomial as a system of linear inequalities
B_{\lambda}
whose integer solutions are exactly the exponent vectors of the monomials that appear. Using the mixed Berele–Regev insertion, we show that a content

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
appears in a
(k,\ell)
-semistandard tableau of shape
\lambda
if and only if it satisfies the hook inequalities together with the size constraint. This yields the exact, purely linear description:

\Supp(S_{\lambda})=B_{\lambda},

as proved in Theorem
2
. In this way, the combinatorics of tableaux is
linearized
into a system of initial-segment inequalities.

We encode those inequalities as a polyhedron:

H=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell}\mid\widetilde{A}u\leq\widetilde{b}\},\qquad H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{k+\ell}=B_{\lambda},

as described in Section
3.2
. After a row sign normalization (Proposition
2
) the constraint matrix becomes an interval (consecutive–ones) matrix, hence totally unimodular (TU)
(Theorem
6
). By the Hoffman–Kruskal criterion (Theorem
5
) the polyhedron
H
is integral (Corollary
7
), thus completing the proof of the SNP property (Theorem
8
). As a consequence, interpreting
S_{\lambda}
in the Berele–Regev framework, the exponent vectors
(a,b)
are precisely the weights that occur (with multiplicity given by tableau counts); hence the weight set equals the integer points of the hook polytope
H
, with no gaps.

Although a related framework appeared in
[
29
]
, to the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first application of total unimodularity to the SNP problem and the first integrality theorem that simultaneously addresses two intertwined alphabets. Our approach integrates the combinatorial structure and representation‑theoretic properties of supersymmetric Schur polynomials through techniques from integer programming. We believe that this same framework can be extended given further analysis of the underlying combinatorial models to other supersymmetric polynomials, such as the super–Stanley symmetric functions of Fomin–Kirillov
[
15
]
and the supersymmetric Macdonald polynomials
[
5
]
.

Moreover, the Newton polytope of
S_{\lambda}
exhibits a richly structured geometry, most notably the
integer decomposition property
(see
[
31
]
). In our forthcoming work, we will investigate these geometric and combinatorial aspects through the lens of matroid theory and demonstrate their applications in algebraic geometry and representation theory.

Organization

Our work contains two parts. In Section
2
, we collect preliminaries and prove the
hook–inequality description
of the support of
S_{\lambda}(x,y)
via the mixed Robinson–Schensted correspondence. Section
3
encodes these inequalities as a polyhedron
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{k+\ell}
and shows that its defining matrix is TU, thus completing the proof.

2.
Supersymmetric Schur polynomials

2.1.
Supersymmetric Schur polynomials

We recall Berele–Regev’s setting in
[
2
, Section 2]
. Fix integers
k,\ell\geq 1
and declare the ordered “super–alphabet”

t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{k}<u_{1}<u_{2}<\dots<u_{\ell}.

A partition
\lambda
lies in the
hook

H(k,\ell)\;:=\;\bigl\{\lambda\mid\lambda_{k+1}\leq\ell\bigr\}.

The main ingredient in Berele–Regev’s theory is the notion
(k,\ell)
–semistandard tableau given as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D_{\lambda}
be the Young diagram of
\lambda
. A
(k,\ell)
–semistandard tableau
of shape
\lambda
, abbreviated by
\text{SSYT}_{k,l}(\lambda)
, is a filling of
D_{\lambda}
with the letters
t_{i},u_{j}
such that

•

t
–letters
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are weakly increasing
along rows
and strictly increasing
down columns
;

•

u
–letters
u_{1},\dots,u_{\ell}
are weakly increasing
down columns
and strictly increasing
along rows
.

For a semistandard tableau
T
set

a_{i}(T):=\#\bigl\{t_{i}\text{ in }T\bigr\},\qquad b_{j}(T):=\#\bigl\{u_{j}\text{ in }T\bigr\},

and write the
content

(\mathbf{a}(T),\mathbf{b}(T))\in\mathbb{N}^{k+\ell}
. Clearly
\sum_{i}a_{i}+\sum_{j}b_{j}=|\lambda|
.

Example 2.1
.

A
(3,2)
-semistandard tableau of shape
(3,3,1)

.

The content of the tableau

(\mathbf{a}(T_{1}),\mathbf{b}(T_{1}))=(1,2,1,2,1).

Example 2.2
.

A
(2,2)
-semistandard tableau of shape
(4,3,1)

.

The content of the tableau

(\mathbf{a}(T_{2}),\mathbf{b}(T_{2}))=(2,1,3,2).

Observation
: For every
1\leq r\leq k
, because the
t
–letters increase
strictly

down
every column, all occurrences of
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}
are therefore confined to the first
r
rows, which contain exactly
\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{r}
boxes; this forces
\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}(T)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}
. The column statement is the left–right mirror image: every
u_{1},\dots,u_{s}
lies inside the first
s
columns. Building on these observations, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\lambda\in H(k,\ell)
be a
(k,\ell)
–hook partition with rows
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots)
and columns
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\geq\cdots)
.
For vectors
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}

and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
define

A_{\leq r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\quad(1\leq r\leq k)\quad,\quad B_{\leq s}:=\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\quad(1\leq s\leq\ell).

We say
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})

satisfies the hook inequalities for
\lambda
if

A_{\leq r}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\quad(1\leq r\leq k)\quad,\quad B_{\leq s}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\quad(1\leq s\leq\ell)\quad,\quad|\mathbf{a}|+|\mathbf{b}|=|\lambda|.

These discussions can be summarized as follows.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\lambda\in H(k,\ell)
and
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{N}^{k+\ell}
. A
(k,\ell)
–semistandard tableau
T
of shape
\lambda
with content
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
satisfies the hook inequalities.

We show that the converse of the above lemma is also true, via Berele–Regev’s
(k,\ell)
-mixed Robinson–Schensted correspondence
(
[
2
, 2.5–2.7]
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{k,\ell}=\{t_{1}<\dots<t_{k}<u_{1}<\dots<u_{\ell}\}
be a super-alphabet. The mixed Robinson–Schensted (RS) correspondence is a bijection that maps a word
w
built from
\mathcal{A}_{k,\ell}
to a pair of tableaux
(P(w),Q(w))
of the same shape
\lambda\in H(k,\ell)
.

(1)

The tableau
P(w)
, called the
insertion tableau
, is a
(k,\ell)
-semistandard tableau whose content is determined by the letter multiplicities in
w
.

(2)

The tableau
Q(w)
, called the
recording tableau
, is a standard Young tableau of the same shape
\lambda
, whose entries record the positions at which new cells were added to the shape during the insertion process.

We derive the correspondence between
(k,l)
-semistandard tableaux and pairs satisfying hook inequalities.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\lambda\in H(k,\ell)
and

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{N}^{k+\ell}
.
There exists a
(k,\ell)
–semistandard tableau
T
of shape
\lambda

with content
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
if and only if

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
satisfies the hook inequalities.

Proof.

Assume the hook inequalities. Form

w:=t_{1}^{a_{1}}\,t_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots t_{k}^{a_{k}}\;u_{\ell}^{\,b_{\ell}}\cdots u_{1}^{\,b_{1}},

the “all
t
’s first, all
u
’s last” word used in their mixed RS correspondence. Insert each letter with the mixed RS rules: row insertion for
t
’s, column insertion for
u
’s. This produces a tableau
P(w)
that is automatically
(k,\ell)
–semistandard with content
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
.

Definition of
w
gives
\sum_{i\leq r}\operatorname{shape}(P(w))_{i}=A_{\leq r}

and
\sum_{j\leq s}\operatorname{shape}(P(w))^{\prime}_{j}=B_{\leq s}
. Because the hook inequalities say those partial sums do not exceed the corresponding partial sums of
\lambda
, the shape
\mu:=\operatorname{shape}(P(w))
is dominated by
\lambda
both row-wise and column-wise, and
|\mu|=|\lambda|
. The standard dominance lemma (row and column dominance plus equal size implies equality) now forces
\mu=\lambda
. Thus
P(w)
is a
(k,\ell)
–semistandard tableau of shape
\lambda
and content
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})

∎

Definition 2.3

(Supersymmetric Schur polynomial)
.

For commuting indeterminates

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\quad,\quad y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell}),

and let
\lambda\in H(k,\ell)
. The
supersymmetric Schur polynomial
of shape
\lambda
is

S_{\lambda}(x,y):=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SSYT}_{k,\ell}(\lambda)}x^{\mathbf{a}(T)}\,y^{\mathbf{b}(T)},

where for a tableau
T
and

x^{\mathbf{a}(T)}:=x_{1}^{a_{1}(T)}\!\cdots x_{k}^{a_{k}(T)},\;y^{\mathbf{b}(T)}:=y_{1}^{b_{1}(T)}\!\cdots y_{\ell}^{b_{\ell}(T)}.

In
[
26
]
, Moens and Van der Jeugt gave a determinantal formula for supersymmetric Schur polynomials. The supersymmetric Schur polynomial
S_{\lambda}(x,y)
can be computed by the following formula.

S_{\lambda}(x,y)=\det\left(H_{\lambda_{i}+j-i}(x,y)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)},

where
\ell(\lambda)
is the number of nonzero components of
\lambda
, and the polynomial

H_{r}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{r}h_{i}(x)e_{r-i}(y),

where
h_{i}(x)
is the
i
-th complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial in
k
variables
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
and
e_{r-i}(y)
is the
(r-i)
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in
\ell
variables
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})
. It is easy to see that
H_{r}(x,y)
is a supersymmetric polynomial, so
S_{\lambda}(x,y)
is. Stembridge
[
32
]
showed that the set

\{S_{\lambda}(x,y)\mid\lambda\in H(k,\ell)\}

is a linear basis for the ring of supersymmetric polynomials in
x
and
y
. It was shown that the supersymmetric Schur polynomials are characters of certain representations of the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
(see
[
21
]
). A good overview of the supersymmetric Schur polynomials can be found in the PhD thesis of Moens (see
[
25
]
).

The first main result of our paper is the following.

Theorem 2

(Hook description of the support)
.

One has

\Supp\bigl(S_{\lambda}\bigr)\;=\;\Bigl\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{N}^{k+\ell}\,\Bigm|\,A_{\leq r}\leq\!\sum_{i\leq r}\lambda_{i}\;(1\!\leq\!r\!\leq\!k),\;B_{\leq s}\leq\!\sum_{j\leq s}\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\;(1\!\leq\!s\!\leq\!\ell),\;|\mathbf{a}|+|\mathbf{b}|=|\lambda|\Bigr\}.

Proof.

Expand using Definition
2.3
:

S_{\lambda}(x,y)=\sum_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})}\bigl|\{T\mid\operatorname{content}(T)=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\}\bigr|\;x^{\mathbf{a}}y^{\mathbf{b}}.

A coefficient is non-zero
if and only if some tableau of shape
\lambda
has content
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
,
and by Proposition
1
this happens precisely when

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})
satisfies the row, column, and size inequalities
displayed above.
∎

Put
\ell=0
so that the
y
-alphabet is empty (equivalently, set

y_{i}=0
for every
i
). Then
\operatorname{SSYT}_{k,0}(\lambda)=\operatorname{SSYT}_{k}(\lambda)
and Definition
2.3
reduces to the classical Schur polynomial

s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=S_{\lambda}(x,\varnothing)=\sum_{T\in\operatorname{SSYT}_{k}(\lambda)}x_{1}^{a_{1}(T)}\cdots x_{k}^{a_{k}(T)},

where
k\geq\ell(\lambda)
where
\ell(\lambda)
is the length of
\lambda
. We get a straightforward consequence which resembles the Rado theorem as in
[
27
]
for the ordinary Schur polynomial and thus Schur-P and Schur-Q polynomials.

Corollary 3

(Rado’s theorem)
.

For any partition
\lambda
and any
k\geq\ell(\lambda)
, one has

\Supp\bigl(s_{\lambda}\bigr)\;=\;\Bigl\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\,\Bigm|\,A_{\leq r}\;\leq\;\sum_{i\leq r}\lambda_{i}\;(1\leq r\leq k),\;|\mathbf{a}|=|\lambda|\Bigr\},

where
A_{\leq r}:=\sum_{i\leq r}a_{i}
.

3.
Total unimodularity and SNP

3.1.
Basic definitions

We introduce a linear algebra tool called total unimodularity to prove the integrality of a polyhedron.

Definition 3.1
.

A matrix
M\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{p\times q}
is

totally unimodular
(TU) if every square sub–determinant of
M

is
0,\,+1
or
-1
.

The next statement is an elementary but useful observation.

Proposition 2

(Row–sign invariance)
.

Let
M
be a matrix and let
D
be a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries in
\{-1,1\}
. Then
M
is totally unimodular (TU) if and only if
DM
is totally unimodular.

Proof.

Let
M^{\prime}=DM
. Let
M^{\prime}_{IJ}
be an arbitrary
k\times k
submatrix of
M^{\prime}
with row indices from the set
I
and column indices from the set
J
. Let
M_{IJ}
be the corresponding submatrix of
M
. The operation of pre-multiplying
M
by the diagonal matrix
D
scales each row
i
of
M
by the corresponding diagonal entry
d_{ii}
. Therefore, the submatrix
M^{\prime}_{IJ}
is related to
M_{IJ}
by the equation

M^{\prime}_{IJ}=D_{I}M_{IJ},

where
D_{I}
is the
k\times k
diagonal submatrix of
D
corresponding to the row indices in
I
.

Using the multiplicative property of determinants, we have:

\det(M^{\prime}_{IJ})=\det(D_{I})\det(M_{IJ}).

Since
D_{I}
is a diagonal matrix with entries in
\{-1,1\}
, its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries, which must be either
-1
or
1
. That is,
\det(D_{I})\in\{-1,1\}
.

(
\Longrightarrow
) Assume
M
is totally unimodular. By definition,
\det(M_{IJ})\in\{-1,0,1\}
for any square submatrix
M_{IJ}
. Therefore

\det(M^{\prime}_{IJ})=(\pm 1)\cdot\det(M_{IJ})\in\{-1,0,1\}.

Since this holds for any square submatrix of
DM
, the matrix
DM
is totally unimodular.

(
\Longleftarrow
) Assume
DM
is totally unimodular. Note that
D
is its own inverse, i.e.,
D^{-1}=D
, because
d_{ii}^{2}=(\pm 1)^{2}=1
. We can write
M=D^{-1}(DM)=D(DM)
. This means
M
is obtained by pre-multiplying the totally unimodular matrix
DM
by a diagonal
\pm 1
matrix
D
. By the same logic as the forward implication, if
DM
is TU, then
D(DM)=M
must also be TU.
∎

We can also characterize total unimodularity using the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

A
\{0,1\}
-matrix is an
interval
(or

consecutive–ones
) matrix when, after some permutation of columns, the
1
’s in each row appear in a single contiguous block.

The following is classic.

Theorem 4

(Heller–Tompkins
[
18
]
;
Fulkerson–Gross
[
16
]
)
.

Every interval matrix is totally unimodular.

Definition 3.3
.

A polyhedron
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
integral
if
P=\operatorname{Conv}(P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, that is, all its extreme points are lattice points.

The following result gives an equivalent characterization of integrality and total unimodularity.

Theorem 5

(Fulkerson–Gross
[
16
]
; Hoffman–Kruskal
[
19
]
)
.

For an integer matrix
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{p\times q}
the following are equivalent:

(1)

M
is totally unimodular.

(2)

For every integral
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}
the polyhedron

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\mid Mx\leq b\}
is integral.

3.2.
Polyhedral model of supersymmetric Schur polynomials

Setting

For integers
k,\ell\geq 1
and
\lambda\in H(k,\ell)=\{\lambda\mid\lambda_{k+1}\leq\ell\}
be a
(k,\ell)
-hook partition, we set

d:=k+\ell,\qquad L_{r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i},\qquad C_{s}:=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\qquad(1\leq r\leq k,\;1\leq s\leq\ell).

We proved in the previous section that for a vector

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\;(=\mathbb{N}^{k}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ell})

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\Supp S_{\lambda}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\bigl(A_{\leq r}\leq L_{r}\;(1\leq r\leq k),\;B_{\leq s}\leq C_{s}\;(1\leq s\leq\ell),\;|\mathbf{a}|+|\mathbf{b}|=|\lambda|\bigr),

where
A_{\leq r}:=\sum_{i\leq r}a_{i}
and
B_{\leq s}:=\sum_{j\leq s}b_{j}
. Denote this common set by

B_{\lambda}:=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\mid\text{the three displayed conditions hold}\}.

Our goal is to prove the
saturated Newton polytope
(SNP) property

(7)

\Conv(B_{\lambda})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=B_{\lambda}.

Polyhedral from hook inequalities

Write
e:=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider the

(k+\ell)\times d
matrix

A:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{1\times 1}&0&\dots&0\\
\mathbf{1}_{1\times 2}&0&\dots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
\mathbf{1}_{1\times k}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\mathbf{1}_{1\times 1}&0&\dots\\
0&\mathbf{1}_{1\times 2}&0&\dots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
0&\mathbf{1}_{1\times\ell}&&\end{pmatrix},\quad(\text{$\mathbf{1}_{1\times r}$ = row of $r$ ones}).

The first
k
rows record the inequalities

A_{\leq r}\leq L_{r}
; the last
\ell
rows record
B_{\leq s}\leq C_{s}
.

Lemma 2
.

A
is an interval matrix.

Proof.

Each row of
A
contains a single block of consecutive
1
’s, so
A
is an interval matrix and therefore TU by Theorem
4
.
∎

Define the block matrix and vector

\widetilde{A}:=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
e^{\!\top}\\
-e^{\!\top}\\
-I_{d}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\widetilde{b}:=\begin{pmatrix}(L_{1},\dots,L_{k},C_{1},\dots,C_{\ell})^{\!\top}\\[2.0pt]
|\lambda|\\[2.0pt]
-|\lambda|\\[2.0pt]
0_{d}\end{pmatrix},

and the polyhedron

H:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\widetilde{A}u\leq\widetilde{b}\}.

By construction, the first
k+\ell
rows of
\widetilde{A}
encode the hook inequalities, the next two rows force the “size” equality
|u|=|\lambda|
, the last
d
rows impose component-wise non-negativity.

Proposition 3
.

We have

H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=B_{\lambda}.

Proof.

To see that
H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=B_{\lambda}
is nothing more than unpacking the definition of
H
, write a generic point

u=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then the block–rows of
\widetilde{A}u\leq\widetilde{b}
satisfy the following.

(1)

Hook–inequalities:
For each
1\leq r\leq k
,

\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\;=\;(A_{r,*}\,u)\;\leq\;L_{r},

and for each
1\leq s\leq\ell
,

\sum_{j=1}^{s}b_{j}\;=\;(A_{k+s,*}\,u)\;\leq\;C_{s}.

(2)

Size equality:

e^{\top}u=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}b_{j}\;\leq\;|\lambda|,\quad-\,e^{\top}u\;\leq\;-\,|\lambda|\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}b_{j}=|\lambda|.

(3)

Nonnegativity: The rows of
-I_{d}
force

-\,u\;\leq\;0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_{i}\geq 0,\;b_{j}\geq 0.

Hence

H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=B_{\lambda}.

∎

Thus, proving (
7
) amounts to showing that every

integral
point of
H
is a vertex of

\Conv(B_{\lambda})=H
; equivalently, that the constraint matrix

\widetilde{A}
is totally unimodular. This will be established in the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

The matrix
\widetilde{A}=\begin{bmatrix}A\\
e^{\!\top}\\
-e^{\!\top}\\
-I_{d}\end{bmatrix}\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{(k+\ell+d+2)\times d}

is totally unimodular.

Proof.

Row–sign normalisation.

Let

D:=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(I_{k+\ell},\,1,\,-1,\,-I_{d}\bigr)

and set
\widehat{A}:=D\widetilde{A}
.
Multiplying the rows coming from
-e^{\!\top}
and
-I_{d}
by
-1
turns every non–zero entry of
\widehat{A}
into
+1
. By Proposition
2
,
\widetilde{A}
is TU iff
\widehat{A}
is TU.

\widehat{A}
is interval.

With the column order inherited from
A
,

\widehat{A}
has the consecutive–ones property:the first
k+\ell
rows are those of
A
; the next row (all–ones) is trivially interval; each of the final
d
rows has exactly one
1
. Hence
\widehat{A}
is an interval matrix, TU by
Theorem
4
, and so
\widetilde{A}
is TU.
∎

Corollary 7
.

H
is an integral polyhedron.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{A}
is TU (Theorem
6
) and
\widetilde{b}
is integral, Theorem
5
implies that
H
is an integral polyhedron.
∎

We obtain the main result of our paper.

Theorem 8
.

The supersymmetric Schur polynomial
S_{\lambda}(x,y)
has a saturated Newton polytope.

Proof.

For any supersymmetric Schur polynomial
S_{\lambda}
, we have

\Newton(S_{\lambda})=\Conv\bigl(\Supp S_{\lambda}\bigr)=\Conv(B_{\lambda})=H

and

\Newton(S_{\lambda})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=B_{\lambda}=\Supp S_{\lambda}.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

Because
H
is integral, for every integer vector
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the linear program

\max\{\,c\cdot u\mid\widetilde{A}\,u\leq\widetilde{b}\}

achieves its maximum at an integral vertex
u^{*}\in H\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=B_{\lambda}
(see
[
31
]
). In other words, optimizing over
H
by linear programming always returns the exponent of a genuine monomial in
S_{\lambda}
that maximizes
c\cdot u
. Furthermore, because the data
(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{b})
are totally unimodular, these linear programs admit strongly polynomial‑time solutions via Tardos’ algorithm
[
33
]
.
