Pluripotency, differentiation, and reprogramming: 20 years of induced pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Sarah J Hainer, Stanley E Strawbridge, Sekyu Choi, Ge Guo, Masaki Kinoshita, Chengchen Zhao, Fan Zhou
Published: 2026

Twenty years after the discovery of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), the study of pluripotency, differentiation, and cellular reprogramming remains one of the most dynamic areas of modern biology. Questions surrounding how cell identities are established, maintained, and changed lie at the heart of developmental biology and increasingly underpin advances in regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and cell-based therapies. To mark the twentieth anniversary of the landmark discovery of iPSCs, BMC Biology is launching the Pluripotency, Differentiation, and Reprogramming Collection, which will highlight emerging concepts, technologies, and challenges that are shaping the next phase of the field.

In the mid-twentieth century, mammalian development was primarily considered unidirectional, whereby embryonic stem cells would differentiate into specific and restricted cell states. This was encapsulated by a famous model by Conrad Waddington in 1957, depicting a ball rolling down the slope of a mountain (pluripotency) to valleys below (differentiation). In August 2006, the results of a landmark experiment from Shinya Yamanaka’s lab in Kyoto reported that the delivery of only four transcription factors, Oct3/4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc (OSKM; often referred to as the “Yamanaka factors”), could convert differentiated mouse fibroblasts into cells with pluripotent properties [1].

This reversibility of cell identity was not itself new; decades earlier, John Gurdon had shown that the nucleus of a differentiated tadpole cell, transferred into an enucleated frog egg, could still give rise to a normal tadpole, demonstrating that specialized cells retain the capacity to build an organism [2]. However, Takahashi and Yamanaka’s breakthrough discovery showed for the first time that pluripotency does not exist only transiently in early embryos but can be induced in terminally differentiated mammalian cells by a small set of transcription factors alone, albeit with low efficiency.

The effort to understand why only a small subset of cells respond and how a cell’s identity is dismantled and rebuilt during the process drew reprogramming into the broader study of pluripotency and differentiation. Because reprogramming and differentiation run in opposite directions across the developmental landscape (Fig. 1), insights into the mechanisms that govern entry, maintenance and exit of pluripotency, and differentiation inform our understanding of reprogramming, and vice versa. These fundamental issues of cell identity, which had historically been the concern of a comparatively small community of developmental biologists, became, almost overnight, central questions across many different fields.

Moreover, the generation of human and patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) [3, 4] became accessible even to non-specialist laboratories using the “Yamanaka factors.” iPSCs are now a cornerstone of modern biomedical research, enabling disease modeling, drug discovery, and regenerative medicine [5]. Work that began as developmental biology is now entering the clinic, as pluripotent stem cell-derived cell therapies work their way through regulatory pathways. Early-phase trials range from iPSC-derived retinal pigment epithelium for macular degeneration [6] to dopaminergic neuron grafts for Parkinson’s disease [7].

As the field continues to evolve, new frontiers in stem cell biology are rapidly emerging. Partial reprogramming can reset markers of cellular aging without erasing a cell’s identity [8]. Chemical reprogramming is maturing as an alternative to genetic delivery, guiding cells through defined intermediate states using small molecules alone [9]. Advances in single-cell multi-omics, spatial transcriptomics, and computational modeling of gene regulatory networks now allow us to ask not only whether a cell can change identity, but also how populations of cells coordinate that change across space and time. Recently, a wide range of stem cell-based embryo models (SCBEMs) or tissue/organ models have emerged, recapitulating key aspects of embryonic development, tissue organization, and organ function [10]. These range from organoids, which capture aspects of tissue physiology, to integrated SCBEMs, which model developmental processes. Coupled with single-cell and spatial -omics, advanced imaging, and biochemical and genetic approaches, it is now possible to visualize cellular and molecular dynamics in real time and to dissect how gene regulatory networks, signaling pathways, chromatin organization, and metabolic programs together govern embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and disease.

The twentieth anniversary of iPSCs provides a timely opportunity to reflect on how the study of cell identity has evolved and where it is heading next. The Pluripotency, Differentiation, and Reprogramming Collection aims to mark this milestone by showcasing the emerging questions, approaches, and concepts that will shape the field’s next phase. We welcome work examining the mechanistic control of pluripotency, cell differentiation, and lineage commitment at the molecular level; developmental plasticity and reprogramming both in vitro and in vivo across diverse developmental systems and species; the spatial organization of cell identity decisions during tissue patterning; and the systems-level models needed to integrate these findings.
