Bacteria isolated from dried tobacco to enhance drought tolerance in wheat
Authors: Anna Hoffmann, Weronika Zenelt, Krzysztof Krawczyk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractDrought poses a major threat to global food security, reducing yields of staple crops such as wheat. Sustainable solutions to improve plant resilience are urgently needed. Tobacco is widely cultivated for cigarette production, but its dried leaves can also harbor thermotolerant bacterial strains with potential plant growth-promoting properties. This study aimed to evaluate the biostimulatory potential of endophytic bacteria isolated from dried tobacco leaves in enhancing drought tolerance in winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L.). Five bacterial strains were characterized for key PGP (plant growth-promoting) traits, including auxin production, phosphate solubilization, siderophore production, and salt tolerance. In greenhouse experiments under controlled drought stress (100%, 75%, and 50% field capacity), selected strains were applied to wheat seedlings, and their effects on plant growth, biomass accumulation, and oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide) were assessed. The effects of bacterial inoculation varied by strain and irrigation level, with the most beneficial responses generally observed under moderate drought stress. Certain strains significantly reduced oxidative damage and enhanced shoot biomass compared to noninoculated controls These findings demonstrate the untapped potential of tobacco leaf-derived PGP bacteria as sustainable bioinoculants to improve drought resilience in wheat, contributing to climate-resilient agriculture and reduced reliance on chemical inputs.Key points• Tobacco leaf bacteria improve wheat growth under drought stress• Isolated strains show biostimulant and drought tolerance effects• Bacterial application may support sustainable wheat farming in dry conditionsSupplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13645-5.Keywords: Drought stress, Tobacco, Plant growth-promoting bacteria

Introduction

Tobacco is a major agricultural crop with significant global economic value, primarily used in cigarette production. The curing process, which involves drying harvested tobacco leaves at approximately 70 °C, not only enables the leaves to develop the desirable characteristics such as aroma and color that are essential for the tobacco industry, but also serves to dry the leaves further and prevent fungal decay development during storage. Furthermore, this process helps eliminate most of the harmful bacterial pathogens of tobacco, such as Pseudomonas syringae pv. tobacco, which can thrive in temperatures ranging from 4 to 42 °C (Meng et al., 2017). Dried tobacco leaves are often traded internationally and, as plant material, are routinely monitored for the presence of bacterial pathogens. Despite the drying process at 70 °C, it is still possible to isolate bacteria other than P. syringae. What caught our attention was the remarkable resistance of these bacteria to extreme water scarcity and high temperatures, conditions that prevail during the curing process. Given the extremity of these conditions, we hypothesized that these bacteria might perform well in the milder drought conditions typical in agriculture. These bacteria are endosymbionts of tobacco plants, adapted to survive under harsh conditions, suggesting that their presence in plants may help mitigate drought stress symptoms. Their existence may also contribute to alleviating stress. Certain microorganisms have the ability to enter the apoplast and even vascular tissues of plants, where they maintain metabolic activity and contribute to stress resilience, especially under abiotic stress conditions such as drought (Enebe and Babalola, 2018).

Given the continuous and increasing demand for new biopreparations to mitigate drought stress in agricultural plants (Farooq et al., 2022), we aimed to explore these bacterial strains for their potential to alleviate drought stress and promote plant growth. The application of biofertilizers to support plant adaptation and survival represents an effective strategy to achieve sustainable crop yields while enhancing soil fertility and structure. This approach is particularly beneficial for managing abiotic stresses in plants (Enebe and Babalola, 2018). Interest in the bacteria stems from their impressive thermotolerance when subjected to high-temperature curing of tobacco, suggesting their potential to survive under drought stress. Drought stress is a critical agronomic issue compounded by dwindling arable land and increasing desertification. New approaches are needed to increase agricultural resilience to drought (Farooq et al., 2022).

Drought stress severely affects plant growth and productivity, primarily by inducing oxidative stress through the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA), markers of cellular damage (Ferioun et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021) While elevated ROS levels can damage cellular components, plants have evolved mechanisms to regulate ROS, balancing their harmful and signalling roles. Understanding and enhancing these mechanisms is crucial for improving crop resilience to drought. The reduction of H₂O₂ and MDA content in inoculated plants compared to controls indicates that plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) enhance plant resistance under drought conditions (Ferioun et al., 2023).

Climate change poses a significant threat to global agriculture, particularly wheat farming (Farhad et al., 2023). Changes in rainfall patterns induced by climate change have caused extended droughts in many regions, disrupting the balance of water in lakes, rivers, and other ecosystems. This hydric stress seriously threatens global food security and has a substantial impact on agricultural production. The most likely consequence of climate change is drought, which can significantly affect wheat production, reducing yield and grain quality. Elevated temperatures and water limitation may intensify heat and water stress, triggering morphological, physiological, and biochemical changes in wheat plants (de Souza et al., 2024). Elevated temperature and water limitation enhance heat and water stress, triggering morphological, physiological, and biochemical changes in wheat plants (Farhad et al., 2023). Additionally, shrinking arable land and increasing desertification further expand the issues, narrowing prospects for cultivation. Balancing these challenges is the necessity for the production of drought and heat stress–tolerant wheat varieties (Brunet et al., 2025). New water management practices and drought and heat stress–tolerant wheat varieties are critical (Brunet et al., 2025). Technological advancements and international coordination are necessary for climate change food security (Farhad et al., 2023).

Since wheat is one of the most important crops worldwide, we decided to investigate the potential of bacteria isolated from dried tobacco leaves to alleviate drought stress in this crop. Wheat is a key staple food crop on the international stage, providing as much as 20% of total dietary calories and proteins globally (Shiferaw et al., 2013). However, it is extremely water-sensitive, and thus, its production is a significant constraint. These constraints must be overcome by enhancing drought-resistant varieties of wheat or by employing effective water management principles to provide consistent wheat production irrespective of fluctuating weather conditions (Vassileva et al., 2023).

The aim of this study was to assess the plant growth-promoting (PGP) properties and the biostimulatory potential of bacteria isolated from dried tobacco leaves to enhance drought tolerance and the growth of winter wheat. The study aims to identify and characterize bacteria that can support plants under water stress conditions, which may contribute to improving wheat crop yields in the face of climate change and the increasing frequency of droughts.

Materials and methods

All tested tobacco leaf samples originated from a routinely monitored batch provided by the Plant Disease Clinic of the Institute of Plant Protection–National Research Institute. In total, five leaf samples were tested. Prior to microbiological analysis, the leaves were surface sterilized to reduce potential exogenous contaminants. Surface sterilization was performed by immersing 1 g of dried tobacco leaves in 70% ethanol for 1 min, followed by rinsing in sterile distilled water. A series of tenfold dilutions was prepared, and 100-µL aliquots of each dilution were streaked onto Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). The inoculated TSA plates were incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. After incubation, morphologically distinct colonies were selected and subjected to further purification by successive streaking onto fresh TSA plates until pure cultures were obtained.

Determination of plant growth-promoting properties of the tested bacterial strains

The bacterial strains were analysed for their potential to promote plant growth by evaluating several biochemical and physiological characteristics including tests for: hydrogen cyanide (HCN) production, cellulase activity, auxin synthesis, lipase and protease activity, siderophore production, ammonia generation, motility, phosphate solubilization, and tolerance to saline conditions. Each experiment was conducted twice, with the detailed methodologies outlined below.

Hydrogen cyanide production was measured using a colorimetric technique involving filter paper. A 100-µL portion of bacterial suspension (107 cfu/mL) was inoculated onto TSA supplemented with glycine, and filter papers soaked with sodium carbonate 0.5% (w/v) and picric acid 2% (w/v) were placed within the Petri dishes. Following a 4-day incubation at 28 °C, changes in color on the filter paper were recorded. A visible color change from yellow to orange or reddish brown was considered a positive result (yes), while no color change was recorded as negative (no), following the procedure described by (Ghodsalavi et al., 2013). HCN is known for its antifungal properties, helping to suppress soil-borne fungal pathogens and enhancing overall plant health. Additionally, it serves as a defence mechanism against competing microorganisms, fostering a more favorable environment for plant growth (Ghodsalavi et al., 2013).

To evaluate cellulase activity, bacterial cultures were grown on Tryptic Soy Agar and treated with hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide to precipitate undegraded carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC). The presence of clear halos around colonies indicated cellulase activity, with enzyme efficiency determined by calculating the ratio of the halo zone to colony diameter (Hankin & Anagnostakis, 1977).

Auxin synthesis, a key indicator of plant growth-promoting ability, was analyzed by culturing bacteria in nutrient broth (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with tryptophan, followed by centrifugation. The resulting supernatant was combined with Salkowski reagent. Development of a red color signified auxin production. Absorbance at 535 nm was used to quantify auxin concentration (Ghodsalavi et al., 2013).

Bacterial motility was determined by inoculating cultures into semisolid motility agar and observing their spread after 24 and 48 h of incubation. A diffused growth pattern extending from the inoculation point indicated motility, a trait that enhances rhizosphere colonization, plant interaction, and nutrient acquisition—factors that contribute to overall plant health (Schaad et al., 2001).

Phosphate solubilization was assessed using Pikovskaya’s agar medium, containing an insoluble phosphate source. After 7 days of incubation, clear halo zones surrounding bacterial colonies indicated their ability to solubilize phosphate, making it more accessible to plants and enhancing nutrient uptake (Sharma et al., 2011).

Lipase activity was determined by growing bacterial cultures on a TSA medium containing Tween 20. The formation of a precipitate around the colonies indicated lipase activity, which was scored on a scale from 0 (no activity) to 4 (very high activity) based on the deposit diameter size (Ghodsalavi et al., 2013).

Siderophore production was assessed using a succinate-based medium. After 48 h of incubation, fluorescence under UV light was classified as the presence of pyoverdines, a type of fluorescent siderophore. These compounds play a critical role in iron uptake, which is essential for plant growth and development (Ghodsalavi et al., 2013).

Salt tolerance was analyzed by culturing bacterial strains in a 1% proteose peptone solution with varying concentrations of sodium chloride (0%, 5%, and 10%). After 24 h of incubation, bacterial growth was measured by determining absorbance at 600 nm. The ability to tolerate high salt levels is crucial for bacterial survival in saline environments, allowing them to maintain their plant growth-promoting activities under stress conditions (Rashid et al., 2012).

Ammonia production was examined by incubating bacterial cultures in proteose peptone broth. After 24 h, a Nessler’s reagent was added, and the formation of a yellow coloration indicated ammonia production. The intensity of the yellow color was proportional to the amount of ammonia generated. Ammonia-producing bacteria contribute to soil fertility by converting organic nitrogen compounds into ammonia, a form that plants can readily absorb (Rashid et al., 2012).

Testing the resistance of bacteria to the physiological drought using PEG6000

Obtained bacterial strains were cultured in nutrient broth to prepare a standardized inoculum. A loopful of bacteria was transferred into 100 mL of nutrient broth and incubated at 28 °C with continuous shaking at 150 rpm for 24 h. After incubation, the cultures were transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of sterile normal saline (0.85% NaCl solution). The optical density (OD) of the standard inoculum was measured at 550 nm using a spectrophotometer and adjusted to OD600 = 0.5 to ensure a standardized bacterial cell concentration. Once the desired OD600 was reached, 10% (v/v) of this inoculum was transferred to flasks containing 100 mL of nutrient broth supplemented with either 5% or 10% PEG6000 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to simulate osmotic stress conditions. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 7 days. Bacterial viability in the PEG6000-containing medium was assessed after 1, 2, 6, and 7 days to monitor changes in bacterial survival over time (Gontia-Mishra et al., 2016; Rafedzi et al., 2024).

The use of PEG6000 in this experiment aimed to simulate physiological drought conditions, as PEG induces osmotic stress without penetrating bacterial cells. In contrast, NaCl tolerance was assessed separately (Sect. 2.1) to evaluate bacterial resistance to salinity, which represents a distinct environmental stress. The combination of both methods provides a comprehensive assessment of bacterial stress tolerance traits relevant to various abiotic conditions.

Soil water retention capacity

To assess the soil’s water retention capacity, the dry weight of the soil was first measured before watering (Ms). The soil was then gradually saturated with water until reaching its full water-holding capacity, meaning no further absorption occurred. Subsequently, the weight of the saturated soil (Mn) was recorded. The soil’s water retention capacity was then calculated as follows:

Water retention capacity (g) = Mn—Ms.

This value represents the amount of water (in grams) that the soil can retain relative to its dry weight.

Based on this, 100%, 75%, and 50% of the water volume used for irrigation were defined.

Each sample was labeled with the bacterial strain and the irrigation level used. Winter wheat plants were grown under three distinct irrigation regimes 100%, 75%, and 50% of full soil field capacity and exposed to five bacterial strains (T1–T5).

The sample codes used in the results section follow the format “T[n]–[irrigation level]%,” where, for example, plants infected with strain T3 and receiving 50% irrigation are referred to as T3–50%, while noninoculated plants grown under optimal watering conditions are referred to as T0.

Greenhouse experiment simulating drought stress

For the greenhouse experiment, five test treatments and one control were prepared. The wheat variety used was Hondia. The greenhouse experiment was conducted under controlled environmental conditions. Daytime and nighttime temperatures were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and 18 ± 2 °C, respectively, with a relative humidity of approximately 70%. To simulate long-day conditions suitable for wheat (Triticum aestivum), supplemental lighting was provided to maintain a 16-h photoperiod (16 h light/8 h dark). No seed treatment was applied prior to sowing. Each treatment was subjected to three irrigation regimes corresponding to 100%, 75%, and 50% of the soil field capacity. Each irrigation regime was applied in triplicate. For each replicate, three pots per treatment (including the control) were used, with five seeds sown per pot.

Bacterial cultures were grown in nutrient broth at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 24 h. After incubation, cultures were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in sterile distilled water. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to an optical density corresponding to 3.0 × 108 CFU/mL. Then, 5 mL of the bacterial suspension was applied to each test pot. This concentration was selected based on commonly used protocols in wheat inoculation experiments, where PGPR strains are typically applied at densities ranging from 108 to 109 CFU/mL to ensure effective colonization without phytotoxic effects (Kalia et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2023; Bejaoui et al., 2024).

Plant’s oxidative stress markers

Samples for oxidative stress marker analysis were collected 28 days after sowing and following the imposition of drought treatments. Three replicates were used for each treatment, with all aboveground parts of the plants from the pots in each replicate collected for analysis. Hydrogen peroxide content and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were determined in the leaf tissues as indicators of oxidative stress.

Determination of hydrogen peroxide content in winter wheat leaves using potassium iodide

The quantification of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content in plant tissues is an important indicator of oxidative stress levels. A commonly used method involves the reaction of H₂O₂ with potassium iodide (KI), leading to the formation of a yellow-colored triiodide complex. The intensity of the resulting color, measured spectrophotometrically at 350 nm, is directly proportional to the concentration of H₂O₂ in the sample.

The plant material used in this study consisted of winter wheat leaves. Plant material was collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen samples were then ground into a fine powder and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. A buffer solution was prepared with the following components: 0.25 mL of 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA), 0.5 mL of potassium iodide (KI) at a specified molarity (0.1 M), and 0.25 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH adjusted to 8 for fruits and 5.8 for leaves).

For homogenization, 150 mg of the powdered plant tissue was weighed and mixed in a 1.5-mL Eppendorf tube with 1 mL of the prepared buffer. The mixture was homogenized using a micropestle for 10 min at room temperature (~ 22–25 °C). The iodide ions (I⁻) in the solution reacted with H₂O₂, oxidizing to iodine (I₂), which then formed triiodide ions (I₃⁻), resulting in a yellow coloration. Absorbance was measured at 350 nm using a spectrophotometer, optimal for detecting triiodide ions, which correlate with H₂O₂ levels. A standard curve was generated with known H₂O₂ concentrations (nmol/mL) and used to calculate the H₂O₂ concentration in the plant samples by comparing their absorbance to the calibration curve (Junglee et al., 2014).

Determining malondialdehyde (MDA) content in plant tissue using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method

The thiobarbituric acid (TBA) assay is a widely used method for measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in plant tissues, serving as an indicator of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress. This method is based on the reaction between MDA and TBA, resulting in a coloured complex that can be quantified spectrophotometrically.

MDA, a product of lipid peroxidation, reacts with TBA under acidic conditions to form a reddish-brown complex, specifically 1,1,3,3-tetramethyl-2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA-MDA adduct). The absorbance of this complex is measured at 532 nm, which correlates with the concentration of MDA in the sample.

For sample preparation, fresh plant tissue (e.g., leaves) was collected and approximately 100–250 mg was weighed. The tissue was homogenized in 1–2 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) to extract MDA. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain a clear supernatant.

To the supernatant, 2 mL of 0.67% TBA solution was added. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min. After heating, the samples were cooled to room temperature. The mixture was centrifuged again to remove any precipitate, and the supernatant was collected. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer.

A standard curve was generated using known concentrations of MDA. Absorbance values were plotted against MDA concentrations to obtain the calibration curve. The concentration of MDA in the plant samples was calculated by comparing their absorbance readings to the standard curve (Zhang et al., 2021).

Identification of the bacterial strains

All bacterial strains were identified using a biochemical-physiological method with the Biolog Gen III system (Biolog Inc., Hayward, CA, USA) (version 2.8.0), following the manufacturer's instructions, as well as a molecular method sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene fragment. Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB protocol (Coleman, 2021). The obtained bacterial DNA was used for the amplification of the 16S rDNA gene fragment via PCR. The primer pair 16SA1/16SB1 was employed to obtain PCR products corresponding to fragments of the 16S rDNA gene (Gerischer & Gerischer, 2008). The resulting PCR product had a length of 1500 bp.

The PCR reaction was conducted under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles consisting of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, primer annealing at 57 °C for 45 s, and product elongation at 72 °C for 1 min and 30 s. The final elongation step was performed at 72 °C for 9 min. PCR products were visualized under UV light following electrophoretic separation using the Midori Green fluorescent dye (Nippon Genetics Europe GmbH). Electrophoresis was performed on a 1% agarose gel containing Midori Green. For each gel well, 3 µL of the PCR product was loaded along with 1 µL of a molecular weight marker. The separation was carried out in 0.5X TBE buffer at 75 V for 40 min. The PCR products were visualized under UV light to confirm successful amplification and to determine the product size relative to the molecular weight marker ØX174 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

PCR products were purified using the Qiaex II Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and integrity of the obtained nucleotide sequences were analyzed using the Chromas software (https://www.basic.nwu.edu/biotools/Chromas.html) and FinchTV (https://www.geospiza.com/finchtv). The nucleotide sequences obtained from single reads for each primer were assembled and edited using the BioEdit software (Hall, 1999).Consensus 16S rRNA gene sequences for each analysed isolate were obtained using the GenDoc program. The resulting consensus sequences were compared to all available 16S rRNA sequences in the GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) using the BlastN tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blastn). The verified 16S rRNA sequences of the studied strains were submitted to the GenBank database for cataloguing and assignment of accession numbers.

The nucleotide sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar, 2004) available at the EMBL-EBI server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/muscle/). Reference sequences used in the analysis were retrieved from the NCBI database. The aligned dataset was then used for phylogenetic reconstruction in MEGA 12 (Kumar et al., 2018). The evolutionary history was inferred using the neighbor-joining method (Saitou & Nei, 1987). The optimal tree with a sum of branch lengths = 0.171 is shown. The percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (500 replicates) is shown below the branches (Felsenstein, 1985). Evolutionary distances were computed using the Kimura two-parameter method (Kimura, 1980) and are expressed as the number of base substitutions per site. Ambiguous positions were removed for each sequence pair using the pairwise deletion option, resulting in a final dataset comprising 1436 positions. Analyses were conducted in MEGA 12 utilizing up to seven parallel computing threads.

Statistical analysis

For each group, the data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and standard errors. To assess differences between group means, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, revealing significant variation among at least two groups. Subsequently, post hoc comparisons were carried out to identify specific group differences. HSD Tukey’s test was used for multiple comparisons and confirmed statistically significant differences. The threshold for statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Boxplots were constructed to visualize the distribution, variability, and outliers within experimental groups. All analyses were performed in Statistica 12.0 (StatSoft Poland Ltd., 2022; set Plus v 2.0, https://www.statsoft.pl).

Results

In total the 23 strains of bacteria were obtained from dry tobacco leaves. Those strains were classified into five morphotypes based on differences in colony morphology, including shape, size, color, and margin characteristics observed on nutrient agar plates., from now on strains T1–T5, which were used for further tests.

Bacteria resistance to osmotic stress

Bacterial drought resistance was assessed by monitoring growth rates in nutrient broth supplemented with 5% (PEG5) and 10% (PEG10) polyethylene glycol 6000, using OD₆₀₀ measurements over time. After 48 h of incubation, sample T1 exhibited increased growth under both PEG conditions, with OD₆₀₀ values rising from 0.191 to 0.587 in PEG5 and from 0.009 to 0.453 in PEG10. Sample T2 showed a similar trend, with OD₆₀₀ values increasing from 0.186 to 0.782 (PEG5) and from 0.002 to 0.806 (PEG10). In contrast, sample T4 demonstrated only a marginal increase in OD₆₀₀, from 0.060 to 0.082 in PEG5 and from 0.002 to 0.078 in PEG10. Sample T5 also showed enhanced growth, with OD₆₀₀ values increasing from 0.224 to 0.503 in PEG5 and from 0.020 to 0.413 in PEG10.

Notably, the initial OD₆₀₀ values measured immediately after inoculation were generally lower in PEG10 than in PEG5. This may be attributed to the higher osmotic pressure and viscosity of the 10% PEG solution, which could temporarily hinder bacterial dispersion, delay early metabolic activity, or affect light scattering in the suspension. Minor variation in the measurements may also result from the physical properties of PEG-rich media, rather than actual differences in viable cell counts.

Plant growth-promoting properties of the tested bacterial strains

None of the strains produced cellulase or auxin, and none showed phosphate solubilization. All strains produced ammonia. Strain T1 demonstrated the highest lipase production with a score of 3, followed by strain T3. Furthermore, strains T2 and T4 showed moderate lipase production (score 2), while strain T5 expressed the lowest lipase production (score 1). Strain T1 was the only strain that produced hydrogen cyanide. In terms of motility, all strains except T4 were motile. Strain T1 exhibited a moderate salt tolerance with OD600 values of 0.096 at 0% NaCl, 0.074 at 2% NaCl, 0.066 at 5% NaCl, and 0.008 at 10% NaCl. Strain T2 had lower salt tolerance with OD600 values compared to T1, particularly at higher NaCl concentrations. Strain T3 showed the highest salt tolerance with an average OD600 value of 0.1605 across all NaCl concentrations; however, the concentration of 10% NaCl may inhibit cell growth for it (OD600 = 0). Strain T4 displayed relatively low OD600 values across all NaCl concentrations, ranging from 0,009 to 0,03. Strain T5 exhibited a low growth rate for all NaCl concentrations with the average OD600 value recorded of 0.04075. Hydrogen peroxide content: marker of oxidative stress in plants

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentrations, indicative of oxidative stress, were measured in winter wheat plants subjected to different irrigation regimes and bacterial treatments. Control samples exhibited a gradual increase in H₂O₂ levels with higher irrigation, reaching 1.74 mmol/L under 50% irrigation, 5.08 mmol/L at 75%, and 6.09 mmol/L under full irrigation (100%) (Table S3). Bacterial inoculation caused strain- and irrigation-dependent variations in H₂O₂ accumulation. Strain T1 significantly elevated H₂O₂ levels at all irrigation levels, reaching 12.70 mmol/L (+ 635%) at 50%, 6.73 mmol/L (+ 32.6%) at 75%, and 9.54 mmol/L (+ 56.7%) at 100%. Strain T2 reduced H₂O₂ concentrations under water deficit conditions (1.53 mmol/L at 50% and 3.64 mmol/L at 75%) but induced a marked increase at 100% irrigation (12.09 mmol/L, + 98.5%). T3 led to a strong increase under severe drought (10.60 mmol/L at 50%, + 509%), whereas its effect diminished at higher irrigation levels (4.40 mmol/L at 75% and 4.57 mmol/L at 100%). Treatment with T4 resulted in no significant changes compared to the controls at any irrigation level. Strain T5 increased H₂O₂ levels mainly at 50% and 75% irrigation (9.58 and 8.26 mmol/L, respectively), while only a slight increase was observed at 100% (6.50 mmol/L, + 6.7%) (Table S3).

Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test (α = 0.05) revealed that many of the observed changes were statistically significant (Table S3). Notably, strain T1 showed highly significant increases in H₂O₂ concentration across all irrigation levels compared to the respective controls (p < 0.001). Significant increases were also observed for T2 at 100% irrigation (p = 0.003), T3 at 50% (p = 0.0002), and T5 at both 50% (p = 0.0002) and 75% irrigation (p = 0.001). Strain T4 did not produce statistically significant differences under any condition. These findings are also visually supported by boxplots illustrating the distribution of H₂O₂ concentrations across all treatments (Fig. 5).

MDA content: marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress in plants

Malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations, an indicator of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress, were measured in wheat leaf samples subjected to different bacterial inoculations and three irrigation levels (Table S4). In the control group (T0), MDA levels decreased with increasing irrigation, from 0.95 µmol/L at 50% irrigation to 0.26 µmol/L at 75% and 0.21 µmol/L at 100%. Inoculation with strain T1 resulted in MDA concentrations of 0.20 µmol/L at 50% irrigation, 0.88 µmol/L at 75%, and 0.60 µmol/L at 100% (+ 237% and + 182% relative to control at 75% and 100%, respectively). Strain T2 showed MDA concentrations of 0.27 µmol/L at 50%, 0.21 µmol/L at 75%, and 0.38 µmol/L at 100% (+ 78.1% vs. control at 100%). Strain T3 had values of 1.03 µmol/L at 50%, 0.22 µmol/L at 75%, and 0.48 µmol/L at 100% (+ 125.8% vs. control at 100%). For T4, MDA concentrations were 0.88 µmol/L at 50%, 0.60 µmol/L at 75%, and 0.82 µmol/L at 100% (+ 286.6% vs. control at 100%). Strain T5 showed MDA levels of 0.31 µmol/L at 50%, 0.22 µmol/L at 75%, and 0.33 µmol/L at 100%. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test (α = 0.05) indicated that several differences were statistically significant, notably T3 at 50% irrigation (p = 0.013), T1 at 50% (p=0.033), and T2 at 75% (p = 0.038), whereas most treatments with T4 and T5 were not significant (Table S4). Summary of the results

Among the tested strains, T2 caused the greatest decrease in plant’s H₂O₂ and MDA concentrations under limited irrigation conditions. In the T2–50% sample, the H₂O₂ concentration was 1.53 mmol/L, representing an 11.97% decrease compared to the control, while the MDA level decreased by 72.2%. Under 75% irrigation, the T2–75% sample also showed a decrease in both parameters: H₂O₂ by 28.29% and MDA by 19.7%.

Strain T1 caused the largest reduction in MDA content under 50% irrigation conditions (T1–50%, −78.8%). In the same sample, an increase in plant length (38.8 cm) and fresh mass (3.88 g) was also recorded compared to the control at the same irrigation level (38.8 cm and 3.26 g, respectively).

Strain T4 in the T4–75% variant has caused the largest decrease in plant’s H₂O₂ concentration for all samples (−41.97%). Under 50% and 100% irrigation, this strain also caused lower H₂O₂ levels (by 12.67% and 10.27%, respectively).

Strain T3, caused the highest reduction in plant’s H₂O₂ concentration under 100% irrigation conditions (T3–100%, −24.93%), while under 75% irrigation, a moderate decrease in this parameter was observed (−11.56%), along with a reduction in MDA level (−16.9%). Under PEG stress conditions (5% and 10%), the highest OD600 values after 2 days of growth were recorded for strain T2, reaching 0.78 (PEG5) and 0.81 (PEG10). For comparison, the OD600 values for strain T1 were 0.59 (PEG5) and 0.45 (PEG10), and for T5: 0.5 (PEG5) and 0.41 (PEG10). Seedlings’ length and plant’s fresh mass were highest for strain T1 under 100% irrigation conditions (44.8 cm and 8.69 g), while for T2–100% high values were also recorded (43.8 cm and 6.61 g). Under limited irrigation conditions (50% and 75%), seedling length and fresh mass were also relatively high for T2 and T1.

Discussion

All stages of plant’s molecular, morpho-physiological, and biochemical metabolism are significantly impacted by drought stress. Dehydration caused by moisture deficit affects plant metabolic processes such as photosynthesis, sugar metabolism, respiration, and nutrient translocation (Fadiji et al., 2022). Moisture stress also lowers cell water potential, leading to stomatal closure and restricting cell elongation and growth. Numerous studies (Pishchik et al., 2024; Begna, 2020; Fahad et al., 2017) have demonstrated the negative impact of drought stress on crops such as barley, rice, wheat, and maize, where stress during the reproductive phase disrupts flowering and reduces yields.

In this context, plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB), particularly those from the Bacillus genus, have emerged as promising candidates for mitigating drought stress effects. Our research on bacteria isolated from dried tobacco leaves has demonstrated their plant growth-promoting (PGP) potential to enhance winter wheat growth and water stress tolerance, which supports findings by Chieb and Gachomo (2023), who showed that bacteria such as Bacillus and Pseudomonas support plant growth under abiotic stress by producing phytohormones, solubilizing phosphorus, and synthesizing siderophores. The incorporation of dried tobacco leaves as a plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) source is an innovative approach that supports sustainable agriculture. Similarly, Pishchik et al. (2024) confirmed that PGP bacteria significantly enhance wheat growth under osmotic stress by promoting root and shoot development, increasing chlorophyll content, and modulating stress-related phytohormones. These findings align with our study, where different irrigation levels led to variations in plant growth and physiological processes. Additionally Pishchik et al. (2024) demonstrated that PGPB inoculation alleviates oxidative stress by reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation and increasing antioxidant enzyme activity. Our results are consistent with Pishchik’s observations, as we observed reduced MDA accumulation following PGPR inoculation (Table S4).

The role of phytohormones in plant adaptation to drought stress has also been explored in several studies. Specifically, Pishchik et al. (2024) emphasized that PGPB can regulate abscisic acid (ABA) levels in response to water deficit, contributing to enhanced drought resistance. Based on their findings, future research should focus on microbial inoculants that influence phytohormone balance, potentially improving wheat tolerance to water scarcity. Similarly, Devi et al. (2021) reported that inoculation with B. aryabhattai significantly increased wheat shoot and root length under 50% and 25% water stress conditions. Additionally, PGPR treatment improved root volume, dry matter accumulation, and relative water content. Electrolyte leakage reduction in inoculated plants suggests improved membrane stability under drought stress, which aligns with our findings. Likewise, our study observed reduced MDA and H₂O₂ accumulation following bacterial inoculation, indicating mitigation of oxidative stress.

Our findings align with other research on the role of PGPB in promoting plant growth under drought stress. Bacterial inoculation improves growth parameters, reduces oxidative stress, and modulates phytohormone metabolism, thereby enhancing plant adaptation to water deficits. The utilization of bacteria isolated from dried tobacco leaves represents an innovative approach that integrates product management with sustainable agriculture, offering a promising strategy to increase plant resilience against abiotic stress and improve crop productivity under changing climatic conditions. What is more, the variability in bacterial strain effectiveness observed in our study is consistent with findings from Devi et al. (2021), where certain PGPR strains provided substantial drought tolerance while others had a limited impact. Similarly, Pishchik et al. (2024) noted that while some bacterial inoculations reduced oxidative stress, others failed to mitigate cellular damage under severe drought conditions. These variations highlight the need for further investigation into strain-specific mechanisms and their interactions with plant species under field conditions.

The role of Bacillus strains in enhancing drought tolerance is further confirmed by their ability to regulate oxidative stress markers. Studies have demonstrated that PGPR inoculation can reduce malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation, thereby mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing plant resilience (Umapathi et al., 2024). Our study also observed a reduction in MDA levels and H₂O₂ accumulation in wheat plants inoculated with Bacillus strains, indicating effective mitigation of oxidative stress (Tables 4 and 5). Moreover, the inoculation of Bacillus strains improved membrane stability, as reflected by the reduction in membrane leakage, suggesting better adaptation to drought stress (Umapathi et al., 2024).

The only factor differing the tested samples from the control samples was the presence of our tested strains (T1–T5). That is why, the observed effects in the plants indicate a beneficial impact of the tested strains on tobacco plants in drought conditions. However, the exact mechanisms of influence of bacteria on plants require further investigation. Future research should incorporate molecular or microbiological techniques to monitor bacterial population dynamics in planta, which would provide deeper insight into the symbiotic interactions and their role in drought tolerance.In summary, our results add to the growing evidence that Bacillus-based bioinoculants enhance plant drought tolerance through several mechanisms, including reducing oxidative stress, stabilizing cell membranes, and improving plant water management.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that endophytic bacterial strains isolated from dried tobacco leaves enhance drought tolerance and growth in winter wheat, particularly under moderate water deficit (50% and 75% irrigation).

The effects were clearly strain-dependent: T1 consistently improved growth and reduced oxidative stress across all irrigation levels, whereas T2, T3, and T5 showed beneficial effects only under specific water regimes. In contrast, T4 did not provide measurable advantages. These results emphasize the importance of carefully selecting the most efficient isolates for practical applications in agriculture.

These findings highlight the potential of tobacco dried-leaf–derived PGP bacteria as sustainable bioinoculants for improving crop performance under water-limited conditions. The use of such microbial resources represents an innovative strategy to enhance the resilience of cereal crops to drought while potentially reducing reliance on chemical inputs.

Future work should focus on field trials to validate these greenhouse findings, assess long-term effects on yield, and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed stress tolerance. Broader testing across different wheat cultivars and other staple crops could further confirm the applicability of these bacteria in climate-resilient and sustainable agriculture.
