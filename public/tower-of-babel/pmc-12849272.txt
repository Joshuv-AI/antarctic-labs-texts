Prognostic factors for mortality and respiratory failure in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 after three-dose vaccination: a retrospective cohort study during the Omicron period
Authors: Chia-Ying Wu, Szu-Yu Liu, Yu-Chien Ho, Wen-Ying Lin, Min-Chi Chang, Chia-Jen Liu, Yi-Tsung Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite high vaccination rates, severe and critical illnesses in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 continue to pose significant challenges to healthcare systems. This study aimed to identify factors associated with mortality among hospitalized patients with COVID-19 who had received three vaccine doses during the spread of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant.MethodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted at the Taipei Veterans General Hospital between April and June 2022. Hospitalized adult patients with confirmed COVID-19 who had received three vaccine doses before diagnosis were included. Demographic data, comorbidities, disease severity indicators, laboratory values, and treatment details were collected from the electronic records. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify the factors associated with 28-day mortality and the need for invasive mechanical ventilation.ResultsA total of 620 patients with a median age of 75 years were hospitalized for COVID-19, and the 28-day mortality rate was 7.9%. The independent predictors of mortality included a higher comorbidity burden, elevated World Health Organization (WHO) ordinal scale scores, elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios (NLR), and thrombocytopenia. Among the 610 initially non-ventilated patients, 6.6% required invasive mechanical ventilation. Hematological malignancy, metastatic cancer, higher WHO ordinal scales, elevated NLR and bacterial secondary infection were significant risk factors for respiratory failure.ConclusionDespite receiving three doses of the COVID-19 vaccines, patients hospitalized with COVID-19 continue to experience notable rates of mortality and respiratory failure. During the current endemic phase of COVID-19, these prognostic indicators can help guide the management and early intervention of high-risk patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12325-z.Keywords: COVID-19, Three doses vaccines, 28-day mortality, Mechanical ventilation, Hospitalization

Introduction

In December 2019, an outbreak of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 [1], caused by the novel SARS-CoV-2, was first reported in Wuhan, China. This outbreak quickly escalated into a global pandemic [2, 3]. On May 5, 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) ended a public health emergency of international concern [4]. Although the severity and incidence of COVID-19 have decreased because of the population’s immunity from vaccination and past infections, COVID-19 remains an endemic disease that affects susceptible individuals worldwide [5].

Taiwan has experienced a community outbreak of SARS-CoV-2 omicron variant since April 2022. According to data from Taiwan’s Central Epidemic Command Center, by the time of the surge of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant, over 84% of the population had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine, with approximately 60% having received three doses of any COVID-19 vaccine [1]. Current evidence suggests that while vaccine efficacy against infection diminishes over time, its protection against hospitalization and mortality remains [6–9]. During the Omicron surge, individuals who had received three vaccine doses were considered adequately vaccinated; however, vulnerable groups—including older adults, individuals with comorbidities, and immunocompromised individuals—continued to experience severe disease or death due to SARS-CoV-2 infections despite vaccination [10, 11]. Although high vaccination coverage has mitigated the overall burden of COVID-19, severe and critical illness still imposes substantial pressures on healthcare systems. Evidence on prognostic factors among fully vaccinated patients hospitalized with COVID-19 remains limited, and understanding these residual risks is crucial to improve risk stratification and guide clinical management in the current endemic phase of COVID-19 [12, 13].

Most studies have focused on the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in population-based settings [10, 14, 15]. Far less is known about the factors associated with mortality among adequately vaccinated patients hospitalized for COVID-19. In this study, we aimed to assess the mortality risk factors in this specific patient group. These findings enrich the current literature and address a critical research gap by focusing on the hospitalized cohorts.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a single-center, retrospective observational cohort study at the Taipei Veterans General Hospital. The study included all adult hospitalized patients (aged ≥ 20 years) diagnosed with COVID-19 based on positive SARS-CoV-2 real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction results from nasopharyngeal swabs between April 1, 2022, and July 31, 2022. The individuals chosen for the analysis received three doses of the COVID-19 vaccine prior to receiving a diagnosis of COVID-19. These vaccines include those manufactured by Oxford/AstraZeneca, Pfizer/BioNTech, Moderna, Novavax, and Medigen. Individuals under the age of 20 who had been diagnosed with COVID-19 before the completion of a three-dose vaccination series or who had received fewer than three doses of the COVID-19 vaccine before hospitalization were excluded from the study. The dominant SARS-CoV-2 variant was Omicron BA.2 from April 17, 2022, to August 12, 2022 [16, 17]. The study received ethical approval from the hospital’s Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was not required.

Exposure and outcome variables

All clinical information was obtained from the electronic patient records. Clinical and demographic data, including age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, and complications, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [18], laboratory parameters, WHO eight-category ordinal scale at admission [19], COVID-19 vaccination status, medication treatment for COVID-19, and concurrent infections, were collected. The timeframe between the last COVID-19 vaccine dose and SARS-CoV-2 infection was calculated as the number of weeks between the final vaccine dose and the date of the first positive RT-PCR result and categorized into three intervals: <12 weeks, 12–24 weeks, and >24 weeks. Vaccine regimen categories (Protein subunit–based, mRNA-based, and adenoviral vector-based) were defined according to the primary two-dose vaccination series. Patients who received mixed vaccine brands were classified based on the dominant platform of their primary regimen, whereas the type of the third (booster) dose was recorded separately. All dosing intervals adhered to national vaccination guidelines. Medications such as remdesivir, steroids, and tocilizumab were prescribed in accordance with the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control therapeutic guidelines [20]. Secondary bacterial infection was defined as the presence of microbiological evidence of bacterial infection at any site 48 h after the diagnosis of COVID-19 [21, 22]. The primary outcomes were 28-day all-cause mortality and the requirement for mechanical ventilation following COVID-19 diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical and continuous variables were represented as numbers and percentages (%) and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), respectively. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify the independent risk factors for mortality and the requirement for mechanical ventilation in patients hospitalized with COVID-19. Variables included in the univariate analysis were selected based on clinical relevance, prior evidence, and statistical suggestiveness (p < 0.10). Clinically important variables with borderline significance, such as chronic kidney disease and solid organ malignancy, were also entered into the multivariable logistic regression for evaluation. Univariate analyses were performed using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. All candidate variables selected from univariate analyses were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using a forward stepwise (Likelihood Ratio) approach to identify independent predictors of mortality and respiratory failure.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

Between April 1, 2022, and July 31, 2022, 1933 hospitalized patients with SARS-CoV-2 infections in our hospital were identified. We excluded 267 patients aged <20 years. Those who received no vaccination (n = 429), only one dose (n = 161), two doses (n = 274), or four doses (n = 182) before being diagnosed with COVID-19 were also excluded from the study. A total of 620 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled, including 10 patients directly admitted to the ICU (Fig. 1). Missing data were summarized in Table 1. Body mass index was available for 591 patients (95.3%), platelet count for 618 patients (99.7%), and C-reactive protein for 606 patients (97.7%). Given the low proportion of missing values, the impact of missing data on the analysis was considered negligible.

The enrollment of study patients. In total, 1933 patients with confirmed COVID-19 were identified during the study period. 620 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled; this cohort was used for evaluating 28-day mortality. For the analysis of progression to invasive mechanical ventilation, 10 patients were excluded because they were either transferred from another hospital already intubated (n = 5) or required mechanical ventilation on admission (n = 5). The median age of these 620 patients was 75 years (interquartile range[IQR], 60–88), and 391 (63.1%) were men. Moreover, 118 patients (19.0%) were diagnosed with COVID-19 during hospitalization in this study. The median CCI score was 6 (IQR, 3–7), and the median WHO eight-category ordinal severity scale score was 4 (IQR, 4–5). Of the 620 patients, 74 (11.9%) had a bacterial co-infection, and 104 (16.8%) had a secondary bacterial infection. The 28-day mortality rate was 7.9% (49/620), whereas the in-hospital mortality rate was 11.0% (68/620). Of the 610 initially non-ventilated patients with COVID-19, 40 (6.6%) progressed to respiratory failure and required invasive mechanical ventilation.

Prognostic factors for 28-day mortality in hospitalized patients with COVID-19

Table 1 shows a comparison of the clinical characteristics between 28-day survivors and non-survivors. Univariate analysis shows that older age, male sex, higher CCI, higher WHO ordinal scale, higher NLR, thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 150 × 10⁹/L), and C-reactive protein (CRP) level > 10 mg/dL were predictors of 28-day mortality. After adjusting for patient characteristics, multivariate analysis identified several independent factors associated with 28-day mortality among hospitalized patients with COVID-19. Higher CCI (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.187, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.049–1.343, p = 0.007) and WHO ordinal severity scale scores (aOR = 1.914, 95% CI = 1.366–2.681, p < 0.001) were strongly predictive of mortality. Higher NLR (aOR = 1.025, 95% CI = 1.006–1.044, p = 0.009) and thrombocytopenia (aOR = 3.639, 95% CI = 1.920–6.896, p < 0.001) were independent factors associated with 28-day mortality. In contrast, none of the treatment variables—including remdesivir, corticosteroids, or tocilizumab—showed an independent association with mortality in the multivariable model (Table 2).

Predictors for respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation among initially non-mechanical ventilated patients

Of the 610 hospitalized patients with COVID-19 who were initially non-mechanically ventilated, 40 (6.6%) progressed to respiratory failure and required invasive mechanical ventilation. Table 3 shows the differences in the clinical characteristics between patients with and without progression to respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. CCI, WHO ordinal scale score, the proportion of solid organ malignancy, hematological malignancy, and metastatic cancer were significantly higher in patients with progression to respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation than in those without progression to respiratory failure. Patients with respiratory failure also had a higher NLR, a longer duration between the last vaccine dose and the diagnosis of COVID-19, and a longer time to receive remdesivir than did those without respiratory failure. Multivariate analyses revealed that underlying hematological malignancy (aOR = 7.553, 95% CI = 1.941–29.390, p = 0.004), metastatic cancer (aOR = 5.401, 95% CI = 2.121–13.754, p < 0.001), disease severity at admission, as measured by the WHO ordinal severity scale, (aOR = 3.912, 95% CI = 2.535–6.035, p < 0.001), systemic inflammation, as indicated by NLR (aOR = 1.031, 95% CI = 1.008–1.055, p = 0.007) and bacterial secondary infection (aOR = 7.529, 95% CI = 3.384–16.751, p < 0.001) were independent predictors of the need for invasive mechanical ventilation (Table 4).

Discussion

We investigated the issues that are rarely discussed in the literature regarding the prognostic factors of hospitalized patients with COVID-19 who had received three doses of COVID-19 vaccines. The 28-day mortality rate among these patients was 7.9%, and 6.6% developed respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. We identified that a greater CCI, a higher WHO ordinal severity score, elevated NLR, and thrombocytopenia were significantly associated with mortality at 28 days. In addition, hematological malignancies, a higher WHO ordinal severity score, and an elevated NLR were associated with progression to invasive mechanical ventilation.

Most studies have investigated risk factors for poor outcomes of COVID-19 in the general population; however, few have specifically examined hospitalized or critically ill patients after full vaccination [23–26]. Although widespread vaccination and prior infections have substantially reduced the incidence of severe illness, a subset of patients continues to experience poor outcomes requiring hospitalization. The residual risk may indicate host vulnerabilities and chronic comorbidities at the population level [27].

Previous studies on hospitalized patients with COVID-19 have included only a small subset of patients who had received three doses of COVID-19 vaccine. During thee Omicron variant period from December 2021 to February 2022, one study from the United States observed an in-hospital mortality rate of 11.4% and a mechanical ventilation rate of 10.0% among hospitalized patients with COVID-19. Less than 10% of the patients (70 of 857) received three doses of COVID-19 vaccines in this study, and the analysis of risk factors for poor outcomes in these 70 patients was not described [28]. Another study was conducted between March 17 and June 29, 2022, during the Omicron BA.2 phase, and examined 3,549 hospitalized patients with COVID-19. Among the 1,216 patients who received three doses of the vaccine, 182 patients (15.0%) either required admission to the intensive care unit or died while they were being treated during hospitalization. However, this study did not identify the independent risk factors for poor outcomes [24]. Several studies have reported that various comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, hypertension, and cancer, are associated with a higher risk of death among hospitalized patients with COVID-19; however, vaccination status was not described in these studies [27, 29, 30]. Our result extended this concept to the post-vaccination era, highlighting that systemic inflammation and comorbidity burden remain key determinants of adverse outcomes. These findings suggested that host vulnerability and systemic inflammatory status were important factors for disease outcome in vaccinated hospitalized patients [31].

In previous studies from April 2020 to July 2021, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated inflammatory markers—including D-dimer and CRP— were associated with severe outcomes in patients with COVID-19 [32–35]. From the early pandemic, a retrospective study in Wuhan identified that increased NLR was associated with in-hospital mortality among patients with COVID‐19 [36]. Similarly, a nationwide study from Japan between February 2020 and November 2021 discovered that the combination of the NLR and oxygen requirement on admission helped identify patients at risk of progression to severe disease [37].

In our study, which focused on hospitalized patients who had received three doses of COVID-19 vaccines, we observed that initial disease severity, an elevated NLR and thrombocytopenia were independently associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality. Furthermore, higher NLR, higher WHO ordinal severity scale score, underlying metastatic cancer, hematological malignancy and bacterial secondary infection were significantly associated with respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. Compared with other inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP, ferritin, or D-dimer, NLR presents several clinical advantages—it is inexpensive, readily obtainable from routine blood counts. These characteristics make NLR particularly valuable for early risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Our findings highlight an important clinical observation: despite vaccine-induced immunity reduced pathogenicity of the Omicron variant, systemic inflammation and hematologic abnormalities, systemic inflammation and hematologic abnormalities —reflected by NLR and platelet counts—remain strong predictors of adverse outcomes in hospitalized patients with COVID-19.

Secondary bacterial infections are frequent and critical complications in patients with COVID-19. A study at West China Hospital between December 2022 and March 2023 revealed that 10.1% of hospitalized patients with COVID-19 experienced secondary infections. Moreover, patients with secondary bacterial infections have higher in-hospital mortality rates and longer hospital stays, particularly those with severe or critical COVID-19 [38]. Another study conducted in an ICU in Brazil in 2022 reported that 29.8% of COVID-19 patients developed secondary infections [22]. In our study, secondary bacterial infection occurred in 16.8% of all hospitalized vaccinated patients and was a significant predictor of respiratory failure in patients even with three doses of COVID-19 vaccines. Our findings emphasized that standard infection-prevention measures should not be ignored in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 to mitigate the threat of subsequent bacterial infection.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Clinical outcomes could vary across institutions due to differences in healthcare resources, patient demographics, and treatment practices. Large-scale, multicenter, or prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings across diverse settings. Second, the single-center design limits the generalizability and number of patients. Some specific comorbidities, such as small numbers of hematologic malignancies among patients who developed respiratory failure, may have contributed to statistical instability and wide confidence intervals in the multivariate analyses. Finally, the study focused on patients who received three doses of vaccines during the Omicron variant period; however, people with repeated infections and additional booster doses of vaccines are currently more common. Therefore, the results may not extend to populations in the current era; our findings fill the knowledge gap in the literature and provide valuable insights into hospitalized patients who have received adequate doses of COVID-19 vaccines.

In conclusion, comorbidities, initial disease severity, elevated NLR, and thrombocytopenia were predictors of poor outcomes in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 who received three doses of COVID-19 vaccines. This study provides insights into the prognostic factors influencing outcomes in patients with adequate immunity. To combat the ongoing COVID-19 endemic, additional research should be conducted to investigate the prognostic characteristics of patients who have received additional vaccine doses.
