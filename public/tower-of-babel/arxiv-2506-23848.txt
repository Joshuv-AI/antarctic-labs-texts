Little q q -Jacobi polynomials and symmetry breaking operators for U q ​ ( s ​ l 2 ) U_{q}(sl_{2})
Q. Labriet, L. Poulain d’Andecy

Abstract
This paper presents explicit formulas for intertwining operators of the quantum group U q ​ ( s ​ l 2 ) U_{q}(sl_{2}) acting on tensor products of Verma modules. We express a first set of intertwining operators (the holographic operators ) in terms of the little q q -Jacobi polynomials, and we obtain for the dual set (the symmetry breaking operators ) a q q -deformation of the Rankin–Cohen operators. The Verma modules are realised on polynomial spaces and, interestingly, we find along the way the need to work with non-commuting variables. Explicit connections are given with the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients of U q ​ ( s ​ l 2 ) U_{q}(sl_{2}) expressed with the q q -Hahn polynomials.

Introduction

The main purpose of this paper is to study the intertwining operators associated to the following decomposition of representations of the quantum group
U_{q}(sl_{2})
:

V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cong\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}\,,

where
V_{\lambda}
is the lowest-weight Verma module of weight
\lambda
, and to show the appearance of orthogonal polynomials from the
q
-Askey scheme and of
q
-differential operators (operators involving the
q
-derivative). In a nutshell, we will show how the family, on
n
, of intertwining operators

\Psi^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}\ :\ \ V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}\to V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}},

(1.1)

involves the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials, while the dual intertwining operators

\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}\ :\ \ (V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{\star}\to V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}^{\star},

(1.2)

are given by
q
-deformations of Rankin–Cohen brackets. In some context, the operators in (
1.2
) are called
symmetry breaking
operators while those in (
1.1
) are called
holographic
operators
[
9
]
. Their link with little
q
-Jacobi polynomials generalises the appearance in the same context for
sl_{2}
of the classical Jacobi polynomials and of the Rankin–Cohen operators
[
16
,
17
]
. However, we will see that the
q
-deformed situation involves new features, not least among them the necessity of working with non-commuting variables.

To put our results into perspective, let us briefly recall the classical
sl_{2}
situation. First of all, Verma modules are realised on the space of polynomials in one variable. Therefore, the intertwining operator (
1.1
) becomes a map between polynomial spaces and it turns out to be possible to give it as follows (see
[
16
]
):

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}\Psi^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}\ :&\mathbb{C}[z]&\to&\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]\cong\mathbb{C}[t,tv]\\[5.0pt]
&z^{k}&\mapsto&t^{k+n}P_{n}(v)\end{array}\ ,

(1.3)

where the polynomials
P_{n}(v)
are the classical Jacobi polynomials, and
t
and
v
are given by
t=x+y
and
v=\frac{y-x}{x+y}
. The main feature of this change of variables is that the polynomials
t^{n}P_{n}(v)
,
n\geq 0
, are the lowest-weight vectors of the representations
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
appearing in the decomposition of the tensor product, while for a fixed
n
, the diagonal action of
U(sl_{2})
acts on the variable
t
on the set of polynomials
\{t^{k+n}P_{n}(v)\}_{k\geq 0}
.

Then, passing to the duals, the intertwining operators (
1.2
) are realised as bidifferential operators (see
[
17
]
):

\begin{array}[]{lrcl}RC^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}\ :&\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]&\to&\mathbb{C}[z]\\[5.0pt]
&f\otimes g&\mapsto&RC^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}(f,g)\end{array}\ .

(1.4)

This can be done through a suitable identification of
V_{\lambda}^{\star}
with a polynomial space and the resulting operators
RC^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}
turn out to coincide with the so-called Rankin–Cohen brackets from the world of modular forms
[
2
]
. Roughly speaking, the connections between (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) can be expressed by the fact that the symbols of the bidifferential operators
RC^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}
are the two variable polynomial in
(x,y)
obtained from the Jacobi polynomials in (
1.3
).

The present paper generalises the above situation to the case of the quantum group
U_{q}(sl_{2})
. On one hand, we find that the role of the classical Jacobi polynomials is played by the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials, which are well-known
q
-deformations of the Jacobi polynomials in the
q
-version of the Askey scheme
[
12
]
. On the other hand, we find for the intertwining operators
RC^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}
explicit
q
-deformations of the Rankin–Cohen brackets. We obtain formulas similar to the ones found in
[
19
]
but the approach through the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials is different. Note that connections between the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and
U_{q}(sl_{2})
are known, such as the fact that they appear as matrix elements (see
[
14
]
and references therein) or via representations of the Askey–Wilson algebra
[
1
]
. However the appearance of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials presented here is different and seems to be new.

An interesting feature of the quantum setting appears immediately when one tries to generalise the change of variables in (
1.3
). Indeed in (
1.3
) all subrepresentations of the tensor product were obtained upon multiplying by the variable
t
a given lowest-weight vector. The multiplication by
t
played the role, for all subrepresentations, of the raising operator moving from the lowest-weight vector to vectors of higher weights. It turns out that in the quantum setting, such a feature can appear only if we impose for the variables
x
and
y
to be
q
-commuting instead of simply commuting. Therefore, all our study here will involve the so-called quantum plane in two variables instead of classical polynomials in two variables.

The explicit decomposition of tensor products involves the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, which are known for
U_{q}(sl_{2})
to be given in terms of
q
-Hahn polynomials (also polynomials in the
q
-version of the Askey scheme
[
12
]
). As a byproduct of our construction, we exhibit explicit connections between the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and the
q
-Hahn polynomials, in the spirit for example of
[
13
,
16
]
. We also find a realisation of the
q
-Hahn algebra in terms of the
q
-difference operator of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials.

The results presented here can be looked upon through the lens of the so-called F-method introduced by Kobayashi–Pevzner
[
7
,
8
]
. Using this method for holomorphic discrete series representations of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the symbols of Rankin–Cohen bidifferential operators are related to solutions of the hypergeometric differential equation and thus to Jacobi polynomials. The heart of the F-method relies on an algebraic Fourier transform defined as a morphism between two Weyl algebras. For example, in
[
17
]
the algebraic Fourier transform was realised explicitly using the adjonction for the Fischer inner product on Verma modules. The F-method was used successfully for other Lie groups and other class of representations (see
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
15
,
18
]
for example) but as far as we know, it is a open question to extend it to the quantum group setting.

Our results show that several features of the F-method may exist also in the quantum setting. First, the intertwining operators in (
1.1
) are related to solutions of a
q
-version of the hypergeometric differential equation, and that is how the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials appear. Then, to dualise the map in (
1.1
) into the intertwining operators in (
1.2
) we identify the contragredient representation spaces with the original ones via a
q
-deformation of the Fischer inner product. This could be seen as an instance of a
q
-version of the algebraic Fourier transform used in the F-method. In some sense the connection between the
q
-Rankin–Cohen brackets and the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials is made through a
q
-analogue of the notion of symbol of a differential operator (see Section
5
).
Thus, the present results may be seen as a first step and a fundamental example towards the formulation and the understanding of an analog of the F-method in the quantum group setting.

As was done in
[
16
,
17
]
, once the two-fold tensor products are well understood, one may be interested in three-fold (and in general
n
-fold) tensor products. Indeed, the Racah problem for three-fold tensor products of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
-representations involves the famous Askey–Wilson polynomials (see for example
[
3
,
20
]
). Based on the present study, interesting connections between little
q
-Jacobi polynomials,
q
-Rankin–Cohen brackets, Askey–Wilson polynomials and their Askey–Wilson algebra can be expected, and we leave this for a future work.

Tensor products of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
-representations

We take
q
to be a non-zero complex number which is not a root of unity.

2.1
The Hopf algebra
U_{q}(sl_{2})
and its Verma modules

The algebra
U_{q}(sl_{2})
is generated by
K,E,F
with
K
invertible and defining relations:

KEK^{-1}=q^{2}E\,,\ \ \ \ \ KFK^{-1}=q^{-2}F\,,\ \ \ \ \ [E,F]=\frac{K-K^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}}\ .

The coproduct and the antipode are given by

\Delta(K)=K\otimes K\,,\ \ \ \ \Delta(E)=E\otimes 1+K\otimes E\,,\ \ \ \ \ \Delta(F)=F\otimes K^{-1}+1\otimes F,

S(K)=K^{-1}\,,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ S(E)=-K^{-1}E\,,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ S(F)=-FK\ .

The Casimir element of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
is the central element

C=(q-q^{-1})^{2}\left(FE+\frac{qK+q^{-1}K^{-1}}{(q-q^{-1})^{2}}\right).

(2.1)

Verma modules.

We denote
V_{\lambda}
the representation of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
on the vector space
\mathbb{C}[z]
of polynomials in one variable, where the action of the generators is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}K^{\pm}P(z)&=&q^{\pm\lambda}P(q^{\pm 2}z),\\[3.00003pt]
EP(z)&=&zP(z),\\[3.00003pt]
FP(z)&=&\displaystyle-\frac{q^{\lambda-1}(P(q^{2}z)-P(z))-q^{-\lambda+1}(P(z)-P(q^{-2}z))}{(q-q^{-1})^{2}z}.\end{array}

(2.2)

On the basis
\{z^{n}\}
we get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}Kz^{n}&=&q^{\lambda+2n}z^{n},\\[3.00003pt]
Ez^{n}&=&z^{n+1},\\[3.00003pt]
Fz^{n}&=&-[\lambda+n-1][n]z^{n-1}.\end{array}

(2.3)

One easily checks that this gives a representation of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
and that the Casimir operator acts as a number, namely,
Cz^{n}=(q^{\lambda-1}+q^{-\lambda+1})z^{n}
for any
n\geq 0
. The representation
V_{\lambda}
is the lowest-weight Verma module associated to the weight
\lambda
.

Remark 2.1
.

An equivalent presentation of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
, used for example in
[
3
,
19
]
, is found by setting
\tilde{K}=K^{1/2}
,
X_{+}=E\tilde{K}^{-1}
and
X_{-}=\tilde{K}F
. Note that the coproduct becomes more symmetric:

\Delta(X_{\pm})=X_{\pm}\otimes\tilde{K}^{-1}+\tilde{K}\otimes X_{\pm}\ .

However, we choose the presentation with
K,E,F
above in order to have in the simplest way a result like in Proposition
2.3
below.
∎

2.2
Tensor product of two Verma modules

We fix now
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{Z}
. The reason for this condition, as will be shown below, is to have the following decomposition:

V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cong\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}\ .

We realise the representation
V_{\lambda}
on the space
\mathbb{C}[x]
and
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
on the space
\mathbb{C}[y]
. We denote the basis elements
x^{k}\otimes y^{l}
simply by
x^{k}y^{l}
. On this basis, the action of the generators of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
is:

\displaystyle\Delta(K)\penalty\ x^{k}y^{l}

\displaystyle=q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2k+2l}x^{k}y^{l}\,,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Delta(E)\penalty\ x^{k}y^{l}

\displaystyle=x^{k+1}y^{l}+q^{\lambda+2k}x^{k}y^{l+1}\,,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\Delta(F)\penalty\ x^{k}y^{l}

\displaystyle=-q^{-\lambda^{\prime}-2l}[\lambda+k-1][k]x^{k-1}y^{l}-[\lambda^{\prime}+l-1][l]x^{k}y^{l-1}\ .

(2.6)

Note that we do not need for now to specify if
x,y
are commuting variables or not. In fact, we will see in a moment that it will be useful to impose a
q
-commutation relation between
x
and
y
, thereby identifying the space
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
with a quantum plane.

We start by explicitly finding the lowest-weight vectors in the tensor product.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
n\geq 0
, the element:

\displaystyle P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)=[\lambda]_{n}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{k}q^{k(\lambda^{\prime}+2n-k-1)}\frac{x^{k}y^{n-k}}{[\lambda]_{k}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n-k}},

(2.7)

is a non-zero lowest weight vector of weight
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n
, i.e.
\Delta(F)P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)=0
and
\Delta(K)P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)=q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)
.

Proof.

To see that
P^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}(x,y)
is non-zero, we look at the coefficients in front of
x^{n}
and
y^{n}
. We find respectively, up to signs and powers of
q
,
[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n}
and
[\lambda]_{n}
. The condition
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{Z}
ensures that
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
can not both be integers, so at least one of these coefficients is not zero.

The action of
\Delta(K)
is found immediately, so it only remains to check that the vector in (
2.2
) is annihilated by
\Delta(F)
. We set
c_{k}=\frac{[\lambda]_{n}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n}}{[\lambda]_{k}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n-k}}
and
\alpha(n,k)=k(\lambda^{\prime}+2n-k-1)
.

\displaystyle-\Delta(F)P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}c_{k}\penalty\ q^{\alpha(n,k)}([\lambda^{\prime}+n-k-1][n-k]x^{k}y^{n-k-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+q^{-\lambda^{\prime}-2(n-k)}[\lambda+k-1][k]x^{k-1}y^{n-k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}c_{k}\penalty\ q^{\alpha(n,k)}[\lambda^{\prime}+n-k-1][n-k]x^{k}y^{n-k-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k+1}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k+1\end{bmatrix}_{q}c_{k+1}\penalty\ q^{\alpha(n,k+1)-\lambda^{\prime}-2(n-k-1)}[\lambda+k][k+1]x^{k}y^{n-k-1}.

We have:

\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}c_{k}[\lambda^{\prime}+n-k-1][n-k]=\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k+1\end{bmatrix}_{q}c_{k+1}[\lambda+k][k+1].

Thus

-\Delta(F)P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)=\\
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}c_{k}[\lambda^{\prime}+n-k-1][n-k]x^{k}y^{n-k-1}(q^{\alpha(n,k)}-q^{\alpha(n,k+1)-\lambda^{\prime}-2(n-k-1)}).

Finally, we just check that the two powers of
q
are equals to get the result.
∎

The quantum plane
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
.

Now that we have lowest-weight vectors, we can construct subrepresentations by repeated applications of
\Delta(E)
. Note that in a Verma module realised on
\mathbb{C}[z]
, the action of
E
is by multiplication by
z
. We are going now to invoke a
q
-commutation relation between
x
and
y
in order to have the action of
\Delta(E)
also given by multiplication.

From now on, we are going to identify the tensor product
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
with the quantum plane
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
, defined as the space of polynomials in
x,y
such that

yx=q^{2}xy\ .

(2.8)

In other words, we put an algebra structure on
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
by asking that
yx=q^{2}xy
. We collect below the first advantage of having this
q
-commutation relation between
x
and
y
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The action of
\Delta(E)
on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
is the left multiplication by
(x+q^{\lambda}y)
:

\Delta(E)\penalty\ x^{k}y^{l}=(x+q^{\lambda}y)x^{k}y^{l}\,.

(2.9)

and the decomposition

\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]\simeq\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{C}[x+q^{\lambda}y]P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y).

(2.10)

gives the decomposition of
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
into the direct sum of subrepresentations
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
.

To be perfectly clear, in order to see an element such as

(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{k}P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)\ ,

as a vector in
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
, we need to use
yx=q^{2}xy
in order to write it in the basis of ordered monomials
x^{k}y^{l}
, which is then identified with the standard basis
x^{k}\otimes y^{l}
of
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Using the relation
yx=q^{2}xy
, we have:

(x+q^{\lambda}y)x^{k}y^{l}=x^{k+1}y^{l}+q^{\lambda+2k}x^{k}y^{l+1}\ .

This reproduces indeed the correct action of
\Delta(E)
in (
2.5
).

From the fact that
P_{n}(x,y)
is a non-zero lowest-weight vector of weight
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n
and the form of the action of
\Delta(E)
, we have that the vectors:

(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{k}P_{n}(x,y)\,,\ \ \ k\geq 0\ ,

form a submodule isomorphic to
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
. The condition
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{Z}
(and
q
not a root of unity) ensures that the Casimir operator
\Delta(C)
takes different values on these different subspaces, that is

q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+n-1}+q^{-(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+n-1)}\neq q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+n^{\prime}-1}+q^{-(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+n^{\prime}-1)}\ \ \ \ \ \text{if $n\neq n^{\prime}$.}

This proves the decomposition in (
2.10
).
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The necessity of the
q
-commutation relation between
x
and
y
should be clear. If the action of
\Delta(E)
is to be by multiplication by a polynomial, this polynomial has to be the result of
\Delta(E)
on
1
. This is
x+q^{\lambda}y
. Then the only way to make the action of
\Delta(E)
coincide with left multiplication by
x+q^{\lambda}y
is to have
yx=q^{2}xy
.

Actually, it is remarkable that such a simple way is possible and of course it is closely related with the fact that
\Delta(E)
is not equal to
E\otimes 1+1\otimes E
but is not too far from it. Another way to see it is as follows.

Due to the PBW basis of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
, the (lowest-weight) Verma module
V_{\lambda}
is naturally identified as a vector space with
\mathbb{C}[E]
. Moreover, this identification is compatible with the action of
E
.

In a similar way, the tensor product
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
of two Verma modules may be identified with
\mathbb{C}[E\otimes 1,1\otimes E]
as a vector space. For
q=1
, the identification is still compatible with the action of
\Delta(E)
since
\Delta(E)=E\otimes 1+1\otimes E
. However, this is not the case anymore for
U_{q}(sl_{2})
. Indeed, the space
\mathbb{C}[E\otimes 1,1\otimes E]
is not stable by multiplication by
\Delta(E)
since
\Delta(E)=E\otimes 1+K\otimes E
.

Thus, the natural identification to make here is

V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cong\mathbb{C}[E\otimes 1,K\otimes E]\ .

The space of polynomials in
E\otimes 1
and
K\otimes E
is now stable by multiplication by
\Delta(E)
. From this the
q
-commutation relation appears naturally, since we have:

(K\otimes E)(E\otimes 1)=q^{2}(E\otimes 1)(K\otimes E)\ .

Note that to identify
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cong\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
with
\mathbb{C}[E\otimes 1,K\otimes E]
respecting the action of
\Delta(E)
, we send
x
to
E\otimes 1
and
y
to
q^{-\lambda}K\otimes E
, see Formula (
2.5
).

As a conclusion, the underlying reason for the
q
-commutation relation between
x
and
y
is the
q
-commutation relation between the two terms in
\Delta(E)
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

The linear map
\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
defined by:

\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\ :\ \mathbb{C}[z]\to\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]\,,\ \ \ \ \ z^{k}\mapsto(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{k}P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)

(2.11)

is, up to a scalar, the unique intertwining operator between

and

.

3
Little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and decomposition of tensor products

In this section, we introduce a linear isomorphism transforming the quantum plane
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
into a more classical polynomial space in two variables. As a result, we show how the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials appear in the lowest-weight vectors.

3.1
Little
q
-Jacobi polynomials

Following
[
12
]
, the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials are defined by

\displaystyle j_{n}(X;a,b;q)

\displaystyle={}_{2}\phi_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q^{-2n},\,abq^{2(n+1)}\\
aq^{2},\end{array};q^{2}X\right)

We use parameters
a=q^{2\alpha}
and
b=q^{2\beta}
, and this becomes in our notations

j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)=j_{n}(X;q^{2\alpha},q^{2\beta};q)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{[-n]_{k}[n+\alpha+\beta+1]_{k}}{[\alpha+1]_{k}}q^{k(\beta+1)}\frac{X^{k}}{[k]!}.

(3.1)

Remark 3.1
.

Taking the limit
q=1
in (
3.1
), we get the classical Jacobi polynomials with parameters
\alpha,\beta
.
∎

The little
q
-Jacobi polynomials are eigenvectors of the
q
-difference operators
\Theta_{X}
given by

\Theta f(X)=\frac{q^{-\alpha-\beta-1}}{(q-q^{-1})^{2}}\left(q^{2\alpha}(q^{2(\beta+1)}X-1)\frac{f(q^{2}X)-f(X)}{X}-(X-1)\frac{f(X)-f(q^{-2}X)}{X}\right).

(3.2)

Namely, we have (see
[
12
]
):

\Theta j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)=[\alpha+\beta+n+1][n]j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)\ .

(3.3)

We will need the following alternative formula for the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials. As we will see in the proof, it follows from the Rodrigues formula.

Proposition 3.2
.

The little
q
-Jacobi polynomials satisfy the following formula:

j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)=[\beta+1]_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{k}\frac{q^{k(\beta+\alpha+1+k)}}{[\alpha+1]_{k}[\beta+1]_{n-k}}\prod_{s=0}^{n-k-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{k}.

(3.4)

Proof.

According to
[
12
]
, the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials satisfy the following Rodrigues formula:

w(X;\alpha,\beta)j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)=c_{n}(\alpha)\left(D_{q^{-2}}\right)^{n}w(X;\alpha+n;\beta+n),

(3.5)

with

w(X;\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{2(i+1)}X}{1-q^{2(\beta+i+1)}X}X^{\alpha}\,,\text{ and }c_{n}(\alpha)=\frac{q^{n(\alpha+\frac{n-1}{2})}}{[\alpha+1]_{n}}.

(3.6)

The operator
D_{q^{-2}}
satisfies the following
q
-Leibniz formula:

D_{q^{-2}}^{n}(fg)(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{-k(n-k)}f^{(k)}(q^{-2(n-k)}X)g^{(n-k)}(X)\ ,

where we use here and below the notation
f^{(k)}=D_{q^{-2}}^{k}(f)
and
g^{(n-k)}=D_{q^{-2}}^{n-k}(g)
.

Introduce
f_{n}(X)=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-q^{2(i+1)}X}{1-q^{2(\beta+n+i+1)}X}
, and
g_{n}(X)=X^{\alpha+n}
so that
w(X;\alpha+n;\beta+n)=f_{n}(X)g_{n}(X)
.
Note that

f_{0}(q^{-2(n-k)}X)=\prod_{s=0}^{n-k-1}\frac{1-q^{-2s}X}{1-q^{2(\beta-s)}X}f_{0}(X)\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ f_{n-k}(X)=\prod_{s=1}^{n-k}(1-q^{2(\beta+s)}X)f_{0}(X)\ .

Combining the two equalities and simplifying, we get:

f_{n-k}(q^{-2(n-k)}X)=\prod_{s=0}^{n-k-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)f_{0}(X)\ .

(3.7)

Now a direct calculation shows that:

D_{q^{-2}}(f_{n})(X)=-q^{\beta+n+1}[\beta+n]f_{n-1}(X)\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ D_{q^{-2}}(g_{n})(X)=q^{-\alpha-n+1}[\alpha+n]g_{n-1}(X)\ .

An easy induction on
k
using also (
3.7
) gives

\displaystyle f_{n}^{(k)}(q^{-2(n-k)}X)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}q^{k(\beta+n+1)-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\frac{[\beta+1]_{n}}{[\beta+1]_{n-k}}\prod_{s=0}^{n-k-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)f_{0}(X),

\displaystyle g_{n}^{(n-k)}(X)

\displaystyle=q^{-\alpha(n-k)-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\frac{[\alpha+1]_{n}}{[\alpha+1]_{k}}X^{k}g_{0}(X).

Thus, using the
q
-Leibniz formula in the form given above, we get:

D_{q^{-2}}^{n}(f_{n}g_{n})(X)=f_{0}(X)g_{0}(X)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{n-k}\frac{[\alpha+1]_{n}[\beta+1]_{n}}{[\alpha+1]_{k}[\beta+1]_{n-k}}\\
\times q^{k(\alpha+\beta+k+1)-\alpha n-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\prod_{s=0}^{n-k-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{k}.

Multiplying by
c_{n}(\alpha)=q^{\alpha n+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}[\alpha+1]_{n}^{-1}
and dividing by
w(X;\alpha,\beta)=f_{0}(X)g_{0}(X)
, we use Rodrigues formula (
3.5
) to obtain the desired formula for
j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Exchanging the role of
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
in the proof, one gets another formula for the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials:

j_{n}^{\alpha,\beta}(X)=[\beta+1]_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{[\alpha+1]_{k}[\beta+1]_{n-k}}q^{k(\beta-\alpha-k+1)}\prod_{s=1}^{n-k}(1-q^{2(\beta+s)}X)X^{k}.

∎

3.2
Little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and lowest-weight vectors

Recall from Proposition
2.2
that the following polynomials in two
q
-commuting variables
x,y
were found to be the lowest-weight vectors in the tensor product
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
:

P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)=[\lambda]_{n}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{k}q^{k(\lambda^{\prime}+2n-k-1)}\frac{x^{k}y^{n-k}}{[\lambda]_{k}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n-k}}.

(3.8)

Our goal now is to make the connection with the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials.

Remark 3.4
.

When
q=1
, we have the following relations between the Jacobi polynomials and these two-variable polynomials:

P^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}(x,y)=[\lambda]_{n}(x+y)^{n}j^{\alpha,\beta}_{n}(\frac{x}{x+y})\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ j^{\alpha,\beta}_{n}(X)=\frac{1}{[\lambda]_{n}}P^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}(X,X-1)\ ,

(3.9)

where
\alpha+1=\lambda
and
\beta+1=\lambda^{\prime}
. Another way of expressing this is through the change of variables
t=x+y
and
X=\frac{x}{x+y}
. This change of variables identifies
\mathbb{C}[x,y]
and
\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
, and in this case it is an algebra isomorphism. In the
q
-deformed setting, we can only have a linear isomorphism between
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
and
\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
, and this is what we find below.
∎

The linear isomorphism
\phi
.

In order to generalise Relations (
3.9
) to the
q
-deformed setting, we are going to construct a linear isomorphism between the quantum plane
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
and the subspace
\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
of polynomials in two commuting variables
t,X
. We define a linear map
\phi:\mathbb{C}[t,tX]\to\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
by:

\phi\bigl(t^{n}(tX)^{m}\bigr)=(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{n}x^{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{(k+\lambda+2m)(n-k)}x^{k+m}y^{n-k}\ .

(3.10)

For the second equality we used the
q
-binomial formula for variables
x,y
satisfying
yx=q^{2}xy
:

(x+y)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{k(n-k)}x^{k}y^{n-k}\ .

(3.11)

Lemma 3.5
.

The map
\phi
is invertible and its inverse is given by :

\phi^{-1}(x^{k}y^{p})=q^{-p(\lambda+2k)}t^{k+p}\left(\prod_{s=0}^{p-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)\right)X^{k}\ .

(3.12)

Proof.

We prove by induction on
p
that

\phi\left(t^{k+p}\left(\prod_{s=0}^{p-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)\right)X^{k}\right)=q^{p\lambda}y^{p}x^{k}.

(3.13)

The case
p=0
is clear. Moreover, note that
\phi(tQ)=(x+q^{\lambda}y)\phi(Q)
for any
Q\in\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
. Using this remark and the induction hypothesis, we make the following calculation:

\displaystyle\phi\left(t^{k+1+p}\prod_{s=0}^{p}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi\left(t^{k+1+p}\prod_{s=0}^{p-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{k}\right)-q^{-2p}\phi\left(t^{k+1+p}\prod_{s=0}^{p-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=q^{p\lambda}(x+q^{\lambda}y)y^{p}x^{k}-q^{-2p}q^{p\lambda}y^{p}x^{k+1}=q^{(p+1)\lambda}y^{p+1}x^{k}.

Formula (
3.12
) follows from (
3.13
) and
yx=q^{2}xy
.
∎

We are ready to state the main property of the map
\phi
, providing the link between the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and the decomposition of the tensor product
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
-representations. Below, through the map
\phi
(more precisely, its inverse
\phi^{-1}
), the tensor product is identified to a space of polynomials in two variables
t
and
tX
as follows:

V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\,\cong\,\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]\,\cong\,\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi^{-1}}}{{\cong}}\,\mathbb{C}[t,tX]\ .

Corollary 3.6
.

The map
\phi
satisfies:

\phi\ :\ [\lambda]_{n}t^{n+k}j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{n}(X)\ \mapsto\ q^{n\lambda}(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{k}P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)\ .

The vector space decomposition

\mathbb{C}[t,tX]\simeq\bigoplus_{n}\mathbb{C}[t]t^{n}j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{n}(X)

gives the decomposition of
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
into a direct sum of subrepresentations
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
.

Proof.

Using the formula in Proposition
3.2
together with Lemma
3.5
, it is follows directly that
\phi
sends
[\lambda]_{n}t^{n}j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{n}(X)
to
q^{n\lambda}P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)
. Then we use that, from the definition of
\phi
we have
\phi(t^{k}Q)=(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{k}\phi(Q)
for any
Q\in\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
.

The decomposition of the representation is the direct translation into the
(t,X)
-picture of Proposition
2.3
giving the decomposition in the
(x,y)
-picture.
∎

As a consequence, we can also express the intertwining operator (
i.e.
the holographic operator) from Corollary
2.5
in the
(t,X)
-picture.

Corollary 3.7
.

The map

\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\ :\ \mathbb{C}[z]\to\mathbb{C}[t,tX]\,,\ \ \ \ \ z^{k}\mapsto t^{k+n}j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{n}(X)

is, up to a scalar, the unique intertwining operator between
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
and
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
.

3.3
Action of the generators in the new variables

Now that we have translated, through the map
\phi
from (
3.10
), the representation of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
acting on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
into a representation on
\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
, we can calculate the action of the generators in the new model in
t
and
X
. The separation of variable mechanism is clearly apparent in the action of
\Delta(K)
and
\Delta(E)
below. Indeed the action reproduces on the variable
t
the original action (
2.2
) of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
on one-variable polynomials, without touching the variable
X
. There is only an additional term in
\Delta(F)
involving the
q
-difference operator having the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials as eigenvectors. Therefore, the action below is another way to formulate the decomposition of the tensor product formulated in Corollary
3.6
.

Proposition 3.8
.

For
P\in\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
we have:

\displaystyle\Delta(K)P(t,X)

\displaystyle=q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}}P(q^{2}t,X),

(3.14)

\displaystyle\Delta(E)P(t,X)

\displaystyle=tP(t,X).

(3.15)

\displaystyle\Delta(F)P(t,X)

\displaystyle=-\frac{q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}-1}(P(q^{2}t,X)-P(t,X))-q^{-\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}+1}(P(t,X)-P(q^{-2}t,X))}{(q-q^{-1})^{2}t}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{t}\Theta_{X}P(t,X)\,,

(3.16)

where
\Theta_{X}
means the operator
\Theta
defined in (
3.2
) applied to the variable
X
.

Note that the given action of
\Delta(F)
is well-defined on
\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
as will be seen during the proof.

Proof.

The verification for
K
and
E
is an immediate calculation using the formula in
(x,y)
found in (
2.4
)–(
2.6
) together with the definition of
\phi
in (
3.10
).

Let
n\geq 0
and set
v_{k}=t^{k+n}j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{n}(X)
for
k\geq 0
. From Corollary
3.7
, we know that
\{v_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
spans the copy of
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
in
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. Moreover, we have
\Delta(K)v_{k}=q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n+2k}v_{k}
and
\Delta(E)v_{k}=v_{k+1}
. Therefore, the action of
\Delta(F)
is uniquely fixed by the relations of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
and, as in (
2.3
), we have

\Delta(F)v_{k}=-[\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n+k-1][k]v_{k-1}\ .

(3.17)

To conclude the proof, we must only check that the right hand side of (
3.16
) reproduces the correct formula on the vectors
v_{k}
. Using the same formula (
2.3
) in
t
for the first term, and the eigenvalue equation (
3.2
) for
\Theta_{X}
, we find

(-[\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+n+k-1][n+k]+[\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+n-1][n])v_{k-1}.

(3.18)

Note that the parenthesis is
0
when
k=0
, which shows that the given action of
\Delta(F)
preserves
\mathbb{C}[t,tX]
as it should. Moreover, for arbitrary
k\geq 0
, it is easy to check that the parenthesis gives the value expected from (
3.17
).
∎

For later use, we also collect the action of the Casimir operator in the
(t,X)
-model. Up to normalisation and up to an additive constant, the element
\Delta(C)
acts only on
X
(and does not touch the variable
t
) and reproduces the operator
\Theta_{X}
having the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials as eigenvectors. This is once again making apparent the mechanism of separation of variables from
(x,y)
to
(t,X)
, and the fact that the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials in
X
provide the decomposition of the tensor product.

Corollary 3.9
.

In the variables
(t,X)
the action of the Casimir operator
C
(
2.1
) is explicitly given by:

\Delta(C)=(q-q^{-1})^{2}\Theta_{X}+q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}-1}+q^{-\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}+1}.

(3.19)

Note that the eigenvalue of
\Delta(C)
on
t^{k+n}j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{n}(X)
is
q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n-1}+q^{-\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-2n+1}
which is the expected eigenvalue on
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
.

4
Little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and Clebsch–Gordan coefficients

The purpose of this section is to formulate the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition in terms of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials. As is well known, the
q
-Hahn polynomials appear as Clebsch–Gordan coefficients for
U_{q}(sl_{2})
. The formulas relating the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and the
q
-Hahn polynomials that will be obtained in this section are

(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{N-k}P^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{k}(x,y)=[\lambda]_{k}q^{-\lambda k}\sum_{l=0}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}N\\
l\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{(\lambda+l)(N-l)}Q_{k}^{(N)}(q^{-2l})x^{l}y^{N-l}\,,\ \ \ \ \ \forall k\leq N\,,

(4.1)

j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{k}(X)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}N\\
l\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{-l(N-l)}Q_{k}^{(N)}(q^{-2l})\prod_{s=0}^{N-l-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{l}\,,\ \ \ \ \ \forall k\leq N\ .

(4.2)

where the polynomials
Q_{k}^{(N)}
appearing in the sums are the
q
-Hahn polynomials. The first formula is written in the
(x,y)
-picture (that is, it is valid in the quantum plane where
x
and
y

q
-commute) while the second one is given in the commuting
(t,X)
-picture. They are equivalent through the linear isomorphism
\phi
from the preceding section.

4.1
Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and
q
-Hahn polynomials

We briefly recall how the
q
-Hahn polynomials appear as Clebsch–Gordan coefficients of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
(see
[
4
,
13
,
19
]
) and sketch the construction and the use in this context of the
q
-Hahn algebra
[
5
]
(see also
[
20
]
). In this section, we assume that
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\notin\mathbb{Z}
to avoid possible cancellations in the
q
-Hahn polynomials.

The
q
-Hahn polynomials.

Fix a positive integer
N>0
. Following
[
12
]
with
q
replaced by
q^{2}
, the q-Hahn polynomials are defined by:

\displaystyle Q^{(N)}_{k}(q^{-2x})

\displaystyle={}_{3}\phi_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}q^{-2k},\,abq^{2(k+1)},\,q^{-2x}\\
aq^{2},\,q^{-2N}\end{array};q^{2}\right)\ \ \ \text{for $k=0,\dots,N$}.

We use parameters
a=q^{2\alpha}
and
b=q^{2\beta}
, with
\alpha,\beta\notin\mathbb{Z}
, and this becomes with our notations

Q^{(N)}_{k}(q^{-2x})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\begin{bmatrix}k\\
i\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{i(\beta+1+N-x)}(-1)^{i}\frac{[k+\alpha+\beta+1]_{i}[-x]_{i}}{[\alpha+1]_{i}[-N]_{i}}.

(4.3)

Seeing them as polynomials in
X=q^{-2x}
, the
q
-Hahn polynomials satisfy the following
q
-difference equation, for
k=0,\dots,N
,

(q^{-2k}+abq^{2k+2})Q^{(N)}_{k}(X)=B(X)Q^{(N)}_{k}(q^{-2}X)+M(X)Q^{(N)}_{k}(X)+D(X)Q^{(N)}_{k}(q^{2}X)\,,

(4.4)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}B(X)=(1-q^{-2N}X^{-1})(1-aq^{2}X^{-1}),\ \ \ \ D(X)=aq^{2}(1-X^{-1})(b-q^{-2(N+1)}X^{-1}),\\[5.0pt]
M(X)=-B(X)-D(X)+1+abq^{2}\ .\end{array}

They also satisfy a three-term recurrence relation of the form

XQ^{(N)}_{k}(X)=A_{k}Q^{(N)}_{k+1}(X)+N_{k}Q^{(N)}_{k}(X)+C_{k}Q^{(N)}_{k-1}(X)\,,\ \ \ \ k=0,\dots,N-1\ ,

(4.5)

where
C_{0}=0
. We will not need the explicit values of the coefficients
A_{k},C_{k},N_{k}
(see
[
12
]
).

The
q
-Hahn algebra.

The
q
-Hahn algebra controls the bispectral properties of the
q
-Hahn polynomials. It is obtained by setting

U=X\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ V=B(X)T_{q^{-2}}+M(X)+D(X)T_{q^{2}}\,,

where we denote
T_{q^{\pm 2}}
the operators acting as
(T_{q^{\pm 2}}f)(x)=f(q^{\pm 2}x)
. So
U
is the left hand side of the recurrence relation (
4.5
) while
V
is the
q
-difference operator in (
4.4
). A straightforward calculation shows that the following relations are satisfied:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\frac{[V,U]_{q}}{q-q^{-1}}=W\,,\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{[U,W]_{q}}{q-q^{-1}}=x_{1}U-aq^{-2N}(1+q^{2})\ ,\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{[W,V]_{q}}{q-q^{-1}}=x_{1}V+ab(1+q^{2})^{2}U-a(1+q^{2})x_{2}\ .\end{array}

(4.6)

where the
q
-commutator is
[A,B]_{q}=qAB-q^{-1}BA
and with

x_{1}=q^{-2N}(1+a+aq^{2+2N}+abq^{2+2N})\,,\ \ \ \ x_{2}=q^{-2N}(1+b+bq^{2+2N}+abq^{2+2N})\ .

Note that the first relation defines
W
in terms of
U
and
V
.

The
q
-Hahn polynomials as transition coefficients.

The exact same line of arguments as in
[
16
, §3]
for example (where the classical version (
q=1
) is explained) results in the following connection between the
q
-Hahn algebra and the
q
-Hahn polynomials. We skip the details and only give the results.

Assume that we have two linear operators
U,V
on an
(N+1)
-dimensional space such that:

Sp(U)=\{q^{-2l}\}_{l=0,\dots,N}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \text{$U,V$ satisfy the $q$-Hahn algebra.}

Then as a consequence we can deduce that
V
is diagonalisable with

Sp(V)=\{q^{-2k}+abq^{2k+2}\}_{k=0,\dots,N}\ ,

and moreover that
U,V
form a Leonard pair in the sense that
V
is tridiagonal in the eigenbasis of
U
and
U
is tridiagonal in the eigenbasis of
V
. Finally, and most importantly for us, the transition coefficients between the two eigenbases are given in terms of the
q
-Hahn polynomials. Namely we have:

w_{k}=\sum_{l=0}^{N}Q^{(N)}_{k}(q^{-2l})v_{l}\,,\ \ \ \ \ k,l=0,\dots,N,

with
\{v_{l}\}_{l=0,\dots,N}
,
\{w_{k}\}_{k=0,\dots,N}
suitably normalised eigenbases of, respectively,
U
and
V
.

Clebsch–Gordan coefficients of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
.

From now on, we identify the parameters
a
and
b
of the
q
-Hahn algebra in terms of our parameters
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
as follows:

a=q^{2(\lambda-1)}\,,\ \ \ b=q^{2(\lambda^{\prime}-1)}\ .

(4.7)

It turns out that the
q
-Hahn algebra appears in
U_{q}(sl_{2})\otimes U_{q}(sl_{2})
. More precisely, we consider the following two elements of
U_{q}(sl_{2})\otimes U_{q}(sl_{2})
:

U=q^{\lambda}K^{-1}\otimes 1\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ V=q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}-1}\Delta(C)\ .

(4.8)

The normalisation factors are chosen such that in the tensor product:

V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cong\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2k}\ ,

the spectra of
U
and
V
are the correct ones with respect to the
q
-Hahn algebra, namely,

Sp(U)=\{q^{-2l}\}_{l=0,\dots,N}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ Sp(V)=\{q^{-2k}+q^{2\lambda+2\lambda^{\prime}+2k-2}\}_{k=0,\dots,N}\ ,

when restricted to the eigenspace of
\Delta(K)=K\otimes K
with eigenvalue
q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2N}
.

Now a straightforward calculation shows that the elements
U
and
V
defined in (
4.8
) satisfy the relations (
4.6
) of the
q
-Hahn algebra, again when restricted to the eigenspace of
\Delta(K)=K\otimes K
with eigenvalue
q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2N}
(see
[
5
]
). These calculations can be done directly in
U_{q}(sl_{2})\otimes U_{q}(sl_{2})
with the following elements (which commute with
U
and
V
) specialised as follows:

C\otimes 1\mapsto q^{1-\lambda}+q^{\lambda-1}\,,\ \ \ \ 1\otimes C\mapsto q^{1-\lambda^{\prime}}+q^{\lambda^{\prime}-1}\,,\ \ \ \ \ \Delta(K)\mapsto q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2N}\ .

Therefore, we deduce that the eigenbases of
U
and
V
are related by the
q
-Hahn polynomials as explained just before.

For a given
N
, that is for the given eigenvalue
q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2N}
of
K\otimes K
, the eigenbases of
U
and
V
are respectively,

\{v^{\lambda}_{l}\otimes v^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{N-l}\}_{l=0,\dots,N}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \{v^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2k}_{N-k}\}_{k=0,\dots,N}\ ,

(4.9)

where
v^{\lambda}_{i}
are the standard basis vectors, as in (
2.3
) for the Verma module
V_{\lambda}
of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
.

The connection coefficients between these two bases are called the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, and therefore we have explained how the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
are given in terms of the
q
-Hahn polynomials. We will give more explicit formulas in the next section.

4.2
Little
q
-Jacobi polynomials and
q
-Hahn polynomials

The two bases involved in the Clebsch–Gordan problem for
U_{q}(sl_{2})
, see (
4.9
), are in our realisation on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
:

\{x^{l}y^{N-l}\}_{l=0,\dots,N}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \{(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{N-k}P_{k}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y)\}_{k=0,\dots,N}\ .

Indeed both bases are in the eigenspace of
\Delta(K)
with eigenvalue
q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2N}
as required, and the first basis is the eigenbasis of
K\otimes 1
while the second basis is the eigenbasis of
\Delta(C)
. For the second basis, this follows from the results in Section
2
.

From the discussion in the preceding subsection, we know that these two bases are related by the
q
-Hahn polynomials. We obtain the explicit formula, for all
k\leq N
,

(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{N-k}P^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{k}(x,y)=[\lambda]_{k}q^{-\lambda k}\sum_{l=0}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}N\\
l\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{(\lambda+l)(N-l)}Q_{k}^{(N)}(q^{-2l})x^{l}y^{N-l}\ .

(4.10)

Note that we had to fix the normalisation factors in front of both sets of vectors. This is easily done by considering the case
k=0
and then the coefficient in front of
y^{N}
. We skip the details.

Through our linear isomorphism
\phi
from Section
3
, we can transport these equalities in the
(t,X)
-picture. The variable
t
disappears and we get:

j^{\lambda-1,\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{k}(X)=\sum_{l=0}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}N\\
l\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{-l(N-l)}Q_{k}^{(N)}(q^{-2l})\prod_{s=0}^{N-l-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{l}\,,\ \ \ \ \ \forall k\leq N\ .

(4.11)

Note that for
k=N
, this reproduces in particular the formula found in Proposition
3.2
, proving in passing that:

Q^{(N)}_{N}(q^{-2l})=(-1)^{l}q^{l(\alpha+\beta+N+1)}\frac{[\beta+1]_{N}}{[\alpha+1]_{N}[\beta+1]_{N-l}}\ .

4.3
The
q
-Hahn algebra from the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials

For convenience for the reader specialised in orthogonal polynomials, we switch back to the usual parameters
a=q^{2\alpha}
and
b=q^{2\beta}
of the
q
-Hahn polynomials (
\alpha+1=\lambda
and
\beta+1=\lambda^{\prime}
).

We have explained above that the two elements
U=q^{\alpha+1}K^{-1}\otimes 1
and
V=q^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Delta(C)
of (a certain specialisation of)
U_{q}(sl_{2})\otimes U_{q}(sl_{2})
satisfy the
q
-Hahn algebra. Here we use our model with the variables
(t,X)
for the tensor product of
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
to deduce a more explicit realisation of the
q
-Hahn algebra, involving the
q
-difference operator of the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials.

First, we recall from Corollary
3.9
that the diagonal Casimir element
\Delta(C)
acts in our model with variables
(t,X)
only on the
X
variable as

\Delta(C)=(q-q^{-1})^{2}\Theta_{X}+(q^{\alpha+\beta+1}+q^{-\alpha-\beta-1}).

where
\Theta_{X}
is the
q
-difference operator having the little
q
-Jacobi polynomials as eigenvectors. It can be written as:

q^{\alpha+\beta+1}(q-q^{-1})^{2}\Theta_{X}=q^{2\alpha}(q^{2(\beta+1)}X-1)\frac{T_{q^{2}}-1}{X}-(X-1)\frac{1-T_{q^{-2}}}{X}\,,

where we denote
T_{q^{\pm 2}}
the operator acting as
(T_{q^{\pm 2}}f)(x)=f(q^{\pm 2}x)
.

Thus we only need to supplement the explicit action of
\Delta(C)
with the action of
q^{\alpha+1}K^{-1}\otimes 1
. This is naturally given in the
(x,y)
-picture, but the next result gives its action in the
(t,X)
-picture.

Proposition 4.1
.

The following elements satisfy the relations (
4.6
) of the
q
-Hahn algebra:

U=q^{-2N}X+(1-X)T_{q^{-2}}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ V=q^{\alpha+\beta+1}(q-q^{-1})^{2}\Theta_{X}+q^{2\alpha+2\beta+2}+1\ .

Proof.

We already know that the formula for
V
gives indeed the action of
q^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Delta(C)
. So we only need to prove that the formula for
U
gives the action of
q^{\lambda}K^{-1}\otimes 1
, when restricted to the eigenspace of
K\otimes K
for the eigenvalue
q^{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2N}
.

This eigenspace is spanned, in the
(x,y)
-picture by
x^{l}y^{N-l}
, with
l=0,\dots,N
. In the
(t,X)
-picture, using Lemma
3.5
, these vectors become proportional to:

t^{N}\prod_{s=0}^{N-l-1}(1-q^{-2s}X)X^{l}\ .

Now it is easy to check that the operator
U
given in the proposition acts diagonally on these vectors, with eigenvalues
q^{-2l}
. Therefore, it coincides with the action of
=q^{\lambda}K^{-1}\otimes 1
, as required.
∎

5
Intertwining operators and
q
-Rankin–Cohen brackets

Using the same ideas as in
[
17
]
, we will construct a
q
-analog of the Rankin–Cohen brackets by constructing explicitly the
U_{q}(sl_{2})
-intertwining operators between the contragredient representations
\left(V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{\star}
and
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}^{\star}
. To do this we will identify the dual space
V_{\lambda}^{\star}
with
V_{\lambda}
via the choice of an inner product on
V_{\lambda}
. Once an inner product is fixed, the action of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
for the contragredient representation
V_{\lambda}^{\star}
is given for
X\in U_{q}(sl_{2})
by

\pi_{\lambda}^{\star}(X)=\pi_{\lambda}\bigl(S(X)\bigr)^{\dagger}\,,

where
\pi_{\lambda}
is the original action on
V_{\lambda}
,
S
is the antipode and
.^{\dagger}
denotes the adjoint for the chosen inner product.

Contragredient representations.

On
V_{\lambda}\simeq\mathbb{C}[z]
we define the
q
-Fischer product by:

\langle P,Q\rangle=P(D_{q^{2}})\overline{Q}|_{z=0}\ .

Through all this section we will assume that
q
is a strictly positive real number to ensure that this formula defines an inner product on
\mathbb{C}[x]
. We recall that
D_{q^{2}}(z^{n})=q^{(n-1)}[n]z^{n-1}
, and so we have on monomials

\langle z^{n},z^{m}\rangle=q^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}[n]!\delta_{n,m}.

Remark 5.1
.

This inner product is such that the multiplication by
z
and the
q
-derivative
D_{q^{2}}
are adjoint to each other, i.e.
D_{q^{2}}^{\dagger}=m(z)
.
∎

With the identification of
V_{\lambda}^{\star}
with
\mathbb{C}[z]
made using the inner product above, the action of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
on
V_{\lambda}^{\star}
is

\displaystyle KP(z)=q^{-\lambda}P(q^{-2}z),

\displaystyle Kz^{n}=q^{-\lambda-2n}z^{n},

(5.1)

\displaystyle EP(z)=-q^{-\lambda-2}D_{q^{2}}P(q^{-2}z),

or

\displaystyle Ez^{n}=-[n]q^{-\lambda-n-1}z^{n-1},

(5.2)

\displaystyle FP(z)=q^{\lambda+2}z\frac{q^{\lambda}P(q^{2}z)-q^{-\lambda}P(z)}{q-q^{-1}},

\displaystyle Fz^{n}=[\lambda+n]q^{\lambda+n+2}z^{n+1}.

(5.3)

Note that
V_{\lambda}^{\star}
is a highest-weight Verma module of weight
-\lambda
.

The dual space
(V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{\star}
of the tensor product is identified with
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
using the product of the two inner products i.e.

\langle P_{1}\otimes Q_{1},P_{2}\otimes Q_{2}\rangle=\langle P_{1},P_{2}\rangle\langle Q_{1},Q_{2}\rangle.

As a representation of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
, we have

(V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{\star}\simeq V_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\star}\otimes V_{\lambda}^{\star}\,

(5.4)

with the trivial isomorphism, where the action of
U_{q}(sl_{2})
on
V_{\lambda}
and
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
is given in (
5.1
)–(
5.3
), and the tensor product is made with the coproduct
\Delta
from Section
2
.

q
-Rankin–Cohen brackets.

We define the
q
-analogue of the Rankin–Cohen brackets as

\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}=\left(\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}\ :\ (V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{\star}\to V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}^{\star}

(5.5)

Using the identifications explained above via the inner products, we can see the operator
\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}
as follows

\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}\ :\ \mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]\to\mathbb{C}[z]\ .

We are now ready to state our final result which gives an explicit formula for the
q
-analogue of the Rankin–Cohen brackets. We recover below formulas similar to the ones found in
[
19
]
where isomorphic representations are used. The main new feature here is to see these operators as adjoints to the operators
\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

The map
\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}
is, up to a scalar, the unique intertwining operator between
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\star}\otimes V_{\lambda}^{\star}
and
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}^{\star}
, and is given,
for
f\otimes g\in\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
, by

\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}(f\otimes g)(z)=[\lambda]_{n}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}(-1)^{k}q^{k(\lambda^{\prime}+2n-k-1)}\frac{f^{(k)}(z)g^{(n-k)}(q^{\lambda+2k}z)}{[\lambda]_{k}[\lambda^{\prime}]_{n-k}},

(5.6)

where
f^{(k)}=D_{q^{2}}^{k}f
and
g^{(k)}=D_{q^{2}}^{k}g
.

Proof.

Since
\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
is an intertwining operator between
V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}
and
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
according to Corollary
2.5
, then it is well-known and straightforward to check that its adjoint is an intertwining operator between the corresponding contragredient representations. The unicity is also immediate. To obtain its explicit expression first notice that

\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}=r_{P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}}\circ ev.

(5.7)

where the polynomials
P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
were given in Proposition
2.2
, the map
ev
is given by

ev\ :\ \mathbb{C}[z]\ni Q\mapsto Q(x+q^{\lambda}y)\in\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]\ ,

and for any element
P\in\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
, the operator
r_{P}
denotes the right multiplication by
P
in
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
. We thus have

\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}=\left(\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}\ :\ (V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{\star}\to V_{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+2n}^{\star}=ev^{\dagger}\circ(r_{P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}})^{\dagger}\ .

(5.8)

The calculations of the adjoints is an exercise in linear algebra. To avoid confusion, we go back to the notation
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
instead of
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
since we consider only the vector space structure here.

Let us treat the map
ev
first, whose explicit expression is:

ev\ :\ z^{n}\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{n}\begin{bmatrix}n\\
k\end{bmatrix}_{q}q^{(k+\lambda)(n-k)}x^{k}\otimes y^{n-k},

We have expanded
(x+q^{\lambda}y)^{n}
using the
q
-commutation relation
yx=q^{2}xy
, and identified
x^{n}y^{m}
with
x^{n}\otimes y^{m}
. A direct computation on the basis
x^{n}\otimes y^{m}
proves that

ev^{\dagger}\ :\ x^{n}\otimes y^{m}\mapsto q^{\lambda m}z^{n+m}\,,\ \ \ \quad\text{or equivalently,}\quad\ \ ev^{\dagger}(P\otimes Q)(z)=P(z)Q(q^{\lambda}z)\ .

(5.9)

To calculate the second adjoint in (
5.8
), note first that

r_{P}^{\dagger}=P(r_{x}^{\dagger},r_{y}^{\dagger}).

for any polynomial
P
. Indeed, for
P=x^{k}y^{l}
, we have
r_{P}=r_{y}^{l}\circ r_{x}^{k}
, and the result follows since the adjoint reverses the composition.

Therefore it remains only to calculate
r^{\dagger}_{x}
and
r^{\dagger}_{y}
. Explicitly, the linear maps on
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
are given by

r_{y}(x^{n}\otimes y^{m})=x^{n}\otimes y^{m+1}\ \ \ \quad\text{and}\quad\ \ \ r_{x}(x^{n}\otimes y^{m})=q^{2m}x^{n+1}\otimes y^{m}.

A direct computation shows that:

r_{x}^{\dagger}\ :\ x^{n}\otimes y^{m}\mapsto q^{2m+n-1}[n]x^{n-1}\otimes y^{m}\ \ \quad\text{and}\quad\ \ r_{x}^{\dagger}\ :\ x^{n}\otimes y^{m}\mapsto q^{m-1}[m]x^{n}\otimes y^{m-1}\ ,

or equivalently, for
P\otimes Q\in\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]

r_{x}^{\dagger}(P\otimes Q)(x,y)=D_{q^{2}}P(x)\otimes Q(q^{2}y)\ \ \quad\text{and}\quad\ \ r_{y}^{\dagger}(P\otimes Q)(x,y)=P(x)\otimes D_{q^{2}}Q(y).

(5.10)

Finally, one gets formula (
5.6
) by using the explicit
expansions of the polynomials
P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}
in the
x^{n}y^{m}
basis and using Formulas (
5.10
) and (
5.9
).
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In the limit
q\to 1
, for which the
q
-derivative
D_{q^{2}}
becomes the usual derivative, the operators
\operatorname{q-RC}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}_{n}
become the classical Rankin–Cohen brackets
[
17
]
.
∎

Adjoints and symbols.

A crucial step of the calculation above is when calculating the adjoint of the operator denoted
r_{P}
, which is the right multiplication in
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
by a polynomial
P(x,y)
. As mentioned during the proof, the action of
r^{\dagger}_{P}
is given on
\mathbb{C}[x]\otimes\mathbb{C}[y]
by:

r^{\dagger}_{P}=P(r_{x}^{\dagger},r_{y}^{\dagger})\ .

(5.11)

So in words, the action of
r_{P}^{\dagger}
is obtained by taking
P
and replacing
x
by
r_{x}^{\dagger}
and
y
by
r_{y}^{\dagger}
. The fact expressed in (
5.11
) is valid whatever the inner product is, but of course the explicit expressions for
r_{x}^{\dagger}
and
r_{y}^{\dagger}
depend on the chosen inner product.

With our inner product, moving between a polynomial
P(x,y)
and the operator
P(r_{x}^{\dagger},r_{y}^{\dagger})
is a
q
-deformation of the notion of symbol of a differential operator. Indeed, when
q=1
, then
r_{x}^{\dagger}
becomes the usual derivative with respect to
x
and similarly for
y
, namely, we have

(q=1)\qquad\ \ \ \ \ r^{\dagger}_{P}=P\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)\ .

For the
q
-deformed setting, denote again
T_{q^{2}}
the operator on
\mathbb{C}[x]
given by
T_{q^{2}}f(x)=f(q^{2}x)
. Then the explicit formula for
r_{P}^{\dagger}
is:

r_{P}^{\dagger}=P(D_{q^{2}}\otimes T_{q^{2}},1\otimes D_{q^{2}}).

(5.12)

Note that this is well-defined in the sense that we evaluate
P\in\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
in two operators which
q
-commute, i.e.

(1\otimes D_{q^{2}})(D_{q^{2}}\otimes T_{q^{2}})=q^{2}(D_{q^{2}}\otimes T_{q^{2}})(1\otimes D_{q^{2}})\ .

Remark 5.4
.

We insist that all the above, including the explicit formula (
5.6
) depends on the choice of inner products on the various spaces under consideration. To generalise naturally the case
q=1
, we require that these inner products go when
q=1
to the usual Fischer inner product for which the multiplication by
x
is adjoint to the derivative.

In the following, we mention another interesting choice of inner product.
On the one-variable polynomial space, we choose the same inner product:

\langle P(z),Q(z)\rangle=\left.P(D_{q^{2}})\overline{Q}\right|_{z=0}

However on the quantum plane
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
we define the following inner product:

\langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle^{\prime}=\left.P(D_{q^{2},x},D_{q^{2},y})\overline{Q(x,y)}\right|_{x=y=0}\,,

where we define operators
D_{q^{2},x}
and

D_{q^{2},y}
on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
as follows

D_{q^{2},x}x^{n}y^{m}=q^{n-1}[n]x^{n-1}y^{m}\ \ \ \quad\text{and}\quad\ \ \ D_{q^{2},y}x^{n}y^{m}=q^{m-1-2n}[m]x^{n}y^{m-1}\ .

(5.13)

These formulas says that
D_{q^{2},x}
and
D_{q^{2},y}
act as usual on their respective variables. However the action of
D_{q^{2},y}
on
x^{n}y^{m}
is understood as follows: first move the
y^{m}
to the left, then differentiate and then move back the remaining
y^{m-1}
to the right using the
q
-commutation relation.

A word of warning here: this inner product is not the same as the product of one-variable inner products. In particular, the isomorphism relating the
U_{q}(sl_{2})
-representations
(V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{\star}
and
V_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\star}\otimes V_{\lambda}^{\star}
will not be the trivial isomorphism (but will be a renormalisation of the product basis of
V_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda^{\prime}}
).

One motivation to consider this alternative is that for
P\in\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
we can interpret the symbol of
r_{P}^{\dagger}
in a similar fashion as in the classical setting since now we will have:

r_{P}^{\dagger}=P(D_{q^{2},x},D_{q^{2},y}).

This formula may look more natural than (
5.12
) but one has to remember to act on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
according to Formulas (
5.13
). Note again that the evaluation above in
r_{P}^{\dagger}
is well-defined since
D_{q^{2},x}
and
D_{q^{2},y}

q
-commute when acting on
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
.

These choices lead to an operator
\left(\Psi_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{\dagger}
acting on the quantum plane
\mathbb{C}_{q}[x,y]
with value in
\mathbb{C}[z]
whose explicit formula will be different from (
5.6
) since different identifications are made. We leave the interested reader to work out the explicit formula.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

As a final remark, we note that several possibilities exist for the
q
-derivative. We have chosen to work with
D_{q^{2}}
but one could have made a different choice, such as
D_{q^{-2}}
or
D_{q}
. The resulting explicit formulas will differ by powers of
q
.
∎
