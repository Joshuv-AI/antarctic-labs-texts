Learned Finite Element-based Regularization of the Inverse Problem in Electrocardiographic Imaging
Manuel Haas, Thomas Grandits, Thomas Pinetz, Thomas Beiert, Simone Pezzuto, Alexander Effland

Abstract
Electrocardiographic imaging (ECGI) seeks to reconstruct cardiac electrical activity from body-surface potentials noninvasively. However, the associated inverse problem is severely ill-posed and requires robust regularization. While classical approaches primarily employ spatial smoothing, the temporal structure of cardiac dynamics remains underexploited despite its physiological relevance. We introduce a space–time regularization framework that couples spatial regularization with a learned temporal Fields-of-Experts (FoE) prior to capture complex spatiotemporal activation patterns. We derive a finite element discretization on unstructured cardiac surface meshes, prove Mosco-convergence, and develop a scalable optimization algorithm capable of handling the FoE term. Numerical experiments on synthetic epicardial data demonstrate improved denoising and inverse reconstructions compared to handcrafted spatiotemporal methods, yielding solutions that are both robust to noise and physiologically plausible.

Introduction

The heart is an electromechanical pump essential to sustaining life. Abnormal electrical activation may lead to life-threatening arrhythmias, which must be promptly diagnosed to prevent further damage.
In electrocardiography, electrical potentials recorded on the chest surface are linked to the underlying cardiac electrical activity and can therefore provide insight into arrhythmic mechanisms and guide therapeutic decisions
[
24
]
.
From a mathematical perspective, reconstructing cardiac potentials from body-surface measurements leads to the inverse problem of electrocardiography
[
5
,
6
]
. This inverse problem is severely ill-posed, and its difficulty is further exacerbated by noise and measurement artifacts arising from imperfect electrode contact and anatomical constraints
[
1
]
. Consequently, the choice of regularization is critical for obtaining physiologically meaningful reconstructions.

A broad range of regularization techniques has been investigated to stabilize the reconstructions
[
17
]
, ranging from classical Tikhonov methods
[
31
]
to total variation
[
28
]
approaches.
While spatial regularization has been widely studied, the temporal dimension in medical imaging remains underutilized, despite its importance for capturing complex spatiotemporal activation patterns, such as the wave-like propagation observed in cardiac electrical potentials
[
15
,
22
]
.
Deep learning techniques demonstrate good accuracy in inverse problem reconstruction
[
30
]
, and optimization-based approaches in machine learning frameworks have been explored for patient-specific cardiac modeling
[
14
]
, but provide limited interpretability.

Recently, machine-learned priors such as Fields-of-Experts (FoE)
[
7
,
13
,
27
]
and variational deep regularizers
[
18
]
have demonstrated strong performance in modeling higher-order dependencies in image reconstruction tasks and retain interpretability, yet their integration with PDE-constrained inverse problems in finite element frameworks remains largely unexplored.
In this work, we propose a novel class of learned finite element regularizers that combine spatial total variation–type terms with a data-driven temporal FoE prior.
Both the cardiac inverse problem and the proposed regularization are discretized within a finite element framework
[
3
,
4
,
29
,
32
]
.

Beyond modeling, our main theoretical contribution is the first Mosco-convergence analysis for inverse problems regularized by learned FoE-type energies in finite element spaces, proving convergence of minimizers from the discrete to the continuous problem on realistic domains
[
8
,
20
,
21
]
.
Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed learning–analysis framework produces reconstructions that are both more accurate and more physiologically plausible than those obtained by classical non-learning-based methods.
The paper is structured as follows: we first introduce a variational model with learned spatiotemporal regularization, then present its finite element discretization on unstructured cardiac meshes, establish Mosco-convergence and convergence of minimizers, develop a scalable convergent optimization algorithm, and finally validate the approach with numerical experiments on synthetic 2D data.

2
Methods

We begin by introducing the forward and inverse problems in electrocardiographic imaging (ECGI), along with the FoE-based regularization.

2.1
Forward Problem

The forward problem consists of computing body-surface potentials resulting from a given epicardial potential, obtained by transferring the epicardial potential through the torso volume conductor via a linear forward operator.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
denote the torso domain with outer boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
.
The body–surface electrodes are represented by a subset
\Sigma\subset\Gamma
, consisting of a disjoint union of
N_{\Sigma}
open and bounded patches
\Sigma=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\Sigma}}\Sigma_{i}
for each electrode, where measurements
z
are available.
The epicardial surface of the heart is denoted by
\Gamma_{H}=\partial\Omega_{H}
, where
\Omega_{H}\subset\Omega
is the heart domain.
We define the torso domain excluding the heart as
\Omega_{0}=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{H}
.
Throughout, we assume that the domain
\Omega_{0}
possesses a
C^{2}
boundary, and that both
\Gamma_{H}
and
\Gamma
are closed and compact disjunct boundary manifolds.
For a time interval
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq(0,\widetilde{t})
with
\widetilde{t}>0
and the epicardium potential function
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}))
, the well-posed elliptic forward problem is defined for almost every
t\in\mathbb{T}

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}\left(\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v(\mathbf{x},t)\right)&=0,&&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0},\\
\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}&=0,&&\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma,\\
v(\mathbf{x},t)&=u(\mathbf{x},t),&&\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma_{H},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1)

for
\mathbf{n}
the outward unit normal vector and
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}_{+})
the torso conductivity tensor or bulk conductivity.
Here, we assume the space-dependent matrix
\sigma
to be symmetric satisfying the ellipticity condition
\zeta^{-1}|\mathbf{y}|^{2}\leq\sigma(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{y}\leq\zeta|\mathbf{y}|^{2}
for some
\zeta>0
and all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The existence, uniqueness, and regularity of a solution follow
[
10
]
.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a unique solution
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))
of (
1
) for
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}))
in a weak sense.

Proof.

For a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
, the surjectivity of the trace operator from
H^{1}(\Omega_{0})
to
H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})
yields a function
g(\cdot,t)\in H^{1}(\Omega_{0})
with
g|_{\Gamma_{H}}=u(\cdot,t)
and

\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C_{E}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})}.

Writing
v(\cdot,t)=g(\cdot,t)+w(\cdot,t)
with
w(\cdot,t)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{0})
reduces (
1
) to homogeneous boundary conditions.
Defining
a(\phi,\varphi):=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\sigma\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
with
\phi,\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{0})
, which is continuous and coercive, the Lax–Milgram theorem gives for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
a unique
w(\cdot,t)\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{0})
solving

a(w(\cdot,t),\varphi)=-a(g(\cdot,t),\varphi)\quad\forall\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{0}),

(2)

with
\|w(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{0})}
.
Hence
v(\cdot,t)
satisfies the weak formulation of (
1
) and
\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})}
. Integration over
\mathbb{T}
yields the claim, and uniqueness follows from coercivity.
∎

Let
v_{u}(\cdot,t)
be the weak solution associated with the boundary data
u(\cdot,t)
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
We define the operator

A_{\mathbb{T}}:H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})\to H^{1/2}(\Gamma),\quad\text{with}\quad A_{\mathbb{T}}[u(\cdot,t)]\coloneqq v_{u}(\cdot,t)|_{\Gamma}

as the solution operator concatenated with the trace operator for the torso boundary.
Therefore, the forward operator is a bounded linear operator, and since
u(\cdot,t):\mathbb{T}\to H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})
is strongly measurable, the composition
A_{\mathbb{T}}[u(\cdot,t)]
is strongly measurable (because
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
is separable), ensuring boundedness by

\left\lVert A_{\mathbb{T}}[u(\cdot,t)]\right\rVert_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\lVert v(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega_{0})}\leq\widetilde{C}\left\lVert u(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})}.

By integrating over time, we define the linear forward operator

A:L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}))\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1/2}(\Gamma)),\quad u\mapsto v_{u}|_{\Gamma}.

Note that the inverse of the forward operator is, in general, unbounded.

2.2
Inverse Problem

For the inverse problem in electrocardiography, we compare to a time series of measurements
z\in(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}))^{N_{\Sigma}}
for each electrode
i=1,\ldots,N_{\Sigma}
defined by the average integral
z_{i}(t)=\,\mathchoice{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\displaystyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\textstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.72743pt\hskip-1.72743pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.72743pt\hskip-2.45831pt\raisebox{-1.50694pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.45831pt\hskip 1.72743pt}\kern-6.78813pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.57988pt\hskip-1.57988pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.57988pt\hskip-2.29166pt\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.29166pt\hskip 1.57988pt}\kern-6.49301pt}\int_{\Sigma_{i}}\widetilde{z}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}S^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x})
of a potential function
\widetilde{z}\in L^{2}(\Sigma\times\mathbb{T})
with the
d-1
-dimensional surface measure
S^{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
.
Since the measurements are defined electrode-wise as scalars, we minimize the cost functional

\argmin_{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}))}\left\{G(u,z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2N_{\Sigma}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\Sigma}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\,\mathchoice{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\displaystyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\textstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.72743pt\hskip-1.72743pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.72743pt\hskip-2.45831pt\raisebox{-1.50694pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.45831pt\hskip 1.72743pt}\kern-6.78813pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.57988pt\hskip-1.57988pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.57988pt\hskip-2.29166pt\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.29166pt\hskip 1.57988pt}\kern-6.49301pt}\int_{\Sigma_{i}}A[u](\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}S^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x})-z_{i}(t)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right\}.

Due to the ill-posedness of the problem, we follow the standard approach in inverse problem theory and introduce a regularization term.
The inverse problem is formulated as the minimization of an energy functional
consisting of a data fidelity term and a regularization term.
For a given measurement
z\in(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}))^{N_{\Sigma}}
, we seek an epicardial potential
u
that minimizes

\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\left\{\mathcal{J}(u,z)\coloneqq G(u,z)+R_{\theta}(u)\right\},

(3)

over the space
\mathcal{V}\coloneqq L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}))
.
The choice of
\mathcal{V}
as space of solutions follows from the continous embedding
H^{1}(\Gamma_{H})\hookrightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H})
on compact
C^{2}
manifolds, hence
\mathcal{V}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}))
.
The norm of the Bochner space
\mathcal{V}
is defined as

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert u(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{H})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}(u(\mathbf{x},t))^{2}+|\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

with surface gradient
\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}
for any
v:\Gamma_{H}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})\otimes\mathrm{d}\lambda(t)
with
S
the
(d-1)
-dimensional surface measure of
\Gamma_{H}
and
\lambda
the
1
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Moreover, we define the space
\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq L^{2}(\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T})
to allow for more convenient notation of norms.

2.3
Spatiotemporal Regularizer

Standard regularization techniques such as Tikhonov
[
31
]
and total variation
[
28
]
are general-purpose approaches that effectively mitigate measurement noise. Here, we aim to design a problem-specific regularizer that embeds data-driven knowledge of the underlying cardiac dynamics while remaining mathematically well-posed and interpretable.

To this end, we adopt the FoE framework, originally developed for imaging, in which, instead of prescribing a fixed penalty, one learns a collection of experts
[
7
,
13
,
27
]
. Each expert
i=1,\dots,N_{C}
is defined by a bounded linear operator
L_{i}
acting on the epicardial potential and an associated nonlinear potential function
\phi_{i}
. The operator response
L_{i}[u]
is penalized pointwise in space and time via
\phi_{i}
. Overall, the regularizer in a continuous spatiotemporal formulation reads as

R_{\theta}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{C}}\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}\phi_{i}\left(L_{i}[u](\mathbf{x},t)\right)\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t).

(4)

In canonical FoE, the operators
L_{i}
are chosen as convolutions with learned kernels, mirroring convolutional layers in neural networks.
However, spatiotemporal convolutions on unstructured finite element grids are computationally expensive due to their irregular structure, whereas temporal convolutions on a typically uniform time grid are straightforward to compute.
We therefore propose a multivariate operator that separates spatial differentiation from temporal filtering while still allowing joint, learned coupling of their responses.
In our formulation, the operators
L_{i}
consist of three components: a zero-order term
\epsilon_{\theta}u
ensuring stability, the surface gradient
\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u
imposing spatial regularity, and a temporally filtered component
K_{i}[u]
such that

L_{i}[u](\mathbf{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\quad\text{with}\quad L_{i}[u]\coloneqq(\epsilon_{\theta}u,\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u,K_{i}[u]),

(5)

where
\epsilon_{\theta}>0
is a learned scalar and
K_{i}
is realised as a bounded temporal cross correlation with a 1D kernel
k_{i}\in L^{1}(\widetilde{\mathbb{T}})
with
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}=(-\omega/2,\omega/2)
for
\omega>0
applied to the zero-extended signal
\widetilde{u}

K_{i}[u]\coloneqq(k_{i}\ast_{\mathbb{T}}u)(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{T}}(t)\int_{\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}}k_{i}(\tau)\,\widetilde{u}(\mathbf{x},\tau+t)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad\widetilde{u}=u\;\text{on}\;\mathbb{T},\;\widetilde{u}=0\;\text{otherwise}.

We adapt the multivariate setting introduced in
[
7
]
such that the corresponding potentials
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\to\mathbb{R}
penalize the joint response of the components in (
5
).
Hence, (
4
) becomes

R_{\theta}(u)=\lambda_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{C}}\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}\phi_{i}\left(\epsilon_{\theta}u(\mathbf{x},t),\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u(\mathbf{x},t),(k_{i}\ast_{\mathbb{T}}u)(\mathbf{x},t)\right)\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t),

(6)

with weighting parameter
\lambda_{\theta}>0
.
The filters
k_{i}
and parameters of
\phi_{i}
are learned from data, jointly with
\epsilon_{\theta}
and
\lambda_{\theta}
; the spatial operator
\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}
is fixed by the geometry.
Following
[
7
]
, to construct flexible and well-behaved multivariate potentials, we rely on the concept of the Moreau envelope
[
19
]
.
Given a proper, lower semi-continuous, convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\to\mathbb{R}
and a parameter
\mu>0
, its Moreau envelope is defined as
M_{\mu}f(\mathbf{x})=\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}\left\{f(\mathbf{z})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}
.
The Moreau envelope can be viewed as a smooth approximation of
f
that preserves key properties of the original function while ensuring differentiability and well-behaved gradients. In particular, its gradient is directly related to the proximal operator of
f
,
\nabla M_{\mu}f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\mu}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathrm{prox}_{\mu f}(\mathbf{x})\right)
, which is non-expansive, implying Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla M_{\mu}f
, and computationally efficient to evaluate for many choices of
f
. These properties make the Moreau envelope particularly suitable for designing potentials in optimization-based inverse problems, where stable and efficient gradient computation is crucial.
Building on this idea, we define our multivariate potential functions as differences of Moreau envelopes of the
\ell^{\infty}
norm, combined with linear transformations and a zero-order Tikhonov term. For each operator
L_{i}
, the potential function is

\phi_{i}(\mathbf{y})=\mu_{i}\,\omega_{\mu_{i}}^{d+2}(\mathbf{y})-\mu_{i}\,\omega_{\eta_{i}\mu_{i}}^{d+2}(\mathbf{Q}_{i}\mathbf{y}),

(7)

with learnable matrices
\mathbf{Q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+2)\times(d+2)}
and scalars
\eta_{i}
, where

\omega_{\mu}^{d+2}(\mathbf{y})=\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mu\,\mathrm{Proj}_{B_{\ell^{1}}}\!\left(\mathbf{y}/\mu\right)\right\|_{\infty}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\mathrm{Proj}_{B_{\ell^{1}}}\!\left(\mathbf{y}/\mu\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon_{\omega}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{y}/\mu\right\|_{2}^{2},

and

\mathrm{prox}_{\iota_{B_{\ell^{1}}}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathrm{Proj}_{B_{\ell^{1}}}(\mathbf{y})=\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in B_{\ell^{1}}}\{\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\},

the projection onto the
\ell^{1}
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
for indicator function of the
\ell^{1}
-ball
\iota_{B_{\ell^{1}}}
.
This construction ensures several desirable properties: it favors weak responses while increasingly penalizing strong ones, captures correlations across multiple components via
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
, and remains smooth and coercive due to the Moreau envelope and zero-order term.
The derivative of
\omega_{\mu}^{d+2}
,

\nabla\omega_{\mu}^{d+2}(\mathbf{y})=\mathrm{Proj}_{B_{\ell^{1}}}(\mathbf{y}/\mu)+\epsilon_{\omega}\mathbf{y}/\mu
,
is computationally efficient and non-expansive, ensuring stability in gradient-based optimization.

According to
[
7
, Theorem 1]
, the conditions
\|\mathbf{Q}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\eta_{i}>\|\mathbf{Q}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
guarantee that the potential
\phi_{i}
is nonnegative and attains a unique global minimum at the origin, which carries over naturally to our framework and ensures that the learned regularizer is bounded from below while favoring weak operator responses.
We also note that the regularization functional
R_{\theta}
is differentiable, allowing for efficient gradient-based optimization.
However, due to the negative part in (
7
), it is generally non-convex, reflecting the trade-off between expressivity and convexity.

3
Finite Element Discretization

In this section, we introduce the discrete domains, function spaces, and operators used throughout this work, constructed via the finite element method (FEM).

3.1
Space-Time Discretization

We define
\mathcal{T}_{h}
with largest element diameter
h>0
as the collection of surface elements discretizing
\Gamma_{H}
and, with a slight misuse of notation, the shape-regular adaptive conforming surface mesh approximating
\Gamma_{H}
.
Furthermore, we assume that the elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
are restricted to vertices lying on
\Gamma_{H}
.
Analogously, we discretize
\Gamma
and consistent as subsets
\Sigma_{i}
by

\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Sigma_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,N_{\Sigma}
.
The torso domain
\Omega_{0}
is discretized by a mesh

\mathcal{U}_{h}
, which is defined as a shape-regular volumetric discretization satisfying
\mathcal{T}_{h}\cup\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}=\partial\mathcal{U}_{h}
either consisting of triangles (
d=2)
or tetrahedra (
d=3
).

The temporal interval
\mathbb{T}
is discretized as a uniform temporal grid
\mathcal{S}_{h}
.
In particular, we assume that temporal measurements are available at
N_{\mathbb{T}}+1
equidistant time points
t_{s}=s\delta
for
s=0,\ldots,N_{\mathbb{T}}

which define
N_{\mathbb{T}}
time intervals of size
\delta>0
, with
\delta=O(h)
.

3.2
Function Spaces

We approximate the continuous space-time function space
\mathcal{V}
by the discrete space
\mathcal{V}_{h}
using
P_{1}
finite elements in space on the adaptive surface mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and in time on the temporal grid
\mathcal{S}_{h}
.
The spatial mesh is identical at each time step and consists of affine elements of dimension
d
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\Xi}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\subset\Gamma_{H}
denote the set of nodes associated with

\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
The tensor product of a spatial element
J\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and a temporal interval
Z\in\mathcal{S}_{h}

defines a space-time element
J\otimes Z\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\otimes\mathcal{S}_{h}
.
The resulting discrete space-time function space is

\mathcal{V}_{h}\coloneqq\Big\{v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h}))\colon v|_{J\otimes Z}\in P_{1}(J)\otimes P_{1}(Z),\forall J\in\mathcal{T}_{h},Z\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\Big\},

with spatial and temporal basis functions
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
and
\{\rho_{s}\}_{s=0}^{N_{\mathbb{T}}}
, respectively. Any discrete function
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
can be expressed as

u_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{s=0}^{N_{\mathbb{T}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\mathbf{u}_{i,s}\,\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{s}(t),

where
\mathbf{u}_{i,s}
is the value at node
(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},t_{s})
with
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{u}_{1,0},\mathbf{u}_{2,0},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{N_{\mathcal{V}},N_{\mathbb{T}}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}\times(N_{\mathbb{T}}+1)}
.
Next, we examine the function spaces induced by the linear operators
L_{i}
.
Since
P_{1}
functions are affine on each mesh element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
their spatial gradients are element-wise constant and belong to the broken
L^{2}
space

P_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega):v|_{T}\text{ is constant for all }T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

This defines a discrete gradient space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}
composed of vector-valued functions
p_{h}=(p_{h_{1}},\ldots,p_{h_{d}})^{\top}
with
p_{h_{k}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T})
for
k=1,\ldots d
, that are piecewise constant in space per element and affine in time:

\mathcal{Q}_{h}\coloneqq\Big\{p\in\left(L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T})\right)^{d}\colon p_{k}|_{J\otimes Z}\in P_{0}(J)\otimes P_{1}(Z),\forall L\in\mathcal{T}_{h},J\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\Big\}.

Here,
\{\vartheta_{l}\}_{l=1}^{N_{\mathcal{Q}}}
denotes the basis of
P_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
with
N_{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathbb{N}
.
The temporal cross-correlation in the regularizer function acts on functions
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
as

k_{h}\ast_{\mathbb{T}}\cdot\colon\mathcal{V}_{h}\to\mathcal{W}_{h}
,
where the cross-correlation of piecewise affine functions yields a piecewise cubic one

\mathcal{W}_{h}\coloneqq\Big\{w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T};H^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h}))\colon w|_{J\otimes Z}\in P_{1}(J)\otimes P_{3}(Z),\forall J\in\mathcal{T}_{h},Z\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\Big\}.

We approximate time-dependent functions using the standard piecewise linear hat functions
\rho_{i}
on the uniform temporal grid
t_{0},\dots,t_{N_{\mathbb{T}}}
with step size
\delta
, defined by
\rho_{i}(t)=\max\{0,1-|t-t_{i}|/\delta\}
for
i=0,\dots,N_{\mathbb{T}}
.
These functions naturally lead to the temporal mass matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}_{ij}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\rho_{i}(t)\rho_{j}(t)\,dt=\frac{\delta}{6}\begin{cases}4,&i=j,1\leq i\leq N_{\mathbb{T}}-1,\\
2,&i=j,i\in\{0,N_{\mathbb{T}}\},\\
1,&|i-j|=1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In space, we employ piecewise affine basis functions
\varphi_{i}
on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, leading to the spatial mass matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq N_{\mathcal{V}}.

To handle functions that depend on both space and time, we combine the temporal and spatial matrices using tensor products
\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{D}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{I}_{S+1}\otimes\mathbf{M}
.
This construction ensures that the resulting matrices correctly account for all degrees of freedom of the discrete solution
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
, linking each spatial node with every time step.
Finally, for vector-valued functions with
d
components, we define the standard
L^{2}
inner product on the space–time domain
\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}
by

(v_{h},w_{h})_{\mathcal{Y}_{h}^{d}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}v_{h,i}(\mathbf{x},t)\,w_{h,i}(\mathbf{x},t)\,d(\mathbf{x},t),

where
\mathcal{Y}_{h}:=L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T})
.
This inner product provides the natural framework for measuring errors and formulating the fully discrete variational problem.

3.3
Forward Operator

The discretization of the forward operator
A
in piecewise affine finite elements follows
[
32
]
.
For almost all

, we consider the discrete version of (
2
)

a_{h}(v_{h}(\cdot,t),\varphi_{h})=\int_{\mathcal{U}_{h}}\sigma_{h}(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{h}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0\quad\forall\varphi_{h}\in P_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{h}),\varphi_{h}|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=0

(8)

with boundary conditions on the epicardium given by

for some

.
Equivalently, we set
v_{h}=w_{h}+g_{h}
, where
w_{h}|_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=0
and

, leading to

a_{h}(w_{h}(\cdot,t),\varphi_{h})=-a_{h}(g_{h}(\cdot,t),\varphi_{h})\quad\forall\varphi_{h}\in P_{1}(\mathcal{U}_{h}),\ \varphi_{h}|{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=0.

(9)

By coercivity and continuity of
a
, for fixed boundary data
u_{h}
, (

) admits a unique solution
v_{h}
.
The discrete forward operator

is then defined by restricting

to the torso boundary, i.e.

In practice, for each timestep

from epicardium potentials to torso boundary.

3.4
Linear Operators in the Regularizer

The regularization functional involves linear operators
L[u]=(\epsilon_{\theta}u,\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u,K[u])
.
For the spatial gradient, we exploit the fact that piecewise affine functions on a manifold mesh admit the exact gradient representation

\left\lVert\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}_{h}^{d}}=\left\lVert\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}_{h}^{d}}

for
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
.
The restriction
u_{h}|_{J}
for
J\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
with nodes
(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})_{i=1}^{3}
is affine, and consequently, the elementwise constant Euclidean gradient can be written as

\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u_{h}|_{J}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}u_{h}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

where
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
are the local nodal basis functions.
The discrete gradient is defined by tangential projection,

\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}|_{J}\coloneqq(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{n}_{J}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{J})\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u_{h}|_{J}
,
where
\mathbf{n}_{J}
denotes the unit normal of
J
.
We denote by
\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\Gamma_{H}}
the matrix representation of
\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}
acting on the nodal values of piecewise affine functions.
By approximating
k\in L^{1}(\widetilde{\mathbb{T}})
with piecewise affine temporal finite elements
k_{h}\in P_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{h})
for a discretization
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{h}
using standard
L^{2}
-projection, we obtain the discrete operator
K_{h}
and a nodal vector
\mathbf{k}
.
The temporal kernel
k_{h}
is defined on an extended interval
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}=[t_{-N_{w}},t_{N_{w}}]
with
N_{w}\in\mathbb{N}
, while
u_{h}
is defined on
\mathbb{T}
.
The temporal cross-correlation
K_{h}[u_{h}](\mathbf{x},t)=(k_{h}\ast_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h})(\mathbf{x},t)
is discretized as

K_{h}[u_{h}](\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{T}}(t)\int_{\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}}k_{h}(\tau)u_{h}(\mathbf{x},\tau+t)\,d\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{l=0}^{N_{\mathbb{T}}}\sum_{j=1}^{2N_{w}}\mathbf{u}_{i,l}\mathbf{k}_{j}\mathbf{D}_{lj}(t)\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

Let
\mathbf{K}
denote the matrix of
K_{h}
.
The resulting discretized operator is

L_{h}:\mathcal{V}_{h}\to\mathcal{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{Q}_{h}\times\mathcal{W}_{h},\quad L_{h}[u_{h}]\coloneq(\epsilon_{\theta}u_{h},\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h},K_{h}[u_{h}]).

(10)

The projection onto the norm ball associated with the potential functions
\phi_{i}
in (
7
) is a pointwise operation and therefore requires all involved discrete quantities to be represented in the same finite element space.
However, the discrete operator
L_{h}
maps into a space of piecewise constant functions, which do not admit well-defined point values on element interfaces.
As a consequence, the projection cannot be applied directly.
We introduce an interpolation operator
\mathcal{P}_{h}:\mathcal{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{Q}_{h}\times\mathcal{W}_{h}\to(\mathcal{V}_{h})^{d+2}
such that
\widetilde{L}_{h}=\mathcal{P}_{h}\circ L_{h}
.
Note that the evaluation of the different dimensions in the linear operator in the same finite element space is not necessary for the squared
\ell^{2}
-norm part of
\phi_{i}
.
We define a variant
\widetilde{\phi}_{i}
of
\phi_{i}
with
\widetilde{\omega}_{\mu_{i}}^{d+2}
such that

\widetilde{\omega}_{\mu}^{d+2}(\mathbf{y},\widetilde{\mathbf{y}})=\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\mu\,\mathrm{Proj}_{B_{\ell^{1}}}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}/\mu\right)\right\|_{\infty}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\mathrm{Proj}_{B_{\ell^{1}}}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}/\mu\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon_{\omega}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{y}/\mu\right\|_{2}^{2},

(11)

accounting for the difference in
L_{h}
and
\widetilde{L}_{h}
.
We start with defining the interpolation operator
\mathcal{P}_{h}
.
The cross-correlation of two piecewise affine functions is piecewise cubic and globally continuous in the nodal basis.
To this end, we evaluate the cross-correlation at the temporal nodes of
\mathcal{V}_{h}
and introduce an interpolation operator

\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{temp}}:C^{0}(\overline{\mathbb{T}})\to P_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{h}),\quad\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{temp}}[u](t)=\sum_{s=0}^{N_{\mathbb{T}}}u(t_{s})\rho_{s}(t),

with continuity for piecewise affine interpolations
\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{temp}}[v]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}
for
v\in C^{0}(\overline{\mathbb{T}})

Remark that for any piecewise cubic function

]
by the boundedness of the second derivative of

\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{temp}}v_{h}-v_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert\partial_{tt}v_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

(12)

We further define a spatial interpolation operator

-projection onto the subspace of piecewise affine spatial functions, allowing for computation of the piecewise affine projection of piecewise constant functions.

This orthogonal projection is computed by its optimality condition

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\widetilde{p}_{h}(\mathbf{x})\varphi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}p_{h}(\mathbf{x})\varphi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\quad\forall\varphi\in P_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})

(13)

with

.
By choosing

as the basis functions

and

, we compute the nodal values

of the projection by

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}\widetilde{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{b}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{b}_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}p_{h}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{j\colon\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in J_{j}}\mathbf{p}_{j}\int_{J_{j}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\sum_{j\colon\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in J_{j}}\mathbf{p}_{j}\frac{|J_{j}|}{d}

with
\varphi_{i}
the basis function associated with node
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,N_{\mathcal{V}}

We define the matrix representations of
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{time}}
as
\mathbf{P}^{\text{sp}}
and
\mathbf{P}^{\text{time}}
, respectively.
These interpolations allow us to define

\widetilde{L}_{h}\colon\mathcal{V}_{h}\to\mathcal{V}_{h}^{d+2},\quad\widetilde{L}_{h}[u_{h}]\coloneq(\epsilon_{\theta}u_{h},\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h},\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{temp}}K_{h}[u_{h}]).

(14)

Since
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{temp}}K_{h}[u_{h}]
is piecewise affine in time, it suffices to evaluate it at the temporal nodes of
\mathcal{S}_{h}

\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{temp}}K_{h}[u_{h}](\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{s,l=0}^{N_{\mathbb{T}}}\sum_{j=1}^{2N_{w}}\mathbf{u}_{i,l}\mathbf{k}_{j}\mathbf{D}_{lj}(t_{s})\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{s}(t).

for integers

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(t_{s})_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{6}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2,&\text{if }j=(i-N_{w})-s,&\ i\in\{0,2N_{w}\}\text{ or }j\in\{0,N_{\mathbb{T}}\},\\

Following

]

We begin by introducing the bijective lifting from the discretized spatial domains to the continuous domain.
The normal projection
\mathbf{x}\mapsto p(\mathbf{x})
from a narrow band around
\partial\Omega_{0}
is then defined as the unique solution of

\mathbf{x}=p(\mathbf{x})+d(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{n}(p(\mathbf{x}))

with the distance function

d(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &-\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{0}}\left\{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\right\},&&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0},\\
&\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega_{0}}\left\{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\right\},&&\mathbf{x}\notin\overline{\Omega}_{0},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

and the normal to the boundary
\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}d(\mathbf{x})
for almost every

We assume that

is chosen small enough such that all vertices of

lie within a narrow band where the distance function and closest point projection are well defined.
Furthermore, the surface gradient of a function

\sup_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left\{|1-\nu_{h}|\right\}\leq ch\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}}\left\{|1-\nu_{h}^{\Gamma}|\right\}\leq ch.

(16)

As a result, we define a projection lifting a discretized signal to the continuous domain.

Definition 1

(Lift and inverse lift)
.

For a function
v_{h}:\mathcal{U}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
we define its
lift
by

v_{h}^{l}:=v_{h}\circ H_{h}^{-1}
on
\Omega_{0}
, and for
v:\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
its
inverse lift
by

v^{-l}:=v\circ H_{h}
on
\mathcal{U}_{h}
.
Analogously, for
u_{h}:\partial\mathcal{U}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
the lift
u_{h}^{l}:\partial\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

u_{h}^{l}(p(\mathbf{x})):=u_{h}(\mathbf{x})
, and for
u:\partial\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
the inverse lift by

u^{-l}(\mathbf{x}):=u(p(\mathbf{x}))
.

As a consequence of these definitions, functions and their lifts are comparable in the relevant Sobolev norms, see
[
9
, Proposition 4.9, 4.13]
.

Proposition 1

(Norm equivalence under lifting)
.

Let
v_{h}:\mathcal{U}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
and
u_{h}:\partial\mathcal{U}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
with lifts

v_{h}^{l}:\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
and
u_{h}^{l}:\partial\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then the
L^{2}
- and
H^{1}
-norms of
v_{h}
and
u_{h}
are equivalent to the corresponding norms of their lifts.
The equivalence constants are independent of the discretization parameter
h
.

4
Convergence

In this section, we prove the Mosco convergence of the finite element discretized energy to the corresponding continuous energy under the convex regularization framework, i.e., assuming that the potential functions
\phi_{i}
in (
7
) are convex for
i=1,\ldots,N_{C}
.
Moreover, we establish the convergence of minimizers.
The proof exploits some concepts of
[
21
]
.
We start by defining the topology for convergence.

Definition 2
.

(
\mathcal{Z}
-topology)
Let
\Gamma_{H}
be a surface with discretizations defined by
(\mathcal{T}_{h})_{h}
, and let
(\mathcal{U}_{h})_{h}
be a family of shape-regular bulk discretizations of
\Omega_{0}
.
We say that
(u_{h})_{h}
on
\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathcal{S}_{h}
converges weakly to
u\in\mathcal{V}
w.r.t. the
\mathcal{Z}
-topology denoted by
u_{h}\xrightharpoonup{\mathcal{Z}}u
if and only if the lifted functions satisfy
u_{h}^{l}\xrightharpoonup{\mathcal{V}}u
.
We say that
(u_{h})_{h}
converges strongly to
u
w.r.t. the
\mathcal{Z}
-topology
u_{h}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{Z}}u
if and only if
u_{h}^{l}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}}u
.

We recall the definition of Mosco convergence
[
20
]
.

Definition 3
.

(Mosco-convergence)
Functionals
\mathcal{J}_{h}:\mathcal{V}_{h}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
for
h>0
are said to converge to
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{V}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
in the sense of Mosco w.r.t. the
\mathcal{Z}
-topology if

1.

for every sequence
(u_{h})_{h}\subset\mathcal{V}_{h}
with
u_{h}\xrightharpoonup{\mathcal{Z}}u
the functional inequality
\mathcal{J}_{h}(u)\leq\liminf_{h\to 0}\mathcal{J}_{h}(u_{h})
holds true (’
liminf-inequality
’).

2.

for every
u\in\mathcal{V}
there exists a recovery sequence
(u_{h})_{h}\subset\mathcal{V}_{h}
such that
u_{h}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}}u
and
\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathcal{J}_{h}(u_{h})
(’
limsup-inequality
’).

If in
1.
the strong convergence
u_{h}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{V}}u
is required, then
\mathcal{J}_{h}
is said to
\Gamma
-converge to
\mathcal{J}
with respect to the
\mathcal{Z}
-topology.

First, we prove the convergence of the discrete operator
\widetilde{L}_{h}
defined in (
14
) to the continuous operator
L=(\epsilon_{\theta}u,\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u,K[u])
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
(u_{h})_{h}\subset\mathcal{V}_{h}
be a sequence whose lifts
u_{h}^{l}
converge weakly in
\mathcal{V}
to some
u\in\mathcal{V}
.
Assume that the kernels
k_{h}
converge strongly to
k
in
L^{1}(T)
.
Then the lifted operator

\widetilde{L}_{h}^{l}[u_{h}^{l}]\coloneqq\big(\epsilon_{\theta}u_{h}^{l},(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l},K_{h}[u_{h}^{l}]\big)

converges weakly in
\mathcal{Y}^{d+2}=\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}
to
L[u]
.
Moreover, if
u_{h}^{l}
converges strongly to
u
in
\mathcal{V}
, then
\widetilde{L}_{h}^{l}[u_{h}^{l}]
converges strongly to
L[u]
in
\mathcal{Y}^{d+2}
.

Proof.

Assume that
u_{h}^{l}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
\mathcal{V}
.
We prove the weak convergence of
\widetilde{L}_{h}^{l}[u_{h}^{l}]
componentwise in
\mathcal{Y}^{d+2}
.
For the first component, we have
\epsilon_{\theta}u_{h}^{l}\rightharpoonup\epsilon_{\theta}u\quad\text{in }\mathcal{Y}
,
which follows directly from the weak convergence of
u_{h}^{l}
in
\mathcal{V}
and the continuous embedding
\mathcal{V}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Y}
.
For the spatial gradient component, let
\varphi\in\mathcal{Y}^{d}
be an arbitrary test function and compute

\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}\cdot\varphi\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t)=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\varphi^{-l}\nu_{h}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t),

where
\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\mathrm{d}S_{h}(\mathbf{x})\otimes\mathrm{d}\lambda(t)
and the last equality follows from self-adjointness of the
L^{2}
-projection
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}
on
\mathcal{Y}_{h}^{d}
.
We then decompose the integrand as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\cdot\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\varphi^{-l}\nu_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\cdot\varphi^{-l}\nu_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\cdot(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\varphi^{-l}-\varphi^{-l})\nu_{h}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t).

For the first term, the weak convergence of the lifted discrete gradients,
(\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}\rightharpoonup\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u
in
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
, together with the change of variables from
\mathcal{T}_{h}
to
\Gamma_{H}
, gives

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\cdot\varphi^{-l}\nu_{h}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\to\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u\cdot\varphi\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t)

as
h\to 0
.
For the second term, we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality together with the uniform boundedness of
\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T})
such that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}\cdot(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\varphi^{-l}-\varphi^{-l})\nu_{h}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\,\min_{v_{h}^{l}\in(\mathcal{Y}_{h}^{l})^{d}}\left(\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}|v_{h}^{l}-\varphi|^{2}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\right)^{1/2}.

By the denseness in
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}
, this last term tends to
0
as
h\to 0
.
Hence we conclude that
(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}\rightharpoonup\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u\quad\text{in }\mathcal{Y}^{d}
.
For the last component,
K_{h}[u_{h}^{l}]
, we first show the weak convergence of the temporal cross-correlation
k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l}\rightharpoonup k*_{\mathbb{T}}u\quad\text{in }\mathcal{Y}
.
Define the flipped kernel functions
\widetilde{k}_{h}(t)\coloneqq k_{h}(-t)
and
\widetilde{k}(t)\coloneqq k(-t)
with
\widetilde{k}_{h}\to\widetilde{k}
strongly in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.
For any test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{Y}
, we decompose

(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l},\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}}=(u_{h}^{l},\widetilde{k}*_{\mathbb{T}}\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}}+(u_{h}^{l},(\widetilde{k}_{h}-\widetilde{k})*_{\mathbb{T}}\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}}.

The second term vanishes as
h\to 0
by Cauchy–Schwarz and Young’s inequality together with

\|\widetilde{k}_{h}-\widetilde{k}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\to 0
.
The first term converges to
(k*_{\mathbb{T}}u,\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}}
by weak convergence of
u_{h}^{l}
in
\mathcal{Y}
.
Including the temporal interpolation operator, we write

(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{temp}}(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l}),\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}}=(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l},\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}}+(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{temp}}(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})-k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l},\varphi)_{\mathcal{Y}},

where the additional term vanishes as
h\to 0
by (
12
).
The first term converges weakly as shown above.
Hence, we conclude
(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\mathrm{temp}}(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l}))\rightharpoonup k*_{\mathbb{T}}u
in
\mathcal{Y}
,
completing the proof for the temporal component and weak convergence.

Next, assume that
u_{h}^{l}\rightarrow u
strongly in
\mathcal{V}
.
We again prove the strong convergence of
\widetilde{L}_{h}^{l}[u_{h}^{l}]
componentwise in
\mathcal{Y}^{d+2}
.
For the first component, strong convergence follows directly from the assumption
\epsilon_{\theta}u_{h}^{l}\rightarrow\epsilon_{\theta}u
in
\mathcal{Y}
.
For the spatial gradient, we decompose the convergence error using an intermediate approximation
v_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
which is a piecewise affine function whose lift satisfies
v_{h}^{l}\rightarrow\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u
strongly in
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
.
The existence of such a sequence is guaranteed by the approximation property of
(\mathcal{Y}_{h}^{l})^{d}
.
We then write

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}-\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}-(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}v_{h})^{l}\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}

\displaystyle+\|(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}v_{h})^{l}-v_{h}^{l}\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}+\|v_{h}^{l}-\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}.

For the first term, lifting back to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and using the stability of
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}
gives

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}-(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}v_{h})^{l}\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\left|\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}(\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}-v_{h})\right|^{2}\nu_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{h}\times\mathbb{T}}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h}-v_{h}\right|^{2}\nu_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\Pi_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\leq\|(\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}-\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}^{2}+\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u-v_{h}^{l}\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}^{2},

which converges to
0
as
h\to 0
by the strong convergence assumption and the choice of
v_{h}
.
The second term vanishes exactly, since
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}v_{h}=v_{h}
.
The third term converges to
0
by construction of
v_{h}
.
Hence we conclude
(\mathcal{P}_{h}^{\text{sp}}\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}^{h}u_{h})^{l}\rightarrow\nabla_{\Gamma_{H}}u
strongly in
\mathcal{Y}^{d}
.
Finally, we prove the strong convergence of the temporal cross-correlation component.
We decompose the error as

\displaystyle\|K_{h}[u_{h}^{l}]-K[u]\|_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\leq\|K_{h}[u_{h}^{l}]-(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle+\|(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})-(k*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}+\|K[u_{h}^{l}]-K[u]\|_{\mathcal{Y}}.

For the first term, the temporal interpolation estimate (
12
) gives

\|K_{h}[u_{h}^{l}]-(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq Ch^{2}\|\partial_{tt}(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow 0.

The second term is bounded using Young’s inequality

\|(k_{h}*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})-(k*_{\mathbb{T}}u_{h}^{l})\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\|k_{h}-k\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\|u_{h}^{l}\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow 0

since
k_{h}\to k
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.
For the last term, Young’s inequality yields

\|K[u_{h}^{l}]-K[u]\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\|k\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\|u_{h}^{l}-u\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow 0

due to the strong convergence
u_{h}^{l}\rightarrow u
in
\mathcal{Y}
.
Combining these three estimates, we conclude
K_{h}[u_{h}^{l}]\rightarrow K[u]
strongly in
\mathcal{Y}
,
which completes the proof of strong convergence for the temporal cross-correlation and the strong convergence.
∎

.

).
Further details are provided in
[
7
]
.
The regularizer is trained using the loss

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa^{m}}}\|v_{h}^{m}-\prox_{R_{\theta,h}}(z_{h}^{m})\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{h}},

This results in a bi-level optimization problem: the inner problem optimizes
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
by minimizing the energy, while the outer problem updates the parameters
\theta
of the multivariate FoE regularizer (
6
). These parameters include the global weights
\lambda_{\theta}
and
\epsilon_{\theta}
, the temporal kernel functions
k_{i}
, and the parameters of the potential functions
\phi_{i}
, which are represented by a neural network.
We compute the prior via denoising because the proximal operator of a regularizer effectively encodes the typical structure of noise-free functions: by learning
R_{\theta}
such that
\prox_{R_{\theta}}(z_{h})
removes Gaussian noise, the regularizer captures the statistical properties of the clean functions
v_{h}^{m}
.
Once trained, the learned prior can be transferred to other inverse problems, providing robust guidance in situations where direct inversion is unstable.

For the optimization of the neural network parameters, we implement the model in PyTorch
[
23
]
and employ implicit differentiation to efficiently compute gradients through the equilibrium point defined by the model.
Specifically, we use the
torchdeq
library
[
12
]
, which provides scalable tools for deep equilibrium models, and adopt the Broyden algorithm as the fixed-point solver.
This quasi-Newton method enables fast and memory-efficient convergence to the fixed point without requiring explicit backpropagation through all iterative solver steps.
For optimizing the neural network parameters, we use the ADAM optimizer.
The training is performed on an NVIDIA A40 GPU.

6
Results

We train and benchmark learned regularizer approaches for denoising and the inverse problem on 2D simulations, comparing them with state-of-the-art handcrafted finite element regularizers, including spatiotemporal first-order Tikhonov and TV.

6.1
Dataset Generation

We simulate
1000
epicardial potential fields on a 2D torso–heart model
[
11
]
illustrated in
Figure

2
, which includes the lungs. The data are randomly divided into
80\%
for training,
10\%
for validation, and
10\%
for testing.
Synthetic data is generated by simulating cardiac activity on a finer 2D heart mesh with a finite element reaction–diffusion model and interpolating to the coarser torso-heart model via nearest-neighbor interpolation to avoid inverse crimes.
The transmembrane potential
v:\Omega_{0}\times T\to\mathbb{R}
is computed as proposed in
[
25
]

C_{m}\partial_{t}v-\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla(\mathbf{G}_{m}\nabla v)+I_{\mathrm{ion}}(v)=I_{\mathrm{stim}},\quad I_{\mathrm{stim}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\begin{cases}I_{\max}\mathbf{1}_{\text{stim}}(\mathbf{x}),&0\leq t<I_{\mathrm{dur}},\\
0,&t\geq I_{\mathrm{dur}},\end{cases}

with spatial indicator function for the randomly localised stimulus region
\mathbf{1}_{\text{stim}}
and element-wise anisotropic intra- and extracellular conductivities
\mathbf{G}_{i}
and
\mathbf{G}_{e}

\mathbf{G}_{\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha,t}\mathbf{I}+(\sigma_{\alpha,l}-\sigma_{\alpha,t})\mathbf{l}\otimes\mathbf{l},\quad\alpha\in\{i,e\},\quad\mathbf{G}_{m}=\mathbf{G}_{i}(\mathbf{G}_{i}+\mathbf{G}_{e})^{-1}\mathbf{G}_{e},

where
\mathbf{l}
is the local fiber direction, which in the heart model is taken to be circumferential around the ventricles, reflecting the typical orientation of myocardial fibers.
The conductivities are computed following
[
26
]
by fixing
\sigma_{i,l}
and determining the remaining components as functions of
\lambda_{\mathrm{LT}},\alpha
, and
\varepsilon
:

\sigma_{i,t}=\frac{\sigma_{i,l}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{LT}}^{2}}\left(\frac{1+\alpha(1-\varepsilon)}{1+\alpha}\right),\quad\sigma_{e,t}=\frac{\sigma_{i,t}}{\alpha(1-\varepsilon)},\quad\sigma_{e,l}=\frac{\sigma_{i,l}}{\alpha}.

With probability
1/3
, a randomly localized region of scar tissue with a prescribed radius is introduced by reducing the conductivity tensor
\mathbf{G}_{m}
by a factor drawn uniformly from
[0.05,0.25]
and with probability
1/6
, a second scar region is added.
Ionic currents follow a Nagumo-type cubic model without repolarization,

I_{\mathrm{ion}}(v)=g_{\max}(v-V_{\mathrm{rest}})(v-V_{\mathrm{th}})(v-V_{\mathrm{dep}}),

with fixed depolarization, resting, and threshold potentials.
Time integration uses backward Euler with
\Delta t
, yielding

\left(C_{m}\mathbf{M}+\frac{\Delta t}{\beta}\mathbf{K}_{m}\right)\mathbf{v}^{n+1}=\mathbf{C}\left(C_{m}\mathbf{v}^{n}-\Delta t(I_{\mathrm{ion}}\left(\mathbf{v}^{n}\right)-I_{\mathrm{stim}})\right),

where
\mathbf{K}_{m}
corresponds to the finite element discretization of

\nabla\cdot(G_{m}\nabla\,\cdot)
,
\mathbf{C}
is the associated mass matrix,
and
\mathbf{v}
contains the nodal values.
The extracellular potential
v_{e}
is computed via the pseudo bidomain model
[
2
]
for each
n_{\mathrm{sample}}
-th timestep

(\mathbf{K}_{i}+\mathbf{K}_{e}+\eta\mathbf{M})\mathbf{v}_{e}=-\mathbf{K}_{i}\mathbf{v}\quad\text{with}\quad\eta=10^{-9}.

6.2
Baseline Methods

As handcrafted baselines, we consider spatiotemporal first-order Tikhonov (
TIK
) regularization,

\widetilde{G}(u,z)+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\Lambda\nabla_{(\mathbf{x},t)}u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}^{d}}^{2},

where
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{\gamma},\ldots,\lambda_{\gamma},\lambda_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}_{+}
, and
\lambda_{\gamma}
and
\lambda_{t}
denote the spatial and temporal regularization parameters, respectively.
The optimization problem is solved by applying the conjugate gradient method to the optimality system.
In addition, we compare against isotropic spatiotemporal total variation (
TV
) regularization
[
15
]
,

\widetilde{G}(u,z)+\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T}}\left\lVert\Lambda\nabla_{(\mathbf{x},t)}u(\mathbf{x},t)\right\rVert_{2}\mathrm{d}\Pi(\mathbf{x},t),

which is minimized using a first-order primal–dual algorithm.
All baseline methods are computed using lumped mass matrices, as this significantly reduces computation time while producing visually and
L^{2}
-error-wise negligible differences.

6.3
Denoising

We first evaluate the FoE models on Gaussian denoising of epicardial potentials, where the ground truth (
GT
) functions are corrupted with Gaussian noise of standard deviation
\kappa

Finally, we evaluate the proposed spatiotemporal FoE–type regularizer in both a convex (
CMFoE
) and a non-convex (
MFoE
) formulation.
The convex version ensures theoretical guarantees and predictable behavior, while the non-convex version offers greater flexibility to capture complex signal correlations and potentially improve empirical performance.
The parameters used for tuning the models are the input noise level
\kappa
and the learned weighting parameter
\lambda_{\theta}
.
To increase the range of the filters, we compute each filter as a composition of three filters defined on a smaller interval, increasing the number of output channels for each cross-correlation, i.e.

for
k_{i}^{j}\in L^{1}(\widetilde{\mathbb{T}})
and
k_{i}^{j}=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}
.

6.4
Inverse Problem

We next consider the inverse problem in electrocardiographic imaging, again comparing

\mathbf{TV}

In contrast to the pure denoising setting, the inverse problem is severely ill-posed, and the reconstructions require substantially longer computation times due to the repeated solution of forward and adjoint problems within the optimization procedure.
The ill-posedness of the inverse problem is clearly reflected in the reconstruction quality.

\mathbf{TV}
regularization improves edge preservation and produces sharper spatial features, but may introduce artificial discontinuities, especially in regions with low signal-to-noise ratio.
The
\mathbf{MFoE}

Despite the increased difficulty of the inverse problem, it is able to capture relevant spatiotemporal activation patterns more accurately and to suppress noise and inversion artifacts more effectively than the classical approaches.
In particular, the learned temporal interactions help stabilize the reconstruction and reduce non-physical oscillations over time.
Importantly, although the inverse problem is more challenging than in the denoising case, the ranking of the regularizers in terms of performance remains the same.

7
Conclusion

We introduced a class of trained spatiotemporal regularizers for inverse problems on unstructured meshes and analyzed them within a rigorous variational framework.
We proved theorectical Mosco-convergence of the discrete functionals to their continuous limits, which implies convergence of minimizers and ensures stability under mesh refinement.
Numerical experiments on denoising and the inverse problem show that the proposed spatiotemporal regularizer consistently outperforms classical handcrafted approaches.
Although the inverse problem is severely ill-posed and reconstructions require longer computation times, the relative performance of the regularizers in contrast to each other remains unchanged.
The current implementation is limited to uniform timesteps, which restricts its applicability in problems requiring adaptive or variable temporal resolution.
Extending the method to variable timesteps would increase computational complexity.
Similarly, extending the model to learn kernel convolutions on spatial manifolds, analogous to temporal convolutions, is computationally demanding, thereby limiting the practicality of spatial regularization.
Overall, this work shows that data-driven regularization can be combined with strong analytical guarantees, yielding methods that are both theoretically sound and practically superior for challenging inverse problems such as ECGI.
