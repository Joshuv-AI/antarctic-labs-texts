Inter-Formula Variability in IOL Power Planning in Short and Long Eyes: A Retrospective Paired Comparison of Seven ESCRS-Hosted Formulas and the Ladas Super Formula 2.0
Authors: Michal Klimek, Andreas Földesi, Dejan Botic, Valerie Golsch, Rafah Khandour, Maximilian Tamberger, Agnes Boltz, Pia Veronika Vécsei-Marlovits
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo quantify the frequency, magnitude, and direction of inter-formula planning variability in intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation at the extremes of axial length, comparing seven European Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgeons (ESCRS)-hosted formulas and the Ladas Super Formula 2.0, and to identify preoperative predictors of clinically relevant disagreement.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center, paired within-eye planning study, the recommended IOL power and predicted spherical equivalent were generated for short (axial length <22 mm) and long (>26 mm) eyes from identical biometry (IOLMaster 700) across the seven formulas on three IOL platforms. A clinically relevant discrepancy (ClinRel) was defined as a within-eye range ≥1 step (≥0.5 D) in recommended power or ≥0.25 D in predicted spherical equivalent across the seven formulas. Firth-penalized logistic regression identified predictors in the long stratum.ResultsA total of 1020 eyes (551 short, 469 long) of 665 patients were analyzed. On the primary platform, ClinRel occurred in 98.2% of short and 62.7% of long eyes, reproduced on all three platforms and in one-eye-per-patient analyses. Per-formula mean signed differences vs Barrett Universal II were small (−0.11 to +0.16 D), yet the seven formulas diverged by ≥2 steps in 33.8% of short eyes. Hill-RBF 3.0 was out-of-bounds in ~30% of eyes; the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 (n = 311) was not a directional outlier. In long eyes, axial length was the strongest predictor (Firth odds ratio 1.55 per 1 SD; 95% CI 1.23–1.95), with no clean threshold (Youden axial length/corneal radius ratio 3.52; sensitivity 35.5%, specificity 96.0%).ConclusionsModern IOL power formulas frequently disagree on the recommended power in short and long eyes despite comparable published accuracy. Because postoperative outcomes were not assessed, these findings quantify planning-level variability only; disagreement should alert surgeons to planning uncertainty in these eyes.

Introduction

Cataract surgery is today as much a refractive as a rehabilitative procedure, and hitting the intended postoperative refraction is central to how patients judge its success.1 Yet even with current optical biometry and present-generation calculation formulas, barely three quarters of eyes (around 73%) land within ±0.50 D of the refractive target.1 Most of the residual error traces back to only a few contributors – chiefly the effective lens position, the determination of the postoperative refraction, and the axial length, which together explain on the order of 79% of the total prediction error.2 In line with this, inaccuracies in the measurement of axial length, keratometry, and anterior chamber depth have been estimated to account for approximately 36%, 22%, and 42% of the error in IOL power calculation, respectively.3 Because the weight of these factors grows as the eye departs from average dimensions, the short hyperopic and long myopic extremes of axial length are precisely where choosing the correct intraocular lens (IOL) power remains hardest.

At the short end (axial length ≤ approximately 22.0 mm), the very high implant powers required mean that even a small misestimate of the effective lens position is translated into a large refractive miss, so prediction errors are accordingly common.4,5 At the long end (axial length > 26.0 mm), the biometry-to-power conversion rests on assumptions that hold less well: implant powers may be very low or even negative, and a posterior staphyloma can distort the optically measured axial length, which historically left older formulas prone to hyperopic surprises.6,7 Addressing these shortcomings drove a newer generation of formulas – built on regression, ray tracing, refined theoretical optics, or artificial intelligence – engineered to stay accurate across the whole axial-length spectrum.8,9

Many of these engines are now openly available through the online IOL power calculator of the European Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgeons (ESCRS), which takes a single set of biometric inputs and displays seven contemporary formulas together – Barrett Universal II, Cooke K6, EVO 2.0, Hill-RBF 3.0, Hoffer QST, Kane, and PEARL-DGS.10 Separately, the Ladas Super Formula merged several classic formulas into one three-dimensional “super surface” and was subsequently sharpened by artificial intelligence into the Ladas Super Formula 2.0.9,11 Most head-to-head evaluations of these engines have looked almost only at postoperative refractive accuracy, where mean absolute prediction errors cluster in a similar 0.4–0.6 D range and the inter-formula differences are small, becoming visible mainly at the axial-length extremes.8,12

From the perspective of a high-volume cataract clinic, however, a complementary and pragmatically important question arises: how often, and by how much, do these formulas disagree on the recommended IOL power when supplied with identical biometric data – particularly in the short and long eyes in which accuracy is already most fragile? A formula output reflects formula-specific modeling choices – effective-lens-position estimation; vergence, ray-tracing, regression, or artificial-intelligence architectures; and rounding to the discrete 0.5 D manufacturing steps – and can therefore change which physical lens is ordered even when published accuracy is comparable; the ESCRS calculator now makes these disagreements visible at the point of care without guidance on which recommendation to trust.12,13

To our knowledge, no study has systematically quantified the frequency, magnitude, and direction of these IOL power disagreements among the ESCRS-hosted formulas and the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 using a paired within-eye design with standardized biometric inputs, specifically at the extremes of axial length where the clinical consequences of an incorrect power are largest. The purpose of this retrospective study was therefore to determine how frequently the recommended IOL power differs between these formulas in short (axial length < 22.0 mm) and long (axial length > 26.0 mm) eyes, to characterize the magnitude and direction of these discrepancies, and to identify the preoperative biometric factors – in particular axial length – that predict clinically relevant formula disagreement. Because eyes of average axial length were not included, the study characterizes disagreement within each extreme stratum rather than the excess disagreement relative to normal eyes.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with Good Clinical Practice, and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the City of Vienna (Ethikkommission der Stadt Wien; MA15-EK 26–094-VK). Because the work was retrospective and observational and used only fully anonymized routine clinical data, the committee waived the requirement for individual informed consent.

We carried out a single-center, retrospective, paired calculation study at the Department of Ophthalmology, Klinik Hietzing (Wiener Gesundheitsverbund), Vienna, Austria. Screening the department’s consecutive cataract surgical records from November 2022 to March 2026, we selected every eye at an axial-length extreme – short (< 22.0 mm) or long (> 26.0 mm) – that had the complete preoperative biometry needed to run all formulas. Eyes of average axial length were left out by design, since the aim was to characterize formula behavior precisely in the range where it is least predictable; accordingly, the study quantifies disagreement within each extreme stratum and does not estimate the excess disagreement attributable to extreme axial length relative to normal eyes. The two strata were kept separate throughout the analysis.

Eyes qualified when they met all of the following: (1) an axial length below 22 mm (short stratum) or above 26 mm (long stratum); (2) complete, good-quality optical biometry from the IOLMaster 700 (axial length, keratometry, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, central corneal thickness, and white-to-white diameter); and (3) sufficient data to run every study formula on identical inputs. We excluded eyes with previous corneal or intraocular surgery (uncomplicated intravitreal injections aside), with corneal pathology such as ectasia or appreciable scarring or with documented irregular astigmatism, and eyes whose measurements were incomplete or of insufficient quality for standardized calculation across all formulas.

A single investigator (M.K.) handled data extraction and management using a structured electronic case-report form with predefined fields for each biometric variable and each formula output. After entry, the source documents – IOLMaster 700 printouts and the calculator outputs archived when the data were collected – were checked back against the database, and the principal investigator (P.V.V.-M.) audited a random 10% of completed records against those source documents; no systematic discrepancies emerged.

For each eligible eye, the recommended IOL power and the predicted residual spherical equivalent were obtained for eight modern formulas under a single standardized set of inputs. Seven formulas were computed through the online IOL power calculator of the ESCRS,10 which returns all engines from one entry of identical biometry: the Barrett Universal II,14 Cooke K6,15 EVO 2.0,16 Hill-RBF 3.0,17 Hoffer QST,18 Kane,19 and PEARL-DGS.20 All calculations were performed between April and May 2026 using the calculator versions publicly hosted during that window; version identifiers, where displayed, were EVO 2.0, Hill-RBF 3.0, and Ladas Super Formula 2.0, and all calculator outputs were archived at the time of calculation. The eighth formula, the Ladas Super Formula 2.0,9,11 was computed separately through its dedicated online calculator. Because this required manual re-entry of the biometric data outside the ESCRS environment, it was obtained for consecutive blocks of the working dataset processed during dedicated data-entry sessions (n = 311; 170 short and 141 long eyes) rather than for the full cohort; this subset was not selected by any biometric, temporal, or clinical criterion. Its biometric comparability with the remaining cohort is reported in Supplementary Table S1. Each formula was evaluated on three IOL platforms with their respective optimized lens constants – the HOYA Vivinex XY1, the HOYA Vivinex Impress XY1-EM, and the Johnson & Johnson Tecnis DIB00 – using the constants recommended by the ESCRS calculator. These constants were applied unchanged, without local personalization, and were as follows. HOYA Vivinex XY1: A-constant 119.2126 (Barrett Universal II, EVO 2.0, Hill-RBF 3.0, Kane), 119.1939 (Cooke K6), 119.2 (PEARL-DGS), and Hoffer QST pACD 5.721. HOYA Vivinex Impress XY1-EM: A-constant 118.9134 (Barrett Universal II, EVO 2.0, Hill-RBF 3.0, Kane, PEARL-DGS), 118.8433 (Cooke K6), and Hoffer QST pACD 5.5042. Johnson & Johnson Tecnis DIB00: A-constant 119.2597 (Barrett Universal II, Hill-RBF 3.0, Kane), 119.3 (Cooke K6, EVO 2.0, PEARL-DGS), and Hoffer QST pACD 5.77. Identical axial length, keratometry, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, central corneal thickness, white-to-white distance, and a single fixed target refraction of −0.3 D were supplied to every formula, so that the only element differing between paired runs was the formula itself. The IOL platform actually implanted was not used as an analysis variable and is reserved for a planned prospective verification study. The seven ESCRS-hosted engines return the recommended power directly on the manufacturer’s 0.5 D step scale; the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 returns a continuous value, which was mapped to the nearest 0.5 D step before set-wide analysis. Recommendations were analyzed as returned by the calculators, without truncation to the commercially available power range of each platform. Hill-RBF 3.0 incorporates a boundary model that declines to return a value for eyes lying outside the limits of its training data; eyes for which Hill-RBF 3.0 returned no value were therefore classified as out-of-bounds.

The primary endpoint was the proportion of eyes with a clinically relevant discrepancy (ClinRel) in the recommended IOL power across the seven ESCRS formulas, defined a priori as a within-eye range of ≥ 1 step (≥ 0.5 D) in the recommended power and/or a range of ≥ 0.25 D in the predicted residual spherical equivalent. The set-wide metric required at least six of the seven formulas to return a valid result. The primary endpoint was reported separately for each stratum and for each of the three IOL platforms, and its consistency across platforms served as a robustness check. Secondary endpoints included the magnitude of the within-eye discrepancy (the step range and the residual-spherical-equivalent range), pairwise discrepancy rates between formula pairs, a paired directional comparison of each formula against the Barrett Universal II, used as a descriptive reference because it is the most widely adopted of the hosted formulas, and explicitly not as an accuracy benchmark, the out-of-bounds rate of each formula, the corresponding eight-formula analysis on the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 subset, and the preoperative biometric predictors of a clinically relevant discrepancy.

Statistical Analysis

For a single-proportion estimate at the 95% confidence level with a target confidence-interval half-width of ±5% points, the minimum required sample ranges from 288 eyes (anticipated proportion 0.75) to a worst-case 384 eyes (proportion 0.50); both strata exceeded the worst-case requirement. Proportions are given with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Because both eyes of some patients were eligible, between-eye dependence was addressed in five ways:21 the numbers of patients contributing one and two eyes are reported per stratum; fellow-eye concordance of the primary endpoint was quantified among bilateral patients (percentage agreement and Cohen’s kappa, complemented by the intraclass correlation of the within-eye power range); all primary proportions were re-estimated using one eye per patient (randomly selected with a fixed seed, and alternatively the right eye where available); cluster-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were computed with the patient as the resampling unit (2000 replicates); and the primary proportions were re-estimated with generalized estimating equations using a logit link and an exchangeable working correlation, with the patient as the clustering variable. The Firth regression was repeated in the one-eye-per-patient set and as a population-averaged model. To assess whether the range-based primary endpoint could be driven by a single outlying formula, a leave-one-formula-out analysis determined, for each eye, whether a clinically relevant discrepancy persisted after excluding the single most deviant formula. Preoperative predictors of ClinRel were sought with univariate and multivariable logistic regression, the candidate variables being axial length, mean keratometry, keratometric astigmatism, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, white-to-white diameter, central corneal thickness, and the axial-length/corneal-radius ratio. Because discrepancy in the short stratum sat near the ceiling, predictor modeling was restricted to the long stratum, where Firth-penalized logistic regression was applied to guard against separation.22 The link between the leading biometric predictor and ClinRel was characterized further with an empirical cut-off from Youden-index maximization. To capture the direction and size of formula disagreement, each formula was set against the Barrett Universal II reference using the scalar counterparts of the Alpins paired-planning framework:23 the signed power difference (the Magnitude-of-Error analogue, formula minus Barrett, in diopters), the formula-specific Bland–Altman bias with 95% limits of agreement for each formula–Barrett pair,24 and the share of eyes in which the formula recommended a higher, equal, or lower step. All tests were two-sided at α = 0.05, and analyses were run in R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria),25 using the logistf package for Firth regression.26

Results

A total of 1020 eyes (487 right, 533 left) of 665 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed, comprising 551 short eyes (axial length <22 mm) and 469 long eyes (axial length >26 mm). In the short stratum, 167 patients contributed one eye and 192 both eyes; in the long stratum, 147 and 161 patients, respectively (two patients contributed eyes to both strata). For every eye, all seven ESCRS-hosted formulas were computed from identical biometry on all three IOL platforms, with the lens-specific optimized constants and a single standardized target refraction applied uniformly across formulas and platforms; the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 was available for a subset of eyes (n = 311) and was analyzed separately. The HOYA Vivinex XY1 platform served as the reference environment for the primary analysis, with the HOYA Vivinex Impress XY1-EM and the Johnson & Johnson Tecnis DIB00 platforms reported in parallel.

Baseline demographic, biometric, and corneal characteristics are shown in Table 1. Patients in the short stratum were older than those in the long stratum (age at biometry 72.9 ± 9.4 vs 66.4 ± 11.5 years). By design, the two strata differed markedly in axial length (21.57 ± 0.38 vs 26.90 ± 0.86 mm) and in the axial length/corneal radius ratio (2.89 ± 0.11 vs 3.43 ± 0.16). The discrepancy was driven predominantly by differences in recommended IOL power rather than by predicted spherical equivalent alone: on the XY1 platform, the within-eye power range reached ≥ 1 step (≥ 0.5 D) in 94.4% of short and 60.8% of long eyes, whereas the predicted spherical equivalent range reached ≥ 0.25 D in 64.1% and 51.6%, respectively (Table 2, Panel A). The magnitude of within-eye disagreement was substantially larger in short eyes (Figure 1B): the seven formulas spanned ≥ 2 power steps (≥ 1.0 D) in 33.8% of short eyes but in only 6.4% of long eyes, while full agreement (zero-step range) was reached in 5.6% of short and 39.2% of long eyes. The median within-eye power range was one step in both strata (IQR 1–2 in short, 0–1 in long eyes).

Sensitivity Analyses for Bilateral Eyes and Single-Formula Outliers

Among patients contributing two eyes to the same stratum, fellow-eye agreement of the primary endpoint was 96.9% in short eyes (185 of 192 pairs concordant positive, one concordant negative, six discordant; Cohen’s κ 0.23, a value limited by the near-ceiling prevalence) and 67.5% in long eyes (74 concordant positive, 34 concordant negative, 52 discordant of the 160 pairs with the set-wide endpoint evaluable in both eyes; κ 0.31); the intraclass correlation of the within-eye step range between fellow eyes was 0.48 and 0.41, respectively. With one eye per patient, ClinRel occurred in 98.6% (95% CI 96.8–99.4) of short and 65.7% (60.2–70.8) of long eyes under random selection, and in 97.8% (95.7–98.9) and 62.3% (56.7–67.5) under a right-eye-preferred rule; the association of axial length with ClinRel in long eyes was essentially unchanged (one-eye univariable Firth OR 1.54 per 1 SD, 95% CI 1.14–2.07; multivariable 1.54, 1.14–2.08). Cluster-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals with the patient as the resampling unit (2000 replicates) closely matched the Wilson intervals (short 96.8–99.3; long 57.7–67.6). Generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable working correlation gave within-patient correlations of 0.27 in short and 0.31 in long eyes and marginal estimates of 98.2% (95% CI 96.6–99.1) and 62.8% (57.9–67.4), with an axial-length odds ratio in long eyes of 1.57 per 1 SD (1.28–1.92).

After excluding, for each eye, the single most deviant formula, a clinically relevant discrepancy persisted in 79.5% (95% CI 75.9–82.7) of short and 40.9% (36.5–45.4) of long eyes − 81.0% and 65.2% of originally discrepant eyes, respectively; the formula whose removal abolished the discrepancy was distributed across all seven engines, with no single formula accounting for more than approximately one quarter of these eyes.

Formula Feasibility

Six of the seven ESCRS-hosted formulas returned a valid recommendation in ≥ 99.5% of eyes on the primary platform. Hill-RBF 3.0, by contrast, returned no in-bounds estimate (an out-of-bounds response) in 160 of 551 short eyes (29.0%) and 150 of 469 long eyes (32.0%; 30.4% overall), reflecting the model’s explicit refusal to extrapolate beyond its validated biometric domain at both axial-length extremes. These feasibility rates use the full baseline strata (Table 1); the marginally smaller set-wide denominators in Table 2 (Panel A) additionally required at least six of seven valid outputs.

Ladas Super Formula 2.0

In the subset of eyes for which a Ladas Super Formula 2.0 output was available (n = 311; 170 short, 141 long), the seven ESCRS-hosted formulas alone produced a clinically relevant discrepancy in 97.6% of short and 67.4% of long eyes; with the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 included as an eighth engine, the corresponding eight-formula rates were 98.8% and 76.6% – the expected behavior of a range-based endpoint when any engine is added. The Ladas Super Formula 2.0 was not a directional outlier – its recommended power sat above the ESCRS-median power in 63 short and 23 long eyes and below it in 33 short and 25 long eyes, and was balanced in long eyes (mean signed difference −0.05 D vs the ESCRS median) – so that the added disagreement reflects its dispersion around the other engines rather than a systematic offset. The Ladas subset did not differ materially from the remaining cohort in axial length or other biometric characteristics (all standardized differences ≤0.17; Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically quantified formula-dependent variability in intraocular lens (IOL) power calculation at the two extremes of axial length, comparing seven ESCRS-hosted formulas and the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 under a standardized, paired within-eye design across three IOL platforms. Three principal findings emerged. First, clinically relevant inter-formula discrepancies were near-universal in short eyes (approximately 98%) and substantial in long eyes (approximately 63%), and this pattern was reproduced across all three IOL platforms. Second, the disagreement was predominantly a phenomenon of recommended power magnitude rather than of systematic bias: per-formula mean signed differences against Barrett were small (−0.11 to +0.16 D) and partly opposing, yet the formula-specific 95% limits of agreement extended to approximately one full power step in either direction in short eyes (within about ±0.7 D in long eyes), and in short eyes the seven formulas frequently diverged by ≥ 2 steps (≥ 1.0 D). Third, among long eyes, axial length and the axial length/corneal radius ratio were the only meaningful predictors of disagreement, and the probability of discrepancy scaled with how extreme the eye was rather than crossing a discrete biometric threshold.

An important methodological consideration concerns the choice of the clinically relevant discrepancy (ClinRel) threshold. Because the IOL power scale is discrete – most lens models are manufactured in 0.5 D increments – a one-step difference represents the minimum possible nonzero discrepancy, and our primary endpoint therefore captures any planning disagreement that would alter the implant ordered. We considered this threshold appropriate because each 0.5 D step at the IOL plane directly determines which physical lens is selected, and because the dual definition (≥ 1 power step OR ≥ 0.25 D difference in predicted spherical equivalent) accounts for the fact that, in short hyperopic eyes, a given IOL-power increment translates into a smaller change at the spectacle plane than in long myopic eyes. Supporting this planning-level threshold, IOLs in this power range are manufactured in 0.5 D increments, so that a half-diopter step is the smallest unit in which a planning disagreement can materialize in the lens ordered; at the IOL plane this corresponds to roughly 0.35 D at the spectacle plane in an average eye, while the complementary 0.25 D criterion matches the resolution at which subjective refraction is performed and at which refractive outcomes are conventionally reported.27 Because a range-based endpoint can in principle be met through a single outlying formula, a leave-one-formula-out analysis was performed: after removing the most deviant engine in each eye, the discrepancy persisted in 79.5% of short and 40.9% of long eyes (81.0% and 65.2% of originally discrepant eyes), and the abolishing formula was distributed across all seven engines. Single-formula outliers therefore contribute to the endpoint – appreciably so in long eyes – but the majority of discrepancies reflect dispersion across several formulas rather than one deviant recommendation.

Unlike most published formula comparisons at the axial-length extremes, which have focused predominantly on postoperative refractive accuracy,4,5,8,28,29 our study addresses a different and complementary question: how often, and by how much, modern formulas disagree on the preoperative recommendation when supplied with identical biometric input. This is clinically relevant because, in routine practice, the choice of formula determines which IOL power is ordered; it does not, however, indicate which of the diverging recommendations is closer to the optimal power. By using a paired within-eye approach with fully standardized inputs and lens constants for every formula run, we isolated the contribution of the formula itself, removing the measurement-related confounding that limits the interpretability of device- or outcome-comparison studies of comparable size.

Modern formulas achieve broadly similar refractive accuracy at the extremes. In short eyes, Kenny et al reported that the Barrett Universal II, EVO 2.0, Kane, Cooke K6, PEARL-DGS, and Hill-RBF 3.0 formulas each placed approximately 67–69% of eyes within ±0.50 D of target,29 and large mixed-cohort analyses have likewise found these engines to be broadly comparable.8,30 Yet our data show that the same formulas, applied to the same short eyes, disagreed on the recommended power step in approximately 98% of cases. This juxtaposition is the central message of the study: comparable average accuracy across a population does not imply interchangeable recommendations in the individual extreme eye, where the formulas disperse around the target rather than converging on a single value.

The marked stratum difference – near-universal disagreement in short eyes vs approximately 63% in long eyes – is mechanistically plausible. Short eyes are optically the most demanding setting for IOL power calculation: the high implant powers required amplify small differences in predicted effective lens position, so that formulas that estimate the cornea-to-IOL vergence and lens position differently diverge most precisely where the optics are most sensitive.2 This is compounded by the highly variable anatomy of short eyes, in which a shallow anterior chamber, small white-to-white diameter, and disproportionate lens position introduce additional uncertainty into every formula’s lens-position term.4,7,29 Long eyes, by contrast, have been the subject of dedicated axial-length-optimization work,28 and the ESCRS-hosted formulas largely incorporate such corrections internally; this likely explains their closer mutual agreement, even though roughly two thirds of long eyes still produced discordant recommendations.

A distinctive finding concerned formula feasibility. Whereas six of the seven formulas returned a recommendation in essentially every eye, Hill-RBF 3.0 returned an out-of-bounds response in approximately 30% of eyes at both extremes. This reflects the calculator’s data-driven boundary model, which flags eyes lying outside the region where the training data support a 90% probability of a ±0.50 D outcome and declines to extrapolate.17,31 We treated such responses as out-of-bounds rather than imputing a value. Clinically, this means that in roughly one of every three eyes at the axial-length extremes, Hill-RBF is simply unavailable as a planning tool – an honest, by-design limitation of the method rather than a computational error, but one the surgeon must anticipate when these eyes are encountered. A companion study by our group applied the same paired within-eye discrepancy approach to toric IOL calculator planning and likewise found clinically relevant inter-tool disagreement in a substantial proportion of eyes,32 indicating that planning-level variability is a general feature of contemporary IOL calculation rather than a peculiarity of spherical power.

The per-formula directional analysis (Figure 3, Table 3) clarifies why disagreement is frequent despite small average biases. In short eyes, EVO 2.0 (+0.16 D) and Hoffer QST (+0.16 D) tended to recommend higher power than Barrett, whereas Cooke K6 (−0.08 D) and Hill-RBF 3.0 (−0.11 D) tended lower; these opposing tendencies act in different directions and would cancel in any pooled estimate, which is why formula-specific rather than pooled limits of agreement are reported. Within this cohort and under the constants applied, no formula showed a directional offset large enough to label it systematically “high” or “low”; this observation is constant-dependent and should not be generalized beyond the platforms studied. Rather, the formulas scatter around a common central tendency, and it is this dispersion – not a shared directional bias – that produces divergent recommendations in the individual eye. Throughout, Barrett Universal II served as a descriptive reference – chosen as the most widely used of the hosted formulas – and not as an accuracy benchmark. Recent comparative work in long eyes indicates that this role should not be conflated with superiority: among sixteen axial-length-adjusted formulas evaluated in long Caucasian eyes, modified third-generation formulas achieved the lowest prediction errors, ahead of the segmented Barrett True AL variant and comparable to newer regression- and artificial-intelligence-based engines.33 The directional findings reported here therefore describe relative positions within the formula set rather than deviations from a gold standard. Incorporating the Ladas Super Formula 2.0 as an eighth engine did not introduce a directional outlier, consistent with its design as a meta-formula that blends established methods.9,11

The predictor analysis identified axial length as the strongest independent correlate of disagreement in long eyes (Firth-penalized odds ratio 1.55 per 1 SD; adjusted 1.56), closely followed by the axial length/corneal radius ratio (1.52 per 1 SD), while anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, white-to-white, and keratometric parameters contributed little. However, the threshold analysis showed that no single biometric cutoff cleanly separates concordant from discordant eyes: the Youden-optimal axial length/corneal radius ratio of 3.52 achieved high specificity (96.0%) but low sensitivity (35.5%), and disagreement was graded across the biometric range – rising steeply above a ratio of about 3.6 yet also elevated at the lowest ratios – rather than separating at a single defined boundary (Figure 4). The practical implication is that disagreement risk scales with how extreme the eye is, and there is no biometric value below which the formulas can be assumed to agree.

The deliberate restriction of this study to the planning level warrants emphasis. From the perspective of postoperative accuracy alone, modern formulas appear nearly interchangeable at the extremes; however, the formula output is not an abstract prediction but the value that determines which physical IOL is ordered for surgery. A one-step disagreement in nearly all short eyes and in the majority of long eyes is therefore a logistically and clinically substantive observation that, to our knowledge, has not previously been systematically quantified across this number of formulas and platforms at both axial-length extremes. These data cannot determine which recommendation is more accurate, whether greater disagreement predicts a larger postoperative refractive error, or whether cross-checking formulas improves outcomes; establishing this requires outcome-based analysis according to the accepted criteria for evaluating formula performance,34 and how conflicting recommendations should be resolved therefore remains a matter of clinical judgment until outcome-anchored decision rules are available. By holding biometry and lens constants constant across paired formula runs, the design isolates the formula-specific modeling contribution from measurement noise and surgical variability. A complementary prospective outcome cohort, currently in preparation, will address which formula produces the most accurate postoperative refraction in eyes with discrepant recommendations.

The formula landscape itself continues to evolve: artificial-intelligence-based and thick-lens approaches are being refined, segmented (sum-of-segments) interpretation of axial length and updated constant sets are increasingly adopted, and both newly developed engines and axial-length-adjusted versions of established formulas continue to appear.35 Planning-level variability of the kind quantified here is therefore a moving target that warrants periodic re-evaluation as formulas are updated, and the present findings characterize the formula versions available during the calculation window rather than a fixed property of IOL power calculation.

Several limitations should be considered. First, no eyes of average axial length were included; the study therefore characterizes inter-formula disagreement within the two extreme strata but cannot quantify the excess discrepancy attributable to axial-length extremity, since some degree of disagreement among seven formulas would also be expected in average eyes. Second, this was a retrospective, single-center analysis; although three IOL platforms were reported, one served as the primary reference environment. Third, the study was conducted at the planning level without postoperative outcome validation, which the prospective cohort in preparation will address. Fourth, both eyes of many patients were analyzed, which departs from methodological recommendations for IOL-formula studies;27 however, fellow-eye concordance was quantified, and one-eye-per-patient, cluster-bootstrap, and population-averaged sensitivity analyses yielded materially unchanged results. Fifth, formula algorithms are periodically updated, and the results reflect the versions accessed between April and May 2026. Sixth, the ESCRS-recommended optimized constants rather than locally personalized constants were applied; because constant optimization shifts each formula’s absolute output, both the absolute power levels and, to a lesser degree, the observed inter-formula offsets are constant-dependent, and locally optimized constants could attenuate or accentuate the disagreement rates reported here, although the paired within-eye design preserves the relative comparison under any common constant set. Seventh, Hill-RBF out-of-bounds responses were treated as missing rather than imputed, which reduces the effective sample for that formula. Eighth, the requirement for complete, good-quality biometry across all formulas may have preferentially selected eyes with regular biometry, so the findings may not extend to eyes with corneal irregularity, poor fixation, dense cataract, or incomplete measurements. Finally, the anatomical heterogeneity of short eyes could not be characterized beyond the biometric parameters recorded; microphthalmic eyes were not identified as a separate subgroup.

In conclusion, formula-dependent variability in IOL power planning is frequent in short and long eyes – affecting nearly all short eyes and approximately two thirds of long eyes in this cohort – and is driven by dispersion of the recommended power rather than by a shared directional bias. Because these are planning-level findings without postoperative outcome validation, they do not indicate which formula should be preferred, nor do they establish that consulting multiple formulas improves refractive outcomes. Their clinical value lies in quantifying, across eight formulas and three platforms, how often the choice of formula alone would change the lens ordered: inter-formula disagreement should alert the surgeon to increased planning uncertainty in the individual eye, disagreement risk in long eyes scales with axial length and the axial length/corneal radius ratio without a clean biometric cutoff, and Hill-RBF 3.0 is unavailable in roughly one third of such eyes. A prospective outcome study is in preparation to determine which formula yields the most accurate refraction in eyes with discrepant planning recommendations.
