Bounded oscillation operators on BMO spaces Thanks: The work was supported by the Higher Education and Science Committee of RA, in the frames of the research project 21AG‐1A045
Grigori A. Karagulyan

Abstract
. Bounded Oscillation ( BO {\rm BO} ) operators were recently introduced in [ 13 ] , where it was proved that many operators in harmonic analysis (Calderón-Zygmund operators, Carleson type operators, martingale transforms, Littlewood-Paley square functions, maximal operators, etc) are BO {\rm BO} operators. BO {\rm BO} operators are defined on abstract measure spaces equipped with a basis of abstract balls. The abstract balls in their definition owe four basic properties of classical balls in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , which are crucial in the study of singular operators on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . Among various properties studied in these papers it was proved that BO {\rm BO} operators allow pointwise sparse domination, establishing the A 2 A_{2} -conjecture for those operators. In the present paper we study boundedness properties of BO {\rm BO} operators on BMO {\rm BMO} spaces. In particular, we prove that general BO {\rm BO} operators boundedly map L ∞ L^{\infty} into BMO {\rm BMO} , and under a logarithmic localization condition those map BMO {\rm BMO} into itself. We obtain these properties as corollaries of new local type bounds, involving oscillations of functions over the balls. We apply the results in the BMO {\rm BMO} estimations of Calderón-Zygmund operators, martingale transforms, Carleson type operators, as well as in the unconditional basis properties of general wavelet type systems in atomic Hardy spaces H 1 H^{1} .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Operators on
{\rm BMO}
spaces

The space of functions of bounded mean oscillation,
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, was introduced by John and Nirenberg
[
11
]
. This space is the collection of locally integrable functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(1.1)

\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}=\sup_{B}\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|<\infty,

where
f_{B}
denotes the mean of
f
over a ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the supremum is taken over all the balls
B
. This is not properly a norm, since any function which is constant almost everywhere has zero oscillation. However, modulo constant,
{\rm BMO}
becomes a Banach space with respect to the norm (
1.1
). The space
{\rm BMO}
plays an important role in harmonic analysis and in the study of partial differential equations. One can check that

L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, but there exist unbounded
{\rm BMO}
functions. However, the fundamental inequality of John-Nirenberg
[
11
]
states that
{\rm BMO}
functions have exponential decay over the balls. Namely, for any ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

(1.2)

|\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-f_{B}|>\lambda\}|\leq c_{1}|B|\exp(-c_{2}\lambda/\|f\|_{\rm BMO}),\quad\lambda>0,

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are positive absolute constants. Some remarkable results about
{\rm BMO}
are the duality relationship between
{\rm BMO}
and the Hardy space
H^{1}
due to C. Fefferman
[
8
]
and a deep connection between the Carleson measures and the
{\rm BMO}
functions established by C. Fefferman and E. Stein
[
7
]
.
There are different characterizations and generalizations of
{\rm BMO}
spaces.

It is of interest the problem of boundedness of certain operators in harmonic analysis on
{\rm BMO}
spaces. The fact that ordinary Calderón–Zygmund operators map boundedly
L^{\infty}
into
{\rm BMO}
was independently obtained by Peetre
[
18
]
, Spanne
[
20
]
and Stein
[
22
]
. Peetre
[
18
]
also observed that translation-invariant Calderón–Zygmund operators actually map
{\rm BMO}
to itself. Bennett-DeVore-Sharpley in
[
1
]
proved that the Hardy-Littlewood uncentered maximal function is bounded on
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

1.2.
Bounded oscillation operators

Bounded oscillation (
{\rm BO}
) operators were recently introduced in
[
13
]
and it was proved that many operators in harmonic analysis (Calderón-Zygmund operators, maximally modulated singular operators, Carleson type operators, martingale transforms, Littlewood-Paley square functions, maximal operators, etc) are
{\rm BO}
operators. Various properties of
{\rm BO}
operators were studied in
[
14
,
13
]
(see also
[
3
]
for a multilinear generalization of
{\rm BO}
operators and those properties). In the present paper we consider the problem of boundedness of
{\rm BO}
operators on
{\rm BMO}
spaces.
General
{\rm BO}
operators are defined on abstract measure spaces equipped with a ball-basis. The definition of the ball-basis is a selection of basic properties of classical balls (or cubes) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are crucial in the study of singular operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{M}
and a measure
\mu
. A family of measurable sets
\mathscr{B}
is said to be a ball-basis if it satisfies the following conditions:

B1)

0<\mu(B)<\infty
for any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
.

B2)

For any points
x,y\in X
there exists a ball
B\ni x,y
.

B3)

If
E\in\mathfrak{M}
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a (finite or infinite) sequence of balls
B_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, such that
\mu(E\bigtriangleup\cup_{k}B_{k})<\varepsilon
.

B4)

For any
B\in\mathscr{B}
there is a ball
B^{*}\in\mathscr{B}
(called hull-ball of
B
), satisfying the conditions

(1.3)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:\,\mu(A)\leq 2\mu(B),\,A\cap B\neq\varnothing}A\subset B^{*},

(1.4)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mu\left(B^{*}\right)\leq\mathcal{K}\mu(B),

where
\mathcal{K}
is a positive constant independent of
B
.

Ball-bases can additionally have the following properties.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
is doubling if there is a constant
\eta>2
such that for any ball
B
with
B^{*}\neq X
one can find a ball
B^{\prime}\supset B
such that

(1.5)

\displaystyle 2\mu(B)\leq\mu(B^{\prime})\leq\eta\cdot\mu(B).

Definition 1.3
.

A ball basis
\mathscr{B}
is said to be regular if there is a constant
0<\theta<1
such that for any two balls
B
and
A
, satisfying
\mu(B)\leq\mu(A)
,
B\cap A\neq\varnothing
we have
\mu(B^{*}\cap A)\geq\theta\mu(B^{*})
.

Here are some basic examples of ball-bases:

(1)

The family of Euclidean balls (or cubes) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a doubling ball-basis.

(2)

Dyadic cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
form a doubling ball-basis.

(3)

Let
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi
be the unit circle. The family of arcs in
\mathbb{T}
is a doubling ball-basis.

(4)

The families of metric balls in measure spaces of homogeneous type form a ball-basis with the doubling condition (see
[
13
]
, sec. 7, for the proof)

(5)

Let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space and
\{\mathscr{B}_{n}:\,n\geq 0\}
be collections of measurable sets (called filtration) such that

•

\mathscr{B}_{0}=\{X\}
and each
\mathscr{B}_{n}
forms a finite or countable partition of
X
,

•

each
A\in\mathscr{B}_{n}
is a union of some sets of
\mathscr{B}_{n+1}
,

•

the collection
\mathscr{B}=\cup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathscr{B}_{n}
generates the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{M}
,

•

for any points
x,y\in X
there is a set
A\in X
such that
x,y\in A
.

For any
A\in\mathscr{B}_{n}
we denote by
\mathrm{pr}(A)
the parent-ball of
A
, that is the unique element of
\mathscr{B}_{n-1}
, containing
A
. One can easily check that
\mathscr{B}
satisfies the ball-basis conditions B1)-B4), where for
A\in\mathscr{B}
the hull-ball
A^{*}
is the maximal element in
\mathscr{B}
, satisfying
A^{*}\supset A
and
\mu(A^{*})\leq 2\mu(A)
. Observe that
A^{*}
can coincide with
A
and it occurs when
\mu(\mathrm{pr}(A))>2\mu(A)
.
Besides, a martingale ball-basis is doubling if and only if
\mu(\mathrm{pr}(A))\leq c\mu(A)
for any
A\in\mathscr{B}
and for some constant
c>1
.

We also note that all these ball-bases are regular (see Definition
1.3
)

1.3.
Notations

Let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space equipped with a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
. Fix
1\leq r<\infty
and denote by
L^{r}_{loc}(X)
the space of local
L^{r}
-integrable functions
f
on
X
, namely,
\int_{B}|f|^{r}<\infty
for any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
. Denote by
L^{0}(X)
the space of almost everywhere finite measurable functions on
X
. We set

(1.6)

\displaystyle f_{B}=\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}f,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{B}=\left(\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}|f|^{r}\right)^{1/r},

(1.8)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{B}^{*}=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:A\supset B}\langle f\rangle_{A},

(1.9)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}=\langle f-f_{B}\rangle_{B}=\left(\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}|f-f_{B}|^{r}\right)^{1/r},

(1.10)

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}^{*}=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:A\supset B}\langle f\rangle_{\#,A},

(1.11)

\displaystyle{\rm SUP}_{E}(f)={\rm esssup\,}_{x\in E}|f(x)|,

(1.12)

\displaystyle{\rm INF}_{E}(f)={\rm essinf\,}_{x\in E}|f(x)|,

(1.13)

\displaystyle{\rm OSC}_{E}(f)={\rm esssup\,}_{x,x^{\prime}\in E}|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|={\rm SUP}_{E}(f)-{\rm INF}_{E}(f),

(1.14)

\displaystyle{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)=\inf_{E\subset B:\,\mu(E)>\alpha\mu(B)}{\rm OSC}_{E}(f),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

where
f\in L^{r}_{loc}(X)
,
A,B\in\mathscr{B}
and
E\subset X
is a measurable set. Note that the
\alpha
-oscillation functional
{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)
can be equivalently defined as follows: that is
{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)=\inf_{a<b}(b-a)
, where the infimum is taken over all the numbers
a<b
satisfying
\mu\{x\in B:\,f(x)\in[a,b]\}>\alpha\mu(B)
.

In the sequel positive constants depending only on our ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and the parameter
1\leq r<\infty
will be called admissible constants. The relation
a\lesssim b
will stand for
a\leq c\cdot b
, where
c>0
is admissible. We write
a\sim b
if the relations
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
hold simultaneously. The notation
\log a
will stand for
\log_{2}a
.

1.4.
Main results

We let
(X,\mu)
be a measurable space equipped with a doubling ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
. We will consider a subclass of
{\rm BO}
operators on
X
, which definition involves certain modulus of continuity. An operator
T:L^{r}(X)\to L^{0}(X)
is called sublinear if it satisfies
|T(f+g)|\leq|T(f)|+|T(g)|
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\omega:[1,\infty)\to[0,1]
be a non-increasing function and suppose
r\geq 1
. We say
T:L^{r}(X)\to L^{0}(X)
is
{\rm BO}_{\omega}
operator if it is sublinear, i.e.
|T(f+g)|\leq|T(f)|+|T(g)|
, and for any function
f\in L^{r}(X)
and a ball
B
we have

(1.15)

\displaystyle{\rm OSC}_{B}\big(T(f\cdot\textbf{1}_{X\setminus B^{*}})\big)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T)\sup_{A\in\mathscr{B}:A\supset B}\bigg(\omega\left(\frac{\mu(B)}{\mu(A)}\right)\cdot\langle f\rangle_{A}\bigg),

where
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T)
is a constant, depending only on
\omega
and the operator
T
. The class of operators satisfying (
1.15
) will be denoted by
{\rm BO}_{\omega}(X)
and we suppose that
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T)
is the least constant such that (
1.15
) holds for all the functions
f\in L^{r}(X)
and balls
B
.

Remark 1.1
.

Condition (
1.15
) is an extended version of the localization property, that was considered in the original definition of
{\rm BO}
operators in
[
13
]
. Moreover, if
\omega(t)\equiv 1
, then (
1.15
) exactly gives the definition of
{\rm BO}
operators on measure spaces with a doubling ball-basis, since in that case the right hand side of (
1.15
) becomes
\mathcal{L}(T)\langle f\rangle_{B}^{*}=\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T)\langle f\rangle_{B}^{*}
(see
[
13
]
). In that case we will use notation
{\rm BO}(X)
instead of
{\rm BO}_{\omega}(X)
.

Definition 1.5
.

We say that an operator
T
is vanishing on constants (or simply vanishing) if for any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
and any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a ball
B^{\prime}\supset B
such that for any ball
B^{\prime\prime}\supset B^{\prime}
the inequality
{\rm OSC}_{B}\big(T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})\big)\leq\varepsilon
holds. A family of vanishing operators
T_{\alpha}:L^{r}(X)\to L^{0}(X)
is said to be uniformly vanishing if the corresponding ball
B^{\prime}
can be chosen independently of
T_{\alpha}
.

Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a doubling ball-basis in a measure space
X
and suppose that
T_{\alpha}\in{\rm BO}_{\omega}(X)
is a family of uniformly vanishing linear operators with
\omega(t)=\log^{-1}(1+t)
. If
T
is one of these operators

(1.16)

T^{+}(f)=\sup_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(f),\quad T^{-}(f)=\inf_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}(f),\quad T^{*}(f)=\sup_{\alpha}|T_{\alpha}(f)|,

and for a number
1\leq r<\infty
we have

(1.17)

\|T\|_{L^{r}(X)\to L^{r,\infty}(X)}<\infty,\quad\sup_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T_{\alpha})<\infty,

then the bound

(1.18)

{\rm OSC}_{B,\beta}\,T(f)\lesssim\left((1-\beta)^{-1/r}\|T\|_{L^{r}(X)\to L^{r,\infty}(X)}+\sup_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T_{\alpha})\right)\langle f\rangle^{*}_{\#,B}

holds for any
f\in L^{r}(X)
, any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
and a number
0<\beta<1
.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that the admissible constant on the right-hand side of (
1.18
) is independent of the parameter
\beta
. The same applies to the theorems presented below.

Remark 1.3
.

Observe that if
\mu(X)<\infty
and
T(\textbf{1}_{X})
is a constant function, then
T
is trivially vanishing, since one can simply choose
B^{\prime}=X
in Definition
1.5
(by Lemma
3.1
the condition
\mu(X)<\infty
implies that
X
itself is a ball, i.e.
X\in\mathscr{B}
). Thus if
\mu(X)<\infty
, then in Theorem
1.1
the condition to be uniformly vanishing can be replaced by the condition
T_{\alpha}(\textbf{1}_{X})\equiv c_{\alpha}
.

Remark 1.4
.

Observe that, in general, operators (
1.16
) can produce non-measurable functions, since the number of operators
T_{\alpha}
can be non-countable. So in some cases we will use outer measure
\mu^{*}
generated from
\mu
(see definition in Section
2
). In particular, the weak
L^{r,\infty}
norm of a function
f
is defined in (
2.9
).
This justifies the consideration of
\|T\|_{L^{r}(X)\to L^{r,\infty}(X)}
in Theorem
1.1
. Besides, the functions, involved in the above notations are allowed to be non-mensurable.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a doubling ball-basis in a measure space
X
and suppose that
\{T_{\alpha}\}\subset{\rm BO}(X)
is a family of general
{\rm BO}
operators (
\omega(t)\equiv 1
in (
1.15
)). If
T
is one of the operators (
1.16
)
and it satisfies conditions (
1.17
), then the bound

(1.19)

{\rm OSC}_{B,\beta}\,T(f)\lesssim\left((1-\beta)^{-1/r}\|T\|_{L^{r}(X)\to L^{r,\infty}(X)}+\sup_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(T_{\alpha})\right)\|f\|_{\infty}

holds for any
f\in L^{\infty}(X)
, any ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
and a number
0<\beta<1
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
if
f\in{\rm BMO}(X)\cap L^{r}(X)
, then
T(f)\in{\rm BMO}(X)
and

(1.20)

\|T(f)\|_{{\rm BMO}(X)}\lesssim\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}(X)}.

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
if
f\in L^{\infty}(X)
, then
T(f)\in{\rm BMO}(X)
and

(1.21)

\|T(f)\|_{{\rm BMO}(X)}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}.

Corollary
1.1
is immediately obtained by a combination of Theorem
1.1
and the following John-Nirenberg type Theorem
1.3
. For its statement, first, recall the definitions of
{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}
and
{\rm BMO}
spaces as families of functions
f\in L^{0}(X)
, satisfying

(1.22)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}{\rm OSC}_{B,\alpha}(f)<\infty,

(1.23)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\langle f\rangle_{\#,B}=\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\langle f-f_{B}\rangle_{B}<\infty,

respectively. One can easily check that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}\leq 2(1-\alpha)^{-1}\|f\|_{\rm BMO},

whereas the following is a more delicate property of these norms.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is an admissible constant
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
such that
{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}(X)={\rm BMO}(X)
. Moreover, for any
f\in{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}(X)
we have
\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}}\sim\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}
.

Remark 1.5
.

Theorem
1.3
is an extension of the analogous result on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
proved in
[
12
,
23
]
. The proof of Theorem
1.3
is based on Proposition
5.1
(see Section
5
), which is itself interesting. In particular, from Proposition
5.1
it follows that:
if
f\in{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then for any ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.25)

\displaystyle|\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-f_{B}|>

\displaystyle c(n+1)\|f\|_{\rm BMO}\}|

(1.26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot|\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-f_{B}|>cn\|f\|_{\rm BMO}\}|

where
c>0
is an absolute constant. Clearly, this implies the classical John-Nirenberg inequality (
1.2
), but the converse statement is not true at all.

Remark 1.6
.

Note that for the proof of Theorem
1.1
the regularity condition (see Definition
1.3
) of the ball-basis is not used, but it is significant in the proofs of Theorem
1.3
and Corollary
1.1
.

2.
Outer measure and
L^{p}
-norms of non-measurable functions

It is well known for classical examples of ball-bases that the union of any collections of balls is measurable and such a property is important to ensure measurability of certain maximal operators. For general ball-bases non-countable union of balls need not to be measurable. So in some cases we will need to use outer measure generated by the basic measure
\mu
. Hence, let
(X,\mathfrak{M},\mu)
be a measure space. Define the outer measure of an arbitrary set
E\subset X
by

\mu^{*}(E)=\inf_{F\in\mathfrak{M}:\,F\supset E}\mu(F).

Clearly, for any set
F\subset X
there exists a measurable cover for
F
, i.e. a set
E\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

(2.1)

E\supset F,\quad\mu^{*}(F)=\mu(E).

In general, a measurable cover is not uniquely determined. However, if
E
and
E^{\prime}
are two measurable covers for a set
F
, then those coincide a.e., i.e.
\mu(E\triangle E^{\prime})=0
. For any function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
(possibly non-measurable) we denote

(2.2)

\displaystyle G_{f}(t)=\{x\in X:\,|f(x)|>t\},\quad t\geq 0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(t)=\mu^{*}\left(G_{f}(t)\right).

Clearly
\lambda_{f}(t)
is a distribution function, i.e. it is right-continuous.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
(possibly non-measurable) there exists a measurable function
\bar{f}
, called a cover function for
f
, such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq\bar{f}(x),\quad x\in X,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(t)=\lambda_{\bar{f}}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

The function
\bar{f}
is uniquely defined a.e.; that is, if
g
is another cover function for
f
, then
g(x)=\bar{f}(x)
a.e..

Proof.

Let
\overline{G}_{f}(t)
be measurable covers for the sets (
2.2
). Making suitable choice of those cover sets and using the countability of the rationals
\mathbb{Q}
, one can ensure
\overline{G}_{f}(r)\supset\overline{G}_{f}(r^{\prime})
for all pairs of rationals
r<r^{\prime}
. Set

(2.6)

\bar{f}(x)=\sup\left\{r\in\mathbb{Q}:\,x\in\overline{G}_{f}(r)\right\}.

One can check that

\left\{x\in X:\,f(x)>t\right\}\subset\bigcup_{r>t,\,r\in\mathbb{Q}}\overline{G}_{f}(r)=\left\{x\in X:\,\bar{f}(x)>t\right\}.

These relations easily imply that
\bar{f}
is measurable and satisfies (
2.4
), (
2.5
). It is also easy to observe that
\bar{f}
is uniquely defined a.e..
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Form Proposition
2.1
it follows that the cover function of any function
f
is the least measurable function dominating
f
. Namely, if
g(x)
is measurable and
f(x)\leq g(x)
then
g(x)\geq\bar{f}(x)
a.e.. This in particular implies that

(2.7)

\overline{f+g}\leq\bar{f}+\bar{g},\quad\overline{fg}\leq\bar{f}\cdot\bar{g}.

For arbitrary function
f
on
X
we define

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}}=\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{p}}=\left(p\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}\lambda_{f}(t)dt\right)^{1/p},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p,\infty}}=\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{p,\infty}}=\sup_{t>0}t(\lambda_{f}(t))^{1/p}.

Using Proposition
2.1
, one can observe that the standard triangle and Hölder inequalities hold in such setting of
L^{p}
norms. In particular, if
f
and
g
are arbitrary functions, then, using (
2.7
), we can write

\|f+g\|_{p}=\|\overline{f+g}\|_{p}\leq\|\bar{f}+\bar{g}\|_{p}\leq\|\bar{f}\|_{p}+\|\bar{g}\|_{p}=\|f\|_{p}+\|g\|_{p}

that gives the triangle inequality for general functions. Similarly it can be proved the Hölder’s inequality. As we have mentioned in Remark
1.4
some maximal operators can produce non-measurable functions. One can state a version of that Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem for such operators. Denote by
L^{0,*}(X)
the space of arbitrary functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say a subadditive operator
T:L^{p}(X)\to L^{0,*}(X)
satisfies weak-
L^{p}
or strong-
L^{p}
estimate if

\displaystyle\|T\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p,\infty}}=\sup_{t>0,\,f\in L^{p}(X)}\frac{t\cdot(\lambda_{T(f)}(t))^{1/p}}{\|f\|_{L^{p}}}<\infty,

\displaystyle\|T\|_{L^{p}}=\sup_{f\in L^{p}(X)}\frac{\|T(f)\|_{L^{p}}}{\|f\|_{L^{p}}}<\infty,

respectively.

The proof of the following generalized Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem is exactly the same as in the classical case (
[
28
]

3.
Preliminary properties of ball bases

Some of lemmas proved in this section are versions of similar statements from
[
13
,
14
]
. We find necessary to provide their proofs though. Let
\mathscr{B}
be a ball-basis in the measure space
(X,\mu)
. From B4) condition it follows that if balls
A,B
satisfy
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(A)\leq 2\mu(B)
, then
A\subset B^{*}
. This property will be called two balls relation. We say a set
E\subset X
is bounded if
E\subset B
for a ball
B\in\mathscr{B}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
(X,\mu)
is a measure space equipped with a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and
\mu(X)<\infty
, then
X\in\mathscr{B}
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in X
and let
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathscr{B}
be the family of balls containing
x_{0}
. By B2) condition we can write
X=\cup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}A
. Then there exists a
B\in\mathscr{A}
such that
\mu(B)>\frac{1}{2}\sup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}\mu(A)
and using B4) condition we obtain

X=\cup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}A\subset B^{*}.

Thus
X=B^{*}\in\mathscr{B}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a measure space equipped with a ball-basis
\mathscr{B}
and
G\in\mathscr{B}
. Then there exists a sequence of balls
G=G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
(finite or infinite) such that

(3.1)

X=\cup_{k}G_{k},\quad G_{k}^{*}\subset G_{k+1}.

Moreover for any
B\in\mathscr{B}
there is a ball
G_{n}\supset B
. If the ball-basis is doubling, then we can additionally claim

(3.2)

2\mu(G_{k})\leq\mu(G_{k+1})\leq\gamma\mu(G_{k}),

with an admissible constant
\gamma>2
.

Proof.

Choose an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in G
and let
\mathscr{A}\subset\mathscr{B}
be the family of balls containing the point
x_{0}
. Choose a sequence
\eta_{n}\nearrow\eta=\sup_{A\in\mathscr{A}}\mu(A)
, such that
\eta_{1}<\mu(G)
, where
\eta
can also be infinity. Let us see by induction that there is an increasing sequence of balls
G_{n}\in\mathscr{A}
such that
G_{1}=G
,
\mu(G_{n})>\eta_{n}
and
G_{n}^{*}\subset G_{n+1}
. The base of induction is trivial. Suppose we have already chosen the first balls
G_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,l
. There is a ball
A\in\mathscr{A}
so that
\mu(A)>\eta_{l+1}
. Let
C
be the biggest in measure among the balls
A
and
G_{l}^{*}
and denote
G_{l+1}=C^{*}
. By property B4) we get
A\cup G_{l}^{*}\subset C^{*}=G_{l+1}
. This implies
\mu(G_{l+1})\geq\mu(A)>\eta_{l+1}
and
G_{l+1}\supset G_{l}^{*}
, completing the induction.
Let us see that
G_{n}
is our desired sequence of balls. Indeed, let
B
be an arbitrary ball. By B2) property there is a ball
A
containing
x_{0}
, such that
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
. Then by property B4) we may find a ball
C\supset A\cup B
. For some
n
we will have
\mu(C)\leq 2\mu(G_{n})
and so once again using B4), we get
B\subset C\subset G_{n}^{*}\subset G_{n+1}
.

The second part of the lemma can be proved by a similar argument. Using the doubling condition (Definition
1.2
) one can easily find a sequence of balls
G=G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
such that

(3.3)

G_{k}^{*}\subset G_{k+1},\quad 2\mu(G_{k}^{*})\leq\mu(G_{k+1})\leq\eta\mu(G_{k}^{*})\leq\eta\mathcal{K}\mu(G_{k}).

If
\mu(X)<\infty
, then clearly the ball sequence will be finite and its last term say
G_{n}
will coincide with
X
. So the proof follows. If
\mu(X)=\infty
, then our ball sequence is infinite and
\mu(G_{n})\to\infty
. As in the first part of the proof, for an arbitrary ball
B
we can find a ball
C\supset B
such that
C\cap G\neq\varnothing
. There is a ball
G_{n}
such that
\mu(G_{n})>\mu(C)
and we get
B\subset C\subset G_{n}^{*}\subset G_{n+1}
. This completes the proof of lemma.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

If
\mu(X)<\infty
, then the sequence
G_{n}
in Lemma
3.2
is finite and its last-most term coincides with
X
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mu)
be a measure space with a ball bases
\mathscr{B}
. If
E\subset X
is bounded and a family of balls
{\mathscr{G}\,}
is a covering of
E
, i.e.
E\subset\bigcup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}G
, then there exists a finite or infinite sequence of pairwise disjoint balls
G_{k}\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
such that
E\subset\bigcup_{k}G_{k}^{*}
.

Proof.

The boundedness of
E
implies
E\subset B
for some
B\in\mathscr{B}
. If there is a ball
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
so that
G\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(G)>\mu(B)
, then by two balls relation we will have
E\subset B\subset G^{*}
. Thus our desired sequence can be formed by a single element
G
. Hence we can suppose that every
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
satisfies
G\cap B\neq\varnothing
,
\mu(G)\leq\mu(B)
and again by two balls relation
G\subset B^{*}
. Therefore,
\bigcup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}G\subset B^{*}
.
Choose
G_{1}\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
, satisfying
\mu(G_{1})>\frac{1}{2}\sup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}\mu(G)
. Then, suppose by induction we have already chosen elements
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
from
{\mathscr{G}\,}
. Choose
G_{k+1}\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
disjoint with the balls
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}
such that

(3.4)

\mu(G_{k+1})>\frac{1}{2}\sup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}:\,G\cap G_{j}=\varnothing,\,j=1,\ldots,k}\mu(G).

If for some
n
we will not be able to determine
G_{n+1}
the process will stop and we will get a finite sequence
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{n}
. Otherwise our sequence will be infinite. We shall consider the infinite case of the sequence (the finite case can be done similarly). Since the balls
G_{n}
are pairwise disjoint and
G_{n}\subset B^{*}
, we have
\mu(G_{n})\to 0
. Choose an arbitrary
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
with
G\neq G_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
and let
m
be the smallest integer satisfying

\mu(G)\geq 2\mu(G_{m+1})
.
So we have
G\cap G_{j}\neq\varnothing

for some
1\leq j\leq m
, since otherwise by (
3.4
),
G
had to be chosen instead of
G_{m+1}
. Besides, we have
\mu(G)<2\mu(G_{j})
because of the minimality property of
m
, and so by two balls relation
G\subset G_{j}^{*}
. Since
G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}
was chosen arbitrarily, we get
E\subset\bigcup_{G\in{\mathscr{G}\,}}G\subset\bigcup_{k}G_{k}^{*}
.
∎

Definition 3.1
.

For a measurable set
E\subset X
a point
x\in E
is said to be density point if for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a ball
B\ni x
such that

\mu(B\cap E)>(1-\varepsilon)\mu(B).

We say a ball basis satisfies the density property if for any measurable set
E
almost all points
x\in E
are density points.

Lemma 3.4
.

Every ball basis
\mathscr{B}
satisfies the density condition.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
3.2
one can check that it is enough to establish the density property for the bounded measurable sets. Suppose to the contrary there exist a bounded measurable set
E\subset X
together its subset
F\subset E
(maybe non-measurable) with an outer measure
\mu^{*}(F)>0

such that

(3.5)

\mu(B\setminus E)>\alpha\mu(B)\text{ whenever }B\in\mathscr{B},\,B\cap F\neq\varnothing,

where
0<\alpha<1
. According to the definition of the outer measure one can find a measurable set
\bar{F}
such that

(3.6)

F\subset\bar{F}\subset E,\quad\mu(\bar{F})=\mu^{*}(F).

By B3)-condition there is a sequence of balls
B_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, such that

(3.7)

\mu\left(\bar{F}\bigtriangleup(\cup_{k}B_{k})\right)<\varepsilon.

We discuss two collections of balls
B_{k}
, satisfying either
B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing
or
B_{k}\cap F\neq\varnothing
. For the first collection we have

\mu^{*}(F)\leq\mu\left(\bar{F}\setminus\bigcup_{k:\,B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}B_{k}\right)\leq\mu(\bar{F})=\mu^{*}(F).

This implies

\mu\left(\bar{F}\bigcap\left(\bigcup_{k:\,B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}B_{k}\right)\right)=0

and therefore, combining also (
3.7
), we obtain

(3.8)

\mu\left(\bigcup_{k:\,B_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}B_{k}\right)<\varepsilon.

Now consider the balls, satisfying

. It follows from (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) that

(3.9)

\mu(B_{k}\setminus\bar{F})\geq\mu(B_{k}\setminus E)>\alpha\mu(B_{k}),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.

Since
E
and so
\bar{F}
are bounded, applying Lemma
3.3
and (

), one can find a subsequence of pairwise disjoint balls
\tilde{B}_{k}
,

, such that

Thus, from B4)-condition, (

), (
3.8
) and (

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(F)<\mu(\bar{F})

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left(\cup_{k}\tilde{B}_{k}^{*}\right)+\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{K}\sum_{k}\mu(\tilde{B}_{k})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}\mu\left(\bigcup_{k:\,\tilde{B}_{k}\cap F=\varnothing}\tilde{B}_{k}\right)+\mathcal{K}\sum_{k:\,\tilde{B}_{k}\cap F\neq\varnothing}\mu(\tilde{B}_{k})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle<\mathcal{K}\varepsilon+\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\alpha}\sum_{k}\mu(\tilde{B}_{k}\setminus\bar{F})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{K}+1)\varepsilon+\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\alpha}\mu\left(\bar{F}\bigtriangleup(\cup_{k}B_{k})\right)<\varepsilon\left(2\mathcal{K}+1+\frac{\mathcal{K}}{\alpha}\right).

Since

\mu^{*}(F)=0
and so a contradiction.
∎

.

and therefore

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}\{x\in B:\,|f(x)-a_{B}|>\|f\|_{{\rm BMO}_{\alpha}}\}

6.
Examples of
{\rm BO}
operators on BMO

6.1.
Calderón-Zygmund operators

A Calderón-Zygmund operator
T
is a linear operator such that

(6.1)

Tf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)f(y)dy,\quad x\notin{\rm supp\,}f,

for any compactly supported continuous function
f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where the kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the conditions

(6.2)

\displaystyle|K(x,y)|\leq\frac{C}{|x-y|^{d}}\text{ if }x\neq y,

(6.3)

\displaystyle|K(x,y)-K(x^{\prime},y)|+|K(y,x)-K(y,x^{\prime})|\leq\omega\left(\frac{|x-x^{\prime}|}{|x-y|}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle\text{ whenever }|x-y|>2|x-x^{\prime}|.

Here
\omega:(0,1)\to(0,1)
is a non-decreasing function, satisfying
\omega(2x)\leq c\cdot\omega(x)
(called modulus of continuity) and the Dini condition
\int_{0}^{1}\omega(t)/tdt<\infty
. Also it is supposed that
T
has a bounded extension on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If the function
\omega
satisfies the logarithmic Dini condition

(6.5)

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega(t)\log(1/t)}{t}dt<\infty,

then the operator (
6.1
) satisfies (
1.15
).

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
B=B(x_{0},R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the Euclidean open ball of a center
x_{0}
and a radius
R
. One can choose
B^{*}=B(x_{0},R^{*})
as a hull ball of
B
, where
R^{*}=(1+2\cdot 2^{1/d})R\in[R,5R]
. For a point
x\in B=B(x_{0},R)
, using (
6.4
) and (
6.5
), we get

(6.6)

\displaystyle|T(f\cdot

\displaystyle\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B^{*}})(x)-T(f\cdot\textbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B^{*}})(x_{0})|

(6.7)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{|y-x_{0}|>R^{*}}|K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)||f(y)|dy

(6.8)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{2^{k+1}R^{*}\geq|y-x_{0}|>2^{k}R^{*}}\omega\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{|x_{0}-y|}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{|x_{0}-y|^{d}}|f(y)|dy

(6.9)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\omega(2^{-k})\frac{1}{k|B(x_{0},2^{k+1}r)|}\int_{B(x_{0},2^{k+1}r)}|f(y)|dy

(6.10)

\displaystyle\lesssim[\omega]\sup_{A\supset B}\bigg(\log^{-1}\left(1+\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B)}\right)\langle f\rangle_{A}\bigg),

where
\sup
is taken over all the balls
A\supset B
. Thus we obtain (
1.15
).
∎

Consider the classical singular operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, corresponding to kernels

(6.11)

K(x)=\frac{\Omega(x)}{|x|^{d}},

where
\Omega(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\Omega(\varepsilon x)=\Omega(x),\,\varepsilon>0,\quad\int_{S^{d-1}}\Omega(x)d\sigma=0,

\displaystyle\omega(\delta)=\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|x-x^{\prime}|\leq\delta}}{{|x|=|x^{\prime}|=1}}}|\Omega(x)-\Omega(x^{\prime})|,\quad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\omega(t)}{t}dt<\infty.

(see
[
21
]
, chap. 2.4, for precise definition). Under these conditions the operator (
6.1
) with the kernel
K(x,y)=K(x-y)
generates a Calderón–Zygmund operator.

Lemma 6.2
.

The singular operator, corresponding to the kernel (
6.11
) is uniformly vanishing.

Proof.

For an arbitrary ball
B=B(x_{0},r)
consider a ball
B^{\prime}=B(x_{0},R)
with a radius
R>r
. Let
B^{\prime\prime}\supset B^{\prime}
be an arbitrary ball. For
x,x^{\prime}\in B
we have

\displaystyle T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})(x)-T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{\prime\prime}}K(x-y)dy-\int_{B^{\prime\prime}}K(x^{\prime}-y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{x-B^{\prime\prime}}K(y)dy-\int_{x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime}}K(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{(x-B^{\prime\prime})\setminus(x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime})}K(y)dy-\int_{(x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime})\setminus(x-B^{\prime\prime})}K(y)dy.

Thus,

(6.12)

|T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})(x)-T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})(x^{\prime})|\leq\int_{(x-B^{\prime\prime})\triangle(x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime})}|K(y)|dy.

Observe that the set
(x-B^{\prime\prime})\triangle(x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime})
is in the
r
neighborhood of the boundary
\partial(B^{\prime\prime})
. That is

(6.13)

(x-B^{\prime\prime})\triangle(x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime})\subset\{y:\,{\rm dist}(y,\partial B^{\prime\prime})<r\}\subset(B(x_{0},R-r))^{c}.

Thus by passing to spherical coordinates, we can write

\int_{(x-B^{\prime\prime})\triangle(x^{\prime}-B^{\prime\prime})}|K(y)|dy=c_{d}\int_{S^{d-1}}\int_{t_{1}(s)}^{t_{2}(s)}t^{d-1}|K(t\cdot s)|dtds,

where
t_{2}(s)-t_{1}(s)<2r
and
t_{1}(s)>R-r
. Then we obtain

(6.14)

\displaystyle|T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})(x)-T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}})(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}\|\Omega\|_{\infty}\int_{S^{d-1}}\int_{t_{1}(s)}^{t_{2}(s)}\frac{t^{d-1}}{t^{d}}dtds

(6.15)

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}\|\Omega\|_{\infty}\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{2r}{t_{1}(s)}ds

(6.16)

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}\|\Omega\|_{\infty}\frac{2r}{R-r}.

The latter can be less than any
\varepsilon>0
by a suitable choice of
R
. This implies that
{\rm OSC}_{B}(T(\textbf{1}_{B^{\prime\prime}}))\leq\varepsilon

for any ball
B^{\prime\prime}\supset B^{\prime}
. That means
T
is uniformly vanishing.
∎

Observe that under the logarithmic Dini condition (
6.5
), the singular operator corresponding to kernel (
6.11
) satisfies (
1.15
) according to Lemma
6.1
. Applying also Lemma
6.2
, we obtain

Theorem 6.1
.

If a modulus of continuity
\omega(t)
satisfies the condition (
6.5
),
then the singular operator
T
, corresponding to the kernel (
6.11
) is bounded on
{\rm BMO}
. Namely, for any
f\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
\|T(f)\|_{\rm BMO}\lesssim\|f\|_{\rm BMO}
.

6.2.
Carleson type operators

Now let
K(x,y)
be a Calderón-Zygmund kernel with a modulus of continuity, satisfying (
6.5
). Consider the family of operators

(6.17)

T_{\alpha}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)g_{\alpha}(x)f(y)dy,

where
\sup_{\alpha}\|g_{\alpha}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. The argument of Lemma
6.1
shows that the operators
T_{\alpha}
satisfy (
1.15
) uniformly. Thus, we can say that
T(f)=\sup_{\alpha}|T_{\alpha}(f)|

satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
, provided
\|T\|_{L^{r}\to L^{r,\infty}}<\infty
. Hence, applying Theorem
1.3
, we conclude that
T
maps boundedly
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into
{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This can be applied for Carleson type operators. Namely,
recently P. Zorin-Kranich
[
27
]
considered operators

(6.18)

Tf(x)=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{d}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)e^{2\pi iQ(y)}f(y)dy\right|

where
K(x,y):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Hölder continuous Calderón-Zygmund kernel and
\mathcal{Q}^{n}
denotes the family of polynomials in
d
variables with degree at most
n
. It was proved that operators (
5.7
) are bounded on
L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
1<r<\infty
. In the case of Hilbert kernel
K(x,y)=(x-y)^{-1}
, this was earlier proved by V. Lie in
[
15
]
. The Hölder continuous Calderón-Zygmund kernels satisfy (
6.4
) with
w(t)=t^{\delta}
,
\delta>0
, and so we will have (
6.5
). Thus, applying Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, we arrive to the following

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\{Q_{\alpha}\}
be a family of polynomials from
\mathcal{Q}^{n}
and let
K(x,y)
be a Calderón-Zygmund kernel with the modulus of continuity
w(t)=t^{\delta}
,
\delta>0
. Then for the operator

(6.19)

Tf(x)=\sup_{\alpha}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(x,y)e^{2\pi iQ_{\alpha}(y)}f(y)dy\right|

we have bounds (
1.19
) and (
1.21
). In particular, the operator
T
maps
L^{\infty}
into
{\rm BMO}
and

\|T(f)\|_{{\rm BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\infty}.

Remark 6.1
.

The same argument is applicable for Calderón-Zygmund operators on bounded intervals, including periodic cases. In particular, the result of Theorem
6.2
also holds
for the maximal Fourier partial sums in trigonometric and Walsh systems.

6.3.
Martingales

Let our measure space
X
be finite and let
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
be a filtration defined in
X
. We suppose that
\mathfrak{B}=\cup_{n\geq 0}\mathfrak{B}_{n}
is the corresponding ball-basis and it satisfies the doubling condition. Given
f\in L^{1}(X)
consider the martingale difference

(6.20)

\Delta_{A}f(x)=\sum_{B:\,\mathrm{pr}(B)=A}\left(\frac{1}{\mu(B)}\int_{B}f-\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\int_{A}f\right)\textbf{1}_{B}(x).

corresponding to our filtration. Recall the definition of Burkholder’s
[
2
]
martingale transform, namely the operators of the form

(6.21)

M_{\varepsilon}f(x)=\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{B}}\varepsilon_{A}\Delta_{A}f(x),\quad|\varepsilon_{A}|\leq 1.

We consider also other operators related to (
6.21
):

(6.22)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon,n}f(x)=\sum_{A\in\cup_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathfrak{B}_{k}}\varepsilon_{A}\Delta_{A}f(x),

(6.23)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}^{*}f(x)=\sup_{n}|M_{\varepsilon,n}f(x)|,

(6.24)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}^{+}f(x)=\sup_{n}M_{\varepsilon,n}f(x),

(6.25)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}^{-}f(x)=\inf_{n}M_{\varepsilon,n}f(x),

(6.26)

\displaystyle Sf(x)=\left(\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{B}}\left|\Delta_{A}f(x)\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Letting
T
to be one of the these operators, one can check that

(6.27)

{\rm OSC}_{B}\big(T(f\cdot\textbf{1}_{X\setminus B^{*}})\big)=0.

for any ball
B\in\cup_{k\leq n}\mathfrak{B}_{k}
. Indeed, if
A\subseteq B^{*}
, then
\Delta_{A}(f\cdot\textbf{1}_{X\setminus B^{*}})
is identically zero. Otherwise we have
B^{*}\subsetneq A
and therefore
\Delta_{A}(f\cdot\textbf{1}_{X\setminus B^{*}})
is constant on
B^{*}
. All these imply that for all above operators
T(f\cdot\textbf{1}_{X\setminus B^{*}})
is constant on
B
. Thus we get (
6.27
). This means all these operators are
{\rm BO}
operators, since (
1.15
) holds for any modulus of continuity
\omega
. Also, it is easy to check that all of them are uniformly vanishing. It is well known that these operators are bounded on
L^{p}
spaces if
1<p\leq\infty
even if the filtration is not doubling (see
[
6
,
5
]
). Thus we can say that all those operators satisfy bound (
1.18
) and are bounded on
{\rm BMO}(X)
.

6.4.
Wavelet type systems

Recall the definition of biorthogonal wavelet type system
\Phi=\{\phi_{k}(x),\psi_{n}(x)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
on
[0,1]
. We will use the function

(6.28)

\xi(x)=\frac{1}{(1+|x|)^{1+\delta}},\quad 0<\delta<1,

as well as the notations

(6.29)

\displaystyle t_{1}=\frac{1}{2},\quad t_{k}=\frac{2j-1}{2^{n+1}},\quad k\geq 2,

(6.30)

\displaystyle\text{where }k=2^{n}+j,\,1\leq j\leq 2^{n},\,n=0,1,2,\ldots.

Hence we suppose

(6.31)

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x)=\psi_{0}(x)\equiv 1,

(6.32)

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}(x)|+|\psi_{k}(x)|\leq c2^{n/2}\cdot\xi\big(2^{n}(x-t_{k})\big),\quad k\geq 1,

(6.33)

\displaystyle|\phi_{k}(t)-\phi_{k}(t^{\prime})|+|\psi_{k}(t)-\psi_{k}(t^{\prime})|

(6.34)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq c2^{n/2}(2^{n}|t-t^{\prime}|)^{\alpha}\cdot\xi\big(2^{n}(t-t_{k})\big)\text{ if }|t-t^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-n},

where
0<\alpha\leq 1
. It is well known that the expansion

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle f,\psi_{n}\rangle\phi_{n}(x),\quad\langle f,\psi_{n}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\psi_{n}(t)dt,

of any function
f\in L^{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, in a wavelet type system converges in
L^{p}
unconditionally. Moreover, the wavelet type systems share the following property of unconditional bases of
L^{p}
: for any sequence
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{k}\}
,
\|\Lambda\|=\sup_{k}|\lambda_{k}|\leq 1
, and a function
f\in L^{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, the series
T_{\Lambda}(f)=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\langle f,\psi_{k}\rangle\phi_{k}
converges in
L^{p}
and
\left\|T_{\Lambda}(f)\right\|_{p}\lesssim\|f\|_{p}
.
This property can be established by a well-known argument (see for example
[
10
]
), proving that
K_{\lambda}(x,t)=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(t)
is a Calderón-Zygmund kernel, namely

(6.35)

\displaystyle\left|K_{\Lambda}(x,y)\right|+\left|K_{\Lambda}(y,x)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{|x-y|},

(6.36)

\displaystyle|K_{\Lambda}(x,y)-K_{\Lambda}(x,y^{\prime})|+|K_{\Lambda}(y,x)-K_{\Lambda}(y^{\prime},x)|\lesssim\frac{|y-y^{\prime}|^{\beta}}{|x-y|^{1+\beta}},\quad\beta>0,

whenever
|x-y|>2|y-y^{\prime}|
. Moreover, if in addition
\{\phi_{k}\}
is a basis in
L^{2}[0,1]
, then it becomes an unconditional basis in all spaces
L^{p}[0,1]
,
1<p<\infty
(see for example
[
10
]
). Concerning to the unconditional wavelet bases in
L^{p}
, we can also refer the readers to the papers
[
17
,
25
,
9
,
26
,
19
]
. Examples of wavelet type systems are many classical wavelet systems, orthonormal spline systems on
[0,1]
. Wavelet type systems were considered also on atomic Hardy space
H^{1}[0,1]
. The existence of an unconditional basis in
H^{1}[0,1]
first was proved by Maurey
[
16
]
. Later L. Carleson
[
4
]
constructed an explicit sequence in
{\rm BMO}
whose biorthogonal system forms an unconditional basis in
H^{1}
. Wojtaszczyk
[
24
]
proved that Franklin system (the picewise linear orthonormal spline system on
[0,1]
) is an unconditional basis for
H^{1}[0,1]
. This was the first explicit example of orthonormal system, which is an unconditional basis for
H^{1}[0,1]
.

Hence we can say that the operators
T_{\Lambda}
with
\|\Lambda\|\leq 1
form a family of Calderón-Zygmund operators with uniformly bounded constants. Clearly, those also are uniformly vanishing. Indeed, from (
6.31
) it follows that
T_{\lambda}(\textbf{1}_{[0,1]})\equiv\lambda_{0}
and then,
{\rm OSC}_{[0,1]}(T_{\lambda}(\textbf{1}_{[0,1]}))=0
. Choose an arbitrary family of sequences
\Lambda_{\alpha}=\{\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\}
with
\|\Lambda_{\alpha}\|\leq 1
. This generate the maximal operators

(6.37)

T^{*}(f)=\sup_{\alpha}|T_{\Lambda_{\alpha}}(f)|,\quad T^{\pm}(f)=\sup_{\alpha}(\pm T_{\Lambda_{\alpha}}(f))

which satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
. Thus we conclude

Theorem 6.3
.

Operators in (
6.37
) satisfy the bound (
1.18
). In particular, they map
{\rm BMO}[0,1]
into itself.

Recall that
{\rm BMO}[0,1]
is the dual space of
H^{1}[0,1]
. So by the duality argument, for
f\in H^{1}
and
g\in{\rm BMO}
we have

(6.38)

\langle T_{\Lambda}(f),g\rangle=\langle f,\bar{T}_{\Lambda}(g)\rangle\leq\|f\|_{H^{1}}\|\cdot\|\bar{T}_{\Lambda}(g)\|_{\rm BMO}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}}\cdot\|g\|_{{\rm BMO}},

where
\bar{T}_{\Lambda}
denotes the adjoint operator of
T_{\Lambda}
. This implies the estimate
\|T_{\Lambda}(f)\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}}
, which is the characteristic bound for the unconditional basis in
H^{1}
. Hence we can get the following.

Corollary 6.1
.

If a biorthogonal wavelet type system is a basis in
L^{2}[0,1]
, then it is an unconditional basis in
H^{1}[0,1]
.
