Macroscopic fluctuation theory of correlations in hard rod gas
Anupam Kundu

Abstract
Recently, a theoretical framework known as ballistic macroscopic fluctuation theory has been developed to study large-scale fluctuations and correlations in many-body systems exhibiting ballistic transport. In this paper, we review this theory in the context of a one-dimensional gas of hard rods. The initial configurations of the rods are sampled from a probability distribution characterized by slowly varying conserved density profiles across space. Beginning from a microscopic description, we first formulate the macroscopic fluctuation theory in terms of the phase-space density of quasiparticles. In the second part, we apply this framework to compute the two-point, two-time correlation functions of the conserved densities in the Euler scaling limit. We derive an explicit expression for the correlation function, which not only reveals its inherent symmetries but is also straightforward to evaluate numerically for a given initial state. Our results also recover known expressions for space-time correlations in equilibrium for the hard rod gas.

Introduction

Understanding non-equilibrium dynamics in many-particle systems is a fundamental problem in statistical physics
[
1
,
2
]
. Often, the focus is on the large-scale evolution of macroscopic quantities, such as the densities of conserved quantities, starting from non-equilibrium states. Studying such evolution from a microscopic perspective is challenging, except in cases where particles (or degrees of freedom) do not interact. In systems with short-range interactions, hydrodynamic (HD) theory offers a universal framework
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. This theory is based on the assumptions of local equilibration and enables one to write evolution equations for conserved densities, which form closed sets of differential equations
[
6
,
5
,
7
]
.

At the largest space-time scales, HD theory yields the Euler equations, describing ballistic evolution. To capture features in density profiles at smaller, sub-ballistic scales, dissipation terms - often introduced phenomenologically, such as viscosity and Fourier terms - are added, as in the Navier-Stokes equations for simple fluids
[
8
]
. Although HD theory is a classical framework, it has been remarkably successful empirically across diverse contexts, including normal fluids
[
9
]
, magnetic liquid
[
10
]
, ultracold atoms
[
11
]
, plasma
[
12
]
, energy transport in low dimension
[
13
,
14
]
and active matter
[
15
]
. However, a universally accepted derivation of HD equations from microscopic dynamics remains elusive.

In the past decade, significant advancements have been made in hydrodynamic theory, extending the hydrodynamic approximation to integrable systems that possess an infinite number of conserved quantities. This extension, known as generalized hydrodynamics (GHD), describes the evolution of these conserved densities, contrasting with the finite set considered in conventional hydrodynamics
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. The GHD equations are elegantly formulated in terms of the evolution of stable quasiparticle densities
[
5
]
. While integrability is a fine-tuned property, many systems near integrability exhibit striking effects on large-scale relaxation dynamics. This has spurred intense interest in exploring GHD, which has proven highly successful in understanding ballistic-scale motion in many systems
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.

Another key objective in the statistical mechanics of many-particle systems is to understand the structure of correlations - both static and dynamic - and the fluctuations of local observables. This requires moving beyond the description of average conserved densities and associated average fluxes to develop a statistical theory of fluctuations on macroscopic scales. Originally such an attempt was made by Landau and Lifshitz
[
6
,
27
,
28
]
by including a stochastic forcing to each dissipative flux
[
28
]
. This approach has recently been applied to compute equilibrium space-time correlations in Toda system
[
29
,
30
]
. However such a procedure fails to describe anomalous energy transport in low-dimensional systems
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
for which a non-linear extension of the linear fluctuating HD theory has been introduced
[
13
,
34
]
. In the realm of diffusive hydrodynamics, the macroscopic fluctuation theory (MFT) provides a universal approach
[
35
]
. MFT, rooted in large deviation theory, characterizes the probabilities of density and current fluctuations at diffusive scales
[
36
]
.

Recently, a similar large deviation framework has been developed for integrable systems, known as ballistic macroscopic fluctuation theory (BMFT)
[
37
,
38
]
. BMFT describes density and current fluctuations at ballistic scales and is broadly applicable, even to systems with a small number of conserved quantities that exhibit ballistic transport.
BMFT is grounded in the principle of local relaxation, which essentially says that fluctuations of any local observable on the largest space-time scale are determined by the fluctuations of the conserved densities carried coherently from initial state.

One of the most striking predictions of BMFT is the emergence of long-range correlations in systems with short-range interactions
[
37
,
38
]
. These correlations develop over long times in non-stationary states, even when starting from initial configurations with only short-range correlations. As an intriguing consequence of these long-range correlations, it has been shown that the standard Navier-Stokes correction to hard-rod dynamics does not remain valid
[
39
,
40
]
except only over a short early period of time
[
41
]
. Using BMFT, such long-range correlations have been analytically computed, demonstrated, and verified numerically for hard rods in one dimension
[
38
,
39
]
. A gas of hard rods is a simple yet non-trivial interacting integrable system for which one can explicitly illustrate many features of GHD and BMFT by performing both microscopic as well as macroscopic calculations
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
.

The goal of this paper is to review BMFT in the context of hard rods and present an alternative description of BMFT in terms of phase space density of quasiparticles and apply this theory to compute the unequal space-time correlation of conserved densities.
A collection of hard rods was first considered by L. Tonks in 1930 as the simplest model of an interacting gas that exhibits thermodynamic behavior distinct from ideal gas laws
[
45
]
.
Due to it’s solvable structure, this model and it’s variants have been widely used in equilibrium physics of simple fluids to compute and understand various thermodynamic quantities such as density fluctuations, pair correlation functions and distribution functions
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
. Later microscopic approaches were also used to study time evolution
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
. Hydrodynamic equation for the phase space density was first derived by Percus from Boltzmann equation
[
54
]
to study dynamics of the gas on macroscopic scale.

In a hard rod gas, particles undergo elastic collisions and are not allowed to approach each other closer than a distance

a
, which can be interpreted as the interaction range or the rod length. Due to elastic collisions, particles exchange momenta upon interaction. Several equilibrium properties, such as density fluctuations, pair correlation functions, and distribution functions, have been analytically computed for this model. As an artefact of integrability, the dynamics of a gas of

N
hard rods can be mapped to that of
N
hard-point particles, and vice versa. This mapping was used previously in several studies
[
50
,
54
]
and has since been exploited to study non-equilibrium dynamics
[
51
]
. With the advent of GHD, interest in this model has surged as a platform to demonstrate and test the predictions of both GHD and BMFT.

In this paper, we exploit the mapping to the hard-point gas to solve the BMFT equations and use these solutions to compute two-point, two-time correlations of conserved densities. We provide explicit expressions that are easy to evaluate to numerically. Our results reproduce previous findings for the two-point correlation in hard-rod gas derived from Landau-Lifshitz theory in equilibrium and from previous account of BMFT in non-equilibrium initial state.

The paper is organised as follows. In section
2
we start by providing microscopic description of the hard rod gas, which is followed by a development of the BMFT in terms of the phase space density (PSD) of hard rod quasiparticles. The BMFT equations are derived through a saddle point method. We end this section by discussing how one can use this theory to compute unequal space-time correlation of the PSD of hard rods. In the next section
3
, we provide an equivalent formulation of BMFT of hard rod gas in terms of the PSD of a hard-point gas using an exact mapping of the microscopic configurations. Solutions of the BMFT equations are obtained in hard point picture sec.
4
. We also show in
C
that, the same solution can also be obtained by solving the equations in hard rod picture as well. These solutions are are finally used in sec.
5
to compute the space-time correlations. We present computation of equilibrium space-time correlations in sec.
5.2
whereas explicit expression of unequal space-time correlation of mass densities in non-equilibrium state is presented in sec.
5.3
. In sec.
6
, we conclude our paper with possible future extensions. To increase the readability of the paper we have relegated a substantial amount of calculations in several appendices.

BMFT for hard rod gas

We consider
N
hard rods, each of unit mass and length
a
, moving on a straight line. The positions and momenta of the rods are denoted by
\{\mathtt{x}_{i},v_{i}\}
for
i=1,2,...,N
. Since mass of each particle is unity, the momentum of each particle is its velocity. These rods move ballistically and when they meet they collide elastically. At each collision they exchange their momenta.
Initially the rods are placed inside a region of length
L
and eventually we take the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
,
L\to\infty
making the initial density profile a finite valued function
\varrho_{0}(\mathtt{x})
everywhere. The dynamics of this system is integrable in the sense that it has
N
locally conserved quantities
Q_{\nu}
which can be chosen as the sum of the moments of the individual velocities

\displaystyle Q_{\nu}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}^{\nu},~~\nu=1,2,...,N.

(1)

We consider the following joint probability distribution from which the initial positions and velocities of the rods are chosen:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rm r}(\{\mathtt{x}_{i},v_{i}\})=\frac{1}{Z_{r}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Psi(\mathtt{x}_{i},v_{i})\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\Theta(\mathtt{x}_{i+1}-\mathtt{x}_{i}-a),

(2)

where
\Theta(\mathtt{x})
is the Heaviside theta function,
Z_{\rm r}
is the normalisation factor and
\int d\mathtt{x}\int dv~\Psi(\mathtt{x},v)=1
. We assume the initial state is slowly varying over space characterised by the following choice

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathtt{x},v)=e^{-\psi(\mathtt{x}/\ell,v)},

(3)

with
\psi(x,v)
being some suitable function which goes to infinity for
|x|\to\infty
and
|v|\to\infty
. The parameter
\ell
represents the length scale of variation which we assume to be large. For large
N
and
\ell
, keeping

, the partition function

can be written as integrals over slowly varying fields. In order to see that, we divide the space into regions of size
\sim\ell
labelled by
\mathtt{y}_{k}=k\ell
(see Fig.
1
). Breaking the integral into blocks of sizes
\ell
, the partition function

can be approximated by

\displaystyle Z_{\rm r}\approx\sum_{\{N_{k}\}}~\delta_{\sum_{k}N_{k},N}\prod_{k}\int_{\Box_{\ell}^{k}}\prod_{j}d\mathtt{x}_{j}dv_{j}~e^{-\sum_{j\in\Box_{\ell}^{k}}\psi(\mathtt{x}_{j},v_{j})}\Theta\left(\mathtt{x}_{j+1}-\mathtt{x}_{j}-a\right),

(4)

where
\Box_{\ell}^{k}
represents phase space corresponding to the
k^{\rm th}
block and
\{N_{k};k=...-1,0,1,...\}
is the possible number distribution of particles over these blocks such that
\sum_{k}N_{k}=N
. Note

is a random distribution.
Since

3

\displaystyle\approx\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\sum_{\{N_{k}\}}~\prod_{k}Z^{k}_{\rm r}(N_{k},\ell),

\displaystyle=\int_{\Box_{\ell}^{k}}\prod_{j}d\mathtt{x}_{j}dv_{j}~e^{-\sum_{j\in\Box_{\ell}^{k}}(\psi(k,v_{j})+\mu N_{k})}\Theta\left(\mathtt{x}_{j+1}-\mathtt{x}_{j}-a\right).

Note

represents the grand partition function of a homogeneous gas of hard rods in a box of size
\ell
. One can anticipate that

\displaystyle\approx\int...\int d\mathfrak{\mathfrak{f}}_{v|k}~\exp\left\{-N_{k}\sum_{v}\Delta v\left[(\psi(k,v)+\mu)\mathfrak{\mathfrak{f}}_{v|k}+\mathfrak{\mathfrak{f}}_{v|k}\ln\left(\frac{N_{k}\mathfrak{\mathfrak{f}}_{v|k}}{\ell-aN_{k}}\right)\right]\right\},

120

As the rods move ballistically, the PSD
\mathfrak{f}(x,v)

The initial fluctuation described by PSD

\mathfrak{f}_{\rm eq}(x,v)

\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)+\partial_{x}\big(v_{\rm eff}[\mathfrak{f}]\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)\big)=0,

\displaystyle v_{\rm eff}[\mathfrak{f}](x,v,t)=\frac{v-a\varrho(x,t)\vartheta(x,t)}{1-a\varrho(x,t)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm r}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)]\asymp\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}e^{-\ell\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm r}_{\mu}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,0)]}~\delta\bigg[\partial_{t}\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)+\partial_{x}v_{\rm eff}[\mathfrak{f}]\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)\bigg],

) and the hydrodynamic representation of the observable
\mathcal{B}(\ell t)
in Eq. (
27
), the generating function (GF) of
\mathcal{B}
can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{B}}(\mathtt{T}=\ell T)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\big\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\big\langle}$}}e^{-\lambda\mathcal{B}(\ell T)}\mathclose{\hbox{${\big\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\big\rangle}$}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm r}}

(30)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\int\mathcal{D}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)]~e^{-\ell\Delta\mathcal{F}_{\mu}^{\rm r}[f(x,v,0)]}e^{-\ell\lambda\int dx\int du\mathfrak{b}(x,v)\mathfrak{f}(x,v,T)}

(31)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\times~\delta\bigg[\partial_{t}\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)+\partial_{x}v_{\rm eff}[\mathfrak{f}]\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)\bigg].~~

(32)

The notation
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}....\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm r}}
represents average over functions (or functionals) of
\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)
with respect to the distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\rm r}[\mathfrak{f}(t)]
.
Introducing an integral representation of the delta functional through an auxiliary field
\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{B}}(\mathtt{T}=\ell T)=\mathopen{\hbox{${\big\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\big\langle}$}}e^{-\lambda\mathcal{B}(\ell T)}\mathclose{\hbox{${\big\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\big\rangle}$}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm r}}=\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\int\mathcal{D}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)]~e^{-\ell\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)]}

(33)

where,

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda,\mu}[\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{h}]=\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm r}_{\mu}[\mathfrak{f}(0)]+\lambda\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}}dt\int dx\int dv\left\{\mathfrak{b}(x,v)\delta(t-T)\mathfrak{f}(t)+\mathfrak{h}(t)\left[\partial_{t}\mathfrak{f}(t)+\partial_{x}v_{\rm eff}\mathfrak{f}(t)\right]\right\},

and
\tilde{T}
is some time larger that
T
. In the above, we have used the short hand notations
\mathfrak{f}(t)=\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)
and
\mathfrak{h}(t)=\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)
. For large
N
and
\ell
, the path integral can be computed by the saddle point method and one gets

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{B}}(\ell T)\asymp\exp\left(-\ell~\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda,T)\right),~~\text{with,}~~\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda,T)=\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda,\mu^{*}}[\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(t),\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(t)],

(35)

where the saddle point solutions
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(t),~\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(t)
and
\mu^{*}
satisfy

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda,\mu^{*}}}{\delta\mathfrak{f}}\right)_{\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda},\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}}=0,~\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda,\mu^{*}}}{\delta\mathfrak{h}}\right)_{\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda},\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}}=0,~~\text{and}~~\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda,\mu}[\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda},\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}]}{\partial\mu}\right)_{\mu=\mu^{*}}=0.

(36)

Explicitly these equations read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)+\partial_{x}\big(v_{\rm eff}~\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)\big)=0

(37a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)+v_{\rm eff}(x,t)\partial_{x}\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)

(37b)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~+\int du\frac{a(v_{\rm eff}-v)}{1-a\varrho(x,t)}\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,u,t)\partial_{x}\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,u,t)=\lambda\mathfrak{b}(x,v)\delta(t-T),

(37c)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,T)=0,

(37d)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)=\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm r}_{\mu}[\mathfrak{f}]}{\delta\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)},

(37e)

along with the normalisation condition

\displaystyle\ell\int dx\int dv~\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)=N,

(38)

and the following boundary conditions

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)\big|_{x\to\pm\infty}=0,~~\text{and}~~\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)\big|_{x\to\pm\infty}=0~~\forall~t.

(39)

The second boundary condition is required for the function to be normalised as in Eq. (
38
). The normalisation condition in Eq. (
38
) fixes the chemical constant
\mu^{*}
. Solving these equations and evaluating
\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda,\mu}[\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{h}]
on these solutions, one gets the cumulant generating function
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda,T)
defined in Eq. (
35
). Taking derivative of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda,T)
with respect to
\lambda
one can compute different cumulants of
\mathcal{B}(\ell T)
. In this paper we are interested to compute two-point-two-time correlation of the PSD using the above described method.

The correlation of PSDs at two macroscopically separated locations at two different times
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathtt{x}_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a};\mathtt{x}_{b},u_{b};\mathtt{t}_{b})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a})\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}_{b},u_{b},\mathtt{t}_{b})\rangle-\langle\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a})\rangle\langle\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}_{b},u_{b},\mathtt{t}_{b})\rangle,

(40)

where
\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x},u,\mathtt{t})
is the PSD in microscopic coordinates such that
\int d\mathtt{x}\int du\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x},u,\mathtt{t})=N
. In order to express
\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x},u,\mathtt{t})
in terms of
\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)
, we notice that
N=\sum_{k}N_{k}\approx\sum_{v}\sum_{k}N_{v|k}
where
N_{v|k}=N_{k}\mathfrak{f}_{v|k}\Delta v
[recall
\mathfrak{f}_{v|k}
was introduced before Eq. (
7
)]. Taking continuum approximations of the sums over
k
and
v
in
N\approx\sum_{v}\Delta v\sum_{k}\ell~\mathfrak{\mathfrak{f}}_{v|k}\varrho_{k}
, one has
N=\int d\mathtt{y}\int dv~\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{y},v,\mathtt{t})\approx\ell\int dy\int dv~\mathfrak{f}(y,v,t)
. Since
d\mathtt{y}=\ell dy
, this suggests one to identify
\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{y}=y\ell,v,\mathtt{t}=t\ell):=\mathfrak{f}(y,v,t)
which in the integral form can be written as

\displaystyle\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}=x\ell,u,\mathtt{t}=t\ell)

\displaystyle=\int d\mathtt{y}\int dw~\delta(\mathtt{y}-\mathtt{x})\delta(w-u)\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{y},w,\mathtt{t})

(41)

\displaystyle=\int dy\int dw~\delta(x-y)\delta(u-w)~\mathfrak{f}(y,w,t).

(42)

It is easy to see that the two point correlation defined in Eq. (
40
) can be recast as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathtt{x}_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a};\mathtt{x}_{b},u_{b};\mathtt{t}_{b})

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\frac{\langle\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}_{b},u_{b},\mathtt{t}_{b})e^{-\lambda\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}(\mathtt{x}_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a})}\rangle_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm r}}}{\langle e^{-\lambda\mathtt{b}(\mathtt{x}_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a})}\rangle_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm r}}}\right]_{\lambda=0}.

(43)

Approximating
\langle...\rangle_{\mathbb{P}_{\rm r}}
by
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}...\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm r}}
, one finds

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{C}&(\mathtt{x}_{a}=\ell x_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a}=\ell t_{a};\mathtt{x}_{b}=\ell x_{b},u_{b};\mathtt{t}_{b}=\ell t_{b})\cr&=-\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left\{\frac{\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\int\mathcal{D}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)]~\mathfrak{f}(x_{b},u_{b},t_{b})e^{-\ell\mathcal{S}^{\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}}_{\frac{\lambda}{\ell}}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)]}}{\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\int\mathcal{D}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)]~e^{-\ell\mathcal{S}_{\frac{\lambda}{\ell}}[\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),\mathfrak{h}(x,v,t)]}}\right\}\right]_{\lambda=0}\end{split}

(44)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}}_{\lambda}[\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{h}]=\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm r}_{\mu}[\mathfrak{f}(0)]+\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}}dt\int dx\int dv\left\{\lambda\delta(x-x_{a})\delta(v-u_{a})\delta(t-t_{a})\mathfrak{f}(t)+\mathfrak{h}(t)\left[\partial_{t}\mathfrak{f}(t)+\partial_{x}v_{\rm eff}\mathfrak{f}(t)\right]\right\},

(45)

where
\tilde{T}
is some time larger than
\max(t_{a},t_{b})
. For large
\ell
, performing the path integrals, both in the numerator and the denominator of Eq. (
44
), using saddle point method one finds that the correlation
\mathcal{C}
has the following scaling form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{C}(\mathtt{x}_{a}=\ell x_{a},u_{a},\mathtt{t}_{a}=\ell t_{a};\mathtt{x}_{b}=\ell x_{b},u_{b};\mathtt{t}_{b}=\ell t_{b})&=\frac{1}{\ell}\mathscr{C}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a};x_{b},u_{b},t_{b}),\cr\text{with,}~~~~~~~~\mathscr{C}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a};x_{b},u_{b},t_{b})&=-\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x_{b},u_{b},t_{b})\right]_{\lambda=0},\end{split}

(46)

where
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x,u,t)
for
0<t<T
is the saddle point PSD profile that minimises the action
\mathcal{S}^{\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}}_{\lambda}
in Eq. (
45
). One now has to solve the saddle point equations (
37
) with
\mathfrak{b}(x,v)=\delta(x-x_{a})\delta(v-u_{a})
with
T=t_{a}
which can be done in two ways. One can solve the Eqs. (
37
) directly in hard rod picture following a similar method considered in
[
38
]
. Another way is to formulate an equivalent version of the the BMFT problem using an exact mapping to hard point gas. For completeness and comprehensiveness, we discuss both ways of solving the BMFT problem. The solution method in hard rod picture is presented in
C
whereas the solution in the hard point picture is presented in the next section.

3
Computation of the saddle point solution using a mapping to hard point gas

To find the saddle point PSD
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)
, it seems convenient to map the microscopic dynamics of
N
hard rods to the dynamics of
N
hard-core point particles.
For each configuration
\{\mathtt{x}_{i},v_{i}\}
of
N
hard rods one can construct a configuration
\{\mathtt{x}_{i}^{\prime},v_{i}^{\prime}\}
of
N
hard point particles (each of unit mass). The mapping is obtained by removing the non-accessible spaces between successive rods and is given by
[
50
,
54
]

\displaystyle\mathtt{x}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathtt{x}_{i}-(i-1)a,~~v_{i}^{\prime}=v_{i},~~\text{for}~~i=1,2,...,N.

(47)

Since for each configuration of the hard rod gas, there is a unique hard point particle configuration, the ensemble of initial configurations of hard rods can in principle be described by an ensemble of hard point particles and vice-versa.
The joint distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\rm p}(\{\mathtt{x}_{i}^{\prime},v_{i}^{\prime}\})
of the initial positions and velocities
\{\mathtt{x}_{i}^{\prime},v_{i}^{\prime}\}
of the corresponding hard point particles can be easily obtained using this mapping and one finds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\rm p}(\{\mathtt{x}_{i}^{\prime},v_{i}^{\prime}\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\rm p}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Psi\left(\mathtt{x}_{i}^{\prime}+a\sum_{j\neq i}\Theta(\mathtt{x}^{\prime}_{i}-\mathtt{x}^{\prime}_{j}),v_{i}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\Theta(\mathtt{x}^{\prime}_{i+1}-\mathtt{x}^{\prime}_{i}),

(48)

with
\Psi(\mathtt{x},v)
slowly varying over length scale
\ell
as chosen by the form in Eq. (
3
). As seen in the case of hard rods in Eq. (
11
), for hard point gas also one expects a macroscopic description of the initial state with PSD
f(x,v)
which occurs with probability density

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm p}[f(x^{\prime},v,0)]

\displaystyle=\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\exp\left(-\ell\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(x^{\prime},v^{\prime},0)]\right)~\delta\left(\ell\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~f(x^{\prime},v,0)-N\right),

(49)

where
\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}=\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f]-\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f^{\rm eq}]
, with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(x^{\prime},v,0)]

\displaystyle=\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~f(x^{\prime},v,0)\left\{\psi(x^{\prime}+aF(x^{\prime},0),v)+\mu+\ln f(\mathtt{x}^{\prime},v,0)\right\},

(50)

where
x^{\prime}=\frac{\mathtt{x}^{\prime}}{\ell}
is the macroscopic scale coordinate. Here
F(x^{\prime},0)=\int dv^{\prime}~f(\mathtt{x}^{\prime},v^{\prime},0)

and
f^{\rm eq}(x^{\prime},v)
is the equilibrium/stationary PSD of the hard-point particles that minimises the above functional
\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f]
. The last term inside the curly bracket in the above equation appears from entropy of a gas of free point particles. As these particles move ballistically this statistical state also evolve, however slowly over a time scale
O(\ell)
. The PSD
f(x^{\prime},v,0)
evolves according to the Euler equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(x^{\prime},v,t)+v\partial_{x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime},v,t)=0.

(51)

Given the initial profile
f(x^{\prime},v,0)
, it is easy to solve this equation

\displaystyle f(x^{\prime},v,t)=f(x^{\prime}-vt,v,0),

(52)

which evidently conserves the total mass
\ell\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~f(x^{\prime},v,t)=\ell\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~f(x^{\prime},v,0)=N
.

In the Euler scaling limit the probability of the history of the PSD
f(x^{\prime},v,t)
profile over (scaled) time duration
0\leq t\leq\tilde{T}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm p}[f(x^{\prime},v,t)]\asymp\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}~e^{-\ell\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(x^{\prime},v,0)]}~\delta\bigg[\partial_{t}f(x^{\prime},v,t)+v\partial_{x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime},v,t)\bigg],

(53)

which, after introducing an integral representation of the delta functional, can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\rm p}[f(x^{\prime},v,t)]&\asymp\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\int\mathcal{D}[h(x^{\prime},v,t)]~e^{-\ell\mathscr{S}_{\mu}[f(x^{\prime},v,t),h(x^{\prime},v,t)]},\cr\text{where,}~~~\mathscr{S}_{\mu}[f,h]=\Delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(0)]&+\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}}dt\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~h(x^{\prime},v,t)\left\{\partial_{t}f(x^{\prime},v,t)+v\partial_{x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime},v,t)\right\}.\end{split}

(54)

The macro-scale coordinates
x^{\prime}
and the PSD
f(x^{\prime},v,t)
of the point particles are related to those of the hard rod gas via the following transformations
[
43
,
44
]

\displaystyle f(x^{\prime}_{f(t)}(x),v,t)=\frac{\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t)}{(1-a\varrho(x,t))},~~\text{with}~~x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}(t)}(x)=x-a\mathscr{F}_{\rm r}(x,t),

(55)

where

\displaystyle\varrho(x,t)=\int dv~\mathfrak{f}(x,v,t),~~\mathscr{F}_{\rm r}(x,t)=\int^{x}_{-\infty}dy~\varrho(y,t),

(56)

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}(x_{f(t)}(x^{\prime}),v,t)=\frac{f(x^{\prime},v,t)}{(1+a\rho(x^{\prime},t))},~~\text{with}~~x_{f(t)}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}+aF(x^{\prime},t).

(57)

where

\displaystyle\rho(x^{\prime},t)=\int dv~f(x^{\prime},v,t),~~F(x^{\prime},t)=\int^{x^{\prime}}_{-\infty}dy^{\prime}~\rho(y^{\prime},t).

(58)

Once the PSD
f(x^{\prime},v,t)
is known from the solution in Eq. (
52
), one can obtain the PSD
\mathfrak{f}_{\rm r}(x,v,t)
of hard rods at time
t
from Eq. (
57
). Recall that the fluctuation of the initial PSD
f(x^{\prime},v,0)
or
\mathfrak{f}(x,v,0)
gives rise to the fluctuation of the phase space densities at time
t
.
In the Euler scaling limit, one can write the observable
\mathcal{B}
defined in Eq. (
26
) in terms of of the point particle PSD
f(x^{\prime},v,t)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\mathtt{t}=\ell t)\approx\ell\int dy^{\prime}\int du~\mathfrak{b}(y^{\prime}+aF(y^{\prime},t),u)~f(y^{\prime},u,t),

(59)

which allows one to write the GF
\mathscr{Z}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{B}}
as path integrals over
f(x^{\prime},v,t)
and the auxiliary field
h(x^{\prime},v,t)
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{Z}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda}(\ell T)=\mathopen{\hbox{${\big\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\big\langle}$}}e^{-\lambda\mathcal{B}(\ell T)}\mathclose{\hbox{${\big\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\big\rangle}$}}_{\mathcal{P}_{\rm p}}=\int d\mu~e^{\mu N}\int\mathcal{D}[f(x,v,t),h(x,v,t)]~e^{-\ell\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\lambda}[f,h]}

(60)

where the action now reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{B}}=\mathscr{S}_{\mu}[f,h]+\lambda\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}}dt\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~\mathfrak{b}(x^{\prime}+aF(x^{\prime},t),v)\delta(t-T)f(x^{\prime},v,t),

(61)

with
\mathscr{S}_{\mu}[f,h]
given in Eq. (
54
). Similarly, the computation of the two-point-two-time (
i.e.,
unequal space-time) correlation defined in Eq. (
40
) can be expressed as path integrals as in Eq. (
44
) but now over
f(x^{\prime},v,t)
and
h(x^{\prime},v,t)
with
\mathfrak{b}(x,v)=\delta(x-x_{a})\delta(v-u_{a})
. Once again, for large
\ell
the path integral can be performed using saddle point method. Finally, in the point particle picture one has to minimise the following action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathtt{f}_{\rm r}}_{\lambda}[f,h]=\mathscr{S}_{\mu}[f,h]+\lambda\int_{0}^{\tilde{T}}dt\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~\delta(x^{\prime}+aF(x^{\prime},t)-x_{a})\delta(v-u_{a})\delta(t-t_{a})f(x^{\prime},v,t).

(62)

The saddle point solutions, denoted by
f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(t)
and
f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(t)
satisfy the following equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,t)+v\partial_{x^{\prime}}f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,t)=0

(63a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,t)+v\partial_{x^{\prime}}h_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,t)=\lambda~\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(t)}[\mathscr{B}(x_{f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(t)}(x^{\prime}),v)]\delta(t-t_{a}),

(63b)

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,\tilde{T})=0

(63c)

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,0)=\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(0)]}{\delta f(x^{\prime},v,0)}\right)_{f(0)=f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(0)},

(63d)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(x,v)=\delta(x-x_{a})\delta(v-u_{a}).

(64)

Derivation of the saddle point equations (
63
) are provided in
B
. We refer the equations (
63
) as the BMFT equations.
Recall from Eq. (
57
) that
x_{f(t)}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}+aF(x^{\prime},t)
and
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x_{f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(t)}(x^{\prime}),v,t)
is the saddle point PSD of hard rods corresponding to the saddle point PSD
f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(t)
of point particles. In the above we have defined the operation
\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{f}(t)}
acting on a function
H(x,v)
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{f}(t)}[H(x,v)]&=H(x,v)+a\int dy\int du~\Theta(y-x)\left\{\partial_{y}H(y,u)\right\}\mathfrak{f}(y,u,t).\end{split}

(65)

The saddle point equations should satisfy the boundary conditions

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime}\to\pm\infty,v,t)\to 0,~~\forall~t.

(66)

The boundary conditions for
h_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,t)
depends on the choices of the observable. The functional derivative of
\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(0)]
can be computed from Eq. (
50
) and it is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f(0)]}{\delta f(x^{\prime},v,0)}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{f}(0)}[\psi(x_{\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\rm n}(t)}(x^{\prime}),v)]+\ln f(x^{\prime},v,0)+\mu.\end{split}

(67)

The constant
\mu
is fixed from the normalisation
\ell\int dx^{\prime}\int dv~f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,0)=N
.
Note if the initial PSD
f_{\lambda}^{\rm sd}(x^{\prime},v,0)
satisfies this normalisation condition then by virtue of the equation (
63a
), the PSD at a later time will also satisfy the normalisation condition.

Given
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\rm n,\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,0)
one solves the saddle point equations (
63
) to find
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\rm n,\lambda}(x,v,t)
. This solution is then transformed to hard rod PSD
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)
using the transformation in Eq. (
57
). Using the solution
\mathfrak{f}^{\rm sd}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)
in Eq. (
46
) would provide the scaled correlation
\mathscr{C}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a};x_{b},u_{b},t_{b})
.

4
Solution of the saddle point equations

In this section we solve the saddle point equations (
63
) with
\mathscr{B}(x,v)=\delta(x-x_{a})\delta(v-u_{a})
. For notational simplicity, we, from now on omit the superscript ‘sd’ from the saddle point functions.
We assume
\tilde{T}
is some arbitrary time larger than
t_{a}

i.e.,

0\leq t_{a}<\tilde{T}
. Integrating both sides of Eq. (
63b
), we get the value of
h_{\lambda}(0)(x^{\prime},v,t_{a}^{-})=h_{\lambda}(0)(x^{\prime},v,t_{a}-\epsilon)|_{\epsilon\to 0}
as

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(0)(x^{\prime},v,t_{a}^{-})

\displaystyle=\lambda~\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(x_{f_{\lambda}(t_{a})}(x^{\prime}),v),

(68)

where we define

\displaystyle\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(x,v)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{R}_{f_{\lambda}(t_{a})}[\mathscr{B}(x,v)]=-\partial_{x_{a}}\left[\Theta(x_{a}-x)\left\{\delta(v-u_{a})-a\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a})\right\}\right].

(69)

Now evolving Eq. (
63b
) from
t=t_{a}^{-}
to
t=0
backward we get

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(0)(x^{\prime},v,0)=h_{\lambda}(0)(x^{\prime}+vt_{a},v,t_{a})=\lambda~\delta\mathtt{g}_{0}(x^{\prime},v),~~\text{with}~~\delta\mathtt{g}_{0}(x^{\prime},v)=\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(x_{f_{\lambda}(t_{a})}(x^{\prime}+vt_{a}),v).

(70)

Inserting this on the left hand side (lhs) of Eq. (
63d
) and using the functional derivative of
\mathcal{F}^{\rm p}_{\mu}[f_{\lambda}(0)]
from Eq. (
67
) along with Eq. (
65
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}[\psi(x_{f_{\lambda}(0)}(x^{\prime}),v)]+\ln f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,0)+\mu=\lambda~\delta\mathtt{g}_{0}(x^{\prime},v)=\lambda~\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(x_{f_{\lambda}(t_{a})}(x^{\prime}+vt_{a}),v).

(71)

One requires to solve this equation to find
f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,0)
which will now depend on
\lambda
as well as on the observable
\mathscr{B}
. In simple words, what we have done is the following: In order to see a particular fluctuation in the PSD at
x=x_{a}
at
t=t_{a}
, one needs to know what was the particular initial random PSD profile that will evolve to the desired fluctuation at
t=t_{a}
and
x=x_{a}
. To find that initial random profile, we have to evolve equation (
63b
) backward to obtain
h_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,0)
. This solution is then inserted in Eq. (
63d
), solving which one would get the particular initial (random) PSD profile that under forward evolution would give rise to the desired fluctuation at
x=x_{b}
at
t=t_{b}
.
in order to produce correlation with the PSD fluctuations at location
x=x_{a}
at
t=t_{a}
.
The density fluctuations at different locations at
t=t_{a}
are now correlated because they are originated from the same random initial profile, evolved ballistically [Eq. (
63a
)] to the desired time.

To proceed we need to solve Eq. (
71
) to obtain
f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,0)
or, equivalantly,
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)
for given
\psi(x,v)

\lambda

as [see
B.1
for details]

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)}{1-a\varrho_{\lambda}(x,0)}\right)=-\psi(x,v)-\frac{a\varrho_{\lambda}(x,0)}{1-a\varrho_{\lambda}(x,0)}-\mu+\lambda\mathscr{R}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}\left[\delta\mathtt{g}_{0}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}(x),v)\right].

(72)

where we have introduced the operation
\mathscr{R}_{\mathfrak{f}(t)}[H(x,v)]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{R}_{\mathfrak{f}(t)}[H(x,v)]&=H(x,v)-a\int dy~\Theta(y-x)~\left[\int du\left\{\partial_{y}H(y,u)\right\}\frac{\mathfrak{f}(y,u,t)}{1-a\varrho(y,t)}\right].\end{split}

(73)

Note the difference between the operations of
\mathscr{R}
and
\mathcal{R}
given in Eqs. (
73

\mathcal{R}_{f(t)}

The equation (

Writing

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)\simeq\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,0)+\lambda\delta\mathfrak{f}(x.v.0),~~~\text{and}~~\mu=\bar{\mu}+\lambda\delta\mu,

in Eq. (

\displaystyle\text{at}~O(\lambda^{0}):~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,0)=\bar{\mathcal{N}}~(1-a\bar{\varrho}(x,0))~e^{-\psi(x,v)}e^{-\frac{a\bar{\varrho}(x,0)}{1-a\bar{\varrho}(x,0)}},~~\text{with}~~~\bar{\mathcal{N}}=e^{-\bar{\mu}},

\displaystyle\text{at}~O(\lambda):~\delta\mathfrak{f}(x,v,0)+\left[a\frac{2-a\bar{\varrho}(x,0)}{(1-a\bar{\varrho}(x,0))^{2}}\delta\varrho(x,0)+\delta{\mu}\right]\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,0)=\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,v)\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,0),

(75)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&~~\text{where}~~~\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,v)=\mathscr{R}_{\bar{f}(0)}\left[\delta\mathtt{g}_{0}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(0)}(x),v)\right],\\
&~~\text{with}~~~~\delta\mathtt{g}_{0}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(0)}(x),v)=\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(x_{\bar{f}(t_{a})}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(0)}(x)+vt_{a}),v),\end{split}

(76)

\bar{f}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(0)}(z),v,0)=\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(z,v,0)}{1-a\bar{\varrho}(z,0)}
and
\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(y,w)

Initial average PSD

4.3
Computation of

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}(x),v,0)=\frac{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)}{1-a\varrho^{\lambda}(x,0)}.

(87)

This equation is essentially an identity between the two functions

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,0)=\frac{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x_{f_{\lambda}(0)}(x^{\prime}),v,0)}{1-a\varrho^{\lambda}(x_{f_{\lambda}(0)}(x^{\prime}),0)}.

) till time

. At time

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x,v,0)\overset{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:f->mf}}}{\Longrightarrow}f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}(x),v,0)\overset{evolve}{\longrightarrow}f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}(x),v,t)\overset{\text{Eq.~\eqref{eq:mf->f}}}{\Longrightarrow}\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x_{f_{\lambda}(t)}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(0)}(x)),v,t),

. In order to do so, we first note that the evolution of PSD of hard point particles can be obtained by solving Eq. (
63a
) which is simply given by

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime},v,t)=f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime}-vt,v,0).

(89)

Transforming back to hard rod PSD at time

55
-

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(z,v,t)=\frac{f_{\lambda}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(t)}(z),v,t)}{1+a\rho^{\lambda}(x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(t)}(z),t)},~~~\text{with}~~x^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(t)}(z)=z-a\mathscr{F}_{\rm r}^{\lambda}(z,t).

(90)

Recall, for the computation of the space time correlation, we need to compute
\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(x,v,t)
to linear order in
\lambda
only,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(z,v,t)=\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(z,v,t)~+~\lambda~\delta\mathfrak{f}(z,v,t)+O(\lambda^{2}),~~~\text{where}~~\delta\mathfrak{f}(z,v,t)=\left[\partial_{\lambda}\mathfrak{f}_{\lambda}(z,v,t)\right]_{\lambda=0},

(91)

and
\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x_{\bar{f}(t)}(z^{\prime}),v,t)=\frac{\bar{f}(z^{\prime},v,t)}{1+a\bar{\rho}(z^{\prime},t)}

\displaystyle\delta\mathfrak{f}(z,v,t)

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(z,v,t)\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}^{\rm dr}(x_{{\bar{f}}(0)}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(t)}(z)-vt),v)-a\partial_{z}\left[\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(z,v,t)\delta F(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(t)}(z),t)\right]

(92)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+a\partial_{z}\left[\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(z,v,t)}{1-a\bar{\varrho}(z,t)}\right]\delta\mathscr{F}_{\rm r}(x_{\bar{f}(0)}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(t)}(z)-vt),0),

(93)

where

\displaystyle\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}^{\rm dr}(x,v)=

\displaystyle\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,v)-a\int du~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,u,0)~\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,u).

(94)

and

\displaystyle\delta F(z^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\int dy~\Theta(x_{\bar{f}(t)}(z^{\prime})-y)\int du~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(y,u,t)~\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}^{\rm dr}(x_{{\bar{f}}(0)}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(t)}(y)-ut),u)

with

Inserting

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{C}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(x_{a},t_{a};x_{b},t_{b})&=\langle\mathfrak{q}_{\nu}(x_{a},t_{a})\mathfrak{q}_{\nu^{\prime}}(x_{b},t_{b})\rangle-\langle\mathfrak{q}_{\nu}(x_{a},t_{a})\rangle\langle\mathfrak{q}_{\nu^{\prime}}(x_{b},t_{b})\rangle\approx\frac{1}{\ell}\mathscr{C}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(x_{a},t_{a};x_{b},t_{b})\\

5.1
Initial two point correlation

\displaystyle=\delta(x_{b}-x_{a})\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x_{b},u_{b},0)\left[\delta(u_{b}-u_{a})-a(2-a\bar{\varrho}(x_{b},0))~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x_{a},u_{a},0)\right].

[

]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{C}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}(x_{a},t_{a};x_{b},t_{b})&=\delta(x_{b}-x_{a})\left[\mathscr{U}_{\nu+\nu^{\prime}}(x_{a},0)-a(2-a\bar{\varrho}(x_{b},0)\mathscr{U}_{\nu}(x_{a},0)\mathscr{U}_{\nu^{\prime}}(x_{b},0)\right],\\
\text{where,}~~~~~~&~~~\mathscr{U}_{\nu}(x,0)=\int du~u^{\nu}~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,u,0).\end{split}

Explicit expressions can be obtained after performing the integral over

5.2

In equilibrium:

In case of homogeneous equilibrium state the expression of the two point correlation in Eq. (

\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(y,w)=-\delta(y-x_{a})\left[\delta(w-u_{a})-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}\mathcal{h}(u_{a})\right]

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{C}_{\rm eq}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a};&x_{b},u_{b},0)=\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}(1-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq})~\frac{1}{t_{a}}\mathcal{h}\left(\frac{\Delta x}{t_{a}}\right)\Bigg\{\delta\left(u_{b}-\frac{\Delta x}{t_{a}}\right)\delta(u_{a}-u_{b})\cr&~~~~~~~~-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}\delta\left(u_{b}-\frac{\Delta x}{t_{a}}\right)\mathcal{h}(u_{a})-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}~\delta\left(u_{a}-\frac{\Delta x}{t_{a}}\right)\mathcal{h}(u_{b})+a^{2}\bar{\varrho}^{2}_{\rm eq}\mathcal{h}(u_{a})\mathcal{h}(u_{b})\Bigg\}\end{split}

where

. Integrating the above equation over
u_{a}
and

6
Conclusion

In this paper we studied the dynamic fluctuations of PSD in a gas of hard rods starting from a slowly varying initial statistical state. To study such fluctuations, recently a general theory called ballistic macroscopic fluctuation theory has been developed for systems supporting ballistic transport. Examples of such systems include certain integrable systems in one dimension. A collection of hard rods moving in one-dimension in absence of any external potential is one such integrable system for which one can perform explicit calculations. For this system we review the BMFT by developing the concepts from microscopic description in detail. We found, as expected from our experience with equilibrium systems and MFT in diffusive systems, the probability of fluctuations in the PSD on macroscopic space-time scale possesses a large-deviation form with an appropriate large deviation functional. One important difference between BMFT and MFT, is that in the former case one looks at the fluctuations at Euler space-time scaling whereas in MFT one looks at the diffusive space-time scaling. Hence the source of the noise in the former case is solely from the initial conditions whereas in the later it is due to both, initial conditions and corse-graining over non-conserved degrees of freedom.

The BMFT can be used to compute cumulants and correlations of additive observables through hydrodynamic projections and the principle of local relaxations
[
38
]
. One of the striking prediction of this theory is the presence of long-range correlations among conserved densities (and also among local observables) that gets established on the Euler scaling limit due to ballistic evolution. In other words, observables over two regions
R
and
R^{\prime}
, each of size
\sim\ell
and separated by distance
\sim\ell
gets correlated in due course of time. After time
t\sim\ell
the correlation between
R
and
R^{\prime}
is also of order
\ell
even when there was no correlation initially. We compute this correlation between PSDs at two locations at two times. We provide explicit expression of the correlation in easily computable form and we found our result to reproduce the previous computation of the correlation
[
38
,
40
]
. Such correlations exists when (i) initial state is inhomogeneous with a length scale of variation
\ell
(ii) the system is interacting and (iii) the system should have at least two ballistic hydrodynamic modes with different velocities. We explicitly show that our expression indeed becomes local
i.e.,
no long-range correlation when any one of the condition is not satisfied. This long-range correlation is different from what one observed in non-equilibrium stationary states. This correlation gets produced by coherent evolution of initial fluctuation by the Euler hydrodynamic equations.

Our study can be applied to compute other quantities like distribution of net integrated current crossing a location and displacement of a tracer quasiparticle. The study of the joint distribution of the displacements of two tracers is an important problem. The correlation between two tracer rods has been used to develop a fluctuating hydrodynamic theory of hard rod gas in equilibrium (homogeneous)
[
56
]
. It would be interesting to see the connection between such fluctuating hydrodynamic equation and BMFT in the context of hard rods and establish a more general fluctuating hydrodynamic theory to study evolution from inhomogeneous initial states. Due to the general structure of BMFT, it would be interesting to apply this theory to other integrable systems such as Toda fluid for which GHD has been established
[
21
,
22
]
. Finally, it would be interesting to explore the possibility of extending the BMFT theory for hard rods in presence of slowly varying external potentials.

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{R}_{f(t_{a})}[\mathscr{B}(y,w)]=-\left[\mathscr{B}(y,w)-a\partial_{x_{a}}[\Theta(x_{a}-y)\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a})]\right].

(270)

It is easy to see from Eq. (

\displaystyle\int dw~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,w,0)~\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}^{\rm dr}(x,w)

\displaystyle=(1-a\bar{\varrho}(x,0))\int dw~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,w,0)\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,w,0),

(271)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{where,}~~\int dw~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,w,0)\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,w,0)&=\bigg[\int dw~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,w,0)\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x,w,0)\cr&~~-a\bar{\varrho}(x,0)\int dy\int dw\frac{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(y,w,0)}{1-a\bar{\varrho}(y,0)}\Theta(y-x)\partial_{y}\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(y,w,0)\bigg].\end{split}

(272)

In Equilibrium:

If the hard-rod gas is in equilibrium initially
i.e.

\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,0)=\bar{\mathfrak{f}}_{\rm eq}(x,v)=\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}~\mathcal{h}(v)
then the PSD at later time also remains the same, which means
\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,t)=\bar{\mathfrak{f}}_{\rm eq}(x,v),~~\forall t
. Since
\bar{\mathfrak{f}}_{\rm eq}(x,v)=\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}~\mathcal{h}(v)
is independent of space the expression of
\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,v)
in Eq. (
266
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,v)

\displaystyle=\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x,v,0)+\frac{a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}}{1-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}}\int dw~\mathcal{h}(w)\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x,w,0),

(273)

which implies

\displaystyle\int dw~\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,w,0)\delta\mathtt{h}_{t_{a}}(x,w)=\frac{\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}}{1-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}}\int dw~\mathcal{h}(w)\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x,w,0).

(274)

Inserting this result in the expression of equilibrium space-time correlation in Eq. (
103
) and simplifying we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm eq}(x_{a},u_{a},t_{a};x_{b},u_{b},0)=-\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}\mathcal{h}(u_{b})\left[\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x_{b},u_{b},0)-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}~\int dw~\mathcal{h}(w)\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x_{b},w,0)\right],

(275)

where,
\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x,v,0)
given in Eq. (
269
) with
\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(y,w)=-\delta(y-x_{a})\left[\delta(w-u_{a})-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}\mathcal{h}(u_{a})\right]
in equilibrium. Inserting this form of
\delta\mathtt{g}_{t_{a}}(y,w)
, we perform the integral. For that we first note that

\displaystyle\delta\left(x_{\bar{f}(t_{a})}(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(0)}(y)+wt_{a})-x_{a}\right)=(1-a\bar{\varrho}(x_{a},t_{a}))~\delta\left(x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(0)}(y)+wt_{a}-x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(t_{a})}(x_{a})\right),

(276)

using which along with
\bar{\mathfrak{f}}(x,v,0)=\bar{\mathfrak{f}}_{\rm eq}(x,v)=\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}~\mathcal{h}(v)
we get

\displaystyle\int dw~\mathcal{h}(w)\delta\mathtt{g}_{n}(x_{b},w,0)

\displaystyle=-(1-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq})\frac{1}{t_{a}}\mathcal{h}\left(\frac{\Delta x}{t_{a}}\right)~\left[\delta\left(u_{a}-\frac{\Delta x}{t_{a}}\right)-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq}\mathcal{h}(u_{a})\right],

(277)

\displaystyle\text{where}~~~~\Delta x=x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}_{\rm eq}}(x_{a})-x^{\prime}_{\bar{\mathfrak{f}}_{\rm eq}}(x_{b})=(1-a\bar{\varrho}_{\rm eq})~(x_{a}-x_{b}).

(278)

Using the above expression of the integral in Eq. (
275
) we get the expression in Eq. (
106
).
