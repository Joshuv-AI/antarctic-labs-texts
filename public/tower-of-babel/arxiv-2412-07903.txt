Application of Madelung Hydrodynamics to Plasmonics and Nonlinear Optics in Two-Dimensional Materials
Simão S. Cardoso, A. J. Chaves, N. Asger Mortensen, N. M. R. Peres

Abstract
This paper explores the application of Madelung hydrodynamic models to study two-dimensional electron gases, with a focus on nonlocal plasmonics and nonlinear optics. We begin by reviewing the derivation of the Madelung equations. Using the Madelung equations in conjunction with Poisson’s equation, we calculate the spectrum of magnetoplasmons and the magneto-optical conductivity in the electrostatic regime, incorporating nonlocal corrections due to the Fermi pressure. In the absence of a magnetic field, we analyze nonlinear and nonlocal second-harmonic generation, demonstrating how plasmon excitation enhances this process. We further discuss the emergence of self-modulation phenomena driven by nonlinearity, leading to the renormalization of the plasmon dispersion. Notably, we show that nonlinearity amplifies nonlocal effects and, leveraging the hydrodynamic formalism, derive a simple analytic expression for the renormalized spectra.

II
The Madelung transformation

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation reads

i\frac{\partial\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{dt}=H\Psi(\mathbf{r},t),

(1)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the system and
\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)
is the wave function, in general, a complex-valued field. The Madelung transformation is given by the following wave function

\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)=\sqrt{n(\mathbf{r},t)}\exp[iS(\mathbf{r},t)],

(2)

where both
n(\mathbf{r},t)
and
S(\mathbf{r},t)
are real-valued functions, and substituting into Schrödinger’s equation, we obtain two new equations (resulting from separating the real and imaginary parts)

\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(n\mathbf{v})=0,

(3a)

and

\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbf{v}^{2}=-\frac{1}{m}\bm{\nabla}U+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\bm{\nabla}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\nabla^{2}\sqrt{n}\right),

(3b)

where
\mathbf{v}\equiv\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}S/m
,
m
being the mass of the particle, and
U(\mathbf{r},t)
represents the potential energy landscape through which the particle moves. Clearly, Eq. (
3a
) has the form of the continuity equation and Eq. (
3b
) has the form of the Euler equation describing the motion of a fluid of velocity
\mathbf{v}
. The term
-m^{-1}\bm{\nabla}U
accounts for the forces applied to the particle. The last term in Eq. (
3b
) does not have a classical analog, being of purely quantum mechanical origin; indeed it goes to zero if
\hbar\rightarrow 0
.
This term is commonly termed the quantum potential or Bohm potential, and it gives rise to spatial dispersion, i.e., physical observables acquire a
\mathbf{k}
-dependence in Fourier space.
When
U(\mathbf{r},t)
represents a self-consistent potential,
V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mathbf{r},t)
, of
electrostatic nature, the solution of the previous two equations must be supplemented by Poisson’s equation

\nabla^{2}V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mathbf{r},t)=-\frac{q}{\epsilon_{0}}n(\mathbf{r},t),

(4)

where
q
is the charge of the particles (for electrons we have
q=-e<0
, where
e
is the elementary charge) and
\epsilon_{0}
is the vacuum dielectric constant. The use of Poisson’s equation is valid in the non-retarded limit. If the inclusion of retardation is necessary then Poisson’s equation should be replaced by Maxwell’s equations.

The Euler equation derived above includes no many-body effects. This is because it was derived from the single-particle Schrödinger’s equation. The inclusion of many-body effects can be done phenomenologically, adding by hand the required many-body terms or more formally, by starting with either a many-body wavefunction, a many-body Hamiltonian, or the Wigner distribution function
[
32
]
(see Appendix
A
).
In the many-body approach, and considering a system of
N
particles, the wave function of the system is written in the form

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{r}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{r}_{N},t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n(\mathbf{r}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{r}_{N},t)}\exp[S(\mathbf{r}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{r}_{N},t)]\,.

(5)

We can further simplify the wave function assuming that it is possible to write it in factorizable form (Hartree approximation; this factorization is not true in general) as
\Psi(\mathbf{r}_{1}\ldots\mathbf{r}_{N},t)=\Pi_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{n_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)}\exp[S_{i}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)]
.
The Madelung equations also follows from this simplifided form of the many-body wave function.

Either way, at the simplest level, the right-hand-side of the Euler equation above is supplemented by the Fermi pressure term

{\cal\bm{F}}_{F}=-\frac{\bm{\nabla}p_{F}[n(\mathbf{r},t)]}{mn},

(6)

where
p_{F}
is the Fermi (or statistical) pressure, which depends on the dimensionality of the system and the particles’ dispersion relation. In both two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) free-electron systems, the Fermi pressure is taken that of a degenerate electron gas and reads

\displaystyle p_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5}\frac{\hbar^{2}n}{m}(3\pi^{2}n)^{2/3},

\displaystyle({\rm 3D\;electron\;gas})

(7a)

\displaystyle p_{F}

\displaystyle=\pi\frac{\hbar^{2}n^{2}}{2m},

\displaystyle({\rm 2D\;electron\;gas})

(7b)

\displaystyle p_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar v_{F}}{3\pi}(\pi n)^{3/2},

\displaystyle({\rm graphene})

(7c)

where
m
is the electron mass and
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity of electrons in graphene.
In addition to the Bohm potential, the pressure term also contributes to the wavevector dependence of the dielectric function.
The distinction between the two arises from the power of the wavevector dependence:
k^{2}
for the Fermi pressure and
k^{4}
for the Bohm potential.

III
Applications to plasmonics and nonlinear optics

In this section, we provide four applications of the hydrodynamic model including the effect of quantum corrections. As we will see, this framework is a rather convenient and transparent way to account for nonlocal terms in the response functions.

III.1
Magnetoplasmons spectrum with nonlocal corrections

The hydrodynamic formulation of quantum mechanics, briefly outlined above, has proven quite effective in addressing problems such as second-harmonic generation by 2D and 3D electron gases as well as the calculation of the linear-response plasmon dispersion both with and without magnetic field – magnetoplasmons. In the presence of both an electric and a magnetic field, the Euler equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\cal\bm{V}}}{\partial t}+({\cal\bm{V}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}){\cal\bm{V}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m^{2}}\bm{\nabla}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\nabla^{2}\sqrt{n}\right)-\frac{q}{m}\bm{\nabla}V-

\displaystyle-\frac{q}{m}\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}+\frac{q}{m}{\cal\bm{V}}\times(\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{A}),

(8)

where the electric field reads
\mathbf{E}=-\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t-\bm{\nabla}V
,
the magnetic field is given by
\mathbf{B}=\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{A}
,
and the velocity field is
{\cal\bm{V}}=\mathbf{v}-q\mathbf{A}/m
.
The quantities
V(\mathbf{r},t)
and
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t)
stand for the scalar and vector potentials. Again, the previous equation excludes the Fermi pressure, which will be added by hand. Also, we assume a weak magnetic field allows the use of the same expressions for the Fermi pressure given above. When an external electromagnetic field is added to the system, the magnetic field part of it needs to be included for the sake of the consistent calculation of nonlinear optical properties.

For determining the magnetoplasmon spectrum of an electron gas, we need to solve Eq. (
8
). As an example, we here consider a 2D electron gas. The force due to the statistical pressure reads

{\cal\bm{F}}_{F}=-\frac{1}{nm}\bm{\nabla}p_{F}=-\frac{\pi\hbar}{2nm^{2}}\bm{\nabla}n^{2}=-\frac{\pi\hbar}{m^{2}}\bm{\nabla}n.

(9)

Treating the problem in the quasi-static approximation see Appendix
B
, we have to solve the Euler equation

\frac{\partial{\cal\bm{V}}}{\partial t}+({\cal\bm{V}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}){\cal\bm{V}}=-\frac{\pi\hbar}{m^{2}}\bm{\nabla}n-\frac{q}{m}\bm{\nabla}V_{\mathrm{sc}}+\frac{q}{m}{\cal\bm{V}}\times\mathbf{B},

(10)

together with the continuity equation (
3a
) and Poisson’s equation (
4
).
To that end, we assume a time and spatial dependence of the fields in the form
e^{i(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{k-}\omega t)}
, where both
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{k}
are 2D vectors lying in the plane of the 2D electron gas. We then expand them up to linear order in the electric field

Note that the electromagnetic field exists throughout all space, not just at the surface of the 2D electron gas. The expansion of the fields read

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},t)=n_{0}+e^{i(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{k-}\omega t)}n_{1}(\mathbf{k},\omega),

(11a)

\displaystyle\bm{{\cal V}}(\mathbf{r},t)=\bm{{\cal V}}_{0}+e^{i(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{k-}\omega t)}\bm{{\cal V}}_{1}(\mathbf{k},\omega),

(11b)

where

is the equilibrium two-dimensional electronic density and we consider the absence of drift currents, i.e.,
\bm{{\cal V}}_{0}=0

\omega n_{1}=n_{0}\mathbf{k}\cdot\bm{{\cal V}}_{1},

(12a)

and

\omega\bm{{\cal V}}_{1}=\mathbf{k}\frac{\pi\hbar}{m^{2}}n_{1}+\mathbf{k}\frac{q}{m}V_{\mathrm{sc}}+i\frac{q}{m}{\cal\bm{V}}_{1}\times\mathbf{B},

(12b)

while the Poisson’s equation reads

\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-k^{2}\right)V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mathbf{k},\omega,z)=-\frac{q}{\epsilon_{0}}\delta(z)n_{1}(\mathbf{k},\omega).

(12c)

The solution of this set of equations at
z=0
is given by

V_{\mathrm{sc}}(\mathbf{k},\omega,0)=\frac{q}{2k\epsilon_{0}}n_{1}(\mathbf{k},\omega),

(13)

and

is given by Eq. (
12a

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}k_{x}^{2}\frac{\hbar^{2}\pi}{m^{2}}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{n_{0}}&\frac{k_{x}k_{y}\hbar^{2}\pi}{m^{2}}+i\frac{q\omega B}{n_{0}m}\\

{\cal V}_{1y}\end{array}\right)=0.

(14)

This equation has nontrivial solutions if the matrix determinant is zero, which gives

\omega^{2}-\beta^{2}k^{2}-ak-\omega_{c}^{2}=0,

(15)

where

, thus obtaining the magnetoplasmons spectrum given by

\omega=\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\omega_{c}^{2}+ak+\beta^{2}k^{2}},

(16)

is the cyclotron frequency. Since the Fermi momentum of a 2D electron gas is given by

\beta
can be written as

\beta^{2}=\frac{v_{F}^{2}}{2},

(17)

where
\beta
corresponds to the speed of the first sound in a 2D electron gas

. The previous result is valid when neglecting the collisional frequency, that is, at high frequencies compared to the electron-electron collisions rate

(18a)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\omega/5+i\gamma/3}{\omega+i\gamma}v_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle({\rm 3D\;electron\;gas})

(18b)

where

and expanding it in powers of the wave vector. If the relaxation rate is finite, the Mermin correction for the dielectric function needs to be used

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{x}\beta^{2}}{\omega}(k_{x}{\cal V}_{1x}+k_{y}{\cal V}_{1y})+\frac{iq}{m}(E_{x}+\omega_{c}{\cal V}_{1y}),

(20a)

\displaystyle\omega{\cal V}_{1y}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{y}\beta^{2}}{\omega}(k_{x}{\cal V}_{1x}+k_{y}{\cal V}_{1y})+\frac{iq}{m}(E_{y}-\omega_{c}{\cal V}_{1x}).

which, after solving for the velocity amplitudes, leads to the solution

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\cal V}_{1x}\\
{\cal V}_{1y}\end{array}\right)=\frac{iq}{mD(\mathbf{k},\omega)}\times

\displaystyle\times\begin{bmatrix}\omega-k_{y}^{2}\beta^{2}/\omega&k_{x}k_{y}\beta^{2}/\omega-i\omega_{c}\\[8.61108pt]
k_{x}k_{y}\beta^{2}/\omega+i\omega_{c}&\omega-k_{x}^{2}\beta^{2}/\omega\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}E_{x}\\
E_{y}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}J_{1x}\\

\displaystyle\times\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega-k_{y}^{2}\beta^{2}/\omega&k_{x}k_{y}\beta^{2}/\omega-i\omega_{c}\\[8.61108pt]
k_{x}k_{y}\beta^{2}/\omega+i\omega_{c}&\omega-k_{x}^{2}\beta^{2}/\omega\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}E_{x}\\

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\sigma}=\frac{iq^{2}n_{0}}{mD(\mathbf{k},\omega)}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega-k_{y}^{2}\beta^{2}/\omega&k_{x}k_{y}\beta^{2}/\omega-i\omega_{c}\\[8.61108pt]

III.3.1
Derivation of the nonlinear current using the polarization

To assess the correctness of our previous approach, we derive the same results using a more
common method
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
,
where the quantum hydrodynamic equation is expressed as an equation for the polarization
\mathbf{P}
, with the current given by
\mathbf{J}=\dot{\mathbf{P}}
, the dot indicating the partial time derivative. As before, we define the current as
\mathbf{J}=qn\mathbf{v}
.
The goal is to write the Euler equation for the charged fluid as a function of
\mathbf{J}
. The first step is the integration of the continuity equation written in the form

\dot{n}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(n\mathbf{v})=\dot{n}+\frac{1}{q}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{P}}=0,

(47)

and upon time integration we find

n=n_{0}-\frac{1}{q}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}.

(48)

The next step is writing the material derivative of the current, which reads

\frac{d}{dt}\left(qn\mathbf{v}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(qn\mathbf{v}\right)+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\nabla})(qn\mathbf{v}).

(49)

This in turn allows the expression of the material derivative of the current as

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=\frac{1}{qn}\dot{\mathbf{J}}+\frac{1}{(qn)^{2}}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\mathbf{J}-\frac{\dot{n}}{qn^{2}}\mathbf{J}-\frac{1}{q^{2}n^{3}}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\bm{\nabla}n)\mathbf{J}.

(50)

With this result, the Euler equation (
32
) reads

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\gamma\right)\dot{\mathbf{P}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{qn}(\dot{\mathbf{P}}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\dot{\mathbf{P}}+\frac{\dot{n}}{n}\dot{\mathbf{P}}+\frac{q^{2}n}{m}\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle+\frac{q}{m}\dot{\mathbf{P}}\times\mathbf{B}-\frac{q}{m}\bm{\nabla}p_{F}.

(51)

What remains is to express
\dot{n}/n
and
\bm{\nabla}p_{F}
in terms of the polarization
\mathbf{P}
. The first result
follows from the continuity equation and reads

\frac{\dot{n}}{n}=-\frac{1}{qn_{0}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{P}}\left(1-\frac{1}{qn_{0}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\approx-\frac{1}{qn_{0}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{P}},

(52)

while the gradient of the pressure is likewise be expressed in terms of the polarization as

-\frac{q}{m}\bm{\nabla}p_{F}=\frac{\pi\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\left(n_{0}-\frac{1}{q}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)\bm{\nabla}\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}\right).

(53)

This latter result, along with the term proportional to
(\mathbf{J}\cdot\bm{\nabla}n)\mathbf{J}

in the material derivative of the velocity, contributes to nonlocal corrections in orders of
\mathbf{k}
beyond the linear and are,
therefore, discarded. With these approximations, the equation for the polarization reads

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\gamma\right)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{P}}\approx-\dfrac{1}{qn_{0}}\left[\left(\dot{\mathbf{P}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\right)\dot{\mathbf{P}}+\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{P}}\right)\dot{\mathbf{P}}\right]+

(54)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{q^{2}n_{0}}{m}\mathbf{E}-\dfrac{q}{m}\left(\mathbf{E}\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)-\dot{\mathbf{P}}\times\mathbf{B}\right).

All the fields entering the previous equation are expanded as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{1}(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\omega t}+\mathbf{A}_{2}(\mathbf{r})e^{-i2\omega t}+\text{ c.c.},

(55)

where
\mathbf{A=\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{J}
. Replacing this expansions in Eq. (
54
), we have for the first
harmonic

\mathbf{P}_{1}=-\frac{q^{2}n_{0}}{m}\frac{1}{\omega(\omega+i\gamma)}\mathbf{E}_{1}e^{-i\omega t}+\text{ c.c.},

(56)

and likewise for the second harmonic

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{2}=-\frac{q^{3}n_{0}}{m^{2}D_{P}(\omega)}\Bigg[\frac{(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{1})\mathbf{E}_{1}+(\mathbf{E}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\mathbf{E}_{1}}{\omega+i\gamma}+

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathbf{E}_{1}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{1})+\mathbf{E}_{1}\times(\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}_{1})}{\omega}\Bigg]e^{-i2\omega t}+\text{ c.c.},

(57)

with

D_{P}(\omega)\equiv 2\omega\left(2\omega+i\gamma\right)\left(\omega+i\gamma\right).

(58)

The nonlinear current
\mathbf{J}_{2}
can be obtained from Eq. (
57
) using that
\mathbf{J}_{2}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{P}_{2}
.
Assuming a longitudinal electric field oriented along the
x
-direction, we find from the previous expression

\displaystyle J_{2,x}=\frac{e^{3}n_{0}k_{x}}{m^{2}}\frac{1}{2\omega+i\gamma}\frac{1}{\omega+i\gamma}\Bigg[\frac{2}{\omega+i\gamma}+\frac{1}{\omega}\Bigg]E_{1,x}^{2}e^{-i2\omega t}

(59)

\displaystyle+\text{ c.c.},

which, upon algebraic simplification, is identical to Eq. (
46
). Therefore, we have recovered our earlier result.

III.4
Plasmon-assisted second-harmonic generation in the 2D electron gas

In this section, we show, within the quasi-static approximation, that a self-consistent treatment of the electric potential produces a strong enhancement of the SHG due to resonant plasmon excitation. We take as an example the 2D electron gas. Nonlocal corrections due to the
Fermi pressure is included. We write the continuity and Euler equations
as:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial n_{\text{ind}}}{\partial t}+n_{0}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{1}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot(n_{\text{ind}}\mathbf{v}_{1})+n_{0}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2}=0,

(60a)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\gamma\right)\mathbf{v}_{1}=-\frac{q}{m}\bm{\mathcal{\nabla}}(V_{\text{ext}}+V_{\text{ind}}),

(60b)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\gamma\right)\mathbf{v}_{2}+(\mathbf{v}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\mathbf{v}_{1}=0,

(60c)

This set of equations is supplemented by the Poisson equation

\nabla^{2}(V_{\text{ext}}+V_{\text{ind}})=-\frac{q}{\epsilon_{0}}(n_{\text{ind}}-n_{\text{ext}})\delta(z),

(61)

where
n_{\text{ext}}
is the external charge (located at
z=0
) creating the external potential
V_{\text{ext}}
, while
V_{\text{ind}}
is the induced potential due to the collective motion of the electron gas, dubbed plasmons. We write the density, external, and induced potentials, respectively, as

\displaystyle n_{\text{ind}}(\mathbf{r},t)=

\displaystyle n_{\omega}e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}+n_{2\omega}e^{i2(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)},

(62a)

\displaystyle n_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r},t)=

\displaystyle n_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)},

(62b)

\displaystyle V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r},z,t)=

\displaystyle\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}(z)e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)},

(62c)

\displaystyle V_{\text{ind}}(\mathbf{r},z,t)=

\displaystyle\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}(z)e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}

\displaystyle+\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}(z)e^{i2(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)},

(62d)

note that here we are using a different definition than the previous sections, with the density and potentials being complex quantities.

With these definitions, the Poisson equation splits into two equations, reading

\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-k^{2}\right)\left(\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}(z)+\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}(z)\right)=\frac{q}{\epsilon_{0}}\left(n_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}-n_{\omega}\right)\delta(z),

(63)

and

\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-4k^{2}\right)\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}(z)=-\frac{q}{\epsilon_{0}}n_{2\omega}\delta(z).

(64)

The solution of these two equations amounts to the calculation of the Green function and the results are

\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}(z)+\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}(z)=\frac{q}{2k\epsilon_{0}}e^{-k|z|}(n_{\omega}-n_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}),

(65)

and

\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}(z)=\frac{q}{4k\epsilon_{0}}e^{-2k|z|}n_{2\omega}.

(66)

We further define
\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}(0)\equiv\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}
,

\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}(0)\equiv\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}
, and
\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}(0)\equiv\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}
.
The external potential connects to the external density via

\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}=-\frac{q}{2k\epsilon_{0}}n_{\omega}^{\text{ext}},

(67)

and the induced densities are

\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}=\frac{q}{2k\epsilon_{0}}n_{\omega},

(68)

and

\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}=\frac{q}{4k\epsilon_{0}}n_{2\omega}.

(69)

Using these results in Eqs. (
60b
) and (
60c
),
we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}=\mathbf{k}\frac{q^{2}}{2\epsilon_{0}mk}\left(\frac{n_{\omega}-n_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}}{\omega+i\gamma}e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}+\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{n_{2\omega}}{2\omega+i\gamma}e^{i2(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}\right),

(70)

and

\mathbf{v}_{2}=\mathbf{k}\frac{q^{4}}{4\epsilon_{0}^{2}m^{2}}\frac{\left(n_{\omega}-n_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}\right)^{2}}{\left(\omega+i\gamma\right)^{2}(2\omega+i\gamma)}e^{i2(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}.

(71)

Using these two results in Eq. (
60a
) and grouping
the terms with the same phase, we find the expressions for
n_{\omega}
and
n_{2\omega}
. With these two results we find from the definition of the linear dielectric function

\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}+\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ind}}=\frac{\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}}}{\epsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)},

(72)

that the dielectric function reads (
\gamma=0
)

\epsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)=1-\omega_{k}^{2}/\omega^{2},

(73)

where
\omega_{k}^{2}=e^{2}n_{0}k/(2\epsilon_{0}m)
is the plasmon dispersion relation in a 2D electron gas. The second-order electrostatic
amplitude,
\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}

\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}=-\frac{3}{8}\frac{e^{3}k^{3}n_{0}}{\epsilon_{0}m^{2}\omega^{4}}\frac{(\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}})^{2}}{\epsilon(2\mathbf{k},2\omega)[\epsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)]^{2}}.

(74)

This result agrees with a calculation based on the Boltzmann kinetic equation
[
48

. Thus, the second-harmonic field has poles at the solutions of
\epsilon(\mathbf{k},\omega)=0
and
\epsilon(2\mathbf{k},2\omega)=0

(\phi_{\omega}^{\text{ext}})^{2}

The vector
\mathbf{k}
is the in-plane wave vector. Since the external perturbation has the same in-plane wave vector, the SHG is finite only for oblique incidence. Thus, normal incidence originates no SHG, as imposed by symmetry.

So far, our calculation has excluded nonlocal corrections. These can be incorporated by including the contribution of the Fermi pressure in the Euler equation. Apart from this modification, the calculation proceeds exactly as before, yielding

\displaystyle\phi_{2\omega}^{\text{ind}}=-\frac{e^{3}k^{3}n_{0}}{2\epsilon_{0}m^{2}}\frac{1}{(\omega+i\gamma)^{2}}\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}(2\mathbf{k},2\omega)}\frac{1}{[\hat{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k},\omega)]^{2}}\times

\hat{\epsilon}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i\gamma)-\beta^{2}k^{2}}.

(76)

III.5
Self-Modulation of SPPs

A surface-plasmon polariton (SPP) is a coupled oscillation of electromagnetic radiation and charge density at an interface between a metal and a dielectric. Because of their ability to concentrate light in subwavelength volumes, SPPs are of particular interest in nonlinear optics, as they enhance nonlinear optical processes in metallic nanoparticles and on extended metal surfaces. In addition, SPPs are intrinsically nonlinear, capable of altering their properties through self-modulation
[
49
]
. This phenomenon arises from the nonlinear interaction between the electric field of the SPP and the medium through which it propagates, resulting in changes to the wave’s phase, amplitude, or frequency. A deeper understanding of SPPs and related phenomena paves the way for significant advancements, including the study and design of nonlinear plasmonic systems and metamaterials. In this section, we study the self-modulation effect on the energy of SPPs. For this purpose, we start by expanding the electron velocity and density fields like we have done previously in (
55

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we have applied the hydrodynamic model to various problems as a means of accounting for nonlocal corrections in the computed physical properties of systems. We have demonstrated that quantum hydrodynamic equations can be derived either from the Schrödinger equation or from the moments of the Wigner function. Four applications of the quantum-hydrodynamic model were considered, and their results were shown to align with previous literature based on solutions of the Boltzmann kinetic equation.

The hydrodynamic model offers a simple and elegant formalism for addressing the optical properties of condensed matter systems. For instance, our calculation of graphene’s nonlinear conductivity can serve as a starting point for discussing second-harmonic generation
in attenuated total reflection experiments. Similarly, the calculation of graphene’s magneto-optical conductivity, including nonlocal corrections, provides a foundation for exploring their role in graphene nanostructures under magnetic fields.

The self-consistent field approach to calculating plasmon-assisted second-harmonic generation highlights the simplicity of the method, bypassing the need for a Boltzmann kinetic calculation.

The proposed results found in this work can be validated experimentally, especially the second harmonic generation. Its measurement is obtained from the ATR configuration (attenuated total reflection) originally proposed by Otto
[
50
]
, where shining light on a prism, separated from graphene by a dielectric spacer, creates a second-order nonlinear response of the material observed in reflection. On the other hand, measurements on the dispersion relation of plasmons are possible using different techniques, such as ATR or SNOM
[
51
]
(scanning near-field optical microscope), requiring small distances between the probe, for example, the SNOM tip and the graphene sheet.

Connection between the Wigner function and Madelung equations

In the following, we derive the Madelung equations starting from the Wigner distribution function, which preserves information about both position and momentum while maintaining the interference properties characteristic of quantum mechanics. Assuming a distribution function of the form (in 2D)

f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}\\
\int d\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\psi_{\alpha}^{\ast}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/2,t)\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/2,t)e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/\hbar},

(90)

where
\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r},t)
is a single-particle wave function obeying the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. The system is assumed to be in a mixed state of the form

\rho(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t)=\sum_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}\psi^{\ast}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r},t)\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t),

(91)

where the probabilities
P_{\alpha}
obey the sum rule
\sum_{\alpha}^{N_{s}}P_{\alpha}=1
, where
N_{s}
is the number of states in the mixture. The time derivative of
f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)
can be obtained using the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. Introducing the following two moments of the Wigner distribution

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},t)=\int d\mathbf{p}\,f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t),

(92a)

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)=\frac{1}{m}\int d\mathbf{p}\,\mathbf{p}\,f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t),

(92b)

the continuity and Euler equations follow, with the latter having both the statistical and quantum (Bohm term) pressures included. We derive the continuity equation first. From the time derivative of the Wigner distribution (
90
), using the results presented in Appendix
D
, we have:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)=-\frac{1}{m}\bm{\nabla}\cdot[\mathbf{p}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)]+V_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t),

(93)

This result, in the present context, is called the Wigner equation
[
32
]
.
It closely resembles the Boltzmann kinetic equation, apart from the
V_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)
term.
Upon integration over the momentum
\mathbf{p}
, the term
V_{W}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)
cancels out and the final result reads

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}n(\mathbf{r},t)+\bm{\nabla}\cdot[n(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)]=0,

(94)

that is, we have obtained the continuity equation. The calculation of the first moment yields the Euler equation, incorporating both the statistical and quantum pressure terms. To derive the Euler equation, we take the first moment of the Wigner distribution function. Starting with

\mathbf{p}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}\hbar^{2}}\mathbf{p}\sum_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}\\
\int d\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\psi_{\alpha}^{\ast}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/2,t)\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/2,t)e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/\hbar},

(95)

integrating over
\mathbf{p}
and dividing by
m
, we obtain

\frac{1}{m}\int d\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}\hbar^{2}}\frac{1}{m}\int d\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}\sum_{\alpha}P_{\alpha}\\
\int d\mathbf{r^{\prime}}\psi_{\alpha}^{\ast}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/2,t)\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/2,t)e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/\hbar}.

(96)

Taking the time derivative of the previous equation and using the Schrödinger as we did before, we have

\frac{\partial[n(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)]}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{m^{2}}\int d\mathbf{p}\bm{\nabla}\cdot[\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p},t)],

(97)

which can be written as

\frac{\partial[n(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)]}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{r},t)={\cal\bm{P}_{W}}(\mathbf{r},t),

(98)

where the term
\bm{\Pi}
represents the statistical and quantum (Bohm) pressure, while the term
{\cal P}_{w}
comes from the contribution of the potential and represents additional force terms.
We now apply the continuity equation (
3a
) to the first term of the previous equation. After explicitly performing the time derivative and carrying out some calculations, as shown in Appendix
E
, we obtain

\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},t)\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}+n(\mathbf{r},t)[\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\cdot\bm{\nabla}]\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)+

\displaystyle+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[\bm{\Pi}-n(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r},t)\right]={\cal\bm{P}_{W}}.

(99)

This corresponds to the Euler equation of the quantum-hydrodynamic model. We can see from the final result that the quantum-pressure terms,
\bm{\Pi}
, appear naturally in the Wigner formulation, which leads to nonlocal corrections. Also, the Wigner distribution accounts for motion in the phase space
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})
, whereas the Madelung approach works only in real space. Thus, in the Wigner approach, quantum phenomena such as interference are naturally incorporated into the formalism.

Appendix B
Quasi-static limit

In this section, we show that for SPPs, that are transverse magnetic (TM) modes, in the long wavenumber regime
q\gg\omega/c
, we can associate a scalar potential that obeys the Poisson equation. We will consider that a 2D material located at
z=0
, described by the surface densities and currents
n(\mathbf{r_{\parallel},t}),\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{r_{\parallel},t})
, is immersed between two semi-infinite dielectrics with dielectric constants
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
. The electromagnetic fields satisfies the Maxwell equations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{E}=n(\mathbf{r}_{\parallel},t)\delta(z),

(100a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}=-\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t},

(100b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{B}=0,

(100c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}=\mu_{0}\mathbf{j}\delta(z)+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\epsilon(z)\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t},

(100d)

We start considering SPPs described by the electromagnetic field
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})
piecewise continuous, that are evanescent in the
z
direction and propagates along the
xy
directions:

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t)=e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\parallel}-i\omega t}\times\begin{cases}\mathbf{E}_{1}e^{-\kappa_{1}z},\mathrm{for}\,\,z>0,\\
\mathbf{E}_{2}e^{+\kappa_{2}z},\mathrm{for}\,\,z<0,\end{cases}

(101)

with
\kappa_{i}=\sqrt{q^{2}-\epsilon_{1}\omega^{2}/c^{2}}
. When
\kappa\gg\epsilon_{j}\omega^{2}/c^{2}
, with
j=1,2
, we can approximate
\kappa\approx q
, therefore:

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t)\approx e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{\parallel}-q|z|-i\omega t}\times\begin{cases}\mathbf{E}_{1},\mathrm{for}\,\,z>0,\\
\mathbf{E}_{2},\mathrm{for}\,\,z<0,\end{cases}

(102)

that satisfies:

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t)=0,

(103)

i.e.,
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t)
admits a scalar potential. To prove (
103
), we consider, without loss of generality, that
\mathbf{q}=q\mathbf{u}_{x}
and therefore
\mathbf{B}=(0,B_{y},0)
which results
\mathbf{E}=(E_{x},0,E_{z})
. Using that, for
z\neq 0
, we have from
(iq,0,-q)\cdot(E_{x},0,E_{z})=0
, from which follows (
103
). As the curl of the electric field is approximate zero in the long wavenumber limit, it results that it can be described by a scalar potential that obeys the Poisson equation.
