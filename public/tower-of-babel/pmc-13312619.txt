Dyadic effects of role adaptation on postpartum anxiety and depression: the mediating role of future time insight and parenting competence
Authors: Congrui Fu, Shuxin Zhang, Ziqian Wei, Xinyao Yu, Bingwen Zhang, Shuo Gu, Jie Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the current status and dyadic mechanism of maternal and spousal role adaptation for postpartum anxiety and depression.MethodsConvenience sampling was employed to select 276 pairs of mothers and their spouses from October 2023 to October 2024 as study participants. Binary analyses were conducted using the actor-partner interdependence model (APIM) and the mediation model.ResultsThe prevalence rates of anxiety and depression during the first postpartum year were 38.5% and 14.5% among mothers, and 36.9% and 12.9% among their spouses, respectively. The APIM revealed that maternal and spousal role adaptation significantly and negatively predicted their own postpartum anxiety (maternal actor effect: β = -0.120; spousal actor effect: β = -0.081) and depression (maternal actor effect: β = -0.165; spousal actor effect: β = -0.113), and that role adaptation similarly predicted each other’s anxiety (maternal partner effect: β = -0.115; spousal partner effect: β = -0.059) and depression (maternal partner effect: β = -0.129, spousal partner effect: β = -0.064). Mediation analyses suggest that future time insight and parenting competence mediate this process.ConclusionsPostpartum anxiety and depression serve as a dyadic phenomenon, where maternal and spousal role adaptation significantly affects mental health outcomes. Future time insights and feelings of parenting competence serve as mediating variables in the relationship.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08134-6.

Introduction

The birth of a child represents a significant milestone that profoundly impacts the family dynamics of both the mother and her partner. While the arrival of a newborn typically brings joy and excitement for most parents, the perinatal period is often characterized by a stressful and challenging transition, leaving both mothers and fathers vulnerable to mental health concerns. In recent years, the incidence of postpartum psychiatric disorders (PPDs) has been increasing annually, with postpartum depression affecting 13–20% of women and postpartum anxiety affecting 10–40% [1]. These conditions can impair mother-infant bonding, disrupt couple relationships, and compromise children’s emotional and behavioral development, thereby hindering overall family functioning [2–4]. Furthermore, in parallel with the diversification of family patterns, most husbands have expressed a desire to be involved in the process of having a child, to accompany and support their partners, gradually take on a role involving increased caregiving responsibilities and emotional investment similar to that of the mother [5, 6]. Although less well-researched, men during this period are also highly susceptible to psychological problems [7].

With the development of contemporary societies, mothers and spouses have different responsibilities in the family, necessitating their adaptation to the profound transformation in their roles during this critical period. According to Meles’ theory of role change [8], this process is termed as “role adaptation”. To date, research on the relationship between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression has predominantly focused on maternal groups, with relatively limited investigation into the spouses of new mothers. For mothers, studies have indicated that the level of role identity tends to increase following childbirth, and that the process of identity formation is negatively correlated with symptoms of depression and anxiety [9, 10]. In contrast, to our knowledge, no quantitative studies have been identified for their spouses, leaving the current status of spousal role adaptation and its impact on postpartum anxiety and depression largely unclear. Therefore, more studies are needed to investigate the effects of both maternal and spousal role adaptation on postpartum anxiety and depression. Such research is essential to comprehensively understand the dynamics of family roles and their implications for mental health during this transformative period.

Family systems theory posits that any factor influencing a family member, whether directly or indirectly, inevitably impacts the other members of the family [11]. This interdependence is particularly salient during the transition to parenthood, as couples simultaneously navigate the adoption of new roles while their relational dynamics undergo significant transformation [12]. Poorly managed role transitions are highly prone to triggering psychological issues. Additionally, depressive symptoms in mothers have been identified as a key predictor of paternal depression, underscoring the interdependence of family members’ mental health during this transformative period [13, 14].

Based on Social Cognitive Theory, negative emotional states, such as anxiety and depression, are perceived as potential sources of distress in anticipation of specific future events [15]. In contrast, future time insight represents a future-oriented personality trait that reflects an individual’s overall evaluation of their future selves [16]. Based on interdependence theory, an individual’s cognitive orientation toward the future may shape their partner’s psychological experiences by influencing dyadic interactions, emotional transmission, and shared coping strategies [11]. A parent with greater future time insight may engage in more proactive planning and supportive behaviors, thereby buffering their partner’s stress during the postpartum period [16]. It can be hypothesized that parents with lower levels of future time insight may experience heightened concerns about current situation and future events, which could impair their capacity to effectively cope with stress and challenges during role transitions and parenting. This, in turn, may increase the likelihood of psychological problems [17, 18]. Therefore, future time insight may act as a key factor in establishing a potential link between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression. However, current studies have primarily focused on individual experiences. Consequently, there is a need to explore the mediating role of future time insight between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression at the dyadic level.

Previous research has indicated that low parenting self-efficacy may represent a significant adverse event for parents during the child-rearing process, potentially leading to various negative health outcomes. Grounded in Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, parenting self-efficacy refers to caregivers’ confidence in their ability to successfully perform parenting tasks [19]. Empirical evidence suggests that mothers with higher parenting self-efficacy demonstrate more positive parenting behaviors and stronger mother-infant bonding [20], whereas parents with lower parenting competence may be more susceptible to negative emotions and cognitive biases [21]. Parenting competence, which evaluates both parenting self-efficacy and parenting satisfaction, serves as a crucial indicator of role adaptation [22]. Clinical studies have established a link between decreased levels of parenting competence and heightened psychological stress and distress in mothers, along with a higher risk of somatic symptoms and postnatal complications [23, 24]. Therefore, based on the existing studies, it is hypothesized that parenting competence, which is an important evaluation indicator for role adaptation, may function as an important mediating variable between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression.

Accordingly, based on the concept of maternal and spousal reciprocity, this study aimed to explore the relationship between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression, with a specific focus on the underlying mechanisms. The hypothesis posited that inadequate maternal and spousal role adaptation was associated with higher levels of postpartum anxiety and depression. Additionally, it was proposed that future time insight and feelings of parenting competence would mediate this association.

Methods

Design

This study was conducted between June 2023 and September 2024 in accordance with the guidelines set forth in the Enhancing Epidemiological Observational Studies in Cross-sectional Studies (EHSECS) report checklist.

Study setting and participants

A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit mothers and their spouses attending postpartum outpatient clinics in a tertiary-level A hospital in Hebei Province, China, for questionnaire surveys as part of the study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: ① age ≥ 20 years; ② within one year postpartum, and couples living together during pregnancy and postpartum; ③ individuals with the ability to understand and express themselves and with sufficient cognitive ability to correctly complete the questionnaire, either independently or under the guidance of the researcher. The exclusion criteria were: ① individuals with a history of severe physical illnesses or mental disorders; ② those who had high-risk pregnancies or serious pregnancy complications.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Hebei medical university (approval number: 2023009) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1995). Prior to their participation, all maternal and spouses provided informed consent.

Measures

The socio-demographic characteristics

The socio-demographic variables included age, educational level, occupation, monthly household income, planned or unplanned pregnancy, childhood traumas, and family history of depression. Additionally, perinatal data pertaining to the mothers encompassed details on the method of delivery, any complications encountered during childbirth, and the infant feeding mode.

Role adaptation survey scale

The Role Adaptation Survey Scale, developed by Lu Hong et al. [25], was employed for assessment purposes. It consists of three key dimensions: role identification, establishment of parent-child attachment, and implementation of caregiving behaviors, with each dimension comprising eight items. A five-point Likert scale (ranging from 0 to 4) was used for the assessment, with a total score between 0 and 96. Higher scores indicate superior levels of role adaptation. Specially, scores from 0 to 57 indicate a poor level, scores between 58 and 76 indicate a moderate level, and scores from 77 to 96 indicate a good level of role adaptation.

Future time insight scale

In this study, the General Future Time Insight Scale [26], developed by Song Qizheng in 2004, was adopted for the perinatal period. The scale comprises three dimensions: behavioral commitment, future imagery, and goal awareness. The scale assesses an individual’s orientation and evaluation of their general future. It consists of 12 items across these three dimensions and uses a 4-point scale (ranging from 1 to 4), with a total score of 48. Higher scores indicate a more profound insight into future time perspectives.

Chinese version of parenting sense of competence scale (C-PSOC)

The Chinese version of the Parenting Sense of Competence Scale [27], initially introduced and culturally adapted by Yang Xiao in 2014, was employed in this study. This scale comprises two dimensions of parenting efficacy (eight items) and parenting satisfaction (nine items), with a total of 17 items. It employs a 6-level scoring system (ranging from 1 to 6), with a total score range of 17–102. Higher scores indicate a higher level of parenting competence.

Generalized anxiety disorder-7 (GAD-7)

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale [28], developed by Spitzer et al. (2006), was employed to assess symptoms experienced over the past two weeks. The scale comprises seven items, each rated on a four-point scale (0–3), with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. A higher score indicates a higher level of anxiety. Specially, scores between 5 and 9 indicate mild anxiety, scores from 10 to 14 indicate moderate anxiety, and scores ranging from 15 to 21 as indicative of severe anxiety.

Edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS)

In this study, the EPDS [29], developed by Cox in 1987, was employed to assess the symptoms experienced over the past seven days. The scale consists of 10 items on a 4-point scale (0–3), with a total score of 0–30. A score of 13 or higher is recognized as the cutoff point for the diagnosis of postpartum depression.

Procedures

This study was conducted by researchers who were professionally trained to collect data. In this study, a total of 300 pairs of questionnaires were distributed, and 276 pairs of valid questionnaires were retrieved.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the questionnaires were meticulously collated, coded, and statistically analyzed using the statistical software packages SPSS 22.0 and Amos 24.0. Descriptive statistics, such as mean ± standard deviation, were utilized to characterize the socio-demographic profiles of the mothers and their partners. Pearson’s correlation analysis was applied to assess the association between the variables pertaining to the mothers and their partners. The Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) [30] was employed to examine the interdependent relationship between neonatal parental role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression. Furthermore, this study incorporated the Actor-Partner Interdependence Mediation Model (APIMeM) [31] to investigate the mediating roles of future time insight and parenting competence. The actor and partner effects were evaluated by Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) using Amos 24.0, with model fit parameters such as the chi-square/degrees of freedom (χ2/df < 3), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; < 0.08), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI; > 0.90), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI; > 0.90). The adequacy of model fit was determined using these indicators, and the mediation effects were tested via a bootstrap method, which involved 5,000 resamples. The mediating effect was confirmed if the 95% CI for the indirect effect did not encompass 0. The difference was considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

As illustrated in Table 1, the mean ages of mothers and their spouses during pregnancy were 29.4 years (SD = 3.7) and 30.9 years (SD = 4.6), respectively. The majority of mothers (88.8%) and spouses (80.8%) had obtained a bachelor’s degree or above, and over 71% of the families had a monthly income of 6,000 yuan or more; 77.2% of the mothers and 68.5% of the spouses were first-time parents. Furthermore, 5.1% of the mothers had experienced planned pregnancy, and 5.4% of the mothers reported a family history of depression. Only 58.7% of the mothers and 69.2% of the spouses had participated in pregnancy-related lectures or training in parenting skills. Additionally, 49.3% of the infants were exclusively breastfed.

Dichotomy between maternal and spousal role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression

The APIM model was then constructed using structural equation modeling, with maternal and spousal role adaptation as predictor variables and postpartum anxiety and depression as outcome variables (Fig. 1). We controlled for parents’ childhood traumas, family history of depression, occupation, and monthly household income in all analyses. The k-values were estimated to assess the relative impact of actor and partner effects. When postpartum anxiety was the dependent variable, the maternal and spousal k-value was 0.490 and 1.419, respectively. When postpartum depression was the dependent variable, the maternal and spousal k-value was 0.386 and 1.148, respectively.

Subsequently, when the maternal k-value was restricted to 0.5 and the spousal k-value to 1, none of the model chi-square changes were significant, and the model fit improved compared with the previous models. This supports the classification of the maternal model as a mixed model and the spousal model as a dyadic model. The data fit of the APIMeM model (postpartum anxiety: RMSEA = 0.043, CFI = 0.996, GFI = 0.995, χ2/df = 1.505; postpartum depression: RMSEA = 0.052, CFI = 0.995, GFI = 0.994, χ2/df = 1.755) was found to be acceptable. These findings indicate that maternal postnatal negative emotions are more strongly affected by their own role adaptation, with actor effects outweighing partner effects. In contrast, spouses’ postnatal negative emotions are influenced by both their own and their partner’s role adaptation, exhibiting comparable actor and partner effects. The path coefficients showed that for the actor effect, maternal role adaptation negatively predicted their own anxiety (β = -0.120, p < 0.001) and depression (β = -0.165, p < 0.001). Similarly, spousal role adaptation negatively predicted their own anxiety (β = -0.081, p < 0.001) and depression (β = -0.113, p < 0.001). Regarding the partner effect, maternal role adaptation negatively predicted spousal anxiety (β = -0.115, p < 0.001) and depression (β = -0.129, p < 0.001). Likewise, spousal role adaptation negatively predicted maternal anxiety (β = -0.059, p < 0.01) and depression (β = -0.064, p < 0.01), as detailed in Table 3.

Binary mediation of maternal and spousal future time insight and parenting competence in the relationship between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression

Subsequently, in order to further explore the underlying mechanism, APIMeM was developed by extending the APIM framework. This was achieved by incorporating future time insight and parenting competence into the model as mediating variables, respectively, to explore their potential mediating roles in the relationship between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression.

Panel A in Fig. 2 and Table S3 show the outcomes of the APIMeM with future time insight as the mediating variable. In the analysis where postpartum anxiety was the outcome variable, both maternal and spousal role adaptation emerged as significant predictors of future time insight (β = 0.230, p < 0.001 for mothers; β = 0.125, p < 0.001 for spouses). The relationship between future time insight and postpartum anxiety was also found to be significantly different (β = -0.205, p < 0.001 for mothers; β = -0.094, p < 0.05 for spouses). Maternal future time insight significantly mediated the relationship between maternal role adaptation and postpartum anxiety, with a significant indirect effect of aA1*bA1 = -0.047, 95% CI [-0.079; -0.024], accounting for 39.17% of the total effect. Similarly, a significant mediating effect was also observed for spousal future time insight, with aA2*bA2 = -0.012, 95% CI [-0.028; -0.002], accounting for 14.81% of the total effect.

The results of the study, which explored the relationship between future time insight and postpartum depressive mood are shown in Fig. 2, Panel B and Table S4. The analysis revealed a significant association between maternal and spousal role adaptation and future time insight (β = 0.230, p < 0.001 for mothers; β = 0.125, p < 0.001 for spouses). Furthermore, their future time insight was strongly associated with postpartum depressive mood for both mothers (β = -0.159, p < 0.001) and their spouses (β = -0.126, p < 0.05). Maternal future time insight mediated the relationship between maternal role adaptation and postpartum depressive mood, with a significant indirect effect of aA1*bA1 = -0.036, 95% CI [-0.070; -0.011], accounting for 21.82% of the total effect. A similar significant mediating effect was also observed for maternal spouses: aA2*bA2 = -0.016, 95% CI [-0.033; -0.005], accounting for 14.16% of the total effect. Furthermore, the direct effect of maternal and spousal role adaptation on both their own and each other’s postpartum anxiety and depression was equally significant.

To further elaborate on the findings, parenting competence was then incorporated into the APIMeM model (Fig. 3, Panel A and Table S5). In the analysis that incorporated postpartum anxiety as the outcome variable, maternal and spousal role adaptation emerged as a significant predictor of their own sense of parenting competence (β = 0.386, p < 0.001 for mothers; β = 0.321, p < 0.001 for spouses). The sense of parenting competence was correlated to postpartum anxiety for both mothers (β = -0.193, p < 0.001) and their spouses (β = -0.156, p < 0.001). The relationship between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety was significantly mediated by maternal parenting competence, with an indirect effect of aA1*bA1 = -0.075, 95% CI [-0.113; -0.045], accounting for 62.50% of the total effect. Similarly, a significant mediating effect was also observed for spousal parenting competence: aA2*bA2 = -0.050, 95% CI [-0.074; -0.033], accounting for 61.73% of the total effect. However, the direct effect of maternal and spousal role adaptation on their own postpartum anxiety was not statistically significant.

Finally, in the model with parenting competence as a mediator and postpartum depressive mood as an outcome variable, maternal and spousal role adaptation significantly predicted their own parenting competence (β = 0.386, p < 0.001 for mothers; β = 0.321, p < 0.001 for spouses). Furthermore, parenting competence was strongly associated with postpartum depressive mood for both mothers (β = -0.217, p < 0.001) and their spouses (β = -0.175, p < 0.001). The relationship between role adaptation and postpartum depressive mood was significantly mediated by maternal parenting competence, with an indirect effect of aA1*bA1 = -0.084, 95% CI [-0.128; -0.050], accounting for 50.90% of the total effect. A similar significant mediating effect was also observed for spousal parenting competence: aA2*bA2 = -0.056, 95% CI [-0.079; -0.039], accounting for 49.56% of the total effect. In addition, the direct effect of both maternal and spousal role adaptation on their own postpartum depression was found to be significant (Fig. 3, Panel B and Table S6).

Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to examine the underlying mechanisms contributing to maternal and spousal postpartum anxiety and depression. The findings indicated that postpartum anxiety and depression serve as a dyadic phenomenon, with both maternal and spousal role adaptation negatively affecting their own and each other’s postpartum anxiety and depression. These associations are mediated by future time insight and feelings of parenting competence.

Dichotomy between maternal and spousal role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression

For couples within the first year postpartum, higher levels of role adaptation were found to predict lower levels of postpartum anxiety and depression, a finding consistent with previous research. Psychologically stressed parents, who are less sensitive to their roles, may concentrate on managing their own emotions and reduce or avoid contact with their infants [6]. Particularly, mothers who are maladapted to their roles may avoid mothering behaviors, which may reduce their self-care and parenting efficacy. This, in turn, may foster a sense of failure, hinder their recovery process, and further diminish their ability to meet role expectations. Consequently, such circumstances can escalate stress, despair and ultimately, anxiety and depression [32, 33]. The perinatal period is characterized by elevated emotional demands and a delicate balance between professional and familial responsibilities, which can exert significant mental strain on both partners. However, men often demonstrate a marked reluctance to seek assistance, a behaviour that, in conjunction with the emotional ramifications of the couple’s relationship, significantly amplifies the risks of postpartum anxiety and depression for both partners [34].

An examination of the APIM pairwise model for mothers and spouses revealed a mixed model for mothers and a dyadic model for spouses. These findings suggested that for mothers, their postpartum anxiety and depression were predominantly influenced by their own role adaptation. This could be attributed to the mother’s more direct involvement in pregnancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding, which facilitates a closer physical and emotional connection with the child. Additionally, the mother experiences heightened physiological discomfort and mood fluctuations due to the significant role transition [35, 36]. For spouses, their negative emotions were equally impacted by their own role adaptation and that of the mother. The difficulties encountered by spouses in the postnatal period may stem from the challenge of balancing the roles of parent and employee, a lack of adequate support and assistance, and changes in the dynamics of the couple’s relationship. Collectively, these factors can collectively lead to stress and negative emotional states [37].

Binary mediation of maternal and spousal future time insight and parenting competence in the relationship between role adaptation and postpartum anxiety and depression

After incorporating future time insight into the model as a mediator, we found that role adaptation in mothers and their spouses influenced postpartum anxiety and depression through future time insight. These findings were consistent with pre-established expectations. Previous study has indicated that individuals experiencing postpartum anxiety and depression often encounter more discomfort during role transition and have more negative emotional experiences [38]. Conversely, those with a more positive outlook on the future exhibited better emotional well-being, characterized by adaptive emotion regulation and lower levels of anxiety, depression, and perceived stress [39, 40].

Subsequently, when the parenting competence variable was incorporated into the model for analysis, the results showed that the role adaptation of mothers and spouses influenced the occurrence of postpartum depression through each other’s parenting competence, aligning with previous studies. Mothers with a high level of awareness of their maternal role are capable of employing their parenting skills, thereby facilitating the healthy development of their infants [38, 41]. Conversely, parents with low parenting efficacy often lack the necessary experience and skills to deal with parenting challenges effectively. This deficiency leads to diminished parenting satisfaction, which in turn affects the parent-child relationship and the parents’ own psychological well-being [42, 43].

Furthermore, the present study found that the spouses’ sense of parenting competence did not predict maternal postpartum anxiety. From the perspective of interdependence theory, during the postpartum period, maternal parenting competence may play a dominant role within the dyadic relationship, potentially overshadowing the influence of spousal parenting competence on maternal emotional outcomes. Additionally, cultural factors may also shape role expectations and help-seeking patterns: in the early postpartum period, mothers may rely more on their own role adaptation rather than their partner’s parenting competence when managing anxiety-related symptoms.This interpretation aligns with prior research suggesting that mothers tend to exhibit more avoidant and intrusive symptoms compared to their spouses [44]. As a result, mothers may be more focused on managing their own emotions during the early postpartum period, and less aware of their spouses’ parenting efforts. Consequently, their spouses’ sense of parenting competence may have a limited impact on them. Furthermore, it was discovered that some parents did not experience postpartum depression, but instead reported a range of symptoms, including insomnia, daytime sleepiness, headaches, nervousness, and anxiety. This led to a higher prevalence of anxiety compared to depression. Additionally, the spouse’s perception of their partner’s parenting competence was identified as a significant factor affecting maternal postpartum depression, although it had a relatively weak mediating effect on anxiety.

In conclusion, this study makes several important theoretical contributions to the existing literature on perinatal mental health. Firstly, it expands the application of the APIM to the context of role adaptation and perinatal mental health, demonstrating how both individual and dyadic processes collaboratively shape postpartum anxiety and depression. Secondly, by pinpointing the mediating roles of future time insight and parenting competence, the study enhances our understanding of the mechanisms through which role adaptation impacts psychological outcomes for both mothers and fathers. Collectively, these contributions deepen our theoretical understanding of dyadic dynamics during the transition to parenthood and offer a foundation for the development of couple-based interventions.

However, it is crucial to acknowledge several limitations of this study. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the present study restricts our ability to elucidate the temporal sequence and causal relationship between variables. Secondly, to maintain the simplicity of the APIM model, we did not incorporate future time insight and sense of parenting competence into it. Finally, the present study was conducted in a single hospital in Hebei Province, leading to a relatively small sample size. And also, parents attending provincial and municipal hospitals may represent a subset with higher cultural and economic status, and may be more inclined to survey participation, exhibiting a general willingness to be surveyed. Consequently, the generalizability of the results warrants further consideration.

Looking head, future research should first focus on developing a screening scale for postpartum anxiety and depression to enhance the sensitivity of identifying these conditions. Secondly, longitudinal studies are essential to establish the causal relationships between variables and to explore the potential pathogenesis of postpartum anxiety and depression. Furthermore, subsequent studies should consider integrating important variables, such as family history of depression and neuroticism, into the model. This could potentially identify these factors as crucial intervention points to improve our understanding and management of postpartum anxiety and depression. Finally, there is a pressing need for more large-scale, mixed-method studies employing dichotomous designs. Such studies would not only explore the relationship between variables but also analyze the differences between mothers and spouses, thereby further elucidating the role of family dynamics in shaping the physical and mental health of mothers, their spouses, and offspring.

Conclusion and relevance to clinical practice

In conclusion, the findings of this study underscore a negative correlation between maternal and spousal role adaptation and the development of postpartum anxiety and depression, with future time insight and the sense of parenting competence serving as mediating factors. Consequently, it is imperative in clinical practice to closely monitor maternal and spousal adjustment during the perinatal period and provide timely interventions. Specifically, healthcare professionals should assist parents in accessing both internal and external resources to support their transition into new roles. This assistance should encompass enhancing their future time insight and sense of parenting competence, as well as fostering the development of effective coping strategies. By doing so, we can reduce the likelihood of negative emotion outcomes and effectively mitigate the risk of postpartum anxiety and depression, ultimately promoting the well-being of both parents and their offspring.
