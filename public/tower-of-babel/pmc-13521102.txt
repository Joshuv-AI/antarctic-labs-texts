Proton Therapy Utilization Among Adult Cancer Patients
Authors: Casey Hollawell, Siqi Zhang, Ian Messing, Alexis Girón Santos, Jonathan Baron, Cole Friedes, Lily Min, Rachel Eisenberg, Karishma Khullar, Russell Maxwell, Jeffrey Bradley, Curtiland Deville, James Metz, Steven Feigenberg, Theresa Busch, Michael LaRiviere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeProton therapy (Pr) can spare normal tissue and reduce toxicity; however, it remains a limited resource. The aim of this study was to evaluate demographic and clinical differences in the use of Pr amongst adult cancer patients at a single institution, with particular attention to the impact of insurance coverage.Materials/MethodsAdult patients who underwent radiation between 2015 and 2021 were retrospectively reviewed. Multivariable (MVA) logistic regression analyses were used to assess associations between Pr use and variables including sex, sexual orientation, age, race, ethnicity, marital status, preferred language, smoking status, year of treatment, treatment intent, diagnosis, and distance to facility. Propensity score matching was then used to further validate associations between race and travel distance. Separately, Medicare-insured patients were considered an individual cohort and analyzed utilizing the same demographic variables noted.ResultsOf 12,320 patients identified, 2342 and 9978 received Pr and Photon therapy (Ph), respectively. On MVA, Black and Asian race, current smoker, and shorter straight-line distance to the facility remained associated with lower likelihood of treatment with Pr. On propensity-matched scoring, Black patients and patients living within 250 mi of the center remained significantly less likely to receive Pr. Of 4258 Medicare-only patients, 1261 and 2997 received Pr and Ph therapy, respectively. On MVA analysis, female sex, Black race, and decreasing distance to the facility were associated with lower likelihood of receiving Pr therapy. On propensity-matched scoring, Black patients and patients living within 250 mi of the center remained significantly less likely to receive Pr.ConclusionsWe identified significant differences in the use of Pr as a function of distance to site and race in both a heterogeneous adult patient cohort and a Medicare cohort. As health systems expand Pr therapy capacity, understanding how clinico-demographic factors shape access, even under conditions of broad insurance coverage, may inform future infrastructure planning.

Introduction

Proton therapy (Pr) is a form of radiotherapy (RT) that exploits the Bragg peak to deposit dose within treatment targets while reducing radiation and toxicity to nearby structures.1 Unlike photon therapy (Ph), charged Pr particles can deposit a dose over a target range with a sharp dose falloff that limits distal dose to radiation-sensitive organs at risk. It may be favored over conventional Ph RT when treating tumors with complex surrounding anatomy. There are several clinical scenarios within a variety of disease sites that warrant consideration of Pr. Per ACR-ASTRO practice parameters, examples such as central nervous system (CNS) and head and neck (H&N) tumors represent anatomic locations in which sparing local structures, such as normal brain tissue or major salivary glands, with Pr may limit long-term toxicity and improve quality of life.2 Despite increasing indications and number of proton centers, it remains a limited resource. In addition to requiring substantial capital investment to establish and maintain treatment facilities, Pr is associated with considerably higher treatment costs than conventional Ph.3 In a Mayo Clinic analysis using standardized Medicare reimbursement rates, the mean cost of Pr was $45 966 compared with $17 541 for Ph.4 Consequently, there are roughly 45 and 32 Pr centers throughout the United States and Europe, respectively. Most of these centers are in large cities and academic centers.5

Given the limited nature of this resource, variability in access is a concern. Prior studies have demonstrated racial and geographic disparities amongst patients receiving Pr versus Ph RT, particularly at a disease site level.6, 7, 8 Additionally, racial disparities can be related to differences in insurance coverage that may limit access to healthcare, particularly advanced or expensive treatments. The interplay between socioeconomic status (SES), health literacy, education level, and other health determinants also play a role in patients’ access and ability to seek advanced or expensive technology.6, 7, 9, 10, 11 The aim of this study was to evaluate demographic and clinical differences in the use of Pr therapy amongst adult cancer patients at a single academic institution, with attention to the impact of insurance coverage.

Methods and materials

We conducted an IRB-approved retrospective analysis of adult patients treated with external beam RT at a single facility between July 2015 and 2021. The electronic medical record identified all patients (N = 16 945) treated with RT between these dates. From this cohort, pediatric patients under age 18 and patients participating on clinical trial were excluded to produce a total cohort of 12 320 patients. These patients were included in subsequent analyses as described below.

Demographic variables included sex, sexual orientation, age, race, ethnicity, marital status, preferred language, smoking status, treatment year, treatment intent, diagnosis category (disease site), financial class, and straight-line distance from home to treatment center. Preferred language was categorized as English or other. Financial class, or insurance carrier, was categorized as Medicare, Managed Medicare, Managed Care, Blue Shield, Medicaid, Commercial, and Blue Cross. Separately, patients covered by Medicare were considered an individual cohort and analyzed utilizing the same demographic variables as noted above.

StatistIcal consideration

The primary objective was to compare the characteristics of all adult cancer patients who underwent Pr versus Ph at our treatment facility. In separate analyses, Medicare insured patients were evaluated in the same manner. Demographic variables were compared between the Pr and Ph groups. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the t-test.

All demographic variables were included in univariate logistic regression models to assess their individual associations with Pr. Variables demonstrating clinical relevance and statistical significance (P < .05) were considered for inclusion in the multivariable (MVA) logistic regression model. To address potential multicollinearity among predictor variables, we calculated Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) to avoid collinearity in the MVA logistic regression. Distance was dichotomized at 250 miles for regression analyses.

Given the focus on race and distance as primary variables of interest, we employed propensity score matching to further investigate their associations with Pr. Separate propensity score models were developed for race (Black vs other) and distance (<250 miles vs >250 miles), matching patients on these variables to create comparable groups. A 250-mile threshold was selected a priori as a pragmatic, study-specific cutoff, as a radius of approximately 250 miles encompasses much of our regional referral base while excluding nearby major academic Pr centers in the Northeast. This cutoff was intended to identify patients traveling beyond the catchment area where another major academic Pr therapy center may reasonably be available. This approach aimed to reduce confounding and provide a clearer understanding of the relationships between these variables and treatment with Pr. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.3.

Results

Most patients were non-Hispanic or Latino (98%), were heterosexual (98%), and had a preferred language of English (97%). Smoking status was reported as 9% current, 46% former and 45% never smokers. Clinical characteristics included 65% of patients treated with curative intent, 33% treated with palliative intent, 1% treated for local control, and 1% with benign disease. Disease sites included 9% breast, 17% thoracic, 9% genitourinary (GU), 2% gynecologic (GYN), 11% CNS, 9% H&N, 1% endocrine, 11% gastrointestinal (GI), 13% hematologic, and 18% musculoskeletal (MSK), which includes bone metastases.

The demographics of the Ph and Pr cohorts differed significantly by marital status (P = .007) and smoking status (P < .001) (Table 1). The mean straight-line distance to the treatment facility was significantly further for patients receiving Pr vs Ph therapy (56 miles vs 32 miles, respectively; P < .001). Of the Pr cohort, 82%, 14%, 2% and 2% of patients were white, Black, Asian, and other, respectively, compared with 69%, 27%, 4% and 1% in the Ph cohort (P < .001).

Treatment years 2016 to 2021 were significantly associated with receiving Pr relative to 2015. Clinically, thoracic, GU, neurologic, H&N, endocrine, and GI disease sites were significantly associated with higher rates of treatment with Pr compared to breast as a disease site. Propensity score matching was performed with attention to race and straight-line distance to the facility. For the Black race, the mean difference and 95% CI for the matched cohort was −0.091 [−0.172, −0.070], indicating that Black patients were significantly less likely to receive Pr compared to other races. For distance (<250 miles versus > 250 miles), the mean difference and 95% CI for the matched cohort was −0.121 [−0.280, −0.024], indicating that patients living within 250 miles of the treatment center were significantly less likely to receive Pr compared to those living >250 miles from PCAM.

This approach attempted to inform on the likelihood that patients receive Pr independent of insurance as a barrier to access. A total of 4258 adult Medicare patients were identified. Patients received either Ph (2997, 70%) or Pr (1261, 30%) during the study period. Discussion

In this analysis, we examined the use of Pr and Ph in an adult cancer population at a large, urban, academic center. Among all patients, MVA analysis revealed that lower likelihood of receiving Pr was associated with Black race and Asian race, palliative intent, decreasing distance to the treatment center, and non-Medicare insurance types, excluding commercial insurance. Black patients and patients living within 250 mi of the center remained significantly less likely to receive Pr on propensity score matching. In the cohort of patients with Medicare insurance we noted similar trends. MVA analysis revealed that lower likelihood of receiving Pr was associated with Black race, female sex, palliative-intent treatment, and decreasing straight-line distance to the treatment center. Black patients and patients living within 250 mi of our center remained less likely to receive Pr on propensity score matching in the Medicare cohort as well.

There have been several publications examining disparities in the utilization of Pr at a disease site level. Woodhouse et al demonstrated that race and residence distance from the facility were associated with less likelihood of undergoing Pr within a prostate cancer population.7 Similarly, in a pediatric population, Bitterman et al demonstrated that Black pediatric patients were less likely to receive Pr when compared to their non-Hispanic white counterparts, despite being enrolled on prospective clinical trials for which treatment is standardized.8 A National Cancer Database analysis of pediatric patients with primary CNS malignancies also revealed that Black patients, as well as those residing in urban or rural areas, were less likely to receive Pr.12 Additionally, in a cross-sectional analysis, Nogueira et al showed that despite having Pr-eligible malignancies, Black patients were less likely to receive Pr therapy when compared with their White counterparts.13 Our findings align with these previously reported conclusions, but within a cohort of adult cancer patients at a single institution.

Along with racial disparities, proximity to a proton center leads to variability in access. In a study from Washington University, it was shown that patients receiving Pr traveled a mean distance of 83.3 miles compared with 47.4 miles for Ph,14 demonstrating patients’ willingness to travel further distances for advanced technology. Burus et al. similarly identified rurality and living below the poverty line as factors associated with the highest risk of geographic isolation from a Pr center (3). In our cohort, patients receiving Pr also traveled on average greater distances than patients receiving Ph. One explanation for this is that patients traveling from a further distance have the means to take off work, secure lodging, and have the social and financial support to facilitate traveling for treatment. These factors likely reflect broader socioeconomic differences that influence patients' ability to access specialized cancer care and may disproportionately affect historically underserved populations.

Additionally, insurance can be a barrier to access to Pr and coverage can be inconsistent among providers. There is also interplay between insurance coverage and race, with a study by McDonald et al demonstrating that Black, Indigenous, and people of color were significantly more likely to have “proton unfavorable” insurance when compared to non-Hispanic White patients.6 In a single institution study, Bishop et al demonstrated that young adult patients were significantly less likely to receive Pr approval when compared to pediatric patients, with more than half of adult patients receiving an initial denial when requested.15 Medicare approval rates are also significantly higher compared to private insurance.16 Again, to eliminate the barrier that insurance creates in access to Pr, to our knowledge, we are the first publication to analyze patients covered by Medicare as an individual. Despite this separate analysis, we still found that Black race and distance traveled, along with female sex, to be associated with a lower likelihood of receiving Pr. Given that older adults are beneficiaries of Medicare, one explanation is that there is a lower concern for late treatment toxicity in this patient population and therefore less of a necessity for Pr; however, this rationale should not vary across race, sex, and distance. Additionally, breast cancer patients make up a considerable proportion of our female Medicare population and are typically treated with standard of care 3D Ph. Pr are infrequently used in this disease site; however, this does not adequately account for the differences in gender and race seen on the MVA analysis. Additional factors likely contribute to these disparities. Differences in referral patterns, health literacy, and awareness of Pr may influence whether patients are evaluated at Pr centers or seek second opinions regarding advanced treatment modalities. Patients with greater resources or familiarity with navigating the healthcare system may be better positioned to advocate for referral to specialized centers. Furthermore, as our institution serves as a regional proton therapy referral center, patients referred from facilities without Pr capability may elect to receive all oncologic care at our institution, irrespective of geographic distance, potentially influencing observed utilization patterns. Collectively, these findings suggest that disparities in proton therapy access are multifactorial and cannot be explained by insurance coverage alone. And while this analysis was conducted in an urban center within the United States, the persistence of racial and geographic access patterns within a Medicare-insured population suggests that coverage alone may be insufficient to ensure equitable access to centralized technologies. These findings are relevant to other health systems as they expand Pr capacity and design referral pathways.

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the retrospective nature of the analysis introduces biases inherent to data collection and patient selection. For example, there are unmeasured confounding factors, such as stage, performance status, radiation field, tumor location, etc. that could influence the decision to recommend Pr. Additionally, our analysis includes all patients who received Pr or Ph therapy across various anatomical sites, which introduces heterogeneity throughout the cohort. Despite providing a comprehensive view of treatment utilization patterns, this broad inclusion criteria limits our ability to assess site-specific disparities in a detailed manner. Another limitation is the study's single-center design, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or geographic regions. The demographics and referral patterns at this center may not fully represent those of the general population. For example, our institution has international partnerships that provide access and coverage for Pr, which undoubtedly impacts distance traveled. Additionally, our institution takes part in reference pricing and price parity with insurers to make Pr more accessible, making our findings less generalizable to hospitals that do not participate. Finally, socioeconomic variables, including income, education level, and other social determinants of health, were not available for analysis. These factors may influence referral patterns, treatment decision-making, travel burden, and access to Pr and should be incorporated into future investigations.

Despite these limitations, this study offers valuable insights into potential variability in access to Pr, highlighting the importance of further investigation. Our findings are amongst a large, heterogeneous population, which underscore the need for a more granular analysis of factors influencing access to Pr. Future studies at our institution are examining disease-specific cohorts, including H&N and GI malignancies, to incorporate more detailed clinical and treatment-related factors and evaluate whether racial and geographic disparities in proton therapy utilization persist within more homogeneous disease populations.17, 18

Finally, as more Pr centers open, an awareness of these trends is critical to consider when planning for these expansions, so that Pr therapy is available regardless of clinical and demographic factors.

Conclusion

We identified significant differences in the use of Pr as a function of distance to site and race in both a heterogeneous adult patient cohort and a Medicare cohort. As health systems expand Pr therapy capacity, understanding how clinico-demographic factors shape access, even under conditions of broad insurance coverage, may inform future infrastructure planning.
