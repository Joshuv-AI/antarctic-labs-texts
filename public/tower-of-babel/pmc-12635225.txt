Directed evolution of phages in biofilms enhances Pseudomonas aeruginosa control through improved lipopolysaccharide recognition
Authors: Luciana Meneses, Lucie Valentová, Sílvio B Santos, Jelena Erdmann, Aurélie Crabbé, Pavel Plevka, Susanne Häussler, Diana P Pires, Tom Coenye, Joana Azeredo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudomonas aeruginosa is a leading cause of chronic lung infections in cystic fibrosis (CF) patients. While bacteriophages hold potential as a treatment for antibiotic-resistant infections, the complex structure and heterogeneity of P. aeruginosa biofilms pose significant challenges to phage therapy. In this study, we investigate the adaptive evolution of the Pbunavirus phage PE1 to biofilms formed by a CF-derived P. aeruginosa isolate. Our findings reveal that biofilm-adapted PE1 mutants exhibit enhanced efficacy in controlling biofilms in vitro under conditions mimicking the CF lung environment. This improvement is attributed to the mutants’ increased ability to recognize the diverse populations within the biofilm. Using a combination of cryo-EM, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) profiling, and adsorption assays, we demonstrate that mutations in tail fiber and baseplate genes of the phage improve adsorption and enable recognition of truncated LPS variants. This study highlights the critical role of biofilm heterogeneity in limiting phage effectiveness, identifies mechanisms to overcome this barrier, and pinpoints specific genomic targets for engineering phages tailored for therapeutic applications in CF patients.Subject terms: Bacteriophages, Biofilms, Bacterial infection, Experimental evolution

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disease that affects multiple organs including the lungs, where impaired chloride secretion results in the buildup of thick layers of mucus, creating the ideal habitat for bacterial colonization1. The main driver of lung function decline and associated mortality in patients with CF is chronic lung infection with Pseudomonas aeruginosa2. The adaptation of this bacterial pathogen to the lung environment, strongly marked by phenotypic alterations and biofilm formation3, together with high levels of antibiotic resistance4, are the basis for P. aeruginosa persistence in the lungs despite prolonged antibiotic treatments.

As for other antibiotic-resistant infections, (bacterio)phages, viruses that specifically and exclusively infect and kill bacteria, are receiving increased interest and showing promising results for managing chronic P. aeruginosa lung infections in CF patients5–9. Besides the efficacy against antibiotic resistant bacteria and the absence of major side effects, phages are recognized for their capacity to combat biofilms, due to their ability to penetrate and replicate inside the biofilm, produce enzymes that degrade the biofilm matrix, and/or infect persister cells typically present within these bacterial communities10,11. However, complete elimination of biofilms with phages remains highly challenging, as in vitro studies indicate the potential for bacterial regrowth and proliferation of phage-resistant bacteria12.

It is well known that phages can be experimentally evolved in the laboratory to improve their efficacy against the target strain and reduce the development of bacterial phage resistance during treatment13. In general, phage adaptation protocols, such as those based on the Appelmans’ protocol14, and many phage-host coevolution experiments are commonly conducted in planktonic (liquid) cultures15,16. However, given that biofilm formation by P. aeruginosa is the main cause of persistent infection in the lungs of CF patients17 we developed a protocol specifically aimed at directing phage adaptation to P. aeruginosa biofilms rather than planktonic cultures. Pseudomonas phage vB_PaeM_PE1 (short name PE1) was selected for the experimental evolution in biofilms formed by P. aeruginosa M800, a clinical strain isolated from a CF patient with chronic lung infection. In addition, to demonstrate the enhanced anti-biofilm properties of these adapted phages, we thoroughly characterized the evolved phage-host interactions to explain the mechanisms behind this improvement. This includes an in-depth analysis of the biofilm-adapted phages and the bacterial biofilms they target, revealing the specific phage alterations responsible for the increased efficacy and the bacterial determinants that influence these interactions.

Results

Phage PE1 characterization and adaptation to P. aeruginosa biofilms

Transmission electron cryomicroscopy (cryo-TEM) of Phage vB_PaeM_PE1 (short name PE1), isolated in the present study using P. aeruginosa PAO1 as host, revealed a myovirus morphology of contractile long tails, with uncontracted (Fig. 1A) and contracted (Fig. 1B) forms, an icosahedral head of 70 nm in diameter, and six tail fibers connected to a baseplate. The double stranded DNA genome of phage PE1, with a size of 66,600 bp and 103 coding sequences (Supplementary Fig. 1), was deposited in GenBank with the accession number PP848306. Comparative genome analysis revealed high levels of similarity with members of the Pbunavirus genus. Intergenomic similarity with all members of this genus was further evaluated using VIRIDIC (Supplementary Fig. 2), and a phylogenetic tree was generated with VICTOR based on amino acid sequences comparison (Supplementary Fig. 3), confirming that phage PE1 belongs to the Caudoviricetes class and Pbunavirus genus, and shares the highest levels of similarity with phages PA8P118 and vB_PaeM_E21519.

A novel protocol was implemented for the adaptation of phage PE1 to biofilms formed by the CF lung isolate P. aeruginosa M800 (Fig. 1C, see Methods), for which the efficiency of plating (EOP) of phage PE1 was low (EOP = 0.09). After this targeted phage adaptation, five phage plaques were selected for small-scale production and were subsequently tested for 24 h against 24-h-old M800 biofilms formed in 24-well plates. The two phages resulting in better visual dispersion of the biofilm aggregates, phages PE1–3 and PE1–5, were selected for further characterization.

Biofilm-adapted phages have enhanced biofilm-control efficacy

The efficacy of the phages against P. aeruginosa M800 was firstly determined by a phage plaque formation assay, revealing higher efficiency of plating for the biofilm-adapted phages PE1–3 (EOP = 1.163) and PE1–5 (EOP = 1.145) compared to the wildtype phage PE1, and formation of clear phage plaques (Fig. 2A). Then, to compare the capacity of the biofilm-adapted and wildtype phages to control P. aeruginosa M800 biofilms, biofilms were formed in 24-well plates for 24 h and treated with phages for 24 h at 1 × 107 PFU/mL. Besides Luria Broth (LB), a synthetic cystic fibrosis sputum medium (SCFM2) was also used to better mimic the lung environment. Visual inspection of the biofilms indicated that suspended aggregates of P. aeruginosa M800 were more affected by phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 than by PE1 (Fig. 2B, C). These results were confirmed following biofilm quantification through the colony forming unit (CFU) assay. Compared to the untreated control, phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 significantly reduced the number of biofilm culturable cells after 24 h (Fig. 2D) both in LB and SCFM2, while no statistically significant CFU reduction was observed for phage PE1. Although phage adaptation was performed against 24-h-old biofilms, the adapted phages were also more effective than the wildtype phage in controlling 48 h biofilms (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Next, we evaluated the capacity of the phages to control host-associated bacteria, using a previously established in vivo-like 3-D lung cell culture model20. For this, 3-D lung epithelial cells were infected with P. aeruginosa M800 for 2 h and exposed to the different phages at 1 × 107 PFU/mL for 4 h. In this model, the biofilm-adapted phage PE1–5 was the only phage capable of causing a significant reduction in the number of host-associated M800 (Fig. 2E). The total period of 6 h used for this experiment was selected to avoid bacterial cytotoxicity to the epithelial cells, based on previous experiments with P. aeruginosa PAO120. To confirm that our selected infection conditions – P. aeruginosa M800, phage lysates, or their combination – preserve epithelial cell viability during the experimental timeframe, the intracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay was performed. The results showed that the viability of the epithelial cells was maintained for all the conditions tested, as shown by similar levels of LDH release after 6 h compared to an uninfected and untreated control (Fig. 2F).

Biofilm-adapted phages have mutations in baseplate wedge and tail fiber genes

To determine the mutations that enable PE1 to invade biofilm, DNA from phages PE1, PE1–3 and PE1–5 was sequenced. The biofilm-adapted phages share two mutations in (gene product) gp76 and gp78, and phage PE1–5 has an extra mutation in gp77 (Fig. 3A). Gp76 is a baseplate wedge protein, in which serine 324 is mutated to phenylalanine (S324F) in both biofilm-adapted phages (Fig. 3A, B). In the tail fiber protein gp78, threonine 896 is mutated to isoleucine (T896I) (Fig. 3A, D). Both in gp76 and gp78, the mutations result in alteration from polar amino acids to amino acids with hydrophobic side chains. Besides these alterations shared by the two biofilm-adapted phages, in the baseplate protein gp77 of phage PE1–5 serine 72 is replaced by proline (S72P) (Fig. 3A, C). All the mutations identified in the biofilm-adapted phages were confirmed by Sanger Sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although the DNA extracted from phages originated from a single phage plaque, the sequencing results for phage PE1–5 revealed two peaks, corresponding to cytosine and thymine at the mutated positions in gp76 (Supplementary Fig. 5A) and gp77 (Supplementary Fig. 5B). When comparing the amino acid sequences of gp76, gp77, and gp78 with their 10 closest homologs using BlastP21, we observed that the mutations acquired by the biofilm-adapted phages were not present in any of the homologs (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To identify the location of the mutated amino acid residues in the phage proteins, structures of gp76 (Fig. 3B), gp77 (Fig. 3C), and gp78 (Fig. 3D) were predicted using AlphaFold2. Proteins E217-gp44, E217-gp45, and E217-gp46 of Pseudomonas phage E21722, a previously characterized phage from Pbunavirus genus, share high levels of sequence identity with proteins gp76 (100%), gp77 (97.6%), and gp78 (81.9%) of the Pbunavirus phage PE1, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Based on the similarity with proteins from phages T4 and E217, the mutations identified in gp76 and gp77 are located in regions of the baseplate that are not involved in the interactions with the bacterial host. Regarding the tail fiber gp78, the mutated amino acid residue is positioned at the tip of its C-terminal loop (Fig. 3D) and is probably implicated in bacterial recognition and binding. Given that gp78 forms a trimer and cryo-TEM images have shown that phage PE1 has six tail fibers, this hydrophobic mutation is propagated 18 times per virion. We thus assume that this mutation enhances the binding capacity of the biofilm-adapted phages, which is in line with the increased EOP observed for PE1–3 and PE1–5 on P. aeruginosa M800 (Fig. 2A).

Phage adaptation to biofilms improved the efficiency of plating

As the mutations observed in the biofilm-adapted phages occurred in genes directly associated with host recognition and attachment, we subsequently tested whether phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 had increased efficiency against colonies isolated from P. aeruginosa M800 biofilms. For that, 48 colonies (M800BF-24hC.1 – M800BF-24hC.48) were isolated from a control 24 h-old M800 biofilm prepared the same way as during the biofilm-adaption process. Also, 48 colonies (M800BF-T.1 – M800BF-T.48) were isolated from a 24 h-old biofilm treated with phage PE1 for 24 h, according to the protocol followed for biofilm formation and first phage treatment in the biofilm-adaptation process. It was observed that both for the untreated (Fig. 4A) and treated (Fig. 4B) biofilms, the efficiency of plating of phage PE1 against the biofilm-isolated colonies is highly variable and generally lower, contrasting with the higher and constant values obtained for the biofilm-adapted phages, except for 3 colonies resistant to phage PE1.

These colonies (Fig. 4B; M800BF-T.5, M800BF-T.6, M800BF-T.11) represent 3 of the 48 colonies isolated from the biofilm treated with phage PE1, and although they have acquired resistance to phage PE1, they are still susceptible to phages PE1–3 and PE1–5, despite the lower efficiency compared to the remaining colonies. The varied susceptibility of the biofilm-isolated colonies to phage PE1, particularly before treatment (Fig. 4A), suggest the existence of heterogeneity among the biofilm cells, which does not impact the biofilm-adapted phages that can target the biofilm population evenly.

To understand if the mutations acquired by the biofilm-adapted phages also had an impact on their efficacy against other P. aeruginosa strains, we next compared the EOP of phages PE1, PE1–3 and PE1–5 against a panel of 45 P. aeruginosa clinical strains (Supplementary Fig. 7). We observed that the host range was maintained, with the three phages each infecting 63% of the strains tested, which was expected as only a single P. aeruginosa strain was used during the phage adaptation process. Nevertheless, the average EOP increased for the biofilm-adapted phages, showing that the biofilm-adapted phages not only demonstrate improved infectivity towards M800 and M800 biofilm-derived isolates, but also to other P. aeruginosa strains.

Increased adsorption of the biofilm-adapted phages

Next, we determined whether the improved infectivity of the biofilm-adapted phages was related to differences in phage cycle parameters. The one-step growth curves performed with P. aeruginosa PAO1, the host used for phage production, revealed no significant differences between the growth parameters of phages PE1, PE1–3 and PE1–5 (Fig. 5A). However, the adsorption assay revealed faster adsorption of the biofilm-adapted phages to the propagating host, compared to the wildtype phage (Fig. 5B). Within the first seconds of infection, nearly 80% of PE1–3 and PE1–5 phage particles are adsorbed to PAO1 cells, whereas over 70% of PE1 virions remain unadsorbed. Also, after 2 min of infection, all biofilm-adapted phages are adsorbed, while phage PE1 is not able to reach 100% of adsorption within the first 8 min of infection. To understand if the increased adsorption of the biofilm-adapted phages was associated with the recognition of an additional receptor in the host cells, we used cryo-TEM to observe the interaction of the three phages with PAO1 cells. Although no interaction of the phages with bacterial structures other than the membrane was observed at 10 min post infection, the number of phages adsorbed to the cells was significantly higher for PE1–3 (Fig. 5D) and PE1–5 (Fig. 5E), compared to the wildtype phage, for which few phages were attached to each cell (Fig. 5C). Taken together, these results point to the improved bacterial recognition by the phages following the biofilm-adaptation process.

Biofilm-adapted phages can infect cells with truncated or absent O-specific antigen

It has been demonstrated for other members of the Pbunavirus genus that these phages use the bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS) as receptors in the host membrane22–24. Therefore, considering the higher number of biofilm-adapted phages attached to PAO1 in Cryo-TEM observation (Fig. 5D, E) and their higher adsorption rates in the adsorption assay (Fig. 5B), we next tested the efficacy of the three phages against 4 LPS isogenic mutant strains of PAO1. The results (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 2) show that the propagation of phage PE1 is dependent on the presence of the O-specific antigen (OSA, formerly called B-band), as this phage does not produce phage plaques on a Δwzy strain (truncated OSA), nor on ΔwbpL and ΔrmlC strains (completely devoid of OSA), being only able to infect Δrmd strain with intact OSA. On the other hand, phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 can infect Δwzy and ΔwbpL strains, revealing that during the biofilm-adaption process, phage PE1 has evolved to infect cells with a truncated OSA or no OSA at all. As phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 propagate on a ΔwbpL strain devoid of OSA with intact outer core, but not on ΔrmlC devoid of OSA with truncated outer core, these data strongly suggest the LPS outer core is a new LPS moiety recognized by the phages after directed adaptation on M800 biofilms.

Considering that phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 have shown improved EOP on P. aeruginosa M800, we next determined the LPS profile of this strain with silver staining, revealing a core similar to the one observed for PAO1 and absence of the long chain OSA (Fig. 6B), which supports the low efficiency of phage PE1 against this strain, given its ineffectiveness against the OSA-deficient mutants of PAO1 (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 2).

The analysis of the LPS profile was also performed for the biofilm-isolated colonies resistant to phage PE1, and compared to the M800 strain, used for biofilm formation. The colonies resistant to phage PE1 revealed distinct LPS profiles that differ from the original M800 strain (Fig. 6B), with the bands corresponding to core plus 1 O-antigen repeat being particularly marked in M800BF-T.5 and M800BF-T.11, and with an uncapped core being mostly pronounced in M800BF-T.6, again supporting the inefficacy of phage PE1 against these isolates.

Taken together, these results indicate that the M800 strain used for phage adaptation already had alterations at the LPS level, marked by reduced production of OSA (Fig. 6B), which can explain the low efficacy of phage PE1. Also, when phage PE1 is used to treat the M800 biofilm, the cells can acquire phage resistance by further LPS modification towards core+1 or uncapped core phenotypes (Fig. 6B), explaining how the biofilm-adapted phages have evolved to be able to infect those resistant isolates and other bacteria with truncated LPS (Fig. 6A).

Dynamics of the biofilm population at genomic level

Our results showed that the increased biofilm-control efficacy of the phages obtained following adaptation on M800 biofilms was due to an improved recognition of the M800 strain, as well as of the bacterial population generated during biofilm growth and treatment with phage PE1. Therefore, in order to understand the dynamics of the biofilm populations at the genomic level, we compared the genome of M800 strain with the genomes of the 48 colonies isolated from the untreated 24 h-old M800 biofilm (M800BF-24hC), as well as the 48 colonies isolated from the 24 h-old biofilm treated with phage PE1 for 24 h (M800BF-T), and a second control with 48 colonies isolated from an untreated 48 h-old biofilm (M800BF-C). The results (Fig. 7) show the existence of two markedly distinct populations formed by the closely related members of the 24 h and 48 h untreated controls, and a separate phage-treated group. We could also confirm that the biofilm population is highly heterogeneous, particularly in the untreated controls, and that this diversity is reduced upon treatment with phage PE1, indicating a shift in the biofilm population in response to the phages. These results, together with the varied EOP of phage PE1 on the biofilm-isolates and the stable and higher levels of PE1–3 and PE1–5 (Fig. 4), again support that biofilm-adapted phages evolved to overcome the population diversity, thus achieving enhanced control of M800 biofilms.

To further confirm the role of biofilm heterogeneity in the biofilm-adaptation process and given that the phages used in this study utilize LPS as a receptor in the P. aeruginosa membrane, we next identified and compared the genes responsible for LPS biosynthesis in M800 and all biofilm-derived isolates. This analysis included genes commonly involved in the production of the common polysaccharide antigen (CPA), O-specific antigen (OSA), core oligosaccharide, and lipid A. Our results showed that the wzy gene (M800_00558), which encodes the O-antigen polymerase, contains a nucleotide deletion in some strains, resulting in a truncated WZY protein (L300*, truncated variant). In contrast, strains without this deletion encode the full-length WZY protein (412 amino acids, complete variant) (Supplementary Fig. 8). The truncated WZY is present in strain M800 (Supplementary Fig. 8). During biofilm formation, isolates from 24 h (M800BF-24hC) and 48 h (M800BF-C) biofilms encode either the truncated or complete WZY (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. 8). Notably, M800BF-24hC isolates encoding the complete WZY show higher susceptibility to phage PE1 (M800BF-24hC: 3,5, 18, 21, 24, 27, 31, 33, 36, 41, 43, 47) (Figs. 4A and 8B). After biofilm treatment with phage PE1 (M800BF-T), all isolates encode the truncated WZY (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. 8) and exhibit lower susceptibility to phage PE1 (Figs. 4B and 8B). Given the essential role of WZY in OSA biosynthesis, these results demonstrate the presence of variable OSA forms in biofilm cells and support the idea that adapted phages evolved to recognize cells with short or absent OSA, while retaining efficacy against cells with long OSA, providing them an advantage in the control of M800 biofilms where both OSA forms can be found.

Discussion

Phage therapy has become increasingly recognized as a promising strategy to treat bacterial infections caused by multidrug resistant bacteria, although the emergence of phage resistance and the presence of bacterial biofilms are still important challenges to overcome25. The adaptation of phages to the target bacteria increases their efficacy and reduces the emergence of bacterial phage resistance in planktonic cultures13,26. However, given that biofilms are the predominant bacterial lifestyle, especially in chronic bacterial infections, including lung infections in CF patients27, it is important to ensure that phages can be efficient against these bacterial communities as well. In the present study we have investigated whether phage adaptation to biofilms formed by the target bacteria increases the biofilm-control efficacy. Our findings reveal the dynamics of this adaptation and its potential to improve phage therapy efficacy in CF patients.

Phage efficacy against biofilms is mainly hindered by diffusion limitations and the emergence of bacterial resistance to phages with the ability to proliferate in the biofilm. Our data indicate that the heterogeneity of bacterial populations within biofilms—comprising multiple variants with different LPS compositions and, consequently, varying susceptibility to phages targeting this receptor—facilitates the emergence of resistant variants when the biofilm is subjected to phage attack. This suggests that phages capable of targeting a broad host range within heterogeneous biofilm populations would yield better therapeutic outcomes. Building on this premise, our work focused on directing the evolution of phages against biofilms to expand their host range, aiming to enhance their effectiveness against the diverse bacterial variants within the biofilm.

The protocol implemented in this work for directed adaptation of phages to biofilms, resulted in the isolation of phages with increased efficacy after only 8 days of experimental evolution. Future studies should aim to determine the minimal adaptation time required, as earlier timepoints were not sampled in the present work. We selected the period of 24 h for treatment with phages, as it is normally the time where the differences in the number of biofilm culturable cells between treated and untreated conditions are typically low, which has been attributed to the proliferation of phage-resistant bacteria12. The P. aeruginosa strain M800 used in this study was specifically selected because it was isolated from a CF patient and displayed a low susceptibility to phage PE1, isolated in this study. Moreover, this strain has a strong biofilm forming capacity, producing dense bacterial aggregates, rather than surface-attached biofilms. These aggregates are the predominant forms of the biofilms found in chronic CF lung infections28 and are now considered a central hallmark of bacterial biofilms from different origins29. We have shown that the biofilm-adapted phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 cause significant disruption of the M800 aggregates and lead to lower CFU counts compared to the wildtype phage PE1. Although the differences in CFU counts are generally less than 1 log, this may be attributed to quantification of the total well content (including both biofilm and planktonic cells) due to the suspended nature of these biofilms. Most published studies that use single phages to control in vitro biofilms over 24 h report CFU reductions in the range of 0–2 log30, which is consistent with our findings. However, demonstrating the therapeutic relevance of these results remains important and is currently limited by the scarcity of suitable in vivo models of CF lung biofilms and the challenges in translating these findings to human CF lung biofilms. We have confirmed the existence of biofilm genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity, both before and after treatment with phage PE1, and we demonstrated that the low efficacy of this phage against M800 biofilms is due to its low capacity to target the different clones within the biofilm population. This difficulty was overcome by the biofilm-adapted phages, resulting in a greater control of the biofilm due to improved recognition of truncated LPS forms. It is known that phenotypic and genotypic heterogeneity are commonly found within bacterial populations and play an important role during infection31,32. Heterogeneity can present a challenge in phage therapy, as phages selected for a specific target strain may not effectively address the full range of population variation in the patient13. In the case of CF patients, various studies have demonstrated high levels of phenotypic diversity among P. aeruginosa strains in the lungs33–35, of which the variation in LPS structure plays an important role in bacterial adaptation to chronic infection36. LPS is the main component of the outer membrane of P. aeruginosa and its structure includes lipid A, core oligosaccharide, and two forms of O antigen known as common polysaccharide antigen (CPA) and O-specific antigen (OSA)37. It is well established that the absence or reduced production of OSA, as observed in the M800 strain, is common among CF isolates from chronic infection, while CPA production is maintained36,38. This alteration has a direct impact on the surface properties of P. aeruginosa, leading to increased cell hydrophobicity and formation of clumped bacterial aggregates39,40, as seen for M800. LPS is the main phage receptor in Gram-negative bacteria41 and the loss of OSA can be a limitation for the effectiveness of phage therapy in CF patients when phages targeting this LPS moiety are used. Given that phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 acquired the capacity to infect cells defective in OSA, they are potentially more recommended for treatment in CF patients.

We showed that biofilm-adapted phages PE1–3 and PE1–5 acquired point mutations in genes encoding baseplate wedge and tail fiber proteins, while the rest of their genomes remained identical to that of the wildtype phage. According to the complete cryo-EM structure and conformational dynamics of the Pbunavirus phage E21722, which are proposed to be conserved among Pbunavirus genus, it is not clear how the mutations observed in the baseplate wedge proteins can contribute to the increased efficacy of the adapted phages, since the baseplate has not been shown to interact with the host cell. Nevertheless, our results show that the mutations acquired by the biofilm-adapted phages could enable the recognition of LPS core in addition to the OSA recognized by the wildtype phage. Other studies demonstrated that single amino acid changes can alter phage’s host range, efficiency of plating, and adsorption rates. These mutations typically occur in receptor binding proteins, such as the tail fiber tips42–48, which is consistent with our findings. Mutations introducing hydrophobic amino acids enable phages to infect bacteria with truncated LPS43,49,50 and we speculate that the enhanced hydrophobic interactions may enable the biofilm-adapted phages to bind to the more hydrophobic cell surface of OSA defective mutants37.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the biofilm-control efficacy of a Pbunavirus can be enhanced through experimental evolution in a P. aeruginosa clinical isolate from a CF patient. The increased biofilm killing efficacy of the adapted phages is attributed not only to improved recognition of the target strain but also to enhanced recognition of the heterogeneous biofilm population formed by that strain. This approach could serve as a valuable strategy to optimize phage therapy outcomes in CF patients (and probably in other bacterial infections). However, since the result may be dependent on the type of phage and strain used for the procedure, it would be important to extend this research to other strains and other phages. Additionally, this study advances our understanding of the phage genes and specific mutations that influence phage host range and efficiency of plating in Pseudomonas phages that bind to LPS. Future work involving detailed structural characterization of these mutated regions along with LPS moieties could facilitate targeted genetic manipulation to efficiently generate phages capable of tackling different bacterial phenotypes.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The reference strain P. aeruginosa PAO1 (DSM22644) was obtained from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures. P. aeruginosa M800 is a clinical strain isolated from a Portuguese patient with CF lung infection. In addition, 45 P. aeruginosa clinical isolates and 4 PAO1 LPS isogenic mutants (University of Minho, Braga, Portugal), and 96 M800 variants isolated in this study, were also used, and are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Pure bacterial cultures were obtained by growth on lysogeny broth (LB) agar (LB with 1.2% (w/v) agar) for 16 h at 37 °C. Bacterial overnight cultures were grown in LB for 16 h at 37 °C. Bacterial lawns were prepared by adding 100 µL of overnight cultures to 3 mL of LB soft agar (LB with 0.6% (w/v) agar) and pouring on LB agar plates. Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) 2× concentrated was used for sample enrichment in phage isolation. Saline solution (0.9% NaCl (w/v)) was used for bacterial dilutions, and saline-magnesium (SM) buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO4) was used for phage dilutions. SCFM2, used for selected biofilm experiments, was prepared as previously described51, except for mucin where UV sterilization was replaced with autoclaving prior to use52.

Phage isolation and production

Pseudomonas phage PE1 was isolated from a sewage sample collected in a wastewater treatment plant (Braga, Portugal), according to a previously described protocol53 and using P. aeruginosa PAO1 as the host for phage isolation.

For phage production, a single phage plaque was picked with a sterile tip, immersed in 500 µL of SM buffer for 10 min to release the phages, and all the volume was added to 50 mL of early-exponentially grown PAO1 (overnight culture diluted 1:100 in 50 mL of LB and incubated at 37 °C and 120 rpm until OD600 nm ≈ 0.3). After 6 h of incubation with agitation at 37 °C, the phage lysates were centrifuged (9000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min), filtered through a 0.22 µm filter, and stored at 4 °C.

Phage adaptation towards P. aeruginosa biofilms

Adaptation of phage PE1 to P. aeruginosa M800 biofilms was performed over 8 days in biofilms grown in LB medium on 24-well plates. For biofilm formation, an overnight culture of P. aeruginosa M800 was diluted in LB (1:100) and 1 mL of this suspension was added to 24-well plates followed by incubation at 37 °C and 120 rpm for 24 h. After 24 h of biofilm formation (day 1), 600 μL of growth medium were removed from the wells and replaced with 100 μL of phage PE1 at 1 × 106 PFU/mL (titer on M800 strain) and 500 μL of fresh LB medium and incubated for additional 24 h. At the same time, a new 24 h biofilm formation was initiated as described above. On day 2, 600 μL of growth medium were removed from the new 24 h old biofilm, replaced with 100 μL of growth medium recovered from the previously phage-treated biofilm (day 1) and 500 μL of fresh LB medium, and incubated for additional 24 h. Formation of a new biofilm was then initiated, and the same process was repeated every day, i.e. on each day a new P. aeruginosa M800 24 h old biofilm was treated for 24 h with phages recovered from the previously treated biofilm (Fig. 1C). On day 9, all the content of the well containing the final phage-treated biofilm was removed, centrifuged (9000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min), and the supernatant was used for a plaque assay to recover the biofilm-adapted phages. Briefly, serial dilutions of the supernatant were made in SM buffer, and 100 μL of each dilution was mixed with 100 μL of an overnight culture of P. aeruginosa M800 and 3 mL of LB soft agar and poured onto LB agar plates. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, 5 individual phage plaques were picked with a sterile toothpick, spread on P. aeruginosa M800 lawns using a paper strip, and incubated overnight, before production of 5 biofilm-adapted phages using PAO1, as described above.

Phage genome sequencing and analysis

Phage genomic DNA extraction was based on the established phenol/chloroform method54. Briefly, 1 mL of phage lysate was treated with 12.5 μL of MgCl2 (1 M), 1 μL DNAse I (10 mg/mL) and 1 μL RNAse A (100 mg/mL). After 1 h at room temperature, 40 μL EDTA (0.5 M), 5 μL of Proteinase K (10 mg/mL) and 50 μL of SDS (10 %) were added and the mixture was incubated overnight at 55 °C, before the addition of 1 volume of phenol-chloroform (1:1). The upper aqueous layer was recovered by centrifugation (13,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min) and followed by a second centrifugation with 1 volume of chloroform. The aqueous layer was recovered, and the phage DNA was precipitated by adding 1 mL of EtOH (100% (v/v)) and 50 μL NaOAc (3 M) for 30 min on ice followed by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min). The pellet containing the phage DNA was washed with 500 μL of EtOH (70% (v/v)), centrifuged (14,000 × g, 4 °C, 5 min), air-dried, resuspended in sterile deionized water, and stored at −20 °C.

For phage DNA sequencing, the library was constructed using the Illumina Nextera XT library preparation kit and sequenced (100 bp or 300 pb paired-end mode) using Illumina HiSeq platform (StabVida). Reads were automatically trimmed and assembled into a single contig using Geneious Prime 2023. Prediction of ORFs and initial homology-based predictions were performed using the BV-BRC tool55. For functional annotation, protein homology results were complemented using BLASTP21 and HMMER56, conserved domains and families were identified using InterPro57 and CDD58, transmembrane domains and signal peptide cleavage sites were identified by TMHMM59, Phobius60, SignalIP61 and TOPCONS62, and pairwise comparison of profile hidden Markov models (HMMs) was performed using HHpred63.

For phage taxonomic classification, comparative genomic analysis was performed with BLASTN64. Then, the genomes of all phages classified as species within the Pbunavirus genus, in the ICTV Virus Taxonomy 2022 Release, obtained from GenBank, were compared to the genome of phage PE1 using VIRIDIC65 and VICTOR66.

Sanger sequencing to confirm the phage mutations after biofilm-adaptation was performed by Eurofins Genomics (Germany) using primers 5′-GCAGTCGCCGATGCGTTG-3′, 5′-GCTACCATTTCCGTTGGTAACG-3′, and 5′-ACGTTCCATTCGCCGCAG-3′, for coding sequences 76, 77, and 78, respectively.

Determination of phage host range and efficiency of plating

The determination of phage host ranges was performed by spotting 10 µL drops of phages on bacterial lawns of the selected strains, following overnight incubation at 37 °C. When lytic zones were observed, phage solutions were serially diluted, plated on the respective bacterial lawns, and incubated overnight at 37 °C to calculate the number of Plaque Forming Units (PFUs). Efficiency of plating (EOP) was determined as the ratio of the raw phage titer on the selected strain and the raw phage titer on the host strain P. aeruginosa PAO1 (used for phage production).

Phage growth and adsorption assays

One-step growth curves and adsorption assays were performed on P. aeruginosa PAO1 as previously described67. Briefly, a bacterial overnight culture was diluted 1:100 in 10 mL of LB and grown until OD600 nm ≈ 0.3. After centrifugation (7000 × g, 4 °C, 5 min), the bacterial cells were resuspended in 5 mL of fresh LB medium and the phage solution diluted in 5 mL of LB medium was added to obtain a MOI of 0.01. This phage-bacteria solution was incubated for 5 min at 37 °C and 120 rpm for phage adsorption. After, the non-adsorbed phages were removed by centrifugation (7000 × g, 4 °C, 5 min), and the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of fresh LB medium and incubated at 37 °C and 120 rpm. Samples (50 μL) were collected at 10-min intervals over a period of 70 min, serially diluted, and plated on P. aeruginosa PAO1 lawns to determine the phage titer (PFU/mL). Values of PFU/infected cell were calculated by the ratio between the phage titer in each time point and the phage titer at t = 0 min.

For the adsorption assays, the phage-bacteria solution prepared as described above was incubated at 37 °C and 120 rpm, after quantification of the total number of phages by serial dilutions and plating on PAO1 lawns. Samples (200 μL) were collected with 1 min interval up to 8 min after infection, immediately diluted in 200 μL of SM Buffer, and filtered through a 0.22 µm filter. The filtrates containing the unadsorbed phages were serially diluted and plated on P. aeruginosa PAO1 lawns to determine the titer of unadsorbed phage (PFU/mL). The percentage of unadsorbed phage was calculated through the ratio between the titer of unadsorbed phage in each time point and the titer of phage added to start the experiment.

Preparation of samples for cryo-TEM

Phage lysate (50 ml) was centrifuged in a 50.2 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter) at 54,000 × g for 2.5 h at 10 °C (Optima XPN-80 Ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter). The pellet was resuspended in LB medium to the final phage concentration of 1011 PFU/ml (titer on PAO1). A sample of concentrated phage lysate (4 µl of 1011 PFU/mL) was applied onto a QuantifoilTM grid (2/1, Cu, mesh 200) glow-discharged in H/O plasma using a Gatan Solarus II. Grids were blotted (blotting force 0, blotting time 2 s, 100% humidity, wait time 5 s), plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV, and stored in liquid nitrogen.

For phage-bacterium interactions, overnight culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 was diluted 1:100 (v/v) in fresh LB medium and incubated at 37 °C, 250 RPM to OD600 nm ≈ 0.3 (108 CFU/mL). At time 0, the phage lysate (1011 PFU/ml) was added to the culture at a multiplicity of infection of 500. The infected culture was incubated at 37 °C, 250 rpm. To increase the cell density and remove the non-adsorbed phages, the cells were pelleted at 5 min post infection (5000 × g, 1 min) and resuspended in fresh LB medium to reach OD600 nm ≈ 9. Samples for vitrification (4 μl) were taken at distinct time points over the course of infection, applied onto a QuantifoilTM grid (2/1, Cu, mesh 200) glow-discharged in H/O plasma using a Gatan Solarus II, plunge frozen in liquid ethane (blotting force 0, blotting time 2 s, 100% humidity, wait time 5 s), and stored in liquid nitrogen. The grids were imagined using Talos ArcticaTM electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 200 kV, equipped with a K2 Summit direct electron detector.

Prediction of phage PE1 wedge protein and tail fibre protein structures

The structures of selected phage PE1 proteins were predicted using AlphaFold2 multimer 2.1.268,69 accessed via the online platform provided by CERIT. For prediction, only structures available in databases (BFD, MGnify, PDB70, PDB, PDB seqres, Uniclust30, UniProt, UniRef90) before August 2023 were used. Confidence of the obtained structures was assessed through the predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score provided by AlphaFold2. UCSF Chimera X was used for structure visualization70.

Biofilm formation, treatment, and quantification in microtiter plates

Biofilm formation, treatment, and quantification in 24-well plates was performed according to previously described protocols for suspended (non-surface attached) biofilms71.

Briefly, for biofilm formation, an overnight culture of P. aeruginosa M800 was diluted 1:100 in LB or SCFM2 and 1 mL was transferred into each well of the 24-well plates. Blanks (containing only LB or SCFM2) were included in each experiment. The plates were incubated at 37 °C and 120 rpm for 24 h.

For biofilm treatment with phages, half of the growth medium in the 24-well plates was replaced with fresh LB or SCFM2 medium and phages at a concentration of 1 × 107 PFU/mL. Untreated controls (containing only bacteria) were included in each experiment. Plates were incubated for additional 24 h, at 37 °C and 120 rpm.

After 24 h treatment with phages, the biofilms on the 24-well plates were photographed and imaged using an EVOS FL Auto Imaging System (Life Technologies) with the 2× objective. For the experiments with LB medium, all the content of each well was then transferred into 2 mL screw cap empty tubes and the biofilms were homogenized in a Bead Ruptor 24 Elite homogeneizer (Omni International) for 1 min at 3 m/s. For the experiments with SCFM2, the biofilms were disrupted by sonication at 40 kHz for 5 min (Hei-MIX Titramax 100/101/1000, Heidolph Instruments, Schwabach, Germany) and shaking at 900 rpm for 5 min (Branson 3510, Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, USA). After biofilm homogenization, the content of the tubes (LB) and wells (SCFM2) was used to perform serial dilutions in saline solution, followed by plating on LB agar plates to determine the number of colony forming units (CFU) after overnight incubation at 37 °C.

Host cell infection, treatment, and bacterial quantification in the three-dimensional lung epithelial cell culture model

The previously established three-dimensional (3-D) lung epithelial cell culture model was generated by culturing the human adenocarcinomic alveolar epithelial cell line A549 (ATCC CCL185) on collagen I-coated microcarrier beads (Cytodex-3 microcarrier beads, Sigma-Aldrich) in a rotating wall vessel (RWV) bioreactor, as previously described20,72. Cells were cultured at 37 °C under static 5% CO2 conditions, in GTSF-2 medium (HyClone, Logan, UT, US) supplemented with 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, US) and 2.5 mg/L insulin transferrin sodium selenite (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). After 11-14 days of incubation, the culture medium of the 3-D cells was replaced with GTSF-2 without FBS, and 250 μL of the aggregates per well containing 2.5 × 105 cells were transferred to 48-well plates.

For infection of the host cells, an overnight culture of P. aeruginosa M800 was resuspended in GTSF-2 without FBS and added to the 3-D lung cells in the 48-well plates at a concentration of 2.5 × 106 CFU/well (targeted MOI of 10). Plates were incubated for 2 h, at 37 °C under static 5% CO2 conditions.

For treatment with phages, half of the growth medium in the 48-well plates was replaced with fresh GTSF-2 medium without FBS and phages at a concentration of 1 × 107 PFU/mL. Plates were incubated for an additional 4 h, at 37 °C under static 5% CO2 conditions.

To assess the effect of phage treatment on the bacteria associated with the 3-D lung epithelial cells, the entire content of each well was transferred into new 48-well plates, to avoid inclusion of bacteria possibly attached to the wells, and the growth medium was carefully removed. The number of host-associated culturable bacteria was determined as previously described20. Briefly, the cells were washed twice with HBSS (Life Technologies) and vigorously pipetted with 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) to disrupt the biofilms and lyse the epithelial cells. The number of bacteria in the lysate was quantified by serial dilutions in saline solution and plating in LB medium for CFU counts.

Viability of the epithelial cells was determined using the intracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay, as previously described73, to avoid interference of P. aeruginosa with the read-out. Epithelial cell lysates prepared as described above were centrifuged (3700 rpm, 15 min) and the supernatant was used to measure the LDH activity using the LDH detection kit (Sigma-Aldrich) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage from an uninfected control (without bacteria and phages), processed with HBSS, 1% Triton X-100, vigorous pipetting, and centrifugation, as described above for the other samples.

LPS extraction and silver staining

LPS extraction was based on a previously described protocol74 adapted from75. Briefly, bacterial colonies grown on LB agar were suspended in 1 ml of saline solution. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 s, the cells were resuspended in 1 ml saline solution, centrifuged again and resuspended in 50 μL of lysis buffer containing 2% w/v SDS, 4% v/v 2-mercaptoethanol, 10% v/v glycerol, 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8) and bromophenol blue. After incubation at 95 °C for 10 min, the cells were allowed to cool down to room temperature, and 10 μL of 2.5 mg/mL Proteinase K was added (freshly prepared by dilution of a 10 mg/mL Proteinase K stock solution with the lysis buffer). After incubation at 56 °C for 1 h, 5 μL samples were loaded on SDS polyacrylamide gels with 4% and 12.5% acrylamide in the stacking and separating gels, respectively. The gels were run at 20 mA in Tris-glycine-SDS buffer.

LPS visualization in the gels was performed according to a previously described silver staining protocol76. Briefly, after overnight fixation in 200 mL of fixation solution (40% v/v ethanol, 5% v/v acetic acid), the gel was incubated for 5 min at 40 rpm in 200 mL of fixer-oxidizer solution (0.7% w/v periodic acid, 40% v/v ethanol, 5% v/v acetic acid). After 3 washes at 40 rpm in 500 mL of MilliQ DI water in a second dish (15 min each wash), the gels were stained for 10 min at 70 rpm with 150 mL of freshly prepared staining solution (28 mL of 0.1 M NaOH, 2 mL of concentrated ammonium hydroxide, 5 mL of 20% w/v silver nitrate, and 115 mL of MilliQ DI water). After 3 washes performed for 10 min each one, as previously described, the gels were developed at 40 rpm in 200 mL of developer solution (50 mg of citric acid, 0.5 mL of 37% formaldehyde, and MilliQ DI water up to 1 L). When completely stained, the gels were washed and stored in distilled water.

Bacterial genomes sequencing and analysis

DNA from the M800 strain and the biofilm-isolated colonies (Supplementary Table 3) was extracted from overnight grown bacteria on LB agar using the Quick-DNA Fungal/Bacterial Kit (Zymo Research).

Libraries were produced following the Hackflex protocol77 and pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovoSeq 6000 Instrument generating 150 base paired-end sequencing reads. Resulting rawreads were processed with bbduk (bbmap = v38.93) and assembled using shovill (v1.1.0). Whole genome sequences were uploaded to NCBI and are available under BioProject ID PRJNA1266112. Pairwise SNP comparison for tree generation was done using snippy (v4.6.0) and visualized with the R package ggtree.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0. Data comparisons were performed using ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc tests. Differences were considered statistically significant if p was less than 0.05, and significant p values were indicated in the graphs.
