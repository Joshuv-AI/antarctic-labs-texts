Digital fatigue and academic resilience among university students with grit and flexibility as mediators
Authors: Ran An, Guanghui Qian, Ayesha Mumtaz, Khalid Abdullah Alotaibi, Xiu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing reliance on digital technologies in higher education has led to a rise in digital fatigue, which negatively impacts students’ academic resilience. While psychological traits such as grit and psychological flexibility enhance resilience, their individual and combined effects on digital fatigue remain underexplored, particularly in Chinese academic settings. This study aimed to investigate the direct effect of digital fatigue on academic resilience and to examine the mediating roles of psychological flexibility and grit, both individually and sequentially, among undergraduate students in Guangzhou, China. Methods: A cross-sectional survey was conducted between November and December 2024, using validated scales to collect data from 2109 participants. Structural equation modeling (SEM) and path analysis explored direct and indirect relationships among the study constructs. The results showed that digital fatigue significantly negatively affected academic resilience (β = -0.481, p < 0.001). Psychological flexibility did not independently mediate this relationship (β = 0.011, 95% CI: -0.009, 0.036), but grit significantly mediated the effect (β = -0.125, 95% CI: -0.218, -0.089). Furthermore, a significant chain mediation effect (β = 0.015, 95% CI: 0.007, 0.122) indicated that psychological flexibility and grit interactively contributed to resilience by enabling cognitive adaptation and sustained effort. The study emphasized that digital fatigue is a significant barrier to academic resilience. Promoting perseverance through grit-building strategies and adaptability through flexibility-enhancing programs can support students in managing digital stress. Introduction

In today’s digitally driven academic landscape, students increasingly depend on digital platforms for learning, research, and collaboration1. While these technologies provide flexibility and access to resources2, they have also introduced the challenge of digital fatigue, a state of mental exhaustion caused by prolonged digital engagement3. Digital fatigue manifests through cognitive overload, emotional exhaustion, and demotivation, which can impair students’ academic functioning1,4. Undergraduate students, in particular, face increased academic pressures due to long hours spent on digital tasks such as online coursework, research activities, and virtual interactions. The shift toward online and hybrid learning models during the COVID-19 pandemic has further intensified the risk of digital fatigue5. Digital fatigue has been linked to decreased cognitive efficiency, lower attention spans, and impaired decision-making, hindering students’ ability to manage academic responsibilities1,6. Over time, the cumulative effects of digital fatigue can erode psychological well-being, resulting in academic disengagement, increased frustration, and anxiety7. These consequences are particularly concerning in higher education, where students must sustain high levels of motivation and performance over long periods. Without effective strategies to cope with digital fatigue, students risk experiencing academic decline and psychological burnout5.

Globally, similar patterns have been observed across diverse educational contexts. Studies from Western and non-Chinese settings have also linked technology overuse and digital exhaustion to academic disengagement and reduced well-being among university students8,9. For instance, research from Europe and North America has shown that prolonged screen exposure and multitasking during online learning contribute to cognitive fatigue and lower academic motivation10,11. Comparative findings underscore that while cultural and institutional contexts vary, the underlying psychological mechanisms of digital fatigue and resilience appear consistent, highlighting the global significance of understanding these constructs in higher education.

In contrast, students who possess strong academic resilience and the ability to persevere and adapt in the face of academic setbacks are better equipped to navigate the challenges associated with digital fatigue12–14. Academic resilience enables students to maintain focus, manage stress, and sustain their motivation despite external pressures15,16. It encompasses cognitive and emotional resources that allow individuals to recover from setbacks and persist toward long-term academic goals15. However, digital fatigue can deplete these resources, diminishing students’ ability to remain resilient. This relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience remains an important area for investigation, particularly in digitally intensive educational contexts such as those in China, where students face high academic expectations and increased digital engagement. The increasing prevalence of digital fatigue in higher education and its potential impact on academic resilience necessitate further research to understand how students cope with digital stressors. Understanding this relationship is critical for identifying factors that can protect students from the adverse effects of prolonged digital engagement, ultimately supporting their academic success and well-being.

Digital fatigue, characterized by cognitive overload and emotional exhaustion, can severely impair students’ ability to maintain academic resilience17. Resilience is a critical factor that enables students to recover from setbacks, regulate stress, and stay motivated to achieve long-term academic goals18. However, prolonged engagement with digital tasks—such as attending online lectures, conducting research, and participating in virtual collaborations—can erode their ability to cope with challenges. This continuous strain ultimately diminishes cognitive and emotional capacity7. Digital fatigue disrupts the cognitive processes necessary for resilience by impairing concentration, decision-making, and the ability to retain information19. Empirical research has demonstrated that digital fatigue directly correlates with academic disengagement and psychological burnout. For example, Besser et al. (2024)20 found that university students who reported high levels of digital fatigue during remote learning were more likely to exhibit lower academic resilience and increased symptoms of academic withdrawal. Similarly, Arbulú Pérez Vargas et al. (2024)21 highlighted that digital fatigue contributed to declines in academic motivation, self-efficacy, and persistence, particularly in students facing high academic demands. These effects are exacerbated by digital task-switching, where students are required to rapidly alternate between multiple online platforms and tasks, leading to further cognitive strain22.

The Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory23 explains this relationship theoretically. COR theory posits that individuals seek to protect and conserve their psychological resources, such as energy, focus, and emotional stability, to cope with stress. When students experience prolonged digital fatigue, these resources are progressively depleted, reducing their ability to adapt to academic stressors. This resource loss may trigger a downward spiral, wherein students become increasingly vulnerable to academic setbacks and less able to sustain the resilience needed to recover from them5.

Moreover, the resilience framework proposed by Masten (2014)18 emphasized the dynamic nature of resilience, suggesting that both internal coping resources and external stressors shape it. Digital fatigue is a significant external stressor that disrupts the cognitive and emotional balance required for resilience. Students may struggle to maintain academic engagement and performance over time without sufficient recovery or protective resources. Based on this evidence, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1

(H1): Digital fatigue negatively affects academic resilience among undergraduates.

Psychological flexibility is a crucial adaptive mechanism that helps individuals regulate their emotions, adjust their behaviors, and maintain focus under stressful conditions24,25. It enables students to reframe negative experiences, persist in challenging tasks, and strengthen resilience despite adversity. In the academic context, psychological flexibility has been associated with better emotional regulation, improved problem-solving, and greater academic engagement26,27. However, digital fatigue can impair psychological flexibility by overwhelming students with cognitive and emotional stress, making it harder for them to adapt to academic demands. When students experience high levels of digital fatigue, their ability to use adaptive strategies declines. Laure et al. (2025)28 found that students facing academic stress due to digital overload were less likely to employ adaptive strategies such as reappraisal and mindfulness, key components of psychological flexibility. As flexibility declines, students become less capable of managing setbacks and maintaining resilience, reducing their ability to cope effectively with academic challenges.

The COR theory supports this pathway by emphasizing that the loss of personal resources weakens individuals’ internal and external adaptive capacities23. Prolonged digital fatigue drains cognitive energy and attention, reducing students’ ability to employ flexible coping mechanisms. Consequently, their thinking and behavior become more rigid, which limits recovery from academic challenges7. Empirical findings align with this view. Kashdan et al. (2015)29 found that psychological flexibility is a protective buffer in demanding academic environments, helping students remain engaged and persistent under pressure. Similarly, research in digital learning settings shows that students with higher flexibility can better manage digital fatigue and sustain academic motivation7. Taken together, these findings suggest that psychological flexibility mediates the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience. When digital fatigue erodes flexibility, students’ ability to adapt and recover from academic stressors is diminished.

Hypothesis 2

(H2): Psychological flexibility mediates the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience among undergraduates.

Grit, defined as the perseverance and passion for long-term goals, plays a critical role in academic success by enabling students to persist through challenges and focus on their objectives over time30. Grit comprises two key components: perseverance of effort and consistency of interest, both essential for sustained academic engagement. In higher education, students with higher grit levels are more likely to overcome obstacles, maintain motivation, and achieve academic goals31. However, digital fatigue can undermine these traits by depleting the cognitive and emotional resources required for persistence, ultimately eroding resilience31. When students experience prolonged digital fatigue may find it harder to remain committed to their long-term academic goals. Fatigue-induced cognitive overload, reduced concentration, and emotional exhaustion can lower their intrinsic motivation and diminish their ability to sustain effort32,33. Studies have found that students facing digital stressors are more likely to experience academic burnout, negatively affecting perseverance and interest in academic tasks5. This decline in perseverance may reduce students’ ability to recover from setbacks and stay resilient in academic contexts.

Empirical evidence further supports this relationship. For example, Song (2024)34 found that grit significantly predicted academic resilience among university students, particularly under conditions of high academic stress. In contrast, students experiencing high digital fatigue exhibited lower perseverance, weakening their ability to remain engaged with their academic responsibilities17. These findings illustrate how digital fatigue disrupts the capacity for grit, diminishing resilience by weakening sustained effort and goal-directed motivation. The resilience framework emphasizes that resilience is not a fixed trait but a dynamic process shaped by internal and external factors18. Within this framework, grit is an internal resource that enables students to persist in their academic pursuits despite external stressors. In this context, grit is an internal resource that allows students to persist in their academic pursuits despite external stressors. However, when digital fatigue depletes this internal resource, students may struggle to maintain academic resilience. Based on these findings, this study hypothesizes that grit mediates the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience. Specifically, digital fatigue may impair perseverance and long-term goal commitment, thereby weakening students’ ability to adapt and recover from academic stress.

Hypothesis 3

(H3): Grit mediates the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience among undergraduates.

While psychological flexibility and grit independently mediate the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience, they may also interact through a chain mediation process. In this pathway, psychological flexibility influences students’ capacity to sustain perseverance and long-term goal commitment (i.e., grit), ultimately affecting their academic resilience. This sequential relationship is grounded in theoretical and empirical research, suggesting that digital fatigue can initiate a cascading effect, where a reduction in psychological flexibility undermines grit, thereby weakening resilience. The COR theory provides a theoretical foundation for this chain effect. Within the COR framework, digital fatigue is a stressor that depletes essential cognitive and emotional resources, such as attention, motivation, and emotional regulation. Psychological flexibility helps conserve these remaining resources by allowing students to reappraise stressors and prevent further depletion through adaptive coping23. Grit, in turn, represents a resource-rebuilding mechanism that enables individuals to invest sustained effort and restore depleted capacities over time31. Together, these mechanisms align with COR’s principle of “resource caravans,” where adaptive and motivational resources operate to buffer digital fatigue’s effects and facilitate resilience recovery. According to COR theory, stressors such as digital fatigue lead to the depletion of key psychological resources23. As psychological flexibility declines, students become less capable of managing cognitive overload and emotional exhaustion. This reduced adaptability can impair their ability to focus on long-term academic goals, a core component of grit. Once perseverance and motivation decline, students become more vulnerable to academic setbacks and disengagement, resulting in diminished resilience. Research supports this interconnection between psychological flexibility and grit. Jeong, Kim, & Park (2023)35 demonstrated that students with greater psychological flexibility were better able to sustain effort and commitment under high academic stress, indicating that adaptability supports grit. Similarly, Levin et al. (2014)36 found that psychological flexibility enhanced self-regulation, allowing students to stay motivated in the face of academic challenges. In contrast, when digital fatigue disrupts flexible thinking, students often exhibit rigid responses to stress, such as avoidance or disengagement, which reduces their perseverance20.

Further evidence suggests that grit is a downstream mediator between psychological flexibility and resilience. Duckworth et al. (2007)30 found that perseverance was a critical predictor of academic resilience. However, its effectiveness depends on students’ ability to adapt to changing circumstances, highlighting the interaction between flexibility and grit. Psychological flexibility enables students to regulate their emotions and stay focused on their goals, while grit allows them to persist despite cumulative stress from digital fatigue37. The resilience framework reinforces that resilience is shaped by a network of interacting psychological and behavioral resources18. In this context, psychological flexibility and grit serve complementary functions: flexibility provides the adaptability needed to manage immediate stressors, whereas grit sustains motivation toward long-term academic goals. When either of these mechanisms is weakened—especially under conditions of digital fatigue, students’ overall resilience can deteriorate. Based on this reasoning, it is hypothesized that digital fatigue affects academic resilience through a sequential mediation pathway involving psychological flexibility and grit. Specifically, digital fatigue reduces psychological flexibility, weakening students’ perseverance and goal commitment, ultimately lowering their capacity to adapt and recover from academic stress.

Hypothesis 4

(H4): Psychological flexibility and grit mediate the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience among undergraduates.

While previous studies have highlighted the negative impact of stress on academic resilience, few have explicitly focused on digital fatigue, which is increasingly relevant in modern educational settings. Research linking digital fatigue to reduced cognitive and emotional resources is still emerging, with limited understanding of how it undermines students’ capacity to recover from academic setbacks. Although constructs such as grit and psychological flexibility have been independently associated with positive educational outcomes, their combined roles in mitigating digital fatigue remain insufficiently explored. In particular, the extent to which grit buffers the effects of digital fatigue and how psychological flexibility contributes to resilience under prolonged digital exposure are not yet well established. Building on these gaps, the present study examines the direct effect of digital fatigue on academic resilience and the indirect effects through grit and psychological flexibility, including a chain mediation pathway. Grounded in the COR theory, this study contributes to multidimensional resilience frameworks by clarifying how adaptability and perseverance interact under digital stress. It also offers practical insights for universities to design interventions that strengthen perseverance and adaptive coping strategies among students in digitally demanding learning environments.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design to examine the relationships between digital fatigue, psychological flexibility, grit, and academic resilience. It was conducted over two months, from November to December 2024, in Guangzhou, the capital of Guangdong Province, China. The target institutions included undergraduate programs from various disciplines across public and private universities in Guangzhou. Medical universities were excluded from the 22 universities in the region to ensure homogeneity in participants’ academic experience and digital workload, as medical students typically engage in specialized digital activities such as virtual clinical simulations and telemedicine training, which differ substantially from other undergraduate programs.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The target population comprised undergraduate students from diverse academic disciplines. To achieve a representative sample, universities offering undergraduate programs across multiple fields, such as social sciences, business, engineering, and education, were prioritized. Eligible participants included those currently enrolled in an undergraduate program, experienced online or digital-based learning as part of their academic activities, and provided informed consent to participate. Students diagnosed with any psychiatric illness or undergoing psychiatric treatment (self-reported) were excluded from the study to minimize potential confounding effects related to mental health and cognitive functioning. Students enrolled in specialized medical training programs were also excluded to maintain consistency in the study’s educational focus and digital workload context.

Sample size determination and sampling approach

The sample size determination and sampling technique were designed to ensure sufficient statistical power for testing direct and mediation effects within structural equation modeling (SEM). Previous research suggested that a sample of at least 200 to 300 participants is required for stable parameter estimates in SEM38,39; however, this study aimed for a larger sample to enhance generalizability and precision. A multi-stage sampling approach was employed to achieve a diverse and representative sample. First, universities offering various undergraduate programs across multiple disciplines were identified and targeted. This was followed by stratified sampling, where students were categorized based on their academic disciplines to ensure proportional representation. Within each stratum, quotas were determined proportionally to the estimated enrollment size of each academic discipline across participating universities. This ensured that larger disciplines contributed more participants, maintaining representativeness of the overall undergraduate population. Random sampling was applied within each stratum to select participants, minimizing selection bias.

A total of 2673 participants were initially recruited for the study. After data screening, which included removing incomplete responses and participants who did not meet the eligibility criteria, 2109 valid cases were retained for analysis. The final sample size provided sufficient statistical power to detect both main and indirect effects in the mediation models.

Handling of missing data

Data were screened for missing responses before analysis. Cases with substantial missing data (>10% of responses) were excluded from the study40. For minor missing values (< 10%), mean substitution was employed for scale-based items, as all constructs demonstrated high internal consistency (α >0.88). This approach minimized potential bias and preserved the validity of the dataset.

Data collection procedure

Data was collected through an online survey via Wenjuanxing; links were distributed via university communication channels and social media platforms commonly used by students. The survey used validated instruments to measure digital fatigue, psychological flexibility, grit, and academic resilience. Survey responses were monitored to ensure completion rates, and reminders were sent to participants to encourage timely submission. The data collection period lasted from early November to December 2024.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study followed the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics review committee of Guangzhou College of Technology and Business (No. 2024/9/21) prior to data collection. Participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks and benefits. Participation was entirely voluntary, and students were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, ensuring that they understood the confidentiality measures in place to protect their data. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all responses were anonymized to maintain privacy. The study adhered to both national and institutional research ethics standards.

Scales

Demographics variables

The demographics questions included participants’ age, gender, socioeconomic status, living situation, and academic discipline.

Digital fatigue

The Zoom Exhaustion & Fatigue Scale (ZEF), originally developed by Fauville et al. (2021)41, was utilized to measure digital fatigue among the study participants. The scale captures five distinct dimensions of fatigue: general, social, emotional, visual, and motivational. It consists of 15 items, each evaluated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1: Not at all – 5: Extremely. Previous studies have demonstrated that the ZEF possesses strong psychometric properties and internal consistency within student populations5,42,43.

As the original ZEF was designed in English, it was translated into Chinese to ensure the study participants fully understood and could respond appropriately to the items. The translation process followed a structured approach to maintain linguistic and conceptual equivalence. Two bilingual experts first conducted a forward translation from English to Chinese. Next, an independent bilingual expert performed a back-translation into English to verify semantic accuracy and consistency. To resolve any remaining inconsistencies, a panel of professors specializing in education and psychology reviewed both versions and refined the final Chinese adaptation. A pilot study with 35 participants was conducted to validate the reliability of the translated scale (Supplementary Table S1). Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which indicated robust reliability across all five dimensions of fatigue. This comprehensive translation and validation process ensured that the Chinese version of the ZEF retained the psychometric integrity of the original scale.

Psychological flexibility

The Chinese adaptation of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II)44, originally developed by Bond et al. (2011)45, was utilized to measure psychological inflexibility. The scale retains its original structure of seven items, rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Never true, 7 = Always true), with higher scores signifying greater inflexibility. The AAQ-II has exhibited strong reliability (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.85) and validity across diverse populations. Since psychological flexibility is conceptually the opposite of inflexibility, lower scores on the AAQ-II indicate higher flexibility. The scale is widely recognized and frequently applied in psychological and educational research, particularly in investigations focusing on well-being, stress management, and academic resilience26,46,47.

Grit

The Chinese version of the grit scale, initially developed by Duckworth et al. (2007)30, is a 12-item instrument to measure perseverance and passion for long-term goals48,49. The scale consists of two subdomains: Consistency of interests, which evaluates sustained focus on goals over time, and Perseverance of effort, which assesses persistence in the face of challenges. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Not at all like me) to 5 (Very much like me). After reversing the necessary items, a higher total score reflects a greater level of grit. The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including high reliability (α >0.70) and validity across various populations, including students50–52.

Academic resilience

The Chinese version of the Academic Resilience Scale (C-ARS-30)53, originally developed by Dr. Simon Cassidy (2016)16, measures a student’s capacity to persevere, adapt, and recover from academic challenges. The C-ARS-30 is structured around three key dimensions: Perseverance (14 items), Reflecting and adaptive help-seeking (9 items), and Negative affect and emotional response (7 items). Participants respond using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly agree) to 5 (Strongly disagree), with higher scores indicating greater academic resilience. This scale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity and has been effectively applied in various academic and cultural contexts54–57.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (v27) and AMOS (v26) for SEM. Prior to analysis, data were screened for missing values and outliers. Cases with more than 10% missing responses were excluded, and mean imputation was applied for the remaining missing data. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and correlations, were calculated to provide an overview of the study variables. Reliability analysis was conducted to assess the internal consistency of the constructs using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR), with acceptable values set at α ≥ 0.70 and CR ≥ 0.70. Convergent validity was evaluated through average variance extracted (AVE), where values greater than 0.50 indicated sufficient validity. A series of structural equation models was applied to test the hypothesized relationships. The models included both direct and indirect pathways to assess mediation effects. SEM was chosen because it can simultaneously estimate multiple relationships among latent variables while accounting for measurement error. Model fit was evaluated using various indices: Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). Path analysis was conducted to test the direct effect of digital fatigue on academic resilience and the indirect effects mediated by psychological flexibility and grit. Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was used to generate bias-corrected confidence intervals (95% CI) for indirect effects. Mediation was considered significant if the confidence intervals did not include zero.

Chain mediation analysis

Table 3 presents the results of the path analysis, controlling for age and gender. Digital fatigue negatively affected academic resilience (β = -0.481, p < 0.001), indicating that higher fatigue levels were associated with lower resilience. For clarity, the AAQ-II measures psychological inflexibility; thus, higher scores indicate lower psychological flexibility. The mediation analysis revealed that psychological flexibility did not significantly mediate this relationship (β = 0.011, 95% CI: -0.009, 0.036), suggesting that adaptability alone did not buffer the negative impact of digital fatigue on academic resilience. However, the indirect effect through grit was significant (β = -0.125, 95% CI: -0.218, -0.089), indicating that lower perseverance partially explained the negative effect of digital fatigue on academic resilience. Furthermore, the chain mediation pathway, where psychological flexibility and grit were included as sequential mediators, was significant (β = 0.015, 95% CI: 0.007–0.122), demonstrating that the combined mediators accounted for a meaningful indirect effect. These findings suggest that while psychological flexibility alone did not mediate the relationship, its sequential influence alongside grit significantly explained the detrimental impact of digital fatigue on academic resilience. Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience, incorporating psychological flexibility and grit as mediating variables, among undergraduate students in Guangzhou, the capital of Guangdong province. The results demonstrated a significant direct negative effect of digital fatigue on academic resilience, indicating that higher levels of digital exhaustion impair students’ ability to recover from academic setbacks. While psychological flexibility did not mediate this relationship independently, grit emerged as a significant mediator. Furthermore, a chain mediation pathway was significant, where psychological flexibility and grit acted sequentially. This suggests that the combined influence of adaptability and perseverance plays a crucial role in mitigating the adverse effects of digital fatigue on academic resilience.

The findings revealed that digital fatigue was significantly associated with lower levels of academic resilience, consistent with previous research linking prolonged digital engagement to diminished well-being and academic functioning19,41. Students reporting higher digital fatigue tended to show reduced focus, motivation, and recovery following academic setbacks34,59. This association is particularly relevant in the current educational landscape, where digital tools and platforms constitute a significant component of learning and assessment60. Sustained engagement with online lectures, digital assignments, and virtual collaborations may contribute to the depletion of cognitive and emotional resources, limiting students’ capacity to manage academic demands effectively1,4,16. In academic contexts, this strain is reflected in reduced concentration, lower intrinsic motivation, and decreased task persistence, which may be linked to poorer academic outcomes61. These findings underscore the importance of promoting digital balance through structured screen breaks, reasonable workload distribution, and digital well-being programs to help students maintain resilience within technology-intensive learning environments.

The mediating role of grit highlighted the importance of perseverance and sustained effort as adaptive factors associated with resilience in the face of digital fatigue. Grit appeared to be a potential buffer, reflecting students’ ability to remain committed to long-term goals despite cognitive and emotional strain. This observation aligns with self-determination theory, which emphasizes that perseverance in goal pursuit supports ongoing motivation and achievement62. Students reporting higher levels of grit tend to manage academic pressures such as demanding coursework, tight deadlines, and repeated challenges more effectively63. Prior studies have consistently indicated that grit is positively associated with academic performance and persistence, suggesting that students who demonstrate perseverance are more likely to stay engaged despite stressors30,31,64. These students may employ more adaptive coping strategies that enable sustained productivity even when experiencing digital fatigue. For example, Duckworth et al. (2007)30 reported that grit significantly predicted academic success, even when controlling for intelligence and other personality traits. Similarly, Eskreis-Winkler et al. (2014)64 found that grit was linked to academic achievement across diverse educational and occupational contexts, while Credé et al. (2017)31 confirmed in a meta-analysis that perseverance—a key component of grit—correlates positively with academic performance. Collectively, these findings reinforce the role of grit as an essential psychological resource associated with students’ ability to sustain effort and engagement under conditions of digital fatigue.

In contrast, psychological flexibility did not significantly mediate the relationship between digital fatigue and academic resilience when considered in isolation. This finding diverged from earlier studies that identified flexibility as a key factor in effective stress management29,45. Psychological flexibility involves adapting one’s thoughts and behaviors to changing circumstances, often through reframing negative experiences or focusing on values-driven actions. However, it may be less effective when dealing with prolonged stressors like digital exhaustion. Persistent digital demands deplete cognitive and emotional resources, making adaptability insufficient23. Instead, action-oriented strategies, such as sustained effort and goal persistence30, may be more critical in mitigating digital fatigue’s impact. Psychological flexibility may thus play a secondary, supportive role, helping students reframe challenges while resilience-building strategies address the root causes of fatigue. Alternatively, this non-significant effect may reflect measurement or contextual influences. The construct of flexibility, though validated, may not fully capture how adaptability manifests in digitally saturated or collectivist settings, where students often emphasize persistence and social harmony over self-directed cognitive reframing. Within such contexts, flexibility may operate less as an individual cognitive process and more as a socially mediated or behaviorally constrained response. Furthermore, it is plausible that psychological flexibility exerts its influence indirectly through grit, enhancing individuals’ capacity to sustain effort and recover from setbacks rather than acting as an independent mediator. This suggests that addressing digital fatigue requires a multifaceted approach, combining adaptability with behavioral interventions and external support systems.

Interestingly, the significant chain mediation effect, where both psychological flexibility and grit contributed to academic resilience in sequence, provided considerable insight into how these constructs interact. The sequential mediation model posits that psychological flexibility can be a precursor to grit. When students are flexible in their thinking, they are better equipped to manage stress and reinterpret setbacks as challenges rather than insurmountable obstacles. This adaptive mindset can foster the perseverance and sustained commitment that characterize grit. In turn, grit contributes directly to academic resilience by enabling students to maintain their long-term academic goals despite difficulties. Hayes’ (2013)65 work underscores that such sequential processes can provide a nuanced understanding of how cognitive and motivational factors interact to produce robust outcomes like academic resilience. For instance, students who practice psychological flexibility may reframe a poor exam performance not as a definitive failure but as a temporary setback that offers an opportunity to learn and improve24,66. This adaptive reframing can enhance the student’s grit—fueling persistence and continued effort toward academic goals67. While adaptability allows students to reinterpret challenges, it may not sustain long-term engagement when stressors are ongoing or cumulative. When coupled with grit, however, this cognitive adaptability is translated into consistent goal-directed behavior, making flexibility functionally effective. Ultimately, the interplay between these constructs equips students with a dynamic coping mechanism that is particularly valuable in digitally-intensive environments where academic pressures and distractions are ever-present.

These findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of resilience in academic settings, particularly under conditions of high digital engagement. They emphasized the need for interventions that address resilience’s cognitive and behavioral components. Programs aimed at fostering both psychological flexibility and grit may better equip students to cope with digital fatigue and maintain their academic performance and well-being over time. Educational institutions should consider integrating strategies that promote adaptability alongside perseverance to help students build sustainable coping mechanisms in today’s digital learning environments.

This study had several significant strengths. First, it employed a comprehensive mediation model, testing both single and chain mediation pathways, which allowed for a nuanced understanding of how digital fatigue impacts academic resilience. Using multiple psychological constructs, i.e., digital fatigue, psychological flexibility, and grit, provided a multi-dimensional perspective on resilience. Furthermore, the sample consisted of undergraduate students from Guangzhou, a group highly relevant for examining digital fatigue, given their extensive exposure to online learning and digital academic demands. Additionally, the study demonstrated strong measurement reliability and model fit, with all key constructs exceeding accepted thresholds for reliability and validity. These factors contribute to the robustness and generalizability of the results within similar academic settings.

However, the study also had some limitations. The cross-sectional design limited the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationships between digital fatigue, resilience, and the mediating variables. Future studies could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to better capture these relationships’ dynamic and potentially causal nature over time. Another limitation was the geographical concentration of the sample, which was restricted to students from one region. Although the findings offer valuable insights, they may not fully generalize to students in different cultural, institutional, or educational contexts. Given that cultural norms emphasizing collectivism may influence grit and psychological flexibility, future cross-country comparative research could examine whether these constructs function similarly in individualistic settings. Also, the self-report measures used in this study may be subject to common method bias and social desirability effects, although efforts were made to minimize these risks through validated instruments and statistical controls. Nonetheless, some degree of common method variance is possible due to the single-source, self-reported nature of the data; future studies could integrate multi-method or time-lagged data collection (e.g., peer assessments or behavioral indicators) to enhance validity. Finally, the study focused on two specific mediators, psychological flexibility and grit, which are essential but may not fully capture the range of mechanisms underlying academic resilience. Future research could explore additional psychological factors, such as coping strategies, social support, or self-efficacy, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how students respond to digital fatigue.

Implications

The findings of this study have important implications for educational practice and student well-being in digitally intensive academic environments. The significant negative effect of digital fatigue on academic resilience underscores the need for educational institutions to adopt concrete strategies that reduce students’ exposure to prolonged digital stress. Universities could implement digital fatigue management initiatives, such as structured “digital detox” days, balanced screen-time schedules, and the redesign of curricula to include more offline and experiential learning components, helping students recover from digital overload. The mediating role of grit highlights the importance of fostering perseverance and sustained motivation. Academic support services can promote grit-building programs, including goal-setting workshops, mentorship schemes, and reflection-based learning modules that strengthen students’ persistence when facing educational challenges. Similarly, although psychological flexibility did not independently mediate the studied relationship, the chain mediation results emphasize that adaptability remains crucial when combined with perseverance. Programs designed to enhance flexibility, such as mindfulness-based resilience training, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)-inspired exercises, and cognitive restructuring sessions, can complement grit-building interventions to improve students’ ability to sustain effort and regulate emotions under pressure. Additionally, faculty and advisors can play a vital role by incorporating flexible course designs that allow students to manage their workload and provide personalized resilience-building plans. Collaborative efforts between educators, counselors, and administrators can create an integrated support system that cultivates grit and adaptability, ultimately promoting academic resilience and mental well-being in digitally demanding university settings.

Beyond the individual and institutional interventions discussed, these findings also carry implications for the evolving landscape of digital pedagogy. As universities increasingly integrate AI-enabled learning platforms and automated digital tools, attention must be given to how these systems contribute to or mitigate digital fatigue and psychological strain. Thoughtful implementation, such as incorporating adaptive pacing algorithms, promoting healthy screen-time patterns, and embedding well-being prompts within AI learning systems, can help align technological innovation with student mental health. Thus, the results underscore the importance of designing digital education strategies that balance technological efficiency with psychological sustainability.
