Causal relationship between hyperthyroidism and cataracts: a two-sample bi-directional mendelian randomization study
Authors: Helin Ding, Taige Chen, Hao Peng, Lu Wang, Jun Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical studies and meta-analyses have suggested a link between thyroid dysfunction and lens opacification. The objective of this study is to investigate the causal relationship between hyperthyroidism and the development of cataracts using the Mendelian randomization approach, with the aim of filling the gap in knowledge about the systemic effects of hyperthyroidism on ocular health. Leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables, the analysis used extensive datasets from the UK Biobank and the FinnGen Database and employed three common approaches for causal inference (the Inverse Variance Weighted method, a regression based method the Weighted Median estimator, and MR-Egger regression) and accompanying sensitivity analyses to ensure robustness. The results demonstrate that there is a strong likely causal relationship, with hyperthyroidism increasing the risk of developing senile cataracts (OR = 361.09, 95%CI 5.024 to 2.60 × 104, P = 0.007). A sensitivity analysis provided no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy and no significant outliers, suggesting the results are robust. In conclusion, our study established a significant causal relationship between hyperthyroidism and increased risk of cataract development, underscoring the importance of considering the systemic effects of hyperthyroidism in clinical and public health interventions and policies. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying this association, exploring the potential benefits of early intervention in hyperthyroidism to prevent cataract development, and investigating whether these findings translate across different ethnic populations. Additionally, further GWAS studies aimed at identifying genetic variants associated with both hyperthyroidism and cataracts are warranted to confirm and expand upon our results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76151-0.Keywords: Hyperthyroidism, Cataracts, Mendelian randomization, GWASSubject terms: Genetics, Diseases, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Hyperthyroidism refers to a metabolic disorder that results from excess production of thyroid hormone1. It is associated with a wide array of clinical symptoms that span from weight loss, palpitations, anxiety, and sleep disorders, to life-threatening risk in severe cases. It affects virtually every organ system in the body, having a broad-based impact on the metabolic, cardiovascular, neurological, musculoskeletal, and ophthalmic systems, among others2. Cataract refers to the opacification of the lens in the eye3, and is the leading cause of blindness and visual impairment in the world. This condition has a profound impact on the quality of life for millions of individuals globally, particularly the senior population. There are numerous systemic diseases that have been linked to the development of cataracts, reflecting the important interplay between overall health and eye health. Many of these conditions have a direct effect on the lens of the eye, causing changes that set the stage for cataract development. Metabolic dysregulation is among the key systemic conditions that can increase the risk of this issue4.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) is a powerful technique for exploring the causal relationship in potential exposure-outcome pathways. It employs genetic variants as unbiased proxies for exposures to assess their impact on an outcome of interest5. Since genetic variants are randomly allocated after conception, MR is less subject to confounding and reverse causality than observational studies6. In contrast to randomised controlled trials (RCTs), which observe the effects of an intervention over a finite timespan, MR examines the influence of lifelong exposure to a genetic variant7. The recent availability of genome-wide association study (GWAS) data has led to the use of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) in MR studies using these SNPs as IVs to determine the causal relationship between a modifiable risk factor and disease, by replicating or debunking findings from previous epidemiological studies8. In doing so, two-sample bi-directional Mendelian Randomization considerably broadens the arsenal for causal inference in genetic epidemiology and yields a more contextualised view of the dynamic interplay between genetic factors, environmental exposures, and health outcomes9.

In the present study, we applied bi-directional two-sample MR to investigate the association between hyperthyroidism and the risk of cataract. Given that hyperthyroidism is a multifactorial disease influenced by both genetic and environmental determinants, employing bi-directional two-sample MR becomes critical. Bi-directional two-sample MR serves as a powerful tool in this context because it enables us to assess causality by leveraging genetic variants associated with hyperthyroidism to infer potential effects on cataract development, while simultaneously minimizing bias from confounding variables. Moreover, by conducting analyses in both directions, we can evaluate the robustness of our findings and rule out reverse causality, thus providing a more comprehensive understanding of the potential causal relationship between hyperthyroidism and cataracts. The SNPs for exposure and outcome were obtained from GWAS summary statistics in populations of European descent and comprised both males and females.

Materials and methods

Summary statistics from a genome-wide association study (GWAS)

The information about hyperthyroidism came from the UK Biobank, a large database containing a wealth of biomedical data10. This database includes a specific section dedicated to non-cancerous diseases, featuring self-reported cases of hyperthyroidism/thyrotoxicosis. The GWAS summary data relevant to these conditions can be accessed through the MRC IEU Open GWAS project database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/).

The latest data set we found was designated as ukb-b-20,289, which was selected by entering the keyword “hyperthyroidism” in the trait contains field. The dataset encompasses 3,545 hyperthyroidism instances, juxtaposed against a control group comprising 459,388 individuals. This significant sample size lends substantial weight to genetic association analyses and bolsters the robustness of instrumental variables selected for Mendelian randomization.

The cataract-related data were obtained from the FinnGen Database11, categorized into three distinct types: senile cataracts, drug-induced cataracts, and other forms of cataracts. These categories are categorized as followed (Table 1). The FinnGen Database is a large-scale biobank and GWAS initiative based in Finland. It integrates genomic data from a large number of participants with their electronic health records to explore genetic influences on diseases.

Independent variables (IVs) selection criteria

This two-sample MR study utilized summary-level statistics obtained from GWAS to evaluate the impact of hyperthyroidism on cataracts, as well as the converse relationship (i.e., the influence of cataracts on hyperthyroidism). The STROBE-MR standard, which stands for strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology - Mendelian Randomization System, is followed in the conduct of this study12.

In MR research, the choice of a suitable IV is crucial and must meet three fundamental principles13:

The linkage disequilibrium calculation between these SNPs: In order to guarantee the autonomy of certain genetic variations, it is customary to establish a linkage disequilibrium (LD) window of 10,000 kb, with a threshold of r2 < 0.01. LD, short for linkage disequilibrium, denotes the nonrandom correlation between alleles located at separate genetic loci. The assessment is conducted based on two metrics, namely r2 and kb. The r2 number, which falls within the range of 0 to 1, is an indicator of the extent of perfect linkage equilibrium between two SNPs. Lower values suggest a greater degree of linkage equilibrium, signifying that the distribution of these SNPs is random. The variable “kb” represents the extent of the region being analyzed for LD. This occurs due to the tendency of genetic loci located closely on a chromosome being inherited together, leading to a high r2 value between closely related loci. The LD window size and r2 threshold are meticulously chosen to attain independence, considering the substantial influence of LD. We removed all further SNPs situated within a 10,000 kb distance from a specific SNP that satisfied the LD criteria of having a r2 value less than 0.00114.

In the forward MR analysis, the exposure factor was hyperthyroidism, and the result was cataract. SNPs significantly associated with cataract genome-wide (P < 5 × 10−8) and without LD (r2 < 0.001, kb = 10,000) were selected. In order to eliminate any possible factors that could influence the results, such as smoking, this study removed the SNP that is directly associated with the risk factors for cataracts using the PhenoScannerV2 website (http://www.PhenoScanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) with the following settings: P < 5 × 10−8 and r2 > 0.8. SNP is significantly associated with cataract (P < 5 × 10−8).

In the reverse MR analysis, cataract was considered an exposure factor and hyperthyroidism as the outcome. Senile cataract and other cataracts applied the same screening threshold as forward MR to obtain a SNP significantly associated with cataract without LD genome-wide, and given the small sample size of drug-induced cataract selected at this threshold, the range was set to P < 5 × 10−6. Considering that smoking was considered as a risk factor for hyperthyroidism, this study utilised the PhenoScannerV2 website (http://www.PhenoScanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to eliminate the SNP that is directly linked to the risk factors for hyperthyroidism (settings P < 5 × 10−8, r2 > 0.8). Finally, the SNP significantly associated with hyperthyroidism genome-wide was excluded (P < 5 × 10−8).

By using these selection criteria, it is ensured that the IVs that are selected have both the genetic traits required for trustworthy analysis and substantial connections with the research outcome.

MR analysis

In MR analysis, SNPs that are inconsistent in alleles between the exposure and outcome, as well as palindromic SNPs with moderate allele frequencies, are excluded. The efficacy of the IVs is evaluated by computing the F-statistic, where an F-statistic exceeding 10 indicates that the MR outcomes are less susceptible to the impact of feeble IVs.

The formula for the F-statistic is: F = β2/SE2. β represents the effect value of the exposure, while SE represents the standard error of the exposure.

This study primarily utilizes the inverse variance weighted (IVW) approach for MR analysis. When all of the SNPs are valid IVs, i.e., single nucleotide polymorphism, the IVW approach provides the most accurate estimates of causal association effects16. When over half of the SNPs are valid IVs, the weighted median (WM) method can provide useful causal estimates17. In MR analyses, when there is uncertainty about the validity of some IVs due to potential horizontal pleiotropy, the WM method can still yield unbiased estimates as long as less than half of the weight is contributed by invalid IVs. Moreover, the WM method combines the strengths of fixed-effects and random-effects models, providing a balance between efficiency and robustness. It is less sensitive to outliers and can handle situations where the effect sizes vary across different SNPs, a common scenario in genetic studies. If all SNPs are deemed to be invalid IVs, the MR-Egger method can yield dependable estimations of causality18(Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

To confirm the validity of the three essential assumptions in MR analysis, it is necessary to perform three sensitivity analyses. These are done in order to assess the robustness of the results, the credibility of the conclusions, and the possible biases such as data heterogeneity and pleiotropy—when a gene affects multiple phenotypes. As well, we assess whether a particular IV drives the result, often using the “leave-one-out” method.

Sensitivity analyses of MR studies were conducted to employ the IVW approach and MR Egger regression to identify heterogeneity. The degree of heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q statistic, with a significance level of Q_pval < 0.05. Horizontal pleiotropy is assessed through the intercept of the MR Egger regression; a P-value less than 0.05 for this intercept indicates the presence of pleiotropy, rendering the MR results potentially unreliable. The MR-PRESSO global test is employed to identify outliers, with a P-value of less than 0.05 suggesting the need to exclude these outliers and reanalyze19. Additionally, leave-one-out analysis is conducted to evaluate whether individual SNPs significantly influence the MR results. Forest plots for each SNP are generated to visually represent effect sizes and confidence intervals, providing a comprehensive overview of each SNP’s contribution to the overall causal estimation.

The R (version 4.3.1) packages TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO were used for all associated MR analyses.

Results

Determination of IVs

In this study, for each type of cataract, 14 SNPs displaying a strong correlation with hyperthyroidism were designated as IVs for subsequent analysis. In addition, in the reverse causality study, 34, 4, and 8 SNPs were identified as the IVs for senile cataract, drug-induced cataract and other cataract, respectively. The F-statistics for each of the included SNPs were higher than 10, suggesting that there was no weak instrument bias in the study and verifying the dependability of the data.

Causal effects of hyperthyroidism on cataract

The findings from the MR analysis indicate that individuals genetically predisposed to hyperthyroidism exhibit a higher chance of senile cataract development than those without hyperthyroidism, as evidenced in Table 2, Figs. 2 and 3. The IVW method revealed a significant association between hyperthyroidism and an elevated risk of senile cataract (OR = 361.09, 95%CI 5.024 to 2.60 × 104, P = 0.007), an association corroborated by the MR-Egger and weighted median methods. The Cochran’s Q test did not detect significant heterogeneity among the analyzed SNPs (Q = 18.860, P = 0.128), and neither MR-Egger (P = 0.100) nor MR-PRESSO (P = 0.132) tests found evidence of horizontal pleiotropy or outliers (Table 3). The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the strong and reliable connection between hyperthyroidism and senile cataract. This association was not influenced by any specific SNP, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Funnel plots illustrated a nearly symmetrical distribution of SNPs on either side of the IVW line, indicating no evidence of selective reporting or skewed publication practices, as depicted in Fig. 5. Collectively, these results revealed a link between hyperthyroidism and senile cataract.

Regarding the connection between hyperthyroidism and other cataract, we observed that heterogenicity existed (Q = 24.123; P = 0.030). Upon application of the MR-PRESSO outlier test, rs58063871 and rs72690932 were identified as outliers with p-values significantly below the threshold of 1. These SNPs exhibited effects that deviated markedly from the rest of the genetic variants, suggesting that their inclusion could introduce bias into the MR results. Heterogenicity was no longer detected (Q = 13.497; P = 0.262), and pleiotropy was not discovered according to the Egger intercept (P = 0.597). IVW results showed a significant association of hyperthyroidism with other cataract (OR = 1982.995; 95% CI = 1.324–2970632; P = 0.042). Weighted median supported this connection, whereas MR-Egger, simple mode, and weighted mode did not, indicating that there is scant evidence to support the hypothesis that hyperthyroidism causes other cataracts.

Moreover, the IVW method suggests a void association of hyperthyroidism on drug-induced cataract by means of any MR model(OR = 34.76, 95%CI 1.27 × 10−10 to 9.53 × 1012, P = 0.792).

Causal effects of cataract on hyperthyroidism

We found no evidence to support the causal effects of cataracts on hyperthyroidism using various MR methods (Table 4; Figure 6). Analysis utilising Cochran’s Q statistic and Egger’s intercept indicated that only drug-induced cataract had not detected significant heterogeneity and pleiotropy (Table 5). Causal estimates specific to genetic variants were depicted through forest plots, revealing no significant heterogeneity among the SNPs associated with drug-induced cataracts (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2).

Discussion

This is the first bidirectional MR analysis that we are aware of that looks into the connection between hyperthyroidism and cataracts. By obtaining summary-level data from previous GWAS, we discovered that the systemic effects of hyperthyroidism, particularly on cardiovascular health and mortality, are well-documented20. This suggests that the impact of thyroid dysfunction spans multiple body systems. Although direct evidence linking hyperthyroidism and cataracts is scarce, thyroid dysfunction is recognized for its ocular manifestations, especially in Graves’ disease and its ophthalmopathy21. The management guidelines for hyperthyroidism take into account the ocular implications, highlighting the acknowledged necessity to address potential eye health concerns in the management of thyroid disease22. Further research, specifically designed to explore the statistical relationship between hyperthyroidism and cataracts, would be valuable for a more comprehensive understanding of this potential association.

Notably, our MR results demonstrate the correlation between hyperthyroidism and different forms of cataract, expanding on earlier research. Among them, we found that hyperthyroidism had the most significant effect on senile cataract. However, there is insufficient evidence to suggest that hyperthyroidism leads to other cataract. Additionally, there is no association between hyperthyroidism and drug-induced cataracts. Using various MR methods, we did not find any evidence supporting a causal effect of cataracts on hyperthyroidism. Although the evidence for a statistically significant causal link between hyperthyroidism and cataracts is robust across various MR methods, interpreting the clinical significance of these MR estimates requires caution. The validity of research findings may be compromised by selective reporting bias, whereby only findings that achieve statistical significance are published, while those that do not are either disregarded or remain undisclosed23. This practice introduces a skew in the conclusions drawn from research. Furthermore, despite the MR approach being designed to mitigate the confounding effects of environmental factors, there remains a scenario where environmental influences and the interactions between genes and the environment could significantly alter outcomes. These critical elements may be inadvertently neglected in MR analyses, undermining the robustness of the findings24.

It is imperative to recognize that, as with any MR study, the causal estimates identified in our research should not be broadly interpreted as the effect of medical intervention on hyperthyroidism to mitigate cataract risk in clinical scenarios. Instead, these estimates are best understood as statistical evidence for a causal hypothesis, adding another layer of etiological evidence to support the causal connection between hyperthyroidism and cataracts.

Our study’s main strength is the fact that we used large sample sizes and summary-level GWAS data on various cohorts to obtain exposure and outcome data. The strategic and careful selection of the sample enhances the reliability and applicability of the study results in the context of a European population. Compared to MR studies utilising study-level data from a more limited group, multiple sensitivity analyses could provide a more accurate assessment of the effect size. In addition, several sensitivity analyses were carried out in order to assess and reduce any possible pleiotropy and heterogenicity.

Bidirectional two-sample MR analysis provides robust causal inference by minimizing bias from confounding factors and reverse causation. However, our study has many limitations. Firstly, the genetic variants indicate lifetime exposure, much like in all other MR investigations. The important association found in our study is probably going to reflect the long-term impact of hyperthyroidism on cataracts. A significant limitation of our study is the lack of individual-level data to confirm the temporal sequence of hyperthyroidism and cataract development. While MR studies offer compelling evidence for causal associations between genetic variants and disease phenotypes, they are inherently limited in their ability to establish the exact temporal sequence of events leading to disease onset. This is primarily because MR studies are based on cross-sectional data, which are collected at a single point in time, and do not provide insights into the dynamic progression of diseases over extended periods. Future studies with longitudinal data tracking the onset of hyperthyroidism and cataracts in the same patients are necessary to validate our findings and establish a clear temporal relationship. Secondly, the overall statistics of exposure and outcome are influenced by multiple overlapping cohorts. The F statistics indicate that the included instruments are relatively strong, which may reduce the likelihood of bias from overlapping sampling25. Thirdly, our results might not apply to other ethnic groups, even though the studies are limited to those of European ancestry in order to reduce the bias resulting from population stratification. Fourthly, we utilised self-reported codes for hyperthyroidism obtained from public GWAS databases rather than depending on clinical diagnoses. While self-reported codes allow for a comprehensive depiction of hyperthyroidism, they can also increase the level of heterogeneity in our findings. This is because participants may interpret and report their experiences differently, potentially affecting the accuracy of our results. Recognizing these limitations, future studies are warranted that utilize clinically validated data to corroborate our findings. Lastly, our study does not address the specific genes involved in hyperthyroidism. The potential molecular mechanisms behind the association between hyperthyroidism and cataracts remain unclear, and MR is only able to draw tentative conclusions about them.

The potential pathophysiological mechanisms linking hyperthyroidism and cataract formation are multifaceted, involving intricate interactions at the molecular and cellular level within the lens of the eye. Excess thyroid hormones can lead to oxidative stress, a condition characterized by an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses26. ROS can damage lens proteins, lipids, and DNA, potentially leading to protein aggregation and lens opacity, hallmark features of cataract pathology27. Moreover, hyperthyroidism may alter the metabolic environment of the lens, affecting its homeostasis and potentially disrupting the delicate balance of fluid and electrolyte transport, essential for maintaining lens clarity. The results of these studies together suggest that an overactive thyroid could cause the biochemical and structural changes seen in cataract development. This shows that more research is needed to fully understand how thyroid hormones affect eye health.

It is noteworthy that one investigation have suggested that hyperthyroidism does not significantly influence the risk of age-related cataract (ARC)28. However, our study, utilizing a more recent and extensive dataset from the UK Biobank, has revealed a significant causal relationship between hyperthyroidism and an increased risk of cataract development. We conducted a detailed exploration of different cataract types, rather than a general investigation into cataracts, and found that the association is particularly notable with senile cataract. This finding refines and expands upon the existing literature, providing new insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the association between hyperthyroidism and the risk of developing specific types of cataracts. The meticulous classification of cataract types in our research offers several advantages. Firstly, it allows for a more precise understanding of how hyperthyroidism affects the risk of developing each specific type of cataract. This specificity helps in identifying particular subgroups that are at a higher risk, which can lead to more targeted and effective preventive measures and treatments. Secondly, by distinguishing between different cataract types, our study can more accurately pinpoint the pathophysiological mechanisms at play, offering deeper insights into how hyperthyroidism influences cataractogenesis. Furthermore, this detailed categorization reduces the potential for confounding factors that might obscure the true relationship between hyperthyroidism and cataract risk. General studies on cataracts might overlook subtle yet crucial differences between various types, potentially leading to incomplete or misleading conclusions. Overall, the thorough classification and examination of different cataract types not only strengthen the validity and reliability of our findings but also contribute significantly to the broader understanding of the intricate link between endocrine disorders and eye health.

In conclusion, our research establishes a significant causal link between genetically induced higher levels of hyperthyroidism and the incidence of cataracts, thereby underscoring the critical role of effective management strategies for hyperthyroidism in enhancing cataract treatment outcomes. Conversely, the present findings do not corroborate a significant causal relationship from cataracts to hyperthyroidism. There is a pressing need for future GWAS to identify additional genetic variants closely linked to cataracts, which will either corroborate or refute this null of association.
