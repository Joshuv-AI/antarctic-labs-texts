Comprehensive epidemiological analysis of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome in Japan, 2013–2023: descriptive observational study
Authors: Tomohiro Ohno, Hirofumi Kato, Yusuke Kobayashi, Masami Kitaoka, Minako Kanesaki, Shimpei Murai, Masaru Jinushi, Mitsuki Aoki, Takuri Takahashi, Tomoe Shimada, Taro Kamigaki, Tomimasa Sunagawa

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSevere fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a tick-borne zoonotic disease that poses a serious public health challenge in several Asian countries. In this study, we aimed to describe the recent epidemiological characteristics of SFTS in Japan using denominator-based test data.MethodsWe analysed reported SFTS case-patients in Japan using national surveillance data from March 2013 to April 2024. The case-fatality ratio (CFR) was calculated using the National Vital Statistics Surveillance System (NVSSS) for 2013–2022. Trends in test numbers and positivity rates were examined using data from annual reports and public health institute surveys.FindingsDuring the study period, 931 case-patients were reported, including 11 veterinary healthcare workers. The annual notification rate rose from 3.12 per ten million in 2013 to 10.46 in 2023, with 30 (64%) estimated infection prefectures, primarily in western and central Japan. Veterinary healthcare workers appear to be a potential high-risk group. These findings highlight that SFTS remains a public health concern in Japan, requiring a comprehensive health strategy.FundingThis work was supported by the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science Kakenhi Grants-in-Aid for Young Scientists (grant number: 24K19277).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), first identified in Central China in 2009, poses a serious public health challenge in several Asian countries. Previous studies have documented the epidemiological situation of SFTS in Japan from 2013 to 2017; however, the recent epidemiological characteristics of SFTS have not been systematically summarised since 2018.

Added value of this study

This study provides an updated analysis of the current epidemiological landscape of SFTS in Japan over a span exceeding 10 years, and conducted a comprehensive analysis utilising national surveillance data combined with the denominator test data of SFTS. The annual SFTS notification rate increased, and estimated infection regions expanded from 2013 to 2023, with high CFRs and increased testing.

Implications of all the available evidence

The key findings of this study highlight the ongoing public health significance of SFTS in Japan and underscore the necessity of a comprehensive health strategy to address this disease.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a tick-borne zoonotic disease caused by SFTS virus (SFTSV, species Bandavirus dabieense). It belongs to the genus Bandavirus within the family Phenuiviridae and is a single-stranded, three-segmented, negative-sense ribonucleic acid (RNA) virus. SFTS was first identified in rural areas of Hubei and Henan provinces in Central China in 2009.1 Subsequently, it was detected in South Korea in 20122 and in Japan in 2013.3 More recently, SFTS cases have been reported in other Asian regions, including Taiwan,4 Thailand,5 Vietnam,6 and Myanmar.7 The primary vector, Haemaphysalis longicornis, is distributed not only in Asian countries but also across two continents, including north-eastern United States, New Zealand, parts of Australia, and several Pacific islands.8 Furthermore, human infected with Heartland virus, which belongs to the same genus as SFTSV, has been reported in the United States.9 Thus, tick-borne viruses, including SFTSV, have the potential to spread globally, posing a significant public health concern for many countries.

The main clinical manifestations of SFTS include fever, thrombocytopenia, leukocytopenia, gastrointestinal disorders, and neurological symptoms.10 SFTSV infection can sometimes progress to severe conditions, including multiple organ failure, and may result in death, with case-fatality rates ranging from 3% to 46% across endemic countries.11 Currently, no approved vaccine for SFTSV is available. However, in June 2024, the antiviral drug favipiravir was approved by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, Japan.12

SFTSV is circulating in the environment through transmission between ticks and wild host animals.13 Although the primary route of human infection is through tick bites, occasional human-to-human and animal-to-human transmission has been reported.11 Evidence also indicates that companion animals, particularly cats, can be infected with SFTSV, suggesting that veterinary workers should be considered a potential high-risk population.14, 15, 16

In most infectious disease surveillance, case data are often used to determine incidence; however, not only numerator case data but also denominator test data are important surveillance indicators for appropriately evaluating the recent epidemiological situation and avoiding surveillance bias.17, 18, 19 For example, in surveillance of certain infectious diseases (e.g. measles, polio, and coronavirus disease 2019), the World Health Organisation recommends collecting denominator data and positivity rates to evaluate the level of surveillance activity.20 This principle also applies to national surveillance of SFTS in Japan, where, to date, denominator data have not been systemically tracked.

Building on previous studies that documented the epidemiological status of SFTS in Japan from 2013 to 2017,21,22 recent epidemiological characteristics have not been systematically summarised since 2018. In this study, we aimed to update the current epidemiological landscape of SFTS in Japan over a span exceeding 10 years and to conduct a comprehensive analysis utilising national surveillance data together with denominator test data.

Methods

Surveillance of SFTS in Japan

In Japan, SFTS has been classified as a category IV infectious disease under the Act on the Prevention of Infectious Diseases and Medical Care for Patients with Infectious Diseases (commonly referred to as the Infectious Diseases Control Law) since 4 March 2013.23 Confirmation of SFTS cases was performed using the laboratory methods outlined in the second edition of the Pathogen Detection Manual for SFTS published by the National Institute of Infectious Diseases24: 1) isolation of the virus or detection of the SFTSV gene via polymerase chain reaction testing of blood, pharyngeal swab, or urine samples; 2) identification of a significant rise in SFTSV-specific antibody titres in paired acute and convalescent serum samples using an immunofluorescence assay (IFA), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), or neutralising antibody assay; or 3) detection of SFTSV-specific Immunoglobulin M antibody using IFA or ELISA. Public health institutes (PHIs) perform diagnostic testing when physicians suspect SFTS and request a confirmation test, based on clinical examination. Physicians are mandated to report all laboratory-confirmed cases to the National Epidemiologic Surveillance of Infectious Diseases (NESID) through the jurisdictional public health centre immediately upon diagnosis. It should be noted that the fatal or non-fatal status of patients at the time of notification is recorded in the system; however, the final outcome often remains unknown unless voluntarily reported later, as follow-up investigation is not mandated. As an exception, SFTS-suspected fatal cases may be reported without laboratory confirmation.

Demographic characteristics of SFTS case-patients in Japan

We aimed to describe the characteristics of SFTS case-patients reported in Japan over time, considering both place and person, using NESID data from March 2013 to April 2024. Japan was divided into nine regions for analysis (Supplemental Fig. S1). For this study, Hokkaido, Tohoku, Kanto, and Koshinetsu were defined as the eastern region; Tokai, Hokuriku, and Kinki as the central region; and Chugoku, Shikoku, Kyushu, and Okinawa as the western region. The estimated area of infection was determined based on the location identified by physicians as the likely site of infection, taking into account case-patients’ medical and social histories. In addition, the time from symptom onset to confirmed diagnosis was documented from 2013 to 2023. The NESID system operates under standardised national reporting guidelines. Sex was classified based on biological factors, and data on race or ethnicity were not collected.ⅱ)Trends of SFTS case-fatality ratios

ⅱ)Trends of SFTS case-fatality ratios

Trends of SFTS case-fatality ratios

To examine trends in fatality of case-patients with SFTS between 2013 and 2022, the reported case-fatality proportions or ratios were calculated using two independent surveillance systems: the NESID and the National Vital Statistics Surveillance System (NVSSS). The total number of reported SFTS case-patients was obtained from NESID, whereas fatal cases were collected from both NESID and NVSSS. When a patient dies, physicians record the name, date of birth, sex, date and place of death, cause of death, and other relevant information on a death certificate. The patient's family then submits the certificate to the municipal office. The government compiles this information from municipal governments through NVSSS and publishes it annually. If SFTS is confirmed during treatment and identified as the cause of death, it is recorded as such on the death certificate. Therefore, the NVSSS reflects the final outcome as determined by a physician at the time of death. According to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, tenth revision, the NVSSS provides complete data on deaths, including foetal deaths.25 The case-fatality ratio was calculated using the number of SFTS case-patients from NESID as the denominator, because the NVSSS does not include data on non-fatal SFTS cases.ⅲ)Trends in testing and positivity

ⅲ)Trends in testing and positivity

Trends in testing and positivity

We collected data on the number of tests and positive results for SFTS from 2013 to 2022 by consulting the annual reports released by PHIs. To date, only PHIs have conducted official diagnostic tests for SFTS because of the absence of commercially available tests. Consequently, we could ascertain the overall trends in testing and positivity in Japan by aggregating testing data from PHIs. In instances where testing data were unavailable in the annual reports, we distributed questionnaire forms directly to the PHIs via e-mails to obtain test number data. Regarding the number of tests, multiple samples were counted as one per case and repeat tests were excluded. Based on these results, the trends in testing and positivity were described according to time and place. To calculate the number of tests per population, population data was collected for the years 2010, 2015 and 2020.26

Statistical analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher's exact test were used to compare age distributions and sex ratios, respectively, between 2013 and 2023. Trends in the number of days from symptom onset to diagnosis of SFTS during 2013–2023 were analysed using the Jonckheere–Terpstra test. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the relationship between testing rate (number of tests per million population) and positive detection rate (number of positive results per million population). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases (approval no. 1563). It was conducted for public health purposes using anonymised national surveillance data. No human samples were collected.

Role of the funding source

The funder had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, report writing, or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

Demographic characteristics of case-patients with SFTS in Japan

During the study period, 931 case-patients were reported through NESID (Fig. 1). Of these, 924 case-patients (excluding seven with unknown onset dates) showed a consistent upward trend, with annual reports from 30 of 47 (64%) prefectures. The number of case-patients ranged from 40 to 132 per year between 2013 and 2023 (n = 40, 61, 60, 60, 90, 77, 102, 75, 111, 116, and 132, respectively) (Fig. 1). The annual notification rate increased from 3.2 per ten million population in 2013 to 10.46 in 2023. Between March and October, 852 case-patients (92%) were reported, with a seasonal peak around June, and 72 (8%) were reported between November and February: 37 (51%) from Kyushu and Okinawa, and 17 (24%) from Shikoku (Supplemental Table S1). In 2013, the estimated regions of infection were primarily located in southwestern Japan: 19 case-patients in Kyushu and Okinawa, nine in Shikoku, 10 in Chugoku, and two in Kinki (Fig. 2). However, in 2023, SFTS case-patients were reported in both the southwestern and central regions: 54 case-patients in Kyushu and Okinawa, 22 in Shikoku, 32 in Chugoku, 12 in Kinki, and four in Tokai, suggesting that the estimated regions of infection gradually expanded eastward over the years. The annual trends of SFTS case-patients by the estimated infection region showed an increasing trend for most regions, with the exception of Hokuriku and Shikoku regions (Supplemental Fig. S2). The median age of case-patients was 75 (interquartile range [IQR]: 67–82) years and 466 (50%) were male (Table 1). The number of case-patients aged ≥65 years was 747, constituting 80% of the total. No significant differences were observed in the male-to-female ratio and the median age between the cohorts in 2013 and 2023 (odds ratio [95% confidence interval]: 0.56 [0.25–1.21], p = 0.15; median [IQR]: 75 [69–81] vs. 73 [65–82], p = 0.48). Furthermore, the analysis by regions showed similar proportions in age-sex distribution (Supplemental Table S2). Discussion

In this study, we present an updated epidemiological status of SFTS in Japan over an 11-year period since 2013, assessing its distribution by time, place, and person. Between 2013 and 2023, SFTS case-patients were reported from western and central Japan, with a steady annual increase in notification rates. To evaluate the impact of testing on this trend, we compiled national data on the number of performed tests and analysed positivity rates, suggesting that increased testing may have influenced the rise in reported case-patients in Japan. Globally, the overall pooled case-fatality rate has been estimated at 7.8% and exhibits an overall decreasing trend.27 In contrast, our findings revealed that the CFRs reported by the NVSSS have remained consistently high at approximately 20% in Japan.

Since SFTSV was first identified in China in 2009,1 SFTS patients have been reported across East and Southeast Asia.8 Among these countries, most patients have been reported in Japan, China, and South Korea, with notification rates of 2.46, 18.59, and 48.49 per ten million population, respectively.27 Japan's notification rate is considerably lower than that of China and South Korea; however, this study reveals that it has continued to increase in recent years. Consistent with previous reports from Japan, the basic demographic characteristics did not display significant changes over the 11-year study period: most case-patients were older adults, and no sex differences were observed.21,22 Geographically, reported case-patients gradually expanded eastward. As discussed below, the increase in testing rate, together with increasing public awareness of SFTS, might have contributed to case detection in new areas. Additional factors, such as human-induced climate change28 and the movement of migratory birds carrying SFTSV-infected vectors,29 may also have played a role. Seasonal patterns mirrored tick activity: most SFTS case-patients occurred between March and October; although a small number occurred from November to February in southern regions, including Kyushu and Okinawa, and Shikoku. This indicates that preventive measures should also be maintained even in winter. Another noteworthy epidemiological observation is that, to date, only one human-to-human transmission event in Japan has been documented, which occurred in a hospital setting.30

A systematic review revealed that the estimated case-fatality rates were 19.42% in Japan, 18.54% in South Korea, and 7.40% in China.27 The difference between the countries may be attributed to several reasons. First, there were differences in surveillance systems. Only a laboratory-confirmed case, mainly genetic testing, is reported to the national surveillance in Japan and South Korea.22,31 Conversely, in China, probable or clinically-diagnosed cases can also be reported11; therefore, underestimation of the case-fatality rate could be due to overdiagnosis in China.32 Second, variations in the age distribution of SFTS patients were observed. The median age of these patients was 75 in this study, compared with 68 in South Korea and 62 in China.8,31 Several studies have indicated that age is a potential risk factor for fatality.21,33 As the median age of the patients decreases, the case-fatality rate may also decrease across countries. Third, social aspects may have influenced this difference. In China, SFTS patients sometimes return home to be cared for by their family just before death for financial and cultural reasons, suggesting that such patients may be unaccounted for as fatal patients.32 Finally, differences in genotypes might also influence the case-fatality rates. To date, six different genotypes of SFTSV have been identified, with genotype F being the most prevalent in China and genotype B (particularly B-2) in Japan and South Korea.34,35 A previous report suggested an association between genotype B strains and fatality,34 whereas another study reported no genotype-specific differences.35 This remains a topic of discussion but should be considered as a possible explanation.

Notably, we identified a discrepancy in the CFRs between NESID and NVSSS. We speculated that the reported CFRs could be underestimated in the NESID, which reported that the CFRs gradually declined to approximately 10% over time. As described above, physicians are required to report temporal outcomes to the NESID on the reporting day, not the final outcome. SFTS case-patients reported to the NESID before death because of earlier diagnosis in recent years than before, possibly because of the establishment of a testing system and heightened awareness among medical and public health personnel in endemic areas. The median time from onset to diagnosis was remarkably shorter in 2023 than in 2013 (the early phase). Considering the general clinical course, the average time from onset to death was 9 days and most patients were fatal within 14 days,10 SFTS case-patients who reported before death could possibly not be counted as a fatality in NESID. To calculate the CFRs that were considered closer to the true values, we used the NVSSS data, which are believed to appropriately reflect final outcomes. Consequently, the CFRs in the NVSSS remained consistent at approximately 20% with slight fluctuations, although the reported CFR according to the NESID was approximately 10%. Notably, the CFRs in the NVSSS appear to be closer to a true value but may still be an underestimation because fatal patients could not be registered in NVSSS if they died without confirmation of SFTS.

In surveillance for infectious diseases, surveillance bias, a non-random type of information bias, should always be considered.17 To assess trends of epidemics of infectious diseases appropriately, it is important to consider three indicators, including the number of tests, positive cases, and positivity, for minimising surveillance bias.18 In this study, we collected and analysed the three indicators to evaluate whether or not the increase in SFTS observed was influenced by the testing number. The number of tests and positive results increased over time; however, the positivity decreased, suggesting that an increase in testing resulted in more positive results. In addition, the positive correlation between positive results and testing rates in endemic areas supported our hypothesis. However, these simple indicators alone may not completely exclude a potential increase. For instance, positivity may decrease when the number of tests increases beyond the true positive cases, as more clinically suspected cases are included. When interpreting surveillance data, it is important to note that both a true increase in incidence and increased testing may significantly contribute to the number of positive results.

During the study period, 11 veterinary healthcare workers, including veterinarians and nurses, were diagnosed with SFTS. To strengthen prevention and control measures, a One Health approach encompassing both humans and animals is crucial.36 SFTSV-infected companion animals, particularly cats, are important in transmission, because they maintain close contact with humans and are highly susceptible to SFTSV, whose viral genomes have been detected in multiple body fluids, including serum, eye swabs, saliva, urine, and rectal swabs.14, 15, 16 However, it was reported that infection control measures against SFTS were inadequate among veterinary healthcare workers.37 To avoid occupational exposure, improving infection control measures, including the appropriate use of personal protective equipment by veterinary workers is essential.

This study has some limitations. First, we could not confirm that the number of tests and positive results were obtained for all PHIs. Nevertheless, the positive results in this study accounted for nearly 85% of SFTS case-patients reported in the NESID from 2013 to 2022, which may be sufficient for understanding overall trends. Second, the NESID and questionnaire survey were based on the calendar year (January–December), whereas annual PHIs reports were based on the fiscal year (April–March). However, because the number of case-patients between January and March was very small, this discrepancy did not significantly affect the conclusions. Third, undetected fatal and non-fatal SFTS cases may exist because SFTS patients often present with non-specific symptoms; SFTS may therefore go undiagnosed unless physicians are experienced in recognising it. This limitation affects both the numerator and the denominator, and as it cannot be quantified, its impact on the CFR remains unclear. Fourth, policy changes within individual PHIs were not investigated. Finally, the correlation coefficient in our analysis could not adequately evaluate causal relationships between notifications and testing.

In summary, we demonstrated that in Japan, the annual notification rate of SFTS increased and the estimated regions of infection expanded from 2013 to 2023, accompanied by a rise in testing rates. Furthermore, the CFRs in the NVSSS remained high. This study also provided a national-level overview of SFTS among veterinary healthcare workers in recent years, highlighting the ongoing challenge of preventing SFTSV transmission in veterinary workers. Recently, favipiravir was approved by the Japanese government for the treatment of SFTS, and has the potential to reduce the fatality rate of SFTS in the future.38, 39, 40 The trends in SFTS, including its epidemiological characteristics, should be continuously monitored along with the denominator test data.
