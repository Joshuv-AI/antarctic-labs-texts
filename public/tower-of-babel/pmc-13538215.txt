The TAF3/SREBP2/cholesterol axis drives tumor progression in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jiali Qiu, Lei Cao, Yi Li, Yan Li, Zhiqi Yin, Dejun Kong, Na Chen, Zhenglu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTATA-binding protein-associated factor 3 (TAF3), a member of the TAF family, plays a crucial role in safeguarding finely balanced transcriptional programs. Previous research identified TAF3 as a critical prognostic marker in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This study aimed to elucidate TAF3’s functional role and mechanistic underpinnings in liver cancer progression. Through the establishment of stable TAF3-knockdown and overexpression cell lines in human HCC cells (HepG2 and MHCC97H), comprehensive functional analyses revealed that TAF3 knockdown significantly inhibited cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while its overexpression promoted these malignant phenotypes. Clinically, elevated TAF3 expression correlated with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor prognosis in HCC patients. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that TAF3 transcriptionally activates sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2), a master regulator of cholesterol synthesis, leading to increased intracellular cholesterol accumulation. This cholesterol elevation functionally contributed to oncogenic processes, as exogenous cholesterol supplementation reversed the impaired malignancy in TAF3-deficient cells. In vivo validation using a subcutaneous xenograft mouse model confirmed that TAF3 knockdown suppressed tumor growth, an effect effectively counteracted by high-cholesterol dietary intervention. Collectively, these findings establish that TAF3 promotes liver cancer progression through transcriptional activation of SREBP2 and subsequent enhancement of cholesterol biosynthesis. The study identifies a novel TAF3/SREBP2/cholesterol axis as a promising therapeutic target in TAF3-overexpressing hepatocellular carcinomas, providing mechanistic insights into the interplay between transcriptional regulation and metabolic reprogramming in cancer progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-026-02267-4.Keywords: TAF3, SREBP2, Cholesterol synthesis, Liver cancer, Transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignancies globally and is the third leading cause of cancer-associated mortality, posing a significant public health burden worldwide [1]. Unlike other parenchymal tumors, liver cancer exhibits unique and complicated biological characteristics, including pronounced metabolic reprogramming, high heterogeneity, and a strong propensity for vascular invasion and metastasis [2–4], which collectively contribute to its poor prognosis. Currently, surgery is the primary treatment, however, the prognosis remains suboptimal due to metastasis and recurrence [5]. Extensive basic and clinical studies have been conducted globally on the occurrence and development of liver cancer using genomics [6], proteomics [7, 8], metabolomics [9], and other methodologies. These efforts have contributed to optimizing the diagnosis and treatment of liver cancer and improving patient survival rates [3, 10, 11]. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms driving liver cancer initiation and progression, particularly the metabolic reprogramming events that facilitate aggressive phenotypes, remain incompletely understood.TATA-binding protein-associated factor 3 (TAF3), a member of the TAFs family, has been reported to be involved in various biological processes. Yvette Stijf-Bultsma et al. demonstrated that TAF3 acts as a direct target of nuclear phosphoinositide, playing a crucial role in signal transduction [12]. TBP-associated factors (TAFs) are a group of proteins that bind to the TATA box and can form a complex with the transcription factor IID (TFIID) to function in a promoter-specific manner [13]. Furthermore, TAF3 can regulate the cell cycle by regulating TBP activity through specific dephosphorylation [14] and is closely associated with directed differentiation in embryonic stem (ES) cells [15]. Shannon M. Lauberth et al. verified that the interactions between TAF3 and TFIID regulate the gene-selective functions of p53 in response to genotoxic stress [16]. Collectively, these studies establish TAF3’s dual functionality: (1) core promoter recognition through TFIID binding, and (2) modulation of critical biological processes, including cell differentiation and tumorigenesis. Within the intricate network of molecular pathways implicated in liver cancer, the transcription factor TAF3 has recently gained attention. Our preliminary studies have identified TAF3 as a factor that is markedly upregulated in HCC patient tissues, with its increased expression correlated with adverse clinical outcomes, including distant metastasis and poor prognosis [17]. These findings suggest a pivotal role for TAF3 in the pathobiology of liver cancer, although its specific functions in this context are yet to be delineated.

Cholesterol metabolism is closely associated with the development of cancer [18]. Indeed, aberrant cholesterol metabolism is one of the characteristics of tumor tissue and substantially contributes to cancer progression [19–22]. For example, it has been reported that increasing cholesterol concentration can enhance the pro-inflammatory effects of NF-κB signaling in liver cancer cells [23]. Another study additionally showed that squalene epoxidase (SQLE) drives tumor progression through cholesterol/cholesteryl ester accumulation in liver cancer [24]. Sterol regulatory element binding protein 2 (SREBP2) serves as a master transcriptional regulator governing cholesterol homeostasis [25] and regulates cholesterol synthesis by transcriptionally activating several enzymes in the mevalonate pathway (MVP), such as 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) [26]and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (HMGCS1) [27]. Given the critical role of cholesterol in cancer development, targeting cholesterol metabolism as a new therapeutic approach has garnered increasing attention. To delve deeper into the role of TAF3 in liver cancer, we conducted a comprehensive study involving both overexpression and knockdown of TAF3, complemented by bioinformatics analysis. Our data revealed a significant association between TAF3 expression and SREBP2, a master regulator of cholesterol metabolism. This unexpected discovery highlights a previously unidentified connection between TAF3 and the cholesterol metabolism pathway in the context of liver cancer.

In the present study, we verified for the first time that TAF3 promoted liver cancer proliferation, migration and invasion by regulating cholesterol synthesis. We identified SREBP2 as a transcriptional target of TAF3, which regulates cholesterol synthesis in liver cancer cells. Therefore, this study revealed a novel mechanism by which TAF3 drives tumor progression in liver cancer. We anticipate that our findings will not only shed light on the pathogenic mechanisms of liver cancer but also provide a rational basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies that could improve patient outcomes in this devastating disease.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue specimens

This investigation utilized paired hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and adjacent non-tumorous liver specimens obtained from 60 patients undergoing liver transplantation at Tianjin First Central Hospital from January 2015 to January 2022. All HCC diagnoses were unequivocally confirmed by histopathological examination. Prior to inclusion, all participants provided documented informed consent in compliance with institutional guidelines. The research protocol received formal approval from the Ethics Committee of Tianjin First Central Hospital and strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Analysis of the TCGA-LIHC cohort

Gene expression data of TAF3 were retrieved from the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), which integrates TCGA and GTEx expression datasets. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier curve module provided by GEPIA. Specifically, patients with LIHC were stratified into high and low TAF3 expression groups based on the database’s default expression threshold. The overall survival (OS) rates between the two groups were compared, and the Kaplan–Meier curve was generated to visualize the survival difference.

Cell lines

All cell lines used in this study were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The human liver cancer cell lines HepG2 (FuHeng, Cat# FH0076) and MHCC97H (FuHeng, Cat# FH0095) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin-glutamine at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. For cholesterol modulation, cells were treated with water-soluble cholesterol (50 μM). This concentration was selected based on its established efficacy in rescuing proliferative capacity in cancer models [28] and is well below the level (400 μg/mL, ~ 1034 μM) demonstrated to preserve > 80% cell viability in studies using the same reagent [29].

Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293T cells (ATCC, Cat# CRL-3216) using a Lentivirus Packaging Kit, with shRNAs targeting TAF3 (shTAF3#1–3) or TAF3 overexpression constructs. Control lentiviruses contained non-targeting shRNA (shNC) or empty vectors (OENC). Viral supernatants were concentrated with PEG8000-NaCl and transduced into target cells, followed by puromycin selection. The siRNA targeting SREBP2 (siSREBP2#1–2) was synthesized by HeYuan Biotechnology (Shanghai). Gene silencing was achieved using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the standard protocol, with functional assays being performed after 48 h of transfection. The target sequences of shRNA and siRNA are detailed in Table S1.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA extraction was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the RNAsimple total RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). cDNA synthesis and qRT-PCR were performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Shiga, Japan) and TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II FAST qPCR (Takara). For normalization, transcript levels were measured against human GAPDH mRNA levels and quantified via the comparative Ct method [30]. All qRT-PCR analyses were performed in triplicate with gene-specific primers. The specific details of the qRT-PCR primers used in this study can be found in Table S2.

Transcriptomic analysis using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA was isolated from TAF3KD and TAF3OE cells and their corresponding negative controls in triplicate for each group using an RNA simple total RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN). The RNA-seq library was subsequently constructed following standard Illumina TruSeq dual-indexing protocols and sequenced on a BGISEQ-500 sequencer (Beijing Genomics Institution, Shenzhen, China). Normalization was performed, and fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKMs) were calculated using the DC.TOM platform. Differentially expressed genes with a log2|fold change|> 1 and a P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Western blotting

The cells were washed twice with PBS, scraped, and resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., catalog #PC 101) containing 1% protease inhibitors (Shanghai Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., catalog #GRF 101). The antibodies utilized in the immunoblotting process included anti-TAF3 (1:1000, Abcam, catalog #ab188332) and anti-SREBP2 (1:1000, Abcam, catalog #ab66217) antibodies. Antibody details are provided in Table S3.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was carried out on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the samples in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a high-pressure cooker for 25 min. After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked with 1% BSA dissolved in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies used in this experiment included rabbit polyclonal anti-TAF3 (1:500 dilution, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and rabbit polyclonal anti-SREBP2 (1:500 dilution, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The specific signals were visualized using appropriate secondary antibodies coupled with the ABC detection system.

Histochemistry score (H-score) system

Protein expression levels were semi-quantitatively assessed using the H-scoring system. To ensure objectivity and minimize bias, all IHC slides were evaluated independently by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to the patients’ clinical data and group assignments. Following IHC staining, both the proportion of immunopositive tumor cells (0–100%) and staining intensity (0: negative; 1: weak; 2: moderate; 3: strong) were evaluated. This yielded a continuous scale from 0 to 300, where higher values indicated greater protein expression. In cases of scoring discrepancy (defined as an H-score difference > 10%), the slides were re-examined jointly by the two pathologists under a multi-head microscope to reach a consensus.

Cell proliferation assay

A CCK-8 assay (Super-Enhanced Cell Counting Kit-8, Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Dalian, China) was performed to assess cell proliferation ability. First, the cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3000 cells/well) and maintained at 37 °C for 24, 48, or 72 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by a 2 h incubation. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured to determine the degree of cell proliferation. This assay was repeated three times.

Wound healing assay

A cell wound scratch assay was performed to assess cell migration ability. A high-density cell monolayer was created once the HepG2 and MHCC97H cells reached confluency in a 6-well plate. The cell monolayer was subsequently scratched uniformly via a 20 μL plastic pipette tip, after which the plate was washed twice with PBS. The scratch was recorded with a Nikon microscope, and images were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h after scratching. ImageJ software was then used to analyze the width of the scratch, providing insight into the migratory behavior of the cells. This assay was repeated three times.

Transwell assay

The Transwell assay was conducted as described previously [31]. Briefly, 105 HepG2 and MHCC97H cells were seeded in the upper chamber (precoated with Matrigel) with 200 μL of serum-free medium. Complete medium was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h, the cells at the lower chamber side were fixed and stained with crystal violet solution (0.5%). The lower chamber side cells were subsequently recorded by a Nikon microscope, and the cell numbers were subsequently quantified via ImageJ software. This assay was repeated three times.

Animal studies and dietary intervention

BALB/c nude mice (aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 18–22 g) were procured from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). To generate subcutaneous xenografts, MHCC97H cells with stable TAF3 knockdown (shTAF3) or negative control (shNC) were inoculated into the flanks of these mice. The animals were randomly assigned into four experimental groups: (1) shNC + control diet, (2) shTAF3 + control diet, (3) shTAF3 + high-cholesterol diet, and (4) shNC + high-cholesterol diet.

The control diet consisted of standard rodent chow, while the high-cholesterol diet was formulated by supplementing the control diet with 1.25% cholesterol and 0.5% cholic acid to promote cholesterol absorption. This dietary intervention was initiated when palpable tumors reached approximately 50 mm3. Tumor dimensions were measured twice per week, and volumes were calculated using the formula:

The study endpoint was set at one-month post-inoculation, at which time all animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Tumors were subsequently resected and weighed.

Cholesterol extraction and quantification

Metabolites were extracted with methanol/water (4:1, vol/vol). For analysis of the cholesterol concentration in liver cancer cells, the cells were washed twice with precooled saline solution, lysed with extraction solvent (adjusted according to the cell number at a ratio of 1 ml per 106 cells), and subsequently scraped from the dish. To analyze the cholesterol concentration in HCC tissues, the extraction solvent (adjusted according to tissue weight at a ratio of 1 ml per 50 mg) was added, and the samples were cut with eye scissors and ground thoroughly. After vortexing vigorously and centrifuging, the supernatant was obtained and dried by a vacuum concentration dryer, redissolved in 50 μL of 80% methanol, and then used for cholesterol measurement via an automatic electrochemical luminescence instrument (Cobas 8000, F. Hoffmann-La Roche AG, Basel, Switzerland).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis

The data analyzed in this study were derived from the NCBI GEO database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) GSM1065008, which was the ChIP-seq high-throughput sequencing data of TAF3 in colorectal cancer cell line HCT116. After obtaining the original sequencing data, the project first used Fastp software to filter low-quality sequencing data, and the filtering standard was sequencing data quality Phred score > 30. After filtering out low-quality sequences, raw reads were mapped to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome using Bowtie2 software, allowing up to two mismatches and preserving only the uniquely aligned sequence. Macs2 software was used to conduct peak calling based on the compared files, and FDR < 0.05 was used as the threshold to obtain the binding peak of significantly enriched TAF3. The ChIPseeker R package was employed for peak annotation, for visualization using the UCSC Genome Browser.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR) assay

The ChIP-qPCR assay was performed using the EZ ChIP Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, we initially attempted ChIP using a commercial TAF3 antibody (Abcam) but failed to obtain reproducible signals despite multiple optimization attempts. We therefore generated stable HepG2/MHCC97H cell lines expressing FLAG-tagged TAF3. Approximately 2 × 10⁷ cells per reaction were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to shear genomic DNA into fragments ranging from 200 to 1000 bp. The lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-FLAG antibody to capture TAF3-DNA complexes, followed by incubation with Protein A Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA Beads (50% slurry). The immunoprecipitated complexes were collected, and cross-links were reversed. The co-precipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using specific primers targeting the SREBP2 promoter (primer sequences are provided in Table S2). Specificity was confirmed by the absence of enrichment in FLAG-empty vector control samples. Input DNA was included as a control.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the regulatory role of the transcription factor TAF3 on the SREBP2 promoter, we conducted a dual-luciferase reporter assay. The SREBP2 promoter was cloned into the pGL3-basic luciferase vector, while the TAF3 coding sequence (CDS) was inserted into the pcDNA3.1 expression vector (both verified by sequencing). 293 T cells were co-transfected with four plasmid combinations, including controls and experimental groups (TAF3 + SREBP2-promoter), alongside the Renilla luciferase-thymidine kinase (pRL-TK) internal control. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), with firefly signals normalized to renilla for transfection efficiency.

Statistical analysis

Data from a minimum of three biological replicates were processed using SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA), with values expressed as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For tumor growth curves, two-way repeated measures ANOVA was applied with group and diet as between-subject factors and time as the within-subject factor. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed; when violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used. Bonferroni’s test was used for post-hoc multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Building upon these in vitro observations, we next sought to determine the clinical relevance of TAF3 expression in HCC patients. While our previous bioinformatics study [17] identified TAF3 as a prognostic marker through computational analysis, here we provide the first validation using a well-characterized cohort of 60 HCC patients with comprehensive clinicopathological data. We first quantified TAF3 mRNA levels by qPCR in 60 HCC specimens and ranked them from lowest (Case 1) to highest (Case 60) expression, with the bottom 30 cases classified as the low-TAF3 group and the top 30 cases as the high-TAF3 group. Clinical analysis revealed no significant association between TAF3 expression and age (P = 0.795) or gender (P = 0.519). However, high TAF3 expression correlated positively with advanced TNM stage (P = 0.015), poor differentiation (P = 0.020), increased tumor number (P = 0.012), larger tumor size (P = 0.020), microvascular invasion (P = 0.035), and high recurrence rate (P = 0.038; χ2 test, Table 1, Page 40–41). The high-TAF3 group showed significantly greater microvascular invasion prevalence than the low-expression group (Fig. 1H). High-TAF3 tumors showed higher frequencies of poor differentiation, multifocal lesions, larger tumor size, and microvascular invasion compared to low-TAF3 tumors. Next, Kaplan–Meier analysis of the 60 patients demonstrated significantly longer recurrence-free survival in the low-TAF3 group (Median = 22 months, IQR: 17–35.25, P = 0.007; Fig. 1I) compared to high expressors (Median = 16 months, IQR: 9.75–24), indicating TAF3’s association with poorer HCC prognosis.

Consistent with an oncogenic role for TAF3, tumor growth was significantly suppressed in the shTAF3 + control diet group compared to the shNC + control diet group, as determined by longitudinal tumor volume measurements and final tumor weight (Fig. 4A-C).

Strikingly, the growth-inhibitory effect of TAF3 knockdown was largely abolished by a high-cholesterol diet (HCD). The tumor growth curve and terminal tumor mass in the shTAF3 + HCD group were markedly restored to a level comparable to that observed in the shNC + HCD group (Fig. 4A-C). Furthermore, the high-cholesterol diet itself promoted a moderate but consistent increase in tumor growth in control shNC tumors.

TAF3 promotes cholesterol synthesis by upregulating SREBP2 expression

Cluster analysis of cholesterol metabolism pathways revealed predominant alterations in synthesis-related genes following TAF3 manipulation (Fig. 5A-C). TAF3 knockdown significantly downregulated cholesterol synthesis genes (including SREBP2 and its downstream targets HMGCR and HMGCS1), whereas overexpression upregulated these genes (all P < 0.05, Fig. 5D-H). Transcriptome analysis confirmed downregulation of multiple cholesterol biosynthesis genes in TAF3-knockdown cells versus controls (Figure S4), with consistently higher expression in HCC tissues than adjacent normal tissues (P < 0.05, Figure S5).

At the protein level, western blotting demonstrated concordant SREBP2 modulation: reduced expression of both the precursor (~ 125 kD) and mature (~ 68 kD) forms of SREBP2 in TAF3-knockdown cells, and elevated expression of both forms in TAF3-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5I, Figure S6). For SREBP2 knockdown, HepG2/MHCC97H cells were transfected with two different SREBP2-specific small interfering RNA (siRNAs), and mRNA levels (Figure S7) were evaluated. Results showed that siSREBP2#1 was more effective, therefore, siSREBP2#1 was selected for subsequent experiments. Notably, we found that the cholesterol content increase induced by TAF3 overexpression was abolished upon SREBP2 knockdown (Fig. 5J-K).

High-TAF3 HCC specimens exhibited significantly elevated SREBP2 mRNA (P < 0.001, Fig. 6A) and protein levels (Fig. 6B-C) compared to low-TAF3 cases. Quantitative H-score analysis revealed that TAF3 H-scores were 120.3 ± 23.2 in high-TAF3 specimens (N = 30) versus 86.5 ± 20.2 in low-TAF3 specimens (N = 30). SREBP2 H-scores were 78.4 ± 18.2 in high-TAF3 tumors versus 65.8 ± 12.5 in low-TAF3 tumors, with adjacent non-tumorous tissues showing minimal staining for both proteins (H-scores < 30). IHC analysis further confirmed a strong positive correlation between TAF3 and SREBP2 expression (P < 0.001, Fig. 6D).

TAF3 is a transcriptional regulator of SREBP2

ChIP-seq analysis of publicly available data from HCT116 cells (GSM1065008) identified 11,765 significant TAF3 binding peaks genome-wide, with predominant enrichment in promoter regions (P < 0.05, Fig. 7A-B). Notably, a strong TAF3 binding peak was detected at the SREBP2 promoter (Fig. 7C). To validate this finding, we performed ChIP-qPCR, which confirmed the direct binding of TAF3 to the SREBP2 promoter in both HepG2 and MHCC97H cells (Fig. 7D-E).

Dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that TAF3 specifically enhanced SREBP2 promoter activity (P < 0.0001 versus control, Fig. 7F), while showing no effect on the empty vector. These findings establish TAF3 as a direct transcriptional regulator of SREBP2 through its binding to the promoter.

In summary, our findings provide compelling evidence for a new regulatory role of TAF3 in liver cancer. Discussion

The TATA-binding protein-associated factors (TAFs) are integral components of the transcription factor IID complex, with emerging roles beyond their canonical function in transcription initiation [14, 32–39]. While certain TAFs have been implicated in tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as cell cycle regulation and p53 signaling [32], the specific function of TAF3 in liver cancer remained largely unexplored. Our study now establishes TAF3 as a critical oncogenic driver in liver cancer, unveiling a novel mechanism through which it promotes tumor progression by activating the SREBP2-mediated cholesterol synthesis pathway. To explore the impact of TAF3 on liver cancer development, stable TAF3-knockdown and TAF3-overexpressing liver cancer cell lines were established. The proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of TAF3KD and TAF3OE cells were tested through CCK-8, cell wound scratch, and Transwell assays, respectively. These results demonstrated that TAF3 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, supporting its role as a positive regulator of tumorigenesis in liver cancer. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that TAF3 dysregulation significantly enriched both DNA replication (including p53 pathway activation) and cholesterol metabolism pathways (Fig. 2C–E). While DNA replication exhibited a higher enrichment ranking, we prioritized the investigation of cholesterol metabolism for three compelling reasons. First, from a biological perspective, DNA replication upregulation is a common hallmark of proliferating cells and often represents a downstream consequence, rather than a specific driver, of the malignant phenotypes central to our study, such as invasion and metastasis [40–42].Second, regarding novelty and specificity, the role of TAF family proteins (e.g., TAF1) in DNA replication and cell cycle regulation has been previously implicated [43], suggesting a potentially redundant function for TAF3 in this process. In stark contrast, a role for any TAF, particularly TAF3, in directly regulating cholesterol metabolic reprogramming was entirely unexplored, presenting a significant knowledge gap and a unique opportunity for discovery. Finally, considering clinical relevance, substantial evidence firmly links dysregulated cholesterol metabolism to increased tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis in liver cancer [44–50]. Therefore, elucidating a novel link between TAF3 and cholesterol synthesis promised to yield insights with greater mechanistic specificity and potential therapeutic implications for liver cancer progression. The intracellular total cholesterol levels in TAF3KD and TAF3OE cells were subsequently measured via electrochemical luminescence, and the results demonstrated that the intracellular cholesterol concentration coordinates with the TAF3 expression level. These findings suggest that TAF3 can disturb normal cholesterol metabolism. Cholesterol supplementation experiments indicated that the addition of extra cholesterol could rescue the impaired functions of liver cancer TAF3KD cells. Interestingly, we observed that cholesterol supplementation restored migration and invasion more potently than proliferatio. This differential effect is consistent with the established roles of cholesterol in membrane organization and cytoskeletal dynamics, which are particularly critical for cell motility and invasiveness [51] and suggests that cholesterol metabolism may preferentially sustain the migratory and invasive phenotypes downstream of TAF3.

Our in vivo xenograft experiments provided further validation, showing that a high-cholesterol diet effectively reversed the tumor growth inhibition caused by TAF3 knockdown. These results collectively demonstrate that TAF3 modulates cholesterol metabolism during liver cancer progression. Notably, the rescue approach used in this study—direct cholesterol supplementation—provides a relatively immediate and interpretable readout of metabolic dependency, circumventing the complexities associated with transcriptional manipulation. In summary, this study is the first to demonstrate that TAF3 affects liver cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by disturbing cholesterol metabolism.

Cholesterol metabolism encompasses four processes, including synthesis, esterification, intake and excretion. To further explore the specific process influenced by TAF3, we assessed key genes associated with these four processes using qRT-PCR on TAF3KD and TAF3OE RNA samples. Our findings revealed a significant transcriptional activation of genes related to cholesterol synthesis in comparison to the control group. These results indicate that TAF3 increased cholesterol concentration by affecting cholesterol synthesis, not other cholesterol metabolic pathways. Previous research has highlighted the crucial role of SREBP2 in modulating cholesterol metabolism and its impact on cancer progression [52, 53]. In this study, we speculate that TAF3 may promote cholesterol synthesis by increasing SREBP2 expression level. In vitro immunoblotting assays showed that SREBP2 expression was lower in the TAF3KD group than in the NC group, whereas higher expression was observed in the TAF3OE group relative to controls. Critically, TAF3 overexpression-induced cholesterol accumulation was abolished upon SREBP2 knockdown, confirming that TAF3 regulates intracellular cholesterol levels through SREBP2. Ex vivo immunohistochemistry assays further revealed a significant positive correlation between TAF3 and SREBP2 expression.

TAF3 was initially identified as a subunit of the TFIID complex, which can bind to the TATA box and initiate transcription [12, 54, 55]. To investigate the specific mechanism by which TAF3 upregulates SREBP2 expression, we performed ChIP-seq analysis, ChIP-qPCR, and dual-luciferase reporter assays. Our analysis of public ChIP-seq data identified the SREBP2 promoter as a putative TAF3 binding target, which was then confirmed by ChIP-qPCR, demonstrating direct and specific binding in liver cancer cells. Furthermore, dual-luciferase assays functionally demonstrated that this binding positively regulates SREBP2 transcriptional activity. Together, these results establish that TAF3 directly binds to the SREBP2 promoter and functions as a transcriptional activator in liver cancer.

Furthermore, our study demonstrates that TAF3 transcriptionally upregulates SREBP2 expression, as evidenced at both the mRNA and total protein levels. However, it is important to note that the full activation of SREBP2, a key transcription factor for cholesterol synthesis, requires its proteolytic processing and nuclear translocation in response to sterol levels. Our western blots detected both precursor (P) and mature (M) forms of SREBP2, and TAF3 manipulation proportionally altered both, indicating that TAF3 primarily regulates total SREBP2 abundance rather than its cleavage efficiency. However, we cannot fully exclude an effect on SREBP2 maturation, which will require future subcellular fractionation studies. Nevertheless, the rescue of TAF3-knockdown phenotypes by cholesterol supplementation strongly implies that the functional output of the SREBP2 pathway is ultimately compromised when TAF3 is deficient, positioning the regulation of SREBP2 expression by TAF3 as a pivotal control point in HCC cholesterol metabolism. SREBP2 is also regulated by other transcription factors. For example, FoxO3 recruits Sirt6 to the SREBP2 promoter, where they establish a repressive chromatin state to suppress SREBP2 and downstream gene expression [56]. Besides, p53 can inhibit SREBP2 activation by transcriptionally inducing the ABCA1 cholesterol transporter gene [25]. Our study first identified TAF3 as a potential transcriptional regulator of SREBP2, thereby providing a novel perspective for investigating cholesterol homeostasis regulation.

Beyond establishing the TAF3/SREBP2/cholesterol axis as a key driver of HCC, our findings prompt important considerations for clinical translation. Firstly, while transcription factors like TAF3 have historically been challenging to drug, emerging strategies such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras [57] or targeting their essential co-factors offer promising avenues [58]. The dependency of TAF3-high tumors on cholesterol synthesis immediately suggests a clinically actionable vulnerability. Statins, as established and widely used HMGCR inhibitors, represent a compelling candidate for drug repurposing [50]. Our data provide a mechanistic rationale for exploring statins in a subset of HCC patients with high TAF3 expression or activated cholesterol synthesis signatures, potentially explaining the heterogeneous outcomes observed in previous epidemiological studies. Future research should validate TAF3 expression across etiologically diverse HCC cohorts (e.g., HBV, HCV, NAFLD) to determine whether our findings are universal or subtype-specific. Furthermore, investigating whether circulating cholesterol levels or a TAF3/SREBP2 gene signature in tumor tissue can serve as predictive or prognostic biomarkers will be crucial for patient stratification.

Overall, our study reveals a novel mechanism whereby TAF3 influences the proliferation, migration, and invasion of liver cancer through transcriptionally activating SREBP2 and promoting cholesterol synthesis. While these findings provide new insight into liver cancer development, it should be acknowledged that the downstream mechanisms through which elevated cholesterol promotes HCC invasion and metastasis remain to be explored. First, our study primarily focuses on the specific role of TAF3; whether its oncogenic function requires the broader TFIID complex or other TAF subunits remains to be determined. In addition, transcriptomic analysis revealed that TAF3 affects multiple pathways beyond cholesterol metabolism, and although our rescue experiments support cholesterol as a major effector, we cannot exclude contributions from other pathways or distinguish direct from indirect effects—questions that warrant further investigation. Second, we note that MHCC97 cells are HBV-positive whereas HepG2 cells are HBV-negative (Cellosaurus RRID: CVCL_4971). The consistent TAF3-dependent phenotypes observed across both lines suggest that the TAF3-SREBP2 axis operates independently of viral status. However, potential synergistic effects between HBV factors and this pathway warrant future investigation using isogenic models. It should also be noted that the RNA-seq analysis in this study was conducted using three biological replicates (n = 3). While this is consistent with the standard adopted in some studies, increasing the sample size (e.g., to N ≥ 5) in future transcriptomic research would help improve statistical power and enhance the robustness of conclusions. Moreover, the assessment of cholesterol metabolism through intracellular total cholesterol measurement reflects the net outcome of synthesis, uptake, and efflux rather than direct synthesis flux; it also does not distinguish between free cholesterol and cholesteryl esters, which have distinct roles in cancer progression. Future studies will prioritize the inclusion of other lipid species (e.g., fatty acids) as specificity controls, as well as isotopic tracer assays (e.g., 14C-acetate) or methods to separately quantify free cholesterol and cholesteryl esters will be prioritized for precise quantification. Additionally, future studies aimed at delineating the pro-metastatic effector pathways of cholesterol and elucidating TAF3’s interplay within the TFIID complex will further define the clinical relevance of this pathway. Nevertheless, the current work establishes TAF3 as a potential therapeutic target for managing liver cancer progression.
