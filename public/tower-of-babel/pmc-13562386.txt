Second ipsilateral locoregional recurrence in patients with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer: a cohort study

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEvidence regarding outcomes in patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer (BC) with a second consecutive ipsilateral locoregional recurrence (LRR) remains limited.MethodsSingle-center, cohort study including consecutive patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative BC who underwent surgery (study baseline) for a second consecutive, ipsilateral LRR, at the European Institute of Oncology (Milan) from January 2001 to September 2025. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Among patients with early breast cancer, locoregional recurrence (LRR) is associated with an increased risk of distant metastasis and breast cancer-related mortality, although evidence on management remains limited and heterogeneous. Data on second consecutive ipsilateral LRRs are extremely scarce. We conducted a literature search on PubMed and Embase to identify studies evaluating outcomes and management of patients with early breast cancer experiencing a second consecutive ipsilateral LRR. We identified English-language research articles and abstracts published before January 31, 2026 using “breast cancer”, “locoregional recurrence”, “second recurrence”, “hormone receptor-positive” as search terms. The CALOR trial represents the only randomized trial in resected LRR, but subgroup analyses included a small number of patients with second recurrences and mixed tumor subtypes, limiting interpretability. Most additional data derive from retrospective series with small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, and high risk of selection bias, often lacking stratification by biological subtype or endocrine sensitivity. Overall, we found no dedicated cohort studies focusing exclusively on HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer with second consecutive ipsilateral LRR, and no robust pooled estimates of prognosis in this population, highlighting a significant evidence gap.

Added value of this study

This study represents, to our knowledge, the largest cohort specifically evaluating patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer experiencing a second consecutive ipsilateral LRR. Using a prospectively maintained database with long-term follow-up, we provide detailed insights into clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes in this understudied population. We showed that second LRR is associated with a substantial risk of subsequent events, with approximately 50% of patients developing an invasive breast cancer-free survival event within 7 years. Importantly, we identify endocrine resistance and site of recurrence (nodal or chest wall) as key factors associated with worse outcomes.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that outcomes in second LRR events are strongly driven by tumor biology, particularly endocrine resistance, and by recurrence site. These results support the need for treatment intensification strategies, including consideration of chemotherapy and novel endocrine-based approaches, especially in endocrine-resistant or non-intraparenchymal disease.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed tumor among women worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality.1 Advances in screening and treatment have substantially improved long-term outcomes in early-stage disease; however, a subset of patients continues to experience cancer recurrence.2 Accordingly, current international guidelines recommend annual breast imaging during follow-up to enable early detection of locoregional recurrences (LRRs), which represent the only potentially curable form of relapse.3,4

LRR is defined as the evidence of BC in the ipsilateral breast, chest wall, or regional lymph nodes in the absence of distant metastases, and is amenable to a local approach, either surgery or radiation therapy, with radical intent.5 LRR events account for approximately 10–15% of all recurrences in early-stage BC. Although their incidence has declined over time with improvements in adjuvant local and systemic therapies, LRRs still occur in approximately 4–7% within 10 years after resection of the primary tumor.6

After local excision alone, LRR is associated with a 50–70% risk of subsequent distant metastasis and BC-related mortality, as well as an approximately 33% risk of a further ipsilateral recurrence, underscoring the need for optimized multimodal management tailored to prior treatments, tumor biology, and patient characteristics.5,7

Evidence informing the multimodal management of LRRs from clinical trials and well-designed observation studies remains limited, and treatment strategies are often extrapolated from those used for the primary tumor.8 Data on second consecutive ipsilateral LRRs are even more scarce, with little information available regarding their management and clinical course, as this scenario has rarely been examined as a distinct clinical entity.9

Addressing this knowledge gap is increasingly relevant in the modern therapeutic landscape, where advances in multimodal treatments and prolonged long-term survival, together with extended follow-up beyond 5 years and a progressive dilution of recurrence risk over time due to screening-related downstaging at diagnosis, have led to a subset of patients experiencing multiple LRRs over the course of their disease.2,10,11

In this cohort study, we evaluated treatment patterns and clinical outcomes among patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive/HER2-negative early BC who experienced a second consecutive ipsilateral LRR, after prior resection, with or without adjuvant treatments, for a first LRR.

Methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study using a prospectively maintained database at European Institute of Oncology (IEO) IRCCS, Milan, Italy.

We included consecutive patients aged ≥18 years old, with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of HR-positive/HER2-negative BC, who experienced a second consecutive ipsilateral LRR at IEO between January 2001 and September 2025.

Second consecutive ipsilateral LRR was defined as a completely excised invasive breast carcinoma, with clear surgical margins, occurring after two prior HR-positive/HER2-negative invasive BC events on the same side, both treated with curative intent. Patients were excluded if they had a prior tumor subtype switch to HR-negative breast cancer, or a bilateral invasive BC, contralateral invasive recurrence, or non-invasive ipsilateral event before the study baseline.

All cases were discussed at the weekly multidisciplinary tumor board including breast oncologists, breast surgeons, radiation oncologists, radiologists and interventional radiologists. For both the first and the second LRR, all patients underwent whole body imaging (either computed tomography [CT] scan, Positron Emission Tomography [PET], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], whole-body MRI, or a combination of them) to exclude distant metastases.

LRR were classified as intraparenchymal when occurring within the breast for patients previously treated with conservative surgery, nodal when involving locoregional lymph nodes, or chest wall recurrences when arising at the mastectomy scar or chest wall. According to guidelines of the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO), first and second recurrences were defined as endocrine-resistant if they occurred during adjuvant endocrine therapy for the first LRR or within 1 year after its completion, and as endocrine-sensitive if they occurred thereafter.12 All of the patients received endocrine therapy for the primary diagnosis and the first recurrence.

Clinical and pathological information was obtained through access to medical records.

Ethics

All patients gave informed consent. The study was developed in accordance with the LX Helsinki Declaration and it has been approved and authorized by the Institutional Review Board (code authorization number: UID 1682). The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Outcomes

The main endpoints were invasive breast cancer-free survival (IBCFS), defined as the time from surgery for the second ipsilateral LRR (study baseline) to the occurrence of ipsilateral invasive BC recurrence, contralateral invasive BC recurrence, local-regional invasive BC recurrence, distant BC recurrence, or death, whichever occurred first; and distant recurrence-free survival (DRFS), measured as the time from the study baseline to distant BC recurrence as the first BC event, or death, whichever occurs first.

Statistical analyses

Patients’ characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%). Clinical and biological variables were grouped in standard categories whenever reasonable.

Time to event outcomes (IBCFS and DRFS) were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method; median follow-up duration was estimated by using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method applied to overall survival.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the association between clinically relevant variables and survival outcomes, reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), including the following a priori defined variables (scientific approach): endocrine sensitivity and site of recurrence.

No missing data were present for the variables included in the survival analyses. However, 2 patients (1.8%) presented with multiple sites of recurrence; given the limited sample size to derive reliable survival estimates for this subgroup, these patients were excluded from the survival analyses involving the site of recurrence variable (Kaplan–Meier estimates and multivariable Cox model).

No formal sample size or power consideration was made during the study design. No formal statistical hypothesis was formulated, therefore no p-value was assessed and reported. No multiplicity adjustment was used to compute the CIs.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Survival analyses were performed using the “survival” (version 3.8.3) and “survminer” (version 0.5.1) packages.

Role of the funding source

There was no funding source for this study.

Overall, all patients underwent surgery for the second LRR: 90 (81%) underwent breast surgery, including lumpectomy (17 [15%]), mastectomy (33 [30%]; radical in 25 cases), and chest wall wide excision (40 [36%]) (Fig. 1C). Axillary surgery was performed in 61 (55%) patients, including axillary dissection or sampling in 36 (32%) and sentinel lymph-node biopsy in 25 (23%) (Fig. 1D).

Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered in 47 (42%) patients: 27 (24%) to the breast, 12 (11%) to lymph nodes, and 8 (7%) to both sites. Breast re-irradiation was performed in 22 cases (including two with proton therapy), with no instances of third-course irradiation. No patient underwent nodal re-irradiation (Fig. 1B).

Most second LRRs displayed endocrine-resistance (77 [69%]) (Fig. 1E). Overall, 16 patients (14%) received (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy: 3 received taxanes, 9 received anthracyclines (with or without taxanes or CMF), and 4 received other regimens (Fig. 1F).

A total of 109 (98%) patients received adjuvant endocrine therapy, and 6 (5%) received an adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor. Overall, 91 of 111 (82%) patients received an aromatase inhibitor (AI), with or without chemotherapy: 62 received exemestane and 29 a nonsteroidal AI (NSAI). Among these, 60 patients switched from a previous NSAI to exemestane, and 17 switched from exemestane to a NSAI. Sixteen patients (14%) received adjuvant fulvestrant, including 15 with endocrine-resistant LRR.

Over the entire follow-up, 57 IBCFS events and 40 DRFS events were observed. The distribution of events contributing to the IBCFS and DRFS composite endpoints is detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

The median follow-up was 6.3 years (95% CI: 4.7–7.9). Median IBCFS was longer in case of endocrine-sensitive LRR compared to endocrine-resistant LRR (8.6 years [95% CI: 4.6–NR] vs. 5.3 years [95% CI: 4.0–6.9]), and in case of intraparenchymal LRR (6.8 years [95% CI: 4.8–NR]) compared to nodal (6.4 years [95% CI: 1.4–NR]) or chest wall (5.1 years [95% CI: 4.0–NR]) recurrence (Fig. 2C and D). Discussion

In this cohort of patients with a second consecutive ipsilateral LRR, we observed a substantial risk of subsequent recurrences, with half of patients developing an IBCFS event within approximately 7 years. Notably, outcomes appeared worse among patients with endocrine-resistant disease, or those with nodal or chest wall involvement. Together, these findings suggest that second LRR reflects a clinically aggressive and treatment-refractory disease state, associated with a persistent risk of distant recurrence over time, rather than an isolated failure of local control, and may warrant consideration of treatment intensification.

Data on second consecutive ipsilateral LRRs are extremely limited. The only available evidence derives from a small subgroup analysis of the CALOR trial, in which 15 of 162 (9%) patients developed a second ipsilateral LRR after a median interval of 1.6 years from the first event.9 Outcomes in that series were poor, with nearly half of patients dying within 3.7 years of follow-up after the second recurrence. However, comparisons with our cohort should be interpreted with caution. In CALOR, the subgroup was small and enriched for biologically higher-risk disease, including 9 patients with hormone receptor-negative tumors and 6 with estrogen receptor-positive but progesterone receptor-negative disease (HER2 status not assessed). By contrast, our cohort was restricted to HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors and was characterized by a substantially longer interval between the first and second LRR (approximately 7 years), suggesting a more initially endocrine-sensitive disease type. Taken together, these findings indicate that second ipsilateral LRR is a heterogeneous clinical entity, with prognosis strongly influenced by tumor biology and recurrence kinetics.

Outcomes appeared less favorable in patients with nodal or chest wall involvement than in those with intraparenchymal recurrences. This observation is consistent with prior evidence showing that the first LRR involving lymph nodes, particularly in the presence of a higher nodal burden, is associated with worse outcomes than breast recurrences.13 Apart from differences in metastatic potential related to tumor invasiveness, an additional explanation is that intraparenchymal LRRs may include second primary tumors, which are generally associated with a more favorable prognosis than true recurrences from a prior resected cancer carrying emerging resistances to systemic treatments.

Local therapy remains a cornerstone of curative-intent management for LRR, and is likely even more relevant in the setting of second LRR, where achieving durable local control is a primary objective.5 In our cohort, repeat local interventions were feasible, including repeat breast-conserving surgery, repeat sentinel lymph node biopsy, and re-irradiation, embedded within a personalized and multidisciplinary approach. For isolated in-breast recurrences after prior breast-conserving therapy, both salvage mastectomy with/out axillary surgery (either staging or dissection) and selected repeat breast-conserving surgery represent potential options.8 Although mastectomy has historically been the standard approach, emerging evidence suggests that repeat breast-conserving surgery may be appropriate in carefully selected patients, particularly in the presence of unifocal disease, long disease-free interval, and the possibility of achieving negative margins.14,15 Axillary restaging remains critical, as nodal status directly informs systemic treatment decisions; in this context, repeat sentinel lymph node biopsy is increasingly used in clinically node-negative patients, even after prior axillary surgery, despite potential technical challenges related to altered lymphatic drainage. Similarly, partial-breast re-irradiation using conformal techniques (including external beam radiotherapy, brachytherapy, or proton therapy) has emerged as a feasible salvage option in selected cases, with acceptable local control and toxicity reported in small series.16 In the absence of prospective data specifically addressing second LRR, treatment strategies largely mirror those adopted for first recurrences and should be tailored according to disease site, prior treatments, and patient characteristics.5

Systemic therapy represents the other cornerstone of LRR management and includes chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and, more recently, CDK4/6 inhibitors. In our cohort, only a minority of patients received (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy, and its impact on outcomes in this setting remains uncertain. Evidence from the CALOR trial, which randomized 162 patients with a first LRR to chemotherapy or no chemotherapy, showed a benefit driven primarily by HR-negative disease, with no clear advantage in HR-positive LRRs.17 However, this finding was based on a secondary analysis in a relatively small subgroup and has led to heterogeneous adoption in clinical practice, with some centers omitting chemotherapy in most cases and others adopting a more restrictive interpretation of the CALOR trial. In the setting of a second LRR, instead, prospective data are lacking, and our sample size does not allow for adjusted comparative analyses. Nevertheless, the overall outcomes observed in this cohort suggest that systemic therapy escalation should be considered, particularly given the high prevalence of endocrine-resistant disease, which was associated with worse outcomes and may reflect clonal evolution under treatment pressure, with reduced sensitivity to further endocrine therapy and a potentially greater role for cytotoxic approaches. This is further supported by recent retrospective data suggesting a potential benefit of chemotherapy at the time of first LRR, particularly in patients with endocrine-resistant or non-intraparenchymal disease.18 On this basis, chemotherapy should be discussed on a case-by-case basis in patients with a second LRR, especially in the presence of endocrine-resistant disease, non-intraparenchymal disease, short interval from the prior event, CDK4/6 inhibitor-resistant disease, or other features of aggressive biology.

Beyond chemotherapy, optimization of endocrine therapy represents a key component of systemic management in patients with LRR. In this setting, switching endocrine agents is commonly adopted to mitigate resistance, based on evidence of incomplete cross-resistance derived from the metastatic setting.19,20 In our cohort, endocrine therapy manipulations included switching between non-steroidal and steroidal aromatase inhibitors or to fulvestrant. Emerging therapies, such as oral selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), may further expand LRR pharmacopeia, given their demonstrated efficacy over fulvestrant in ESR1-mutant metastatic BC and over aromatase inhibitors in the early setting, irrespective of ESR1 mutational status.21,22 Although the prevalence of ESR1 mutations in LRR remains to be fully characterized, the evaluation of their efficacy in randomized clinical trials would be particularly relevant in endocrine-resistant disease.

In addition, the use of CDK4/6 inhibitors in this setting is largely extrapolated from evidence in early and metastatic HR-positive BC, depending on tumor burden and endocrine-sensitivity, in the absence of dedicated data in LRR.23,24 The ongoing POLAR (NCT03820830) and AURA (JCOG2313) clinical trials are evaluating the addition of palbociclib or abemaciclib to endocrine therapy in patients with first LRR.25,26 These studies reflect the growing recognition that LRR represents a high-risk clinical state that may require treatment intensification beyond single-agent endocrine therapy.

Our study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, single-center setting, broad temporal window during which the systemic treatment landscape for HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer evolved substantially, lack of data on genomic profiles (not performed in this cohort of patients) and molecular determinants of endocrine resistance, lack of data on the margin status related to the primary BC diagnosis and to the first LRR, and the relatively small number of events within certain subgroups, which may limit the generalizability and statistical power of the findings. Furthermore, the lack of a cohort of patients with a first LRR precludes direct assessment of the extent to which outcomes are worsened by a second recurrence. However, it also has important strengths. To our knowledge, this represents the largest cohort of patients with second consecutive ipsilateral LRR, a clinically relevant but poorly characterized scenario. The use of a prospectively maintained database and the availability of long-term follow-up provide valuable insights into the prognosis and management of LRR in this population.

In conclusion, second consecutive ipsilateral LRR represents a clinically relevant and understudied condition associated with a substantial risk of subsequent events, identifying an unmet clinical need and providing epidemiological justification for prospective trials evaluating intensified systemic strategies in this population. In this setting, outcomes appear to be driven by tumor biology, particularly endocrine resistance and site of recurrence, underscoring the need for a personalized, multidisciplinary approach.
