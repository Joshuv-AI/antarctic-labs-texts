Heat stress and stocking density disrupt telomere maintenance through shelterin complex dysregulation and oxidative damage in broiler chickens
Authors: Shahab Bahadoran, Saeed Hashemi, Hossein Hassanpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeat stress and high stocking density are major stressors in broiler chickens, compromising welfare and productivity. These stressors can induce oxidative damage and disrupt DNA structure, and potentially accelerate cellular aging. This study investigated the combined effects of heat stress and stocking density on telomere length, shelterin complex gene expression, and oxidative damage in broilers.MethodA total of 198 male Ross 308 broilers were assigned to four groups: control (standard density, 10 birds/m²), heat (standard density + 38 °C), dense (high density, 17.5 birds/m²), and dense + heat (high density + 38 °C). At 42 days, liver tissues and blood were collected to measure telomere length, shelterin gene expression (TPP1, RAP1, POT1, TRF1, TRF2), and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels as an oxidative stress marker.ResultsMDA levels increased significantly in heat and dense + heat groups compared to controls (P < 0.05), indicating elevated oxidative stress. Telomere length was reduced in all stressed groups (heat: -43.0%, dense: -21.5%, dense + heat: -38.7%; P < 0.05). Growth performance was compromised, with significant reductions in weight gain in the heat and dense + heat groups, and a higher feed conversion ratio in the dense + heat group (P < 0.05). POT1 and TPP1 gene expression was upregulated in stressed groups (P < 0.05), with the highest increase in dense groups (POT1: +282.4%, TPP1: +109.5%). No significant changes were observed in RAP1, TRF1, or TRF2 expression.ConclusionHeat stress and high stocking density disrupt telomere maintenance, likely through oxidative damage and dysregulation of shelterin components (POT1, TPP1). These findings highlight the need for improved management practices to mitigate stress-induced cellular damage in broiler production.Keywords: Stocking density, Heat stress, Broiler chickens, Telomere, Shelterin complex

Introduction

Heat stress poses a significant threat to broiler chickens due to their unique physiology. Selective breeding for rapid growth increases their metabolism and internal heat [1]. However, they have limited cooling methods, mainly panting and some blood flow changes, as they cannot sweat. Their dense feathers and large body size, relative to their surface area, reduce their ability to lose heat efficiently [2].

When environmental temperatures rise above the birds’ comfort zone, physiological problems arise. Chickens reduce feed intake to protect themselves, which lowers growth and feed efficiency. At the same time, they increase water intake, leading to wet litter conditions that can promote disease [3]. As core body temperature increases, vital cellular functions fail: proteins denature, enzymes lose activity, and membranes destabilize. Oxidative stress rises due to an imbalance between reactive oxygen species and antioxidants, causing damage to key cellular components [4]. This is worsened by inflammation and higher pro-inflammatory cytokines [5].

High stocking density is another significant stressor in commercial broiler production, where space constraints can exacerbate aggression, reduce air quality, and limit movement. At the most basic physical level, the lack of sufficient floor space restricts normal movement and behaviors, forcing birds into constant close contact. This physical confinement leads to increased aggressive interactions as chickens compete for access to feeders, waterers, and resting spaces, resulting in more pecking injuries and feather damage [6, 7].

The high stocking density also significantly degrade the microenvironment within the poultry house. Air quality suffers as ammonia levels rise from the concentrated waste production, while humidity increases due to the dense population’s respiration and excretion. These changes irritate the birds’ respiratory systems and create ideal conditions for pathogen transmission. This sustained stress state has profound physiological consequences [8]. The birds’ immune systems become dysregulated, making them more susceptible to infections while simultaneously increasing inflammatory responses. Metabolic pathways shift as energy is diverted from growth to coping mechanisms, altering nutrient utilization and reducing feed efficiency [9].

Telomeres are protective nucleotide repeats at chromosome ends that shorten with each cell division and under oxidative stress. In chickens, telomere length is a potential biomarker of stress exposure and aging, as shorter telomeres are associated with reduced cell viability and increased disease susceptibility [10]. Environmental stressors may accelerate telomere attrition, leading to premature cellular senescence and compromised tissue function. Monitoring telomere dynamics in broilers could provide insights into long-term health and productivity outcomes [11].

The shelterin complex, a group of specialized proteins including telomeric repeat-binding factors 1 (TRF1) and 2 (TRF2), repressor/activator protein 1 (RAP1), TIN2-interacting protein 1 (TPP1), and protection of telomeres 1 (POT1), plays a pivotal role in telomere protection and regulation. It prevents chromosomal instability by shielding telomeres from DNA damage responses and regulating telomerase activity [12]. Each shelterin component has distinct but interdependent roles: TRF1 and TRF2 bind directly to telomeric DNA, forming the structural foundation of the complex, while POT1-TPP1 heterodimers regulate telomere length by controlling access of telomerase to chromosome ends. RAP1, though recruited by TRF2, has broader functions in transcriptional regulation and DNA repair, and TIN2 serves as a central scaffold stabilizing the entire complex [11]. Dysregulation of shelterin components under stress conditions can lead to telomere dysfunction, genomic instability, and apoptosis [13, 14].

This research examines the combined effects of heat stress and stocking density on telomere maintenance, shelterin complex integrity, and oxidative damage in broiler chickens. By integrating molecular and physiological data, we aim to elucidate the molecular mechanisms specifically, the roles of oxidative damage and telomere regulation linking environmental stress to genomic instability in broilers.

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that both heat stress and high stocking density disrupt telomere maintenance in broiler chickens, primarily through oxidative damage and dysregulation of the shelterin complex. The observed increase in MDA levels in the heat and dense + heat groups confirms that these stressors induce lipid peroxidation, a marker of oxidative damage, aligning with studies showing heat stress elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) in poultry [15], while high stocking density exacerbates oxidative stress due to overcrowding-induced inflammation [16]. However, the dense group alone did not show significant MDA elevation, contrasting with reports that density alone increases oxidative stress; this discrepancy may stem from differences in experimental conditions such as ventilation or litter management, which could mitigate oxidative damage in densely stocked birds.

The observed declines in growth performance and increased mortality under stress conditions further underscore the physiological toll of heat and high stocking density. The significant reduction in weight gain and elevated FCR in the heat and dense + heat groups align with the metabolic diversion of energy from growth to stress coping mechanisms, such as panting and increased maintenance needs [3]. The lack of a significant effect on feed intake suggests that the reduced growth was not due to lower consumption but rather to impaired nutrient utilization and metabolic efficiency under stress. Mortality was highest in the dense + heat group, indicating a synergistic negative effect when both stressors are combined, likely due to compounded oxidative damage, immunosuppression, and behavioral restrictions.

Significant telomere shortening in all stressed groups supports the hypothesis that environmental stressors accelerate cellular aging and mirrors findings in mammals where chronic stress correlates with telomere attrition [17], though it conflicts with poultry studies reporting minimal telomere changes under heat stress [18]. This discrepancy may arise from differences in stress duration or genetic resilience in broiler strains. Notably, the dense + heat group exhibited less severe telomere shortening than the heat group alone, suggesting a potential adaptive response to combined stressors [19, 20]. This attenuated telomeric response under combined stress may be explained by the concept of cross-adaptation, where prior exposure to a chronic stressor like high density primes protective physiological pathways. The persistent density likely induced an early upregulation of cytoprotective mechanisms, such as heat-shock proteins (HSPs) and antioxidant enzymes, which subsequently buffered the birds against the acute oxidative insult of heat stress [21]. Furthermore, high stocking density may have behaviorally mitigated heat load by enforcing reduced activity and huddling, thereby lowering metabolic heat production compared to the heat-alone group where greater space permitted more activity and thus higher internal heat generation [22]. Therefore, the interaction of these stressors is not merely additive but complex, potentially triggering an adaptive state that reduces overall cellular damage.

Marked upregulation of POT1 in stressed broilers likely represents a compensatory mechanism to counteract oxidative damage, since POT1 binds single-stranded telomeric DNA and prevents its recognition as damaged DNA, thereby averting activation of repair pathways that could lead to telomere dysfunction. This aligns with human cell studies where POT1 overexpression reduced telomere fragility and aberrant recombination events [23]. In broilers, disproportionate POT1 induction in the dense group despite milder telomere shortening compared to the heat group suggests density stress may trigger a distinct protective response, possibly through inflammation-mediated pathways [24] selectively activating POT1 transcription. The lack of stress-induced changes in TRF1, TRF2, and RAP1 contrasts sharply with mammalian models; it has been demonstrated that oxidative stress downregulated TRF1 and TRF2, leading to telomere deprotection [25]. This discrepancy may arise because avian telomere biology differs from mammals in baseline dynamics (e.g., chickens express telomerase constitutively in somatic cells), and the shelterin complex in birds may prioritize POT1/TPP1-mediated protection over TRF-dependent mechanisms under stress [26]. Additionally, the stress intensity in this study (38 °C heat, 17.5 birds/m² density) may have been insufficient to disrupt TRF stability, as poultry studies using more severe stressors report TRF2 depletion [27], supporting a dose-response relationship. Liver-specific analyses might miss stress responses evident in other tissues [28]. The selective upregulation of POT1/TPP1 over other shelterin components suggests an evolutionary adaptation in birds to prioritize single-strand telomere protection, a strategy potentially more efficient against ROS-mediated damage. From an applied perspective, these findings highlight POT1 as a potential biomarker for stress resilience in poultry breeding programs. However, the lack of coordinated shelterin responses (e.g., unchanged TRF2 despite telomere shortening) implies that prolonged stress could overwhelm this compensatory mechanism, ultimately leading to genomic instability. While this study demonstrates POT1/TPP1 induction, other poultry research reports TRF2 upregulation and POT1, RAP1 and TPP1 downregulation under cold stress, reflecting possible methodological differences (e.g., primer specificity, stress duration, and type of stress) [11].

This study has several limitations: telomere length was measured only in liver tissue, so including other tissues (e.g., blood, muscle) could provide a systemic view of stress impacts. The liver was selected for analysis due to its high metabolic activity and acute inflammatory response to heat stress, making it a sensitive indicator of systemic oxidative and metabolic disruption, which is central to studying telomere attrition mechanisms. While blood offers a circulating biomarker and muscle is crucial for productivity, the liver’s heightened reactivity to thermal challenge offered a powerful model to investigate the underlying mechanisms of telomere disruption. The study duration (42 days) may not capture long-term telomere dynamics or recovery potential. While oxidative stress was assessed via MDA, additional markers (e.g., 8-OHdG for DNA damage) [29] could strengthen mechanistic insights, and genetic variability in broilers was not accounted for, which may influence individual stress responses.

Conclusion

This study advances understanding of how heat and crowding disrupt telomere homeostasis in broilers, highlighting the shelterin complex’s role in stress adaptation. The findings underscore the need for management strategies that mitigate oxidative damage, such as antioxidant supplementation or optimized stocking densities, to promote poultry welfare and productivity. Future research should explore genetic and epigenetic modifiers of telomere maintenance to develop targeted interventions.

Methods

Chicken housing and heat/high-density stress induction

The experiment was conducted at the experimental facility of Shahrekord University. A total of 198 day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks, obtained from Behjojeh Company (Chahar-Mahal-o-Bakhtiyari, Iran), were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: control (36 birds), heat (36 birds), dense (63 birds), and dense + heat (63 birds) groups. Each group was divided into three replicates, control and heat groups with 12 birds per replicate, and dense and dense + heat groups with 21 birds per replicate. All birds were housed in identical 1.2 m² floor pens with deep litter from 1 to 42 days of age under standard management conditions (23 L:1D lighting, ad libitum access to feed and water, consistent humidity and ventilation). The chickens of heat groups were reared in a separate room to induce heat stress. Environmental temperature and relative humidity were monitored manually daily using a calibrated digital thermo-hygrometer (Fig. 4). Measurements were taken at the same time each day at bird level within multiple locations in the room to ensure a representative sampling of conditions.

Tempreture and humidity changes in the experimental groups during rearing

The control group was maintained at standard stocking density (10 birds/m²) with temperatures decreasing from 31 °C on day 1 to 21 °C by day 21, which was then maintained through day 42. The heat group experienced the same stocking density but with increasing temperatures from 31 °C to 38 °C from days 1–21, which was then maintained through day 42. The dense group was kept at high density (17.5 birds/m²) with the same decreasing temperature regimen as the control group. The dense + heat group combined both stressors, high stocking density (17.5 birds/m²) and increasing temperatures (31 °C to 38 °C) (Fig. 3). All groups received identical corn-soybean meal-based diets formulated according to Aviagen (2019) requirements [30], with phase feeding (starter:1-10d, grower:11-22d, finisher:23-42d). Vaccination schedules and other management practices were uniform across all treatments.

Assesment of growth performance and mortality

Body weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio were determined for the entire experimental duration of 1–42 days. At day 42, all birds underwent a 6-hour fasting period with unrestricted access to water to empty the digestive tracts.

Blood and liver sampling

At 42 days of age, 12 birds per treatment group were randomly selected for blood collection and subsequent processing. Blood samples (4 mL) were obtained from the brachial vein and centrifuged at 2500 g for 10 min to separate the serum. Following blood collection, the birds were euthanized by CO2, and the liver was excised. The liver tissues were stored at −70 °C until RNA extraction.

Malondialdehyde measurement

Malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration, a key biomarker of lipid peroxidation, was quantified in plasma samples using a thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay adapted from an established method [31]. Briefly, 250 µL aliquots of plasma were deproteinized with 250 µL of 20% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min to precipitate proteins. A 250 µL volume of the resulting supernatant was then reacted with 250 µL of 0.67% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid (prepared in 0.2 M NaOH) by incubating the mixture in a boiling water bath (95 °C) for 10 min. After cooling on ice, samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min to precipitate debris. The absorbance of the final supernatant was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer (Corning 480). A standard curve was prepared for each assay using authentic MDA standards (generated from hydrolyzed 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane) in a concentration range of 0 to 50 µM. Sample absorbance values were interpolated from this curve to determine the concentration of MDA equivalents in the reaction mixture, with final results expressed as µM MDA equivalents in plasma. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (Louis, MO).

DNA and RNA extraction, and cDNA synthesis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the whole blood samples using a DNA extraction kit (Sinaclon, Karaj, Iran). Total RNA was isolated from liver samples employing the RNXPUS reagent (Sinaclon, Karaj, Iran) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA/RNA quality was verified via Nanodrop spectrophotometry (A260/A280 ratio: 1.8–2.0); only samples meeting this criterion were analyzed further.

cDNA synthesis was conducted from RNA samples using the PrimeScript™ Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Japan) with random hexamer primers. All samples, including both DNA and cDNA, were stored at −70 °C until quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis.

RT-qPCR analysis of telomere length

To measure the relative telomere length of liver tissue, relative quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using a 2× SYBR Green Real-Time PCR kit (Parstous Co., Mashhad, Iran). The telomere and tyrosine 3-monooxygenase/tryptophan 5-monooxygenase activation protein zeta (YWHAZ), as a reference gene, primers were synthesised according to Hassanpour et al. (2023) (Table 2) [11]. The amplification was done in a final volume of 10 µl for both telomere and YWHAZ. A sample of 15 ng DNA was added in each reaction. The final concentration of forwardand reverse primers of telomere was 250 and 750 nM, respectively. The final concentration of each primer of YWHAZ was 250 nM. Amplifications were performed in triplicate for each liver sample in a Rotor-Gene 6000 thermocycler (Qiagen, Australia). The PCR programme for the telomere was 95 °C for 10 min and 20 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 54 °C for 2 min. The PCR programme for YWHAZ was 95 °C for 2 min and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 62 °C for 30 s. A notemplate control reaction was run to ensure no contamination. The melt curves of telomere and YWHAZ following amplification showed a single peak. The threshold cycle number (Ct) of PCR and mean efficiency values (E) for telomere and YWHAZ were determined using LinRegPCR software (2012.0, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Relative telomere length was calculated according to Hassanpour (2023), followed as E YWHAZ (Ct sample) / E telomere (Ct sample) [11]. In this method, the level of telomere length relative to YWHAZ was counted.

RT-qPCR analysis of TPP1, RAP1, POT1, TRF1, TRF2 gene expression

To determine the possible changes in the transcriptional levels of TPP1, RAP1, POT1, TRF1, and TRF2 genes in two groups of control and SM-exposed groups, relative RT-qPCR was performed using the PCR kit mentioned and YWHAZ was used as a stable control gene to normalise the input load of cDNA and quantify the target gene’s expression relatively gene. The specific primers of the genes used are represented in Table 2. The primers of the YWHAZ gene were designed so that they could be used for the amplification of cDNA, in addition to DNA in the measurement of telomere. The PCR for each sample was performed in three replicates, whereby 10 ng cDNA and 400 nM of each specific primer were used in a total volume of 10 µl. The programme of PCR amplification was as 94 °C for 10 min, then 30–40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, 56–62 °C for 15–30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s (only for RAP1, TRF2, and TPP1). The no-template and no-reverse transcriptase controls were used in each PCR reaction. The threshold cycle numbers and mean efficiency values were recorded and calculated using LinRegPCR software. The relative gene expression (target/YWHAZ) was calculated according to the formula mentioned above.Primer specificity for all genes and telomere was confirmed by observing a single peak in the melt curve analysis and by verifying a single band of the expected size on an agarose gel. The amplification efficiency for each sample, determined using LinRegPCR software, calculates a robust, per-reaction efficiency value based on the linear phase of each amplification curve. Only reactions with an efficiency between 1.85 and 2.0 were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). All gene expression data were normalized. The mean value for each of the three replicate pens per treatment group was used as the statistical unit for analysis to account for the unequal number of birds per replicate across treatments. Statistical comparisons were then performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).
