Endoscopic vacuum therapy for a refractory sigmoid fistula
Authors: Connor John Rodriguez, Robert Jay Sealock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsSigmoid fistulas pose significant management challenges, often requiring prolonged antibiotics, percutaneous drainage, and surgical intervention. Endoscopic vacuum therapy (EVT), used for gastrointestinal tract leaks and fistulas, promotes granulation and controlled closure using continuous negative pressure. Its application in sigmoid fistulas is less commonly reported. Thus, we present a case demonstrating successful EVT for a persistent sigmoid fistula, highlighting technical considerations and treatment course. The aims of this article are to share clinical rationale, illustrate procedural steps, and summarize EVT applications and benefits to describe EVT as a potential option for managing complex gastrointestinal tract defects.MethodsAn initial closure attempt using a helical tack—based suturing system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA) was unsuccessful. A later attempt using a full-thickness endoscopic suturing system also failed. Granulation and defect closure were subsequently achieved using EVT via intracavitary sponge placement and serial exchanges. Imaging and endoscopic findings were documented to illustrate technique, healing progression, and treatment outcomes.ResultsThe patient's sigmoid fistula was successfully closed using EVT over the course of approximately 2 months.ConclusionsEVT is an effective minimally invasive closure technique in the management of sigmoid fistulas. This case highlights EVT as a valuable therapeutic option for lower gastrointestinal fistulas, offering safe, reproducible, and organ-preserving management.

Case description, procedure, outcome

A 52-year-old male with a history of a severe motor vehicle collision necessitating a right hemicolectomy, small-bowel resection, and duodenal repair subsequently developed a chronic sigmoid cutaneous fistula requiring a diverting loop ileostomy. This fistula led to recurrent intra-abdominal abscesses requiring percutaneous drainage. Gastroenterology was consulted to assist in luminal closure of the sigmoid fistula to prevent further intra-abdominal infections. An initial closure attempt was performed using a helical tack—based suturing system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA) with a percutaneous drain in place. A later attempt using a full-thickness endoscopic suturing system also failed. Eventual fistula closure was achieved via endoscopic vacuum therapy (EVT) (Fig. 1).Figure 1Endoscopic exchange of endoscopic vacuum therapy sponge.

Given the recurrent nature of the patient's fistula and abscesses, further evaluation was pursued to better characterize the fistulous defect, as demonstrated in the case video (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org). A fistulogram demonstrated a contained cavity with a single tract leading to the sigmoid colon (Fig. 2). Endoscopy identified a chronic colonic fistula at 30 cm from the anal verge, with adjacent helical suturing tacks and a percutaneous drain protruding through the tract. During this procedure, the prior suturing material was removed (Fig. 3), and the internal defect was ablated using forced argon plasma coagulation, followed by attempted closure using a full-thickness endoscopic suturing system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA) via a double-channel gastroscope (Fig. 4). At repeat endoscopy 8 days later, the closure had failed with complete dehiscence of sutures and re-exposure of the drain (Fig. 5). Given this recurrence and persistent cavity, EVT was initiated (Fig. 6). EVT is a strategy increasingly recognized as effective in the management of persistent gastrointestinal leaks and fistulas and more frequently reported in upper gastrointestinal spontaneous or iatrogenic defects.1, 2, 3 Thus, this case adds to the less frequently reported literature regarding EVT use in lower-gastrointestinal defects.4Figure 2Initial fistulogram.Figure 3Endoscopic view of removal of helical suturing tacks.Figure 4Endoscopic view of sigmoid lumen after placement of sutures using full-thickness endoscopic suturing system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Mass, USA).Figure 5Endoscopic view of removal of sutures.Figure 6Endoscopic view of initial endoscopic vacuum therapy sponge placement.

In this case, a custom EVT device manufactured with a foam sponge and a 16-gauge nasogastric tube was placed transanally and connected to continuous negative pressure at −125 mm Hg, a suction level shown to optimize tissue perfusion and granulation.5 Using this level of pressure in our case is consistent with standard negative-pressure wound therapy principles applied in EVT for upper and lower gastrointestinal defects and reflects current best practice. Over the course of approximately 2 months, 5 EVT exchanges were performed at intervals of approximately 8 days. As the cavity progressively decreased in size with development of robust granulation tissue, the interval between exchanges was extended to minimize anesthesia exposure. Although EVT exchanges are typically performed every 3 to 5 days, this adjusted interval did not compromise treatment efficacy in this case.1

A percutaneous drain communicating with the fistula cavity was present at the time of EVT initiation. The drain was left in place during the initial 3 exchanges to allow continued control of intra-abdominal sepsis and was gradually withdrawn as the cavity decreased in size, with complete removal after the fourth exchange. Although external drainage may theoretically impair the ability to maintain an effective vacuum seal, effective cavity collapse and fistula closure were achieved in this case with a stepwise approach to drain management.

Serial evaluations demonstrated steady improvement: early sessions showed decreasing cavity size with robust granulation tissue (Fig. 7), reduced visualization of the percutaneous drain, and eventual complete closure of the drain tract. As healing progressed, the sponge was positioned increasingly within the sigmoid lumen to facilitate closure of the fistulous opening, consistent with the evolving practice of adjusting EVT placement as cavities close.6 Accordingly, this stepwise repositioning of the sponge proved effective in our case.Figure 7Endoscopic view of granulation tissue formation during endoscopic vacuum therapy exchange.

At the final session, only a small residual opening leading to a shallow blind-ending cavity remained (Fig. 8). A follow-up contrast barium enema also demonstrated a minor outpouching without contrast extravasation, confirming successful closure (Fig. 9). The patient subsequently underwent ileostomy takedown with creation of an ileocolonic anastomosis approximately 8 months after completion of EVT. At follow-up 9 months after EVT, there has been no recurrence of the sigmoid fistula following restoration of intestinal continuity. This case demonstrates the feasibility of EVT in managing chronic colorectal fistulas, particularly after failure of invasive surgical management and conventional endoscopic suturing and supports growing evidence for EVT's effectiveness in complex lower gastrointestinal wall defects.7Figure 8Endoscopic view of sigmoid lumen after final endoscopic vacuum therapy sponge removal.Figure 9Final barium enema.
