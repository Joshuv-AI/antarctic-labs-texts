Sex Differences in Nonadherence to Secondary Stroke Prevention Medications Among Patients With First‐Ever Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Chen Chen, Mathew J Reeves, Karen B Farris, Lewis B Morgenstern, Lynda D Lisabeth
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMore women than men experience recurrent strokes. Medication adherence is critical to prevent recurrence; however, studies investigating sex differences are limited. We examined sex differences in poststroke medication adherence, overall and by drug class, and identified influencing factors.Methods and ResultsPatients with first‐ever ischemic stroke were identified from a population‐based study (2008–2019). At 90 days after a stroke, self‐reported medication adherence was defined as never or rarely missing a dose in a typical week for each secondary stroke prevention medication (antihypertensives, cholesterol‐lowering drugs, antiplatelets, anticoagulants). We generated prevalence ratios (PRs) using modified Poisson models to assess sex differences with and without adjustment for potential confounding factors, including demographics, social factors, health system–related, lifestyle‐related, health condition–related, prestrokehealth–related, and stroke‐related factors. Among 1324 participants (48.4% women, 58.0% Mexican American individuals), women were more likely to report nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs (PR, 1.80 [95% CI, 1.14–2.84]) and antiplatelets (PR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.003–2.34]). Adjusting for obesity attenuated while adjusting for age, marital status, access to care, smoking, and alcohol consumption accentuated sex differences. Race and ethnicity modified the sex difference in nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs (P interaction=0.054) such that the sex difference was larger in Mexican American individuals (PR, 3.00 [95% CI, 1.65–5.48]) than in non‐Hispanic White individuals (PR, 1.30 [95% CI, 0.52–3.27). No significant sex differences were found for nonadherence to antihypertensives and overall nonadherence.ConclusionsPoststroke medication nonadherence was more prevalent among women than men. This was partially due to the confounding effects of lifestyle, marital status, and access to care, suggesting potential subgroups for interventions to improve adherence.Keywords: medication adherence, secondary stroke prevention, sex differencesSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Secondary Prevention, Women, Ischemic Stroke, Social Determinants of Health

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This study shows that there are persistent sex differences in nonadherence to secondary stroke prevention medications at 90 days after a stroke, with women having a higher prevalence of nonadherence, even after adjusting for sex differences in factors such as sociodemographics and health behaviors.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Sex differences in nonadherence to medications need to be addressed, with a particular focus on Mexican American women, to prevent recurrent strokes for both sexes.

Stroke remains a major public health challenge, with an estimated 7.6 million adult stroke survivors in the United States. 1 Women bear a disproportionate burden of stroke than men, including more recurrent strokes, higher risk of death, and more severe disability after stroke. 2 Although studies have demonstrated that adherence to secondary stroke prevention medications reduces the risk of recurrence and death, 3 , 4 , 5 poststroke medication adherence is suboptimal. Around one third of stroke survivors discontinue ≥1 secondary preventive medications within 1 year of hospital discharge. 6 , 7 Therefore, determining if women have a higher risk of nonadherence may aid in the understanding of factors contributing to poorer poststroke outcomes among women.

The current body of research on sex differences in poststroke medication adherence is limited and inconclusive, with studies reporting no significant sex differences, 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 worse adherence among men, 13 , 14 , 15 or worse adherence among women. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Furthermore, few studies have assessed both overall adherence to all prescribed secondary preventive medications and adherence to specific drug classes. 20 Evaluating sex differences in overall adherence is important, as persistent combined medication use has a larger effect on reducing the risk of poor outcomes, which are more common among women. 21 , 22 On the other hand, because adherence rates of different drug classes may vary greatly, 6 , 20 information on sex differences in adherence to specific drug classes may help with more precisely targeted interventions to improve adherence.

Additionally, most prior studies were not designed to evaluate sex differences in medication adherence. Using data from hospital‐based convenience stroke samples, administrative health care records, or stroke registry, 9 , 10 , 11 , 17 , 18 these studies often had an underrepresentation of women (26%–42%) and reported only unadjusted sex differences. The only 2 studies designed to examine sex differences in adherence were conducted in Canada and Sweden, where universal health insurance coverage is available, 12 , 13 and they adjusted for just a handful of factors limited to age, stroke severity, and previous medication use.

The lack of attention to confounding factors in the prior literature limits our understanding of how predictors of adherence, distributed differentially among women and men, may influence sex differences in adherence. Additionally, there is a gap in data concerning sex differences in medication adherence among Mexican American (MA) individuals and potential effect modifications on sex differences by age and race and ethnicity, despite evidence of interaction between these factors and sex on adherence to primary prevention medications for cardiovascular disease. 23 , 24 , 25 Identifying factors influencing or modifying sex differences in adherence is fundamental for identifying stroke survivors at high risk for nonadherence within each sex group and the generation of sex‐specific interventions aimed at improving medication adherence.

This study aimed to examine sex differences in adherence to secondary stroke prevention medications, overall and by individual drug class, at 90 days after stroke in a biethnic US population‐based study. This study also aimed to identify factors influencing or modifying sex differences in poststroke medication adherence.

Methods

Study Setting and Participants

Data came from the Brain Attack Surveillance in Corpus Christi (BASIC) Project, which was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at the University of Michigan and the 2 local hospital systems in Corpus Christi. All study participants provided their written informed consent. Given the restricted nature of the data, deidentified data and analytical code will be available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Taking place in the biethnic community of Nueces County, Texas, BASIC is an ongoing population‐based stroke surveillance study with a majority of the participants being nonimmigrant MA and non‐Hispanic white (NHW) individuals. 26 , 27 Details of the BASIC Project have been described elsewhere. 26 , 27 Briefly, individuals eligible for the BASIC Project are patients with stroke who are residents of the community and aged ≥45 years. BASIC captures all possible eligible stroke cases through active and passive surveillance in all 7 acute care hospitals in Nueces County, who are further validated by fellowship‐trained stroke neurologists. Patients with validated stroke are invited to participate in structured, in‐person baseline interviews shortly after stroke onset and followed for outcome interviews at ≈90 days after stroke, in English or Spanish. Telephone interviews are conducted, if necessary (<5% for both interviews). Proxy interviews are administered if patients are unable to communicate (11%).

In the current study, we included all patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke who were identified during 2008 to 2019, completed a baseline interview, and were alive at 90 days to complete the outcome interview. We excluded those who lived in nursing homes before or after stroke, and those without any prescribed secondary stroke prevention drugs at the outcome interview (Figure 1).

Outcome: Medication Nonadherence

At the 90‐day outcome interview, we followed the methods used in the AVAIL (Adherence Evaluation After Ischemic Stroke–Longitudinal) registry, 28 where medication‐taking behaviors were assessed by a modified Morisky scale. 29 This scale has been validated in underserved and minority populations and includes a Spanish translation. 30 Four classes of secondary stroke prevention medications were ascertained, including antihypertensives, cholesterol‐lowering drugs, antiplatelets, and anticoagulants. 31 Specifically, study participants were first asked to show interviewers all pill bottles of the medications they were currently prescribed or supposed to take. For each medication they showed that belongs to the 4 secondary stroke preventive drug classes, they were then asked: “How often in a typical week have you missed a prescribed dose of this medication? Never, Rarely, Occasional, Often, Very Often, Discontinued or Unfilled, or Don't Know.”

Due to a limited number of responses in some categories of the adherence measure (Table S1) and to be consistent with prior research, 32 we defined adherence to each prescribed secondary preventive drug class as never or rarely missing a dose in a typical week. All other response categories were considered as nonadherence. This definition of adherence is more equivalent to the 80% cutoff point for the proportion of days covered when adherence is measured using data from electronic health records, and therefore facilitates comparison of our results with prior research. Overall adherence across all prescribed secondary preventive drug classes was summarized as a binary all‐or‐none measure (ie, patients were considered overall adherent only if they were adherent to all prescribed drug classes; otherwise, they were considered as overall nonadherent). Outcomes of this study were nonadherence to each individual drug class and overall nonadherence.

Sex and Other Potential Confounders

Sex and other potential confounders (factors associated with sex and medication nonadherence) were ascertained at baseline from interviews and medical records. Guided by the World Health Organization multidimensional medication adherence framework, 33 potential confounders were categorized into patient factors (age and self‐reported race and ethnicity), social factors (education, marital status, and social support), health system–related factors (health insurance type and routine physician access), lifestyle‐related factors (current smoking status, alcohol consumption, and obesity), health condition–related factors (cardiovascular disease histories and comorbidities), prestroke health–related factors (cognition, disability, and depression), and stroke‐related factors (thrombolytic treatment and initial stroke severity). Of note, we did not include recurrent stroke as a potential confounder because we have shown no sex difference in stroke recurrence in BASIC (Chen Chen, MPH, unpublished data, 2024). In addition, only 27 (2%) of the participants included in this study had a recurrent stroke within 90 days. Details for collection and measurement of these factors are in Data S1.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were calculated. For missing data on baseline characteristics (<16% missing), assuming missing at random, 10 imputed data sets were created using multiple imputations by chained equations to fill the missingness. 34 The following statistical analyses were performed in each of the 10 imputed data sets, and the results were combined using Rubin's rule. 34 Details of the multiple imputations are in Data S1.

Prevalence of nonadherence was summarized for the study sample. The unadjusted prevalence ratio (PR) of nonadherence comparing women with men was estimated by a Poisson model with robust standard errors (also known as the modified Poisson model) where sex was the only predictor (model 1). Since sex and other factors associated with medication nonadherence may also influence baseline and outcome participation, 35 potential selection bias of the sex and medication adherence association may exist in our analytic sample. We, therefore, applied inverse probability weighting techniques to reflect the original patient population that was alive at 90 days by upweighting study participants in the analytic sample. 36 The inverse probability weights were constructed as the reciprocal of the product of probabilities of completing baseline interviews and completing outcome interviews. We further stabilized the weights and applied them to all of the following statistical models. Of note, we did not use inverse probability weighting to account for death before 90 days or living in nursing homes since our target population was those who survived 90 days after stroke and who did not live in nursing homes before or after stroke. Details for the construction of the weights are provided in Data S1. Due to missing data on variables used for constructing the weights, participants with missing weights were excluded from our final analytic sample (Figure 1).

To understand how key groups of factors impacted the crude sex differences in outcomes and to facilitate the comparison of sex differences in nonadherence across different drug classes, we constructed the following models on the basis of the unadjusted modified Poisson model 1. Model 2 was model 1 plus all patient factors. Model 3 was model 1 plus all social factors. Model 4 was model 1 plus all health system–related factors. Model 5 was model 1 plus all lifestyle‐related factors. Model 6 was model 1 plus all health condition–related factors. Model 7 was model 1 plus all prestroke health–related factors. Model 8 was model 1 plus all stroke‐related factors. The adjusted PR of nonadherence comparing women with men was estimated in all 8 models.

To identify the possible confounding effect of each factor on sex differences in the medication nonadherence outcomes as shown in the directed acyclic graph in Figure S1, we estimated adjusted sex differences by separately adding each of the above‐mentioned individual factors to the unadjusted modified Poisson models. We then quantified the confounding effect of each factor by calculating the percentage change in the β estimate for sex (Δβsex) brought by the separate adjustment for the factor (βNEW) compared with the unadjusted model (β0), according to the formula 100*([β0]‐[βNEW])/(β0). 37 Potential confounders that changed the β estimates for sex by at least 10% (Δβsex ≥10% or ≤−10%) were identified as confounders of the sex and nonadherence association. 37 Of note, some factors may act as positive confounders (Δβsex≥10%), while others may be negative confounders (Δβsex≤−10%). 37 For example, if women were more likely to have a certain risk factor for nonadherence, then adjusting for the factor would attenuate the unadjusted β estimates for sex, and this factor is a positive confounder for the underlying true association between sex and nonadherence. Conversely, if women were more likely to have a certain protective factor for nonadherence, then adjusting for the factor would accentuate the unadjusted β estimates for sex, and this factor is a negative confounder. More details including other scenarios of positive and negative confounding are explained in Data S1 and Table S2. For ease of interpretation, both positive and negative confounders were labeled as contributors hereafter.

A final model was run adjusting for all contributors. Potential effect modifications on a multiplicative scale were evaluated by adding interactions between sex and race and ethnicity or sex and age separately into the final model. Stratified models by race and ethnicity were also conducted to generate ethnic‐specific sex differences in nonadherence.

The above analyses were performed for both overall nonadherence across all individual drug classes and for each drug class.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and all P values were 2‐sided tests. Given that one of the main goals of this study was to identify contributors to sex differences in poststroke medication nonadherence rather than hypothesis testing, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Participants and Prevalence of Nonadherence

Figure 1 shows that among 2796 patients with first‐ever ischemic stroke identified between 2008 and 2019 who survived 90 days after stroke, 2068 (74.0%) completed the baseline interview. A total of 1566 (75.7%) of the 2068 patients completed the outcome interview. After further excluding those who lived in nursing homes, were not prescribed any secondary stroke prevention drugs, and had missing inverse probability weights, a total of 1324 patients remained for the analysis of overall nonadherence. Further restricting participants to those who were prescribed antihypertensives, cholesterol‐lowering drugs, antiplatelets, and anticoagulants resulted in sample sizes of 1113, 1025, 1022, and 191 for each drug class, respectively (Figure 1).

Table 1 presents characteristics of patients in final analytic samples before imputation (mean age, 66.0 ± 11.7 years, 48.4% women, 58.0% MA individuals). Compared with men, women were more likely to be older and widowed; have higher social support; have public insurance; have routine physician access; have a more severe stroke; be obese; and have prestroke cognitive impairment or dementia, depression, or more severe disability, while they were less likely to have higher education, have private insurance, have coronary artery disease, smoke, or consume alcohol. Characteristics for patients who completed a baseline interview and outcome interview are shown in Tables S3 and S4. There were no sex differences in baseline interview participation, but women were more likely than men to participate in outcome interviews.

Sex Differences in Poststroke Medication Nonadherence

In unadjusted analysis, women were 1.27 to 1.51 times more likely than men to report nonadherence to secondary stroke prevention drugs at 90 days after a stroke, although none were statistically significant (Table 2). After separate adjustments for patient factors, social factors, and health system–related factors, the sex differences were accentuated, and women were significantly more likely than men to report overall nonadherence as well as nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs and antiplatelets. Adjustment for health condition–related factors, prestroke health status, and stroke‐related factors did not appreciably impact the sex‐nonadherence association for all outcomes. Although no significant sex differences in nonadherence to antihypertensives were found after any group adjustments, the crude estimate was substantially accentuated after adjustment for patient factors, social factors, and health system–related factors and considerably attenuated after adjustment for prestroke health status.

Individual Contributors to Sex Differences in Poststroke Medication Nonadherence

Identified individual contributors to sex differences in overall nonadherence (Figure 2 and Table S6) were age, marital status, social support, insurance type, routine physician access, smoking status, alcohol consumption, obesity, and coronary artery disease, where controlling for each of these factors one at a time changed the sex coefficient by at least 10%. Except for obesity and coronary artery disease, which attenuated the sex‐nonadherence association (ie, reducing the sex coefficient), adjustment for all other identified confounders accentuated the sex difference in nonadherence (ie, increasing the sex coefficient). The strongest 3 contributors on the basis of the magnitude of the change in sex coefficient were age, marital status, and insurance type, all 3 of which increased the sex disparity after adjustment.

The impacts of potential contributors on sex differences in nonadherence to each drug class were generally similar to the results of overall nonadherence (Figure 2 and Table S7). However, history of coronary artery disease did not contribute to the sex differences in nonadherence to individual drug classes. Prestroke cognition, disability, and depression status substantially attenuated the sex difference in nonadherence to antihypertensives but not to other drug classes (Figure 2).

In the final model adjusting for all identified contributors simultaneously (Table 2), significant sex differences were found for nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs (PR, 1.80 [95% CI, 1.14–2.84]) and antiplatelets (PR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.003–2.34]), with women more likely to report nonadherence. Female sex was not associated with nonadherence to antihypertensives (PR, 1.31 [95% CI, 0.82–2.09) and overall nonadherence (PR, 1.34 [95% CI, 0.95–1.90]).

Ethnic‐Specific Sex Differences in Poststroke Medication Nonadherence

The adjusted sex difference in overall nonadherence was statistically significant (PR, 1.59 [95% CI, 0.996–2.53]) in MA but not in NHW individuals, and no significant effect modification on the multiplicative scale was found (P interaction by race or ethnicity=0.62). The sex difference in nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs was modified by race and ethnicity on the multiplicative scale (P interaction by race or ethnicity=0.054) such that MA women were 3 times more likely to report nonadherence than MA men (PR, 3.00 [95% CI, 1.65–5.48]) as opposed to NHW individuals, among whom no significant sex difference was observed (PR, 1.30 [95% CI, 0.52–3.27]). There was no effect modification of the sex differences in nonadherence by race and ethnicity for antihypertensives and antiplatelets on the multiplicative scale (Figure S2). Effect modification by age on the multiplicative scale was also not significant for all sex‐nonadherence associations (Pinteraction by age>0.15).

Discussion

In this population‐based study of patients with stroke in a biethnic community in south Texas, after comprehensive adjustment for confounding and potential selection bias, women were more likely than men to report nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs and antiplatelets at 90 days after a stroke, but not for overall nonadherence and nonadherence to antihypertensives. Social factors, health system–related factors, and lifestyle‐factors but not health condition–, prestroke health– or stroke‐related factors were identified as contributors to sex disparities consistently across nonadherence outcomes. Specifically, obesity attenuated the disparities, while age, marital status, access to care, smoking, and alcohol consumption accentuated the disparities. Additionally, the sex difference in nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs was more pronounced in MA than NHW individuals on the multiplicative scale.

There are few US‐based studies for comparison with our findings. In the AVAIL registry, 6 sex differences in nonadherence to individual drug classes were not reported, and no association was found between sex and overall nonadherence at 12 months after hospital discharge from stroke, regardless of confounding adjustment. 6 Similarly, we did not find significant sex differences in overall nonadherence in the model without any confounder adjustment or in the model including all identified confounders simultaneously. However, our results suggest higher overall nonadherence among women at 90 days after a stroke after partial adjustments for groups of contributors, including demographics, social factors, and health system–related factors, consistent with the observed sex disparities in nonadherence to individual drug classes (except antihypertensives).

The findings for specific drug classes align with some of the studies in non–US‐based stroke populations 12 , 13 , 17 , 18 , 19 and large‐scale meta‐analyses on sex differences in adherence to statins and antihypertensives for primary or secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease. 24 , 38 These meta‐analyses found that nonadherence rates were higher in women than in men for statins but similar between sexes for antihypertensives. 24 , 38 Our findings suggest that the lack of sex differences in nonadherence to antihypertensives might be partially explained by the confounding effects of prestroke cognition, disability, and depression, as these factors contributed to the association between sex and nonadherence to antihypertensives but not to that of other drug classes. Although some other non–US‐based studies have reported either no sex difference in medication nonadherence or worse adherence among men, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 14 conflicting with our results, direct comparison of results from different studies can be difficult due to various measures and definitions of adherence, different follow‐up times after stroke, inconsistent adjustment for confounders, and the diverse patient samples from countries with different health care systems. Therefore, more specifically designed studies among US populations are needed to replicate our findings and understand the unique pattern of sex differences in nonadherence to different secondary stroke prevention drugs.

With respect to individual potential contributing factors, we found that lifestyle factors, access to care, and marital status influenced sex disparities in nonadherence. The sex disparities were reduced after adjusting for obesity, which was more prevalent among women, suggesting that interventions to improve poststroke medication adherence targeting women who are overweight and obese may be considered. In contrast, considering that smoking and moderate to heavy alcohol consumption were more prevalent while access to care was less prevalent among men, the accentuated sex disparities after adjusting for these factors suggest that targeted interventions among those who have unhealthy lifestyles and lack access to care may be particularly beneficial in men to improve poststroke medication adherence. In our study, no sex differences were found in being single or divorced/separated, but women were 3 times more likely to be widowed. Therefore, the accentuated sex disparities after adjusting for marital status suggest that widowhood status is beneficial for medication adherence, contrasting with the limited existing research among stroke survivors, which has found that being unmarried was either associated with worse adherence or not associated with it, compared with those currently married or cohabitating. 6 , 8 , 12 In this MA‐predominant population, it is possible that those who were widowed were more likely to have a nonspouse caregiver managing medications. In contrast, those who were married were more likely to manage medications on their own, and spousal caregivers provided additional assistance when needed. The relationship between marital status and poststroke medication adherence requires further study.

Our results of a stronger and significant association between sex and nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs in MA compared with NHW individuals suggest that the sex disparity in nonadherence in the overall study population is driven by the association among MA individuals. This further suggests the need to focus on MA individuals, especially MA women, to administer potential interventions to improve adherence to this drug class. Potential mechanisms of this effect modification include the intersection of race and ethnicity and sex on social determinants of health behaviors and postacute care, such as caregiving patterns and material resources. MA individuals, especially MA women, may bear more caregiving burden, 39 and thus may be less able to prioritize and manage their own health, contributing to a higher likelihood of medication nonadherence. Long‐term caregiving responsibility may also exert a negative influence on the mental health of MA women such that they may lack motivation to take medicines regularly. 40 Additionally, White men were most likely to have a caregiver presence after stroke, while women and race and ethnic minority individuals were much less likely to be living with their informal caregivers, 41 who may help them manage medications. It is also possible that MA women are more honest about their nonadherence behavior than other groups who may be inclined to underreport nonadherence. Finally, the specific adverse effects of statins, such as muscle pain and weakness, may also explain this effect modification unique to cholesterol‐lowering drugs. Statin toxicity and intolerance may differ by race and ethnicity with rare data specific for MA individuals, 42 while such an association has not been reported for antiplatelets.

The persistent disparities after accounting for multiple factors suggest that other potential contributing factors should be further investigated. For example, the impact of other dimensions of health care, such as quality of care and access to neurologists, should be considered. Psychological factors (eg, patients' concerns and perceived benefits in medications) and interpersonal factors (eg, patient–physician sex concordance) may also play a role in explaining sex differences in nonadherence. 43 , 44 Some studies have reported that women are less likely to have patient–provider sex concordance and that the concordance is associated with more preventative service use and better medication adherence. 45 , 46 However, we cannot test whether and how these factors contribute to sex differences in nonadherence due to a lack of data in the current study.

Our study has several limitations. First, medication nonadherence was self‐reported, which may be susceptible to underestimation due to social desirability bias and recall bias. However, self‐report of medication adherence has been shown to be a relatively reliable measurement in diverse populations including Hispanic populations 30 and agrees with pharmacy claims data. 47 Second, we did not collect data on reasons for missing medications from the patients' perspective, and the extent of nonadherence was not captured by the binary measure. Nevertheless, in our data set, there is no better way to capture the extent of nonadherence while ensuring sufficient statistical power. Third, our results of factors impacting sex differences in medication adherence depend on the statistical model. However, we used different confounding adjustment strategies, which suggested similar results. Fourth, the changes in regression coefficients after adjustment could also be due to mediation rather than confounding effects of the factors. However, we hypothesized factors adjusted for in our models were confounders rather than mediators as shown in our directed acyclic graph. Fourth, our results are susceptible to unmeasured confounding. For example, we lack information on some important social and behavioral factors, such as income and caregiver roles the patients play. Finally, our study was based on a single community in south Texas, where NHW and US‐born MA individuals are the 2 predominant race and ethnicity groups, limiting the generalizability of results to immigrant MA individuals or other Hispanic populations. Nonetheless, the diverse population‐based sample provided us a unique opportunity to examine the sex differences in nonadherence within the MA population.

Conclusions

In a biethnic population‐based stroke study, women were more likely than men to report nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs and antiplatelets at 90 days after a stroke, which was partially attributable to the higher prevalence of obesity among women, higher prevalence of alcohol consumption and smoking among men, lesser access to care among men at stroke onset, and the sex disparities in nonadherence in the MA population. Our findings suggest that MA women, women with obesity, men with unhealthy lifestyles, and men without access to care are potential subgroups of patients with stroke susceptible to nonadherence to medications after a stroke and therefore may be targeted in future interventions for improving adherence.
