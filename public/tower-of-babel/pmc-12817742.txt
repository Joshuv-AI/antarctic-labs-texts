How multiracial emerging adults experience discrimination: a qualitative study
Authors: Elizabeth M. Butler, Karina G. Blanco, Rachel E. Kantor, Stephanie A. Grilo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe number of multiracial people in the United States is increasing each year, yet there is a lack of research, particularly qualitative research, that sheds light on their distinctive lived experiences. Multiracial people have unique experiences compared to their monoracial peers because face multiple forms of discrimination: white supremacy-based racism, similar to that faced by non-white monoracial people, and unique forms such as identity invalidation. Previous research often frames a multiracial identity as strictly a source of challenges, which is a harmful stereotype promoting racism and overlooking the resilience, community strength, and other positive aspects that can accompany multiracial identities. This research is intended to explore the nuanced perspectives of multiracial individuals to better address and prevent the harmful impacts that racism has on mental and physical wellbeing. The purpose of this study is to explore how emerging adults who identify as multi-racial describe experiences with discrimination due to their multiracial identity.MethodsThis was a qualitative study conducted with emerging multiracial adults (aged 18–30 years) living in the United States. The data was collected through 21 virtual in-depth interviews using a semi-structured interview guide covering racial and ethnic identification, childhood and upbringing, family influence, peer engagement, health and wellbeing, experiences of discrimination, forming resilience, language, and demographics. The interviews were transcribed for thematic analysis.ResultsThematic analysis of transcripts explored the various forms of discrimination participants experienced and its impacts on their lives, health, and wellbeing. Participants described diverse experiences of discrimination, along with social and professional difficulties and benefits tied to their identity. Many also reported adverse mental and physical health effects resulting from racism, but also spoke about the resilience, skills, and sense of community they developed in response.ConclusionParticipants’ identities, experiences, and outcomes varied greatly, supporting the fact that multiracial people are not a monolithic group and that their identities carry unique benefits and challenges. The importance of understanding the lived experiences of multiracial people is becoming more essential as the number of people who identify as multiracial increases.

Background

The number of people who identified as two or more races on the United States Census rose from 9 million in 2010 to 33.8 million in 2020, and while this increase is partly due to Census question improvements, the multiracial population in the US is clearly on the rise [1]. Multiracial identity in the US has long been shaped by racist ideas of racial hierarchy, including the ‘one-drop rule,’ a law that deemed anyone with even 1% African ancestry as Black, and the broader ‘hypo-descent rule,’ which assigned multiracial people to subordinate status to uphold white supremacy [2]. These legacies continue to influence how race is defined and measured today. Multiracial people in particular are impacted by two levels of discrimination: the general, white supremacy-based racism, similar to what non-white monoracial people experience, along with more unique forms of discrimination, such as identity invalidation [3, 4]. Dr. McKenzie Strokes developed a groundbreaking multiracial Black socialization model that explores key aspects of how bi- and multiracial Black youth navigate societal labels. She emphasizes the significance of racial identification for multiracial individuals and examines how factors such as physical appearance and closeness to a particular parent shape their self-perception. These distinctions are imperative for their psychosocial well-being, with direct implications for health outcomes, such as depression, behavioral challenges, peer relationships, and self-esteem [5]. For multiracial women and gender-expansive people, these experiences are further shaped by gendered-racism, the combined impact of racism and sexism, which further uniquely affects their daily lives and health outcomes. This study focuses specifically on the experiences of multiracial women and gender-expansive emerging adults, whose perspectives remain underrepresented in the literature. According to a 2018 Franco and O’Brien study, “racial identity invalidation occurs when there is misalignment between an individual’s self-defined racial identity and the way that others perceive them within a particular context… Invalidation can manifest directly or indirectly when others passively misperceive or actively deny an individual’s self-defined race.” [6]. While there is an abundance of research about the detrimental impacts of experiencing discrimination on an individual, interpersonal, community and structural level, these ideas have not been as widely explored for multiracial people, particularly with qualitative data [4, 7–9].

In previous statistical analyses evaluating health, wellbeing and behavior patterns, multiracial people were often grouped together into one homogenous category. Quantitative methods such as household surveys, administrative forms, and records have often struggled to accurately capture complex demographic realities. Given the countless ways communities integrate and evolve, data collection categories for race and ethnicity must be consistently re-evaluated to better reflect diverse populations. Identities that challenge the binary are constantly overlooked, reinforcing exclusionary practices. Historically, compartmentalizing identities has been used to promote uniformity and comparability across indicators, enabling precise evaluations of social and health trends that inform sustainable interventions. However, failing to account for the lived experiences of multiracial individuals erases their visibility and limits their inclusion in meaningful solution-planning [10].

In quantitative analysis, it is common for White participants to serve as the de facto comparison group, reifying notions of Whiteness as the standard in the United States [11]. This practice is compounded by the fact that many datasets lack sufficient racial diversity, making it difficult to make meaningful statistical comparisons. Also, while previous research into outcomes for multiracial adolescents has shown that they are at higher risk for health and behavioral complications, as well as peer-influenced behavior problems compared to their monoracial peers, further analysis has shown that the observed differences could not be attributed solely to a multiracial identity [12, 13]. Other factors aside from racial identity, such as socioeconomic status, membership in a single-parent family and immigration status, have an impact on mental and physical outcomes for youth. Multiracial people have rich, varied identities and life experiences that are difficult to quantify and analyze, especially when the convention is to combine such a diverse group into one monolithic category. Qualitative data is able to add much-needed depth and specificity to the existing body of research about how discrimination and racism impact people who identify as multiracial. By analyzing the narratives of multiracial people, in their own words, we can deepen our understanding of myriad benefits and challenges associated with a multiracial identity.

This study uses qualitative methods to analyze transcripts of one on one interviews with 21 emerging adults who identify as multiracial. The data was gathered using an interview guide that asked participants about their racial and ethnic identification, childhood and upbringing, family influence, peer engagement, health and wellbeing, experiences of discrimination, forming resilience, language, and demographics. The overall goal of this study is to explore how emerging adults who identify as multi-racial describe experiences with discrimination due to their multiracial identity.

This study is extremely timely and necessary because it provides further insight into the unique life experiences of multiracial people and will allow for better understanding of the impact of discrimination at the individual, interpersonal, community and structural level.

Research question and hypothesis

In this study, we examine the layered and complex social realities that multiracial individuals navigate in an effort to widen our understanding of the nuances behind the discrimination that multiracial people face. We hypothesize that multiracial individuals experience distinct forms of discrimination shaped by intersecting factors, differing from those faced by monoracial individuals. Understanding these experiences is an imperative step toward developing interventions that address the complex needs of multiracial communities.

Study aim

The aim of this study was to investigate how emerging adults (aged 18–30 years) who identify as multi-racial describe experiences of discrimination due to their multi-racial identity.

Methods

This was a qualitative study conducted with emerging adults (aged 18–30 years) who identify as multiracial.

Study setting

The data used in this study was collected through 21 virtual in-depth interviews with each of the participants from June through August 2021. Participants were recruited through social media, namely Facebook and Instagram, using a digital flyer that was posted to different groups on the platforms. Snowball sampling was also utilized in recruitment, as participants were asked at the end of their in-depth interviews if they knew anyone who may be a good fit for the study. The in-depth interviews were conducted virtually on Zoom and led by one or two members of the research team. Before conducting each interview, researchers presented participants with a study information sheet and answered any participant questions. Then, participants were asked for verbal consent to participate in the study before beginning the in-depth interview. Written consent was waived so that participants names were not attached to interview transcripts. Participants were compensated for their participation in this study but were not compensated for referring individuals to the research team. This study design was approved by Columbia University’s Institutional Review Board.

The participants in this study (n = 21) all identify as multi-racial. English fluency was recruitment criteria for this study. More than half of the participants (61.9%) were in the 25–29 age group, with 28.6% of participants in the next-largest age group of 18–24. The vast majority of participants used she/her pronouns (85.7%), however 4.7% of participants used they/them pronouns, and 9.5% used both she/her and they/them pronouns.

Due to the nature of recruitment methods, the participant sample skewed heavily towards people with post-secondary degrees. Just under one-third of participants were pursuing or had completed a BA (28.6% total; 4.7% pursuing BA, 23.9% completed BA). Almost half of the participants were pursuing a post-secondary degree at the time of the study (42.9% total; 14.3% MA; 28.6% PhD) while almost a quarter of participants had completed a post-secondary degree (24.2% total; 19.5% MA; 4.7% PhD).

All participants currently reside throughout the United States of America. Additionally, all but two of the participants (n = 19) reported growing up in the United States of America. Participants were organized into one of seven geographic regions (Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, Pacific, Rocky Mountains, Southwest and Noncontiguous). At the time of the study, the majority of participants were living in the Midwest (38.1%). The Southeast was the second most populous category, with 28.6% of participants living there. No participants reported living in a Noncontiguous region of the United States.

Demographic information from Table 1 was collected by the Columbia University research team during the in-depth interview.

Data collection

The research team at Columbia University collected the data for this study. In-depth interviews were conducted using a semi-structured in-depth interview guide that was developed and edited by the research team. The in-depth interview guide prompted participants to discuss their racial and ethnic identification, childhood and upbringing, family influence, peer engagement, health and wellbeing, experiences of discrimination, forming resilience, language, and cs. While one section of the interview guide specifically asked about discrimination, participants discussed facing racism and discrimination throughout their lifecourse in all parts of the interview, as well as the impact those experiences had on them. After researchers went through the in-depth interview guide, participants were asked to add anything that they felt had not been discussed during the interview. All interviews were conducted in English and took place on HIPPA compliant Zoom meetings.

Data analysis

All interviews were recorded on Zoom and transcribed. The final transcripts were uploaded into Dedoose for analysis [14]. The research question guiding this study was developed by the lead author, as a secondary data analysis, after reviewing a sample of five transcripts. The researcher took margin notes during this sample review and used these notes to draft the initial research question. The research team discussed and gave feedback on the initial research question, which led to the development of the final research question: “How do emerging adults who identify as multi-racial describe experiences with discrimination due to their multiracial identity?”. Before creating a code book, all transcripts were read and annotated several times. After initial transcript readings, the preliminary codebook was created, using in vivo codes where necessary and applicable, applying inductive coding. The chunk coding method (coding both interviewer question and respondent response together) was used throughout data analysis to include as much context as possible in the coded excerpts. This coding method enhances contextual understanding and facilitates thematic analysis [15]. This preliminary codebook contained eight codes. After several meetings with the research team and continuing to code transcripts, the codebook expanded to 17 codes, preserving the use of in vivo codes when possible, to preserve participant voice. Throughout codebook development, the codebook was continuously reviewed and tested by the research team. The earlier iterations of the codebook were tested by the research team on different transcripts, and any instances of disagreement with code definition or application were discussed and resolved. Researcher bias was mitigated through the use of memos throughout the coding process and discussion during research team meetings.

The final codebook contained 19 codes, with codes falling into one of four categories: identity, impact on identity, discrimination experiences and impact of discrimination. This final codebook was applied to all 21 transcripts. The coding process stopped once saturation occurred, meaning no new ideas or themes were uncovered. A data display was created after coding concluded to highlight specific themes. The data display had two sections based on two of the four code categories: discrimination experiences and impact of discrimination. Three distinct excerpts from different transcripts were selected for each code in each category to illustrate the range of participant responses. Overall, the display was organized to depict the range of discriminatory experiences, as well as their impact on participants.

Reflexivity

In order to contextualize the knowledge generated throughout this research within the time, space and identities of the research team, the researchers engaged in reflexivity, or self-reflection, on their own intersectional identities and biases. Reflexivity in qualitative research is essential because the knowledge generated from data collection and data analysis is completely contingent upon researcher the specific perspectives of the research team [16]. The practice of reflexivity is crucial to naming and mitigating, as much as possible, the inherent subjective nature of qualitative research [17].

This data was collected by two graduate-level students who identify as female. The primary researcher who conducted the data analysis was a graduate-level student who identifies as a White female. As graduate students, the research team shared common experiences with the study sample, the majority of whom were also involved in higher education. However, the researchers’ racial and gender identities differed from those of many study participants. The data collection team conducted a reflexivity conversation where we discussed potential biases and strategies to mitigate those biases. Whenever possible two team members joined the interviews to monitor for bias during the interview process. For the analysis team, memos were used to unearth and discuss potential biases that appeared during the data collection and data analysis phases. Memos were used so that if a coder was coding and came across a section of text where they had a strong reaction or wanted a second opinion, they would leave that note so the coding application could be checked by a team member.

Results

The data analysis and this results section is structured to highlight both participants’ descriptions of discriminatory experiences they faced as a result of being multi-racial, as well as the impact that facing discrimination has had on their lives, health and wellbeing. Importantly, one goal of this analysis was to capture reports of discriminatory experiences that are outside the bounds of what is typically thought of as discriminatory. To do this, the entire in-depth interview was analyzed for discrimination experiences and their impact, not just the sections that specifically asked participants about facing discrimination.

Discrimination experiences

We identified six categories to capture the range of discriminatory experiences that participants faced: misidentified, racist talk, forms, healthcare discrimination, professional discrimination and academic discrimination.

Misidentified

Participants did not have uniform experiences when discussing instances of misidentification throughout their life courses. Several participants reported being racially misidentified by others, as well as questioned or even told that they are wrong about their own racial identity. When asked to discuss her experiences with being misidentified, one participant talked about one exchange when they were mistakenly identified as Hispanic. She said “…sometimes I’ll be like no, I’m Black and White, and they’ll be, your hair, your skin, it doesn’t make sense…” (011, she/her, 18–24, Pacific). At times, this back and forth dialogue persisted even after participants explicitly disclosed their identity.…He just said so seriously and matter-of-factly, “You’re not Black.” I laughed, ’cause I didn’t think he was serious, and I asked him if he’s serious. He goes, “Yeah, you’re not Black.” Then when people say that to me, then I say to them, “Well, I’m half-Korean and I’m half-African-American, so if you’re telling me I’m not Black, does that mean I’m not Korean, and does that mean I’m not anything? Misidentification was a widely reported experience by almost all participants in this study. The setting and circumstances of these experiences varied widely, from having a partner questioning their identity to colleagues inquiring at work to peers or even strangers making seemingly offhanded comments. The interpretation of and reaction to these experiences also varied widely. However, it is clear that the participants were consistently placed in a position to explain or defend their multi-racial identity to others.

Forms

While filling out forms may not typically be thought of as a discriminatory experience, the participants in this study explained how restrictive, frustrating and upsetting filling out forms as a multi-racial person can be. Overall, participants did not see their authentic identities represented in forms. The frustration of having to choose an option that does not accurately represent their racial and ethnic identity led to feelings of confusion and discontent. One participant describes her struggle with selecting race and ethnicity on a form, and how none of the options align with her identity.Usually, I struggle, because I’m like, well, if I’m White if I mark White, then they’re just gonna see me as White, and if I mark Hispanic heritage, I guess, technically that’s accurate, but then it’s also well, identifying as a Hispanic is very different than identifying as Latinx or Latina. That bothers me… It’s hard to get an answer racially how we’re descended, and on the Mexico side, so, yeah, I just struggle with just putting White. That doesn’t feel accurate. I usually just put other or multi-racial. (010, she/her, aged 25–29, Southeast)

Academic discrimination

This study sample skewed heavily toward people in pursuit of or in possession of advanced degrees, so many participants spoke about the discrimination they faced in an educational setting. Experiences of academic discrimination involved mistreatment by both peers and professors.

Several participants recounted racist comments they endured beginning at a very young age in school settings.There is a very particular instance. Discussion

This research explored the intersecting discriminatory experiences that multiracial emerging adults faced throughout their lives, as well as the impact those experiences have on their health, wellbeing, social and professional lives. There was a wide variety in the discriminatory experiences that participants faced, from outright racist interactions with peers, coworkers and health professionals, to more nuanced experiences with filling out forms that didn’t accurately reflect their identity. In terms of impact, participants explained the social and professional benefits and challenges that have resulted because of their identity, as well as the adverse mental and physical health impacts they have faced because of racism. When speaking about resilience, participants highlighted the skills they have had to learn in the face of racism, in addition to the power of community that they have found. Finally, it is important to highlight that the study sample interviewed for this research was skewed towards individuals who use she/her pronouns and who are currently or had previously studied at the graduate level. This is important to remember when contextualizing this research within the current body of literature about multiracial young adults. Overall, this research further supports the fact that multiracial people are not a monolithic group, and that their lived experiences are often distinct from those of both their monoracial peers as well as their multiracial peers.

This qualitative evaluation of multiracial individuals’ experiences with discrimination is a crucial contribution to the existing literature. Previous cross-sectional studies highlight that multiracial people navigate distinct and nuanced challenges compared to their monoracial counterparts. For instance, they are more likely to experience a sense of liminality- existing “in-between” racial identities- along with rejection from their own communities and a lack of shared identity with other multiracial individuals. Respondents described the paradox of not feeling fully represented within any demographic while simultaneously being privileged to be recognized in several racial categories.

Building on this foundation, our work expands on prior works by focusing on the distinct and often overlooked population of highly educated multiracial emerging adult women. This focus is significant because it highlights the ways structural and interpersonal discrimination intersect, revealing how race and gender jointly shape the experiences of multiracial women. In doing so, our study extends understandings of racism and gendered racism by examining how it is uniquely experienced within this group, capturing the complexities of identity, marginalization, and privilege that may be obscured in studies of monoracial or male populations. Additionally, recent studies have shown that multiracial students in higher education in the US had greater odds of certain mental health outcomes, such as loneliness, languishing, and perceived need for help [18]. This is not to say that non-White monoracial people cannot experience those same challenges, they certainly can and do. However, studies have indicated that multiracial people are more likely to experience them. Both monoracial and multiracial individuals experience discrimination, which accentuates a central implication of our findings- racial and ethnic discrimination remain deeply embedded in the fabric of the United States. Addressing this reality requires urgent and sustained efforts to implement anti-racist policies and practices [18].

Discrimination experiences

Participants candidly explained the diverse discrimination experiences they have faced throughout their lives. Instances of racist comments came from peers, coworkers and from those in positions of power. Many were subject to racist comments similar to what their non-White monoracial peers might experience. In these instances, they were singled out because others perceived them as non-White, based on of parts of their presenting identities. Participants also experienced racism as a direct result of their multiracial identity. Here, participants were seen as not fully belonging to one racial group, which led to others making racist comments about them.

Participants also experienced discrimination in all areas of life. In the healthcare setting, some participants were discriminated against by providers because they presented as non-White, and this impacted the kind of care they received. Others had experiences where their provider perceived their racial identity differently than they identified, which caused tension. These findings are in line with Snyder et al., who found that for multiracial individuals and their families, racial microaggressions negatively impact their care and communication with healthcare providers [19].

Professionally, participants reported discriminatory interactions with co-workers who said racist things or who questioned their identity and culture. Some participants also experienced being singled out as a result of their multiracial identity. They were subject to workplace questions and commentary about their identity because they did not fit neatly into someone else’s idea of race. Recent studies have found that individuals who report experiencing discrimination at work are at a higher risk for having hypertension and cardiovascular disease, which can significantly impair quality of life [20].

As previously mentioned, the participants in this sample skewed heavily to those involved in higher education. As a result, a majority of participants experienced discrimination in an academic setting. Some participants felt like higher education settings in general were discriminatory toward non-White students. Others recalled that professors give preferential treatment to White students, while excluding non-White students from the learning environment. Participants were also subject to racism and mistreatment at the hands of their peers in various academic settings. Discrimination in an academic setting, particularly for young people is linked to poor mental health and increased risk of suicide and substance use [21].

The wide variety of discrimination experiences discussed during this study show that the multiracial participants were subject to racism similar to what non-White peers might face, as well as racism particular to their multiracial status. These findings affirm earlier research that shows multiracial people experience multiple types of discrimination: discrimination as a result of their perceived non-White racial identity, as well as discrimination unique to multiracial people [3, 4].

In addition to more direct racism and discrimination, participants also had their identities invalidated by being misidentified, having their identities questioned and filling out forms that did not allow for authentic racial identification. A majority of participants experienced misidentification, although the circumstances of the experiences varied greatly. Even after explicitly clarifying their racial identity, some participants were questioned and accused of lying by other people. Participants reacted differently towards being misidentified; some were deeply offended, and others tried to understand or justify their perceived racial ambiguity. In short, being misidentified was a commonly reported experience, but how participants felt about the experience was not.

Additionally, filling out forms that did not allow for authentic racial identification was widely reported by almost all participants in the study. Participants especially struggled with forms that forced them to choose only one race, or that conflated racial identity and ethnicity incorrectly. These experiences were discouraging, frustrating and upsetting for participants, and some remarked how inaccurate data collection negatively impacts multiracial people. Participants’ discussion and feelings about filling out forms as a multiracial person in this study aligns with previous research about forms and identity invalidation. These findings support the conclusion that filling out forms that do not account for a multiracial identity is discriminatory and can lead to lowered motivation and self-esteem [22].

Impact of discrimination experiences

Just as participants’ lived experiences were unique, so were the impacts of the discrimination they faced. Professionally, participants described a range of impacts. Many participants explained that they felt singled out or called out at work because of their identity; some even reporting being pulled aside by coworkers to explain or defend their identity.

Overall, several participants reported negative physical and mental health impacts as a result of facing discrimination in a healthcare setting. Participants described being discriminated against by healthcare providers directly, and by the healthcare system as a whole. Both of these experiences led to poor health outcomes, as reported by participants during their interviews. Other participants reported depression and anxiety that resulted from being discriminated against in general, not just in a healthcare setting.

It is essential to reiterate that the term “multiracial”, as it is used in literature and throughout this research, is a complex term and those who identify as multiracial should not be treated as one monolithic group. As evidence of this, Miller et al. found that when comparing mental and self-reported health among multiracial young adults, those whose multiracial identities comprised of White-Nonwhite components reported worse mental and self-reported health than multiracial people with Nonwhite-Nonwhite multiracial identities [23]. This research bolsters the conclusions of Miller et al., as the impact of the variety of discriminatory experiences faced by participants were diverse and nuanced.

In this same vein, when investigating substance use among multiracial young adults, Atkin et al. found that multiracial young adults who reported more perceived racial ambiguity, and depression were more likely to binge drink, compared to multiracial young adults who reported less [24]. People with multiracial identities do not have the same lived experiences, and as a result, do not have the same behavioral and health outcomes.

The findings in this research support the already robust evidence that discrimination is linked to significant mental health outcomes, such as depression and anxiety, as well as physical health outcomes like self-reported poor health and cardiovascular disease [4, 7–9, 20, 21, 25]. Furthermore, this research contributes to the evidence that there are significant differences in reported mental and physical health outcomes within the larger multiracial category [18, 23, 24].

Social impact & resilience

While a large part of these results and other research outlines the challenges associated with a multiracial identity, this study also revealed various strengths and benefits that coexist alongside those difficulties. Socially, participants discussed how they learned to stand up for themselves and be resilient in the face of the racism they experience. This resilience could manifest as being more comfortable learning new things or demanding the respect they deserve from others. Some felt like they were more able to fit into different groups of people or were more motivated to seek out supportive communities of people who identify similarly to them. Resiliency among people who identify as multiracial has been previously documented and discussed, and these findings further expand upon the research that focuses not only on the risks associated with a multiracial identity, but also the strengths [4, 11]. It must be said that the formation of skills and strengths as a result of facing racism and discrimination does not justify or condone those acts and institutions. People and communities should not have to experience racism in order to develop resiliency. In the context of this study, some participants framed their identity and experiences positively, which is entirely their prerogative.

Implications for public health and education

The discriminatory and identity-invalidating experiences described by participants point to the urgent need for public health and educational systems to address the intersecting effects of racism and gendered racism on multiracial women and gender-expansive people. Institutions must implement comprehensive anti-racist and gender-inclusive policies, provide ongoing training to educators and providers, and create supportive environments that validate the complexity of multiracial identities. By prioritizing these changes, systems can begin to dismantle structural barriers contributing to disparities in mental and physical health.

Limitations

As previously mentioned, this study is over representative of emerging adults who are involved in post-secondary education at the master’s or PhD level. This is likely a result of the study’s sampling strategy that utilized specific social media groups to recruit participants. The sample is also almost entirely made up of participants who use she/her pronouns. While pronoun usage does not reflect gender identity, this study sample is intentionally centered on the perspectives of women and gender-expansive people, a group whose experiences of multiracial identity and gendered racism are significantly underexplored in the existing literature. Although some participants self-disclosed other important aspects of identity during their interviews, such as sexual orientation or gender identity, this was not officially collected by interviewers in the demographics section and therefore was not available for all participants and did not factor into our data analysis.

Regarding geography, the sample was under representative of people from the Rocky Mountain and Southwest region and did not include any participants from Alaska or Hawaii. All of these sample limitations can impact the data collected and narrow the scope of experiences that the participants reported.

Conclusion

The importance of unearthing and understanding the unique lived experiences of multiracial people in the United States is becoming more essential as the number of people who identify as multiracial continues to increase. Qualitative studies such as this are necessary to challenge the harmful narratives that that having a multiracial identity can only result in risks for a multiracial person. This study added to existing research that highlights both the benefits and strengths that can be derived from a multiracial identity. This study also dispels the belief that all multiracial people have similar experiences and a similar understanding of those experiences. In fact, this study repeatedly showed how multiracial emerging adults have vastly diverse experiences and perceive different impacts from those experiences in all areas of life, which adds to the body of research that asserts the multiracial individuals have unique lived experiences. The study results provide clear evidence that multiracial people should not be lumped together in one catch-all category in data collection or in life, because doing so results in false narratives and perceived commonalities that can have negative impacts on health and wellbeing. Furthermore, this false grouping of multiracial people into one monolithic category perpetuates racism and makes it harder to understand the complex needs of multiracial individuals and communities.

Future research should aim to sample a more diverse cross-section of multiracial individuals at all stages in life, so that we can continue to expand our understanding of the rich and varied lived experiences that multiracial people have. Broadening the scope of participants in future studies in terms of geography, age, gender identity and sexual orientation will unearth different and more nuanced narratives and experiences. Deepening our understanding in this way can help shape policies at all levels, from data collection procedures used by the Census to helping healthcare providers deliver more effective and appropriate care to their multiracial patients.

Additionally, it is essential that future research should investigate both the challenges and benefits associated with a multiracial identity. This research framing is necessary to counter the racist narrative that Whiteness is the ideal racial identity, and that all multiracial people are inherently at a deficit compared to their White peers.
