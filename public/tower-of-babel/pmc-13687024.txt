Lifestyle and BrainAGE in adult depression
Authors: Nicole Sanford, Yuetong Yu, Ruiyang Ge, Shalaila S. Haas, Liisa A. M. Galea, Sophia Frangou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates how heterogeneity in lifestyle and physical fitness shapes patterns of brain ageing in depression. The study sample included medically healthy adults from the UK Biobank (44–82 years) with a history of depression (n = 896) and non-psychiatric controls (n = 36,206). Heterogeneity through Discriminative Analysis (HYDRA) was used to derive data-driven subgroups within the depression sample based on 224 behavioural and physical health features. Global and voxel-based brain-age-gap-estimates (brainAGE) computed from structural neuroimaging data were used for personalized assessment of brain ageing. Four depression clusters were identified: the “balanced-active” cluster (n = 253) characterized by an active lifestyle and balanced diet; the “low metabolic risk-active” cluster (n = 178) characterized by a health-conscious dietary and exercise lifestyle; the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster (n = 315) who had higher body mass, poor diet, and sedentary behaviour; and the “frailty-moderately active” cluster (n = 150) who had a varied diet but coupled with physical frailty. Mood symptoms were lowest in the “balanced-active” cluster and highest in the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster. While depression overall was associated with minimally accelerated global and local brain ageing in fronto-limbic regions, the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster showed additional, regionally specific acceleration in prefrontal, parahippocampal, and thalamic areas. This work underscores the importance of lifestyle and fitness heterogeneity when examining associations between depression and neurobiological ageing and supports lifestyle-informed stratification as a pathway toward more targeted intervention strategies.

Introduction

Brain health is increasingly recognized as relevant to the treatment and prognosis of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) based on evidence from the brain-age-gap estimate (brainAGE), a robust and individualized measure of the biological ageing of the brain [1]. Patients with MDD typically show higher brainAGE than healthy individuals, with a mean increase of approximately one to two years, although there is substantial variability across studies and between individuals [2].

Older brainAGE in MDD has been linked to poor antidepressant response [3, 4], poor working memory [5], and greater symptomatic severity [6]. Higher brainAGE is not specific to MDD but is observed in multiple neurological and psychiatric conditions [7]. This observation suggests that higher brainAGE may partly reflect factors beyond disorder-specific mechanisms. Lifestyle factors are therefore plausible candidates, given their association with brain ageing in the general population.

Most research on lifestyle and brain ageing has been conducted in generally healthy populations. In these populations, lifestyle factors associated with older brainAGE in the general population [8], notably social support, sleep, physical exercise, and healthy dietary intake, are also associated with increased risk of MDD [9, 10]. By contrast, factors that promote brain health, such as physical activity and a healthy diet, may mitigate the risk of depression [11, 12]. Moreover, depression has been associated with systemic biological changes that overlap with pathways implicated in neural ageing, including cardiometabolic risk and metabolic and autonomic/HPA-axis dysregulation [13, 14]. In parallel, converging evidence implicates immune activation and inflammatory signalling in depression, and with alterations in neurotransmitter systems and neurocircuits that may also be relevant to variation in brain ageing observed in relation to lifestyle factors [15]. Therefore, the association between adverse lifestyle exposures and brain ageing may be more pronounced in individuals with a history of depression than in healthy individuals. To date, it remains unclear which lifestyle and fitness factors are most salient in the context of depression, and whether they map onto distinct patterns of brain ageing.

This study addresses further gaps in the depression-brain ageing literature by integrating behavioural heterogeneity with spatially resolved neurobiological ageing and leveraging the rich dataset of the UK Biobank (UKB) using neuroimaging data and detailed information on health and lifestyle [16]. The analytical design benefits from two innovative elements. First, we used Heterogeneity through Discriminative Analysis (HYDRA) [17] to derive data-driven lifestyle/fitness phenotypes of UKB participants with a history of depression, capturing co-occurring health-lifestyle factors and physical traits relating to general health and their relationships to socioeconomic status and mood symptoms; this approach was motivated by evidence that health-promoting and health-compromising behaviours tend to cluster within individuals rather than being evenly distributed across the population [18]. Second, we computed two indices of brainAGE, a global (G-brainAGE) measure of age-related brain changes [19], and local, voxel-level estimates (L-brainAGE) [20]. By moving beyond G-brainAGE we test whether depression-related brain ageing is spatially non-uniform, an approach that may be particularly informative given the modest and transdiagnostically shared nature of global brainAGE effects. Combining these two innovations allows a direct examination of whether distinct lifestyle/fitness profiles map onto differential regional brain ageing patterns, rather than assuming a single brain ageing signature across all individuals with depression. Our working hypotheses were that higher brainAGE would be most prominent in those subgroups of individuals with depression characterized by co-occurring unhealthy lifestyle and physical traits and that these same subgroups would show greater symptom severity, reflecting the association of these factors with both brain ageing and clinical outcomes.

Patients and methods

Sample

The UKB is a population-based study of over 500,000 self-referred individuals from the United Kingdom who were initially assessed at one of 22 centres in Scotland, England, and Wales between 2006 and 2010 [21]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain scans were collected at return visits along with detailed information on health and lifestyle. The UKB has ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (MREC) under reference number 16/NW/0274. All participants provided informed consent. Details on the UKB Ethics and Governance framework are available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/learn-more-about-uk-biobank/governance. The present analyses were conducted under Application Number 69022.

A total of 37,102 participants were selected for the current study after excluding participants who rated their overall health as “poor” (UKB field 2178), had a history of stroke (field 4056), had a history of any ICD-10 diagnoses of nervous system disorders at their first assessment (field 41270, ICD Chapter VI), or had neuroimaging data of poor quality and/or preprocessing errors (Supplemental Figure S1). After applying the above exclusions, we defined two subsamples: a depression group and a non-psychiatric comparison group. Participants with a history of at least one ICD-10 defined depressive episode (field 41270, ICD Chapter V, ICD codes F32-F33) comprised the depression group. We adopted this definition of MDD because it has been shown to effectively capture depression-related brain alterations in the UKB cohort [22]. Also, this definition was prioritized over questionnaire-derived measures as it identifies individuals with a documented clinical history of depressive episodes, thus reducing heterogeneity arising from subclinical or transient presentations. ICD-10 diagnostic codes also provided a consistent basis for inclusion and exclusion across the depression and comparison groups. Because primary care linkage data are not currently available for the UKB neuroimaging subsample, individuals whose mental health problems were managed exclusively in primary care may not have been identified. We allowed prior anxiety and substance use disorders in the depression group to preserve representativeness of the UKB depression sample. The most common lifetime comorbidities were anxiety disorders (27.57% of participants) and substance use disorders (9.38%). Across all other mental and behavioural disorders in the ICD-10, the percentage of individuals in the depression group with a lifetime history of at least one such disorder was 5.58%. Participants without a history of major psychiatric disorders as per their ICD-10 diagnoses (field 41270, ICD Chapter V, ICD codes F20-F39) formed the comparison group. The final study sample comprised 36,206 comparison individuals (18,974 females; mean age = 63.84 years, SD = 7.67) and 896 individuals with a history of MDD (591 females; mean age = 63.10 years, SD = 7.85) (details in Supplementary Table S1).

Neuroimaging

The whole-brain T1-weighted structural MRI scans of the UKB sample were acquired across three sites on the same scanner model (Siemens Skyra 3 T) running VD13A SP4, with a standard Siemens 32-channel RF head coil using a 3D MPRAGE sequence with 1 mm isotropic resolution and a 208 × 256 × 256 field-of-view (full standardized UKB acquisition protocols are detailed at https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/crystal/docs/brain_mri.pdf). The participants’ NIFTI images were downloaded from the UKB repository and processed locally at the Advanced Research Computing Services at the University of British Columbia. For computing G-brainAGE, the images were processed using standard pipelines applied in FreeSurfer 7.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/rel7downloads) to yield brain morphometric measures of regional cortical thickness and surface area and regional subcortical volumes. For computing L-brainAGE, the images were segmented into grey and white matter using SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The Diffeomorphic Anatomic Registration Through Exponentiated Lie algebra algorithm (DARTEL) [23] was applied to normalize the segmented scans into a standard MNI space (MNI-152 space), followed by resampling to 1.5 mm3 with a 4 mm smoothing kernel. No site correction was applied, consistent with our previous work demonstrating that site correction can reduce the accuracy of G-brainAGE models [19].

G-brainAGE was computed using pretrained sex-specific models developed by applying support vector regression (SVR) with a radial kernel function and 10-fold cross-validation to 150 regional measures of cortical thickness (n = 68) and surface area (n = 68) and subcortical volume (n = 14), extracted with the Freesurfer Suite from a pooled sample of 18,965 healthy individuals (age range: 40–90 years) [19]. Both the scripts and the models can be freely accessed at https://centilebrain.org/#/brainAge_global. We applied these pre-trained models to the brain morphometric data of the study to estimate each participant’s G-brainAGE, which was residualised for chronological age because brainAGE can show age-related bias due to regression to the mean [24] (Supplemental Figure S2).

L-brainAGE estimates were computed using voxel-based models pre-trained from an independent sample of 4155 healthy individuals (age range: 18–90 years) using a U-net architecture that incorporates sex as a model feature (https://github.com/SebastianPopescu/U-NET-for-LocalBrainAge-prediction) [20]. These pre-trained models were applied to the grey and white matter segments extracted from the brain scans of the current study sample. For efficient age bias adjustment, chronological age was included as a covariate in all downstream analyses of L-brainAGE.

Non-imaging measures

Initially, 113 variables (Supplemental Table S2), collected at the same assessment visit as the neuroimaging data, were selected based on prior literature on brain ageing pertaining to lifestyle (i.e., current diet, nutritional supplements, physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking history, pastimes, use of electronics and screentime, sleep quality, quality of relationships) and to physical health and fitness (e.g., BMI, grip strength) [8, 25]. Of these, only 55 variables were retained in the subsequent analyses; excluded variables had limited variability (i.e., >90% of the sample endorsed the same response) or high missingness (i.e., >10% missing values). In the retained 55 variables, missing values were imputed using Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations implemented with the “mice” R package (version 3.14.0) [26]. A total of 25 imputed datasets were generated using random forest models with 5 iterations and a fixed seed for reproducibility. For each participant and variable, the final imputed value was derived by taking the median (for continuous and binary variables) or the mode (for factors with more than two levels) across the 25 imputed datasets.

Statistical approach

The Heterogeneity through Discriminative Analysis (HYDRA; https://github.com/evarol/HYDRA) [17] algorithm, run in MATLAB R2019b [27], was used to identify clusters within the depression group, relative to the comparison group, based on these 55 variables. Variables with more than two response levels were recoded, resulting in a total of 224 input features (Supplemental Table S3). Sex and age were modelled as covariates. HYDRA is a semi-supervised machine learning tool that clusters cases (here the depression group) based on their differences from a reference sample (here the comparison group) by finding multiple linear hyperplanes, which together form a convex polytope, thus extending linear max-margin classifiers to the non-linear space. We used 5-fold cross-validation to determine the clustering stability for a cluster range of 2–4, balancing model complexity while maintaining sufficient sample sizes in each cluster. The solution with the highest adjusted Rand index (ARI) was chosen. ARI is a statistical measure that quantifies similarity between data clusters and is adjusted for the chance grouping of datapoints. In addition, an internal robustness check was carried out using a split-half analysis to test the stability of the cluster model in smaller samples (details in Section 6, Supplementary Material).

BrainAGE measures, socioeconomic status, and psychopathology were not used for clustering but to compare the resulting clusters (Supplemental Table S4). For all tests of group differences (case-control and between clusters), the statistical approach was selected based on variable type: t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, Mood’s median test for ordinal variables, and χ2 contingency tests for categorical variables. Welch’s test variants were used for continuous data that did not meet the assumption of homogeneity of variance, as indicated by adjusted degrees of freedom. Effect sizes of cluster differences were calculated for variables that were not used in cluster derivation to provide external characterization of the derived clusters. We report partial eta-squared for continuous variables and Cramér’s V for categorical variables.

Differences between non-psychiatric controls and depression clusters in L-brainAGE were assessed using general linear models in SPM12, with age as a covariate, assuming unequal variance between groups, and with a Family Wise Error corrected statistical threshold of PFWE < 0.05 and a minimum cluster size threshold of 400 voxels (1350 mm3) to enhance spatial specificity and reduce false positives. All significance tests were two-sided. Thereafter, the average L-brainAGE within each significant cluster was derived by applying a binary mask of the thresholded cluster to each participant’s L-brainAGE map and computing the mean value across all voxels within the mask. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between groups were subsequently performed using general linear models with age as a covariate.

Results

Depression clusters

HYDRA identified four clusters (ARI = 0.44; Supplemental Figure S3) within the depression group based on lifestyle factors and physical fitness. The cluster solutions and characteristics were similar within each of the split-half sample subsets (Supplementary Material, Section 6) and regardless of modelling sex as a covariate (Supplementary Material, Section 7). There were no cluster differences in age or sex (all Ps > 0.05). The differentiation of the clusters was driven by 39 features presented in Table 1 and Fig. 1. In addition, the clusters differed in mood-related symptoms, area deprivation, and education (Table 2, Fig. 2, and Supplemental Table S6). Cluster 1, “balanced-active” (n = 253; 38.74% male): This cluster included individuals with strong social support, regular physical activity, good overall fitness, moderate outdoor activities, minimal insomnia, a low proportion of smokers, and alcohol typically consumed with meals. Their diet was varied yet stable and their computer use was the lowest among clusters (Fig. 1, purple bars; Supplemental Figure S4).

Cluster 2, “low metabolic risk-active” (n = 178; 29.78% male): This cluster consisted of individuals who generally adhered to recommended healthy lifestyle guidelines. Their diets were characterized by low intake of meat and salt, and high consumption of fruits, vegetables, water, and dietary supplements (e.g., glucosamine and multivitamins). They also had the lowest proportion of current or former smokers, the least TV use, and the highest levels of outdoor exposure in both summer and winter (Fig. 1, blue bars; Supplemental Figure S4). Additionally, they were the most likely to have a university education (Fig. 2).

Cluster 3, “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster (n = 315; 34.60% male): This cluster was characterized by a diet high in red and processed meat and added salt, and low in fruit, vegetables, cereals, water, and dietary supplements and reported the highest rates of insomnia and highest BMI (Fig. 1, green bars). Members of this cluster had predominantly sedentary lifestyles, including infrequent physical exercise and social engagement, high screen time (computer and TV), and limited outdoor exposure, particularly in winter. They were more likely to live in socially deprived areas and had the lowest proportion of individuals with a university education (Fig. 2).

Cluster 4, “frailty-moderately active” cluster (n = 150; 30.00% male): Members of this cluster reported low levels of alcohol use and a varied diet with a predominance of dairy products. Individuals in this cluster also had suboptimal physical health characterized primarily by physical frailty inferred from their lower grip strength (Fig. 1, yellow bars).

Cluster differences in mood symptoms

Cluster differences in mood-related psychopathology are reported in Table 2, Supplemental Table S6, and Fig. 2. The “balanced-active” cluster had minimal psychopathology. The “low metabolic risk-active” cluster reported mostly moderate fed-up feelings. The “high metabolic risk-sedentary” and the “frailty-moderately active” clusters were comparable and had the highest levels of mood-related psychopathology.

Cluster differences in G-brainAGE and L-brainAGE

The G-brainAGE prediction model demonstrated good predictive performance, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 4.20 years. The mean G-brainAGE of the comparison group was near zero (mean = −0.01, SD = 4.11), confirming that this group did not show evidence of deviation from the typical pattern of age-related brain changes. Compared with the non-psychiatric group, the depression group showed a statistically significant [T(929.41) = 2.04, P = 0.042] but small elevation in mean G-brainAGE corresponding to an average difference of 0.32 years (approximately four calendar months). The G-brainAGE of the four depression clusters did not differ from each other (P > 0.05 for the main effect of group).

Compared to the non-psychiatric group, the whole depression group had significantly higher L-brainAGE in two voxel clusters (PFWE < 0.05; Fig. 3). The largest cluster was centred in the anterior cingulate and extended to several medial and subcallosal frontal regions. Within this cluster, mean L-brainAGE was 1.43 years higher in the depression group [F(1, 37,097) = 28.14, P < 0.001]. The second cluster was restricted to the right insula, where the mean L-brainAGE was 1.16 years higher in the depression group [F(1, 37,097) = 12.70, P < 0.001]. Discussion

The present study showed that individuals with a history of depression can be stratified into four lifestyle and fitness-based clusters: “balanced-active”, “low metabolic risk-active”, “high metabolic risk-sedentary”, and “frailty-moderately active”. Higher L-brainAGE and minimally higher G-brainAGE were associated with a history of depression irrespective of cluster membership, consistent with prior literature [2–7]. Critically, the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster showed additional evidence of higher L-brainAGE in the thalamus and frontotemporal regions. There were significant cluster differences in psychopathology favouring the “balanced-active” cluster, while the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” and “frailty-moderately active” had the highest levels of psychopathology across clusters.

Heterogeneity in lifestyle and fitness factors among adults with a history of depression

The study findings suggest that both positive and negative lifestyle choices and physical traits are not evenly distributed among individuals with a history of depression but tend to segregate into distinct clusters. The “balanced-active” and the “low metabolic risk-active” clusters included individuals that appeared to attend to general principles of healthy living. Members of both clusters had higher levels of education and lived in more prosperous areas. These findings reinforce the association between socioeconomic status and healthier living, which is not restricted to people with depression but is seen also in general population samples [28–30].

A novel finding is that people with a history of depression and lower socioeconomic status are not a monolithic group, as we identified two different clusters within this population. The “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster showed a constellation of cardiometabolic risk factors pertaining to poor diet and sedentary lifestyle which have previously been associated with lower socioeconomic circumstances [31, 32]. However, the “frailty-moderately active” cluster reported a varied diet but low physical activity which may be a consequence of suboptimal physical well-being.

Diet and mood symptoms among adults with a history of depression

We identified four distinct dietary patterns centred on (1) meat and fruit/vegetable intake, (2) whole grain consumption, (3) dairy product intake, and (4) use of nutritional supplements – each showing distinct associations with mood symptoms.

Meat and fruit/vegetables

The “balanced-active” cluster, which displayed the lowest levels of mood-related psychopathology, reported the highest consumption of salad and raw vegetables compared to the other clusters, aligning with existing evidence that a higher intake of raw fruits and vegetables is beneficial for mental health [33]. However, findings from the “low metabolic risk-active” cluster suggest a more nuanced picture. This cluster also reported elevated intake of fruits and vegetables but minimal meat consumption and exhibited moderate levels of mood-related psychopathology. It is plausible that low animal protein diet may be associated with depressive symptoms as indicated by another study conducted within the UKB which reported a higher prevalence of depression and lower dietary intakes of vitamins B6 and B12 among vegetarians compared to non-vegetarians [34]. On the other extreme, the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster, which was characterized by the highest level of mood-related symptoms, reported high consumption of red meat and processed meats coupled with low intake of fruits and vegetables, consistent with prior reports linking a high intake of red and processed meat to an increased risk of depression [35].

Whole grain foods

The “balanced-active” cluster consumed more cereals and wholegrain bread while the “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster had the lowest intake of cereal and a marked preference for white bread over any other type of bread, setting it apart from the other three clusters. It is unclear to what degree the higher severity of psychopathology in the latter cluster is specifically related to failure to benefit from consuming whole grains [36] or reflects cluster difference in overall dietary patterns.

Dairy products

The consumption of low-fat dairy [37] and fermented dairy foods like cheese and yogurt [38] has been linked to a reduced risk of depression. The “balanced-active” cluster preferred semi-skimmed milk over both skimmed and full-cream varieties, whereas the “frailty-moderately active” cluster showed the opposite pattern, favouring full-cream milk. The level of mood symptoms in these groups aligned with these dietary patterns, supporting the expected association.

Nutritional supplements

High levels of multivitamins/minerals supplement were reported in the “low metabolic risk-active” cluster which also showed moderate levels of psychopathology. By contrast, the lowest supplement utilization and lowest levels of symptoms were observed in the “balanced-active” cluster. The underlying motivations for supplement use in these clusters remain unclear and could reflect either lifestyle preferences or health concerns related to a low protein diet. Moreover, the relationship between supplement use and depression is inconclusive, suggesting that overall diet quality and broader lifestyle factors may play a more influential role than supplementation alone.

Contextualizing alcohol use among adults with a history of depression

The “balanced-active” cluster exhibited the highest intake of alcohol with meals and in the context of social activities involving visits to pubs. This suggests that the alcohol intake within this cluster may be tied to social interactions rather than excessive alcohol use. It is also plausible that the cluster’s other health-promoting behaviours, including physical exercise and healthy dietary choices, co-occur with a more favourable overall profile, potentially attenuating the association with alcohol intake. This underscores the intricate interplay of various dietary and lifestyle factors, highlighting how healthy practices can counterbalance less favourable ones.

Older brainAGE in adults with a history of depression

The depression group, irrespective of cluster membership, exhibited slightly older G-brainAGE and higher L-brainAGE in frontal areas that was especially pronounced in medial and ventral prefrontal cortex including the subcallosal gyrus. These findings are noteworthy given the involvement of these regions in the pathophysiology of depression, particularly through their role in regulating emotional behaviour through cortico-limbic networks [39–42]. The older L-brainAGE of the subcallosal gyrus is also interesting as this region has been identified as both a site of dysfunction [43, 44] and a promising therapeutic target [45–47].

The “high metabolic risk-sedentary” cluster exhibited higher L-brainAGE compared to the non-psychiatric group not only in prefrontal areas but also in the parahippocampal gyrus and thalamus. This pattern is consistent with prior research suggesting that these regions, particularly the hippocampal complex, show stronger associations with metabolic risk and physical inactivity [48–51].

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes any inference about causality or temporal ordering. The directionality of the relationships between lifestyle factors, brain ageing, and depressive symptoms cannot be established in the present study. Depression may be associated with sedentary behaviour and poor dietary habits that relate to older brain age, but accelerated regional brain ageing may also be associated with motivational and behavioural difficulties that coincide with less healthy lifestyle patterns. Longitudinal examination or Mendelian randomisation approaches would be needed to begin disentangling the directionality in future work. The list of lifestyle variables and physical health measures considered was extensive but not exhaustive. Lifestyle and dietary measures were self-reported and may be prone to recall and social desirability bias. Objective measures, including accelerometry and metabolic biomarkers available in UKB, were not examined here but could help validate the metabolic risk clusters in future studies. The subset of participants with a known history of depression, although substantial, may still have insufficient statistical power to fully disambiguate the heterogeneity of depression. In addition, as the UKB sample does not include adolescents and younger adults, the generalizability of the study findings to these age groups remains to be established. Finally, the majority of UKB participants identified as White, reflecting the population composition of the UK. Future research using samples from more diverse geographic, cultural, and racial backgrounds may offer additional insights and help capture a broader range of contributing factors.

Conclusions

This study advances a more nuanced understanding of the complex relationships between lifestyle, physical fitness factors, and brain ageing and psychopathology in individuals with a history of depression. The study provides novel evidence that lifestyle and physical fitness heterogeneity is meaningfully associated with neurobiological ageing in adults with a history of depression. By integrating data-driven behavioural stratification with both global and spatially resolved brainAGE metrics in a large, well-characterized population cohort, we demonstrate that depression-related brain ageing is not a unitary phenomenon. Instead, accelerated brain ageing is selectively expressed in a specific subgroup characterized by high metabolic risk and sedentary behaviour, with additional regional vulnerability in prefrontal, parahippocampal, and thalamic circuits beyond the modest fronto-limbic effects observed at the group level.

Methodologically, this work advances the field by combining HYDRA-based semi-supervised clustering with voxel-wise brain-age estimation, enabling the identification of biologically meaningful subtypes that would be obscured by conventional case-control or global brainAGE approaches. This multilevel framework moves beyond average effects to reveal how co-occurring lifestyle factors and physical traits interact with depression in ways associated with differential neurobiological ageing risk.

Clinically, the findings underscore the relevance of lifestyle-informed stratification for understanding heterogeneity in depression-related brain health. The selective association between adverse fitness and lifestyle profiles and regionally specific brain ageing suggests potentially modifiable targets worth evaluating in future intervention research, particularly in individuals with elevated cardiometabolic burden and low physical activity.

Together, these results support a shift toward more personalised models of depression that integrate behavioural, physical, and neurobiological dimensions to better inform risk assessment, monitoring, and tailored intervention strategies aimed at preserving brain health across the lifespan.
