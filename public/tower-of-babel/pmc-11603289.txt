Effect of multivalent ion doping on magnetic, electrical, and dielectric properties of nickel ferrite nanoparticles
Authors: Rutuja S Rajenimbalkar, Vidya J Deshmukh, K K Patankar, Sandeep B Somvanshi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nanoscale spinel structured ferrites co-doped with multi-valence ions are of great interest and proving to be promising for numerous applications. In light of this, herein we report tetravalent titanium ions (Ti4+) and divalent zinc ions (Zn2+) co-doped nickel ferrites with generic formula of NiFe2 − 2xTixZnxO4 (x = 0.00 ≤ x ≤ 0.20 in step of 0.05). The sol gel self combustion method in assistance with citric acid as fuel was employed to prepare the samples. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis with Rietveld refinement was performed to confirm the single-phase cubic spinel structure. The sphere type grain morphology of the samples was revealed by SEM images. The compositional studies carried out by EDAX showed desired formation of the composition with absence of impurities in the samples. The characteristics bands belonging to the spinel ferrites were appeared in IR spectra confirming the successive sample forming. The different surface parameters including surface area and pore volume was estimated using Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis. The low coercive nature with soft magnetism was observed with the co-doping as revealed by M-H plots. The electric and dielectric parameters showed decrementing behaviour with increment in Ti4+-Zn2+ co-doping. This study outcome signifies that the Ti4+-Zn2+ co-doping in nickel ferrites could be a prominent for many nanoelectronics applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81222-3.Keywords: Magnetic materials, Nickel ferrites, Ti-Zn co-doping, DCR, Dielectric parameterSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Materials science and nanotechnology have seen considerable advancements in recent years, leading to groundbreaking developments across various fields1–3. Advances in nanomaterials and hybrid materials have significantly impacted fields such as medicine, energy, electronics, and environmental science4–6. These innovations have created opportunities for the development of highly efficient, sustainable, and multifunctional materials7–9. The advancements in oxide materials are driving innovations across various industries, promising a future with smarter, more efficient technologies and solutions to complex challenges10–13. Advances in oxide-based nanoelectronics have led to the development of smaller, faster, and more energy-efficient electronic devices14–16. Advanced materials for nanoelectronics and other high-tech applications are playing a pivotal role in pushing the boundaries of technology, offering faster, smaller, more efficient, and flexible devices17–19. Advances in materials science are continually opening new possibilities in nanoelectronics and beyond, pushing the frontiers of what is possible in computation, energy storage, and healthcare20–22. Moreover, nanomaterials are also proving to be excellent candidate for catalytic and photocatalytic activities23–25. Magnetic alloys and compounds in hybrid form are promising for many applications due to their superior properties26–28. Researchers are exploring the concept of multifunctional magnetic materials that combine magnetic properties with other functionalities such as electrical conductivity, optical properties, or catalytic activity29–31. These materials could lead to new hybrid technologies and multifunctional devices32–34. The future of magnetic materials is promising, with ongoing research and development pushing boundaries in several key directions35–37. The future directions of magnetic materials involve enhancing their performance, expanding their functionalities, and exploring new applications across diverse fields, driven by advancements in material science, nanotechnology, and interdisciplinary research efforts38–40.

Ferrites are a fundamental type of magnetic materials with a broad variety of applicability and significant impact across multiple technological fields41–45. Their ability to be customized and their inherent properties make them invaluable in both current technologies and future developments46–48. Advances in ferrite materials have been significant across various fields, driven by the need for improved performance in electronics, magnetics, and other applications49,50. These advancements are helping to expand the range of applications for ferrite materials and improve their performance in existing uses, leading to innovations in electronics, communications, energy storage, and environmental management51. Spinel ferrites are indeed one of the most prominent types of ferrites and are widely recognized for their distinctive properties and versatile applications52. The spinel structure allows for a wide range of combinations of metal cations, including both transition metals and rare-earth elements, which can significantly influence the material’s properties. Spinel ferrite nanoparticles with combined magnetic, electric, and optical properties are being developed for advanced multifunctional applications, including sensor technologies and energy storage devices53.

Nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) is one of the most extensively studied spinel ferrites due to its significant magnetic properties and wide range of applications54. In the lattice structure of nickel ferrite, a large number of oxygen atoms are arranged in a face-centered cubic configuration, forming interpenetrating ‘tetrahedral (A-site)’ and ‘octahedral (B-site)’ positions. Only a fraction of these A-sites and B-sites are occupied by nickel and iron cations. This structure creates significant spacing between the constituent ions, making nickel ferrite a tunable magnetic system. When foreign metal elements are introduced as dopants, they can influence ion occupancy and interactions, thus modifying the overall magnetic properties55. Consequently, ion doping is a important method for enhancing the physicochemical properties of nickel ferrite. A variety of metal cations, including rare metals such as Ce³⁺, La³⁺, Nd³⁺, Y³⁺, and Ho³⁺, as well as common metals like Co²⁺, Mn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Sn⁴⁺, and Cr³⁺, have been used for doping to improve magnetic characteristics54,56,57. Recent research has also explored co-doping with two different metal elements, typically of the same valence—for instance, using two types of divalent metal ions to replace nickel ions, or two types of trivalent ions to replace iron ions56,58. Spinel ferrites can be prepared by employing different routes, which offers distinctive benefits and produces materials with specific properties59. The sol-gel auto combustion route offers significant benefits for synthesizing nanoscale spinel ferrites, including high purity, precise control over composition, low processing temperatures, and enhanced properties60. These advantages make it a preferred choice for researchers and industries seeking to produce high-quality ferrite materials for various applications.

Co-doped nickel ferrite nanoparticles are a promising area of research due to their enhanced and tunable properties, which can be tailored for a multiple applications. Shafiq et al.61 investigated the synthesis and photocatalytic performance of nickel ferrite nanoparticles co-doped with cobalt and holmium. Their findings revealed that these co-doped nanoparticles exhibit excellent photocatalytic activity, indicating their potential for environmental cleanup, particularly in treating wastewater from the textile industry. Ali et al.62 studied the magneto-structural properties of Zr4+-Co2+ co-doped nickel ferrite nanoparticles made via the microemulsion method. Their results suggest that the dielectric and magnetic properties of these nanoparticles hold promise for use in high-frequency devices and magnetic data storage. In a another study, Yan et al.63 explored how Ce-Zn co-doping affects the magnetic and microwave absorption properties of nanoscale nickel ferrites prepared by wet chemical route. Their analysis showed that co-doping significantly enhances microwave absorption properties. Hssaini et al.64 examined the magnetic properties of Gd-Er co-doped Co-Ni ferrite nanoparticles synthesized through a co-precipitation method, finding that these nanoparticles exhibit weak ferrimagnetic behavior at room temperature but show hard ferrimagnetic characteristics at low temperatures. However, there are still certain limitations to improving properties of the nickel ferrite through co-doping. Also, nickel ferrites co-doped with multivalent cations are not thoroughly reported in the literature. While individual doping with either Ti4+-Zn2+ has been previously studied in ferrites, the combination of both in a controlled stoichiometric ratio offers a synergistic effect that leads to distinctive changes in the material’s properties. Ti4+ ions have a larger ionic radius and a different oxidation state compared to Fe3+, which can induce lattice distortions, modify grain boundaries, and enhance electrical resistivity. Zn2+ions, on the other hand, are known for their influence on enhancing magnetic properties and dielectric behavior. Their substitution can help in stabilizing the crystal structure and improving the material’s magnetic softness, which is highly desirable for high-frequency applications. By combining these dopants in specific proportions, we achieve a balance of enhanced electrical resistivity, improved dielectric properties, and modified magnetic behavior. These are critical factors for optimizing performance in applications such as electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, high-frequency transformers, and magnetic storage devices.

Given this context, we present a study on nickel ferrite nanoparticles co-doped with divalent Zn2+ and tetravalent Ti4+ ions, resulting in a general composition of NiFe2 − 2xZnxTixO4, synthesized using the ‘sol-gel self combustion’ method. We employed various characterization techniques to systematically explore the ‘magnetic, electrical, and dielectric’ parameters of these nanoparticles. In our study, the novelty of our approach lies in the co-doping of NiFe2O4 nanoparticles with Ti4+-Zn2+, a combination that has not been widely explored in the context of nickel ferrite. Doping nickel ferrite with Ti and Zn significantly enhances its properties, making it more suitable for fundamental applications such as sensors, catalysis, and high-frequency devices. Ti4+ ions, being non-magnetic, reduce the crystallite size and improve dielectric properties by minimizing dielectric losses, which is beneficial for high-frequency applications. Zn2+ ions enhance the magnetic and electrical properties by influencing the cation distribution within the spinel structure, leading to increased electrical conductivity and optimized magnetic behavior. The synergistic effect of Ti and Zn co-doping enhances the overall performance of nickel ferrite, making it a promising material for advanced technological applications. This unique co-doping strategy brings significant modifications to the structural, electrical, and magnetic properties of the material, offering potential advantages for specialized applications, particularly in nanoelectronics. This research could serve as a valuable foundation for the practical application of nanoparticles.

Experimental

Various metal-ion sources in the form of nitrates, ‘titanium tetrabutoxide (Ti(OCH2CH2CH2CH3)4)’, citric acid [C6H8O7⋅H2O], ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH), acetone, and distilled water, were utilized as precursors. All the chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich and are of analytical grade with purity of 99.98%. The ‘sol-gel self combustion synthesis route was applied to obtain NiFe2 − 2xTixZnxO4 (x = 0.00 to 0.20 in steps of 0.05). The doping ratios of Ti and Zn in NiFe2 − 2xTixZnxO4 (x = 0.00 to 0.20) were carefully chosen to avoid the formation of secondary phases like TiO2, which could degrade the structural, electrical, and magnetic properties of the nanoparticles. Higher Ti doping can lead to phase segregation, negatively impacting conductivity and magnetization. By limiting x to 0.20, we ensure the preservation of the spinel structure and maximize the synergistic effects of co-doping while maintaining desirable material properties. The nitrates were first dissolved separately in distilled water followed by mixing. The values of molar concentration of all the precursor solution are provided in supporting information Table S1. Citric acid [C6H8O7⋅H2O] as fuel was added to the nitrate mixture in a 3:1 molar ratio. The pH was adjusted to neutral (~ 7) by incorporating ammonia. The temperature of the mixed solution was steadily raised from 60 °C to 90 °C and then to 120 °C. At first, a ‘sol’ was formed, which then transformed into a ‘gel’. After gel formation, the temperature was increased to 130 °C, causing the ‘gel’ to burn and convert into a fine loose powder. This loose powder was then grounded and annealed at 650 °C for 5 h to eradicate moistures and impurities. The resulting nanoparticles were characterized using different technique. ‘X-ray diffraction’ (XRD) (Rigaku, Ultima IV) was employed to study cubic spinel formation and nanoscale crystallinity. ‘Scanning electron microscopy’ (SEM) combined with ‘energy dispersive X-ray analysis’ (EDAX) (ZEISS, EVO LS15) and ‘transmission electron microscopy’ (TEM) (Tecnai, TF20) were employed to examine morphology and composition. ‘Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy’ (FT-IR) (Thermo Scientific, Nicolet iS10) provided infrared studies. The specific surface area was measured using ‘Brunauer-Emmett-Teller’ (BET) analysis (Microtrac, BELSORP-maxII). Magnetic properties were assessed with a ‘vibrating sample magnetometer’ (VSM) (Lakeshore, VSM 7407). DC resistivity was studied using a two-probe technique, and dielectric parameters were obtained with the help of LCR-Q meter.

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction study

Structure analysis was conducted using powder ‘X-ray diffraction’ (XRD). The XRD plots were refined through Rietveld analysis using the ‘FullProf’ software, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a-e). The superiority of the ‘refinement’ was evaluated based on the Rietveld values tabulated in Table 1. The ‘XRD patterns’ shown in Fig. 1(a-e) show peaks analogous to the reflection planes ‘(220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), (440), (620), and (533)’. The attendance of these peaks confirms the successful synthesis of a monophase ‘cubic spinel’ structure, with peak positions aligning well with reported data for NiFe2O465,66. The nonappearance of additional contamination peak point outs the pure phase formation of the samples. The Bragg angles in the XRD patterns shifted to lower angles due to the co-doping in NiFe2O4, reflecting their successful incorporation (Fig. 1(a-e)). When Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions replace Fe3+ ions in the NiFe2O4 lattice, lattice expansion occurs67. This effect is discussed in the literature for similar kind of materials68. Zn2+ (~ 0.74 Å) and Ti4+ (~ 0.68 Å) have larger ionic radii compared to Fe3+ (0.645 Å). This discrepancy leads to lattice distortion and an increment in ‘interplanar spacing’, which results in shift of XRD peaks to lower angle69. The incorporation of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion may also cause structure disorder and ‘microstrain’ in NiFe2O4 lattice, contributing to peak broadening and further shifts to low angles because of the increase in lattice defect and strains around doping ion70. Thus, the noted Bragg angle shifts in the XRD patterns of Zn2+-Ti4+ co-doped NiFe2O4 nanoparticles confirms the successful integration of these ions into the nickel ferrite structure.

Morphology and composition analysis

The morphology of ‘NiFe2 − 2xZnxTixO4 (x = 0.05 and 0.15)’ samples was examined using ‘scanning electron microscopy’ (SEM), as depicted in Fig. 2. The SEM images reveal a spherical-like morphology with nanosized grains. Additionally, some accumulation in the morphology is observed, likely due to interactions between the magnetic nanoparticles. The grain sizes as obtained by ‘linear intercept method’, ranges from 35 to 36 nm. These ‘nanometer-scale’ grain sizes affirm the nanocrystalline behaviour of the specimens, consistent with the structural (XRD) results. Elemental analysis of ‘NiFe2 − 2xZnxTixO4 (x = 0.05 and 0.15)’ samples was conducted using ‘energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy’ (EDAX), as depicted in Fig. 3. The EDAX spectra confirm the occurrence of ‘nickel’ (Ni), ‘iron’ (Fe), ‘oxygen’ (O), ‘titanium’ (Ti), and ‘zinc’ (Zn), with the ‘elemental percentages and weight and atomic percentages’ aligning well with the expected stoichiometry. No additional metal traces were detected, indicating the high purity of the samples. ‘Transmission electron microscopy’ (TEM) analysis of the same samples are given in Fig. 4. The ‘TEM images’ reveal sphere-shaped nanoparticles, with some agglomeration observed. This aggregation is likely due to the ‘magnetic interactions’ among the ions. The particle size, estimated from the TEM images ranges around 20 nm. This nanometric particle size further supports the nanocrystalline nature of the nanoparticles, consistent with structural (XRD) findings.

Infrared spectroscopy analysis

The ‘FT-IR’ spectra for all the samples in the range of 380 cm− 1 to 4000 cm− 1 is given in Fig. 5. The ‘FTIR spectra’ exhibit two distinct ‘absorption bands’ near 600 cm− 1 and 400 cm− 1, corresponding to the cubic ‘spinel structures’74. These ‘absorption bands’ are attributed to the ‘tetrahedral (A)’ and ‘octahedral [B]’ sites in the ‘spinel structure’75. The absorption frequencies ν₁ and ν₂ are detailed in Table 6. Table 6 indicates that ν₁ shifts to higher frequencies with increase in Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion. This shifting is due to the displacement of Fe3+ ion to the ‘oxygen’ ions at the ‘(A)’ site, which decrements the ‘Fe3+-O2−’ distance76. Similarly, ν₂ also shifts to higher frequencies. The ‘force constants’ Kt and K₀ were calculated by equations given in supporting information Table S2 (Eqs. 29–31) and are tabulated in Table 6. The force constants for the [B] site is found small in comparison to those for the (A) site. Force constant values for ‘octahedral and tetrahedral sites’ in materials, particularly in nickel ferrites, are essential for understanding a wide range of physical, chemical, and mechanical properties. These constants provide insights into the ‘stiffness and rigidity’ of the chemical bonds within the ‘crystal structure’, directly influencing the ‘vibrational frequencies’ of atoms and ions in the lattice. In spinel structures, which consist of both ‘tetrahedral (T) and octahedral (O) coordination sites’, the force constants are a measure of the energy required for atomic displacements or the propagation of phonons around these sites77. This interaction significantly affects ‘lattice dynamics’, thermal properties such as thermal conductivity, and thermal expansion behavior of the material, crucial for applications that involve heat management or thermal cycling. Furthermore, the force constants are closely related to the material’s mechanical properties, including hardness, elasticity, and brittleness. The higher the stiffness of the bonds at the ‘octahedral or tetrahedral sites’, the greater the material’s resistance to deformation, impacting its overall ‘mechanical strength and durability’. This, in turn, influences how well the material performs in structural applications, its resistance to wear, and its ability to withstand mechanical stress without breaking. Therefore, understanding the variations in force constants across these crystallographic sites is key to tailoring the performance and longevity of spinel-based materials for various technological uses78.

BET analysis

The ‘N2 adsorption-desorption’ technique was employed to record ‘isotherm curves’, from which different ‘surface properties’ are derived, as shown in Table 7. The ‘BET’ curves for the ‘typical specimens’ are illustrated in Fig. 6. Figure 6 indicates that all the ‘isotherms’ exhibit a ‘Langmuir type I-V isotherm’ with an ‘H3 hysteresis loop’79. The surface area values obtained from the BET analysis, listed in Table 7, range from 44.2 m²/g to 56.77 m²/g. Table 7 also shows that the ‘BET surface area’ values increment with the level of Zn2+-Ti4+ co-doping. The BET surface area of NiFe2O4 nanoparticles rises with co-doping, mainly because of alterations in structure that augment ‘surface defects, active sites, porosity, and dispersion properties’. These modifications expand the ‘accessible surface area’ for gas adsorption, leading to a higher ‘BET surface area’80. When Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions are co-doped into NiFe2O4, they can alter the crystal structure, which might involve ‘lattice expansion or contraction’ based on the relative ‘ionic radii’ of the dopant compared to the parent ions (Ni2+ and Fe3+). Co-doping of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion also establish ‘surface defects or active sites’ on the NiFe2O4 nanoparticles, which act as nucleation sites for nitrogen gas adsorption during BET analysis.

M-H hysteresis loop analysis

The ‘M-H hysteresis loops’ shown in Fig. 7 indicate that all samples exhibit soft magnetic properties. Table 8 presents various magnetic parameters, revealing that ‘saturation magnetization (MS)’ decreases with increase in Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion. The MS value ranges from 35.73 emu/g to 55.21 emu/g. The reduction in MS with higher concentrations of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions is accredited to the substitution of Fe3+ ion by Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion. This decrease in magnetization is also linked to the increased Zn2+-Ti4+ content, which alters the ‘exchange interactions’ between (A) and [B] site, either strengthen or weaken the ‘[B]-[B]’ and ‘(A)-[B]’ interaction81. The occurrence of non-magnetic ion lessens superexchange interaction, leading to lower magnetization82. Additionally, the coercivity decreases with increment in Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion, with the low coercivity recorded at 71.63Oe for x = 0.20 sample. The decrease in coercivity with increased Zn2+ and Ti4+ co-doping is due to several factors. Co-doping disrupts magnetic ordering and alignment within the NiFe2O4 lattice, weakening the alignment of ‘magnetic domains’ and the strength of ‘magnetic interactions’ that contribute to coercivity83. Consequently, the noted decrement in coercivity with higher Zn2+ and Ti4+ co-doping is a result of ‘magnetic dilution, disruption of magnetic ordering, structural modifications, and altered magnetic interactions’. The values of the ‘magneton number (nB)’ was estimated by the Eq. 32 in supporting file Table S2. The magneton numbers are listed in Table 8. These values exhibit a analogous trend to the saturation magnetization with increase in Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion in nickel ferrite. Table 8 also shows that remanence magnetization decreases with higher co-doping of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions. The ‘Mr/MS’ ratio values are noted to be below ~ 0.5, indicating that the samples have a multi-domain nature84.

Electrical properties

The electrical properties of nanomaterials are crucial in determining their behavior and suitability for a wide range of applications, particularly in electronics, energy, sensing, and nanotechnology85–87. At the nanoscale, materials often exhibit electrical properties that differ significantly from their bulk counterparts due to quantum effects, high surface area, and the confinement of electrons. These unique electrical characteristics make nanomaterials essential for developing advanced technologies like nanoelectronics, energy storage devices, and nanoscale sensors88–90. The ‘DC electrical resistivity’ of the present series of nanoparticles was measured to study the electrical properties. Figure 8 illustrates how electrical resistivity (ρ) varies with the co-doping of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions. The investigation into the DC electrical resistivity of nanoparticles, particularly in relation to the co-doping of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions, reveals significant insights into the electrical properties of these materials. It is clear from Fig. 8 that resistivity increases with higher levels of Zn2+ and Ti4+ co-doping. This increment in resistivity can be accredited to the reduced content of Fe3+ ion, which decreases the possibility of ‘electron hopping’ among ‘Fe3+ and Fe2+’ ions91. As Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions reinstate Fe3+ ions in the NiFe2O4 lattice, the concentration of Fe3+ ions decreases. Zn2+ ions, with a ‘small ionic radius’ (0.74Å) in comparison to Fe3+ (0.645Å), and Ti4+ ions (0.68 Å), though differing in charge, contribute to a reconfiguration of the crystal lattice, which may displace or substitute Fe3+ ions. This reconfiguration can lower the overall Fe3+ concentration in the materials. The introduction of ‘Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions’ alters the charge distribution and stability within the lattice, which can further decrease Fe3+ ion content as the material adjust to preserve charge balance. This suggest that their incorporation into the lattice alters the structural configuration, which can further diminish the concentration of Fe3+ ions as the lattice adjusts to maintain charge neutrality92. This phenomenon is consistent with findings in the literature, where the substitution of larger ions (such as Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions) for smaller Fe3+ ions leads to a decrease in the availability of charge carriers, thereby impeding electron hopping between Fe3+ and Fe2+’ ions93,94. Moreover, the presence of grain boundaries in nanocrystalline spinel ferrites plays a crucial role in influencing electrical resistivity. Grain boundaries act as barriers to electron flow, which is corroborated by studies indicating that smaller grain sizes result in increased resistivity due to the insulating nature of these boundaries95. The Verwey model, which describes conduction mechanisms in spinel ferrites, emphasizes the importance of electron hopping between ions of varying valences at the B sites, further supporting the notion that structural modifications due to co-doping can significantly affect electrical conduction pathways96. In addition to the structural changes induced by co-doping, factors such as porosity and stoichiometry are critical in determining the electrical properties of these materials.

DC resistivity plot of NiFe2 − 2xTixMnxO4 (x = 0.00, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20) nanoparticles.

The literature highlights that variation in resistivity parameters can lead to noteworthy change in the resistivity of nanocomposites, as seen in studies where the incorporation of nanoparticles enhances the electrical characteristics by increasing the number of free charge carriers97,98. This is particularly relevant in the context of the current study, where the interplay between doping levels and the resultant microstructural changes is pivotal in understanding the electrical behavior of the nanoparticles. In summary, the increase in electrical resistivity observed with higher levels of Zn2+ and Ti4+ co-doping can be accredited to the reduced concentration of Fe3+ ions, structural reconfigurations within the lattice, and the influence of grain boundaries. These findings are well-supported by existing literature, which underscores the complex relationship among doping, structural characteristics, and electrical parameters in nanostructured material.

Dielectric studies

The ‘dielectric properties’ was analyzed by measuring the ‘dielectric constant (ε′)’, ‘dielectric loss (ε′′)’, and ‘dielectric loss tangent (tan δ)’ across varying frequencies at 300 K. Figure 9 illustrates how the ‘dielectric constant (ε′)’ changes with frequency. The data displays that ε′ decreases as frequency increments, eventually leveling off to a frequency-independent value. The observed decrease in ε′ with increase in frequency, which eventually stabilizes to a frequency-independent value, is consistent with the behavior noted in various studies on dielectric materials. This phenomenon can be accredited to the interplay between ‘space-charge polarization’ at lower frequencies and ‘electronic polarization’ at high frequencies, as supported by the literature99–101. Specifically, the presence of ‘well-conducting grains’ separated by ‘poorly conducting grain boundaries’ leads to this frequency-dependent behavior, where space-charge polarization dominates at low frequencies and electronic polarization becomes more significant as frequency increases100,102. Figure 10 shows the varying nature of ‘dielectric loss (ε′′)’ with frequency, demonstrating an exponential decrease. The ‘exponential decrease’ in ‘dielectric loss (ε′′)’ with frequency, as illustrated in the current study, is also corroborated by previous research. For instance, Dong et al. reported similar trends in their studies on co-doped TiO2 ceramics, where the dielectric loss decreased significantly with frequency, indicating improved dielectric performance at higher frequencies103,104. The relationship between dielectric loss and frequency is often explained by ‘Maxwell-Wagner interfacial polarization’, which is influenced by structural homogeneity and carrier concentration105,106. The current findings that increased Zn²⁺ and Ti⁴⁺ co-doping leads to a reduction in dielectric parameters align with the observations of other researchers who noted that co-doping can disrupt lattice regularity, thereby diminishing the material’s ability to polarize under an electric field107,108.

The ‘dielectric loss tangent (tan δ)’, determined from ε′ and ε′′, is shown in Fig. 11. It also exhibits an exponential decrease with incrementing frequency. The noted nature of ‘tan δ’ can be clarified by ‘Maxwell-Wagner interfacial polarization’. Factors such as structural homogeneity and carrier concentration influence tan δ. The ‘dielectric parameters’ decreased with increment in Zn2+ and Ti4+ ion. This trend is linked to alterations in the ‘crystal structure and lattice parameters’ caused by co-doping. Increased doping concentrations can disrupt the regularity of the lattice, lowering the material’s ability to polarize in an electric field and thus lowering the dielectric constant109. Moreover, the decrease in tan δ with increasing frequency, as reported in this study, reflects energy dissipation mechanisms that are influenced by various factors, including defect concentrations and interfacial polarization effects106,110. The literature supports this notion, indicating that co-doping can modify these loss mechanisms, leading to lower tan δ values, which is critical for applications requiring low energy dissipation101,111. For instance, studies have shown that co-doping strategies can enhance dielectric properties by optimizing the balance between permittivity and dielectric loss, thus improving overall performance111,112. In summary, the dielectric properties of the nanoparticles under investigation exhibit trends that are well-documented in the literature. The decrease in ε′ and ε′′, along with the behavior of tan δ, can be accredited to the structural characteristics of the materials and the effects of co-doping. These findings not only reinforce the understanding of dielectric behavior in ferrite materials but also highlight the importance of optimizing doping strategies to achieve desired electrical properties for various applications. Conclusions

The study investigated the synthesis NiFe2O4 co-doped with Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions, using compositions NiFe2 − 2xZnxTixO4 (x = 0.00 to 0.20 in steps of 0.05) via the ‘sol-gel self combustion’ method. XRD studies confirmed pure cubic spinel structure, with ‘lattice constants’ increasing from 8.327 Å to 8.367 Å due to the varying ionic radii of Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions. The ‘structural parameters’ were notably influenced by the level of increase in Zn2+ and Ti4+ ions. SEM images showed sphere shape grains with some agglomeration, characteristic of nanosize particles (35 –36 nm). Magnetic measurements revealed a decrease in magnetization from 55.21 to 35.73emu/g and a drop in the magneton number from 2.32µB to 1.50µB with increased Zn2+-Ti4+ co-doping, indicating changes in magnetic properties. ‘DC electrical resistivity’ rose with high co-doping concentration, while ‘dielectric properties’ showed decrementing behaviour with increase in frequency at room temperature. In conclusion, the present study on Ti4+-Zn2+ co-doped nickel ferrites opens several avenues for future research. Further exploration of doping concentrations and synthesis parameters could enable precise tuning of magnetic, electrical, and dielectric properties for applications in high-frequency devices, sensors, and EMI shielding. Additionally, scalability for industrial applications, especially in electronics and energy storage systems, should be investigated. The multifunctional nature of these ferrites also presents potential for use in advanced technologies such as spintronics, supercapacitors, and environmentally stable energy-harvesting devices, offering exciting prospects for future development.
