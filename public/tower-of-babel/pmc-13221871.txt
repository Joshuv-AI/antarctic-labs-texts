Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and health care use. Hospital readmissions after HF hospitalization remain common, with nearly half of patients readmitted within 6 months, contributing significantly to health care costs and patient burden.1,2 In the United States, HF-related expenditures exceed $30 billion annually and are projected to rise substantially over the next decade.2

Guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) markedly improves outcomes in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Contemporary guidelines emphasize early initiation and continuation of foundational therapies, including RAS (renin-angiotensin system) inhibition, evidence-based beta-blockers, MRAs (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists), and SGLT2i (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) inhibitors.3 However, real-world uptake remains inconsistent, with fewer than one-quarter of patients achieving target doses after hospitalization.4 Medication nonadherence further compounds this gap and is associated with increased readmissions and mortality.5

Evidence increasingly suggests that discharge represents a critical opportunity to influence early outcomes. Initiation of GDMT prior to discharge has an association with reduced 30-day readmissions, and each additional GDMT class prescribed confers incremental benefit.6,7 Nonetheless, clinical barriers such as hypotension, renal dysfunction, and competing comorbidities often limit optimization at discharge.

At our 489-bed community teaching hospital, the 30-day HF readmission rate was 23.0% in 2024. To address this gap, we implemented a resident-led quality improvement (QI) initiative aimed at strengthening the discharge processes through structured patient education and systematic GDMT review.

Case Summary Prompting the Project Launch

A representative case involved an older adult admitted with acute decompensated HF who was readmitted within 2 weeks of discharge for recurrent volume overload. Review of the index hospitalization revealed limited patient understanding of symptom monitoring, uncertainty regarding medication purpose, and clinical considerations that limited initiation of guideline-recommended therapies. This case highlighted the challenges and informed the development of a standardized, resident-led QI initiative focused on discharge-level processes that are feasible to address during hospitalization.

Project Rationale

Prior to implementation of the resident-led QI initiative, patients hospitalized with HF underwent the standardized discharge workflow used for all admissions. This included provision of a generalized discharge summary outlining diagnoses, medications, and follow-up appointments when appropriate.

However, HF-specific education and formalized teach-back processes to assess patient understanding was not consistently delivered in a structured format. Medication reconciliation occurred as part of routine procedures, however there was no dedicated review of GDMT optimization or systematic documentation of contraindications.

HF readmissions arise from a convergence of clinical, social, and systems-level factors. While not all drivers are modifiable during hospitalization, discharge remains a critical transition point where patient understanding, medication reconciliation, and identification of therapeutic barriers can be addressed. Residents are well positioned to deliver structured education, review medications, and escalate concerns to supervising clinicians, offering a low-cost and reasonably scalable strategy to improve care quality.

Project Description

Design and setting

This single-center, resident-led quality improvement initiative was conducted at a 489-bed community teaching hospital between September 2024 and March 2025 (212 days). The project followed a pragmatic plan-do-study-act framework targeting 2 discharge-level processes considered feasible to modify during hospitalization: 1) standardized patient education using teach-back methodology and 2) systematic review of GDMT eligibility and barriers in patients with HFrEF.

At our institution, patients with HF may be admitted to several inpatient services, including internal medicine resident teams as well as nonresident hospitalist teams composed primarily of private attending physicians. The initiative was implemented within the internal medicine graduate medical education service, where residents are responsible for inpatient care and discharge planning under supervision of the attending physician. Based on institutional admission patterns, approximately one-quarter of general medical admissions are managed by resident teams.

Patients were identified prospectively from resident services during hospitalization. All adult patients with a clinical diagnosis of HF who were admitted to resident-covered teams and were nearing discharge during the intervention period were eligible for inclusion. Residents approached eligible patients prior to discharge to deliver standardized HF education using American Heart Association discharge materials (Supplemental Figure 1). Patients were included in the QI dataset if the education encounter was completed and documented in the electronic medical record, allowing tracking of readmission outcomes.

Owing to staffing and scheduling constraints, not all eligible HF admissions received the intervention. Patients were excluded if discharge occurred before education could be completed or if documentation required for outcome tracking was unavailable. The analysis therefore reflects a consecutive cohort of resident-covered patients receiving the intervention as part of routine inpatient care.

At discharge, patients were advised to follow up with their primary care physician and/or cardiologist within 1 to 2 weeks. The timing and structure of follow-up visits, including medication adjustments and GDMT titration, were determined by outpatient providers and were not standardized as part of this initiative. The intervention focused specifically on inpatient discharge processes and did not include a protocolized postdischarge management pathway.

Intervention: standardized discharge education

Residents delivered structured HF discharge education using American Heart Association materials within 48 hours of discharge. Education was focused on symptom recognition, daily weight monitoring, dietary and volume management, medication adherence, and outpatient follow-up. Teach-back consisted of asking patients to restate concepts in their own words, providing clarification as needed. Teach-back was selected based on evidence demonstrating improved comprehension and reduced readmissions across HF populations.1,8, 9, 10

Medication reconciliation and GDMT review

During the education encounter, residents reviewed discharge medications and assessed GDMT eligibility for patients with HFrEF according to contemporary guidelines.3 When GDMT was not prescribed, residents documented barriers such as hypotension or acute kidney injury after confirmation with the supervising attending physician. The intervention did not mandate medication changes or override attending physician decision-making.

Workflow and documentation

Residents educated HF patients on their assigned service. To expand coverage, designated project residents completed additional education encounters weekly. Education delivery and teach-back completion were documented using a standardized template within the electronic medical record.

Project Deliverables

Deliverables included standardized HF education using validated materials, teach-back encounters, systematic identification of GDMT barriers, and communication with supervising clinicians regarding potential opportunities for therapy optimization. These tools were designed for integration into inpatient workflows without additional staffing requirements.

Project Outcome, Impact, and Future Directions

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause readmission. A total of 60 patients were included. During the implementation period, the observed 30-day readmission rate among patients receiving resident-led education was 11.7%, compared with a hospital-wide baseline rate of 23.0% (Figure 1). Seven patients were admitted within 30 days, including 5 with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and 2 with HFrEF. The intervention cohort included patients with HFpEF (40.0%), HFrEF (43.3%), and HF with moderately reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF) (16.7%) (Figure 2). Among patients with HFrEF, 46.2% were discharged on GDMT, while 53.8% were not (Figure 3). Hypotension and acute kidney injury were the most commonly documented contraindications, though additional barriers included financial limitations, atrioventricular block, provider hesitancy, anticipated medication intolerance, polypharmacy, fragmented outpatient follow-up, and clinical inertia. Nonmedical factors such as medication or dietary nonadherence and intercurrent illness were also observed.

Discussion

This resident-led QI initiative demonstrates the feasibility of implementing protocolized education involving GDMT for HF patients at discharge. Among patients in a resident-covered cohort, the initiative was associated with lower observed 30-day readmissions. Notably, these observations occurred despite persistently low rates of discharge on full GDMT (Figures 4 and 5). This further emphasizes the multifactorial nature of HF readmissions and the importance of the nonpharmacologic interventions. While GDMT remains foundational to HF management, real-world barriers frequently limit optimization at discharge.4, 5, 6, 7 Interventions focused exclusively on medication initiation may therefore be insufficient. Structured patient education using teach-back addresses modifiable behavioral and systems-level contributors to readmission and has demonstrated benefit across multiple studies.1,8, 9, 10 Our findings suggest that pairing education with identification of GDMT barriers may enhance transitional care without substantial workflow burden.

Additionally, this initiative provided residents with structured exposure to guideline-based decision-making and systems-level care delivery, reinforcing core competencies in transitional care and implementation science.

Limitations

This project has limitations inherent to its pragmatic design. It was conducted at a single center within a resident-covered cohort, limiting generalizability. The absence of a contemporaneous control group precludes causal inference, and unmeasured confounders may have contributed to observed outcomes. Patient understanding was assessed qualitatively using teach-back, without formal competency testing. Finally, outcomes were limited to 30-day readmissions, and longer term effects were not assessed.

Among the 60 patients included, 7 were readmitted within 30 days. Although GDMT underuse was observed in the HFrEF population overall, the small number of readmissions limits the ability to draw a definitive association between GDMT status and readmission risk. Chart review suggested that the predominant contributing factors to readmission were medication and dietary nonadherence as well as intercurrent illness, rather than clear failure of GDMT optimization alone.

Given the modest sample size, subgroup analysis stratifying readmission risk by GDMT status was limited in statistical power. A larger cohort would be necessary to better characterize whether incomplete GDMT independently predicts early readmission. Future iterations of this project should incorporate expanded sample size and more in-depth subgroup analysis to further define these relationships.

Conclusions

A resident-led quality improvement initiative focused on standardized discharge education and GDMT review was associated with lower observed 30-day HF readmissions. Leveraging residents as an embedded workforce may represent a scalable, low-resource strategy that may be adaptable to other community hospitals and training programs.
