Functional data analysis of ground reaction forces combined with clinical measures for early knee osteoarthritis detection
Authors: Elisa Aragón-Basanta, Alvaro Page, Guillermo Ayala, Enrique Viosca-Herrero, Ana Alabajos-Cea, Luz Herrero-Manley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly detection of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is essential to improve treatment outcomes and reduce its long-term impact. However, early diagnosis of KOA (EKOA) remains difficult due to the absence of standardised criteria and the subtle or intermittent nature of early symptoms. This study evaluates the utility of Functional Logistic Regression (FLR) as a robust and efficient classifier that integrates clinical data and biomechanical signals–specifically, ground reaction force (GRF) curves represented through Functional Data Analysis—to support EKOA diagnosis. Fifty-six individuals with knee pain were assessed; 25 were diagnosed with EKOA based on Mahmoudian’s clinical criteria, and 31 were classified as healthy. Anthropometric data and pain scores (Visual Analogue Scale, VAS) were collected, and GRF signals in three directions (vertical, anteroposterior, and mediolateral) were recorded during walking trials. A FLR model was developed by combining GRF curves, anthropometric measurements, and VAS scores. The mediolateral GRF component showed the strongest discriminative power. The model that combined biomechanical and clinical variables outperformed models using either data source alone, achieving higher accuracy and sensitivity while maintaining parsimony and robustness. Functional Logistic Regression offers key advantages over classical logistic models based on scalar features and black-box machine learning approaches: it allows the direct use of biomechanical signals without prior discretisation and reduces the risk of overfitting while preserving statistical rigour. These results support FLR as a robust and efficient method for early KOA classification based on combined clinical and biomechanical data.Keywords: Early knee osteoarthritis (EKOA), Functional Data Analysis (FDA), Functional logistic regressionSubject terms: Biomedical engineering, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a fundamental medical and social concern, as it is a leading cause of pain and disability in older adults. Its prevalence continues to increase due to an ageing population and rising obesity rates1. Early detection is critical to prevent irreversible structural damage and improve the efficacy of therapeutic interventions2,3. Therefore, there is growing recognition of early knee osteoarthritis (EKOA) as a distinct clinical and research category, defined by subtle symptoms and minimal radiographic changes4,5. However, early diagnosis remains difficult due to the absence of consensus criteria and the intermittent and nonspecific nature of early symptoms6,7. Standard methods, such as the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) classification system, lack the sensitivity to detect early-stage changes7. As a result, complementary tools are being explored, including functional and biomechanical assessments that can reveal joint dysfunction before radiographic damage is evident8,9. Biomechanical gait analysis provides information on joint loading and motor control during movement and is helpful in joint gait research9–11. Kinematic and kinetic variables, such as ground reaction forces (GRF) and knee external adduction moment (KAM), are frequently studied. KAM, in particular, is associated with medial compartment loading and disease severity, although its relationship with pain remains controversial11. However, its estimation requires motion capture and inverse dynamics, making it expensive, technically complex, and highly sensitive to methodological variability12. This complexity has limited the application of KAM-based models from research to clinical practice.

Ground reaction forces (GRFs) have emerged as a simpler alternative to the knee adduction moment (KAM) to overcome these limitations. GRFs are easier to acquire, require less preprocessing, and have demonstrated sensitivity to gait abnormalities associated with knee osteoarthritis (KOA)13–17. Although less specific than KAM in capturing internal joint loading, GRFs reflect functional adaptations and are more compatible with routine clinical assessments.

Despite their potential, biomechanical features such as KAM or GRFs are often used in isolation. Most studies overlook complementary clinical variables, such as pain intensity, which are highly relevant for diagnosis and decision-making. This fragmented approach may reduce biomechanical models’ interpretability and clinical utility18.

Regardless of the specific features used, developing a diagnostic aid requires an underlying classification system. Logistic regression (LR) is a classical statistical method applied to KOA but remains underutilised19,20. It offers robustness and interpretability, but requires motion data to be reduced to scalar features, which can lead to a loss of information and sensitivity.

In contrast, machine learning (ML) techniques—including random forests, support vector machines (SVMs), nearest neighbour classifiers, and neural networks—can handle raw or minimally processed motion data21–24. These methods often yield higher classification accuracy but suffer from limited interpretability, dependence on large datasets, and increased risk of overfitting–issues that are especially problematic in biomedical applications with small sample sizes20,24,25. Therefore, balancing performance and transparency is critical, particularly if these models are to gain acceptance in clinical practice26.

Functional Data Analysis (FDA) is a middle-ground alternative. Unlike traditional approaches, the FDA does not require prior discretization or feature extraction, reducing the risk of information loss and avoiding arbitrary signal segmentation27. Functional Logistic Regression (FLR), as an extension of classical logistic regression, retains the advantages of statistical inference, including the estimation of coefficients, confidence intervals, and p-values, while enabling direct use of functional biomechanical data28. This balance between interpretability and fidelity to the original signal structure makes FLR especially suitable for analyzing complex clinical-biomechanical interactions. However, despite its potential, FLR has almost no application in KOA research.

In this study, we explored the use of Functional Logistic Regression to support early diagnosis of KOA by combining functional biomechanical signals (GRFs) and clinical pain measures. We aimed to determine whether this integrative approach improves the classification performance while maintaining interpretability and clinical applicability.

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional analytical observational study was conducted in accordance with the international guidelines for Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)29. All procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital La Fe (CEIm La Fe 2017/0147). All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion in the study.

Participants

Participants were recruited and evaluated at Hospital La Fe (Valencia, Spain) within the framework of the H2020 project OACTIVE6. Inclusion criteria were: (a) age 40 years; (b) Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade 0–1. Exclusion criteria included: (a) cognitive impairment that hindered understanding of audiovisual materials; (b) illiteracy; (c) comprehension or communication difficulties; (d) insufficient Spanish language proficiency to follow measurement instructions; (e) diagnosis of rheumatic, autoimmune, or infectious diseases; and (f) current knee injuries identified by magnetic resonance imaging.

Participants were assessed by a team of three experienced clinicians specialised in physical medicine and rehabilitation, who consistently performed the same roles throughout the evaluation process. Group classification into healthy subjects (HS) and early knee osteoarthritis (EKOA) was carried out before gait analysis, based on the criteria proposed by Mahmoudian et al.27, which include KL grade 0–1, presence of risk factors, and early symptoms. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for average daily knee pain was also collected during the clinical evaluation, prior to the biomechanical gait assessment, alongside other patient-reported outcomes. While KOOS was used as part of the classification criteria, the VAS score was not involved in group assignment and was therefore considered suitable for inclusion as an independent clinical predictor in the model. Although both scores relate to pain, KOOS incorporates aspects of function and quality of life, whereas the VAS provides a direct, continuous measure of pain intensity independently reported by the patient. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) has demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability in knee osteoarthritis populations, with intraclass correlation coefficients up to 0.97 and minimal detectable changes as low as 0.08 points, supporting its use for assessing pain intensity in this context30.

Although the initial sample was larger, certain cases were excluded due to COVID-19-related restrictions. Only participants who completed the full biomechanical gait analysis, including ground reaction force (GRF) recordings (see Section of Ground Force Reaction measurement), were included in the current study.

The final sample consisted of 56 individuals: 31 healthy and 25 classified as EKOA. Table 1 shows means and standard deviations for anthropometric and clinical variables by sex and group (HS and EKOA) and for the full sample.

Ground force reaction measurement

Biomechanical assessments were conducted at the Institute of Biomechanics of Valencia (IBV), using force platforms (Dinascan/IBV31) to record Ground Reaction Forces (GRFs) and spatiotemporal parameters. Each trial consisted of walking five steps at a self-selected comfortable speed, stepping on a force platform. Six valid repetitions were recorded for each leg. Anthropometric data (height, weight, and hip height) were collected during the gait assessment to support normalization procedures and model inputs.

Ground reaction forces were expressed according to the coordinate system recommended by the International Society of Biomechanics (ISB), with the X axis oriented anteroposteriorly, the Y axis vertically and upward, and the Z axis mediolaterally, to the right. The force components are therefore referred to as , , and .

The functional median, defined as the deepest curve according to h-mode method defined in32, was selected from the six repetitions to obtain a representative curve for each subject and leg. This method reduces the influence of outliers and avoids the oversmoothing observed when using the functional mean, which can obscure meaningful features of the original signals32.

In addition to force signals, we computed stance time and stride duration for each valid trial, directly from the force platform data. These temporal variables reflect the dynamic timing of each gait cycle and are closely associated with the rhythm and speed of walking at the step level. While global parameters such as average walking speed or cadence were not analyzed, these time-based measures provided step-specific insight into gait dynamics. They were evaluated during model construction but did not significantly contribute to classification performance and were excluded from the final model.

The curves were projected onto a reduced basis of bivariate functional principal components (FPCA), following the methodology described by Ramsay and Silverman35, in order to reduce the dimensionality inherent in functional predictors and prevent overfitting.

To optimize model performance and avoid overfitting, the number of components was selected using a combination of the following criteria: (a) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)36; (b) Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)37; (c) area under the ROC curve (AUC); and (d) model sensitivity and specificity38. The goal was to achieve a trade-off between model complexity and predictive accuracy. The greater the number of predictor variables, the higher the percentage of correct classification, but usually, a less robust model will be obtained, and the problem of overfitting may arise. The selection of the number of PC seeks a compromise between low values of AIC and BIC and high values of AUC, sensitivity, and specificity.

Discussion

Early diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis (EKOA) is a growing priority in both clinical and research settings, as it can enable timely intervention before irreversible joint damage occurs7. However, the current diagnostic criteria based on symptoms and radiographs are not sufficiently sensitive in the early stages8. This has led to growing interest in biomechanical testing as a complementary approach, with studies suggesting that gait alterations may precede structural changes9–11. Nevertheless, most biomechanical studies focus on detecting group-level alterations in kinematic or kinetic variables without integrating them with clinical indicators, such as pain, to develop early classification models.

The development of decision-support tools for early KOA detection requires not only relevant clinical and biomechanical information but also appropriate classification methods. Traditional statistical approaches, such as logistic regression, are interpretable but limited in handling time-dependent or functional data. In contrast, machine-learning methods can model complex interactions and high-dimensional signals; however, they often lack transparency, are challenging to interpret and validate, and require large datasets for reliable performance26.

Consequently, interest has emerged in intermediate approaches that balance the interpretability of classical statistics with the flexibility of machine learning. In this context, our study introduces a classification model based on Functional Logistic Regression (FLR) to support the early diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis (KOA). Using ground reaction force (GRF) curves and VAS scores as predictive variables, the proposed method integrates biomechanical and clinical data to enhance diagnostic accuracy. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply FLR models to diagnose early knee osteoarthritis (EKOA).

Among the three ground reaction force curves analysed (, , and ), only the mediolateral force () showed a statistically significant association with early KOA classification. This finding is consistent with that of the study by Costello et al.15, in which this component was associated with patients with OA. This study also found differences in vertical force patterns between healthy and pathological subjects, but only after controlling for walking velocity. In contrast, mediolateral force patterns appeared to be less affected by velocity. Indeed, in our study, stance time and stride duration were extracted from each trial and evaluated during model construction. These step-level temporal descriptors, which are closely related to gait speed, did not significantly improve classification performance, suggesting that any potential effect of walking velocity is likely already embedded in the mediolateral GRF signal itself.

Among the three models analysed, the mediolateral force-based model () showed a slightly higher classification accuracy than the clinical model () (80.36% vs. 78.57%). However, this improvement comes at the cost of higher AIC and BIC values and slightly lower sensitivity (0.76 vs. 0.80), illustrating the trade-off between predictive accuracy and model robustness when incorporating more predictors.

In contrast, model , which integrates the mediolateral GRF component with the clinical pain intensity (VAS), outperformed and . It achieved the highest accuracy (83.93%), sensitivity (0.88), and AUC (0.92), while also showing better robustness (lower AIC and BIC) than despite including an additional variable. Moreover, a nested model comparison confirmed that adding the VAS improved the force-only model significantly (p < 0.001). These results confirm that combining clinical and biomechanical information yields a better classification performance than using either alone.

Additionally, the probability curve as a function of VAS (Fig. 3) suggests that the contribution of the mediolateral force signal is particularly relevant at intermediate pain levels, where the predictive power of (based solely on pain intensity) is reduced. This supports the idea that functional gait alterations (captured by GRF) and subjective pain intensity provide complementary diagnostic values in early KOA.

Nevertheless, most classification models for knee pathologies rely exclusively on clinical or biomechanical data. Only one previous study has integrated both, using the WOMAC index–but in the context of knee instability, not KOA classification18.

Previous research has explored classification models incorporating kinematic and kinetic variables, relying primarily on traditional statistical techniques, such as logistic regression or more complex machine learning-based methods. Logistic regression studies reported classification rates between 74%43 and 83%44, with accuracy increasing with KOA severity. ML techniques, such as Random Forest, have demonstrated accuracies of approximately 77.8%13, with further improvements observed in methods such as the Nearest Neighbour Classifier22,45. However, the classification accuracy is highly contingent on the severity of KOA. For instance, support vector machine (SVM) models achieve classification rates of 89% for mild KOA (KL 1-2) and 91.5% for severe cases (KL 3-4)23. Thus, it is particularly important to develop models capable of distinguishing healthy individuals from those with early-stage KOA. Notably, only one study specifically addressed mild KOA (KL 1-2), achieving 91% accuracy using ML techniques14, although it was based on equilibrium data rather than GRF. Other approaches based on neural networks have reported classification rates exceeding 95%24,46, but their lack of KOA severity specifications limits their clinical applicability, particularly for early stage KOA detection.

If we focus exclusively on classification accuracy, our results are lower than those reported by most ML techniques (except for Random Forest) but higher than those achieved by traditional logistic regression models using scalar variables. However, direct comparisons of the accuracy between the methods must be cautiously approached. First, the number of predictor variables in previous studies was often much higher than ours (seven functional components and three scalar variables, remaining well below the sample size). For example, Mezghani et al. achieved 91% accuracy using 31 components per force curve; however, the accuracy dropped to 67% with a simpler model22. In contrast, our model outperformed other studies that used more than 20 predictor variables and reported 78% accuracy13. Second, many studies have applied classification techniques to samples including healthy individuals and patients with severe KOA (KL 2–4), a scenario where classification is inherently easier. Our study focused on knee pain cases, aiming to distinguish healthy individuals with knee pain from those with early KOA (KL 1).

In addition to classification accuracy, it is important to consider other criteria such as preprocessing requirements, robustness, and interpretability. Functional Logistic Regression (FLR) offers specific advantages that make it well-suited for clinical gait analysis. Unlike classical logistic regression, which requires selecting a few discrete features from biomechanical signals (such as peak or average values), FLR uses the entire signal as input. By preserving the full-time structure of the data, it captures information that is particularly valuable in gait analysis, where meaningful patterns often lie in the shape and timing of the curves rather than in single summary values.

Compared to machine learning models, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests, or Neural Networks, which have been used successfully in KOA classification, FLR provides more transparent and interpretable results. It allows for direct estimation of coefficients, statistical testing of variable effects, and model evaluation using criteria such as AIC and BIC. These features are essential for clinical adoption, where black-box models are often difficult to justify. Although ML methods may achieve higher accuracy, they typically require large datasets, are sensitive to overfitting, and do not provide uncertainty estimates, making FLR a more robust and interpretable option in clinical applications.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was modest, which limits the generalizability of the findings and increases the risk of overfitting–even when using dimensionality reduction techniques such as Functional Principal Component Analysis (FPCA). This constraint may have led to optimistic estimates of model performance, and external validation in larger, independent cohorts is necessary to confirm the robustness and applicability of the results. Given the modest sample size, formal internal validation procedures (e.g., k-fold cross-validation or bootstrapping) were not performed and will be prioritized in future research with larger cohorts.

Second, the analysis focused exclusively on ground reaction forces (GRFs), without incorporating other biomechanical signals such as joint kinematics, knee adduction moments (KAM), or internal knee loading. While this choice increases clinical feasibility, it may also exclude relevant gait features that could enhance diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, only one representative curve per leg–the functional median–was used to characterize the GRFs. This approach improves robustness and reduces the influence of outlier strides, but does not capture intra-subject variability across steps, which may hold diagnostic value. Although bilateral asymmetry was not explicitly assessed, both legs were analyzed independently and included in the model, allowing asymmetry-related information to be incorporated implicitly. Future studies could explore the added value of integrating other biomechanical signals, wearable sensor data, or metrics of stride-to-stride variability to improve classification performance.

Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation and limits the ability to assess the prognostic value. It remains unclear whether the observed GRFs patterns reflect early pathological changes or are merely adaptive responses to the symptoms. Longitudinal studies are required to determine whether these gait signatures precede structural degeneration or predict clinical progression.

Finally, in this study, we have limited the clinical input to the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain, as it was the only clinical variable not used in the initial classification of participants into EKOA and non-EKOA groups. Future studies should consider incorporating additional clinical variables such as functional impairment, joint stiffness, or psychosocial factors, which may enhance the discriminate power of the model6. Nevertheless, our results demonstrate that pain alone, when combined with mediolateral ground reaction force patterns, provides sufficient information to achieve a clinically meaningful classification.

Despite these limitations, this study shows that integrating functional biomechanical signals with clinical pain measures improves the early KOA classification. Mediolateral ground reaction force () emerges as a relevant gait marker, and Functional Logistic Regression offers a transparent and statistically grounded framework for combining heterogeneous data types. These results support the development of accessible and interpretable screening tools for early KOA detection in clinical settings.
