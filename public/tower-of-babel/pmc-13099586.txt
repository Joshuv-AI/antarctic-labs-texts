Selective octopaminergic tuning of mushroom body circuits during memory formation
Authors: Ulrike S. Franke, Alexandra Großjohann, Samantha Aurich, Ines Köhler, Marius Lamberty, Sara Granato, Alice Fallaha, Jana Breitfeld, Peter Kovacs, Mareike Selcho, Robert J. Kittel, Andreas S. Thum, Dennis Pauls
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAnimals adapt their behavior by integrating past experiences with current conditions. Our study uncovers how octopamine signaling adds an additional layer of complexity to the neuronal circuits essential for learning and memory in Drosophila. By selectively modulating appetitive dopaminergic neurons through the Octß3R receptor, octopamine gates sugar-reinforced memory formation. In addition, octopamine broadly tunes dopaminergic circuits, including aversive neurons, suggesting a mechanism for context-dependent reinforcement and providing insight into how neuromodulators fine-tune behavioral flexibility.

On the basis of several key studies, it has become an accepted principle that memory formation in Drosophila is regulated by the differential involvement of distinct catecholamines. This has been demonstrated in both Drosophila larvae (1, 2) and flies (reviewed in refs. 3–5) as well as in other invertebrate species (e.g., refs. 6–14). Remarkably, the proposed functional separation for octopamine (OA; mediating reward during appetitive learning) and dopamine (DA; mediating punishment during aversive learning) did not withstand further testing. Several studies have convincingly shown that DA plays a critical role in punishment and reward learning in both Drosophila larvae and flies (15–23). Specifically, larvae and flies lacking the dopamine type-1 receptor dDA1 display a reduced performance not only in punishment learning but also in reward learning (18, 22). In addition, selectively silencing DA neurons of the protocerebral anterior medial (PAM) cluster only during training specifically impaired appetitive learning in both larvae and flies (15, 17, 19, 21, 24). Overall, these results suggest a developmentally conserved wiring principle in the Drosophila brain, where two distinct clusters of DA neurons in the Drosophila brain mediate appetitive and aversive reinforcement: the PPL1 (protocerebral posterior lateral 1) and PAM clusters in adults, and the DL1 (dorsolateral 1) and primary PAM (pPAM) clusters in larvae.

Yet, what is the role of OA, and how should the previous findings be interpreted in this context? The findings of Burke and colleagues suggest that the OA-dependent effect is upstream of DA neurons, as memory formation induced by thermogenetic activation of OA neurons was impaired in a dopamine receptor (dDA1/dumb1) mutant background (15). Furthermore, blockage or ablation of OA neurons disrupted appetitive learning in larvae and flies when arabinose—a sweet-tasting sugar with no nutritional value—was used as a reward (15, 25). In contrast, appetitive learning with nutritive sugars such as sucrose or fructose remained unaffected. Interestingly, odor-arabinose learning was impaired following the downregulation of the OA receptor OAMB in PAM neurons, indicating that OAMB is required in DA neurons for the OA-dependent memory formation (15). Collectively, these findings suggest that the sweetness of a sugar is conveyed via OA neurons to the PAM cluster through OAMB, where it is integrated with additional sensory information to generate a positive reinforcement signal. This model was further refined by Huetteroth et al., who demonstrated that the OA-dependent sweetness signal specifically establishes short-term memory via OAMB-mediated signaling in a subset of PAM neurons that innervate the ß’2am and y4 regions of the mushroom body (MB) (16). However, another OA receptor, Octß2R, also appears to be involved. Knockdown of Octβ2R in MB-MP1-type DA neurons similarly impaired reward learning (15). The dopaminergic MB-MP1 neurons not only contribute to odor-specific aversive memory formation but also relay information about the animal’s nutritional state to the mushroom body as they are modulated by dNPF (Drosophila neuropeptide F), a neuropeptide representing hunger state (26). These findings imply that OA signaling might encode more than a simple reward signal via OAMB; it may also modulate negative reinforcement or hunger-related signals via Octβ2R. Nonetheless, this compelling model may require further refinement in light of additional findings. First, oamb mutant flies show severely impaired sucrose learning, and this deficit can be rescued by expressing OAMB in intrinsic mushroom body Kenyon cells, suggesting an OA-dependent function that operates independently of DA neurons (27). On top, OA is also essential for aversive olfactory learning. Flies lacking tyramine-ß-hydroxylase (TßH), the enzyme hydroxylating tyramine (TA) to OA, exhibit impaired odor-shock memory, which could be rescued by targeted TßH expression in OA neurons (28). Moreover, Octβ1R mutant flies display deficits in aversive odor-shock memory, and these impairments could be rescued by Octβ1R expression in Kenyon cells (29). Finally, the anterior paired lateral (APL) neuron, which innervates the whole mushroom body, was shown to be octopamine-positive and function via Octß2R in Kenyon cells in anesthesia-resistant memory in flies (30), further suggesting an OA-dependent mechanism that functions independently of DA neurons.

Given the diverse modes of action attributed to OA, we focused this study on reexamining the role of OA in Drosophila larval learning. To this end, we employed a combination of optogenetics, loss-of-function behavioral assays, and Ca2+ imaging. Our results indicate that optogenetic activation of OA neurons is sufficient to drive both appetitive and aversive memory formation. Interestingly, we found that the temporal order of optogenetic US presentation dictates whether appetitive or aversive memory is formed. This sequence effect is specific to OA neurons and does not occur upon activation of other cell types in the MB circuit. Further, we provide evidence that OA modulates distinct clusters of DA neurons, thereby adding an additional layer of complexity to the mushroom body circuitry underlying learning and memory. Consequently, the function of OA in learning appears to center on the contextual adaptation of neuronal processes, such as aligning behavioral responses with internal states and physiological needs.

Results

Previous work on larval learning has comprehensively shown that associative olfactory memories are stored within the mushroom bodies (31–33). Moreover, DA neurons of the DL1 cluster provide an aversive teaching signal (1, 22, 34) while DA neurons of the pPAM cluster convey an appetitive one (20, 21, 24, 35). However, this working hypothesis somehow neglects the role of OA, despite clear evidence that OA signaling is essential for establishing larval reward memories (1, 25). A conundrum arises from anatomical studies: Neither light microscopy nor EM data provide evidence for direct innervation of the medial lobes by OA neurons. In contrast, the medial lobes—mushroom body compartments where reward memories are stored—are directly innervated by pPAM neurons (25, 31). Nevertheless, the prevailing hypothesis posits that OA neurons convey reinforcement signals directly to mushroom body Kenyon cells during memory formation.

To initially set aside any specific role of OA neurons during memory formation and instead confirm their general involvement, we optogenetically activated Tdc2-positive neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–H) during training. Tyrosine decarboxylase (Tdc) is crucial for the synthesis of TA, thus Tdc2-GAL4 includes all TA and OA neurons (25, 36, 37). In detail, we employed a well-established reciprocal two-group design (Fig. 1A), in which one odor (CS+) was coupled with blue light exposure (US) and the other was presented under dim red light (CS−) (2, 21). Following the protocol by Lyutova et al. (20), we applied blue light (475 nm) at an intensity of ~10−1mW/mm2 to induce neuronal activity through the activation of the channelrhodopsin XXL variant [ChR2XXL; (38)]. CantonS wildtype larvae showed no significant approach or avoidance, suggesting that light exposure at this intensity is not sufficient to elicit memory expression (20, 39) (Fig. 1B). Surprisingly, and in contrast to previously published data (2), Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae did not show any memory expression in our substitution learning assay (Fig. 1C). As a positive control, we used optogenetic activation of DA neurons from the pPAM cluster (R58e02>chop2XXL; SI Appendix, Fig. S1 I–P), which have been shown to convey reward signals directly to the medial lobes of the mushroom body (20, 21, 24). In line with previous reports, R58e02>chop2XXL larvae showed robust appetitive memory expression (Fig. 1C).

Although high-intensity blue light has been successfully used to substitute the US during learning by activating mushroom body Kenyon cells (20), chop2XXL has been shown to function more efficiently at light intensities three orders of magnitude lower than previous variants, such as ChR2-wt (38). Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that both light intensity and frequency are critical factors influencing the strength of the behavioral outcome following the optogenetic activation of OA neurons (40). We therefore repeated the substitution learning, but this time with reduced light intensity (Fig. 1D). R58e02>chop2XXL exhibited appetitive memory expression using blue light with an intensity of ~10−4mW/mm2. Interestingly, learning scores were significantly lower compared to those obtained with an intensity of 10−1mW/mm2. In contrast, no memory expression was detected in Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae at the lower light intensity (Fig. 1D). However, we noted that data variability in Tdc2>chop2XXL was different between the two light intensities. Our previous work has shown that learning performance can vary, albeit rarely, depending on whether the US is paired with the first odor (CS1 learning; Fig. 1E) or the second odor (CS2 learning; Fig. 1E) during training (41). Therefore, we tested whether sequence effects could account for the increased data variability observed with 10−4mW/mm2 light intensity. Indeed, Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae showed significantly different performance depending on whether the 1st odor was paired with blue light (CS1 learning) or the 2nd odor (CS2 learning; Fig. 1 E and F). Surprisingly, CS1 learning resulted in a positive learning score, whereas CS2 learning induced a negative learning score (Fig. 1F), suggesting that OA neurons induce both appetitive and aversive memory formation, depending on the timing of activation. In contrast, no sequence effect was detected in R58e02>chop2XXL larvae. Because our sequential training protocol produced distinct memories for CS1 and CS2 learning specifically in Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae, we reanalyzed previously published data on the optogenetic activation of aversive dopaminergic neurons, namely DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 (42). Consistent with activation of pPAM neurons (Fig. 1F), no differential memory expression between CS1 and CS2 learning was observed in MB054>chop2XXL larvae (SI Appendix, Fig. S1Q). Interestingly, activation of the “reward-mediating” DANs (pPAM neurons) resulted in higher learning scores during CS1 learning (Fig. 1F), whereas activation of the “punishment-mediating” DANs (DL1 neurons) yielded higher learning scores during CS2 learning (SI Appendix, Fig. S1Q). In addition, we tested wildtype larvae for both odor-fructose and odor-salt learning and found no sequence effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 R and S).

To further validate this OA neuron-specific effect, we used chop2XXM to optogenetically activate OA neurons (43). Consistently, activation of Tdc2-positive neurons with chop2XXM (with ~10−2mM/mm2 of blue light) induced appetitive memory expression for CS1 learning and aversive memory expression for CS2 learning (Fig. 1G). These findings suggest that the observed sequence effect is not due to the prolonged open state or extra high expression levels of chop2XXL (38). Next, we tested the specificity of the optogenetically induced CS1 memory in Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae by presenting fructose or aspartic acid in the test situation. While the presence of fructose impaired memory expression, aspartic acid had no effect, indicating that the memory is specifically related to reward quality and valence (44) (Fig. 1H). Taken together, these findings suggest that OA neurons are involved in both larval appetitive and aversive memory formation. Next, we aimed to gain further insight into the nature of the two distinct optogenetically induced memories observed in CS1 and CS2 learning. Optogenetic stimulation of OA neurons leads to reward memory expression in CS1 learning, whereas it induces aversive memory expression in CS2 learning. This raises the question of whether these distinct memories are based on value assignment specifically to the CS+ (CS1: positive, CS2: negative), or whether any value is also assigned to the CS−. To address this, we trained Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae using our standard substitution training paradigm (Fig. 1A) but this time tested the larvae comparing either the CS+ versus a novel odor or the CS− versus a novel odor. Benzaldehyde was used as the third odor at a balanced concentration (SI Appendix, Fig. S1T). Strikingly, our findings indicate that CS1 reward memory expression is driven by the assignment of positive value to the CS+, while no value is assigned to the CS− (Fig. 1I). We observed the same principle for CS2 learning: Aversive memory expression is likewise based on negative value assignment to the CS+, with no detectable value assigned to the CS− (Fig. 1J). We further excluded the possibility that the two distinct optogenetically induced memories arise from repeated stimulus exposure during the three training cycles of our standard paradigm. To this end, we applied a single-cycle training paradigm using Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae. Under these conditions, we again observed robust CS1 reward memory expression and CS2 aversive memory expression, indicating that the observed memory valence does not depend on stimulus repetition (Fig. 1K).

Using single cell anatomical description of all OA neurons in the brain, we previously identified three OA cell types—two unpaired and one paired—that directly innervate the mushroom bodies (25). sVUMmx1 and sVUMmd1 (also called OAN-a1 and OAN-a2) innervate the calyces, while sVPMmx (also called OAN-g1) projects to the base of the vertical lobe. These three cell types were later confirmed via EM connectome data, which additionally revealed a fourth OA (paired) neuron innervating the tip of the vertical lobe [OAN-e1; (31)]. We therefore investigated the response dynamics of larval mushroom body Kenyon cells to OA and TA, as all OA-positive neurons in the subesophageal clusters are also TA-positive (25). To this end, we genetically expressed the Ca2+ sensor GCaMP6m in Kenyon cells (H24>GCaMP6m) and bath applied OA and TA at various concentrations on isolated brains. DA was applied as a reference stimulus. Consistent with findings in the adult mushroom body (45), we observed only mild, concentration-dependent effects in intracellular Ca2+ levels in response to OA, TA, and DA (Fig. 2 A–H). Changes in fluorescence intensity were detected for all stimuli at a concentration of 10−1M compared to vehicle controls, while TA also elicited significant changes at 10−2M (Fig. 2 C–H). Significant changes in fluorescence intensity to 10−1M OA and TA were detected in the calyx (Fig. 2 C–H) and different lobe compartments (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–H). These results confirm that OA and TA signals modulate the activity of larval mushroom body Kenyon cells.

The conundrum remains: How does activation of OA neurons induce an appetitive memory despite the absence of direct innervation of the medial lobes? To address this, we used available split-GAL4 lines specific to OAN-a1, a2 and OAN-g1 (no line was available for OAN-e1) (46) and activated those OA neurons that directly innervate the mushroom body Kenyon cells during substitution learning. Consistent with our previous results SS24765>chop2XXL and SS25844>chop2XXL larvae showed aversive memory expression compared to genetic controls (Fig. 3G). Specifically, activation of OAN-a1, a2 and OAN-g1 each elicited aversive memory expression specifically during CS2 learning (Fig. 3H) comparable to Tdc2>chop2XXL larvae (Fig. 1F). However, in contrast to Tdc2>chop2XXL, no appetitive memory expression was observed during CS1 learning (Fig. 3H). The absence of appetitive memory formation in CS1 learning aligns with the lack of medial lobe innervation by OA neurons, further reinforcing the notion that OA-mediated appetitive memory formation is anatomically constrained.

We next pursued an alternative hypothesis regarding how OA neurons contribute to larval reward learning. Specifically, we considered the possibility that OA neuron activation indirectly facilitates reward learning by stimulating DA neurons of the pPAM cluster, which are known to directly innervate medial lobe compartments important for memory formation, as previously shown in flies (15, 47). To test this, we expressed GCamp6m in pPAM neurons (R58e02>GCaMP6m) and performed bath applications of OA and TA, respectively, on isolated brains. Both OA and TA elicited calcium transients in pPAM neurons of R58e02-GAL4, suggesting excitatory effects (Fig. 4 A and B). However, closer inspection of individual calcium traces revealed substantial variability: Some neurons showed little to no response, whereas others exhibited Ca2+ peaks with distinct onset times, indicating heterogeneous response dynamics within the pPAM cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 I and J). To achieve greater specificity, we next expressed GCaMP6m in split-GAL4 lines targeting individual DA neurons of the pPAM cluster. Given the isolated preparation and manual bath application, all four pPAM dopaminergic neurons showed calcium responses within a comparable temporal range (∼2 to 3 min), with neuron-specific response dynamics. DAN-h1 (SS01696>GCaMP6m; Fig. 4 C and D) and DAN-j1 (MB316B>GCaMP6m; Fig. 4 G and H) exhibited significant increases in intracellular Ca2+ in response to OA, whereas TA elicited either only mild or no responses. In contrast, DAN-k1 (SS01757>GCaMP6m; Fig. 4 I and J) responded significantly to both OA and TA, while DAN-i1 did not show a detectable response to OA and TA (SS00864>GCaMP6m; Fig. 4 E and F). To determine whether DAN-i1 is indeed nonresponsive to either amine, we monitored intracellular cAMP levels using the genetically encoded Epac1-camps sensor (48–50). In contrast to Ca2+ imaging, OA and TA bath application induced a significant fluorescence change in DAN-i1, consistent with an increase in intracellular cAMP (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 K and L). Taken together, these findings suggest that all four pPAM neurons respond to OA and/or TA, engaging either Ca2+- or cAMP-dependent signaling mechanisms.

Our behavioral data further support the idea that activation of Tdc2-positive neurons is sufficient to induce aversive memory formation (Fig. 1 F and G). To examine whether OA and/or TA influence aversive reinforcement, we expressed GCaMP6m in DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 neurons of the DL1 cluster (MB054b>GCaMP6m) and applied OA and TA on isolated brains. Previous studies have shown that DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 activation is both necessary and sufficient to establish taste punishment learning in the larva (34, 46, 51, 52). Interestingly, bath applications of both OA and TA led to a significant reduction of Ca2+ levels in these neurons (Fig. 4 K and L). These results may suggest that OA neurons/TA neurons do not convey aversive reinforcing information to the mushroom bodies, but may rather modulate DA neurons that encode aversive signals. Consistent with this, we found that interfering with Tdc2-positive neurons does not affect aversive learning. Neither conditional silencing via shibirets expression, nor constitutive hyperpolarization via Kir2.1, nor inhibition of synaptic transmission via tetanus toxin disrupted larval aversive odor-salt memory (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Our findings suggest that OA and TA signals modulate the activity of individual pPAM neurons (Fig. 4 A–J). To confirm that OA to pPAM signaling contributes to appetitive learning, we constitutively knocked down individual OA and TA receptors in pPAM neurons via RNA interference expression using R58e02-GAL4 (targeting DAN-h1, DAN-i1, DAN-j1). For this screen, we primarily employed a one-cycle training regime, previously shown to yield robust immediate memory scores (53, 54). To systematically assess receptor function, we tested the effects of knocking down receptor levels on both odor-fructose and odor-arabinose associative learning. Interestingly, decreasing both TA receptor (TyrR and TyrRII) and several OA receptor (OAMB, Octα2R, Octß1R, and Octß2R) levels did not impair memory performance in either paradigm (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–L). The efficiency of panneuronal RNAi-mediated knockdown for all tested TA and OA receptors was validated by RT-qPCR (SI Appendix, Fig. S4M). In contrast, the knockdown of Octß3R in pPAM neurons (R58e02>Octß3R-RNAi) significantly impaired both odor-fructose and odor-arabinose memory (Fig. 5 A and B). Consistently, Trojan-Octß3R larvae also failed to form appetitive memory in both paradigms (Fig. 5 C and D) (55). To further validate these findings, we repeated the Octß3R knockdown experiments using a three-cycle training paradigm. Unlike single-cycle training, repeated training alters the composition of the established memory phases (54, 56), thereby providing a more rigorous test for the role of Octß3R in larval reward learning. As before, interfering with Octß3R reduced memory scores for both odor-fructose and odor-arabinose learning compared to genetic controls. However, memory performance remained above chance level, suggesting that Octß3R is specifically or dominantly required for nonconsolidated forms of memory (Fig. 5 E–H). Importantly, both R58e02>Octß3R-RNAi and Trojan-Octß3R larvae exhibited normal odor and sugar preferences (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–H), ruling out general sensory or motivational deficits. To provide further evidence of Octß3R function in pPAM neurons, we rescued Octß3R in pPAM neurons in the MiMIC mutant background. Consequently, Octß3R expression restored memory performance for both fructose and arabinose learning (Fig. 5 I and J). In contrast, knockdown of Octß3R in mushroom body Kenyon cells did not impair memory, reinforcing the conclusion that its role is specific to pPAM neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 I–L). Together, these results support a specific role for Octß3R in pPAM neurons during larval appetitive learning and memory.

As only DAN-h1 has previously been shown to be necessary for odor-sugar learning (24), we focused our analysis to this neuron type using SS01696-GAL4 and determined whether OA modulation of DAN-h1 is required for appetitive memory performance. Interestingly, SS01696>Octß3R-RNAi larvae showed significantly reduced memory scores in odor-arabinose learning, while odor-fructose learning remained indistinguishable from controls (Fig. 6 A and B). The specific impairment in arabinose-associated learning, along with the preserved fructose learning are consistent with previous findings (25). However, these results appear somewhat inconsistent with the Octß3R knockdown effect using R58e02-GAL4 (Fig. 5 A and B), which shows specific labeling of the pPAM cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 I–P). To further confirm the central role of DAN-h1 in OA-dependent modulation of appetitive memory, we performed Ca2+ imaging with simultaneous application of OA and downregulation of Octß3R in DAN-h1 (SS01696>GCaMP6m;Octß3R-RNAi). As expected, OA application failed to elicit Ca2+ transients under these conditions, underlining the necessity of this particular OA receptor for modulating DAN-h1 activity (Fig. 6 C and D). In addition, our findings revealed that DA neurons responded robustly to OA both in the absence and presence of tetrodotoxin (TTX), indicating that the effect on DA neurons does not require synaptic transmission (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 M and N). Taken together, our findings suggest that OA neurons exert a specific and selective modulatory role on distinct DA neuron types. In this context, DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 are essential for aversive learning, while DAN-h1 is crucial for appetitive learning.

To further validate that OA: DAN-h1 signaling is fundamental for the induction of positive CS1 memory in our optogenetic substitution experiment, we performed a combined gain-of-function and loss-of-function approach. Specifically, OA neurons were optogenetically activated while pPAM neurons, including DAN-h1, were simultaneously ablated. Contrary to our expectations, Tdc2>chop2XXL; R58e02>reaper larvae still showed appetitive memory expression for CS1 learning (and as expected, an aversive memory expression for CS2 learning; Fig. 6 E and F). Surprisingly, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that DAN-j1 escaped reaper-induced apoptosis (ablation failed in 20/21 brains), while DAN-h1 and DAN-i1 were ablated as expected (ablation was successful in 100% of cases for DAN-h1 and DAN-i1; Fig. 6G). These results suggest that, in addition to DAN-h1, which is both necessary and sufficient for appetitive learning (Fig. 6 A–C) (24), DAN-j1 is also sufficient to orchestrate OA-dependent appetitive memory. Notably, DAN-k1, which is not covered by R58e02-GAL4 line and therefore not targeted in either the receptor knockdown or ablation experiments, was found to be dispensable for appetitive memory expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 O and P).

Discussion

In this study, we confirm the modulatory role of OA in learning and memory in Drosophila larvae, consistent with previous research. Importantly, we refine the current understanding of OA’s mode of action by identifying the functional mechanisms of OA neurons in larval aversive memory. We also provide single cell level evidence for the involvement of the Octß3R receptor in modulating the dopaminergic pPAM cluster during appetitive memory formation. Our findings reveal the bimodal functionality of the OA system in both appetitive and aversive learning in larvae, highlighting its distinct sites of action.

Previous research (33, 40, 57) has shown that individual OA neurons primarily convey reward signals during learning and memory (1, 5, 7, 27). However, accumulating evidence from both larval and adult Drosophila challenges this model. First, OA signaling has been implicated in both adult appetitive and aversive learning, as shown by studies on TßH mutants and Octß2R and Octß1R mutants (28–30, 58). Consistent with this, we now demonstrate that optogenetic activation of OA neurons, when paired with an odor stimulus, is sufficient to induce aversive memory in the CS2 training paradigm (Fig. 1 F and G). Second, interfering with OA signaling during adult odor-sucrose learning (15), or ablating OA neurons in larval odor-fructose (25) or aversive odor-salt learning (Fig. 5 D–F), does not impair memory formation, suggesting that OA neurons are not essential for these types of learning. Third, at the anatomical level, OA neurons do not directly innervate the medial lobes of the mushroom bodies in larvae, despite these regions being critical for appetitive memories storage (25, 33).

Based on these findings, the role of OA neurons can be refined in light of the orchestration hypothesis (40, 57, 59). According to this model, OA neurons do not directly mediate reinforcement information but instead modulate other neurons of the mushroom body circuitry in a context-dependent manner. Our results now provide direct support for this hypothesis. Previous studies have provided extensive evidence that DA neurons of the pPAM cluster are essential during memory formation (15, 17, 21, 23). In larvae, the pPAM cluster comprises four neurons (DAN-h1, DAN-i1, DAN-j1, and DAN-k1) which directly innervate the medial lobes of the mushroom bodies. The activity of these neurons during memory formation is both necessary and sufficient to shift mushroom body output toward attraction. Our study now provides further information on the functional division of the pPAM cluster. We show that individual pPAM neurons are selectively modulated by OA. First, DAN-h1 activity is enhanced by OA through Octß3R, and knockdown of Octß3R specifically in DAN-h1 significantly impairs appetitive memory formation (Fig. 6 A–D). Further, DAN-h1 activity has shown to be both necessary and sufficient to induce larval attraction suggesting that DAN-h1 is the central cell of the pPAM cluster during memory formation (24). DAN-j1 appears to contribute to reward learning as well, as evidenced by its response to OA stimulation (Fig. 4G) and the preservation of learning following ablation of DAN-h1 and DAN-i1 (Fig. 6 E–G). We also confirm that DAN-k1 is not sufficient to induce memory expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). This is in line with an earlier study: Data presented in Rohwedder et al. showed that ablation of all four dopaminergic pPAM neurons using R64H06-GAL4 impaired odor-arabinose learning (figure 3 I–K in ref. 21). However, in figure 4 of the same study, ablation of DAN-k1 alone, using cell-type specific GAL4 lines NP7139 and NP7231, did not impair odor-arabinose learning. This rather suggests that DAN-k1 is not individually necessary but likely acts in concert with other pPAM cluster neurons. This interpretation is consistent with our results and supports the extended model proposed here, in which DAN-h1 functions as the principal neuron, whereas the remaining pPAM neurons contribute in a more supportive capacity. However, we demonstrate that DAN-k1 is activated by OA and TA, potentially enabling context-dependent modulation of sensory signal evaluation. Notably, OA and TA both elicit an excitatory response in DAN-k1 (Fig. 4I), a property unique to this neuron within the pPAM cluster and they do not act antagonistically in this context as they do, for example, in other processes such as locomotion (37, 60–64). Strikingly, in larvae where only DAN-j1 and DAN-k1 are functional—and where DAN-k1 alone does not impact reward learning—OA neuron stimulation still induces a positive CS1 memory (Fig. 6 E–G), highlighting the importance of DAN-j1. On top, DAN-i1 does also respond to OA or TA, this time cAMP-specific, in line with previous evidence supporting its role in appetitive memory (Fig. 4 E and F). This underscores a nuanced, cell-type-specific modulation within the pPAM cluster.

OA orchestrates pPAM activity along a gradient of modulation strength, following the sequence of DAN-h1, DAN-j1, DAN-k1, and DAN-i1, from strong to specific responsiveness. Given that pPAM neurons are synaptically segregated from each other, as revealed by EM reconstructions (31), this organization likely enables individual, context-dependent modulation of reinforcement signals within distinct mushroom body compartments. This organization differs markedly from that in adult Drosophila, where crosstalk between dopaminergic input neurons has been proposed and shown to be essential for the specificity of signals to the distinct medial lobe compartments (65). Moreover, additional modulators likely contribute to tuning pPAM activity. For instance, recurrent feedback from Kenyon cells via sNPF signaling has been implicated in memory stabilization within the larval pPAM cluster (20). This is consistent with our observation that optogenetic activation of pPAM neurons at high light intensity resulted in learning scores exceeding those of both lower light stimulation and even wild-type sugar learning (Fig. 1). These findings may imply that the dopaminergic system can dynamically adapt to modulatory input through both feed-forward (OA and TA) and feedback (sNPF) mechanisms, highlighting the integrative and dynamic nature of reinforcement signaling in the larval brain.

Can we rule out that OA neurons convey reinforcement signals directly? For larval appetitive learning, a direct modulation of mushroom body Kenyon cells, such as that mediated by OAMB in flies (27), has not been documented, making this scenario unlikely. However, our findings provide evidence that such a mechanism may be possible for larval aversive memory. Specifically, optogenetic activation of OAN-a1, OAN-a2 (targeting the calyx), as well as OAN-g1 (targeting the lower vertical lobe), induces aversive CS2 memory (Fig. 3). Interestingly, the optogenetic activation of these neurons using Cs-Chrimson in a previous study (46) showed a similar trend toward aversive learning, but the effect was not significant. This highlights the selective effect of different optogenetic tools on OA neurons (Fig. 1). Further experiments are needed to determine whether the effect results from direct OA signaling to mushroom body Kenyon cells or occurs indirectly via modulation of specific dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 4K). In the calyx, modulation may occur via the GABAergic APL neuron, and in the lower vertical lobe via the dopaminergic DAN-g1 (24, 31, 66). Connectome data support this, showing direct synaptic connections between these OA neurons and either APL or DAN-g1 (31, 66). In contrast, direct OA-mediated signaling to Kenyon cells remains unlikely in larvae, as there is currently no evidence for a larval counterpart to the Octß1R-dependent mechanism described in flies (29). Instead, our data show that DAN-g1 is modulated by OA signaling (Fig. 4K), supporting an indirect modulatory role of OA in aversive memory formation.

Previous studies have shown that the larval aversive learning system is similarly organized to its appetitive counterpart described here and that four DA neurons of the DL1 cluster provide aversive teaching signals (46, 51, 52). Among these, the combined activity of DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 has been shown to be both necessary and sufficient for aversive odor-salt learning (56). While these neurons act as central neurons mediating reinforcement information, DAN-c1 and DAN-d1 appear to act in a supportive manner, mirroring the hierarchical structure of the pPAM cluster, where DAN-h1 functions centrally and other neurons supportively (Fig. 7). Our data further reveal that DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 receive input from both OA and TA, but in contrast to cells of the pPAM cluster, DAN-f1 and DAN-g1 are inhibited by OA and TA. This inhibition aligns with appetitive learning, where OA/TA signaling activates reward-related DA neurons and suppresses aversive DA neurons, thus promoting attraction (Fig. 7). Given that optogenetic activation of OAN-g1 induces aversive CS2 memory (Fig. 3 D–H), it seems unlikely that this effect is mediated by DAN-g1. Instead, the effect may arise from direct synaptic modulation of mushroom body output neurons or Kenyon cells. Indeed, connectome data support this, as the strongest connection of OAN-g1 is to MBON-g1, g2, accounting for 28% of all output synapses, while connections to DAN-g1 (0.02% of output synapses) and mushroom body Kenyon cells (0.04% of output synapses) are rather limited (31). In summary, these findings suggest that OA and TA orchestrate learning and memory by engaging distinct mechanisms at multiple circuit levels. Their effects depend on the valence of the learning experience, the specific neuronal targets, and are modulated by context, prior experience, and the organism’s internal state.

The question remains: Why is the activation of OA neurons sufficient to induce conflicting memories (Fig. 1)? Notably, natural stimuli can also induce conflicting memories in larvae, even when applied using identical training protocols. For example, a mixture of 20 amino acids has been shown to elicit both appetitive and aversive memories, depending on the context (67). Similarly, in adult Drosophila, a single alcohol conditioning protocol initially generates an aversive memory, which later transitions into a long-term appetitive memory (68). A comparable phenomenon is observed with sugar-DEET mixtures, which establish simultaneous positive and negative memories in distinct compartments of the mushroom body (69). These findings suggest that DA neurons can encode multiple memories in parallel, targeting different mushroom body compartments, each characterized by unique valence, dynamics, storage capacities, and flexibility (70). A particularly striking result from the amino acid study is the context-dependent retrieval of these memories: Positive memory is retrieved only in the absence of the rewarding stimulus, while negative memory is recalled in the presence of an aversive cue (67). Additional studies in larvae have extended this principle to a variety of stimuli, including sugars, salts, and bitter compounds (41, 44, 53), and even the optogenetic activation of neurons (20), showing that memory retrieval is tightly gated by context and internal state. However, aversive odor-electric shock memories remain robustly expressed even in the absence of the aversive US during testing (41, 71). This also applies to the optogenetic activation of OA neurons, where CS2 training induces aversive memory even on a neutral test plate (Fig. 1). In summary, numerous studies in Drosophila demonstrate that identical reinforcement stimuli can produce opposing memories, which are selectively retrieved in a context-dependent manner. Our results therefore suggest that OA may thus play a key role in orchestrating both the formation and retrieval of divergent memories, ensuring behavior remains adaptively tuned to context.

Moreover, our findings confirm that a single reinforcement stimulus can induce opposing memories depending on the stimulus presentation regime: CS1 learning elicits appetitive memory expression, whereas CS2 learning elicits aversive memory expression, and not—for example—vice versa. Our data indicate memory specificity, with positive value in CS1 learning and negative value in CS2 learning assigned to the CS+, independent of stimulus repetitions. Importantly, this sequence effect appears to be specific to the activation of OA neurons, as it is not observed following optogenetic activation of pPAM neurons (Fig. 1), mushroom body Kenyon cells (20), aversive DANs of the DL1 cluster (42), or in wildtype odor-sugar and odor-salt learning (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 R and S). The absence of a sequence effect in these experiments further indicates that the effect does not arise from a general feature of the protocol but is specific to OA manipulation of different subsets (Fig. 1: Tdc2-GAL4 and Fig. 3: SS24765-GAL4 and SS25488-GAL4). A recent study on OA- and DA-dependent conditioning in crickets suggests a circuit model for the parallel formation of appetitive and aversive memories and their competition for the conditioned response. Here, different types of OA and DA neurons impact on both the formation and retrieval of memories through mutual activation and inhibition within the mushroom body circuitry (72). This may well be consistent with the findings of this study on the selective modulation of extrinsic dopaminergic neurons by OA and TA. Alternatively, it has been shown that a temporal shift of less than a second is sufficient to switch behavioral responses from avoidance to attraction in optogenetic association learning experiment in adults. This bidirectional tuning is based on plasticity at Kenyon cell to mushroom body output synapses, triggered by activity of distinct DA neurons (65). Optogenetic activation of OA neurons using chop2XXM provides evidence that the sequential memory effect observed in our study (CS1 vs. CS2) is not due to the prolonged open state or to an exceptionally high expression of chop2XXL. However, it is still tempting to speculate that the expression of opposing memories relies on temporal dynamics within the mushroom body circuitry at the level of mushroom body output neurons, which can also be directly triggered by optogenetic activation of OA neurons (Fig. 4) (73). Finally, in adult Drosophila, DA neurons are known to encode relative reinforcement signals (i.e., whether an experience is better or worse in comparison to another). Thus, whether an odor is initially presented in a neutral context or in conjunction with OA neuron modulation could significantly influence the resulting memory formation (74).

In vertebrates, it has been shown that vertebrate reward-mediating neurons are also sensitive to aversive input, a concept described as the “single-dimension” model. In contrast, the “two-dimension” model posits that such neurons are exclusively tuned to reward processing and thus other neurons must encode for aversive input (75). Our findings now support the “two-dimension” model, in which the OA neurons add another level of complexity to the mushroom body circuitry through different modes of modulation (76). DA neurons of the pPAM cluster encode for reward, while neurons of the DL1 cluster encode for punishment. However, OA neurons act upstream of these neurons and selectively modulate their signaling. Our Ca2+ imaging data suggest that OA induces pPAM neuron activity, while inhibiting DL1 cluster DA neurons. This shifts the balance at the mushroom body input level in favor of approach and approach memory. Further research is needed to understand whether OA orchestrates also at the level of output neurons. This could explain the systemic effect of OA on learning and memory, where the complexity arising from multiple interactions partially obscures a precise characterization of its cellular mechanism of action.

Materials and Methods

Fly Strains.

Flies were cultured on standard food at 25 °C and 60% humidity in a standard light:dark cycle. Experimental transgenic lines were obtained by crossing driver lines to UAS lines, whereas genetic controls were obtained by crossing those lines to w1118. Transgenic and mutant lines used in this study are shown in SI Appendix, Table S1.

Olfactory Learning and Memory.

Learning experiments were performed in Petri dishes filled with a thin layer of either 1.5% or 2.5% agarose (CAS 9012-36-6, Roth). Depending on the experiment, a 1-cycle or 3-cycle two-odor reciprocal training regime was used (53, 56). Amyl acetate (AM; CAS 628-63-7, Merck), diluted 1:80 in paraffin oil (CAS 8012-95-1, Merck), undiluted 3-octanol (3-OCT; CAS 589-98-0, Merck), or benzaldehyde (BA; CAS 100-52-7, Merck) were used as conditional stimulus (CS) or naïve odor and loaded into Teflon containers with perforated lids. For odor-sugar learning, 2 M fructose (CAS 57-48-7, Roth) or 2 M arabinose (CAS 10323-20-3, Merck) were used as unconditioned stimulus. For odor-salt learning 1.5 M sodium chloride (CAS 7647-14-5, Roth) was used. Aspartic acid (CAS 56-84-8, Merck) was used for experiments on test plates containing amino acids. Nω-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester hydrochloride (L-NNA; CAS 51298-62-5, Merck) was used as nitric oxide production inhibitor in a final concentration of 80 mM within the fly food.

Substitution learning.

Fly cultures were wrapped in red foil before the experiment to protect them from blue light and thus the constant neuronal activation through chop2XXL/chop2XXM. 250 µM of all-trans-retinal (dissolved in ethanol; CAS 116-31-4, Merck) was applied to the standard food for experiments using chop2XXM (38, 43). During the experiment, we optogenetically substituted the unconditioned stimulus with constant blue light (475 nm) of either ~10−1mW/mm2 or ~10−4mW/mm2 for experiments using chop2XXL and ~10−2mW/mm2 for experiments using chop2XXM.

During the test, larvae were exposed to both odors (CS+ and CS−) in a binary choice test. After 5 min larvae were counted on each side and a preference index was calculated by subtracting the number of larvae on one side from the number of larvae on the other side divided by the total number of larvae. The final learning index was then calculated from the mean value of two reciprocal experiments in which each of the two odors served as CS+ once. The final number of repetitions varied between the experiments. While we typically performed N = 20 in optogenetic experiments (10× CS1, 10× CS), the standard N number in the other learning experiments was usually 15.

Ca2+ Imaging.

Functional imaging was performed as described in refs. 20 and 77. To monitor intracellular Ca2+ levels in mushroom body Kenyon cells or dopaminergic neurons in response to bath application of OA and TA, respectively, GCaMP6m was expressed in cells of interest using the GAL4/UAS system (78). Larval brains were dissected and subsequently put in a Petri dish containing 405 μL hemolymph-like HL3.1 saline solution. Before the experiment, brains were maintained for about 15 to 20 min for settling. Images with ROIs selecting cell bodies (for dopaminergic neurons) or the calyx and lobe compartments (for mushroom bodies) were recorded with an AXIO Examiner D1 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) with a Zeiss W Plan-Apochromat 20×1.0 DIC (UV) VIS-IR objective and ×20/1.0 water immersion objective and a Axiocam 506 camera (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). Data are presented as fluorescence intensity (F) normalized to the baseline before stimulus application (F0). To analyze changes in fluorescence intensities in response to application of either octopamine (770-05-8, Merck), tyramine (CAS 60-19-5, Merck), or dopamine (CAS 62-31-7, Merck), we compared the maximum difference from baseline to vehicle control [i.e., HL3.1 containing 0.1% DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide; CAS 67-68-5, Merck)]. Unless stated otherwise in the figures, a final concentration of 10−2M of OA and TA was used. The nonselective cholinergic agonist carbamoylcholin chloride (Carbachol; CAS 51-83-2, Merck) was used as positive control.

To test whether OA-induced responses were mediated directly or via synaptic input, a subset of experiments was performed in the presence of 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX; CAS 4368-28-9, Roth) in HL3.1 saline. Brains were preincubated for 20 min with TTX prior to OA application (10−1M final concentration), and Ca2+ responses were recorded as described above.

FRET Imaging.

The sample preparation of the brain followed the same protocol described for Ca2+ imaging. To monitor changes in intracellular cAMP levels, specimens were imaged with an upright epifluorescence microscope setup (microscope: BX51WI, Olympus, Japan; objective: XLUMPlanFLN20xW Olympus, Japan; LED light source: pE-4000, CoolLED Ltd., UK; beam splitter (T445lp), dichroic mirror (T 505 LPXR), and emission filters for CFP (480/30 nm) and YFP (535/30 nm) from Chroma, USA; emission image splitter: OptoSplit II, Cairn Research, UK). Images were captured with VisiView (v4.3.0.2 by Visitron, Germany) at a sampling interval of 5 s, illumination time of 120 ms and excitation wavelength of 435 nm. Octopamine or tyramine was bath applied one min after start of each recording to a final concentration of 10−2M. Vehicle solution without OA or TA was applied to control brains.

ROIs were set around DAN cell bodies. Average intensities per ROI were extracted for each frame. Further processing was conducted in MS Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA): Background signal was subtracted from YFP and CFP fluorescence signals. The YFP signal intensity was additionally corrected for spillover (48). The spillover factor was determined as 0.6. Ratios were calculated as,"CFP/YFP"=CFP (corrected)/YFP (corrected).

Resulting values were normalized to the average baseline intensity during the minute before application of OA, TA, or vehicle, respectively.

Immunohistochemistry.

Third instar larvae were dissected in ice-cold 1× PBS. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 40 min and rinsing with 0.3% PBT (1× PBS with 0.3% TritonX-100; Merck) at room temperature, specimens were blocked overnight at 4 °C in 0.3% PBT with 5% normal goat serum (NGS).

Consecutive overnight incubation with primary and secondary antibodies (SI Appendix, Table S2) in blocking solution was intermitted by 6× 10 min washing in 0.3% PBT. Specimens were washed 5× 10 min in PBT and 1× 10 min in PBS and stored at 4 °C in Vectashield H-1000 (Vector laboratories) until they were mounted. Samples depicted in Fig. 6 were scanned in confocal mode using an Olympus BX63F (EVIDENT Europe GmbH, Hamburg, DE) equipped with a UPLXAPO 60×, NA1.42 oil objective as part of an upright Abberior INFINITY LINE microscope (Abberior Instruments, Göttingen, DE). Image stacks of mushroom bodies were taken at a pixel size of 80 nm and a z-step size of 1 µm. Other samples were scanned at a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS LSM800, ZEISS Microscopy). Overview image stacks of the brain lobes were taken at a pixel size of 0.83 µm and a z-step size of 1.5 µm using a LD LCI Plan-Apochromat 25×/0.8 Imm Korr DIC M27 objective, while detailed image stacks of mushroom bodies were taken at a pixel size of 100 nm and a z-step size of 1 µm using a 63×/1.4 NA oil DIC M27 objective. Image J [version 2.9.0/1.53t; (79)] was used to adjust brightness and contrast.

Antibodies used in this study are presented in SI Appendix, Table S2.

qRT-PCR.

Total RNA from 20 L3 larval brains was isolated and extracted using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and 250 µg RNA was reverse transcribed with standard reagents (Life Technologies). Drosophila melanogaster gene expression was measured by quantitative real-time RT–PCR by using TaqMan methodology and fluorescence was detected on an ABI PRISM 7500 sequence detector (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Applied Biosystems; assay Dm02150048_m1 for OAMB; Dm03420183_m1 for Octα2R; Dm02143278_m1 for Octß1R; Dm02139710_g1 for Octß2R; Dm02148913_g1 for Octß3R; Dm02141694_m1 for TyrR and Dm02141688_m1 for TyrRII). D. melanogaster mRNA expression was calculated relative to the mRNA expression of Rpl32 (Applied Biosystems; assay Dm02151827_g1 for Rpl32) and to elav control larvae.

Statistics.

Data were analyzed using Graph Pad Prism 9. Data were analyzed for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, we used ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Šidák’s multiple comparisons test. When comparing only two genotypes, we used either an unpaired t test or the Mann–Whitney test. For data that were not normally distributed, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. To test against chance level, we performed a one-sample t test.
