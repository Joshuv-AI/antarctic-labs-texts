Photoinduced Cross-Metal Charge Transfer over Dual-Atom Z‑Scheme Catalysts Governing Cooperative Urea Synthesis from CO2 and N2
Authors: Cun-biao Lin, Fu-li Sun, Wen-xian Chen, Jin Zhao, Ming Wah Wong, Gui-lin Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photocatalytic urea synthesis from CO2 and N2 is limited by the simultaneous requirement for inert-molecule activation and selective C–N coupling. Herein, we theoretically designed a dual-atom-loaded C7N6/CTF Z-scheme catalyst to uncover that the photoinduced cross-charge transfer between heteronuclear Ti–V atoms dynamically facilitates the simultaneous activation and coupling of CO2 and N2. Specifically, nonadiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD) combined with real-time time-dependent density functional theory (RT-TDDFT) identifies a near-spin-symmetric shallow trap state at the Ti–V site that accelerates weak electron–hole annihilation yet sustains high-energy carriers, ultimately enabling a photoinduced directional electron flux across the metals. The resultant femtosecond-scale charge redistribution synchronously preactivates both substrates, bending CO2 and elongating N2, into geometries inaccessible under thermal conditions. While competing pathways to CO or NH3 are suppressed, the critical coupling barriers under explicit solvent are substantially reduced: the initial C–N coupling (*CO + *N2 → *NCON) to 0.66 eV and the final urea formation step (*CO + *NH2NH2 → *NH2CONH2) to 0.27 eV, clarifying the origin of high catalytic selectivity for urea synthesis. Generally, heteronuclear dual-atom Z-scheme heterojunctions represent a promising design for regulating photocatalytic states to achieve cooperative activation and selective C–N coupling.

Introduction

Urea (CO­(NH2)2), with a nitrogen content as high as 46 wt %, is an indispensable bulk chemical for global fertilizer production; however, its industrial synthesis still relies on the energy-intensive Bosch–Meiser process, which consumes most of the ammonia (NH3) produced via the Haber–Bosch route. , An ideal sustainable alternative would be the direct, solar-driven coupling of CO2 and N2 at ambient conditions to forge the essential C–N bond. − However, realizing this strategy is exceptionally challenging due to the inherent stability of the feedstock molecules: the strong CO bond in CO2 (∼526 kJ mol–1) , and the exceptionally robust NN triple bond in N2 (∼941 kJ mol–1). , This imposes a dual challenge of simultaneously activating both inert molecules while selectively steering their reactivity toward C–N coupling over other pathways.

To this end, atomically dispersed catalytic centers offer an ideal platform for probing and manipulating molecular activation. , While single-atom catalysts (SACs) offer well-defined sites and high atomic efficiency, their isolated, localized character often limits the cooperative stabilization of two distinct reactants. − Dual-atom catalysts (DACs) present a promising advance, as their paired metal sites, with tunable metal–metal interactions and coupled d-orbital systems, can partition the reduction of CO2 and N2 across adjacent centers. , This spatially differentiated activation better stabilizes key intermediates and alleviates the kinetic mismatch between the two molecules, thereby directly promoting the likelihood of C–N bond formation. Recently, Meng et al. demonstrated that Fe–Co–doped TiO2 achieves regioselective and cooperative activation of CO2 and N2 by simultaneously modulating proton–electron coupling transfer (PCET) and spin polarization, increasing the photocatalytic urea formation rate to 10.64 μmol g–1 h–1 and highlighting the substantial potential of bimetallic centers in light-driven C–N coupling. Despite these advances, the electronic structure and photoexcited behavior of DACs are critically governed by metal pairing and local coordination, while the photodynamics, the generation, recombination, and intermetal redistribution of charge carriers under illumination, remain poorly resolved. Consequently, prevailing mechanistic models for photocatalytic urea synthesis, which rely predominantly on static descriptors like steady-state adsorption energies and ground-state potential energy surfaces to describe substrate activation and intermediate (e.g., NCON) formation, , are fundamentally limited. These static approaches fail to explain key experimental observations, such as the strong dependence of product distributions on light intensity and temporal drifts in reaction selectivity. Unlike single-substrate photoreductions, urea synthesis requires that CO2 and N2 undergo coordinated geometric rearrangements while accepting directionally injected electrons. , A central, unresolved question is whether photoexcitation can simultaneously and controllably induce CO2 bending and N2 weakening, and how this coupled charge–geometry evolution dictates subsequent C–N bond formation. Addressing this demands a shift from static models to a real-time, excited-state monitoring framework. In this context, Z-scheme heterojunctions operate through a Z-schemed carrier flow: photogenerated low-energy electrons from one component recombine with low-energy holes from the other at the interface driven by the built-in electric field, while high-energy electrons and holes are preserved on their respective components. This selective recombination simultaneously maximizes both reductive and oxidative driving forces, an outcome inaccessible in a conventional type-II scheme. , Ultrasonication-assisted π–π assembly of 2D COF/g-C3N4 van der Waals heterojunctions has been reported to enhance visible-light H2 production by approximately 5-fold relative to pristine g-C3N4. Recently, Mortazavi and co-workers reported a porous nitrogen-rich monolayer, C7N6, whose pentagonal skeleton affords high structural robustness, pronounced visible-light absorption, , and well-defined N anchoring sitesmaking it an ideal platform for stabilizing dual-atom catalytic centers. Covalent triazine frameworks (CTFs) have been theoretically predicted to exhibit an even stronger driving force for water oxidation than g-C3N4. , Tailoring monomer structures and synthetic routes offers additional leverage to modulate the electronic structures and optical responses of CTFs, thereby boosting their photocatalytic activity. Notably, a C7N6/CTF van der Waals heterojunction establishes tunable band offsets and a built-in interfacial electric field that promotes directional charge separation and transfer. This energy-resolved charge regulation is conducive to maintaining high redox potentials and enabling unidirectional electron injection into dual-metal active sites, thereby synergistically enabling dual activation of CO2 and N2 at adjacent catalytic centers.

Herein, we theoretically design a series of early transition-metal dual-atom centers embedded within a C7N6/CTF van der Waals heterostructure. Based on our systematic screening, we employed the resultant Ti–Ti (homonuclear) and Ti–V (heteronuclear) pairs as representative models to elucidate how excited-state processes govern cooperative CO2 and N2 activation. Simulation analysis reveals that the heteronuclear Ti–V center supports a spin-symmetric trap state, mediating efficient carrier capture–release cycles. This dynamic converts the inherent type-II band alignment into a Z-scheme charge-transfer route, which under illumination drives a directional π–d–π* cross-metal electron flux. The consequent light-induced charge redistribution orchestrates the simultaneous bending of CO2 and weakening of N2, yielding preactivated configurations that lower the barrier for C–N bond formation while inhibiting side-reactions toward CO and NH3. These results provide a mechanistic framework where heteronuclear DACs exploit trap-state engineering in concert with excited-state charge transport to direct cooperative multimolecule activation, advancing the potential for selective photocatalytic urea synthesis.

Results and Discussion

Screening and Structural Stability of Dual-Atom Catalytic Centers

Fundamentally, the photocatalytic urea synthesis proceeds via coupled CO2 reduction and carbon–nitrogen coupling pathways, as schematized in Figure a. The initial protonation of CO2 branches into two competing intermediates: carboxyl (*COOH), which evolves into the pivotal *CO precursor for C–N coupling, and bidentate formate (*HCOO), which diverts to formic acid. Achieving high urea selectivity requires a dual-atom active site that thermodynamically stabilizes *COOH while suppressing the *HCOO pathway. The √3 × √3 supercell structures of monolayer C7N6 and CTF are shown in Figure S1. The optimized C7N6/CTF van der Waals heterostructure exhibits an in-plane lattice constant of 12.17 Å, an interlayer spacing of 3.41 Å, and a lattice mismatch of only 3%. The exothermic interface formation energy of −1.67 eV indicates strong interlayer affinity, consistent with experimental assembly routes for layered COF heterostructures driven by π–π stacking and van der Waals interactions. To enable molecular-level coactivation of CO2 and N2, we systematically screened all 15 homonuclear and heteronuclear dual-atom combinations M1–M2 (M = Ti, V, Cr, Mo, W), comprising early transition metals from the 3d, 4d, and 5d series, anchored at the central pore of the C7N6 scaffold (Figure b). These metals possess moderately filled d orbitals, flexible oxidation states, and an inherent propensity for metal–metal bonding, enabling them to simultaneous coordinate O-containing CO2 intermediates while providing effective back-donation into the π*­(N2) orbital to weaken the NN bond. , Each metal atom is chelated by two pyridinic nitrogens of the C7N6 framework, forming a planar MN2 coordination environment that preserves structural integrity while maximizing substrate accessibility (Figure S2). Because the standard PBE functional underestimates the strong electronic correlation associated with localized d electrons in transition metals, leading to inaccuracies in energetics and electronic-structure predictions, we employed the DFT+U method with U values obtained from the linear-response approach to more reliably capture on-site Coulomb interactions (Figure S3). − A hierarchical four-step screening workflow (Figure S4a) was then used to assess (i) structural stability, (ii) metal–metal cooperativity, (iii) favorable N2/CO2 coadsorption that suppresses hydrogen evolution, and (iv) thermodynamic preference for the C-bound *COOH intermediate over the competing *HCOO route. All candidates exhibit negative formation energies, confirming synthetic feasibility. However, Ti–Mo, Ti–W, and V–V have electrochemical dissolution potentials of −0.16, −0.03, and −0.18 V vs SHE, respectively, which fall below the required stability threshold and thus fail to meet the electrochemical stability criterion (Figure S4b). The V–Mo, V–W, Cr–Cr, and V–Cr pairs show moderate cooperativity but are unsuitable for selective urea synthesis because they thermodynamically favor the competing *HCOO pathway (Figure c). Notably, Ti–Ti and especially Ti–V exhibit pronounced negative synergy (–0.73 to –0.92 eV) and a clear thermodynamic bias toward *COOH, with ΔG*COOH lower than ΔG*HCOO by 0.28 eV for Ti–Ti and 0.24 eV for Ti–V. Additionally, both Ti–Ti and Ti–V preferentially coadsorb N2 and CO2 at the active site while significantly suppressing the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (Figure S4c), establishing them as promising dual-atom candidates for selective photocatalytic urea synthesis.

Optimized geometries show that the metal–metal separations are 2.53 Å for Ti–Ti and 1.99 Å for Ti–V (Figure S5), both falling within the typical range of dual-atom configurations and providing sufficient space for C–N bond formation at the shared interface. , A 10 ps AIMD simulation confirms that the total energy and temperature remain well maintained throughout, with no discernible bond elongation or cleavage observed (Figure S6). Further AIMD simulations under representative operating conditions (500 K vacuum, 300 K aqueous, and 300 K aqueous with coadsorbed *CO2 and *N2) corroborate this finding: the RMSD of C–N framework atoms converges to stable plateau values below 0.82 Å with no discontinuities throughout all trajectories, and the metal–metal RDF peaks retain their positions and peak shapes without significant variation across different solvation environments and adsorbate loadings (Figures S7,S8), indicating that neither the C–N framework nor the dual-atom active-site geometry undergoes appreciable structural degradation under realistic catalytic conditions. Mechanical stability is crucial for handling catalysts and ensuring operational durability. Elastic-stiffness analysis (Table S1) confirms that all systems satisfy the Born–Huang stability criteria (C11 > 0 and C11 > |C12|). The pristine C7N6/CTF heterostructure exhibits E2D = 156.3 N·m–1, G2D = 56.2 N·m–1, and ν = 0.390 (Figure d). Incorporating Ti–Ti causes a slight in-plane stiffening (E2D = 161.4 N·m–1; G2D = 57.8 N·m–1), whereas Ti–V reduces the moduli to E2D = 146.1 N·m–1 and G2D = 50.8 N·m–1 while increasing ν to 0.438, indicating enhanced lattice deformability without compromising mechanical integrity. Consistent with these observations, CN-supported dual-metal centers exhibit mechanical robustness and align with experimentally established strategies for stabilizing metal species on CN frameworks, suggesting their suitability for incorporation into practical catalyst architectures. , Metal leaching and aggregation are critical factors governing the long-term durability of DACs. To assess their chemical robustness, metal-desorption kinetics were simulated at the solid–liquid interface using a slow-growth approach within AIMD (Figure e,f). Here, the collective variable (CV) was defined as the displacement of the Ti or V atom away from the anchoring site during leaching. The free energy increases monotonically as the metal atom detaches from its coordination site, yielding desorption barriers of 3.51 eV for Ti and 4.83 eV for V. These high barriers signify strong resistance to dissolution under typical working potentials, confirming that Ti–Ti and Ti–V dual-atom sites are structurally robust platforms for mechanistic studies of photocatalytic urea synthesis.

Dual-Atom–Induced Electronic Reconstruction and Interfacial Built-In Fields

Following the identification of active dual-atom motifs, projected density of states (PDOS) analysis was employed to investigate their influence on the interfacial electronic structure and excited-state carrier landscape. Electronic structure analysis reveals that the pristine C7N6/CTF interface adopts a spin-symmetric type-II band alignment, featuring a reduced band gap of 0.92 eV (Figure a). This narrowing relative to the isolated monolayers (Figure S9) promotes improved light absorption. A spatial charge separation is indicated by the valence-band maximum (VBM) and conduction-band minimum (CBM) being contributed by the C7N6 and CTF components, respectively (Figure S10). Anchoring the homonuclear Ti–Ti dual-atom center induces a manifold of spin-polarized in-gap states (Figure b). These states originate from hybridization between Ti-3d orbitals and the σ-type lone-pair orbitals of the coordinating pyridinic nitrogen, further modulated by direct Ti–Ti interactions that produce bonding-antibonding splitting (Figure S11). Due to exchange splitting and local crystal-field effects, a pronounced spin asymmetry emerges. Two well-resolved in-gap peaks appear at 0.34 and 0.86 eV above the VBM in the spin-up channel, compared to a single feature at 0.58 eV above the VBM in the spin-down channel. This distribution of shallow-to-intermediate trap states establishes multiple capture pathways, laying the electronic foundation for subsequent trap-mediated ultrafast recombination of low-energy carriers. Notably, the heteronuclear Ti–V center introduces a single, nearly spin-symmetric in-gap state centered at 0.41 eV above the VBM (Figure c). This shallow state arises from heteronuclear d–d hybridization driven by mild de-electronation of Ti and concomitant electron enrichment of V (Figure S12), which aligns their 3d levels. The distinct in-gap state configurations of homonuclear and heteronuclear dual-atom centers have direct consequences for spin-channel-dependent interlayer weak-carrier recombination dynamics. For Ti–Ti, the energetically split spin-up and spin-down trap levels create inequivalent capture pathways for the two spin channels, giving rise to asymmetric interlayer recombination kinetics. For Ti–V, the near-degeneracy of the spin-up and spin-down trap levels ensures that both channels experience comparable capture-release dynamics.

(a–c) Projected density of states of pristine C7N6/CTF, Ti–Ti@C7N6/CTF, and Ti–V@C7N6/CTF, with energy referenced to the Fermi level; (d–f) Time evolution of interlayer weak carrier capture–release recombination for the spin-up (↑) and spin-down (↓) channels of Ti–Ti@C7N6/CTF and for Ti–V@C7N6/CTF; (g–i) Electron–phonon spectral density distributions associated with different carrier transition channels.

The interfacial electric field (IEF) in semiconductor heterojunctions is the primary driving force for directional carrier migration along a Z-scheme pathway and is determined by the work-function contrast between the constituent layers. The much lower work function of C7N6 (3.80 eV) relative to CTF (5.52 eV) triggers spontaneous electron transfer from C7N6 to CTF (Figure S13), establishing an intrinsic interlayer IEF directed toward the CTF domain. The electrostatic-potential profile along the Z direction shows limited interfacial polarization in the pristine junction, with an interfacial potential drop of only 2.09 V (Figure S14). Upon incorporating the Ti–Ti dual-atom motif, this potential difference increases to 6.38 V, indicating that metal–nitrogen coordination induces substantial charge redistribution and strengthens the interlayer dipole moment. In the heteronuclear Ti–V system, the potential drop further increases to 8.07 V, consistent with the more pronounced differential charge transfer (Figure S15). The enhanced interfacial IEF in Ti–V thus enables more efficient separation of photogenerated carriers with high redox capability, thereby facilitating the activation of surface-adsorbed inert molecular species.

Trap-State–Mediated Excited-State Carrier Dynamics and Z-Scheme Transfer

The trap state of the dual-atom center established by PDOS analysis provides a thermodynamic basis for Z-scheme carrier routing. However, whether this ground-state prediction is kinetically realized under photoexcitation, and specifically whether low-energy interfacial carriers are quenched rapidly enough to preserve the high-redox-potential electrons required for substrate activation, cannot be ascertained from static electronic structure alone and necessitates explicit time-domain analysis. Dual-atom incorporation concurrently narrows the optical gap by introducing metal-centered in-gap states and metal–nitrogen hybrid transitions, extending light harvesting across 350–800 nm into the near-infrared (Figure S16), thereby providing the photon flux that drives the subsequent carrier dynamics. Excited-state carrier dynamics further reveal how Ti–Ti and Ti–V regulate interlayer recombination within the heterojunction (Figure d–f). In the pristine system, the interlayer recombination lifetime (63.4 ps) is markedly shorter than the intralayer recombination time (192 ps) (Figure S17), indicating that low-energy interfacial electron–hole annihilation dominates under the influence of a moderate built-in interfacial electric field. For effective Z-scheme operation, the migration of high-redox-potential carriers across layers must substantially outlive this recombination channel to support multielectron processes. However, in the pristine heterojunction, the interlayer migration times for electrons (15.17 ps) and holes (20.39 ps) are markedly faster than the weak-carrier recombination time. Such migration-dominated, low-energy recombination is a hallmark of type-II heterojunction, which funnels carriers into premature loss channels and limits the overall photocatalytic efficiency. Introduction of the Ti–Ti dual-atom center shifts the recombination behavior from interfacial band-edge recombination to a trap-assisted mechanism. In the spin-up channel, two in-gap traps generate a cascade capture–stepwise release sequence (Figure d). Trap1 exhibits a capture time of 0.49 ps and a release time of 1.49 ps, whereas trap2 shows a capture time of 0.70 ps and a release time of 4.36 ps. Together, these states compress the weak-carrier recombination lifetime to 3.21 ps and rapidly drain low-energy carriers accumulated at the interface. In the spin-down channel, a single trap with capture and release times of 0.72 and 3.59 ps yields a slightly longer recombination lifetime of 4.52 ps (Figure e). The resulting spin-dependent trap asymmetry produces distinct recombination times for the two spin channels, reflecting the influence of exchange splitting and local orbital perturbations. In contrast, the heteronuclear Ti–V center approaches a nearly spin-symmetric trap regime (Figure f). A single trap governs the process, with capture and release times of 0.71 and 2.72 ps, corresponding to a weak-carrier recombination lifetime of 4.24 ps. The shallow depth and spin symmetry of this trap support an energetically balanced and reversible capture–release cycle under illumination, effectively shifting the interfacial carrier-transfer behavior from a conventional type-II mode toward a Z-scheme charge-separation mechanism (Figure S18). With low-energy carriers recombining rapidly, high-energy electrons in C7N6 and holes in CTF remain on their respective sides of the Z-scheme junction for longer times, enabling stronger redox driving forces. Such long-lived, high-energy carriers are critical for cooperative photoactivation of CO2 and N2 and for promoting C–N bond formation.

After establishing the trap-mediated carrier dynamics, we further examined how the trap states regulate phonon-assisted carrier migration along the Z-scheme pathway. Temporal fluctuations in carrier energy serve as a measure of electron–phonon interactions and thus reflect the extent of nuclear motion involved. Fourier-transform (FT) analysis of energy trajectories was employed to identify the phonon modes participating in the dynamics. Figure g–i presents the corresponding FT spectra associated with electron–hole migration and recombination processes in the diatomic-modified C7N6/CTF heterojunction. In the direct recombination pathway, the dominant low-frequency peak of ∼100 cm–1 corresponds to the interlayer shear mode at the C7N6/CTF interface (Figure S19). Mid- and high-frequency vibrational modes (∼500 cm–1 and 800 cm–1), associated with the out-of-plane vibrational modes of C atoms in the C7N6 layer, are essential for the interlayer transfer of photogenerated electrons (holes). In the spin-up channel of Ti–Ti, high-frequency skeletal vibrations in the 700–900 cm–1 range provide additional vibrational coupling, facilitating carrier capture and transfer between the trap states, maintaining the continuity of the cascade trap pathway. Meanwhile, Ti–N bending and in-plane breathing modes centered around 350 cm–1 support carrier release. In the spin-down channel, the spectral weight associated with the trap state decreases markedly near 820 cm–1, reducing the phonon-assisted coupling between the trap and the band-edge states and consequently slowing the release process. For Ti–V, carrier relaxation involving the trap state is primarily assisted by Ti–N and V–N coordination modes near 400 cm–1. In addition, a newly introduced high-frequency out-of-plane skeletal vibration at approximately 780 cm–1 provides further phonon compensation (Tables S2–S7). This vibrational phase space, characterized by mid-frequency coupling and high-frequency compensation, enables rapid trap capture and release, thereby maintaining the temporal robustness required for Z-scheme recombination dynamics.

Electron–vibronic interactions give rise to both elastic and inelastic scattering, which influence electron–hole recombination and carrier capture through distinct mechanisms. Inelastic scattering promotes carrier transitions between coupled electronic states while dissipating energy into phonons, a process directly governed by the nonadiabatic coupling (NAC). The time evolution and mean absolute values of the NAC associated with interlayer transfer and recombination pathways are summarized in Figure S20. Elastic scattering disrupts the coherent superposition of paired electronic states through NAC-mediated fluctuations, leading to decoherence; within photoresponse theory, this process is treated as pure dephasing. The dephasing times were obtained by fitting the autocorrelation functions to a Gaussian form, exp­(−0.5­(t/τ)2), as shown in Figure S21. According to Fermi’s golden rule, strong NAC enhances transition probabilities and accelerates recombination, whereas rapid decoherence suppresses quantum evolution, consistent with the quantum Zeno effect.

In pristine C7N6/CTF, the coupling between the CBM and VBM is weak (18.36 meV), and electron–hole dephasing occurs rapidly (7.41 fs), resulting in insufficient wave function overlap time to sustain efficient nonadiabatic energy exchange. In the spin-up channel of Ti–Ti (Ti–Ti↑), two trap states construct a cascade in which each transition maintains an enhanced nonadiabatic interaction. The NAC values increase stepwise along the sequence, reaching 68.71 meV for the CBM–trap2 transition, accompanied by an extended dephasing time of 27.48 fs. The progressive reinforcement of coupling and coherence across the cascade ensures that carrier capture, intertrap transfer, and final release occur within a well-defined temporal window, establishing a highly coordinated trap-mediated recombination pathway. In the spin-down channel of Ti–Ti (Ti–Ti↓), weak-carrier recombination proceeds through only two transitions: an initial trap–hole capture step followed by a CBM–trap release step. Although the CBM–trap coupling remains relatively strong (51.71 meV) with a longer dephasing time (23.36 fs), the faster decoherence of the trap–hole transition (15.64 fs) restricts the temporal overlap between the two steps. The Ti–V system exhibits a trap-capture NAC (31.41 meV) comparable to that of the Ti–Ti↓ channel, but the CBM–trap transition associated with carrier release shows a stronger coupling (58.80 meV) and a longer dephasing time (24.32 fs). These features accelerate interlayer weak-carrier recombination while preventing long-lived trapping and, unlike Ti–Ti, avoid spin-selective behavior.

Electronic Origin of Cooperative CO2 and N2 Pre-Activation on Dual-Atom Sites

The foregoing dynamical analysis delineates the time scales over which photogenerated carriers partition between recombination and preservation, yet leaves unaddressed the orbital mechanism by which the surviving electron density is partitioned between the two coadsorbed substrates at the dual-atom centers. To assess whether the dual-atom centers possess an intrinsic tendency to cooperatively activate inert CO2 and N2, we first examined their static coadsorption electronic structures on Ti–Ti and Ti–V. The two sites exhibit markedly different charge-transfer patterns and orbital responses. On Ti–Ti sites, CO2 accepts 0.41 e– while N2 gains 0.24 e– (Figure a,b), indicating a donation bias toward CO2. In contrast, intermetallic polarization within Ti–V depletes Ti (Tiδ+) and enriches V (Vδ–), shifting their d-band centers in opposite directions. This redistribution reduces O-terminal electron accumulation on CO2 and increases the availability of empty Ti-3d states aligned with the C-terminal π manifold. Meanwhile, the electron-rich V center contributes an additional 0.05 e– to the π*­(N2) manifold, elongating the N–N bond from 1.10 to 1.19 Å. The C–O bond in CO2 is also extended by 0.13 Å. Bond-resolved −COHP analysis further corroborates this synergistic response (Figure c,e). Ti–V exhibits consistently lower −ICOHP values for both C–O (7.21 eV) and N–N (10.58 eV) than Ti–Ti (7.47 and 10.95 eV), indicating more pronounced bond weakening at the heteronuclear site. Both active sites operate through the same canonical π/σ→d donation and d→π* back-donation interactions, yetthe homonuclear Ti–Ti site engages in largely independent interactions at each metal atom, resulting in two modestly coupled, parallel activation channels. In Ti–V, the intrinsic asymmetry enables a trans-metal relay mechanism. Electrons from the C-terminal π­(CO2) orbital first hybridize with empty Ti-3d states, and are then relayed through Ti–V d–d coupling into the π*­(N2) manifold (Figure d).

Moreover, the PDOS further corroborates this mechanism (Figure f). Gas-phase CO2 exhibits a π–π* energy separation of 8.31 eV, whereas the π*­(N2) level lies even higher at 9.89 eV, underscoring the closed-shell inertness of both molecules. At Ti–Ti sites, the π orbitals of CO2 and N2 exhibit clear energetic overlap with Ti 3d states, consistent with π→d Ti donation and d Ti→π* back-donation that collectively weaken their core bonds. In Ti–V, the V 3d states show substantially greater energetic overlap with the antibonding π*­(N2) manifold, enabling more pronounced d V→π* back-donation. Meanwhile, both Ti 3d and V 3d states strongly couple near the Fermi level, suggesting the emergence of a cross-metal energy bridge that provides a more favorable electronic basis for cooperative CO2/N2 activation and coconversion. These static coadsorption results, while establishing the thermodynamic and orbital prerequisites, are inherently limited to the ground-state configuration. Whether photoexcitation translates this electronic asymmetry into a spatially directed, femtosecond-scale charge flux capable of synchronously distorting both CO2 and N2 toward reactive geometries before vibrational relaxation dissipates the photochemical driving force remains an open question that demands real-time dynamical resolution.

Photoinduced Charge–Geometry Coupling in Dual-Molecule Activation

Normally, static descriptors alone cannot determine whether illumination merely supplies energy or actively reshapes the electronic and geometric states of adsorbed molecules. To resolve these ultrafast processes, we applied real-time time-dependent density functional theory (RT-TDDFT), systematically validated for capturing interfacial photoexcitation, − to track the configurational evolution of CO2 at the Ti–Ti and Ti–V sites. In the dark, gaseous CO2 retains its intrinsic linear geometry. Upon illumination, however, its bending dynamics exhibit clear temporal segmentation. At the Ti–Ti site, CO2 undergoes a rapid initial response within the first 8 fs, during which the O–C–O angle-change rate (OCR) drops to – 0.12 °·fs–1 (Figure a), consistent with π* occupation and activation of the ν2 bending mode. Beyond 8 fs, the OCR gradually diminishes and reaches zero at 43 fs, yielding a steady-state bent configuration with an O–C–O angle of 160°. In contrast, the Ti–V site induces both a faster and deeper photoinduced bending response (Figure b). Within 14 fs, the OCR sharply decreases to −0.19 °·fs–1, driving an earlier departure from linearity and allowing CO2 to reach 160° substantially sooner than on Ti–Ti. When the OCR returns to zero at 36 fs, CO2 becomes stabilized in a markedly more bent geometry of 139°. Such time-resolved dynamics demonstrate that Ti–V not only triggers a more rapid initial response but also sustains a stronger structural driving force and a deeper excited-state basin throughout relaxation.

ng to slow activatioTo elucidate the transient charge response of CO2 under photoexcitation, we monitored its time-resolved Hirshfeld charges. Upon illumination, CO2 at both dual-metal sites shows net charge enrichment with superimposed temporal oscillations, evidencing the femtosecond-scale injection of photogenerated electrons from the metal centers (Figures c,S22). The pronounced heteronuclear polarization at the Ti–V site enables a remotely located linear CO2 molecule to approach the active region more rapidly and interact with the active site, thereby accelerating the population of the antibonding π* orbital and producing a more substantial negative shift in the overall molecular charge, a signature of enhanced electron-injection capability. During excitation, the charge associated with the C atom decreases only slightly, whereas the two O atoms remain negatively charged and display substantially larger and mutually inequivalent fluctuations, reflecting light-induced local polarization and preferential electron accumulation at the O termini. Because COOH formation involves protonation at the electron-rich O site along with weakening of the adjacent C–O bond, while HCOO formation requires C–H bond construction at a less electron-rich carbon center, the enhanced O-end electron accumulation increases the proton affinity at that site, rendering activated CO2 more likely to undergo O-terminal protonation to yield COOH as the first hydrogenated intermediate. This mechanistic picture is consistent with experimental observations that COOH is typically the first detectable intermediate in CO2 activation. , The linear correlation between the instantaneous Hirshfeld charge and the bending angle further confirms that π* occupation directly drives the geometric distortion of CO2 (Figure d). Relative to Ti–Ti, the tighter charge–angle coupling observed at the Ti–V site indicates a higher degree of femtosecond-scale synchrony between electron injection and vibrational response.

With its strong triple bond and closed-shell configuration, N2 exhibits pronounced chemical inertness, making its activation the primary bottleneck in nitrogen reduction. Along the same photoexcitation trajectory in which CO2 and N2 coexist near the active center, we next examined the ultrafast activation dynamics of N2. On Ti–Ti, the N–N bond undergoes a slight quasi-harmonic oscillation around 1.10 Å, with an amplitude of ∼0.06 Å and a vibrational period of ∼25 fs (Figure a). This behavior indicates weak population of the π*­(N2) orbital and only minor perturbation to the excited-state potential-energy surface, leading to sluggish activation. Notably, the average N–N bond length on Ti–V increases to 1.18 Å, with peak values approaching 1.28 Å (Figure b), near the range often associated with early-stage N–N hydrogenation (NNH/NNH2) intermediates. The instantaneous charge distribution of N2 on Ti–Ti is narrow with a median charge of about –0.015 e, whereas that on Ti–V is broader and shifted to more negative values, with a median of roughly –0.028 e (Figures c, S23), indicating a longer residence of photogenerated electrons in the π*­(N2) orbital. Such sustained occupancy is fully consistent with the deeper N–N elongation observed on Ti–V. These ultrafast simulations not only reveal the internal timing and energetic sequence of N2 activation, but also align with available in situ FTIR observations. Experimentally, dark-state FTIR spectra of MIL-101­(Fe) show only framework vibrations and no NN or NH x signatures, consistent with unactivated N2. Under illumination, however, δ­(N–H) bands at 1450–1550 cm–1 and a weakened NN absorption near 2100 cm–1 emerge, signaling the onset of hydrogenation and bond weakening. Together, these results establish that femtosecond π*­(N2) population and oscillatory N–N elongation are the microscopic origins of the experimentally observed bond weakening.

At the metal-center level, the two Ti atoms in Ti–Ti exhibit synchronous charge oscillations, indicating nearly identical photoexcitation responses (Figure d). In contrast, the instantaneous charges of Ti and V in Ti–V display strictly anticorrelated oscillations, demonstrating that the light field induces a dynamic and reversible electron-shuttling channel between the two metal centers (Figure e). Charge–dipole correlation analysis further reveals a light-induced reversal of interfacial polarization (Figures f, S24). In the dark state, electrons preferentially migrate from C7N6 to the CTF layer, resulting in an upward-oriented interfacial dipole component (μ z ). Upon illumination, interfacial electron transfer from CTF to C7N6 establishes the built-in electric field underpinning the Z-scheme junction, leading to accumulation of high-energy photoelectrons in the conduction band of C7N6; these electrons are subsequently injected into the Ti–V dual active center and the adsorbed molecules’ antibonding orbitals, thereby reversing the dipole direction and continuously increasing μ z as charge accumulates. This process shows that the light field can directly drive directional electron migration from the substrate to the reactant molecules and concurrently promote the coevolution of electron density and molecular geometry. Accordingly, the static π→d→π* energy-level bridge at the Ti–V site evolves into a time-resolved, trans-metal directional electron flow under illumination. This electron flow simultaneously injects electrons into the π*­(CO2) and π*­(N2) orbitals, synchronizing the two inert molecules into prebent and prestretched reactive configurations on the femtosecond time scale. Such light-induced cooperative activation is expected to lower the initial barriers for protonation and C–N coupling. It provides a mechanistic rationale for the experimentally observed superiority of heteronuclear dual-atom centers in activating otherwise inert molecules.

Catalytic Activity and Selectivity of Dual-Atom Sites toward Urea Formation

The femtosecond-resolved simulations establish that photoexcitation at the Ti–V center drives both substrates into preactivated geometries well beyond their thermal equilibrium configurations. Whether this photoinduced activation translates into kinetically accessible and chemoselective C–N coupling under realistic aqueous conditionsand whether competing reduction channels toward CO and NH3 are effectively suppressedrequires explicit free-energy characterization of the downstream reaction network. The overall reaction network for cooperative CO2 and N2 reduction toward urea formation (Scheme ) accordingly involves two parallel activation pathways examined here under explicit solvation: CO2 accepts electrons to form a bent *CO2 –-like intermediate, which is protonated to *COOH and subsequently converted into the reactive *CO species. Meanwhile, N2 undergoes progressive NN bond weakening and stepwise hydrogenation, generating various N-containing intermediates that are poised for subsequent C–N coupling or further proton–electron transfer steps. Guided by previous mechanistic studies, two distinct C–N coupling routes were examined: an early formation path, in which *CO directly couples with *N2 to form *NCON followed by PCET hydrogenation to *H2NCONH2, and a late-stage coupling path, which follows a sequential hydrogenation-first mechanism where N2 is hydrogenated to *H2NNH2 before coupling with *CO to yield *NH2CONH2. Together, these complementary mechanismsone establishing the C–N bond early and the other utilizing a more reactive hydrogenated nitrogen species converge on urea formation.

The reaction free-energy profiles reveal distinct mechanistic routes for cooperative CO2/N2 conversion on Ti–Ti and Ti–V centers (Figure a,b). Adsorption of *CO2 and *N2 is exergonic (-1.32 and -1.56 eV, respectively), indicating favorable binding at both sites. However, the heteronuclear Ti–V center exhibits superior performance: its charge polarization promotes asymmetric substrate activation, reflected in consistently lower barriers for the initial PCET steps converting *CO2 to *CO (+0.18 and +0.14 eV). A decisive divergence occurs at the early C–N coupling step. On Ti–Ti, CO–N2 coupling to form *NCON is endergonic (+0.35 eV), constituting the thermodynamic rate-limiting step. In contrast, the same step becomes mildly exergonic on Ti–V (−0.14 eV). This shift stems from heteronuclear d-level modulation: the electron-deficient Ti center enhances the electrophilicity of *CO, while the electron-rich V center preweakens the NN bond, cooperatively lowering the barrier for C–N bond formation. The hydrogenation of *NCON is thermodynamically favorable on both catalysts but proceeds with a consistently deeper free-energy descent on Ti–V. On Ti–Ti, hydrogenation to *NCONH is exergonic by 0.45 eV, while distal hydrogenation to *NCONH2 incurs a slight 0.17 eV increase. Subsequent rearrangement to *HNCONH stabilizes the system by an additional 0.18 eV, followed by moderately exergonic steps to *NHCONH2 (−0.14 eV) and finally *NH2CONH2 (−0.18 eV). Urea desorption requires only a 0.27 eV increase, yielding a moderately exergonic overall profile for Ti–Ti. In contrast, Ti–V exhibits a more favorable thermodynamic trajectory. The transformations from *NCON to *NCONH and then to *HNCONH release 0.27 and 0.55 eV, respectively. Protonation to *NHCONH2 further deepens the free-energy well by 0.35 eV, and the final step to *NH2CONH2 requires a modest 0.27 eV input. Urea desorption entails a similarly low energy cost of 0.29 eV. Overall, Ti–V not only provides stronger stabilization for urea-forming intermediates but also maintains a low barrier for product release, resulting in a smoother and more thermodynamically driven reaction pathway.

In the hydrogenation-first branch of the late-stage coupling pathway, adsorbed N2 undergoes successive proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps, progressively weakening the NN bond and generating hydrogenated nitrogen intermediates. The initial hydrogenation to *NNH is strongly exergonic on both Ti–Ti (−0.49 eV) and Ti–V (−0.64 eV) sites. Subsequent PCET leads to *NNH2 or *HNNH formation, with their relative stabilities diverging between the two centers: Ti–Ti favors the alternating intermediate (−0.22 eV), whereas on Ti–V the distal route becomes mildly endergonic (+0.21 eV). Further hydrogenation proceeds with moderate barriers on Ti–Ti (+0.43 eV) and Ti–V (+0.35 eV) to form *NH2NH2. After complete nitrogen hydrogenation, *CO regeneration from *COOH requires a lower energy input on Ti–V (+0.12 eV) than on Ti–Ti (+0.30 eV). The final C–N coupling between *CO and *NH2NH2 exhibits a pronounced thermodynamic divergence: it is nearly thermoneutral on Ti–Ti (−0.08 eV) but strongly exergonic on Ti–V (−0.73 eV). This enhanced coupling propensity originates from the heteronuclear d-level alignment of the Ti–V center, which redistributes electron density between the two metals. This redistribution improves electronic compatibility between the *CO and *NH2NH2 termini, thereby lowering the energy cost of C–N bond reorganization. Overall, Ti–V accumulates thermodynamic advantages throughout both the hydrogenation sequence and the late-stage coupling step, resulting in a smoother free-energy descent and a more adaptable catalytic landscape. This cooperative electronic asymmetry endows the heteronuclear site with greater pathway flexibility and overall efficiency, making Ti–V a more superior active center for urea formation.

Given that photocatalytic urea synthesis proceeds in an aqueous medium, and that the C–N coupling step presents a substantial kinetic barrier in CO2/N2 coreduction systems, , this step was further examined under explicit water solvation to capture the hydrogen-bond reorganization and interfacial electrostatic screening that modulate its activation barrier. Explicit-solvent free-energy surfaces for the early (*CO + *N2) and late (*CO + *NH2NH2) C–N coupling pathways were constructed using nonequilibrium DFT–MD sampling along the reaction coordinate (Figure c,d). This method captures the evolution of the potential-energy surface, including electronic structural responses and solvent reorganization at the interface. On the Ti–Ti dual-atom site, the activation barrier for early and later C–N coupling are 0.79 eV and 0.34 eV, respectively.. In contrast, the corresponding barriers on Ti–V decrease to 0.66 and 0.27 eVrepresenting a consistent reduction across both pathways. This substantial lowering indicates that the heteronuclear site significantly accelerates C–N bond formation in explicit solvent, enabling a faster and lower-barrier urea-formation pathway. The observed enhancement can be attributed to the previously identified cross-metal charge-transfer channel, which persists into the reactive manifold. This channel acts as a steady-state feedback interaction, stabilizing the coupling geometry and reducing the reorganization penalty within the solvated environment.

To quantitatively evaluate the intrinsic kinetics of the C–N coupling step, turnover frequencies (TOFs) were estimated using transition-state theory (TST) at 298 K according to TOF=kBThexp(−δEkBT)[/s] , where k B is the Boltzmann constant, h is Planck’s constant, and δE is the energy barrier for C–N coupling. , For the early-stage C–N coupling, the intrinsic TOFs are 9.75 × 10–6 s–1 on Ti–Ti and 0.40 s–1 on Ti–V, while for the late-stage coupling, the corresponding values are 1.67 × 10–3 s–1 and 13.4 s–1, respectively, indicating markedly accelerated C–N bond formation upon V incorporation. Owing to the exponential dependence of TOF on the activation free-energy barrier, even moderate barrier reductions translate into substantial rate enhancements. Furthermore, the catalytic selectivity of the DACs was further examined by assessing potential competing reaction pathways relative to the C–N coupling route (Figure a, b). Starting from the *CO + *NH2NH2 precursor, the competing reaction routes were analyzed: On the CRR (CO2 reduction) branch, *CO desorption and *CO protonation to *COH require 0.47 and 1.13 eV on Ti–Ti, and 0.59 and 0.67 eV on Ti–V, respectively. Although protonation is somewhat eased on Ti–V, the overall CRR pathway remains energetically uphill, with its final states substantially higher than the thermodynamic wells stabilized by C–N coupling (−0.07 eV on Ti–Ti; –0.73 eV on Ti–V). On the NRR (N2 reduction) branch, protonation-assisted cleavage of *NH2NH2 to *NH2 and NH3 is thermodynamically favorable but kinetically hindered, with high activation barriers of 1.19 eV on Ti–Ti and 1.31 eV on Ti–Vboth significantly exceeding the late C–N coupling barrier on Ti–V (0.69 eV). Moreover, on Ti–V, the resulting NH3 state lies 0.50 eV above the urea precursor, indicating the absence of a comparably stabilized thermodynamic minimum. Collectively, these results show that Ti–V disfavors both CRR and NRR relative to C–N coupling from both kinetic and thermodynamic perspectives, thereby rendering C–N bond formation the most accessible pathway toward urea generation.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrate how heteronuclear dual-atom engineering, integrated with a C7N6/CTF heterostructure, enables cooperative CO2/N2 activation and selective C–N coupling. Among all screened configurations, the Ti–V dual-atom center stands out by constructing a nearly spin-symmetric in-gap trap state. High-frequency electron–phonon modes together with slow decoherence facilitate rapid capture–release of weak carriers across layers, thereby reconfiguring the system into a Z-scheme migration pathway with enhanced redox capability. Under photoexcitation, a cross-metal π–d–π dynamic electron flux emerges, rapidly bending CO2 and weakening N2 to generate preactivated geometries conducive to subsequent protonation and coupling. In explicit solvent, Ti–V substantially lowers the barriers for both early and late C–N coupling, while the formation pathways of CO and NH3 remain suppressed. Overall, combining heteronuclear dual-atom centers with Z-scheme trap-state regulation unlocks a multimolecule activation regime inaccessible to ground-state descriptors and provides a new design strategy for photocatalysts toward urea synthesis.
