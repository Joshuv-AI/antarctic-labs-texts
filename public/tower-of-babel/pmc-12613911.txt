Malnutrition among children under 7 years of age linked to ascariasis: evidence from West Sumba, Indonesia, despite ongoing deworming program
Authors: Rizqiani Amalia Kusumasari, Hanindyo Adi, Tri Baskoro Tunggul Satoto, Abdul Wahab, E. Elsa Herdiana Murhandarwati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil-transmitted helminth (STH) diseases persist in various Asian nations, including Indonesia. The West Sumba District, located in East Nusa Tenggara Province, Indonesia, has been implementing a deworming program targeting children under 7 years of age since 2017. This study aims to assess the prevalence, intensity, and distribution of soil-transmitted helminthiasis and its association with nutritional status among children under 7 years of age in West Sumba District.MethodsIn this community-based cross-sectional study, children under 7 years of age registered at integrated health posts in eight primary health facilities in West Sumba District were randomly sampled (443 subjects) from October 2022 to January 2023. Kato-Katz methods were applied to detect STH infections. Malnutrition was determined using a Height-for-Age table with a Z-score (HAZ).ResultsThe prevalence of STH in children under 7 years of age was 32.5% (144/443). Kota Waikabubak sub-district had the highest STH cases, with Trichuris trichiura (34.0%; 49/144)) and Ascaris lumbricoides (27.8%; 40/144) being the two common species. The following variables have been significant risk factors for STH infection: children aged > 2 years (OR: 5.9, 95% CI: 3.64-9.55, p < 0.001), parents with basic (OR: 1.8, 95% CI: 1.07–2.73, p < 0.001) and no educational level (OR: 4.1, 95% CI: 2.30–7.03, p < 0.001), family with monthly income < 2.5 million rupiahs (OR: 5.6, 95% CI: 1.70-18.78, p < 0.001), a preference for ground-based play (OR: 5.2, 95% CI: 2.65–10.04, p < 0.001), the presence of a private toilet at home (OR: 3.4, 95% CI: 2.22–5.19, p < 0.001), and have no septic tank for final disposal of stool (OR: 3.3, 95% CI: 1.91–5.75, p < 0.001). Malnutrition, indicated by stunted and severely stunted children with a range of cases from 5% to 37%, was strongly linked with A. lumbricoides infection (OR: 3.45, 95% CI: 1.70–7.02, p < 0.001) occurring in Kota Waikabubak and supported by spatial map analysis.ConclusionsCurrent research indicates a notable link between STH infection, i.e. ascariasis, and stunting as a malnutrition status in children under 7 years of age in Kota Waikabubak, a densely populated capital city in the district’s centre. This finding addresses a key public health gap by providing evidence on the early burden of helminthiasis in low-resource tropical settings.

Background

Soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections remain a considerable health issue in nations with low to moderate mortality rates, with the highest prevalence observed in Sub-Saharan Africa, South America, and Asia, including Indonesia [1]. Over 350 million people were afflicted by parasitic helminths in 2015 [2]. Although this disease is rarely fatal or leads to death, STH infections can cause relatively high morbidity [3]. School-aged children are the most susceptible demographic to STH infections owing to their underdeveloped immune systems, propensity for outdoor play in contaminated environments, and inadequate hygiene standards [4].

In economically disadvantaged and resource-limited areas, soil-transmitted helminths (STHs) are a significant public health issue. Due to their growth, preschool-age children (PSAC) are more vulnerable to polluted settings. Cleanliness, water source integrity, and sanitation remain essential in reducing STH infections. It is found that children in families without toilets but with clean water have higher infection rates [5]. Also, handwashing before eating or after defecation significantly affects STH susceptibility [6]. Environmental exposure and child behaviour significantly contribute to STH transmission. Children who play in dirt or polluted soil are more likely to get STHs [7]. Walking barefoot on contaminated soils increases the likelihood of STH infections, especially for hookworm species [8]. PSAC diets must be monitored by their families. Unwashed or undercooked fruits and vegetables can contain STH eggs, which can cause intestinal helminthic infections [9].

Previous investigations have demonstrated that STH infections can induce anaemia, decreased food absorption, and stunted growth in children [1, 4]. Young people are especially vulnerable to malnutrition. Insufficient staple food consumption, meal screen time, and excessive supplementation put 20% of New Zealand PSAC at moderate to high risk of malnutrition [10]. About half of the PSAC are malnourished, according to recent studies. However, most developing nations still struggle with hunger, despite rising obesity rates [11, 12]. In 2022, on a global scale, data indicated that 148.1 million children below 5 years of age encountered stunting, 45 million children faced wasting, and 37 million children were classified as overweight [13, 14].

A systematic review in Indonesia on the paediatric population reported STH prevalence ranging from 12.5% to 56.5% depending on region and age group [15]. However, data specific to PSAC remains limited. In the Bandung district, West Java, the prevalence of STH among PSAC was less than 6.0%, with 5.7% in stunted children and 3.4% in non-stunted children (p = 0.156) [16]. A study in Nangapanda, Ende, East Nusa Tenggara, conducted in 2013–2014, revealed that 59.0% of 272 PSAC were infected, mainly with A. lumbricoides (47.4%), followed by T. trichiura (36.8%) and hookworm (9.2%) [4].

In this context, Geographic Information Systems (GIS) play a critical role by mapping the spatial distribution of infectious diseases, such as STHs, thereby supporting targeted interventions and informing public health strategies in regions where epidemiological data remain limited [11, 12, 17–19]. Building on this approach, the present study utilises GIS to determine the prevalence and spatial distribution of STH infections. It also examines associated risk factors and their correlation with malnutrition in children under 7 years of age in West Sumba District, East Nusa Tenggara, Indonesia, which is geographically located at coordinates − 9.56667 for latitude and 119.45 for longitude. Focusing on remote, data-scarce areas, this study addresses a significant public health gap in resource-constrained tropical settings and contributes essential evidence on the burden of early childhood helminthiasis.

Methods

Sample characteristics

This research used a community-based approach with a cross-sectional study design. Samples were taken using a random cluster sampling method based on a list of children registered at Integrated Health Service Posts (posyandu) in eight primary health care facilities across the West Sumba district. Samples from participants were taken from October 2022 to January 2023. The inclusion criteria comprised all PSAC between 0 and 7 years of age, whose parents consented to participate in the the collection of stool samples. Exclusion criteria included the inability to provide informed consent, incomplete stool samples, or damaged or unsuitable samples for laboratory analysis. The sample size was calculated using a two-proportion difference formula [20] with an alpha level of 5% and 80% power, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 435 participants.

West Sumba District has five sub-districts: Kota Waikabubak, Lamboya, Loli, Tana Righu, and Wanokaka. Sampling was performed using the cluster random sampling technique, encompassing eight designated primary health centres (Fig. 1).

Data, stool sample collection, and assessment of malnutrition

Health centre personnel and local cadres convened the selected children together with their mothers or caregivers to secure informed consent for participation in the study. Following consent, anthropometric measurements were conducted on the children, caregivers were interviewed, and stool samples were collected from the participants’ residences on the subsequent day in a plastic stool collection container (60 mL). Assessment of malnutrition in children under 7 years using anthropometric measurements, based on age and body size. These anthropometric standard measures child growth and diet. According to WHO growth standards, anthropometric measures were used to calculate Z-scores for height-for-age (HAZ) [21]. Stool samples were examined for species identification (direct method) and egg density per species (Kato Katz method) [22, 23] by technicians from an ISO/IEC 17,025-accredited laboratory [24]. The Kato Katz examination was conducted twice, and the mean values were recorded.

Data processing and analysis

Parasitological data—including species type, mean egg per gram (EPG), and intensity level for each STH species—along with data on potential risk factors were included in the analysis. These factors comprised socio-demographic variables (age, sex, parental education, parental occupation, monthly household income, and number of siblings); behavioural factors (eating habits, handwashing practices, playing on soil, and use of footwear while on the ground); and environmental conditions (availability of clean water for handwashing and bathing, access to latrines, and stool disposal methods). All data were entered using Epicollect5 (https://five.epicollect.net), and statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/MP version 14.2 by StataCorp.

Spatial distribution of STH

Quantum GIS (QGIS) 3.34 Prizren software, available from https://qgis.org/, was used to assess the spatial distribution of STH, utilising EPSG 4236–WGS 1984 coordinates. Georeferenced on the Indonesian map, QGIS converted text files to shapefiles. The heat map shows infection rates of helminthiasis and malnutrition status (stunted and severely stunted) among participants.

Statistical analysis

Bivariate analysis, using the chi-squared (χ²) test, assessed the associations between STH infection, malnutrition, and other individual risk factors, identifying those with a p value < 0.25 for multivariate inclusion. Multivariate analysis was then used to assess the association between STH infection and malnutrition, adjusting for factors such as age, sex, economic status, and hygiene. Results were reported as Odds Ratios with corresponding p-values, which assessed the odds of infection and/or malnutrition.

Logistic regression was used due to the categorical data of the dependent variable (with two categories) and the inclusion of multiple independent variables in the model. Modelling with logistic regression analysed the relationship between nutritional status with independent variables simultaneously (adjusted) to obtain a fit model for predicting nutritional status in children under 7 years old. In this analysis, the Adjusted-Odds Ratio (Adj-OR) was used to obtain the strength of the relationship.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

The characteristics of the participants (443) are described in Table 1. A majority of the children ( 55.9% ) were > 2 years old (n = 248), reflecting typical weaning patterns, indicating reduced maternal immunity and increased risk of infection. Over half of the participants (51.6%) were male (n = 228), suggesting a balanced sex distribution unlikely to confound outcomes. Parental education was polarised, with 41.1% (n = 182) completing elementary-level, 49.4% (n = 179) tertiary education, and 18.5% (n = 82) having no formal education, highlighting disparities that might impact health-related behaviours. Slightly more parents engaged in soil-related occupations (52.1%, n = 231), suggesting increased potential for environmental exposure to STH. The majority of families had income below the regional minimum wage, which potentially limits access to proper sanitation. Household size was balanced, with 50.5% (n = 224) having 1–2 siblings and 49.4% (n = 219) having more than 2 siblings, indicating a similar caregiving context.

Prevalence and distribution of STH infections

Among all samples collected, 144 participants (32.5%) were positive for STH infection (Table 2). Among those, 65.9% (95/144) were single infections, while 34.0% (49/144) were mixed infections. Single infections were predominantly trichuriasis (34%), followed by ascariasis (27.7%) and hookworm infection (4.2%). The predominant mixed infection was A. lumbricoides and T. trichiura (31.3%) with a small proportion involving T. trichiura and hookworm (1.4%) and cases involving all three species (1.4%).

Intensity of STH infections

As shown in Table 2, among STH infections detected, T. trichiura was the most frequently detected species (n = 98, 50.3%), followed by A. lumbricoides (n = 87, 44.6%), and hookworm (n = 10, 5.1%). Analysis of infection intensity indicated that most cases across all species were classified as light. Infections caused by A. lumbricoides showed a broader distribution of intensity, with 57.5% being light, 36.8% being moderate, and 5.7% being heavy, indicating a higher worm burden in a significant proportion of cases. In contrast, T. trichiura infections were predominantly light (91.8%), with only 8.2% classified as moderate, and no heavy infections were detected, suggesting a generally lower intensity profile. All positive hookworm infections were classified as light, reflecting limited worm burden in the affected participants.

Distribution of STH infections by participant characteristics and risk factors

Distribution of STH infections in relation to socio-demographic, behavioural, and environmental variables across sub-districts in West Sumba is presented in Table 3. Children over two years of age were significantly more likely to be infected than those < 2 years (OR = 5.9, 95%CI = 3.63–9.55, p = 0.001), suggesting increased exposure following breastfeeding cessation. Parents with no formal education and income were both linked to higher infection risks in children (OR = 4.1, 95%CI = 2.30–7.03, p = 0.001 and OR = 5.6, 95%CI = 1.70–18.78, p < 0.05). Having more than two siblings slightly raised infection risk (OR = 1.3, 95%CI = 0.88–1.97, p = 0.170), but not statistically significant. Children’s preference for playing on the ground indicates a substantial rise in the risk of STH infection (OR = 5.2, 95%CI = 2.65–10.04, p = 0.001). Improper disposal of stool into the surrounding environment significantly increases the risk of infection (OR = 3.3, 95%CI = 1.91–5.75, p = 0.001). Interestingly, no significant association were found for occupational soil exposure (OR = 0.9, 95%CI = 0.66–1.48, p = 0.980), or gender OR = 0.9, 95%CI = 0.62–1.38, p = 0.720). Hygiene-related behaviour, such as handwashing with soap and eating with cutlery, did not appear to lower infection rates. Likewise, environmental factors, such as the availability of clean water, did not demonstrate a protective effect.

Association of STHs and malnutrition status

In the final multivariate model, variables with p < 0.25 were included, excluding the number of siblings due to a higher p-value. Table 4 shows a significant association between STH infection and stunting (HAZ), particularly for A. lumbricoides (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 1.70–7.02; p = 0.001) and mixed infections (OR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.10–4.65; p = 0.025). Age was also associated with stunting (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.12–2.51; p = 0.013), though its effect diminished after adjusting for STH status. Notably, only STH infection remained a consistent and statistically significant predictor of stunting in the final model. Other covariates showed minimal changes between crude and adjusted odds ratios, suggesting no meaningful association with malnutrition in this population.

Discussion

Prevalence of STH infections

In this study, the prevalence of STH in West Sumba among PSAC was 32.5%, higher than the Southeast Asia average for SAC (~ 25%) [25]. Our finding is comparable to a study conducted in another part of Indonesia, i.e. Suka Village in North Sumatra [26]. However, the prevalence of West Sumba is notably higher than that reported in Bandung District, West Java (6%) and central Aceh, where STH prevalence among toddlers was 9% [16, 27].

Species distribution of STH infections in West Sumba

In West Sumba, the three main STH species identified were T. trichiura (34.0%), A.lumbricoides (27.8%), and hookworm (4.2 4.%) and mixed infections observed in 34.03% of cases. When considering each species individually- regardless of whether they occurred alone or as part of mixed infection- the prevalence increased to 68.1% for T. trichiura, 60.4% for A. lumbricoides, and 6.9% for hookworm. Their distribution varied across sub-districts in both prevalence and intensity. T. trichiura was the most prevalent species among PSAC, with mostly light intensity and a few with moderate intensity. The highest number of T. trichiura positives was recorded in Lamboya district. T. trichiura is also known as the most resistant STH species to standard deworming with albendazole [28].

Although the prevalence of A. lumbricoides was not as high as T. trichiura, the proportion of individuals with moderate and heavy infection is higher in ascariasis (42.5%) compared to trichuriasis (8.2%)." The percentage 42.5% for A. lumbricoides is correspond to Table 2 (36.8% moderate plus 5.7% heavy); and trichuriasis is 8.2% moderate plus 0.0% heavy. The highest number of A. lumbricoides infections was documented from Kota Waikabubak. Ascariasis is known as a contributor to malnutrition and stunting due to its nutrient-depleting effects [29]. This is also supported by the fact that the Kota Waikabubak sub-district had the highest stunting rates compared to other areas.

As the least prevalent species in West Sumba, all hookworm infections were detected at light intensity. They were found exclusively in Tana Righu, Loli and Wanokaka sub-districts, likely linked to exposure to contaminated soil, especially among children who walk barefoot [8]. The presence of STH across West Sumba reflected poor sanitation, lack of hygiene and exposure to a contaminated environment, poor faecal disposal, and low hygiene practices [4].

The predominance of light and moderate intensities, with very few cases of heavy intensity, is likely due to the routine administration of the preventive deworming program targeting children under 7 years of age through the local integrated health posts [30]. However, while treatment is highly effective against A. lumbricoides, its cure rate for T. trichiura remains low [31]. Albendazole combined with ivermectin were proven to be more effective than albendazole alone (e.g., in Uganda) [32].

Risk factors for STH infection

The significant risk factors for STH infection include: children over 2 years old, parents with minimal or no education, families with a monthly income below the regional wage, a preference for ground-based play, and the absence of a septic tank for stool disposal. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in regions East Kupang [33], North Sumatra [26], and South East Nigeria [34] in PSAC and/or SAC populations.

Wearing footwear provides strong protection against STH infections, particularly hookworm [35]. However, uncontrolled confounding factors—such as environmental conditions, individual behaviours, or sanitation—may lead to a misleading interpretation of a lower infection risk among individuals who do not wear footwear [36], suggesting that contextual factors may distort the actual protective effect of footwear.

Contrary to expectation, this study found that individuals with access to potable water had a higher risk of STH infection. This suggests that access to clean water alone may not be a protective factor against STH infection. A study in Venezuela [35] reported that treated drinking water significantly reduced STH risk; however, poor water storage practices were associated with an increased risk of trichuriasis.

STH association with malnutrition

Our study reveals that there is an association between STH and malnutrition/stunting. Children with A. lumbricoides had three times higher risk of getting malnutrition, while those with T. trichiura or mixed infection had a ~ two times higher risk. STHs disrupt nutrient absorption by damaging the intestinal lining, leading to malabsorption syndrome and micronutrient and iron deficiencies [37]. Malnutrition can increase susceptibility to STH infections, while STH infections can also contribute to malnutrition, creating a vicious cycle between the two [29].

A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a strong association between STH infection and increased stunting risk in children, with an estimated 44% of stunting cases in Indonesia attributable to STH [38]. Ascaris lumbricoides was commonly reported as a STH species linked with stunting across various regions [29].

Spatial distribution and environmental factors

Heat map analysis showed that Kota Waikabubak had the highest prevalence of ascariasis, while Lamboya and Tana Righu sub-districts had the highest prevalence of trichuriasis and hookworm infections, respectively. Interestingly, these three areas also reported the most stunting cases in West Sumba, suggesting a possible link between high STH prevalence and child growth problems. However, STH infections were found in all sub-districts, indicating widespread transmission across the region.

West Sumba is characterised by densely populated settlements surrounded by rice fields and substantial land covered with greenery. These environmental conditions may support favourable conditions for the proliferation of STH worm eggs and larvae [39, 40, ]. Environmental factors, along with relevant risk factors and demographic characteristics—such as the continued practice of open defecation in the surrounding environment, as well as the lack of household toilets/latrines —may serve as important contributors to the sustained transmission of STH in this region.

Conclusion

STH infection prevalence among PSAC in West Sumba was 32.5%, exceeding regional averages, even though a deworming program has been ongoing since 2017. These infections are closely linked with malnutrition, perpetuating a cycle that further compromises child health. Despite the availability of toilets/latrine in the house, and proper final disposal of stool in PSAC, environmental contamination likely sustains transmission. The concurrence of high STH prevalence and stunting in certain areas underscores the urgent need for integrated interventions that combine effective treatment, education, improved sanitation, and environmental management.
