Association between serum uric acid to creatinine ratio with metabolic profile in subjects with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
Authors: Amr Ali Mohamed Abdelgawwad El-Sehrawy, Samer Saleem Alshkarchy, Ali Kamil Kareem, Marwah Suliman Maashi, Zahraa Abbas Al-Khafaji, Ahmed Hjazi, Chou-Yi Hsu, Wesam R Kadhum, Faria Jafarzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a prevalent chronic liver condition strongly linked with metabolic syndrome components. The serum uric acid to creatinine (SUA/Cr) ratio has recently emerged as a potential biomarker of metabolic dysfunction. However, its clinical relevance in individuals with NAFLD remains underexplored. The objectives of the current research was to investigate the association between the SUA/Cr ratio and metabolic risk factors in adults diagnosed with NAFLD.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 226 adults with ultrasonography-confirmed NAFLD (grades 1 and 2). Participants were categorized into tertiles based on their SUA/Cr ratio. Anthropometric indices, biochemical parameters (lipid profile, liver enzymes, glucose), and lifestyle factors were assessed. Differences across SUA/Cr tertiles were analyzed using ANOVA and general linear models adjusted for confounders.ResultsHigher SUA/Cr tertiles were associated with significantly elevated total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels (p = 0.011 and p < 0.001, respectively), and higher alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (p = 0.010), indicating hepatic injury. Central obesity indices (waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio) increased significantly across tertiles (p = 0.023 and p = 0.025, respectively), although BMI did not. SUA/Cr was not significantly associated with fasting blood glucose or triglycerides. These findings suggest a strong association between elevated SUA/Cr and an unfavorable metabolic and hepatic profile in NAFLD patients.ConclusionThe SUA/Cr ratio is a simple, non-invasive biomarker associated with dyslipidemia, liver enzyme elevation, and central obesity in NAFLD individuals. It may serve as a practical tool for early risk stratification and metabolic monitoring in this high-risk population. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to validate its role in NAFLD progression.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Serum uric acid to creatinine ratio, Metabolic dysfunction, Liver enzymes, Dyslipidemia, Central obesity

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder globally, affecting approximately 25–30% of the adult population worldwide [1, 2]. It encompasses a spectrum of liver conditions, from simple hepatic steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which may progress to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. NAFLD is defined by excessive fat accumulation in the liver in the absence of significant alcohol consumption or other identifiable causes of hepatic steatosis [4]. The etiology of NAFLD is multifactorial, involving insulin resistance, dysregulated lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, gut microbiota alterations, and chronic low-grade inflammation [5, 6]. NAFLD is strongly associated with components of metabolic syndrome, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia, and is increasingly regarded not only as the hepatic manifestation of metabolic dysfunction but also as a contributor to cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and other extrahepatic complications [7, 8].

Uric acid is the final oxidation product of purine metabolism in humans and is primarily excreted by the kidneys. While traditionally regarded as a mere metabolic waste product, increasing evidence suggests that uric acid plays a complex role in human physiology and pathology. On one hand, it functions as a potent antioxidant in plasma; on the other, elevated serum uric acid levels are associated with pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative effects that has great role in insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and adipose tissue inflammation [9, 10]. Hyperuricemia has been consistently linked to several components of metabolic syndrome, including abdominal obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [11]. However, uric acid levels are significantly influenced by renal clearance, making it difficult to interpret their significance without accounting for kidney function. Creatinine, a breakdown product of creatine phosphate in muscle, is widely used as a marker of glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Since creatinine levels are relatively stable and primarily excreted by the kidneys, they offer an indirect measure of renal function [12]. The uric acid to creatinine ratio (UACR) has therefore emerged as a valuable metric that reflects endogenous uric acid levels adjusted for kidney function [13]. Several studies have suggested that UACR may be a better indicator of metabolic risk than uric acid alone, as it normalizes for variations in renal excretion capacity [14, 15]. This ratio has been shown to correlate with systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk in various populations, and may serve as a practical, non-invasive biomarker for early identification of individuals at heightened metabolic risk, particularly in those with conditions like NAFLD.

Given the rising burden of NAFLD and its tight link to metabolic dysfunction, early identification of individuals at higher metabolic risk is essential for timely intervention. While liver biopsy remains the gold standard for assessing disease severity, its invasiveness and cost limit its use in clinical practice [3]. Therefore, non-invasive, cost-effective biomarkers capable of reflecting metabolic derangements in NAFLD are of high clinical interest [16]. Uric acid, a byproduct of purine metabolism, has been implicated in the development of insulin resistance, hypertension, and inflammation—key contributors to metabolic dysfunction [9, 17]. However, since serum uric acid levels can be influenced by renal function, the serum uric acid to creatinine ratio (UACR) has emerged as a more stable and informative index that accounts for kidney excretion capacity [18]. Recent studies suggest that UACR may serve as a better predictor of metabolic risk than uric acid alone [19]. Despite these observations, the potential of UACR to reflect metabolic complications in individuals with NAFLD has not been thoroughly investigated. This gap in evidence forms the basis of the current study, which aims to evaluate the association between UACR and metabolic risk factors in a population with NAFLD.

Given the close link between metabolic dysfunction and NAFLD, as well as the emerging utility of the uric acid to creatinine ratio (UACR) as a non-invasive biomarker of metabolic health, further investigation is warranted to clarify its role in this high-risk population. While previous studies have explored the association between serum uric acid and metabolic risk, limited data exist on the association of UACR with metabolic profile in individuals with NAFLD. Therefore, the present study was designed to examine the association between serum UACR and a range of metabolic risk factors among adults diagnosed with NAFLD. We hypothesized that higher UACR values would be significantly associated with an unfavorable metabolic profile, including markers of insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, elevated blood pressure, and central obesity.

Methods

Study design and participants

In the current cross-sectional a total of 226 subjects with confirmed NAFLD were enrolled (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

The diagnosis of NAFLD was established using abdominal ultrasonography, performed by a trained radiologist who was blinded to participants’ clinical and biochemical data. Hepatic steatosis was graded semi-quantitatively according to standard sonographic criteria, including increased echogenicity of the liver parenchyma relative to the renal cortex (liver–kidney contrast), clarity of intrahepatic vessel borders, and diaphragm visualization. Based on these criteria, NAFLD was classified as follows: Grade 1 (mild steatosis): slight diffuse increase in fine echoes in the liver parenchyma with normal visualization of the diaphragm and intrahepatic vessels; Grade 2 (moderate steatosis): moderate increase in fine echoes with slightly impaired visualization of intrahepatic vessels and the diaphragm. Individuals with grade 3 steatosis, known liver diseases, alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis or any other liver disease, renal impairment, defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m² or elevated serum creatinine (≥ 1.5 mg/dL in men, ≥ 1.3 mg/dL in women) [20], Participants receiving medications known to significantly alter uric acid or creatinine metabolism (e.g., diuretics, allopurinol, febuxostat) or drugs with known hepatotoxic/nephrotoxic effects were excluded. Those who underwent weight loss or any other dietary interventions in the past three months were also excluded. The use of other medications, including antihypertensive agents or statins, was recorded and reported as part of participants’ baseline clinical characteristics. Participants were consecutively recruited from outpatient clinical centers in Al-Kharj city between January and May 2025. This single-center design may reflect the demographic and lifestyle characteristics of the local population, which should be considered when generalizing the findings.

We excluded patients with grade 3 steatosis because it is typically associated with advanced hepatic fat accumulation, and more importantly, a higher risk of progressing to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Including patients with grade 3 may introduce heterogeneity in the sample, as their pathophysiological characteristics, metabolic profiles, and biomarker patterns often differ markedly from those with grade 1 or 2 [3, 21]. A more homogenous population (i.e., those with mild to moderate steatosis) helps improve internal validity and reduce potential confounding due to liver-related comorbidities. The study was performed in Al-Kharj city.

Anthropometric and diet assessments

Body weight and height were measured using standard protocols, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was also calculated. Body composition, including fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM), was assessed using InBody 770 device (InBody Co., Seoul, South Korea). Physical activity (PA) was evaluated using the metabolic equivalent of task (MET) scores derived from international physical activity questionnaire (IPAQ), which its validity and reliability was established before [22]. Smoking status (current smoker vs. non-smoker) was also recorded. Dietary intake information was collected using a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), designed to capture participants’ habitual diet over the previous year [23].

Biochemical assessments

After a 12-hour overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected from each participant under sterile conditions. Serum concentrations of uric acid and creatinine were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods with commercially available kits (e.g., DiaSys Diagnostic Systems GmbH, Holzheim, Germany), and the SUA/Cr ratio was calculated by dividing serum uric acid (mg/dL) by creatinine (mg/dL). Lab measurements were performed in a single certified laboratory to ensure consistency and accuracy. Other biochemical parameters—including fasting blood sugar (FBS), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)—were also measured using the same autoanalyzer (e.g., BT 1500, Biotecnica Instruments, Italy) and corresponding commercial kits. All laboratory measurements were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions and standard quality control protocols. Fasting serum insulin concentrations were measured using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Monobind Inc., Lake Forest, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Insulin resistance was estimated by the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as:

HOMA-IR = Fasting insulin (µU/mL) × Fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL)/405 [24].

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into tertiles based on their SUA/Cr ratio to ensure approximately equal group sizes, which enhances the statistical power to detect significant differences across groups. With a total sample size of 226, dividing participants into three groups provides around 75 individuals per tertile, which is adequate to detect medium effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.5) with 80% power at an alpha level of 0.05 in between-group comparisons [25]. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Differences across SUA/Cr tertiles were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables. For selected biochemical variables, a trend analysis across tertiles was performed using general linear models (GLM) after adjusting for potential confounding factors. Potential confounders for first model were selected based on prior evidence of their association with both SUA/Cr and the outcomes of interest, as well as significant differences across tertiles. The models were adjusted for age, sex, WHR and physical activity. Second model was further adjusted for smoking, disease grade, hypertension, family history of diabetes, CVD, hypertension and liver disease, medication and supplement use. P for trend values were derived from these adjusted models. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). To provide a data-driven justification for sample size we used between-group lipid differences reported by Ali et al. [26]. Reported values across serum uric acid quartiles were used to derive pooled within-group standard deviations and the SD of group means, from which Cohen’s f was calculated for metabolic parameters. Using a one-way ANOVA framework (α = 0.05, power = 0.80), the highest required total sample sizes was achieved for LDL ≈ 210 considering Cohen’s f of 0.2301, pooled SD of 39.16, SD of group means of 9.01. Our final recruited sample was set as n = 226 to cover the possible drop-outs. Results

A total of 226 participants with NAFLD were categorized into tertiles based on their serum uric acid to creatinine (SUA/Cr) ratio. Table 1 represents demographic and anthropometric variables across SUA/Cr tertiles. The mean ages across the tertiles were similar (p = 0.208), with no significant difference observed. However, the proportion of males increased significantly across tertiles, from 36% in the first tertile to 56% in the third tertile (p = 0.014). Anthropometric measures showed significant differences between tertiles: waist circumference (WC) increased from 100.28 ± 10.02 cm in the first tertile to 104.36 ± 9.40 cm in the third tertile (p = 0.023). Similarly, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was significantly higher in the upper tertiles (p = 0.025). Body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly across groups (p = 0.340). Physical activity levels, expressed in METs, were significantly lower in the second and third tertiles compared to the first (p = 0.027). Fat-free mass (FFM) increased across tertiles from 54.46% to 60.09% (p = 0.004), while fat mass (FM) showed no significant differences (p = 0.114). Smoking status did not differ significantly across tertiles (p = 0.31). The distribution of NAFLD grade 2 also did not differ significantly (p = 0.315). There was no significant difference in dietary energy and macronutrients intake across tertiles of SUA/Cr. In comparison of clinical characteristics of study participants across tertiles of SUA/Cr, no significant difference was observed in NAFLD grade, the history of disease, medication or supplement use (Table 2). Comparison of biochemical variables across SUA/Cr tertiles (Table 3) showed no significant differences in FBS levels across SUA/Cr tertiles (p = 0.648). TC showed a significant increasing trend across SUA/Cr tertiles, rising from 174.47 ± 39.45 mg/dL in the lowest tertile to 191.93 ± 29.40 mg/dL in the highest (p = 0.011; p for trend = 0.007). TG and HDL-C did not differ significantly. Serum LDL-C levels significantly increased across tertiles, from 97.44 ± 28.07 mg/dL to 131.50 ± 29.27 mg/dL (p < 0.001). Similarly, ALT was significantly higher in the upper tertiles (p = 0.010). AST showed no significant difference (p = 0.167). Serum insulin concentrations was significantly lower in the highest versus lowest tertile only in the fully adjusted model (P = 0.026). While there was no significant difference for HOMA-IR across different tertiles of SUA/Cr. Serum uric acid concentrations differed significantly across tertiles (p = 0.001), consistent with the SUA/Cr categorization. Creatinine levels significantly decreased from the first tertile to the third tertile (p < 0.001), reflecting the inverse relationship in the ratio calculation. These significance levels remained after adjusting for multiple confounders (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that an elevated serum uric acid to creatinine (SUA/Cr) ratio is significantly associated with an unfavorable metabolic profile in individuals with NAFLD. Specifically, higher SUA/Cr levels were linked to increased total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol, elevated alanine aminotransferase indicative of hepatic injury, and greater central adiposity. These findings underscore the potential of the SUA/Cr ratio as a simple, yet powerful biomarker for identifying NAFLD patients at increased risk of cardiometabolic complications. Graphical summary of study findings

It should also be noted that participants’ habitual dietary intake was assessed, and no remarkable differences in major food group consumption were observed.

Our findings revealed a significant increase in TC and LDL-C levels across higher tertiles of the SUA/Cr, while TG and HDL-C remained statistically unchanged. These results suggest a close association between elevated SUA/Cr and dyslipidemia, particularly atherogenic lipid patterns. Uric acid has been shown to promote hepatic cholesterol synthesis via the upregulation of sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs), especially SREBP-2, leading to increased LDL receptor expression and cholesterol accumulation in hepatocytes [27]. In addition, hyperuricemia may impair endothelial nitric oxide availability, indirectly contributing to lipid abnormalities and cardiovascular risk [28]. The elevation in LDL-C and TC in individuals with higher SUA/Cr may reflect underlying oxidative and inflammatory processes contributing to lipid metabolism dysregulation in NAFLD.

Importantly, the clinical implications of NAFLD severity extend beyond liver health. Evidence indicates that even mild and moderate grades of steatosis are associated with early atherosclerotic changes. In this regard, Tarantino G. et al. [29] demonstrated that increasing liver fat content was independently associated with higher carotid intima–media thickness (cIMT), an established marker of subclinical atherosclerosis. In addition to the findings of Tarantino et al., other studies have further highlighted the relationship between NAFLD severity and early markers of atherosclerosis. Cai et al. [30] conducted a meta-analysis showing that individuals with NAFLD had significantly higher carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) compared to those without NAFLD, indicating an association between hepatic steatosis and early atherosclerotic changes. Moreover, Bhatia et al. [31] demonstrated that improvement in NAFLD severity was associated with a reduction in CIMT progression, suggesting that effective management of NAFLD may help mitigate cardiovascular risk. These findings support the concept that even mild to moderate grades of NAFLD, as assessed in our study, may reflect early subclinical vascular changes.

A significant elevation in ALT levels was observed among participants in higher SUA/Cr tertiles, whereas AST did not differ significantly. ALT is a sensitive biomarker of hepatocellular injury and is often elevated in early NAFLD. Uric acid has been implicated in hepatotoxicity through the induction of mitochondrial oxidative stress, hepatic lipid accumulation, and inflammasome activation [32]. Furthermore, experimental studies indicate that uric acid may activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in liver macrophages (Kupffer cells), triggering IL-1β production and exacerbating liver inflammation [33]. The observed elevation in ALT without a corresponding rise in AST may suggest a localized hepatic insult rather than broader tissue injury, in line with the early stages of NAFLD in this cohort.

Significant differences were noted in waist circumference (WC) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) across SUA/Cr tertiles, with higher values observed in the upper tertiles, while BMI did not differ significantly. This pattern suggests a stronger link between SUA/Cr ratio and central adiposity rather than general obesity. Central obesity, characterized by visceral fat accumulation, is more closely associated with insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and hepatic steatosis [34]. Uric acid may promote adipogenesis and lipid accumulation through activation of NADPH oxidase and inhibition of AMPK, thereby encouraging fat deposition in visceral depots [35]. Furthermore, the significant increase in FFM in higher SUA/Cr tertiles may reflect a compensatory mechanism or altered muscle metabolism. Interestingly, our findings revealed that individuals in the higher SUA/Cr category also exhibited a significantly greater fat-free mass (FFM). As it is clear, most of our participants had high BMI and the mean BMI of participants across SUA/Cr categories was higher than 30 kg/m2. While this may initially appear paradoxical, it is consistent with prior evidence that obese individuals often present with higher absolute lean mass despite an adverse metabolic profile. This phenomenon is likely driven by the mechanical load of excess body weight, which increases muscle mass but not necessarily its quality or metabolic efficiency [36]. In fact, muscle quality in obesity is frequently impaired due to intramuscular lipid infiltration and insulin resistance, meaning that higher FFM does not equate to better metabolic health [37]. Moreover, creatinine—used in the denominator of the SUA/Cr ratio—is a byproduct of muscle metabolism, and variations in muscle mass may partially influence the ratio itself [38]. Thus, the observed elevation of FFM in participants with higher SUA/Cr should be interpreted cautiously, as it likely reflects body size rather than a favorable metabolic phenotype. Mechanistic pathways underlying the association between elevated serum uric acid to creatinine ratio in NAFLD progression

In the present study, fasting insulin concentrations decreased across higher tertiles of SUA/Cr, whereas no significant differences were observed in HOMA-IR. These findings suggest that although SUA/Cr has been proposed as a surrogate marker of metabolic dysfunction, its relationship with insulin resistance may not be straightforward in all populations. One possible explanation is that SUA/Cr reflects the interplay between uric acid production and renal clearance rather than directly mirroring insulin resistance status. Despite our null findings for HOMA-IR, growing evidence supports a link between SUA/Cr and glucose dysregulation. Kuwabara et al. reported that higher SUA/Cr was significantly associated with elevated HOMA-IR and increased risk of insulin resistance in a large Japanese cohort [39]. Likewise, Liu et al. demonstrated that SUA was an independent predictor of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome [40]. At the mechanistic level, uric acid has been shown to interfere with insulin signaling pathways through oxidative stress, impaired endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, and promotion of low-grade inflammation [41]. Therefore, although our study did not reveal a significant association between SUA/Cr and HOMA-IR, the observed reduction in fasting insulin at higher SUA/Cr tertiles may reflect an early adaptive or compensatory response. Taken together, while our findings highlight the complexity of this association, the existing literature consistently supports the role of SUA/Cr as a potential contributor to insulin resistance and NAFLD progression.

Recent literature has increasingly validated non-invasive biomarker approaches for the prediction and stratification of NAFLD/MASLD. De Matteis et al. [42] showed that the Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP = log [TG/HDL-C]) closely correlates with metabolic impairment and discriminates severe steatosis (optimal cut-off 0.31), with significant correlations with fasting glucose, HbA1c, LDL and total cholesterol; their findings were validated in an independent cohort. In a related observational study, De Matteis et al. [43] also reported that hyperhomocysteinemia is more prevalent among patients with MASLD and is independently associated with MASLD and a worse cardiometabolic profile (including impaired glucose metabolism, adverse lipid changes and lower vitamin D. Finally, Novielli et al. [44] reviewed current non-invasive fibrosis assessment tools and emphasized the utility of combining serum biomarkers with imaging and the need for sex-adjusted cutoffs to improve risk stratification and clinical applicability. Together, these findings underline the growing relevance of multidimensional biomarker approaches, complementing traditional indices such as serum uric acid to creatinine ratio, in the comprehensive evaluation of metabolic and hepatic health.

This study has several strengths. First, it focuses on a novel yet clinically accessible biomarker, the SUA/Cr ratio, which integrates both metabolic and renal aspects, potentially offering improved its utility over serum uric acid alone. Second, the study comprehensively assessed a range of metabolic, hepatic, and anthropometric variables, allowing for a multidimensional evaluation of cardiometabolic risk. Third, the sample included individuals with well-defined NAFLD based on ultrasonography, limited to grades 1 and 2, which enhances the specificity of the findings to early-stage disease. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causality between SUA/Cr levels and the observed metabolic abnormalities. Second, while abdominal ultrasonography is a widely used, non-invasive, and practical tool in both clinical and epidemiological settings, it has limited sensitivity for detecting mild steatosis compared to advanced imaging or histological assessments. Third, patients with grade 3 NAFLD were excluded to reduce heterogeneity and ensure sufficient statistical power across disease categories; however, this restricts the applicability of our findings to individuals with mild to moderate steatosis, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating results to patients with severe disease. Fourth, this was a single-center study, and participants were consecutively recruited from outpatient clinics in Al-Kharj city. As such, the findings may not be fully generalizable to NAFLD populations in other geographic or ethnic groups, where demographic, genetic, and lifestyle characteristics may differ. Future multi-center longitudinal studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between SUA/Cr and metabolic dysfunction.

In conclusion, our results highlight that serum uric acid to creatinine ratio was in association with adverse metabolic alterations in NAFLD, encompassing dyslipidemia, liver enzyme elevation and central obesity. The mechanistic pathways linking SUA/Cr to metabolic disturbances likely involve oxidative stress, inflammation, and adipose tissue dysfunction. Given its accessibility and clinical relevance, SUA/Cr could serve as a useful tool for risk stratification and early intervention in NAFLD patients. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to explore whether lowering SUA/Cr can improve metabolic outcomes and slow disease progression.
