Phycocyanin-functionalized selenium nanoparticles attenuate osteoarthritis through ferroptosis suppression and modulation of the MAPK pathway
Authors: Jiawei Hu, Peng Luo, Wei Zhang, Tian Chen, Xiaowei Yang, Zhiyi Chen, Shaorong Huang, Xiaoyong Zhang, Zhihui Kuang, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a widespread degenerative disorder of the joints, in which chondrocyte oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and ferroptosis play pivotal roles in disease progression, yet current therapeutic strategies remain largely insufficient. In this study, we developed Se@PC NPs as a nanotherapeutic and assessed its efficacy against OA by leveraging selenium's role in redox regulation and ferroptosis inhibition along with phycocyanin's dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The resultant Se@PC NPs demonstrated excellent stability and biocompatibility. In IL-1β-stimulated ATDC5 cells, they effectively scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS), suppressed iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, and upregulated key ferroptosis-related proteins (GPX4 and SLC7A11). Se@PC NPs can also promote the synthesis of cartilage extracellular matrix by reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and catabolic enzymes. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that Se@PC NPs modulate the MAPK signaling pathway, as well as pathways associated with ferroptosis and oxidative stress. Furthermore, Se@PC NPs preserved mitochondrial structural integrity and membrane potential, thereby alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction. In a murine model of destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM), Se@PC NPs attenuated cartilage erosion and osteophyte formation, restored the expression of COL2A1 and GPX4, suppressed MMP13 and COX2. In summary, Se@PC NPs synergistically exert antioxidant, anti-ferroptotic, and anti-inflammatory effects, effectively delaying OA progression and presenting a promising nanotherapeutic strategy.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Ferroptosis, Selenium nanoparticles, Phycocyanin

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disorder on a global scale, impacting over 500 million individuals and constituting a principal cause of disability. While OA can affect nearly any joint, the knee is the most frequently affected site. Clinically, affected joints demonstrate symptoms such as swelling, pain, deformity, and functional impairment [1,2]. Currently, clinical management of OA primarily emphasizes pain alleviation and surgical procedures during advanced stages, as there are no established treatments capable of curing or halting the progression of the disease [3]. The development of novel alternatives that effectively and safely target the fundamental pathological mechanisms of OA constitutes a critical and urgent research priority. During the development of OA, chondrocytes undergo cell death and the extracellular matrix (ECM) undergoes progressive degradation [4]. Lipid peroxide–mediated ferroptotic cell death has emerged as a contributing factor in OA progression [5]. Emerging studies indicated that chondrocytes under pathological conditions display hallmark features of ferroptosis, including disrupted iron homeostasis, lipid peroxide accumulation, and mitochondrial impairment. Illustratively, Yao et al. showed that IL-1β affects cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and regulates ferroptosis-related proteins in cartilage cells [6]. Furthermore, Erastin, a pharmacological ferroptosis inducer, was shown to upregulate matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP13) expression while suppressing type II collagen synthesis in these cells [7]. Therefore, Wang et al. developed an elegant therapeutic strategy that targets the hyperoxidative stress environment and aberrantly activated MMP13 in osteoarthritis [8]. Li et al. recently developed a gold nanorod–based photothermal nanoplatform that alleviated cartilage degeneration by suppressing chondrocyte ferroptosis [9]. Similarly, Shi et al. reported a magnetothermal nanoplatform that delayed osteoarthritis progression by modulating the osteoarthritic inflammatory microenvironment and ferroptosis, further emphasizing ferroptosis as a key pathological mechanism and therapeutic target in OA [10]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring the cartilage antioxidant defense system and inhibiting chondrocyte ferroptosis provide a strategic basis for the development of novel interventions to decelerate OA progression.

Selenium is crucial for the biosynthesis of selenium-related proteins and plays a vital role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [11,12]. It is evident that GPX4 performs a pivotal function in the regulation of lipid peroxidation and the prevention of ferroptosis within cells [13]. A deficiency in selenium impairs GPX4 synthesis, which compromises cellular antioxidant defenses, exacerbates oxidative stress and ferroptosis, and ultimately accelerates chondrocyte death and OA progression [14]. Consequently, selenium supplementation has been proposed as a potential etiological intervention. With high biocompatibility, sustained-release ability, and low toxicity, selenium nanoparticles (Se NPs) have garnered considerable interest. Research has shown that Se NPs can gradually release selenium in vivo, thereby safely and efficiently upregulating GPX4 and other related proteins, and exhibiting potent antioxidant, anti-ferroptotic, mitochondrial-protective, and anti-inflammatory effects across various disease models [[15], [16], [17]]. In addition, Se NPs have been reported to suppress the activation of the P65 and MAPK signaling pathways in osteoarthritis, further contributing to their chondroprotective potential [18]. Nonetheless, the intrinsic hydrophobicity of Se NPs often leads to aggregation and precipitation in biological fluids, compromising their stability and bioavailability and thus posing a significant challenge for in vivo applications and clinical translation [19]. The modified selenium-based nanomaterials exhibit efficient penetration of biological barriers, suppression of oxidative stress, and attenuation of inflammatory responses, thereby demonstrating promising therapeutic potential in inflammatory diseases [20]. Moreover, OA pathophysiology is multifaceted, involving a complex interplay of ferroptotic and inflammatory pathways. Consequently, developing Se NPs with high aqueous dispersibility, good biocompatibility, enhanced bioavailability, and multi-target therapeutic effects is of considerable importance for OA treatment.

Phycocyanin (PC) is a water-soluble pigment–protein from cyanobacteria that has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects [21]. PC exhibits a high fluorescence quantum yield and stable spectral characteristics, enabling efficient absorption and emission of light in the red to near-infrared region, which makes it a promising natural fluorescent probe for bioimaging applications [22]. In addition, PC exhibits remarkable antioxidant stress resistance, effectively scavenging various ROS and free radicals, while enhancing the activity of cellular antioxidant enzyme systems [23]. Empirical evidence has demonstrated that PC effectively inhibits cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and markedly suppresses the synthesis of critical pro-inflammatory mediators, all of which play pivotal roles in driving synovial inflammation and pain associated with OA [24]. In addition to its therapeutic potential, PC exhibits amphiphilic characteristics, with a molecular surface abundant in hydrophilic functional groups, including carboxyl and amino moieties, conferring excellent hydrophilicity and biocompatibility [25]. These properties position PC as a promising drug delivery vehicle. Consequently, leveraging these attributes through integration with Se NPs offers a strategic solution to harness their synergistic potential for dual therapeutic benefits.

In this study, we synthesized Se@PC NPs through a straightforward and environmentally friendly biosynthetic method, using PC as both a stabilizing agent and a functional modifier that enabled in situ reduction. This process yielded water-soluble, uniformly dispersed, and highly stable nanoparticles. The therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms of Se@PC NPs were systematically evaluated in vitro using a chondrocyte ferroptosis model and in vivo in a mouse model of osteoarthritis (Scheme 1). Results demonstrated that Se@PC NPs significantly alleviated oxidative stress in chondrocytes, reduced lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation, and thereby effectively inhibited chondrocyte ferroptosis. The nanocomposite also attenuated inflammation by decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. In vivo, Se@PC NPs markedly suppressed cartilage degradation, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation in the DMM model. Importantly, the therapeutic efficacy of Se@PC NPs exceeded that of pure Se NPs or PC alone. These findings highlight the promising potential of Se@PC NPs as a novel therapeutic strategy for OA.

Experimental sections

Materials

Phycocyanin (CAS No.11016-15-2), Sodium Selenite (CAS No.10102-18-8) and Ferrostatin-1 (CAS No. 347174-05-4) were procured from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). Anisomycin (CAS No. 22862-76-6) was procured from R&D Systems (Minnesota, USA). Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; CAS No. 16561-29-8) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Dulbecco's modified eagle medium/Nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Gibco (California, USA). Primary antibodies: GAPDH (CST, Boston, USA), GPX4 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), COX2 (CST, Boston, USA), COL2A1 (CST, Boston, USA), MMP13 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), SLC7A11 (Zenbio, Chengdu, China), P-JNK/JNK (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), P-ERK/ERK (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), P-P38/P38 (Zenbio, Chengdu, China). All other reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers at analytical or specified purity grades.

Synthesis of Se NPs and Se@PC NPs

Synthesis of Se NPs: According to established protocols, 10 mg of selenium powder was dissolved in 10 mL of polyethylene glycol 200 (PEG200) and subjected to magnetic stirring at 200 °C for 2 h. The reaction was terminated upon observing a color change from colorless to dark brown. The product was isolated via centrifugation and subsequently rinsed three times with ultrapure water to yield Se NPs. Synthesis of Se@PC NPs: Initially, 100 mg of phycocyanin (PC) was dissolved in 5 mL of ultrapure water, followed by sonication to ensure complete dissolution. Subsequently, 3 mL of a 50 mM Na2SeO3 solution was added and thoroughly mixed. The pH of the mixture was then adjusted to 8.4 by the gradual addition of 0.2 M NaOH solution. Under continuous stirring, 6 mL of a 50 mM ascorbic acid solution was introduced dropwise, and the reaction was maintained overnight at ambient temperature. The resulting solution was collected, dialyzed against ultrapure water for 48 h, and subsequently lyophilized to yield Se@PC NPs.

Characterization of Se@PC NPs

The chemical structure of Se@PC by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FR-IR) (Nicolet 5700, Nicolet, USA) for confirmation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan) was employed to examine the morphology and size of the Se@PC NPs, while their hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were assessed using a nanoparticle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK). UV–Vis spectrophotometry (UV-1700, Persee, China) was used to record the absorption spectra of the samples. The elemental composition was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Escalab XI+, Thermo Scientific, USA).

In vitro antioxidant activity

Initially, 0.2 mL of ABTS diammonium salt stock solution was combined with 0.2 mL of K2S2O8 stock solution. The mixture was adjusted with PBS (pH 7.4) until its absorbance at 734 nm reached 0.70 ± 0.02. This prepared solution was designated as the working ABTS radical solution for subsequent analyses. Subsequently, 3.2 mL of the working solution was transferred into a 5 mL centrifuge tube, to which varying volumes of PBS were added and thoroughly mixed. Thereafter, aliquots of Se@PC NPs solution (0, 200, 400, 600, and 800 μL) were introduced into separate tubes, ensuring that the final reaction volume in each tube was adjusted to 4 mL. Following a 5-min incubation period, the absorbance of each sample was recorded at 734 nm. ABTS•+ scavenging activity (%) was calculated as (A0 − Aᵢ)/A0 × 100%, with A0 and Aᵢ representing the absorbance of the ABTS•+ solution prior to and following treatment with Se@PC NPs.

Results and discussion

Synthesis, characterization, and antioxidant activity of Se@PC NPs

Phycocyanin (PC), an edible bioactive compound with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and drug delivery potential, was synthesized as a stabilizer and functional modifier to produce Se@PC NPs via a simple redox system using sodium selenite and ascorbic acid. This was done with the aim of overcoming the narrow therapeutic window and poor water solubility of conventional selenium formulations [[26], [27], [28]]. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the aqueous solution of Na2SeO3 is colorless, whereas the Se NPs exhibit a brick-red coloration, and PC itself is characterized by a blue hue. The resultant Se@PC NPs display a yellow coloration, with this distinct chromatic transition serving as evidence for the successful formation of Se@PC NPs. Morphological and dimensional analyses of the Se@PC NPs were conducted via TEM. Fig. 1B and C shows that Se@PC NPs are spherical, with an average size of 142 nm. Elemental mapping shows that Se@PC NPs contain C, N, O, S and Se, proving effective coating of PC on the nanoparticle surface (Fig. S1). Dynamic light scattering measurements revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 156 nm for the Se@PC NPs, which is marginally larger than the TEM-derived size, likely attributable to the hydration layer surrounding the nanoparticles. The zeta potential of the Se@PC NPs was −17.8 mV, and the polydispersity index (PDI) was 0.272. Nanoparticles employed in biological applications typically exhibit a low polydispersity index (PDI), generally below 0.2. In contrast, the PDI value obtained for Se@PC NPs in this study was comparatively elevated. This increase may be attributed to heterogeneity in the core size of selenium nanoparticles generated during the reduction of Na2SeO3 by ascorbic acid. Furthermore, variability in the quantity of phycocyanin coating the selenium nanoparticle surfaces could contribute to this observation. Another potential factor is the conformational alterations in the spatial structure of phycocyanin occurring during dialysis purification and lyophilization processes. Despite these considerations, the PDI value of Se@PC NPs remains within an acceptable range for their intended applications. This suggests favorable colloidal stability and dispersibility (Fig. 1D). Stability constitutes a critical parameter in assessing the potential applicability of novel pharmaceutical agents. In this study, Se@PC NPs were individually dispersed in PBS and FBS, with their particle size monitored continuously over a period of seven days. The findings demonstrated that the nanoparticles maintained satisfactory stability in both media (Fig. 1E). Comparative analysis of the UV absorption spectra of PC and Se@PC NPs indicated that neither ascorbic acid nor Na2SeO3 exhibited significant absorption within the 300–800 nm wavelength range. In contrast, both PC and Se@PC NPs displayed a prominent absorption peak near 620 nm. A notable observation is the red shift in the absorption peak of Se@PC NPs relative to PC (Fig. 1F). This red shift may be attributed to the interaction between PC and the Se NPs surface, possibly altering the chromophore conformation of PC. It is noteworthy that PC functions solely as a coating layer on the Se NPs. Consequently, the FT-IR spectra closely resembled those of PC alone (Fig. 1G). To verify the presence of selenium and determine its chemical valence state, XPS analysis was conducted, revealing a Se 3d binding energy at 63.1 eV (Fig. 1H and I). This result further substantiates that the PC coating comprises elemental selenium in its zero-valent state. To assess the antioxidant capacity of PC and Se@PC NPs, the ABTS assay was employed to quantify their free radical scavenging activity. Fig. 1J and K shows that both PC and Se@PC NPs efficiently scavenged free radicals in a dose-dependent manner, achieving 90.4% and 99.3% scavenging at 200 μg/mL, respectively. These findings suggest that Se@PC NPs possess enhanced antioxidant activity relative to pure PC.

Cytocompatibility evaluation of Se@PC NPs

The synthesized Se@PC NPs were tested to determine whether they are biocompatible for biomedical purposes. Given the inherent insolubility of pure Se NPs in aqueous media, surface modification is essential to improve their stability in such environments. Typical approaches involve coating Se NPs with polymers such as polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), tocopherol polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS), poly(methylvinylether-co-maleic anhydride) (PMVE-MA) and polyethylene glycol (PEG) [29,30]. To minimize the impact of other compounds on this study, we chose PEG2000 to modify the Se NPs. Both the selenium powder and the Se NPs produced via the reduction of Na2SeO3 with ascorbic acid comprise elemental selenium and exhibit comparable physicochemical properties. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that Se@PC NPs exhibited markedly improved biocompatibility compared with bare Se NPs. Both PC and Se@PC NPs maintained high cell viability across a wide concentration range, whereas Se NPs significantly decreased viability at higher concentrations (20 and 40 μg/mL) (Fig. 2A). In addition, hemolysis assay results show that Se@PC NPs induce negligible hemolysis at all tested concentrations, indicating favorable biocompatibility (Fig. S2). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PC-functionalized selenium nanoparticles are markedly superior to bare Se NPs. PC modification not only effectively reduces the adverse effects of selenium nanoparticles on cell viability but also exerts minimal impact on blood cells. This superior biosafety profile highlights the critical role of PC functionalization in enabling the safe and effective application of selenium-based nanomaterials. Therefore, to ensure consistency in the subsequent comparative experiments between bare SeNPs and PC-functionalized Se NPs, a relatively safe concentration (10 μg/mL) was selected for further studies. The high cell viability observed in live/dead staining after 48 h of Se@PC NP exposure further supports their excellent cytocompatibility, with comparable results among all groups (Fig. 2B). Cell proliferation was not adversely affected by Se@PC NPs, as shown by EdU staining in Fig. 2C. PC, enriched with hydrophilic functional groups, served as an effective drug delivery matrix that not only protected the nanostructure of selenium but also partially preserved its own structural integrity and bioactivity, thereby facilitating cellular uptake (Fig. 2D). Overall, Se@PC NPs integrated the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and chondroprotective activities of both PC and selenium, yielding a synergistic therapeutic effect characterized by reduced toxicity, improved stability, and enhanced biological efficacy.

Inhibition of extracellular matrix degradation and inflammation by Se@PC NPs

An in vitro OA model was established by stimulating ATDC5 cells with IL-1β (10 ng/mL) to assess the ability of Se@PC NPs to prevent extracellular matrix degradation and alleviate chondrocyte inflammation [31,32]. COL2A1 is a pivotal element of the cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM), playing a critical role in preserving the structural integrity and functional capabilities of cartilage; MMP13 degrades matrix proteins such as COL2A1, leading to cartilage destruction; COX2 is an important inflammatory enzyme that contributes to inflammation-induced cartilage damage [33]. Treatment with PC, Se NPs, or Se@PC NPs counteracted IL-1β–induced changes in gene expression, restoring COL2A1 levels and inhibiting COX2 and MMP13 upregulation, with Se@PC NPs showing the strongest protective effects (Fig. 3A). A consistent trend was observed at the protein level: following IL-1β stimulation, COL2A1 expression in ATDC5 cells decreased, whereas COX2 and MMP13 levels increased. Treatment with PC, Se NPs, or Se@PC NPs reversed these changes, with Se@PC NPs exhibiting the most pronounced regulatory effect (Fig. 3B and C). In addition, immunofluorescence staining of COL2A1 and MMP13 yielded consistent results, demonstrating that Se@PC NPs effectively inhibited IL-1β–induced COL2A1 degradation and the upregulation of MMP13 expression (Fig. 3D and E). To investigate how Se@PC NPs protect the extracellular matrix, ECM composition was analyzed using toluidine blue staining [34]. In the control group, intense blue staining indicated abundant glycosaminoglycan content. Following IL-1β treatment, the staining became markedly lighter, whereas the addition of Se@PC NPs restored the staining intensity, suggesting a significant protective effect on the cartilage matrix (Fig. 3F). To further validate the advantages of Se@PC nanoparticles, a physical mixture of PC and Se NPs was used as a control. Briefly, PC and Se NPs were mixed at an equal volume ratio (1:1, v/v), corresponding to a mass ratio of 1:1 (w/w), to obtain a final working concentration of 10 μg/mL. As shown in Fig. S3, compared with the simple physical mixture group (PC + Se NPs), the Se@PC NPs group exhibited superior protective effects on the cartilage extracellular matrix. Although treatment with PC + Se NPs partially counteracted the IL-1β-induced changes in gene expression, restored COL2A1 levels, and suppressed the upregulation of MMP13, its protective effects were less pronounced than those of Se@PC NPs (Fig. S3A). At the protein level, PC + Se NPs also partially reversed the IL-1β-induced decrease in COL2A1 expression; however, the effect remained weaker than that of Se@PC NPs (Fig. S3B). Both treatments reduced COX2 expression, and Se@PC NPs showed a stronger inhibitory trend, although the difference between the two groups did not reach statistical significance.

Potential mechanisms for chondroprotective effects of Se@PC NPs

To elucidate the mechanisms through which Se@PC NPs preserve chondrocyte extracellular matrix and inhibit cartilage inflammation, we conducted transcriptomic analysis of ATDC5 cells cultured for 48 h in IL-1β-containing medium supplemented with 10 μg/mL Se@PC NPs. Principal component analysis (PCA) separated the control (IL-1β alone) and treatment (IL-1β + Se@PC NPs) groups, showing that Se@PC NPs caused a transcriptomic change (Fig. 4A). Se@PC NPs treatment resulted in significant gene expression changes, with a total of 4236 DEGs, including 1920 upregulated and 2316 downregulated genes (Fig. 4B and C). GO analysis revealed DEGs' associations with OA progression (e.g., extracellular matrix organization, inflammatory response, oxidative stress response, and lipid oxidation) (Fig. 4D). Subsequent KEGG analysis revealed a substantial enrichment of DEGs in pathways such as MAPK signaling and ferroptosis, indicating that these pathways function as pivotal regulatory targets that are modulated by Se@PC NP treatment, thereby corroborating the designed rationale of our material (Fig. 4E). The MAPK pathway is a crucial intracellular signaling network composed of ERK, JNK, and P38 subfamilies, which convert extracellular stimuli into intracellular responses through a kinase cascade. Activation of MAPK signaling promotes inflammation, matrix degradation, and chondrocyte apoptosis, all of which accelerate OA progression [35,36]. Activation of the P38 and JNK pathways promotes chondrocyte apoptosis and suppresses proliferation, while ROS further enhance p38 signaling, aggravating inflammation and cell injury [37,38]. Excessive ERK activation also leads to extracellular matrix degradation and chondrocyte death [39]. We confirmed that the MAPK signaling pathway was activated upon IL-1β stimulation, and treatment with Se@PC NPs partially inhibited this activation (Fig. 4F and G). To further determine whether the MAPK signaling pathway is functionally involved in the chondroprotective effects of Se@PC NPs, rescue experiments were performed using pharmacological MAPK activators. Anisomycin (10 μg/mL) and PMA (100 nM) were applied to significantly enhance IL-1β–induced phosphorylation of JNK, P38, and ERK, respectively, confirming effective reactivation of MAPK signaling (Fig. S4A and S4B). However, Western blot analysis revealed that excessive MAPK activation partially reversed the protective effects of Se@PC NPs on extracellular matrix metabolism. Compared with cells treated with Se@PC NPs alone, MAPK reactivation led to increased COL2A1 degradation and upregulation of COX2 and MMP13 (Fig. S4C and S4D). Furthermore, under inflammatory and ferroptosis-inducing conditions, aberrant MAPK activation attenuated the protective effects of Se@PC NPs on ferroptosis-related proteins. Specifically, Anisomycin or PMA treatment markedly reduced the Se@PC NP–mediated restoration of SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression, indicating that excessive MAPK activation counteracts the anti-ferroptotic effects of Se@PC NPs. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that suppression of MAPK activation is functionally required for the chondroprotective activity of Se@PC NPs (Fig. S4E and S4F). The findings, when considered collectively, suggest that Se@PC NPs may exert a protective effect on cartilage by modulating MAPK signaling, mitigating oxidative stress, and regulating ferroptosis.

Inhibition of oxidative stress and ferroptosis in chondrocytes by Se@PC NPs

Oxidative stress, driven by excessive ROS, plays a central role in the development and progression of OA. ROS directly damage chondrocytes, suppress extracellular matrix synthesis, and enhance matrix metalloproteinase expression. In addition, ROS activate inflammatory signaling pathways and impair antioxidant defenses, thereby disrupting redox homeostasis and accelerating OA progression [40,41]. To assess the intracellular ROS levels, DCFH-DA staining was performed. IL-1β induced a significant rise in ROS fluorescence, which was effectively attenuated by PC, Se NPs, and Se@PC NPs, with Se@PC NPs showing the highest ROS-scavenging capacity (Fig. 5A and B). Unlike apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy, ferroptosis represents an iron-dependent regulated form of cell death, driven primarily by GSH depletion and GPX4 inactivation, which allows lipid hydroperoxides to accumulate. In the presence of ferrous ions, excessive lipid peroxidation generates large amounts of ROS. Oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation mutually reinforce each other, constituting the core mechanism underlying ferroptosis-induced cellular injury [[42], [43], [44]]. IL-1β was used to mimic the osteoarthritis-related inflammatory microenvironment in chondrocytes. As IL-1β stimulation primarily reflects inflammation-associated oxidative stress, erastin (5 μM) was introduced as a classical ferroptosis inducer to further enhance ferroptotic stress and clarify the involvement of ferroptosis in Se@PC NP–mediated chondroprotection [[45], [46], [47]]. FerroOrange staining revealed increased intracellular ferrous ion levels due to IL-1β and Erastin, which were reduced by Se@PC NPs treatment (Fig. 5C). Consistently, BODIPY C11 staining demonstrated a reduction in red fluorescence and enhancement of green fluorescence following IL-1β and Erastin exposure, indicating aggravated lipid peroxidation, while Se@PC NPs notably reversed these changes (Fig. 5D and E). Furthermore, Se@PC NPs upregulated the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, corroborating their inhibitory effect on ferroptosis (Fig. 5F and G). To further investigate the basic mechanism by which Se@PC NPs participate in oxidative stress–induced chondrocyte injury through inhibition of ferroptosis, Ferrostatin-1(10 μM), a classical ferroptosis inhibitor, was used as a reference control. In intracellular lipid ROS staining assays, Se@PC NPs similarly and significantly suppressed lipid peroxidation, exhibiting an inhibitory effect comparable to that of Ferrostatin-1 (Fig. S5A). In addition, both Ferrostatin-1 and Se@PC NPs treatments alleviated COL2A1 degradation and suppressed the upregulation of COX2 and MMP13 in osteoarthritic chondrocytes. Similarly, both Ferrostatin-1 and Se@PC NPs increased the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, with no significant difference observed between the two treatments. Notably, compared with Ferrostatin-1, Se@PC NPs exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect on COX2 expression, indicating a stronger anti-inflammatory potential (Fig. S5B and S5C). These results indicate that while both Ferrostatin-1 and Se@PC NPs effectively inhibit ferroptosis, Se@PC NPs confer additional anti-inflammatory benefits, thereby contributing to more comprehensive chondroprotection. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that Se@PC NPs attenuate oxidative stress and ferroptosis by reducing ROS accumulation, limiting iron overload, alleviating lipid peroxidation, and restoring SLC7A11/GPX4 expression, ultimately protecting chondrocytes from OA-related injury.

Se@PC NPs preserve mitochondrial integrity and functional homeostasis

Mitochondrial homeostasis is vital for maintaining cartilage integrity. Oxidative stress directly compromises mitochondrial structure, dissipates membrane potential, and impairs energy metabolism in chondrocytes. Ferroptosis further aggravates mitochondrial injury by disturbing intracellular iron balance and driving lipid peroxidation, ultimately reducing cell viability and extracellular matrix synthesis [[48], [49], [50]]. TEM images revealed that mitochondria in control chondrocytes were intact and morphologically normal, whereas IL-1β and Erastin induced typical ferroptotic alterations, including mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae disruption. Treatment with PC, Se NPs, or Se@PC NPs progressively restored mitochondrial ultrastructure, with Se@PC NPs showing the most prominent recovery (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, Ferrostatin-1 was used for comparison, and both Se@PC NPs and Ferrostatin-1 similarly rescued inflammation and ferroptosis induced mitochondrial morphological alterations, exhibiting comparable protective effects (Fig. S6A). Correspondingly, MitoTracker staining showed filamentous and evenly distributed mitochondria in control cells, while IL-1β and Erastin triggered severe mitochondrial fragmentation. Notably, these morphological alterations were significantly reversed following treatment with Se@PC NPs (Fig. 6B). JC-1 detection indicated that IL-1β + Erastin exposure led to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Treatment with PC, Se NPs, and particularly Se@PC NPs significantly restored ΔΨm, indicating effective mitochondrial recovery (Fig. 6C). In comparison with Ferrostatin-1, Se@PC NPs exhibited a comparable protective effect on mitochondrial membrane potential, similar to that of this classical ferroptosis inhibitor (Fig. S6B). Notably, whereas Ferrostatin-1 selectively suppresses lipid peroxidation, Se@PC NPs display good aqueous dispersibility and multifunctional protective effects, including modulation of the MAPK pathway, inhibition of ferroptosis, and anti-inflammatory activity, thereby contributing to enhanced chondroprotection. Taken together, these data indicate that oxidative stress and ferroptosis impair mitochondrial integrity and function in chondrocytes. In contrast, Se@PC NPs effectively maintain mitochondrial structure, morphology, and membrane potential, thereby preserving mitochondrial homeostasis.

Se@PC NPs attenuate DMM-induced OA progression in mice

The therapeutic effects of Se@PC NPs against OA were investigated using a DMM-induced mouse model that effectively mirrors human OA pathology [51]. Due to the small volume of the mouse joint cavity and the rapid turnover of synovial fluid, intra-articular injections were administered every three days to balance sustained local bioavailability of the therapeutic agent with the avoidance of secondary synovial inflammation and joint injury caused by frequent puncture [52,53]. Our experimental results confirmed that micro-CT showed that the sham group maintained intact subchondral bone and normal joint space, whereas the DMM group exhibited severe subchondral bone destruction, osteophyte formation, and joint space narrowing. Intra-articular administration of PC, Se NPs, or Se@PC NPs alleviated these lesions, with Se@PC NPs showing the greatest structural restoration (Fig. 7A). Quantitative bone morphometry confirmed that Se@PC NPs mitigated bone loss and trabecular rarefaction, delaying OA progression (Fig. 7B). Histological analysis further supported these results. The DMM group displayed markedly elevated OARSI scores and severe cartilage erosion, whereas Se@PC NPs significantly preserved cartilage integrity and reduced scores (Fig. 7C). H&E staining showed cartilage thinning and disorganized chondrocytes in the DMM group, whereas Se@PC NPs restored cartilage thickness and orderly cell arrangement. SO/FG staining revealed pronounced proteoglycan loss in the DMM group but marked retention in the Se@PC NP group (Fig. 7D). In summary, Se@PC NPs protect subchondral bone and cartilage matrix, slowing OA progression and offering a promising OA treatment.

Se@PC NPs preserve cartilage phenotype and in vivo biocompatibility in OA mice

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to assess key cartilage metabolism markers (MMP13, COX2), extracellular matrix synthesis marker (COL2A1), and oxidative stress marker (GPX4). In the Sham group, MMP13 and COX2 expression was minimal, whereas COL2A1 was abundantly expressed and GPX4 levels were normal. In contrast, DMM-induced OA mice exhibited markedly increased MMP13 and COX2 expression, with significant reduction of COL2A1 and decreased GPX4, indicating active cartilage inflammation and degradation, matrix loss, and oxidative stress imbalance. Intra-articular administration of Se@PC NPs effectively suppressed MMP13 and COX2 expression, while restoring COL2A1 and upregulating GPX4, demonstrating potent inhibition of OA-related inflammation and cartilage degradation, as well as amelioration of oxidative stress (Fig. 8A and B). Furthermore, in vivo biocompatibility analysis revealed that major organs in all groups exhibited intact tissue architecture, normal cellular morphology, and no evidence of inflammation, necrosis, or structural damage (Fig. 8C). In addition, serum biochemical analysis showed no significant abnormalities in organ function–related parameters, indicating the absence of obvious systemic toxicity (Fig. S7). These results confirm that Se@PC NPs not only exert therapeutic effects in OA but also possess favorable in vivo safety. To further determine whether the therapeutic advantage of Se@PC nanoparticles arises from their integrated nanostructure rather than simple co-administration, a physical mixture of phycocyanin (PC) and Se nanoparticles (PC + SeNPs) was introduced as a control. As shown in Fig. S8, although the PC + SeNPs group partially alleviated DMM-induced joint damage, its protective effects were consistently inferior to those of Se@PC NPs. Micro-CT and histological analyses demonstrated that Se@PC NPs more effectively preserved subchondral bone architecture and cartilage integrity compared with the PC + SeNPs group (Fig. S8A and S8B). Consistently, H&E staining revealed that the DMM group exhibited cartilage thinning and disorganized chondrocyte arrangement, whereas Se@PC NPs more effectively restored cartilage thickness and promoted a more orderly cellular organization than the PC + SeNPs group. SO/FG staining further showed pronounced proteoglycan loss in the DMM group, while Se@PC NPs markedly preserved proteoglycan content. Accordingly, the Se@PC NPs group displayed the lowest OARSI scores, indicating superior attenuation of cartilage degeneration (Fig. S8C and S8D). Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed that Se@PC NPs more effectively restored COL2A1 expression and suppressed MMP13 expression compared with the PC + SeNPs group, suggesting enhanced protection of the cartilage extracellular matrix. Although both treatments reduced COX2 expression, Se@PC NPs exhibited a stronger inhibitory trend; however, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (Fig. S8E and S8F). Collectively, these findings indicate that simple physical mixing of PC and Se nanoparticles cannot fully recapitulate the therapeutic efficacy of Se@PC NPs, highlighting the critical role of nanostructure integration in achieving synergistic chondroprotective effects.

As a prevalent degenerative joint disease, OA remains a clinical challenge with limited treatment options, primarily due to its complex and multifactorial pathogenesis involving oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation such as ferroptosis. Recent research into OA pathophysiology has highlighted the crucial role of trace elements and bioactive compounds in modulating cellular redox homeostasis and inflammatory responses [11,12,24]. Selenium, an essential micronutrient, is integral to the activity of antioxidant selenoproteins like GPX4, which directly regulates ferroptosis. On the other hand, a natural biliprotein derived from Spirulina, PC exhibits notable anti-inflammatory and free radical-scavenging properties, positioning it as a promising bioactive carrier. Despite their individual potentials, both selenium and PC face limitations in bioavailability, stability, and targeted delivery when administered in conventional forms. The emergence of nanotechnology offers a compelling platform to overcome these constraints, enabling the design of integrated nanosystems with improved pharmacokinetics, tissue penetration, and multifunctionality. In particular, Se NPs have attracted attention for their enhanced biocompatibility and antioxidative capacity compared to ionic selenium. However, their tendency to aggregate in physiological environments limits their therapeutic utility. Functional modification with biopolymers such as PC not only enhances colloidal stability but also introduces complementary bioactivities, thereby creating synergistic therapeutic effects. Within this context, the development of Se@PC NPs represents a rational strategy to concurrently address multiple pathological pathways in OA. By conjugating Se with PC at the nanoscale, this system merges the anti-ferroptotic function of Se with the anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective properties of PC, while leveraging the nanocarrier's ability to sustain release and enhance intracellular delivery. The present study demonstrates that such a nanocomposite effectively mitigates oxidative damage, regulates iron metabolism, suppresses catabolic enzyme expression, and preserves mitochondrial function in chondrocytes. Moreover, its efficacy in ameliorating cartilage degradation and synovitis in vivo underscores the translational promise of combinatorial nanomedicine in treating complex joint disorders. These findings align with growing evidence supporting the use of multifunctional nanoparticles in OA therapy, where single-target approaches often fall short. Future investigations may focus on optimizing nanoparticle formulation, exploring targeted delivery strategies to articular cartilage, and evaluating long-term safety and efficacy in preclinical models. Furthermore, the fabrication of Se@PC NPs relies on a simple, aqueous, and environmentally friendly biosynthetic strategy, using phycocyanin as both a stabilizing and functional component, without the involvement of toxic organic solvents or harsh reaction conditions, which further facilitates the potential translation of Se@PC NPs toward clinical applications. Overall, the integration of bioactive selenium with functional phycocyanin within a nanosystem opens a new avenue for developing effective disease-modifying OA treatments, potentially extending to other inflammatory and oxidative stress-related musculoskeletal conditions.

Although Se@PC nanoparticles have shown promising therapeutic effects in osteoarthritis, we should acknowledge some limitations of this study to promote future research and clinical translation. First, although the DMM model-induced osteoarthritis mimics the most common form of osteoarthritis caused by mechanical stress imbalance in clinical practice, the applicability and efficacy of Se@PC NPs in other OA subtypes, such as post-traumatic OA, remain to be verified. Second, we did not systematically investigate the long-term biocompatibility, pharmacokinetic characteristics, and in vivo metabolic pathways of Se@PC nanoparticles. Finally, phycocyanin as a natural carrier offers good biocompatibility and functional plasticity. Future studies could explore its use as a base for delivering other active components (e.g., small-molecule anti-inflammatory agents or chondroprotective factors) to develop multi-target combination nano-delivery systems for enhanced OA treatment. In summary, these deficiencies are the focus of our future work.

Conclusions

This study introduces a selenium–phycocyanin nanocomposite (Se@PC NPs) as a novel therapeutic strategy for OA. Results demonstrate that Se@PC NPs integrate the antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic properties of selenium with the anti-inflammatory and carrier functions of phycocyanin, effectively maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis and preserving extracellular matrix integrity in vitro, while significantly attenuating cartilage degradation and improving joint microstructure in vivo. Se@PC NPs attenuate IL-1β–induced aberrant activation of the MAPK pathway and reduce the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix degradation. In parallel, Se@PC NPs suppress lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis by restoring redox balance and regulating the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, which preserves mitochondrial integrity and membrane potential and ensures energy metabolism and functional homeostasis in chondrocytes. Furthermore, the one-pot synthesis of Se@PC NPs offers a more straightforward and efficient approach to obtain a stable nanoplatform compared to multi-step physical mixing, which is advantageous for ensuring structural reproducibility. In summary, Se@PC NPs represent a promising nanotherapeutic agent with combined antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-ferroptotic activities for OA treatment. By further investigating their systemic safety, deepening mechanistic insights, extending validation across diverse OA models, and advancing toward multifunctional composite nanosystems, this approach holds considerable potential to provide an innovative and effective nanomedicine strategy for OA and other degenerative joint diseases.
