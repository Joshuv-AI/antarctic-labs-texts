Thermodynamic universality across dissipative quantum phase transitions
Laetitia P. Bettmann, Artur M. Lacerda, Mark T. Mitchison, John Goold

Abstract
We study finite-time driving across second-order dissipative quantum phase transitions described by Lindblad dynamics. We show that the nonadiabatic entropy production, which quantifies deviations from the instantaneous nonequilibrium steady state, exhibits universal power-law scaling with the ramp duration in analogy to the Kibble–Zurek mechanism for closed systems. This establishes the universality of irreversible dissipation induced by driving an open quantum system near criticality. Furthermore, in systems described by bosonic Gaussian states, our scaling laws predict that the nonadiabatic entropy production is independent of driving speed to leading order, revealing a distinctive feature of Gaussian dissipative quantum phase transitions. We validate these analytical predictions in the thermodynamic limit of the driven-dissipative Dicke model and via finite-size scaling in the open Kerr model. Our results establish a general framework for understanding universal nonequilibrium response and thermodynamic irreversibility in critical open quantum systems.



Introduction
.—Phase transitions are characterized by diverging relaxation times and correlation lengths, giving rise to universal behavior near critical points

;

;

Finite-rate driving protocols inevitably generate nonequilibrium excitations. If the driving is slow relative to
\tau_{R}

;

;

;

In general, one can separate the rate of change of the von Neumann entropy as

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}(t)=\dot{\Sigma}(t)-\dot{\Pi}(t),

(2)

with
\dot{\Sigma}\geq 0

the entropy flux to the environment. In a driven system, the entropy production rate further decomposes as

Here,

Crucially, unlike in closed systems, there is no natural notion of a “number of excitations” in open dynamics: only the NESS is a normalized density operator, whereas all other Liouvillian eigenmodes are traceless decay modes rather than states. Their “occupation” (i.e. spectral weight) therefore has no interpretation in terms of excitations above a ground state, which motivates

Our central result is that the total nonadiabatic entropy production
\Sigma_{\rm na}(0,\tau_{q})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{q}}\mathrm{d}t\,\dot{\Sigma}_{\rm na}

\tau_{q}

\Sigma_{\mathrm{na}}(0,\tau_{q})\sim\tau_{q}^{\frac{\alpha-2}{\gamma+1}}.

Universality of dissipation.
—In driven open quantum systems, finite driving speeds cause deviations from instantaneous steady states. A key thermodynamic quantity characterizing these deviations is the nonadiabatic entropy production rate
\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{na}}
, which can be expressed as

;
Esposito and Van den Broeck 2010a
;
Horowitz and Sagawa 2014
;

\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{na}}(t)=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}D(\hat{\rho}_{s}\|\hat{\pi}_{g(t)})\biggr|_{s=t},

Bosonic Gaussian DQPTs
.—We now highlight a remarkable feature of DQPTs in the mean-field, Ising-like universality class
Stanley 1999
. This includes paradigmatic fully connected open quantum systems exhibiting spontaneous weak
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry breaking, such as the open Dicke
Nagy et al. 2011
, Rabi
Hwang et al. 2018
, Kerr
Zhang and Baranger 2021
, and Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick
Ferreira and Ribeiro 2019
models. These models, which are generally non-Gaussian, can be approximated near criticality as a bosonic Gaussian system with a single soft mode. In this case, we show in Appendix
C.9
that the QFI diverges with critical exponent
\alpha=2
. Notably, this exponent is universal within bosonic Gaussian models, in the sense that it does not depend on the Liouvillian gap exponent or any other critical exponent that typically appears in scaling predictions, making it a distinctive feature of these systems.
This has a striking consequence for the integrated entropy production in both the adiabatic and impulse regimes.
To leading order, the total nonadiabatic entropy production is independent of the quench duration:

\Sigma_{\mathrm{na}}(0,\tau_{q})\sim\tau_{q}^{0}.

(19)

In other words, nonadiabatic effects always contribute to dissipation for such DQPTs, even for arbitrarily slow driving. This differs from the near-equilibrium physics of isolated, fully connected models, where the work dissipated by finite-speed driving across a QPT scales universally to zero as
\tau_{q}\to\infty

;
Hwang et al. 2015
.

Conclusion.
—We have developed a general framework for critical quantum dynamics far from equilibrium, extending the Kibble–Zurek paradigm to second-order DQPTs. By deriving scaling laws for the nonadiabatic entropy production, a measure of excess dissipation analogous to the dissipated work in closed systems
Deffner 2017
;
Fei et al. 2020
;
Nazé et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2025
;
Shen et al. 2025
, we have shown that the closing of the Liouvillian gap and the divergence of the Kubo-Mori-Bogoliubov QFI govern the universal behavior of irreversible thermodynamic signatures. The Fisher information metric has already been recognized as a powerful tool to identify critical points and map out phase diagrams even in the absence of a local order parameter
Ruppeiner 1995
;
Brody and Rivier 1995
;
Zanardi and Paunković 2006
;
Venuti and Zanardi 2007
;
You et al. 2007
;
Zanardi et al. 2007
;
Banchi et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2014
;
Marzolino and Prosen 2017
.
By connecting the information geometry of quantum NESS to thermodynamic irreversibility and critical slowing down, our work establishes a new framework for characterizing dissipative universality classes beyond near-equilibrium and unitary settings.

Remarkably, for Gaussian bosonic systems with a single soft mode, the quadratic divergence of the QFI near criticality yields a total nonadiabatic entropy production that is, to leading order, independent of the driving speed. Our predictions are validated in the driven-dissipative Dicke model and confirmed by finite-size scaling in the open Kerr oscillator. Since the open Kerr, Rabi, and Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick models share the same universality class as the open Dicke model (see e.g. Ref.
Liu et al. 2025
), i.e. that of the classical mean-field Ising model, they are expected to exhibit identical thermodynamic signatures near criticality, reflecting a common origin in the effective classical stochastic dynamics of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-symmetric order parameter.

Our results highlight the need for experimental probes of entropic currents in quantum devices. While stochastic entropy production has been measured in classical systems to verify fluctuation relations such as the Hatano–Sasa relation
Trepagnier et al. 2004
;
Mounier and Naert 2012
;
Granger et al. 2015
;
Joubaud et al. 2008
;
Wang et al. 2002
;
Hatano and Sasa 2001b
;
Esposito and Van den Broeck 2010b
, only a few quantum experiments have accessed entropic currents directly

;

;
Pekola 2015
;

;
Rossi et al. 2020
;
Aguilar et al. 2022
;
Aamir et al. 2025
;
Wadhia et al. 2025
.
Looking ahead, our work also motivates time-resolved probing of other routinely measured currents, such as the photon flux in cavity-QED realizations of open Dicke or Kerr models, which we have shown to also exhibit Kibble–Zurek scaling
Bettmann, L. P. et al.
. The cavity photon flux is directly related to the entropic flux
Goes et al. 2020

. Because the nonadiabatic entropy production rate can be expressed in terms of both the rate of change of the von Neumann entropy and the excess entropy flux to the environment

Appendix A
Slow-driving expansion of the state and the thermodynamic metric

For sufficiently slow driving through
\mathcal{L}_{g(t)}
, away from the critical point, and starting from the NESS
\hat{\pi}_{g(0)}
, the system will remain close to
\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}
, up to a small correction. We consider the expansion

\hat{\rho}_{t}=\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}+\delta\hat{\rho}_{t}.

(22)

Inserting this expansion into
\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}/\mathrm{d}t=\mathcal{L}_{g(t)}[\hat{\rho}]
and using that
\mathcal{L}_{g(t)}[\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}]=0
, we obtain a differential equation for
\delta\hat{\rho}_{t}

Scandi and Perarnau-Llobet 2019
;
Mandal and Jarzynski 2016
;
Cavina et al. 2017

\left(\mathcal{L}_{g(t)}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)[\delta\hat{\rho}_{t}]=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}},

(23)

which can be formally solved using the Drazin inverse
Gower et al. 1972
of the Liouvillian superoperator,
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{+}
. The Drazin inverse is defined as the unique operator satisfying the properties
\mathcal{L}_{g}\mathcal{L}_{g}^{+}[\hat{A}]=\mathcal{L}_{g}^{+}\mathcal{L}_{g}[\hat{A}]=\hat{A}-\hat{\pi}_{g}\Tr[\hat{A}]
,
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{+}[\hat{\pi}_{g}]=0
and
\Tr[\mathcal{L}_{g}^{+}[\hat{A}]]=0
. It has an integral representation given by
Gower et al. 1972

\mathcal{L}_{g}^{+}[\hat{A}]:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{\mathcal{L}_{g}\tau}\quantity(\hat{\pi}_g \Tr[\hat{A}]-\hat{A}).

(24)

By applying
\mathcal{L}_{g(t)}^{+}
on both sides of Eq. (
23
) and formally inverting the superoperator on the left-hand side, one obtains the geometric series expansion

\hat{\rho}_{t}=\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\mathcal{L}_{g(t)}^{+}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^{n}\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}.

(25)

Notice that the
n^{\text{th}}
term in this series contains an
n^{\text{th}}
-order derivative of
\hat{\pi}_{g(t)}
, and is thus proportional to
\dot{g}^{n}
, which means that Eq. (
25
) can be seen as an expansion in powers of the driving speed
\dot{g}
.

Using the above expansion of the state, the nonadiabatic entropy production along a slow driving protocol
g(t)
becomes
Lacerda et al. 2025
, to leading order in
\dot{g}
,

\Sigma_{\mathrm{na}}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}t\,\dot{g}^{2}(t)\,\zeta_{g(t)},

(26)

where
\zeta_{g}
denotes the thermodynamic metric in the space of NESS parameterized by
g

\zeta_{g}=-\Tr\!\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\hat{\pi}_{g}}{\mathrm{d}g}\,\mathcal{L}^{+}_{g}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{g}}{\mathrm{d}g}\right)\right].

(27)

Equivalently, using Eq. (
24
), this metric can be expressed as a correlation integral,

\zeta_{g}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{F}_{g}(\tau)\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}},

(28)

where the Kubo–Mori–Bogoliubov (KMB) inner product is

\langle\hat{A},\hat{B}\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,\Tr\!\left[\hat{\pi}_{g}^{s}\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{\pi}_{g}^{1-s}\hat{B}\right],

(29)

and
\hat{F}_{g}=\partial_{g}\log\hat{\pi}_{g}
is the logarithmic derivative operator, evolved in the Heisenberg picture as
\hat{F}_{g}(\tau)=e^{\mathcal{L}_{g}\tau}[\hat{F}_{g}]
.
The metric is closely related to the KMB quantum Fisher information (QFI),

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB}}_{g}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\hat{\pi}_{g}}{\mathrm{d}g}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{g}}{\mathrm{d}g}\right]=\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{F}_{g}\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}},

(30)

via the integral relaxation time

\tau_{g}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\frac{\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{F}_{g}(\tau)\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}}}{\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{F}_{g}\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}}}.

(31)

By construction, therefore,

\zeta_{g}=\tau_{g}\,\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB}}_{g}.

(32)

Appendix B
Scaling of the integral relaxation time with the inverse Liouvillian gap

We now show that the integral relaxation time
\tau_{g}
scales with the inverse Liouvillian gap near a dissipative critical point.
Expanding the correlation function in the eigenbasis of the vectorized Liouvillian
\{\lambda_{n},|\hat{r}_{n}\rangle,\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\}
, defined by
\mathcal{L}_{g}|\hat{r}_{n}\rangle=\lambda_{n}|\hat{r}_{n}\rangle
and

\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\mathcal{L}_{g}=\lambda_{n}\langle\hat{l}_{n}|
, and biorthonormal, so that
\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\hat{r}_{m}\rangle=\delta_{nm}
, we obtain

|\hat{F}_{g}(\tau)\rangle=e^{{\mathcal{L}}_{g}\tau}|\hat{F}_{g}\rangle=\sum_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}|\hat{r}_{n}\rangle\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\hat{F}_{g}\rangle,

(33)

where
\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\hat{F}_{g}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{l}_{n}\hat{F}_{g}]
.
Substituting into the KMB correlation function yields

\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{F}_{g}(\tau)\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}}=\sum_{n}e^{\lambda_{n}\tau}\,\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\hat{F}_{g}\rangle\,\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{r}_{n}\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}},

(34)

and the integral relaxation time

\tau_{g}=-\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n>0}{\lambda_{n}}^{-1}\,\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\hat{F}_{g}\rangle\,\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{r}_{n}\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{n>0}\langle\hat{l}_{n}|\hat{F}_{g}\rangle\,\langle\hat{F}_{g},\hat{r}_{n}\rangle_{\hat{\pi}_{g}}}.

(35)

Since
\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{n})<0
for all
n>0
, the integral converges.
Close to a dissipative phase transition, the slowest mode (
n=1
) dominates the dynamics. As the Liouvillian gap is given by
\Delta_{g}:=-\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{1})
, we obtain

\tau_{g}\sim\frac{1}{\Delta_{g}}.

(36)

Importantly, this result mirrors the divergence of relaxation times in equilibrium critical phenomena. In fact, in Ref.
Nazé et al. 2022
the integral relaxation time near a closed-system quantum phase transition was shown to diverge with the inverse of the closing Hamiltonian gap.

C.1
General Gaussian bosonic Lindblad dynamics

We assume dynamics obeying a Lindblad master equation of the form

\mathcal{L}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}^{-}\mathcal{D}[\hat{b}_{i}]\hat{\rho}+\gamma_{i}^{+}\mathcal{D}[\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}

(37)

with a bosonic quadratic Hamiltonian

\begin{split}\hat{H}=&\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}(B_{ij}\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}+B^{*}_{ij}\hat{b}_{j}\hat{b}_{i}))\end{split}

(38)

with
A=A^{\dagger}
and
B=B^{T}
.
We define quadratures

\begin{split}\hat{q}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{b}_{i}+\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger})\\
\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}-\hat{b}_{i}),\end{split}

(39)

and construct the column vector
\vec{R}=(\hat{q}_{1},\dots,\hat{q}_{N},\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{p}_{N})^{T}
.
The general quadratic Hamiltonian can be written in terms of quadratures as

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2}\vec{R}^{T}\cdot H_{R}\cdot\vec{R},

(40)

where
H_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
is a real, symmetric matrix representing the Hamiltonian coefficients, given by

\begin{split}H_{R}=&\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{2}\otimes(A+A^{T})+\sigma_{z}\otimes(B+B^{*})\\
&+\sigma_{y}\otimes(A^{T}-A)-i\sigma_{x}\otimes(B-B^{*}))\end{split}

(41)

The covariance matrix is given by

\Theta_{ij}=\frac{i}{2}\Omega_{ij}+\langle\hat{R}_{j}\hat{R}_{i}\rangle-\langle\hat{R}_{i}\rangle\langle\hat{R}_{j}\rangle,

(42)

with symplectic form

\Omega=(i\sigma_{y})\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N}.

(43)

The covariance matrix obeys the Lyapunov differential equation of the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Theta}{\mathrm{d}t}=-(W\Theta+\Theta W^{\dagger})+Y

(44)

with

\begin{split}W&=-\Omega H_{R}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{2}\otimes\Gamma,\\
Y&=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{2}\otimes(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-}).\end{split}

(45)

and
\Gamma=\gamma_{-}-\gamma_{+}
.
Consequently, the steadystate covariance matrix solves the Lyapunov equation

W\Theta+\Theta W^{\dagger}=Y

(46)

Further, it is often useful to express Gaussian bosonic states in the form

\hat{\rho}=\displaystyle{\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}R^{T}\cdot M\cdot R}}{Z}},\

(47)

with
M^{\dagger}=M
(real, symmetric), and partition function
Z=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}\left(\Theta+\frac{i\Omega}{2}\right)}
.

C.2
Constructing the Liouvillian spectrum from the drift matrix

The full Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
acts on the operator space of the density matrix, which is infinite-dimensional.
The spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
in such a basis includes all finite non-negative integer linear combinations (assuming the Liouvillian is diagonalisable)
Prosen and Seligman 2010
;
Barthel and Zhang 2022

\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{L})=\left\{-\sum_{i}k_{i}\lambda_{i}\middle|\lambda_{i}\in\mathrm{Spec}(W),k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\},

(48)

where
W
is the drift matrix in the Lyapunov equation (
46
).
Therefore, the Liouvillian gap is given by

\Delta=\min_{w\in\mathrm{Spec(W)}}|\mathrm{Re}(w)|.

(49)

C.3
Relative entropy for Gaussian bosonic states

Given two bosonic Gaussian states, and assuming zero mean,

\hat{\rho}=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}R^{T}\cdot M\cdot R}}{Z}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\hat{\sigma}=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}R^{T}\cdot N\cdot R}}{Y},

(50)

their relative entropy is given by

D(\hat{\rho}||\hat{\sigma})=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(M-N)\Theta\right]-\log\frac{Z}{Y},

(51)

where
\Theta
denotes the covariance matrix with respect to the state
\hat{\rho}
.
Note that

Z=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}\left(\Theta+\frac{i\Omega}{2}\right)}

(52)

and

M=2i\Omega\coth^{-1}(2\Theta i\Omega),\quad\Theta=\frac{1}{2}\coth\left(\frac{i\Omega M}{2}\right)i\Omega.

(53)

C.4
Thermodynamic metric for Gaussian bosonic states

Generalizing the results of Ref.
Mehboudi and Miller 2022
from Gaussian bosonic thermal states to Gaussian bosonic NESS (assuming zero mean, see Eq. (
47
)), we find that the single-parameter thermodynamic metric is given by

\zeta_{g}=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\mathcal{I}(\chi_{g})\,\Big(\Theta-\tfrac{i}{2}\Omega\Big)\,\mathcal{F}(\chi_{g})\,\Big(\Theta+\tfrac{i}{2}\Omega\Big)\right].

(54)

Here, we define
\chi_{g}=\partial_{g}M
. Furthermore,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(\chi_{g})&=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,\big(e^{is\Omega M}\big)^{T}\chi_{g}e^{is\Omega M},\\
\mathcal{F}(\chi_{g})&=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\nu\,\big(e^{\nu A}\big)^{T}\chi_{g}e^{\nu A},\\
\end{split}

(55)

with
A=\Omega\big(H-\mathrm{Im}(CC^{\dagger})\big)
, where the the matrix
C=(\vec{c}_{1}^{T},\dots,\vec{c}_{N_{L}}^{T})^{T}
encodes the jump rates of the associated linear Lindblad jump operators
\hat{L}_{i}=\vec{c}_{i}^{T}\cdot\vec{R}
.
In the following, we discuss how this metric can be evaluated efficiently numerically.

C.5
Unitary contribution

We first evaluate the integral in
\mathcal{I}(\chi_{g})
.
To this end, we define

A:=(i\Omega M)^{T},\qquad B:=i\Omega M.

(56)

Diagonalizing yields

A=U_{A}D_{A}U_{A}^{-1},\qquad B=U_{B}D_{B}U_{B}^{-1},

(57)

with
D_{A},D_{B}
diagonal, and we can write

e^{sA}=U_{A}e^{sD_{A}}U_{A}^{-1},\qquad e^{sB}=U_{B}e^{sD_{B}}U_{B}^{-1}.

(58)

Substituting into
\mathcal{I}(\chi_{g})
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\chi_{g})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{sA}\chi_{g}e^{sB}

\displaystyle=U_{A}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{sD_{A}}{\tilde{\chi}_{g}}e^{sD_{B}}\Big]U_{B}^{-1},

\displaystyle{\tilde{\chi}_{g}}

\displaystyle=U_{A}^{-1}\chi_{g}U_{B}.

Let
D_{A}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{2n})
and
D_{B}=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{2n})
. Then

\big[e^{sD_{A}}{\tilde{\chi}_{g}}e^{sD_{B}}\big]_{ij}=(\tilde{\chi}_{g})_{ij}e^{s(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j})}.

(59)

Hence

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{s(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j})}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j}}-1}{\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j}},&\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j}\neq 0,\\
1,&\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j}=0.\end{cases}

(60)

Define

J_{ij}=(\tilde{\chi}_{g})_{ij}\,f(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{j}),\qquad f(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{z}-1}{z},&z\neq 0,\\
1,&z=0.\end{cases}

(61)

Then

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{s(i\Omega M)^{T}}\chi_{g}e^{si\Omega M}=U_{A}JU_{B}^{-1}.

(62)

C.6
Dissipative-like contribution

For the dissipative-like term we evaluate

\mathcal{F}(\chi_{g})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\nu\,e^{\nu A^{T}}\chi_{g}e^{\nu A}.

(63)

This integral is the solution of the continuous Lyapunov equation

A^{T}Y+YA=-\chi_{g},

(64)

which can be solved efficiently using standard numerical routines.

C.7
Kubo-Mori-Bogoliubov Quantum Fisher information for Gaussian bosonic states

The Kubo-Mori-Bogoliubov (KMB) quantum Fisher information (QFI) is defined as

\mathcal{I}_{g}^{\mathrm{KMB}}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\hat{\rho}^{s}(\partial_{g}\log\hat{\rho})\,\hat{\rho}^{1-s}(\partial_{g}\log\hat{\rho})\Big].

(65)

Performing an analogous calculation to that presented in Appendix
C.4
and Refs.
Mehboudi et al. 2019
;
Mehboudi and Miller 2022
, we find that for Gaussian bosonic states with zero mean (see Eq. (
47
)) , the KMB QFI is given by

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{g}^{\mathrm{KMB}}=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{Tr}[\chi_{g}\check{\Theta}S^{T}(s)\chi_{g}S(s)\check{\Theta}^{T}],\end{split}

(66)

with
S(s)=e^{si\Omega M}
,
\chi_{g}=\partial_{g}M
and
\check{\Theta}=\Theta+i\Omega/2
.

C.8
Critical scaling of the symplectic eigenvalue for Gaussian bosonic states

We consider a Gaussian bosonic system whose covariance matrix evolves under a Lyapunov differential equation (see Eq. (
44
)), so that the steady-state covariance matrix solves the Lyapunov equation (see Eq. (
46
)).
In this section, our aim is to understand the behaviour of the soft mode symplectic eigenvalue of the steadystate covariance matrix in the vicinity of a critical point
g_{c}
in a second-order DQPT.
Let
\lambda_{s}
denote the eigenvalue of
W
, assumed to be diagonalisable, with smallest absolute real part (Liouvillian gap), and

Wv_{s}=\lambda_{s}v_{s},\qquad w_{s}^{\dagger}W=\lambda_{s}w_{s}^{\dagger},

(67)

where
v_{s}
is the right eigenvector and
w_{s}^{\dagger}
is the left eigenvector, such that

w_{s}^{\dagger}v_{s}=1.

(68)

We define the projection of
\Theta
onto the subspace of the slowest-decaying (soft) mode as

\theta_{s}=w_{s}^{\dagger}\,\Theta\,w_{s}.

(69)

Multiplying Eq. (
46
) from the left by
w_{s}^{\dagger}
and from the right by
w_{s}
gives

(w_{s}^{\dagger}W)\Theta w_{s}+w_{s}^{\dagger}\Theta(W^{\dagger}w_{s})=w_{s}^{\dagger}Yw_{s}.

(70)

Using the eigenvector relations and (
68
), we obtain the equation

\lambda_{s}\,\theta_{s}+\lambda_{s}^{*}\,\theta_{s}=y_{s},

(71)

where

y_{s}=w_{s}^{\dagger}Yw_{s},

(72)

which we assume remains finite and non-zero across the transition.
Thus,

\theta_{s}=\frac{y_{s}}{\lambda_{s}+\lambda_{s}^{*}}=\frac{y_{s}}{2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{s}}.

(73)

To understand the structure of divergences, recall that the Lyapunov equation admits the integral solution

\Theta=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{tW}Ye^{tW^{\dagger}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i,j}\frac{v_{i}\,(w_{i}^{\dagger}Yw_{j})\,v_{j}^{\dagger}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}^{*}},

(74)

using
e^{tW}=\sum_{i}e^{t\lambda_{i}}v_{i}w_{i}^{\dagger}
and
e^{tW^{\dagger}}=\sum_{j}e^{t\lambda_{j}^{*}}w_{j}v_{j}^{\dagger}
.
As
\Delta_{g}=\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{s}\to 0^{+}
, the dominant term is the contribution

\Theta=\frac{y_{s}}{\lambda_{s}+\lambda_{s}^{*}}\,v_{s}v_{s}^{\dagger}+\Theta_{\mathrm{reg}},

(75)

where
\Theta_{\mathrm{reg}}
remains finite at the transition.
The divergent part is rank-one. Hence in the two-dimensional canonical subspace of the soft mode,
\Theta_{s}
has one eigenvalue
\propto\Delta_{g}^{-1}
and the other
\propto\mathcal{O}(1)
.
The covariance matrix of the slowest decaying mode, in canonical coordinates, can be written as

\Theta_{s}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle X_{s}^{2}\rangle&\tfrac{1}{2}\langle X_{s}P_{s}+P_{s}X_{s}\rangle\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\langle P_{s}X_{s}+X_{s}P_{s}\rangle&\langle P_{s}^{2}\rangle\end{pmatrix},

(76)

where
(X_{s},P_{s})
are the associated quadratures, chosen such that they satisfy canonical commutation relations.
The symplectic eigenvalue of this matrix is

\nu_{s}=\sqrt{\det\Theta_{s}}.

(77)

From the rank-one structure above, we have

\det\Theta_{s}\sim\Delta_{g}^{-1},\qquad\nu_{s}\sim\Delta_{g}^{-1/2}.

(78)

The scaling behaviour of the soft mode symplectic eigenvalue is confirmed in the Gaussian driven-dissipative Dicke model, see Fig.

C.9
Scaling of the Kubo-Mori-Bogoliubov Quantum Fisher information for Gaussian bosonic states

Here we show that the Kubo-Mori-Bogoliubov (KMB) quantum Fisher information (QFI) diverges as
\mathcal{I}_{g}^{\mathrm{KMB}}\sim\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}|g-g_{c}|^{-2}
near a critical point for bosonic Gaussian NESS with a single soft mode.
In Appendix
C.7
, we have shown that for zero mean Gaussian states, the KMB QFI can be expressed as

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{g}^{\mathrm{KMB}}&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{Tr}[A\check{\Theta}S^{T}(s)AS(s)\check{\Theta}^{T}],\end{split}

(79)

with
S(s)=e^{si\Omega M}
.
Let us first express
X=i\Omega M
in its biorthonormal basis

X=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}v_{i}w_{i}^{T},\qquad w_{j}^{T}v_{i}=\delta_{ij},

(80)

so that

S(s)=\sum_{j}e^{\lambda_{j}s}v_{j}w_{j}^{T},\qquad S^{T}(s)=\sum_{j}e^{\lambda_{j}s}w_{j}v_{j}^{T}.

(81)

The KMB QFI then reads

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB}}_{g}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\mathrm{Tr}\left[A\check{\Theta}\int_{0}^{1}e^{(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})s}\,dsw_{i}v_{i}^{T}Av_{j}w_{j}^{T}\check{\Theta}^{T}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}-1}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[A\check{\Theta}w_{i}v_{i}^{T}Av_{j}w_{j}^{T}\check{\Theta}^{T}\right].\end{split}

(82)

Equation (
82
) contains the factors
\check{\Theta}=\Theta+i\Omega/2
and
\check{\Theta}^{T}=\Theta-i\Omega/2
. We expect the soft mode component to the
\Theta\Theta
-contribution to dominate near the critical point, since, as we have shown in Appendix
C.8
,
\nu_{s}\sim 1/\sqrt{\Delta_{g}}
and
\Delta_{g}\to 0
.
We further use that

\begin{split}A&=\partial_{g}M=i\Omega\partial_{g}X\\
&=\sum_{j}\underbrace{i\Omega\partial_{g}\lambda_{j}v_{j}w_{j}^{T}}_{A_{j}^{\lambda}}+\underbrace{i\Omega\lambda_{j}\partial_{g}(v_{j}w_{j}^{T})}_{A_{j}^{P}}\\
&=\sum_{j}A_{j}^{\lambda}+{A_{j}^{P}},\end{split}

(83)

so that
A
can be split into components involving the derivatives of the symplectic eigenvalues (
A_{j}^{\lambda}
) the projectors (
A_{j}^{P}
), respectively.
We now perform the explicit computation of the term involving the
g-
derivatives of
\lambda_{i}
, labelled with
\Theta\Theta\lambda\lambda
.
We express
\Theta=(\Theta i\Omega)i\Omega=\sum_{j}\nu_{j}v_{j}w_{j}^{T}i\Omega
, and find

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB,\Theta\Theta\lambda\lambda}}_{g}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijklmn}\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}-1}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\underbrace{i\Omega\partial_{g}\lambda_{k}v_{k}w_{k}^{T}}_{A_{k}^{\lambda}}\underbrace{\nu_{l}v_{l}w_{l}^{T}i\Omega}_{\Theta_{l}}w_{i}v_{i}^{T}\underbrace{i\Omega\partial_{g}\lambda_{m}v_{m}w_{m}^{T}}_{A^{\lambda}_{m}}v_{j}w_{j}^{T}\underbrace{\nu_{n}v_{n}w_{n}^{T}i\Omega}_{\Theta_{n}}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}-1}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}(\partial_{g}\lambda_{j}\nu_{j})^{2}\,\,w_{j}^{T}i\Omega w_{i}\,\,v_{i}^{T}i\Omega v_{j},\end{split}

where have used that
(i\Omega)^{2}=1
, the cyclic property of the trace as well as the biorthonormality condition.
Since
X=2\coth^{-1}(2\Theta i\Omega)
,
\Theta i\Omega
shares an eigenbasis with
X
and their respective eigenvalues are related via

\lambda_{j}=2\coth^{-1}(2\nu_{j})
,
and

\partial_{g}\lambda_{j}=-\frac{4\partial_{g}\nu_{j}}{4\nu_{j}^{2}-1}\overset{g\to g_{c}}{\longrightarrow}-\frac{\partial_{g}\nu_{j}}{\nu_{j}^{2}}.

(84)

From our analysis of the soft mode in Appendix
C.8
, we know that in the vicinity of the critical point, the soft mode symplectic eigenvalue of the covariance matrix diverges as
\nu_{s}\sim 1/\sqrt{\Delta_{g}}
. Therefore, the soft mode symplectic eigenvalue of
M

\lambda_{s}\sim 1/\nu_{s}
vanishes.
Further, the factors
w_{j}^{T}i\Omega w_{i}
and
v_{i}^{T}i\Omega v_{j}
are nonzero only for the labels
i
and
j
referring to symplectic eigenvalues of opposite sign. Therefore, there are two nonzero identical contributions to the sum, relevant to the scaling
(s,-s)
and
(-s,s)
.
Further, we note that the prefactor
\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}-1}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}
reduces to 1, for the dominant soft mode contribution, since
\lambda_{s}\to 0
, and find

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB,\Theta\Theta\lambda\lambda}}_{g}\sim(\partial_{g}\lambda_{j})^{2}\nu_{j}^{2}\approx\left(\frac{\partial_{g}\nu_{s}}{\nu_{s}}\right)^{2}\sim\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2}|g-g_{c}|^{-2},

(85)

Similarly, we find for the contribution involving the projector derivatives

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB,\Theta\Theta PP}}_{g}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijklmn}\frac{e^{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}-1}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\underbrace{i\Omega\lambda_{k}\partial_{g}(v_{k}w_{k}^{T})}_{A_{k}^{P}}\underbrace{\nu_{l}v_{l}w_{l}^{T}i\Omega}_{\Theta_{l}}w_{i}v_{i}^{T}\underbrace{i\Omega\lambda_{m}\partial_{g}(v_{m}w_{m}^{T})}_{A^{P}_{m}}v_{j}w_{j}^{T}\underbrace{\nu_{n}v_{n}w_{n}^{T}i\Omega}_{\Theta_{n}}\right]\\
&\sim(\partial_{g}\lambda_{j})^{2}\nu_{j}^{2}\sim\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2}|g-g_{c}|^{-2}.\end{split}

All other terms can be computed in an analogous fashion and can be shown to be subleading or vanishing by the biorthonormality condition.
Combining these findings, we obtain that the dominant scaling of the KMB QFI is

\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{KMB}}_{g}\sim\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}|g-g_{c}|^{-2},

(86)

so that the KMB QFI critical exponent is given by
\alpha=2
. Note that the prefactor
\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}
depends on the gap exponent
\gamma
.

Appendix D
Driven-dissipative Dicke model in the thermodynamic limit

The quantum Dicke model
Dicke 1954
;
Hepp and Lieb 1973
;
Wang and Hioe 1973
;
Hioe 1973
;
Klinder et al. 2015
;
Lang and Piazza 2016
;
Paz and Maghrebi 2022
;
Dimer et al. 2007
;
Nagy et al. 2011
;
Öztop et al. 2012
describes the interaction between a single-mode bosonic field and a large ensemble of two-level atoms (spins). The Hamiltonian (in units where
\hbar=1
) reads

\hat{H}=\omega_{c}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\omega_{z}\hat{J}_{z}+\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})(\hat{J}_{+}+\hat{J}_{-}),

(87)

where
\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are the annihilation and creation operators of the cavity mode,
\hat{J}_{z},\hat{J}_{\pm}
are collective spin operators for
N
two-level atoms,
\omega_{c}
is the cavity photon frequency,
\omega_{z}
is the atomic transition frequency, and
g
is the atom-light coupling strength.
In the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
, we assume that the system stays close to the collective spin ground state. We apply the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
Holstein and Primakoff 1940
;
Dimer et al. 2007

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}^{\dagger}\sqrt{2J-\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}\approx\sqrt{2J}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger},

(88)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{-}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2J-\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}}\,\hat{b}\approx\sqrt{2J}\,\hat{b},

(89)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{z}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}-J,

(90)

where
J=N/2
and
\hat{b}
is a bosonic operator with
[\hat{b},\hat{b}^{\dagger}]=1
.
Substituting into Eq. (
87
) and taking the large-
N
limit, the Hamiltonian in the normal phase becomes (
g<g_{c}
)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{N}

\displaystyle\approx\omega_{c}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\omega_{z}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+g(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}),

(91)

which is a quadratic Hamiltonian in the bosonic modes
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
.
To include dissipation, we add a Lindblad term modeling photon loss:

\dot{\hat{\rho}}=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\kappa\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho},

(92)

where
\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\hat{\rho}=\hat{L}\hat{\rho}\hat{L}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L},\hat{\rho}\}
, and
\kappa
is the cavity decay rate.
For the driven-dissipative Dicke model, we find the matrices (see Appendix
C.1
)

A_{N}=\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{c}&g\\
g&\omega_{z}\end{matrix}\right)

(93)

and

B_{N}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&g\\
g&0\end{matrix}\right)

(94)

for the normal phase Hamiltonian.
Further,

\begin{split}\Gamma&=\begin{pmatrix}2\kappa&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(95)

We can obtain the critical point by first setting
\mathrm{det}(W)=0
, and find
Kirton et al. 2019

g_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{c}^{2}+\kappa^{2}}{\omega_{c}}\omega_{z}}.

(96)

Appendix E
Open Kerr parametric oscillator

In this section, we provide a discussion of the single-mode Kerr parametric oscillator, a nonlinear oscillator subject to two-photon driving and single photon loss, near a dissipative quantum phase transition.
The Hamiltonian of a Kerr parametric oscillator in the rotating frame reads
Zhang and Baranger 2021

\hat{H}=-\Delta\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{U}{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}\hat{a}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(G\hat{a}^{\dagger 2}+G^{*}\hat{a}^{2}),

(97)

where
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
are the bosonic ladder operators,
\Delta
is the detuning between the driving and cavity frequency,
U
the Kerr nonlinearity, and
G
the parametric drive amplitude. Here
1/U\to\infty
corresponds to the thermodynamic limit
Vicentini et al. 2018
.
The system is coupled to a Markovian environment with one-photon loss at rate
\kappa
, described by the Lindblad master equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\hat{\rho}=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]+\kappa\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho},\quad\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho}=\hat{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}\}.

(98)

E.1
Finite-size scaling

In infinitely coordinated models, or models without spatial degrees of freedom, the scaling analysis relies on the so-called coherence number, instead of the correlation length, which is assumed to diverge in the thermodynamic limit, as
Botet et al. 1982
;
Hwang et al. 2018

N_{c}\sim|g-g_{c}|^{-\nu^{*}}.

(99)

We are interested in the finite-size scaling behaviour of a quantity that diverges in the thermodynamic limit with proximity to the critical point as

A_{\infty}\sim|g-g_{c}|^{-\alpha_{A}}.

(100)

The scaling hypothesis assumes the existence of a regular function
F_{A}
, s.t. near
g_{c}
and for large N

A_{N}\sim|g-g_{c}|^{-\alpha_{A}}F_{A}\left(\frac{N}{N_{c}}\right)

(101)

Therefore, we expect a collapse of the curves when plotting

A_{N}|g-g_{c}|^{\alpha_{A}}\quad\mathrm{vs.}\quad N|g-g_{c}|^{\nu^{*}}
and further
A_{N}(g_{c})\sim N^{\alpha_{A}/\nu*}
.
Interestingly,
\nu^{*}
is related to the mean field
\nu_{\mathrm{MF}}
and the upper critical dimension
d_{c}
of a corresponding short-range mean field model in dimension
d=d_{c}
via
\nu^{*}=\nu_{\mathrm{MF}}d_{c}
.
In Fig.
7
we present the finite-size scaling results for the KMB QFI of the dissipative Kerr model. The numerical findings confirm
\alpha=2
and are in agreement with
\nu_{\mathrm{MF}}=1/2
and
d_{c}=4
of the mean-field Ising universality class with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-symmetry in the critical dimension. Further, our results agree with similar analysis of models in the same universality class
Hwang et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2025
.
