Influence of edge Notch geometry with and without presence of ice filling on dynamic behavior of rock
Authors: Shadman Mohammadi Bolbanabad, Vahab Sarfarazi, Masoud Monjezi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the effects of edge notch characteristics including inclination (0° and 90°), length (0.2–0.6 L), position relative to the incident bar (L/3, L/2, 2 L/3; top/bottom surfaces), and filling condition (dry or ice-filled) on the dynamic response, energy distribution, and fragmentation characteristics of cylindrical iron ore rock specimens under high strain rate loading. Understanding these effects is essential for improving the design of blasting and dynamic loading processes in open-pit mining. Experiments were conducted using a Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) at a constant strain rate of 772.7 s⁻¹. Results show that notch geometry and ice filling significantly affect mechanical properties, energy distribution, and fracture patterns. shorter notch lengths, higher inclinations, increased distances from the incident bar, and ice-filled specimens increased peak strength, elastic modulus, fracture energy, work at peak load, energy consumption efficiency, and fractal dimension. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) observations revealed tensile cracks at lower notch inclinations and shear cracks at higher notch inclinations. Ice-filled specimens exhibited better stress wave transmission and more uniform fragmentation. The X-shaped fracture pattern was mainly observed in dry specimens with 0° notches. Reducing notch length or moving the notch far from the incident bar decreased reflected energy and increased both transmitted and dissipated energies.

Introduction

In open-pit mining, blasting operations are among the most common and effective methods for mineral extraction. This process generates high-intensity shock waves that propagate through the rock mass, inducing crack initiation, fracture development, and ultimately rock fragmentation. Discontinuities such as notches within the rock mass play a critical role in influencing shock wave propagation, stress concentration, and fracture patterns, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The impact of these discontinuities becomes even more complex in cold-region mines, where notches may become saturated with infilling materials such as ice due to water infiltration and subsequent freezing. The presence of ice within notch, owing to its distinct mechanical properties compared to empty voids, alters wave transmission characteristics, crack propagation paths, and the extent of energy dissipation. Consequently, a comparison of rock specimens with ice-filled cracks and those with an unfilled (empty) notch can provide important information about how notch infill affects the failure processes and fracture patterns of iron ore over dynamic loading circumstances. The Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) system’s introduction and extensive use has inspired many scientists to carry out in-depth studies to investigate the mechanical characteristics of rocks1–3. Both the intrinsic strength of the rock mass and the magnitude of the impact load affect how much damage is done to it4–7. The impact forces that come from blasting events usually cause the rock mass to fracture and spall8. Joints can essentially be regarded as inherent damage features within the rock mass, leading to crack propagation and coalescence when subjected to load9–13. It is commonly known that joints are common in rock masses and are essential in determining how they behave mechanically14–18. As such, in order to avoid any possible risks and make well-informed design decisions, engineers must have a thorough understanding of the mechanical behavior of jointed rock masses19–21 and to prevent potential hazards.

Previous studies have primarily focused on the effects of strain rate, joint geometry, and type of infilling material on the geomechanical and dynamic properties of rocks. Li et al.22–24 conducted a comprehensive study on how joint properties and wave of incident characteristics affect scatters of stress wave and dissipation of energy in jointed rock masses, using experiments and theory. Understanding these interactions is essential to predict the dynamic response of jointed rocks under varying loading conditions. Strain rate effects, driven by varying input energy levels, significantly affect rock behavior, failure mechanisms, and fragmentation efficiency25–27. To assess engineering efficiency, it is essential to comprehend how dynamic strain rates affect the loss of energy and fragment distribution in jointed rocks28. Low strain rates cause macroscopic failure through-going fractures, while high strain rates produce widespread fractures, breaking the rock into fine fragments29,30. Dai et al.31 investigated high-strain-rate SHPB tests on granite, investigating the factors that influence compressive and tensile strength, such as specimen dimensions, friction, dynamic stress equilibrium, and the application of static equations to estimate dynamic tensile strength. Ge et al.32 carried out dynamic compressive experiments on gypsum applying SHPB at varying strain rates, finding that peak dynamic strength and strain increased exponentially, while fragmentation size decreased exponentially with strain rate, as confirmed by simulations. These findings emphasize the importance of strain rate as a key factor in the dynamic mechanical response of jointed rocks.

In addition to strain rate, joint geometry significantly affects rock failure. Yang et al.33 examined the impact of joint thickness on the dynamic rock characteristics. Their results showed that while energy dissipation increased with joint thickness, the dynamic compressive strength of the jointed rock dropped. Wang et al.34 conducted impact tests on cement mortar specimens containing 0 to 3 horizontal penetrating joints using the Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB). They determined how joint count affected the failure modes of the rock specimens, compressive strength, and transference of energy under dynamic loading. Cai et al.35 conducted dynamic tests on argillite samples using the SHPB device. They discovered that failure happened via spalling in dynamic uniaxial compression tests alongside the direction of loading with radial cracks in dynamic tensile tests. The effects of joint length, position, and type on the dynamic strength of cylindrical samples were examined by Wang et al.36, who discovered that strength rose with shorter, closed joints and fell as the joint approached the center. Edge joints had higher strength than middle joints and shifting the joint from center to edge altered strength and crack propagation under constant strain rate. Li et al.37 tested dynamic strength in jointed cylindrical samples of coal and cement mortar at 30°, 60°, and 90° angles, finding that strength increased with strain rate. Joint angle and position influenced strength, particularly at 30°, with cement mortar samples showing higher dynamic strength than coal-cement mortar mixtures. These studies collectively highlight the necessity of carefully considering joint geometry when evaluating dynamic rock behavior.

Beyond geometry and strain rate, the type of notch or joint filling plays a crucial role in fracture behavior and energy dissipation. Feng et al.38 studied sandstone with symmetric and asymmetric intersecting joints, finding: dynamic strength increased with strain rate, while elastic modulus stayed constant, asymmetric joints had higher strength, failure mode was X-shaped shear, and energy dissipation rose with strain rate, but energy efficiency was less affected. Li et al.39 used SHPB to test jointed cement mortar samples (0°–90°) and found: dynamic strength increased then decreased with joint angle, damage degree negatively correlated with strength, and crack initiation was tensile in intact and 0°/90° samples, but both tensile and shear at 15°–75°. Using jointed concrete samples with 0–4 parallel joints at a 0° angle to the loading axis, Li et al.40 performed dynamic compression experiments. They discovered that adding joints increased sample damage, improved ductility, and changed the single-peak stress-strain curve to a multi-peak one. Furthermore, as the number of joints rose, the peak dynamic strength and energy loss ratio declined. Lei et al.41 studied cubic sandstone samples with a single joint under dynamic and quasi-static loading, varying joint angles (0°–90°). They found that crack behavior and failure mode were affected by strain rate and joint angle. Under quasi-static loading, Joint angle caused a decrease in static strength and an increase in elastic modulus. Under dynamic loading, both dynamic strength and elastic modulus significantly increased with strain rate, with changes depending on joint angle. These results underline the importance of understanding joint filling effects, particularly when predicting energy dissipation and failure patterns.

Complex interactions among confining pressure, joint spacing, and joint orientation further influence rock dynamic behavior. You et al.42 investigated the effects of confining pressure, row spacing, and joint orientation using three-axis dynamic compression tests on sandstone samples with four joints. All samples displayed X-shaped failure, with shear band failure occurring at specific row distances. They also discovered that dynamic strength and elastic modulus increased with confining pressure, that dynamic strength positively correlated with strain rate, and that dynamic strength fluctuated with changes in joint dips, initially decreasing and then slightly increasing with greater joint row distance. Ke et al.43 studied freeze-thaw effects on the static and dynamic compressive strength of sandstone using SHPB, finding a decrease in strength with more cycles and proposing a model to predict dynamic strength reduction based on strain rates and sample sizes. Water-saturated frozen jointed rock masses exhibit more complex deformation and failure than continuous ones, with properties influenced by joint penetration, failure modes, and geometry44–48. Xu et al.49 performed dynamic compression tests on water-saturated concrete at − 30 °C with varying bonding degrees (0–100%). Results showed that strength initially increased then declined with bonding, and peak stress decreased linearly with joint length. These findings demonstrate that environmental and boundary conditions interact with joint and filling characteristics, significantly affecting dynamic response.

Temperature and moisture conditions within joints or notches are also critical factors. Yin et al.50 investigated the influence of bond length (50, 60, 70 mm) and moisture situations (natural, dry, and saturated) on the sandstone dynamic behavior with two edge joints. The results showed that elastic modulus and dynamic strength rose with bond length, with dry samples having the maximum modulus. Failure mode shifted from tensile to tensile-shear with longer bonds, and energy dissipation was influenced with longer linkages, the failure mode changed from tensile to tensile-shear, and both characteristics affected the loss of energy. Kumar et al.51 investigated the effects of strain rate (40–140 s⁻¹) and joint filling (unfilled, cement grout, epoxy resin) on dynamic compressive strength and crack propagation in cubic sandstone. Results showed that strength increased with strain rate; epoxy-filled samples exhibited comparable strength to intact samples and tended to develop tensile or radial cracks, unlike the shear cracks observed in unfilled and cement grout-filled specimens. Kumar et al.52 investigated the influence of temperature (30–650 °C), heating duration (0–15 h), and joint filling materials (unfilled, cement grout, epoxy resin) on marble. Results showed that mechanical properties remained stable up to 500–650 °C, after which they declined. Epoxy resin was more effective at low temperatures, while cement grout performed better at elevated temperatures and prolonged heating. Fracture typically initiated at the joint tip, except in epoxy-filled joints at low temperatures. Tensile cracks dominated under lower temperatures and shorter durations, while shear failure was more prevalent in porous specimens. Levy and Molinari53 and Zhou et al.54 highlighted the need of taking into account preexisting imperfections in rock materials during fragmentation and offered empirical models for forecasting the fragment dimension dispersion. Furthermore, the fragment size distribution of rock materials has been characterized using statistics and geometry methodologies55. These results emphasize the combined influence of filling type and environmental conditions on fracture mechanics and energy dissipation.

Despite these extensive studies, the impact of notch factors, including inclination, length, and position with respect to the incident stress wave, both with and without filling, on the failure pattern, energy absorption, fragmentation, elastic modulus, and dynamic peak strength of rock specimens under dynamic impact loading is still unclear, though. Magnetite iron ore specimens with non-persistent edge notches were subjected to a battery of Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) tests in this investigation. The objective was to investigate the influence of notch inclination, length, and position, as well as the presence or absence of frozen water filling, on the failure patterns, fragmentation characteristics, fractal dimension, and mechanical properties of the samples. In order to improve blasting design in open-pit mining operations, findings of this study can provide light on the mechanisms of failure, energy dissipation, and fragmentation.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

For this investigation, iron ore from the Galali open-pit mine in Iran’s Kurdistan Province was used to create rock specimens. All samples were cored from a single large iron ore block to maintain sample uniformity and homogeneity, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The iron ore used in this investigation comprises magnetite, pyrite, goethite, marcasite, and chalcopyrite minerals (Fig. 2d), and their optical microscopic images are presented in Fig. 2c. Every specimen was cut and polished in compliance with the guidelines of the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM). To guarantee complete and consistent contact between the specimen and the loading bars during dynamic testing, the top and bottom surfaces of the cylindrical samples were machined with extreme accuracy to guarantee a deviation in parallelism of less than 0.02 mm and a deviation in perpendicularity of less than ± 0.8°. A total of 30 cylindrical specimens with dimensions of 22 mm in diameter and 22 mm in length (length-to-diameter ratio = 1) were prepared for dynamic loading tests (Fig. 2a). Every test was carried out under the same loading circumstances. Artificial edge notches were made at predetermined spots using a diamond wire saw that was 3 mm thick. The geometric configuration of the notches was defined based on their inclination angle and relative length. In this experimental series, two notch inclination angles were considered: 0° (perpendicular to the incident wave) and 90° (parallel to the incident wave). For each angle, three notch length-to-sample length ratios were investigated: 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6. At the 0° inclination, the notches were introduced at three different positions along the specimen height: one-third, one-half, and two-thirds of the total length. The notches were positioned on either the specimen’s top or bottom surface at the 90° inclination).

The specimens were divided into two groups based on notch condition: (1) dry-notched (SD) and (2) ice-filled-notched (SF) specimens (Fig. 3). To eliminate all moisture, 15 specimens for the SD group were oven-dried for 24 h at 70 °C. For the SF group, 15 specimens were water-saturated for 48 h, then frozen at − 5 °C for 24 h to ensure complete ice formation within the notches. The freezing temperature of − 5 °C was selected to represent the typical ambient temperature observed at the Galali open-pit mine during winter, providing field-representative yet easily controllable laboratory conditions. Labeling followed the SD and SF codes, as shown in Fig. 2b.

Test equipment

The Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) apparatus is commonly used to examine the mechanical reactions of brittle materials in accordance with the guidelines established by the International Society for Rock Mechanics and Rock Engineering (ISRM)56–58. The striker bar, incident bar, and transmission bar are the three primary bars that make up this system, as shown in Fig. 4, in addition to a data collecting and processing system. Table 1 provides an overview of the mechanical and dimensional parameters of the apparatus. The dynamic strain rate applied to the rock specimens is determined by the gas pressure in the striker subsystem, which regulates the velocity of the striker bar. In this study, dynamic tests were conducted under a constant strain rate condition, with a strain rate of 772.7 s⁻¹, achieved by setting the gas pressure at 12.5 bar, resulting in a projectile velocity of approximately 17 m/s. This strain rate was calculated as the ratio of projectile velocity to specimen length, considering the specimen length of 22 mm. To reduce frictional effects, the rock specimens were positioned between the incident and transmission bars after the SHPB loading system had been precisely calibrated. They were then greased with grease at the contact interfaces. To shape an appropriate stress pulse, a tiny copper disc that is 11 mm in diameter and 1 mm thick was positioned between the striker and incident bars. During the loading process, this disc helps to create stress equilibrium at both ends of the specimen by producing a stress pulse with a moderate rising time. The uniformity of the strain rate was verified by analyzing the strain signals recorded by strain gauges mounted on both the incident and transmission bars. This ensured that all specimens were tested under consistent loading conditions, confirming the constant strain rate throughout the experiments.

A compressive stress wave is created when the striker bar and incident bar collide, and it enters the test specimen through the incident bar. A portion of this wave is transmitted as a compressive wave into the transmission bar and a portion is reflected as a tensile wave in the incident bar due to an impedance mismatch between the specimen and the bars. To capture these waveforms, strain gauges are mounted at appropriate positions: While the strain gauge on the transmission bar records the transmitted strain (εₜ), the gauges on the incident bar measure the incident strain (εi) and the reflected strain (εr).

Experimental results

Equation (1) was used to determine the average stress on the rock specimen’s two sides. Rock specimens with non-persistent edge notches, with and without notch infill, orientated at angles of 0° and 90°, and with notch surface-to-total shear surface ratios of 0.2 and 0.4 are shown in Fig. 5 with typical dynamic stress histories recorded at both ends. The dynamic stress waves on both sides of the specimens exhibit a high degree of consistency throughout the loading phase, except for minor variations observed during the initial stage. This constancy, which is especially apparent close to the peak stress region, attests to the successful establishment of dynamic stress equilibrium.

The dynamic stress–strain curves of the rock samples can be acquired as soon as the dynamic stresses on both sides of the specimens equalize. With notch surface-to-total shear surface ratios of 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 and distinct notch positions for the 0° orientation at one-third, one-half, and two-thirds of the specimen length, as well as for the 90° orientation at the top and bottom surfaces under dynamic loading, Fig. 6 shows the stress-strain curves of rock specimens with non-persistent edge notches subjected to two orientations of 0° and 90°. During the first loading phase, these stress-strain curves, which were acquired at a constant loading rate, concurrently rise. Subsequently, due to the influence of parameters such as notch angle, length, position relative to the incident bar, and the presence or absence of notch filling, the linear portion of the curves exhibits different slopes. The dynamic strength is represented by dynamic stress, which grows nonlinearly as the loading process progresses and reaches its maximum value. The macroscopic behavior of the specimens in the post-failure stage is reflected in the stress–strain curve, which exhibits a downward tendency after achieving the peak stress.

Figures 7 and 8 present scatter plots showing the dynamic strength and dynamic elastic modulus as functions of fissure configuration, under varying notch angles, lengths, positions, and the presence of notch filling, as detailed numeric results summarized in Table 2. These plots clearly illustrate the effects of these parameters on both dynamic strength and dynamic elastic modulus of rock specimens with non-persistent edge notches. In both dry and ice-filled conditions, the observed trends are consistent: increasing the notch angle from 0° to 90°, decreasing the notch length from 0.6 to 0.2, and moving the notch farther from the incident bar all result in higher dynamic strength and elastic modulus. Moreover, the presence of notch filling significantly enhances both properties. comparison between parts (a) and (b) in Fig. 7, and similarly, in Fig. 8, further reveals that for specimens with fixed notch angles, lengths, and positions, both dynamic strength and dynamic elastic modulus are consistently higher in the ice-filled condition than in the dry condition. This highlights the critical role of notch filling in improving the mechanical properties of the specimens. The results underscore the interaction between notch geometry and material filling, which significantly influences the rock’s performance under dynamic loading.

As illustrated in Figs. 9, 10 and 11, which present the dry specimens with non-persistent 0° edge notches, the fracture morphology commonly exhibits a distinctive “X-shaped” pattern on the specimen surfaces. This pattern is primarily attributed to the interaction of shear bands initiated from both the notch tip and the specimen boundaries. In these dry specimens, the shear bands propagate under the influence of dynamic stress concentration and impact wave distribution, and their intersection and superposition generate inclined failure paths deviating from the principal loading direction. The collision and merging of these shear bands form a funnel-shaped fracture zone that guides crack propagation along intersecting trajectories, thereby producing the characteristic X-shaped pattern observed on the specimen surfaces. Therefore, the formation of this pattern is directly associated with the initiation and evolution of shear bands governed by the dynamic stress distribution and the geometric configuration of the specimen with a 0° non-persistent edge notch.

By comparing the behavior of rock specimens containing non-persistent edge notch oriented at 0° and 90°, under both filled and unfilled conditions, significant differences in energy transmission patterns and fragmentation mechanisms are observed. A significant amount of the wave of incident is either dissipated or reflected at the notch interface when the notch is oriented at 0° with respect to the direction of propagation of the stress wave (i.e., perpendicular to the compressive wave front). This results in reduced effective energy transfer into the specimen, thereby lowering its capacity to absorb energy. As a result, the average size of the resultant pieces increases, indicating a drop in fragmentation intensity and a reduction in the extent of fracture and fragmentation. In contrast, when the notch is oriented at 90° (i.e., parallel to the direction of the compressive wave), the stress wave passes through the notch with minimal attenuation, allowing a greater portion of dynamic energy to be transmitted into the internal structure of the specimen. This enhanced energy transfer activates more intense failure mechanisms, resulting in increased fragmentation. Accordingly, specimens with a 90° notch orientation absorb more energy and exhibit smaller average fragment sizes, indicating higher efficiency in the fragmentation process.

In addition to the progressive failure behavior of specimens examined in Figs. 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to illustrate the differences between tensile and shear fractures from a mesostructured perspective. The images obtained from SEM analysis, as shown in Fig. 19, reveal two distinctly different fracture patterns on the failure surfaces of the specimens. In Fig. 19a, the fracture surface exhibits a smooth, polished, and even texture, with no evidence of crushed or pulverized particles. Such microstructural features indicate the predominance of a tensile fracture mechanism, in which separation of the material planes occurs under the influence of tensile stresses. The presence of uniform surfaces accompanied by straight and orderly fracture lines is indicative of Splitting fracture, characterized by the propagation of radial cracks from the loading zone toward the periphery and terminal regions of the specimen. This pattern suggests that the concentration of tensile stresses at the crack tip served as the primary driving factor for crack propagation and the separation of material planes. In contrast, Fig. 19b depicts a fracture surface with a rough, uneven, and undulating texture, densely populated with fine crushed and pulverized particles resulting from the fracture process. These characteristics are indicative of a shear fracture mechanism and correlate with the development of circumferential cracks oriented at an oblique angle relative to the specimen boundaries. The abundant presence of abrasive particles, wear marks, and localized surface damage in these regions confirms the dominance of frictional interaction and slip between fracture planes under the influence of significant shear stress components. Furthermore, these microstructural evidences suggest that a considerable portion of the fracture energy was dissipated through sliding and friction along the crack surfaces, leading to grain refinement and the generation of pulverized debris on the fracture surface. Moreover, a quantitative analysis of the SEM images was performed to complement the qualitative observations of tensile and shear fracture mechanisms. For the specimen with a 0° notch, the fracture surface is predominantly characterized by tensile features, covering approximately 79% of the surface in dry specimens and 73% in ice-filled specimens, while shear features account for the remaining 21% and 27%, respectively. In contrast, the specimen with a 90° notch exhibits a clear predominance of shear features, representing 75% of the surface in dry specimens and 83% in ice-filled specimens, with the remainder corresponding to tensile features (25% for dry and 17% for ice-filled specimens). These results indicate that the 0° specimen is predominantly tensile, while the 90° specimen is predominantly shear.

Fragmentation and size distribution

Considering that the distribution of fragment dimensions in rock specimens containing inclined, non-persistent edge notch under varying notch angles, lengths, positions relative to the incident bar, and with or without notch fillers is a critical and fundamental parameter for evaluating iron ore fragmentation in open-pit blasting operations, sieve analysis was conducted on the final fragmented specimens. This was carried out using a circular vibrating sieve equipped with mesh sizes of 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 9.5, and 19 millimeters (Fig. 20).

Figure 21 illustrates the fragment size distribution of specimens containing non-persistent edge notch under varying notch angles, lengths, positions relative to the incident bar, and with or without notch filling. At a constant notch inclination, the cumulative percentage of fragmented pieces indicates that greater fragmentation occurs in specimens with shorter notch lengths, notch positioned farther from the incident bar, and notch filled with ice. Furthermore, for a fixed notch length, increasing the notch inclination from 0° to 90°, along with a notch location farther from the incident bar and the presence of a notch filler, evidently promotes the formation and spread of internal rock cracks, leading to finer fragmentation.

A statistical examination of the fragment size distribution is necessary to provide a quantitative depiction of the fracture behavior of rock specimens under dynamic loading. The fragment dimension distribution of rock samples has been extensively described using the three-parameter generalized extreme value (GEV) distribution function, which is written as follows65,66:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F({\upxi\:},\:{\upmu\:},\:{\uplambda\:})\:=\:\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\:\{-{[1+{\upxi\:}\left(\frac{d-{\upmu\:}}{\:{\uplambda\:}}\right)]}^{-\raisebox{1ex}{$1$}\!\left/\:\!\raisebox{-1ex}{${\upxi\:}$}\right.}\}\:$$\end{document}

where d represents the fragment dimension, the shape variable ξ controls the skewness and tail behavior of the distribution curve, the location variable µ indicates the broken sample average fragment size and describes the potential of fragment size concentration, and the scale variable λ represents the range of the fragment size distribution. Therefore, further analysis of the variations in µ and λ can provide valuable insights into the fragment size distribution and offer a precise characterization of the crack angle impacts, length, position, and the presence or absence of crack filler on rock fragmentation.

Figure 22 shows the location variable µ and scale variable λ of the fragment size distribution for samples containing non-persistent edge notch under varying notch angles, lengths, notch positions relative to the incident bar, and with or without notch filler. Detailed values are provided in Table 3. For a constant notch angle, the location parameter µ increases linearly with increasing notch length, notch position closer to the incident bar, and dry (unfilled) notches; similarly, the scale parameter λ also increases. Conversely, for a fixed notch length, the notch angle can be increased from 0° to 90°, shifting the notch position farther from the incident bar, and the presence of notch filler result in a linear decrease of both the location parameter µ and the scale parameter λ. These observations indicate that increasing notch angle, decreasing notch length, moving the notch position away from the incident bar, and the presence of notch filler led to greater fragmentation of the samples in dynamic loading.

Mandelbrot and Wheeler67 developed the notion of fractal geometry, which has been effectively used to characterize extremely irregular and self-similar objects. Research has shown that the size distribution of rock fragments exhibits unique fractal features68–70. While minor fracture involves the evolution and aggregation of microscopic cracks, macroscopic breakage of rock specimens is characterized by a small group of fragments; the energy dissipation process and broken fragments demonstrate self-similarity as a result of this similar behavior. In this study, the distribution of rock fragments subjected to dynamic loads is therefore investigated using the fractal theory. The following expression71 can be used to determine the fractal dimension of rock fragments:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{f}=\:3-\frac{Log(M<{d}_{i})/{M}_{0}}{Log({d}_{i}/{d}_{max})}$$\end{document}

Here, ​ M(< di) is the cumulative mass of rock fragments with diameters smaller than di; M0 denotes the overall mass of the rock fragments, and dmax indicates the highest fragment diameter observed in this study.

The fractal dimensions resulting from the fragmentation of samples under varying angle, length, notch position relative to the incident bar, and with or without notch filler were calculated using Eq. (6). The impacts of these variables on the fractal dimension of the rock fragments are illustrated in Fig. 23. Relevant details are also summarized in Table 3. In traditional Euclidean geometry, the dimensions of objects are defined as integer values: a point, line, plane, and solid correspond to zero through three dimensions, respectively. However, the fractal dimension resulting from rock fragmentation under effect loading ranges between 2 and 3. This indicates that the fracture structure and the resulting fragment distribution do not conform to classical Euclidean geometry but exhibit more complex and irregular characteristics. Specifically, a fractal dimension between 2 and 3 signifies that the spatial distribution of the fragmented pieces is neither purely surface-like nor fully volumetric, but rather an intermediate state with a complex, self-similar structure. This highlights the significance of fractal geometry theory in analyzing rock fracture and fragmentation phenomena under dynamic conditions72.

Whereas a result close to 3 indicates that the final breakdown of the rock specimen was accompanied by catastrophic collapse, a value close to 2 indicates a fractal dimension process that is essentially two-dimensional. A more uniform fragment size distribution is associated with a larger fractal dimension, which is indicative of greater self-similarity. At an inclination angle of 0 degrees, with changes in the length and position of the notch relative to the incident bar, the fractal dimension of the samples increased from 2.332 to 2.804 for samples with notch filling and from 2.287 to 2.754 for samples without notch filling. Furthermore, for a notch inclination angle of 90 degrees with varying notch lengths and positions relative to the incident bar, the fractal dimension grew from 2.596 to 2.846 for samples with notch filling and from 2.518 to 2.824 for samples without notch filling. Under conditions of increased notch length, the impact pressure applied to the rock sample is more significantly dissipated, resulting in a greater extent of the compressive wave being reflected. Consequently, a reduced compressive wave penetrates the rock sample, leading to the propagation of multiple cracks, which ultimately results in lower fragmentation of the sample and the formation of larger fragments. Furthermore, a larger percentage of the compressive wave can be transmitted or absorbed by the rock sample when the notch length is shorter since the impact pressure imparted to the sample is less attenuated. As a result, several cracks are started, and several new fractured surfaces are created. As a result, the rock samples are severely fragmented into numerous smaller pieces. Consequently, samples with shorter notch lengths show traits including lower fragment sizes, a more uniform size distribution of the fragments, and larger fractal dimensions. Additionally, the fractal dimension rises as the inclination angle increases from 0° to 90° degrees at a constant notch length. Thus, it can be said that under dynamic loading conditions, the fractal dimension of the samples increased as a result of a decrease in notch length, an increase in notch inclination, a notch position that was further away from the incidence bar, and the existence of notch filling.

Energy analysis

Microcracks start at the tips of preexisting flaws due to stress concentration during the fragment creation of brittle materials under dynamic strain rate. These cracks then spread throughout the entire specimen and combine with branching cracks to produce the fragment. From an energy standpoint, the failure process of rock specimens can be thought of as a process in which energy mostly dissipates into heat energy, kinetic energy for flying pieces, and fracture energy for creating new free surfaces. The effects of notch inclination, length, position with respect to the incidence bar, and whether notch filling is present on the reflected, transmitted, and dissipated energy in rock specimens are depicted in Fig. 24a and f. Table 4 displays the values in detail. Under both filled and unfilled situations, a decrease in notch length and a notch position farther from the incident bar result in a drop in reflected energy and a significant increase in transmitted and dissipated energies at a constant inclination angle. Furthermore, when compared to notch conditions with ice filling, the reflected, transmitted, and dissipated energy are consistently lower in the dry notch condition.

Figure 25a and b illustrate the energy consumption efficiency of rock specimens with edge non-persistent notches under varying notch inclination angles, lengths, positions relative to the incident bar, and with or without notch filling. At inclination angles of 0° and 90°, under both filled and unfilled notch conditions, different notch lengths and positions relative to the incident bar result in energy consumption efficiency values that vary as follows: at 0° notch inclination, from 36.454 to 60.361 and 34.711 to 57.865, and at 90° notch inclination, from 50.513 to 65.613 and 47.373 to 63.204, respectively. These results indicate a clear dependency of energy consumption efficiency in rock materials on the notch inclination, length, position relative to the incident bar, and the presence or absence of notch filling. The effects of mentioned parameters on the energy dissipation density in rock specimens are illustrated in Fig. 25c and d. The energy dissipation density in the specimens rises at a constant inclination angle when the notch length decreases, the notch’s distance from the incidence bar increases, and the notch filling is present, with the exact values provided in Table 4. With decreasing notch length, notch filling, increasing notch distance from the incidence bar, and increasing inclination angle, the energy dissipation density shows an increasing trend. Therefore, at greater notch lengths, energy dissipation is lower, and the specimens fail earlier. With decreasing notch length, rocks containing edge non-persistent notches can absorb more energy and develop a greater number of shear and tensile cracks.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that notch geometry and configuration, inclination (only 0° vs. 90° tested), length (0.2–0.6 L), position relative to the incident bar (L/3, L/2, 2 L/3; top/bottom surfaces), and the presence of ice infill collectively govern the dynamic response of magnetite iron ore under SHPB loading. Across all configurations, decreasing notch length, increasing inclination to 90°, increasing the notch distance from the incident bar, and adding ice infill led to: (i) higher dynamic strength and elastic modulus, (ii) greater dissipated energy and energy utilization efficiency, and (iii) finer fragmentation characterized by lower GEV location/scale parameters and higher fractal dimensions.

These trends are broadly consistent with recent SHPB studies on jointed rocks. Yao et al.73 tested sandstone cylinders with ice-filled joints over − 20 °C to 0 °C and joint inclinations from 0° to 90°. They reported that lower temperatures raised peak dynamic strength but reduced dynamic elastic modulus; joints diminished dynamic mechanical properties and promoted tensile cracking, with failure modes transitioning among tensile, compressive shear, and mixed depending on inclination. Our ice-filled notches similarly enhanced energy transmission and promoted extensive tensile and shear cracking, increasing both energy dissipation density and fractal dimension. The observed strengthening with ice infill in our data (via larger external work and fracture energy at peak load) aligns with Yao’s finding that temperature-controlled ice interfaces modify wave transmission and crack nucleation pathways. Since the mechanical properties of ice are temperature-dependent, lower freezing temperatures could potentially enhance the strength of ice-filled notches and further influence the dynamic response.

To further elucidate the mechanism responsible for the observed strengthening in ice-filled specimens, the observed increase in dynamic strength for specimens with ice-filled notches can be attributed to a combination of micromechanical effects. First, the ice provides cohesive bridging across the notch, which reduces the local stress concentration at the notch tip and delays crack initiation. Second, the presence of ice alters the local mechanical impedance, facilitating more uniform transmission of the incident stress wave through the specimen and thereby reducing the likelihood of premature localized failure. Third, the ice partially constrains lateral opening of the notch, modifying the crack initiation angles and promoting more distributed fracture patterns. Collectively, these effects contribute to the enhanced strength observed in ice-filled specimens. The quantitative and qualitative SEM observations support this interpretation, showing that ice filling reduces the propagation of localized tensile cracks and increases shear-dominated regions, consistent with the more uniform stress distribution.

Li et al.74 combined quasi-static and dynamic tests on single-jointed sandstone cubes and showed that (a) under quasi-static loading, strength and modulus first decline then rise with joint inclination, and (b) under dynamic loading, both increase significantly with strain rate, modulated by joint angle. While our SHPB tests used a constant strain rate and only two inclinations (0° and 90°), the strong coupling among rate, angle, and flaw configuration that Li et al. emphasized helps explain why, at fixed rate, our 90° configuration, shorter notches, and greater notch distances from the striker collectively activated more microcracks, shortened failure duration, and produced smaller fragments (higher D), all while raising dissipated energy and external work. From an energy perspective, the consistent decreases in reflected energy and increases in transmitted and dissipated energies as notch length shortens or the notch moves away from the incident bar support a fracture mechanics interpretation. when stress-wave impedance mismatch at the notch interface is reduced (90°, ice infill, shorter/remote notches), more input energy becomes available for crack initiation, branching, and new surface creation. The statistical signatures, lower GEV µ and λ and higher fractal D approaching ~ 2.8, indicate a more uniform, fine fragmentation regime, echoing the self-similar fragmentation behavior reported in72,75.

Although this study addresses key aspects of fracture and fragmentation in edge-notched magnetite iron ore under dynamic loading, it is limited to two notch inclinations (0° and 90°), specific sample dimensions, and two filling conditions (dry and ice-filled). Future research should expand the parameter range by investigating intermediate notch inclinations, different filling materials, varying strain rates, and the combined effects of multiple cracks or more complex flaw geometries. Furthermore, numerical modeling could provide additional insight into stress wave interactions and crack evolution, complementing the experimental observations.

Limitation of the present study

The experimental investigation in this study was conducted exclusively on cylindrical magnetite specimens obtained from a single block of the Galali iron ore open-pit mine. While this approach ensured uniformity in lithology, microstructure, and specimen geometry, it inherently limits the generalizability of the findings to other rock types or geological settings. Variations in mineral composition, microstructural features, natural flaws, joint orientations, and in-situ stress conditions across different sites could significantly influence the dynamic fracture behavior, crack propagation patterns, energy dissipation, and fragmentation characteristics. Moreover, the study considered only two notch inclinations (0° and 90°), three notch lengths, two filling conditions (dry and ice-filled), and a fixed strain rate. The influence of the variations of freezing temperature on the dynamic behavior was not investigated in this study and will be addressed in future work. Factors such as intermediate notch angles, alternative filling materials, different specimen sizes, or varying loading rates could result in different dynamic responses. Therefore, while the results provide valuable insights into the coupled effects of notch geometry and ice filling on rock fragmentation under high strain rates, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to other materials, flaw configurations, or loading conditions, and the interpretations presented here are primarily representative of settings similar to those tested.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the dynamic mechanical behavior, fracture mechanisms, and fragmentation characteristics of magnetite iron ore specimens containing non-persistent edge notches under high strain rate loading using the Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) system. The effects of notch inclination (0° and 90°), notch length, notch position relative to the incident bar, and notch filling condition (dry or ice-filled) were systematically investigated. The main conclusions are as follows:

Peak strength, elastic modulus, fracture energy, and external work at the peak load point are strongly influenced by notch length, position, inclination, and filling condition.
