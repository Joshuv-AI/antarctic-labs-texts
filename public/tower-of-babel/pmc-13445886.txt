Factors related to employment across vocational levels in patients with schizophrenia: focus on subminimum wage non-competitive employment
Authors: Genki Koyama, Masaki Nakano, Yusuke Shimizu, Yu Mimura, Shin Kurose, Satoyuki Ogino, Taketo Takata, Hiroyuki Uchida, Michitaka Funayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with schizophrenia often face difficulty in employment. Non-competitive supported employment serves as an important option for individuals with schizophrenia, yet research focusing on such type of work is rare. This study aimed to investigate factors related to employment in schizophrenia. We particularly focused on patients engaging in subminimum wage non-competitive supported work by comparing such patients to those receiving minimum wage or above and those unemployed.MethodsWe recruited 282 outpatients from 16 to 65 years old diagnosed with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. Data were collected from September 2024 to February 2025. Participants were categorized into two groups: employed and unemployed. The employed group consisted of patients with full-time jobs, part-time jobs, those employed through a special system for individuals with disabilities, minimum wage or above non-competitive supported employment, and subminimum wage non-competitive supported employment. We evaluated schizophrenia symptoms using the Brief Evaluation of Psychosis Symptom Domains (BE-PSD), social functioning using the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF), and collected demographic and clinical data. Comparative and subgroup analyses with focus on subminimum wage non-competitive employment were conducted.ResultsEmployment was associated with milder negative symptoms (p = 2.64 × 10− 6), less excitement (p = 0.0173), younger age (p = 0.00580), and male sex (p = 0.0274). Additionally, achieving employment paying minimum wage or above was linked to milder negative symptoms (p = 0.00150), disorganization symptoms (p = 0.00271), and depression/anxiety symptoms (p = 0.0389). No significant differences were observed between participants in subminimum wage paying non-competitive employment and those unemployed.ConclusionsNegative symptoms and excitement symptoms may be important clinical correlates of employment among patients with schizophrenia. The lack of significant differences between those engaged in subminimum wage paying non-competitive employment and those unemployed should be interpreted with caution, but it may suggest that subminimum wage paying non-competitive employment represents a level of vocational engagement that remains accessible even for individuals with more severe symptom burden.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Patients with schizophrenia often suffer from impaired social function, as reflected by their low employment rate varying from 8% to 35% [1–3], despite 55% to 70% of patients showing interest in work [4–6]. Employment is crucial for individual patients not only because it provides financial security, but also because it has sometimes been reported to increase quality of life (QOL) [7–10], elevate physical activity [11], boost self-esteem [12–13], and deepen sense of connection with society [14, 15]. Recent studies further suggest that engagement in vocational activity—whether competitive or non-competitive—may support well-being, self-confidence, and a sense of purpose, although the magnitude of these benefits may differ across employment types [13]. While job seeking in the competitive labor market using the Individual Placement and Support (IPS) model has widely been regarded effective [16–18], non-competitive employment serves as an important option for those without successful IPS interventions and for those who have limited access to IPS.

Several factors have been reported to be related to employment in general in patients with schizophrenia. Among these are lower levels of negative symptoms, better cognitive function, longer or higher level of education, premorbid employment, a married state or presence of a cohabitant, and low to moderate antipsychotic drug doses [19–25]. Previous studies have focused primarily on competitive employment, and very rarely have studies aimed to compare different vocational levels including non-competitive supported employment been investigated. The few studies that have done so have identified factors such as disorganization and processing speed to be associated with non-competitive employment [26, 27]. Further research is necessary to clarify what facilitates participation across different types of employment. This would be helpful in setting treatment goals to assist patients’ integration into society.

Many countries around the world have implemented supported employment measures for individuals with disabilities in both non-competitive and competitive environments [28–31]. This comes at a time when emphasis is being placed on deinstitutionalization and outpatient treatment of psychiatric patients [32–34]. Initially, paid non-competitive work and vocational training, usually funded publicly, were the most common approach for providing patients with mental disabilities access to employment [35–37]. These are usually referred to as a “train then place” approach, in which participants are provided with vocational rehabilitation and training before seeking competitive jobs [32, 36]. Non-competitive employment including vocational workshops may confer a range of psychosocial benefits despite financial compensation. Previous studies have suggested that participation in structured work activity may support daily structure, social interactions, and sense of purpose [38, 39]. These studies highlight the potential value of such employment as a means of facilitating engagement in meaningful activity.

In contrast to this, the 1990s saw the development of an employment model called IPS, which is an intervention consisting of rapid job search in the competitive market followed by support in the work environment, or in other words, a “place then train” approach [16–18, 32, 35]. IPS was first introduced in the United States and then spread to other countries [29, 40]. Though IPS has gained evidence for its effectiveness over time, non-competitive employment is still an important option for patients with schizophrenia for two main reasons. First, not all patients with schizophrenia participating in IPS achieve employment. Literature shows a 20 to 60% achievement rate of employment among patients with schizophrenia over 18 to 24 months of IPS intervention [18, 35, 41], which means 40 to 80% of patients with schizophrenia could not obtain or maintain jobs through this approach. Lower success rates have been attributed to factors like severe negative symptoms [18, 42], older age [43], and longer illness duration [43]. Non-competitive employment is a reasonable alternative for individuals unable to achieve employment through IPS. Secondly, implementation of IPS has been a challenge in many countries due to financial and organizational barriers [32, 40]. Many low- and middle-income countries lack IPS programs mainly due to lack of policy and shortage of funding [35, 44]. Among high-income countries, Spain, Italy, Portugal, Poland, Greece, Romania, and Japan have limited or patchy implementation due to regulatory emphasis on non-competitive employment [32, 45–48]. Even in countries like the United States where IPS is most common, a minority of those in need of this program have access [35]. In such environment, non-competitive supported jobs may potentially play a role to integrate patients with schizophrenia into society, although there is mixed evidence regarding the value of non-competitive employment [49].

Japan is one example of a country with heavy reliance on non-competitive supported employment. In Japan, roughly three different types of supported employment are available: employment in the competitive market using the employment for individuals with disabilities system, type-A non-competitive supported employment, and type-B non-competitive supported employment [32, 50]. Employment for individuals with disabilities is a special application system for individuals with physical and mental disabilities. This system is used in the competitive labor market. On the other hand, type-A and type-B non-competitive supported employment are government-subsidized workshops outside of the competitive labor market. Type-A involves employment contracts and guarantees users minimum wage or above, whereas type-B, typically less intense in terms of work content, does not require employment contracts and pays users below the minimum wage [32]. In 2022, the national mean wage for type-A and type-B non-competitive supported employment was 947 yen (approximately 6.3 USD) and 243 yen (1.6 US) per hour, respectively, whereas the national mean minimum wage of 961 yen (6.4USD) per hour the same year [51, 52]. Given the inadequate implementation of IPS in Japan, non-competitive supported work is a reasonable option for patients experiencing difficulty with employment. Type-B non-competitive supported employment, a subminimum-wage-paying vocational workshop, may especially serve as a low-threshold option for occupational participation, warranting distinction from Type-A, which is much closer to competitive jobs in terms of vocational intensity and employment structure.

In this study, we aimed to identify factors related to employment in outpatients with schizophrenia. Particularly, we focused on patients participating in subminimum wage non-competitive supported employment by comparing such patients to those receiving minimum wage or above and those unemployed. Findings of this study may apply not only to Japan but to countries worldwide with patchy or limited implementation of employment support in the competitive market.

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted from September 2024 to February 2025 at the outpatient clinic associated with Ashikaga Red Cross Hospital. We recruited all outpatients receiving treatment at the clinic from ages 16 to 65 (working age) with a diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition [53]. Diagnosis was made by seven psychiatrists, each with at least five years of experience in diagnosing psychiatric disorders in clinical practice, and six of whom are Board-Certified Psychiatrists. Patients going to school were excluded, as education may increase likelihood of gaining employment later. Out of 285 patients selected, 3 declined to participate, and 282 participated in the study. The primary caregiver provided answers to questions whenever the patient could not do so. The frequency of proxy responses was not systematically recorded, therefore ability to assess the potential impact of proxy reporting on the findings is limited.

Definition of employment

Those occupied in all types of employment comprised the employed group. Five types of jobs were included: full-time, part-time, employment for individuals with disabilities, type-A non-competitive supported employment, and type-B non-competitive supported employment. Patients not participating in any form of employment were included in the unemployed group. Though the exact earning of each individual patient was unknown, we included 2 self-employed participants in the full-time group as well, as the fact that both were working full-time without any pension support suggested adequate earnings.

Although the employed group included heterogenous forms of employment with varying work intensity, wage structure, and degree of integration into the competitive labor market, these categories were combined to reflect participation in any form of occupational activity. The reason for this is because the aim of our study was to investigate factors related to any type of employment including subminimum wage non-competitive work with a lower threshold, given the aforementioned potential benefits. While supported employment studies typically focus on competitive employment outcomes, our approach aligns with past studies in a broader context that combine both competitive work and vocational workshops into one group [54, 55].

Hours spent in work have been considered representative of work intensity in literature [56, 57]. However, we could not determine vocational intensity based solely on number of hours spent in employment, because a patient participating in non-competitive supportive employment could be working the same number of hours as a patient with a full-time job in a competitive setting. We therefore collected such information but did not include them in our analyses.

Study design and variables

This study was cross-sectional. We surveyed participants’ employment status as previously mentioned. The seven psychiatrists noted above evaluated patients’ schizophrenia symptoms using Brief Evaluation of Psychosis Symptom Domains (BE-PSD) Version 2.0 [58, 59] in face-to-face interviews. This is a validated scoring method that allows us to grade five symptom domains including psychotic symptoms, disorganization, negative symptoms, excitement/mania, depression/anxiety each on a scale of 0 (none) to 6 (most severe). The psychiatrists also graded participants’ overall functioning using Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) [60]. BE-PSD and GAF scores were always clinician-rated rather than caregiver-reported. Each participant or their caregiver had independently decided which type of employment to participate in instead of being assigned to a certain employment type based on clinical assessments. Although formal inter-rater reliability was not assessed, all raters were experienced clinicians familiar with the use of the scale. Prior to data collection, raters reviewed the scoring guidelines and discussed the interpretation of individual items to promote consistency. When uncertainty arose during the rating process, cases were discussed among raters to ensure a shared understanding of scoring criteria.

We collected information previously considered relevant to employment using a survey. Answers to this survey were the only information that were provided by caregivers when the patient could not do so. The questionnaire surveyed times hospitalized, number of years of education [20], premorbid employment status [56], and presence of cohabitants [21] from each patient or their primary caregiver. We also acquired data on the presence of physical comorbidities considered to cause long-term sick leaves. In specific, we surveyed history of malignant neoplasms and circulatory diseases (including neurovascular and cardiovascular diseases), the two most frequent causes of work disability following mental disorders in Japan [61], as well as the 341 intractable diseases designated by the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare of Japan as of April, 2024 [62]. Receipt of disability pensions was also surveyed, as it has been associated with lower rates of employment in individuals with schizophrenia [57]. However, this relationship may partly reflect underlying illness severity and functional impairment rather than a direct causal effect. Moreover, causal direction between receipt of pensions and employment status is unclear, because inability to work may prompt a patient to apply for a pension as well.

Medical records were reviewed to obtain demographic and clinical data previously reported to be potentially associated with employment, including age, sex, age of onset, and total chlorpromazine (CP) equivalent of antipsychotics administered [19–23].

Statistical analysis

Initially, a case-control study of employed versus unemployed was conducted to identify factors related to employment. GAF scores were not included in the statistical analyses because the causal relationship between employment status and GAF scores is unclear. Similarly, the proportions of patients receiving a disability pension were not included either.

Categorical data were compared using chi-squared-test, while numerical data were compared using Welch’s t-test. Bonferroni adjustment was used to correct for multiple comparisons by multiplying the number of potentially related comparisons to p-values. Age, age at onset, the five domains of BE-PSD, times hospitalized, and dose of antipsychotics are all potentially related with one another. Therefore, we multiplied the p-values of these comparisons by 9. Subsequently, we conducted a multiple logistic regression analysis using all variables mentioned above. We simultaneously entered all variables into this model since they were all reported to be associated with employment in literature.

Given our focus on subminimum wage non-competitive employment, we performed two additional analyses. First, to identify the factors associated with earning minimum wage or above within the employed group, we compared those using type-B non-competitive supported employment, which is the only form of employment that does not guarantee minimum wage in our study, and all others employed. Again, categorical data were compared using chi-squared-test except for presence of physical comorbidities, which was examined using Fisher’s exact test since it contained a cell with an expected frequency of less than 5. Numerical data were compared using Welch’s t-test. Bonferroni adjustment was conducted here as well. We also conducted a multiple logistic regression analysis for this comparison. Since the association between different domains of psychiatric symptoms and employment were the main focus of our study, we entered the 5 domains of BE-PSD into this model. Given the limited number of events, the number of additional covariates were restricted to maintain model stability, as according to the “rule of ten events per variable”, which suggests that ten events per variable are necessary for logistic regression analyses [63, 64].

Second, to further clarify the factors related to achievement of type-B non-competitive supported employment, we compared participants of type-B non-competitive supported employment and those unemployed. Categorical data were compared using chi-squared-test except for presence of physical comorbidities, which was examined using Fisher’s exact test since it contained a cell with an expected frequency of less than 5. Numerical data were compared using Welch’s t-test. Bonferroni adjustment was conducted likewise to the main analysis. Since none of the variables displayed significant differences, we did not perform a multiple logistic regression analysis.

A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant for all statistical tests. For regression analyses, CP-equivalent antipsychotic dose was modeled per 100 mg increase to improve interpretability. R (4.4.2) was used to conduct all statistical analyses.

Results

Out of 282 participants, 154 (54.2%) were female. The mean age of patients was 46.4 ± 11.7 years, the mean age of onset was 28.6 ± 10.1 years, and the mean GAF score was 53.4 ± 13.6. The overall employment rate including both competitive and non-competitive employment was 45.7%.

The purpose of presenting this table is to demonstrate the overall background of these patients rather than to make statistical observations. As mentioned in the previous section, GAF and reception of a disability pension was not included in further statistical analyses because it could both affect and be affected by employment status. Also, we did not use days working per week as an outcome, because it did not necessarily represent work intensity.

Main analysis: multiple logistic regression analysis

The results of the multiple logistic regression analysis are shown in Table 3. The model was statistically significant (χ2 = 72.1, df = 14, p = 7.91 × 10− 10). Factors associated with employment were less severe negative symptoms of BE-PSD (odds ratio(OR): 0.495, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.363–0.655, p = 2.64 × 10− 6), younger age (OR: 0.957, 95% CI: 0.928–0.987, p = 0.00580), less severe excitement/mania symptoms of BE-PSD (OR: 0.686, 95% CI: 0.497–0.927, p = 0.0173), and male sex (OR: 0.503, 95% CI: 0.270–0.919, p = 0.0274). A variance inflation factor of less than 3 for each variable indicated minimal multicollinearity [64].

Secondary analysis between type-B non-competitive supported employment and other types of employment

Table 4 presents a comparison of variables between patients participating in type-B non-competitive supported employment and patients earning minimum wage or above. Patients earning minimum wage or above had a significantly higher mean length of education, significantly lower mean BE-PSD scores in disorganization, negative symptoms, and depression/anxiety symptoms.

The results of a logistic regression analysis performed using the five domains of BE-PSD are presented in Table 5. The model was statistically significant (χ2 = 49.2, df = 5, p = 2.06 × 10− 9). Factors associated with achieving minimum wage were less severe negative symptoms, (OR: 0.452, 95% CI: 0.269–0.726, p = 0.00150), less severe disorganization (OR: 0.426, 95% CI: 0.236–0.728, p = 0.00271), and less severe depression/anxiety (OR: 0.630, 95% CI: 0.397–0.964, p = 0.0389) of BE-PSD. A variance inflation factor of less than 3 for each variable indicated minimal multicollinearity [65].

Discussion

The present study aimed to identify factors related to employment in patients with schizophrenia. We particularly focused on patients engaging in subminimum wage paying non-competitive supported employment by comparing such individuals to those receiving minimum wage or above and those unemployed. Subminimum wage non-competitive employment is an option for patients finding difficulty in employment, especially for those without successful IPS interventions and for those who have limited access to IPS. However, such jobs have rarely been considered in previous studies. To address this point, we took Japan as an example and investigated factors associated with employment including type-B non-competitive supported employment, a subminimum wage paying occupational workshop. Further, we conducted secondary analyses comparing patients with such jobs with those earning minimum wage or above and with those unemployed.

We found that negative symptoms and excitement are associated with employment overall including non-competitive work. Furthermore, negative symptoms, disorganization, and depression/anxiety were significantly associated with earning minimum wage within those employed. However, we did not identify any significant differences between those participating in type-B non-competitive supported employment and those unemployed. The notion that negative symptoms are related to employment is in consistency with previous reports [19, 20, 66, 67]. However, the lack of significant difference in negative symptoms between those participating in type-B non-competitive supported employment and those unemployed is a new finding. While this finding may be interpreted as indicating limited measurable differences in clinical status, it may also suggest that individuals with relatively severe symptoms are still able to engage in this form of employment. In this sense, subminimum wage non-competitive supported employment possibly represents a low-threshold opportunity for occupational participation among individuals who may otherwise remain disengaged from the workforce. Though IPS is widely considered the most effective approach in improving vocational outcomes among patients with schizophrenia, many patients have limited access to IPS or experience unsuccessful interventions [18, 32, 35, 40, 41]. In such context, non-competitive supported employment may serve as a complementary option, providing opportunities for structured activity and social participation [38, 39].

One point warranting caution is that quantifiable measurement of intensity of work was not statistically incorporated into the analysis. Although data on days of work (mean 4.55 ± 0.89 days, range 2–5 days) were collected, they were not included in the statistical analysis. This may have obscured the differences in the intensity of vocational engagement among type-B supported employment participants, limiting the interpretability of this comparison.

Another point to consider is the allocation of limited vocational resources between non-competitive supported employment and IPS. IPS is widely recommended as the first-line approach and emphasizes rapid placement into competitive employment regardless of symptom severity [56, 68]. Nonetheless, it has also been suggested that vocational support should be individualized and can include initial non-competitive pathways [49]. For patients without successful IPS interventions or patients who are not yet ready for competitive employment, non-competitive supported employment may play a complementary role. Therefore, a balanced approach may be necessary, in which IPS is prioritized while non-competitive supported employment is still available for individuals of a broader population.

Association of excitement symptoms to employment overall, and association of disorganization and depression/anxiety to earning minimum wage within those employed are also new findings. Psychopharmacological treatment is often noted to be effective for delusions, hallucinations, excitement, or depression more than for negative symptoms [22, 69, 70]. Therefore, in patients without significant negative symptoms but with excitement, putting therapeutical emphasis on excitement may be effective in order for them to achieve any kind of employment at all. Likewise, adequate medication to control disorganization and depression/anxiety symptoms may help patients obtain minimum wage paying jobs. On the other hand, contradictory to past reports [20], the CP equivalent of antipsychotic dosage was not associated with employment in our study. Therefore, controlling excitement with adequate doses of antipsychotics may be prioritized over reducing dosage for the sake of reduction, though paying attention to cognitive impairment, oversedation, and extrapyramidal symptoms due to medication is crucial [20, 71, 72].

Younger age being associated with employment is consistent with previous reports [19, 20, 23, 73]. This association may be explained by factors identified in prior studies, including shorter illness duration and less accumulated functional disadvantage in younger individuals, both of which have been shown to influence long-term outcomes in schizophrenia [74].

Male sex was associated with employment in our logistic regression analysis, despite no significant difference projected in the case-control study. Evidence on the relationship between gender and employment in patients with schizophrenia remains mixed. Some studies, like ours, show a better vocational outcome for males, especially in East Asia and North Africa and the Middle East [19, 20, 75]. The reason for this has not been investigated in depth, but it may reflect the employment gender gap in the overall population, in part likely due to the assumed cultural roles of women [75–80]. East Asian females having one of the highest marriage rates among patients with schizophrenia may have also influenced our results [58]. Other studies have suggested that female patients have better outcome in employment, attributed to the generally more benign disease course and later onset in female patients [81–84].

Our study has several limitations. First, the employed group encompassed a heterogeneous range of employment types, including full-time, part-time, disability employment, minimum wage or above paying supported employment, and subminimum wage paying supported employment, which differ substantially in vocational demands and socioeconomic context. This heterogeneity may obscure important differences between employment types and therefore our findings should be interpreted as reflecting general engagement in work rather than specific employment outcomes. Moreover, type-B non-competitive supported employment may not be viewed as work in the normal sense but rather as an occupational workshop. However, including this in the employed group was necessary in our study because our aim was to investigate factors related to any type of employment including subminimum wage non-competitive work with a lower threshold, given the potential benefits. Second, the employment rate in our study was notably higher compared to other reports [1–3], though still substantially lower than the employment rate of general population in Japan, 79.4% [85]. Excluding type-B non-competitive supported work participants from the employment group would have made the employment rate 28.0%, which is within the range of previously reported data. Third, the direction of association between psychiatric symptoms and employment warrants careful consideration. While lower symptom severity may increase the likelihood of obtaining employment, literature suggests that engagement in work itself may be associated with improvements in clinical and functional outcomes [86]. Given the cross-sectional design on this study, causal direction cannot be determined. Further longitudinal studies are necessary to address this issue, i.e. determine whether lower symptom burden facilitates employment, employment leads to symptom improvement, or both. Fourth, cognitive function such as processing speed, previously considered to affect vocational outcome, was not included in our analysis [73, 87–91]. Though evaluation of cognitive function is important, severity of negative symptoms may be indicative of cognitive impairment, as past studies show that cognitive impairment is associated with negative rather than positive symptoms of schizophrenia [92]. Fifth, surveying subjective QOL in patients may be helpful in clarifying the importance of encouraging patients to participate in non-competitive employments rather than to not [7–10]. Sixth, we have not confirmed consistency between raters in symptom evaluation, although BE-PSD has been reported to demonstrate good inter-rater reliability. As previously mentioned, raters carefully reviewed scoring guidelines, discussed the interpretation of individual items, and discussed individual cases to ensure a shared understanding of scoring criteria. Finally, legal and cultural differences between countries may yield varying results depending on the research setting. However, as we have mentioned, many countries value non-competitive employment as a way for patients to participate in society in a way similar to Japan [43–46]. The above issues should be addressed, and the findings of this article should be confirmed in future studies.

Conclusions

The findings of our research suggest that negative symptoms and excitement symptoms may be important clinical correlates of employment among patients with schizophrenia. Additionally, disorganization and depression may be important clinical correlates of obtaining jobs paying minimum wage or above. No significant differences were observed between individuals engaged in subminimum wage-paying non-competitive employment and those unemployed. While this finding should be interpreted with caution, it may suggest that such employment represents a level of vocational engagement that remains accessible even for individuals with more severe symptom burden, particularly negative symptoms.
