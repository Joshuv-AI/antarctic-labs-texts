scMarkerGene: an interpretable neural network framework for cell-type-specific marker gene discovery
Authors: Jingkai Zhang, Si Hoi Kou, Jiulu Zhao, Xiaomin Li, Yongbing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn single-cell transcriptomics, the accurate identification of cell-type-specific marker genes is fundamental for understanding cellular heterogeneity. However, existing approaches often rely on arbitrary thresholds and clustering heuristics, making them sensitive to noise, annotation bias, and prone to capturing highly expressed rather than truly specific genes. Here, we present scMarkerGene, an interpretable neural network framework for marker gene discovery. scMarkerGene constructs a Contribution Score (CS) matrix that quantitatively measures each gene’s influence on cell-type discrimination, transforming neural network predictions into gene-level importance signals. Through a downstream specificity filtering process, scMarkerGene robustly captures cell-type-distinguishing features and demonstrates strong robustness to dropout noise, diverse cell population sizes, varying annotation resolutions, and heterogeneous sequencing technologies. We comprehensively validated scMarkerGene across scRNA-seq datasets from multiple species, as well as on spatial transcriptomics and discretized pseudotime data, where it effectively identified dynamic and spatially resolved marker genes. Collectively, scMarkerGene provides an efficient, accurate, and interpretable framework for single-cell transcriptomic analysis, with broad potential for extension to multi-omics data integration and interpretation. The source code is available at https://scmarkergene.zhaopage.com/.Keywords: single cell data analysis, cell identity, marker gene discovery, artificial intelligence

Introduction

Rapid advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled transcriptome-wide profiling at single-cell resolution, uncovering cellular heterogeneity, rare subpopulations, and lineage trajectories that are hidden in bulk analyses [1, 2]. As the scale of scRNA-seq studies expands, the identification of reliable marker genes has become central to analysis, enabling comprehensive profiling of cell types across nearly any tissue and species [3–5]. Marker genes are typically a small subset of genes whose expression profiles can distinguish the sub-populations of cells in a dataset. They serve as molecular signatures for defining cellular identity and states, provide the foundation for accurate cell type annotation, and facilitate the identification of disease-associated cell programs. Ideally, Marker genes should exhibit strong specificity-being highly expressed in one cell type while minimally expressed in others. Consequently, robust marker gene selection is a critical step in single-cell data analysis.

In response to the need for identification of marker genes, numerous computational frameworks have been proposed and developed. Traditional approaches, such as those implemented in widely used toolkits like scanpy [6], rely on differential expression tests (e.g. Wilcoxon rank-sum, t-test, or log-fold change) to identify cell-type-enriched genes, but their results are often unstable due to noise and batch effects. scMAGs [7] incorporates a preliminary gene filtering step somewhat analogous to differential expression tests, but the final selection of marker genes is based on clustering validity indices rather than statistical significance testing. Machine learning models such as scGeneFit [8] and SMaSH [9] apply regression or sparse matrix decomposition to link expression patterns with cell identity, while deep learning strategies like MarkerMap [10] employ graph-based architectures or interpretable neural models to capture nonlinear cell-gene relationships. scVI combines bayesian statistics with deep generative modeling to detect differentially expressed genes [11]. Although these methods based on machine learning improve accuracy, they often act as ‘black boxes,’ offering limited interpretability and reproducibility [12]. Tree-based approaches like Ns-Forest [13] attempt to address interpretability by ranking genes through feature importance, but their reliance on single models makes them sensitive to randomness and less generalizable. Collectively, existing methods face three major challenges: (i) unstable and irreproducible marker gene lists, (ii) lack of transparent interpretability, and (iii) poor generalizability across datasets with varying noise or dropout levels. These limitations underscore the need for a robust, interpretable, and reproducible framework for marker gene discovery.

In our previous work, we explored explainable neural networks to improve interpretability in transcriptomic data analysis. We showed that gene contribution scores quantitatively capture each gene’s influence on model predictions, revealing biologically meaningful features such as tissue-specific genes and cancer biomarkers. However, explainability methods originally developed for computer vision often exhibited poor reproducibility on biological data due to the stochastic nature of neural network training and the complexity of gene expression distributions. To address this, we introduced a model aggregation strategy that stabilizes contribution scores across multiple independently trained models [14].

Building upon these findings, we developed scMarkerGene, an interpretable neural network framework for cell-type-specific marker gene discovery. scMarkerGene aggregates contribution scores from multiple multilayer perceptrons to reduce variance and enhance robustness, generating a cell-type–gene contribution score matrix that quantifies each gene’s relative importance in distinguishing cell types. Candidate genes are initially selected according to their aggregated contribution scores, and subsequently re-ranked using a newly introduced ‘marker score’ metric that quantifies cell-type specificity. This two-step strategy yields highly specific markers, enhances stability and reproducibility, and ensures applicability across datasets of varying scales, including those with high sparsity or dropout rates. By combining interpretability with ensemble stability, scMarkerGene offers a flexible and biologically meaningful solution for marker gene discovery in scRNA-seq analysis.

Methods

Data information

scRNA-seq

We collected a total of 10 publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets spanning multiple species, tissues, and technologies (Supplementary Table 1). The datasets include arabidopsis leaf [15], arabidopsis root [16], drosophila heart [17], drosophila intestine [17], mouse myeloid [18], mouse cortex [19], human oral cavity [20], human carotid [21], human bone marrow [22], and human COVID-19 lung samples [23]. Sequencing technologies used include 10X Genomics, MARS-seq, SMART-Seq2, and single-nucleus RNA-seq. Cell numbers range from 2018 to 116 314, genes from 3451 to 53 678, and the number of annotated cell types ranges from 5 to 19. These datasets provide diverse transcriptional landscapes for evaluating marker gene identification and cell-type classification performance across species and platforms. In addition, we leveraged lineage datasets from the BEELINE benchmark [24] to assess marker gene performance along pseudotime trajectories, each providing (i) a gene-by-cell expression matrix and (ii) pseudotime assignments.

Spatial transcriptome data

We used spatial transcriptomics datasets from 10x Genomics (https://www.10xgenomics.com/), including FFPE mouse brain (CytAssist_FFPE_Mouse_Brain_Rep1; Date Published: 2022-07-14) and FFPE human melanoma (CytAssist_FFPE_Human_Skin_Melanoma; Date Published: 2022-07-13), both provided in HDF5 format. Supplemental files with graph-clustering results and spatial images were also retrieved for marker gene visualization.

Data preparation

scMarkerGene preprocesses the input single-cell RNA-seq dataset according to its format. For raw count data, it performs TP10K normalization (transcripts per 10 000 reads), filters out genes that are expressed below 0.01 in more than 80% of the cells within each cell type, and then applies log2 transformation. For pre-normalized data (e.g. log-normalized CPM or Seurat’s NormalizeData output), it skips normalization and log transformation, and instead calculates the 25th percentile (Q1) of the non-zero expression values to use as the cutoff. After filtering, for each retained gene, the mean and standard deviation across all cells are computed, and reference sample is generated by sampling from a normal distribution with these statistics. Small perturbations are added to the reference data to enhance variability. The reference sample is used as the background input for DeepLIFT [25]. Finally, for each cell type, the cells are split into training and testing sets at a 9:1 ratio for model training and evaluation.

All ensemble models were trained using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate = 0.01 × lr, weight decay = 0.001), with a 30-step linear warm-up and early stopping (patience = 5) based on improvements in training loss. The batch size was fixed at 1024.

Methods compared with scMarkerGene

This study compared nine commonly used methods for cell marker gene identification. For scanpy [6], raw expression data were normalized using sc.pp.normalize_total and log-transformed with sc.pp.log1p, followed by Wilcoxon rank-sum testing with tl.rank_gene_groups. scMAGs [7] (https://github.com/doganlab/scmags) and SMaSH [27] (https://gitlab.com/cvejic-group/smash) were executed strictly following their official example codes. For NsForest 4.0 [13], since its default pipeline outputs gene sets of varying sizes, we modified the myDecisionTreeEvaluation function. Specifically, all possible three-gene subsets were evaluated, their classification performance quantified by the Fβ-score, and the subset with the highest Fβ-score was selected as the marker gene set for the corresponding cell type, thereby restricting the output to exactly three genes per cell type. For scGeneFit [8] and MarkerMap [10] (https://github.com/Computational-Morphogenomics-Group/MarkerMap.git), we set the parameter num_markers to ‘number of cell types × 3’, ensuring that each cell type yielded three marker genes. For scVI/scANVI [11] and Memento [28],we followed the respective official tutorials and ranked the output genes based on the ‘bayes_factor’ and ‘de_pval’ metrics as provided by each method. With these adjustments, all methods were benchmarked under a consistent Top3 marker gene level. All methods were applied directly to the raw gene expression matrix as input.

Results

scMarkerGene workflow overview

The detailed workflow of scMarkerGene is outlined in the Methods section and illustrated in Fig. 1, which is roughly consisted of two steps. In the step 1, scMarkerGene calculated the importance (a.k.a contribution score) of each gene in a specific cell type based on ensembled interpretable neural network models. In the step 2, top-N most important genes are extracted as putative markers based on their contribution score in each cell population, and then re-ranked by marker scores. A single machine learning model often yields inconsistent and potentially biased results, and thus relying on a single interpretation is inherently unreliable [29]. Building on the ensemble modeling strategy established in our previous work [14], scMarkerGene further advances the framework by incorporating automatic hyperparameter optimization using Optuna [26]. Applied to Mouse_cortex and Human_carotid datasets, the optimized ensemble markedly improved cell type classification accuracy and generalization compared with a non-optimized baseline (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Visualization of misclassified cells across 15 ensemble models showed a reduction from 413 to 23 errors (Supplementary Fig. 1B–C), underscoring the benefit of automated hyperparameter tuning for both accuracy and reliability.

scMarkerGene workflow. In step 1, the input scRNA-seq expression matrix is randomly split into training and validation sets (9:1) and used to train an MLP classifier supervised by cell type annotations. The training process is guided by validation accuracy, with hyperparameters iteratively optimized using Optuna. Once the optimal hyperparameter set is determined, ensemble learning is further applied to generate a more robust attribution score matrix. In step 2, the top-N genes (user-defined) with the highest contribution scores are selected as candidate marker sets for each cell type. These gene lists are then re-ranked by computing a marker score, yielding the final marker lists (This figure is created by BioRender (http://www.biorender.com)).

In addition, we also conducted ablation experiment to evaluate the rationale of our workflow. First, removing the specificity-based filtering step (Step 2) resulted in marker genes selected solely based on contribution scores, which may include non-specific genes highly expressed across multiple cell types (Supplementary Fig. 2). Next, when we replaced contribution scores with mean or median expression values for ranking candidate marker genes within each cell type (Step 1), it led to a relatively high proportion of non-target genes among the selected markers, even when the candidate set size was fixed at N = 50. Together, these findings highlight the critical role of Step 1, in which contribution scores provide a robust and appropriate gene pool that underpins accurate and effective downstream specificity filtering.

To explore the relationship between contribution scores and transcriptional features, we quantified their correlations with gene expression at multiple resolutions—including single-cell, gene, and cell-type levels—allowing comparison between raw expression and log2 fold-change (log2FC) across cell types. Top 10 genes ranked by contribution scores are correlated more strongly with expression than random genes and showed tighter association withlog2FC across cell types than with raw expression in specific cell type (Supplementary Fig. 3A–B). Across cell types, delta_cor were consistently higher for log2FC (Supplementary Fig. 3C), indicating that contribution scores preferentially capture relative expression specificity across cell types. Thus, contribution scores provide a robust basis for identifying cell-type-specific marker genes in scMarkerGene.

Performance assessment of methods for cell-type-specific marker gene identification

To systematically benchmark the performance of scMarkerGene, we compared it against nine widely used marker gene detection methods, including scMAGs, scanpy, SMaSH, NsForest, scGeneFit, MarkerMap, scVI/scANVI, and Memento, spanning both statistical approaches and machine learning-based methods. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, scMarkerGene identified marker genes with substantially higher expression specificity, achieving a clearer separation between target and non-target cell populations in the Human_carotid dataset [21].

We next evaluated these methods on the same dataset and quantified performance by log2FC and normalized Z-score to measure expression difference of maker genes between target and non-target cell types (Fig. 2B). scMarkerGene consistently identified highly discriminative markers across diverse biological species. As MarkerMap and scGeneFit only produce dataset-level marker sets without cell type specificity, and showed poor ability in selecting highly specific markers (Fig. 2A), they were excluded from this comparison and subsequent analyses.

Building on the initial comparison, we further extended the benchmarking to eight diverse single-cell transcriptome datasets, spanning mouse, human, fly, and arabidopsis [15–17, 19–22]. Across all datasets, scMarkerGene consistently outperformed other methods (Fig. 2C). Notably, NsForest failed to identify marker genes in 3 out of 8 datasets, underscoring its limited applicability and leading to its exclusion from further analyses. scANVI, a semi-supervised variant of scVI, was retained for further evaluation [30]. This cross-dataset consistency demonstrates the robustness of scMarkerGene in handling biological variability, sequencing noise, and dataset complexity. Together, these results establish scMarkerGene as a reliable and specific tool for marker gene detection across species.

Superior performance of scMarkerGene in identifying high-specificity markers in simulated datasets

To further benchmark scMarkerGene, we designed a simulation framework incorporating five distinct gene expression patterns with representative profiles shown in Fig. 3A. Among these genes, Type I and II represent cell-type specific marker genes, with Type I highly expressed in all cells of given cell population, whereras Type II is highly expressed in only 80% of the cells. For evaluation, we compared the top 20 ranked markers identified by each method against these two types of high-specificity markers using F1-score. The result shows that scMarkerGene consistently achieved the highest F1-scores across 16 simulated datasets varying in cell and gene numbers (Fig. 3B). Averaged across all datasets, scMarkerGene reached to 0.992 for Type I and 0.938 for Type II, substantially outperforming all other methods (Fig. 3C, top). Moreover, scMarkerGene effectively filtered out the non-specific Type III genes (F1-score = 0.173). In contrast, scanpy showed the greatest tolerance to Type III genes, likely because their abnormally elevated expression in target populations masked their lack of specificity, leading to misleadingly low P-values.

We further evaluated the robustness of different methods under sparse data conditions by systematically introducing dropout noise (10%–40%) into Type I and Type II expression matrices. scMarkerGene consistently achieved superior F1-scores across nearly all noise levels, reflected in its high performance on Type I/II matrices while maintaining low scores on Type III. In contrast, SMaSH—another deep-learning interpretability-based approach—exhibited a pronounced decline in performance with increasing noise (Fig. 3C, bottom). These results demonstrate that scMarkerGene retains robust performance under sparse data conditions, a common challenge in real scRNA-seq datasets, and underscores its reliability in identifying genuinely cell-type-specific markers.

Robustness of scMarkerGene in marker gene identification across cell population scales

To evaluate the robustness of scMarkerGene across different cell-type annotation resolutions, we compared its performance with scANVI, scanpy, scMAGs, and SMaSH on the Bone marrow organoids (BMO) dataset. At the coarse cell-type level, expression heatmaps (Supplementary Fig. 4) revealed that scMarkerGene, scANVI, scanpy, and scMAGs all identified cell type-specific markers with comparable effectiveness, effectively capturing the major populations. By contrast, the markers identified by SMaSH showed weaker cell-type specificity, consistent with its inferior performance observed in Fig. 2. At the sub cell-type level, where finer population distinctions make classification more challenging, scMarkerGene not only revealed clearer and more distinct expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5) but also achieved the highest specificity across most cell types at both coarse (Fig. 4A, top) and sub (Fig. 4A, bottom) resolutions, outperforming competing methods. We further quantified marker specificity in relation to population size by stratifying subpopulations into ‘small’ (≤100 cells) and ‘large’ (>100 cells). Even within small populations with limited statistical power, scMarkerGene maintained the highest log2FC (Fig. 4B), demonstrating its robustness across heterogeneous group sizes.

To further assess robustness under varying sample sizes, we applied scMarkerGene to a Human COVID-19 lung dataset [23]. To preserve the characteristics of the original single-cell data, we used scValue [31] to randomly sample 6000 cells from each of five major cell types (Supplementary Fig. 6, left). Subsequently, we generated three replicate subsamples at population sizes ranging from 50 to 2000 cells by non-replacement sampling (Supplementary Fig. 6, right). A 3D surface plot (Fig. 4C) illustrates the log2FC of marker genes across different population levels. Compared with alternative methods, scMarkerGene consistently achieved the highest global log2FC value. Moreover, heatmaps of the top three marker genes at population levels of 50, 100, and 200 cells (Fig. 4D) show that scMarkerGene maintained stable specificity despite reduced sample size.

Together, these findings demonstrate that scMarkerGene not only delivers superior performance across annotation resolutions, but also retains robustness under limited cell numbers. This dual robustness—across both annotation granularity and population scale—highlights the generalizability of scMarkerGene for diverse single-cell analysis scenarios.

Identification of marker genes in spatial transcriptomics data using scMarkerGene

Unlike single-cell transcriptomics, where cell type annotations are readily available, spatial transcriptomics relies on spatial coordinates or other prior information, such as known marker genes, histological features, or tissue architecture, to define clusters [32]. To assess the performance of scMarkerGene on sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics data, we applied it to two 10X Visium datasets. We compared five methods on a mouse brain tissue dataset, with each method selecting the top marker gene for each cluster (Fig. 5). For cluster 4, which spans a large spatial region but contains relatively few peripheral cells, scMarkerGene and scANVI identified Cst3 as the most spatially specific marker. In contrast, for cluster 5, scanpy was confounded by ‘highly expressed but non-specific’ signals, consistent with our observations in the simulated datasets. For cluster 6, the marker gene Arhgap36 identified by scMarkerGene exhibited higher spatial specificity than those detected by the competing methods. In another dataset from human skin melanoma, marker genes identified by scMarkerGene also displayed strong spatial localization (Supplementary Fig. 7). Together, these results demonstrate that scMarkerGene effectively leverage prior annotations to identify markers that capture both spatial and cluster specificity.

Application of scMarkerGene in identifying ‘discretized’ pseudotime-related marker genes

In single-cell pseudotime analysis, marker gene selection is typically based on correlations between gene expression and pseudotime (commonly inferred using methods such as Monocle3) [33] or on discretizing pseudotime into early/mid/late phases, followed by differential expression analysis [34]. We collected datasets from the BEELINE benchmark, where scRNA-seq samples are provided with developmental pseudotime. We first performed uniform discretization of pseudotime. It is important to note that although this binning procedure results in varying numbers of cells per bin, it ensures minimal temporal differences among cells within the same bin. The continuous pseudotime annotations of the mHSC-E dataset (Mouse Hematopoietic Stem Cells, Erythroid lineage) were discretized into five bins (phases 1–5) using the pandas.cut function (Fig. 6A). Figure 6B displays the pseudotime-dependent dynamics of the contribution scores for bin-specific marker genes predicted by scMarkerGene. We further compared scMarkerGene with scANVI, scanpy, scMAGs, and SMaSH in predicting phase-specific marker genes (Fig. 6C). The results show that markers selected by scMarkerGene exhibit higher specificity within the target bin, whereas other tools produced markers with varying degrees of expression outside the intended phase. For example, scanpy predicted APOE in bin 3 and scMAGs predicted NAB1 in bin 4, both of which displayed expression beyond their designated pseudotime phases. Additionally, we compared scMarkerGene with other competing methods under different numbers of bins (3, 5, 7). Supplementary Fig. 8 demonstrates that the top-three genes identified by scMarkerGene achieve the highest log2FC values across all bin resolutions (3 bins: 2.155; 5 bins: 2.171; 7 bins: 1.763) compared to all other evaluated tools.

Discussion

Marker gene identification remains fundamental to single-cell analysis, because it anchors cell identity and functional interpretation. Our study emphasizes that, beyond detecting marker genes, the order in which candidate genes are presented is decisive, especially for non-specialists who often heavily rely on top-list. However, existing ranking-based marker selection methods have limitations: scanpy can be skewed toward highly expressed but non-specific genes, while deep learning-based approaches such as SMaSH, despite conceptual similarities, often yield unstable rankings due to oversimplified training strategies and limited interpretability, especially in small or heterogeneous datasets.

To overcome these limitations, we developed scMarkerGene centered on gene contribution scores (CS). Contribution scores quantitatively measure how strongly each gene supports cell-type discrimination, thereby linking model predictions to interpretable features. By integrating CS across multiple independently trained models, scMarkerGene generates a robust and stable cell type–gene contribution score matrix.

The integration of contribution scores, ensemble learning, hyperparameter optimization, and specificity-based marker ranking confers scMarkerGene with robustness and facilitates the identification of highly cell-type-specific marker genes. Validation across diverse datasets, including spatial transcriptomics and discretized pseudotime trajectories, demonstrated that scMarkerGene can reliably extract cell-type-specific or phase-specific markers.

Despite its strengths, scMarkerGene still faces several limitations related to interpretation of contribution scores. Specifically, contribution scores quantify model-level feature importance, which reflects how strongly a gene contributes to cell-type discrimination within a supervised learning framework, rather than direct evidence of gene function, or regulatory interactions. Thus, while contribution scores-based ranking can facilitate the discovery of novel or non-canonical marker genes, it should not be interpreted as indicating causal regulatory relationships or causal biological mechanisms. Furthermore, the current framework is primarily designed for single-modality transcriptomic data and would require optimization to effectively handle other data types, such as scATAC-seq, chromatin conformation, and spatial epigenomic profiles. Future development will focus on extending scMarkerGene toward multi-omics integration, enabling the joint discovery of regulatory markers that link transcriptional variation with epigenetic states. Such integration could allow the model to capture upstream regulatory mechanisms and transcription-factor–target relationships that underpin cell-type specificity and dynamic state transitions.

In summary, scMarkerGene offers a transparent, reproducible, and generalizable framework for marker gene identification. By leveraging contribution scores to guide candidate selection and ranking, it combines interpretability with strong predictive performance. With future extensions toward multi-omics and regulatory modeling, scMarkerGene could evolve into a comprehensive platform for dissecting cellular heterogeneity and transcriptional regulation at single-cell resolution.
