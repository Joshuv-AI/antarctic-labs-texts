Groups and Inverse Semigroups in Lambda Calculus
Antonio Bucciarelli, Arturo De Faveri, Giulio Manzonetto, Antonino Salibra

Abstract
We study invertibility of λ \lambda -terms modulo λ \lambda -theories. Here a fundamental role is played by a class of λ \lambda -terms called finite hereditary permutations (FHP) and by their infinite generalisations (HP). More precisely, FHP \mathrm{FHP} s are the invertible elements in the least extensional λ \lambda -theory 𝝀 ​ 𝜼 \boldsymbol{\lambda\eta} and HP \mathrm{HP} s are those in the greatest sensible λ \lambda -theory \EuScript ​ H ∗ \EuScript H^{*} . Our approach is based on inverse semigroups, algebraic structures that generalise groups and semilattices. We show that FHP modulo a λ \lambda -theory \EuScript ​ T \EuScript T is always an inverse semigroup and that HP modulo \EuScript ​ T \EuScript T is an inverse semigroup whenever \EuScript ​ T \EuScript T contains the theory of Böhm trees. An inverse semigroup comes equipped with a natural order. We prove that the natural order corresponds to η \eta -expansion in FHP / \EuScript ​ T \mathrm{FHP}/\EuScript T , and to infinite η \eta -expansion in HP / \EuScript ​ T \mathrm{HP}/\EuScript T . Building on these correspondences we obtain the two main contributions of this work: firstly, we recast in a broader framework the results cited at the beginning; secondly, we prove that the FHP \mathrm{FHP} s are the invertible λ \lambda -terms in all the λ \lambda -theories lying between 𝝀 ​ 𝜼 \boldsymbol{\lambda\eta} and \EuScript ​ H + \EuScript H^{+} . The latter is Morris’ observational λ \lambda -theory, defined by using the β \beta -normal forms as observables.

Introduction

The
\lambda
-calculus, established by Alonzo Church in the 1930s, has played a prominent role in theoretical computer science for almost a century
[
6
,
5
,
7
]
.
This stems from its position at the crossroads of programming language theory and various branches of mathematics, which draws the attention of a diverse community of researchers.
Its denotational semantics has been studied using domain theory
[
2
]
, category theory
[
40
,
3
]
and bicategory theory
[
29
,
37
]
, game semantics
[
26
,
9
,
13
]
, linear logic
[
1
,
22
]
, set theory
[
39
,
21
]
, and universal algebra
[
45
]
.
Operational aspects of its semantics have been also mechanised in proof assistants
[
16
,
41
]
.

This article fits within the line of research that studies the
\lambda
-calculus from an algebraic perspective.
In
[
12
]
, Church formulated the
\lambda
-calculus as a semigroup where
M\circ N=\lambda x.M(Nx)
, whence its connection with algebra begins at the syntactic level,
and this perspective also inspired subsequent works
[
11
]
.
A problem that naturally arises is the characterisation of those
\lambda
-terms
M
that are
invertible
in the sense that
M\circ N=N\circ M=\mathbf{I}
(the identity), for some
N
.
The set of invertible
\lambda
-terms modulo a
\lambda
-theory is a group.
When the notion of equality is
=_{\beta}
the group is trivial, as the only invertible
\lambda
-term is
\mathbf{I}
.
The problem becomes more interesting when considering extensional
\lambda
-theories: Dezani-Ciancaglini, Bergstra and Klop have characterised the
\lambda
-terms invertible modulo
=_{\beta\eta}
as the
finite hereditary permutations
(FHPs)
[
20
,
8
]
.
Intuitively, FHPs are obtained from
\eta
-expansions of
\mathbf{I}
by arbitrarily permuting the subtrees of their Böhm trees, and it can be proved that they are the only realisers of type isomorphisms in the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus
[
15
]
.
The invertibility modulo
=_{\beta\eta}
has also been studied by Folkerts
[
23
]
, who proved that a
\lambda
-term is invertible if and only if it is both injective and surjective–a property that
seems
natural but fails in other
\lambda
-theories.
More recently, Statman has shown that the semigroup of
\lambda
-terms modulo
=_{\beta}
is Sq-universal
[
49
]
, a profound result stating that every countable semigroup is embeddable in the semigroup of
\lambda
-terms modulo a suitable
\lambda
-theory.

There are other
\lambda
-theories that are of interest for computer scientists because they capture operational properties of programs.
In particular, Morris defined observational equivalences where two
\lambda
-terms are equivalent if they display the same behaviour when they are plugged in any context
[
46
]
.
Clearly, this notion depends on the behavioural property one decides to observe.
The observational equivalence
\EuScript{H}^{*}
, where one observes head termination, is by far the most well-studied
\lambda
-theory: it arises as the theory of Scott’s model
\mathcal{D}_{\infty}
and captures the Böhm tree equality modulo an infinitary notion of extensionality
[
32
]
.
In
[
8
]
, Bergstra and Klop characterised the class of invertible
\lambda
-terms modulo
\EuScript{H}^{*}
by generalising the finite hereditary permutations to permutations having a possibly infinite Böhm tree (
\mathrm{HP}
s).

It is natural to consider the problem of characterising invertibility modulo
\lambda
-theories lying between
=_{\beta\eta}
and
\EuScript H^{*}
;
one of these is for instance
\EuScript H^{+}
, Morris’s observational theory in which the observed behavioural property is
\beta
-normalisation
[
34
]
.
In this paper, we aim to advance the study of this problem by extending the results presented in
[
6
, Chapter 21]
.
Our novel approach consists in showing that
\mathrm{HP}
, which is a monoid modulo
=_{\beta}

and a group modulo
\EuScript H^{*}
, is an
inverse semigroup
(actually, an inverse monoid)
modulo the
\lambda
-theory of Böhm trees.
An inverse semigroup
[
42
]
is a semigroup in which every element admits a unique inverse in the generalised sense of semigroup
theory.
By virtue of their rich structure, inverse semigroups are connected with various areas of logic and theoretical
computer science, including topos theory
[
24
,
25
]
, Stone duality
[
43
]
, and automata theory
[
17
]
.
They simultaneously and faithfully generalise groups–since they are equipped with a unary operation extending
the group inverse–and semilattices, as the idempotent elements of an inverse semigroup always form a
semilattice with respect to a natural order.
An inverse semigroup is a semilattice if and only if every element is idempotent, and it is a group if and only if the identity is the only idempotent.
The prototypical example of an inverse monoid is given by the partial injective endofunctions on a set;
viewed in this way, inverse monoids provide a natural framework for describing partial symmetries, as opposed to the total ones described by groups.

We focus on the study of a monoid whose elements we call
permutation trees
, which can be put into correspondence with a monoid of Böhm-like trees.
We show that this is an inverse monoid, characterise its idempotents and the associated order, and prove that it is in fact
F
-inverse, a technical concept that will be appropriately explained.
The analysis of this monoid has two important consequences:
\mathrm{HP}
modulo Böhm tree equality and
\mathrm{FHP}
modulo
=_{\beta}
form inverse monoids;
these sets remain so when considered modulo any theory extending Böhm and
=_{\beta}
, respectively.
A noteworthy aspect is that the natural order on these inverse monoids coincides with the preorder induced by infinite
\eta
-expansion (on Böhm-like trees) and finite
\eta
-expansion (on
\lambda
-terms).
Quotienting an inverse monoid by a suitable congruence
\sigma
, called minimum group congruence, yields a group in which all idempotents lie in the equivalence class of the identity.
In this group, the generalised inverses become actual inverses in the usual group-theoretic sense.
This naturally raises the question of what such a congruence corresponds to in the cases of
\mathrm{HP}
modulo Böhm and
\mathrm{FHP}
modulo
=_{\beta}
.
Now, the minimum group congruence is related to the natural order, and in Böhm and
=_{\beta}
the natural order corresponds to infinite and finite
\eta
-expansion, respectively.
Therefore we obtain the key result that quotienting
\mathrm{HP}
by Böhm and then by
\sigma
gives precisely
\mathrm{HP}
modulo
\EuScript H^{*}
;
similarly, quotienting
\mathrm{FHP}
by
=_{\beta}
and then by
\sigma
gives precisely
\mathrm{FHP}
modulo
=_{\beta\eta}
.
This provides a new perspective on invertible
\lambda
-terms modulo these
\lambda
-theories.

The
\lambda
-theories
\EuScript H^{*}
and
=_{\beta\eta}
are special in that they arise precisely from the minimum group congruence, but this is not always the case.
A counterexample is given by the theory
\EuScript H^{+}
, whose equality has been characterised in terms of the interaction between Böhm trees and
finite

\eta
-expansions
[
14
,
47
,
34
]
.
To characterise when two hereditary permutations are equal in
\EuScript H^{+}
, we therefore need to take a further step and study the interaction between infinite trees equipped with an order that nonetheless originates from
finite
idempotents.
In other words, we are led to consider a congruence inspired by, but distinct from, the minimum group congruence.
In the theory of Böhm trees, the order constructed in this way coincides with finite
\eta
-expansion.
Building on the characterisation of the invertibles we gave for
\EuScript H^{*}
, we demonstrate that the
\lambda
-terms invertible modulo
\EuScript H^{+}
are precisely
\mathrm{FHP}
s.
As a consequence,
\mathrm{FHP}
is the group of invertible
\lambda
-terms in all the
\lambda
-theories lying between
=_{\beta\eta}
and
\EuScript H^{+}
.
This result settles a conjecture formulated by Barendregt in his book
[
6
, p. 547]
.
The conceptual reconstruction of invertibles modulo
\EuScript H^{*}
and
=_{\beta\eta}
together with the characterisation of invertibles modulo
\EuScript H^{+}

constitute the main contributions of this work.
Some directions for future research are outlined in the conclusion.

2.1
Inverse semigroups

Given a semigroup
S

we say that

is an

also holds.
A semigroup is called

An extremely important class of regular semigroups is that of inverse semigroups.
An
inverse semigroup
is a semigroup

endowed with a unary operation
(-)^{*}
that satisfies:

(u^{*})^{*}=u,\qquad(uv)^{*}=v^{*}u^{*},\qquad uu^{*}u=u,\qquad uu^{*}vv^{*}=vv^{*}uu^{*}

(1)

for all
u,v\in S
.
An
inverse monoid
is an inverse semigroup together with a unit for multiplication.
In an inverse semigroup
u^{*}
is the unique inverse of
u
.

The prototypical example of an inverse monoid is the set
I(X)
of partial injections on a given set
X
.
An element
u
is a bijection between two subsets of
X
, the domain and the range of
u
, and
u^{*}
is its partial inverse.

An
idempotent
in a semigroup

is

such that
uu=u
.
The idempotents of

are the partial identities.
We denote by

the set of idempotents of
S

In any inverse semigroup, every element of the form
uu^{*}
is idempotent.
Conversely, since if

is idempotent, then
u=u^{*}
, idempotents are precisely those elements of the form
uu^{*}
.
The last identity in (
1

If

is a surjective homomorphism of semigroups and

is an inverse semigroup, then
T
is also an inverse semigroup.

The natural order

Any inverse semigroup
S
comes equipped with a natural order that corresponds to inclusion between functions of
I(X)

By definition,

if
uu^{*}v=u
, or, equivalently, if
vu^{*}u=u

Actually, one can prove that the following are equivalent: 1)

.
Therefore, if

is idempotent.
Moreover,

and

If

Two important relations in the study of an inverse semigroup

1.

the relation of
compatibility

2.

the relation
\sigma_{S}

When

The relation
\sigma_{S}

Green’s relations

Certain equivalence relations, called Green’s relations, have played a central role in the development of the theory.
They can be defined on any semigroup, but assume a particularly simple form on inverse semigroups.

Definition 3
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup.
We define two equivalence relations

.

Traditionally, given

It is easy to check that

Let

.
A celebrated theorem of Green states the following.

Proposition 4

For any
\mathcal{H}
-class

, in which case

forms a subgroup of

If

Moreover, no

2.3
Lambda calculus

The set
\Lambda
of
\lambda
-terms
over a countably infinite set
\mathrm{Var}
of variables is defined by the grammar:
M,N::=x\mid\lambda x.M\mid MN,\textrm{ for }x\in\mathrm{Var}
.
We assume that application associates on the left, and has higher precedence than abstraction.
For instance,
\lambda xyz.xyz
stands for
\lambda x.(\lambda y.(\lambda z.(xy)z))
.
From now on,
\lambda
-terms are considered up to renaming of bound variables.
A
\lambda
-term without free variables is called a

.
The
\beta
-reduction

\to_{\beta}
and
\eta
-reduction

are defined as the contextual closures of the rules:

(\lambda x.M)N\to_{\beta}M[N/x],\qquad\qquad\lambda x.Mx\to_{\eta}M,\,\text{if $x$ does not occur free in $M$}

where we denote by
M[N/x

capture-free substitution
of
N
for all free occurrences of
x
in
M
.
Given a notion of reduction
\to_{r}
between
\lambda
-terms, we denote by
\twoheadrightarrow_{r}
the reflexive, transitive closure of
\to_{r}
, and by
=_{r}
the least equivalence relation containing
\to_{r}
.
When
\to_{r}
is confluent,
\nf_{r}(M)
stands for the unique
r
-normal form
of
M
(if any).
We define
\beta\eta
-reduction
as
\to_{\beta\eta}\ =\ \to_{\beta}\cup\to_{\eta}
.
The combinators below are used throughout the paper:

\mathbf{I}=\lambda x.x,\qquad\mathbf{1}=\lambda xy.xy,\qquad\mathbf{B}=\lambda fgx.f(gx),\qquad\mathbf{Y}=\lambda f.(\lambda x.f(xx))(\lambda x.f(xx)).

We recall that
\mathbf{I}
is the identity,
\mathbf{1}
the 1st Church numeral,
\mathbf{B}
stands for the composition and

Theories

As

Hereditary permutations

t^{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}}:=\begin{cases}(\langle\pi;t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1}\rangle)^{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}},&\text{ if }t_{n}\in E^{\prime}\text{ and }\pi n=n,\\
\langle\pi;(t_{1})^{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}},\ldots,(t_{n})^{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}}\rangle,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proposition 29

For every

2.

is the unique maximal element in

Lemma 30

For every

, if
t\leq^{\prime}v
and
u\leq^{\prime}v

such that

and
w\leq^{\prime}u

Now, in analogy with the minimum group congruence, we define a relation

by letting

if there is

such that
w\leq^{\prime}t
and

.

Lemma 31
.

The relation
{\sigma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{PT}}}
is a congruence such that
\sigma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{PT}}\subseteq\sigma_{\mathrm{PT}}
.

Proof.

Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate and transitivity follows from Lemma

The fact that

\leq^{\prime}
is compatible.
∎

Example 32
.

The infinite permutation tree
e=\{1^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
,
is idempotent.

(\Longrightarrow)

Related and further work

Inverse semigroups were first employed in the
\lambda
-calculus to study properties of
\beta
-reduction
[
18
]
.
Subsequently, they played a role in Girard’s Geometry of Interaction
[
27
]
.
More recently, Goubault-Larrecq, inspired by the work
[
18
,
19
]
, has shown that a category constructed from an inverse semigroup models a fragment of linear logic
[
28
]
.
Hofmann and Mislove employed the theory of inverse semigroups to prove that every compact Hausdorff topological model of
\lambda
-calculus is degenerate
[
30

\lambda

\lambda

Looking ahead, we aim to characterise elements that are invertible modulo

\EuScript H^{+}

\EuScript H^{*}

The role played by

Proof of Lemma

Let

u=\langle\rho;u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\rangle

1.

Assume that
t<u
; then either
n>m
or
n=m

In the first case,

\left\lVert t\right\rVert=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert t_{j}\right\rVert+\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{j}\right\rVert>1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert t_{j}\right\rVert\geq 1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert.

In the second case, there exists
i
such that
t_{i}<u_{i}

Then

\left\lVert t\right\rVert=1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{j}\right\rVert>1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert\text{,}

because
\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert>\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert t_{j}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert
for every
j\neq i
.

2.

The proof is by induction on
t

Observe that

\left\lVert t^{*}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert(t^{*})_{i}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert(t_{\pi^{-1}i})^{*}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{\pi^{-1}i}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert

which equals
\left\lVert t\right\rVert
, because
\pi^{-1}
is a permutation of
n
.

3.

We show (3) by induction on
\left\lVert t\right\rVert+\left\lVert u\right\rVert
.
If
\left\lVert t\right\rVert+\left\lVert u\right\rVert=2
, then
t=u=\bar{\epsilon}
, and the conclusion is trivial.
If
\left\lVert t\right\rVert+\left\lVert u\right\rVert>2
, then we have two cases: (a)
n\geq m
and (b)
m\geq n
.

(a)

If
m=0
, then
u=\bar{\epsilon}
and
tu=t
.
Then the conclusion is trivial.
Assume now
m>0
.
Since
(tu)_{i}=u_{i}t_{\rho i}
for every
1\leq i\leq m
and
(tu)_{i}=t_{i}
for every
\rd(u)<i\leq\rd(t)

\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert,\left\lVert t_{\rho i}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert
for every
1\leq i\leq m
.
Then we have:

\left\lVert tu\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert tu_{i}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert tu_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert tu_{i}\right\rVert\geq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert=\left\lVert u\right\rVert

and

\left\lVert tu\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert\geq 1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert u_{\rho i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert=\left\lVert t\right\rVert

because
\rho
is a permutation of
m
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert t_{\rho i}\right\rVert=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert t_{j}\right\rVert
.
Finally

\displaystyle\left\lVert tu\right\rVert

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert\leq 2+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert+\left\lVert t_{\rho i}\right\rVert)+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert\right)+\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert t_{\rho i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert u\right\rVert+\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{j}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\left\lVert t_{i}\right\rVert\right)=\left\lVert u\right\rVert+\left\lVert t\right\rVert\text{,}

because
\rho
is a permutation of
m
.

(b)

Assume
m>0
.
Since
(tu)_{i}=u_{i}t_{\rho i}
for every
1\leq i\leq m
and
t_{\rho i}=\bar{\epsilon}
for every
\rho i>n
, then
(tu)_{i}=u_{i}
for every

such that
\rho i>n
.
Let
A=\{i:\rho i>n,1\leq i\leq m\}
and
B=\{i:\rho i\leq m,1\leq i\leq n\}
.
Then we have:

\left\lVert tu\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i\in A}\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i\in A}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert\text{,}

so that

\left\lVert tu\right\rVert\geq 1+\sum_{i\in A}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert=\left\lVert u\right\rVert

and

\left\lVert tu\right\rVert\geq 1+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert(tu)_{i}\right\rVert=1+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert t_{\rho i}\right\rVert=\left\lVert t\right\rVert\text{,}

because
\rho

.
Finally,

\left\lVert tu\right\rVert\leq 2+\sum_{i\in A}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert t_{\rho i}\right\rVert=\left\lVert u\right\rVert+1+\sum_{i\in B}\left\lVert t\right\rVert_{\rho i}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert+\left\lVert t\right\rVert

4.

The second follows from (2). ∎

Proof of Theorem

Since

,
\EuScript T
is sensible and thus
\EuScript T\subseteq\EuScript H^{*}
.
Therefore, we have the following chain of surjective homomorphisms of monoids:

\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript B\xrightarrow{f}\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript T\xrightarrow{g}\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript H^{*}
.
As
\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript B
is an inverse monoid,
\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript T
is such.
We now prove that
\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript T
is
E

If

\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript T

g(e)\leq g(t)
in
\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript H^{*}
with
g(e)
idempotent.
Since
\mathrm{HP}/\EuScript H^{*}

g(e)=g(t)

g(t)=\mathbf{I}
.
Let
