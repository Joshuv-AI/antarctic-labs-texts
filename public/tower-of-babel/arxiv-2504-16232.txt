On evolutionary equations related to skew-symmetric spatial operators
Evgeny Yu. Panov

Abstract
We study generalized solutions of an evolutionary equation related to some densely defined skew-symmetric operator in a real Hilbert space. We establish existence of a contractive semigroup, which provides generalized solutions, and suggest a criteria of uniqueness of this semigroup. We also find a stronger criteria of uniqueness of generalized solutions. Applications to transport equations with solenoidal (and generally discontinuous) coefficients are given.

Introduction

Let
H
be a real Hilbert space,
A_{0}
be a skew-symmetric linear operator in
H
defined on a dense domain

X_{0}=D(A_{0})\subset H
. We recall that the operator
A_{0}
is skew-symmetric if
(A_{0}u,v)=-(u,A_{0}v)

\forall u,v\in X_{0}
, where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the inner product in
H
.
Alternatively, the skew-symmetricity may be described as
-A_{0}\subset(A_{0})^{*}
, where
(A_{0})^{*}
is the adjoint operator to
A_{0}
. Since an adjoint operator is closed, there exists the closure
A
of the operator
A_{0}
. This operator
A
is a closed skew-symmetric operator, and
A^{*}=(A_{0})^{*}
.
We underline that in view of the identity
2((A_{0}u,v)+(u,A_{0}v))=(A_{0}(u+v),u+v)-(A_{0}(u-v),u-v)
the skew-symmetricity of
A_{0}
is equivalent to the condition
(A_{0}u,u)=0

\forall u\in X_{0}
.

The operator
A^{*}
is an extension of the operator
-A
, and may be not skew-symmetric. Skew-symmericity of
A^{*}
holds only in the case
A^{*}=-A
, that is, when the operator
A
is skew-adjoint.

We consider the evolutionary equation

u^{\prime}-A^{*}u=0,\quad u=u(t)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H),

(1.1)

where
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,+\infty)
, with the initial condition

u(0)=u_{0}\in H.

(1.2)

Definition 1.1
.

An
H
-valued function
u=u(t)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)
is called a generalized solution (g.s.) of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) if

\forall f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
, where the space
X_{0}
equipped with the graph norm
\|u\|^{2}=\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\|A_{0}u\|_{H}^{2}
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\pm}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt+(u_{0},f(0))=0.

(1.3)

Remark 1.1
.

(1) It follows from relation (
1.3
) with
f(t)=v\varphi(t)
,
\varphi(t)\in C_{0}^{1}((0,+\infty))
,
v\in X_{0}
, that

\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),v)=(u(t),Av)
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}((0,+\infty))
, where
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(I)
denotes the space of distributions on an open set
I
. This relation readily implies weak continuity of
u(t)
(after possible correction on a set of null measure).
Besides, (
1.3
) implies initial condition (
1.2
), understood in the sense of weak convergence
u(t)\rightharpoonup u_{0}
as
t\to 0+
;

(2) Let
X=D(A)
be the domain of the operator
A
, equipped with the graph norm
\|u\|^{2}=\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\|A(u)\|_{H}^{2}
,

is a Banach space in view of the closedness of
A
. Since the operator

is the closure of
A_{0}
, the space
X_{0}
is dense both in

and in

. In turn, this implies density of the space

C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
in
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
. Therefore, every test function
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
can be approximated by a sequence

f_{r}(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
,

, so that
f_{r}(t)\to f(t)
,
f_{r}^{\prime}(t)\to f^{\prime}(t)
,
A_{0}f_{r}(t)\to Af(t)
as
r\to\infty
in
H
, uniformly with respect to
t
. Passing to the limit as
r\to\infty
in relations (
1.3
) with the test functions
f=f_{r}
, we arrive at the relation

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\pm}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+Af(t))dt+(u_{0},f(0))=0.

Thus, (
1.3
) remains true for all
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
. Since
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)\supset C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
, then we may replace
A_{0},X_{0}
by
A,X
in Definition
1.1
and could initially assume that
A_{0}=A
is a closed skew-symmetric operator.

One example

Let

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, that is,

\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits a(x)=0

(2.1)

in the sense of distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

u_{t}+a(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}u=u_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(x)u_{x_{i}}=0

(here

,

By the solenoidality condition this equation can be written (at least formally) in the conservative form

u_{t}+\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{x}(au)=0.

(2.2)

This allows to introduce the notion of g.s.
u(t,x)
to the Cauchy problem for equation
(
2.2

(2.3)

Definition 2.1

A function

We introduce the real Hilbert space

In fact, for

\displaystyle(A_{0}u,v)_{H}+(u,A_{0}v)_{H}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a(x)\cdot(v(x)\nabla u(x)+u(x)\nabla v(x))dx=

that the distribution
-\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(au)\in H

3
Contractive semigroups of generalized solutions

We consider a
C_{0}
-semigroup
T_{t}=e^{tB}
of bounded linear operators in
H
with an infinitesimal generator
B
. The operator
B
is known to be a densely defined closed operator, and its domain
D(B)
is a Banach space with respect to the graph norm. The following statement holds.

Theorem 3.1
.

The functions
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}
are g.s. to the problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) for each
u_{0}\in H
if and only if

B\subset A^{*}
.

Proof.

First assume that
B\subset A^{*}
and
u_{0}\in D(B)
. Then
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{+},D(B))\cap C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)
and
u^{\prime}(t)=T_{t}Bu_{0}=BT_{t}u_{0}=Bu(t)
. Therefore, for all
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
there exists the continuous derivative

\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),f(t))=(u^{\prime}(t),f(t))+(u(t),f^{\prime}(t))=(Bu(t),f(t))+(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)).

(3.1)

By our assumption
B\subset A^{*}
and therefore.

(Bu(t),f(t))=(A^{*}u(t),f(t))=(u(t),Af(t))=(u(t),A_{0}f(t)).

In view of (
3.1
), we arrive at the equality

\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),f(t))=(u(t),A_{0}f(t)+f^{\prime}(t)),

which implies, after the integration, the relation

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),f(t))dt=-(u_{0},f(0)).

Hence, the identity (
1.3
) holds and
u(t)
is a g.s. of (
1.1
), (
1.2
).
In the case of arbitrary
u_{0}\in H
we choose a sequence
u_{0k}\in D(B)
, convergent as
k\to\infty
to
u_{0}
in
H
. As we have already established, the function
u_{k}(t)=T_{t}u_{0k}
is a g.s. of the problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) with initial data
u_{0k}
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It is known that for a
C_{0}
-semigroup
T_{t}
the operator norms
\|T_{t}\|
are exponentially bounded,
\|T_{t}\|\leq Ce^{\alpha t}
for some constants
C>0
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Therefore,

\|u_{k}(t)-u(t)\|_{H}\leq\|T_{t}\|\|u_{0k}-u_{0}\|_{H}\mathop{\to}_{k\to\infty}0

uniformly on any segment
[0,T]
. This allows to pass to the limit as
k\to\infty
in the relations (
1.3
)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u_{k}(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt+(u_{0k},f(0))=0,

corresponding to g.s.
u_{k}
and derive that for each test function
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt+(u_{0},f(0))=0.

Hence,
u(t)
is a g.s. of the problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
), as was to be proved.

Conversely, assume that the functions
u(t)=T_{t}u_{0}
are g.s. of (
1.1
), (
1.2
) for all
u_{0}\in H
. If
u_{0}\in D(B)
, then
u(t)=T_{t}u_{0}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)
and
u^{\prime}(0)=Bu_{0}
. Therefore, for all
v\in D(A_{0})

the scalar function
I(t)=(u(t),v)
lies in
C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and
I^{\prime}(0+)=(Bu_{0},v)
.
On the other hand, for all
h(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}I(t)h^{\prime}(t)dt=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),vh^{\prime}(t))dt=-h(0)(u_{0},v)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),A_{0}v)h(t)dt

by the equality (
1.3
) with
f=h(t)v
. Integration by parts then implies that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}I^{\prime}(t)h(t)dt=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),A_{0}v)h(t)dt

and by arbitrariness of
h(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
we find
I^{\prime}(t)=(u(t),A_{0}v)
. In particular,

(u_{0},A_{0}v)=I^{\prime}(0+)=(Bu_{0},v)
. Since this identity holds for all
v\in D(A_{0})
, then by definition of the adjoint operator
A^{*}=(A_{0})^{*}
we find that
u_{0}\in D(A^{*})
and
A^{*}u_{0}=Bu_{0}
. Hence,

B\subset A^{*}
. The proof is complete.
∎

By the Lumer-Phillips theorem (see, for instance,
[
10
, Theorem 1.1.3]
) a
C_{0}
-semigroup
T_{t}=e^{tB}
is contractive (i.e.,
\|T_{t}\|\leq 1

\forall t>0
) if and only if its generator
B
is an
m
-dissipative operator. Remind that an operator
A
in a Banach space
H
is called dissipative if
\|u-hAu\|\geq\|u\|

\forall u\in H
,
h>0
. A dissipative operator
A
is called
m
-dissipative if
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-hA)=X
for all
h>0
(it is sufficient that this property is satisfied only for one value
h>0
). It is known that an
m
-dissipative operator is a maximal dissipative operator, in the case of a Hilbert space
H
the inverse statement is also true. Notice also that in the case of Hilbert space
H
dissipativity of an operator
A
reduces to the condition
(Au,u)\leq 0

\forall u\in H
. In particular, skew-symmetric operators are dissipative. The following property means that the requirement
B\subset A^{*}
of Theorem
3.1
for an

m
-dissipative operator is equivalent to the condition that
B
is an extension of the operator
-A
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
B
be an
m
-dissipative operator in
H
. Then
B\subset A^{*}\Leftrightarrow-A\subset B
.

Proof.

Assume that
B\subset A^{*}
. Since we have also that
-A\subset A^{*}
, then
-Au=Bu=A^{*}u
on
D(A)\cap D(B)
. This allows to construct a common extension
\tilde{B}
of both operators
-A
and
B
, which is defined on the space
D(A)+D(B)
by the equality

\tilde{B}u=-Au_{1}+Bu_{2},\quad u=u_{1}+u_{2},\ u_{1}\in D(A),u_{2}\in D(B).

Since the operators
-A
and
B
coincide on their common domain
D(A)\cap D(B)
, this definition is correct and does not depend on the representation
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
. By the construction we have
-A\subset\tilde{B}
,
B\subset\tilde{B}
. Let us show that the operator
\tilde{B}
is dissipative. For arbitrary
u\in D(A)+D(B)
we find such
u_{1}\in D(A)
,
u_{2}\in D(B)
that
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle(\tilde{B}u,u)=(-Au_{1}+Bu_{2},u_{1}+u_{2})=

\displaystyle-(Au_{1},u_{1})+(Bu_{2},u_{2})+((u_{1},Bu_{2})-(Au_{1},u_{2}))=

\displaystyle(Bu_{2},u_{2})+((u_{1},A^{*}u_{2})-(Au_{1},u_{2}))=(Bu_{2},u_{2})\leq 0

(3.2)

by dissipativity of
B
. We also take into account that
(Au_{1},u_{1})=0
in view of skew-symmetricity of the operator
A
and that
B\subset A^{*}
. It follows from (
3
) that the operator
\tilde{B}
is dissipative. But the operator
B
is a maximal dissipative operator, and we conclude that
\tilde{B}=B
. In particular
D(A)+D(B)=D(B)
, that is,

D(A)\subset D(B)
. Hence,
-A\subset B
.

Conversely, assume that
-A\subset B
. Let
u\in D(A)
,
v\in D(B)
. Then
u+sv\in D(B)
for all
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and by dissipativity of
B
the inequality
f(s)\doteq(B(u+sv),u+sv)\leq 0
holds for all real
s
. The function
f(s)
is quadratic and can be written in the form

\displaystyle f(s)=(Bu,u)+s((Bu,v)+(Bv,u))+s^{2}(Bv,v)=

\displaystyle s((Bu,v)+(Bv,u))+s^{2}(Bv,v),

(3.3)

because by the assumption
-A\subset B
we have the equality
(Bu,u)=-(Au,u)=0
. Notice also that
(Bv,v)\leq 0
. From representation (
3
) and the condition
f(s)\leq 0

\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}}
it follows that
(Bu,v)+(Bv,u)=0
. Hence, for all
v\in D(B)
,
u\in D(A)

(Au,v)=-(Bu,v)=(Bv,u)=(u,Bv).

This identity implies that
v\in D(A^{*})
and
A^{*}v=Bv
for all
v\in D(B)
. This means that
B\subset A^{*}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We underline that the implication
-A\subset B\Rightarrow B\subset A^{*}
was also established by R.S. Phillips in
[
10
, Lemma 1.1.5]
.

4
Main results

By Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.1
generators of contractive semigroups of g.s. of equation (
1.1
) are exactly
m
-dissipative extensions of the operator
-A
. First, we describe skew-symmetric extensions of the operator
A
. Such extensions reduce to symmetric extensions of the symmetric operator
iA
(
i^{2}=-1
). Theory of symmetric extensions is well-developed and based on the Cayley transform. In the framework of skew-symmetric operators the Cayley transform
Q=(E+A)(E-A)^{-1}
is even more natural (it does not require complexification of the space and the operators). Since

\|u\pm Au\|^{2}=\|u\|^{2}\pm 2(Au,u)+\|Au\|^{2}=\|u\|^{2}+\|Au\|^{2},\quad\forall u\in D(A),

the operator
Q
is an isometry between the spaces
H_{-}=\mathop{\rm Im}(E-A)
and
H_{+}=\mathop{\rm Im}(E+A)
(notice that the spaces
H_{\pm}
are closed due to the closedness of operator
A
). The Cayley transform is invertible, the inverse transform is defined by the formula
A=(Q-E)(Q+E)^{-1}
(under the assumption that the domain

D(A)=\mathop{\rm Im}(Q+E)
is dense the operator
Q+E
is always invertible). Hence, skew-symmetric extensions of the operator
A
corresponds to isometric extensions of the operator
Q
, which are reduced to construction of isometric maps from
(H_{-})^{\perp}
into
(H_{+})^{\perp}
. Remind that Hilbert dimensions of these spaces (i.e., Hilbert codimensions of
H_{\pm}
) are called the deficiency indexes of
A
. We denote them
d_{+}
,
d_{-}
, respectively, so that
d_{\pm}=d_{\pm}(A)=\mathop{\rm codim}H_{\pm}
. Maximal skew-symmetric operators
A
are characterized by the condition that at least one of the deficiency indexes
d_{\pm}(A)
is null. The condition
d_{+}=d_{-}=0
describes skew-adjoint operators, their Cayley transforms
Q
are orthogonal operators. It is known that any skew-symmetric operator can be extended to a maximal skew-symmetric operator. Using this property, we can now prove the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists a contractive semigroup
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}
of g.s. to equation (
1.1
). Moreover, the following two cases are possible:

(i) The generator
B
is skew-symmetric. In this case the operators
T_{t}=e^{tB}
are isometric embeddings and the conservation of energy
\|u(t)\|=\|u_{0}\|
holds for all
t>0
;

(ii) the adjoint operator
B^{*}
is skew-symmetric. Then the operators
(T_{t})^{*}
are isometric embeddings.

Proof.

The skew-symmetric operator
A
admits a maximal skew-symmetric extension
\tilde{A}
. Let
d_{\pm}=\mathop{\rm codim}(E\pm\tilde{A})
be the deficiency indexes of
\tilde{A}
. By the maximality, one of these indexes is null. If
d_{+}=0
, then
\mathop{\rm Im}(E+\tilde{A})=H
and therefore the operator
-\tilde{A}
is
m
-dissipative. In this case we set
B=-\tilde{A}
. Similarly, if
d_{-}=0
then the operator
\tilde{A}
is
m
-dissipative. It is known (cf.
[
10
, Theorem 1.1.2]
) that the adjoint operator
(\tilde{A})^{*}
is
m
-dissipative as well, and
-\tilde{A}\subset(\tilde{A})^{*}
by skew-symmetricity of
\tilde{A}
. Setting
B=(\tilde{A})^{*}
,
we obtain a
m
-dissipative extension of
-A
in the case
d_{-}=0
. By Theorem
3.1
the corresponding semigroup
e^{tB}
provides g.s. of equation (
1.1
). Properties (i), (ii) follows from the fact that the semigroup
e^{tB}
consists of isometric embeddings whenever the generator
B
is skew-symmetric. We also used that
B^{*}
is a generator of the adjoint semigroup
(T_{t})^{*}
.
∎

We underline that in paper
[
10
]
existence of an
m
-dissipative extensions was established by different methods,
with the help of the Cayley transform for dissipative operators.

Concerning uniqueness of the constructed in Theorem
4.1
semigroup, the following statement holds.

Theorem 4.2
.

A contractive semigroup of g.s. to equation (
1.1
) is unique if and only if the skew-symmetric operator
A
is maximal.

Proof.

Let the skew-symmetric operator
A
be maximal and
d_{\pm}
be its deficiency indexes. If
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}
is a contracrive semigroup of g.s. then the infinitesimal generator
B
is
m
-dissipative and, by Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.1
,

-A\subset B\subset A^{*}
. By the maximality, one of the operators
-A
,
A^{*}
is
m
-dissipative,
-A
in the case

d_{+}=0
and
A^{*}
if
d_{-}=0
. Since
B
is an
m
-dissipative operator as-well, we conclude that necessarily
B=-A
if
d_{+}=0
and
B=A^{*}
if
d_{-}=0
(in the case
d_{+}=d_{-}=0
we have
B=-A=A^{*}
). Thus, the operator
B
is uniquely defined, which implies uniqueness of the corresponding semigroup.

Conversely, if the operator
A
is not maximal then both its deficiency indexes are not zero. Evidently, then there exist different maximal skew-symmetric extensions
\tilde{A}_{1}
,
\tilde{A}_{2}
of
A
with the same deficiency indexes
d_{\pm}
. The corresponding
m
-dissipative operators

B_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}-\tilde{A}_{1}&,&d_{+}=0,\\
(\tilde{A}_{1})^{*}&,&d_{-}=0,\end{array}\right.\quad B_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}-\tilde{A}_{2}&,&d_{+}=0,\\
(\tilde{A}_{2})^{*}&,&d_{-}=0\end{array}\right.

are different and therefore generate different contractive semigroups of g.s.
∎

Now we are ready to formulate a criteria of uniqueness of g.s. to problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
).

Theorem 4.3
.

A g.s. of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) is unique if and only if the deficiency index
d_{-}
of the operator
A
is zero.

Proof.

Assume that a g.s. of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) is unique. We have to prove that
d_{-}=0
. Supposing the contrary,
d_{-}>0
, we conclude that the closed subspace
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-A)
is strictly contained in
H
. Then
\ker(E-A^{*})=(\mathop{\rm Im}(E-A))^{\perp}\not=\{0\}
and there exists a nonzero vector
u_{0}\in D(A^{*})
such that
A^{*}u_{0}=u_{0}
. The function
u=e^{t}u_{0}
is a g.s. of (
1.1
), (
1.2
) different from the semigroup g.s.
T_{t}u_{0}
(constructed in Theorem
4.1
), because the latter is bounded. Moreover, for each
t_{0}\geq 0

\tilde{u}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}e^{t}u_{0},&,&0\leq t\leq t_{0},\\
