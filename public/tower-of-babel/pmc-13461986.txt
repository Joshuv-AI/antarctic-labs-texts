Development and functional assessment of a polymer supported nanoscale iron hybrid for sustainable enhancement of water based drilling fluids
Authors: Pshtiwan T. Jaf, Mohammed J. Awl, Mohammed A. Jamal, Arya Fallah, Ahmed R. AlBajalan, Samim Sherzod
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eco-friendly biopolymers in water-based drilling fluids often suffer from thermal degradation and inadequate filtration control under harsh downhole conditions. To overcome these limitations, this study aimed to develop a high-performance, sustainable hybrid nanocomposite that synergistically combines the structural resilience of biopolymers with the mechanical strength and pore-plugging capabilities of metallic nanoparticles. A ternary nanocomposite (IRON-NC) was synthesized via an in-situ reduction-stabilization method, embedding nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) within a crosslinked chitosan and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) matrix. The material’s morphology, chemical interactions, and electrokinetic properties were characterized, followed by comprehensive rheological and filtration evaluations under both standard and high-pressure/high-temperature (HPHT) conditions. Unlike standalone biopolymer additives, this hybrid architecture leveraged strong electrostatic repulsion (zeta potential of -48.5 mV) and robust polymer-metal bonding to ensure uniform dispersion and exceptional thermal reliability. At a remarkably low optimal dosage of 1000 ppm, IRON-NC induced pronounced shear-thinning behavior, increasing plastic viscosity to 37.6 cP and yield point to 13 Pa to ensure effective cuttings suspension. Furthermore, the nZVI-reinforced matrix facilitated the deposition of a highly compact, structurally resilient filter cake, reducing both API and HPHT fluid loss by 43–47% compared to the base fluid. The synergistic integration of nZVI with the chitosan-CMC network effectively resolves the thermal and mechanical vulnerabilities associated with conventional eco-friendly additives. Ultimately, IRON-NC presents a highly efficient, multifunctional, and environmentally compliant solution for improving rheology, stability, and fluid loss control in advanced drilling operations.

Introduction

Drilling operations represent one of the most complex and capital‑intensive stages in petroleum exploration, directly dictating the cost efficiency and safety of well construction [1]. The success of these operations fundamentally depends on the performance of the drilling fluid, a multifunctional medium responsible for cooling and lubricating the drill bit, stabilizing the borehole, suspending cuttings, and maintaining hydrostatic balance [2]. Contemporary drilling systems typically rely on three fluid categories: water‑based, oil‑based, and synthetic‑based muds. Among these, water‑based drilling fluids are widely favored due to their environmental compatibility, lower toxicity, and ease of preparation and treatment. However, despite these advantages, the water–clay interaction intrinsic to water‑based systems introduces undesirable complications such as shale hydration, swelling, and structural collapse, posing persistent challenges to borehole stability and drilling safety.

The development of high-performance drilling fluids and wellbore materials is increasingly informed by interdisciplinary advancements in nanotechnology, predictive modeling, and reservoir geomechanics. For instance, recent innovations in well cementing utilize microencapsulated phase change materials to control microcracks and regulate hydration heat under complex deepwater conditions [3]. Furthermore, optimizing modern drilling operations heavily relies on real-time data fusion for rock strength prediction [4] and the application of advanced machine learning models, such as response surface and random forest methods, for optimizing precision drilling parameters [5]. Beyond the immediate wellbore, understanding complex subsurface interactions is critical, whether characterizing multicomponent gas migration in deep shale inorganic nanopores [6], evaluating hydrogen transfer mechanisms during the in-situ pyrolysis of oil shale [7], or assessing the mechanical degradation of sandstones under complex mixed-wettability and reactive CO2 environments [8]. Consequently, the integration of advanced nanomaterials, such as specialized nanofluids engineered to alter wettability and reduce interfacial tension [9], represents a critical frontier in formulating next-generation fluids capable of addressing these multifactorial subterranean challenges.

The operational importance of improving drilling fluid performance has intensified as global demand for deeper wells, harsh high‑pressure and high‑temperature settings, and unconventional reservoirs continues to rise. The drilling fluid cost typically accounts for less than one‑sixth of total drilling expenditure, yet it influences nearly every operational variable affecting non‑productive time, rate of penetration, and overall economic viability. Inadequate control over fluid rheology or filtration behavior can precipitate catastrophic formation damage or wellbore instability, resulting in escalation of costs [10]. Conventional testing protocols, such as Marsh funnel and viscometric methods, provide only partial or delayed assessment of fluid performance, underscoring an urgent need for formulations with intrinsic self‑stabilizing features that can sustain optimal flow and filtration properties under dynamic downhole conditions [11, 12].

The complexity of downhole challenges has spurred progressive integration of advanced chemical and nanostructured components into water‑based drilling systems [13, 14]. The first generation of additives targeted hydration and dispersion control through cationic or amphoteric polymers, surfactant blends, and inorganic bridging agents. Cationic polymers such as polyethyleneimine demonstrated electrostatic attraction toward negatively charged clay surfaces, indicating potential for shale inhibition and rheological enhancement [2, 15]. Nonetheless, they exhibited inadequate thermal stability and rapid molecular degradation at elevated temperatures. At the same time, inorganic particulates provided physical bridging effects but lacked the flexibility and dispersibility necessary to maintain uniform suspension in dynamic circulation systems. These limitations accentuated the need for integrated hybrid materials combining the chemical reactivity of polymers with the structural robustness of nanoparticles to achieve sustainable mud stability under extreme thermodynamic conditions [16, 17].

The subsequent introduction of polymer nanocomposites emerged as a pivotal advancement in drilling fluid design. Polymer nanocomposites, consisting of nanoscale inorganic fillers dispersed or covalently grafted within a polymeric matrix, offer multifunctional behavior beyond the sum of their constituents. The synergy between organic matrices and nanophase structures provides enhanced viscoelasticity, regulated thermal response, and improved environmental conformity [18–20]. The significance of polymer–nanoparticle phase behavior and interfacial compatibility is crucial, as the crystallinity, chain flexibility, and degree of substitution in the polymer determine both rheological adaptation and colloidal stability. Well‑designed polymer nanocomposites exhibit superior heat tolerance, filtration control, and shear response due to their ability to form interconnected micro‑networks that resist structural collapse under high shear fields typical of drilling circulation.

Several benchmark attempts have demonstrated the transformative role of nanosized reinforcements in drilling fluid systems. Incorporation of oxide‑based nanoparticles has been reported to elevate viscosity and heat transfer, while layered nanomaterials such as graphene derivatives and clay platelets enhance lubrication and thermal resistance [14, 18, 21]. Benchmark investigations also emphasize the necessity of fine control over dispersion and interaction mechanisms within polymer–nanoparticle systems. The influence of nanoparticle geometry, surface functionalization, and concentration on rheological and filtration characteristics has been systematically evaluated. Hybrid architectures, wherein flexible polymer chains encapsulate or bridge nanoparticles, have proven effective at suppressing aggregation and maintaining colloidal stability during thermal exposure [16, 22, 23]. Such combinations produce congruent filter‑cake microstructures capable of micro‑pore blockage and strengthening of the borehole wall under HPHT conditions [24, 25]. Comparative assessments of biopolymeric nanocomposites highlight significant potential for replacing non‑biodegradable or toxic additives historically utilized in oil and gas operations. Integrating biodegradable organic matrices within metallic or oxide nanocomposites allows sustainable, recyclable formulations that align with current environmental mandates and corporate sustainability goals [18, 20, 26].

The present work is important because it integrates sustainability with performance by introducing an eco‑friendly chitosan–CMC‑based nanocomposite capable of improving both fluid stability and drilling efficiency. From an industrial perspective, achieving such multifunctional performance with biodegradable components directly addresses the pressing need for greener drilling technologies. The research follows a systematic workflow consisting of the synthesis of the IRON‑NC nanocomposite, its structural and colloidal characterization, and comprehensive evaluation within water‑based drilling fluids under API and HPHT conditions. The main hypothesis tested is that the synergistic combination of chitosan, CMC, and Fe⁰ nanoparticles generates a stable hybrid capable of enhancing viscosity, yield stress, and filtration control simultaneously. The overall objective is to determine the optimum nanocomposite concentration providing maximum rheological reinforcement and minimal filtrate loss, thereby validating IRON‑NC as a viable next‑generation green drilling additive.

Methodology and materials

Materials section

All materials used in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Medium-molecular-weight chitosan (CS, degree of deacetylation ≈ 85%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and served as the organic matrix for nanocomposite formation. Carboxymethyl cellulose sodium salt (CMC, MW ≈ 250,000 g·mol⁻¹, Sigma-Aldrich) was employed as a green stabilizing and dispersing agent to improve the aqueous stability of the nanocomposite through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. Sodium borohydride (NaBH₄, ≥ 98%, Merck) and ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6 H2O, ≥ 99%, Merck) were used as the reductant and iron precursor, respectively, for the in situ generation of nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) within the CS–CMC framework. The synthesis utilized deoxygenated distilled water prepared by bubbling nitrogen gas to prevent oxidation of Fe⁰ species during the reduction step.

The preparation of base brines and mud formulations employed high-purity salts and additives. Sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥ 99.5%) and potassium chloride (KCl, ≥ 99.5%) were procured from Merck and used to simulate different salinity conditions relevant to drilling environments. Polymer additives commonly utilized in water-based drilling fluids, including polyanionic cellulose (PAC), partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM), and xanthan gum (XG), were supplied by Merck and used as received. These viscosifiers and filtrate-reducing agents provided a standard polymeric baseline for evaluating the rheological enhancement imparted by the synthesized IRON‑NC nanocomposite.

Representative drilling solids were supplied by the Iraqi Petroleum Exploration Company to simulate realistic field conditions. The solids primarily consisted of well-characterized bentonitic clay and quartz-based cuttings, pre-dried and sieved to uniform size before use. All reagents were stored in sealed containers at 25 ± 2 °C under a dry atmosphere to minimize moisture absorption and compositional changes. The selection and purity control of these materials ensured reproducibility and minimized external variability in the synthesis, characterization, and performance evaluation of the IRON‑NC-based drilling nanofluid system.

Experiments

Nanocomposite synthesize

The IRON-NC was prepared via a stepwise reduction–stabilization route designed to integrate zero-valent iron nanoparticles (nZVI) within a biopolymeric chitosan–carboxymethyl cellulose framework. This dual-polymer approach represents a significant improvement over previously reported single-polymer composites, as the combination of chitosan and CMC via in situ reduction provides superior spatial confinement, colloidal stability, and oxidation resistance. In the first stage, 1.0 g of chitosan was dispersed in 100 mL of an aqueous 1% (v/v) acetic acid medium (pH ~ 3.5) and stirred magnetically at 500 rpm until complete dissolution produced a transparent, uniform solution. Functional amino and hydroxyl moieties along the chitosan backbone acted as reactive coordination sites for Fe3+ ions, facilitating homogeneous nucleation and immobilization. A solution containing 2.0 g of ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3⋅6H2O) was then introduced slowly into the chitosan matrix under a continuous nitrogen flow (100 mL/min) to suppress oxidation, allowing complexation of Fe3+ through both electrostatic attraction and chelation [27–29].

Transformation of ferric to metallic iron was carried out by the controlled dropwise addition of 0.5 M sodium borohydride (NaBH4) solution (maintaining a NaBH4-to-Fe molar ratio of 3:1) under vigorous agitation (800 rpm) and inert conditions. The strong reductive drive of NaBH4 generated hydrogen gas, signaling complete conversion of Fe3+ species to Fe0. Freshly formed Fe0 nanoparticles were immediately confined within the viscous chitosan matrix, which effectively limited particle coalescence and promoted nanoscale dispersion. Continuous stirring for 30 min produced a dark-colored suspension, confirming successful nZVI formation within the polymeric phase.

Surface stabilization was achieved in a subsequent coating step by introducing 50 mL of a 1.0 wt% aqueous solution of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) into the intermediate dispersion. The interactions between the negatively charged carboxylate groups of CMC and the protonated amino sites of chitosan, together with hydrogen bonding on Fe0 surfaces, resulted in the construction of a uniform polymeric shell. This outer CMC layer minimized agglomeration, enhanced hydrophilicity, and markedly increased resistance to oxidation, yielding a colloidally stable system after 2 h of gentle aging (300 rpm) [30, 31] (Fig. 1).

After structural stabilization, the synthesized material was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 15 min and subjected to multiple washing cycles with deionized water and ethanol to eliminate residual salts and reaction by-products. The purified solid was vacuum-dried at 50–60 °C for 12 h, producing a fine black powder with strong magnetic behavior and well-dispersed nanoparticles confined within a bio-polymeric network. The dried IRON-NC was stored in sealed containers to prevent air oxidation before undergoing physicochemical characterization and performance evaluation as a green clay-swelling inhibitor.

Following the synthesis and vacuum-drying of the nanocomposite, its structural, morphological, and chemical properties were systematically characterized. The morphology, particle dimensions, and elemental distribution were evaluated using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, Philips CM200 at 200 kV), with samples prepared by dispersing the powder in ethanol under ultrasonication and drop-casting onto carbon-coated copper grids. Crystallographic structure was determined via X-ray Diffraction (XRD) using Cu Kαradiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), scanning over a 2θ range of 10° to 80° to capture both the amorphous biopolymer halo and the crystalline bcc Fe0 peaks. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was conducted in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm− 1 utilizing the KBr pellet method to analyze the chemical bonding and metal-polymer complexation. Finally, the textural properties were quantified through N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K using a surface area and porosity analyzer; prior to analysis, the samples were degassed under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h. The specific surface area and pore characteristics were subsequently calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) methods, respectively.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The hydrodynamic size distribution and colloidal stability of IRON-NC were evaluated using DLS [32, 33]. Aqueous dispersions ranging from 100 to 10,000 ppm were prepared in deionized water under a nitrogen atmosphere and homogenized using a probe sonicator (40 kHz, 200 W) for 30 min. Measurements were conducted in triplicate at 25 ± 1 °C using a laser particle size analyzer equipped with a 633 nm He–Ne laser. To assess both instantaneous and long-term colloidal stability, the hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of the samples were recorded 1 h after sonication, and once again after 21 days of storage in sealed, light-protected amber vials at room temperature.

Drilling fluid preparation

Water-based drilling fluids were formulated as a base fluid (without nanoparticles) and nanocomposite-enhanced fluids. The base fluid was prepared by incrementally adding standard polymeric additives (PAC, HPAM, and XG) to deionized water under continuous agitation using a rod mixer at 1200 rpm. Each additive was mixed for 10 min to ensure complete hydration [34–36]. For the nanocomposite drilling fluids, a pre-determined concentration of IRON-NC powder was first dispersed in deionized water and sonicated at ambient temperature for 30 min to achieve uniform suspension. Following sonication, the polymers were added using the identical sequence and mixing parameters applied to the base fluid. Finally, all formulations were sealed to prevent evaporation and conditioned for 24 h at ambient temperature prior to performance evaluation to allow for complete polymer hydration and structural stabilization.

Rheology measurement

The rheological behavior of both base and IRON-NC-enhanced drilling fluids was evaluated after thermal conditioning in a rolling oven at 60 °C for 16 h to simulate downhole aging conditions. Post-conditioning, the fluids were brought to ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C), and shear stress measurements were performed across a broad range of shear rates (3 to 1021 s⁻¹) using a rotational viscometer equipped with a standard F1 bob and cup geometry. These data provided a detailed profile of flow behavior under varying shear conditions representative of laminar and turbulent regimes encountered during drilling operations [18, 37, 38].

The measured shear stress values were fitted to several rheological models, including Newtonian, Power-law, Bingham Plastic, and Herschel–Bulkley equations. The best-fit model for each formulation was identified based on the coefficient of determination (R²) and residual error analysis. This procedure enabled precise quantification of yield stress and consistency indices, providing insight into the structural influence of IRON-NC nanoparticles on the fluid’s internal network formation, shear thinning response, and overall flow stability under operational stress.

Filtration tests

Filtration measurements were conducted to assess the fluid loss control capability of both base and IRON-NC based drilling fluids under low-pressure and high-pressure conditions. The standard API Filtration Test was first performed at ambient temperature using an API filter press with a 100 psi differential pressure for 30 min. Each test used a 9 cm diameter filter paper, and filtrate volumes were recorded at successive time intervals [39, 40]. The low-pressure data provided a benchmark for evaluating the impact of nanoparticles on mud cake formation and permeability reduction.

To simulate more severe downhole conditions, high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) filtration tests were carried out at 150 °C and a differential pressure of 500 psi using an HPHT filter press assembly. The fluids were pre-heated to the test temperature, and filtrate volumes were collected over 30 min with careful monitoring of thermal equilibrium [41]. The sealing integrity and rheological stabilization of the nanofluids were observed to exhibit improved resistance to filtration under elevated conditions.

All filtrate volumes were corrected for temperature-induced viscosity variations and compared between the two fluid classes. The results provided a quantitative basis for evaluating fluid loss behavior, identifying the efficiency of polymer–nanocomposite interactions in minimizing filtrate invasion, and confirming the hydrophilic coating effectiveness of the IRON-NC particles in forming a compact, low-permeability filter cake structure [42].

Rheology modeling

The rheological modeling of drilling fluids provides a mathematical framework to describe flow behavior under varying shear conditions and to quantify the influence of additives or nanostructures such as IRON-NC on fluid performance. Experimental shear stress–shear rate data obtained from viscometric measurements were used to evaluate the suitability of various rheological models. The purpose of this modeling is to identify the constitutive equation that best represents the real flow response and to extract key parameters such as plastic viscosity, yield stress, and consistency index. These parameters are essential for predicting flow regimes, optimizing equivalent circulating density, and minimizing pressure losses during drilling operations. Results and discussion

Characterization of IRON-NC

The molecular architecture of the IRON-NC hybrid is schematically represented in Fig. 2, illustrating the critical physicochemical interactions that govern its structural integrity and functional efficacy. The fundamental scaffold consists of an interpenetrating polyelectrolyte complex driven by strong electrostatic interactions between the protonated primary amines of the polycationic chitosan and the deprotonated carboxylate groups of the polyanionic carboxymethyl cellulose. This primary ionic network is further reinforced by an extensive array of intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonds among the abundant hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl moieties across the biopolymer backbones, yielding a highly stable, three-dimensional hydrogel-like matrix capable of serving as a nanoscale reactor.

Within this robust organic matrix, the zero-valent iron nanoparticles are synthesized in situ and firmly encapsulated. As depicted in the schematic, the oxygen and nitrogen atoms inherent to the biopolymer chains act as potent coordinating ligands, forming a passivating shell around the highly reactive metallic core. This direct polymer-metal coordination not only restricts the outward crystalline growth of the iron nanoparticles, keeping them within the nanoscale domain, but also acts as a formidable steric and electrostatic barrier against agglomeration and extensive oxidation. Consequently, this engineered core-shell conformation seamlessly integrates the mechanical flexibility and dispersibility of the amorphous biopolymers with the reactive potential of the crystalline iron core.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Table 1) provided direct quantitative evidence of the composite’s elemental composition aligned with its biopolymer–metal design. The dominant oxygen content (56.2 wt%, 63.3 at %) originated from hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of chitosan and CMC as well as from slight surface oxidation. Carbon accounted for 17.0 wt% (26.9 at %), confirming the polysaccharide component, whereas nitrogen at 0.76 wt% (0.95 at %) validated the existence of chitosan amine groups. Crucially, the iron (Fe) content was quantified at 26.04 wt% (8.85 at %), confirming the successful loading and retention of the metallic core within the hydrogel matrix. The resultant organic–metal ratio demonstrated a tightly integrated hybrid structure where polymer chains surrounded the metallic core, enhancing structural uniformity and dispersion stability.

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis (Fig. 5a) exhibited a type IV isotherm with a clear H3 hysteresis loop, showing that IRON-NC possessed a mesoporous texture with slit shaped pores. Quantitative analysis of the isotherm data revealed a high BET specific surface area of approximately 980m2/g and a total pore volume of 0.74 cm3/g. The significant adsorbed volume (reaching ~ 480 cm³/g STP) at higher relative pressures highlighted efficient capillary condensation within the interconnected pore network formed by polymeric crosslinking and nanoparticle aggregation. Furthermore, the BJH pore size distribution (Fig. 5b) showed an average pore diameter narrowly centered at 3.0 nm, confirming the dominance of mesopores and a highly uniform pore geometry. This high specific surface area and ordered structural geometry enhance fluid accessibility and provide the abundant active sites required for the nanocomposite’s superior performance in clay stabilization systems [52].

Electrokinetic properties serve as a fundamental and highly reliable indicator of colloidal stability and interaction potential within complex drilling fluid systems, dictating how particles will behave under various operational conditions. The continuous evolution of surface charge during the multi-step synthesis process was quantitatively tracked and meticulously recorded using high-precision zeta potential measurements (Table 2) to ensure the functionalization was progressing as theoretically anticipated. The initial bare nanoscale zero-valent iron (nZVI) particles exhibited a slightly negative surface charge, which is characteristic of their inherent surface oxidation state in an aqueous medium. However, upon targeted functionalization with the biopolymer chitosan, the intermediate composite material demonstrated a distinct and dramatic charge reversal to a highly positive electrokinetic state. This significant shift is primarily driven by the abundant presence of protonated amine (-NH3+) functional groups distributed extensively along the rigid biopolymer backbone of the chitosan molecules wrapping the iron core.

Following the final critical integration phase involving the addition of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), the overall surface charge of the composite structure underwent another dramatic and purposeful shift, transitioning firmly to a highly negative state. The remarkably dense incorporation of strongly anionic carboxylate groups originating from the CMC matrix completely overshadowed the previously dominant cationic nature of the underlying chitosan layer, resulting in a strongly electronegative final functionalized nanocomposite exhibiting a robust zeta potential of -48.5 mV. This exceptionally elevated negative surface charge is scientifically crucial for the application’s success, as it provides an immense degree of continuous electrostatic repulsion among the dispersed composite particles. This repulsive force effectively and consistently prevents undesirable nanoparticle agglomeration and yields exceptional steric and electrostatic stabilization when the composite is thoroughly dispersed alongside the similarly negatively charged bentonite platelets within the complex matrix of the drilling mud system.

The intricate surface elemental composition and the complex chemical state transitions occurring throughout the synthesis were further elucidated and definitively confirmed using high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as systematically illustrated in Fig. 6-a to d. The broad survey spectra presented in Fig. 6-a unambiguously confirm the highly successful integration and stable anchoring of the organic biopolymers onto the inorganic iron substrate. This successful functionalization is distinctly marked by the prominent emergence of strong N 1s and C 1s elemental peaks in the functionalized sample spectra, which are expectedly and completely absent in the baseline scan of the bare nZVI material. This fundamental comparative observation confirms beyond reasonable doubt the successful execution of both the initial chitosan coating procedure and the subsequent complex crosslinking steps involving the carboxymethyl cellulose layer.

High-resolution XPS analysis provides a substantially deeper and more granular insight into the specific chemical binding environments and the diverse oxidation states present within the synthesized nanocomposite. Figure 6-b carefully displays the detailed Fe 2p spectra, which clearly indicates the simultaneous coexistence of Fe0, F2+, and Fe3+ oxidation states within the metallic core and its immediate passivation layer. This crucial finding demonstrates conclusively that the reactive core-shell structural integrity of the nanoscale iron particles is perfectly maintained even while intimately interacting with the surrounding dense polymer matrix. Furthermore, Fig. 6-c graphically illustrates the high-resolution N 1s spectra, clearly showing a distinct chemical transition and definitively confirming the active presence of protonated amine groups (-NH3+). These specific nitrogen environments are absolutely essential, as they directly participate in the critical electrostatic bridging and molecular interlocking mechanisms with the anionic chains of the carboxymethyl cellulose.

`ure 6-d prominently highlights the mathematically deconvoluted C 1s peaks, which accurately correspond to a diverse array of critical carbon bonding environments, including specific C-N, C = O, and O-C = O structural configurations. The verified presence of these crucial carboxylate and amide-like molecular linkages confirms the robust and highly stable complexation of the intertwined biopolymer chains as they physically and chemically wrap around the reactive iron nanoparticles. Together, these uniquely characteristic spectral features provide definitive, undeniable proof of the extensive covalent and non-covalent crosslinking networks established within the final functionalized nanocomposite structure. Ultimately, the powerful synergistic combination of remarkably high electrostatic repulsion, evidenced by the strong negative zeta potential, and the highly robust, interpenetrating chemical networks confirmed by the XPS analysis, definitively validates the successful synthesis of the composite and provides the fundamental structural basis for its expected macroscopic performance in drilling fluid applications.

The enhanced colloidal stability and robust cross-linked network of the IRON-NC nanocomposite align with recent global advancements in functionalized biopolymers and advanced filler-reinforced systems. Colloidal stability analysis

The colloidal stability and particle dispersion behavior of the synthesized nanocomposite were investigated using DLS and zeta potential measurements. The hydrodynamic diameter distribution profiles for dispersions at various concentrations (100–10000 ppm) are shown in Fig. 7. As the IRON-NC concentration increased, a clear trend toward larger particle sizes and broader distributions was observed, indicating a gradual aggregation tendency. Quantitative analysis of the DLS data revealed that at lower concentrations (100–1000 ppm), the mean hydrodynamic diameter (Z-average) increased progressively from approximately 35 nm at 100 ppm to 85 nm at 1000 ppm. In this regime, the nanocomposite displayed well-defined monomodal size distributions with low Polydispersity Index (PDI) values ranging from ≈ 0.15to 0.22, reflecting uniform dispersion of the chitosan–CMC-coated nZVI particles within the aqueous medium. Conversely, concentrations above 1000 ppm exhibited broader spectra with significantly larger hydrodynamic diameters (reaching a Z-average of ≈ 550 nm and a PDI > 0.40 at 10000 ppm), implying strong interparticle interactions and partial clustering driven by reduced electrostatic repulsion at higher solid loadings.

Rheology measurement

The rheological performance of both the base and IRON-NC-modified drilling fluids, illustrated in Fig. 8, reveals a clear non‑Newtonian shear‑thinning nature consistent with viscoplastic systems typically encountered in water‑based muds. The incremental increase of shear stress with shear rate is apparent across all formulations, with nanocomposite loading yielding a general elevation of the stress levels compared with the base fluid. The trend indicates that the introduction of IRON-NC improves the fluid’s structural integrity, primarily by reinforcing polymeric interactions within the continuous phase through hydrogen bonding and Fe-based interparticle associations. These effects enhance the resistance to deformation at low shear while preserving mobility at elevated shear conditions, critical for efficient cuttings transport and suspension control in drilling operations.

The consistent rheological enhancements observed upon the addition of IRON-NC also provide crucial indirect evidence regarding the nanocomposite’s stability within the actual drilling fluid matrix. Formulated drilling fluids present a complex, high-salinity environment heavily loaded with various polymers and salts, which typically compress the electrostatic double layer of bare nanomaterials and induce severe agglomeration. Had the IRON-NC particles been unstable in this high-ionic-strength medium, they would have rapidly agglomerated and precipitated, leading to erratic shear stress responses, a collapse of the yield point, and visible settling during the measurements. Instead, the steady improvement and maintenance of critical rheological parameters confirm that the chitosan-CMC biopolymeric framework successfully shields the zero-valent iron nanoparticles. This steric and electrostatic shielding preserves their uniform dispersion and functional efficacy, allowing them to interact beneficially with the mud components even under harsh chemical conditions.

Microscopic performance mechanism analysis

To elucidate the fundamental interactions governing the macroscopic rheological and filtration improvements, the microscopic behavior of the bentonite dispersions was investigated. Analyzing the interfacial dynamics between bentonite platelets and the sequential additive modifications provides a comprehensive, empirical understanding of the stabilization mechanism within the drilling fluid system. Zeta potential serves as a critical indicator of colloidal stability by quantifying the electrostatic repulsion between particles. As presented in Table 7, the pure bentonite suspension exhibits a baseline negative zeta potential (-28.4 mV). The addition of 1000 ppm bare nZVI reduces this negativity to -19.6 mV, suggesting partial charge neutralization and a subsequent tendency for particle agglomeration. Introducing the Chitosan-nZVI composite further diminishes the magnitude to -11.2 mV; this significant drop is directly attributed to electrostatic attraction and bridging flocculation caused by the highly cationic nature of the primary chitosan chains interacting with the negatively charged clay surfaces.

In stark contrast, the incorporation of 1000 ppm Chitosan-CMC-nZVI profoundly modifies the surface charge dynamics, yielding a highly negative zeta potential of -42.8 mV. The abundant anionic carboxylate groups introduced by the CMC polymer matrix effectively override the charge reduction induced by the core materials, imparting robust electrostatic repulsion between the bentonite platelets and the composite particles. This substantial increase in repulsive forces prevents platelet stacking and is a primary driver for the enhanced colloidal stability observed in the macroscopic fluid testing.

Novelty, comparisons, and study implications

Water-based drilling fluids increasingly rely on biodegradable polymers and advanced nanomaterials to overcome harsh downhole conditions while minimizing environmental impact. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of standalone biopolymers, such as corn starch and chitosan [62], hydroxyethyl acrylate chitosan [63], and hydrophobically modified carboxymethylcellulose [64], in reducing fluid loss and modifying rheological profiles. However, single-component systems often struggle under severe thermal and saline stress. Our study introduces the IRON-NC system, which uniquely integrates a highly reactive zero-valent iron core (Fe0) with a dual-polymer (chitosan and CMC) stabilization matrix. This synergistic architecture provides superior electrokinetic repulsion and redox buffering, significantly outperforming individual chitosan or CMC additives and conventional metal oxide nanoparticles by achieving optimal fluid performance at a markedly lower dosage (1000 ppm) while maintaining exceptional colloidal stability.

To rigorously contextualize the incremental advancements achieved by the IRON-NC system, Table 8 presents a quantitative comparison of our findings against recently developed green drilling fluid additives and hybrid nanocomposites reported in the existing literature. As detailed thoroughly in the table, while single-component derivatives like carboxymethyl chitosan [65] or extracted nano-CMC [66] achieve commendable filtration reductions in the range of 15% to 50%, they often require higher concentrations or lack the robust thermal resilience provided by an inorganic core. Furthermore, compared to conventional metal-doped solid-free systems [67] or other agricultural waste-derived CMCs (Mohammed), the newly formulated IRON-NC composite demonstrates a highly competitive 43–47% reduction in API filtration at a relatively low active concentration, clearly validating the exceptional efficiency and necessity of the ternary crosslinked structure over isolated single components.

The practical and industrial implications of this research are highly significant for the sustainable optimization of modern drilling operations across the petroleum sector. By successfully demonstrating that a remarkably low dosage of the IRON-NC composite can effectively stabilize rheology and form a highly impermeable, ultra-thin filter cake, this formulation directly translates to improved wellbore hydraulic management and a substantially reduced risk of differential pipe sticking. Furthermore, the reliance on abundantly available, renewable biopolymers like chitosan, which can be sustainably derived from crustacean waste [70], and CMC significantly reduces the overall dependency on synthetic, non-biodegradable polymeric additives, paving the way for more ecologically responsible drilling fluid designs without compromising technical performance in challenging subterranean environments.

Despite the highly promising empirical results obtained in these laboratory-scale evaluations, several critical limitations must be explicitly acknowledged regarding the current developmental stage of the synthesized IRON-NC system. Foremost, while the encompassing polymer envelope is inherently biodegradable, the long-term ecotoxicological fate and potential aquatic toxicity of the nanoscale zero-valent iron core have not been quantified in this study, necessitating a highly cautious approach to broad environmental claims. Additionally, the experimental data are primarily constrained to standardized API static filtration and rheological thermal aging tests; therefore, the fluid’s dynamic performance under the extreme, continuous shear conditions and complex multiphase interactions typical of real-world drilling scenarios remains unverified.

Moving forward, the future scope of this research must critically prioritize the transition from controlled laboratory environments to dynamic, pilot-scale testing to validate the scalability of the IRON-NC formulation. Subsequent studies should focus heavily on conducting comprehensive lifecycle assessments and rigorous ecotoxicity bioassays to definitively evaluate the environmental impact of the zero-valent iron nanoparticles upon eventual wellbore discharge. Additionally, investigating the tribological behavior of the composite, particularly its potential to safely reduce mechanical wear on drill pipe joints, similar to established studies evaluating particle content at casing interfaces [71] or polymeric reductions in bit wear [72], will provide a much more holistic understanding of its mechanical benefits, ultimately facilitating its safe commercial deployment in advanced drilling operations.

Conclusion

This study successfully synthesized a biodegradable and stable chitosan–CMC‑based nanocomposite (IRON-NC) containing uniformly dispersed Fe⁰ nanoparticles for application in water‑based drilling fluids. The workflow included in situ synthesis, structural and colloidal characterization, rheological modeling, and filtration testing. Characterization confirmed the hybrid’s mesoporous nature, homogeneous Fe⁰ confinement, and strong organic–metal coordination. Colloidal analysis identified 1000 ppm as the optimum dosage ensuring long‑term dispersion and sufficient surface charge stability. Rheological evaluation demonstrated that IRON-NC incorporation strengthens the viscoplastic structure of drilling muds, increasing plastic viscosity and yield point while maintaining flowability. The Bingham Plastic model effectively captured the shear‑thinning behavior with high statistical confidence (R² > 0.95). Filtration tests under API and HPHT conditions showed substantial fluid‑loss reduction and improved filter‑cake compactness due to nanoparticle bridging and polymeric reinforcement. Despite these positive results, the study’s controlled‑environment design and lack of long‑term cyclic testing define its main limitations. Future work should expand to field validation, storage stability assessment, and interaction modeling with reservoir brines. Overall, the nanocomposite demonstrated a promising balance between environmental compatibility and technical performance, confirming its potential as a next‑generation green additive for efficient, thermally stable, and low‑permeability drilling fluid systems.
