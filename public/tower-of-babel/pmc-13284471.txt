Fat phenomics reveals shared genetic architecture of abdominal fat and yolk lipids: Insight into the potential for divergent selection
Authors: Wenxin Zhang, Fangren Lan, Ronglang Cai, Ning Yang, Congjiao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcessive abdominal fat deposition reduces production efficiency and metabolic health, whereas yolk lipid content is directly linked to egg quality and reproductive output. These two fat-related traits are often in conflict from a breeding perspective, yet whether they share common genetic and molecular determinants—and whether such sharing could inform divergent selection for lean abdomen with lipid‑rich yolk—remains unclear. Here, we analyzed 22 fat deposition-related traits in a population of 248 laying hens, together with genome-wide genotyping data, transcriptomic data from 10 tissues. Our results showed marked heterogeneity in the genetic architecture of fat deposition-related traits, with a higher degree of signal sharing among biologically related traits but limited overlap across different trait categories. Multi-trait association and trait–trait colocalization analyses further identified multiple shared loci between abdominal fat-related (AF) and egg yolk-related (EY) traits, including several shared candidate signals on chromosomes 2, 10, and 11. Integrative analyses incorporating multi-tissue cis-eQTL data further prioritized shared candidate regulatory genes, including MYO10, GPT2, VPS35, ADAM10, ALDH1A2, and MYO1E. Representative AF–EY shared loci, including 2__74614272, rs318039150, and rs741038415, showed opposite effect tendencies between abdominal fat- and yolk-related traits, whereas loci involving ADAM10, ALDH1A2, and MYO1E showed more directionally consistent associations, indicating both potential antagonistic and coordinated regulatory relationships. Together, these findings reveal a complex shared molecular basis linking abdominal fat deposition and yolk lipid deposition during the extended laying period and provide a framework for dissecting coordinated regulatory mechanisms and informing molecular breeding in laying hens.Keywords: Laying hens, Extended laying period, Fat deposition, Shared genetic architecture, Multi-omics integration

Introduction

Extending the laying cycle has become an important objective in modern layer breeding and production, as genetic improvement continues to increase laying performance (Bain et al., 2016). However, a prolonged laying period not only enhances the lifetime egg output but also exposes hens to sustained demands for energy utilization and lipid metabolism, thereby reshaping lipid synthesis, transport, and deposition (Gu et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that aged laying hens are more susceptible to lipid metabolic imbalance, hepatic lipid accumulation, and altered body composition during the extended laying period (Liu et al., 2018; Gu et al., 2021; van Eck et al., 2023). These changes are closely related to hen health and can directly affect egg quality and persistence of egg production (Shini et al., 2019).

Lipid metabolism in chickens exhibits marked species specificity. Unlike in many mammals, the liver is the major site of de novo fatty acid synthesis in chickens and also the central organ for the synthesis and export of yolk precursor substances (Mellouk et al., 2018; Gloux et al., 2019; Zaefarian et al., 2019). During the laying period, hepatic triglycerides, phospholipids, and apolipoproteins are transported through the circulation and contribute to yolk deposition (Liu et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2024). Consequently, abdominal fat deposition, hepatic lipid status, and yolk lipid deposition are not independent physiological processes but are linked through a coordinated network involving hepatic lipogenesis, lipoprotein transport, and yolk formation. With advancing age, this network may be remodeled, thereby altering the fat deposition patterns and production performance.

To date, studies on fat deposition in laying hens have largely focused on individual phenotypes or single tissues, such as abdominal fat deposition (Guo et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024), hepatic lipid metabolism (Gu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024), and yolk lipid composition (Peng et al., 2024). By contrast, the extent to which these traits share genetic architecture and candidate regulatory genes remains poorly understood. This issue is particularly relevant during the extended laying period, when excessive abdominal fat deposition is generally unfavorable from a production perspective (Fouad and El-Senousey, 2014; Guo et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024), whereas yolk lipid deposition is essential for egg quality and embryonic nutrient supply (van der Wagt et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). Therefore, clarifying the correlation structure and shared regulatory mechanisms among abdominal fat, yolk, and hepatic lipid deposition traits and identifying shared regulators that may simultaneously reduce excessive abdominal fat deposition while maintaining appropriate yolk lipid deposition is of substantial biological and breeding importance. Recent advances in multi-trait GWAS summary-statistic analysis, colocalization analysis, and integrative genetic regulatory approaches have provided new opportunities to dissect the shared genetic basis of complex traits (Koskeridis et al., 2024). MTAG improves locus discovery by jointly leveraging GWAS information across correlated traits, even when sample overlap is present (Turley et al., 2018). HyPrColoc enables the assessment of whether multiple traits share putative causal variants within the same genomic region (Foley et al., 2021), whereas SMR integrates GWAS and eQTL summary statistics to prioritize candidate functional genes associated with complex traits (Zhu et al., 2016). Together, these approaches provide an effective framework for moving from shared loci to candidate genes and putative functional pathways.

In this study, we used 248 purebred Rhode Island Red laying hens at 100 weeks of age and focused on 22 fat deposition-related phenotypes, including abdominal fat, egg yolk, liver lipid, and body weight traits. Specifically, we sought to determine whether these fat deposition-related traits share a common genetic architecture, whether shared abdominal fat–yolk lipid signals show directionally opposite or directionally consistent effects, and which tissue-specific regulatory genes may underlie these shared genetic signals. To address these questions, we integrated whole-genome resequencing data with multi-tissue transcriptomic data and combined GWAS, MTAG, HyPrColoc, SMR, GIFT, and bidirectional GSMR analyses to prioritize shared candidate loci and regulatory genes. This cross-trait and cross-tissue framework enabled us to dissect the coordinated regulation of fat deposition-related phenotypes in laying hens during the extended laying period and to provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies and genetic improvement.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Agricultural University, China (Approval No. AW02205202-01).

Experimental animals and management

A total of 248 purebred Rhode Island Red laying hens were used in this study. All birds were obtained from Beijing Huadu Yukou Poultry Industry Co., Ltd. (Pinggu, Beijing, China) and housed individually under uniform management conditions. The birds were fed a corn–soybean meal diet and had free access to feed and water.

Sample collection

All samples and phenotypic measurements used in this study were obtained from the same population of 248 purebred Rhode Island Red laying hens. Whole blood was collected from the wing vein of each bird for whole-genome resequencing. To support subsequent biochemical measurements of egg yolk traits, eggs laid by each hen during one week at 100 weeks of age were collected. At 100 weeks of age, all hens were euthanized by cervical dislocation and subjected to necropsies. After opening the abdominal cavity, the liver and abdominal fat depots, including the fat surrounding the gizzard, cloaca, and adjacent abdominal muscles, were carefully dissected. Abdominal fat weight (AFW) and liver weight (LW) were measured using an electronic balance with a precision of 0.1 g, and the abdominal fat percentage (AFP) was subsequently calculated.

For transcriptomic profiling, samples of the hypothalamus, pituitary, liver, pancreas, duodenal mucosa, ileal mucosa, cecal mucosa, ovary, magnum, and uterus were collected from each bird. These tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection and stored at −80°C until further processing. Unless otherwise stated, all downstream analyses in this study, including GWAS, eQTL mapping, MTAG, colocalization, SMR, GIFT, and GSMR, were based on this same population.

Biochemical measurements

Biochemical measurements and the corresponding primary phenotypic data analyzed in this study were obtained from our previous study of the same population of 248 laying hens (Han et al., 2025). Briefly, liver, abdominal fat, and egg yolk samples were assayed for liver dry matter content (LDM), liver triglyceride content (LTG), liver free fatty acid content (LFFA), liver total cholesterol content (LTC), abdominal fat dry matter content (AFDM), abdominal fat triglyceride content (AFTG), abdominal fat free fatty acid content (AFFFA), yolk dry matter content (EYDM), yolk triglyceride content (EYTG), and yolk free fatty acid content (EYFFA). In the present study, these previously generated phenotypic data were further used for downstream cross-trait genetic and multi-omics integrative analyses, and additional derived indicators were calculated as described below.

Phenotypic definitions and derived traits

The 22 phenotypic variables analyzed in this study included directly measured traits, biochemical measurements, and derived indicators from the same population of 248 laying hens (Han et al., 2025). The sample size for each analyzed phenotype is provided in Supplementary Table S1. To distinguish directly measured percentage traits from derived mass traits, the percentage measurements of dry matter content in abdominal fat, egg yolk, and liver were denoted as AFDMP, EYDMP, and LDMP, respectively, where “P” indicates percentage. The dry matter mass for abdominal fat, egg yolk, and liver was calculated as tissue weight multiplied by the corresponding dry matter proportion, and the moisture mass was calculated as the difference between tissue weight and dry matter mass. Accordingly, AFDM, EYDM, and LDM denote dry matter mass, whereas AFMC, EYMC, and LMC denote moisture mass for the corresponding tissues. AFW, EYW, and LW represent the abdominal fat weight, egg yolk weight, and liver weight, respectively. Average yolk weight was obtained and used as the EYW phenotype in the present study. Body weight-related phenotypes were also included. The mean body weight at 100 weeks of age (mBW100) was defined as the average of the body weights measured at the beginning and end of the 99–100-week experimental period, whereas BW100 was defined as the body weight recorded at the end of this period.

Whole-genome resequencing and data processing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the blood samples using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, MD, USA). DNA concentration and purity were initially assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and DNA concentration was further quantified using the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, MA, USA). High-quality genomic DNA was randomly fragmented to an average size of approximately 350 bp using a Bioruptor Pico sonication device (Diagenode, NJ, USA), followed by library construction using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, CA, USA). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA) to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

To reduce technical artifacts, raw reads were filtered by removing reads that met any of the following criteria: (1) contained > 5% ambiguous bases (N); (2) contained adapter contamination; or (3) had an average Phred quality score of ≤ 20. High-quality clean reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (Gallus gallus, Ensembl v110) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.13) (Li and Durbin, 2009) with the parameters “mem -t 4 -k 32 -M”. SAMtools (v1.3.1) (Li et al., 2009) was then used to convert, sort, and index the SAM files to generate BAM files. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard to improve the alignment accuracy. Variant calling was performed using the HaplotypeCaller module in GATK (Genome Analysis Toolkit, v4.2.0.0) (McKenna et al., 2010).

Variants were filtered separately for SNPs and InDels using standard quality-control criteria. SNPs were filtered using the following thresholds: QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, SOR > 3.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < −12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. InDels were filtered using the following thresholds: QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, FS > 200.0, and ReadPosRankSum < −20.0. High-confidence variants were further subjected to quality control in PLINK (v1.90) (Purcell et al., 2007) using the following thresholds: sample call rate > 95%, SNP call rate > 95%, and minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1%. Genotype imputation was subsequently performed using BEAGLE (v5.1) (Browning and Browning, 2007), and the imputed variants were filtered again using PLINK with the same criteria. After quality control, a total of 6,524,337 SNPs from 248 individuals across 39 chromosomes were retained for downstream analyses.

Multi-tissue RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the tissue samples using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA yield and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit. Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) greater than 7.0 were used for library preparation. RNA-seq libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England BioLabs, #E7760L) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to produce 150-bp paired-end reads. As described previously (Zhang et al., 2026), transcriptomic data were obtained from 248 individuals across 10 tissues, including the hypothalamus, pituitary, liver, pancreas, duodenum, ileum, cecum, ovary, magnum, and uterus. The chicken reference genome and corresponding gene annotations were used for read processing and quantification. Low-quality reads, including reads containing adapter contamination, ambiguous bases (N), or low-quality bases, were removed using Cutadapt (v1.9) (Martin, 2011). The resulting high-quality reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome (Gallus gallus, GRCg7b; Ensembl v110) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) (Kim et al., 2015) with the default parameters. Gene-level read counting and transcript abundance estimation were performed using FeatureCounts (v1.6.3) (Liao et al., 2014) and StringTie (v2.1.6) (Pertea et al., 2015), respectively, and the expression levels were represented as transcripts per million (TPM). To reduce the biases introduced by library size and RNA composition, expression profiles were normalized across samples using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method (Robinson and Oshlack, 2010). After tissue-specific quality control, the final sample sizes used for downstream transcriptomic and eQTL analyses were 244 for the hypothalamus, 246 for the pituitary, 248 for the liver, 248 for the pancreas, 247 for the duodenal mucosa, 248 for the ileal mucosa, 245 for the cecal mucosa, 248 for the ovary, 247 for the magnum, and 246 for the uterus.

Genome-wide association analysis

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were performed for each phenotype using genotype data from the 248 resequenced individuals and phenotype-specific valid records for the 22 analyzed traits, with the sample size for each phenotype provided in Supplementary Table S1. Association testing was conducted using the linear mixed model implemented in GEMMA (v0.98.4) (Zhou and Stephens, 2012). In this framework, SNP effects were tested while accounting for relatedness among individuals through a random polygenic term. The fixed effects included an intercept and the top five genetic principal components to control for population stratification. The statistical significance of each SNP was assessed using the likelihood ratio test. Because the conventional Bonferroni correction can be overly conservative for dense genome-wide markers in linkage disequilibrium, the effective number of independent tests was estimated using the R package simpleM (Gao et al., 2008), yielding 226,205 effective tests. Based on this value, the genome-wide significance threshold was set at 2.21×10−7 (0.05/226,205), and the suggestive significance threshold was set at 4.42×10−6 (1/226,205).

In addition to the above phenotypes, association analyses were conducted for selected gene expression traits using fastGWA (Jiang et al., 2019) implemented in GCTA. In these analyses, gene expression levels were treated as dependent variables, and SNP genotypes were treated as independent variables within a linear mixed model framework. To run fastGWA, a full-density genomic relationship matrix (GRM) was first constructed, followed by the derivation of a sparse GRM using a truncation threshold of 0.05. The sparse GRM was then used for association testing. The significance thresholds were set consistently with those used in the primary GWAS analyses, with 2.21×10−7 defined as the genome-wide significance threshold and 4.42×10−6 as the suggestive significance threshold.

Multi-trait association analysis based on GWAS summary statistics

To investigate the shared genetic architecture among fat deposition-related traits and improve the power to detect association signals, we performed a multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) (Turley et al., 2018) using summary statistics from the single-trait GWAS analyses described above. Before MTAG, GWAS summary statistics for each trait were harmonized and reformatted to extract the required fields, including SNP identifiers, effect estimates, standard errors, and sample sizes, thereby generating standardized input files for downstream multi-trait analyses.

Based on GWAS summary statistics for 22 fat deposition-related traits (AFDM, AFDMP, AFFFA, AFMC, AFP, AFTG, AFW, EYDM, EYDMP, EYFFA, EYMC, EYTG, EYW, LDM, LDMP, LFFA, LMC, LTC, LTG, LW, BW100, and mBW100), a total of 231 pairwise MTAG analyses were carried out. Because substantial sample overlap was expected across traits, MTAG incorporated bivariate linkage disequilibrium score regression to estimate the cross-trait genetic correlation and error covariance, thereby accounting for overlapping samples. Each pairwise MTAG analysis generated trait-specific association statistics for the two included traits, yielding 380 trait-specific summary-statistics files in total. As the MTAG outputs are interpretable in a manner analogous to single-trait GWAS summary statistics, they were used in subsequent locus prioritization and regional colocalization analyses.

Multi-tissue eQTL mapping

To identify genetic variants associated with gene expression across tissues, cis-eQTL mapping was performed separately for each tissue, with final sample sizes ranging from 244 to 248 individuals and matched genotype data from the same population. The covariates were estimated separately for each tissue. Hidden confounding factors were inferred using PEER (Stegle et al., 2012), whereas genotype principal components were calculated from LD-pruned imputed SNPs using PLINK (v1.90) (Purcell et al., 2007) and EIGENSOFT (v8.0.0) (Price et al., 2006). The final covariate set for each tissue included the top genotype principal components together with the corresponding PEER factors, with the number of PEER factors determined according to tissue-specific sample size (Barbeira et al., 2021). cis-eQTL mapping was performed using tensorQTL (v1.0.4) (Taylor-Weiner et al., 2019), testing variants located within ±1 Mb of the transcription start site of each gene. Nominal association statistics were computed for all variant–gene pairs within the cis window. Gene-level empirical significance was assessed using permutation testing. After multiple-testing correction of the empirical beta-approximated P values using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, genes with at least one significant cis-associated variant at FDR < 0.05 were defined as eGenes. In addition, a unified threshold of P < 1 × 10−7 was used to define significant SNP–gene associations for downstream integrative analyses.

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis

To prioritize candidate genes underlying trait-associated loci, summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) (Zhu et al., 2016) was performed by integrating trait-association summary statistics with multi-tissue cis-eQTL data. For each gene, the top-ranked cis-eQTL identified in the corresponding eQTL analysis was used as an instrumental variant. Instruments were filtered using a cis-eQTL significance threshold of P < 1 × 10−6, and allele-frequency consistency between datasets was controlled using the default SMR allele-frequency filtering strategy. Heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) testing was performed to distinguish pleiotropic or potentially causal associations from those driven by the linkage disequilibrium. When fewer than two eligible SNPs were available for HEIDI testing, the heterogeneity statistics were treated as unavailable. Prior to the SMR analysis, BESD files were generated after updating the SNP coordinates, gene coordinates, and effect allele frequencies. For each trait association dataset, including GWAS and MTAG results, loci showing significant or suggestive association signals were taken forward for integrated with cis-eQTL signals to prioritize candidate genes showing evidence of a shared genetic association between gene expression and the trait.

In addition, bidirectional generalized summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (GSMR) (Zhu et al., 2018) was applied to investigate putative causal relationships between gene expression and phenotypic traits. Instrumental SNPs were selected using the suggestive association threshold (P < 4.42 × 10−6), pruned for linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.2), and further filtered by HEIDI-outlier analysis to reduce bias from horizontal pleiotropy. Forward and reverse GSMR analyses were both conducted to evaluate the directionality of these putative causal effects.

Transcriptome-wide association analysis

To obtain a more refined set of putative causal genes, we applied GIFT (gene-based integrative fine-mapping through conditional transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS)) (Liu et al., 2024), which integrates multi-tissue eQTL data with GWAS summary statistics for fine-mapping of complex traits. When evaluating the association between genetically predicted expression (GReX) of a target gene and a trait, GIFT jointly accounts for the GReX of other genes within the same locus and explicitly models both gene expression correlation and linkage disequilibrium (LD) among cis-SNPs. By conditioning local genetically regulated expression signals, this framework reduces spurious marginal associations and improves the resolution of candidate causal gene prioritization. For each gene within a given genomic region, cis-SNPs with nominal eQTL association P < 1 × 10−7 were first extracted from the eQTL summary statistics. For each GWAS summary dataset, SNPs showing genome-wide significant or suggestive associations with the trait were selected. Overlapping cis-SNPs between the eQTL and GWAS datasets were used to construct the SNP-weight matrix, GWAS effect estimates (beta) and corresponding standard errors (SE). Simultaneously, the local LD matrix was calculated from the individual-level genotype data. The SNP weights, GWAS summary statistics, and LD matrix were jointly provided to the GIFT model to perform conditional TWAS analysis and prioritize candidate causal genes associated with the traits. Statistical significance was defined using a nominal threshold of P < 0.05.

Trait–trait colocalization analysis using HyPrColoc

To determine whether the association signals observed for different fat deposition-related traits within the same genomic region were likely driven by a shared underlying causal variant, we performed a trait–trait colocalization analysis for significant or suggestive signals prioritized by MTAG. Colocalization analysis was performed using the R package HyPrColoc (Foley et al., 2021), a Bayesian multi-trait colocalization framework that evaluates whether multiple traits in a genomic region are consistent with a shared causal variant and, if so, prioritizes the variants contributing most strongly to the shared association signal.

For each candidate association signal prioritized by MTAG, the most significant variant in the corresponding result was treated as the lead variant, and a candidate colocalization interval was defined by extending 200 kb upstream and downstream of this position. For all available SNPs within each interval, effect estimates and corresponding standard errors across traits were extracted to construct matrices of regression coefficients and standard errors, which were then provided to the HyPrColoc model for a joint inference. The posterior probability (PP) was used to quantify the strength of the colocalization evidence. Regions with PP > 0.5 were considered to show evidence of colocalization, with 0.5 < PP ≤ 0.7 interpreted as moderate evidence and PP > 0.7 as strong evidence. For loci supported by colocalization, the corresponding trait combination, candidate SNP, and proportion of posterior colocalization probability explained by that SNP were recorded. Variants accounting for at least 50% of the shared association signal were considered prioritized and shared candidate variants.

Trait–eQTL colocalization analysis using HyPrColoc

To further resolve the putative functional genes and tissue contexts underlying shared genetic signals, we next performed trait–eQTL colocalization analysis based on loci supported by trait–trait colocalization. This analysis was used HyPrColoc (Foley et al., 2021) to jointly model trait association and tissue-specific eQTL summary statistics within the same candidate region and evaluate whether the two signals were consistent with a shared underlying causal variant (Koskeridis et al., 2024).

Candidate genes selected for trait–eQTL colocalization were derived from three sources. The first category comprised genes identified by SMR as being associated with putative causal variants within previously detected trait–trait co-localized regions. The second category comprised candidate genes jointly supported by both SMR and GIFT across trait–tissue combinations in the same way. The third category comprised genes further identified by SMR at loci lying at the intersection between significant or suggestive MTAG signals and candidate loci from the trait–trait colocalization analysis.

For these candidate genes, summary statistics from trait-association analyses and cis-eQTL mapping across the 10 tissues were integrated. For each candidate region, SNP-level effect estimates and corresponding standard errors were extracted for both the trait and gene-expression signals, and matrices of effect estimates and standard errors were constructed for HyPrColoc analysis using the same inferential framework as in the trait–trait colocalization analysis. A high posterior colocalization probability between a trait and the eQTL signal of a specific gene in a given tissue was interpreted as evidence that the genetic association with the trait and the regulation of gene expression in that tissue may share a common genetic basis. By combining trait–trait and trait–eQTL colocalization results, we further refined the prioritization of candidate loci and genes and improved the interpretability of downstream functional analyses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for all phenotypic traits were calculated in R (v4.4.1). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and traits that deviated from normality (P ≤ 0.05) were subjected to a rank-based inverse normal transformation.

Results

Shared genetic signals across abdominal fat, egg yolk, and liver lipid traits

Phenotypic characterization of several fat deposition-related traits in this same population of 248 laying hens, including the primary biochemical measurements, has been reported in our previous study (Han et al., 2025). Here, we reanalyzed and expanded this dataset by incorporating additional derived indicators and performing cross-trait genetic analyses to investigate shared genetic signals among abdominal fat, egg yolk, and liver lipid traits. Descriptive statistics and sample sizes for all 22 analyzed phenotypes are provided in Supplementary Table S1. In brief, the analyzed traits showed substantial heterogeneity in variability. Abdominal fat-related traits were highly variable, as illustrated by AFW (mean ± SD, 90.17 ± 43.62 g; CV = 48.38%) and AFP (4.22 ± 1.64%; CV = 38.89%). Egg yolk-related traits showed comparatively lower variability, including EYW (17.32 ± 1.87 g; CV = 10.81%) and EYTG (44.81 ± 6.05 μmol/g; CV = 13.49%). Liver lipid-related traits showed heterogeneous variation, with LTG displaying high variability (30.73 ± 16.26 μmol/g; CV = 52.92%) and LTC showing moderate variation (4.41 ± 1.67 μmol/g; CV = 37.77%). By contrast, body weight at 100 weeks was relatively stable (BW100, 2075.26 ± 258.17 g; CV = 12.44%). Overall, abdominal fat- and liver-related traits generally showed greater variability than body weight and most egg yolk-related traits. For clarity, the analyzed phenotypes were grouped into abdominal fat (AF)-, egg yolk (EY)-, liver (L)-, and body weight (BW)-related trait categories. To assess the extent of shared genetic architecture, we first examined the overlap among suggestively significant GWAS loci and their annotated genes (Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Table S2). Shared signals were observed between BW100 and mBW100 and among abdominal fat-related traits, particularly AFDM, AFP and AFW. Egg yolk-related traits also shared a subset of loci, especially EYDM and EYW, with additional overlap with EYDMP. In contrast, liver-related traits showed more limited overlap (Fig. 1A). Overall, locus sharing was more common within trait groups than across trait categories, indicating both shared genetic effects among biologically related traits and substantial trait specificity. Annotation of overlapping loci highlighted recurrent candidate genes within each trait group (Fig. 1B). Shared abdominal fat-associated loci were linked to BAK1, APOBEC2, SAP30BP, OARD1, and NFYA, whereas shared yolk-associated loci were implicated in ARAP3, C16orf87, DLX4, GPT2, and SHCBP1. Overlapping loci involving liver- and body weight-related traits were annotated to FBN1, JAK1, PDE4B, NECTIN1, and RNF26 (Fig. 1B). Together, these results indicate substantial heterogeneity in the shared genetic architecture of fat deposition-related traits in laying hens during the extended laying period, with pronounced signal sharing among biologically related traits but limited overlap across trait categories.

SMR and GIFT analyses prioritize candidate genes for fat deposition-related traits

To identify candidate genes robustly associated with fat deposition-related traits, we jointly evaluated multi-tissue cis-eQTL-based SMR and GIFT results. After filtering, SMR identified 518 unique candidate genes across 22 traits, whereas GIFT identified 54 unique candidate genes across 11 traits (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Matching the two approaches at the trait–tissue–gene level yielded 52 unique candidate genes supported by both methods, spanning 38 trait–tissue combinations (Fig. 2A, B). Overall, these overlapping signals showed marked trait and tissue specificity. Traits such as EYFFA, LFFA, AFDMP, EYDM, LDM, and LTG harbored jointly supported candidate genes across multiple tissues (Fig. 2A). At the gene level, FREM2, GPT2, EXOC4, NET1, ARHGAP24, and SEC31A were identified across multiple tissues, whereas VPS35, HSD17B13, SCD5, SGIP1, and ADAM10 were detected primarily in specific trait–tissue combinations (Fig. 2B). Notably, GPT2 was implicated in association with both EYDM and EYW. Together, these integrated analyses prioritized a set of comparatively high-confidence candidate genes for downstream functional investigation.

Multi-trait association and colocalization analyses reveal shared loci underlying fat deposition-related traits

To further dissect the shared genetic basis of fat deposition-related traits, we first performed extensive pairwise multi-trait analysis of GWAS (MTAG) across 22 traits, and compared the distribution of suggestively significant signals obtained from single-trait GWAS and bivariate MTAG analyses (Fig. 3A, B; Supplementary Tables S2 and S5). Single-trait GWAS identified suggestively significant associations across multiple chromosomal regions, but these signals were relatively dispersed. In contrast, bivariate MTAG detected a larger number of suggestively significant associations across multiple regions, with noticeably increased signal strength at several loci, consistent with the improved power gained by leveraging the genetic correlation between traits. Across all pairwise MTAG analyses, we identified 5,461 unique SNPs associated with 20 fat deposition-related traits at the suggestive significance threshold (P < 4.42 × 10−6), with rs73069394 showing the strongest association signal for AFDM (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Table S5).

To identify shared genetic loci underlying different fat deposition-related traits, we performed colocalization analysis using HyPrColoc in ±200 kb windows centered on MTAG lead signals. Using posterior probability (PP) > 0.5 as evidence for colocalization, we identified multiple shared association regions across abdominal fat-related (AF), egg yolk-related (EY), liver-related (L) and body weight-related (BW) traits (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table S6). Overall, EY-related traits harboured a relatively large number of shared candidate loci, and evidence consistent with colocalization was observed between AF and EY traits. In total, 12 candidate loci showed colocalization evidence between AF- and EY-related traits (PP > 0.5; Fig. 3C), indicating that abdominal fat deposition and yolk deposition are partly influenced by shared genetic determinants. Among the loci showing colocalization between AF- and EY-related traits, 2__74614272 showed moderate colocalization evidence suggestive of a shared signal. The corresponding colocalization cluster included AFDM, AFP, AFW, and EYDM, with a posterior probability of 0.60, a regional probability of 0.83, and the lead variant explaining 67.5% of the shared association signal (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table S6). These findings identified 2__74614272 as a shared candidate locus between abdominal fat- and egg yolk-related traits.

Shared regulatory genes underlying abdominal fat and egg yolk traits

Trait–eQTL colocalization identifies shared candidate regulatory genes

Building on the trait–trait colocalization results, we integrated multi-tissue cis-eQTL data to identify candidate regulatory genes underlying shared genetic signals between abdominal fat-related (AF) and egg yolk-related (EY) traits. Candidate genes included in the trait–eQTL colocalization analysis were derived from three sources: genes identified by SMR within trait–trait colocalized regions, genes jointly supported by SMR and GIFT, and locus-related genes further prioritized by SMR from SNPs jointly supported by MTAG significant or suggestive signals across multiple trait categories and by trait–trait colocalization analysis.

At the AF–EY shared colocalization region represented by 2__74614272, trait–eQTL colocalization implicated multiple AF- and EY-related traits, including AFDM, AFP, AFW, EYDM, and EYW, across several tissues, particularly the pituitary, duodenum, ileum, cecum, and ovary (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S7). The associated signals were not explained by a single variant alone, but rather by a candidate SNP set including 2__74614272, rs318039150, and rs740364459, which collectively prioritized genes such as CDH18, ZNF622, MYO10, BASP1, ENSGALG00010000940, and ENSGALG00010000945 (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S7). These results identified a broader AF–EY association interval containing multiple candidate variants and genes.

Extending the analysis to candidate genes jointly supported by SMR and GIFT across different fat deposition-related trait–tissue combinations identified several regulators with convergent support from trait–eQTL colocalization analysis (Fig. 4B, C; Supplementary Table S8). Several genes colocalized with multiple AF- and EY-related traits in specific tissues, providing evidence consistent with shared regulatory effects across related phenotypes. Notably, rs741038415 accounted for the colocalization between AFDM, AFP, AFW, EYDM, and EYW and the expression of GPT2 (PP = 0.56; SNP-explained PP = 100%) and VPS35 (PP = 0.59; SNP-explained PP = 100%) in the ileum (Supplementary Table S8). This variant was negatively associated with AFDM, AFP and AFW, but positively associated with EYDM and EYW (Fig. 4D), while also showing positive effects on GPT2 (Beta = 0.31, P_eQTL = 3.01 × 10−8) and VPS35 (Beta = 0.37, P_eQTL = 6.99 × 10−8) expression (Fig. 4E). In addition, several prioritized shared variants showed a directional pattern suggestive of divergent effects on abdominal fat and yolk deposition. In the MTAG results, 2__74614272, rs318039150, and rs741038415 all showed positive effects on EYW, whereas their effects on AF-related traits (AFW, AFP, and AFDM) were negative, although these AF-related associations were not statistically significant (Fig. 4D). This pattern indicates opposite effect tendencies between abdominal fat- and egg yolk-related traits at several shared candidate loci. Further integration with fastGWA-based SNP–gene association results showed that these variants were also significantly associated with expression of candidate genes in specific tissues (Fig. 4E). Specifically, rs318039150 showed negative associations with pituitary expression of ZNF622 and ENSGALG00010000940. Likewise, rs740364459 showed positive associations with MYO10, BASP1, and ENSGALG00010000945 expression, but negative associations with CDH18 expression. Together, these results support a coherent regulatory pattern in which prioritized shared AF–EY variants are linked both to tissue-specific expression of candidate genes and to coordinated, yet potentially divergent, effects on abdominal fat and egg yolk phenotypes. Accordingly, GPT2, VPS35, MYO10, BASP1, ZNF622, and CDH18, together with their corresponding candidate SNPs, represent prioritized regulatory signals for further functional investigation.

We next focused on a third class of candidate genes derived from SNPs jointly supported by MTAG significant or suggestive signals shared across AF-, EY-, and l-related traits and by trait–trait colocalization analysis. By integrating phenotype-level GWAS and MTAG signals with multi-tissue cis-eQTL data through SMR, we identified 11 locus-related candidate genes, including ADAM10, ALDH1A2, ENSGALG00010025076, ENSGALG00010025077, ENSGALG00010025217, FAM81A, LRRC49, MYO1E, POLR2M, TARSL2 and TM2D3. We then performed trait–eQTL colocalization analysis for these candidates and further prioritized loci supported simultaneously by three criteria: MTAG significant or suggestive association, trait–trait colocalization, and trait–eQTL colocalization. This integrative filtering identified three key candidate loci, rs735740491, 10__6789287 and 10__6979889. Trait–eQTL colocalization analysis showed that candidate variants at these three loci could explain the colocalization of AF-related traits, EY-related traits, and tissue-specific expression of multiple genes, particularly ADAM10, ALDH1A2, ENSGALG00010025076, ENSGALG00010025217, MYO1E, and POLR2M (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S1A; Supplementary Table S9). Overall, the posterior colocalization probabilities for these genes were generally more concentrated among EY-related traits (PP range, 0.49–0.98; median, 0.90), whereas the corresponding probabilities for AF-related traits were comparatively more dispersed (PP range, 0.43–0.90; median, 0.56).

Among the three prioritized loci, 10__6979889 was the only variant showing suggestive associations across both AF- and EY-related traits in the MTAG analyses, with positive associations with AFDMP, LTC, and BW100, but negative associations with AFMC and EYDMP (Fig. 5B). In contrast, 10__6789287 and rs735740491 were driven mainly by EYTG-centered signals. At the expression level, candidate SNPs showed concordant positive effects on ADAM10 and ENSGALG00010025076 expression across multiple tissues, whereas concordant negative effects were observed for ALDH1A2 in intestinal tissues and ENSGALG00010025217 mainly in the hypothalamus and pituitary. MYO1E showed concordant SNP–gene effects in a more limited tissue context, while POLR2M was supported primarily by colocalization (Fig. 5C). Overall, trait–eQTL colocalization revealed tissue-dependent regulatory signals across AF- and EY-related phenotypes.

Bidirectional GSMR prioritizes a subset of colocalization-supported candidates

To further refine the colocalization-supported candidates, we next investigated the putative causal associations between candidate gene expression and fat deposition-related traits using bidirectional GSMR analyses (Fig. 5D, E; Supplementary Fig. S1B, C). Among the first two candidate-gene classes, bidirectionally significant positive associations were observed for BASP1 in the cecum with EYW and for ENSGALG00010000945 in the ovary with both EYW and EYDM (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Fig. S1B). In contrast, MYO10, CDH18, and ZNF622 were retained mainly through direction-specific GSMR support. For example, ileal MYO10 was positively associated with EYW and EYDM, whereas cecal MYO10 was negatively associated with AFW. In the cecum, CDH18 showed a negative association with EYDM, and EYW likewise showed a negative association with cecal CDH18. ZNF622 in the pituitary was negatively associated with EYDM (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Fig. S1B). These GSMR-supported patterns were broadly consistent with coherent SNP–gene–trait regulatory relationships for the first two candidate-gene classes, providing convergent support for their potential roles in AF–EY-related regulation. For the third candidate gene class, ADAM10, ALDH1A2, ENSGALG00010025076, ENSGALG00010025217, and MYO1E showed colocalization-supported GSMR associations with both AF- and EY-related traits within at least one tissue (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S1C). In the liver, ADAM10 showed bidirectionally significant positive associations with AFDMP and BW100, together with positive associations with EYTG and LTC. In the duodenum, ALDH1A2 showed bidirectionally significant negative associations with AFDMP and a negative association with EYTG. MYO1E in the duodenum also showed bidirectionally significant negative associations with AFDMP and BW100, together with negative associations with EYDM and EYTG. ENSGALG00010025076 in the pancreas showed positive associations with AFDMP and EYTG. In the pituitary, ENSGALG00010025217 showed bidirectionally significant negative associations with AFDMP and an additional negative association with EYDM (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Fig. S1C). Unlike the first two candidate-gene classes, these third-class candidates more often showed significant associations with both AF- and EY-related traits in the same overall direction.

Discussion

Our integrative analyses revealed that fat deposition-related phenotypes in laying hens during the extended laying period are shaped by tissue-dependent regulatory networks and a limited set of shared cross-trait signals. In particular, the present results suggest two complementary patterns underlying the relationship between abdominal fat and yolk lipid traits. One pattern is consistent with a potential divergent tendency, in which some shared loci may contribute to increased yolk deposition while tending to reduce abdominal fat accumulation. The other pattern reflects a broader shared regulatory network, in which shared loci and candidate causal genes are associated with multiple lipid-related phenotypes in the same overall direction. This distinction helps reconcile why some AF–EY candidate signals showed opposite SNP–trait tendencies, whereas others were more consistent with coordinated regulation of lipid deposition state, compositional traits, or metabolic balance across tissues.

A first major implication of our results is that abdominal fat-, egg yolk-, and liver-related traits are biologically connected yet genetically heterogeneous. Traits measured within the same tissue shared more suggestive loci than those measured across tissues, indicating that different indices within a tissue often capture related aspects of the same biological process. Previous studies have shown that abdominal fat deposition in chickens has a clear genetic basis and is regulated by multiple genomic factors, whereas laying performance-related traits also display substantial genetic variation and age-dependent dynamics (Abdalla et al., 2018; Ni et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024). In hens, abdominal fat reflects whole-body energy surplus and lipid storage (van Eck et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024), whereas yolk lipid deposition depends largely on hepatic lipogenesis and the transport of yolk precursors such as VLDL and vitellogenin to developing follicles (Cui et al., 2020; Song et al., 2023). Accordingly, these traits share a common basis in lipid supply while remaining subject to distinct tissue-level regulation. Previous work has also shown that aged laying hens undergo age-related changes in lipid metabolism, hepatic function, and body composition (Gu et al., 2021; van Eck et al., 2023; Lv et al., 2025). In this context, the more limited locus overlap observed among liver-related traits may reflect the greater physiological plasticity of hepatic lipid metabolism in aged laying hens.

Furthermore, we found that the AF–EY shared region encompassing 2__74614272, rs318039150, rs740364459, and rs741038415 was particularly informative, supporting the presence of at least partially shared upstream genetic architecture. This is physiologically reasonable, as both abdominal fat deposition and yolk lipid deposition are closely linked to lipid synthesis, transport, and partitioning, despite serving different biological endpoints (Alvarenga et al., 2011; Nematbakhsh et al., 2021; van Eck et al., 2023). These variants tended to show positive effects on EYW or other EY-related traits, whereas their effects on AF-related traits were generally negative, although the AF-side associations did not always reach statistical significance. In parallel, multi-tissue colocalization and fastGWA analyses linked this region to candidate genes such as GPT2, VPS35, MYO10, BASP1, ZNF622, and CDH18 in the ileum, cecum, pituitary, and ovary. Bidirectional GSMR further showed that several of these candidates, especially BASP1, ENSGALG00010000945, and MYO10, were directionally consistent with the corresponding SNP–trait and SNP–gene associations. MYO10 encodes an unconventional myosin involved in cytoskeletal dynamics and membrane-associated transport (Mayca Pozo et al., 2021), whereas BASP1 is a membrane-associated signaling and transcriptional regulatory protein with lipid-binding properties (Loats et al., 2021). A chicken regulatory-element atlas reported that MYO10 and CDH18 were represented by intestine-shared enhancer regions enriched for selection sweeps between modern broilers and layers (Pan et al., 2023). This provides supportive regulatory context for the intestinal signals involving MYO10 and CDH18 observed in the present study. In broilers, genistein supplementation was reported to reduce abdominal fat percentage and upregulate MYO10, MYO1E, and other myosin-associated genes, although these genes were mainly interpreted in relation to muscle and growth development rather than direct fat deposition regulation (Lv et al., 2018). BASP1 has been studied in chicken embryonic neural development (Mac Donald and Iulianella, 2022). ZNF622 has been linked to FLHS-associated hepatic epigenomic changes in chickens (Zhu et al., 2021), providing indirect evidence for its relevance to hepatic lipid-related regulation. GPT2 and VPS35 are also biologically plausible candidates, given their known links to amino acid and energy metabolism (Martino et al., 2022) and intracellular trafficking (Yang et al., 2016), respectively. Previous chicken transcriptomic studies have linked GPT2 to amino acid and energy metabolic responses, including hepatic responses to heat stress (Kim et al., 2022), adaptation to low-energy diets (Jehl et al., 2019), and abdominal fat-related expression changes in male chickens (Duan et al., 2013). However, direct functional evidence linking these prioritized genes to abdominal fat deposition or yolk lipid deposition in chickens remains limited. Together, these findings suggest that at least a subset of AF–EY shared loci may participate in regulatory relationships consistent with a potential divergent tendency between abdominal fat and yolk traits, although the current evidence remains inferential rather than definitive. In this sense, our data do not demonstrate a simple “lean abdomen–lipid-rich yolk” axis, but they do identify a set of candidate loci and genes compatible with that biological possibility.

By contrast, the third candidate-gene class, centered on 10__6979889, 10__6789287, and rs735740491, supports a different interpretation. Although these loci were jointly supported by MTAG, trait–trait colocalization, trait–eQTL colocalization, and, for a subset of genes, bidirectional GSMR, their downstream effects did not mainly follow an AF-negative/EY-positive pattern. Instead, genes such as ADAM10, ALDH1A2, ENSGALG00010025076, ENSGALG00010025217, and MYO1E were more often associated with AF- and EY-related phenotypes in the same overall direction within specific tissues. This pattern suggests that these loci are more likely to contribute to the shared regulation of lipid-related phenotypes, including lipid deposition state, dry matter proportion, or broader metabolic coordination, rather than to a potential divergent tendency between AF and EY. Among these candidates, ADAM10 and ALDH1A2 may point to inflammatory pathways (Marino et al., 2024) and retinoic-acid-related regulatory processes (Feng et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2022), respectively, whereas MYO1E participates in actin-dependent membrane trafficking and cargo transport (Cheng et al., 2012). ADAM10 has appeared in chicken preadipocyte miRNA-target analyses involving abdominal-fat-divergent broiler lines (Wang et al., 2015), whereas MYO1E has been implicated in chicken intestinal or myogenic transcriptomic contexts (Xiao et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022). In addition, ALDH1A2 has been reported to be differentially expressed in abdominal fat between genetically fat and lean chickens (Resnyk et al., 2015), providing associative evidence for its potential relevance to fat deposition. Nevertheless, their roles in abdominal fat or yolk lipid deposition remain to be functionally validated. In the present study, 10__6979889 showed mixed MTAG effect directions across AFDMP, AFMC, EYDMP, LTC, and BW100, whereas candidate genes in this region, including ADAM10, ALDH1A2, and MYO1E, showed more coordinated associations with AF- and EY-related traits. This pattern suggests multidimensional effects on lipid-related composition and physiology rather than a single directional shift in fat partitioning. Thus, our results suggest that shared loci between AF and EY traits do not correspond to a single biological meaning. Some loci may be relevant to potential trait divergence, as illustrated by intestinal signals involving MYO10, CDH18, GPT2, and VPS35, whereas others may underlie broader shared regulatory networks, as suggested by more directionally consistent signals involving liver ADAM10 and duodenal ALDH1A2 and MYO1E. Differences in trait definition may also contribute, because the analyzed phenotypes included tissue weight, dry matter mass, moisture mass, percentage-based traits, and biochemical lipid measurements. Although some direction differences may be influenced by suggestive signals, moderate colocalization probabilities, correlated or derived phenotypes, and uncertainty in MTAG effect estimates, these loci and genes were prioritized through multiple complementary analyses and represent candidate signals for future validation. Because this study was based on a single sampling age of 100 weeks, future age-gradient studies are needed to assess whether these shared loci and regulatory genes exert stable or age-dependent effects.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically revealed the shared regulatory signals underlying fat deposition-related traits in laying hens during the extended laying period. The findings suggest that some shared loci may contribute to a potential divergent tendency between abdominal fat and egg yolk traits, whereas others more likely reflect broader cross-tissue regulation of lipid-related phenotypes. In addition, we prioritized several shared candidate causal genes, including MYO10, GPT2, VPS35, ADAM10, ALDH1A2, and MYO1E. These results provide important insights into the complex shared molecular basis linking abdominal fat deposition and yolk lipid deposition, and offer a valuable foundation for future mechanistic studies and molecular breeding in laying hens.
