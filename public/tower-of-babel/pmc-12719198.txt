Active fluctuations of cytoplasmic actomyosin networks facilitate dynein-driven transport
Authors: Takayuki Torisawa, Kei Saito, Ken’ya Furuta, Akatsuki Kimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryInside cells, molecular motors transport cargo through a highly crowded cytoplasmic environment. While such an environment is assumed to hinder transport, its precise effect remains unclear. Here, we investigated how the dynamics of cytoplasmic environments affect dynein-driven transport in C. elegans early embryos. In living embryos, we found that an artificial dynein-cargo complex exhibited significantly faster transport than in vitro, indicating an active acceleration mechanism in vivo. By altering the activity of actomyosin networks, we found that dynein-driven transport was accelerated by actomyosin-driven cytoplasmic fluctuations, with speed increasing upon myosin upregulation and decreasing upon its depletion. Furthermore, in vitro force measurements of dynein suggest that the asymmetric force response to random forces, generated by fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks, may contribute to acceleration. This study provides insights into a regulatory mechanism of molecular motors within fluctuating cytoplasm, harnessing cytoplasmic fluctuations to enhance transport efficiency in a highly crowded environment.

Introduction

Intracellular transport mediated by molecular motors is essential for various cellular processes, including neuronal development, organelle positioning, and cellular homeostasis. Microtubule-based motors, such as dynein and kinesins,1,2 are involved in the transport and positioning of diverse cargoes, including endosomal organelles,3,4,5 mitochondria,6 and Golgi.7 Beyond cargo delivery, motor-driven intracellular transport contributes to positioning large cellular structures, such as the nucleus and spindle, through mutual reaction force arising from vesicle transport in the cytoplasm.8,9,10

The cytoplasm, where intracellular transport occurs, is a highly crowded environment densely packed with macromolecules. Intuitively, this molecular crowding is expected to slow down transport speed due to increased viscous resistance.11,12,13,14,15 In contrast, the transport speed from in vitro studies is often slower than the speed obtained from in vivo studies.16,17,18 In the case of dynein-dependent early endosome transport, the transport achieves a mean speed of 2–4 μm/s in mammalian epithelial cells and the maximum speed up to even higher than 5 μm/s,19,20 whereas the mean transport speed obtained from in vitro studies was typically up to 1.2 μm/s even in the transports driven by multiple dynein motors.21,22,23,24 These observations suggest an uncharacterized property of cytoplasm that enhances intracellular transport.

The cytoplasm is also known as an active matter, which involves energy-consuming processes generating internal random forces that produce non-equilibrium fluctuations. Such processes include metabolism25 and dynamics of cytoskeletal networks,26,27 which together influence the mechanical and rheological properties of intracellular environments.26,28 Since all the active processes share the cytoplasm as their working space, the forces generated by one process can affect other processes. Previous in vitro studies using the optical tweezer have revealed that external force can change the dissociation kinetics of microtubule-based motors.29,30,31,32 Thus, the environmental forces from other processes would affect the motors’ activity. In addition, a recent study has also shown that the artificially generated non-Gaussian fluctuation, designed to mimic active intracellular non-thermal fluctuations, can enhance kinesin motility.33 However, although previous studies have suggested that environmental forces can regulate motor-driven transport, the effects of the actively fluctuating forces on in vivo transport remain elusive.

This study aimed to reveal the role of fluctuating dynamics in the cytoplasm on motor-driven transport in vivo, focusing on the effects of actomyosin dynamics in the cytoplasm. Actomyosin networks play a key role in various cellular dynamics. In the cell cortex, they generate contractile forces, which drive the morphological dynamics of cells.34,35 In addition, they also form networks in the cytoplasm.36,37,38,39 These actomyosin networks contribute to the directed motion of several cellular components, such as the cell nucleus and chromosomes.34,35,36,37,40,41 At the same time, they generate active random forces within the cytoplasm, influencing the physical properties of the cytoplasm.26

In this study, we used dynein-driven transport in C. elegans early embryos as our model system. We found that the dynein-cargo complex moved more rapidly in vivo than when observed in vitro by directly comparing the motility of an artificial motor-cargo complex, which contains a minimum truncated construct of dynein, between in vitro and in vivo. This result suggests that the faster transport in vivo is not induced by the regulatory proteins in a cell, but by other cellular factors. By modulating the fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks within the cytoplasm, we found that actomyosin dynamics positively regulate the speed of endogenous dynein-driven transport in C. elegans embryos. Finally, combined with the asymmetric force response of dynein measured in vitro, we propose that the random forces generated by cytoplasmic actomyosin networks contribute to faster microtubule-based transport by dynein.

Results

Dynein-driven transport in living embryos is faster than in vitro situations

As we mentioned in the introduction, the speed of motor-driven transport from in vitro studies is mostly slower than the speed obtained from in vivo studies. It should be noted that the previous observations are a mixture of in vitro and in vivo studies with various species, cell types, and cargo types, making it difficult to directly compare the motility in both situations. In this study, we directly compared the motility of dynein-driven transport between in vitro and in vivo, using C. elegans early embryo as a model system (Figure 1A). Using the gonad injection technique,42 we introduced the SNAP-tagged dynein conjugated to the benzylguanine-modified 100-nm beads (BG-beads), the size of which falls within the range of typical vesicles.43,44 To minimize the influence of endogenous C. elegans regulatory factors, which might modulate dynein motility,2,45,46,47,48 we used the truncated human dynein construct (hsDHC-1382k) (Figure S1A), which is a minimum motile construct including only the motor head and the linker domain.24,47,49,50 The injected dynein-bead complexes were successfully introduced into the embryos and showed unidirectional movements with an average speed of 0.69 ± 0.45 μm/s (mean ± SD) (Figures 1B, S1B, and S1C; Video S1).Figure 1Dynein-driven transport in living embryos is faster than in vitro situations(A) Schematic diagram of experiments for directly comparing the motility of the artificial motor-cargo complex between in vitro and in vivo. To ensure a direct comparison, we examined the in vitro motility using the same dynein-bead complexes prepared and used for the in vivo experiments. In a typical buffer environment (see STAR Methods), we found that the average speed of dynein-bead complexes was 0.29 ± 0.21 μm/s (mean ± SD), significantly slower than in vivo and comparable to those from previous in vitro studies24,47,49 (Figures 1C–1E). Since a single molecule of the truncated construct typically binds to a microtubule for around 1 s,24,50 the movements are considered to be driven by multiple motors in both in vitro and in vivo conditions. By considering the geometric constriction, we estimated that the maximum number of dynein molecules attached to a microtubule is around 10 (Figures S1D and S1E).

The clear difference of velocity between in vitro and in vivo situations suggests that some cytoplasmic factors caused the acceleration of dynein-driven transport, because the viscosity of the C. elegans embryo cytoplasm has been estimated in the previous studies to be 10.3 ± 0.8 × 102 cP51 and 6.3 ± 0.17 × 102 cP,52 which is several hundred to a thousand times greater than that of water or the buffer without crowding agents. Assuming Stokes’ law,53 such high viscosity should significantly reduce transport speed in vivo compared to in vitro buffer conditions. We also considered the possibility that, in cellular environments, dynein-cargo complexes interact with multiple microtubules, leading to an increase in the number of transport-mediating dynein and the faster transport. However, a previous study has shown that simply increasing the number of dynein molecules has only a limited effect on transport velocity.24 Therefore, the observed acceleration in vivo is likely due to cytoplasmic factors that enhance the transport. We also confirmed that the acceleration was not caused by endogenous motors, which were non-specifically attached to the beads, since the beads without dynein conjugation did not show unidirectional movement but instead exhibited diffusive motion (Figures S1C, S1F, and S1G), indicating that the beads-conjugated hsDHC-1382k caused the unidirectional movements. We hypothesized that the non-thermal fluctuations generating random forces in the cytoplasm, driven by energy-consuming cellular processes, facilitated faster in vivo transport. While several processes could contribute to generating random forces in the cytoplasm, we focused on the forces generated by the fluctuating dynamics of cytoplasmic actomyosin networks, which is mainly driven by the force generation of non-muscle myosin proteins.26

Early endosome transport in C. elegans embryos as a model system of in vivo transport exclusively driven by cytoplasmic dynein

To examine whether fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin facilitate dynein-driven transport, we analyzed the transport in vivo under the perturbations on actomyosin dynamics. Using 1-cell stage C. elegans embryos, we first characterized the transport of early endosomes, one of the main cargoes of cytoplasmic dynein2,3, in the unperturbed condition (Figures 2A and 2B; Video S2). The mean transport speed of unidirectionally moving early endosomes was 0.81 ± 0.38 μm/s (mean ± SD; Figures 2C and 2D), consistent with that of the artificial dynein-cargo complex and previous studies.19,20 All early endosome transport events were directed toward the centrosomes, suggesting the transport was dynein-driven. We further examined the dominant contribution of dynein by depleting it using the auxin-induced degron (AID) system (Figures S2A–S2C).54,55,56 We observed almost no unidirectional early endosome transport in the dynein-depleted embryo. During the analysis, we found the asymmetries in transport speed and fluctuations of actomyosin networks. The transport speed of early endosomes on the posterior side was significantly higher than that on the anterior side (Figure 2E), and fluctuations of actin networks showed a similar asymmetry (Figure 2F), suggesting that cytoplasmic random forces, originating from fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks, facilitated dynein-driven transport.

However, we also observed an asymmetry in actin density between the anterior and posterior regions (Figures 2G and 2H). Since actin density can influence the drag force acting on the dynein-cargo complex, this asymmetry might also contribute to the difference in transport speed. In addition to these two factors, we observed an asymmetry in the fluorescent intensity of early endosomes (Figure 2I). Assuming that the size of early endosomes correlates with the intensity of surface-bound GFP::RAB-5, we hypothesized that the cargo size differed between the anterior and posterior regions. Such a size difference could generate a difference in drag force, contributing to the asymmetry of transport speed.

Taken together, while the asymmetry of transport speed was consistent with the degree of fluctuation of actomyosin, the above uncertainties remain. Thus, we analyzed the dynein-driven transport under the perturbations to actomyosin activity.

Speed of dynein-driven intracellular transport depends on fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks in the cytoplasm

For the downregulation, we reduced NMY-2 levels by RNAi, expecting it to weaken forces driving fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks. In nmy-2 (RNAi) embryos, where NMY-2 levels were reduced to approximately 24% (Figure S3A), the transport speed of early endosomes decreased to less than 70% (0.52 ± 0.32 μm/s; Figures 3B, 3C, and 3F; Video S3). We also observed the reduction of transport speed in the presence of a myosin II inhibitor, blebbistatin (Figure S3B). These results suggest that actomyosin activity correlated with the speed of dynein-driven transport. Since our observation using the degradation system of dynein showed that unidirectional movements of early endosomes were almost completely abolished in the absence of dynein (Figure S2C), the results above further suggest that actomyosin regulates transport activity indirectly. Additionally, the results from the degradation experiments also suggest that transport-mediating processive myosins do not contribute to or interfere with microtubule-based transport in C. elegans early embryos, unlike the cases reported in previous studies using cultured cells.60,61

We then examined how upregulating actomyosin activity affected the transport. We introduced the purified NMY-2 complex into the embryo by injecting the protein into the gonad (Figures 3A and S3C). In NMY-2 injected embryos, the transport speed of early endosomes was faster than that in the unperturbed embryos (1.21 ± 0.50 μm/s, mean ± SD; Figures 3D–3F; Video S3). These results suggest that the actomyosin dynamics influence the activity of dynein-driven transport. However, we also found that the perturbations altered the size of early endosomes, which can affect the drag force on dynein-cargo complexes, assuming that early endosome size correlates with the fluorescent intensity of surface-attached RAB-5 (Figures S3D and S3E). To examine the possibility that the change of endosome sizes caused the changes in transport speed, we compared the speed of early endosomes with similar sizes. We confirmed that the transport speed decreased upon NMY-2 depletion and increased upon NMY-2 injection, even among early endosomes of similar sizes (Figure S3F).

We then analyzed how the perturbations changed the fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks. For this purpose, we evaluated the movement of the actomyosin network by optical flow analysis (Figures 3G and S3G). The mean flow of actin signal in the cytoplasm significantly decreased in nmy-2 (RNAi) embryos and increased in NMY-2 injected embryos (Figure 3H), indicating a positive correlation between the degree of actin fluctuation and the speed of dynein-driven transport.

Furthermore, we examined whether our perturbations altered the structure of actomyosin networks, which could influence the drag force on transport and affect transport speed. To address this, we quantified the length scale of actin signal density in the cytoplasm by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV)62 of actin intensity and analyzing its dependency on the window size used for the calculation (Figure S3H). The dependency of CV on the window size is expected to reflect the length scale of actin networks. When the window size is smaller than the length scale of signal density, the CV increases with the window size. In contrast, when the window size becomes larger than the length scale, the CV will approach a plateau. Consistently, we found that the CV of actin intensity increased with the window size and reached ∼0.99 at around 3.5 μm, which was comparable to the length scale of the large holes of networks observed in the confocal images (Figures S3I and S3J). This result suggests that the CV analysis captures the structural characteristics of the actin networks in the cytoplasm. To further characterize the CV curve, we calculated the characteristic length from the one-phase exponential fitting and found that the value was 0.58 μm. When we performed the same analysis on the shuffled images, the characteristic length was reduced to ∼0.2 μm, consistent with the resolution of our imaging system (∼0.14 μm/pixel). This again supports the validity of CV analysis. Finally, by comparing the characteristic lengths among the conditions, we found that the values did not substantially change upon the decrease or increase of NMY-2 level in the embryo (Figure S3K). These results suggest that the perturbations on NMY-2 have little effect on the network structure of actin in the cytoplasm.

Our results indicate that fluctuations of cytoplasmic actomyosin networks accelerate microtubule-based transport driven by endogenous full-length cytoplasmic dynein, as suggested by the observation using the truncated construct. Since the truncated construct lacks the tail region, it is expected not to bind regulatory factors such as dynactin, which produces the difference in motile behavior between the full-length and truncated constructs.24,50 Nevertheless, our finding that fluctuating dynamics in the cytoplasm accelerate the transport suggests that the acceleration by cytoplasmic factors reflects an intrinsic motile property of dynein, independent of the regulation by the accessory proteins. In the next section, to directly evaluate the effect of fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks on the movement of materials within the cytoplasm, we analyzed the movement of cargo-size particles introduced into the C. elegans embryo.

Random forces in the cytoplasm changed depending on the perturbations of myosin

To investigate how the fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks in the embryo evoke random forces in the cytoplasm, we analyzed how the movements of tracer particles in the cytoplasm are affected by the actomyosin dynamics (Figures 4A and 4B). We used PEG-coated fluorescent beads with a diameter of 100 nm as a tracer particle. The beads were introduced into the embryos through the gonad injection,42,51 under the control and the NMY-2 perturbed conditions (Figure S4A; Video S4). To examine the effect of perturbations on myosin, we calculated the mean squared displacement (MSD) of bead movements (Figure 4C). Bead diffusivity varied significantly depending on the embryonic NMY-2 level. In nmy-2 (RNAi) embryos, the diffusion coefficient (D) of beads decreased to approximately 53% of that of the control embryos (D = 5.0 ± 0.3 × 104 nm2/s for control and 2.7 ± 0.2 × 104 nm2/s for nmy-2 (RNAi), mean ± SEM). Note that we confirmed that the analyzed trajectories reflected the movements of single beads, not including the aggregated ones, by checking that the intensity distributions of beads have a single peak (Figure S4B). Conversely, in NMY-2 injected embryos, the bead diffusivity increased by approximately 70% (D = 8.6 ± 0.6 × 104 nm2/s). These results indicate a correlation between cytoplasmic fluctuating force and embryonic NMY-2 levels. By calculating the MSD exponents from the log-log plots, we found that the exponents were lower than 1 (see Figure S4C), suggesting the viscoelastic property of the cytoplasm.51,52,63,64 Additionally, we found that the exponents were not significantly different between the control and perturbed conditions (Figure S4C). Since the exponent reflects environmental confinement, we speculated that the actomyosin mesh structure remained unchanged upon the perturbations on NMY-2, consistent with the CV analysis of actin images (Figure S3I). We then hypothesized that the difference in bead diffusivity reflected the forces exerted by fluctuating actomyosin networks surrounding the beads, which is also suggested by a non-Gaussian characteristic of bead displacements (Figure S4D). To examine whether actomyosin-driven random forces in the cytoplasm directly accelerate transport or if the change in environmental viscosity is a crucial factor, we analyzed the movement of particles smaller than the characteristic size of actomyosin meshes. Using the smaller beads, we expect to reduce the direct influence of random forces generated by actomyosin networks and to evaluate viscosity changes upon the perturbations of myosin. Although there is no measurement of actomyosin mesh size in C. elegans embryos, several studies using other systems showed that the characteristic size was about 50 nm.65,66,67 We then analyzed the movements of 40 nm beads, expecting that the size was below the mesh size. By calculating the MSD plot of 40 nm beads, we found that the differences in the diffusivity of beads among the conditions were not prominent in the case of 40 nm (Figure 4C; D = 8.7 ± 0.6 × 104 nm2/s for control, 7.3 ± 0.5 × 104 nm2/s for nmy-2 (RNAi), and 10.1 ± 0.5 × 104 nm2/s for NMY-2 injected). These results suggest that environmental viscosity was not the dominant factor, at least on the short timescale of transport events (several seconds), but rather that the active forces driven by fluctuating actomyosin dynamics were critical for regulating transport. Furthermore, we found a positive correlation between the diffusivity of 100 nm diameter beads and the transport speeds of early endosomes (Figure 4D). This finding supports our hypothesis that the randomly fluctuating forces within the cytoplasm, partially driven by the dynamics of the actomyosin networks, influence dynein-driven transport.

Rectification of environmental fluctuations as a potential mechanism for intracellular transport acceleration

Our perturbation experiments revealed that suppressing actomyosin activity altered the fluctuations of actin networks in the cytoplasm and the cytoplasmic diffusivity measured by the motion of injected tracer beads, which is supposed to reflect the strength of cytoplasmic random forces (Figures 3H and 4C). These changes in diffusivity were positively correlated with the speed of dynein-driven transport (Figure 4D), suggesting that cytoplasmic random forces arising from actomyosin dynamics facilitate microtubule-based transport (Figure 5A). While NMY-2 functions as both a force generator and a crosslinker in actomyosin networks, our results suggest that the perturbations on myosin do not alter the confinement structure of actin networks, at least within the typical timescale of dynein-driven transport. This is supported by the CV analysis of actin intensity and the MSD exponents from the movements of injected beads, which showed no significant differences under NMY-2 perturbations (Figures S3I, S3J, and S4C). How could randomly fluctuating forces promote unidirectional transport? A possible answer can be found in a mechanistic insight reported by a previous study, which demonstrated that cytoplasmic dynein from S. cerevisiae exhibited an asymmetric force response: dynein detaches from a microtubule more easily under forward-directed load than under backward-directed load. The kinetic-based approach in the study showed that such asymmetry allows dynein to rectify randomly fluctuating forces into directional motion, functioning effectively as a mechanical ratchet. This model suggests that random cytoplasmic forces can become a source of directional bias for dynein-driven transport.

To test whether a similar mechanism works in our system, we directly examined the force response of human dynein used in our in vivo and in vitro comparison. Using the optical tweezer, we applied directional forces to hsDHC-1382k molecules bound to a bead when it interacts with a microtubule (Figures S5A and S6). In the experiments, we were concerned that, at low loading rates, the applied strain to dynein would not reach sufficient strength before detachment due to the rotational freedom of the trapped bead. To avoid this problem, we used beads with a diameter of 200 nm, smaller than those in the previous study, and pulled the beads at sufficiently high loading rates by rapidly moving them by 240 nm in ∼70 μs (Figures S5B and S5C). Consistent with the previous study using yeast dynein, human dynein displayed direction-dependent detachment behavior: detachment occurred more easily under forward-directed forces and was suppressed under backward-directed forces (Figures S5D and S5E). Moreover, strong-binding events were more frequent during backward (plus-end-directed) movements (Figures S5F–S5H). These results indicate that the asymmetric force response is a conserved feature of dynein motors across species. The asymmetry in force response of dynein was most pronounced at larger external forces (∼6 pN). However, it was also detectable at weaker forces (∼1 pN), comparable to the active forces measured in human cells.68 In the cytoplasm, microtubules are mechanically coupled with actin networks.69 Thus, fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin can induce fluctuations of microtubules, acting as an origin of external force on dynein. According to the previous study, dynein harnesses active fluctuations of microtubules to achieve efficient transport.32 This raises the possibility that the in vivo acceleration of dynein-driven transport is mediated by random forces originating from actomyosin-induced microtubule dynamics. In the current study, we could not directly examine the correlation between actomyosin and microtubule dynamics. Simultaneous imaging of both cytoskeletal networks, coupled with correlation analysis, may provide further mechanistic insights into the in vivo acceleration of dynein-driven transport. Of course, it is also possible that actomyosin-driven random forces act directly on the dynein-cargo complex, or that other indirect pathways contribute to the transmission of random forces. Since these pathways are not necessarily mutually exclusive, transport regulation in vivo may result from a superposition of multiple mechanisms.

Nonetheless, we hypothesize that the in vivo acceleration of dynein-driven transport is fundamentally mediated by dynein’s asymmetric force response and the rectification of external fluctuations, regardless of the pathways through which fluctuating random forces act on motors (Figure 5B). In the model, forward-directed external forces promote more frequent detachments of dynein from microtubules, allowing the motor-cargo complex to drift forward and rebind, resulting in a net increase in transport speed.

Discussion

Intracellular transport driven by molecular motors occurs within the cytoplasm, where various force-generating processes occur simultaneously. However, the effect of these active processes on intracellular transport remains unclear. In this study, we utilized dynein-driven transport in C. elegans early embryos to demonstrate that fluctuating dynamics of actomyosin networks are one of the factors that enhance directed transport along microtubules. Based on the results of force measurement of dynein from previous studies of our own, we hypothesized that the rectification of external fluctuations would play a key role in facilitating the activity of dynein-driven transport in the fluctuating cytoplasmic environments.

Size-dependent effects of actomyosin fluctuations on transport

The acceleration mechanism by rectifying external force may work strongly when the molecular motor forms a complex bound to cargo. Our experiments with injected beads revealed that the effects of NMY-2 perturbations depended on the size of the beads. Beads with a 40 nm diameter, comparable in size to full-length dynein,70 showed smaller changes in MSD plots in response to the perturbations on NMY-2. On the other hand, beads of 100 nm diameter, similar in size to small endosomes,43 exhibited notable changes upon myosin II perturbations. This contrast may be caused by the characteristic mesh size of the cytoplasmic actomyosin networks, previously reported to be around 50 nm in mammalian cells.65,71 Although the mesh size of actomyosin networks in C. elegans early embryos remains uncharacterized, the size-dependency of bead movements suggests a comparable length scale.

On the other hand, our CV analysis yielded a characteristic length of ∼0.5 μm. However, note that this value characterizes the hierarchical and complex structure of actin networks and does not necessarily reflect the minimum mesh size. Of course, there is a possibility that the characteristic mesh size measured in the previous studies65,71 is intrinsically different from that in the bulk cytoplasm of C. elegans embryos. It is also possible that our observations using confocal microscope images fail to capture finer structures. Future studies to resolve the structures of actomyosin networks in embryos will elucidate the structural properties of actomyosin networks and their role in transport regulation.

Previous studies, including ours, have shown that injecting materials into C. elegans gonads allow in vivo observations of material dynamics in embryos,42,51 which can be compared with in vitro experiments. In this study, by injecting covalently conjugated motor-cargo complexes, we observed faster movements in vivo than in vitro. Future studies using DNA nanostructures such as DNA-origami, will enable a more detailed examination of the reconstituted motor-cargo complexes in vivo,24,72,73 including the impact of the motor number and geometric configuration on cargo transport.

Biological implications of transport acceleration by cytoplasmic fluctuations

The acceleration of dynein-driven transport in fluctuating cytoplasm raises a question about its biological implications. One possibility is that dynein is inherently adapted to rectify external forces, enabling efficient transport even in a highly viscous environment, which would slow down large vesicles.74,75 Another possibility involves cellular energetics: ATP-driven forces from actomyosin, although their direction and strength fluctuate, contribute to enhancing directed transport and can result in the transport of larger cellular components. In C. elegans embryos, dynein-driven transport produces the mutual reaction force, called the cytoplasmic pulling force, and pulls the nucleus through the centrosome.8,18,59,76,77 Thus, the observed acceleration of microtubule-based transport may contribute to the efficient transport of nuclei in C. elegans embryos. Our results suggest a mechanism by which the inevitable byproduct of random forces in the cytoplasm is converted into the unidirectional motion of small vesicles and large organelles. It is worth noting that C. elegans embryos have a rapid cell cycle consisting of S and M phases, without intervening gap phases,78 which may require more efficient and timely positioning of cellular components. This specialized context may amplify the importance of transport regulation by cytoplasmic fluctuations. Therefore, future studies integrating live-cell observations in other organisms and in vitro experiments will be essential to determine how broadly the in vivo acceleration mechanism applies and how the random forces in the cytoplasmic environment regulate cellular processes across diverse biological systems.

Limitations of the study

While our optical trap experiments suggest that the force-response properties of dynein may contribute to in vivo acceleration through the rectification of environmental random forces, the quantitative extent of this contribution to intracellular transport remains to be fully elucidated. Moreover, the specific molecular pathways that transmit cytoplasmic random forces to promote directed motion are still being uncovered. In this study, we were not able to determine whether fluctuations of cytoplasmic microtubules dynamically change in response to actomyosin perturbations, which may further influence dynein-based transport. Future work combining simultaneous high-spatiotemporal-resolution imaging of multiple cytoskeletal components and mechanical perturbation analyses will be important to directly visualize how actomyosin-driven fluctuations are coupled to microtubule dynamics and motor activity in living cells.

Caenorhabditis elegans strains and maintenance

The C. elegans strains used in this study are listed in the Table (Table S1). Some strains were provided by the CGC, which is funded by the NIH Office of Research Infrastructure Programs (P40 OD010440). Worms were maintained at 22°C. For the observation of cytoplasmic actin signal, the MG589 strain, expressing GFP::utrophin, was incubated at 25°C overnight before the observation to match the condition with the myosin knocked-down experiment, in which the worms were incubated at 25°C overnight.

Method details

C. elegans - RNA interference conditions

RNAi was conducted by directly injecting dsRNA into the worms42 for nmy-2 and by feeding for perm-1.79 For nmy-2 RNAi, the dsRNA was synthesized using the primers with T7 and T3 promoters:

5’-TAATACGACTCACTATAGGAAGTGCGTCATCTCTGGCTT-3’ and 5’-AATTAACCCTCACTAAAGGGAGGAACGACGAAGAACTCG-3’

The dsRNA was synthesized from the genomic DNA of the N2 strain, dissolved into the TE buffer, and stored at -20 °C until the injection. After injection, the worms were incubated at 25°C for 16-20 hours before the observations. For perm-1 RNAi, the Escherichia coli clone for C. elegans PERM-1 T01H3.4 from the Ahringer library was used.80,81 The bacterial culture (200 μL/plate) was spread onto 35 mm NGM agar plates containing 0.01 mM IPTG (isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside). Ten to twenty L4 worms were transferred onto each plate and incubated at 20 °C for 14-20 hours before observations.

C. elegans embryo - Live imaging

To observe the fluorescent signal in the early embryos, we first dissected worms in 0.75× egg salt (118 mM NaCl, 40 mM KCl, 3.4 mM CaCl2, 3.4 mM MgCl2, 5 mM HEPES pH 7.2). The embryos from the dissected worms were mounted in 0.75× egg salt, which was placed on a 26 × 76-mm custom-made coverslip (Matsunami Glass). No coverslip was mounted on the embryos to eliminate the effects of deformation. The egg-mounted coverslips were set to the inverted microscope (Olympus, IX71), which was equipped with the CSU-X1 spinning-head (Yokogawa). The images were captured with a 60× silicon-immersion objective lens (Olympus, UPlanSApo, 60×/1.30Sil), a 2.0× intermediate magnification lens, and an EM-CCD camera (Andor, iXon), which was managed by the NIS elements software (Nikon). The exposure times were 300 ms for GFP::RAB-5 and mCherry::RAB-5, 180 ms for DHC-1::degron::GFP and GFP::utrophin, and 30 ms for 100 nm and 40 nm fluorescence beads. The experimental room was air-conditioned, and temperature was maintained at 21–23°C. The embryos which were confirmed to hatch were analyzed except for the myosin-perturbing experiments (nmy-2 RNAi, NMY-2 injected, and blebbistatin treated).

C. elegans embryo - Drug treatments

For the dynein degradation assay, the CA1215 strain expressing TIR1::mRuby and dhc-1::degron::GFP was used.54 To degrade endogenous dynein, acetoxymethyl indole-3-acetic acid (IAA-AM), which is an analog of natural auxin IAA and can efficiently permeate the eggshell of C. elegans,56 was used. Worms were dissected in the 0.75× egg salt, and the derived embryo was transferred into the egg salt containing 500 mM of IAA-AM.

To inhibit the activity of NMY-2 in the embryos, we increased the permeability of eggshells by knocking down the perm-1 gene. In the observations of blebbistatin-treated embryos, the worms cultured on the perm-1 feeding RNAi plate were dissected using 0.75×egg salt buffer supplemented with 2.5 μM blebbistatin (Sigma, B0560-1MG). After dissection, the worms were mounted using the same buffer.

Plasmid construction and protein purification

The minimum motile construct of dynein (hsDHC-1382k), the heavy meromyosin (HMM) fragment of C. elegans NMY-2 (1-1364 aa), full-length MLC-4, and full-length MLC-5 were purified from mammalian cultured cells (FreeStyle 293-F cell, Invitrogen). For purifying hsDHC-1382k, the truncated 382 kDa heavy chain of human cytoplasmic dynein I was used as previously described.24 For the purification of the NMY-2 complex, the plasmids coding NMY-2, MLC-4, and MLC-5 were co-transfected. The sequence for the amplification of the NMY-2 fragment was determined based on a previous study.82 The sequences of the three genes were amplified from the cDNA of N2 strain worms. The amplified sequences were inserted into pcDNA3.4 vector for nmy-2 and pcDNA5/FRT/TO for mlc-4 and mlc-5, with the insertion of 6×His-tag for nmy-2 and Streptavidin binding peptide (SBP)-tag for mlc-4 and mlc-5 in the upstream of each gene. The plasmids were transfected to the cultured cells with Polyethylenimine (PEI).83 The cells were harvested 72 h after the transfection, flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C. For the purification, the frozen cells were thawed and suspended in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 10% (w/v) sucrose, and pH 7.2) supplemented with the ProteoGuard EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Clontech, 635673). The suspended solution was put on ice and homogenized using a sonicator (Qsonica, Q125) with 60% amplitude and 1-s ON/1-s OFF pulses. The total sonicating time was 10 minutes. The homogenized solution was centrifuged at 75,000 rpm for 15 minutes (Beckman, TL100.3). The supernatant was loaded onto a StrepTactin Sepharose column (IBA) with a bed volume of 1 mL. After the loading, the column was washed with lysis buffer. The proteins were eluted with lysis buffer supplemented with 2.5 mM desthiobiotin (IBA, 2-1000-002) and flash frozen by liquid nitrogen. The concentration of purified proteins was determined by the Bradford method using TaKaRa Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Takara Bio, T9310A).

Tubulin used for in vitro observations was purified from porcine brain tissue through 2 successive cycles of polymerization and depolymerization. A high-molarity PIPES buffer was used to remove contaminating microtubule-associated proteins.84 To prepare fluorescently labeled microtubules, we labeled tubulin with Cy-3 (GE Healthcare, PA23001) for the optical trap experiments or ATTO647N (ATTO TEC, AD 647N) for in vitro motility experiments. For in vitro motility experiments, the tubulin labeled with biotin (Tokyo Chemical Industry, S0956) was also used for immobilizing microtubules. Labeling of tubulins was performed according to the published method.85 To prepare microtubules double-labeled with biotin and ATTO647N, non-labeled tubulins, ATTO647N-labeled tubulins, and biotinylated tubulins were mixed at the molar ratio of 20:1:1. Microtubules were polymerized in BRB80 (80 mM K-PIPES (pH 6.8), 1 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM EGTA) in the presence of 1 mM GTP and 5 μM taxol at 37°C for 15 minutes and then stabilized with additional taxol (50 μM for 15 minutes). Microtubules were then pelleted at 80,000 rpm in TLA-120.1 rotor at 25°C, and the pellet was resuspended in BRB80 with 50 μM taxol. The microtubules prepared were stored at room temperature and used within two weeks.

Preparation of PEG-coated fluorescent beads

The fluorescent beads for analyzing cytoplasmic movements were coated by PEG to prevent non-specific binding of cytoplasmic proteins.86 For the coating, the solution of carboxylated beads was diluted into 400 μL of 1× Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) (Takara Bio, T900) containing 100 mg/mL PLL-g-PEG (SuSoS) with the particle number of 1.4 ×1012 particles for 40 nm bead (Invitrogen, F8794) and 7.2 ×1011 particles for 100 nm bead (Invitrogen, F8801). After the dilution, the mixtures were sonicated for 10 s and incubated for 1 h with 5 s sonication every 20 minutes. The beads were washed and concentrated using a centrifugal filter (Millipore, UFC510024), with 1× PBS. The particle density was adjusted to 0.7 ×1010 particles/μL for both beads.

Preparation of BG-modified beads and conjugation with SNAP-fused motors

To prepare benzylguanine-modified beads, carboxylated 100 nm beads (Invitrogen, F8801) were subjected to modification using BG-NH2, as previously described.24 A 10 nM fluorescent bead solution was mixed with 250 μM of sulfo-NHS (N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide) (Thermo Scientific, 22980) and 250 μM of EDC (1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride) (Thermo Scientific, 24510) in Borate buffer (100 mM Borate-NaOH, pH 8.5). The mixture was incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Following sonication, 650 μM of BG-NH2 (New England Biolabs, S9148) was added to the mixture, and the reaction proceeded for 2 hours at room temperature. To remove excess chemicals, the solution underwent three washes using a centrifugal filter (Millipore, UFC510024) with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Finally, the particle density was adjusted to 1.44 ×1010 particles/μL by adding 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). Beads less than 2 weeks from the modification were used for the conjugation with dynein. For the conjugation of dynein to BG-modified bead, the 120 nM hsDHC-1 and 1 nM BG-beads were mixed in 1×PBS and incubated at 25°C for 30 minutes. After the conjugation, the mixed solution was stored at 4 °C until injections.

Gonad injection of recombinant proteins or motor-cargo complex

Purified proteins or the artificial motor-cargo complex were diluted using 1× PBS (Takara Bio, T900) and loaded into custom-made microneedles prepared with the P1000IVF micropipette puller (Sutter Instrument). In the case of NMY-2 injection, the concentration of total protein, including the HMM fragment of NMY-2 and two light chains (MLC-4 and MLC-5), was set to 100 μg/mL. Young adult worms were placed on a thin layer of 2% agarose (Lonza, SeaKem LE agarose) prepared on a 24 × 55-mm coverslip (Matsunami Glass, C024551, thickness No.1). After covering the worms with halocarbon oil, the coverslips were mounted on the inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss, Axiovert135). The protein solution supplemented with Texas Red-labeled dextran with a molecular weight of 3k (Invitrogen, D3329) was injected into the gonad of worms using the microinjector (Eppendorf, FemtoJet). After the completion of injections, 3 μL of M9 buffer87 was placed on the oil to release the worms. After the release, the worms were transferred to a new NGM plate and incubated at 22 °C for at least 3 h to incorporate the injected components into the embryos through oogenesis.

Imaging of in vitro motility of the dynein-bead complex

In vitro motility assays were performed using an inverted microscope (Ti, Nikon) equipped with Epi-fluorescence imaging optics.88 Image acquisition was performed using NIS-Elements software (ver 4.51.00, Nikon) with a 100× objective (1.49 NA, Nikon), fluorescence filters (chroma 49008 for microtubule; chroma ET705/72m for dynein beads), and EMCCD camera (Andor, iXon Ultra). Time-lapse images were acquired with 100 ms exposure without intervals. Flow chambers were assembled by attaching a glass coverslip (Fisher, 12548A) on a glass slide (Matsunami Glass, FF-001) using a pair of double-stick tape aligned in parallel with ∼3 mm separation. Before assays, the glass surface was pre-coated with a mixture of biotinylated PEG and non-biotinylated PEG (Laysan Bio Inc.) to minimize nonspecific protein binding.89 The chamber was pre-coated sequentially with the following reagents: 1.0 mg/mL α-casein (Sigma-Aldrich, C6780) for 2 minutes, 0.2 mg/mL neutravidin (Invitrogen, A2666) for 5 minutes, and ATTO647N-labeled, biotinylated microtubules for 8 minutes. Then, dynein-conjugated, BG-coated beads which were diluted in Assay Buffer (12 mM K-PIPES pH 7.0, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 50 mM KCl, 2 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, 20 μM taxol, and oxygen scavenging mix (4.5 mg/mL glucose, 0.20 mg/mL glucose oxidase and 0.035 mg/mL catalase)) supplemented with 0.25 mg/mL α-casein, was infused into the chamber.

Instrumentation for optical trapping experiments

Optical trapping was performed essentially as described.90 A detailed parts list is provided in Table S2. Beads were trapped and positioned over polarity-marked microtubules by a focused Nd:YAG laser (1064 nm, ATLAS, Coherent) using a 60×/NA1.45 oil immersion objective lens (Olympus, custom-made for maximum transmission of IR light; PlanApo). For rapidly moving the trap center, the trapping laser was passed through an electro-optic deflector (Quantum Technology, Model 41) and controlled with a multifunction generator ( NF Corporation, WF1974). The bead was illuminated diagonally by a collimated red laser beam (644 nm; Excelsior-640C-60-CDRH, Spectra Physics). The light scattered by the bead was gathered by the same objective lens and passed through a custom-made perforated mirror (5 mm in diameter when viewed from the optical axis, Sigma Koki) placed below the objective lens.91 The dark-field image of the bead was projected onto a quadrant photodiode (Hamamatsu Photonics, S994-13) coupled to a differential amplifier (Sentec, OP711G-2). The bead position was recorded at a sampling rate of 24 kHz and anti-aliasing filtered to 11 kHz before digitization by a 16-bit A/D module (National Instruments, USB-6251). Cy3-microtubules and dark-field images of the beads (just for manipulation) were simultaneously visualized by using TIRFM with a diode-pumped solid-state laser (532 nm; μGreen-SLM, JDS Uniphase). The images were split into two side-by-side images of Cy3-microtubule and beads using a dichroic mirror and projected onto a cooled EMCCD detector (DU-897E, Andor Technology). The exposure time was 70 ms. The sample was mounted on a piezoelectric stage (Physik Instrumente, P-517.3CL). Linearity between the monitored and actual displacement of the bead from the trap center was verified up to ±200 nm by applying a sinusoidal movement to the piezo stage and analyzing the response of the photodiode sensor. The bead position was calibrated for each bead by moving the photodiode sensor in a stepwise manner.92 The trap stiffness was calculated for each bead based on the amplitude of the thermal diffusion using the equipartition theorem. The trap stiffness calculation was cross-checked by using power spectral measurements. A sinusoidal movement was applied to the sample to obtain a calibration peak, as previously described.93

Preparation of dynein-coated beads for optical trap experiments

Twenty microliters of 1% carboxylate-modified 200 nm polystyrene beads (Invitrogen, F8811) were incubated with 0.6 μL of anti-SNAP antibody (New England Biolabs, P9310S) in a total of 50 μL BRB80 buffer (80 mM PIPES-KOH pH 6.8, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM EGTA) for 20 minutes at room temperature. The bead surface was then blocked with 6–7 mg/mL casein for 10 minutes, followed by extensive washing with BRB80 buffer using a tabletop centrifuge (Eppendorf, 5424R). The collected beads were resuspended with 50 μL of BRB80 buffer and sonicated for 15 s (Velvo-Clear, VS-25). The resulting anti-SNAP beads were mixed with dynein for 15 minutes at room temperature and washed with BRB80 buffer. For single-molecule measurement, dynein was mixed with the bead at a sufficiently low concentration such that the binding fraction of the beads onto microtubules was less than 30%. This ensures that >90% of the beads that moved on a microtubule were driven by a single molecule.94

Optical trapping experiments

A flow chamber was constructed using two coverslips (18 × 18 mm and 24 × 32 mm, thickness No.1; Matsunami Glass) and Parafilm (Heathrow Scientific LLC) to create a chamber that was 3 mm wide and 18 mm long. The Parafilm was used as a spacer and melted by placing the flow chamber on a 98°C hot plate. For immobilizing microtubules, the flow chamber was first coated with 10 μg/mL anti-β-tubulin antibody (Santa Cruz, SC-58884) in BRB80 buffer, allowed to adsorb for 5 minutes, and blocked with 1% (w/v) Pluronic F-127 (Sigma-Aldrich, P2443) in BRB80 buffer. After washing with 0.6–0.7 mg/mL casein in BRB80 buffer, the flow chamber was incubated with microtubules labeled by Cy3 in BRB80 buffer for 5 minutes. After washing with casein solution, the chamber was filled with imaging solution containing motor protein, 12 mM PIPES-KOH, pH 6.8, 2 mM MgSO4, 1 mM EGTA, 25 mM K-acetate, 10 μM paclitaxel, 0.7 mg/mL casein, 2 mM DTT, 25 mM glucose, 21.3 U/mL glucose oxidase, 800 U/mL catalase, and 1 mM ATP. The trapping experiments were performed at 24 ± 1°C. The force and duration were analyzed manually using custom-made LabVIEW (National Instruments)-based software. The average maximum force was determined by averaging the force corresponding to the maximum height of each peak. Data were low-pass filtered to 25 Hz to measure the maximum force prior to the analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Tracking analysis - early endosome transport, artificial motor-cargo complex, and injected beads

Movements of early endosomes, artificial motor-cargo complex, and injected beads in 1-cell stage C. elegans embryo were analyzed by the custom-made software95 (Mark2). For the analysis of unidirectional movements, the tracked 2D trajectories were rotated to align the y-axis to the direction of the movements. The rotating angles were determined by the least squared fitting of individual 2D trajectories. The trajectories with the standard deviation in lateral position of less than 200 nm and more than 3-frame duration were categorized as unidirectional movements. The rotated 1D trajectories of unidirectional movements were fitted by a linear line, and the averaged speed was calculated. To calculate the diffusion coefficient of two-dimensional movements of beads, we fitted the MSD curves of individual trajectories by a linear function (y = 4Dt, D is the diffusion coefficient) using the time interval up to 1/4 of the whole tracking time. To calculate the MSD exponent of beads’ movements, we fitted the MSD curves of individual trajectories by the function y = Atα (A is a fitting constant and α is the MSD exponent) by using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). We used the time interval up to 1/4 of the whole tracking time. The mean diffusion coefficient and MSD exponent were calculated as the mean values of individual trajectories.

Estimating the possible maximum number of bead-bound dynein interacting with a microtubule

To estimate the possible maximum number of bead-bound dynein interacting with a single microtubule in our artificial motor-cargo experiments, we considered a geometrically simplified model (Figure S1D). In this model, a microtubule is represented as a rod with a diameter of 25 nm (effectively infinite in length), and a bead is modeled as a sphere with a diameter of 100 nm. For the simplification, a truncated dynein molecule is represented as an object consisting of a sphere with a diameter of 12.5 nm attached to one end of a rod with a length of 12.5 nm. The spherical part of dynein is always attached to the surface of the large sphere (bead).

Suppose we assume that N dynein molecules are uniformly distributed over the bead surface. In that case, the maximum number of dyneins that can simultaneously contact both the bead and the microtubule is proportional to the ratio of the accessible surface area Aattach to the total bead surface area Aattach. Here, Aattach is defined as the bead surface area where a dynein object can interact with both the bead and the microtubule. In the real situation, the steric hindrance near the bead-microtubule contact point would prevent dyneins from binding, but for simplicity, we neglected this effect. Furthermore, to make the calculation easy, we normalized the length by setting 50 nm = 1 arbitrary length unit.

To determine Aattach, we examined the limiting geometry where a dynein can contact both the bead and the microtubule, considering two orthogonal viewpoints. One is from the side of the microtubule (side view) and the other is along the axis of the microtubule (front view).

In the side view, the limiting configuration is depicted in Figure S1C. The center of the bead is denoted as O, the center of dynein as B, and the foot of the perpendicular from O to the microtubule axis as A. The foot of the perpendicular from B to the line OA is denoted as P. The angle ∠AOB (denoted as θ) corresponds to the maximum angle at which dynein can reach the microtubule. The distance OB equals the bead radius plus the radius of the sphere part of dynein (1 + 1/8 = 9/8). The shortest distance between point B and the microtubule surface is 3/8, giving OP = 1 - 3/8 = 5/8. Under this condition, bead-bound dynein can move along the microtubule within the range [-θmax, θmax].

The limiting configuration corresponds to the case where dynein can simultaneously attach to both circles. The situation depends on the value of θ, the angle considered in the case of side view. The configuration (ii) indicates the case of θ = 0, while the configuration (iii) shows the case of θ ≠ 0. Image analysis - Optical flow analysis

To analyze fluctuating movements of actomyosin networks in the cytoplasm, we calculated the optical flow in the time-lapse confocal fluorescence images of GFP::utrophin in C. elegans embryo using the Farneback method,96 which is implemented in the OpenCV library (www.opencv.org). The images of embryos from the MG589 worm strain (GFP::utrophin) were captured at 180 ms intervals. The captured images were rotated so that the embryo’s long axis appeared parallel to the horizontal axis. The images acquired with 180 ms intervals were averaged over four frames to calculate flows. After the averaging, the optical flow between the image at the n-th frame and at (n+4)-th frame was calculated. The mean flow speed was calculated by averaging the lengths of all the flow vectors. For the comparison among the myosin perturbed conditions, the averaged flow speed from 100 images from each embryo was used for each condition. Because the worms for nmy-2 RNAi condition were incubated at 25°C for 16-20 h as described in the “RNAi section.” Thus, the worms for other conditions were also incubated at 25°C.

Image analysis - Coefficient of variation analysis of actin network

To analyze the spatial pattern of actin networks in the cytoplasm, we calculated the coefficient of variations of the signal of GFP::utrophin with varying the window size of n pixel (n = 2 - 25). After scanning the image with the window, the mean and standard deviation of intensity in the window and the following CV were calculated. After scanning the whole area of the image, the mean CV of the image was calculated. To obtain the CV of pixel-shuffled images, we randomly shuffled the intensity values in the pixels, and the CV was calculated for the shuffled image as described above. The obtained CV curves were fitted by a one-phase exponential model by using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software).
