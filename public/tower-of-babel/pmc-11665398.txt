Wetland disturbance level and macrophyte composition in natural wetlands and paddy fields in the eastern part of Lake Tana, Ethiopia
Authors: Aklilu Agidie, Ayalew Wondie, Getachew Beneberu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural wetlands and paddy fields support a rich diversity of life forms. The study objective includes assessing the macrophyte community in relation to environmental variables and providing information on floristic compositions. The research is significant for determining the extent of disturbance and potential remedies from the standpoint of the health of the wetland ecosystem. Macrophytes were sampled and water quality parameters were measured from 20 sites across five wetlands. Cluster analysis grouped the sites into three categories based on their level of disturbance. An ordination method was employed to elucidate the gradients and relationships between environmental factors and species assemblages. Agricultural activities in the area disrupt wetland ecosystems, significantly altering vegetation composition and reducing biodiversity. These changes were primarily driven by variations in water quality and nutrient levels. In total, 37 macrophyte species were recorded in natural wetlands, while 33 species were identified in paddy fields. Key species, including Digitaria sanguinalis, Phalaris paradoxa, and Echinochloa stagnina, served as crucial animal fodder, while Cyperus papyrus and Typha latifolia supported the subsistence livelihoods of local communities. Dilmo Wetland exhibited the highest species richness in both natural wetlands (S = 29) and paddy fields (S = 21). The greatest Shannon-Wiener index values were observed at Dilmo Wetland, with H′ values of (2.91) in natural wetlands and (2.67) in paddy fields, while Shesher Wetland had the highest dominance index (D = 0.28). Current research has shown that wetlands near the Lake Tana littoral zone faced the most severe disturbances due to human activities, seasonal flooding, and invasive species (Eichhornia crassipes), which caused significant declines in biodiversity. To preserve the biodiversity and ecosystem health in floodplain areas, implementing sustainable wetland management interventions is essential.Keywords: Diversity, Environmental variables, Floodplain, Invasive species, Water quality

Introduction

Wetlands are referred to as biodiversity islands as they sustain large food chains and a diverse range of organisms [1,2]. These habitats are crucial for conserving and managing biodiversity [3]. Wetlands are affected by hydroperiod and hydrological features. Hydrology shapes the physiochemical properties of wetlands and is the main driver of wetland community assemblage and species richness [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Disturbance level refers to the degree of changes or disturbances in a wetland's ecosystem, caused by factors such as human actions and natural events. Disturbance can affect the vegetation, wildlife, and hydrology of wetlands, which leads to the degradation of their ecological functions [7]. Paddy fields are flooded areas of arable land that are used to grow rice and other semiaquatic crops [8]. They are distinguished by their wet conditions that are conducive to rice production. On the other hand, Natural wetlands are ecosystems that have seasonal or permanent water saturation, which facilitates the development of hydric soils and offers habitats for aquatic plants [9].

Macrophyte abundance and composition in wetlands can be predicted by several characteristics, including size, height, land use, water chemistry, and hydrological changes [7]. Understanding and measuring the environmental factors impacting macrophyte distribution patterns is critical for integrated management practices [10,11].

Macrophytes respond to changes in the physical and chemical environment of the ecosystem. They serve as indicators of the environmental conditions that exist within the wetland and long-term ecological changes [12]. The main threats to the wetland ecosystem degradation include the spread of invasive species, anthropogenic and natural eutrophication, seasonal variations in water level and unbalanced inlets and outlets [10]. Different macrophytes respond differently to environmental gradients depending on nutrient sources [13].

Measuring the diversity, richness, and composition of the flora is essential to understanding the state of the wetland ecosystem. This facilitates the process of classifying the water quality according to the presence of macrophyte plants. Macrophytes play a significant role in the ecosystems of wetlands and aquatic environments. On the other hand, environmental elements including temperature, light, the bed rock's parent material, the degree of area degradation, and water quality all affect its composition [[14], [15], [16]].

Among the elements of Lake biota that are least understood and researched are the abundance and richness of macrophytes in natural wetlands and paddy fields. Rice is an increasingly important staple food in Africa. Among the biological constraints to rice production, competition from weeds is the most important [17]. In the eastern part of Lake Tana, rice farming is a common activity. The paddy field is home to a high species diversity of macrophytes that are derived from the original vegetation and are carried by the seasonal floods in the area [18].

Currently, there is little data available on macrophyte floristic composition in paddy fields and natural wetlands. Some research reports on macrophytes targeted mainly the littoral zone of Lake Tana [5,19]. Particularly the Lake Tana sub-basin wetland ecosystem's quantity, diversity, and pattern of vegetation distribution in the basin have not yet been elucidated. Compared to other important rice production areas worldwide, the available information about weed species of rice in East Africa is fragmentary and needs inventory [17].

Knowing about the degree of disturbance is necessary to assess the wetlands' ecological health. It is possible to characterize the degree of habitat change and biodiversity loss by examining the macrophyte composition in natural wetlands and paddy fields. Furthermore, the research output will benefit the sector by providing evidence-based knowledge on wetland ecological protection. Therefore, the objective was: 1) To assess the macrophyte dynamics in the selected wetlands about environmental variables. 2) To describe the existing macrophyte diversity of the natural wetland and paddy fields and provide information on floristic compositions.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

The study wetlands are found in the Lake Tana sub-basin, which is a highland freshwater body in northwest Ethiopia. The nature of the five study sites (Dilmo, Bebeks, Sendye, Shesher, and the littoral zone of the lake) was described in detail, as seen in Fig. 1 and Table 1. Rainfall is unimodal, with the main portion falling between June and September, with a total annual precipitation range of 1100–1530 mm [19]. Lake Tana basin is home to various biodiversity with pervasive flora and fauna including fishes, birds, and vegetation, and comprises more than thirty islands and numerous seasonal and permanent wetlands. The lake provides different services to the community including irrigation, hydroelectric power, transportation, agricultural services, and goods [20]. The biomes are in the afro-tropical highland zone groups [21]. The two main enormous rivers, Gumara and Rib, over-run and strengthen the permanent wetlands while producing a large number of seasonal ponds (Fig. 1).

Sampling and data collection

It can be challenging to compare the vegetation of natural wetlands with that of paddy fields because the natural wetland has greater macrophyte diversity due to limited human interference, with species adapted to a range of environmental conditions. Whereas, paddy fields cultivation activity decreased the number of weeds and allowed the growth of some species. To handle the comparison, the macrophyte inventory includes the count before the initial weed control in the paddy field.

Macrophytes and water samples were collected twice a year from 20 sampling sites from the five wetlands between December 2021 to November 2022 in the dry (December to May) and wet (June to November) seasons. The macrophytes were collected using a fixed quadrat (1 m × 1 m) plot size following the method of [13].

Physicochemical parameters were assessed in situ based on [22] research protocol procedures. Different parameters, such as electrical conductivity (EC), dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, and water temperature, were measured in situ using a HACK multi-meter portable probe (HQ40d). Total dissolved solid (TDS) was measured by a multi-probe (Pro 30 Professional Series). Water depth was measured using a standard measuring pole. Turbidity was measured with a portable turbidimeter. For the measurement of total nitrogen (TN), nitrate (NO3), phosphate (PO4), and total phosphorus (TP), a composite grab sample of 500 ml water was collected from each of the sampling sites using a plastic bottle pre-washed with hydrochloric acid. The water samples were transported to the lab for nutritional analysis while they were refrigerated. Based on protocols from the [23], total nitrogen and total phosphorus were assessed using the Kjeldahl and Persulfate digestion methods, respectively. The Palintest Phosphate LR method was used to measure phosphate concentration, while the Palintest Nitratest was used to analyze nitrate concentration [24].

Sample plots were located in each wetland and randomly selected based on the type of vegetation. Rice farms near natural wetlands were chosen to compare floristic compositions. The plots were randomly selected, and 25 quadrats in five paddy fields and 25 quadrats in five natural marshes were laid. A total of 50 quadrats were laid. Frequency and density are the most popular methods of measuring in species inventory following [25]. Density measures the number of individuals per unit area, whereas frequency is the proportion of sampling sites that contain the species.

Each macrophyte in the quadrats was counted and noted. Aquatic macrophytes are defined broadly to include submerged, floating-leaf, and emergent plants. Herbarium specimens were prepared and identified using Ethiopian flora, such as those of [26,27] and some plant pictures were referred to the East African Aquatic Vegetation Field Guide manual for identification. Local elder men and agronomists assisted in recording the local names of each species in each sampling site. Each plot's geographic coordinates and elevation were recorded using a Garmin GPS.

Data analysis

The measured values of physicochemical parameters were summarized and computed. The relationships between environmental variables and macrophyte communities were depicted using canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) following [28]. The significance of the resulting ordination was evaluated using principal component analysis (PCA) [29]. All variables were assessed for normality, and water quality parameters were transformed to Log(x+1) (except pH) to improve normality and normalized before analysis [13,30]. The correlation between environmental variables and macrophyte species was verified by CANOCO version 4.5 [31,32]. To show significant differences between the five wetlands, and to illustrate species richness curves, rarefaction/extrapolation curve analysis was exercised using iNEXT Software [32,33]. The mean comparison was tested with SPSS version 21. Raw data arrangement, some graphs, and trend analysis were computed using Microsoft Excel 2010. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the mean variations of physicochemical variables among clustered sites. Macrophyte species diversity indices were computed using Shannon and Weiner following [3]. Using Ward's method and Euclidean distance in PAST software version 4.13, cluster analysis was utilized to group sites based on physicochemical data [34].

Results

Species composition and diversity indices

In the fifty sample quadrats, about 16,900 macrophyte individuals, representing a total of 55 species and 25 families, were recorded both from natural wetlands and paddy fields. A total of 37 macrophyte species in natural wetlands and 33 species in paddy fields were identified during the study period (Table 2). Eleven species were commonly found in wetlands and paddy fields. In the proportion of macrophytes at family level, Cyperaceae (25 %), Salviniaceae (22 %), Pontederiaceae (20 %), and Poaceae (16 %) were the most abundant families in natural wetland, while Cyperaceae (27 %), Poaceae (25 %), Amaranthaceae (12 %), and Acanthaceae (11 %) were the important weed families in paddy fields. Polygonaceae and Acanthaceae were the smallest number of families in natural wetlands, while Onagraceae was the least number of families in paddy fields. A. africana, E. crassipes, and C. latifolius species represented approximately 37.7 % of all the species (Table 2).

In natural wetlands, the percentage of emergent aquatic macrophytes was 55.81 %, free-floating 42.61 %, and submerged 2 % (Table 2). In paddy fields, emergent species had 91 %, submerged (2 %), and floating 7 % values. The species C. latifolius, H. schulli, and P. senegalensis were observed in all natural wetlands, while C. latifolius, E. indica, and P. paradoxa were observed in all paddy fields. The highest diversity of species was observed during the wet season than dry seasons in both habitats. In natural wetlands, 7288 and 3799 macrophyte individuals were counted throughout the wet and dry seasons, respectively. In contrast, 4102 and 1711 macrophytes were found in paddy fields during the wet and dry seasons respectively (Supplementary Table S1).

Species diversity was higher in natural wetlands than in paddy fields. The macrophyte species richness in wetlands was noted over the research seasons. Dilmo wetland had the highest species richness (S = 29), while Shesher wetlands had the lowest species richness (S = 11), respectively. The greatest Shannon-Wiener values were found in the Dilmo natural wetland and Sendye paddy fields (H′ = 2.91 and 2.74, respectively). Conversely, Shesher wetland had the lowest Shannon-Wiener value (H′ = 1.60) and the highest dominance index (D = 0.28) respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

Macrophytes species diversity in the flooded plain

The highest diversity of species was observed during the wet season, most probably due to the minimal interference of humans and livestock. Moreover, excessive volumes of water and flooding in the area allow the wetland to grow vegetation. Hydroperiod is one of the fundamental drivers of aquatic macrophyte richness and composition [35].

The excessive richness of cypress and sedges reported in this study was similar to the pattern observed in other studies carried out in southern Brazilian paddy fields and natural wetlands [36]. The macrophyte assemblage observed in the studied sites was predominantly annual and native. C. latifolius, C. aromaticus, P. paradoxa, C. africana, L. peploides, and crab grasses were common in the paddy fields. These macrophyte species are widely found in the flooded plain in the area.

Cyperaceae, Salviniaceae, Pontederiaceae, and Poaceae were the most abundant families in natural wetlands, whereas Cyperaceae, Poaceae, Amaranthaceae, and Acanthaceae were the most abundant weed families in paddy fields. The most observed weed families were Cyperaceae and Poaceae. The findings were in agreement with reports from paddy fields in Brazil and rice agroecosystems in Vietnam and the Philippines [37]. Species of the Poaceae (grasses) and Cyperaceae (sedges) families are more important than others in rice because they are most adapted to the wet growing conditions and because grasses and sedges are most difficult to control due to their resemblance (both physiologically and morphologically) to rice, in particular at the early phenological stages.

Recurrent cultivation, seasonal flooding, inorganic fertilizers, and herbicide application may reduce the stability of aquatic macrophyte assemblages, ecological interactions, and population richness [[38], [39], [40]]. In general, seasonal variation in water level influenced the dominance of some macrophyte species diversity in the area.

Factors affecting macrophytes community composition

A combined physicochemical variable including hydrogen ions (pH) and electrical conductivity appeared as highly important factors for the species richness of all life-form groups. An increased amount of total phosphorus also typically increases species richness based on previous studies. These characteristics of water quality are likely to be linked to the amount and form of carbon useable for macrophytes [41]. In the examination of the water chemistry, pH (6.2–7.82), EC (103.7–250 μS/cm), DO (4.8–7.34 mg/l), TDS (49.5–165 mg/l), and TP (0.01–0.9 mg/l) were recorded at the study sites. The mean value of EC (208.49 μS/cm) in the slightly disturbed classes was higher than the reported value of previous studies by Refs. [17,42].

Cluster analysis showed that DO values varied significantly from 5.02 to 7.34 mg/L in slightly disturbed areas and from 5.1 to 6.2 mg/L in moderately disturbed sites respectively. This showed that the degree of DO concentration was a crucial component in indicating the ecological state of the wetland and being appropriate for the survival of biological communities. According to Ref. [43], DO values greater than 5 mg/L (80 % saturation) correspond to healthier aquatic ecosystems.

The range of phosphate values was (0.05–2.8 mg/l), and the range of nitrate values was (0.3–4.66 mg/l), whereas TP was (0.01–0.9 mg/l) and TN was (0.008–1.01 mg/l). The phosphate and nitrate values in the wetland water are higher than total nitrogen and phosphorus due to factors such as external sources, processes in the wetland, and interactions between different forms of nitrogen and phosphorus. External sources like agricultural runoff and pollution may lead to elevated levels of nitrate and phosphate in the water. Processes within the wetland, such as microbial activity and plant uptake, can also influence the concentrations of nitrogen and phosphorus in the water. The outcome is in line with the findings of the studies by Refs. [14,44,45].

Thirty-seven species of macrophytes were found in natural wetlands, while thirty-three species were in paddy fields. Factors that may contribute to lower macrophyte richness in paddy fields might be due to farm management (seasonal flooding, herbicide application, soil cultivation, etc.) and environmental heterogeneity. In general, paddy fields can make a contribution as complementary environments for the conservation of diverse aquatic macrophytes. According to Ref. [16], the weed species composition of seasonally flooded paddy fields in East Africa is diverse.

Invasive macrophytes have their own impact on other macrophytes' distribution and abundance [14]. Water hyacinth (E. crassipes) has become a threat to reducing the diversity, abundance, and distribution of macrophytes in Lake Victoria, Lake Abya, and Lake Tana [[46], [47], [48]]. Apart from the impact of human interventions, the expansion of water hyacinth (E. crassipes) in the heavily disturbed sites aggravated biodiversity degradation and disturbed the ecology. All study sites were also bordered by agricultural activities, irrigation, and free grazing. The moderately impacted sites are partially protected, and the seasonal increment in water level decreases the interference of human activities. The less impacted sites were protected from anthropogenic activities, and the community uses the wetland resources on behalf of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church administration.

Limitations of the study

The study aimed to compare macrophytes in both natural wetlands and paddy fields. Field studies of this nature require sampling across open water, dense vegetation, and varied landscapes. Paddy field weeds were present only during the rice production season, which spans from May to December for lowland rice varieties. As a result, data collection was aligned with the rice production calendar. Additionally, the lack of previous research on rice weeds in the target area limited the scope for further discussion. The vegetation composition of paddy fields was disturbed by cultivation activities and weed control, which makes it difficult to extrapolate results between natural wetlands and paddy fields.

Conclusions

In assessing the macrophyte dynamics in relation to environmental factors; temperature, pH, EC, and total phosphorus were the physicochemical variables that appear to be quite significant in determining how the species richness of macrophytes varies throughout all living forms. Various human activities and climatic factors affected the distribution and community of macrophytes in the floodplain. A total of 55 macrophyte species were found based on floristic compositions; 37 were found in naturally occurring wetlands and 33 in paddy fields. Important species like as D. stagnalis, P. paradoxa, and E. stagnina provided vital animal feed, and Typha latifolia and Cyperus papyrus sustained the subsistence lives of the surrounding communities. Dilmo and Bebeks wetlands were classified as relatively less and moderately disturbed, respectively. Lake Tana's peripheral wetlands are severely disturbed by excessive human interference, periodic flooding, and infested by water hyacinths, degrading the area's biodiversity. In natural wetlands, the most common macrophytes were A. africana, E. crassipes, C. latifolius, E. stagnina, and C. papyrus; the most common rice weed species were C. latifolius, H. schulli, E. stagnina, C. africana, A. aspera, E. crassipes, E. indica, P. paradoxa, and C. distans. In comparison to other sites, the Dilmo wetland contains a great diversity of macrophyte species. Some native macrophytes are disappearing from time to time due to successive tillage of the wetland field and the expansion of invasive species (E. crassipes). To maintain the vegetation in the littoral zone and wetlands, restoration, and plantation of natural vegetation are essential. Wetland vegetation conservation, and application of appropriate buffer zones with the conservation and rehabilitation objectives such as contour buffer strips, filter strips, and grassed riparian buffers (permanent vegetation and tree plantations) should be implemented.
