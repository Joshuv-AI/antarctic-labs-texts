Mixed regularity and sparse grid approximations of N N -body Schrödinger evolution equation
Long Meng, Dexuan Zhou

Abstract
. In this paper, we present a mathematical analysis of time-dependent N N -body electronic systems and establish mixed regularity for the corresponding wavefunctions. Based on this, we develop sparse grid approximations to reduce computational complexity, including a sparse grid Gaussian-type orbital (GTO) scheme. We validate the approach on the Helium atom ( He {\rm He} ) and Hydrogen molecule ( H 2 {\rm H}_{2} ), showing that sparse grid GTOs offer an efficient alternative to full grid discretizations.

35J10, 35B65, 41A25, 41A63

1.
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the mathematical study of sparse grid approximations for the following time-dependent
N
-body electronic system in molecular dynamics:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(t,x)=H_{N}(t)u(t,x),&t\in[0,T]=:I_{T},\;x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\end{cases}

with

(1.2)

\displaystyle H_{N}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}V(t,x_{j})+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}W(x_{j},x_{k}),

where

\displaystyle V(t,x_{j})=-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{\mu}}{|x_{j}-a_{\mu}(t)|},\qquad W(x_{j},x_{k})=\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}.

Here
\Delta_{j}:=\Delta_{x_{j}}
denotes the Laplacian operator acting on the variable
x_{j}
.

In physics and quantum chemistry, Eq. (
1.1
) describes the quantum mechanical
N
-body problem, where
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
electrons interact with
M\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
nuclei of total charge
Z=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}Z_{\mu}
through Coulomb attraction and repulsion. The Hamiltonian (
1.2
) acts on wavefunctions with variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, representing the coordinates of
N
electrons. Each moving nucleus
\mu
with charge
Z_{\mu}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
is treated as a classical particle located at
a_{\mu}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
at time
t
. This model serves as the basis for studying dynamical phenomena such as chemical reactions. An analogous setting for the time-dependent Hartree–Fock model coupled with classical nuclear dynamics can be found in
[
4
]
.

In mathematics, the evolution equation (
1.1
) has been well studied. In the time-independent case
H_{N}(t)\equiv H_{N}(0)
, the Stone theorem ensures the existence and uniqueness of solutions. When
H_{N}(t)
depends explicitly on time, the problem becomes much more delicate: by using the Duhamel formula and Strichartz estimates, local-in-time existence and uniqueness of solutions in
C^{0}(I_{T},H^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}))
has been proved in
[
22
]
for the one-body problem and in
[
23
]
for the general
N
-body problem (with
T\lesssim(ZN+N^{2})^{-2-}
).

In numerical analysis, this electronic configuration space
({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}
is typically high-dimensional for large
N\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
. Conventional discretizations of partial differential equations by finite differences or finite element methods scale exponentially with respect to the dimension of the configuration space. This is known as the
curse of dimensionality
.

To overcome this problem, various reduced models have been introduced. Time-dependent (multiconfiguration) Hartree–Fock and time-dependent density functional theory provide effective approximations (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
5
,
14
]
), however they are not based on a direct discretization of (
1.1
). Other approaches, such as the time-dependent density matrix renormalization group (TD-DMRG) and the time-dependent variational Monte Carlo (TD-VMC) method, have been developed in physics to study
N
-body dynamics (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
1
]
and references therein), but face challenges for Coulomb systems or lack rigorous mathematical understanding.
The Gaussian wave-packet method is also widely used in quantum dynamics (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
11
,
7
]
), but typically treats (
1.1
) within a semiclassical regime.

In this paper, we study the
N
-body problem (
1.1
) via sparse grid methods to improve computational efficiency. To this end, we first analyze the mixed regularity of (
1.1
), which then enables the construction of sparse-grid-type approximations.

1.1.
Mixed regularity and sparse grid approximation for eigenvalue problem

For the eigenvalue problem

(1.3)

\displaystyle H_{N}(0)u_{*}=\lambda u_{*}\qquad\textrm{with}\qquad\lambda<0,

the computational complexity can be reduced by employing sparse grid methods (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
for numerical implementations and
[
24
,
26
,
10
,
16
,
27
]
for the mathematical foundations), thanks to the fact that the antisymmetry of the wavefunction
u_{*}
can improve its regularity. According to Pauli’s principle, the antisymmetry of
u_{*}
is an inherent physical property of fermions. In this paper, we follow the definition of antisymmetry given in
[
16
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Generalized antisymmetric function)
.

Let
I\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
. When
|I|>1
, a wavefunction
u
is
antisymmetric with respect to
I
if and only if, for any
j,k\in I
,

u(P_{j,k}x)=-u(x),\qquad P_{j,k}(\cdots,x_{j},\cdots,x_{k},\cdots):=(\cdots,x_{k},\cdots,x_{j},\cdots).

In particular, when
|I|=1
or
I=\emptyset
, every wavefunction
u
is antisymmetric with respect to
I
.

According to Pauli’s principle, the electronic wavefunction (with spin
\{\pm\frac{1}{2}\}
) satisfies the following property.

Proposition 1.2

(Antisymmetry of the electronic wavefunction)
.

For any electronic wavefunction
u
and for any fixed spin state, there exist two disjoint index sets satisfying
I_{1}\bigcap I_{2}=\emptyset
and
I_{1}\bigcup I_{2}=\{1,\cdots,N\}
such that
u
is antisymmetric w.r.t.
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.

Further details on Proposition
1.2
can be found in
[
24
, Section 1]
. In this paper we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1.3
.

The initial datum
u_{0}
is antisymmetric w.r.t. the index sets
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, where
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are as given in Proposition
1.2
.

Let
I\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
. The mixed regularity is characterized by the following fractional Laplacian operator:

(1.4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{I}=\prod_{j\in I}(1-\Delta_{j})^{1/2},

which is defined via the Fourier transform (see Section
2.1
). In the special case
I=\emptyset
, we set
{\mathcal{L}}_{I}=\mathbbm{1}
.
Relying on the antisymmetry of the wavefunction
u_{*}
, mixed regularity associated with operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{I}
are studied in
[
24
,
26
,
10
]
. A typical result can be stated as follows: under Assumption
1.3
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{I_{\ell}}u_{*}\in H^{1}\big(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}\big),\qquad\ell=1,2.

This type of regularity naturally leads to hyperbolic cross space approximations of eigenfunctions. For
R>0
, define

(1.5)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}_{R}u)(x):=\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}(R)}(\xi)\,\mathcal{F}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}}{u}(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\,d\xi,

where
\mathcal{F}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}}{u}
denotes the
N
-body Fourier transform given in (
2.2
), and
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}(R)}
is the characteristic function of the hyperbolic cross domain
\mathcal{D}(R)
,

(1.6)

\mathcal{D}(R):=\Big\{(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N})\in({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}\;\Big|\;\sum_{\ell=1,2}\prod_{i\in I_{\ell}}\big(1+|\xi_{i}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}\leq R\Big\}.

Then one obtains the approximation estimate

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u_{*}\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}\leq R^{-1}\big\|\sum_{\ell=1,2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u_{*}\big\|_{H^{1}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}\leq R^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u_{*}\|_{H^{1}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}.

In numerical analysis, such hyperbolic cross approximations provide the foundation for constructing sparse grid methods, which significantly reduce computational complexity and allow for the treatment of systems with more electrons than standard discretizations (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).

1.2.
Mixed regularity and sparse grid approximation of (
1.1
)

We now consider the mixed regularity and sparse grid approximation of (
1.1
). As in the stationary case, the mixed regularity is also characterized by the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{I}
.
Our main result on mixed regularity (Theorem
2.4
) states that, under Assumption
1.3
, for
p=3^{-}
and for
T\lesssim(ZN+N^{2})^{-2-}
(see Remark
2.2
), the solution
u
to (
1.1
) satisfies

(1.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}))}\;\leq\;\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u\|_{X_{p,T}}\;\lesssim_{p}\;\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})},

where
X_{p,T}
, defined in Section
2.2
, is our functional space used for the evolution problem (
1.1
).

Remark 1.4

(Regularity of the initial datum
u_{0}
)
.

Under Assumption
1.3
, the condition
{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u_{0}\in L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})
,
\ell=1,2
, is natural. For models such as Hartree–Fock, the initial datum
u_{0}
can be written as a Slater determinant,

u_{0}(x)=\Big(\bigwedge_{j\in I_{1}}\phi_{j}\Big)\otimes\Big(\bigwedge_{j\in I_{2}}\phi_{j}\Big),

with orbitals
\phi_{j}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. In this case,
\mathcal{L}_{I_{\ell}}u_{0}\in L^{2}((\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N})
holds for
\ell=1,2
.

With the mixed regularity result established above, we now turn to the sparse grid approximation of (
1.1
) under Assumption
1.3
. As a preliminary step, we introduce an abstract framework by defining a truncation operator
\mathcal{P}_{R}
on
L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})
.

Assumption 1.5
.

Let
R>0
. We assume that
\mathcal{P}_{R}\in{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}))
satisfies:

•

(Projector)
0\leq\mathcal{P}_{R}^{2}=\mathcal{P}_{R}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}
,

•

(Commutativity)
[\mathcal{P}_{R},\Delta_{j}]=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
,

•

(Approximation) For any
u\in L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})
,

\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}\leq\frac{1}{R}\Big\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u\Big\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}.

Using
\mathcal{P}_{R}
, we have the following approximation of (
1.1
)

(1.9)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u_{R}=H_{N,R}(u_{R}),&t\in I_{T},\\
u_{R}(0,x)=\mathcal{P}_{R}(u_{0})(x),\end{cases}

where

H_{N,R}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{j}u+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{P}_{R}(V(t,x_{j})u)+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\mathcal{P}_{R}(W(x_{j},x_{k})u).

Our main result on the sparse grid approximation (Theorem
2.5
) states that, under Assumption
1.3
, a solution
u_{R}=\mathcal{P}_{R}u_{R}
to (
1.9
) exists in
X_{p,\infty}
. Moreover, for
p=3-
and
T\lesssim(ZN+N^{2})^{-2-}
, the following error bound holds:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{R}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N}))}\;\leq\;\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}\;\lesssim_{p}\;\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}.

In Lemma
5.1
, we establish an estimate analogous to (
1.7
), showing that the operator
\mathcal{P}_{R}
defined in (
1.5
) satisfies Assumption
1.5
. However, this operator corresponds to the continuous frequency domain
\mathcal{D}(R)
defined in (
1.6
), which is not directly suitable for computations.
For numerical purposes, a discrete approximation is required. To this end, we construct a discretized operator
\mathcal{P}_{R}
that also satisfies Assumption
1.5
, and we present a discretized version of (
1.9
).
In addition, as Gaussian-type orbitals (GTOs) are often preferable in molecular computations (including molecule dynamics), we further propose a sparse grid GTO approximation, motivated by the mixed regularity property.

1.2.1.
Discretization of (
1.9
)

The projector
\mathcal{P}_{R}
can be constructed in various ways (see, e.g.,
[
25
,
3
,
8
]
). Here we present one example and illustrate how it leads to a discretization of (
1.9
) together with the error bound (
1.10
).

Let
L>0
and define the function
\phi_{\mathbf{j}}^{(L)}
by its Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}_{y}(\phi_{\mathbf{j}}^{(L)})(\xi_{y})=L^{3/2}e^{-2\pi iL\mathbf{j}\cdot\xi_{y}},\quad\xi_{y}\in[0,L^{-1})^{3},\;\;\mathbf{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},

which form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,L^{-1})^{3})
. We extend these functions to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
by setting

\mathcal{F}_{y}(\psi_{\mathbf{j}}^{(L)})(\xi_{y})=\mathcal{F}_{y}(\phi_{\mathbf{j}}^{(L)})(\xi_{y})\,\mathbbm{1}_{[0,L^{-1})^{3}}(\xi_{y}).

To obtain a complete system in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we consider the shifted functions

\psi_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{l}}^{(L)}(y):=\psi_{\mathbf{j}}^{(L)}(y-L^{-1}\mathbf{l}),\quad\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},

The collection
\{\psi_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{l}}^{(L)}\}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}

thus forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Accordingly, an
N
-electron orthonormal basis function is given by

(1.11)

\displaystyle\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(x):=\prod_{\ell=1}^{N}\psi^{(L)}_{\mathbf{j}_{\ell},\mathbf{l}_{\ell}}(x_{\ell}),\qquad\vec{\mathbf{j}}=(\mathbf{j}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{j}_{N}),\;\vec{\mathbf{l}}=(\mathbf{l}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{l}_{N})\in(\mathbb{Z}^{3})^{N}.

Any wavefunction
u(x)\in L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})
can be written as

(1.12)

\displaystyle u(x)=\sum_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{3})^{N}}u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}\,\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(x),\qquad u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}=\left<\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}},\,u\right>.

Based on this basis, we define the truncated projector

(1.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{R}^{(L)}:=\sum_{(\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}})\in\mathcal{D}_{L,R}}\left|\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}\right>\left<\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}\right|,

where
\mathcal{D}_{L,R}
denotes a hyperbolic-cross-type index set used for sparse grid approximation, defined by

(1.14)

\mathcal{D}_{L,R}:=\Big\{(\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}})\in(\mathbb{Z}^{3})^{N}\;\Big|\;\big(L^{-1}\vec{\mathbf{l}}+([0,L^{-1})^{3})^{N}\big)\cap\mathcal{D}(R)\neq\emptyset\Big\}.

Later we will show in Proposition
2.6
,
\mathcal{P}_{R}^{(L)}
satisfies Assumption
1.5
, and the corresponding solution can be written as

(1.15)

\displaystyle u_{R}^{(L)}(t,x)=\mathcal{P}_{R}^{(L)}u_{R}^{(L)}(t,x)=\sum_{(\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}})\in\mathcal{D}_{L,R}}u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(t)\,\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(x).

Here the coefficients
u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(t)
satisfy the evolution system

(1.16)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(t)=\sum_{(\vec{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime},\vec{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{L,R}}\left\langle\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}},H_{N}(t)\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime},\vec{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime}}\right\rangle u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}}^{\prime},\vec{\mathbf{l}}^{\prime}}(t),\\[6.0pt]
u^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}(0)=\left\langle\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}},u_{0}\right\rangle,\end{cases}\qquad(\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}})\in\mathcal{D}_{L,R}.

This provides a sparse grid discretization of (
1.9
), and the resulting solution
u_{R}^{(L)}
satisfies the error bound (
1.10
) (see Proposition
2.6
).

Remark 1.6

(Constraints on
\vec{\mathbf{j}}
)
.

In practical computations, additional constraints on
\vec{\mathbf{j}}
are required to evaluate (
1.16
) numerically. Since

\|u_{R}(t)\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}=\|\mathcal{P}_{R}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})},

it follows that
\|\mathcal{P}_{R,J}^{(L)}u_{R}-u_{R}\|_{L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})}\to 0
as
J\to\infty
for any
t\geq 0
, where

\mathcal{P}_{R,J}^{(L)}:=\sum_{(\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}})\in\mathcal{D}_{L,R,J}}\left|\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}\right>\left<\psi^{(L)}_{\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}}}\right|,\qquad\mathcal{D}_{L,R,J}:=\big\{(\vec{\mathbf{j}},\vec{\mathbf{l}})\in\mathcal{D}_{L,R}:\|\vec{\mathbf{j}}\|_{\infty}\leq J\big\}.

Therefore, in practice, the infinite index set
\vec{\mathbf{j}}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{3})^{N}
can be truncated by imposing
|\vec{\mathbf{j}}|_{\infty}\leq J
for large
J
.

Refinements of such constraints on
\vec{\mathbf{j}}
(or on
L
) depend on a more precise understanding of the decay properties of the solution
u
to (
1.1
), which remains a natural numerical challenge on unbounded domains such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
(see, e.g.,
[
25
,
8
]
). For the eigenvalue problem (
1.3
), such constraints have been derived in
[
8
, Section 7]
by exploiting the exponential decay of eigenfunctions. For the time-dependent problem, favorable decay properties of wavefunctions are often assumed in quantum chemistry. A rigorous analysis of these properties and their numerical implications for the evolution problem will be addressed by the authors in future work.

1.2.2.
Sparse grid Gaussian-type orbital approximation

Since linear combinations of Gaussian-type orbitals (GTOs) can efficiently approximate the singular behavior of electronic wavefunctions near nuclei while preserving the analytical tractability of two-electron integrals, GTOs have become the standard basis in quantum chemistry for eigenvalue problems and also for dynamics (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
12
,
15
,
20
]
). Motivated by this, we propose a sparse grid GTO approximation, which provides a promising framework for reducing computational complexity.

The GTO approximation can be regarded as a special case of partial-wave decomposition with given radial functions. In the following, we first recall the general partial-wave expansion without any restriction on the radial part, and then specialize to the Gaussian-type choice used in Gaussian orbital theory.

For any
v\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
and a fixed
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the partial-wave expansion of
v
takes the form

(1.17)

\displaystyle v(y)=\sum_{l=0,1,\cdots,\;}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}v_{l,m}(r_{y})\,Y_{l,m}(\Omega_{y}),\qquad r_{y}:=|y-a|\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+},\quad\Omega_{y}:=\frac{y-a}{|y-a|}\in\mathbb{S}^{2},

where
\{Y_{l,m}\}_{l,m}
denotes the spherical harmonics, which forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and satisfies

(1.18)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{l,m}=-l(l+1)Y_{l,m},

with
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
the Laplace operator on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

For the
N
-body wavefunction
u\in L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
, we apply this decomposition to each electron
x_{j}=(r_{j},\Omega_{j})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
for
j=1,\ldots,N
. Let
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{N})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
,
{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, the angular part of
u
takes the form

\mathbb{Y}_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}}}(\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{N}):=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{N}Y_{l_{k},m_{k}}(\Omega_{k}).

Accordingly, any
v\in L^{2}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{N})
admits the expansion

(1.19)

\displaystyle v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\sum_{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}v_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}}}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})\,\mathbb{Y}_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}}}(\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{N}),

where

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}:=\Big\{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}\times\mathbb{Z}^{N}:l_{j}\geq 0,\;-l_{j}\leq m_{j}\leq l_{j},\;j=1,\ldots,N\Big\}.

Based on this decomposition, we introduce the truncated projector

(1.20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{R}:=\sum_{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R}}\big|\phi_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}}}\big\rangle\big\langle\phi_{{\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}}}\big|,

where the sparse grid index set is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{\mathrm{SG}}:=\Big\{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}:\sum_{\ell=1,2}\prod_{j\in I_{\ell}}(l_{j}+1/2)\leq R\Big\}.

Proposition
2.7
then shows that
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{R}u
provides a good approximation of the solution
u
to (
1.1
), namely

(1.21)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|(1-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{R})u(t)\|_{\mathcal{V}_{I_{1},I_{2}}}\;\lesssim\;R^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|\mathcal{L}_{I_{\ell}}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N})},

where
\mathcal{V}_{I_{1},I_{2}}
is the functional space defined in (
2.13
). We also point out that for the eigenvalue problem (
1.3
), there exists a similar result
[
27
]
but in different functional spaces.

In the atomic-orbital framework, localized Gaussian-type orbitals (GTOs) centered at the nuclear positions

\{a_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{M}
are of the form

\psi_{\mu,n,l,m}(y)=f_{n,l,m}(t,|y-a_{\mu}(t)|)\,Y_{l,m}\!\big(\Omega_{y,a_{\mu}}\big),

where

f_{n,l,m}(t,s):=\sum_{k}c^{(n)}_{l,m,k}(t)\,s^{l}e^{-\zeta^{(n)}_{l,m,k}\,s^{2}},\qquad\Omega_{y,a_{\mu}}:=\frac{y-a_{\mu}}{|y-a_{\mu}|}.

Here the contraction coefficients satisfy
c^{(n)}_{l,m,k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and the exponents
\zeta^{(n)}_{l,m,k}>0
, while the index
n
labels different contracted orbital sets (i.e., linear combinations of primitive Gaussians).

In this representation, any
N
-body wavefunction
u
can be expanded in terms of GTOs as

(1.22)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\sum_{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\psi_{\mu,n_{j},l_{j},m_{j}}(t,x_{j})\right),

where
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{N})
is a multi-index labeling the different radial functions corresponding to each
(l_{j},m_{j})
.
In Gaussian orbital theory, however,
\boldsymbol{n}
ranges over a fixed finite set determined by the chosen basis (e.g., in the cc-pVDZ basis, Helium has two
s
-type orbitals with
n=1,2
for
l=0
).
Hence, in the subsequent sparse grid truncations, no further restriction is imposed on
\boldsymbol{n}
, as the number of contracted radial functions is already finite and fixed by the chosen basis set in Gaussian orbital theory.

We now explain how the sparse grid (
1.20
) is extended to GTO approximation.
For the atomic case (
M=1
), by (
1.21
) the construction is straightforward: the wavefunction
u(t,x)
can be approximated by

(1.23)

\displaystyle u(t,x)\;\approx\;u^{\rm SG}(t,x):=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{R}u(t,x)=\sum_{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{\rm SG}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{1,n_{j},l_{j},m_{j}}^{(p)}(t,x_{j}).

For molecular systems (
M>1

\Omega=(y-a_{1}(t))/|y-a_{1}(t)|
and

\Omega^{\prime}=(y-a_{2}(t))/|y-a_{2}(t)|
are centered at different nuclei.
Therefore, (
1.21

However, as molecular orbitals in quantum chemistry are typically expressed as linear combinations of atomic orbitals, it is natural to extend the sparse grid approximation from the atomic to the molecular case by applying the same sparse grid truncation in the angular indices:

(1.24)

\displaystyle u(t,x)\;\approx\;u^{\rm SG}(t,x):=\sum_{({\boldsymbol{l}},{\boldsymbol{m}})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{\rm SG}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{\mu_{j}=1}^{M}\psi_{\mu_{j},n_{j},l_{j},m_{j}}^{(p)}(t,x_{j})\right).

As an illustration, we apply this sparse grid GTO approximation to the Helium atom (
{\rm He}
) and the Hydrogen molecule (
{\rm H}_{2}
), and demonstrate its efficiency; see Figure

For the

Numerical experiments.

We now illustrate the effect of sparse-grid truncation in a time-dependent setting
for the helium atom (
{\rm He}
) and hydrogen molecule (

comparing the sparse grid (SG) Gaussian-type orbital approximation (

The one-electron basis is taken as a fixed Gaussian atomic orbital (AO) basis frozen at the
t=0
geometry:
AO centers and contraction coefficients remain unchanged during propagation. The electronic wavefunction is propagated in real time with a uniform time step

\Delta t=0.001,\qquad n_{\text{steps}}=1000,\qquad T=n_{\text{steps}}\Delta t=1.0.

For simplicity, the nuclei are modeled as fixed point charges; that is, their positions remain unchanged during the simulation and only the electronic degrees of freedom are propagated dynamically. This corresponds to the standard Born–Oppenheimer approximation, where the nuclei are assumed to move much more slowly than the electrons.

For the He atom, the nucleus is fixed at the origin with 65 contracted AOs centered there, and the two electrons are initially placed in Gaussian-type orbitals of the form
\exp(-0.5r^{2})
, one centered at the origin
(0.0)
and the other slightly shifted to

\exp(-0.5(r-0.2)^{2})
), both spin-up. For the H
2

R=1.4\penalty\ \text{Bohr}
, with each hydrogen contributing 65 contracted AOs, and the two electrons are initially placed in Gaussian-type orbitals of the form
\exp(-0.5r^{2})
, centered at the two nuclei and both spin-up. In both cases, the resulting CI vector is projected onto the chosen basis set, ensuring that the initial wavefunction has the desired decay properties.

For each truncation type and radius
R
, we measure the error

\mathcal{E}_{R}^{\mathrm{type}}(t)=\big\|u^{(R,\mathrm{type})}(t)-u_{\mathrm{full}}(t)\big\|_{H^{1}(({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{2})},

where “type” denotes either the

(SG) or the

(FG) truncation and

is computed with a full grid using

(3.2)

\displaystyle Qu(t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}S\!\Big(V(\cdot,x_{j})u(\cdot,x)\Big)(t)+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}S\!\Big(W(x_{j},x_{k})u(\cdot,x)\Big)(t).

With this notation, Duhamel’s formula shows that solutions
u
of (
1.1
) satisfies

(3.3)

u(t)=U_{0}(t)u_{0}-iQu(t).

To establish the standard Strichartz estimate (Lemma
3.2
),
we first prove a dispersive estimate (Lemma
3.1
).
Note that both results can be found in
[
23
, Lemmas 2.2 and 2.3]
,
but here our versions are global-in-time.
This is because in
[
23
]
, an additional time-dependent magnetic potential
A(t,x)
is added, which complicates the arguments.

Lemma 3.1

(Dispersive estimate)
.

Let
D\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
with
1\leq|D|\leq 2
. Then

\|U_{0}(t)g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{D}}\lesssim|t|^{-3/2}\|g\|_{L^{1,2}_{D}}.

Proof.

For the case
D=\{j\}
(i.e.,
|D|=1
), the claim reduces to the standard dispersive estimate
(see, e.g.,
[
19
, Eq. (2.22)]
):

\displaystyle\|U_{0}(t)g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{D}}=\|e^{\frac{1}{2}it\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta_{k}}g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{j}}=\|e^{\frac{1}{2}it\Delta_{j}}g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{D}}\lesssim|t|^{-3/2}\|g\|_{L^{1,2}_{D}}.

For the case
D=\{j,k\}
(i.e.,
|D|=2
), we use the change of variables

r_{j,k}=\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{j}-x_{k})
and
R_{j,k}=\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{j}+x_{k})
.
Note that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{j,k}\nabla_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+\nabla_{R_{j,k}})\mathcal{R}_{j,k},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{j,k}\nabla_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{R_{j,k}}-\nabla_{r_{j,k}})\mathcal{R}_{j,k}

and

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{j,k}\Delta_{j}+\mathcal{R}_{j,k}\Delta_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{r_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{R_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}.

Thus,

\mathcal{R}_{j,k}U_{0}(t)u=\widetilde{U}_{0}(t)\mathcal{R}_{j,k}u,\quad\text{with}\quad\widetilde{U}_{0}(t)=\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{i}{2}\Big(\sum_{m\neq j,k}\Delta_{m}+\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{r_{j,k}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{R_{j,k}}\Big)\Big).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|U_{0}(t)g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{j,k}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{R}_{j,k}U_{0}(t)g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{r_{j,k}}}=\|\widetilde{U}_{0}(t)\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{\infty,2}_{r_{j,k}}}\lesssim|t|^{-3/2}\|\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{1,2}_{r_{j,k}}}\leq|t|^{-3/2}\|g\|_{L^{1,2}_{j,k}}.

Hence the lemma.
∎

Using the above dispersive estimates, we obtain the following Strichartz estimates.

Lemma 3.2

(Strichartz estimates)
.

For
D,D^{\prime}\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
with
1\leq|D|,|D^{\prime}|\leq 2
and
2\leq p,\widetilde{p}\leq 6
, we have

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\|U_{0}(t)g\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}\lesssim_{p}\|g\|_{{\mathcal{H}}},

(3.6b)

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U_{0}(s)^{*}u(s)\,ds\Big\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\lesssim_{\widetilde{p}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{\widetilde{p}^{\prime},2}_{D^{\prime}})},

(3.6c)

\displaystyle\|Su\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}\lesssim_{p,\widetilde{p}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{\widetilde{p}^{\prime},2}_{D^{\prime}})}.

These are the standard Strichartz estimates. One can easily obtain these estimates by using
[
9
]
.

3.2.
Hardy inequalities and Sobolev’s inequalities

To study mixed regularity, we need some Hardy-type inequalities established in

[
16
, Lemma 3.7 and Corollary 3.8]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
16
, Lemma 3.7]

Let
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and define

Y_{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}):=\Big\{f(y)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\;:\;|y-a|^{2s-2}\nabla_{y}f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\Big\}.

Then, for
s\in[1,3/2)
and
f\in Y_{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, we have

\left\|\frac{f}{|\,\cdot-a|^{s}}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\;\leq\;\frac{2}{|2s-3|}\left\|\frac{\nabla_{y}f}{|\,\cdot-a|^{s-1}}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

and

Lemma 3.4
.

[
16
, Corollary 3.8]

Define the functional space
Y_{\mathrm{anti},s}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
by

Y_{\mathrm{anti},s}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}):=\Big\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})\;:\;f(y,z)=-f(z,y),\;\;|y-z|^{\,s-2}\,\nabla_{y}\otimes\nabla_{z}f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})\Big\}.

Then for
s\in[2,5/2)
and
f\in Y_{\mathrm{anti},s}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, we have

\left\|\frac{f}{|y-z|^{s}}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})}\;\leq\;\frac{4}{|2s-5|\,|2s-3|}\left\|\frac{\nabla_{y}\otimes\nabla_{z}f}{|y-z|^{s-2}}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3})}.

We also need some Sobolev-type inequalities on the functional spaces
L^{p,2}_{D}
.
To this end, we recall the Mikhlin multiplier theorem in the setting of
L^{p,2}_{j}
.
Let
a:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a bounded measurable function.
It defines a bounded linear operator
T_{a}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C})
acting on
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\mathbb{C})
by

T_{a}u:=\widecheck{a\widehat{u}}

Here
\widehat{u}(\xi_{1},y_{2}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-2\pi iy_{1}\cdot\xi_{1}}u(y_{1},y_{2})dy_{1}
is the Fourier transform for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\widecheck{u}(y_{1},y_{2})
is the inverse.

Theorem 3.5

(Mikhlin multiplier)
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Suppose
a:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a
C^{n+2}
function satisfying

|\partial^{\alpha}a(\xi)|\leq C|\xi|^{-\alpha}

for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and every multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
|\alpha|\leq n+2
.
Then for any
1<p<\infty
,

\|T_{a}f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))}\;\lesssim_{n}\;\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{m}))}.

The proof of Theorem
3.5
is exactly the same as the standard Mikhlin multiplier theorem (see, e.g.,
[
17
, Theorem 8.2]
). The only difference is that we use the Calderón-Zygmund inequality in
[
13
, Theorem 2.1.9]
instead of the normal one.

We now state Sobolev inequalities associated with the functional spaces
L^{p,2}_{j}
and
L^{p,2}_{j,k}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
D\subset\{1,\dots,N\}
with
1\leq|D|\leq 2
. Then we have

(3.7a)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{j}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{D}}

\displaystyle\;\lesssim_{p}\;\|(1-\Delta_{j})^{1/2}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{D}},

(3.7b)

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{p,2}_{D}}

\displaystyle\;\lesssim_{p}\;\|(1-\Delta_{j})^{1/2}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{D}}.

Proof.

We first consider the case
j\not\in D
. By applying the Plancherel theorem on variable
x_{j}
, it is easy to see that (
3.7a
)-(
3.7b
) hold. Now suppose
D=\{j\}
. Estimates (
3.7a
) and (
3.7b
) follow directly from Theorem
3.5
with
n=3
and multiplier

a(\xi)=\xi(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1/2}\quad\mbox{or}\quad a(\xi)=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1/2},\qquad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

Finally, suppose
D=\{j,k\}
with
k\in\{1,\cdots,N\}\setminus\{j\}
.
We only prove for (
3.7a
). The estimate (
3.7b
) follows analogously. Using the change of variables (
2.8
),
the identities (
3.4
)–(
3.5
), and applying the Plancherel theorem in
R_{j,k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{j}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{j,k}}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{R}_{j,k}\nabla_{j}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{j,k}}=\frac{1}{2}\|(\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+\nabla_{R_{j,k}})\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{F}_{R_{j,k}}\left((\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+\nabla_{R_{j,k}})\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\right)\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\|(\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+i2\pi\xi_{R_{j,k}})\mathcal{F}_{R_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\exp(i2\pi r_{j,k}\cdot\xi_{R_{j,k}})(\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+i2\pi\xi_{R_{j,k}})\mathcal{F}_{R_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{r_{j,k}}\exp{(i2\pi r_{j,k}\cdot\xi_{R_{j,k}})}\mathcal{F}_{R_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\|(1-\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{r_{j,k}})^{1/2}\exp{(i2\pi r_{j,k}\cdot\xi_{R_{j,k}})}\mathcal{F}_{R_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}=\|(1-\frac{1}{4}|\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+i2\pi\xi_{R_{j,k}}|^{2})^{1/2}\mathcal{F}_{R_{j,k}}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}

\displaystyle=\|(1-\frac{1}{4}|\nabla_{r_{j,k}}+\nabla_{R_{j,k}}|^{2})^{1/2}\mathcal{R}_{j,k}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{r_{j,k}}}=\|(1-\Delta_{j})^{1/2}g\|_{L^{p,2}_{j,k}}.

Here we use the fact that for any function
f(y)
with
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,

\displaystyle(1-\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{y})^{1/2}\exp{(i2\pi a\cdot\xi_{y})}f(y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{y}^{-1}\Big[(1-\frac{1}{4}\cdot(i2\pi\xi_{y})^{2})^{1/2}\mathcal{F}_{y}\big(\exp{(i2\pi a\cdot\xi_{y})}f\big)(\xi_{y})\Big](y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{y}^{-1}\Big[(1+\pi^{2}|\xi_{y}|^{2})^{1/2}\mathcal{F}_{y}\big(f\big)(\xi_{y}+a)\Big](y)=(1-\frac{1}{4}|\nabla_{y}+i2\pi a|^{2})^{1/2}f(y)

and for the last identity, we use (
3.5
).
∎

3.3.
Approximation estimates

Normally, operators bounded on
{\mathcal{H}}
are not bounded on
L^{p,2}_{D}
. However, the next result shows that if a bounded operator
\mathcal{P}
on
{\mathcal{H}}
commutes with the free propagator
U_{0}
, i.e.,
[\mathcal{P},U_{0}]=0
,
then after composition with the operator
S
it is also bounded on
L^{p,2}_{D}
.
This property is a key ingredient in the analysis of our sparse grid approximation.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
2\leq p,{\widetilde{p}}<6
. Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a bounded operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
such that
[\mathcal{P},U_{0}]=0
and
\|\mathcal{P}\|_{\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})}\leq 1
.
Then we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}Su(\cdot,x)\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}\;\lesssim_{p,{\widetilde{p}}}\;\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime},2}_{D^{\prime}})}.

Proof.

We’re going to use the Christ–Kiselev lemma
[
6
]
to prove this lemma. Since
\mathcal{P}
and
U_{0}
commute, we have

\left\|\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{0}(t-s)u(s,x)ds\right\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}=\left\|U_{0}(t)\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{0}(s)^{*}u(s,x)ds\right\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}.

Applying (
3.6a
) and using
[\mathcal{P},U_{0}]=0
gives

\left\|\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{0}(t-s)u(s,x)ds\right\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}\lesssim_{p}\left\|\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{0}(s)^{*}u(s,x)ds\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Since
\|\mathcal{P}\|_{\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})}\leq 1
, by (
3.6b
) we further obtain

\left\|\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{0}(t-s)u(s,x)ds\right\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}\lesssim_{p}\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}U_{0}(s)^{*}u(s,x)ds\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\lesssim_{p,{\widetilde{p}}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime},2}_{D^{\prime}})}.

Then (
3.8
) follows from the Christ–Kiselev lemma, hence the proposition.
∎

In addition, if the projector
\mathcal{P}_{R}
further satisfies Assumption
1.5
,
we obtain the following estimate (
3.9b
) as a corollary of Proposition
3.7
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{R}
satisfy Assumption
1.5
.
For
D,D^{\prime}\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
with
1\leq|D|,|D^{\prime}|\leq 2
and
2\leq p,\widetilde{p}<6
, we have

(3.9a)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{R}Su\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,\widetilde{p}}\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{\widetilde{p}^{\prime},2}_{D^{\prime}})},

(3.9b)

\displaystyle\left\|\Big(\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq 2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}\Big)^{-1}(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Su\right\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{p}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{p,2}_{D})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,\widetilde{p}}\frac{1}{R}\,\|u\|_{L_{t}^{\theta_{\widetilde{p}}^{\prime}}({\mathbb{R}},L^{\widetilde{p}^{\prime},2}_{D^{\prime}})}.

Proof.

By the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{R}
, we have

[\mathcal{P}_{R},U_{0}]=0,\qquad\|\mathcal{P}_{R}u\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\leq\|u\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Thus, Proposition
3.7
with
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{R}
gives (
3.9a
). For (
3.9b
), recall from Assumption
1.5
that

(3.10)

\displaystyle R\Big\|\Big(\sum_{\ell=1,2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}\Big)^{-1}(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Since
[{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}},U_{0}]=0
, we may set

\mathcal{P}:=R\,(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})\Big(\sum_{\ell=1,2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}\Big)^{-1}
.
Then
[\mathcal{P},U_{0}]=0
and
\|\mathcal{P}\|_{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}\leq 1
. Applying Proposition
3.7
to this
\mathcal{P}
yields (
3.9b
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

5.
Justification of the sparse grid approximation (
1.9
)

Now we are going to prove Theorem
2.5
, in particular (
2.11
). Before going further, we need the following result which can be regarded as an evolution version of (
1.7
).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in H_{I_{1},\rm mix}^{1}\bigcap H_{I_{2},\rm mix}^{1}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{R}
be a projector satisfying Assumption
1.5
. Under Assumption
2.1
we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u\|_{X_{p,T}}\lesssim_{p}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u_{0}\|_{H_{I_{\ell},\rm mix}^{1}}.

Proof.

By Duhamel formula(
3.3
), we have that

(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u(t)=(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})U_{0}(t)u_{0}-i(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t).

Thus, by (
3.6a
),

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u\|_{X_{p,T}}

\displaystyle\leq\|U_{0}(t)(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u_{0}\|_{X_{p,T}}+\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t)\|_{X_{p,T}}\lesssim_{p}\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}+\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t)\|_{X_{p,T}}.

According to (
3.10
), we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\leq\frac{1}{R}\big\|\sum_{\ell=1,2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}u_{0}\big\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\leq\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u\|_{H_{I_{\ell},\rm mix}^{1}}.

Thus, using (
3.6a
),

(5.3)

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})u\|_{X_{p,T}}\lesssim_{p}R^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u_{0}\|_{H_{I_{\ell},\rm mix}^{1}}+\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t)\|_{X_{p,T}}.

It remains to show that
\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t)\|_{X_{p,T}}\lesssim_{p}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u_{0}\|_{H_{I_{\ell},\rm mix}^{1}}
. To do so, here we are going to prove

(5.4)

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t)\|_{X_{p,T}}\lesssim_{p}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u(t)\|_{X^{1}_{I_{\ell},p,T}}.

Then by (
2.10
), under Assumption
2.1
we have

\displaystyle\|(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t)\|_{X_{p,T}}\lesssim_{p}\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u_{0}\|_{H_{I_{\ell},\rm mix}^{1}}.

This and (
5.3
) give (
5.1
). Hence this lemma.

To end the proof, we prove (
5.4
) by using Corollary
3.8
.
As in the proof of mixed regularity, we consider this problem in the scalar product:
for any
D\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
with
1\leq|D|\leq 2
, and for any

u(t,x)\in X^{1}_{I,p,T}
and
v(t,x)\in L_{t}^{\theta^{\prime}_{p}}(I_{T},L^{p^{\prime},2}_{D})

or
v(t,x)\in L_{t}^{\infty}(I_{T},{\mathcal{H}})
, we aim to show

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\langle(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t),v(t)\rangle\;dt\right|\lesssim T^{\theta}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u\|_{X^{1}_{I_{\ell},p,T}}\min\{\|v\|_{L_{t}^{\theta^{\prime}_{p}}(I_{T},L^{p^{\prime},2}_{D})},\|v\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}(I_{T},{\mathcal{H}})}\}

for some
\theta>0
and
p>2
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\langle(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})Qu(t),v(t)\rangle\;dt\right|\leq\sum_{\ell=1,2}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}V(s,x_{j})u(s),S^{*}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\ell=1,2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}\Big)^{-1}(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})v\Big](s)\right\rangle\;ds\right|

(5.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1,2\\
1\leq j<k\leq N\end{subarray}}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}W(x_{j},x_{k})u(s),S^{*}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\ell=1,2}{\mathcal{L}}_{I_{\ell}}\Big)^{-1}(1-\mathcal{P}_{R})v\Big](s)\right\rangle\;ds\right|.

The proof of the terms on the right-hand side of (
5
)
is essentially the same as in Step 1 and Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
2.4
:
we only need to replace the Strichartz estimate (
3.6c
) used there
by the Strichartz estimate (
3.9b
).
Then, under Assumption
2.1
, we obtain (
5.4
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

We first consider the existence of solutions to (
1.9
).
The proof is essentially the same as for Theorem
2.3
,
with one modification: the Strichartz estimate (
3.6c
) used there
should be replaced by (
3.9a
), as in Lemma
5.1
.
It then follows that for every
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, the problem (
1.9
) has
a unique global-in-time solution
u_{R}\in X_{p,\infty}
.
Moreover, by (
3.3
) and the identity
\mathcal{P}_{R}^{2}=\mathcal{P}_{R}
,
we have
\mathcal{P}_{R}u_{R}=u_{R}
.

We now turn to the error estimate (
2.11
).
Observe that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}\leq\|u-\mathcal{P}_{R}u\|_{X_{p,T}}+\|\mathcal{P}_{R}u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}.

It remains to control the difference
\mathcal{P}_{R}u-u_{R}
.
By the Duhamel formula (
3.3
), we have

\mathcal{P}_{R}u-u_{R}=-\,i\,\mathcal{P}_{R}Q(u-u_{R}).

Replacing the Strichartz estimate (
3.6c
) by (
3.9a
)
and proceeding as for Step 1 and Step 2 in the proof of Theorem
2.3
,
we deduce that, under conditions (
1
)–(
2
) in Assumption
2.1

and for
0<T<1
, there exists a constant

C_{T,3}:=C_{T,3}(\alpha,p,\widetilde{p})\geq 1
such that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{P}_{R}Q(u-u_{R})\|_{X_{p,T}}\leq C_{T,3}(Z+N)N\,T^{1/\theta^{\prime}_{\widetilde{p}}-1/\theta_{p}}\,\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}.

Hence, under Assumption
2.1
,

\|\mathcal{P}_{R}u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}=\|\mathcal{P}_{R}Q(u-u_{R})\|_{X_{p,T}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}.

Substituting this bound into (

), we obtain

\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}\leq\|u-\mathcal{P}_{R}u\|_{X_{p,T}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}.

Therefore, by Lemma
5.1
, under Assumption
2.1
,

\|u-u_{R}\|_{X_{p,T}}\leq 2\|u-\mathcal{P}_{R}u\|_{X_{p,T}}\;\lesssim_{p}\;\frac{1}{R}\sum_{\ell=1,2}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{I_{\ell},\mathrm{mix}}}.

This proves (
2.11
) and completes the proof.
