Abstract
In this paper, we derive the Onsager–Machlup functional for stochastic differential equations driven by time-varying fractional noise: X t = x 0 + ∫ 0 t b s ​ ( X s ) ​ 𝑑 s + ∫ 0 t σ s ​ d ​ B s H . X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}(X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}. Our main results are established for H ∈ ( 1 / 4 , 1 ) H\in(1/4,1) by extending small ball probability estimates and the Girsanov theorem for fractional Brownian motion to the case with time-dependent coefficients. Regarding the choice of norms, for 1 4 < H < 1 2 \frac{1}{4}<H<\frac{1}{2} , the analysis is valid under the supremum norm and Hölder norms of order 0 < β < H − 1 4 0<\beta<H-\frac{1}{4} . For 1 2 < H < 1 \frac{1}{2}<H<1 , the analysis applies to Hölder norms of order β \beta satisfying H − 1 2 < β < H − 1 4 H-\frac{1}{2}<\beta<H-\frac{1}{4} . In the case H = 1 / 2 H=1/2 , the admissible norms depend on the spatial regularity of the drift coefficient b b . Specifically, if b b is n n -times continuously differentiable, then Hölder norms of order 0 < β < 1 2 − 1 2 ​ n 0<\beta<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2n} are permissible. To validate our theoretical findings, we conduct numerical simulations for a classical double-well potential system, illustrating how time-varying fractional noise affects the transition dynamics between metastable states.

Introduction

Consider the following stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by time-varying fractional noise:

X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}(X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s},

(1)

where
\sigma
is a deterministic function of time satisfying a non-degeneracy condition, and
B^{H}=\{B^{H}_{t},t\in[0,1]\}
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
.
The integral with respect to the fractional Brownian motion is interpreted in the Wiener sense.

This paper investigates the limiting behavior of the following ratio:

\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\|X_{\cdot}-\phi_{\cdot}\|\leq\varepsilon\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(\|\textstyle{\int_{0}^{\cdot}}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\|\leq\varepsilon\big)},

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We define the Onsager-Machlup functional by

\exp\big(J(\phi,\dot{\phi})\big)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(\|X_{\cdot}-\phi_{\cdot}\|\leq\varepsilon\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(\|\textstyle{\int_{0}^{\cdot}}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\|\leq\varepsilon\big)},

(2)

where
\phi-x_{0}=K_{H}^{\sigma}(\dot{\phi})
,
\dot{\phi}\in L^{2}([0,1])
. The operator
K_{H}^{\sigma}
, which depends on both
\sigma
and
H
, will be defined subsequently.

In this work, we establish a general form of the Onsager–Machlup (OM) functional for SDEs driven by time-varying fractional noise. Our main result can be expressed as

J(\phi,\dot{\phi})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\phi}_{s}-(K_{H}^{\sigma})^{-1}\!\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}b_{u}(\phi_{u})\,du\right)(s)\right)^{2}+d_{H}\,\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\,ds,

(3)

where
d_{H}
is a constant depending on
H
. The explicit form of the operator
(K_{H}^{\sigma})^{-1}
in our results varies with

H\in(1/4,1/2)
,
H=1/2
, and
H\in(1/2,1)
,
as detailed in Theorems
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
.
This result extends the work of Zhang et al.
[
18
]
to the case of fractional noise with time-dependent coefficients.
Our proof combines a generalized Girsanov transformation with techniques from fractional calculus
[
11
]
. By expressing the fractional Brownian motion as an integral with respect to standard Brownian motion,

\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}=\int_{0}^{T}(K_{H}^{*}\psi)(s)\,dW_{s},

we obtain explicit expressions for the exponential term in the OM functional and derive small ball estimates under non-stationary increments.
For
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
, we recover stronger results under Hölder norms using Itô’s calculus.

The Onsager–Machlup functional characterizes the most probable paths of diffusion processes and serves as an analogue to the Lagrangian in dynamical systems, describing the optimal evolution trajectory of a particle along a given path. A fundamental challenge is to understand how systems transition between states under stochastic perturbations. Given two states
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, and considering paths
\phi
from an appropriate function space connecting
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
, the path that maximizes
\exp(J(\phi,\dot{\phi}))
can be determined via variational methods. This optimal path represents the most probable transition path from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
. Thus, the Onsager–Machlup functional provides a deterministic characterization of the most probable transition path through the variational principle, with significant applications in physics and chemistry (see
[
1
,
5
]
).

The Onsager–Machlup functional was first introduced by Onsager and Machlup
[
13
]
in 1953. Ikeda and Watanabe
[
7
]
later established rigorous results for
\phi\in C^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
under the supremum norm. Moret and Nualart
[
11
]
pioneered the study of the Onsager–Machlup functional for SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motion, employing techniques from fractional calculus and a fractional Girsanov transform.

Recently, research on the Onsager-Machlup functional has attracted considerable attention. Zhang et al.
[
18
]
extended the theory to the case of time-varying Brownian motion, while in
[
19
]
they combined KAM theory with the Onsager-Machlup functional and large deviation principles for the stochastic nonlinear Schrödinger equation on infinite lattices, establishing the persistence and probabilistic stability of low-dimensional invariant tori. Huang et al.
[
6
]
derived the Onsager-Machlup functional for jump-diffusion processes using the probability flow approach for Markov processes. Carfagnini and Wang
[
3
]
interpret Loewner energy as the Onsager-Machlup functional for
\text{SLE}_{\kappa}
loop measure for any fixed
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
. Li and Li
[
9
]
proved that the
\Gamma
-limit of the OM functional on the space of curves is the geometric form of the FW functional in a proper time scale
T=T(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces notation and preliminary results. Section
3
presents the main theorems and their proofs. Section
4
derives the Euler–Lagrange equation for the OM functional and validates our theory through numerical simulations of a double-well system.

2.1
Fractional calculus

We first introduce the basic concepts of fractional calculus; for further details, refer to
[
14
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b])
. The integrals

\displaystyle(I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{a}^{x}(x-y)^{\alpha-1}f(y)dy,\quad x\geq a,

\displaystyle(I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{b}(y-x)^{\alpha-1}f(y)dy,\quad x\leq b,

where
\alpha>0
, are respectively called the right and left Riemann–Liouville fractional integrals of order
\alpha
.

For any
\alpha\geq 0,
any
f\in L^{p}([a,b])
and
g\in L^{q}([a,b])
where
1/p+1/q\leq\alpha,
we have:

\int_{a}^{b}f(s)(I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}g)(s)ds=\int_{a}^{b}(I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}f)(s)g(s)ds.

(4)

If
1\leq p<\infty
, we denote by
I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
the image of
L^{p}([a,b])
under the operator
I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}
. Similarly,
I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
can be defined.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\in I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
,
g\in I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
.
Each of the expressions

\displaystyle(D_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{[\alpha]+1}I_{a^{+}}^{1+[\alpha]-\alpha}f(x),

\displaystyle(D_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}g)(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(-\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{[\alpha]+1}I_{b^{-}}^{1+[\alpha]-\alpha}g(x),

are respectively called the right and left fractional derivative.

From (
4
), we deduce the formula

\int_{a}^{b}f(s)(D_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}g)(s)ds=\int_{a}^{b}(D_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}f)(s)g(s)ds,\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(5)

which holds under the assumptions that
f\in I^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}(L^{p})
and
g\in I^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}(L^{q})
satisfy
1/p+1/q\leq 1+\alpha
.

If
f\in I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
, the function
\phi
such that
f=I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(\phi)
is unique in
L^{p}
. Fractional derivatives can be regarded as the inverse operation of fractional integrals.

When
\alpha p>1
, any function in
I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
is

(\alpha-\tfrac{1}{p})
-Hölder continuous. Moreover, every Hölder continuous
function of order
\beta>\alpha
admits a fractional derivative of order

\alpha
; see
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Although fractional derivatives are originally defined as derivatives of
fractional integrals, they also admit an explicit representation in terms of
Weyl’s formula (see
[
14
, Remark 5.3]
):

D^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}f(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{f(x)}{(x-a)^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{a}^{x}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{(x-y)^{\alpha+1}}\,dy\right),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(6)

where the improper integral converges in the
L^{p}
sense. Therefore, if
f
has Hölder continuity with an exponent strictly greater than
\alpha
, the fractional derivative of order
\alpha
exists.

For the case of the right fractional derivative, we can also easily prove a similar representation.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
f\in I_{T^{-}}^{\alpha}\!\left(L^{2}[0,T]\right)
. Then

D_{T^{-}}^{\alpha}f(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{f(s)}{(T-s)^{\alpha}}-\alpha\int_{s}^{T}\frac{f(u)-f(s)}{(u-s)^{\alpha+1}}\,du\right).

(7)

Proof.

We restrict ourselves to the case
f\in C^{1}([0,T])
; situations with lower
regularity can be treated by mollification. For
0<\alpha<1
, the right fractional derivative can be written as

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{T^{-}}f(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{d}{ds}\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{-\alpha}f(u)\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f(u)-f(s)\big)\,du+f(s)\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{-\alpha}\,du\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Bigg(-\lim_{u\to s}(u-s)^{-\alpha}\big(f(u)-f(s)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\int_{s}^{T}\alpha(u-s)^{-\alpha-1}\big(f(u)-f(s)\big)\,du

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad-\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,du+\frac{(T-s)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)-(T-s)^{-\alpha}f(s)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Bigg(\frac{f(s)}{(T-s)^{\alpha}}-\alpha\int_{s}^{T}\frac{f(u)-f(s)}{(u-s)^{\alpha+1}}\,du\Bigg).

∎

2.2
Fractional Stochastic integration with respect to fractional Brownian motion

Let
W=\{W_{t},t\in[0,1]\}
be a Wiener process defined in the canonical probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, where

\Omega=C_{0}([0,1])

and
\mathbb{P}
is the Wiener measure.
A real-valued continuous process
\{B^{H}_{t},\ t\in[0,T]\}
is called a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
if it is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function

\mathbb{E}[B^{H}_{t}B^{H}_{s}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\big):=R_{H}(t,s).

For simplicity, we assume through all that
\alpha=|H-1/2|
.

When
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
, fractional Brownian motion reduces to the classical Brownian motion.
However, if
H\neq\tfrac{1}{2}
, it is neither a semi-martingale nor a Markov process. Moreover, its sample paths belong to
C^{H-\varepsilon}_{0}([0,T])

P
-a.s. for every
\varepsilon>0
.

It is well known that an fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}_{t}
admits a Wiener integral representation: there exists a deterministic kernel
K_{H}(t,s)
and a standard Brownian motion
W
such that

B^{H}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t,s)\,dW_{s},

where

K_{H}(t,s)=\begin{cases}c_{H}\,s^{-\alpha}\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)^{\alpha-1}u^{\alpha}\,du,&H>\tfrac{1}{2},\\[8.61108pt]
b_{H}\Big[(t/s)^{-\alpha}(t-s)^{-\alpha}+\alpha s^{\alpha}\int_{s}^{t}(u-s)^{-\alpha}u^{-(\alpha+1)}\,du\Big],&H<\tfrac{1}{2},\end{cases}

with constants

c_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{H(2H-1)}{\mathrm{B}(2-2H,H-\tfrac{1}{2})}},\qquad b_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{2H}{(1-2H)\mathrm{B}(1-2H,H+\tfrac{1}{2})}}.

The operator
K_{H}
, mapping from
L^{2}([0,1])
to
I_{0^{+}}^{H+\frac{1}{2}}(L^{2}([0,1]))
, is given by:

(K_{H}h)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t,s)h(s)\,ds.

(8)

From
[
11
, Lemma 10]
, the operator
K_{H}
can be expressed using fractional integrals as follows:

(K_{H}h)(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle I^{1-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}h,&H<1/2,\\
\displaystyle I^{1}_{0^{+}}\,s^{\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}h,&H>1/2.\end{cases}

The inverse operator
(K_{H})^{-1}
is then defined as:

\displaystyle(K_{H})^{-1}h

\displaystyle=s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}D^{1-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}h,\quad H<1/2,

\displaystyle(K_{H})^{-1}h

\displaystyle=s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}h^{\prime},\quad H>1/2,

(9)

for all
h\in I^{H+\frac{1}{2}}_{0^{+}}(L^{2})
. If
h
is differentiable, the operator simplifies to:

(K_{H})^{-1}h=s^{-\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{\alpha}h^{\prime},\quad H<1/2.

(10)

Moreover, the operator
(K_{H})^{-1}
preserves the adaptability property.

Stochastic integrals of deterministic functions with respect to a Gaussian process are called Wiener integrals. Stochastic integrals with respect to fractional Brownian motion can be defined by its Gaussianity. For the step function
I_{[0,t]}(\cdot)
, we assign the inner product

\langle I_{[0,t]}(\cdot),I_{[0,s]}(\cdot)\rangle_{H}=R_{H}(t,s).

We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the space obtained by completing the step functions under the above inner product.
Then the mapping
\mathcal{I}:I_{[0,t]}\longmapsto B^{H}_{t}
corresponds to an isometry between the step function under the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}
and the Gaussian variable in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
. By extending this isometry, we obtain our integral
\mathcal{I}
as an isometry from
\mathcal{H}
to the space of Gaussian random variables in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.

In order to illustrate the elements of
\mathcal{H}
and the relation between the fractional Brownian integral and the Brownian motion integral, we first introduce the following operator:

(K_{H}^{*}f)(s)=\begin{cases}c_{H}\Gamma(\alpha)\,s^{-\alpha}I_{1-}^{\alpha}\big(s^{\alpha}f_{s}\big),&H>\tfrac{1}{2},\\[4.30554pt]
b_{H}\Gamma(1-\alpha)\,s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{1^{-}}\big(s^{-\alpha}f_{s}\big),&H<\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Theorem 2.4

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
H\in(0,1)
.
If
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}:=\int_{0}^{T}(K_{H}^{*}\psi)(s)\,dW_{s}.

In particular, for
\psi_{s}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]}(s)
, we recover

B^{H}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t,s)\,dW_{s}.

Analogous to the Itô isometry, we have the following isometry for stochastic integrals with respect to fractional Brownian motion.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
2
]
)
.

If
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
, then

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\int_{0}^{T}f_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\int_{0}^{T}g_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\right)=\int_{0}^{T}(K_{H}^{*}f)(s)\,(K_{H}^{*}g)(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

For
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
, we have a more intuitive equality:

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\int_{0}^{T}f_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\int_{0}^{T}g_{s}\,\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\right)=H(2H-1)\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}f_{t}g_{s}|s-t|^{2H-2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t.

(11)

In this paper, we assume that
\sigma
is Hölder continuous of order
\gamma
with
\gamma+H>1
. Under this condition, the stochastic integral

\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}

is well defined pathwise in the sense of Young integration(see Lemma
2.6
), and this definition agrees with the Wiener integral.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
17
]
)
.

For
f\in C^{\beta}([0,1]),g\in C^{\gamma}([0,1])
, if
\beta+\gamma>1,

\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dg_{s},\quad\int_{0}^{t}g_{s}d\sigma_{s}

are well-defined. Furthermore, we have

\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dg_{s}=\sigma_{t}g_{t}-\sigma_{0}g_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}g_{s}d\sigma_{s}.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
f,g:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be functions such that
f\in C^{\beta}

and
g\in C^{\gamma}
with
\beta,\gamma\in(0,1)
and
\beta+\gamma>1
.
Then for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
, one has

\left|\int_{s}^{t}f_{r}\,dg_{r}-f_{s}(g_{t}-g_{s})\right|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\,[f]_{\beta}\,[g]_{\gamma}\,|t-s|^{\beta+\gamma}.

(12)

Proof.

For simplicity, it suffices to prove the reduced case
s=0,t=T
.
We first divide the interval
[0,T]
into
2^{n}
equal parts and set

S_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}f_{\tfrac{iT}{2^{n}}}\left(g_{\tfrac{(i+1)T}{2^{n}}}-g_{\tfrac{iT}{2^{n}}}\right).

Then,

\displaystyle|S_{n+1}-S_{n}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}f_{\tfrac{2iT}{2^{n+1}}}\left(g_{\tfrac{(2i+1)T}{2^{n+1}}}-g_{\tfrac{2iT}{2^{n+1}}}\right)+f_{\tfrac{(2i+1)T}{2^{n+1}}}\left(g_{\tfrac{(2i+2)T}{2^{n+1}}}-g_{\tfrac{(2i+1)T}{2^{n+1}}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad-\left.f_{\tfrac{iT}{2^{n}}}\left(g_{\tfrac{(i+1)T}{2^{n}}}-g_{\tfrac{iT}{2^{n}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(f_{\tfrac{(2i+1)T}{2^{n+1}}}-f_{\tfrac{iT}{2^{n}}}\right)\left(g_{\tfrac{(2i+2)T}{2^{n+1}}}-g_{\tfrac{(2i+1)T}{2^{n+1}}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}[f]_{\beta}[g]_{\gamma}\frac{T^{\beta+\gamma}}{2^{(n+1)(\beta+\gamma)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{[f]_{\beta}[g]_{\gamma}}{2^{\beta+\gamma}}\cdot\frac{T^{\beta+\gamma}}{2^{n(\beta+\gamma-1)}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}f_{s}\,dg_{s}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|S_{0}|+\frac{[f]_{\beta}[g]_{\alpha}}{2^{\beta+\gamma}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{T^{\beta+\gamma}}{2^{n(\beta+\gamma-1)}}

\displaystyle\leq|f_{0}(g_{1}-g_{0})|+\frac{[f]_{\beta}[g]_{\gamma}}{2^{\beta+\gamma}}\cdot\frac{T^{\beta+\gamma}}{1-\tfrac{1}{2^{\beta+\gamma-1}}}.

∎

2.3
Approximate limits in Wiener space

For the Onsager-Machlup functional of fractional Brownian motion,
the measurable norm and trace theory serve as powerful tools for studying
the asymptotic behavior of exponential conditional expectations.
The following content is adapted from
[
11
]
.

Let
H^{1}
be the Cameron-Martin space:

H^{1}:=\{h\in\Omega,\text{ $h$ is absolutely continuous and $h^{\prime}$}\in L^{2}([0,1])\}.

Let
Q:H^{1}\to H^{1}
be an orthogonal projection such that

\dim(QH^{1})<\infty
. Then
Q
can be represented as

Qh=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle h,h_{i}\rangle h_{i},

where
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
is an orthonormal sequence in
QH^{1}
.
We may also define the
H^{1}
-valued random variable

QW=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}(s)\,dW_{s}\right)h_{i}.

Note that
QW
does not depend on the particular choice of

(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
.

A sequence of orthogonal projections
\{Q_{n}\}
on
H^{1}
is called an

approximating sequence of projections
if
\dim(Q_{n}H_{1})<\infty

and
Q_{n}
converges strongly and monotonically to the identity operator on
H^{1}
.

Definition 2.8
.

A semi-norm
N
on
H^{1}
is called a
measurable semi-norm
if there
exists a random variable
\tilde{N}<\infty
almost surely such that
for every approximating sequence of projections
\{Q_{n}\}
on
H^{1}
,
the sequence
N(Q_{n}W)
converges in probability to
\tilde{N}
, and

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{N}\leq\varepsilon)>0\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

If, in addition,
N
is a norm on
H^{1}
, then it is called a

measurable norm
.

We will make use of the following result on measurable semi-norms.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\{N_{n}\}
be a nondecreasing sequence of measurable semi-norms.
Suppose that

We recall that an operator

is

iff

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big|\langle Ke_{n},g_{n}\rangle\big|<\infty,

for all orthonormal sequences

,

The trace of a nuclear operator

is defined by

\operatorname{Tr}K=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle Ke_{n},e_{n}\rangle,

for any orthonormal sequence

in

.
The definition is independent of the sequence we have chosen.

Given a symmetric function

If

To this end, it is necessary to first establish a small-ball probability estimate

To this end, we first derive an upper bound for its second moment.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\sigma
satisfy Assumption
(A)

Define

U_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s},\qquad V_{h}^{t}=\mathbb{E}\big[(U_{t+h}^{H}-U_{t}^{H})^{2}\big].

There exists a constant
c_{*}>0
such that

\frac{h^{2H}}{V_{h}^{t}}\geq c_{*},\quad\forall\,0\leq t,h\leq 1,\;t+h\leq 1.

Proof.

Our proof is based on Lemma
2.5
.

For
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(U_{t+h}^{H}-U_{t}^{H})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=H(2H-1)\int_{t}^{t+h}\!\!\int_{t}^{t+h}\sigma_{r}\sigma_{s}\,|s-r|^{2H-2}\,ds\,dr

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2H}.

For
H<\tfrac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(U_{t+h}^{H}-U_{t}^{H})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big\{b_{H}\Gamma(1-\alpha)s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{1^{-}}\!\big(s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}I_{[t,t+h]}(s)\big)\big\}^{2}ds,

where

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{1^{-}}(s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}I_{[t,t+h]}(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Bigg(\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}I_{[t,t+h]}(s)}{(1-s)^{\alpha}}-\alpha\int_{s}^{1}\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}I_{[t,t+h]}(u)-s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}I_{[t,t+h]}(s)}{(u-s)^{\alpha+1}}\,du\Bigg).

Moreover,

\left|\frac{\sigma_{u}-\sigma_{s}}{(u-s)^{\alpha+1}}\right|\leq\frac{[\sigma]_{\gamma}}{(u-s)^{\alpha+1-\gamma}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(U_{t+h}^{H}-U_{t}^{H})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Bigg(\int_{t}^{t+h}s^{2\alpha}\Bigg(\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}}{(t+h-s)^{\alpha}}-\alpha\int_{s}^{t+h}\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}-s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}}{(u-s)^{\alpha+1}}\,du\Bigg)^{2}ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}s^{2\alpha}\Bigg(\int_{t}^{t+h}(u-s)^{-\alpha-1}u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}\,du\Bigg)^{2}ds\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{2H}+C\int_{t}^{t+h}(t+h-s)^{2(\gamma-\alpha)}\,ds+C\int_{0}^{t}\big((t+h-s)^{-\alpha}-(t-s)^{-\alpha}\big)^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2Ch^{2H}+Ch^{2(\gamma-\alpha)+1}+C\int_{0}^{t}\big((h+x)^{-\alpha}-x^{-\alpha}\big)^{2}dx.

Finally,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\big((h+x)^{-\alpha}-x^{-\alpha}\big)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t-h}\big((h+x)^{-\alpha}-x^{-\alpha}\big)^{2}dx+\int_{t-h}^{t}\big((h+x)^{-\alpha}-x^{-\alpha}\big)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{h}^{1}\frac{h^{2}}{(x+h)^{2\alpha}x^{2}}dx+\int_{0}^{h}\frac{h^{2\alpha}}{(x+h)^{2\alpha}x^{2\alpha}}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(h^{1-2\alpha}+h^{2-2\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Ch^{2H}.

Hence

\frac{h^{2H}}{V_{h}^{t}}\geq c^{*}.

∎

\displaystyle\bigl|\sigma_{s}(B^{H}(h)(t)-B^{H}(h)(s))\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\,[\sigma]_{\alpha}\,[B^{H}(h)]_{\beta}\,|t-s|^{\alpha+\beta}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{|B^{H}(h)(t)-B^{H}(h)(s)|}{|t-s|^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\,C_{\alpha,\beta}\,[\sigma]_{\alpha}\,[B^{H}(h)]_{\beta}\,|t-s|^{\alpha}.

Hence, for
0\leq s,t\leq T<1
,

\displaystyle[B^{H}(h)]_{\beta,T}\leq\frac{1}{m}\,C_{\alpha,\beta}\,[\sigma]_{\alpha}\,T^{\alpha}\,[B^{H}(h)]_{\beta,T}.

Choosing
T
sufficiently small so that
\frac{1}{m}C_{\alpha,\beta}[\sigma]_{\alpha}T^{\alpha}<1
, we deduce
[B^{H}(h)]_{\beta,T}=0
.
Together with
B^{H}(h)(0)=0
, this implies
B^{H}(h)=0
on
[0,T]
. Iterating this argument yields
B^{H}(h)=0
on
[0,1]
.

Next, we prove
N^{H}(h)
is a measurable norm on
H^{1}
.
For
0\leq t\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}(Q_{n}W)(s)-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\Bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}^{\prime}\,ds)^{2}\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,d\int_{0}^{s}K^{H}(s,u)h_{i}^{\prime}(u)\,du\Big)^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}^{*}\sigma_{s}h_{i}^{\prime}\,ds\right)\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,d\int_{0}^{s}K^{H}(s,u)h_{i}^{\prime}(u)\,du\Big)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}(K_{H}^{*}\sigma)^{2}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 0,\quad n\to\infty.

(24)

We define a sequence of semi-norms
\{N^{H}_{m}\}
on
H^{1}
by

N^{H}_{m}(h):=\sup_{1\leq j\leq 2^{m}}\left|\int_{0}^{j/2^{m}}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}(h)(s)\right|.

According to (
24
), we obtain

N^{H}_{m}(Q_{n}W):=\sup_{1\leq j\leq 2^{m}}\left|\int_{0}^{j/2^{m}}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right|.

Moreover, it is clear that

\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{N}^{H}_{m}\leq\varepsilon\big)>0,

since
\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}
is a centered Gaussian process.
Consequently,
\tilde{N}^{H}_{m}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\tilde{N}
, and

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{N}\leq\varepsilon)\geq\exp\Big(-c\,\varepsilon^{-1/H}\Big)>0.

Therefore, by Lemma
2.9
,
N^{H}
defines a measurable norm on
H^{1}
.

∎

[

)

.

For a non-negative random variable

, its exponential moment can be calculated using its tail probabilities:

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Y}]=1+\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\lambda s}\mathbb{P}(Y>s)\,ds,\quad\forall\ \lambda>0.

(

(25)

3
Main results

In this section, we compute the Onsager–Machlup functional for the singular case (
1/4<H<1/2
), the regular case (
1/2<H<1
), and the standard case (
H=1/2
).
The distinction among them stems from the fact that the expression of
(K_{H}^{\sigma})^{-1}
corresponds to fundamentally different properties of fractional integrals and fractional derivatives for different values of
H
.
We first demonstrate how to simplify the Onsager-Machlup functional by means of Girsanov’s theorem, and then present the detailed computation of the main results.
Moreover, for the standard case
H=1/2
, we provide a stronger result that holds in terms of the Hölder norm via Itô’s formula—an approach not feasible in the nonstandard cases due to the presence of fractional integrals or derivatives.

3.1
Girsanov transform

We define the operator
K_{H}^{\sigma}
on
L^{2}([0,1])
, associated with
\sigma_{\cdot}
and
K_{H}
, by

(K_{H}^{\sigma}f)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,d(K_{H}f)(s),\qquad t\in[0,1].

(26)

From the proof of Theorem
2.19
, we deduce that
K_{H}^{\sigma}
is injective.
Hence, we can define its inverse

(K_{H}^{\sigma})^{-1}:K_{H}^{\sigma}(L^{2}([0,1]))\to L^{2}([0,1]),\qquad f\mapsto(K_{H})^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\,df_{s}\right).

For any
\phi
such that
\phi-x_{0}\in K_{H}^{\sigma}(L^{2}([0,1]))
, we denote by
\dot{\phi}
the element of
L^{2}([0,1])
satisfying

\phi-x_{0}=K_{H}^{\sigma}(\dot{\phi}).

It is worth noting that the operator
K_{H}
is an isomorphism from
L^{2}([0,1])
onto

I_{0^{+}}^{H+\frac{1}{2}}(L^{2}([0,1]))

(see
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
).
Consequently,
K_{H}(\dot{\phi})
is
H
-Hölder continuous, and therefore
\phi=x_{0}+K_{H}^{\sigma}(\dot{\phi})
also enjoys
H
-Hölder continuity by Lemma
2.7
.
In the following, we introduce a method to simplify the Onsager–Machlup functional by means of Girsanov’s theorem.

Let

\tilde{X}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}+\phi_{t}.

We define a new process

\tilde{W}_{t}=W_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\dot{\phi}_{s}-(K_{H}^{\sigma})^{-1}\!\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}b_{u}(\tilde{X}_{u})\,du\right)(s)\right]ds.

(27)

If
\tilde{W}_{t}
satisfies the Novikov condition (as will be proved in the following lemma),
there exists a probability measure
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
, absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
,
such that
\tilde{W}_{t}
is a Brownian motion under
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
.
The corresponding fractional Brownian motion associated with
\tilde{W}_{t}
is given by

\tilde{B}^{H}_{t}=B^{H}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\,d\phi_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\tilde{X}_{s})\,ds.

Taking the differential of
\tilde{X}_{t}
yields

\displaystyle d\tilde{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=d\phi_{t}+\sigma_{t}\,dB^{H}_{t}

\displaystyle=d\phi_{t}+\sigma_{t}\,d\!\left(\tilde{B}^{H}_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\,d\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\tilde{X}_{s})\,ds\right)

\displaystyle=b_{t}(\tilde{X}_{t})\,dt+\sigma_{t}\,d\tilde{B}^{H}_{t}.

This implies that
(\tilde{X},\tilde{B}^{H})
is the strong solution of (
1
) under the probability measure
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
.

Define

u_{s}=\dot{\phi}_{s}-(K_{H}^{\sigma})^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}b_{u}(\tilde{X}_{u})\,du\right)(s).

(28)

Thus, the Onsager-Machlup functional can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(\|X-\phi\|\leq\varepsilon)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\|\tilde{X}-\phi\|\leq\varepsilon)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}u_{s}\,dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u_{s}^{2}\,ds\right)\,\middle|\,\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\right].

Next, we prove the validity of this Girsanov theorem.

Lemma 3.1
.

If the coefficients satisfy
Assumption (A)
,
the transformation (
27
) satisfies the Novikov condition.

Proof.

We need to prove that

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u_{s}^{2}\,ds\right)\right)<\infty.

(29)

For the case
H\leq 1/2
, we have

u_{s}=\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}\big(s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})\big),

where

\displaystyle\big|s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}\big(s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{s^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{s}(s-u)^{\alpha-1}u^{\alpha}\sigma_{u}^{-1}b_{u}\!\left(\phi_{u}+\int_{0}^{u}\sigma_{v}\,dB^{H}_{v}\right)du

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty,

due to the boundedness of
b
. Hence, condition (
29
) follows.

For the case
H>1/2
, we enlarge
|u_{s}|^{2}
into a bounded part and a stochastic part
by applying Young’s inequality with
\varepsilon
. Specifically,

\displaystyle|u_{s}|^{2}\leq(1+1/\epsilon_{1})\dot{\phi}_{s}^{2}+(1+\epsilon_{1})\Big|s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\big(s^{-\alpha}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})\big)\Big|^{2},

where

\displaystyle\Big|s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\big(s^{-\alpha}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})\big)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s^{2\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})}{s^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})-r^{-\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}b_{r}(\phi_{r}+\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s^{2\alpha}(1+1/\epsilon_{2})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})}{s^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})-r^{-\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{s^{2\alpha}\alpha^{2}(1+\epsilon_{2})}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{2}}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})-b_{r}(\phi_{r}+\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})}{r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Bigg)^{2}.

We further enlarge the stochastic part in the above inequality:

\displaystyle\Bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})-b_{r}(\phi_{r}+\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u})}{r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}^{2}}{m^{2}}\Bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{|\phi_{s}-\phi_{r}|+|\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}-\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}|}{r^{\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}^{2}}{m^{2}}\Bigg((1+1/\epsilon_{3})\Big(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{|\phi_{s}-\phi_{r}|}{r^{\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+\epsilon_{3})\Big(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\big|\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}-\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\big|}{r^{\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Big)^{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}^{2}}{m^{2}}\Bigg((1+1/\epsilon_{3})\Big(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{|\phi_{s}-\phi_{r}|}{r^{\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}\,dr\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1+\epsilon_{3})\Big\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\Big\|_{\beta,T}^{2}\,s^{2(\beta-2\alpha)}\,\mathrm{B}(1-\alpha,\beta-\alpha)^{2}\Bigg).

Consequently, we require

\mathbb{E}\Bigg(\exp\!\Bigg(\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{2}^{2}T^{2\beta+1}\mathrm{B}(1-\alpha,\beta-\alpha)^{2}}{2(2\beta+1)m^{2}\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}(1+\epsilon_{i})\Big\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\Big\|_{\beta,T}^{2}\Bigg)\Bigg)<\infty.

(30)

According to Lemma
2.21
and Lemma
2.22
, if

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}L_{2}^{2}T^{2\beta+1}\mathrm{B}(1-\alpha,\beta-\alpha)^{2}}{2(2\beta+1)m^{2}\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{2}}<\frac{1}{2M^{2}T^{2(H-\beta)}},

that is,

T^{2H+1}<\frac{m^{2}(2\beta+1)\Gamma(1+\beta)^{2}}{M^{2}\alpha^{2}L_{2}^{2}\Gamma(\beta-\alpha)^{2}},

(31)

then condition (
30
) holds.
∎

In the following, we compute the exponential term obtained above to derive the main results.

3.2
Singular case

In this section we will compute the Onsager-Machlup functional for
\frac{1}{4}<H<\frac{1}{2}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be the solution of (
1
), and assume that the coefficients satisfy Assumption
(A)
.
For any
\phi\in K_{H}^{\sigma}(L^{2}([0,1]))
with
\phi_{0}=x_{0}
, when
\tfrac{1}{4}<H<\tfrac{1}{2}
,
the Onsager–Machlup functional of
X
with respect to the norms

\|\cdot\|_{\beta}
, where
H-\tfrac{1}{2}<\beta<H-\tfrac{1}{4}
,
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, can be expressed as

J(\phi,\dot{\phi})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\,\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}\,s^{\alpha}\,\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\right)^{2}+d_{H}\,\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\,ds,

where

d_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{2H\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+H\right)\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{3}{2}-H\right)}{\Gamma(2-2H)}}.

Proof.

Here we only provide the proof for the Hölder norm case; the proof for the supremum norm is similar.

According to the Girsanov theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|X-\phi\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\exp\Big[-\int_{0}^{1}u_{s}dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u_{s}^{2}ds\Big]I_{\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}B^{H}_{s}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp(A_{1}+A_{2})I_{\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}B^{H}_{s}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon}\right),

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}\right)dW_{s},

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}\right)\right)^{2}ds.

The limit of
\mathbb{E}(\exp(A_{2})|\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}B^{H}_{s}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)
is

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)\right)^{2}ds\right),

which can derived by
\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}dB^{H}_{s}\|_{\beta}\to 0
.

Define

A_{1}=B_{1}+B_{2},

where

B_{1}=-\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\phi}_{s}dW_{s},

and

B_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}\right)dW_{s}.

According to Theorem
2.10
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(B_{1}\right)\mid\tilde{N}<\varepsilon\right)=1.

Using Taylor’s expansion, we divide
B_{2}
into three parts:

B_{2}=C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3},

where

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)dW_{s},

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}\right)dW_{s},

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}R_{s}dW_{s}.

According to Theorem
2.10
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(C_{1}\right)\mid\tilde{N}<\varepsilon\right)=1.

Next, we consider the
C_{2}
term:

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{s})\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\,dW_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{s})\left(\int_{0}^{1}(K_{H}^{*}\sigma I_{[0,s]})(u)\,dW_{u}\right)dW_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{s})\left(\int_{0}^{1}b_{H}\Gamma(1-\alpha)u^{\alpha}D_{1-}^{\alpha}u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}I_{[0,s]}(u)\,dW_{u}\right)dW_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}s^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{s}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{r})\left(\int_{0}^{r}b_{H}\Gamma(1-\alpha)u^{\alpha}D_{r-}^{\alpha}u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)dr\,dW_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s}f(s,u)\,dW_{u}dW_{s},

where

\displaystyle f(s,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b_{H}\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}D_{r-}^{\alpha}u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}\,dr

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\left(\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(r-u)^{\alpha}}-\alpha\int_{u}^{r}\frac{v^{-\alpha}\sigma_{v}-u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(v-u)^{\alpha+1}}dv\right)dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{1}(s,u)+D_{2}(s,u)+D_{3}(s,u).

In particular,

\displaystyle D_{1}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(r-u)^{\alpha}}\,dr,

\displaystyle D_{2}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\int_{u}^{r}\frac{v^{-\alpha}\sigma_{v}-u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(v-u)^{\alpha+1}}dv\,dr,

\displaystyle D_{3}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\int_{u}^{r}\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{v}-u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(v-u)^{\alpha+1}}dv\,dr.

Then we compute the limits of
D_{1}(s,u)
,
D_{2}(s,u)
and
D_{3}(s,u)
as

:

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to s}D_{1}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\lim_{u\to s}\frac{b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b_{r}(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(r-u)^{\alpha}}\,dr

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\frac{b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\lim_{u\to s}\int_{u}^{s}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}(r-u)^{-\alpha}\,dr

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\frac{b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha-1}x^{-\alpha}\,dx

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\frac{b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}B(1-\alpha,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\,b_{H}\,\Gamma(1-\alpha).

Next, for the second term we have

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to s}D_{2}(s,u)

\displaystyle=\lim_{u\to s}\frac{-\alpha b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b_{r}(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\int_{u}^{r}\frac{v^{-\alpha}\sigma_{v}-u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{v}}{(v-u)^{\alpha+1}}\,dv\,dr

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})s^{-1}\lim_{u\to s}\frac{\alpha^{2}b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)(1-\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}(r-u)^{1-\alpha}\,dr

\displaystyle=0.

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to s}|D_{3}(s,u)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to s}\left|\frac{\alpha b_{H}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{u}^{s}s^{-\alpha}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}\partial_{x}b_{r}(\phi_{r})u^{\alpha}\int_{u}^{r}\frac{u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{v}-u^{-\alpha}\sigma_{u}}{(v-u)^{\alpha+1}}\,dv\,dr\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha b_{H}[\sigma]_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(\alpha)m}\left|\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\lim_{u\to s}\int_{u}^{s}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}\int_{u}^{r}(v-u)^{\gamma-\alpha-1}\,dv\,dr\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha b_{H}[\sigma]_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(\alpha)m(\gamma-\alpha)}\left|\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\lim_{u\to s}\int_{u}^{s}(s-r)^{\alpha-1}(r-u)^{\gamma-\alpha}\,dr\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Hence

f(s,s)=D_{1}(s,s)=\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\,b_{H}\,\Gamma(1-\alpha).

It follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(C_{2})\,\middle|\,\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}f(s,s)\,ds\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{d_{H}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{s})\,ds\right),

where

\displaystyle d_{H}

\displaystyle=b_{H}\Gamma(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(1/2+H)\sqrt{\frac{2H}{(1-2H)\mathrm{B}(1-2H,H+\tfrac{1}{2})}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2H\Gamma(1/2+H)\Gamma(3/2-H)}{(1-2H)\Gamma(1-2H)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2H\Gamma(1/2+H)\Gamma(3/2-H)}{\Gamma(2-2H)}}.

It only remains to study the behaviour of the term
C_{3}
.
Define

M_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}R_{s}dW_{s},

which is a martingale due to the adaptedness of
R_{s}
. We have that
its quadratic variation satisfies

\langle M\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}R_{s}ds\leq C_{R}\varepsilon^{4}.

(33)

Applying the exponential martingale inequality (
2.24
), we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{1}s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}R_{s}dW_{s}\right|>\xi,\left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}\right).

Combining the small ball probability estimate (
19
), we obtain the tail estimate for the conditional probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{1}s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}R_{s}dW_{s}\right|>\xi\ \middle|\ \left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}\right)\exp(K_{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{-1}{H-\beta}}).

Using above inequality, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp(C_{3})\ \middle|\ \left\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\right)\leq 1.

For a detailed proof, we refer to
[

,
20
]
.

Combining the above results, the functional takes the form

\displaystyle D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{s^{2\alpha}\sigma_{s}}+\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}}-\frac{b_{r}(\phi_{r}+\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}}}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}dr\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\Bigg(\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{s^{2\alpha}\sigma_{s}}+\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{s^{\alpha}\sigma_{s}}-\frac{b_{r}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}}}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}dr

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\frac{b_{r}(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}}-\frac{b_{r}(\phi_{r}+\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u})}{r^{\alpha}\sigma_{r}}}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}dr\Bigg)

\displaystyle D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\left(\phi_{s}^{\prime}-b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)\right),\quad\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}dB^{H}_{s}\|\to 0.

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)\right)^{2}ds\right).

Next, we decompose

\displaystyle B_{2}

, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\left(b_{s}(\phi_{s})+\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}+R_{s}\right)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s})ds,

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3}.

In particular,

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{H}\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}b(\phi_{s})u^{-\alpha}I_{s^{-}}^{\alpha}(u^{\alpha}\sigma_{u}),

\displaystyle D_{2}

Next, decompose

\displaystyle|E_{2}(s,u)|

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(C_{2})\,\middle|\,\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}f(s,s)\,ds\right)

\displaystyle R_{s}

\displaystyle=b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}\right)-b_{s}(\phi_{s})-b_{s}(\phi_{s})\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}dB^{H}_{u}

\displaystyle|R_{s}-R_{r}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\Big|\partial_{xx}b_{s}\!\left(\mu\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}+\phi_{s}\right)

\displaystyle-\partial_{xx}b_{r}\!\left(\mu\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}+\phi_{r}\right)\Big|\,d\mu\,d\lambda

\displaystyle+\Bigg|\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}-\bigg(\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\Big|\partial_{xx}b_{s}\!\left(\mu\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}+\phi_{s}\right)\Big|\,d\mu\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}L_{3}\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}\Big(\mu\Big|\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}-\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\Big|+|\phi_{s}-\phi_{r}|\Big)\,d\mu\,d\lambda

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}L_{3}\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}\Big(2\|b\|_{\infty}|s-r|+2|s-r|^{H}\Big)\,d\mu\,d\lambda

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\lambda}\mu\|b_{xx}\|_{\infty}\Bigg|\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}-\bigg(\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}\Bigg|\,d\mu\,d\lambda

\displaystyle L_{3}\Big(|\phi_{s}-\phi_{r}|+\Big|\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}-\int_{0}^{r}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\Big|+2\|b\|_{\infty}|s-r|+2|s-r|^{H}\Big)\bigg(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dB^{H}_{u}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle|s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}R_{s}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}s^{\alpha}\left|\left(\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}R_{s}}{s^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{0}^{s}\frac{s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}R_{s}-r^{-\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}R_{r}}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}dr\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left|\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{s^{\alpha}}+s^{-\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{-\alpha})(1-x)^{\alpha+1}dx\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\int_{0}^{s}\frac{r^{-\alpha}R_{s}(\sigma_{s}^{-1}-\sigma_{r}^{-1})}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}dr+\int_{0}^{s}\frac{r^{-\alpha}\sigma_{r}^{-1}(R_{s}-R_{r})}{(s-r)^{\alpha+1}}ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\varepsilon^{2}.

By proceeding in the same way as in the case
H<\tfrac{1}{2}
, we obtain

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(C_{3})\,\middle|\,\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB^{H}_{s}\right\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\right]=1.

Combining the above results, the functional takes the form

J(\phi,\dot{\phi})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\right)^{2}+d_{H}\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\,ds.

∎

3.4
Standard case

In this section, we will prove the case
H=1/2
.
By applying Itô’s formula, we can extend the range of the Hölder norm considered in
[
18
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
X
be the solution of (
1
), and assume that the coefficients satisfy Assumption
(A)
.
For any
\phi\in K_{H}^{\sigma}(L^{2}([0,1]))
with
\phi_{0}=x_{0}
, when
H=1/2
, the Onsager–Machlup functional of
X
with respect to the norms

\|\cdot\|_{\beta}
with
0<\beta<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2n}
, and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
can be expressed as follows:

J(\phi,\phi^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\phi_{s}^{\prime}-b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)}{\sigma_{s}}\right)^{2}+\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})ds.

Proof.

We only need to consider the limiting behavior of the stochastic integral term.
By Taylor’s expansion, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}\!\left(\phi_{s}+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)dW_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{s}^{-1}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\,dW_{s}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k}dW_{s}+\int_{0}^{1}R_{s}\,dW_{s}.

For
k\geq 2
, integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\sigma_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k}dW_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})}{(k+1)\sigma_{s}^{2}}d\!\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k+1}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k}{2}\,\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k-1}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})}{(k+1)\sigma_{s}^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k+1}\right|_{s=1}-\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k+1}\frac{d}{ds}\!\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})}{(k+1)\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)ds

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{k}{2}\,\partial_{x}^{k}b_{s}(\phi_{s})\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma_{u}\,dW_{u}\right)^{k-1}ds

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;0,\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

Moreover, the quadratic variation of the remainder satisfies

\left\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}R_{s}\,dW_{s}\right\rangle_{t}\;\leq\;C\varepsilon^{2n},

and hence

\mathbb{P}\!\Bigg(\Big|\int_{0}^{1}R_{s}\,dW_{s}\Big|>\xi\;\Big|\;\Big\|\int_{0}^{\cdot}\sigma_{s}\,dW_{s}\Big\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon\Bigg)\;\leq\;\exp\!\Big(-C_{1}\tfrac{\xi^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2n}}+C_{2}\varepsilon^{-2/(1-2\beta)}\Big).

Since
2n>2/(1-2\beta)
, we require

\beta<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2n}.

Summing up, we obtain:

J(\phi,\phi^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\phi_{s}^{\prime}-b_{s}\left(\phi_{s}\right)}{\sigma_{s}}\right)^{2}+\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s})ds.

∎

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we illustrate and validate our main theoretical results through numerical simulations of a specific stochastic differential equation (SDE). For a given SDE, we determine the most probable transition path from
X_{0}=x_{0}
to
X_{1}=x_{1}
using the Onsager–Machlup functional. To rigorously validate our results, we compare the obtained most probable path against simulated sample paths and their empirical mean trajectories.

The necessary condition for the most probable path is characterized by the following Euler–Lagrange equations:

•

For
1/4<H<1/2
:

2\left(\frac{d}{ds}+\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})\right)\left[\sigma_{s}^{-1}s^{\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{1^{-}}s^{-\alpha}\left((\phi_{s}^{*})^{\prime}-s^{-\alpha}I_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{\alpha}\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})}{\sigma_{s}}\right)\right]=d_{H}\partial_{xx}b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*}),

•

For
1/2<H<1
:

2\left(\frac{d}{ds}+\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})\right)\left[\sigma_{s}^{-1}s^{-\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{1^{-}}s^{\alpha}\left((\phi_{s}^{*})^{\prime}-s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}\frac{b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})}{\sigma_{s}}\right)\right]=d_{H}\partial_{xx}b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*}),

•

For
H=1/2
:

2\left(\frac{d}{ds}+\partial_{x}b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})\right)\left[\frac{(\phi_{s}^{*})^{\prime}-b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right]-\partial_{xx}b_{s}(\phi_{s}^{*})=0.

All equations are subject to the boundary conditions:

\phi_{0}^{*}=x_{0},\quad\phi_{1}^{*}=x_{1}.

We now consider an illustrative example: a double-well system driven by time-varying fractional noise.

Example 4.1
.

Consider the stochastic differential equation

dX_{t}=\frac{-X_{t}(X_{t}^{2}-a^{2})}{(1+X_{t}^{2})^{2}}\,dt+c\bigl(2+\sin(2n\pi t)\bigr)\,dB^{H}_{t},

(34)

where
a>0
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
The coefficients of this equation satisfy our theoretical assumptions.

The corresponding noise-free system

\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}=\frac{-X_{t}(X_{t}^{2}-a^{2})}{(1+X_{t}^{2})^{2}}

(35)

exhibits stable equilibrium states at
x=\pm a
and an unstable equilibrium at
x=0
.
For system (
35
), the solution starting from
-a
remains at
-a
.
However, when noise is introduced in system (
34
),
solutions starting from
-a
can escape this stable equilibrium,
with some sample paths reaching the other stable equilibrium at
a
.

We investigate the most probable transition path of system (
34
) from
X_{0}=-a
to
X_{t}=a
.
Fixing
a=1
, we perform numerical simulations to plot the most probable paths,
sample paths, and their mean trajectories for different values of
H
,
n
, and
c
.

The drift term satisfies
|b(x)|=\left|\frac{-x(x^{2}-1)}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}\right|<\infty
and

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}b(x)|=\left|\frac{x^{4}-6x^{2}+1}{(1+x^{2})^{3}}\right|\leq 1.

It is straightforward to verify that Assumption
(A)
is satisfied when
0<H\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Moreover, for
H=0.6
and
\beta=0.34
, we have

\frac{(2\beta+1)\Gamma(1+\beta)^{2}}{9\alpha^{2}\Gamma(\beta-\alpha)^{2}}\approx 1.015>1,

(36)

which indicates that Assumption
(A)
is also fulfilled in this case.

Thus, assumption
(A)
are satisfied for
H=0.3,0.5,
and
0.6
.
We conduct numerical simulations for these three values of
H
with parameter pairs
(c,n)=(1,1),(1/2,1),
and
(1,4)
.

The simulations, illustrated in Figures
1
–
3
, yield the following observations:

1.

The introduction of noise enables the system to transition between the two stable equilibria, a phenomenon that is precluded in the deterministic system.

2.

The smoothness of the sample paths varies significantly with the Hurst parameter
H
. Paths for
H=0.6
are notably smoother, which is consistent with the higher regularity of the fractional Brownian motion for
H>1/2
. In contrast, paths for

H<1/2
.

3.

The periodic modulation of the noise coefficient, through the
\sin(2n\pi t)
term, is clearly imprinted on the dynamics. Both the sample paths and the most probable path display oscillations whose frequency correlates directly with the parameter
n
. This is particularly evident when comparing cases with
n=1
and
n=4
.

4.

The empirical mean of the sample paths converges closer to the theoretically derived most probable path when the noise intensity coefficient
c
is reduced from
1
to
1/2
. This can be understood from the perspective of the OM action functional: any deviation from the most probable path
\phi^{*}
incurs a higher “action” or “energy cost”. In the small noise regime, paths with significantly higher action are exponentially suppressed. Therefore, the sample paths are statistically concentrated around the minimizer of the action, which is the most probable path.

These results demonstrate that our theoretical framework effectively captures the essential features of the transition dynamics, including the influence of noise regularity (governed by
H
), noise intensity (
c
), and temporal structure.
