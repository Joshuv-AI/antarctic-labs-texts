Clinical Epidemiology, Patterns and Treatment of Maxillofacial Fractures in Children and Adolescents: A 15-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Canyang Jiang, Yan Jiang, Xiaojing Zhang, Yali Wen, Lisong Lin, Li Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to describe the main clinical epidemiology, characteristics and treatment of pediatric maxillofacial fractures in different age groups.MethodsThis research was a retrospective study that was conducted for 15 years in a tertiary hospital for oral and maxillofacial surgery from southeast China. Age, sex, etiology, type of fracture, treatment and concomitant injury were collected and analyzed from the data of the enrolled patients aged 0-18 years. The patients were categorized into three groups: ≤6 years old, 7–12 years old, and 13–18 years old. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, Version 27.ResultsThe incidence was 16.37% (n=346/2114) and a total of 341 patients with maxillofacial fractures were followed up for at least 6 months. Fall from a height (FAH) was the primary cause of maxillofacial fractures in 0-6 age group, whereas Fall at ground level (FAG) predominated in 7-18 age group. Among the mandibular fractures, isolated condylar fractures were predominantly unilateral (p < 0.001, Cramér's V= 0.439), whereas condylar fractures that occured alongside a symphysis fracture were more likely to be bilateral (p< 0.001, Cramér's V=0.297).ConclusionsThere were different clinical epidemiological features and characteristics of pediatric maxillofacial fractures in different age groups. And condylar fractures exhibit distinct pathogenic patterns and require specific therapeutic considerations. However,there were also some limitations in this retrospective study.

Background

Maxillofacial fractures in minors account for less than 15% of all facial fractures [1–3]. Several factors contribute to the lower incidence of facial fractures in pediatrics compared to adults. These include the relatively lower mineralization degree of the facial skeleton, thicker fat pads, reduced pneumatization of the paranasal sinuses, and greater flexibility of the sutures [4]. Besides, from infancy through the mixed dentition, the cranium is proportionally larger than the face providing relative protection against facial injuries [5–7]. The characteristics of pediatric maxillofacial trauma are influenced by age, gender and region, with a certain degree of variation [8, 9]. Studies have shown that maxillofacial fractures account for only 0.87% to 1% under 5 years old, and in kids and teenagers till they turn eighteen, it ranges from 4.6% to 14.7% [1, 2, 10]. These indicate that there may be different incidence rates for different age groups.

According to a recent meta-analysis [11], the global prevalence of trauma in children and adolescents was mainly attributed to motor vehicle accidents (33.8% of all cases) and falls (20.7% of all cases). Maxillofacial trauma attributed to falls was most commonly observed in Asian countries (44.1%). Craniofacial trauma in North America was attributed mainly to interpersonal violence (27.6%) and involvement in sports activities (13.3%). Falls (47%) were the significant cause, followed by road traffic accidents/motor vehicle collisions and injuries from sports among children and adolescents of South Asia [12]. However, a limited number of studies have delved into the variations in etiology across different age groups. Since children and adolescents are in the critical stage of growth and development, the anatomical structural characteristics of facial bones vary among different age groups [13]. Moreover, the growth patterns of facial bones in different anatomical structures are different. Therefore, the treatment of pediatric facial fractures varies based on the confirmed diagnosis and must account for children’s and adolescents’ active growth. There are different treatment strategies for different types of maxillofacial fractures, then improper treatment can lead to deformities or functional impairments, significantly affecting their future quality of life [6, 14]. Therefore, research on maxillofacial fractures in children and adolescents holds significantly practical importance for improving treatment outcomes, reducing complications and protecting the mental health.

The aim of this study is to analyze the main clinical epidemiology, characteristics and management of pediatric maxillofacial fractures in different age groups treated at our institution in the past 15 years, as well as to explore effective interventional methods and rehabilitation guidance to provide scientific evidence for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

Object of study

To complete this retrospective study, medical records and imaging data of patients with maxillofacial fractures admitted to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between January 2010 and December 2024 were analyzed. The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) aged ≤ 18 years; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of acute traumatic maxillofacial fractures by computed tomography (CT) or cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT); (3) a complete clinical follow-up of at least 6 months post-treatment. Patients who lacked complete medical records or imaging data, had a history of prior or pathological maxillofacial fractures, or were lost to follow-up were excluded from this study.

Data collection

Age, sex, etiology, fracture type, treatment, and concomitant injuries were collected from the data of the enrolled patients. Patients were divided into three age groups: ≤6 years, 7–12 years, and 13–18 years. Injury etiology was classified as: fall at ground level(FAG), traffic accidents(TA), fall from a height(FAH), and unspecified trauma(UT). Fractures in the maxillofacial region can be categorized into lower, middle, and upper regions. Sites of mandibular fractures included condyle, symphysis, parasymphysis, body, angle, and other regions(OR; e.g., ramus, coronoid, alveolar process). Sites of midfacial fractures were categorized as zygomatic complex(ZC), maxilla, nasal bone, orbit, naso-orbital-ethmoid (NOE), and other regions(OTR; e.g., alveolar process). Fractures of the upper face specifically referred to fractures of the frontal bone.

The incidence of unilateral versus bilateral condylar fractures, as well as the number of condylar fractures associated with other mandibular fractures (symphysis, parasymphysis, body, angle, OR) was meticulously documented. Specifically, condylar fractures were categorized into intracapsular and extracapsular types when analyzing treatment approaches. Furthermore, concomitant injuries—including craniocerebral, thoracic, abdominal, limbs and other injuries—were recorded in patients with maxillofacial fractures.

Treatment strategies

Treatment modalities for maxillofacial fractures comprised conservative managements and open surgical interventions.

Conservative managements included observation, anti-infection, ice and medication hemostasis, mouth opening training, dental arch splint fixation of dental arch, intermaxillary fixation and traction, etc. Indications were as follows: (1) maxillofacial fractures with ambiguous dislocation, such as greenstick fractures; (2) intracapsular condylar fractures wihtout temporomandibular joint disc displacement (conformed by MRI) in patients aged 13–18 years old; (3) intracapsular fractures of the condyle with age less than 13 years old.

Open surgical interventions: reduction and fixation through open incisions approach with bio-resorbable or mini-titanium plates or screws. Indications were as follows: (1) Significant displacement of the maxillofacial fractures, such as mandibular fracture accompanied by abnormal bone mobility or open wounds; (2) intracapsular condylar fractures with temporomandibular joint disc displacement(conformed by MRI) in patients aged 13–18 years old; (3) extracapsular fractures of the condyle with the angle of ramus more than 45°or the height of ramus reducing more than 5 mm; (4) brow-out and brow-in orbital fracture accompanied by impaction of eye contents or a difference of more than 2 mm in protrusion between the two eyeballs.

Additionally, it was worth mentioning that for maxillofacial fractures involving the temporomandibular joint region (such as intracapsular condylar fractures), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. This modality allowed for a more comprehensive evaluation of soft tissue injuries associated with the fracture, including disc displacement, disc perforation, as well as intra-articular hemorrhage and effusion.

Methodology for follow-up assessment

At each visit (1 week, 3 months, 6 months post-treatment), a comprehensive clinical assessment was conducted. The clinical evaluation included occlusion, facial symmetry, maximal mouth opening, temporomandibular joint function, and neurosensory status. These clinical parameters, however, were part of the standard follow-up care and did not constitute systematically recorded endpoints per the study protocol. CT or CBCT were primarily employed to assess fracture healing at over six-month, and MRI was additionally utilized to evaluate the temporomandibular joint for condylar fractures (intracapsular fractures especially).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS, Version 27. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. A Chi-Square test (or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate) was performed to compare proportions, with statistical significance defined as p ≤ 0.05. For significant chi-square results, post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.05/n, where n = number of comparisons) were conducted to identify specific group differences. Effect sizes were reported using Cramer’s V (interpretation thresholds: small ≥ 0.1; medium ≥ 0.3; large ≥ 0.5).

Upper facial fractures

All the upper facial fractures were frontal fractures, in which the conservative treatment rate was higher than the surgical rate, 83.3% (10/12) versus 16.7% (2/12), respectively.

Complications and management

All patients were followed up for at least 6 months, 11 patients developed wound infections and implant exposure after surgery, 5 children experienced temporomandibular joint bone-ankylosis, and 7 children showed significant facial asymmetry. A structured management protocol for various postoperative and developmental conditions should be employed in this study. For patients with postoperative infections, we actively implemented anti-infection management strategies, such as debridement and drainage, or targeted antibiotic therapy based on results of bacterial culture and drug susceptibility testing. For some patients with exposed implants, the implants should be removed when the fracture healed, which was conducive to the healing of the wound. Patients with temporomandibular joint ankylosis first underwent joint arthroplasty. Following the release of ankylosis, subsequent jaw deformities were addressed via distraction osteogenesis (DO) to stimulate mandibular growth. For jaw deformities in growing patients, orthodontic treatment or orthognathic-orthodontic treatment was also recommended after growth completion to achieve stable and optimal results.

Concomitant injuries

Of the 341 maxillofacial fracture patients, a total of 58 were combined with fractures in other parts of the body, 32 with craniocerebral injuries, 27 with thoracic injuries, and 6 with abdominal injuries.

Discussion

In this study, the incidence of maxillofacial fractures among children and adolescents was 16.37%, which is consistent with the reported trend that such fractures in minors generally represent less than 15% of all facial fractures [3]. Maxillofacial injuries in minors are mostly confined to soft tissue and alveolar bone, with a low incidence of maxillofacial fractures [15]. However, pediatric maxillofacial fractures are associated with higher mortality and more severe injuries than non-maxillofacial fractures, as well as longer hospital stays, intensive care unit stays and higher hospital costs [2, 16]. Furthermore, pediatric maxillofacial fractures exhibit diverse clinical patterns influenced by multiple factors, varying in severity and location, and potentially leading to complications [3]. Therefore, the related research on pediatric maxillofacial fractures still needs to be carried out to better assist clinicians in the diagnosis and treatment process and the accurate judgment at the first visit.

The observed male predominance of pediatric maxillofacial fractures in our study (68.0%, male: female = 2.13:1) aligns with findings from prior studies, possibly attributed to boys’ higher levels of physical activity and participation in outdoor pursuits, thereby elevating the susceptibility to injuries [14, 17, 18]. The gender distribution across different age groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.077). Nevertheless, males comprised the highest proportion within 13–18 age group (72.5%, male: female = 2.64:1). Based on this, attention should also be given to socio-cultural influences and biological developmental factors. Traditional societal norms often encourage boys to be courageous, adventurous, and active. Such social expectations may implicitly contribute to behavioral differences between males and females. Furthermore, existing research indicates that sex hormone levels significantly influence adolescent bone health, and their relationship with bone density exhibits gender-specific patterns [19]. This may provide a plausible explanation for the gender differences observed in the present study.

The patients included in this study were categorized into three different age groups, 0–6 years old, 7–12 years old and 13–18 years old, respectively. This type of age distribution was preferred because it encompasses three critical stages of development (preschool, school and adolescence) [3]. FAG was the most common cause of injury for maxillofacial fractures in minors which is consistent with previous studies [13]. However, some studies have shown that TA was the most common cause of injury for maxillofacial fractures in minors [14, 18]. Overall, the causes of maxillofacial fractures in pediatric patients vary by region, culture, living environment and socioeconomic characteristics [17]. This study found there were statistically significant differences in the causes of injury among three age groups (P < 0.001). It is worth mentioning that FAH was most common in 0–6 age group(adjusted α = 0.0167). This distribution may be related to the unique developmental behavioral characteristics of children in this age group (curiosity, lack of motor skills, lack of danger awareness). The above result suggests that a multi-faceted comprehensive strategy is needed to prevent such injury. On the one hand, the safety of children’s usual environment should be optimized (such as installing window rails and eliminating the risk of climbing). On the other hand, relevant safety education for parents and other caregivers should also be strengthened to improve their care awareness and intervention ability. For children in 7–18 age group, with the expansion of their range of activities and the increasingly complex social environment, prevention strategies need to be adjusted accordingly. Not only should we continue to promote traffic safety legislation and law enforcement, but also enhance children’s ability to identify risks and avoid harm through age-appropriate health education, so as to build a comprehensive protection system that pays equal attention to the environment and behavior.

Previous literature indicated that mandibular fractures are a common type of pediatric maxillofacial fracture, comprising an estimated 40% to 70% of pediatric facial trauma cases [20, 21]. Mandibular fractures accounted for 77.1% (263/341)in our study. Among these mandibular fractures, condyle and symphysis were found to be the most common sites in minors, accounting for 61.6% and 38.4%. This may be attributed to the inherent structural vulnerability of these two locations, combined with the existence of tooth germs within the mandibular body and the insufficient calcification of cortical bone in minors [4]. ZC was the most common part of midfacial fracture in minors, accounting for 63.31%. This is due to pneumatization and the development of the paranasal sinuses, which ensure additional resistance to fractures in children [22]. In addition, our study found a significantly higher incidence of symphysis fractures in 0–6 age group than in the other two groups(adjusted α = 0.0167). Therefore, clinicians should be highly vigilant about the possibility of median symphysis fractures in children aged 0–6 years. Even if the external swelling is not obvious, detailed imaging tests (e.g., curved tomography or CT) are required to avoid overlooking the detection of the problem due to the children’s limited expressive ability.

As the growth center of mandible, the high frequency of condylar fractures (61.6%) in this study raises concerns regarding potential pediatric facial deformities, malocclusion and dysfunction (even temporomandibular join ankylosis) in the future [23, 24]. Previous studies have found that isolated unilateral condylar fractures are often more severe than unilateral condylar fractures associated with other fractures, and unilateral condylar fractures are more often associated with mandibular angle fractures [25, 26]. In this study, children with unilateral condylar fractures were found to have a higher incidence of isolated fractures compared with bilateral condylar fractures (p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.439). Therefore, in cases of isolated unilateral condylar fractures, clinical evaluation should place particular emphasis on the severity of the condylar fracture itself. The biomechanical rationale lies in the fact that the traumatic energy is not dissipated through fractures in other sites (such as the symphyseal region) but is instead concentrated on the condylar area. This often indicates that the condyle may have sustained more severe localized damage, such as significant displacement, angulation, or articular surface disruption. In such scenarios, accurately assessing the degree of displacement, loss of height, and angulation of the fracture becomes critical for formulating individualized treatment plans, whether involving conservative functional therapy or surgical open reduction and internal fixation. Bilateral condylar fractures were more likely to be associated with fractures in the Symphysis compared with unilateral condylar fractures (p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.297). The core mechanism stems from an alteration in biomechanics. Bilateral condylar fractures render the mandible a freely floating structure, devoid of the stabilizing constraints from the bilateral glenoid fossae. Subsequent force application to the anterior mandible causes a transverse widening of the mandibular arch. This “arch-widening” effect generates significant tensile stresses concentrated on the relatively thin lingual surface of the symphysis, predisposing it to fracture and culminating in the pathognomonic “symphysis-bilateral condyles” fracture pattern [27, 28]. A study confirms that this combined injury pattern carries a substantially higher risk of clinical complications [29]. The simultaneous disruption of three key biomechanical pillars of the mandible—the bilateral condylar processes and the anterior body—results in a catastrophic loss of structural stability. Consequently, patients exhibit a significantly increased incidence of overall complications and malocclusion. This fracture pattern is also a significant risk factor for persistent neurosensory deficits. Given its high-risk nature, postoperative management for these patients should deviate from standard protocols. A more vigilant and long-term follow-up strategy is imperative. Early signs of complications, such as subtle occlusal instability or reduced mouth opening, should prompt proactive intervention rather than a watchful-waiting approach. For instance, timely implementation of intermaxillary elastics can finely adjust occlusion, while early, systematic physiotherapy can prevent irreversible sequelae like ankylosis, ultimately improving long-term functional outcomes.

In recent years, with the advent of new surgical techniques and instruments, the debate over whether to choose surgical or conservative treatment for pediatric maxillofacial fractures has continued [30–32]. Surgical intervention constituted a significant proportion of cases in this study, and we have accordingly summarized several relevant intraoperative experiences. Similar to some studies [33, 34], although certain principles for the reduction and stabilization of oral and maxillofacial fractures applicable to adults can also be uesd in pediatric patients, this study found that there are some key points or precautions for surgical intervention. For example: 1) minimizing iatrogenic injuries to avoid secondary damages that are detrimental to fracture healing during the surgery;2) trying to use absorbable materials to avoid secondary surgery and affecting bone development; 3) restoring the continuity of facial bones, but don’t pursue anatomical reduction and solid internal fixation; 4) attempting to preserve the periosteum and reposition it as much as possible. In addition, condylar fractures in children were classified into intracapsular and extracapsular condylar fractures [31, 35]. In the study, the treatment methods of condylar fracture under this classification were focused and there are significant differences in the treatment methods between the two types of condylar fractures (p < 0.001). This suggests that we can focus on the comparison of the efficacy of different treatment measures for these two different fracture types in subsequent clinical studies.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, the findings may lack generalizability to other regions with different patient populations and clinical protocols. Secondly, while necessary, the inclusion criterion of a minimum six-month follow-up might have introduced selection bias by excluding patients lost to earlier follow-up, who might have systematically differed in their outcomes. Third, this study encountered a high rate of loss to follow-up during the long-term period. The resulting data on long-term complications (such as long-term growth disturbances and functional sequelae in pediatric patients) and their risk factors were consequently incomplete. This limitation precluded a thorough analysis and discussion of these outcomes, which were therefore only summarized in the results section. Furthermore, the long-term and multi-source nature of the medical records resulted in heterogeneous data collection. Although key clinical parameters were documented, this heterogeneity precluded a systematic quantitative analysis of their correlations, which constrains the interpretation of the relevant findings. Finally, this study did not include a quantitative analysis of radiographic parameters for occlusion, condylar remodeling, or facial symmetry. Moreover, the variability in the imaging follow-up protocol—particularly the selective use of MRI only for TMJ cases—may have led to an underestimation of subclinical or asymptomatic complications. Based on these, future prospective studies are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the long-term outcomes.

Based on the aforementioned considerations, the present study employed the classical Bonferroni correction method for multiple comparisons. Although this approach is considered conservative and may reduce statistical power to some extent, it substantially enhances the reliability of the conclusions by effectively minimizing the risk of type I errors. This rigorous statistical strategy provides a robust foundation for the validity of inferences in subsequent clinical research.We would also like to point out, the research hospital has the largest medical center of oral and maxillofacial surgery in southeastern China.

Conclusions

There were different clinical epidemiological features and characteristics of pediatric maxillofacial fractures in different age groups. And condylar fractures exhibit distinct pathogenic patterns and require specific therapeutic considerations. Accordingly, preventive strategies and treatment plans in children or adolescents should be formulated differently.
