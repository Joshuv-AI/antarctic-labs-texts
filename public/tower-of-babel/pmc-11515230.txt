Internet addiction: the Czech and Slovak adolescent population
Authors: Gabriel Kňažek, Barbora Kňažek Považanová, Martin Dolejš
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is often seen as an important period for further personality development. In today's world, it is therefore important to keep track of current trends in adolescents. One of them is addictive behaviour in the context of the online environment. Spending too much time online can have a negative impact on the quality of life of adolescents. It is therefore important to pay increased attention to this phenomenon and to respond adequately to the current situation.MethodsOur study focuses on the prevalence of Internet addiction among Czech and Slovak adolescents. In total, 3,741 respondents participated in the project (N = 2,642 CZ; N = 1,099 SK); their ages ranged from 11 to 19 years (M = 14.38; SD ± 2.27). The research addressed differences among individual types of schools and between sexes in relation to Internet addiction. The research focused on adolescents attending secondary schools (ISCED 2 and 3) between the ages of 11 and 19 in the Czech and Slovak Republics. A questionnaire battery consisting of a sociodemographic questionnaire and the Internet Addiction Test (IAT) questionnaire was used for data collection.ResultsIn relation to the category of Internet use, girls did not score higher than boys either in the Czech group F(1, 2112) = .089, p = .765, or in the Slovak group F(1, 927) < .001 p = .994. There is a significant effect of school type both in the Czech group F(4, 2100) = 11.483, p < .001, and in the Slovak group F(4, 859) = 2.859, p = .023.ConclusionsOur research indicates that some adolescents, particularly boys, face issues with excessive Internet use, affecting social interactions. Further studies in the Czech Republic could explore the link between psychosocial factors and adolescent Internet use. This highlights the need to raise awareness among professionals about Internet addiction in Czech and Slovak adolescents.Keywords: Internet addiction, Adolescents, School, Prevalence, IAT

Introduction

Adolescence

Adolescence may be understood as a period marking an important developmental milestone in the life of an individual [1] and a formative period for his/her personality [2]. Adolescence is traditionally described as a tumultuous period full of changes. These changes occur on multiple levels, from the biological level to the social, spiritual, and psychological levels. A characteristic feature of the period is a gradual shift from dependence on parents, a typical feature of a child, to an independent individual who matures and becomes an adult [3, 4]. Excessive or too early contact with technologies and the Internet may have a devastating impact on the brain structure of youths [5]. This phenomenon is also connected to risky behaviour and related differences observed in adolescents [6].

One of the possible manifestations of risky behaviour in adolescence may be the problematic use of the Internet, which may negatively influence the adolescent’s quality of life. At present, a significant proportion of youths own various digital technologies enabling almost nonstop access to the Internet. It is therefore important to pay greater attention to the ways in which adolescents use the Internet and react to them adequately. There is a link between risky factors related to use of the Internet in adolescence and various issues (such as depression, impulsivity, relationship problems) in adulthood [7, 8].

Internet addiction

Addictive behaviour is generally linked to the concept of craving and substance addictions. Gradual abuse of a substance forms an addiction; there is a continuous need to increase the dosage of the substance, and omission of a dose is manifested in withdrawal symptoms [9, 10]. At present, science uses the concept of addictive disorder, which underlines the fact that an addiction is a medical condition and as such may be formed even without any psychoactive substance. Addictions may be divided into substance and non-substance ones [9, 11]. It is also important to note the difference between the terms Internet addiction (IAD) and Internet gaming disorder (IGD). They are not identical issues and they are not identical use disorders, which is confirmed by research. IGD was much more strongly associated with males and IAD was positively associated with online chatting, online gaming, and social networking, whereas IGD was only associated with online gaming [12, 13]. Excessive Internet use can lead to Internet addiction. Excessive use is clinically understood as an activity that can have negative consequences, while addiction is understood as a chronic disease. In other words, excessive use is a set of physiological, behavioural, and cognitive symptoms associated with continued use despite problems, difficulties, and disorders associated with use, such as impaired control and risky use. While addiction is understood as a state of psychological or physical dependence on use [9].

Pathological gamblers or pathological Internet users manifest similar symptoms as individuals addicted to a substance. The withdrawal symptoms of non-substance addictions are slightly less severe compared to substance addictions [14]. Internet addiction means using the Internet for other purposes than work for more than six hours per day over a longer period of time which negatively impacts, for example, one’s social life or school/work performance [15]. A typical feature is high comorbidity involving Internet addiction and various psychiatric disorders such as obsessive–compulsive tendencies, depression, anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder, or sleep disorders [16, 17]. In order to prevent the aforementioned problems of excessive Internet use and Internet addiction, experts recommend primary prevention. Research suggests that appropriate and early intervention, appropriately combined with proven intervention practices, can reduce time spent on smartphones and social media [18, 19]. Interestingly, in countries with a higher prevalence of heavy Internet use, intense users reported higher levels of family support and life satisfaction than non-intense users, and similar levels of psychological complaints. In all countries, intensive users reported more support from friends than non-intensive users [20].

Determining the prevalence of Internet addiction is not an easy task, concerning, for example, differences in access to the Internet in various countries or continents. Another problematic aspect is the lack of a unified concept for determination of Internet addiction and research into the field [14, 21] argue for a variance in prevalence of Internet addiction between 0.8% (in Italy) and 26.7% (in Hong Kong). The Czech Republic has 9.43 million Internet users, which represented 88.0% of the population in the year 2021 [22]. In Slovakia, the number of Internet users is 4.64 million, which amounted to 85.0% of the population in 2021 [23]. In general, excessive use of the Internet has a negative influence on the user’s well-being [24]. Internet addiction is one of a sub-group of behavioural addictions, which may be described with six symptoms and manifestations: salience, mood switches, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, interpersonal and intrapersonal conflicts, and relapse [25, 26].

Prevalence of internet addiction in adolescence

It is generally agreed that adolescents are the most at-risk group of Internet users. Excessive use of the Internet negatively affects their overall well-being and health condition [2, 8, 27]. A large-scale research project involving 18,709 adolescents in 25 European countries did not show any high prevalence concerning the category of highly excessive users (1.4%) [28]. Adolescents and university students are the population that is most exposed to Internet addiction. Even within this group, however, it is possible to observe great variance in prevalence ranging from 0.8% to 18.3% [29]. Such a significant variance may be caused by inconsistent terminology, as well as an irregular approach towards diagnosing the phenomenon [14]. DSM-5 includes an experimental diagnosis called Internet gaming disorder, while scientific literature frequently uses the term Internet addiction [9]. Research projects, focusing on differences between the sexes in relation to Internet addiction, point out a higher propensity for excessive use of the Internet manifested by boys compared to girls [30–32].

Slovakia lacks a sufficient number of research studies investigating the prevalence of Internet addiction. In the Czech Republic, a similar research project was conducted in 2017/2018; its findings show that 0.4% of adolescents are severe users [2]. Our research objective was therefore to bring up-to-date findings from the period of the Covid-19 pandemic in the Czech Republic and complement them with findings related to Internet addiction in Slovakia. The results of our research suggest that the specific period of the Covid-19 pandemic may have influenced the increased levels of Internet use among adolescents, compared to the aforementioned research.

Methods

Sampling procedure and study participants

The selection of participants was carried out using the method of random stratified sampling in order to create a representative sample of adolescents aged 11–19 attending secondary schools (ISCED 2 and 3) in the Czech Republic and the Slovak Republic. For the selection, we have used the ISCED International Classification of Educational Programmes. These are the levels of education that have been applied in all EU data collections since 2014. ISCED 2 covers lower secondary education, while ISCED 3 covers upper secondary education. For the selection, a list of all primary and secondary schools was created, broken down by level of education (ISCED 2 and ISCED 3) and by country. Numbers were subsequently assigned to each school and a random number generator was used, which had a locked function to select a number already drawn. The schools were then contacted. The research population was selected from all primary schools and secondary schools in the Czech and Slovak Republics. Thus, grammar schools, secondary vocational schools, secondary industrial schools, business academies, and secondary vocational schools participated in the research. The selection also focused on a particular region and type of school.

The test battery was administered within a nationwide representative study to adolescents attending the lower or higher years of general secondary schools, secondary schools with a school-leaving examination, and secondary schools without a school-leaving examination from the second half of the year 2020 to the first half of the year 2022 in the pen-paper form. Data collection was influenced by the Covid-19 pandemic; the time span was consequently lengthened. The research involved 1,185 Czech boys and 1,457 Czech girls, as well as 422 Slovak boys and 677 Slovak girls. An overview of the numbers of boys and girls, in relation to the respective types of schools, is provided in Table 1. The estimated maximum sampling error, with a research sample equalling 2,642 in the Czech Republic, is 1.9%; for the research sample of 1,099 in Slovakia, it equalled 2.9%, using the confidence interval 95% and concerning the size of the population. The error rate could have been caused by many factors, most likely by issues on the part of the participants, such as loss of motivation to complete the questionnaire, lack of attention, reluctance to answer certain items, as well as failure to understand an item, or the length and level of difficulty of the questionnaire. Data collection was conducted by a research team from Palacký University Olomouc, Department of Psychology, and the administration was supervised by trained and experienced administrators. The questionnaires were administered in school lessons; their completion took approximately 35 min, and the total number of schools participating in the research was 28.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

In our research, the code of ethics according to the European Federation of Psychologists'’ Associations was followed. In addition to the EFPA code of ethics, we complied with the standard of personal data protection laws for both countries (CZ 110/2019 Coll. and SK 18/2018 Coll.) and the European Union's GDPR regulations. The research ethics were supported by project funding from Palacký University Olomouc.

The research team followed the legal regulations of the Czech and Slovak Republic related to this issue. As concerns respondents younger than 15 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians prior to the administration of the test battery, as well as verbal consent from the respondents themselves at the beginning of the test battery administration. Written informed consent from the legal guardians was secured by either the research team or the participating school. Respondents aged 16 years and over were informed of their rights and options prior to the actual administration of the test battery and provided informed consent concerning the processing of the data they provided in the test battery.

Measures

The questionnaire battery consisted of a questionnaire inquiring about sociodemographic data (age, sex, type of school, etc.) and the Internet Addiction Test (IAT) questionnaire [33]. It is a tool used in many countries, such as the USA, Japan, Thailand, Turkey, Italy, etc. [34–39]. IAT was translated into Czech in 2018 [40] and into Slovak in 2020 [41]. This psychometric tool consists of 20 items. Participants respond to them on a 5-point scale ranging from Rarely (1) to Always (5). Individual items are subsequently divided into six subscales: Salience (5 items), Excessive Use (5 items), Neglect Work (3 items), Anticipation (2 items), Lack of Control (3 items), and Neglect Social Life (2 items). It is recommended, however, to use IAT as a unidimensional test due to the high correlations between the individual subscales [2, 42]. The maximum possible score within the method is 100 points, with a higher score indicating that the particular individual manifests greater severity and risk in relation to Internet addiction. In order to interpret the results, the author of the questionnaire [43] recommends dividing the score into four levels of Internet utilization: normal use (0–30 points), mild addiction (31–49 points), moderate addiction (50–79 points), and severe addiction (80–100 points).

Statistical analysis

All statistical operations were carried out in the program SPSS 25.0; the final analysis involved only questionnaires meeting all the criteria necessary for their inclusion in the analysis. The total number of questionnaires included after data cleaning was 3,741 (more than 3,821 questionnaires were collected). The analysis employed the method of descriptive statistics including parametric and non-parametric tests.

Results

The IAT questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency for both groups. The values of Cronbach’s alpha were 0.882 for the Czech group and 0.881 for the Slovak group. The reliability of the individual scales ranged from acceptable (0.771) to good (0.838). The skewness values of the IAT score reached 0.702 for the Czech group and 0.794 for the Slovak group. The kurtosis values of the IAT score reached 0.638 for the Czech group and 0.851 for the Slovak group. Skewness and kurtosis approach zero, i.e. the research sample has standard distribution.

The most at-risk groups of adolescents are those in the category of severe addiction, where the highest percentage proportion was reached in the Czech Republic by 13-year-old boys (1.1%) and 11-year-old girls (3.3%); in Slovakia, the percentage in the category of severe addiction was slightly higher. The highest percentage proportion was reached by 16-year-old boys (5.1%) and 13-year-old girls (4.7%). A potentially problematic group are adolescents from the category of moderate addiction. In the Czech Republic, the highest percentage proportion was reached by 12-year-old boys (12.3%) and 13-year-old girls (12.3%). In Slovakia, the risk group were 13-year-old boys (12.7%) and 15-year-old girls (16.9%). In the categories of moderate addiction and severe addiction, it is recommended to pay increased attention and launch intervention activities related to the area of use of the Internet. In the Czech Republic, a significant result was found for the difference between severe and moderate risk on the IAT F (3, 2110) = 4,67, p = 0.003. No significant difference between severe and moderate risk on the IAT was found in SR F (3, 925) = 2,28, p = 0.078.

As concerns the selection based on the year of school and biological age, it was possible to question this selection, since both levels were highly correlated both in the Czech Republic (r = 0.94) and Slovakia (r = 0.97). Questioning the selection is based on the assumption that the two nationalities achieved similar values. One might assume that the nationalities are therefore similar in the area under study. When comparing the relationship between these two variables and the IAT score for the Czech group (Fig. 1), the age reached lower values R2 = 1.5%, bootstrap 95% confidence interval (CI) [23.18%, 31.60%] compared to the year of school R2 = 4.2%, bootstrap 95% confidence interval (CI) [21.25%, 29.92%]. Discussion

Our study was focused on an estimation of the level of Internet addiction among adolescents in the Czech Republic and Slovakia. Our findings provide up-to-date data complementing existing research projects with a similar focus, not only under our conditions, and constitute the background for intervention activities and further research. Concerning the type of school, the category of severe addiction appears most among adolescents attending primary schools. In the Czech Republic, these are 0.8% and in Slovakia 2.0% of adolescents. A potential risk group are adolescents in the category of moderate addiction, where the most at-risk group in the Czech Republic are adolescents attending secondary schools without a school-leaving examination (33.3%) and in Slovakia adolescents attending lower general secondary schools (10.2%).

As of 31 December 2021, the population of Czech adolescents aged 11–19 equalled 975,604 [44]. In Slovakia, the population of adolescents of the same age range in 2020 was 487,310 [45]. In the population of Czech adolescents, signs of mild addiction, moderate addiction, and severe addiction were manifested in 40.5% individuals, while in Slovakia in 40.4% individuals. Extrapolation to the population of Czech adolescents yields 14,955 adolescents manifesting problem use of the Internet; in Slovakia, it is 17,913 individuals. Excessive use of the Internet may be caused by many factors, such as parental conflicts and subsequent coping with emotional burden [46], lowered number of social interactions, which may be a predictor for the development of depression [47], as well as lowered self-assessment and use of the Internet as a mediator [48].

Comparing the group of adolescents from the research of Procházka et al. [2] with our findings, it is apparent that within our research sample, boys are more at-risk consumers of the Internet than girls,the difference between girls and boys in our sample is, however, not significant. The sum of categories of mild, moderate, and severe addiction in the research conducted by Procházka et al. [2] yields 17.5% of boys and 25.5% of girls. In contrast, the results of the same operation for our research sample were the following: 40.6% of Czech boys, 40.3% of Czech girls, 42.6% of Slovak boys, and 39.2% of Slovak girls. As the results show, one can observe an actual increase in the number of risk users among young users of the Internet in both the Czech Republic and Slovakia.

For our research, we chose the IAT method, which shows a good correlation coefficient of 0.62—0.84 [49]. Another interesting method is the CIUS (Compulsive Internet Use Scale) correlation coefficient is around > 0.96, it is a newer test than the IAT [50]. Another widespread method for measuring Internet addiction is the GPIUS2 (Generalized Problematic Internet Use Scale 2) with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.72 to 0.89 [51]. Other methods used include the PIUQ (Problematic Internet Use Questionnaire) with a correlation coefficient of 0.87 [52].

Concerning our study, it is important to bear in mind several limitations related to interpretation of the results. Adolescents attending secondary schools without a school-leaving examination are represented in our research sample with a limited number of individuals. It will be therefore necessary to conduct further research concerning this group. The chosen research design involving self-assessment could influence the obtained data as a result of the participants’ dishonest responses and social desirability bias. In order to reduce this effect, the participants were guaranteed anonymity. A certain limitation of the research is the cross-sectional nature of our data, which limits the subsequent possibility of drawing causal conclusions. A presumed limitation of the study may be biased responses resulting from misunderstanding the task and questions used in the questionnaire battery. A measure taken to lessen this limitation was the presence of trained administrators throughout the administration of questionnaires. They answered questions and provided explanations if needed. We also sought to provide as comprehensible a formulation of the task as possible concerning the age of the participants.

Notwithstanding the above-listed limitations, the research output may be considered beneficial, since it provides up-to-date findings on the current prevalence of Internet addiction among Czech and Slovak adolescents. The prevalence is complemented with interpretation and the study also defines the most at-risk groups of adolescents that require preventive measures. The results of the study provide insight into the current situation and contribute to continuous monitoring of the phenomenon. Another benefit of the study is revalidation of the psychometric properties of the Czech version of IAT and its further use for screening and research in the Czech Republic and Slovakia.

Conclusion

Our research results indicate that part of the adolescent population manifests problems in the area of use of the Internet which may be reflected in the adolescents’ daily life and its quality. According to the results of research from 2021,1 adolescent boys are more likely to belong to the most at-risk group in relation to excessive use of the Internet and manifestation of Internet addiction. The results of our research do not support this claim and there were no statistically significant differences by gender. The research outlines further options for subsequent studies of Internet addiction among adolescents in the Czech Republic and Slovakia, possibly focusing on relationships between psychosocial factors and use of the Internet. Our findings also raise awareness of the professional public, such as school psychologists, addictologists, methodists, preventionists, etc. concerning the prevalence of Internet addiction in the population of Czech and Slovak adolescents and the related consequences. From the available results and knowledge, it is possible to develop intervention programmes aimed at preventing excessive use of the Internet by adolescents, e.g. through education, but also through experiential workshops.
