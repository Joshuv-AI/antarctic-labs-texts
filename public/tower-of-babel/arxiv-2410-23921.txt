Transient Elasticity – A Unifying Framework for Thixotropy, Polymers, and Granular Media
Mario Liu

Abstract
Thixotropic yield stress fluids, such as paint or ketchup, are traditionally viewed as elastic structures that, under shear rates, break into viscous liquids with lumps, and reconnect at rest. An alternative framework is presented here: Upon shear, structural destruction is incomplete, leaving sufficient connections. As a result, elasticity fundamentally underlies their non-Newtonian behavior, though they do appear purely viscous under a steady shear, displaying little direct evidence of elastic rebound—same as granular media and polymers. Consequently, all three are described by the same set of evolution equations, differing only in their parameters. These equations are set up by starting from solid dynamics and allowing the elastic strain ε e \varepsilon^{e} to relax, which in effect interpolates between solid and fluid behavior, appropriate for systems that display both types of behavior. Incorporating in addition a two-temperature framework, tracking how energy is dissipated in two consecutive stages, yields the nonlinear model of Transient Elasticity (TE). It describes an elasticity that is transient in time yet persistent under shear. Previously validated for polymers and granular media, TE is here applied to thixotropic yield-stress fluids. As shown, it successfully accounts for a wide range of characteristic phenomena, including over- and undershoot, viscosity bifurcation, shear banding, and oscillatory rheography. Given its appropriateness across structurally diverse systems, TE offers a unified, surprisingly general account of non-Newtonian phenomena.

Introduction

The energy

Fluid-dynamics is given by the evolution of its state variables.
Solid-dynamics has an additional state variable, the elastic strain

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\varepsilon^{e}}={{\dot{\gamma}}}-{\varepsilon^{e}}/\tau.

Usually, elasticity accounts for solids that execute reversible deformations, while viscosity accounts for liquids’ irreversible motion. Yet adding one term to solid

Both fluid- and solid-dynamics comply with general principles of physics, especially energy conservation and positive entropy production, which are local versions respectively of first and second law of thermodynamics.
Their equilibrium properties are encoded in the energy expression,
and dynamic equations are divided into
reactive
and
dissipative

entropy production. Such models may be called

\eta=

In exploring TE for how it accommodates TYF, even in circumstances that have not yet been probed experimentally, the calculations are made as simple as possible, employing mostly linear elasticity and constant coefficients, leading to simple, analytic results. Model sculpting is generally avoided. (If more realism is desired, to fit specific experiments, these simplifying assumptions probably need to be abandoned.) The claim that TE accounts for TYF is therefore quick to verify, showing it is indeed a viable alternative.

The Expressions of TE

II.2
A Second Temperature

A system with two temperatures is nothing unusual: Pouring cold milk into hot tea creates such a system, briefly. TYF sustain two temperatures for a longer time span, call them

Generally speaking, temperature is the average energy of a group of

If there are two groups, their respective temperatures may easily be different.
There are many such systems, a well known one is a fully ionized plasma
[
32
]

Granular media also have two temperatures: The usual temperature

the random motion and deformation of the grains
[
33
,
32
]

Water and air in soil
[
3
,
30
]
, and Clays
[
4
]
, have part of their atoms organized in mesoscopic structures. The elastic and kinetic energy of their vibration is quantified by the temperature
{T_{m}}
(for

). Equilibration is typically slow.
The same circumstance should also apply to TYF:
T
for the atoms and
T_{m}
for the flocs and clusters. If the flocs are strongly elongated, as with polymer solutions, equilibration

is, due to the large surface to bulk ratio, sufficiently fast that

is not an independent state variable.

The dynamics for a second temperatures
is always the same, determined by energy conservation, entropy production, and

: The energy that goes into

feeds the energy into

basic to Onsager relations) is employed below, some results may appear sudden for a person unfamiliar with it. They may simply skip such discussions.

Taking thixotropy as a viscous property, it is typically modeled along two seemingly orthogonal lines,

1/\tau\sim T_{m}^{3}\sim|{\dot{\gamma}}|^{3}
for higher ones,
which yields Herschel–Bulkley behavior. Combining
1/\tau\sim T_{m}^{2}\sim|{\dot{\gamma}}|^{2}
for low rates, and
1/\tau\sim T_{m}\sim|{\dot{\gamma}}|
for higher ones leads to Fig.
2
.

Next, we show that linearized TE is equivalent to a specific Jeffrey model:
Taking
\bar{\eta}\hat{=}K\tau+\eta
and
\bar{\lambda}\hat{=}\eta\tau
in the Jeffrey model,
\sigma+\tau{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\sigma=\bar{\eta}{\dot{\gamma}}+\bar{\lambda}{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\dot{\gamma}}
, we arrive at

{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}(\sigma-\eta{\dot{\gamma}})=K{\dot{\gamma}}-(\sigma-\eta{\dot{\gamma}})/\tau
,
same as Eq.(
1
) if
\sigma=K\varepsilon^{e}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}
holds. In other words, the special Jeffrey expression,
\sigma+\tau{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\sigma=(K\tau+\eta){\dot{\gamma}}+\eta\tau{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\dot{\gamma}}
,
with
K,\eta=
const and
\tau
given by Eq.(
10
) may equally well be employed to calculate all linear effects obtained in this paper (even though it is mathematically more complex).
For
\eta=0
, implying
\sigma=K\varepsilon^{e}
, we have the Maxwell model with a purely elastic stress.

This may be a bit surprising, because Maxwell is usually deduced by combining
{\dot{\gamma}}=\sigma/\bar{\eta}
for fluid and
{\dot{\gamma}}={\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\sigma/K
for solid, yielding
{\dot{\gamma}}=\sigma/\bar{\eta}+{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\sigma/K

that identifies
\bar{\eta}{\dot{\gamma}}
as the viscous stress, naming
\bar{\eta}
the Maxwell viscosity. Yet this interpretation contradicts Eq.(
1
), a relaxation equation
that confines the duration of elasticity (hence the name TE), because elastic relaxation and viscous damping are two distinct channels of dissipation. The former diminishes elasticity,
\varepsilon^{e}\to 0
, and the latter let the velocity become
uniform (or a solid-rotation),
{\dot{\gamma}}\to 0
. The first dissipates elastic, the second kinetic, energy, with the respective rates given by
(K/\tau)(\varepsilon^{e})^{2}
and

\eta{\dot{\gamma}}^{2}
, see Eq.(
53
).
In Eq.(
1
),
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\varepsilon^{e}}
is odd under time inversion. Being also odd,
{\dot{\gamma}}
is—according to the Onsager Relation
[
36
]
—reactive, reversible, and does not produce entropy, while
{\varepsilon^{e}}/\tau
is even, irreversible, and produces entropy at the rate
(K/\tau)(\varepsilon^{e})^{2}
.
The two corresponding terms in the Maxwell model,
{\dot{\gamma}}=\sigma/\bar{\eta}+{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\sigma/K
, are
{\dot{\gamma}}
and
\sigma/\bar{\eta}
, where the former is reactive, and the latter dissipative. Not producing any entropy,
\bar{\eta}{\dot{\gamma}}
is not the viscous stress, and
\bar{\eta}
not a viscosity.

Maxwell viscosity
is a misnomer.

Requiring Maxwell/Jeffrey to reduce to Eq.(
1
) renders them compliant. Arbitrary coefficients do not do this.
In terms of mechanical analogues, the Maxwell model is given
by a spring
K
and a dashpot
\bar{\eta}
in series, while Jeffrey and TE are given by the Maxwell strand in parallel with a dashpot
\eta
, see eg. Fig. 2 of
[
5
]
. Clearly, a dashpot simply accounts for the spring’s damping. It does not distinguish between elastic relaxation (
\bar{\eta}
) and viscosity (
\eta
).

III.2
Rate-Jumps

After a rate-jump,
{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}\to{\dot{\gamma}}
, we consider how the system goes from the initial critical state,
\varepsilon_{i}^{c}=\tau{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}
(assuming first
{\dot{\gamma}}\tau\ll 1
) to the final one,
\varepsilon_{f}^{c}=\tau{\dot{\gamma}}
.
Before the jump, we have stationarity,
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varepsilon^{e}={\dot{\gamma}}_{i}-\varepsilon^{e}/\tau={\dot{\gamma}}_{i}-\varepsilon^{e}\lambda_{1}|{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}|=0
. Right after it, this is no longer the case,

{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varepsilon^{e}={\dot{\gamma}}-\varepsilon^{e}\lambda_{1}|{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}|\not=0
, because it takes time for
T_{m}
to acquire the new stationary value, going from
{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}
to
{\dot{\gamma}}
.
If the jump is up,
{\dot{\gamma}}>{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}
, we have
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varepsilon^{e}>0
, hence
\varepsilon^{e}
overshoots, before returning gradually to
\varepsilon^{c}(1)
. If the jump is down,
{\dot{\gamma}}<{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}
, we have
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varepsilon^{e}<0
, hence
\varepsilon^{e}
undershoots. The same physics holds including the convective derivative, and for
1/\tau=\lambda_{n}T_{m}^{n}
.

Rate ramps, where
{\dot{\gamma}}
changes continuously from
{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}
to
{\dot{\gamma}}_{f}
, operates under the same logic:
T_{m}
lags behind, is always too small ramping up, causing
\varepsilon^{e}
to hover above the instantaneous critical value. Ramping down,
T_{m}
is too large, causing
\varepsilon^{e}
to stay below it. The hysteresis vanishes if the
{\dot{\gamma}}
-change is sufficiently slow.

III.2.3
Rate Ramps

As the rate ramps up and down,
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\dot{\gamma}}\not=0

{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\dot{\gamma}}>0

{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\dot{\varepsilon}<0

III.3
Oscillatory Rates:
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}

Given an oscillatory rate:
\delta{\dot{\gamma}}\sim e^{-i\omega t}
, the elastic strain
\delta\varepsilon^{e}

also oscillates, as
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\delta\varepsilon^{e}=\delta{\dot{\gamma}}-\delta\varepsilon^{e}/\tau
, Eq.(
4
), or
\delta\varepsilon^{e}/\delta\gamma=-i\omega\tau/(1-i\omega\tau)
, where
\delta\gamma\equiv\delta{\dot{\gamma}}/(-i\omega)
is the strain amplitude. Hence for
G\equiv G^{\prime}-iG^{\prime\prime}\equiv\delta\sigma/\delta\gamma=(K\delta\varepsilon^{e}+\eta\delta{\dot{\gamma}})/\delta\gamma
, we
have

\displaystyle{G^{\prime}}=\frac{K\omega^{2}\tau^{2}}{1+\omega^{2}\tau^{2}},\,\,{G^{\prime\prime}}=\frac{K\omega\tau}{1+\omega^{2}\tau^{2}}+\eta\omega.

(19)

If
\tau=
constant, these are the familiar expressions of the Jeffrey model. Sweeping
\omega
, one has
G^{\prime}
going from 0 to
K
, and
G^{\prime\prime}
maximal at
\omega\tau=1
.

For
1/\tau=\lambda_{1}T_{m}
, circumstances change drastically: For
\omega\ll r_{m}
, since
T_{m}
follows
{\dot{\gamma}}
instantly, the term
\delta\varepsilon^{e}/\tau=\lambda_{1}\delta{\dot{\gamma}}\delta\varepsilon^{e}
is quadratic, leading to anharmonicity.
For
\omega\gg r_{m}
,
T_{m}
is too slow to follow, it grows slowly until the time average of Eq.(
6
) vanishes,
T_{m}^{2}=\langle\delta{\dot{\gamma}}^{2}\rangle=\omega^{2}\langle{\gamma}^{2}\rangle
, or

\displaystyle T_{m}/\omega=\langle|\gamma|\rangle\equiv\bar{\gamma},

(20)

implying
\omega\tau=\omega/\lambda_{1}T_{m}=1/(\lambda_{1}\bar{\gamma})
, and

\displaystyle{G^{\prime}}=\frac{K}{1+(\lambda_{1}\bar{\gamma})^{2}},\,\,{G^{\prime\prime}}=\frac{K\lambda_{1}\bar{\gamma}}{1+(\lambda_{1}\bar{\gamma})^{2}}+\eta\omega,

(21)

as plotted in Fig.
6
.

III.4
Aging and Rejuvenation

As observed by many, see the review articles of Sec.
I
, the stress overshoot after a rate jump increases with the waiting time
t_{w}
at rest – called
aging and rejuvenation
. In the case of C. Derec et al,
t_{w}
ranges from
10^{2}
to
10^{4}
s, see Fig. 8 of
[
37
]
. This is a different timescale than
\tau
or
1/r_{m}
. Yet introducing a new state variable with such a timescale appears to be overdoing the modeling, as this alters the structure of TE, risking phantom predictions.
In other words, this effect does not “pre-exit” in TE—unlike those considered elsewhere, especially the Bingham-like critical state, the oscillatory coefficients:
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}
, and viscosity bifurcation of Sec.
IV.2
. Some model sculpting is necessary here. The TE modifications below are tentative, qualitative, serving merely to point out a possible path.

The maxima of overshoots for different
t_{w}
coincide, see Fig. 8 of
[
37
]
. This indicates we may leave the dynamics of
\varepsilon^{e}
unchanged,
and employ an elastic coefficient
K
that becomes larger for
T_{m}\to 0
. This is also physically plausible, as the structure does get stronger with less
excitation. (As stated in
[
37
]
,
the experiments…are extremely sensitive to noise, particularly the ones with a waiting time.
) We take, with
\alpha,\Delta,T_{1}=
const,

\displaystyle K=K_{0}[1+\Delta\exp{(-\alpha T_{m}^{2}/T_{1}^{2})}],

(23)

such that
K=K_{0}(1+\Delta)
for
\alpha T_{m}^{2}\ll T_{1}^{2}
, and
K=K_{0}
for
\alpha T_{m}^{2}\gg T_{1}^{2}
. Only when the waiting time
t_{w}
is sufficiently long, does the stress overshoot with
\Delta\not=0
.

Furthermore, we need to slow down the exponential relaxation of
T_{m}
when it is very small, by replacing
r_{m}
, say, with
r_{m}/(1+T_{1}^{2}/T_{m}^{2})
, changing Eq.(
6
) to

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}T^{2}_{m}=-r_{m}\frac{T_{m}^{2}-{\dot{\gamma}}^{2}}{1+T_{1}^{2}/T_{m}^{2}},

(24)

such that little changes for
T_{m}^{2}\gg T_{1}^{2}
,
yet for
T_{m}^{2}\ll T_{1}^{2}
, the dynamics becomes
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}T^{2}_{m}=-(r_{m}/{T_{1}^{2}}){T_{m}^{2}}(T_{m}^{2}-{\dot{\gamma}}^{2})
, reflecting a slow, algebraic decay, whose solution, for
{\dot{\gamma}}=0
and the initial value
T_{i}
at
t=0
, is the slow, inversely linear evolution,
{T_{m}^{2}}/{T_{i}^{2}}=[1+{r_{m}}({T_{i}^{2}/{T_{1}^{2}}})t]^{-1}
. Inserting Eq.(
24
) into (
23
), and noting that
T_{m}
does not change quickly enough, shows an aging effect that, with appropriate values for
\alpha,\Delta,T_{1}
, should be close to observation.

IV
Stress-Controlled Experiments

IV.1
Static Yield-Stress
{\sigma^{\rm Y}}

Solid systems cannot sustain arbitrarily high stresses at rest. There is always a yield-stress
\sigma^{\rm Y}
, at which the system looses elasticity, starts to move, is no longer in equilibrium. Such
static yield
is the actual plastic transition, not the Bingham-like critical state. Since static yield starts in a quiescent system, for
{\dot{\gamma}}=0
, its value is encoded in the energy
w
(see Sec.
I
on compliant theories). Only the post-yield behavior is given by the dynamics.

The static yield stress,
\sigma^{\rm Y}\equiv\sigma^{e}(\varepsilon^{\rm Y})
occurs at
{\dot{\gamma}}=0
, an
example is a lump of fluid at rest on a tilted plane that starts to flow when the tilt reaches a
certain angle. It is different from the dynamic yield stress
\sigma^{c}
, the rate-independent critical state, see Eq.(
4
). That a single yield stress is conceptually inappropriate was noted by
Larson and Wei
[
11
]
, who stated that yield is
“the minimum stress required to trigger a
major microstructure breakdown in an initially fully-structured material. It is thus a quantity related to the fully structured state only. Hence, to use the term yield stress to denote a quantity that is a function of the
structuring level is clearly not consistent with the concept of yield stress”.

In the same vein, Mewis and Wagner
[
10
]
wrote:
“It could be argued on physical grounds that attributing a Bingham yield stress to a structure that exists during flow is not suitable.”

Thermodynamic stability with respect to fluctuations of the state variables requires the energy
w
to be a convex function of them. This is why compressibility and specific heat, both second
derivatives of
w
, are positive. This also applies to elasticity: If the elastic part of
w
is convex with respect to the elastic strain, the system is stable towards elastic fluctuations. Yield occurs at the inflection
point of
w
, where it turns concave, eg.

\displaystyle w=w_{0}\left(s,\rho\right)+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}K_{1}\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}-\textstyle\frac{1}{4}K_{2}\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}\varepsilon^{e}_{jk}\varepsilon^{e}_{k\ell}\varepsilon^{e}_{\ell i}.

(25)

Since the energy is minimal for
\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}=0
,
K_{1}
must be positive.
Given any state variable
z
, the energy
w
is convex for
\partial^{2}w/\partial z^{2}>0
.
More generally, writing
\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}
as a 6-tuple vector,
\varepsilon^{e}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=1,2,\dots,6
, stability is given if its Hessian matrix
has only positive eigenvalues. Otherwise, there is no elasticity.
In our case, with
x=\varepsilon^{e}_{12}=\varepsilon^{e}_{21}
,
y=\varepsilon^{e}_{11}=-\varepsilon^{e}_{22}
, the Hessian matrix is

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\partial^{2}w/\partial x^{2}&\partial^{2}w/\partial x\partial y\\
\partial^{2}w/\partial y\partial x&\partial^{2}w/\partial y^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

With

\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}=2(y^{2}+x^{2})
,

\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}\varepsilon^{e}_{jk}\varepsilon^{e}_{k\ell}\varepsilon^{e}_{\ell i}=2(y^{2}+x^{2})^{2}
,
we have

w=w_{0}+K_{1}(y^{2}+x^{2})-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}K_{2}(y^{2}+x^{2})^{2}
, with the stress and Eigenvalues given as

\displaystyle\sigma^{e}_{ij}=[K_{1}-K_{2}(y^{2}+x^{2})]\varepsilon^{e}_{ij},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=2K_{1}-6K_{2}(y^{2}+x^{2}),

(26)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=2K_{1}-2K_{2}(y^{2}+x^{2}).

We have
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
for
K_{2}<0
, with an infinite elastic region. For
K_{2}>0
, the region is confined by the yield circle (similar to
Hencky-von Mises
condition),

\displaystyle x^{2}+y^{2}=(\varepsilon^{e})^{2}+(\varepsilon^{d})^{2}<K_{1}/3K_{2}.

(27)

A drop of TYF at rest, sitting on a tilted plane of angle
\alpha
, has
\varepsilon^{e}\sim\sin\alpha
, and (neglecting the weight of the drop)
\varepsilon^{d}=0
. It will start moving if this condition is breached.
In a shear flow, we have
y^{2}+x^{2}={({\dot{\gamma}}\tau)^{2}}/[1+(2{\dot{\gamma}}\tau)^{2}]
, cf. Eqs.(
12
). As Eq.(
27
) also applies to it, elastic shear flow must obey the inequality,

\displaystyle({\dot{\gamma}}\tau)^{2}<{K_{1}}/({3K_{2}-4K_{1}}).

(28)

IV.2
Viscosity Bifurcation

For given stresses, TYF have two steady-states to relax to. With
1/\tau=\lambda_{1}T_{m}
and
\varepsilon^{c}=1/\lambda_{1}\ll 1

the critical state,

,

\sigma=\sigma^{e}<\sigma^{\rm Y}

[
34

35
]

4

6

\sigma=K\varepsilon^{e}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}=

4
) to eliminate
{\dot{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varepsilon^{e}={\dot{\gamma}}-\frac{\varepsilon^{e}}{\tau}=\frac{\sigma}{\eta}-\frac{\varepsilon^{e}}{\tau^{\star}}.

Assuming Eq.(
46
), or
\eta/K\ll 1/r_{m}

6

\displaystyle{{\dot{\gamma}}}/{T_{m}}=\varepsilon^{e}\lambda_{1}=\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c}.

(30)

Inserting this into Eq.(
6

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{T_{m}}=-r_{T}T_{m},\quad r_{T}\equiv\textstyle\frac{1}{2}r_{m}\left[1-(\varepsilon^{e}/{\varepsilon^{c}})^{2}\right].

(31)

Clearly,
T_{m}
relaxes until either
T_{m}=0
or

, ie. equilibrium or the critical state. [With Eq.(
30

\varepsilon^{e}=\varepsilon^{c}
implies
{\dot{\gamma}}=T_{m}
.]
For

and
{\dot{\gamma}}
increasing, while
\varepsilon^{e}
decreases (to keep

, there are two cases: the fluid relaxes into

, and becomes critical for

, see Fig.

The square bracket of Eq.(

At the jump
\sigma_{i}\to\sigma_{f}

The subscripts

and

{\dot{\gamma}}_{2}-{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}

\displaystyle\sigma_{f}

\displaystyle=K\varepsilon^{e}_{2}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}_{2},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{e}_{2}/\varepsilon^{c}

\displaystyle={\dot{\gamma}}_{2}/{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}=\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{i}.

(35)

[The second line is given by taking

,

The slope of the slow dynamics depends on

, implying
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}{\dot{\gamma}}<0
, see Eq.

For the data of Fig. 3, the above chain of events is broken by a stress pause of 20 s (about the timescale of the fast dynamics), probably such that the pre-shear stress
\sigma_{i}
comes down
significantly, as does

, though

(as it were not adiabatically applied), leaving

To solve Eqs. (

For polymers,

V
Non-Uniform Experiments

Same as solid- or fluid-dynamics, TE is a model capable of dealing with spatial variations. Until now, we considered uniform
T_{m},\varepsilon^{e},{\dot{\gamma}}
. In this section, we first consider two coexisting uniform systems, then continuous spatial variations.

V.1
Shear Band

A steady-state shear band may be taken as two steady-states in coexistence, one solid and quiescent,

\sigma_{S}=K\varepsilon^{e}
, the other fluid and critical,
\sigma_{F}=K\varepsilon^{c}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}=K/\lambda_{1}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}
(assuming
1/\tau=\lambda_{1}T_{m}
). Consider a
1D-system, width
L
, the solid of width
(L-d)
, the fluid of
d
. The outer rim of the fluid moves at
v
, implying
T_{m}={\dot{\gamma}}=v/d
.
(In truth,
T_{m}
will spill slightly into the solid, see Sec.
V.2
.) The connecting conditions at the fluid-solid interface are

\displaystyle\sigma_{S}=\sigma_{F}\equiv\sigma=(K\varepsilon^{e})_{F}/\gamma,

(36)

The first equal sign is the result of momentum conservation:
Integrating
{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}(\rho v_{i})-\nabla_{i}\sigma=0
over the volume of an an infinitesimally narrow slab containing the interface, and applying the Gauss Divergence Theorem,
we obtain
\int{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}(\rho v_{i})\text{dVol}=\oint\sigma\text{dA}
, where the left side vanishes, implying
\sigma_{F}-\sigma_{S}=0
.
The
\equiv
-sign is a definition, and the second equal sign is an Onsager relation, with
\gamma=
const. It is a result of considering the interface entropy production, see App.
C
. As the elastic stress of the fluid is constant,
(K\varepsilon^{e})_{F}=K/\lambda_{1}
, so is the stress
\sigma
. Hence the three unknowns of the problem are connected as

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{e}_{S}=\frac{\sigma}{K},\quad{\dot{\gamma}}=\frac{v}{d}=\frac{\sigma-K/\lambda_{1}}{\eta}.

(37)

And we may conclude:
\varepsilon^{e}_{S}
,
\sigma
and
{\dot{\gamma}}
are all constant, while
d
grows with
v
, as long as there is a shear band. (Without it,
{\dot{\gamma}}
and
\sigma=K/\lambda_{1}+\eta(v/L)
grow with
v
, as usual.) The relation
d\sim v
was observed in
[
39
]
and depicted in Fig.
9
.

That
\sigma=
const as long as there is a shear band, independent of the velocity
v
, is a striking result, which apparently has not been reported. It is a direct consequence of the connecting condition
\gamma\sigma=\sigma^{c}_{F}
, derived in App.
C
under a few assumptions that appear obvious but may not be generally true. Hence it is useful to be verified, or falsified, experimentally.

With
L
the system width,
d
the shear band width, and

. According to Eqs.(

as long as there is a shear band, until

{\dot{\gamma}}

[

and depicted in their Fig. 2, which is quite the same as this plot.

Unfortunately, circumstances are more complicated, as

\sigma
must be smaller than
K\varepsilon^{c}
in the solid to be stable, cf. Sec.
IV.2
, but larger than
K\varepsilon^{c}
in the fluid the system to flow, or

\displaystyle(K\varepsilon^{c})_{S}>(K\varepsilon^{e})_{S}=\sigma_{S}=\sigma_{F}=(K\varepsilon^{c})_{F}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}>(K\varepsilon^{c})_{F}.

(38)

This we shall consider in more detail, while noting Eqs.(
37
) remain valid. As
K\varepsilon^{c}
is typically a function of densities, the inequality

(K\varepsilon^{c})_{S}>(K\varepsilon^{c})_{F}
may imply a density difference.
TYF are all multi-component systems, we consider two:
\rho_{1}
of the background fluid, and
\rho_{2}
of the substance forming the meso-structure that causes elasticity and maintains
T_{m}
. Hence

K,\varepsilon^{c}
depend on
\rho_{2}
. We take
\rho_{1}
as much less compressible, varying only slightly to compensate the
\rho_{2}
-discontinuity at the boundary, maintaining a uniform pressure.
Both
K
and
\varepsilon^{c}=1/\lambda_{1}
should increase with
\rho_{2}
, as a larger
\rho_{2}
means more elasticity and longer
\tau=1/(\lambda_{1}T_{m})
. If so,
\rho_{2}^{S}>\rho_{2}^{F}
implies
(K\varepsilon^{c})_{S}>(K\varepsilon^{c})_{F}
, satisfying Eq.(
38

Defining
\rho_{2}^{F}\equiv\rho_{2}-\delta\rho^{F}_{2}
,
\rho_{2}^{S}\equiv\rho_{2}+\delta\rho^{S}_{2}
means
\delta\rho^{F}_{2},\delta\rho^{S}_{2}>0
. Given the mass,
M_{2}=\rho_{2}^{F}d+\rho_{2}^{S}(L-d)
, we have
\delta\rho^{F}_{2}=\delta\rho^{S}_{2}(L/d-1)
, leaving only
\delta\rho^{F}_{2}
as an additional unknown. It is determined by the condition of equal chemical potentials, expressing zero mass transfer across the
interface. With the energy
w=\textstyle\frac{1}{2}s_{m}^{2}/A+\textstyle\frac{1}{2}K(\varepsilon^{e})^{2}+w_{0}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and
{}^{\prime}{}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{2}}
, the condition

\displaystyle\mu^{S}_{2}=\mu^{F}_{2}\equiv\partial w/\partial\rho_{2}\quad\text{may be written as}

(39)

\displaystyle[(

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{e})^{2}K^{\prime}{}+w_{0}^{\prime}{}]_{\rho^{S}_{2}}=[(\varepsilon^{c})^{2}K^{\prime}{}-T_{m}^{2}A^{\prime}{}+w_{0}^{\prime}{}]_{\rho^{F}_{2}}.

This condition may be solved analytically by expanding around
\rho_{2}
, to linear order of
\delta\rho^{F}_{2}
.
More generally, we take
\varepsilon^{c}=X_{n}/(4+X_{n}^{2})
, see Eqs.(
13
). As
\sigma=K\varepsilon^{c}_{F}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}=K\varepsilon^{c}_{F}/\gamma
or
\eta{\dot{\gamma}}=K(1/\gamma-1)\varepsilon^{c}
, and

is a function of

alone, this expression determines

to be a constant, same as

V.2
Nonlocal Fluidization

In this section, we complete the shear band picture of the last section, showing how
T_{m}
spills into the solid portion, with decreasing
{\dot{\gamma}}
, and leading to interesting consequences.

In Sec.
V.1
, we considered the solid-fluid coexistence, in which the stress
\sigma
is uniform, while
\rho_{2}
and
T_{m}\sim{\dot{\gamma}}
are discontinuous. Yet Eq. (
8
) prescribes a finite decay length for
T_{m}
. To ameliorate this, we note that since
\varepsilon^{e}
is stationary, Eq. (
30
) holds (for both rate- and stress-control). Inserting it into (
8
), we find
T_{m},{\dot{\gamma}}
spatially decaying on the solid side,
x\geq 0
, from the fluid temperature
T_{m}^{F}
,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{i}T_{m}=\frac{T_{m}}{\xi^{2}_{m}},

\displaystyle\quad\xi_{m}^{2}\equiv\frac{\kappa}{\beta}\frac{1}{[1-(\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c})^{2}]},

(40)

\displaystyle(\varepsilon^{c}/\varepsilon^{e}){\dot{\gamma}}=T_{m}

\displaystyle=T_{m}^{F}\exp(-x/\xi_{m}).

The velocity difference from this
T_{m}
-diffusion is
\int{\dot{\gamma}}{\rm d}x={\dot{\gamma}}_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-x/\xi_{m}){\rm d}x={\dot{\gamma}}_{0}\xi_{m}
, where
{\dot{\gamma}}_{0}\equiv T_{m}^{F}(\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c})
. This implies
{\dot{\gamma}}=(v-{\dot{\gamma}}_{0}\xi_{m})/d
in the fluid, or

\displaystyle T_{m}={\dot{\gamma}}=v/(d+\xi_{m}\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c}).

(41)

[If
\beta\not\approx 0
in Eqs.(
59
),
\Delta v=\beta\sigma
needs to be added.] Assuming Eq. (
46
), the change of
\varepsilon^{e}
(in the solid) due to the finite

{\dot{\gamma}}
is negligible. (The solid dynamics in fact has terms
\sim\nabla^{2}\varepsilon^{e}
, cf. Eq. (
51
) and
[
40
]
. The associated length scale is typically small and has been set to zero here, allowing a discontinuous change of
\varepsilon^{e}
.) So the modified
T_{m},{\dot{\gamma}}
-behavior on the solid side are the only changes to the shear band results of Sec.
V.1
. As the decay length
\xi_{m}
diverges for
\varepsilon^{e}\to\varepsilon^{c}
, there is then no solid left.

With
\tau=
const, polymers, being always in the fluid state for given shear stress, cannot suspend a ball with a higher density. In contrast, TYF can stay in
the solid state,
T_{m}=0
, and suspend this ball (assuming it is not too heavy, such that
\sigma<\sigma^{\rm Y}
). However,
T_{m}
spilled over from a shear band will fluidize the solid, because
1/\tau\not=0
and the stress will relax. Under such
non-local fluidization
, static stresses decay, and the yield-stress vanishes. A ball will sink, or rise, depending on its density—as the Archimedes law prescribes. (Periodic tapping has the same effects.) Same considerations holds for granular media, cf.
[
28
]
.

V.3
Narrow Shear Bands

The
T_{m}
-diffusion of Eq.(
40
) has a spatially confined solution for
\varepsilon^{e}>\varepsilon^{c}
,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{i}T_{m}

\displaystyle=-T_{m}/\hat{\xi}^{2}_{m},\,\,T_{m}=A\sin(x/\hat{\xi}_{m}),

(42)

where
A
is the amplitude; and
\hat{\xi}_{m}^{2}\equiv(\kappa/\beta)/[(\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c})^{2}-1]>0
. It may be seen as two solid blocks slipping past each other, sandwiching a fluid layer of thickness
\pi\hat{\xi}_{m}
. Assuming strong elasticity, Eq.(
46
),
\sigma=
const implies
\varepsilon^{e}\approx
const, given by its solid value, irrespective how
{\dot{\gamma}}
varies. The density
\rho_{2}
is lower in the fluid (cf. Sec.
V.1
), such that
\varepsilon^{c}|_{S}>\varepsilon^{e}|_{S}>\varepsilon^{c}|_{F}
.
Employing Eq. (
30
), or
{\dot{\gamma}}=T_{m}(\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c})
, to calculate
A
, we have
v=\int{\dot{\gamma}}{\rm d}x=A(\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c})\int_{0}^{\pi\hat{\xi}_{m}}\sin(x/\hat{\xi}_{m}){\rm d}x=2A(\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c})\hat{\xi}_{m}
, or
A=(\varepsilon^{c}/\varepsilon^{e})v/2\hat{\xi}_{m}
.

V.4
Elastic Shear Waves

Employing TE and assuming
1/\tau=\lambda_{1}T_{m}
, we consider the propagation of elastic shear waves in TYF, showing that they should exist for high frequencies,
\omega\tau\gg 1
, even post-yield, as long as the wave lengths is large compared to meso-structures.

We start from
\rho{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}v=K\nabla\varepsilon^{e}
,

{\textstyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\varepsilon^{e}={\dot{\gamma}}-\varepsilon^{e}/\tau
, see Eqs.(
2
,
3
),
with
v=v_{1}
,
\nabla=\nabla_{2}
. (The terms
\rho v_{i}v_{j}
and
\eta{\dot{\gamma}}_{ij}
are neglected, because the first is quadratic in
v_{i}
, and
\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}/\tau
delivers the typically dominant damping, larger than the viscous stress.)
Together, they form the telegraph equation,

\displaystyle 2\rho\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)\varepsilon^{e}=K\nabla^{2}\varepsilon^{e}.

(43)

With
\delta\varepsilon^{e}\sim e^{iqx-i\omega t}
, we find

c^{2}q^{2}={\omega^{2}+i\omega/\tau}
with
c^{2}\equiv K/2\rho
, or

\displaystyle cq=\pm\omega\left(1+i/{2\omega\tau}\right),

(44)

propagating for
\omega\tau\gg 1
.
We take
1/\tau=\lambda_{1}T_{m}
and
\omega\gg r_{m}
, such that Eq.(
22
) holds,with
T_{m}\approx|{\dot{\gamma}}_{0}|=
const. Inserting Eq.(
44
) into
\delta\varepsilon^{e}\sim e^{iqx-i\omega t}
, we find

\displaystyle\delta\varepsilon^{e}\propto e^{\left[-i\omega\left(t\mp x/c\right)\mp x({\lambda_{1}T_{m}}/{2c})\right]},

(45)

or that the shear wave propagates for the distance of approximately
2c/\lambda_{1}T_{m}
. Placing a piezoelectric detector around this distance, one should be able to pick up the wave signal. The fluid to choose should have strong elasticity, with
K
large; the steady shear
{\dot{\gamma}}_{0}
should be kept small, as should the wave amplitude
\delta{\dot{\gamma}}
.

Without a steady shear rate, the elastic shear wave will always propagate for the initial time span
t\leq 1/r_{m}
, because
T_{m}\approx 0
then, and for all time
t
if the system is accounted for by the hybrid relaxation time of Eq.(
15
).

VI
Conclusion

The prevalent picture of thixotropic yield stress fluids is that of a fluid with a complex viscosity. This paper suggests that it may simply be taken as
“not quite solid”
: Interpolating solid- and fluid-dynamics and incorporating a second temperature for the mesoscopic structure, yields the model called TE, for transient elasticity. It blurs the line between fluid and solid, accounting for a system that flows readily, appears liquid, yet manifests its elastic, solid part by producing effects at rate and stress jumps that we call non-Newtonian, including overshoots, viscosity bifurcation, shear band, shear waves, and oscillatory rheography.
Some of the more interesting results are summarized below:

Rate jumps
and
rate ramps
are typical rate-controlled experiments, accounted for by
Eq.(
1
) directly. For polymers, one has
\tau=
const. With only one timescale, the stress relaxation after a rate jump is monotonic.
For granular media and TYF, there are two timescales,
\tau\sim 1/T_{m}
for elastic relaxation and
1/r_{m}=
const for the equilibration
T_{m}\to 0
, rendering non-monotonic stress relaxation possible, eg. overshoots. As the first timescale depends on the second, its relaxation behavior is intricate.
In granular media, the relaxation
T_{m}\to{\dot{\gamma}}
is fast, typically
1/r_{m}\ll\tau
, such that right after the rate jump
{\dot{\gamma}}_{i}\to{\dot{\gamma}}_{f}
, elasticity starts to relax with
\tau\sim 1/{\dot{\gamma}}_{f}
. Hence one is back at the one-timescale case, and overshoot is not routinely reported.
In the opposite case,
1/r_{m}\gg\tau
, the stress curve is discontinuous at
t=0
, as explained in Sec.
III.2.2
. This case is drawn in Fig.1(c) of both
[
11
,
10
]
and labeled as
“inelastic/ideal thixotropic”
.
Taking both timescales as comparable, appropriate for TYF, leads to continuous, fully-developed over- and undershoots, as in Fig.
4
.

Oscillatory rheography:

The oscillatory stress rates,
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
, linearly calculated, matches the observed amplitude-dependence well
[
37
]
. This puzzling result is easy to understand for an TE-system, less so for a viscous one: Given the Maxwell/TE-dynamics,
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}

are functions of
\omega\tau=\omega/\lambda_{1}T_{m}
. For
\omega\gg r_{m}
, instead of following
{\dot{\gamma}}
instantaneously,
T_{m}
grows slowly until
T_{m}=\langle{\dot{\gamma}}\rangle=\omega\bar{\gamma}
, see Eq.(
6
), or
\omega\tau=1/\lambda_{1}\bar{\gamma}
, where
\bar{\gamma}
is the oscillation amplitude. However, the frequency dependence of
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}
returns under a steady shear.

Viscosity bifurcation:

For stress-controlled dynamics, one needs to insert
\sigma=
const into Eq.(
1
) to eliminate
{\dot{\gamma}}
. This changes the relaxation rate for
\varepsilon^{e}
, from
1/\tau
to
1/{\tau^{\star}}\equiv 1/\tau+K/\eta
, see Sec.
IV.2
. In critical state, the stress is
\sigma=(K\tau+\eta){\dot{\gamma}}
. As the first term is responsible for non-Newtonian effects, thixotropy is prominent, well-developed, if
\eta/K\ll\tau
. Taking
\tau
and
1/r_{m}
as comparable, we proceed in Sec.
IV.3
with

\displaystyle\eta/K\ll\tau,1/r_{m}.

(46)

Hence
1/{\tau^{\star}}\approx K/\eta=
const, and the dynamics of
\varepsilon^{e}
becomes simple, as there are now two independent relaxations, a fast one for
\varepsilon^{e}

and a slow one for
T_{m}
. When solving the latter, we may take the former to have run its course,
{{\dot{\gamma}}}/{T_{m}}=\varepsilon^{e}/\varepsilon^{c}
. This
alters the relaxation rate of
T_{m}
, from
r_{m}
to
r_{T}\equiv(r_{m}/2)\left[1-(\varepsilon^{e}/{\varepsilon^{c}})^{2}\right]
. As a result, the dynamics of
T_{m}
will stop either in equilibrium, for
T_{m}={\dot{\gamma}}(\varepsilon^{c}/\varepsilon^{e})=0
, or at the critical state,
\varepsilon^{e}={\varepsilon^{c}}
, when the rate vanishes. This is the TE-account of viscosity bifurcation. And since the fast and slow dynamics have different slopes, the logarithmic
{\dot{\gamma}}(t)
-curve may well be non-monotonic.

Shear band:

In TE, a shear band is taken as the coexistence of two states,
critical and static, in Sec.
V.1
. The shear band width
d
is shown to grow with the velocity difference
v
, such that the local rate
{\dot{\gamma}}=v/d=
const
[
39
]
.
Because
T_{m}
-diffuses into the solid part, the Archimedes law should hold in the vicinity of a shear band, see Sec.
V.2
.

Static yield:

Because
static yield
occurs in a quiescent system, at equilibrium, it must be encoded in the energy. Hence
\varepsilon^{e}=\varepsilon^{\rm Y}
needs to be taken as the
inflection point
of the elastic energy
w(\varepsilon^{e})
,
where
w
ceases to be convex, rendering elasticity unstable for
\varepsilon^{e}>\varepsilon^{\rm Y}
.
We have
\varepsilon^{\rm Y}>\varepsilon^{c}
(the critical strain), because
\varepsilon^{c}
is an elastic solution, while
\varepsilon^{\rm Y}
is where elasticity ends.
Because of thermodynamic stability,
\partial\sigma^{e}/\partial\varepsilon^{e}=\partial^{2}w/(\partial\varepsilon^{e})^{2}>0
, the elastic stress
\sigma^{e}(\varepsilon^{e})
is a monotonic function. Hence
\sigma^{\rm Y}>\sigma^{c}
also holds. This is why the stress that initiates a flow,
\sigma^{Y}
, exceeds the one sustaining it,
\sigma^{c}

[
41
]
.
Nether
\varepsilon^{\rm Y}
nor
\sigma^{\rm Y}
are material constants, because elastic coefficients are functions of temperature and pressure.

On a
tilted plane
, when the stress breaches
\sigma^{\rm Y}
, elasticity collapses, converting the elastic energy to
{\dot{\gamma}}
and
T_{m}
, and the fluid starts flowing. The initial state will converge gradually onto the critical state, see Sec.
IV.2
. If it has a long horizontal stretch, as in Fig
8
, the strain rate has to accelerate to cut the line on the far end. If the tilted plane is not long enough, one only observes the acceleration, as in
[
34
,
35
]
, not the final steady-state.

Appendix A
Hydrodynamic Theories

In condensed matter physics, the qualification
hydrodynamic
goes well beyond Newtonian fluids. It is employed to refer to any continuum mechanical (or long wave length) model that
complies with general principles of physics, including especially energy conservation and entropy production obeying the Onsager relation. In the main text, I use the term
compliant
instead,
because many rheologists deem the generalization of a term as familiar as
hydrodynamics
offensive and presumptuous.

There are four groups of member systems, each with its established hydrodynamic theory:
Newtonian fluids
[
42
]
, liquid crystals
[
43
,
40
,
44
]
(including subgroups such as nematics, cholesterics and smectics),
superfluids
[
42
,
45
,
46
]
, and solids
[
40
,
47
,
48
]
. Every member obeys its hydrodynamics, which are generally accepted as
complete
, in the sense that all macro-scale experiments are accounted for, but no more—there are no phantom predictions. (All macro-scale theories have the same known limits, such as the size of infinitesimal volume elements, the time needed for local equilibrium to form, also relativistic effects, see eg.
[
49
]
.)
A prerequisite for this is
compliance
: Real systems in nature are compliant, they conserve energy and have a proper entropy production. If a model fails at compliance, it is bound to disagree with some observations. Having constructed a non-compliant model for some experiments, it is doubtful whether other experiments will abide by the same model.

One may be tempted to take these hydrodynamic theories as random constitutive models that its respective members happens to comply with, implying the groups are uniform, with members that are similar. Yet this is hardly true: Solids can be metals, insulators, or semi-conductors that,
irrespective of their atomic composition and interaction,
all obey elasticity. Liquid helium (both
4
He and
3
He) is superfluid at low temperatures, same as cold atomic gases, and ultra dense neutron star material. Newtonian fluids and liquid crystals include materials of even more diverse atomic and chemical compositions.

Generally speaking, given a set of state variables, the
structure
of its hydrodynamic theory, the form of its equations, is fixed by adherence to general principles. Interactions and composition only
influence how energy and transport coefficients depend on state variables, which are the
constitutive choices
. The structure differentiates the groups, the constitutive choices member systems.
A hydrodynamic theory is a set of partial differential equations for its state variables, including mass, entropy and momentum densities. They all have unique time-inversion parity: Energy, mass, entropy and the elastic strain are positive, momentum odd. Their respective fluxes have dissipative and reactive terms, with parities that are the same or opposite to that of the state variable. Only the former contribute to entropy production.
[
36
]

The energy is a function of the state variables, while the entropy production
R
depends on
thermodynamic forces
such as
\nabla_{i}T
or
{\dot{\gamma}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{i}v_{j}+\nabla_{j}v_{i})
.
State variables characterize the equilibrium of a volume element; the forces measure how far equilibrium is, by comparing the state variables of neighboring volume elements. The Onsager relations guaranteeing the
compliance
of a theory cover only the lowest order expansion of
R
in the forces. In this order, the viscosity
\eta
depends on state variables, not the forces.
Going to an
{\dot{\gamma}}
-dependent
\eta
implies higher order terms while ignoring the generalized Onsager relations.

Appendix D
Glossary

TYF:
Thixotropic Yield-Stress Fluid, typically with
1/\tau=\lambda_{1}T_{m}
.

State variables
are the variables of the energy density
w
, which in TE are the
elastic strain
\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}
and the densities of entropy
s
, meso-entropy
s_{m}
, various masses
\rho_{\alpha}
(with
\rho=\sum\rho_{\alpha}
), and momentum
g_{i}=\rho v_{i}
.
Their conjugate variables are respectively
chemical potentials
\mu_{\alpha}=\partial w/\partial\rho_{\alpha}
, temperature
T=\partial w/\partial s
, meso-temperature
T_{m}=\partial w/\partial s_{m}
, velocity
v_{i}=\partial w/\partial g_{i}
, and elastic stress
\sigma^{e}_{ij}=\partial w/\partial\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}
.

Mesoscopic Temperature
\bm{T_{m}}
accounts for the fluctuating kinetic and elastic energy of the meso-structures in TYF. In equilibrium,
T_{m}=T
holds.

Elastic Strain
\bm{\varepsilon^{e}_{ij}}
is the reversible, energy-storing part of the strain, where
\varepsilon^{e}_{12}\equiv\varepsilon^{e}
. The critical value of the elastic strain,
\varepsilon^{c}_{12}\equiv\varepsilon^{c}
, is the stationary solution after both
\varepsilon^{e}
and
T_{m}
have fully relaxed.

Stress and Yield:

\sigma_{12}\equiv\sigma=\sigma^{e}+\rho v_{1}v_{2}+\eta{\dot{\gamma}}
, with
\sigma^{e}=\partial w/\partial\varepsilon^{e}
. Mostly, we take
\sigma^{e}=K\varepsilon^{e}
with
K
the elastic coefficient. Static yield-stress
\sigma^{\rm Y}\equiv\sigma^{e}(\varepsilon^{\rm Y})
is the inflection point
\varepsilon^{\rm Y}
of
w(\varepsilon^{e})
. Dynamics yield
\sigma^{c}=\sigma^{e}(\varepsilon^{c})
is the stationary solution.

Rate
\bm{{\dot{\gamma}}}\equiv\varepsilon
:
The total rate is
\varepsilon\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{1}v_{2}+\nabla_{2}v_{1})=D_{t}\varepsilon^{e}+\varepsilon^{p}
, with
\varepsilon^{p}=\varepsilon^{e}/\tau
. The stress
\sigma
depends on
\varepsilon^{e}
and
{\dot{\gamma}}
. Forcing it to depend on a fictitious total strain,
\varepsilon\equiv\int{\dot{\gamma}}\textrm{d}t
, leads to hysteresis (cf. Sec.
III.2.4
).
