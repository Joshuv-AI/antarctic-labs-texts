Incidence and characteristics of catheter-related thrombosis in children with malignant tumors: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Guilian Chen, Aiyi Lin, Runnan Shen, Jun Ge, Ting Zhong, Guochang You, Lin Cao, Yumei Deng, Kai Huang, Xuemei Qiu, Yihong Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is a frequent complication during the treatment of pediatric malignancies. The use of central venous access devices (CVADs) represents a significant risk factor for VTE. While prior studies have investigated VTE and catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) in general pediatric populations, comprehensive data on the incidence and risk factors specific to Chinese pediatric oncology patients remain scarce.AimTo retrospectively evaluate the incidence and characteristics of CRT in a single-center cohort of pediatric oncology patients in China, thereby addressing this existing evidence gap.DesignSingle-center retrospective cohort study.MethodsMedical records from 785 pediatric oncology patients who underwent CVAD placement at our institution were retrospectively reviewed. Collected variables included age, sex, cancer type, catheter type, CRT occurrence, laboratory indices, and clinical outcomes. Statistical analysis were performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent or paired t-tests for continuous variables.ResultsAmong the 785 patients, 20 ( 2.5%) developed CRT. The majority (94.1%) received peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), which were associated with a lower CRT incidence (1.9%) compared with central venous catheters (CVCs) (16.7%) and totally implantable venous access ports (TIVAPs) (10.7%) (P < 0.001). A significant post-diagnosis increase in D-dimer levels was observed in patients with CRT (P = 0.040). The incidence of CRT did not differ significantly between patients with hematologic malignancies (2.6%) and those with solid tumors (2.4%). Most CRT cases (70.0%) occurred within the first month after catheter insertion. Symptomatic CRT predominated in the PICC group (78.6%), while asymptomatic CRT was more frequent in the CVC and TIVAP groups (83.3%). No CRT-related complications were observed during the 6-month follow-up period after CRT diagnosis.ConclusionPICCs were associated with a lower incidence of CRT compared with other catheter types. Although elevated D-dimer levels were observed following CRT diagnosis, further research is warranted to clarify its potential screening value.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06334-9.

Introduction

Central venous access devices (CVADs) are indispensable in the management of cancer-directed treatments, facilitating the administration of chemotherapy, parenteral nutrition, and blood product transfusions [1]. However, there are risks associated with their use. Catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) is a significant complication that can lead to loss of venous access, post-thrombotic syndrome, pulmonary embolism and increased healthcare costs [2, 3], all of which may substantially diminish patients’ quality of life. The pathophysiology of CRT aligns with Virchow’s triad: endothelial injury resulting from catheter insertion and mechanical irritation; venous stasis caused by luminal obstruction and reduced blood flow; and hypercoagulability, which is often exacerbated by an underlying malignancy or certain chemotherapeutic agents. For instance, asparaginase, which is commonly administered in the treatment of paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, induces a hypercoagulable state by depleting natural anticoagulants. With five-year survival rates for pediatric malignancies now surpassing 80% [4], the reliance on CVADs has markedly increased. This growing utilization highlights the urgent need for a deeper understanding of CRT incidence, risk factors, and outcomes to inform strategies that enhance patient safety and optimize long-term management.

CRT is a specific type of venous thromboembolism (VTE) that is associated with CVADs, accounting for approximately 50–80% of all deep vein thrombosis (DVT) cases in children [5]. Diagnosis is established through venography or vascular ultrasonography, confirming the presence of either occlusive or non-occlusive thrombosis in one or more vessels [6]. Symptomatic CRT, which manifests as pain, swelling or catheter occlusion, is generally straightforward to identify. However, asymptomatic CRT often goes undetected due to the absence of overt clinical signs [7]. Despite its sometimes silent presentation, CRT poses significant clinical risks. These include unplanned treatment interruptions, extended hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and serious long-term complications. Among these, post-thrombotic syndrome is particularly concerning, as it may cause persistent limb swelling, pain, skin alterations, and functional impairment, all of which can profoundly diminish quality of life. Moreover, CRT carries potentially fatal consequences such as pulmonary embolism and death [8]. Although anticoagulation remains the cornerstone of CRT management, it inherently increases the risk of bleeding [9]. Therefore, prevention is paramount. It is important not only to reduce haemorrhagic complications, but also to avoid treatment delays, relieve pressure on healthcare systems and improve long-term outcomes for children undergoing cancer treatment.

CRT poses distinct challenges in paediatric oncology. While it accounts for only around 10% of DVT cases in adults, it represents 50–80% of DVT events in children [10]. In the pediatric population, both the anatomical site and duration of catheter placement significantly influence CRT risk [11]. Despite growing awareness, the incidence of CRT across different catheter types remains insufficiently defined in pediatric oncology cohorts. Current clinical guidelines and meta-analysis recommend the use of anticoagulants in children only after CRT has developed, a strategy largely extrapolated from adult data due to the paucity of robust pediatric evidence [12]. This reactive approach inadequately addresses the need for proactive prevention in children with cancer, who often require prolonged CVAD use in the context of chemotherapy-induced hypercoagulability and thrombocytopenia-related bleeding risks. Previous epidemiological studies have focused on VTE [11] and CRT [13] in children or on VTE in children with malignant tumors, primarily ALL [14–16]. However, the incidence and risk factors of CRT in Chinese children with malignant tumors remain largely unknown. To address this gap, the present retrospective study investigated the incidence and characteristics of CRT in pediatric oncology patients, aiming to establish a theoretical foundation for the prevention and management of CRT in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study population

This study included pediatric patients with malignant tumors who were first admitted to Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, between November 2016 and July 2022, and who underwent indwelling CVAD placement during hospitalization. Inclusion criteria were as follows: confirmed malignant tumor based on pathological diagnosis; age under 18 years; fulfillment of catheterization indications with regular post-insertion maintenance; and tolerance of Doppler color ultrasound examination. All catheterization procedures were performed under ultrasound guidance, and catheter tip position was verified in the superior vena cava via radiography. Exclusion criteria included: pre-existing venous thrombosis prior to catheterization; ongoing anticoagulation therapy; previous central venous catheterization before the current admission; catheter removal due to mechanical factors such as detachment or breakage; incomplete medical records (e.g., missing documentation of catheter type or indwelling duration); and inability to track catheterization outcomes.

CRT was confirmed by Doppler color ultrasound. CRT was classified into the following four categories based on clinical manifestations [17]: (1) DVT presenting with edema symptoms or signs in the limb, neck, shoulder, chest, or face on the side of catheterization; ultrasound indicating DVT, with or without associated symptoms such as pain, numbness, skin temperature increases, and neck or limb movement disorders. (2) Superficial vein thrombosis, presenting with redness, swelling, and pain of the skin along the catheter vessel, with or without skin temperature increases, and/or ultrasound indicating corresponding vessel thrombosis. (3) Asymptomatic thrombosis, where thrombosis is detected through imaging without any subjective or objective patient-reported symptoms. (4) Thrombotic catheter dysfunction, causing partial or complete infusion blockage due to fibrin or thrombosis within or around the catheter.

Ultrasonography was typically performed based on clinical indications (e.g., symptoms). It is important to note that not all asymptomatic patients underwent routine ultrasound screening. Ultrasound screening for asymptomatic patients was performed at the discretion of the treating physician. This clinical decision incorporated the patient’s D-dimer levels with the physician’s own expertise and assessment of individual high-risk factors. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

Central venous access device maintenance protocol

All CVADs were maintained in accordance with a standardized institutional protocol to ensure uniformity of care. Detailed maintenance procedures followed the recommendations outlined in established clinical consensus guidelines [18]. The following sections focus on catheter flushing and locking, dressing replacement, and connector management—procedures that are easily standardized and supported by high-level evidence. (1) Solutions and concentrations: Catheter locking and flushing were performed using preservative-free 0.9% sodium chloride solution or heparinised saline. PICCs and CVCs were locked with heparinised saline at 0–10 U/mL, whereas TIVAPs were locked with heparinised saline at 100 U/mL. (2) Technique and devices: Flushing was carried out with a 10 mL syringe (or larger) or a 10 mL prefilled saline syringe, employing a pulsatile “push-pause-push” technique to generate turbulence within the catheter lumen. (3) Volumes: The flushing volume was required to be sufficient to completely clear residual medication from the catheter and its accessories, amounting to at least twice the total internal volume of the catheter system. The locking solution volume was ≥ 1.2 times the internal volume of the catheter and its connected components. (4) Frequency: Pulsatile flushing with normal saline was recommended both before and after medication administration. During the non-use period, PICCs and CVCs were flushed at least once weekly, whereas TIVAPs were flushed every four weeks. (5) Dressings: Gauze dressings should be changed at least every 48 h, and transparent semi-permeable dressings should be changed at least every 5–7 days. Dressings should be replaced immediately if the site is exudating, bleeding, moist, soiled, or otherwise compromised in integrity. (6) Connector management: Use Luer-lock devices to securely connect the CVAD to the connector. Before each access, the end face and outer surface of the connector were rigorously disinfect using an appropriate antiseptic and mechanical friction. Each maintenance procedure was documented in the electronic nursing record, including data such as time, catheter type and flush/lock solution.

Patient demographics

Demographic and clinical data were collected retrospectively, including age, sex, type of malignancy, type of catheter, adjustment to insertion length, CRT site and laboratory parameters obtained within one week before and after the CRT diagnosis. Laboratory parameters included prothrombin time (PT), prothrombin activity (PTA), prothrombin ratio (PT/R), international ratio of prothrombin (PTINR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen (FIB), thrombin time (TT) and D-dimer levels. Additional data included the interval from catheter insertion to CRT confirmation, associated complications and patient outcomes. Symptomatic and asymptomatic CRT were confirmed via vascular ultrasonography, supported by the patient’s clinical presentation, such as pain, swelling or catheter obstruction, and corresponding D-dimer measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis were conducted using SPSS version 25.0. All variables were classified as either categorical or continuous. Categorical variables, including demographic characteristics (e.g. sex), clinical data (e.g. malignancy type, catheter type) and outcomes (e.g. CRT incidence), were summarised as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test, continuity-corrected chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (for expected frequencies < 5) was applied as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. When these assumptions were met, independent or paired samples t-tests were used. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution or homogeneity of variance, non-parametric tests were employed, including the Mann–Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Pairwise comparisons were performed when significant differences were observed among groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Catheter type

The majority of children with malignant tumors (739/785, 94.1%) underwent PICC placement, with over 98.0% receiving 2Fr–4Fr single- or double-lumen catheters. All PICCs were composed of silicone, whereas CVCs and TIVAPs were made of acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene copolymers and polyurethanes, respectively. The incidence of CRT following PICC placement was 1.9% (14/739). Statistically significant differences in CRT incidence were observed between PICCs and CVCs (3/18, 16.7%) and between PICCs and TIVAPs (3/28, 10.7%) (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Symptomatic CRT predominated among PICC insertions (11/14, 78.6%), while most CRT cases associated with CVCs and TIVAPs were asymptomatic (5/6, 83.3%), a difference that was statistically significant (P = 0.018). No significant difference in CRT incidence was found among PICCs, CVCs, and TIVAPs across different venous puncture sites. Additionally, no procedural complications occurred during catheterization in any children with malignant tumors who developed CRT.

Characteristics of CRT for malignant tumor type

The incidence of CRT was similar between patients with hematologic malignancies (14/534, 2.6%) and those with solid tumors (6/251, 2.4%). Among children with hematologic cancers, 14 CRT cases were identified—8 in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), 4 in acute myelogenous leukemia (AML), and 1 in other malignancies. The highest incidence occurred in the AML subgroup (4/65, 6.2%). Of these CRT cases, symptomatic and asymptomatic presentations were evenly distributed (7 each). The most common clinical manifestations were pain and/or swelling (8 cases), followed by catheter dysfunction (4 cases). Detailed data are provided in Table 2.

Time to confirmation of CRT diagnosis

The median number of days between catheterisation and CRT diagnosis was 12 days (range 3–147 days). All CRT cases in this study were diagnosed during tumour treatment. The majority of CRT cases (14/20, 70.0%) were diagnosed within the first month following catheterisation. However, patients continued to be at risk of VTE after this period. Within three weeks, the PICC group had a lower incidence of CRT than the CVC/TIVAP group, with approximately 43.0% (6/14) of CRT cases occurring in the PICC group versus 100% (6/6) in the CVC/TIVAP group (P = 0.042). Notably, no new cases occurred in the CVC/TIVAP group after the initial three-week period. Further details are presented in Table 3.

Location of thrombosis

This study identified a total of 20 CRT events. Thrombosis was found in six different veins. Particularly high incidence rates were observed in the femoral vein (1/5, 20.0%) and the internal jugular vein (5/33, 15.2%). Eleven of these events occurred at the initial catheter puncture site, while the remaining nine developed in veins unrelated to the puncture location. Furthermore, two children experienced occlusion in two or more veins. All cases of multiple vein occlusion occurred in children with malignant haematological diseases.

Outcomes of children with CRT

Twenty children were diagnosed with CRT, and none developed pulmonary embolism. Among the eight children who required catheter removal, two underwent removal immediately after CRT diagnosis, one following thrombus extraction, and five after completion of anticoagulant therapy. Anticoagulation was administered in nine cases—five received low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), three were treated with warfarin, and one with unfractionated heparin. Four children were admitted to the ICU; three achieved clinical stabilization and were subsequently discharged, while one child died due to disease progression. No deaths were directly attributable to VTE. All patients were followed for six months, with subsequent management guided by follow-up findings. During the observation period, no cases of recurrent VTE, post-thrombotic syndrome, or pulmonary embolism were reported.

Discussion

CRT constitutes a major complication in pediatric patients with CVADs. Findings from this study indicate that PICCs are associated with a lower incidence of CRT compared with CVCs and TIVAPs. An association between CRT diagnosis and elevated D-dimer levels was noted; however, its role as a reliable screening tool requires further investigation in prospective studies.

In this study, the majority of children underwent PICC insertion, and the incidence of CRT associated with PICCs was significantly lower than that observed with CVCs or TIVAPs. The widespread use of PICCs in pediatric patients can be attributed to their ease of placement, minimal invasiveness, and practical advantages in clinical management. Consistent with these findings, a previous pediatric study also reported a lower incidence of CRT with PICCs compared to tunneled central venous catheters [19]. The widespread adoption of PICCs in China likely reflects their practical advantages in outpatient care, including greater cost-effectiveness and improved patient adherence [20]. Material and structural differences between PICCs and CVCs or TIVAPs may also contribute to the observed variation in CRT incidence. The lower CRT rate associated with PICCs may be attributed to their smaller caliber, single- or double-lumen configuration, and stable positioning within the superior vena cava—all factors that minimize endothelial injury and reduce thrombogenic risk [21, 22]. Furthermore, PICCs exhibited longer dwell times compared with CVCs and TIVAPs, likely due to their stable positioning within the superior vena cava. This stability supports prolonged chemotherapy administration and infusion of vesicant agents, thereby minimizing the risks of drug extravasation and vascular irritation. The use of ultrasound-guided PICC placement [23], combined with their demonstrated safety in high-risk populations such as patients with thrombocytopenia or those receiving anticoagulant therapy [24, 25], further reinforces their clinical value. These advantages, including procedural ease, low complication rates and reliable functionality, align with their established role as the primary choice of CVAD in adults [24]. This suggests a similar potential for broader adoption in paediatrics.

This study showed significantly elevated D-dimer levels following CRT diagnosis in children with malignant tumors, consistent with previous reports associating D-dimer elevation with increased thrombotic risk in pediatric patients with CVADs [2]. Importantly, D-dimer possesses a high negative predictive value (NPV >90%) for excluding asymptomatic CRT [3], underscoring its potential as a useful screening adjunct [26, 27]. However, its low positive predictive value necessitates confirmatory imaging, as elevated D-dimer levels may also reflect tumor burden or inflammatory responses rather than thrombosis. Distinct symptom patterns were observed across catheter types: PICC-related CRT was predominantly symptomatic, whereas CVC- and TIVAP-related CRT tended to be asymptomatic, consistent with the findings of Thom et al. [4]. These observations allow for the speculative consideration of a risk-stratified screening approach, which must be viewed as hypothetical. For patients with PICC, vascular ultrasound should be performed immediately upon the onset of CRT-related symptoms such as pain, swelling, or catheter dysfunction. For patients with CVCs or TIVAPs, a strategy combining D-dimer levels to guide ultrasound examination may be explored, even in the absence of symptoms. Overall, this study only show a post-diagnostic rise in D-dimer. However, whether D-dimer screening can aid early detection of CRT remains to be tested in prospective cohorts.

CVCs are recognized as the most significant risk factor for VTE development in children [28]. In this study, the median interval between CVAD insertion and CRT diagnosis was 12 days, consistent with findings from an Italian cohort reporting a median of 10 days [13]. Notably, all CRT cases in this study occurred during active treatment, with the majority developing within the first month following catheterization. Furthermore, CRT associated with CVCs and TIVAPs was diagnosed within three weeks post-insertion. These findings underscore the importance of heightened clinical vigilance during the first month after catheter placement. As long as a CVAD remains in situ, the risk of CRT persists in children with malignant tumors [29]. Therefore, it is recommended that the device be removed promptly upon completion of chemotherapy to minimize chronic vascular injury, including venous stenosis and collateral vessel formation [30, 31].

The incidence of CRT observed in our study was similar to previous reports, slightly exceeding the 1.8% reported by Xue et al. [32] in a single-center in Chinese pediatric oncology cohort and significantly higher than the 0.4% reported in Chinese children without malignant tumors [33]. In agreement with prior evidence [7], asymptomatic CRT frequently goes undetected in children due to the development of robust collateral circulation during vascular occlusion, which masks clinical symptoms [34]. Nevertheless, our incidence remains lower than Western cohorts (e.g., 4.8% reported in Canada [16] and 3.5% reported in Austria [35]). Genetic predispositions such as factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A mutations, which are rare in Asian populations [36], are thought to contribute to the higher VTE risk observed among North American and European Caucasian adults compared with Asian adults [37]. Therefore, racial and ethnic variations should be considered when interpreting CRT incidence.

Our findings also show that CRT risk extends beyond acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) to other pediatric malignancies, underscoring the importance of maintaining vigilance across all cancer types. This observation aligns with VTE patterns reported in Korean pediatric populations [14], supporting the need for universal surveillance. Regarding treatment, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was the most commonly administered anticoagulant among the 20 children who developed CRT in our study. LMWH offers notable advantages in pediatric patients [38], including reduced monitoring requirements and convenient subcutaneous administration, and has gradually replaced unfractionated heparin in many settings [13]. However, the efficacy and safety of anticoagulant therapy for CRT prevention in children with malignancies remain uncertain. Current guidelines and meta-analysis recommend anticoagulant use only after CRT onset [4, 12]. Prospective studies are needed to target high-risk subgroups (e.g., children with AML, prolonged CVC use) to evaluate preventive strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center design restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader pediatric oncology populations. Second, the retrospective nature of the analysis may have resulted in incomplete data extraction from hospital records, particularly for parameters such as catheter diameter. Although data were obtained from a clinical repository to minimize recall bias, the absence of routine ultrasound screening in asymptomatic children likely led to an underestimation of asymptomatic CRT incidence. Nevertheless, given the clinical presentations reported in prior studies, the current findings likely provide a reliable estimate of symptomatic CRT incidence. Additionally, comparisons of CRT incidence among different catheter types were constrained by substantial sample size disparities, as PICCs accounted for 94.1% of all devices. The limited number of CVC and TIVAP cases consequently reduced the statistical power and generalizability of between-group analysis. To confirm and expand upon these findings, larger, prospective, multicenter studies are warranted.

Conclusions

This single-center study indicates that PICCs are associated with a lower incidence of CRT compared with CVCs and TIVAPs. Asymptomatic CRT occurred predominantly with non-PICC devices, typically within three weeks of insertion. This study reveals a post-diagnosis rise in D-dimer levels, suggesting a potential area for inquiry. However, current evidence remains insufficient to support the routine use of D-dimer for screening. Further prospective, multicenter investigations are needed to validate these findings, refine CRT prevention strategies, and optimize long-term catheter management in this population.
