Craton-margin lithosphere drives rare earth element ore-forming carbonatite magmatism
Authors: Chunfei Chen, Yu Wang, Svyatoslav S Shcheka, Michael W Förster, Weicheng Jiang, Yanjing Chen, Stephen F Foley, Dorrit E Jacob, Yongsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe majority of light rare earth element (LREE) resources are derived from carbonatite-associated deposits at cratonic margins. However, the origin of ore-forming carbonatite magmas remains unclear. Here, we conducted high pressure-temperature experiments to investigate LREE behavior during carbonate-rich sediment recycling. Results show that, during subduction, the calcic carbonates progressively concentrate LREE, becoming the principal LREE carriers and, upon melting at higher pressures, generate LREE-rich melts. As the melts ascend through cratonic lithospheric mantle, precipitation of extremely LREE-poor magnesite induces further enrichment, producing melts with LREE concentrations up to 156 times those of sedimentary carbonates. These melts are comparable to natural ore-forming carbonatites, highlighting that craton-margin lithosphere drives LREE ore-forming carbonatite magmatism. Our global compilation shows that LREE concentrations in carbonatites positively correlate with the associated deposit tonnage, suggesting that ore-forming carbonatites fundamentally control the deposit reserve magnitude. These findings provide a unified framework for the formation and global distribution of carbonatite-hosted deposits at cratonic margins.Subject terms: Geochemistry, Geodynamics

Introduction

Carbonatites have the highest rare earth element (REE) concentrations among all known igneous rocks1, and are the premier source for light REEs (LREEs) hosting the majority of primary LREE deposits2,3. Globally, both carbonatite-related REE deposits and carbonatites are spatially associated with cratonic margins (Fig. 1a, b), suggesting a close genetic relationship. The metallogeny of LREEs from carbonatites to deposits is currently receiving enhanced attention, focusing on the enrichment and mineralization of LREEs during the late magmatic and hydrothermal stages4,5. However, the origin and enrichment mechanisms of LREEs in the parental carbonatite melts have been overlooked6 despite carbonatites providing the LREEs during metallogeny2,3. We have compiled REE contents of carbonatites and associated magmatic and hydrothermal REE deposits and have found a strong positive correlation between REE2O3 reserves and REE contents of corresponding carbonatites, suggesting that the REE enrichment in carbonatites controls the scale of REE mineralization (Fig. 1c). Therefore, deciphering the genesis of LREE-rich carbonatites is essential for unraveling the deep mantle processes responsible for the formation of carbonatite-hosted REE deposits.

Carbonatite melts in the mantle are generally enriched in REE and were previously thought to form through very low-degree partial melting of mantle peridotite (F ≤ 0.1%)7. Although such low-degree melts can produce LREE-enriched carbonatites (La ≈ 80–101 ppm for F = 0.05–0.1% estimated by our modeling calculation, “Methods”), their LREE concentrations remain far lower than those observed in natural REE-rich carbonatites (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, melting of the asthenospheric mantle typically occurs at higher degrees, yielding carbonated silicate melts rather than carbonatitic melts, because mantle temperatures are generally well above the near-solidus conditions required for carbonatite generation from peridotite8,9. Moreover, partial melting of the mantle alone cannot account for the observed positive correlation between La contents and initial Sr isotope ratios (Fig. 1d). Therefore, the formation of natural REE-rich carbonatites should probably involve additional processes or alternative mechanisms beyond low-degree melting of peridotite.

Geochemical characteristics, including trace elements and Sr-Nd-Pb isotopic compositions, of the carbonatites associated with the REE deposits (e.g., Baiyan Obo10, Mianning-Dechang11, and Laiwu-Zibo in China12, Afghanistan13, as well as the Roman Region of Italy14,15) indicate involvement of the EMII and/or EMI endmembers, suggesting a significant contribution from carbonated sediments in their sources11,13. However, carbonated sediments generally have trace element contents much lower than carbonatites1,16. How these REE-poor subducted carbonates could produce REE-rich carbonatites has remained mysterious. Ore-forming carbonatite magmas have been interpreted to be formed by low degree melting of REE-rich and metasomatic mantle sources7,17 or carbonated sediments13,18, or by melting of REE-rich sediments, such as phosphorus-rich sediments19. Melting of REE-rich marine sediments would produce magmas with pronounced Ce anomalies20. Moreover, low-volume carbonatitic melts produced by low-degree melting could not survive metasomatic entrapment and rapid devolatilization during ascent21, meaning that formation of carbonatite melts cannot simply be attributed to low-degree melting of either mantle peridotite or carbonated sediments22. Only an exceptionally high sedimentary carbon flux in the subduction zone could potentially produce carbonatitic magmas voluminous enough to reach the surface12,23–25, as suggested, for instance, for Afghanistan carbonatites13. But overall, the REE behavior during recycling of carbonate-rich sediments into the mantle is largely unknown.

In this work, we use high-pressure-temperature experiments and model calculations to investigate LREE behavior during carbonate-rich crust recycling. Carbonate-rich crust experiences extensive phase transitions or melting during subduction23 and during the interaction with mantle peridotites via complex dynamic processes. Our study reconstructs REE partitioning at various stages of the recycling of carbonated sediments into the mantle and reveals a two-stage LREE enrichment mechanism for forming REE ore-forming carbonatite magma at cratonic margins.

Results

We conducted high-pressure-temperature experiments on the melting of carbonate-rich sediment at 750–1150 °C and 4–5 GPa and on their reaction with peridotites (1:1 weight proportion; 1050 °C, 5 GPa), and measured the trace element concentrations in the experimental products by in situ LA-ICP-MS (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Data 1, and “Methods”). Major element compositions of the products from the melting experiments are reported in ref. 23. Trace element data for the melting experiments and all data on the reaction experiment are presented in this study. The carbonated sediment CS2 used as starting material has REE contents similar to global average sediments (GLOSS-II) and carbonate-rich sediments in subducting trenches16 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The carbonate in the starting material has REE contents lower than the bulk carbonated sediment. The harzburgite starting material is a geochemically depleted peridotite from the Hannuoba, North China and is depleted in REE with abundances lower than depleted MORB mantle24 (Supplementary Fig. 3). These starting materials allow representation of a realistic scenario for convergent margin processes and can be used to experimentally simulate REE behavior during recycling of carbonated sediments into the mantle.

REE enrichment in calcic carbonates during subduction

The major minerals of eclogites—garnet (Grt) and clinopyroxene (Cpx)—do not incorporate significant amounts of LREEs26, whereas hydrous silicate phases (i.e., allanite (epidote) or lawsonite) can host substantial amounts of trace elements (e.g., REE and large ion lithophile elements)26–28 (Fig. 2b). However, these LREE-rich accessory hydrous phases tend to break down during devolatilization and melting as subduction proceeds29 and release trace elements, LREE in particular, into highly mobile melts/fluids, resulting in low LREE contents in the residual slab. Accordingly, both devolatilization and melting of silicate lithologies are more likely to extract and remove LREEs than to transport them into the deeper mantle, leaving the mechanism of deep LREE transfer unresolved. Melting experiments on carbonate-rich sediments and oceanic crust confirmed that, i.e., epidote is stable only below ~4 GPa and 800–850 °C23 and cannot be the main carrier of REEs into the deep mantle, whereas most carbonates can persist in the subducting slab through such melting conditions23. During carbonate-rich sediment subduction, aqueous fluids occur at T < 800 °C and preferential melting of carbonate-rich sediments produces silicate melts at T ≥ 950 °C for 4 GPa, but carbonatite melt at T ≥ 900 °C for 5 GPa23 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 2). Carbonate is represented by calcite at <4 GPa, whereas aragonite appears at 4 GPa (about 10.9%) and coexists with calcite (37.4%), marking the onset of the calcite–aragonite phase transformation (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 2). At 5 GPa, abundant aragonite (32.9–34.5%) coexists with (Mg, Fe)-calcite (6.5–9.1%). Importantly, calcite and aragonite in our experiments have REE concentrations comparable to those of hydrous accessory minerals and are much higher than the REE contents of clinopyroxene (Fig. 2b).

Experimental calcic carbonates do not exhibit Ce anomalies and show clear enrichment of LREEs relative to the carbonates in the starting materials, but are consistent with the bulk REE patterns in the starting mixture (Fig. 2b). This suggests that REEs achieve equilibrium partitioning among coexisting silicate minerals, carbonate, and melts at high pressure, effectively erasing the original REE patterns of the starting carbonates. In sharp contrast to silicate minerals, such as Cpx (partition coefficients DREEmineral/melt<1 for most trace elements30; Supplementary Fig. 6b), the D of REEs between calcic carbonates and silicate melts are well above 1 at high pressures (Fig. 2c, d, and Supplementary Data 2). The elevated D values for Ca-carbonates reflect the close match in ionic radii of REE3+ (VI-coordination: La–Lu ≈ 1.03–0.861 Å) with the Ca2+ (VI ≈ 1.00 Å) site in the calcite lattice31 (Fig. 2d). This minimizes elastic strain and is further aided by the relatively high dielectric constant and compliant bonding environment in carbonates31,32. As a result, REEs are preferentially concentrated in carbonate minerals during high-pressure melting (Fig. 2c) and during metamorphism due to the limited capacity of aqueous fluids to transport large amounts of REEs33. Given their modal abundances in subducted carbonate-rich crust, calcite and aragonite act as the principal REE carriers subducting into the deeper (~150 km) mantle, becoming progressively enriched in LREE during subduction-related metamorphism and partial melting.

However, if melting of carbonate-rich crust with REE-high calcic carbonates does occur at 5 GPa, melts are carbonatitic and contain markedly higher REE concentrations than silicate melts at lower pressures, particularly those equilibrated with aragonite, which exhibit the highest REE abundances (Supplementary Data 1 and Fig. 2a). The experimental carbonate melts generated at 5 GPa are significantly more enriched in REEs than crustal carbonatites produced by melting of sedimentary carbonates at shallow depths without subduction34,35 (Figs. 2a and 3d). The REE contents of the carbonatite melts increase systematically with decreasing temperature, reflecting lower degrees of melting and REE partitioning between carbonate melt and carbonate minerals (Supplementary Data 2). DREE values between calcite and carbonatite melts are ~1 due to their similar bonding environment, and DREE values between aragonite and carbonatite melts are slightly <1 (Fig. 2c, d). The DREE difference between calcite and aragonite is attributed to the higher elastic strain energy of the Ca2+ site (IX-fold coordination) in aragonite compared to the VI-coordinated Ca2+ site in calcite, as suggested by the steeper parabola (higher Young’s modulus E) for aragonite in the Onuma diagram than that of calcite (Fig. 2d)27,34. Note that if a subducting slab stalls at higher pressures, as hypothesized for the big mantle wedge36, then melting can occur due to a temperature increase at constant pressure. Crossing of the solidus leads to the formation of near-solidus melts at relatively low temperatures, and they have the highest DREE values. Our experiments clearly show that subduction is crucial for generating ore-forming carbonatite magmas. Nevertheless, the REE contents of melts at this stage remain lower than those of most natural carbonatites (Fig. 2a), and therefore additional processes are required to further concentrate REE during ascent and emplacement.

Craton-margin lithosphere facilitates ore-forming carbonatite magmas

Generated in subduction zones, carbonate-rich melts ascend through the overlying mantle, and their evolution depends strongly on the prevailing geotherm. We consider two end-member settings with contrasting thermal regimes (Figs. 3b and 4): (a) hot off-craton or intra-oceanic subduction systems; and (b) cold cratonic margins. In hot mantle wedges beneath off-craton regions, geotherms lie well above the stability field of carbonatite, and ascending carbonatite melts undergo extensive interactions with peridotite, progressively evolving toward silicate-rich compositions through dissolution of silicate minerals37. In contrast, cratonic margins are underlain by thick, cold lithosphere38. Carbonatite melts migrating along these colder geotherms can bypass major reactions with silicates, persisting and accumulating at the base of the cratonic lithospheric mantle39.

Our reaction experiment at 1050 °C and 5 GPa with a melt/peridotite ratio of 1:1 reveals the behavior of REEs during migration of carbonate-rich melts through mantle peridotite (Fig. 3a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 5). The experiment yielded magnesite, garnet, clinopyroxene and a residual (Ca-Mg) carbonatite melt (“Methods”). Magnesite is the main carbonate mineral (Fig. 3b) owing to the high MgO content of the starting mixture and high pressure7,40,41. The most remarkable finding is that the residual carbonatite melts coexisting with magnesite have REE abundances at least 5 times higher than carbonated silicate melts from melting experiments at the same pressure and temperature (1050 °C, 5 GPa; Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5). This pronounced REE enrichment in carbonatite melt reflects partitioning between the melt and the coexisting residue, mainly represented by clinopyroxene, garnet and magnesite. Garnet and clinopyroxene host very low REE amounts (garnet incorporates preferentially HREE; Fig. 2b). In agreement with previous studies7, DLREE (Grt/carbonatite melt) and DREE (Cpx/carbonatite melt) in our experiments are both <1 (Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6). Moreover, magnesite is extremely (by a factor of 10–100) depleted in trace elements, compared to the starting material. REE partition coefficients between magnesite and carbonatite melt are exceptionally low (0.0005–0.02), too. DREE (magnesite/carbonatite melt) values are similar to or lower than previously reported values at 1400–1600 °C7,41 and substantially lower than DREE between calcite/aragonite and carbonate-rich melts (Fig. 2c, d). The low REE concentrations in magnesite are caused by the large misfit between ionic radii of VI-fold coordinated REE3+ (La–Lu ≈ 1.03–0.861 Å)31 and an optimum radius of ~0.765 Å for the Mg2+ site (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 7) calculated using the lattice-strain model of Blundy and Wood36,42. Entering of larger REE3+ ions into the Mg2+ site would increase the elastic strain in the magnesite structure, whereas the coexisting melt—dominated by Ca2+ and Mg2+— readily accommodates REEs. To sum up, the precipitation of minerals with low affinity to REEs, magnesite in particular, results in the progressive REE enrichment of the concomitant carbonatite melts (Fig. 2).

REE partitioning data from the carbonate-rich melt/peridotite reaction experiment were used to model REE behavior during the ascend of carbonate-rich melts along both cratonic and off-cratonic margins. The simulations applied a box-model framework that couples Rayleigh fractional crystallization with wall-rock assimilation (“Methods”) confirmed that in hot off-cratonic or intra-oceanic settings, carbonate-rich melts undergo extensive reaction with peridotite (melt fraction increasing from 10 wt% to 30.4 wt%), evolve toward silicate-rich compositions, and become diluted in trace elements (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, melts migrating beneath cold cratonic margins remain carbonate-rich (melt fraction decreasing from 50 wt% to 10 wt%) and become exceptionally enriched in REEs, particularly in LREEs, in agreement with precipitation of magnesite, garnet and clinopyroxene (Figs. 3b and 4). These calculations demonstrate that high-pressure melting of carbonate-rich sediments followed by reactions with mantle peridotite results in the “final” carbonatite melts with LREE contents 156 times higher than in calcite of the starting material and 51 times higher than in the bulk carbonated sediment (Fig. 4b). The LREE/HREE ratio of melts increases due to the control of HREEs by garnet (Fig. 3b). Calculations further indicate that enrichment is significantly greater when magnesite forms than when it is absent (Fig. 4a, c). Magnesite is stable at T < 1300 °C and P > 3–5 GPa in both anhydrous and hydrous peridotite systems40, therefore, the cold geotherms characteristic of cratonic margins exert a primary control on the genesis of REE-rich carbonatites. The degree of REE enrichment in carbonatite melts is controlled by their migration pathways and the geothermal structure of cratonic margins. Both the experimental data and modeling highlight cratonic margins as a key environment for the generation and preservation of REE-rich carbonatites.

Upon ascent, these melts subsequently solidify beneath cratonic margins, forming REE-rich mantle sources (Fig. 4). At this stage, the REEs become stored in carbonate- and phlogopite-rich and/or apatite-bearing veins and dykes in the mantle42 due to the high fluorine, phosphorus and water contents in subduction-related carbonatite magmas. Subsequent remobilizing (melting) of this enriched mantle source may occur during rifting processes, forming REE-rich carbonatites8, particularly fluorine-rich carbonatitic melts that are considered especially favorable for extreme REE enrichment43. The suggested 2-stage mechanism provides a simple but plausible explanation for the preferential location of REE-rich carbonatites and giant REE deposits at the margins of thick cratonic lithosphere (Fig. 1a, b), for the rare occurrence of REE-rich carbonatites in arc systems44, and for the low REE contents of carbonated silicate volcanism sourced from recycled carbonated crust at hot geotherms in intra-oceanic settings (i.e., Kerguelen)45. This REE enrichment mechanism applies to carbonatite melts derived from the mantle, rather than to those formed through liquid immiscibility or fractional crystallization in shallow crust46.

Another proposed scenario is that carbonate-rich sediments may form buoyant diapirs that intrude into the overlying, hotter mantle47–52, where melting is likely to occur. The depth of diapirism was estimated to be between 72 and 105 km47. At such shallow mantle depths of above the 72–105 km, partial melting of carbonated sediment diapirs in the mantle would initially produce silicate melt (Fig. 3a). Moreover, this depth falls outside the stability field of magnesite, implying that REEs of carbonated sediment-derived melts would not have undergone stage 2 enrichment via reaction with peridotite. Accordingly, melting of carbonate-rich sediment diapirs would not generate REE ore-forming carbonatite melts at low pressures; instead, it would produce REE-poor silicate magmas or crustal carbonatites derived from the melting of carbonated sediment in the crust.

Carbonate subduction, carbonatites, and REE deposits at cartonic margins

A global compilation shows that carbonatites with depleted mantle-like initial 87Sr/86Sr signature (0.7026)24 have relatively low La contents (Fig. 1d). Therefore, low-degree partial melting of a depleted or primitive mantle alone cannot produce extremely REE-rich signatures. These REE-rich carbonatites plot away from the mixing trend between mantle-derived carbonatite melts and the upper continental crust in the 87Sr/86Sr ratio versus La diagram, thereby ruling out significant crustal contamination (Fig. 1d). Instead, REE-rich carbonatites overlap with our experimental melts in both La abundance and initial 87Sr/86Sr ratio, assuming a significant contribution from melts derived from recycled carbonated sediments (Fig. 1d). Elevated trace element concentrations and Sr-Nd-Pb isotopic compositions of natural carbonatites associated with the giant REE deposits at Baiyan Obo10, Maoniuping11, and Afghanistan13 indicate the involvement of carbonate-rich sediments in their mantle sources11. The two-stage REE enrichment process of carbonatite melts suggested here (operating in subduction and cratonic margin settings, as discussed above)—largely mediated by the stability and trace-element preferences of calcic carbonates and magnesite—yields LREE-rich carbonatite melts similar to natural deposit-associated carbonatites (Fig. 4), capable of forming giant ore systems.

Carbonatite-related REE deposits include magmatic-hydrothermal type and weathering type53. As the aim of this study was to decipher the genetic relationship between the deep mantle source and shallow carbonatite mineralization, the weathering-type deposits are not considered. The formation of primary carbonatites and their associated REE deposits depends strongly on the physical and chemical framework of the geological setting8,44, being concentrated at cratonic margins (Fig. 1a, b). We found a positive correlation between the LREE contents of the host carbonatites and associated REE deposit reserves (as REE2O3) (Fig. 1c), suggesting that magmatic enrichment is a major factor controlling deposit scale. The proposed LREE enrichment mechanism— from subduction of carbonated sediments to the generation of sub-deposit magmatic sources at cratonic margins—reveals how the mantle source is preconditioned for the formation of the primary ore-forming magmas. Although subsequent fractional crystallization may further enhance REE enrichment of magmas, its contribution is likely limited due to the rapid ascent of carbonatite magmas. Instead, primary carbonatite magmas play a dominant role in controlling their REE concentrations. Consequently, the formation of ore-forming carbonatite magmas, initiated by LREE enrichment during deep processes beneath cratonic margins, controls the first-order distribution of REE deposits (Fig. 1a).

Primary REE-rich carbonatite magmas formed at depth are not, by themselves, sufficient for mineralization, but rather provide the ore-forming source. Subsequent magmatic and hydrothermal processes in shallow crust (i.e., the precipitation of alkaline minerals in the crust5, reactions with wall-rocks4,54, and transport of REEs by aqueous fluid55) are responsible for the REE re-mobilization and mineralization, converting carbonatites to REE deposits. Notably, higher initial REE contents in the primary ore-forming magmas enhance the potential of subsequent magmatic–hydrothermal processes to generate large REE deposits, as evidenced by the positive correlation between LREE contents in host carbonatites and the reserves of associated REE deposits. Together, the primary LREE enrichment of ore-forming magmas and the later magmatic–hydrothermal mineralization jointly govern the formation of REE deposits at cratonic margins.

Methods

Compilation of carbonatite-related REE deposits and cratonic margins

The margins of cratons are defined following Chen et al.8, based on the latest global spatial compilation of tectonic plates and cratonic terranes from Hasterok et al.56. This dataset integrates geological provinces and tectonic boundaries using attributes derived from geophysical, geochemical, and geochronological data. Cratonic terranes are defined as continental domains older than 2.3 Ga, delineated by tectonic boundaries that have been retrospectively reconstructed to ca. 2.3 Ga by incorporating multiple geological interpretations56. Accordingly, cratonic margins (boundaries) include not only present-day tectonic boundaries between cratonic and off-cratonic regions, but also ancient paleo-suture zones preserved within cratonic interiors (Fig. 1).

The mineralization and genetic mechanism of LREE deposits are generally associated with carbonatites and alkaline rocks. In this study, we restrict our compilation to carbonatite-related REE deposits, as the REE enrichment processes in carbonatite melts are investigated here. Our database for carbonatite-related REE deposits (Supplementary Data 3) was compiled and collated from Chen et al.57. Carbonatite-related REE deposits include primary magmatic-hydrothermal type and weathering type53. As this study aims to decipher the genetic relationship between magmatic processes and shallow carbonatite mineralization, weathering-type deposits are excluded. It remains debated whether carbonatite-related REE deposits are dominantly magmatic or hydrothermal in origin; therefore, we do not distinguish between these two types in this study.

Starting materials

The chemical compositions of the starting materials are listed in Supplementary Data 1. The carbonated sediment starting material in this study is CS2 from Chen et al.23, representing carbonate-rich rocks in the subduction zone as one of the main subducting carbon reservoirs23,47. Its trace element abundancies are similar to carbonate-rich sediments in the subducting trench58, slightly lower than in subducting average sediments at the trench (GLOSS-II16), and are significantly lower than in REE-rich marine phosphate19. CS2 consists of approximately 50% silicate sediments and 50% carbonate. REE contents of carbonates in CS2 are lower than the bulk rocks, consistent with the generalization that pure limestones or chalks generally have low REE contents and exhibit negative Ce anomalies. The silicate sediments have similar REE content and pattern with GLOSS-II16, which lacks negative Ce anomalies. The bulk rock CS2 does not exhibit negative Ce anomalies, similar to carbonate-rich sediments in trenches16, because its REE contents are dominated by the silicate sediment component.

The harzburgite starting material is a mixture of 65 wt% of natural olivine (Ol, Mg# = 89.6), 30 wt% of orthopyroxene, and 5 wt% of clinopyroxene, picked from a lherzolite xenolith from Hannuoba, China47, to represent depleted peridotite in a cratonic setting. The harzburgite is depleted in REEs and incompatible elements with a lower than that of DMM24 ((La/Yb)N ratio of 0.25; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Experimental techniques

The experimental procedure for reaction experiments is the same as that used in melting experiments, as described in detail by Chen et al.23; a brief summary is provided here. Starting materials were loaded into Ag70Pd30 capsules. The experiments were conducted using a 1000 t walker-type multi-anvil apparatus at the School of Natural Sciences, Macquarie University, with Hawedia tungsten carbide cubes (7% Co) serving as secondary anvils. An 18/11 assembly (octahedral edge length/truncation edge length, mm) was employed. The high-pressure assembly consisted of spinel-doped MgO octahedral pressure medium, ZrO2 sleeve, a stepped graphite heater, and MgO sleeve containing the sample capsule. Ceramic components of the assembly were annealed at 1000 °C for 1 h and dried at 125 °C overnight prior to each experiment. Temperature was monitored using an axial W97Re3/W75Re25 thermocouple. Samples were compressed to the target pressure within 3 h and then heated at 100 °C/min. Quenching was achieved by power shutoff (~500 °C/s), followed by decompression over 16 h. All experiments lasted >48 h (Supplementary Data 1).

Analytical methods

Major element compositions of garnet, clinopyroxene, magnesite, and carbonatite melt were determined using a JEOL JXA-8530F-Plus field emission electron microprobe at the Centre of Advanced Microscopy, the Australian National University. Analytical conditions were an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a beam current of 8 nA, with peak counting times of 20–30 s for all phases. A focused beam with a diameter of 2–5 μm was used for garnet, clinopyroxene, and magnesite. In contrast, a defocused beam of 10 μm was applied to the carbonatite melt to minimize the effects of chemical heterogeneity induced by quenching. A variety of minerals and synthetic materials on Standards for Microanalysis-Astimex MINM25-53 were used as calibration and reference materials. Calibration materials included albite (for Na), orthoclase (for K, Al, Si), rutile (Ti), hematite (Fe), rhodonite (Mn), periclase (Mg), diopside (Ca), and apatite (P). For the analysis of carbonates and carbonatite melts, calcite was used as a calibration material for Ca. Additionally, dolomite, calcite (Iceland spar), and diopside were used as monitoring reference materials.

Trace element analyses were carried out using an Agilent 7700× ICP-MS coupled with a Photon Machines Excite Analyte 193 nm excimer laser ablation with HelEx II system at Macquarie Geoanalytical (MQGA) laboratories at the School of Natural Sciences, Macquarie University. The samples were ablated with a laser beam of 30–50 µm diameter. Laser fluence and repetition rate were 7.07 J/cm2 and 5 Hz, respectively. All ablations were carried in He carrier gas with a total flow rate of 0.8 l min−1. Element contents were calibrated against NIST 610 and NIST 612 with applying internal standardization of Ca. CaO contents of our samples are from the electron microprobe analyses23. Data reduction was carried out using the online software program GLITTER 4.459. The USGS reference BCR-2G, BIR-1G, BHVO-2G are used to estimate the accuracy which generally agree with recommended values within 5–10% for trace elements.

Experimental results

Partial melting of carbonated sediments produces silicate melts at T ≥ 800 °C for 3 GPa and at T ≥ 950 °C for 4 GPa, and carbonatite melt at T ≥ 900 °C for 5 GPa23 (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1). Phase relationships in the CS2 melting experiments (phase assemblages, melting reactions, and major element compositions of run products) are from Chen et al.23. The chemical compositions of the silicate melts are consistent with those formed by melting of carbonated sediments with CO2 contents of 3.0–5.0 wt% at low pressure60,61. The carbonatite melts have SiO2 contents higher than carbonatites produced by the melting of carbonated sediments26,27. The REE contents of silicate melts at 4 G Pa are much lower than those of the bulk starting materials, especially for heavy REE due to the presence of abundant residual garnet (Fig. 2a). A striking difference is that the carbonatite melts at 5 GPa have higher REE contents than silicate melts (Fig. 2a). The variation of REE contents in carbonatite melts depends on the melting degree (or temperature): their REE contents increase significantly with decreasing temperature. At high temperature (1050 °C), the REE contents of carbonatites are only slightly higher than those of the starting materials.

In contrast to melting experiments with CS2 starting mixture, the phase assemblage in the reaction experiment is composed of 46.6 wt% clinopyroxene, 24.2 wt% garnet, 14.6 wt% magnesite, 5.0 wt% olivine, and 9.6 wt% carbonatite melt. Significant increase in Cpx and Grt modes compared to melting experiments is due to the reaction of carbonated silicate melts with harzburgite: low-MgO carbonated silicate melt + Ol + Orthopyroxene (Opx) = Grt + Cpx + Ca-Mg carbonatite melt62. The phase compositions of the reaction experiment are significantly different from those in melting experiments. The clinopyroxene has lower Na2O (0.78 wt%) than those from melting experiments of CS2 (8.8–11.2 wt%), and the garnet has higher MgO contents (Supplementary Data 1). Magnesite has 42.0 wt% MgO, 5.25 wt% FeO, and 2.36 wt% CaO. Carbonatite melts are enriched in both CaO (21.6 wt%) and MgO (19.3 wt%), contain 6.07 wt% SiO2, similar to partial melts of carbonated peridotite at 6–7 GPa22,40,63,64 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Carbonatite melts in the reaction experiments have lower SiO2 and higher MgO contents than melts from melting experiments at the same temperature and pressure (1050 °C and 5 GPa). A remarkable finding is that REE abundances in the carbonatite melts in reaction experiments (stage 2 enrichment) are at least 5 times higher than in carbonated silicate melts from melting experiments (stage 1 enrichment) at the same pressure and temperature (1050 °C and 5 GPa) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5).

The calcites in the melting experiments have high REE, but low HFSE relative to the starting material CS2, and much higher than the calcites in the starting materials. Partition coefficients of REEs between calcite and silicate melt are higher than 1 (Fig. 2c, d), and the HFSE are lower than 1. Partition coefficients for REEs between calcite and carbonatite melt are close to, or slightly lower than 1. The LREE contents of aragonites are similar to those of the calcites, but their HREE contents are lower (Fig. 2c, d). REE partition coefficients between aragonite and carbonatite melt are all between 0.2 and 1, with the lowest values for LREE and HREE (Fig. 2c, d). Divalent cations (Sr2+, Ba2+) exhibit higher D than trivalent REE3+ (Fig. 2c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 7), incorporation of REE3+ into the Ca2+ site requires coupled substitutions as a charge-balance mechanism (i.e., Ca2+ + (CO3)2− = (3Na+)/REE3+ + [(P,As)O4]3−)65,66. Magnesite is the main carbonate phase in the reaction experiments between carbonate-rich sediment and harzburgite due to high MgO of the bulk system and high pressure7,40,41. Interestingly, the magnesite has extremely low trace element contents, 10-100 times lower than the starting materials, and extremely low partition coefficients for REE between magnesite and carbonatite melt (0.0005–0.02) (Fig. 2c, d).

Due to analytical issues for silicate minerals with a small size, we were only able to measure the trace elements in garnet and clinopyroxene. Grt, as expected, is enriched in HREEs and depleted in LREEs (Fig. 2b). Erratic LREE patterns for some garnet grains may be caused by overlapping adjacent melts or minerals. REE partition coefficients of between garnet and silicate melts are higher than those between garnet and carbonatite melts, especially for the LREEs (Supplementary Fig. 6). Partition coefficients of HREEs are higher than 1, and higher than those of LREEs. Cpx has REE patterns similar to the carbonatite melts but much lower abundances. Partition coefficients of REEs between Cpx and carbonatite melts are far below 1 (0.01–0.03; Supplementary Fig. 6).

Approach to equilibrium

Compositionally homogeneous minerals and regular compositional trends of minerals and melts confirm that chemical equilibrium in the melting experiments was closely approached23. REE contents of carbonatite melts in the melting experiments at 5 GPa correlate negatively with melting degree (Fig. 2a). The REE partition coefficients between carbonates and melts match the crystal-melt partition coefficients calculated from elastic moduli25 (Fig. 2c, d), suggesting that all experiments were closely equilibrated. Furthermore, the partition coefficients between carbonate (calcite and aragonite) and silicate melts (also carbonatite melts) show reproducibility across two pairs of experiments (M21-73 versus M21-71, and M65 versus M42) under similar equilibration conditions (calcite/silicate melt at 950 °C vs. 1000 °C and 4 GPa; aragonite/carbonatite melt at 900 °C vs. 950 °C and 5 GPa) (Supplementary Data 1), suggesting that equilibration of REEs between experimental phases was reached.

We first modeled the REE behavior during the interaction of slab-derived carbonated silicate melts with peridotite during ascent through a peridotite wall rock at 1050 °C, reproducing the conditions of our high-pressure experiments at 5 GPa and 1050 °C. The phase relations and initial geochemical compositions were directly constrained by these experiments (Supplementary Data 6). The modeled REE contents of the resulting carbonatite melts are consistent with those analyzed in the experimental melts (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Two tectonic scenarios, including the migration of carbonatite melts at off-cratonic settings and cratonic margins, are simulated (Fig. 4). In the off-cratonic setting, carbonate-rich melts ascending through hot mantle lithologies undergo vigorous reaction with peridotite. This process dilutes the carbonate component and drives the melts toward silicate-rich compositions22. The extent of melt–rock processing is measured by the mass-increase factor (Fmass ≥ 1), which records how much wall-rock material is assimilated into the melt. Larger Fmass values indicate stronger reaction, greater dilution of trace elements, and a shift toward silicate-dominated melts typical of hot off-cratonic environments. The calculation parameters are from previous high-pressure experiments62: the calculated initial rock is set as 51.7 wt% Ol, 8.5 wt% Opx, 20.8 wt% Cpx, 19 wt% Grt and 10 wt% carbonatite melts, and the reaction product is set as 47.1 wt% Ol, 4.1 wt% Opx, 12.7 Cpx, 5.6 wt% Grt, 30.4 wt% carbonate-bearing silicate melts. The mineral/carbonatite melt partition coefficients from this study and Dasgupta et al.7, and minerals/silicate melt partition coefficients from Adam et al.68 were used in first 25 steps and last 25 steps, respectively.

In the cratonic margin setting, carbonate-rich melts ascend beneath cold and thick lithospheric roots. Reaction with peridotite is limited, allowing the melts to remain carbonate-dominated rather than evolving toward silicate-rich compositions8. Consequently, the melts avoid dilution of trace elements and can preserve or enhance their enrichment in light rare earth elements (LREEs). This scenario reflects the stabilizing factor of cratonic lithosphere, where low temperatures and reduced melt flux suppress extensive melt–rock interaction and promote the survival of carbonatite veins with exceptionally high REE concentrations. The second process addresses the behavior of REEs during the ascent of carbonatite melts along a cold cratonic geotherm. In this case, the melt remains carbonate-rich, and the REE enrichment is tracked by the remaining melt fraction F remain ∈ (0.2,1]. For modeling (Fig. 4), we define two stages: stage 1 involves reaction and exchange with the major silicate minerals, with initial modes of 30 wt% Ol, 12.5 wt% Opx, 5 wt% Cpx, 2.5 wt% Grt, and 50 wt% melt. The reaction product is constrained by experimental results69, yielding 34 wt% Ol, 23.3 wt% Cpx, 14 wt% Grt, and 28.7 wt% melt. The assimilation factor α decreases linearly from 0.010 to 0.003. Stage 2 corresponds to magnesite formation along melt channels near the solidus of carbonated peridotite, with α declining from 0.004 to 0.000, reflecting diminishing assimilation during cooling and progressive channelization.
