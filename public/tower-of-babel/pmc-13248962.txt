Effects of Acoustic Environments on Well-Being in Sensory-Vulnerable Populations: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jingyi Mu, Zhe Qin, Tian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcoustic environments profoundly influence the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations. This review aimed to systematically examine the effects of acoustic environments on the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations across three domains: health, cognition, and emotion.MethodsThis review was reported based on the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase were searched for peer-reviewed studies published between 2000 and 2025. The certainty of evidence (CoE) was assessed using GRADE approach.ResultsA total of 180 articles were included in the review. Among sensory-vulnerable populations, unfavorable acoustic environments were generally associated with poorer health status, reduced cognitive performance, and more adverse emotional outcomes. The consistency of evidence varied across outcome categories, and the research focus differed among population groups. Specifically, studies on older adults focused on disease risk and general cognitive status; children primarily exhibited cognitive and learning-related impacts; the hearing loss group showed issues predominantly related to speech perception and auditory burden; and patient populations were characterized by effects on clinical recovery, sleep quality, and emotional well-being. The CoE for most outcome categories was rated as low or very low.ConclusionAcoustic environments are closely associated with the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations. However, the certainty of the current evidence remains limited. These findings may provide useful implications for acoustic design in schools, hospitals, and nursing homes. Further high-quality research is needed to strengthen the evidence base and improve the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations.Keywords: acoustic environment, sensory-vulnerable populations, well-being, systematic review

Introduction

The acoustic comfort in buildings is crucial to occupants’ health, well-being, communication, and productivity. Poor acoustic environments can have both short and long-term adverse effects on residents’ health.1 As vulnerable populations, children and older adults are particularly sensitive to indoor acoustic environments.2 Studies have shown that noise exposure may negatively affect children’s physiological growth and cognitive development.3 With advancing age, sensory functions, particularly vision and hearing, gradually decline, and this deterioration typically becomes more pronounced from around the age of 40 onward. Compared with younger adults, older adults tend to perform worse on speech recognition tests.4 In white noise conditions, increasing age has been associated with lower word perception scores. For older adults, especially those with sensory impairments such as hearing or visual vulnerability, or with cognitive impairment, the acoustic environment of living spaces can significantly influence their well-being and anxiety levels.5

Noise assessment is context-specific, as people’s perception of noise varies depending on the surrounding environment and the activities they are engaged in.6 The World Health Organization’s guidelines on the disease burden of environmental noise emphasize the need to pay particular attention to vulnerable populations, especially children and older adults, who may be more susceptible to noise exposure and its adverse effects on well-being.7 Although previous studies and several reviews have examined the effects of acoustic environments on specific vulnerable populations, existing reviews have largely focused on a single population, setting, or outcome.8,9 Comprehensive evidence on sensory-vulnerable populations remains limited, and few reviews have systematically evaluated the effects of acoustic environments on well-being across the three domains of health, cognition, and emotion. Therefore, this review aimed to examine the effects of acoustic environments on well-being outcomes among sensory-vulnerable populations (Figure 1) by addressing the following three research questions: 1) How do acoustic environments affect the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations? 2) Do the effects of acoustic environments differ across sensory-vulnerable populations? 3) How can acoustic environments be optimized to improve the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations?

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.10 The PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Peer-reviewed articles were retrieved from four electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase. These databases were selected because of their extensive multidisciplinary coverage across health sciences, social sciences, and medicine, thereby helping to ensure comprehensive retrieval of relevant studies in this field.11

To capture studies related to sensory-vulnerable populations, acoustic environments, and well-being, the search strategy included three groups of keywords: a) acoustic environment, including “acoustic environment*”, “soundscape*”, or “noise*”; b) sensory-vulnerable populations, including “aged”, “elderly”, “older adult*”, “child*”, “adolescent*”, “teenager*”, “hearing loss”, “hearing impairment”, “patient*”, or “inpatient*”; c) well-being, including “well-being*”, “wellness*”, or “happiness*”. The keyword groups were combined using AND to construct the search strategy. The full search strings for each database are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The review covered studies published between 2000 and 2025, as research in this field has expanded considerably since 2000. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table 1.

Screening Process

Figure 2 illustrates the literature search and study selection process. A total of 3789 records were identified through searches of four electronic databases. After the removal of duplicates, 3109 records remained for screening. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts, reducing the number of potentially eligible articles to 241. Following full-text assessment, 61 articles were excluded, leaving 180 studies for inclusion. Any disagreements arising during the screening process were resolved through discussion with a third independent reviewer. Ultimately, 180 studies were included in this review, comprising various study designs, including cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and randomized controlled trials. Subsequently, the reviewers independently extracted key information from the included studies, including title, author, publication year, study location, study design, participant characteristics, exposure, and main results and conclusions.

Quality of Evidence Assessment and Data Synthesis

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the OHAT Risk of Bias Rating Tool. The assessment covered six domains (Supplementary Table 3): selection bias, confounding bias, attrition/exclusion bias, exposure characterization, outcome characterization, and selective reporting bias.12 Two independent reviewers rated each study using four categories: “definitely low”, “probably low”, “probably high”, and “definitely high”.13 The OHAT ratings for all included studies are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

For each category of well-being outcome, the overall certainty of evidence (CoE) was assessed. The GRADE approach (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) was used to evaluate the CoE for different well-being outcomes.12 The assessment considered five domains that may lower the certainty rating (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias) and three factors that may increase it (large or very large magnitude of effect, dose-response gradient, and opposing residual confounding).14 Based on these criteria, the CoE for each outcome was ultimately categorized into four levels: high, moderate, low, or very low.

Substantial heterogeneity across study populations, acoustic exposure metrics, outcome measures, and study designs may have limited the suitability of meta-analysis. The included studies involved diverse sensory-vulnerable populations, a wide range of acoustic exposures, and health-, cognition-, and emotion-related outcomes. Given the considerable variation in measurement indicators and methodological approaches across studies, a structured narrative synthesis was conducted in accordance with the SWiM recommendations.15

As some studies investigated multiple well-being-related outcome categories, a single study could be classified into more than one category; therefore, the study counts presented in Table 2 are not mutually exclusive. The analysis showed that the CoE for the effects of acoustic environments on the well-being of vulnerable groups was mostly rated as low or very low. Age Groups

Several studies have reported associations between environmental noise and common diseases in older adults. Azuma et al conducted a study involving 181 older adults and found significant associations between subjective symptoms related to cardiovascular disease, including chest pain, pulse irregularities, acute tongue movement disorders, and limb paralysis, and various sources of environmental noise. Notably, nighttime construction noise was identified as a key risk factor.27 Furthermore, using data from two large prospective cohort studies of older adults, MESA and CHAP, D’souza et al found that urban noise exposure was associated with elevated blood pressure (BP) and greater difficulty in hypertension management.18 In contrast, favorable soundscape interventions may improve cardiovascular health and emotional well-being in older adults.28

Compared with adults, children are more susceptible to adverse acoustic conditions, such as excessive noise and prolonged reverberation. A national cross-sectional study found a significant positive association between high levels of noise annoyance and both diastolic BP and mean arterial pressure in children.20 Furthermore, prolonged loud speaking in noisy kindergarten environments may lead to poor vocal habits in children. Effective acoustic interventions can reduce indoor noise levels and may help alleviate stomachache and headache in children.29 The relationship between traffic noise and children’s health has also been widely investigated. Findings suggest that the effects of noise on children’s well-being vary by source, with road traffic noise being associated with cardiovascular and neuroendocrine effects.3,21

Hearing-Impaired Populations

Hearing impairment is a prevalent chronic disease, with age-related hearing loss (HL) being a common sensory disorder among the elderly.30,31 Epidemiological and experimental studies have shown that HL is associated with walking difficulties, reduced overall physical function, and an increased risk of falls in older adults.32 Research has demonstrated that when road traffic noise in residential areas exceeded 70 dB(A), the likelihood of clinically diagnosed HL increased substantially.25 Furthermore, in background-noise conditions, individuals with HL showed reduced high-frequency heart rate variability power and increased skin conductance, suggesting that greater physiological effort was required during listening.26 HL may also affect fatigue and overall health in children. Adams et al reported that children with bilateral HL experienced higher levels of daily fatigue than peers with normal hearing (NH).33 An experimental study of 34 children with mild-to-moderately severe HL found that, compared with baseline, the children exhibited greater distractibility, longer reaction times, reduced P300 amplitude, and increased self-reported fatigue after completing demanding speech-processing tasks.34

Patient Populations

Patients hospitalized for illness are also relatively sensory-vulnerable during treatment, and the acoustic environment in hospitals may affect both physiological and psychological well-being. Ou et al investigated the effects of ward noise on postoperative recovery in patients undergoing cesarean section and found that higher levels of postoperative noise exposure were associated with more severe pain and greater analgesic demand.35 Ukegjini et al conducted a prospective controlled trial involving 664 patients and reported that operating-room noise may have potentially negative effects on both surgeons and patients.36 Reducing indoor noise levels may lower surgeons’ stress levels and lead to improved patient outcomes. Using virtual reality, Zhou et al analyzed the effects of acoustic environments on patient recovery and found that mechanical and human-generated sounds negatively affected stress recovery, whereas music played an important role in reducing stress and facilitating both physiological and psychological recovery.37

Overall, the health effects of acoustic environments vary across different population groups. In older adults, noise exposure may increase the risk of autonomic and cardiovascular symptoms.18 In children, noise exposure may affect BP and vocal habits and may also lead to physiological symptoms such as headache, stomachache, and fatigue. Among hearing-impaired populations, noise exposure may induce physiological stress and subjective fatigue. In healthcare settings, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs), noise arising from medical equipment, conversations, and caregiving activities may worsen patient outcomes by increasing cardiovascular stress, prolonging recovery time, increasing medication use, and raising readmission rates.38 In contrast, sound-based interventions may support disease recovery and help alleviate symptoms of neuropsychiatric disorders.37

Age Groups

Age is a potential confounding factor when evaluating the effects of noise on attention, as older adults may be more sensitive to acoustic disturbances. Cross-sectional findings from The Irish Longitudinal Study on Ageing suggested a potential negative association between long-term exposure to road traffic noise and executive function in older adults.43 Similarly, Weuve et al reported that higher levels of community noise were associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease.42 In contrast, favorable soundscape interventions may help improve cognitive status in older adults. A controlled trial by James et al demonstrated that, compared with music sensitization training, piano training resulted in greater improvements in cognitive performance, including working memory, executive function, information-processing speed, abstract reasoning, and auditory selective attention.49 A cluster randomized trial by Kosters et al further showed that soundscape awareness intervention effectively reduced neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with dementia.50

The effects of acoustic environments on children’s cognition are mainly reflected in speech perception and learning ability.51 Caviola et al conducted a study involving 162 children aged 11–13 years and found that the negative impact of noise on mathematics performance was related to both task difficulty and children’s age, with younger children being more vulnerable to the adverse effects of noise.45 In addition, children’s attention and cognitive performance may also be influenced by the acoustic environment surrounding their schools.52 Foraster et al studied 2680 children aged 7–10 years in Barcelona and reported that road traffic noise around schools was associated with slower development of working memory and attention over a one-year period in school-aged children. These findings suggest that effective sound insulation and noise reduction measures in schools may help protect children’s health and support their cognitive development.53

Hearing-Impaired Populations

In the cognitive domain, research on hearing-vulnerable populations has mainly focused on speech recognition and listening effort. Rahne et al analyzed speech recognition performance and listening effort under noisy conditions in patients with HL aged 18–80 years. They found that speech recognition in noise declined progressively with age, with a significant decline beginning in the 50–51 age group.48 Cueille et al reported that, under optimal signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions, the effects of reverberation did not differ markedly between individuals with NH and those with HL; however, reverberation substantially reduced speech intelligibility in both groups.54 Wu et al investigated real-world SNR characteristics and speech-hearing conditions in 20 older adults with mild-to-moderate HL and found that communication in the HL group did not always occur in extremely noisy environments. Instead, it more often took place in settings with some background noise, where face-to-face interaction and visual cues could still support communication.55 However, as noise levels increased, speech levels also increased, but not sufficiently to fully compensate for the rise in noise, resulting in a gradual decline in SNR. Noise reduction may significantly reduce listening effort in individuals with HL, particularly in adults with severe HL.47

Patient Populations

Sleep is essential for patients’ recovery and contributes to improvements in cognitive and psychological status.38 In a controlled trial involving 60 patients, Foo et al found that high noise levels and frequent procedural interruptions in acute care wards may represent barriers to sleep and recovery.56 Similarly, Pal et al reported that noise can increase cortisol release, elevate oxygen consumption, exacerbate sleep disturbances, and disrupt circadian rhythms.57 Jespersen et al showed that soft music effectively improved the overall condition of hospitalized patients and improved nighttime sleep quality.58 Given the close relationship between sleep and immune function, resistance to infection, and cognitive performance in hospital care settings,59 reducing hospital noise is of considerable importance for patient recovery.

Overall, studies on the cognitive effects of acoustic environments have focused on different aspects across population groups. In children, research has primarily examined attention, memory, speech perception, and academic performance, whereas studies in older adults have focused more on general cognitive status, with some attention to memory and executive function. In HL groups, research has mainly concentrated on listening and speech perception. In hospitalized patients, noise in hospital wards may impair sleep quality, thereby adversely affecting cognitive functioning and recovery. The acoustic environment in hospital wards may also influence clinicians’ work efficiency. In noisy environments, healthcare staff are more likely to experience fatigue, reduced attention, and poorer work performance.60 Therefore, acoustic interventions should be implemented in settings with high concentrations of sensory-vulnerable populations, such as hospitals and nursing homes, to improve their well-being.

Age Groups

A favorable acoustic environment plays an important role in improving emotional well-being. An experimental study by Long et al highlighted the stimulating and restorative effects of outdoor soundscapes on the psychological and physiological well-being of nursing home residents, whereas overly quiet environments may be detrimental to their mental health.64 Mu et al also found that music played in nursing home activity spaces significantly influenced the emotions and behaviors of older residents. Compared with fast-tempo music, slow-tempo music tended to elicit higher levels of positive affect and satisfaction.63 However, satisfaction declined as crowd density in activity spaces increased.

The acoustic environment on campus also influences children’s emotions and behaviors. A questionnaire-based survey showed that noisy environments can trigger negative emotions, such as sadness, apprehension and irritability; such conditions may also elicit negative behavioral responses, ranging from vocal outbursts to withdrawal from verbal communication.71 A longitudinal cohort study conducted in Finland found that each 10 dB increase in traffic noise was associated with a higher risk of depression and anxiety among adolescents and young adults, particularly when sound pressure levels (SPLs) exceeded 53 dB.66 Regarding emotional restoration and social interaction, Shu et al reported that the restorative effects of specific sounds were context-dependent; birdsong showed stronger restorative effects in classroom environments, whereas fountain sounds were more restorative in park settings.65 Additionally, the type of acoustic stimuli may also influence children’s social tendencies. Compared to silent conditions, exposure to children’s voices or natural sounds tended to promote peer interaction, whereas animal sounds and classical music were associated with a greater tendency for children to seek solitude.72

Hearing-Impaired Populations

Compared with individuals with NH, those with HL exhibit reduced acoustic sensitivity in acquiring and processing everyday auditory information, which poses greater challenges in daily life. A randomized clinical trial by Huang et al showed that HL in older adults may adversely affect mental health and increase the risk of depression.31 On the other hand, music has been shown to improve emotional well-being in individuals with HL. In a preliminary case–control study, Inguscio et al found no significant differences in workload between NH individuals and unilateral cochlear implant users in either quiet or noisy environments; however, differences in pleasantness were observed.69 Cochlear implantation enabled HL individuals to listen to music in noisy environments and to enjoy music more fully in quiet settings.

Patient Populations

Hospitalized patients are often physically and psychologically vulnerable, and positive sound sources, such as music, may help improve their emotional well-being. Zhao et al conducted a study involving 108 patients with gastric cancer and found that combining noise reduction technology with music therapy effectively reduced ward noise and improved patients’ clinical condition.73 Similarly, Sayilan et al investigated 111 patients in a surgical ICU and reported that frequent exposure to high noise levels increased anxiety and adversely affected sleep quality.68 Beyond direct clinical symptoms, the acoustic environment may also influence recovery-related behaviors. Research suggests that improving the indoor acoustic environment during lunchtime in hospital wards may enhance the dining atmosphere, patients’ well-being, and social interaction, thereby potentially increasing food intake and improving nutritional status.74

Overall, the emotional effects of acoustic environments vary across population groups. Research involving children has mainly focused on emotional fluctuations triggered by school acoustic environment, including the effects of noise and beneficial auditory stimuli on mental health. In older adults, studies have paid greater attention to soundscapes in nursing homes. In patient populations, research has primarily examined the effects of ward noise on anxiety and depression, as well as the positive role of music in promoting recovery.

Discussion

Effects of Acoustic Environments on Overlapping Vulnerable Subgroups

Beyond the major categories of sensory-vulnerable populations, such as older adults, children, patients, and individuals with HL, some studies have also examined overlapping subgroups characterized by two or more vulnerability traits. These mainly include pediatric patients, geriatric patients, children or older adults with HL. Evidence suggests that when factors such as age, disease status, and hearing impairment coexist, the adverse effects of acoustic environments may be further amplified.48

Among studies involving overlapping vulnerable subgroups, health and recovery-related research has mainly focused on hospitalized children and geriatric patients. A retrospective study of older adults with proximal humerus fractures showed that higher noise levels in hospital wards were associated with poorer sleep quality, worsened emotional status, greater physiological stress, and prolonged recovery time, highlighting the importance of noise-reduction interventions in orthopedic wards.75 Studies involving pediatric patients have primarily focused on mental health and sleep quality. Findings indicate that hospital noise levels significantly affect fear and subjective perceptions among children hospitalized with pneumonia, with high-noise environments being associated with increased medical anxiety and heightened pain perception.76 Conversely, noise-reduction management has been associated with better autonomic nervous function, improved sleep quality, and enhanced inpatient recovery.77

In studies of overlapping subgroups related to hearing impairment, the primary focus has been on older adults and children with HL. In older adults, when HL coexists with aging or cognitive decline, individuals tend to exhibit poorer speech perception under noisy or reverberant conditions, greater listening effort, and more pronounced fatigue or difficulties in auditory scene analysis.48,78 A similar pattern has also been observed in children, in whom HL and cochlear implant use have been associated with greater listening fatigue and poorer speech recognition in noise.33 Overall, evidence suggests that multiple vulnerabilities may exacerbate adverse experiences in noisy, reverberant, and complex auditory environments.79,80 However, the number of relevant studies remains limited, and substantial heterogeneity exists in study design and outcome measures. More systematic comparative research is therefore needed in the future.

Strategies to Improve the Well-Being of Sensory Vulnerable Groups

Noise Control

Reducing environmental noise can improve the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations. Noise control is therefore needed in settings such as nursing homes, hospitals, and kindergartens. In nursing homes, common noise sources include mechanical sounds, background music, activity sounds, and conversations, highlighting the need for effective control of SPLs. In hospitals, noise generated by medical equipment, conversations, and caregiving activities can disturb patients’ rest and adversely affect the health of both patients and healthcare staff, and therefore requires appropriate control.81 In schools, major noise sources include external traffic noise, children’s play, and background music. Excessive noise may impair children’s language ability, cognitive development, and physical health.82

Nursing homes, hospitals, and schools are settings with relatively high concentrations of sensory-vulnerable populations. For rooms with higher SPLs or greater importance in these buildings, sound insulation, sound absorption, vibration isolation, and noise elimination treatments can be applied to walls, floors, ceilings, and other structures to minimize noise interference. For rooms such as elderly bedrooms, patient wards, operating rooms, and classrooms, measures like floor sound insulation and the addition of sound-absorbing materials can be employed to reduce interference from adjacent spaces and avoid drastic fluctuations in SPLs.

Several countries have established regulations on indoor SPLs in nursing homes, hospitals, and schools (Table 6). It is observed that the requirements for nursing homes and schools are relatively stringent, while those for hospitals are relatively lower. This may be due to the complexity of personnel flow and instrumentation in hospitals, which are more prone to generating noise. Therefore, measures should be taken to improve the indoor sound environment in hospitals. Furthermore, indoor RT should be effectively controlled. To ensure good speech intelligibility, reverberation should be limited to approximately 1 second. During the optimization of the acoustic environment, it is essential to consider the current indoor conditions and incorporate materials or structures with superior sound-absorbing properties to fully leverage their effectiveness.

Soundscape Design

The creation of a comfortable acoustic environment can significantly enhance the health and well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations.73 Studies have demonstrated that music can positively influence emotional experiences and ambient comfort for the elderly and auditory-sensitive groups,63,69 elevate emotional valence and arousal among hospital patients,61 and foster neural plasticity and language development in disadvantaged children.83,84 Therefore, it is crucial to promote healthy and soothing acoustic environments tailored to sensory-vulnerable individuals in nursing homes, hospitals, and schools.

In the process of soundscape design, it is important to devise appropriate strategies based on the sound preferences, activity requirements, and spatial characteristics of vulnerable groups. For instance, in public activity spaces of nursing homes, incorporating natural sounds (eg, bird chirps, flowing water) or classic songs and piano pieces can enhance sound comfort for the elderly. Within hospital wards, playing soft, calming music while managing SPLs prevents the music from masking vital medical equipment sounds or disrupting healthcare professionals, simultaneously alleviating patients’ anxiety and facilitating sleep. In schools, considering the varied behavioral activities, learning content, and cognitive abilities across different age groups, a tailored acoustic environment should be created to meet children’s specific needs. By optimizing the indoor acoustic environments in these settings, we can improve the emotional states and health outcomes of sensory-vulnerable populations.

Environmental Factors and Potential Biological Mechanisms

Environmental Factors

In addition to the acoustic environment, physical environmental factors such as the light environment, thermal environment, and air quality may all have an impact on the health, cognition, and emotions of sensory-vulnerable populations.38,85 This may introduce biases in some research findings. Among the existing studies, some are experimental (randomized or non-randomized controlled trials, within-subject designs, etc.) that alter sound conditions while controlling other environmental variables to explore the impact of the acoustic environment on human health and cognition.28,37 However, these studies generally have relatively small sample sizes. To broaden the research population, some studies have explored the impact of the acoustic environment on vulnerable groups using regional prospective cohort study or cross-sectional data.18,43 Due to the complexity of regional environments, these studies typically adjust for factors such as socio-economic status and air pollution to more accurately isolate the impact of noise on populations and ensure the scientific validity of the findings.

Potential Biological Mechanisms

The biological mechanisms underlying the impact of the acoustic environment on human health and cognition have been extensively studied. Schmidt et al found that a single night of exposure to aircraft noise can induce endothelial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress.86 Hypertension observed in response to nighttime noise exposure may be attributed to increased sympathetic activation and vascular dysfunction. The cessation of noise exposure can partially alleviate some physiological symptoms, yet endothelial dysfunction in cerebral microvessels persists for four days after noise cessation.87 In terms of cognition, Cheng et al demonstrated that chronic exposure to noise and stress impairs working memory among fighter pilots, resulting in neuronal loss and reduced neuronal activity in the hippocampus and other brain regions crucial for cognitive functions, thereby adversely affecting their cognitive abilities.88

Limitations and Future Directions

This review has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity existed across the included studies in terms of population characteristics, acoustic exposure metrics, outcome measures, and study design. Therefore, a meta-analysis was not considered appropriate, and a structured narrative synthesis was adopted instead.15 Second, some studies had relatively small sample sizes and inadequate control of potential confounding factors, such as baseline hearing status, lighting, and thermal conditions, which may introduce potential bias into the findings. Third, according to the GRADE assessment, the CoE for many outcome categories was rated as low or very low.

To address these limitations, future research should include more high-quality longitudinal and interventional studies, particularly those with larger sample sizes, standardized acoustic metrics, and more consistent outcome measures, in order to improve comparability across studies and strengthen the reliability of the evidence. Additionally, research on overlapping vulnerable subgroups remains limited and should be further expanded. It is also important to evaluate the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of acoustic interventions across diverse settings. Finally, integrating emerging methodologies will be important for providing more targeted, evidence-based support for different sensory-vulnerable populations.89,90

Conclusion

This review examined the effects of acoustic environments on the well-being of sensory-vulnerable populations. The main conclusions are as follows. First, acoustic environments are closely associated with health-, cognition-, and emotion-related well-being outcomes in sensory-vulnerable populations. Unfavorable acoustic environments may adversely affect physical health, clinical recovery, sleep, academic performance, listening and speech perception, and emotional status, whereas noise control and positive soundscape interventions are generally associated with more favorable outcomes. Second, the effects of acoustic environments differ across sensory-vulnerable populations. In older adults, research has mainly focused on the risk of cardiovascular symptoms and general cognitive status; in children, the effects are primarily reflected in cognition- and learning-related outcomes; in individuals with HL, the main concerns are speech perception and auditory burden; and in patient populations, the effects are mainly related to disease recovery, sleep, and emotion. Third, strategies such as noise control and soundscape design should be adopted according to the characteristics of vulnerable groups and the requirements of specific settings in order to improve their well-being. Notably, the current evidence base is limited by substantial heterogeneity in population characteristic, outcome measurement, and study design, and the CoE for most outcome categories was rated as low or very low. Therefore, further high-quality research is needed to provide a stronger evidence base for the design and optimization of acoustic environments in settings such as nursing homes, hospitals, and schools.
