Abstract
Gaussian Boson Sampling (GBS) have shown advantages over classical methods for performing some specific sampling tasks. To fully harness the computational power of GBS, there has been great interest in identifying their practical applications. In this study, we explore the use of GBS samples for computing a numerical approximation to the Gaussian expectation problem, that is to integrate a multivariate function against a Gaussian distribution. We propose two estimators using GBS samples, and show that they both can bring an exponential speedup over the plain Monte Carlo (MC) estimator. Precisely speaking, the exponential speedup is defined in terms of the guaranteed sample size for these estimators to reach the same level of accuracy ϵ \epsilon and the same success probability δ \delta in the ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) multiplicative error approximation scheme. We prove that there is an open and nonempty subset of the Gaussian expectation problem space for such computational advantage.

Introduction

Gaussian Boson Samplers (GBS) are special-purpose quantum computers designed to perform specific types of sampling tasks. The photonic implementation of these platforms is most common, where squeezed states of light are injected into a linear optical network and get measured out by photon detectors. The outcome of measurement are photon-count patterns, that are distributed according to the specific probability distribution determined by the network’s configuration. This sampling problem has been proven to be intractable for classical computers
[
1
,
15
,
21
]
. Moreover, it has been proven that, up to some mild random matrix theory conjectures, even sampling from an approximation to the GBS distribution is hard for classical computing
[
1
,
13

. In parallel to these theoretical developments, there has also been great progress in realizing GBS experimentally. Today, small-scale GBS devices, with up to 216 optical modes, have been realized
[
35
,
34
,
22

, marking one of the earliest breakthroughs in the experimental demonstration of quantum advantages. Recent proposals
[
23
,
16
]
using time-bin encoding further enhance the possibility of achieving scalable GBS devices in the near term.

The impressive advancements of GBS in both the theories and the experimental realizations have naturally led to questions about their practical utility. Applications of GBS have been proposed towards solving, e.g., vibronic spectroscopy in quantum chemistry
[

,

[

, stochastic optimization

, graph similarity

Small-scale experiments and numerical simulations have shown promising results

the theoretical validation for whether these proposals can outperform the best possible classical methods, or even surpass their chosen baseline at scale, is missing. Therefore, it remains as an open question whether GBS, compared to classical methods, can bring computational advantage for problems of practical interests.

We now introduce an important family of special cases, which we denote by

\displaystyle\mu_{\Haf^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}{f}(p,q){h}(p,q)\,\differential p\differential q

\begin{gathered}\hskip 7.0pt{f}(p,q)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=k}a_{I}p^{I}q^{I},\\

(6)

Assume that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\,\sum_{|I\rvert=k}|a_{I}p^{I}q^{I}|\,h(p,q)\,\differential p\differential q<\infty.

To solve for

The first estimator, GBS-I, is based on a well-known statistical method called importance sampling, and it is tailored to

\displaystyle P(I)=p_{I}=\frac{d}{I!}\Haf(B_{I})^{2},

(8)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{I_{i}!}{d}a_{I_{i}}.

The GBS-I estimator is unbiased, meaning that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P(|\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}]-\mu_{\Haf^{2}}|>\epsilon)=0.

Thus, GBS-I solves

for sufficiently large

is finite, we can further use Chebyshev’s inequality to derive a

The second estimator, GBS-P, is a direct probability estimation, and it is applicable to the more general
\mathcal{I}^{\times}_{\Haf}(\epsilon,\delta)
problem. The key difference here is that
\mu_{\Haf}
involves the hafnians instead of the square of hafnians as given in the GBS distribution. To address this, we first approximate
p_{J}
for each

S^{(J)}_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}1_{J}(I_{l}),~~~~~~~~~1_{J}(I)=\begin{cases}1&I=J\\
0&I\neq J\end{cases}.

and then take a weighted average of the square root of

to define

\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{|J|=k}a_{J}\sqrt{\tfrac{I!}{d}}\sqrt{\tfrac{S^{(J)}_{n}}{n}}.

Unlike the importance estimator, the GBS-P estimator is biased, and therefore we cannot use WLLN to show that GBS-P solves
\mathcal{I}^{\times}_{\Haf}(\epsilon,\delta)
with a large enough

From Markov’s inequality, a guaranteed sample size

2.1
Gaussian expectation; Wick’s theorem

We have already defined the Gaussian expectation problem in the introduction. Below we show how it can be transformed into a form that is compatible with GBS sampling. A famous result in quantum field theory due to Gian-Carlo Wick is the following.

Theorem 2.1

(Wick
[
33
]
)
.

Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
be standard coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})
. Let
h
be the zero-mean Gaussian distribution defined as in (
2
). Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}x^{I}h(x)\,\differential x=\Haf(B_{I}).

In words, the expected value of
x^{I}=x_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots x_{N}^{i_{N}}
against a multivariate Gaussian distribution can be explicitly computed from the entries of
B
by a matrix function called the
hafnian
(see definition below) and
B_{I}
is the corresponding sub- or super-matrix of the covariance matrix
B
determined by the
N
-tuple
I
. Precisely, for each column
m
, we construct a new vector by repeating the
B_{m,n}
entry
i_{n}
many times and then we repeat this new vector
i_{m}
many times.

The definition of the matrix hafnian function is provided below.

Definition 2.2

(Hafnian)
.

Let
M
be a matrix of size
2m\times 2m
for some
m\geq 1
. Then,

\Haf(M)=\frac{1}{m!2^{m}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{2m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}M_{\sigma(2j-1),\sigma(2j)},

where
\mathcal{S}_{2m}
denotes the symmetric group on
2m
elements.
If
M
is an empty matrix, then
\Haf(M)=1.
If the size of the matrix is odd, then
\Haf(M)=0.

From Wick’s theorem, we can rewrite (
1
) as

\displaystyle\mu_{\Haf}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=k}a_{I}x^{I}h(x)\,\differential x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=k}a_{I}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}x^{I}h(x)\,\differential x

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=k}a_{I}\Haf\left(B_{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}a_{I}\Haf\left(B_{I}\right).

Interchanging the sum and the integral is allowed by (
A1
) and Fubini’s theorem.
Since
\Haf(B_{I})=0
when
|I|
is odd, we can remove those corresponding terms in the sum. Therefore, we can compute
\mu_{\Haf}
by (
3
).

Similarly,
\mu_{\Haf^{2}}
can be computed using (
7
). Since
h(p,q)
is the standard zero mean Gaussian distribution with covariance function
B\oplus B
. Using Wick’s theorem, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}p^{I}q^{I}h(p,q)\,\differential p\,\differential q=\Haf\left(\begin{bmatrix}B_{I}&0\\
0&B_{I}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\Haf(B_{I})^{2}.

From (
A1’
) and Fubini’s theorem, we get
\mu_{\Haf^{2}}
is computed by (
7
).

2.2
Sampling distribution of GBS

In this section, we give a short review about the GBS sampling distribution. Mathematically speaking, the sampling distribution of GBS is obtained by measuring an arbitrary Gaussian states against multi-mode number operators. Gaussian states are those quantum states whose Wigner functions are Gaussian. Any
N
-mode Gaussian state
\hat{\rho}
can be uniquely characterized by its first moment
{\xi}\in\mathbb{C}^{2N}
and second moment
\Sigma\in\mathbb{C}^{2N\times 2N}
of annihilation and creation operators. The outcome space upon measurement is
\mathbb{N}^{N}
. For convenience, let us first introduce the notations

\displaystyle\Sigma_{Q}=\Sigma+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2N},~~~~C=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}_{N}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N}&0\end{bmatrix}\left[\mathbb{I}_{2N}-\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}\right].

(12)

In the simplest setup, if
\hat{\rho}
is a pure Gaussian state with zero displacement
\xi=0
, then the matrix
C
has a block diagonal form
C=M\oplus M^{*}
with
M\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and
-1<\textnormal{spec}(M)<1
(see Appendix
B.1
). In this case, the probability distribution of obtaining
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})
is described as in
[
15
]
(see also (
8
)), where

\begin{gathered}P(I)=p_{I}=\frac{d}{I!}|\Haf(M_{I})|^{2},~~~~d=1/\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{Q}},~~~~I!=i_{1}!\dots i_{N}!\end{gathered}

(13)

The constant
d
is the normalization factor such that the probabilities sum to 1. When
M
is real,
d
can also be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues of
M
. Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
denote the eigenvalues of
M
. Then,

\displaystyle d=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}_{j}}.

(14)

Formula (
14
) is proved in Appendix
B.2
.

More generally, if
\hat{\rho}
is an arbitrary pure Gaussian state with nonzero displacement
\xi\neq 0
, then the sampling distribution is provided in
[
27
]
, where

\begin{gathered}P(I)=p_{I}=\frac{d}{I!}\lvert\lHaf(\mathcal{M}_{I})\rvert^{2},~~~~{d}=\frac{\exp{-\frac{1}{2} {\xi}^\dagger\Sigma_Q^{-1} {\xi}}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{Q}}}\end{gathered}

(15)

Here,
\lHaf
denotes the loop hafnian function and

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}M_{i,j}&\text{if }i\neq j\\
\gamma_{i}&\text{if }i=j\end{cases},~~~~\gamma=\xi^{\dagger}\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}.

Other GBS variations, such as using threshold detectors for measurement, may produce photon patterns consisting of only binary strings. Specifically, the sampling space for an
N
-mode Gaussian state becomes
\{0,1\}^{N}
, and the probability distribution is written in terms of a new marix function called the Torontoian. See
[
26
]
for details. Furthermore, Boson Sampling as proposed in
[
1
]
uses single photons as inputs, generating photon patterns that sum to the input photon number. The probability distribution is expressed in terms of the matrix permanent.

In general, we write the abstract sampling distribution to be

\displaystyle P(I)=p_{I}=w_{I}\phi(M,I),~~~~~I\in\mathcal{S}

(16)

where
\mathcal{S}
denotes an arbitrary countable sampling space,
w_{I}
is some non-negative scalar value depending on
I
, and
\phi
is some non-negative real-valued matrix function such that

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}w_{I}\phi(w,I)=1.

For a function
a:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}|a_{I}|w_{I}\phi(M,I)<\infty,

we define the expectation value to be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(a)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}a_{I}w_{I}\phi(M,I).

3
Two estimators using GBS samples

In this section, we describe two estimators using GBS samples. Since most of the results are true when using the abstract sampling distribution given as in (
16
) with an arbitrary matrix function, we will introduce the estimators in this generality. First, we need a definition for the generalized Gaussian expectation problem.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\{a_{I}\}_{I\in\mathcal{S}}
be a collection of real coefficients with index set
\mathcal{S}
and let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
. For a complex-valued matrix function
\psi
,
we assume

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}|a_{I}||\psi(M,I)|<\infty,

and we define
\mu_{\psi}
to be

\mu_{\psi}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}a_{I}\psi(M,I).

(17)

Note that if the
a_{I}
’s in (
17
) are complex, we can reduce the computation of
\mu_{\psi}
to the computation of its real and imaginary part.
We define the approximation problem
\mathcal{G}^{\times}_{\psi}(\epsilon,\delta)
analogously to (
4
).

Definition 3.2
.

Assuming
\mu_{\psi}\neq 0
, for any given
0<\epsilon,\delta<1
we seek an estimated value
e
such that

P\left(\lvert\mu_{\psi}-e\rvert>\epsilon|\mu_{\psi}|\right)<\delta.

3.1
The importance estimator

We first introduce an estimator based on the well-known
importance sampling
method, and hence the name
importance estimator
. If
\phi
from the abstract sampling distribution (
16
) and
\psi
from the generalized Gaussian expectation problem are the same, i.e.,
\phi=\psi
, then,

\mu_{\psi}=\mu_{\phi}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}a_{I}\phi(M,I)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{I}}{w_{I}}p_{I}.

We now define the importance estimator as follows.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
I_{1},I_{2},\dots
be i.i.d with probability density
P
defined as in (
16
) and let

Z_{i}=\frac{a_{I_{i}}}{w_{I_{i}}}.

The importance estimator is defined as

\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}.

It is straightforward that
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}
is unbiased, since

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{I}}{w_{I}}p_{I}=\mu_{\phi}.

Then, using WLLN, one gets that
for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P(|\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}-\mu_{\phi}|\geq\epsilon)=0.

If one further assumes that
\text{Var}(\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n})<\infty
, which is equivalent to assuming

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a^{2}_{I}}{w_{I}}\phi(M,I)<\infty,

then a lower bound for
n
can be derived using Chebyshev’s inequality. Precisely,

Lemma 3.4
.

The importance estimator
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}
solves
\mathcal{G}_{\phi}^{\times}(\epsilon,\delta)
with

n\geq\frac{Q^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{\phi}-\mu_{\phi}^{2}}{\delta\epsilon^{2}\mu_{\phi}^{2}}\equiv n_{\phi}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}},

provided that

\displaystyle Q^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{\phi}<\infty,

(A2’)

where

Q^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{\phi}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a^{2}_{I}}{w_{I}}\phi(M,I).

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward application of Chebyshev’s inequality, and we just need to compute the variance of
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}
.

\displaystyle\textnormal{Var}(\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textnormal{Var}(Z_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{Var}(Z_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\left(Z_{1}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}]\right)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]-\mu_{\phi}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\left(\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a^{2}_{I}}{w_{I}}\phi(M,I)\right)-\mu_{\phi}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(Q^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{\phi}-\mu_{\phi}^{2}\right)<\infty.

Using Chebyshev’s inequality, we get

P\left(|\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}-\mu_{\phi}|>\epsilon|\mu_{\phi}|\right)<\frac{Q^{\Haf}_{\text{MC}}-\mu_{\phi}^{2}}{n\epsilon^{2}\mu_{\phi}^{2}}.

Setting the right-hand side to be less than
\delta
, we obtain the desired lower bound for
n
.
∎

3.2
The probability estimator

In this section, we describe a second technique, based on a straightforward probability estimation, and hence the name
probability estimator
. If
\phi
from the abstract sampling distribution (
16
) and
\psi
from the generalized Gaussian expectation problem satisfy
\psi(M,I)=\sqrt{\phi(M,I)}
and
\phi(M,I)\geq 0
for all
I
. Then,

\mu_{\psi}=\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}a_{I}\sqrt{\phi(M,I)}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}a_{J}\sqrt{\frac{p_{I}}{w_{J}}}

Definition 3.5
.

Let
I_{1},I_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. with probability distribution
P
defined as in (
16
).
For each
J\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, we define the random variable
S^{(J)}_{n}
which counts the number of occurrences of
J
in
n
draws; that is

S^{(J)}_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}1_{J}(I_{l}),~~~~~~~~~1_{J}(I)=\begin{cases}1&I=J\\
0&I\neq J\end{cases}.

We define the probability estimator to be

\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}},~~~~\alpha_{J}=a_{J}\sqrt{\frac{1}{w_{J}}}.

(18)

Remark
.

It is worthwhile to note that
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}
is always finite regardless of
K
being finite or infinite, since we can always use a dynamically sized lookup table for tracking
S^{(J)}_{n}
. Upon each draw, this table is updated by the following rule. If a draw yields
J
and there is no existing entry for
J
in the table, we add the key
J
with a value of 1. If
J
is already a key, its value is incremented by 1. Consequently, the size of the lookup table is bounded by
n_{\text{GBS-P}}
, and computing the sum in
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}
involves at most
n_{\text{GBS-P}}
terms.

Unlike the importance estimator,
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}
is biased, meaning

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}]=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}]\neq\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}]}=\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}

for some
n
.
The bias is due to the non-linearity of the square root function, and we can no longer use the WLLN.

The next result computes the expectation of the squared error. We use it to show that
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}
converges to
\mu_{\phi}
in probability as
n
grows, and in turn it gives a lower bound for
n
that is conveniently expressed in the same form as in Lemma
3.4
.

Theorem 3.6
.

If all
a_{I}\geq 0
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{I}}{w_{I}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi(M,I)}}<\infty,

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}\right)^{2}\right]<\frac{1}{n}\left(Q_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{2}\right)

(19)

where

Q_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}=\sum_{I,J\in\mathcal{S}}a_{I}a_{J}\frac{1}{w_{J}}\frac{\sqrt{\phi(M,I)}}{\sqrt{\phi(M,J)}}.

(20)

Proof.

We introduce the following shorthand notations for convenience. Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}
,
\mu=\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mu\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{n}^{2}\right]+\mu^{2}-2\mu\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{n}\right].

(21)

We first show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{n}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\sqrt{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}/{n}}\right]\leq\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{J}p_{J^{\prime}}}.

(22)

This is because

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\sqrt{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}/{n}}\right]=\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\sqrt{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}/{n}}\right].

We can swap the order of the sum and the expectation because for a fixed
n
the sum is taken over only finitely many terms.
Further,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\sqrt{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}/{n}}\right]\leq\sqrt{p_{J}p_{J^{\prime}}}.

If
J=J^{\prime}
, the result clearly holds. If
J\neq J^{\prime}
, then Jensen’s inequality on concave functions yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}}\sqrt{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{{S^{(J)}_{n}}}{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}}\right]^{1/2}.

We further compute that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{{S^{(J)}_{n}}}{{S^{(J^{\prime})}_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}1_{J}(I_{l})\sum_{l=1}^{n}1_{J^{\prime}}(I_{l})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}1_{J}(I_{l})1_{J^{\prime}}(I_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{l\neq m}^{n}1_{J}(I_{l})1_{J^{\prime}}(I_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l\neq m}p_{J}p_{J^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=p_{J}p_{J^{\prime}}n(n-1),

which gives the desired inequality. In the computation, we have used the fact that if
l=m
and
J\neq J^{\prime}
, then
1_{J}(I_{l})1_{J^{\prime}}(I_{m})=0
since
I_{l}
cannot be both
J
and
J^{\prime}
.

We now show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\right]\geq\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\left(\sqrt{p_{J}}-\frac{1-p_{J}}{2n\sqrt{p_{J}}}\right).

(23)

We first notice that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\right]=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}/{n}}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}}\right].

Again, we can swap the order of the sum and the expectation because for a fixed
n
the sum is taken over only finitely many terms. Furthermore, let
Y=X/\mathbb{E}[X]
. Then by using

\displaystyle\sqrt{x}\geq\frac{3}{2}x-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}

we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{X}]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X]}}=\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{Y}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{E}[Y]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\text{Var}[X]}{\mathbb{E}[X]^{2}}\right).

By plugging
X=S^{(J)}_{n}
into the above expression, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}}\right]\geq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[{S^{(J)}_{n}}\right]}\left(1-\frac{\text{Var}({S^{(J)}_{n}})}{2\mathbb{E}\left[{S^{(J)}_{n}}\right]^{2}}\right).

(24)

Further, since
{S^{(J)}_{n}}
is binomial and has variance
np_{J}(1-p_{J})
, the RHS of (
24
) can be computed as

\displaystyle\sqrt{np_{J}}\left(1-\frac{np_{J}(1-p_{J})}{2n^{2}p_{J}^{2}}\right)=\sqrt{np_{J}}\left(1-\frac{1-p_{J}}{2n{p_{J}}}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{S^{(J)}_{n}}}\right]\geq\sqrt{np_{J}}-\frac{1-p_{J}}{2\sqrt{np_{J}}}

and (
23
) is established.

Plugging (
22
), (
23
) and
\mu=\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{I}\sqrt{p_{I}}
into (
21
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{E}_{n}-\mu\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\sqrt{p_{J}p_{J^{\prime}}}-2\sum_{J\in\mathcal{S}}{\alpha}_{J}\sqrt{p_{J}}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\alpha_{J^{\prime}}\left(\sqrt{p_{J^{\prime}}}-\frac{1-p_{J^{\prime}}}{2n\sqrt{p_{J^{\prime}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\alpha}_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}(1-p_{J^{\prime}})\frac{\sqrt{p_{J}}}{\sqrt{p_{J^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\alpha}_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\frac{\sqrt{p_{J}}}{\sqrt{p_{J^{\prime}}}}-\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\alpha}_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}{\sqrt{p_{J}p_{J^{\prime}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\alpha}_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\frac{\sqrt{p_{J}}}{\sqrt{p_{J^{\prime}}}}-\frac{1}{n}\mu^{2}.

From
p_{J}=w_{J}\phi(M,J)
and
\alpha_{J}=a_{J}\sqrt{\frac{1}{w_{J}}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle Q_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}=\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}{\alpha}_{J}{\alpha}_{J^{\prime}}\frac{\sqrt{p_{J}}}{\sqrt{p_{J^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{J}a_{J^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{w_{J}{w}_{J^{\prime}}}}\frac{\sqrt{w_{J}\phi(M,J)}}{\sqrt{w_{J^{\prime}}\phi(M,{J^{\prime}})}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{a_{J}a_{J^{\prime}}}{{w}_{J^{\prime}}}\frac{\sqrt{\phi(M,J)}}{\sqrt{\phi(M,{J^{\prime}})}}

and we thus get the expression given as in (
19
) and (
20
).
∎

Since
Q_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}<\infty
by assumption,
a lower bound for
n
can be obtained via Markov’s inequality.

Corollary 3.7
.

The probability estimator
\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}
solves
\mathcal{G}_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\times}(\epsilon,\delta)
with

n\geq\frac{Q_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{2}}{\delta\epsilon^{2}\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{2}}\equiv n_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}},

(25)

provided that

\displaystyle Q_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}<\infty.

(A2)

Proof.

Using Markov’s inequality on
\left(\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}\right)^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle P(|\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}|>\epsilon|\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}|)

\displaystyle=P(\left(\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}\right)^{2}>\epsilon^{2}\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{2})

\displaystyle<\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{n}-\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}\right)^{2}\right]}{\epsilon^{2}\mu_{\sqrt{\phi}}^{2}}.

Setting the right-hand side to be less than
\delta
and solving for
n
, we get (
25
).
∎

4
Estimates for the guaranteed sample size

For the rest of the paper, we return to the original Gaussian expectation problems
\mathcal{I}_{\Haf}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\Haf^{2}}
. We discuss how to use GBS-I to solve for
\mathcal{I}^{\times}_{\Haf^{2}}(\epsilon,\delta)
and how to use GBS-P to solve for
\mathcal{I}^{\times}_{\Haf}(\epsilon,\delta)
. From this point onward, we only concern ourselves with the GBS sampling distribution described as in (
8
) (or (
13
)), and the matrix
B
is real, symmetric with eigenvalues bounded strictly between 0 and 1.
Our main focus is to prove the bounds for the guaranteed sample size
n_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}
and
n_{\Haf}^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}
as stated in Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. We also discuss how to use the plain Monte Carlo (MC) estimator to solve these problems and provide estimates for their corresponding guaranteed sample size.

We start off with two useful lemmas, which give upper and lower bounds for the hafnian of a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}
with
k\geq 1
. Let
m_{\textnormal{max}}
denote the maximum of the absolute value of the entries of
M
and let
m_{\textnormal{min}}
denote the minimum entry of
M
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given notations as above,

\displaystyle\Haf(M)\leq m_{\textnormal{max}}^{k}\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}.

If
M_{ij}>0
for all
i
and
j
, then

\Haf(M)\geq m_{\textnormal{min}}^{k}\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}.

Proof.

For a
2k\times 2k
matrix
M
with completely identical entries
m
everywhere,

\displaystyle\Haf(M)=m^{k}(2k-1)!!=m^{k}\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}.

(26)

Then, to bound
\Haf(M)
, we use the hafnian of the matrix with all entries equal to
m_{\textnormal{min}}
as the lower bound, and similarly use the hafnian of the matrix with all entries equal to
m_{\textnormal{max}}
as the upper bound.
∎

The next result follows immediately from Lemma
4.1
by using Stirling’s formula on both sides.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given notations as above,

\displaystyle\lvert\Haf(M)\rvert\leq\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{2km_{\textnormal{max}}}{e}\right)^{k}.

If
M_{ij}>0
for all
i
and
j
, then

\Haf(M)\geq\sqrt{2}e^{\frac{1}{25}-\frac{1}{12}}\left(\frac{2km_{\textnormal{min}}}{e}\right)^{k}.

Proof.

Using Stirling’s formula, one can bound
k!
by

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi k}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}e^{\frac{1}{12k+1}}\leq k!\leq\sqrt{2\pi k}\left(\frac{k}{e}\right)^{k}e^{\frac{1}{12k}}.

Therefore, we can bound
\lvert\Haf(M)\rvert
by

\displaystyle\lvert\Haf(M)\rvert\leq\sqrt{2}m_{\textnormal{max}}^{k}\left(\frac{2k}{e}\right)^{k}e^{\frac{1}{24k}-\frac{1}{12k+1}}\leq\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{2km_{\textnormal{max}}}{e}\right)^{k}.

If we further assume that all entries of
M
are positive, then

\displaystyle\Haf(M)\geq\sqrt{2}m_{\textnormal{min}}^{k}\left(\frac{2k}{e}\right)^{k}e^{\frac{1}{24k+1}-\frac{1}{12k}}\geq\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{2km_{\textnormal{min}}}{e}\right)^{k}e^{\frac{1}{25}-\frac{1}{12}}.

∎

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We first look at using GBS-I to solve
\mathcal{I}_{\Haf^{2}}^{\times}(\epsilon,\delta)
. Suppose the samples are drawn i.i.d with the probability density function given as in formula (
8
) (or (
13
)). It is straightforward from the two assumptions (
A1’
) and (
A2’
), and
from Lemma
3.4
that

\mathcal{E}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{n}
solves
\mathcal{I}_{\Haf^{2}}^{\times}(\epsilon,\delta)
with

n\geq n_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}=\frac{Q_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}-\mu_{\Haf^{2}}^{2}}{\delta\epsilon^{2}\mu_{\Haf^{2}}^{2}},

(27)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}a_{I}^{2}I!\Haf(B_{I})^{2}.

Our primary goal in this section is to prove the estimates for
n_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}
stated as in Theorem
1.1
.
To motivate the conditions imposed there, we will discuss the two assumptions (
A1’
) and (
A2’
) in turn and illustrate that those conditions are near optimal.

Recall that (
A1’
) is given by

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2N}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\,\sum_{|I\rvert=k}|a_{I}p^{I}q^{I}|\,h(p,q)\,\differential p\differential q<\infty

and it implies
|\mu_{\Haf^{2}}|<\infty
so as to make
\mathcal{I}_{\Haf^{2}}
well-defined. When
K<\infty
, the assumption is of course vacuous, and we focus on the case when
K=\infty
.
We begin by looking at a sufficient condition for
|\mu_{\Haf^{2}}|<\infty
.
First, from the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle|\mu_{\Haf^{2}}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}|a_{I}|\Haf(B_{I})^{2}.

Then, it follows from Lemma
4.2
that

\displaystyle|\mu_{\Haf^{2}}|\leq|a_{0}|+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}|a_{I}|\left(\frac{2kb_{\text{max}}}{e}\right)^{2k},

(28)

where
a_{0}
denotes the coefficient
a_{(0,0,\dots,0)}
corresponding to the all-zero tuple.
The right-hand side of (
28
) is a power series. By the root test, the series converges absolutely if

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}|a_{I}|\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\left(\frac{2kb_{\text{max}}}{e}\right)^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}|a_{I}|k^{2k}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\left(\frac{2b_{\text{max}}}{e}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle<1

If
C>1
, then (
28
) diverges, and if
C=1
, then the convergence of the series is undetermined.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose for all
k\geq 1
, there exists
\alpha_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\left|I\right|=2k}|a_{I}|\leq\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{e}{2k}\right)^{2k}\gamma^{k}

(30)

where

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq 1,

(31)

Lemma 5.4

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{k}\frac{k^{q}}{(2k)!}z^{2k}\leq G_{q,K,N}(z).

Proof.

We begin by truncating the sum over

F_{s}=\{k\mid sN+1\leq 2k\leq(s+1)N\},~~~~\text{where }s=0,1,2,\dots

We compute the sum over each

, notice that
m_{k}=1
for all

Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in F_{0}}m_{k}\frac{k^{q}}{(2k)!}z^{2k}=\sum_{k\in F_{0}}\frac{k^{q}}{(2k)!}z^{2k}=\textnormal{Hi}_{q,\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor}(z),

\displaystyle m_{k}=(s!)^{N}(s+1)^{r_{k}},

\displaystyle 2k=Ns+r_{k},

\displaystyle k^{q}\leq\left(\frac{(s+1)N}{2}\right)^{q}\leq\left(\frac{2sN}{2}\right)^{q}=(sN)^{q}.

\displaystyle k^{q}\leq\left(\frac{sN}{2}\right)^{q}.

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in F_{s}}m_{k}\frac{k^{q}}{(2k)!}z^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq z^{Ns}2^{-q^{-}}(sN)^{q}\frac{(s!)^{N}}{(Ns)!}\sum_{k\in F_{s}}\frac{(s+1)^{r_{k}}z^{r_{k}}}{(Ns+1)\dots(Ns+r_{k})}

\displaystyle\frac{(s!)^{N}}{(Ns)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(\sqrt{2\pi s}\right)^{N}\left(\frac{s}{e}\right)^{Ns}e^{\frac{N}{12s}}}{\sqrt{2\pi Ns}\left(\frac{Ns}{e}\right)^{Ns}e^{\frac{1}{12Ns+1}}}

\displaystyle\frac{(s+1)^{r_{k}}z^{r_{k}}}{(Ns+1)\dots(Ns+r_{k})}=\frac{N^{-r_{k}}(s+1)^{r_{k}}z^{r_{k}}}{(s+\frac{1}{N})\dots(s+\frac{r_{k}}{N})}\leq\left(\frac{2z}{N}\right)^{r_{k}},

for all
1\leq i\leq N
and
s\geq 1
.
Together, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in F_{s}}m_{k}\frac{k^{q}}{(2k)!}z^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-q^{-}}\pi^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}N^{q-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{N}{12}}s^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{1}{2}+q}\left(\frac{z}{N}\right)^{Ns}\sum_{k\in F_{s}}\left(\frac{2z}{N}\right)^{r_{k}}.

We further compute

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in F_{s}}\left(\frac{2z}{N}\right)^{r_{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{2z}{N}\right)^{j}=\textnormal{Li}_{0,N}\left(\frac{2z}{N}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in F_{s}}z^{2k}\frac{m_{k}}{(2k)!}k^{q}=2^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-q^{-}}\pi^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}N^{q-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{N}{12}}\textnormal{Li}_{0,N}\left(\frac{2z}{N}\right)s^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{1}{2}+q}\left(\frac{z}{N}\right)^{Ns}.

We collect the sum over

with

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{k}\frac{k^{q}}{(2k)!}z^{2k}

Lemma 5.15

Appendix B
Compatibility with GBS hardware

In order to use samples from the GBS hardware for the Gaussian expectation problem as presented in the text, we must know how to prepare the device to encode the matrix
B
. Since
B
is symmetric, it can be diagonalized with a unitary matrix
U
such that

\displaystyle B=UDU^{T},~~~~D=\textnormal{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{N}),~~~~1>d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\dots\geq d_{N}\geq 0.

The next theorem specifies the needed Gaussian state detailed in Section

2.2
in terms of
U
and the
d_{i}
’s. The proof largely follows
[
15
]
.

Theorem B.1
.

Given
B
,
U
and
D
as above,
the Gaussian state
\hat{\rho}(0,\Sigma)
with

\Sigma=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}U&0\\
0&U\end{bmatrix}RR^{\intercal}\begin{bmatrix}U^{\intercal}&0\\
0&U^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}

where

R=\begin{bmatrix}\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh r_{n}&\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\sinh r_{n}\\
\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\sinh r_{n}&\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh r_{n}\end{bmatrix},~~~~r_{n}\equiv\tanh^{-1}(d_{n})

gives rise to
C=B\oplus B.

Remark
.

The assumption that all eigenvalues of
B
must be strictly bounded below 1 is necessary for
r_{n}
to be well-defined, as the range of
\tanh
is
(-1,1)
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that
in order to produce
C
, we must have

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}_{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{N}&-B\\
-B&\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

Equivalently, we must show

\displaystyle\Sigma_{Q}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(B^{2}-\mathbb{I}_{n})^{-1}&0\\
0&(B^{2}-\mathbb{I}_{n})^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbb{I}_{N}&-B\\
-B&-\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{bmatrix}

(120)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}\cosh^{2}(r_{j})U^{\intercal}&0\\
0&U\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}\cosh^{2}(r_{j})U^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{N}&B\\
B&\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{bmatrix}

(121)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh^{2}(r_{n})U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(r_\indn)\sinh(r_\indn)U^{\intercal}\\
U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(r_\indn)\sinh(r_\indn)U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh^{2}(r_{n})U^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}

(122)

In (
121
), we used the identity of hyperbolic functions
\cosh^{2}=1/(1-\tanh^{2})
and the decomposition
B=UDU^{\intercal}
to compute the inverse of
B^{2}-\mathbb{I}_{n}
. In (
122
), we used the identity of hyperbolic functions
\cosh(r)\tanh(r)=\sinh(r)
.

We start computing

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(2r_\indn)U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\sinh(2r_\indn)U^{\intercal}\\
U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\sinh(2r_\indn)U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(2r_\indn)U^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}(\cosh^{2}(r_{n})-1/2)U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(r_\indn)\sinh(r_\indn)U^{\intercal}\\
U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(r_\indn)\sinh(r_\indn)U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}(\cosh^{2}(r_{n})-1/2)U^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}.

The last equality is derived from

\cosh(2r)=2\cosh^{2}(r)-1,~~~~\sinh(2r)=2\cosh(r)\sinh(r).

Given
\Sigma_{Q}=\Sigma+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2N}
, we get

\displaystyle\Sigma_{Q}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh^{2}(r_{n})U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(r_\indn)\sinh(r_\indn)U^{\intercal}\\
U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh(r_\indn)\sinh(r_\indn)U^{\intercal}&U\bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\cosh^{2}(r_{n})U^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix},

which is the result we aimed to demonstrate.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
d
be given as in (
13
). Suppose
B
is real and has eigenvalues
-1<\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}<1
. Then

\displaystyle d=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}_{j}}.

Proof.

As observed in the proof of Theorem
B.1
,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}_{Q}=\mathbb{I}_{2N}-\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}_{N}\\
\mathbb{I}_{N}&0\end{bmatrix}\left(B\oplus B\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{N}&-B\\
-B&\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\text{det}\left(\Sigma_{Q}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\text{det}\left(\Sigma^{-1}_{Q}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{det}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{N}&-B\\
-B&\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{det}\left(\mathbb{I}_{N}-B^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}(1-\lambda^{2}_{j}),

which completes the proof of the formula. The block matrix determinant formula is given by

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}\right)=\det(A)\det\left(D-CA^{-1}B\right)

provided that
A
is invertible.

∎

Appendix C
Total computation cost

It is important to highlight that the comparison made between
n^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}_{\Haf^{2}}
and
n^{\textnormal{MC}}_{\Haf^{2}}
or
n^{\textnormal{GBS-P}}_{\Haf}
and
n^{\textnormal{MC}}_{\Haf}
as stated in the main results inherently favors the plain MC estimator when looking from the perspective of the total computation cost.
In Proposition
C.5
below, we will see that plain MC requires evaluating
f
at each sampled point, and each evaluation involves a summation over as many terms as the size of the index set, which can also be expensive to compute for large
N
and
K
. Further, when
K=\infty
, we must truncate the evaluation at a finite
K
, resulting in round-off errors that do not vanish with larger sample size. On the other hand, we will see from Propositions
C.1
and
C.3
that when
K
is finite the GBS estimators display a polynomial runtime in
N
and
K
for processing each GBS sample. Moreover, the GBS estimators will avoid truncation when
K=\infty
, as indicated in Corollaries
C.2
and
C.3
.

C.1
Use GBS-I for
\mathcal{I}^{\times}_{\Haf^{2}}(\epsilon,\delta)

In this section, we give an estimate to the total computation cost of using GBS-I to solve
\mathcal{I}^{\times}_{\Haf^{2}}(\epsilon,\delta)
. Let us denote it by
C_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}
.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
K<\infty
. If
a_{I}
can be computed or retrieved using constant time steps in the worst case, then

\displaystyle C_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}\leq c_{1}N^{3}+c_{2}Kn_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}

(123)

for some constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle C_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}\leq C_{\text{init}}+n_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}\left(C_{\text{eval}}+C_{\text{sample}}\right),

where
C_{\text{init}}
denotes the cost for initializing the sampling process,
C_{\text{eval}}
denotes the cost of evaluating
\frac{d}{I_{i}!}a_{I_{i}}
, and
C_{\text{sample}}
denotes the cost of a single draw.
For a covariance matrix
B
with eigenvalues strictly bounded below 1, programming
B
onto a GBS device using the Takagi decomposition incurs a runtime complexity of
O(N^{3})
, where
N
is the size of
B
. Hence,
C_{\text{init}}\leq c_{1}N^{3}
for some constant
c_{1}
. Additionally, computing
d
at this stage costs at most
O(N)
.
Once the GBS device is configured, obtaining a sample takes constant time, yielding
C_{\text{sample}}=O(1)
. For
C_{\text{eval}}
, computing
I!
for each sampled
I
takes worst-case
O(K)
steps, along with computing
a_{I}
, which requires worst-case
O(1)
steps. Thus, the cost at this stage amounts to
c_{2}Kn_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}
for some constant
c_{2}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

For
K=\infty
, the analysis is almost the same, except in
C_{\text{eval}}
we cannot simply use
O(K)
as an upper bound for the cost of
I!
, and use the worst case cost of
a_{I}
. Instead, we give an approximation to the computational cost in the average case.

Corollary C.2
.

If
K=\infty
, and suppose
a_{I}
can be computed on the fly using
C^{\prime}_{a}
steps. Then, on average,

\displaystyle C_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}\leq c_{1}N^{3}+c_{2}d\textnormal{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(N^{2}b_{\text{max}}^{2}\right)n_{\Haf^{2}}^{\textnormal{GBS-I}}.

(124)

for some constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{\text{init}}
,
C_{\text{eval}}
and
C_{\text{sample}}
be given as in Proposition
C.1
.
We begin by noting that the average number of photons in the GBS samples can be approximated by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k)\sum_{|I|=2k}p_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k)\sum_{|I|=2k}\frac{d}{I!}\Haf(B_{I})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k)\sum_{|I|=2k}\frac{1}{I!}\frac{(2k)!^{2}}{2^{2k}k!^{2}}b_{\text{max}}^{2k}

(use formula (
26
))

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k)\frac{N^{2k}}{(2k)!}\frac{(2k)!^{2}}{2^{2k}k!^{2}}b_{\text{max}}^{2k}

(use Lemma
G.5
)

\displaystyle\leq d\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(2k)N^{2k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}}b_{\text{max}}^{2k}

(use Lemma
G.1
)

\displaystyle=\frac{2d}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{k}N^{2k}b_{\text{max}}^{2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2d}{\sqrt{\pi}}\textnormal{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(N^{2}b_{\text{max}}^{2}\right).

Then, it takes on average
O(d\textnormal{Li}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(N^{2}b_{\text{max}}^{2}\right))
many steps to compute
I!
. In total, we have

Appendix E
Use plain MC as a baseline

Classical numerical integration methods, such as those in the quadrature family, approximate integrals by discretizing the integration domain into grid points and summing weighted function evaluations at those points. These methods are well-tuned for integrals of a few variables but suffer from the curse of dimensionality. High-dimensional quadrature methods are computationally intractable because the number of grid points grows exponentially with the number of variables

N
.

Monte Carlo methods, on the other hand, are well suited for approximating high-dimensional integrals. As we have seen from Chebyshev’s inequality, their computational cost does not grow with
N
(at least not in an explicit way). Therefore, to integrate an arbitrary function against a multivariate Gaussian function as described in the Gaussian expectation problem, the plain MC estimator defined in the paper is in fact the most natural choice.

Improvements to MC methods can be made via variance reduction techniques. For example, in importance sampling, one samples from a different probability density distribution in order to reduce the variance of the function to be evaluated and thus improve the efficiency of the MC method. In that sense, GBS-I is indeed a variance reduction technique. Other strategies for variance reduction include the following.
In antithetic sampling, one exploits symmetry in the integrand function to impose negative correlation among samples. However, since the functions
f
and
h
in (
1
), the symmetry only increases the variance.
In stratified sampling, the integration domain is divided into smaller sub-regions, and sampling is enforced from each sub-region. The drawback of stratified sampling strategies is clear: they suffer from the curse of dimensionality.
In Quasi Monte Carlo or its randomized version is about using samples that are spread out more evenly. However, unlike the standard MC, QMC suffers from the curse of dimensionality.
These methods are standard and covered comprehensively in Monte Carlo textbooks; see, for example,
[
28
,
29
,
24
,
25
]
for detailed treatments.
Given the clear disadvantages of these methods, plain MC remains the best choice as a baseline method that can address an arbitrary family of Gaussian expectation problems.

Appendix F
The Guassian expectation problem related to Boson Sampling

In this section, we describe the Gaussian expectation problem in complex variables related to Boson Sampling.
Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
take the form of

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{\left|I\right|=k}a^{\prime}_{I}|z|^{2I},

(131)

with
a^{\prime}_{I}\in\mathbb{C}
and
|z|^{2I}=(z\bar{z})^{I}
. Here,
\bar{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of
z
. Recall the complex Gaussian distribution function is defined in the following way. Let

\mathcal{Z}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\exp( - \bar{z} S z)\,\differential z\wedge\differential\bar{z}

where
S
is a symmetric invertible complex matrix of size
N\times N
, and

\differential z\wedge\differential\bar{z}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\differential z_{i}\differential\bar{z}_{i}.

It is well known that

\mathcal{Z}=(-2\pi i)^{N}(\det(S))^{-1}.

Then, the complex Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance function
B^{\prime}=S^{-1}
is given by

h(z)=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\exp(- \bar{z} S z).

(132)

The Gaussian weighted problem in complex variables is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mu_{\textnormal{Per}}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}f(z)h(z)\,\differential z\wedge\differential\bar{z}.

(133)

It turns out that one can use the second form of Wick’s theorem to transform the integral (
133
) into a weighted sum of a different matrix functions, known as the
permanent
.

Recall the permanent of a matrix is given by the following.

Definition F.1

(Permanent)
.

Let
M
be an aribtrary (real or complex) matrix of size
m\times m
, where
m
can be even or odd. The permanent of a matrix
M
is

\Per(M)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}M_{j\sigma(j)},

where
\mathcal{S}_{m}
is the symmetric group on
m
elements. The permanent of an empty matrix is 0.

The second form of Wick’s theorem is given below.

Theorem F.2

(Wick
[
33
]
)
.

Let
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
|z|^{2I}=(z\bar{z})^{I}
. Let
g
be the zero mean Complex Gaussian distribution (
132
). Then

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}|z|^{2I}h(z)\,\differential z\wedge\differential\bar{z}=\Per(B^{\prime}_{I}).

(134)

In words, the expected value of
|z|^{2I}
is the permanent of
B^{\prime}_{I}
, where
B^{\prime}_{I}
is the corresponding sub- or super-matrix of
B^{\prime}
determined by

We therefore obtain that

\displaystyle\mu_{\Per}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\left|I\right|=k}a^{\prime}_{I}\Per(B^{\prime}_{I}),

(135)

and one can proceed solving this problem using the general GBS estimators described in Section

Appendix G
Technical lemmas

We give all the technical lemmas used in the paper.

Lemma G.1
.

For
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}}e^{\frac{1}{25}-\frac{1}{6}}\leq\frac{(2k)!}{2^{2k}k!^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}}.

Proof.

The proof is a direct application of Stirling’s formula. We compute that

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2\pi 2k}\left(\frac{2k}{e}\right)^{2k}e^{\frac{1}{24k+1}}}{2\pi k\left(\frac{2k}{e}\right)^{2k}e^{\frac{2}{12k}}}\leq\frac{(2k)!}{2^{2k}k!^{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi 2k}\left(\frac{2k}{e}\right)^{2k}e^{\frac{1}{24k}}}{2\pi k\left(\frac{2k}{e}\right)^{2k}e^{\frac{2}{12k+1}}}

which further simplifies to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}}e^{\frac{1}{25}-\frac{1}{6}}\leq\frac{(2k)!}{2^{2k}k!^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi k}}.

∎

Lemma G.2
.

For all
k\geq 1

\displaystyle k!\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}e^{\frac{1}{25}-\frac{1}{6}}\leq\frac{(2k)!}{k!}\leq k!\frac{4^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k}}

Proof.

This follows from Lemma

∎

Lemma G.3
.

For all

,

\frac{\sqrt{\pi l}}{2}\leq\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=l}\frac{l!^{2}}{(2k_{1})!(2k_{2})!}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\pi l}}{2}\,e^{\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{25}}.

Proof.

From Lemma

\displaystyle\frac{(2l)!}{2^{2l}}\sqrt{\pi l}\leq l!^{2}\leq\frac{(2l)!}{2^{2l}}\sqrt{\pi l}\,e^{\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{25}}.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\pi l}}{2^{2l}}\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=l}\frac{(2l)!}{(2k_{1})!(2k_{2})!}\leq\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=l}\frac{l!^{2}}{(2k_{1})!(2k_{2})!}\leq e^{\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{25}}\frac{\sqrt{\pi l}}{2^{2l}}\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=l}\frac{(2l)!}{(2k_{1})!(2k_{2})!}.

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=l}\frac{(2l)!}{(2k_{1})!(2k_{2})!}=\sum_{k_{1}\geq 0}\binom{2l}{2k_{1}}=2^{2l-1}
