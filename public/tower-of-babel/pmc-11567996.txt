Progression of Crohn’s Disease in Newly Diagnosed Patients: Results from an Observational Study Using US Claims Data
Authors: Yanni Fan, Ling Zhang, Negar Omidakhsh, Rhonda L. Bohn, Kathleen Putnam, A. Shola Adewale, Gil Y. Melmed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with Crohn’s disease (CD) experience disease progression over time, including strictures/stenoses, penetrating fistulae, and abscesses.AimsThis retrospective US population-based study aimed to characterize CD progression in newly diagnosed patients.MethodsPatient-level data from the Optum® Market Clarity database from January 1, 2016, to June 30, 2020, were used. The study comprised a 12-month baseline period (pre-diagnosis), an index date (diagnosis date), and a follow-up period. The risk of, and time to, CD progression since CD diagnosis, dispensed treatment changes following CD progression, and healthcare resource utilization before and after CD progression were assessed.ResultsOverall, 6804 newly diagnosed patients were included. Of these, 1714 (25.2%) experienced CD progression as follows: 19.3% (1183/6117) in the first 6 months, 21.6% (1188/5503) by 1 year, 24.6% (953/3875) by 2 years, and 26.6% (444/1668) by 3 years. Intestinal obstruction/stenosis was more common than fistula or abscess. Among patients with CD progression, the median (interquartile range) estimated time to progression was 2 (0–140) days; the shortest time to progression was seen with a first intestinal obstruction/stenosis (0 [0–137] days). The frequency of several dispensed treatments increased following CD progression. Among patients who experienced progression, CD-related inpatient hospital admissions/visits increased from 436 of 1714 patients (25.4%) in the month before progression to 965 (56.3%) in the month after progression.ConclusionsOver one quarter of patients with newly diagnosed CD experienced CD progression and complications within 3 years of diagnosis, highlighting the importance of monitoring for progression and early intervention to limit progression.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10620-024-08591-7.

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a relapsing, chronic, inflammatory disease that primarily affects the gastrointestinal tract, and often presents with abdominal pain, fever, and clinical signs of bowel obstruction or diarrhea with blood and/or mucus [1]. Although CD can involve any part of the gastrointestinal tract, the distal ileum and the colon are primarily affected [2]. In addition, patients can experience extraintestinal manifestations and associated autoimmune disorders [1].

At diagnosis, CD is predominantly inflammatory, without stenoses (i.e., non-stricturing) and without fistulae (i.e., non-penetrating), but the disease can progress over time to involve complications, including strictures/stenoses, penetrating fistulae, and abscesses. These complications appear more prevalent in patients with terminal ileal involvement than in those with colonic involvement [3–7]. The rates of stricturing and penetrating complications have been reported as 34–52% and 39–69% at 5 and 10 years from diagnosis, respectively [4–7]. CD complications can require surgery [2, 8]; the rate of surgery has been reported as 25–59% within 5 years of diagnosis [4, 5, 9, 10].

To improve the prognosis of CD, the treatment paradigm has shifted toward early intervention, a “treat-to-target” strategy, and proactive disease monitoring to prevent complications [11, 12]. However, although the use of biologics, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, reduce the risk of needing surgical interventions/procedures [13], many patients still experience disease progression and require surgery [14, 15].

There are limited US population-based studies quantifying the time to disease progression in CD [6]. To help fill this evidence gap, the objectives of this study were to characterize CD progression in newly diagnosed patients without CD-related complications prior to diagnosis, and describe treatment and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) before and after CD progression.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This non-interventional, retrospective cohort study was conducted using de-identified patient-level data from the Optum® Market Clarity database, a retrospective integrated claims and electronic health record database [16]. This research database originated from a large US commercial health plan with a geographically diverse patient population and is representative of the US commercially insured population. This longitudinal data set links medical and pharmacy claims with electronic health records, and includes data from medical records, clinical laboratory results, and pharmaceutical and medical claims history. The database includes approximately 50% of patients with ≥ 1 diagnosis of CD in the USA during the data coverage period of 2007–2020.

Assessment Periods

The study period was between January 1, 2016, and June 30, 2020, and consisted of baseline, patient selection, and follow-up periods. The index date was defined as the date of the first CD diagnosis and was also the date of study entry. The baseline period was defined as 365 days preceding (but not including) the index date, and was used to confirm study eligibility and that patients were newly diagnosed with CD at the index date. The patient selection period ran from January 1, 2017, to June 30, 2019, during which time patients were selected at any point if they fulfilled the study eligibility criteria. The follow-up period ran from each patient’s index date until June 30, 2020 (i.e., 1 year after the end of the patient selection period).

Study Population

Patients were required to be newly diagnosed with CD, with ≥ 2 outpatient CD diagnoses or ≥ 1 inpatient diagnosis, between January 1, 2017, and June 30, 2019. Patients also had to have been continuously enrolled in the database for 1 year prior to the index date.

Patients were excluded if they had existing CD (i.e., a previous CD diagnosis) or CD complications prior to the index date, namely intestinal obstruction/stenosis, fistula, or intestinal abscess, or CD-related surgery, before the index date.

Study Outcomes

The primary objectives were to characterize CD progression in patients without CD-related complications at baseline. These included the estimation of the risk of, and time to, CD progression since CD diagnosis (the primary outcome), a description of changes in dispensed treatments following CD progression, and assessment of CD-related HCRU before and after CD progression.

The definition of CD progression was the occurrence of CD complications, including intestinal obstruction/stenosis, fistula, or intestinal abscess. Certain CD-related surgeries occurring on or after the index date were included in the definition of progression (Online Resource 1, Supplementary Table 1). Regional extension of disease involvement or other symptom-related signs of disease progression (e.g., flares of CD activity) were not included in the definition. The earliest date the patient fulfilled the criteria for CD progression was defined as the start of progression (the progression date).

Risk of progression was estimated as the proportion of patients with CD at risk of progression who experienced progression at 6 months, and 1, 2, and 3 years following CD diagnosis. Risk of intestinal obstruction/stenosis (diagnosis or obstruction/stenosis-related surgery), fistula (diagnosis or fistula-related surgery), and intestinal abscess (diagnosis or abscess-related surgery) were estimated separately; complication-related surgeries are listed in Online Resource 1, Supplementary Table 1.

Time to progression from CD diagnosis was estimated as the duration between the index date and the date of the first progression event. Time to first occurrence of each individual complication, time to both intestinal obstruction/stenosis and fistula, and time to all three complications were estimated separately. Patients who had multiple events reported on the same day were categorized into > 1 group.

To assess change in dispensed treatment, the proportion of patients dispensed a CD-related treatment or drug class was estimated for the periods 1, 3, and 6 months before and after the progression date. Treatments for analysis were selected based on clinical relevance.

HCRU was assessed among patients who experienced CD progression. The number of visits (outpatient, emergency department, and inpatient) was estimated for the period 1 month before and 1 month after the progression date. An emergency department visit that led to hospital admission was categorized as inpatient site of service.

Statistical Analysis

Formal sample size calculations were not required as no formal comparisons were made across study cohorts. Diseases were identified using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 9th or 10th Revision Clinical Modification diagnosis codes (listed in Online Resource 1, Supplementary Table 2).

Demographic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Risk of progression was calculated as the proportion of patients experiencing progression during each time interval (6 months, and 1, 2, or 3 years) divided by the number of patients at risk of progression. Patients were censored at the first date of discontinuation of medical insurance coverage (among patients not continuously enrolled to the end of the study period), at the end of the study period, or at the date of death. For risk-of-progression estimates, all patients included in the analysis had to be eligible for the entire follow-up time for each time interval (6 months, and 1, 2, and 3 years).

Time to progression was estimated using survival analysis and reported as Kaplan–Meier estimates taking person–time into account. Time from CD diagnosis to first progression event was assessed for the entire follow-up period and was estimated as the time from the index date to the progression date. Patients were censored on the date of the first occurrence of CD progression or discontinuation of medical insurance coverage (for patients not continuously enrolled through the end of study period), at end of the study period, or on date of death. If no progression event was observed during the available follow-up, the patient was censored at the end of their follow-up and contributed their full follow-up time. Analyses were performed for all enrolled patients and for those who experienced a CD progression event.

Comparisons of dispensed treatment and HCRU were based on descriptive statistics. Among patients who experienced CD progression and had an inpatient visit, the median (interquartile [IQR] range) length of stay was calculated.

The most common medications dispensed during the baseline period were analgesics and systemic steroids (Table 2). The most common CD-related medications dispensed were antibiotics and aminosalicylic acids; biologics and immunomodulators were dispensed to relatively few patients (Table 2).

Risk of Progression

Of the 6804 patients at risk for CD progression, 25.2% (n = 1714) experienced CD progression during the total follow-up period: 19.3% (1183/6117) in the first 6 months, 21.6% (1188/5503) by 1 year, 24.6% (953/3875) by 2 years, and 26.6% (444/1668) by 3 years (Fig. 2). Of the 1714 patients who experienced CD progression, 1083 patients (63.2%) did so within 30 days of the index date. During the total follow-up period, the estimated risk (95% confidence interval [CI]) of intestinal obstruction/stenosis (stricture diagnosis or surgery) was 0.180 (0.171–0.190), the highest of all complications. The estimated risk (95% CI) was 0.063 (0.058–0.069) for fistula diagnosis or surgery, 0.065 (0.060–0.071) for intestinal abscess diagnosis or drainage procedure, and 0.052 (0.047–0.058) for CD-related surgery. However, not all patients had complete follow-up data for the duration of the study.

The most common CD-related surgery was intestinal abscess drainage in 221 patients (3.3%), followed by esophagogastroduodenoscopy-related dilations in 77 patients (1.1%), endoscopic dilation in 52 patients (0.8%), fistula fistulectomy, fibrin glue, and advancement flap in 38 patients (0.6%), and seton placement in 27 patients (0.4%).

Time to Progression

In the total follow-up period, 1714 patients experienced 1843 first progression events. The first CD progression event experienced by most of these patients was a stricture diagnosis in 1118 patients (65.2%). A fistula diagnosis and an intestinal abscess diagnosis were the first CD progression events for 277 (16.2%) and 244 (14.2%) patients, respectively. Intestinal abscess drainage was the first CD progression event for 111 patients (6.5%); other CD-related surgeries accounted for < 6% of patients’ first CD progression events.

Among the whole study cohort, the mean (SD) and median (IQR) estimated times to CD progression event were 657.8 (398.3) days and 709 (415–987) days, respectively (Fig. 3). The plots for estimated time to progression were right-skewed, leading to notably different mean and median values. Treatments

The most frequently dispensed treatments in the 6 months before and after progression were analgesics, systemic steroids, and antibiotics. Proportionally more patients were dispensed CD-related treatments following CD progression than before progression at equivalent timepoints across several treatment types; treatments selected based on clinical relevance before and after CD progression are presented in Fig. 4. This increase was particularly pronounced for TNF inhibitors and thiopurines, whereby dispensing of these treatments approximately doubled, or more than doubled, at all time points following CD progression compared with the equivalent pre-progression time period. Healthcare Resource Utilization

In the month following CD progression, the proportion of patients with inpatient hospital admissions or visits, and the number of inpatient hospital admissions or visits, approximately doubled compared with the month prior to CD progression (Fig. 5). The proportion of patients attending outpatient visits and the number of outpatient visits also increased following CD progression, albeit to a smaller extent. The proportion of patients attending the emergency department and the number of emergency department visits remained similar in the month pre- and post-CD progression. Discussion

This study characterized CD progression among newly diagnosed patients with CD and found that over one quarter of these patients experienced CD progression within 3 years of diagnosis. The most common CD complication was intestinal obstruction/stenosis, and a stricture diagnosis was the first complication to occur for most patients. It also appears that some patients already had complications at the time of diagnosis. The risk of CD progression increased over time. Proportionally more patients had treatments dispensed across several treatment types following CD progression than at equivalent timepoints pre-progression, and overall HCRU also increased post-CD progression. Although the number of inpatient hospitalizations and outpatient visits increased following CD progression, the number of emergency department visits did not. This could have been due to those patients who attended the emergency department before admission as an inpatient being included in the inpatient count, rather than in the emergency department count, but data would be required to confirm this.

In previous studies, the cumulative incidence of CD progression at 1-year post-diagnosis has ranged from 22 to 33% [4, 6]; among patients with no complications at diagnosis, the cumulative risk of CD progression at 1 year has ranged from 3 to 12% [17–19]. Risk of CD progression at 2 years post-diagnosis has been reported as 16% [20]; the 3-year incidences of stricturing and penetrating disease have been reported at 15 and 24%, respectively [4]. However, comparisons are limited between the present study and these previous studies because the study designs and definitions of CD progression differed substantially between studies. Many studies used the Vienna or Montreal Classifications [21, 22] to describe the behavior and course of disease [4, 6, 17–20], which was not used in the present study. In addition, the time periods varied between the studies, from as early as 1970 [6] and up to 2020 [17]. There have been considerable advancements in CD treatments over this period, including the introduction of biologics, such as TNF inhibitors in 1998 [23, 24], vedolizumab in 2014 [25], and ustekinumab in 2016 [26]. Such changes could have influenced CD progression and thus further complicated comparisons between studies.

We observed that a considerable proportion of CD complications occurred within the first 6 months of diagnosis, which may reflect a delay in diagnosing CD in clinical practice, as well as some complications being present at diagnosis. Furthermore, an increase over time in the cumulative probability of CD progression following diagnosis was observed in the present study, which aligns with many other studies despite differences in how CD progression was defined and the time periods that patient data were taken from [4, 6, 7, 17–19, 27, 28]. This suggests that despite the development of new treatments for CD, current therapies may not halt CD progression. For example, a population-based study of 946 patients in Hungary showed a reduction in the probability of CD progression in a cohort from 2009 to 2018 (comprising more biologic users) compared with earlier cohorts from 1977 to 2008 (comprising fewer biologic users) [28]. Conversely, another population-based study of 1162 patients in the Netherlands found no notable difference in CD progression in cohorts from 2006 to 2011 relative to earlier cohorts from 1991 to 2005; the study also found that the use of immunomodulators or TNF inhibitors within 2 years of diagnosis was not associated with lower disease progression, hospitalization, or surgery rate [27]. Taken together, these findings emphasize the need for treating physicians to be aware of the risk of complications in patients with newly diagnosed CD, and highlight the need for effective therapies to halt CD progression.

It is crucial that data on the incidence of CD progression are available because changes in disease characteristics, such as the development of internal fistulizing disease, have been associated with early CD-related hospitalization [29]. In addition, worsening CD has been associated with considerable increases in HCRU and medical financial burden [30]; moreover, stricturing/penetrating complications and a higher number of hospitalizations have been associated with an unfavorable clinical evolution of CD [31].

A strength of the present study is the relatively large geographic area covered by the Optum® Market Clarity database, which accounted for approximately 50% of patients with CD in the USA during the data coverage period. In contrast, the above studies predominantly used data from single institutions, or databases covering relatively small areas within a country [4, 6, 7, 17–20, 27, 28]. As such, this study helps fill a gap in evidence related to CD progression in the USA.

This study has several limitations. We focused on a relatively short time period of 3 years, which is considerably shorter than that assessed in other studies (5–25 years) [4–7, 17–20, 27, 28]. It is also possible that the risk of CD progression was underestimated, as other disease outcomes could indicate progression but were not captured in this study. The use of a claims database in this study introduces the risk of misclassification of disease due to coding, and possible inaccurate identification of progression using diagnosis and procedure codes. In addition, patients could have had a diagnosis prior to the baseline period, as the full patient history could not be reviewed. Finally, generalizability is limited to US patients with CD enrolled in Medicare/Medicaid and/or commercial health insurance plans; as such, results may not accurately reflect patterns in the broader CD population.

In conclusion, this study showed that over one quarter of patients with newly diagnosed CD in the USA experienced disease progression in 3 years, and that both the dispensing of several treatment types and HCRU increased following CD progression. Notably, a minority of patients are prescribed advanced therapies, even after CD progression. These findings highlight the importance of disease progression awareness among treating physicians and the need for effective therapies to halt disease progression.
