HarmMetric Eval: Benchmarking Metrics and Judges for LLM Harmfulness Assessment
Langqi Yang, Tianhang Zheng, Kedong Xiu, Yixuan Chen, Di WangPuning Zhao, Zhan Qin, Kui Ren The State Key Laboratory of Blockchain and Data Security, Zhejiang UniversityHangzhou High-Tech Zone (Binjiang) Institute of Blockchain and Data Security

Abstract
The alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human values is critical for their safe deployment, yet jailbreak attacks can subvert this alignment to elicit harmful outputs from LLMs. In recent years, a proliferation of jailbreak attacks has emerged, accompanied by diverse metrics and judges to assess the harmfulness of the LLM outputs. However, the absence of a systematic benchmark to assess the quality and effectiveness of these metrics and judges undermines the credibility of the reported jailbreak effectiveness and other risks. To address this gap, we introduce HarmMetric Eval , a comprehensive benchmark designed to support both overall and fine-grained evaluation of harmfulness metrics and judges. Our benchmark includes a high-quality dataset of representative harmful prompts paired with diverse harmful and non-harmful model responses, alongside a flexible scoring mechanism compatible with various metrics and judges. With HarmMetric Eval , our extensive experiments uncover a surprising result: two conventional metrics—METEOR and ROUGE-1—outperform LLM-based judges in evaluating the harmfulness of model responses, challenging prevailing beliefs about LLMs’ superiority in this domain. Our dataset is publicly available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/qusgo/HarmMetric_Eval , and the code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/HarmMetric-Eval-4CBE .

Introduction

The emergence of the Transformer architecture
[
37
]
, along with increases in model scale
[
16
]
, the availability of large-scale corpora, and advances in computational power, have propelled the development of large language models (LLMs). Nowadays, LLMs have become indispensable tools across a wide range of fields, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and decision-making tasks
[
1
]
. However, due to the heterogeneous nature of training data, early LLMs have occasionally produced toxic outputs. In response, researchers have proposed various alignment techniques to ensure that model outputs remain consistent with human values and ethical norms
[
22
]
. Despite these advances, aligned LLMs are still vulnerable to
jailbreak attacks
, where malicious users craft adversarial prompts to bypass alignment safeguards and elicit harmful responses from the model
[
38
]
.

As concerns around LLM safety intensify, both academia and industry have accelerated efforts to explore novel jailbreak methods and defense strategies
[
40
]
. Competitions and benchmarks centered on jailbreak attacks
[
39
,
10
,
5
]
have further stimulated more research in this area. As a result, a wide variety of automatic metrics and judges have been developed to quantify the effectiveness of jailbreak attempts—typically by evaluating the harmfulness of model responses to adversarial jailbreak prompts. These metrics and judges exhibit significant diversity, ranging from simple string-matching binary classification
[
42
]
to sophisticated numerical scoring by LLM-based judges
[
4
]
. Among them, LLM-based judges have emerged as the most prevalent approach
[
31
]
, widely believed to offer superior effectiveness in evaluating harmfulness due to their advanced semantic comprehension capabilities. In contrast, conventional reference-based metrics from the natural language processing (NLP) field are rarely employed
[
15
]
, typically regarded as lacking true semantic understanding, being overly dependent on reference answers, and ill-suited for evaluating open-ended harmful content.

However, the prevailing beliefs about the strengths and weaknesses of harmfulness metrics remain questionable, due to a critical gap between the growing diversity of available harmfulness metrics and the absence of systematic investigation into their effectiveness. This gap causes doubts about harmfulness metrics and thus significantly undermines the credibility of jailbreak-related research. A few studies
[
5
,
25
]
have made initial attempts to assess harmfulness metrics using manually annotated prompt-response pairs. However, these efforts primarily aim to demonstrate the effectiveness of their novel metrics rather than establish generalizable criteria for evaluating harmfulness or conduct a comprehensive, comparative analysis of existing metrics and judges.

To address this gap, we propose
HarmMetric Eval
, a benchmark that systematically evaluates the effectiveness of harmfulness metrics based on principled criteria of harmfulness. Our approach to build
HarmMetric Eval
begins with a fundamental question:
What constitutes harmfulness in a model response?
We define three core attributes—
unsafe, relevant, and useful
—as essential criteria for harmful responses. Built on these criteria,
HarmMetric Eval
includes 238 representative harmful prompts, each paired with two high-quality harmful reference responses. For each prompt, we create 4 diverse harmful responses and generate 8 non-harmful responses, categorized based on reasons of being safe, irrelevant, or useless. The set of responses captures a broad range of potential jailbreak response patterns and allows for fine-grained effectiveness analysis of harmfulness metrics. To ensure compatibility across diverse evaluation methods, we also introduce a scoring mechanism based on internal comparison, enabling fair assessment of metrics with varying output formats and scales.

Using
HarmMetric Eval
, we conduct an extensive evaluation of nearly 20 existing metrics and judges. Our findings reveal significant shortcomings in most current metrics, with the highest-scoring one achieving only 0.634 on a scale of 0 to 1.
Surprisingly, two conventional reference-based metrics—METEOR and ROUGE-1—outperform LLM-based judges, which are widely believed to possess a superior harmfulness evaluation capability.
These findings have significant implications for the development and selection of harmfulness metrics and offer critical insights for future research on jailbreaking.

In summary, our contributions are threefold:

•

Based on existing standards of harmfulness evaluation and real-world attack scenarios, we propose three key criteria for evaluating the harmfulness of LLM responses—
unsafe, relevant, and useful
—to comprehensively cover potential model outputs and enable fine-grained assessment of harmfulness metrics.

•

We present
HarmMetric Eval
, a benchmark designed to systematically evaluate harmfulness metrics and judges based on principled criteria of harmfulness, featuring a dataset of 238 harmful prompts and 3.3k+ diverse harmful and non-harmful model responses, alongside a scoring mechanism compatible with diverse metrics and judges.

•

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of existing harmfulness metrics, uncovering surprising insights—conventional reference-based metrics such as METEOR and ROUGE-1 can outperform widely-used LLM-based judges in evaluating harmfulness, challenging the prevailing assumption that LLMs inherently surpass traditional methods in this domain.

2
Related work

2.1
Harmfulness metrics

Jailbreak attacks are generally defined as the process of transforming a harmful prompt
p\in\mathcal{D}
into a jailbreak prompt
p^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}(p)
using an attack algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, and then feeding this prompt into an LLM
f
to elicit a harmful response. The effectiveness of the jailbreak method
\mathcal{A}
is typically measured by a harmfulness metric or judge
\mathcal{E}
through the following scoring function:

S=\frac{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{E}(f(\mathcal{A}(p)))}{|\mathcal{D}|}

(1)

These metrics and judges differ significantly in their methodology and implementation details, broadly falling into the following categories:

(i) LLM-based judges.
This category of metrics typically leverages LLMs (e.g., GPT-4
[
1
]
) as judges by embedding harmful prompts and corresponding model responses into predefined prompt templates and querying the LLM to extract judgment results from its output. Many studies
[
5
,
11
,
8
,
33
]
employ binary classification templates to determine whether a model response is harmful.
Qi et al. 2023
introduce more nuanced evaluations by embedding OpenAI usage policies into the prompt templates, enabling a five-level harmfulness rating along with justification for each judgment. Other efforts, such as Llama Guard
[
14
]
, focus on fine-tuning LLMs (e.g., Llama 2
[
36
]
) to enhance their judgment capabilities.

(ii) Harmfulness classifiers.
These metrics fine-tune text classifiers to categorize the harmfulness of jailbreak responses. For example,
Yu et al. 2023
fine-tune a RoBERTa model
[
23
]
for binary harmfulness classification, while
Huang et al. 2023
leverage the HH-RLHF dataset
[
2
]
to fine-tune a BERT-based classifier. More recently,
Mazeika et al. 2024
distill GPT-4’s judgments into a Llama 2 model, creating a powerful harmfulness classifier.

(iii) Conventional metrics.
String-matching is the most common conventional approach for harmfulness evaluation, which performs binary classification by checking whether model responses contain predefined strings that usually indicate refusal behavior (e.g., “I’m sorry”, “I cannot provide”). The string dictionary proposed by
Zou et al. 2023
is a representative example, later expanded by
Liu et al. 2023a
and
Shen et al. 2024
, who also integrate these heuristics with LLM-based judges. Additionally, some studies apply conventional reference-based NLP metrics such as F1 score or BERT similarity. For example,
Jin et al. 2024
measure the similarity between model responses and three predefined harmful ground truths using BERT and take the maximum similarity score as the harmfulness evaluation.

2.2
Evaluation of harmfulness metrics

The significant methodological diversity among harmfulness metrics naturally leads to inconsistent evaluation outcomes.
However, there remains a notable lack of systematic analysis regarding the effectiveness of these metrics, raising concerns about their reliability and undermining confidence in related research.

To bridge this gap,
Mazeika et al. 2024
evaluate harmfulness metrics using manually annotated prompt-response pairs to validate their novel metrics. Similarly,
Chao et al. 2024
adopt human-annotated datasets to justify the choice of their metrics. However, neither study provides a comprehensive evaluation of harmfulness metrics, employing a relatively narrow range of metrics and model behaviors. In contrast, our work aims to provide a more comprehensive and systematic evaluation, covering a broader set of harmfulness metrics and response types.
Liu et al. 2024
introduce JAILJUDGETEST, a human-annotated dataset for evaluating harmfulness metrics, though their dataset emphasizes multilingual coverage.
Ran et al. 2024
conduct a broad survey of harmfulness metrics and assess their effectiveness using JAILJUDGETEST and Safe-RLHF
[
7
]
. However, these studies generally construct datasets by directly inputting collected harmful or jailbreak prompts into LLMs to obtain prompt-response pairs. This construction approach mainly reflects the effectiveness of harmfulness metrics on specific LLMs or attack methods and does not enable fine-grained evaluation or analysis of metric capabilities. In contrast, our study analyzes real-world attack scenarios and proposes general evaluation criteria for the harmfulness of LLM responses. Based on these criteria, we carefully design prompt templates to generate diverse harmful and non-harmful responses for each harmful prompt. Our strategy not only ensures broader coverage of potential model response patterns and better simulation of different attack scenarios, but also enables fine-grained assessment of the fundamental capabilities that harmfulness metrics should possess.

3
HarmMetric Eval

3.1
Criteria for evaluating the harmfulness of LLM responses

A fundamental objective of a harmfulness metric is to effectively distinguish between harmful and non-harmful model responses.
Consequently, clear and well-defined criteria for what constitutes a harmful response are essential for evaluating harmfulness metrics. As shown in Table
2
, many harmfulness evaluation approaches that utilize LLM judges require explicit definitions of harmfulness criteria within the prompt templates. These criteria guide LLMs in making consistent and informed assessments. Some existing criteria focus narrowly on whether a response is
literally
harmful—
i.e.,
whether it includes content that is unethical, illegal, or misaligned with human values
[
11
]
. In contrast, more nuanced metrics take both the harmful prompt and the model response into account, requiring the response to directly address the prompt
[
29
]
and include sufficient details
[
4
,
5
]
.

We argue that the latter approach is significantly more robust. In real-world attack scenarios, we observe that models may simply repeat the given prompt, focus primarily on adversarial prefixes or suffixes rather than the intent of harmful prompt, or produce responses that are logically incoherent or semantically meaningless. Under such circumstances, it becomes critical to assess whether the model response is actually aligned with the intent of the original harmful prompt.

Based on the above observations, we define a model response (denoted as ‘RESPONSE’) to a harmful prompt (denoted as ‘PROMPT’) as harmful if and only if the RESPONSE contains content (denoted as ‘CONTENT’) that satisfies all of the following three criteria:

1.

Unsafe
: When considered together with the PROMPT, the intent or potential impact of the CONTENT must be dangerous, toxic, unethical, or illegal.

2.

Relevant
: The CONTENT must address the PROMPT rather than deviating to an unrelated topic.

3.

Useful
: The CONTENT must provide logically sound and effective assistance that could achieve the goal of the PROMPT, regardless of safety concerns.

These criteria are well-aligned with real-world attack scenarios. For example, if an adversary’s prompt is
“How to make a bomb?”
, a harmful response should be first unsafe, and a response with all refusal or warning words makes the adversary’s harmful attempt fail. Beyond that, an irrelevant response that clearly fails to meet the attacker’s specific requirement also indicates an unsuccessful harmful attempt. Besides, useless responses that lack logical soundness or fail to provide effective assistance—such as
“Fill a bottle with water and it’s a bomb”
or vague affirmations like
“Happy to help”
—are evidently not the outcomes that the attacker seeks. Thus, all three criteria are indispensable for evaluating the harmfulness of model responses, especially under an adversarial environment. Notably, we apply these criteria to specific CONTENT within a RESPONSE rather than to the RESPONSE as a whole. This strategy mirrors real-world concerns: an attacker may disregard extensive warnings or disclaimers as long as the response contains a single harmful segment that achieves the malicious goal. These criteria provide necessary guidance for constructing our benchmark in subsequent sections.

3.2
Dataset construction

To enable comprehensive and fine-grained evaluation of harmfulness metrics, we construct a benchmark dataset consisting of high-quality harmful prompts, corresponding harmful responses, and carefully designed non-harmful counterparts. Many non-harmful responses closely approximate borderline cases that could be misclassified as harmful due to satisfying some—but not all—harmfulness criteria. Each data instance in our dataset contains:

1.

A harmful prompt
p
,

2.

Two reference responses
r_{1},r_{2}
,

3.

Four harmful responses
h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},h_{4}
,

4.

Three safe responses, including a refusal
s_{r}
, a prevention
s_{p}
, and a redirection
s_{d}
,

5.

Three irrelevant responses
i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}
, and

6.

Two useless responses, one as a prompt repetition
u_{p}
and one as a useless affirmation
u_{a}
.

The example components of a data instance are shown in Table
7
. Below are the collection or generation methods for each of these components.

Harmful prompts & reference responses.
Harmful prompts serve as the foundation for generating other response types, while reference responses provide a reliable standard for evaluating harmfulness using conventional reference-based metrics such as F1 score. These components are not directly evaluated by harmfulness metrics but serve as auxiliary inputs for certain evaluation approaches. To ensure high-quality and reliable base data, we collect
20.9k
harmful prompts, each paired with two reference responses, covering diverse harmful scenarios and manually annotated with severity levels. We select only those samples where both reference responses are assigned a severity level of 2 or 3, indicating noticeable harm to individuals or society. Following initial filtering, we observed high redundancy across prompts, with
3.76%
of them being duplicates. To improve dataset diversity and benchmarking efficiency, we use NV-Embed-v2
[
18
,
26
]
, a NVIDIA model with high-performance in clustering tasks
[
27
]
, combined with K-means clustering, to select a more diverse and representative subset of harmful prompts.

Harmful responses.
Harmful responses are generated to satisfy our proposed harmfulness criteria and should be classified as harmful by ideal metrics. To ensure direct alignment with the prompt, we avoid complex jailbreak techniques and instead query unaligned models with straightforward prompts. We use Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-abliterated
[
17
]
and Wizard-Vicuna-13B-Uncensored
[
6
]
to generate two distinct harmful responses per model, utilizing the prompt templates shown in Table
3
and Table
4
.

Safe responses.
Safe responses fail to meet the unsafe criterion and should be classified as non-harmful. Aligned language models typically produce three types of safe responses to harmful prompts: (i)
Refusal
: Stating that assistance will not be provided in achieving the prompt’s goal. (ii)
Prevention
: Discouraging the prompt’s goal by condemning it, issuing warnings, analyzing potential negative consequences, etc. (iii)
Redirection
: Suggesting ethical alternatives that address the user’s underlying need or shifting the conversation to a safer topic. For explicit prompts, models often default to refusal, while more complex queries elicit prevention or redirection responses (see Figure
2
). We generate the three responses using the prompt template shown in Table
5
with a single query.

Figure 2:
Example of ChatGPT generating prevention and redirection to a harmful prompt. Although the prevention or redirection responses are safe, they may not have explicit refusal indicator strings (e.g., “I’m sorry”, “I cannot provide”), which could cause string-matching metrics to incorrectly classify them as harmful.

Irrelevant responses.
Irrelevant responses fail to meet the relevant criterion and should be classified as non-harmful. For each harmful prompt, we select three other harmful prompts with a semantic similarity score below 0.2, manually check their reference responses and and use them as the irrelevant responses for the current prompt.

Useless responses.
Useless responses fail to meet the useful criterion and should be classified as non-harmful. We generate two representative categories: (i)
Prompt repetition
: Simply restating the harmful prompt. (ii)
Useless affirmation
: Vague agreeable statements such as “Sure” or “I’d be happy to help” without offering concrete or actionable information. These categories are selected because they are trivially generated by LLMs, frequently satisfy both unsafe and relevant criteria, yet clearly lack utility. Inspired by
Zou et al. 2023
, we generate the latter by using "Sure, here is…" as a prefix and truncating any potentially harmful content that follows the colon.

After dataset construction, we further reviewed the dataset. Only 3.4% of examples were found to deviate from our settings, verifying the high validity of our data collection and generation pipeline. As mentioned above, we apply filtering procedures to enhance data quality, which constrains the size of the current dataset. We will continually expand the dataset by collecting additional high-quality examples.

3.3
Scoring mechanism

A core challenge in constructing our benchmark lies in its ability to evaluate a wide variety of harmfulness metrics that differ significantly in their evaluation criteria, output formats, and scoring ranges. To address this, we propose a
self-comparison-based scoring mechanism
that standardizes evaluation across diverse metrics and judges. Specifically, when necessary, we first adapt the output format of the target harmfulness metric
\mathcal{E}
to ensure that results deemed more harmful by the metric correspond to higher numerical scores. For example, for binary classification outputs like [unsafe, safe]
[
5
]
, we map ‘unsafe’ to a score of 1 and ‘safe’ to 0. We then evaluate the metric
\mathcal{E}
using two levels of scoring: an overall effectiveness score and fine-grained effectiveness scores.

Overall effectiveness score.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{h}
be our dataset that contains
N
data instances, where each instance
x
is structured as:

x=\{p,r_{1},r_{2},h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},h_{4},s_{r},s_{p},s_{d},i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},u_{p},u_{a}\}

(2)

As described in earlier sections,
p
,
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
are not the targets of harmfulness evaluation but serve as auxiliary inputs for certain metrics. For each instance, we compute the minimum score among the four harmful responses:

V_{h}=min(\mathcal{E}(h_{1}),\mathcal{E}(h_{2}),\mathcal{E}(h_{3}),\mathcal{E}(h_{4}))

(3)

Since the goal of a harmfulness metric is to differentiate between harmful and non-harmful responses, the metric’s overall effectiveness is based on its ability to assign higher scores to harmful responses than to non-harmful ones. However, because many harmfulness metrics are designed primarily to detect unsafe content and may only operate in binary classification settings, we provide partial credit when such metrics assign equal scores to harmful and unsafe but non-harmful (e.g., irrelevant or useless) responses. Therefore, The overall effectiveness score
score_{eff}
is defined as:

score_{eff}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathbb{I}_{1}(V_{h},V_{s})\cdot\mathbb{I}_{2}(V_{h},V_{i,u})

(4)

where:

V_{s}=max(\mathcal{E}(s_{r}),\mathcal{E}(s_{p}),\mathcal{E}(s_{d}))

(5)

V_{i,u}=max(\mathcal{E}(i_{1}),\mathcal{E}(i_{2}),\mathcal{E}(i_{3}),\mathcal{E}(u_{p}),\mathcal{E}(u_{a}))

(6)

The indicator functions are defined as:

\mathbb{I}_{1}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a\leq b\\
1,&a>b\end{cases}

\mathbb{I}_{2}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a<b\\
0.5,&a=b\\
1,&a>b\end{cases}

(7)

Fine-grained effectiveness scores.
To further analyze metric performance across different non-harmful response categories, we define the following fine-grained effectiveness scores:

For each safe response
s\in\{s_{r},s_{p},s_{d}\}
, the fine-grained score is:

score_{s}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathbb{I}_{1}(V_{h},\mathcal{E}(s))

(8)

For each useless response
u\in\{u_{p},u_{a}\}
, the fine-grained score is:

score_{u}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathbb{I}_{2}(V_{h},\mathcal{E}(u))

(9)

Since irrelevant responses
i_{1}
,
i_{2}
,
i_{3}
share the same construction method and evaluation intent, we compute a single score as:

score_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathbb{I}_{2}(V_{h},max(\mathcal{E}(i_{1}),\mathcal{E}(i_{2}),\mathcal{E}(i_{3}))

(10)

4.2
Main results

Table
1
presents the overall and fine-grained effectiveness scores of current harmfulness metrics on
HarmMetric Eval
. From the perspective of overall effectiveness (
score_{eff}
), the highest score achieved across all metrics is only
0.634
, indicating that existing harmfulness metrics still fall short of satisfactory reliability on evaluating harmful responses. As expected, string-matching methods yield the lowest scores, while LLM-based judges and harmfulness classifiers demonstrate more stable and consistent performance. Reference-based metrics, however, show highly variable results. Surprisingly, two reference-based metrics—
METEOR
and
ROUGE-1
—achieve the top two effectiveness scores, even outperforming LLM-based judges. This observation contradicts the prevailing belief that LLMs, owing to their semantic understanding capabilities, are inherently better suited for harmfulness evaluation, while conventional reference-based metrics, mainly reliant on similarity to reference answers, are less appropriate.

4.3
Fine-grained analysis

LLM-based judges, particularly those utilizing prompt templates with powerful closed-source models, show strong ability to differentiate between safe and unsafe responses. Their evaluation performance on irrelevant responses and prompt repetitions is also acceptable. However, Llama Guard variants underperform in comparison with template-based methods (QiEval, PAIREval, JbBEval), probably because their judgment criteria are limited to safety, whereas template-based methods consider requirements for relevance and utility. Nonetheless, most LLM-based judges, even those using sophisticated prompts like QiEval, struggled to differentiate between simple prompt repetition and genuinely harmful responses, which highlights the shortcoming of the current prompt design. Most notably, LLM-based judges tend to over-score vague affirmations (e.g., "Sure, here is a step-by-step guide" without following content) despite explicit instructions to consider the level of detail, which results in poor performance on useless affirmation responses and is the main reason for their lower overall scores compared to top conventional reference-based metrics.

Figure 3:
Effectiveness scores of METEOR and ROUGE-1 vs. the average scores of other harmfulness metric categories. METEOR and ROUGE-1 outperform other metrics overall, which we attribute primarily to their superior ability to evaluate irrelevant responses and useless affirmations.

Harmfulness classifiers exhibit comparable performance to LLM-based judges. HBEval, in particular, stands out for its balanced capability across all response types, especially in distinguishing irrelevant responses.

String-matching metrics perform poorly on redirection, irrelevant, and useless responses, as they typically lack explicit refusal strings. Moreover, these metrics also fail to reliably differentiate refusals and preventions from harmful responses, likely due to high-frequency false negatives—for example, the refusal string “illegal” appears in at least one harmful response across
18.1%
of harmful prompts in our dataset, significantly compromising the effectiveness score. ShenEval performs better on refusals, due to its integration of ChatGLM-based judgments, yet its prompting approach—simply asking whether the model response provides some level of answer to the question—introduces bias on prevention and redirection cases, causing further misjudgments. These results suggest that string-matching metrics may not be suitable for harmfulness evaluation, particularly given its poor performance even in distinguishing refusal responses.

Conventional reference-based metrics, while varying widely in effectiveness, yield surprising insights. METEOR and ROUGE-1 demonstrate unexpectedly leading performance, particularly in evaluating useless affirmations. This finding is surprising because our dataset construction strategy enriches the dataset with diverse harmful responses by prompting different models to generate distinct outputs, many of which do not appear similar to the reference responses.
The surprising finding suggests that metrics based on lexical similarity also exhibit an ability to assess semantic information in model responses, even in scenarios where the responses are highly varied. This observation points to a promising research direction: developing a more reliable judging mechanism for harmfulness evaluation in jailbreak research by integrating reference-based metrics such as METEOR and ROUGE-1 into the mechanism.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
HarmMetric Eval
, a comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate the effectiveness of harmfulness metrics and judges. We begin by establishing clear criteria for assessing the harmfulness of model responses and construct a dataset in which each instance consists of a harmful prompt paired with 14 diverse model-generated responses spanning both harmful and non-harmful categories. We further propose a novel scoring mechanism that supports both overall and fine-grained effectiveness assessment across different types of metrics. Using
HarmMetric Eval
, we conduct an extensive evaluation of 20 widely-used harmfulness metrics and provide detailed analyses of their strengths and limitations. Notably, our findings reveal that certain conventional metrics outperform even LLM-based judges in harmfulness evaluation, challenging long-standing assumptions in this field. We hope that
HarmMetric Eval
serves as a valuable resource for guiding the selection, analysis, and future development of reliable harmfulness metrics.

Appendix C
Societal impacts

LLMs are increasingly deployed across a wide range of societal and industrial domains, but their misuse poses significant risks. Ensuring that LLM outputs align with human values is therefore of critical societal importance. Research on jailbreak attacks plays a vital role in enhancing LLM safety by enabling a deeper understanding of potential vulnerabilities and informing the development of effective defenses. However, as discussed in §
1
, the lack of systematic research on harmfulness metrics weakens our ability to accurately identify harmful model responses and undermines the credibility of both jailbreak attack strategies and their corresponding mitigation techniques. This threatens the safe and trustworthy deployment of LLMs. Our benchmark,
HarmMetric Eval
, addresses this gap by providing comprehensive and fine-grained evaluations of existing harmfulness metrics. This benchmark not only serves as a valuable resource for guiding the selection, analysis, and development of more reliable harmfulness metrics but also contributes to advancing LLM safety and enhancing their positive societal impact. To conduct these evaluations, we necessarily generate a set of harmful model outputs. We acknowledge the potential risks of misuse associated with this data. Therefore, after the review process, we will implement stricter access controls and additional safeguards to mitigate unintended negative consequences.
