Surgical management of a traumatic periprosthetic glenoid fracture following a reverse shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Ethan D Kosco, Maria LaMontagne, Neal Kapoor, Michael Makowski
Published: 2026

There are 3 main types of shoulder replacements — anatomic shoulder arthroplasty, reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA), and humeral hemiarthroplasty. These differ based on which part of the joint is replaced and the orientation of the implant. The indications for a shoulder replacement include osteoarthritis, cuff tear arthropathy, inflammatory arthropathy, acute trauma, and avascular necrosis.13 The rTSA has become the most common type of shoulder replacement and has been particularly effective in patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears and low function. However, there remains significant variability around the world in the preferred type of shoulder replacement.3,11,17

The increased shoulder function and decreased pain associated with shoulder replacements has led to a 103.7% increase in number of procedures per year.19 Furthermore, several estimates predict the number of shoulder replacements will increase to nearly 350,000 in 2025 — outpacing the growth rate of both total hip and total knee arthroplasties.19

This type of joint replacement has proven highly successful, and numerous recent studies have examined both intraoperative and post-operative complications. These complications can include fractures of the humerus, acromion, scapula, and glenoid, as well as infections, deltoid injuries, hematomas, and heterotopic ossification.2,4,10,15,16,22 Among these complications, post-operative glenoid fractures are by far the rarest. Shah et al reviewed the outcomes of 5,539 rTSA and reported 6 post-operative glenoid fractures (0.1%). The second most infrequent periprosthetic shoulder fracture is an intraoperative glenoid fracture, with a rate of 0.3%, further emphasizing the stability of the reconstructed glenoid in a rTSA.16 Due to their low prevalence, there is a lack of literature on whether post-operative glenoid fractures are typically nontraumatic (secondary to factors such as deteriorating bone quality and stress risers) or traumatic. Additionally, although there are reports on the management of periprosthetic shoulder and glenoid fractures, they are focused on intraoperative, rather than post-operative traumatic fractures.4,21 Therefore, there is currently no consensus on the appropriate management of post-operative traumatic glenoid fractures in patients who have undergone rTSA.

In this manuscript, we present the etiology, diagnosis, and operative course of an 82-year-old male who suffered a glenoid fracture after sustaining a fall from ground level 19 months after undergoing a rTSA. This case represents one of the first reported studies of this post-operative complication.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old male presented to the emergency department following a ground-level fall onto his right shoulder. On physical examination, he was alert and oriented. His right upper extremity showed a healed surgical incision over the anterior deltoid with no signs of drainage or dehiscence. There was also no swelling, ecchymosis, or erythema, but there was a slight fullness over the posterior aspect of the shoulder. Sensation to light touch was intact over the axillary, median, ulnar, and radial nerve distributions, and radial and ulnar pulses were palpable. His medical history is notable for a right rTSA performed 19 months prior. The procedure was successful, and he had no shoulder complications since that time. He was otherwise healthy with no significant medical comorbidities.

Radiographs obtained in the emergency department demonstrated an osseous fragment overlying the scapula that was not present on imaging prior to the fall (Fig. 1). Computed tomography scan was obtained for further evaluation, which revealed a fracture through the posterior aspect of the glenoid with associated displacement of the entire glenosphere, warranting surgical intervention for stabilization and restoration of shoulder function (Fig. 2).

The patient was subsequently taken to the operating room for open reduction and internal fixation of the right glenoid and scapula. He was positioned in a left lateral decubitus position on a radiolucent table to facilitate access to the scapular body via the posterior approach. A curvilinear incision along the right scapular spine and medial border of the scapula was performed. The fascial layer was incised, and the deltoid was identified and retracted superiorly. The interval between the infraspinatus and the teres minor was identified and developed. The infraspinatus was incised along the medial border of the scapula and retracted laterally toward its insertion on the humerus (Fig. 3A). The displaced glenoid fragment within the glenoid component of the rTSA was visualized and reduced under direct visualization using a ball-spike pusher to preliminarily align the articular surface, followed by two-pointed reduction forceps to maintain compression across the fracture site. Temporary fixation was obtained with Kirschner wires to maintain alignment while definitive fixation was performed (Fig. 3B). A Stryker Pangea 5-hole 3.5 mm locking compression plate (LCP) was applied along the lateral border of the scapula across the periprosthetic glenoid fracture. The plate was secured to the scapula with 4 cortical/locking screws. To augment the fixation and further buttress the periprosthetic glenoid fracture, a second Stryker Pangea 5-hole 3.5 mm LCP was applied perpendicularly and over the previous 3.5 mm plate along the inferior border of the scapular spine. The second 3.5 mm LCP was linked to the first plate using a cortical screw and secured to the scapula with 4 cortical/locking screws (Fig. 3C). Ultimately, the rTSA was reduced prior to definitive fixation to restore physiologic deltoid tension and assist in maintaining compression across the glenoid fragment during fixation. It did not require dislocation to obtain reduction.

Intraoperative images demonstrating the interval used to access the scapula (A), the use of Kirschner wires to maintain reduction of the fracture pattern (B), and the 2 lateral compression plates secured to the scapula using cortical screws (C).

Also, the baseplate did appear slightly more inferior on the post-operative radiographs compared with the preinjury images. This likely reflects minor changes in orientation related to the fracture pattern and subsequent reduction. However, the baseplate remained well fixed with stable peripheral screw fixation and maintained appropriate alignment without evidence of loosening or instability. Given the stable construct and satisfactory clinical progress, no additional intervention was required.

Final anteroposterior and scapular-Y radiographs of the right shoulder demonstrated acceptable length, alignment, and rotation of the periprosthetic glenoid fracture, with appropriate implant positioning (Fig. 4). The infraspinatus tendon was repaired prior to layered closure of the fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin. A sling was applied to the right upper extremity to immobilize the shoulder in adduction and internal rotation. The patient was instructed to remove the sling only for hygiene and to perform gentle hand, wrist, and elbow range of motion exercises to minimize stiffness and edema. He was maintained on non–weight-bearing precautions and scheduled for follow-up in three weeks. At the 3-week post-operative visit, two-view X-ray imaging demonstrated maintained periprosthetic glenoid fracture reduction with stable orthopedic implants (Fig. 5). The patient was neurovascularly intact and reported controlled pain with ice and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. There was no drainage or erythema over the surgical incision. Staples were removed and he was instructed to begin gentle shoulder range of motion exercises below 90°. The patient was instructed to follow-up again in eight weeks.

Intraoperative anterior-posterior (A and B) and axial (C) fluoroscopic imaging confirming correct placement of the 2 lateral compression plates.

Three-week post-operative anterior-posterior (A) and lateral (B) X-rays of the right shoulder demonstrating maintained periprosthetic glenoid fracture reduction and stable hardware.

At the 11-week post-operative visit, the patient remained neurovascularly intact, with pain adequately controlled using nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and acetaminophen as needed. He was minimally using ice and elevation of the right upper extremity for pain control and swelling reduction. The surgical incision was well healed without signs of infection. He demonstrated active range of motion to less than 90 degrees of elevation and was therefore encouraged to initiate formal physical therapy and resume activities of daily living/weight-bearing as tolerated of the right upper extremity.

Discussion

Approximately 350,000 shoulder arthroplasties are projected to be performed annually in the United States by 2025, with utilization increasing at a rate exceeding that of total hip and total knee arthroplasty.19 rTSA has emerged as the predominant surgical treatment for advanced glenohumeral pathology, surpassing anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty and hemiarthroplasty. This shift is largely attributable to the high prevalence of concomitant rotator cuff pathology in the shoulder arthroplasty population. The mean age of patients undergoing shoulder arthroplasty is 67 years, and rotator cuff tears are present in approximately 20% of individuals aged 60 to 69 years.18,20 Although utilization occurs in both sexes, a slight female predominance has been reported (0.294% vs. 0.221%).5 rTSA restores active shoulder elevation in the setting of cuff deficiency by medializing and inferiorly translating the center of rotation, thereby increasing the deltoid moment arm and improving deltoid efficiency while reducing shear forces across the glenoid.14

With the dramatic rise in shoulder arthroplasty utilization, increasing attention has been directed toward characterizing the mechanisms of implant failure. A meta-analysis reported that the most common complications following total shoulder arthroplasty were glenoid component loosening (5.3%) and humeral component loosening (1.1%) at a mean follow-up of 5.3 years. The next most common complications were glenohumeral instability (4.9%) and post-operative periprosthetic fracture (0.7%).1 These data suggest that traumatic periprosthetic fractures represent a relatively uncommon complication of shoulder arthroplasty. However, several studies have examined which components of shoulder arthroplasty constructs are most susceptible to failure in the setting of acute trauma.4,10,15,16,21,22

Shah et al performed a systematic review of periprosthetic fractures following rTSA and identified multiple components susceptible to failure, including the humeral stem, acromion, scapula, and glenoid. The reported incidence of acromial and scapular fractures was 2.6% at a mean follow-up of 4.3 years. These fractures were most frequently atraumatic and associated with severe inflammatory arthritis or massive rotator cuff deficiency.16 Consequently, most acromial and scapular periprosthetic fractures are initially managed nonoperatively with 4 to 6 weeks of immobilization.6

Post-operative periprosthetic shoulder fractures are reported to occur in approximately 1.3% of patients. Of these, 62.7% are attributed to traumatic events, with the remainder resulting from stress risers or compromised bone quality.16 Among traumatic post-operative periprosthetic fractures, 1.2% involve the humerus and only 0.1% involve the glenoid.16 After extensive review of the literature, we identified only 1 previously reported case of a traumatic post-operative periprosthetic glenoid fracture following rTSA. In that case, the patient had rheumatoid arthritis with suspected pre-existing glenohumeral erosive changes and sustained a fall in the bathtub. The patient underwent open reduction and internal fixation through a standard deltopectoral approach in the beach-chair position, which was subsequently followed by conversion to hemiarthroplasty. Recent literature suggests that structural bone grafting may occasionally be required in similar cases to achieve stable baseplate fixation in the setting of glenoid bone loss. In such cases, a humeral head autograft can be harvested via osteotomy, contoured to fill the glenoid defect, and secured prior to reaming. The glenoid baseplate is then implanted and fixed with 4 peripheral screws to achieve stable fixation.7,8

Traumatic periprosthetic glenoid fractures are thought to be rare following rTSA due to the altered biomechanics of the construct. As previously described, rTSA medializes and inferiorly translates the center of rotation, thereby reducing the magnitude of shear stress across the glenoid while increasing stress transmission to the scapula and acromion. This biomechanical shift allows the deltoid to function as the primary driver of shoulder elevation rather than the rotator cuff musculature.14 Consequently, redistribution of forces away from the glenoid and toward the scapular spine and acromion may explain the increased incidence of scapular spine and acromial fractures observed after rTSA.9,12

We suspect that the patient sustained a direct fall onto the lateral aspect of his right shoulder, generating a substantial axial load transmitted through the humeral component to the glenoid vault. In the setting of obesity (body mass index 32), a near–90-degree impact likely produced sufficient force concentration to overcome the structural integrity of the glenoid despite the absence of pre-existing bony insufficiency. This mechanism may explain the occurrence of this otherwise rare fracture pattern.

Nevertheless, there are several options for addressing periprosthetic glenoid fractures in patients with rTSA. The management strategy depends on fracture morphology, implant stability, and underlying bone quality. Nonoperative management with immobilization may be considered for minimally displaced fractures with stable implants. However, as in our case, patients who have displaced fractures or component instability usually require operative management. Surgical options include open reduction and internal fixation, revision arthroplasty with baseplate revision, and reconstruction with structural bone grafting in the setting of significant glenoid bone loss. Each strategy carries unique advantages and limitations: nonoperative management has the potential to manage the fracture nonoperatively. However, it carries the risk of nonunion, malunion, or prolonged immobilization and decreased functional recovery. Open reduction and internal fixation preserves the implant and restores native anatomy at the expense of risk of fixation failure. Revision arthroplasty restores the implant stability but is invasive and has a higher complication risk. Finally, bone graft reconstruction with revision baseplate restores bone stock but requires graft incorporation (Table I). Essentially, treatment decisions should be individualized based on fracture pattern, implant stability, and patient functional demands.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a traumatic periprosthetic glenoid fracture following rTSA in a patient without significant medical comorbidities or pre-existing glenoid pathology. We present detailed intraoperative imaging and technical documentation of successful open reduction and internal fixation of the glenoid and scapula. This case underscores both the rarity of this complication and the potential for favorable outcomes with timely recognition and appropriate surgical management.

Conclusion

This manuscript represents the first reported case detailing the etiology, diagnostic imaging findings, surgical management, and intraoperative documentation of a traumatic periprosthetic glenoid and scapular fracture following rTSA. We present a healthy 82-year-old male with no significant medical comorbidities or underlying bony abnormalities who sustained a traumatic fall onto his shoulder. He subsequently underwent successful open reduction and internal fixation of the fracture using a two-point reduction clamp and 2 LCPs. Post-operative imaging and outpatient follow-up demonstrated stable hardware and maintained alignment of the fracture lines. This case highlights key surgical principles for optimizing shoulder function and range of motion in patients with this rare fracture pattern.
