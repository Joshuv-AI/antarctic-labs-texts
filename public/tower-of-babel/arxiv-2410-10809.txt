Response theory for locally gapped systems
Joscha Henheik, Tom Wessel

Abstract
We introduce a notion of a local gap for interacting many-body quantum lattice systems and prove the validity of response theory and Kubo’s formula for localized perturbations in such settings. On a high level, our result shows that the usual spectral gap condition, concerning the system as a whole, is not a necessary condition for understanding local properties of the system. More precisely, we say that an equilibrium state ρ 0 \rho_{0} of a Hamiltonian H 0 H_{0} is locally gapped in Λ gap ⊂ Λ {\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}}\subset\varLambda , whenever the Liouvillian − i ⁡ [ H 0 , ⋅ ] -\mathrm{i}\,[H_{0},\,\cdot\,] is almost invertible on local observables supported in Λ gap {\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}} when tested in ρ 0 \rho_{0} . To put this into context, we provide other alternative notions of a local gap and discuss their relations. The validity of response theory is based on the construction of non-equilibrium almost stationary states (NEASSs). By controlling locality properties of the NEASS construction, we show that response theory holds to any order, whenever the perturbation ϵ ​ V \epsilon V acts in a region which is further than | log ⁡ ϵ | \lvert\log\epsilon\rvert away from the non-gapped region Λ ∖ Λ gap \varLambda\setminus{\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}} .

Introduction

Spectral gaps lie at the heart of many areas of physics and mathematics, and their existence has several profound consequences.
Classical mathematical examples include (i) Cheeger’s inequality
[
11
,
32
]

[

In physics, questions concerning the spectral gap lie at the center of several of the most challenging problems, e.g. the Berry-Tabor

The great interest in spectral gaps, far beyond these famous conjectures, is rooted in the fact that its existence has tremendous effects on fundamental properties of the system: It is textbook material in condensed matter theory, that the (non)-existence of a band gap determines the isolating (conducting) behavior of a material, and in superconductivity, the existence of a non-zero solution of the BCS gap equation
[
5
]
decides whether a system is superconducting.

Moreover, in quantum many-body physics, the existence of a spectral gap above the ground state eigenvalue has far-reaching consequences for entanglement properties
[

,
20
]
and ground state correlations
[
21
]
.
On the other hand, the

[

37

.
Finally, the assumption of a spectral gap played a crucial role in recent proofs of adiabatic theory and linear response for many-body systems (see Section

However, an overall drawback in all of the above examples is that the desired spectral property is of
global
nature, i.e. involving the studied system as a whole, and thus seldomly compatible with a notion of

in an underlying physical space.
Therefore, a natural question to ask is:

How can one express that a system is
locally
gapped, and which consequences that one has for globally gapped systems persist?

In this paper, we study this question in the setting of locally interacting many-body quantum spin lattice systems; see Section

More precisely, in the above spirit of our guiding question, this paper has two main goals:

(i)

We propose a notion of a
local gap
via an (equivalent) dynamical characterization and exemplary prove that local perturbations of Hamiltonians with a frustration free product ground state satisfy this condition.
Moreover, we study possible alternative notions of local gaps and their relations among each other (see Sections

(ii)

As an application to a physically relevant problem, we show that for Hamiltonians with a local gap, response theory approximately holds to any order and thus justify

There are only few works in the literature studying many-body quantum systems under a non-standard gap condition, i.e. one differing from the clean separation of eigenvalues: In

1.1
Response theory in many-body quantum systems

The purpose of response theory is to express how quantum expectation values change, after a small perturbation is slowly turned on.
More precisely, one considers an unperturbed Hamiltonian
H_{0}
with equilibrium state (usually a ground state)
\rho_{0}
and slowly turns on a small additive perturbation
\varepsilon V

\rho_{\varepsilon}
the state of the system after
\varepsilon V
has been turned on, one aims to understand, how the expectation value of an observable
B

\langle B\rangle_{\rho_{\varepsilon}}-\langle B\rangle_{\rho_{0}}=\varepsilon\,\sigma_{B}+o(\varepsilon)

(1)

at least to leading order in the strength
\varepsilon

A central piece of response theory is

[
28

in (
1

Despite the simplicity and empirical success of Kubo’s formula, the problem of justifying it in a very general framework has so far escaped rigorous treatment.
The fundamental difficulty lies in the fact that, in general, the state
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is no longer an equilibrium state, and therefore determining it is outside the powerful realm of equilibrium statistical mechanics.
This problem has prominently been pointed out by Simon
[
40
]
in 1984 in his “Fifteen problems in mathematical physics”, containing a rigorous justification of Kubo’s formula from first principles as problem (4B).

However, in the particular setting of many-body lattice systems with a spectral gap at zero temperature, it has recently been possible
[

,

25

to actually prove Kubo’s formula and justify the applicability of linear response theory to compute the change in expectation values (

The more general underlying results establish

2.1

We consider a quantum spin system on a finite graph

To each vertex

, which we assume to be of finite dimension,
\sup_{x\in\varLambda}\dim\mathcal{H}_{x}<\infty

Moreover, for each

be the many-particle Hilbert space on

and denote the associated

-algebra of observables by

.
Whenever

, then

is naturally embedded as a subalgebra of

Since a very similar construction is common for fermionic lattice systems (see, e.g.,
[

,

]

2.2
Interactions and SLT operators

An

is a map

\varPhi\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{\ordinarycolon}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\{\nonscript\,X\subset\varLambda\nonscript\,\}\to\mathcal{A}^{N}\,,\ X\mapsto\varPhi(X)=\varPhi(X)^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{X}.

(18)

With any interaction, one associates a

(19)

Note that, while every interaction defines a unique operator, there are multiple interactions realizing the same operator, i.e. the assignment

is not invertible.
Note that all interactions and SLT operators are by definition always self-adjoint.

For any

and

, we consider the stretched exponential function

\chi_{b,p}:[0,\infty)\to(0,1]\,,\ x\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{-bx^{p}}

and define the associated

\lVert\varPhi\rVert_{b,p}:=\sup_{z\in\varLambda}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\varLambda:\\
z\in Z\end{subarray}}\frac{\lVert\varPhi(Z)\rVert}{\chi_{b,p}\l\lparen\diam\lparen Z\rparen\bigr\rparen}

(21)

on interactions.
The quality of the localization for an interaction will be expressed by the finiteness of a norm

,
independent
of the (size of the) graph
\varLambda
.
Operators with this property will often be referred to as
(b,p)
-localized SLT operators
.

Moreover, in order to further quantify, how well an interaction is localized in a region

, we introduce the
localized SLT interaction norm

z\in Z\end{subarray}}\frac{\lVert\varPhi(Z)\rVert}{\chi_{b,p}\l\lparen\diam\lparen Z\rparen\bigr\rparen\,\chi_{b,p}\bigl\lparen d\lparen z,\varOmega\rparen\bigr\rparen}

(22)

and refer to operators with the property that

-localized SLT operators
.
Whenever it is clear from the context, or irrelevant for the discussion, we will often also omit the arguments
(b,p)
, and simply refer to
\varOmega
-localized SLT operators
.
Finally, observe that
\lVert\varPhi\rVert_{b,p;\varLambda}=\lVert\varPhi\rVert_{b,p}
.

The following simple lemma, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.4
, relates conceptually easier notions of locality of SLT operators to boundedness of the norm (

We shall first list all the different variants and afterwards compare their respective features in view of the above listed three requirements and explain their relations in Section
4.3

Finally, in Section

(name=(LDG

Throughout the entire section (except differently stated explicitly), we will use

In this section, we describe several ways of

4.2
Intrinsic local gap conditions

In this section, we describe two ways of
intrinsically
expressing that an equilibrium state
\rho
of a Hamiltonian
H
is locally gapped.
In contrast to the previous extrinsic formulation, they are called
intrinsic

Local spectral gap conditions
only
for ground states

In case of
\rho
being the (unique)
ground state
, a very natural way to connect the notion of locality with spectral analysis is to require that a variational condition characterizing the spectral gap is tested only locally.
Generally speaking, assume that
\psi_{0}
is the unique ground state with eigenvalue
E_{0}
of a local Hamiltonian
H
in some underlying physical space
\varLambda
.
Then, a spectral gap above
E_{0}
of size (at least)
g>0
is characterized by

\inf_{\psi\perp\psi_{0}}\frac{\langle\psi,(H-E_{0})\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}\geq g.

(39)

If the minimization in (
39
) is restricted a smaller set of
\psi
’s, i.e. those which are localized to a region, say,
{\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}}\subset\varLambda
in some appropriate sense, one could say that
H
is locally gapped in
{\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}}
.
Alternatively, for every fixed
\psi
, the gap size
g
could be assumed to be non-constant but dependent on the distance of the support of
\psi
to the region
{\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}}
.

For quantum spin systems on the graph
\varLambda
, it can easily be checked that the analog of (
39
) for an SLT Hamiltonian
H
with unique ground state
\rho
is

\mathrm{i}\,\langle A^{*}\mathcal{L}\llbracket A\rrbracket\rangle_{\rho}\geq g\,\Big\lparen\langle A^{*}A\rangle_{\rho}-\big\lvert\langle A\rangle_{\rho}\big\rvert^{2}\Big\rparen,

(40)

for all observables
A\in\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{L}\llbracket\,\cdot\,\rrbracket:=-\mathrm{i}\,[H,\,\cdot\,]
denotes the Liouvillian.
We now give two options to turn (
40
) into a local gap condition.

As a first option, one could require that the
gap size
g
decays
as the support
X
of the observable

approaches the complement of
{\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}}

\mathrm{i}\,\langle A^{*}\mathcal{L}\llbracket A\rrbracket\rangle_{\rho}\geq g\,\left\lparen 1-C\diam(X)^{\ell}\,\chi_{b,p}\l\lparen d\lparen X,\varLambda\setminus{\varLambda^{\mathrm{gap}}}\rparen\bigr\rparen\right\rparen\,\Big\lparen\langle A^{*}A\rangle_{\rho}-\big\lvert\langle A\rangle_{\rho}\big\rvert^{2}\Big\rparen.

(41)

In case that
\rho=\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert
with
\psi
being a
product state
, variants of Proposition 14 and Lemma 15 in
[
44

can be used to show the following: The gap decay condition (
41

We further remark that, if one is interested in

4.3
Summary and comparison

In the previous two Sections
4.1
–
4.2
we described several different ways of expressing that a Hamiltonian is locally gapped, distinguishing between
extrinsic
(Section
4.1
) and
intrinsic
(Section
4.2
) formulations.

While the extrinsic conditions are easy to formulate, they rely – by definition – on a reference (parent) Hamiltonian with a global gap satisfying some form of LPPL principle (cf. (
35
)–(
38
)).
Since for a system of interest, it is not guaranteed to have such a well-understood parent Hamiltonian available, it is conceptually more desirable to formulate a local gap condition in an intrinsic way.
Or, in other words, saying that a system is (or behaves as if it were)
locally
gapped should not only make sense relative to another
globally
gapped system.

In the intrinsic category, we formulated two local
spectral
gap conditions (
41
)–(
42
), which, however, are (i) only meaningful for (non-degenerate) ground states and (ii) although mathematically clean, hardly applicable in physical problems in a direct way (apart from the connection in Proposition
4.1
(viii)).
These two issues are then resolved by our local dynamical gap condition (LDG) in (
43
) and its weakened version in (
45
).
