Cell sheet-based approach to study the diabetic corneal stroma
Authors: Shrestha Priyadarsini, Tina B McKay, Paulina Escandon, Sarah E Nicholas, Jian-Xing Ma, Dimitrios Karamichos
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractProlonged hyperglycemia during diabetes mellitus (DM) is associated with severe complications that may affect both the anterior and posterior ocular segments, leading to impaired vision or blindness. The cornea is a vital part of the eye that has a dual role as a protective transparent barrier and as a major refractive structure and is likewise negatively affected by hyperglycemia in DM. Understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the phenotypic changes associated with DM is critical to developing targeted therapies to promote tissue integrity. In this proof-of-concept study, we applied a cell sheet-based approach to generate stacked constructs of physiological corneal thickness using primary human corneal fibroblasts isolated from cadaveric control (healthy), Type 1 DM and Type 2 DM corneal tissues. Self-assembled corneal stromal sheets were generated after 2 weeks in culture, isolated, and subsequently assembled to create stacked constructs, which were evaluated using transmission electron microscopy. Analysis of gene expression patterns revealed significant downregulation of fibrotic markers, α-smooth muscle actin, and collagen type 3, with stacking in Type 2 DM constructs when compared to controls. IGF1 expression was significantly upregulated in Type 2 DM constructs compared to controls with a significant reduction induced by stacking. This study describes the development of a thicker, self-assembled corneal stromal construct as a platform to evaluate phenotypic differences associated with DM-derived corneal fibroblasts and enable the development of targeted therapeutics to promote corneal integrity.Keywords: Cornea, Tissue engineering, Stacking, In vitro models, Stroma

A large population of the world suffers from vision loss caused by systemic conditions, such as diabetes mellitus (DM) (Leasher et al., 2016). While diabetic retinopathy is a common complication of uncontrolled DM, permanent damage to the innervating nerves and other corneal cell populations may lead to recurrent epithelial erosions, scarring, and inept wound healing responses in the cornea (Yu et al., 2022). In general, the wound healing process is a complex mechanism that normally involves cell signaling between the corneal epithelium, stromal keratocytes, and innervating sensory neurons derived from the trigeminal ganglion that leads to tissue closure and matrix deposition (Kamil and Mohan, 2021; Zieske, 2001). During this process, stromal keratocytes may either promote scarless tissue regeneration or develop into persistent myofibroblasts causing irreparable tissue opacity (Kamil and Mohan, 2021). During DM, the hyperglycemic environment may lead to structural and functional changes in the cornea that contribute to decreased wound healing capacity, corneal edema, and altered epithelial basement membrane integrity (Ljubimov, 2017).

To study corneal wound healing, a number of in vitro models have been proposed and investigated to-date (McKay et al., 2020; Shiju et al., 2020). Our lab has previously developed a 3D self-assembled model that utilizes primary human corneal fibroblasts derived from the corneal stroma cultured in the presence of a stable Vitamin C derivative to generate a construct sustainable to long-term culture (up to 8 weeks) (Karamichos et al., 2010). This model has been applied to study the effects of riboflavin-mediated collagen crosslinking (Sharif et al., 2018), viral and fungal infections (Brown et al., 2021; Drevets et al., 2015), adipose tissue fibrosis (Iwayama et al., 2015), hypoxia (Lee et al., 2018), and keratoconus (Karamichos et al., 2012), among other processes. We have also previously utilized this model to study corneal fibroblasts derived from Type 1 DM (T1DM) and Type 2 DM (T2DM) donors compared to normal controls and identified an increase in pro-fibrotic markers and mitochondrial defects in T1DM and T2DM constructs (Priyadarsini et al., 2016) with moderate effects also seen when cultured with differentiated neurons derived from a neuroblastoma cell line (Priyadarsini et al., 2017).

The goal of the current study was to develop a physiological 3D corneal stromal construct consistent with the average thickness of the adult human cornea using a cell sheet-layered approach to study DM-associated differences. We evaluated the thickness of stacked constructs generated from control, T1DM, and T2DM-derived human corneal fibroblasts cultured for 4 weeks in vitro and investigated whether stacking promoted differential changes in basal gene expression. This short communication describes the development of a stacked construct to study DM-associated differences in a self-assembled model sustainable for long-term culture that may be potentially applicable to study other processes and diseases.

We applied a tissue engineering approach to develop stacked, layered constructs using self-assembled 3D stromal matrices containing stromal cells from control and diabetic (T1DMs/T2DMs) donors. These stacked constructs contained 8 layers of 2-week old stromal constructs, which were then cultured for a total of 4 weeks to allow tissue adherence and maturation (Fig. 1). We evaluated the thickness of stacked constructs using toluidine blue staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis (Fig. 2A). Gross morphological analysis showed overall assimilation of the 8 stacked layers with thicker constructs formed in the stacked control compared to T1DM and T2DM constructs. The average thickness measured by TEM were 322.8 μm (±16.6 μm), 67.1 μm (±6.1 μm), and 92.9 μm (±2.1 μm) for control, T1DM, and T2DM stacked constructs, respectively (Fig. 2B).

To determine if stacking influenced gene expression patterns in healthy- and DM-derived constructs, we utilized qRT-PCR to analyze the relative mRNA levels of ACTA2, COL1A, COL3A, and COL5A (Fig. 2C). DM-derived corneal fibroblasts appeared to show the most substantial response to stacking with a significant reduction in ACTA2 (fold-change (± standard deviation) = 0.18-fold (±0.05), p = 0.3 and 0.09-fold (±0.02), p = 0.04 in T1DM and T2DM stacked constructs, respectively). In general, stacking promoted reduced collagen gene expression in control and T1DM and T2DM stacked constructs compared to single-layer constructs. Significant downregulation of COL1A (0.03-fold (±0.01), p = 0.0008 and 0.02-fold (±0.009), p < 0.0001) and COL5A (0.06-fold (±0.02), p = 0.001 and 0.08-fold (±0.04), p < 0.0001) gene expression patterns were identified in HCF stacked and T2DM stacked constructs, respectively. While COL1A, COL3A, and COL5A appeared to be downregulated in all stacked constructs compared to their unstacked controls, no significant differences were detected between healthy (control) and DM (T1DM or T2DM) stacked constructs. Consistent with our previous findings showing upregulation in collagen type 3 expression by DM corneal fibroblasts (2), COL3A was upregulated in T2DM controls (8.2-fold (±3.4)) compared to healthy controls with stacking promoting a significant decrease in COL3A (0.01-fold (±0.003), p = 0.0002) in parallel to the decreases in COL1A or COL5A.

Since DM is a metabolic disease, we sought to determine if stacking influenced the expression of genes related to glucose uptake, such as IGF1 and IGF1-R (Fig. 2C). We found that stacking decreased IGF1 levels by 18-fold (p = 0.007) in T2DM constructs with little change in its receptor, IGF1-R. Both control and T1DM stacked constructs showed trends of lower IGF1 (22-fold, p = 0.214 and 2-fold, p = 0.684, respectively) with no significant reduction in IGF1-R compared to their single-layer control constructs.

We know that DM is associated with significant ocular complications, including corneal epithelial erosions, corneal scarring, and damage to corneal nerves. In this proof-of concept study, we attempted to closely mimic corneal in vivo thickness to study the cellular differences associated with DM. A cell sheet-based approach was used to stack multiple 3D constructs with self-assembled ECM containing either human corneal fibroblasts derived from normal or DM donors to determine if significant thickening of the ECM influenced gene expression. The stacked constructs were composed of 8 layers and were allowed to adhere and maturate in cell culture conditions for 4 weeks. Stacking led to a significant increase in total construct thickness with a more pronounced effect observed in controls compared to T1DM or T2DM constructs suggesting that constitutive differences present in DM-derived cells may persist in stacked constructs thus limiting ECM deposition.

Our previous studies using self-assembled stromal constructs generated from primary corneal fibroblast derived from DM donors revealed an increase in the fibrotic ECM component, collagen type 3, in T2DM constructs compared to their healthy counterparts (Priyadarsini et al., 2016, 2017). The inclusion of neuron-like cells (SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell line) seemed to inhibit this upregulation in co-cultures with T2DM-derived stromal corneal fibroblasts (Priyadarsini et al., 2017) providing evidence that the fibrotic phenotype observed in vitro can be modulated by the culture microenvironment. Mitochondrial and metabolic changes have also been associated with T1DM and T2DM-derived corneal fibroblasts (Priyadarsini et al., 2016; Whelchel et al., 2019), as well as corneal endothelial cells (Aldrich et al., 2017) and lens capsule epithelial cells (Kubota et al., 2016).

In summary, this study utilized a cell sheet-stacking approach to generate thicker stromal constructs within a 4-week period using control, T1DM, and T2DM-derived corneal fibroblasts. Differential gene expression patterns were observed with stacking and between control and DM-derived constructs. Further development and characterization of this model are needed to establish a defined medium in the absence of serum in order to maintain the keratocyte phenotype. Isolation of a sufficient number of stromal cells from human corneal tissue is heavily limited in the absence of serum, and as of yet, we have not developed a defined medium to permit adequate cell migration and proliferation without activation of keratocytes to fibroblasts. Studies using human corneal stromal stem cells isolated in a defined medium followed by differentiation to keratocytes have shown the most promise for deriving sufficient cell numbers (Kureshi et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2014), but generally require limbal tissue (Basu et al., 2014). Future studies are expected to shed light on the constitutive factors of DM that are retained in isolated corneal fibroblasts and the role of the ECM in regulating wound healing and downstream cell signaling.
