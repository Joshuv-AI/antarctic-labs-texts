Analgesic Efficacy of Different Doses of Esketamine as an Adjuvant to Ropivacaine for Thoracoscopy-Guided Thoracic Paravertebral Block After Thoracoscopic Radical Resection for Lung Cancer: A Prospective Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Lu-Yao Wei, Ming-Can Luo, Jin-Xian He, Xia Xu, Li-Hong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThoracoscopy-guided thoracic paravertebral block (TG-TPVB) is a new analgesia technique; however, the duration of a single TG-TPVB is short. Esketamine combined with local anesthetics can prolong the duration of nerve block. This study compared the analgesic efficacy of three doses of esketamine combined with ropivacaine for TG-TPVB in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical resection for lung cancer.Methods160 patients were randomly allocated into the control, S1, S2, and S3 groups. All patients received TG-TPVB with 20 mL of study solution. The control group received 0.375% ropivacaine alone, whereas the S1, S2, and S3 groups received 0.375% ropivacaine combined with esketamine at doses of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg/kg, respectively. The time to first patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) demand, postoperative numerical rating scale (NRS) pain scores, sufentanil consumption, effective PCIA demands, recovery outcomes, and adverse events were compared between the four groups.ResultsThe time to first PCIA demand in the three esketamine groups were longer than in the control group. However, S2 and S3 groups were longer than S1 group. NRS pain scores in the three esketamine groups were lower than in the control group at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively. Postoperative sufentanil consumption within 24 h, the number of effective PCIA demands and rescue analgesia administrations within 48 h were lower in the three esketamine groups than in the control group. Wake-up time was longer in the S3 group than in the control, S1, and S2 groups. The times to first ambulation and chest tube removal were shorter in the esketamine groups than in the control group (All P<0.001).ConclusionEsketamine combined with ropivacaine for TG-TPVB prolonged postoperative analgesia, reduce pain scores, reduced analgesic consumption, and facilitated recovery. Esketamine at 0.2 mg/kg seems to show a good balance effect in many aspects.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most prevalent malignant tumor worldwide and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality.1 Minimally invasive thoracoscopic surgery has become the mainstay of modern thoracic surgical practice.2 Nevertheless, postoperative pain and perioperative stress responses following minimally invasive thoracoscopic surgery remain relatively significant and may delay patients’ functional recovery process. Optimized perioperative analgesic strategies can mitigate postoperative pain and surgical stress, facilitate effective coughing and deep breathing, promote early ambulation, and improve postoperative recovery.3

Ultrasound-guided thoracic paravertebral block (UG-TPVB) is a percutaneous paravertebral block (TPVB) technique. UG-TPVB can alleviate postoperative pain and is widely used in multimodal analgesia regimens for thoracic surgical procedures.4 However, due to limitations such as the deep location of the block site, a larger volume and higher concentration of local anesthetic must be used during UG-TPVB procedure.5 Thoracoscopy-guided thoracic paravertebral block (TG-TPVB) involves precisely administering local anesthetics into the paravertebral space via a thoracoscopic approach. Previous studies by our group have demonstrated that TG-TPVB is feasible, safe, and efficient.6,7 Comparative evidence further suggests that TG-TPVB may be simpler to perform than UG-TPVB, with a higher technical success rate and a broader sensory block range.8 TG-TPVB holds significant potential for application in perioperative analgesia during thoracic surgery.

Postoperative pain after thoracic surgery is typically most intense during the first 48 h after surgery. Ropivacaine is commonly used for TPVB in current clinical practice; however, its block duration generally lasts only 6–12 h, which may not provide sufficient analgesic coverage during the early postoperative period. Evidence suggests that glucocorticoids, and ketamine, when used as adjuvants to local anesthetics such as ropivacaine, may extend the duration of paravertebral block and improve postoperative analgesia.9–12 Previous studies by our group confirmed that the addition of dexamethasone to ropivacaine can prolong the action time of TG-TPVB and improve the analgesic effect.13 However, glucocorticoids may affect patients’ perioperative blood glucose levels; and ketamine is associated with neurological and psychiatric adverse reactions.

Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, is a novel pharmaceutical agent with sedative, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory effects. Compared with ketamine, esketamine has been reported to have a higher affinity for the NMDA receptor, greater analgesic potency, and milder neurological and psychiatric symptoms, enabling clinically effective analgesia at a lower dose and with a potentially more favorable safety profile.14 In regional anesthesia, esketamine has attracted increasing interest as a local anesthetic adjuvant. Previous studies have suggested that esketamine (0.1 or 0.5 mg/kg) combined with ropivacaine in UG-TPVB can prolong analgesia, improve postoperative pain control, and facilitate recovery in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical surgery for lung cancer.15,16 Since TG-TPVB and UG-TPVB operate on the same mechanism of action, we hypothesize that the use of esketamine as an adjunctive therapy in TG-TPVB may also yield similar clinical benefits. Compared to UG-TPVB, TG-TPVB ensures complete and effective diffusion of the drug solution within the paravertebral space. However, whether combining this approach with esketamine can achieve an optimal blockade effect remains unclear. Determining the efficacy and appropriate dosage of esketamine in this clinical scenario may help optimize postoperative recovery following thoracic surgery. Therefore, the present study was designed to compare the postoperative analgesic effects of three different low-dose esketamine regimens as adjuvants to ropivacaine for TG-TPVB in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical resection of lung cancer, with specific focus on the duration of analgesia, pain scores, adverse events, and postoperative recovery. The aim was to confirm the clinical efficacy of esketamine combined with ropivacaine in TG-TPVB and to determine the most appropriate dosage for routine clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This single-center, randomized, double-blind, controlled trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital, Ningbo, China (Approval No. KY2023PJ206). The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Approval No. ChiCTR2300075510, First registration at 7st Sep 2023, https://www.chictr.org.cn/). The study was designed and is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Between September 7, 2023, and September 6, 2024, a total of 160 patients scheduled for elective thoracoscopic lung cancer resection at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Ningbo Medical Center Lihuili Hospital, were enrolled. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 20–75 years; (2) scheduled to undergo elective thoracoscopic radical resection for lung cancer; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II; and (4) provision of written informed consent to participate in the study. Patients were excluded if they had a known allergy to ropivacaine or esketamine, abnormal coagulation function, a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, a history of spinal deformity, fracture, trauma, or previous spinal surgery, chronic pain or recent use of analgesic medications, or a history of thoracic aortic aneurysm. Patients were further excluded after enrollment if intraoperative pathology revealed a benign pulmonary tumor or carcinoma in situ, if conversion to open thoracotomy was required, if severe pleural adhesions were identified intraoperatively, or if they were unable to comply with the study procedures or requested withdrawal.

Randomization and Blinding

This study employed a simple randomization method to randomly assign patients in a 1:1:1:1 ratio to the control, S1, S2, or S3 group (40 patients per group). First, the independent investigator used the random number generator in SPSS v24.0 (I(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to generate 160 random number sequences; next, these random number sequences were converted into a ranked sequence ranging from 1 to 160 based on their numerical values; finally, the “Visual Binning” function was employed to divide this ranked sequence into four groups, and each ranked sequence should be placed in an envelope numbered 1–160. After the patient randomly selects an envelope, the grouping results were concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. An independent investigator prepared the TG-TPVB study solutions in accordance with the group assignments. All solutions were dispensed in identical syringes, were visually indistinguishable, and had a total volume of 20 mL. Patients, attending anesthesiologists, surgeons, postoperative outcome assessors, and statisticians remained blinded to group allocation throughout the trial.

Anesthesia Procedure

All patients followed a standardized preoperative fasting protocol, with fasting from solid food for at least 8 h and from clear fluids for at least 2 h before surgery. After arrival in the operating room, peripheral intravenous access was established. Standard monitoring was applied, including electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry (SpO2), end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2), and bispectral index (BIS). Under local anesthesia, radial artery catheterization and internal jugular venous cannulation were performed for invasive arterial pressure monitoring and central venous access. Anesthesia was induced using a standardized rapid intravenous induction protocol consisting of midazolam 0.05 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.3 μg/kg, propofol 2 mg/kg, and rocuronium bromide 0.6 mg/kg. After induction, orotracheal intubation was performed using either a double-lumen endotracheal tube or a single-lumen endotracheal tube with a bronchial blocker. Correct positioning was confirmed by fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Mechanical ventilation was delivered in intermittent positive-pressure ventilation mode, with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg and an initial respiratory rate of 12 breaths/min. Ventilatory parameters were adjusted intraoperatively to maintain PETCO2 at 35–40 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with total intravenous anesthesia using continuous propofol infusion at 6–8 mg/(kg h) and remifentanil infusion at 0.1–0.3 μg/(kg min). Rocuronium bromide 0.2 mg/kg was administered intermittently every 30 min or as clinically required to maintain adequate neuromuscular blockade. The depth of anesthesia was continuously monitored and titrated to maintain the BIS value between 40 and 60 throughout the procedure. The same anesthesia team was responsible for administering anesthesia.

Surgical Procedure

All procedures were performed using a video-assisted thoracoscopic approach. According to tumor location and surgical indication, patients underwent radical pulmonary resection, including lobectomy or segmentectomy, via either a uniportal or three-port approach. After intraoperative pathological confirmation of malignancy, hilar and/or mediastinal lymph node dissection or sampling was performed according to the surgical indication. A 28-Fr chest tube was routinely placed at the end of surgery. The same surgical team was responsible for performing both the surgical procedure and the TG-TPVB operation.

Intervention Protocol and Analgesia Methods

Prior to chest closure, TG-TPVB was performed under thoracoscopic visualization. A No. 5 scalp needle connected to an extension tube was inserted perpendicularly to a depth of approximately 0.5 cm beneath the parietal pleura at the T5–T6 intervertebral level, 1 cm lateral to the vertebral body. After negative aspiration for blood and cerebrospinal fluid, patients in the control group received 20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine. Patients in the S1, S2, and S3 groups received 0.375% ropivacaine combined with esketamine at doses of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg/kg, respectively, with the total injectate volume adjusted to 20 mL in each group. The injection site was observed for 5 min to assess bleeding or hematoma formation (Figure 1).Figure 1TG-TPVB procedure.Intraoperative image showing the TG-TPVB procedure with a needle and ultrasound probe near the thoracic paravertebral space.

After completion of the surgical procedure, all patients were connected to a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pump. The analgesic solution consisted of sufentanil 1.5 μg/kg and tropisetron 5 mg, diluted with 0.9% sodium chloride injection to a total volume of 100 mL. The PCIA pump was programmed with a loading dose of 2 mL, a background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, a bolus dose of 0.5 mL, and a lockout interval of 15 min. Postoperatively, if the numerical rating scale (NRS) score was ≥4 either at rest or during movement, or if the patient requested additional analgesia, sufentanil 0.05 μg/kg was administered as rescue analgesia.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the time to the first PCIA demand, defined as the interval from initiation of PCIA to the first patient-initiated analgesic demand recorded by the pump. This variable served as an objective measure of the duration of effective postoperative analgesia.

Secondary outcomes included numerical rating scale (NRS) pain scores at rest and during evoked pain, defined as pain elicited by deep breathing or coughing, at 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively. Postoperative sufentanil consumption within 24 h, and the number of effective PCIA demands and rescue analgesia within 48 h after surgery were also recorded. Hemodynamic variables, including mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR), were assessed at four predefined time points: before block placement, at the end of surgery, and at 1 and 6 h postoperatively. Postoperative recovery outcomes included wake-up time, time to first ambulation, and time to chest tube removal. Wake-up time was defined as the interval from discontinuation of anesthetic agents to extubation after recovery of consciousness. The incidence of postoperative adverse events, including nausea, vomiting, dizziness, somnolence, diplopia, and atelectasis, was also recorded.

Sample Size

A pilot study was conducted with 10 patients in each group to estimate the minimum required sample size. In the pilot cohort, the time to the first PCIA demand was 14.12 ± 1.35 h in the control group, 18.15 ± 1.85 h in the S1 group, 22.52 ± 3.05 h in the S2 group, and 22.75 ± 3.08 h in the S3 group. On the basis of these preliminary data, the sample size was calculated using PASS software (Version 15.0, NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA). Assuming a statistical power of 80% and a two-sided α level of 0.05, at least 31 patients were required in each group. Considering a potential 20% rate of missing data or dropout and to ensure adequate statistical power, 40 patients were ultimately enrolled in each group.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0, IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA). Figure generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (version 8.0.2, GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Mixed-effects models were used to compare overall differences. If a statistically significant overall difference was found, pairwise comparisons were then conducted. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]); comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H-test. For repeated-measurement data (eg, NRS, MAP, and HR), mixed-effects models were employed for comparison, with the Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons; a two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage; comparisons were conducted using the χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test. For overall comparisons, a two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant; for pairwise comparisons among the four groups, the Bonferroni correction was applied, yielding a corrected significance threshold of 0.0083 (0.05/6); thus, a P-value <0.0083 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present randomized dose-comparison trial demonstrated that esketamine, when used as an adjuvant to ropivacaine for thoracoscopy-guided thoracic paravertebral block (TG-TPVB), prolonged the duration of postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical resection for lung cancer. Across the dose range tested, esketamine was associated with prolonged analgesic duration, reduced opioid requirements, and improved early postoperative recovery. Considering analgesic efficacy, emergence characteristics, and safety, 0.2 mg/kg esketamine appeared to provide the most favorable overall balance in this study.

For patients with early- and intermediate-stage lung cancer, surgical resection remains the mainstay of curative treatment.1,17 Although thoracoscopic minimally invasive surgery involves less tissue trauma, the perioperative pain and surgical stress remain significant. Inadequate postoperative analgesia after thoracic surgery may limit effective coughing, deep breathing, and early ambulation, thereby impairing postoperative recovery.3

Over the past two decades, UG-TPVB has been widely used for analgesic management in thoracic surgical procedures due to its ability to alleviate perioperative pain and stress responses, as well as to facilitate postoperative recovery.18 However, the analgesic effect of single-injection TPVB usually lasts only approximately 6–12 hours, which is often insufficient to fully cover the early postoperative pain period. Previous studies have shown that adding adjuvants such as dexamethasone or ketamine to local anesthetics for peripheral nerve blocks can prolong block duration and enhance analgesic efficacy.9–12,19 Previous findings from our research group have shown that, when compared with intravenous dexamethasone, adding dexamethasone as an adjuvant to ropivacaine in the TG-TPVB regimen extended the duration of analgesia and improved analgesic efficacy.13 However, because dexamethasone may affect patients’ blood glucose levels, its perioperative use, especially in diabetic patients, may have certain limitations. Ketamine is also associated with adverse effects, including neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, has gained increasing attention in perioperative analgesia in recent years. Compared with ketamine, esketamine has a 3 ~ 4 -fold higher affinity for NMDA receptors and a 2 ~ 2.5-fold greater analgesic potency, while being associated with a lower incidence of adverse effects.14 Esketamine does not increase blood glucose levels in patients.20 Yang et al and Wen et al reported that 0.1 mg/kg or 0.5 mg/kg esketamine combined with ropivacaine for UG-TPVB prolonged the time to first PCIA demand, reduced overall PCIA use, and promoted enhanced recovery in patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical lung cancer surgery.15,16

Considering the benefits of TG-TPVB, namely its simpler procedure, higher success rate, and broader sensory block, exploring strategies to prolong its analgesic duration and potentiate its analgesic effect has significant clinical relevance.6–8 Given the pharmacological advantages of esketamine and its reported efficacy as a local anesthetic adjuvant in UG-TPVB, we hypothesized that esketamine may also prolong block duration and enhance analgesic efficacy when used as an adjuvant for TG-TPVB. To date, no study has reported the use of esketamine as an adjuvant to ropivacaine for TG-TPVB. Accordingly, the present study was designed to evaluate the effects of esketamine as an adjuvant to ropivacaine for TG-TPVB and to identify the clinically optimal dosage, so as to enhance analgesic efficacy, relieve postoperative pain, and facilitate postoperative recovery.

Current studies using esketamine as a local anesthetic adjuvant for nerve blocks have generally adopted doses ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mg/kg.15,16,20 However, esketamine-related adverse effects may become more apparent with increasing dose. Given that TG-TPVB enables accurate delivery of local anesthetics into the thoracic paravertebral space, satisfactory blockade may potentially be achieved with a relatively small volume or low concentration of local anesthetics. To avoid or minimize these potential adverse effects, the present study selected three doses of esketamine: 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg/kg. In this study, the time to first PCIA demand was significantly longer in the S1, S2, and S3 groups than in the control group. NRS pain scores began to increase from 6 hours postoperatively in the control group, whereas a significant increase was not observed until 24 hours postoperatively in the three esketamine groups. In addition, 24-hour postoperative sufentanil consumption was significantly lower in the esketamine groups than in the control group. These findings suggest that esketamine, when used as an adjuvant to ropivacaine, may prolong the effective analgesic duration of TG-TPVB. This result is consistent with the findings reported by Yang et al and Wen et al.15,16 Several mechanisms may explain the prolongation of analgesic duration by esketamine. First, esketamine acts as a noncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist, thereby inhibiting glutamate-mediated receptor activation, reducing calcium influx, and decreasing neuronal excitability.21,22 Second, esketamine may exert sympathomimetic and anti-inflammatory effects, potentially slowing the systemic absorption of local anesthetics and reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to prolonged analgesic duration.15,23 Third, esketamine may modulate the inactivated state of voltage-gated sodium channels and prolong the inhibitory effect of local anesthetics on sodium influx, thereby extending analgesic duration.24 Finally, esketamine dissociates slowly from NMDA receptors and may maintain receptor binding even after glutamate dissociation, resulting in sustained channel blockade.25 In the present study, the time to first PCIA demand was significantly longer in the S2 and S3 groups than in the S1 group, suggesting that the prolongation of analgesic duration may be partly dose-dependent. However, no significant difference was observed between the S2 and S3 groups, indicating that beyond a certain dose, further increases in esketamine may confer limited additional benefit in extending the duration of analgesia. Compared with previous studies that used 0.5 mg/kg of esketamine in UG-TPVB, the present study found that a lower dose of esketamine was sufficient to effectively prolong the duration of blockade. This may be attributed to the fact that the TG‑TPVB procedure is simple and convenient, which ensures precise injection of the local anesthetic into the thoracic paravertebral space and effective infiltration of the nerve branches within that space.

In this study, NRS pain scores at 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours postoperatively, 24-hour sufentanil consumption, the number of effective PCIA demands and rescue analgesia within 48 hours were significantly lower in the S1, S2, and S3 groups than in the control group. These findings suggest that when three different doses of esketamine are used as an adjuvant to ropivacaine, they may enhance the analgesic efficacy of TG-TPVB to varying degrees. These findings are consistent with those reported by Yang et al and Wen et al.15,16 Several mechanisms may contribute to this effect. NMDA receptors are expressed in both the spinal cord and cerebral cortex, and esketamine, as a noncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist, may exert analgesic effects at central, spinal, and peripheral sites.26 Esketamine can inhibit excitatory neurotransmission, increase mechanical pain thresholds, reduce hyperalgesia, and thereby alleviate postoperative pain and reduce the risk of breakthrough pain after nerve block.27 In addition, esketamine may exert local anesthetic-like effects through tonic inhibition of sodium channels, blockade of sodium influx, and suppression of action potential generation. It may also enhance the sodium channel-blocking effects of local anesthetics, producing a synergistic effect when combined with conventional local anesthetics.24 When administered alone for neuraxial or peripheral nerve blockade, esketamine may also exert clinically relevant local anesthetic effects.28 Its analgesic properties may additionally involve interactions with several opioid receptor subtypes, including the μ-opioid receptor.29 Furthermore, esketamine has been reported to increase the binding of local anesthetics to α1-acid glycoprotein.30 In the present study, NRS pain scores from 6 to 48 hours postoperatively were significantly lower in the S3 group than in the S1 and S2 groups, whereas no significant differences were observed between the S1 and S2 groups. This finding suggests that the analgesic effect may be at least partly concentration-dependent. One possible explanation is that the higher esketamine concentration in the S3 group may have increased systemic exposure after absorption from the paravertebral space, thereby producing additional systemic sedative and analgesic effects and further enhancing postoperative analgesia. In this study, patients’ subjective experiences of postoperative pain variations and their level of satisfaction were not evaluated; therefore, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was not applied to objectively assess the clinical significance of the NRS scores.

The hemodynamic findings further support the potential benefit of esketamine as a ropivacaine adjuvant for TG-TPVB. Mean arterial pressure and heart rate at the end of surgery and 1 hour postoperatively were significantly lower in the S1, S2, and S3 groups than in the control group, whereas no significant differences were observed among the three esketamine groups. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Wen et al.16 A possible explanation is that esketamine enhanced the analgesic effect of TG-TPVB and attenuated pain-related stress responses, thereby reducing blood pressure and heart rate compared with ropivacaine alone. However, these hemodynamic parameters remained within the normal range in all groups. These results suggest that esketamine may help maintain a more favorable peri-emergence hemodynamic profile by extending the analgesic effect of TG-TPVB and reducing pain-induced circulatory fluctuations during recovery from anesthesia.

Although esketamine is associated with fewer adverse effects than ketamine, it may still cause dizziness, drowsiness, hallucinations, blurred vision, vivid dreams or nightmares, nausea, and vomiting. Moreover, the incidence of esketamine-related adverse effects tends to increase with dose.12,31 In the present study, wake-up time was significantly longer in the S3 group than in the control, S1, and S2 groups, whereas no significant difference was observed among the control, S1, and S2 groups. This may be attributable to the highest dose of esketamine used in the S3 group, which could have produced greater systemic sedative effects after absorption into the circulation, thereby prolonging emergence from anesthesia. There were statistically significant differences among the four groups in terms of the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting. This may be related to the shorter duration and relatively weaker analgesic effect in the control group, which led to more frequent PCIA demands, greater need for rescue analgesia, and consequently higher opioid consumption. In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the four groups in the incidence of dizziness, drowsiness, diplopia, atelectasis, or other adverse events. One possible explanation is that the three esketamine doses used in this study were substantially lower than the commonly used intravenous dose range of 0.5–1 mg/kg; moreover, injection into the paravertebral space may have limited systemic absorption and exposure. These findings further suggest that the esketamine doses used as local anesthetic adjuvants in this study were clinically tolerable.

The time to first ambulation and the time to chest tube removal were significantly shorter in the S1, S2, and S3 groups than in the control group. These improvements may be attributable to the prolonged analgesic duration and enhanced analgesic efficacy provided by esketamine, which may have reduced chest tube–related irritation and pain during breathing or coughing. Adequate analgesia may allow patients to cough and expectorate more effectively, perform deep breathing, and initiate ambulation earlier, thereby facilitating postoperative recovery. Optimal perioperative analgesia is therefore a core component of thoracic enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways. The above findings also indicate that the use of esketamine as an adjuvant to local anesthetics in TG-TPVB is beneficial for promoting postoperative recovery in patients undergoing thoracic surgery, and is therefore highly worthy of promotion in the context of ERAS.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, doses of esketamine higher than 0.3 mg/kg were not included; therefore, the upper dose–response profile and safety margin of esketamine as an adjuvant for TG-TPVB could not be determined. Third, postoperative pain was assessed only during the first 48 h postoperatively, and longer-term analgesic outcomes were not investigated. Fourth, additional recovery-related outcomes, such as quality-of-recovery scores and postoperative length of hospital stay, were not evaluated. Fifth, this study did not incorporate the MCID to objectively assess the clinical relevance of NRS pain scores. Finally, plasma concentrations of esketamine and ropivacaine were not measured, and inflammatory biomarkers were not assessed; therefore, the pharmacokinetic profile and potential anti-inflammatory mechanisms underlying the observed effects could not be fully elucidated.

Conclusion

In patients undergoing thoracoscopic radical surgery for lung cancer, esketamine at doses of 0.1–0.3 mg/kg, when used as an adjuvant to ropivacaine for TG-TPVB, prolonged the duration of postoperative analgesia, reduced sufentanil consumption and PCIA demand, lowered the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, and facilitated early postoperative recovery, as reflected by earlier ambulation and chest tube removal. Among the tested doses, 0.2 mg/kg esketamine appeared to offer the most favorable overall balance between analgesic efficacy and safety, with effective opioid-sparing effects, stable hemodynamic responses, and no apparent delay in emergence. The administration of esketamine as an adjuvant to local anesthetics in TG-TPVB merits recommendation in the context of ERAS for thoracic surgery procedures.
