Aesthetic Surgical Tourism: Physiological Changes During Flight That May Affect Patient Safety and Outcomes
Authors: Brian Ramírez, Hugo Aguilar, Nora Stanford, Zamir Paez, Juan Pablo Menendez, Jaime Pachon, Edison Osorio, Agustina Varela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Aesthetic surgery tourism has seen a significant increase in recent years. However, patients undergoing long-haul flights after surgery may be at higher risk for complications due to physiological changes associated with air travel. This study examines the impact of commercial flight conditions on postoperative patients traveling after aesthetic procedures.Methods:This prospective study evaluated physiological variables in postoperative patients undergoing aesthetic surgery tourism during flights exceeding 4 hours. Oxygen saturation, heart rate, and popliteal vein diameter were monitored throughout the flight. The findings were compared with a control group of nonsurgical travelers. The study also analyzed the potential contribution of these physiological changes to postoperative complications.Results:All postoperative patients experienced oxygen desaturation, with an average drop of 4%–7% in hemoglobin saturation within the first 4 hours of flight. Postoperative patients also exhibited significantly lower baseline hemoglobin levels compared with presurgical levels. Heart rate increased by 15–20 bpm in all participants, and popliteal vein diameter progressively expanded throughout the flight due to hypoxia-induced vasodilation and prolonged sitting. These physiological alterations align with risk factors for thrombosis, flap necrosis, infection, and postoperative bleeding.Conclusions:Patients undergoing aesthetic surgery tourism experience decreased oxygen saturation, increased heart rate, and an enlarged popliteal vein diameter due to the body’s intrinsic responses to hypoxia. These physiological changes, combined with their postoperative state, may increase the risk of complications such as thrombosis, necrosis, infection, hematomas, and bleeding. Postoperative care protocols and preflight counseling are essential to ensure patient safety and mitigate these risks.

Takeaways

Question: How do commercial flights affect the physiological stability and safety of patients undergoing aesthetic surgical tourism?

Findings: This prospective study identified significant physiological changes in postoperative aesthetic surgery patients during commercial flights lasting more than 4 hours. Key findings include increased oxygen desaturation, venous stasis, and popliteal vein dilation.

Meaning: Patients undergoing aesthetic surgery tourism experience decreased oxygen saturation, increased heart rate, and an enlarged popliteal vein diameter due to the body’s intrinsic responses to hypoxia. These physiological changes, combined with their postoperative state, may increase the risk of complications such as thrombosis, necrosis, infection, hematomas, and bleeding.

INTRODUCTION

Medical tourism represented a $165.3 billion market in 2023 and engaged 14–16 million patients worldwide.1 Specifically, aesthetic surgical tourism, the practice of traveling abroad for cosmetic surgical procedures,2 has become a global phenomenon with increasing acceptance; according to the International Society of Plastic Surgery, 18% of the 2022 patients (2,697,656) treated by any plastic surgeon around the world were medical tourists.3

The growing popularity of aesthetic surgical tourism is driven by extensive marketing on social media and specialized websites, lower costs abroad, and all-inclusive packages that combine surgery with a vacation-like experience. Additionally, some procedures may not be available in patients’ home countries, such as rib reshaping.4–8 Although the exact global incidence of complications from aesthetic surgical tourism remains unknown, studies have reported an association between aesthetic surgical tourism and a higher rate of complications.9 Additionally, numerous reports have documented complications such as necrosis, infections, bleeding, pain, and wound dehiscence related to this practice.4,10,11

These complications often arise from inadequate patient counseling and lack of proper postoperative follow-up.11,12 Additionally, air travel after surgery poses inherent risks, including deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, fatigue, dehydration, disorientation, and pain.13,14 Long-haul flights (lasting more than 4 h) have been associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism, estimated to occur in approximately 1 passenger per 4600 flights.15,16 Risk factors for venous thromboembolism include a history of thrombosis, thrombophilia, recent surgery, pregnancy, and the use of estrogen-containing medications.17,18

An analysis of the most common origins and destinations for aesthetic surgical tourism in 2023, as reported by the International Society of Plastic Surgery, shows that more than 80% of these journeys involve flights longer than 4 hours, many of which are intercontinental (Fig. 1).3 The combination of recent surgery, prolonged immobility, and reduced oxygen pressure at altitude further increases the likelihood of complications during or after the flight.15,16,18

The objective of this study was to evaluate the physiological variables affected during air travel in patients undergoing aesthetic surgical tourism on flights longer than 4 hours. By understanding these contributing factors in greater detail, we aimed to identify potential risks and improve patient safety in this growing medical practice.

METHODS

A prospective observational analytical study was conducted, collecting data from patients during a 2-year period (2023–2025).

Subjects

The study population consisted of a sample of patients from the clinical practice of Dr. Ramírez, an aerospace medicine specialist who collaborates with plastic surgeons. All patients underwent surgical intervention and received postoperative care for 15 days from one of these surgeons. Additionally, Dr. Ramírez conducted preflight consultations and follow-ups to ensure the safest return home. Patients were monitored in person until the third day after their return flight and subsequently followed up via telephone every month for 6 months, with a final follow-up after 1 year of the surgery.

Inclusion criteria for the study were patients residing outside Colombia, younger than 45 years, with no medical history or preexisting conditions, who voluntarily agreed to participate. Eligible patients had undergone body contouring procedures such as abdominoplasty, liposuction, breast augmentation, or gluteal augmentation and remained in the city of surgery for a minimum of 15 days postoperatively.

Exclusion criteria included patients whose return flights were shorter than 4 hours or who traveled in first class, patients using homeopathic medications, patients who smoke, and those unable to receive heparin as an anticoagulant or who declined its use. Patients were withdrawn from the study if Dr. Ramírez was unable to accompany them on their return flight.

Materials and Methods

A group of patients who underwent body contouring procedures and adhered to proper postoperative care was monitored for physiological variables during their return flight and for 3 days afterward. Variables included heart rate, popliteal vein dilation, and oxygen saturation. Monthly follow-ups were conducted for 1 year to assess their health status.

Patients were selected based on their surgery dates and the feasibility of Dr. Ramírez accompanying them on their return flights, aligning with his academic travel schedule during 2 years. A total of 20 postoperative patients were included and compared with a control group that met the same inclusion criteria except for undergoing surgery. Most controls were family members accompanying the patients, and when unavailable, healthy volunteers from the same flight were recruited. In these cases, only in-flight physiological data were collected.

The popliteal vein was selected over the femoral vein, primarily for practical and safety reasons. The popliteal fossa allows for rapid, noninvasive, and discreet measurements that can be standardized across participants. To minimize variability, all ultrasound assessments were performed with participants in a supine position and knees slightly flexed to optimize vein visualization. Minimal probe pressure was applied to avoid artificial compression of the vessel, and all measurements were obtained by a single trained examiner (an aerospace medicine physician) to reduce interobserver variability, and then, the measures were confirmed by a radiologist after the flight.

Regarding SpO2 and heart rate, we acknowledge their susceptibility to motion and cabin-related artifacts. To address this, we used validated portable oximeters (Food and Drug Administration–approved) previously used in aviation physiology studies.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize patient data. Differences between preflight, during flight, and postflight measures were assessed using the Student t test for normally distributed data and equal variances (homoscedasticity), the Welch t test for normal distributions but unequal variances, and the Mann-Whitney U test for nonnormal distributions. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (The Jamovi Project, Australia; version 2.5; https://www.jamovi.org).

Ethical Considerations

All patients were informed about the surgical risks, study purpose, methods, funding sources, and any conflicts of interest. Research procedures and database management complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (Fortaleza 2013; Taipei 2016). Patients were informed of their right to refuse participation or withdraw at any time. Informed consent was obtained before the flight.

Popliteal Vein Diameter

Regarding the primary variable of interest, popliteal vein diameter, the median preflight diameter was 5.14 mm (IQR = 0.16 mm). However, no statistically significant differences were found between the postoperative patient group and the control group at any time point. Additionally, no statistically significant difference was observed between the preflight diameter and the diameter measured 3 days postflight in postoperative patients (median 5.07 mm, IQR = 0.18 mm versus median 5.08 mm, IQR = 0.17 mm; Mann-Whitney U test = 362, P = 0.81) (Table 5).

Oxygen Saturation

Regarding blood oxygen saturation, the median preflight oxygen saturation was 98% (IQR = 1.75%); this value was statistically different from the measurements recorded at 2 hours of flight (94%, IQR = 3.0%; Mann-Whitney U test = 0.0, P < 0.001; median differences = −5.0%, 95% CI = −5.0% to −4.0%), 4 hours of flight (95%, IQR = 2.0%; Mann-Whitney U test = 1875, P < 0.001; median differences = −4.0% 95% CI = −4.0% to −3.0%), and 5 hours of flight (96%, IQR = 3.0%; Mann-Whitney U test = 3574, P < 0.001; median differences = −3.0%, 95% CI = −3.0% to −3.0%). Additionally, differences in median oxygen saturation trends were observed between groups during the flight; at 2 hours, the patient group exhibited a significantly lower median oxygen saturation compared with the control group (91.0%, IQR = 2.25% versus 94.0%, IQR = 2.0%; Mann-Whitney U test = 794, P < 0.001; median differences = 3.0%, 95% CI = 2.0%–3.0%), with a similar trend at 4 hours (94%, IQR = 2.25% versus 95%, IQR = 2.0%; Mann-Whitney U test = 2052, P = 0.046; median differences = 1.0%, 95% CI = 0.00002%–1.0%). Specifically, within the postoperative patient group, statistically significant reductions in oxygen saturation were observed at various time points during the flight compared with the preflight saturation: at 2 hours (91.5%, IQR = 2.25% versus 99%, IQR = 2.00%; Mann-Whitney U test = 0.0, P < 0.001; median differences = −7.0%, 95% CI = −8.0% to −6.0%), at 4 hours (94%, IQR = 2.25% versus 99%, IQR = 2.00%; Mann-Whitney U test = 0.0, P < 0.001; median differences = −4.0%, 95% CI = −5.0% to −3.0%), and at 5 hours (95%, IQR = 3.0% versus 99%, IQR = 2.00%; Mann-Whitney U test = 18.0, P < 0.001; median differences = −3.0%, 95% CI = −4.0% to −2.0%) (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

This study highlights the physiological changes that occur during flight in patients undergoing aesthetic surgery tourism, particularly on flights lasting more than 4 hours. Understanding these changes provides deeper insights into how they may contribute to the complications previously reported in this patient population.

To begin, it is important to contextualize the environment to which individuals traveling on commercial aircraft are exposed. Commercial aircraft cabins are pressurized to an equivalent altitude of 8000 feet, corresponding to an atmospheric pressure of 560 mm Hg (73.6% of sea-level pressure), which results in a decreased oxygen pressure (from 159.6 to 117.6 mm Hg O2). Additionally, passengers are confined to a reduced space that promotes prolonged sitting and limited mobility.19

All subjects in this study experienced oxygen desaturation during the flight. The most significant drop occurred within the first 2–4 hours (a decrease of 4%–7% in hemoglobin saturation), followed by stabilization at an average decrease of 3% after 5 hours of flight. This is attributed to the body’s acute compensatory mechanisms for hypoxia.20 Notably, postoperative patients who had undergone body contouring surgery exhibited statistically lower hemoglobin saturation levels compared with controls. This may be explained by a mean hemoglobin reduction of 3.2 g/dL due to intraoperative blood loss; furthermore, postoperative patients demonstrated a reduced capacity to mount the same compensatory response to hypoxia compared with controls.

All participants also experienced an increase in heart rate, ranging from 16 to 18 bpm throughout the flight. This is a compensatory mechanism in response to acute hypoxia, leading to a 30% increase in cardiac output, vasodilation, and increased capillary permeability.20

Furthermore, all participants exhibited a progressive increase in popliteal vein diameter during the flight; this can be attributed to hypoxia-induced vasodilation and prolonged sitting.21 No statistically significant differences were observed between groups, as there were no additional factors contributing to distinct outcomes in the postoperative group. Notably, follow-up assessments 3 days after returning home showed that popliteal vein diameters in the postoperative patients had returned to baseline preflight values.

The literature suggests that patients undergoing aesthetic surgery tourism have a 3.6-fold higher likelihood of experiencing complications compared with those treated locally (adjusted odds ratio = 4.6; 95% CI = 2.6–8.2; P < 0.001).9 The physiological changes observed during flight, the inadequate patient counseling, and lack of proper postoperative follow-up may contribute to these adverse outcomes.11,12 Therefore, we analyze the role of air travel as a contributory factor to the most common complications in this patient group.

Thrombosis

Thrombosis is defined as the formation of a blood clot within a vessel, potentially obstructing blood flow. According to the concept of Virchow triad, all postoperative patients in this study presented with the 3 key components for thrombosis formation during air travel: venous stasis and vasodilation due to immobility and the physiological hypoxia response; recent endothelial injury, an inherent condition in all postoperative cases; and hypercoagulability, resulting from surgical blood loss combined with homeostatic changes triggered by surgical stress and inflammatory responses, leading to elevated fibrinogen and platelet levels alongside a hypofibrinolytic state.17,18,22 Given that these patients meet all 3 components of the Virchow triad during flight, we recommend, based on our experience, that they implement at least one of the following preventive measures: moderate compression stockings or pre- and postflight anticoagulation with enoxaparin.23,24

Flap Necrosis

Necrosis is a pathological process in which cell death is characterized by a loss of plasma membrane integrity, leading to extracellular content release and a subsequent inflammatory response that perpetuates tissue damage.25–27 Mild hypoxia in the cabin environment can exacerbate this condition in at-risk patients through the following mechanisms: hypoxia promotes vasodilation and blood stasis, which, in a compromised flap, hinders adequate circulation, oxygenation, and nutrient delivery. Reduced oxygen saturation in the blood further limits oxygen supply to vulnerable tissues, favoring anaerobic metabolism and leading to lactate accumulation and metabolic acidosis; this cascade exacerbates cellular injury and promotes necrotic cell death.28,29

Infection

Although infection requires a predisposing pathogenic factor before, during, or after the flight, cabin hypoxia may contribute to its development. The human immune system is oxygen-dependent for optimal function. Neutrophils require the production of reactive oxygen species to effectively kill bacteria; under hypoxic conditions, neutrophils release cytotoxic contents that damage surrounding healthy tissues, perpetuating a chronic inflammatory state30,31; additionally, natural killer cell cytotoxic activity and signaling pathways, including STAT3 and ERK, are impaired under hypoxia32; Furthermore, hypoxic conditions favor biofilm formation, which enhances bacterial resistance to immune responses33

Bleeding and Hematomas

Commercial air travel can exacerbate bleeding and hematoma formation when wound healing is incomplete. This occurs due to several factors: hypoxia induces vasodilation and increases capillary permeability, and reduced cabin pressure facilitates gas expansion (Boyle law), which, in the case of hematomas, can lead to rupture and subsequent bleeding. Additionally, sympathetic activation due to travel-related stress or anxiety can elevate blood pressure, increasing the risk of postoperative bleeding.19,20

Given these risks, patient safety—especially for international patients—must be emphasized. Various intraoperative protocols currently exist to minimize blood loss, reduce immediate complications, and accelerate recovery, including hyperbaric oxygen therapy34 and other interventions that optimize patients’ preflight condition. As medical professionals, we must ensure that the best available options are implemented for our patients.

Despite its valuable contributions to understanding the physiological changes experienced by postoperative patients during flight, this study has important limitations. It is a prospective study with a small sample of postoperative patients, which precluded more detailed bivariate analyses to identify specific factors associated with each complication. Our cohort included only young, healthy, low-risk patients, which limits the generalizability of our findings. High-risk groups, such as patients with obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2), patients who smoke, or those with comorbidities, may experience more pronounced physiological changes and greater vulnerability to complications. Future studies should specifically address these populations to establish tailored aeromedical recommendations. In addition, due to the design of the study, neither randomization nor stratification of patients could be performed, which reduces the internal validity of the results.

CONCLUSIONS

Patients undergoing aesthetic surgery tourism experience decreased oxygen saturation, increased heart rate, and an enlarged popliteal vein diameter due to the body’s intrinsic responses to hypoxia. These physiological changes, combined with their postoperative state, may increase the risk of complications such as thrombosis, necrosis, infection, hematomas, and bleeding. Postoperative care protocols and preflight counseling are essential to ensure patient safety and mitigate these risks. At present, our conditions for patients boarding their return flight may be used as general preventive measures to reduce the occurrence of postflight postoperative complications. Future research is needed to better understand this phenomenon and to develop new aeromedical guidelines aimed at improving patient outcomes.
