A Prodrug Approach for Activity‐Based Chemical Modulation toward Multiple Pathological Targets in Alzheimer's Disease
Authors: Jimin Lee, Eunseo Hong, Chanju Na, Yan Li, Jun Go, Ju‐Eun Kim, Hyun‐Hee Seo, Jong‐Min Suh, Jimin Kwak, Young‐Ho Lee, Kyoung‐Shim Kim, Chul‐Ho Lee, Mingeun Kim, Mi Hee Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTargeting disease‐specific chemical signals enables precise therapeutic control over complex pathologies. In Alzheimer's disease (AD), elevated hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) accompanies hallmark features, including amyloid‐β (Aβ) aggregate deposition and metal ion dyshomeostasis, creating an oxidative milieu primed for selective chemical activation. Here, we show a rationally designed prodrug platform that harnesses H2O2 as an endogenous trigger for redox‐based therapy. Boronic ester‐masked precursors (BE‐1 and BE‐2) remain inert under physiological conditions but undergo rapid oxidative deboronation in the presence of H2O2, releasing redox‐active aminophenols. These activated molecules exhibit multimodal pathological modulation, as revealed by molecular‐level biochemical and biophysical analyses: scavenging reactive oxygen species, inducing residue‐specific oxidative modifications of Aβ, and redirecting aggregation pathways of both metal‐free and metal‐bound Aβ. In AD transgenic mice, BE‐1 undergoes conversion to its active form within the brain tissue. Long‐term administration of BE‐1 markedly reduces hippocampal oxidative stress, lowers amyloid plaque burden, and improves cognitive performance. This pathology‐responsive, activity‐based prodrug strategy provides a chemically precise framework for simultaneously modulating multiple, interconnected drivers of neurodegeneration.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, chemical modulators, metal‐free and metal‐bound amyloid‐β peptides, prodrugs, reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Prodrugs are powerful tools in medicinal chemistry, enabling conditional activation of therapeutics to enhance pharmacological properties, improve target selectivity, and minimize systemic toxicity [1, 2, 3]. Clinically established examples, such as levodopa, lisdexamfetamine, and omeprazole, achieve optimal therapeutic efficacy through enhanced blood−brain barrier (BBB) penetration, controlled enzymatic activation, or site‐specific release (Figure 1a) [1]. While these cases demonstrate improvements in discrete drug properties by exploiting endogenous biomolecules or physiological conditions, emerging therapeutic challenges increasingly call for pathophysiology‐driven design strategies.

One promising strategy is to harness disease‐associated chemical cues for selective, spatiotemporally controlled activation of therapeutics. Among these, reactive oxygen species (ROS) have emerged as particularly attractive triggers. Of the endogenous ROS, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is notable for its relatively long half‐life, allowing it to accumulate to higher levels than other ROS [4, 5, 6, 7]. Under normal physiological conditions, H2O2 acts as a signaling molecule, maintained at low concentrations by antioxidant defenses [8]. In pathological states, such as cancer [9, 10, 11], inflammation [12], and neurodegeneration [13, 14, 15, 16, 17], this redox homeostasis is disrupted, leading to sustained H2O2 elevation and oxidative stress. Given its pathological relevance and chemical accessibility, H2O2 offers a potent trigger for prodrug activation [1, 2, 5]. Yet, this molecular design principle remains largely unexplored in the context of neurodegenerative disorders.

In Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most prevalent form of dementia, severe oxidative stress is a central driver of disease progression [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Hallmark pathological features, including amyloid‐β (Aβ) aggregate accumulation [26, 27, 28] and metal ion dyshomeostasis [15, 29, 30, 31], exacerbate oxidative damage and contribute to neuronal death. In particular, redox‐active metal ions, such as Cu(I/II), coordinate to Aβ, undergo redox cycling, and catalytically generate H2O2 [16, 29, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. These multifactorial processes establish a chemically distinct microenvironment enriched in H2O2, providing an opportunity for selective, pathology‐triggered chemical activation. Such conditions offer a dual advantage: enabling targeted activation of chemical modulators while simultaneously engaging ROS. We therefore hypothesized that a therapeutic strategy leveraging H2O2 both as a pathological hallmark and as a chemical trigger could selectively modulate AD‐relevant targets. Building on our prior redox‐based design framework [37, 38], we focused on aminophenol derivatives, electron‐rich para‐substituted phenylene scaffolds that exhibit oxidation‐driven reactivities toward AD‐associated pathological factors. We further envisioned that boronic ester masking of these motifs would suppress their basal redox reactivities and enable H2O2‐responsive activation, thereby placing their reactivities under pathological control.

In this study, we present an activity‐based chemical design strategy that harnesses H2O2‐triggered oxidative deboronation of boronic acid pinacol ester (Bpin)‐masked precursors to generate multifunctional small molecules under AD‐relevant oxidative conditions. This approach leverages H2O2‐induced electronic modulation to confer multiple reactivities toward key pathological targets of AD, including ROS, metal‐free Aβ, and metal‐bound Aβ (metal–Aβ). To evaluate its feasibility and therapeutic potential, as outlined in Figure 1b, we rationally selected small molecules and systematically investigated their H2O2‐triggered activation, reactivities against pathogenic factors, and in vivo efficacies in AD transgenic (Tg) mice. We anticipate that this H2O2‐responsive chemical framework will serve as a versatile platform for prodrug development capable of engaging multifactorial disease mechanisms, thereby advancing efforts to treat oxidative stress‐associated disorders.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Design and Properties

As illustrated in Figure 2a, we rationally designed Bpin‐masked small molecules, BE‐1 and BE‐2, which undergo oxidative deboronation in response to H2O2 to release aminophenol products, AP‐1 and AP‐2, respectively. We hypothesized that this transformation would markedly alter the electrochemical profile of the molecules, thereby enabling multiple reactivities toward key pathological factors associated with AD. This concept builds on minimalistic redox‐based principles: para‐substituted phenylene scaffolds bearing two electron‐donating groups that display negative half‐wave potentials [E 1/2; vs. Ag/Ag(I)] readily participate in oxidative processes, engaging with free organic radicals, metal‐free Aβ, and metal–Aβ [37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. These electron‐rich molecules, however, often suffer from poor chemical stability, potential cytotoxicity, and limited selectivity under pathophysiological conditions. In contrast, relatively electron‐poor aromatics are chemically stable and redox‐inactive under similar conditions [37, 38]. To reconcile these trade‐offs, we introduced an electron‐withdrawing Bpin moiety to suppress undesired redox activity while enabling H2O2‐responsive activation.

We confirmed the H2O2‐triggered chemical transformations and electrochemical properties of BE‐1 and BE‐2. Upon exposure to H2O2, both compounds underwent oxidative deboronation to yield their corresponding aminophenols, as verified by electronic absorption (Abs) spectroscopy and electrospray ionization–mass spectrometry (ESI–MS) (Figures 2b and S1). Cyclic voltammetry showed that BE‐1 and BE‐2 undergo irreversible oxidation, with anodic peak potential (E pa) values of 464 mV and 444 mV vs. Ag/Ag(I), respectively, whereas AP‐1 and AP‐2 displayed quasi‐reversible and reversible redox behavior, respectively, with negative E 1/2 values (Table S1 and Figures S2 and S3). Consistent with these redox characteristics and our previous in vitro studies [37, 38, 39], H2O2‐mediated conversion into AP molecules is expected to enhance Aβ oxidation and promote covalent adduct formation, thereby redirecting aggregation pathways. In line with this expectation, the aminophenol products retained aggregation‐modulating activity in complex biological environments, including cell lysates containing heterogeneous protein mixtures, as well as in the presence of excess human serum albumin (Figures S4 and S5). Similar aggregation modulation was also observed for another amyloidogenic peptide, human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP), with distinct extents and modes of chemical modification (Figure S6).

We then compared the stability profiles of BE and AP compounds. AP‐1 and AP‐2 underwent spontaneous autoxidation [43, 44, 45], in contrast to BE‐1 and BE‐2, which remained stable for 24 h, undergoing only hydrolysis to the corresponding boronic acid (Figure S7) [46, 47]. This hydrolytic conversion does not compromise H2O2 responsiveness, as the resulting boronic acid species remain competent for oxidative deboronation to yield the corresponding AP compounds (Figure S8). In the presence of Aβ, AP‐1 and AP‐2 can undergo oxidative deamination to form benzoquinone (BQ), which conjugates to Aβ via nucleophilic amino acid residues [37, 38, 39, 48], providing a chemical basis for residue‐specific modifications. Overall, the Bpin group functions as a stability‐enhancing protecting group and a switch for H2O2‐responsive activation, enabling controlled Aβ modifications and mechanistic investigations of its aggregation behavior.

H2O2‐Responsive Chemical Modifications of Aβ

To determine whether BE compounds mediate H2O2‐responsive, site‐specific modifications of Aβ, we performed ESI–MS and tandem MS (ESI–MS2) analyses. In the absence of H2O2, Aβ40 treated with BE‐1 or BE‐2 showed no detectable mass shifts (Figure 2c). Upon H2O2 activation, however, an intensified peak at 1,449 m/z and a new peak of 1,483 m/z appeared. The peak at 1,449 m/z, which was also observed with H2O2 alone or AP compounds’ addition, corresponds to singly oxidized monomeric Aβ40. Collision‐induced dissociation (CID) analysis consistently produced oxidized b fragments starting from b 35, confirming Met35 as the oxidation site (Figure S9a).

The peak at 1,483 m/z, showing a + 120 Da shift from native monomeric Aβ40, was further examined by ESI–MS2 (Figures 2c and S9b). Fragmentation revealed a 104 Da adduct localized to b 16–b 34 and a 120 Da modification starting from b 35, indicating dual modification (covalent adduct formation combined with Met35 oxidation). A similar 104 Da adduct was detected in Aβ40 samples directly treated with AP compounds (e.g., 1,478 m/z). AP compounds can undergo oxidative conversion to BQ in the presence of Aβ40, which then forms covalent cross‐links with primary amines, such as Lys16 (Figure S9c) [37, 38, 39, 48, 49]. This mechanism was supported by electronic Abs spectroscopy, which displayed a sequential change at 250 nm followed by an intensified signal around 350 nm, consistent with oxidative transformations of AP molecules and their covalent binding to Aβ40 (Figure S10) [37, 39]. Overall, these results demonstrate that BE compounds, upon H2O2 activation, induce both Aβ oxidation and intermolecular cross‐linking between chemically transformed AP compounds and Aβ, providing a basis for selective modulation of Aβ aggregation (vide infra).

Impact on Aβ Aggregation

Having established that H2O2 converts BE compounds into AP molecules capable of chemically modifying Aβ peptides, we next evaluated how this transformation influences Aβ aggregation. The aggregation kinetics of Aβ were first monitored using the thioflavin‐T (ThT) fluorescence assay, which quantifies the formation of β‐sheet‐rich assemblies [50]. To further characterize the resulting Aβ aggregates, molecular weight (M.W.) distributions were analyzed by gel electrophoresis and Western blotting (gel/Western blot) using an anti‐Aβ antibody (6E10), while Aβ assemblies too large to penetrate the gel matrix were visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). Two major Aβ isoforms were investigated: (i) Aβ40, predominant in cerebrovascular amyloid deposits, and (ii) Aβ42, the primary component of parenchymal senile plaques [51, 52].

Modulation of Aβ40 Aggregation

The effects of BE and AP compounds on the generation of Aβ40 aggregates were examined in the absence and presence of H2O2 (Figure 3a). Aβ40 displayed a typical sigmoidal profile consistent with nucleation‐dependent amyloid fibril formation [53, 54, 55]. Sigmoidal fitting analyses [53] yielded lag times (t lag) of 5.00 (± 0.07) h and 4.62 (± 0.07) h and half‐times (t 1/2) of 7.30 (± 0.03) h and 6.67 (± 0.03) h in the absence and presence of H2O2, respectively (Figure 3b). In the absence of H2O2, BE compounds produced only minor modulation of Aβ40 aggregation kinetics, showing slight reductions in the t lag and t 1/2 values [t lag = 4.60 (± 0.06) h and 4.21 (± 0.05) h; t 1/2 = 6.29 (± 0.03) h and 5.86 (± 0.02) h for BE‐1 and BE‐2, respectively], accompanied by a ca. 10–20% decrease in the final ThT fluorescence intensity. In contrast, upon treatment with H2O2, aggregation kinetics became comparable to those of compound‐untreated Aβ40, while the plateau fluorescence intensity was markedly reduced by ca. 82% and 42%, respectively. A similar trend was observed for the corresponding AP compounds, which produced minimal changes in aggregation kinetics but significantly reduced the final ThT fluorescence intensity (ca. 81% and 34% decreases for AP‐1 and AP‐2, respectively). These results suggest that the activated compounds suppress the formation of β‐sheet‐rich aggregates without substantially altering overall aggregation kinetics. It should be noted that the potential influence of covalent adduct formation between BQ and Aβ40 on the ThT fluorescence signal cannot be excluded, as such interactions introduce a broad Abs band around 350 nm (Figure S10). Consistent H2O2‐responsive modulation by BE‐1 was also observed across sub‐ to supra‐equimolar H2O2 concentrations, with a ca. 52% reduction in the plateau fluorescence intensity even at 0.05 equiv of H2O2 (Figure S11). Under equimolar to supra‐equimolar conditions, aggregation profiles closely resembled those of AP‐1.

Changes in Aβ40 aggregation behavior were further reflected in the M.W. distribution and morphology of the resulting aggregates (Figure 3c–e). H2O2‐activated BE‐1 produced broad smearing across the 11–245 kDa range, whereas BE‐2 induced discrete bands at 11–25 kDa and above 245 kDa. As illustrated in Figure 3d,e, TEM and AFM measurements revealed distinct fibrillar morphologies in the presence of the activated BE compounds compared to the compound‐untreated Aβ40 samples. Compound‐free Aβ40 aggregates formed thick fibril clusters with heights of ca. 20–40 nm, as measured by AFM. In the presence of H2O2, BE‐1 generated short and thin amyloid fibrils with heights below 10 nm, and BE‐2 produced thin but relatively longer fibrils with heights below 8 nm (Figure 3e). Note that no measurable changes in the M.W. and morphology were observed in the absence of H2O2.

To evaluate the effects of the compounds on preformed Aβ40 assemblies, Aβ40 was first incubated for 24 h to produce aggregates and subsequently treated with the compounds in the absence and presence of H2O2 for an additional 24 h (Figure S12a). Without H2O2, BE compounds had negligible impacts on the size distribution and morphology of preformed Aβ40 aggregates. Conversely, under H2O2‐present conditions, BE‐1 promoted the formation of heterogeneous oligomeric species, as indicated by increased smearing above 11 kDa, whereas BE‐2 enhanced high‐M.W. aggregates above 245 kDa (Figure S12b). TEM investigations confirmed morphological differences, showing thinner and fragmented amyloid fibrils similar to those generated by AP compounds (Figure S12c).

Control of Aβ42 Aggregation

Like Aβ40, Aβ42 exhibited sigmoidal aggregation kinetics with the t lag and t 1/2 values of 5.17 (± 0.07) h and 6.80 (± 0.03) h, respectively, and showed no substantial changes upon treatment with H2O2 alone (Figure 4b). In the absence of H2O2, BE compounds modestly accelerated aggregation kinetics [t lag = 4.44 (± 0.05) h and 4.25 (± 0.06) h; t 1/2 = 5.95 (± 0.02) h and 5.77 (± 0.03) h for BE‐1 and BE‐2, respectively], consistent with the trend observed for Aβ40. These treatments also resulted in a more pronounced reduction in β‐sheet‐rich aggregate formation, reflected by a ca. 35–42% decrease in the ThT fluorescence intensity at 24 h. Upon exposure to H2O2, BE‐1 and BE‐2 further reduced the plateau fluorescence intensity by ca. 93% and 61%, respectively, reaching levels comparable to those observed for the corresponding AP compounds (ca. 91% and 67% reductions for AP‐1 and AP‐2, respectively), while maintaining aggregation kinetics similar to those observed without H2O2.

As expected from the investigations with Aβ40, BE compounds in the presence of H2O2 also altered the M.W. distribution and morphology of Aβ42 aggregates. Treatment with BE‐1 or BE‐2 and H2O2 increased smearing above 25 kDa while reducing the band intensity at 5 kDa (Figure 4c). Aggregates formed in the presence of the activated BE compounds appeared as fewer, thinner, and shorter amyloid fibrils, typically exhibiting heights below 10–15 nm, whereas compound‐free Aβ42 formed more fibrillar clusters reaching ca. 30–40 nm (Figure 4d,e). Moreover, H2O2‐activated BE compounds also modulated preformed Aβ42 aggregates, altering both their M.W. distribution and morphology (Figures S13 and S16). Collectively, these results demonstrate that H2O2‐responsive activation of BE compounds modulates both the formation and disassembly of Aβ40 and Aβ42 aggregates. This activity‐based chemical regulation, mediated by oxidation and covalent modifications of Aβ (vide supra), highlights the potential of this prodrug strategy to modulate Aβ aggregation dynamics.

Effects on ROS Levels and Cu(II)–Aβ Aggregation during Cu(I/II)–Aβ‐Mediated Redox Cycling

Cu(I/II)–Aβ complexes catalytically generate ROS through redox cycling under reducing conditions, contributing to oxidative stress in AD [16, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. We examined whether BE compounds could modulate both ROS production and Cu(II)–Aβ aggregation under these conditions. BE and AP compounds were incubated with Cu(II)–Aβ40 in the presence of L‐ascorbic acid (Asc) as a reducing agent, and H2O2 levels were measured using the Amplex Red H2O2/peroxidase assay (Figure 5a). BE‐1 and BE‐2 markedly decreased H2O2 levels by ca. 54% and 69%, respectively, consistent with H2O2 consumption during BE‐to‐AP conversion and the intrinsic ROS‐scavenging capacity of AP compounds.

We next assessed the effects of BE compounds on Cu(II)–Aβ40 aggregation by gel/Western blot, TEM, and AFM (Figures 5b, S17, and S18). In the absence of Asc, where H2O2 is not generated (Figure 5a), BE compounds had little to no impact on the M.W. distribution or fibril morphology. Under reducing conditions with Asc, however, H2O2‐triggered activation of BE compounds produced pronounced aggregation changes, resembling those induced by AP compounds: gel/Western blots showed intensified smearing across 11–245 kDa (Figure 5b), while TEM images revealed smaller amorphous aggregates and thinner, shorter fibrils. These contrast sharply with the larger amorphous assemblies and thick fibril bundles observed in the compound‐untreated Cu(II)–Aβ40 sample (Figures 5b and S18). Consistent with TEM data, AFM measurements further visualized that Cu(II)–Aβ40 aggregates formed in the presence of BE compounds and Asc predominantly consisted of smaller, non‐fibrillar aggregates (Figure 5b). Therefore, these results verify that BE compounds, once activated by H2O2 generated during Cu(I/II)–Aβ redox cycling, simultaneously scavenge ROS and modulate Cu(II)–Aβ aggregation. This dual functionality underscores the potential use of H2O2‐responsive prodrugs in mitigating both oxidative and aggregation‐associated pathogenic mechanisms.

Biological Properties

Before initiating in vivo studies, we assessed the cytotoxicity of BE and AP compounds. The compounds’ toxicity was first examined in human neuroblastoma SH‐SY5Y (5Y) cells using the MTT assay [MTT = 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide]. As shown in Figure 6a, BE compounds exhibited lower cytotoxicity than their AP counterparts, as reflected in higher IC50 values. Notably, BE‐1 showed minimal toxicity in neuronal cells, maintaining greater than 80% cell viability at concentrations up to 300 µM over 24 h. The toxicity of the compounds was further evaluated in human liver (HepG2) and embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells. In both cell lines, BE compounds consistently exhibited higher IC50 values than their corresponding AP analogues, supporting comparatively low cytotoxicity. In particular, BE‐1 displayed the lowest toxicity among the tested compounds, maintaining over 75% cell survival up to 300 µM in both HepG2 and HEK293 cells (Figures 6a, S19, and S20).

In addition to cytotoxicity, we examined the BBB permeability of the compounds by both calculated and experimental approaches. Calculated logBB values were estimated based on polar surface area (PSA) and calculated partition coefficient (clogP), while −logP e values were determined in vitro using the parallel artificial membrane permeability assay adapted for the BBB (PAMPA–BBB). BE compounds displayed favorable brain penetration, with logBB values above −1.0 [56] and −logP e values below 5.4 (Figure 6b) [57, 58]. PAMPA–BBB measurements for AP compounds were not feasible due to their limited chemical stability, although calculated logBB values suggested good BBB permeability.

Moreover, the in vivo brain uptake of BE‐1 (2.5 mg/kg) was investigated in C57BL/6J (B6) mice following intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration. As summarized in Table S2, BE‐1 was detectable in both the brain and plasma within 5 min post‐treatment, confirming its ability to cross the BBB in vivo. The concentration of BE‐1 declined over time and became undetectable in the brain and plasma after 30 min, suggesting its relatively rapid clearance. Notably, AP‐1 was not detected in either the brain or plasma at any examined time point in non‐Tg B6 mice, indicating no measurable conversion under normal physiological conditions. On the basis of these in vitro and in vivo assessments, BE‐1 was advanced to subsequent in vivo behavioral and histochemical evaluations of AD Tg mice (vide infra).

In Vivo Efficacies in AD Tg Mouse Models

To determine whether the H2O2‐responsive prodrug strategy of BE‐1 observed in vitro translates into therapeutic efficacy in vivo, we investigated its impact on AD‐related pathology in Tg mouse models. Specifically, we evaluated whether BE‐1 administration could attenuate oxidative stress, modulate Aβ aggregate deposition, and improve cognitive performance. These effects were examined using two well‐established AD Tg mouse models: (i) APP/PS1 Tg mice and (ii) 5xFAD Tg mice [59, 60].

Investigations in APP/PS1 Tg Mice

APP/PS1 Tg mice carry mutations in the amyloid precursor protein (APP; Swedish, K670N/M671L) and presenilin‐1 (PSEN1‐ΔE9) genes, and develop early amyloid pathology and elevated oxidative stress [59]. BE‐1 was dissolved in DMSO and diluted with 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) containing 150 mM NaCl to a final concentration of 1% v/v DMSO. The solution was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 1 mg/kg once daily to 6‐month‐old female APP/PS1 Tg mice for 37 consecutive days (Figure 7a). Control groups received the same vehicle solution (1% v/v DMSO in 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl). Body weights remained stable across all groups, including vehicle‐treated wild‐type (WT) mice, vehicle‐treated APP/PS1 Tg mice, and BE‐1‐administered APP/PS1 Tg mice (Figure S21a).

Behavioral assessments began on day 28. In the open field test, BE‐1 treatment increased locomotor activity, but the effect was not statistically significant (Figure S21b,c), indicating no major change in anxiety‐like behavior or exploratory drive. In contrast, the novel object recognition (NOR) test revealed a significant improvement in recognition memory. BE‐1‐treated APP/PS1 Tg mice spent more time exploring and made more visits to the novel object (ca. 63% for exploration time and ca. 60% for visits), whereas vehicle‐treated APP/PS1 Tg controls showed no clear preference (ca. 49% for time and ca. 48% for visits), indicating enhanced cognitive performance (Figure 7b).

To examine whether the observed behavioral improvements were associated with broader changes in neuroinflammatory signaling or synaptic integrity, we analyzed the mRNA levels of representative pro‐inflammatory cytokines [interleukin‐1β (Il1β), tumor necrosis factor‐α (Tnfα), and interleukin‐6 (Il6)] [61, 62, 63, 64] in the cerebral cortex and the protein levels of synaptic markers [postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD‐95), gephyrin, and synaptophysin] [65] in the hippocampus. As described in Figures S22–S24 and Table S3, under the current dosing conditions, BE‐1 treatment did not produce statistically significant changes in these inflammatory or synaptic markers relative to vehicle‐treated APP/PS1 Tg controls. These results suggest that the observed cognitive benefits were not accompanied by the detectable modulation of inflammatory gene expression or synaptic protein abundance.

At the tissue level, immunohistochemical analyses were performed to assess BE‐1’s influence on oxidative stress and amyloid deposition. As depicted in Figure 7c, vehicle‐treated APP/PS1 Tg mice showed marked hippocampal accumulation of 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE), a major byproduct of lipid peroxidation and a marker of oxidative stress [22, 66]. BE‐1 treatment substantially reduced 4‐HNE‐positive areas, particularly within the CA1 region (ca. 43% decrease), consistent with its ROS‐scavenging capability. Despite the in vitro evidence of Aβ aggregation modulation, amyloid plaque burden in the hippocampus and parietal cortex was not significantly altered under these treatment conditions (Figure S25), possibly due to limited brain exposure or insufficient conversion of BE‐1 to its active form at the administered dose and treatment duration.

We therefore conducted an additional in vivo study using a 2.5‐fold higher dose of BE‐1 to examine its brain exposure, chemical conversion, and potential impact on Aβ aggregation. BE‐1 (2.5 mg/kg; 3% v/v DMSO in saline), the maximum feasible dose based on its limited aqueous solubility, was administered to 6‐month‐old female APP/PS1 Tg mice for 20 consecutive days. Quantitative liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis of brain tissues collected 10 min after the final injection revealed detectable conversion of BE‐1 to AP‐1 [0.34 (± 0.20) ng/g; Table S4], whereas no corresponding conversion was observed in the plasma or blood cell pellets. These results indicate that BE‐1 reaches the brain and undergoes chemical transformation to AP‐1 in vivo. Brain homogenates were further analyzed by gel/Western blot using an anti‐Aβ antibody (6E10). Compared with vehicle‐treated APP/PS1 Tg mice, BE‐1‐treated mice displayed a shift in Aβ species toward more gel‐permeable, lower‐M.W. assemblies with increased signal intensity in the 25–75 kDa range (Figures S26 and S27). These findings suggest that higher‐dose BE‐1 administration can modulate the distribution of Aβ assemblies in vivo.

Investigations in 5xFAD Tg Mice

Given the limited plaque modulation observed under the initial dosing conditions in APP/PS1 Tg mice, we next evaluated the in vivo efficacy of BE‐1 with extended and higher‐dose treatment in a second AD Tg mouse model characterized by rapid and severe Aβ accumulation [60]. The 5xFAD model harbors five familial AD mutations, including three mutations in human APP (Swedish K670N/M671L, Florida I716V, and London V717I) and two mutations in human presenilin‐1 (PSEN1; M146L/L286V) [60]. As illustrated in Figure 8a, 3‐month‐old male 5xFAD Tg mice received daily i.p. injections of BE‐1 (2.5 mg/kg; 3% v/v DMSO in saline) for 72 days. Reduced L‐glutathione (GSH), a well‐established antioxidant, was included as a positive control and administered at a dose of 50 mg/kg under the same regimen (Figure 8a) [67, 68, 69, 70]. Throughout the treatment period, no mortality was observed during treatment, and body weights remained stable across all experimental groups, including vehicle‐treated WT and 5xFAD Tg mice, as well as BE‐1‐ and GSH‐treated Tg mice (Figure S28).

In the NOR test, BE‐1‐treated 5xFAD Tg mice exhibited significant improvements in recognition memory, as evidenced by increased exploration time and more frequent visits to the novel object (ca. 62% for time and ca. 61% for visits), while vehicle‐treated Tg controls showed minimal discrimination between objects (ca. 52% for time and ca. 50% for visits) (Figures 8b and S29). In the Morris water maze (MWM) test, BE‐1‐administered 5xFAD Tg mice spent more time in the target quadrant and reached the target location more rapidly than vehicle‐treated Tg mice, indicative of improved spatial learning and memory (Figures 8c and S30). Specifically, BE‐1‐treated Tg mice spent ca. 31% of the total time in the target quadrant compared to ca. 24% for vehicle‐treated Tg mice, a value close to the chance level (25%). Comparable behavioral improvements were observed in the GSH‐treated Tg group (Figure 8b,c). Together, these results demonstrate that BE‐1 administration effectively ameliorates both recognition and spatial memory deficits in 5xFAD Tg mice.

We next evaluated pathological features associated with these cognitive improvements. BE‐1‐treated 5xFAD Tg mice exhibited markedly reduced 4‐HNE‐positive areas, particularly in the hippocampal CA3 region (ca. 27% decrease), comparable to the reduction observed following high‐dose GSH administration (Figure 8d). Immunostaining with the anti‐Aβ antibody 6E10 further revealed a ca. 39% reduction in hippocampal Aβ aggregate deposition following BE‐1 treatment (Figure 8e). This trend is consistent with previous reports demonstrating reduced amyloid plaque burden following treatment with AP‐1 or related analogues in AD Tg mice [37, 38, 39], supporting the effective engagement of BE‐1 under extended dosing conditions. A similar decrease in Aβ deposition (ca. 54%) was observed in the GSH‐treated Tg group (Figure 8e), in line with studies suggesting that restoration of glutathione homeostasis can modulate AD‐related pathology [67, 69].

Taken together, these in vivo studies demonstrate that BE‐1 can be activated within AD‐affected brain environments and modulate AD‐relevant pathological features in Tg mouse models. In APP/PS1 Tg mice, BE‐1 administration attenuated hippocampal oxidative stress and improved recognition memory despite minimal changes in amyloid plaque burden. Importantly, LC–MS/MS analyses confirmed the formation of AP‐1 in the brain tissue following BE‐1 administration, providing direct evidence of the prodrug conversion in vivo. In the 5xFAD Tg model, extended higher‐dose treatment resulted in broader efficacy, including reduced hippocampal oxidative stress, decreased Aβ aggregate deposition, and improvements in both recognition and spatial memory. Collectively, these findings highlight our activity‐based prodrug strategy as a promising framework for developing redox‐responsive therapeutic approaches targeting AD‐related pathology.

Conclusion

Neurodegenerative diseases are driven by interconnected pathological processes, including oxidative stress, amyloidogenic protein aggregation, and metal ion dyshomeostasis, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that selectively engage diseased microenvironments and address multiple disease hallmarks. In this study, we present an H2O2‐responsive prodrug approach that exploits endogenous H2O2 as a pathological trigger for site‐ and condition‐specific molecular activation.

The designed precursors, BE‐1 and BE‐2, incorporate a Bpin masking group that undergoes oxidative deboronation in response to H2O2, releasing redox‐active AP‐1 and AP‐2. Once activated, these compounds display multifunctional reactivities: efficient ROS scavenging, residue‐specific chemical modifications of Aβ peptides, and modulation of the aggregation of both metal‐free and metal‐bound Aβ. This Bpin masking strategy also reduces cytotoxicity and improves BBB permeability, enabling in vivo application. Notably, BE‐1 was confirmed to undergo oxidative conversion to AP‐1 within the brains of APP/PS1 Tg mice, whereas such conversion was not detected in the plasma and blood cell pellets or brains of non‐Tg mice. These findings support preferential activation within pathological AD‐affected brain environments rather than under physiological conditions. Long‐term BE‐1 administration markedly improved cognitive function and attenuated hippocampal oxidative stress in both APP/PS1 Tg and 5xFAD Tg mouse models, while prolonged higher‐dose treatment in 5xFAD Tg mice further reduced Aβ aggregate deposition.

While this study establishes the feasibility of an H2O2‐responsive, activity‐based prodrug platform in AD, further optimization will be required to enhance brain delivery and maximize localized activation efficiency. As this strategy progresses toward translational development, comprehensive evaluations of the optimized compounds for long‐term safety (e.g., potential off‐target oxidative modifications and systemic redox imbalance) will be essential. Building on the mechanistic insights gained in this study, future chemical design should more explicitly account for the complex redox landscapes of diseased microenvironments, enabling more selective and context‐dependent engagement with pathological targets. Overall, this work establishes a broadly applicable chemical framework for conditionally‐activatable, redox‐based therapeutics and underscores Bpin masking as a powerful design principle for developing H2O2‐responsive, activity‐based chemical modulators capable of intervening in the multifactorial pathology of neurodegenerative disorders.
