Ventricular Tachycardia Ablation in Myocarditis as a Therapeutic Option
Authors: Hiroshige Murata, Yu-ki Iwasaki, Wataru Shimizu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: catheter ablation, myocarditis, ventricular tachycardia

Myocarditis is the inflammatory disease that primarily involves the myocardium and includes a variety of pathological conditions. It develops as a result of an abnormal immune response caused by viral infections, exposure to chemicals including drugs and vaccines, and autoimmunity such as collagen diseases. Although clinical courses are various, the pathological findings are characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration and surrounding cardiomyocyte injury. Therefore, endocardial myocardial biopsy (EMB) is the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and determining the stage of myocarditis.1,2

In acute myocarditis (AM), defined as the acute stage (<30 days after onset) of myocarditis, drug therapy is recommended with mechanical circulatory support for myocardial protection. Treatment strategies for arrhythmias associated with myocarditis have not been established, although sudden cardiac death may occur from ventricular tachyarrhythmias. Particularly the efficacy of catheter ablation (CA) for ventricular tachycardia (VT) associated with myocarditis remains unclear.

Table 1 summarizes the previous and present studies that have evaluated the efficacy and safety of CA for VT associated with myocarditis.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 In the 2010s, the efficacy of CA for VT associated with the chronic stage of myocarditis, so-called “chronic myocarditis,” or postmyocarditis or previous myocarditis (PM) was reported.3, 4, 5, 6 Despite the studies with a small number of cases, they showed that CA may have a relatively favorable therapeutic effect with a VT recurrence rate of 10% to 23%. Based on these favorable outcomes, the 2022 ESC guidelines advocate considering CA for PM patients in whom antiarrhythmic drugs are ineffective or not tolerated for sustained monomorphic VT (Class IIa).11 Although these past reports have demonstrated the efficacy of CA for PM patients, Peretto et al7 have reported that CA was effective in PM but less effective in AM, suggesting that active inflammatory status had a negative impact. Recent studies have evaluated the efficacy of CA in patients with nonischemic cardiomyopathy, including idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, and have shown that CA in patients with postmyocarditis was as effective as or more effective than the other etiologies.8,9 Importantly, the epicardial arrhythmogenic substrate was reported as a therapeutic target for this form of VT; hence, an epicardial approach CA was often required in patients with myocarditis.5,7, 8, 9 Novel mapping systems and epicardial approach methods for CA may have enhanced the therapeutic effect for myocarditis-related VT. Considerable study is needed regarding the timing and strategies for CA in myocarditis.

These findings contribute to our understanding of the role of CA in the management of patients with myocarditis and VT. The 1-year VT recurrence rate in the CA group was 35%, which is higher compared with the average rate in the past studies and identical to that of Peretto et al (Table 1). This discrepancy may be caused by the inclusion of both AM and PM patients in the 2 studies. Indeed, VT recurrence rate was high (63%) in AM patients, whose VT may primarily result from impaired cardiomyocyte electrophysiology caused by persistent inflammation. Conversely, VT is often associated with myocardial scarring in PM patients. Thus, AM patients usually exhibit irregular, polymorphic ventricular arrhythmias, whereas PM patients exhibit regular, monomorphic VTs. This fundamental difference in pathophysiologic mechanism is responsible for the differences in outcomes. Because arrhythmic outcomes after optimal therapy in myocarditis are unpredictable, this study used univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis to identify potential risk factors. The result showed that AM was an independent risk factor for VT recurrence in myocarditis.

The clinical picture of AM resembles that of acute myocardial infarction and often presents chest pain with ST-segment elevation, dyspnea, and/or syncope. It is then associated with heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and fatal arrhythmias, making it a cause of sudden cardiac death. On the other hand, myocarditis after 30 days of onset is defined as PM, but the diagnostic criteria are vague. Pathologically, it is called chronic active myocarditis or chronic inflammatory cardiomyopathy, in which scar formation caused by fibrosis is the primary pathology, although inflammatory cell infiltration is observed. Furthermore, it is called postmyocarditis cardiomyopathy if there is no persistent inflammation in myocardial tissues long after myocarditis.2 These various conditions should require different treatment strategies than those in the acute stage of myocarditis.

The mechanism of myocarditis-related VT may be variable, depending on the stage of the disease.12 There is a phase in which the re-entry circuit of VT is not stable but multifocal and ectopic firing occurs, and the putative mechanism of VT is a triggered activity associated with inflammation. After the acute stage of myocarditis, a stable re-entry circuit, which may be targeted by CA, is gradually formed by the healing of inflammation associated with scar. The inflammation finally disappears and only scar is left with pathological features similar to cardiomyopathy. The heterogeneity of clinical presentation in myocarditis poses an important challenge to its timely diagnosis. EMB is the gold standard for diagnosing myocardial inflammation, but its use is often limited by its invasive nature. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR), which can detect myocardial inflammatory indicators such as edema and fibrosis, has attracted attention as an alternative noninvasive diagnostic method. However, the diagnostic sensitivity of CMR for myocardial inflammation remains less than ideal. In the present study, the sensitivity of CMR was only 63% for diagnosing myocarditis. CA is suggested to be a beneficial strategy for managing VT in drug-refractory myocarditis; optimal patient selection is critical for successful VT ablation in myocarditis. EMB may be recommended when deciding the treatment plan of CA for myocarditis-related VT. On the other hand, the latest CMR may be a noninvasive tool that enables visualization of transmural myocardial damages and/or focal epicardial lesions rather than EMB, which evaluates only the endocardium. Advances in multimodality imaging methods are also expected to effectively treat myocarditis-related VT.

CA in patients with previous biopsy-proven myocarditis is a promising therapeutic option in suppressing the drug-refractory re-entrant VT. It is also important to raise awareness for management of VT in the acute stage of myocarditis. Future trials are needed to clarify the efficacy of CA on patients with myocarditis-related VT.
