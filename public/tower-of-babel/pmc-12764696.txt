Serum miR-493-3p as a diagnostic biomarker and epigenetic regulator targeting DPY30 in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Hong Yang, Yong Xu, Xiaoying Zhang, Yanli Wang, Zhaojian Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most prevalent hematologic malignancy in children. This study aimed to investigate the diagnostic value and functional role of serum microRNA-493-3p (miR-493-3p) in pediatric ALL. Serum miR-493-3p levels were quantified by quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) in103 ALL patients and 85 healthy controls. Bioinformatic tools were used to screen potential target genes of miR-493-3p, and dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed for validation. Functional experiments were conducted by regulating miR-493-3p and its target gene expression to assess changes in leukemic cell malignant phenotypes. Serum miR-493-3p expression was notably downregulated in ALL patients compared with healthy controls (P < 0.001), demonstrating good diagnostic accuracy with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.881. Low miR-493-3p expression was associated with advanced risk stratification (P = 0.001) and poor survival (P < 0.05). Mechanistically, we identified Dpy-30 histone methyltransferase complex regulatory subunit (DPY30) as a direct target of miR-493-3p, showing an inverse correlation in clinical samples (r=-0.755, P < 0.001). Functional experiments revealed that miR-493-3p overexpression suppressed leukemic cell proliferation and invasion (P < 0.001) while promoting apoptosis through targeting DPY30, effects that were partially reversed by DPY30 restoration. Serum miR-493-3p serves as a promising noninvasive biomarker for pediatric ALL diagnosis and prognosis Our findings demonstrate that miR-493-3p functions as a tumor suppressor in ALL by targeting DPY30, suggesting the miR-493-3p/DPY30 axis as a potential therapeutic target for ALL treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06681-8.

Introduction

Pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the leading childhood hematologic malignancy, arises from uncontrolled clonal multiplication of developmentally arrested lymphoid precursors in bone marrow. These abnormal cells not only accumulate excessively in the bone marrow but also disseminate throughout the body via the bloodstream [1, 2]. This process inhibits normal hematopoiesis, leading to anemia, thrombocytopenia, and immunodeficiency. Consequently, life-threatening complications such as infections and bleeding often occur. Although the optimization of chemotherapy protocols and the implementation of stratified treatment have improved the overall survival rate for pediatric ALL to over 80%, 15% to 20% of affected children still face poor outcomes [3, 4]. This is primarily due to disease relapse, drug resistance, or the presence of high-risk biological features. Patients with complex chromosomal abnormalities or residual leukemia stem cells (LSCs) are particularly prone to treatment failure [5, 6]. As a result, there is an urgent need for more precise molecular markers for early diagnosis and prognostic assessment, as well as the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) act as key regulators of gene expression. They modulate post-transcriptional gene expression by binding to target mRNAs, playing critical roles in biological processes such as hematopoietic cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [7, 8]. Increasing evidence has linked miRNA dysregulation to the initiation, progression, and treatment resistance of leukemia, positioning miRNAs as promising targets for diagnosis and therapy [9, 10]. miR-493-3p is a novel miRNA that has gained significant attention in solid tumor research. In various cancers, it inhibits cell proliferation, invasion, and induces apoptosis by targeting specific signaling pathways; for instance, it targets AKT2 in ovarian cancer [11, 12], and suppresses DKK1-mediated activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in gastric cancer [13], it inhibits cell proliferation, invasion, and induces apoptosis by targeting signaling pathways such as AKT2 and Wnt/β-Catenin. Notably, previous studies on Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+) B-ALL have shown that miR-493-3p is specifically underexpressed in LSCs [14]. This finding suggests its potential involvement in maintaining the stem-like properties of leukemic cells. However, the expression pattern of this miRNA in T-ALL and its role in the broader context of ALL remain unclear. Given the shared abnormalities in stem cell regulation across different ALL subtypes, it is hypothesized that miR-493-3p may influence ALL development through conserved molecular pathways.

Against this backdrop, this study aims to decipher the clinical value and molecular mechanisms of miR-493-3p in pediatric ALL through multidimensional strategies. First, we analyze miR-493-3p expression levels in serum from a clinical cohort of pediatric ALL patients to evaluate its diagnostic utility and prognostic correlation. Second, we integrate bioinformatics predictions with in vitro experimental validation to screen key target genes of miR-493-3p in leukemic cells. Finally, functional rescue assays are used to elucidate its regulatory effects on malignant phenotypes. This research endeavors to uncover the molecular mechanisms of miR-493-3p in ALL, providing experimental evidence for developing non-invasive diagnostic methods and targeted therapeutic strategies based on miRNAs.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and grouping

Between February 2018 and October 2020, 103 newly diagnosed pediatric ALL patients were enrolled at Zhoushan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated to Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. Diagnosis was confirmed according to the Pediatric Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia, Version 2.2025, NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology [15]. All cases underwent comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including bone marrow aspiration morphology, immunophenotyping by flow cytometry, cytogenetic analysis (G-banding), and molecular profiling (e.g., PCR-based fusion gene detection). The details are as follows: Immunophenotyping was performed using flow cytometry to detect leukemia cell surface markers. B-cell ALL was defined by positivity for CD19, CD22, or CD79a, along with negativity for myeloid markers (CD13/CD33); T-cell ALL was defined by positivity for CD3, CD7, or CD5. Cytogenetic analysis was carried out using G-banding karyotyping to identify numerical and structural chromosomal abnormalities. An “abnormal karyotype” was defined as any deviation from the normal 46,XX/XY karyotype, including ploidy abnormalities (such as hyperdiploidy or hypodiploidy) and structural abnormalities (such as translocations, e.g., t(9;22)). Molecular testing included reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) for detection of the BCR-ABL fusion gene. Risk stratification was conducted according to the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology, encompassing both low-risk and intermediate-/high-risk patients.

No specific risk-based selection was applied; all consecutive pediatric patients with newly diagnosed ALL who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Therefore, the present cohort is representative of the general pediatric ALL population.

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) Confirmation of ALL per the above multimodal diagnostics; (2) Newly diagnosed cases; (3) Aged 2–12 years; (4) Signed informed consent provided by guardians. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunomodulatory therapy, or surgery before enrollment; (2) History of drug allergy; (3) Concurrent other hematological diseases, major organ dysfunction, malignancies, or autoimmune diseases; (4) Incomplete treatment or withdrawal from the study. Additionally, 85 healthy children undergoing routine physical examinations at Jiangxi Provincial Children’s Hospital during the same period were recruited as the healthy control (HC) group, excluding those with any history of hematological or cardiovascular diseases. The HC group consisted of 45 males and 40 females, aged 2–12 years (mean 6.62 ± 3.12 years). Gender and age distributions showed no significant difference compared to the ALL group (P > 0.05). Approval for the study was obtained from the Zhoushan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated to Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Medical Ethics Committee. A 3-year follow-up for all ALL patients was conducted using telephone contacts, WeChat communication, or outpatient reviews, achieving a 100% follow-up rate. Survival time was defined as the interval from diagnosis to the follow-up cutoff date or death.

Sample collection and processing

During the clinical evaluation and diagnostic workup for pediatric ALL, complementary samples were collected from each patient to enable comprehensive clinical and molecular characterization. A 5 mL of bone marrow aspirate (heparin-anticoagulated) was obtained for the following analyses: morphological examination to determine bone marrow blast percentage, immunophenotyping by multiparameter flow cytometry to distinguish T-ALL and B-ALL subtypes, karyotype analysis using R-banding techniques to detect chromosomal abnormalities, and BCR-ABL fusion gene detection via RT-PCR.

Simultaneously, 3 mL of peripheral venous blood was drawn from the same patients. This sample was used for two distinct purposes: one portion was analyzed for complete blood count (including white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin level, and platelet count) using an automated hematology analyzer (DP-H10, Shenzhen Dimai Biotechnology); the remaining blood was processed for serum isolation.

For serum preparation, blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 3000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C using a swing-out rotor. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for subsequent total RNA extraction. The extracted RNA was used for quantification of miR-493-3p and Dpy-30 histone methyltransferase complex regulatory subunit (DPY30) expression levels. For healthy controls, venous blood collected during routine physical examinations was processed under identical conditions, and the serum was stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction and subsequent miR-493-3p and DPY30 analysis.

Bioinformatic prediction and selection of target genes

Potential target genes of miR-493-3p were predicted using four independent algorithms: TargetScan Human (version 7.2, https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/), miRDB (https://mirdb.org/), miRWalk (version 3.0, http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/), and StarBase (version 3.0, https://rnasysu.com/encori/panCancer.php). To ensure prediction reliability, stringent cutoff criteria were applied for each database: genes with a Target Score ≥ 60 in miRDB; a |Total context + + score| ≥ 0.4 in TargetScan; a TDMDScore ≥ 1 in StarBase; and a binding probability ≥ 1 in miRWalk were considered high-confidence candidates. The intersection of predictions from all four databases was visualized using a Venn diagram. This cross-database screening identified a single common gene, DPY30. Given its unique status as the only gene predicted by all four algorithms under stringent parameters and its established role as a core component of epigenetic regulatory complexes, DPY30 was selected for further experimental validation.

Cell culture

Cell lines were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., including the non-cancerous bone marrow stromal cell line HS-5 (control), B-ALL cell lines (Reh, RS4;11, NALM-6), and the T-ALL cell line Molt-4. Primary human CD19 + B cells and CD3 + T cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs, ATCC) of healthy donors using the EasySep™ Human B Cell Isolation Kit and EasySep™ Human T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Canada), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were thawed and cultured in an RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA) that was enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL of penicillin. The cultures were then kept at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. The medium was changed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged upon reaching 50–80% confluence.

Cell transfection and construction of pcDNA3.1-DPY30 plasmid

The fourth passage of NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells—both cultured to the exponential growth phase as described above— were separately seeded into 24-well plates. Each cell line was divided into 5 groups for subsequent processing: one non-treated blank control group, and four groups designated for transfection treatment. Plasmid construction and transfection protocols were adapted from established methods in the literature [16]. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine® 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol: briefly, the reagent was diluted in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA) and incubated with nucleic acids (mimics or plasmids) for 5–10 min at room temperature to form transfection complexes, which were then added dropwise to the cell cultures. The four transfection groups were as follows: (1) miR-NC group: Transfected with negative control mimic (50 nM, RiboBio, China); (2) miR-mimic group: Transfected with miR-493-3p mimic (50 nM, RiboBio, China); (3) miR-mimic + oe-NC group: Co-transfected with miR-493-3p mimic (50 nM) + empty pcDNA3.1 vector (1 µg/well, Invitrogen, USA, as vector control); (4) miR-mimic + oe-DPY30 group: Co-transfected with miR-493-3p mimic (50 nM) + pcDNA3.1-DPY30 overexpression plasmid (1 µg/well, self-constructed).

Construction of pcDNA3.1-DPY30 plasmid: The full-length coding sequence of human DPY30 (GenBank: NM_001321209.2) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers designed with EcoRI and XhoI restriction sites. PCR products were double-digested with EcoRI and XhoI (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 2 h, then ligated into the EcoRI/XhoI-digested pcDNA3.1 vector using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 16 °C overnight. The recombinant plasmid was verified by Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology, China) to confirm no base mutations or reading frame shifts.

Transfection efficiency was validated at both mRNA and protein levels. t 48 h post-transfection, transfection efficiency was validated by qRT-PCR to assess miR-493-3p and DPY30 mRNA expression levels, and Western blot analysis to confirm DPY30 protein expression, ensuring successful overexpression or inhibition in the respective groups.

Detection of miR-493-3p and DPY30 mRNA expression

To quantify the expression levels of miR-493-3p and its target gene DPY30 mRNA, quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on serum samples (from ALL patients and HC) and cell samples (including primary cells, HS-5, ALL cell lines and these cell lines after specific treatments: miR-493-3p mimic transfection or oe-DPY30 transfection). Expression levels of miR-493-3p and target gene mRNA in serum and cells were quantified using qRT-PCR. Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR procedures were performed as described in previous reports [17].Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Beijing Pufei Biotechnology). cDNA was synthesized using specialized reverse transcription kit from Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). For miR-493-3p, a miRNA-specific kit with stem-loop primers was used. For DPY30 mRNA, a universal kit with oligo(dT) primers was employed. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) on a real-time PCR system. U6 small nuclear RNA served as the internal control for miR-493-3p, and GAPDH used for DPY30 mRNA. All primers were supplied by Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology (China), and their sequences are listed in Table S1. Each 20 µL reaction contained SYBR Green Master Mix, specific primers, cDNA template, and RNase-free water. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The 2⁻ΔΔCt method was employed to calculate relative expression levels.

Western blot (WB) validation of DPY30 protein expression

Protein expression of DPY30 was evaluated by western blotting 48 h after transfection. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China) using bovine serum albumin as a standard. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against DPY30 (1:1000, Abcam, USA) and GAPDH (1:5000, Abcam, USA). Following washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000, Abcam, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent (Millipore, USA), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (v1.8.0). Relative expression of DPY30 was normalized to GAPDH.

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

Referring to established protocols for validating miRNA-target interactions in previous studies, this study employed the dual-luciferase reporter assay system [18]. DNA fragments containing either the wild-type (WT) binding site or a mutated (MUT) sequence within the DPY30 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) were amplified. These fragments were cloned into the pGL3 dual-luciferase reporter vector. NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells were co-transfected with either the WT-DPY30 or MUT-DPY30 reporter plasmid, along with either: miR-493-3p mimic or mimic-NC (negative control mimic), or miR-493-3p inhibitor or inhibitor-NC (negative control inhibitor). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the activities of Firefly and Renilla luciferases were determined using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system.

Cell proliferation assay

The assay was performed in NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells under five experimental conditions: blank control, miR-NC, miR-mimic, miR-mimic + oe-NC, and miR-mimic + oe-DPY30. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was conducted according to methods described in previous studies [19]. Treated cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10³ cells per well. After allowing the cells to adhere, the following volumes of CCK-8 reagent were dropped into each well: 0 h, 10 µL; 24 h, 10 µL; 48 h, 10 µL; 72 h, 10 µL. The plates were then placed in an incubator at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm by a microplate reader (Multiskan GO, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Cleaved Caspase-3 activity assay

This assay was conducted in both NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells across all five experimental groups. The activity of the cleaved caspase-3 enzyme was evaluated using a colorimetric assay. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were collected, then washed with PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min using a commercial RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitors. The lysates were then subjected to centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was harvested. Fifty microliters of cell lysate and 50 µL of reaction buffer containing the DEVD-pNA substrate (specific for caspase-3) were added to a 96-well plate according to kit instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, absorbance was read at 405 nm. Enzyme activity (Units per mg of protein) was calculated using a standard curve.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis Assay was performed in transfected NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells (including all treatment groups: miR-mimic, miR-NC, miR-mimic + oe-DPY30, miR-mimic + oe-NC) and untransfected blank control NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells. These cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and culture medium was removed before trypsinization. Cell staining employed an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, USA). Following 15-minute incubation with reagents in dark conditions, apoptotic rates were quantified via BD FACSCalibur flow cytometry.

Cell invasion assay

Cell invasion was assessed in NALM-6 and RS4;11 cells representing all five experimental conditions. Transwell inserts pre-coated with Matrigel were seeded with 150 µL serum-free suspensions (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) of NALM-6 or RS4;11 cells. The lower chamber was filled with 10% FBS-supplemented medium. Following 24-hour incubation at 37 °C, membranes were processed by swab removal of non-invaded cells, methanol fixation (30 min), and crystal violet staining (0.1%, 30 min). Dry membranes were microscopically examined at high magnification for invaded cell counts.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0). Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). Differences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. Differences among multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical data were expressed as counts and compared using the Chi-square (χ²) test. The diagnostic value of serum miR-493-3p levels was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical significance of miR-493-3p in ALL patients

This study enrolled 85 HC and 103 ALL patients. Evaluation of serum specimens showed a significant decrease in miR-493-3p expression in ALL patients relative to HC (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A). ROC curve analysis confirmed that this molecule had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.881 (95% CI: 0.859–0.935) for distinguishing ALL from HC (Fig. 1B). After grouping patients based on the median expression value, the low miR-493-3p expression group showed significant differences from the high expression group in risk stratification (P = 0.001), bone marrow blast proportion (P = 0.003), karyotype (P = 0.035), platelet count (P = 0.012), and white blood cell count (P < 0.001) (Table 1), while no statistical differences were observed in gender, immunophenotype, BCR-ABL fusion gene, or hemoglobin level. Notably, it was found that there was no significant difference in serum miR-493-3p levels between patients with B-cell and T-cell leukemia subtypes (Fig. S1, P = 0.875). Survival analysis further indicated that the overall survival rate of the low expression group was significantly worse than that of the high expression group (Fig. 2A). Dysregulated expression of miR-493-3p and DPY30 in ALL: clinical and cellular validation

Cross-screening of four databases identified DPY30 as a potential target gene of miR-493-3p (Fig. 3A). Clinical sample validation showed that DPY30 expression was significantly higher in ALL patients than in healthy controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B), and its expression level was strongly negatively correlated with miR-493-3p (r = −0.755, P < 0.001, Fig. 3C). Compared with HS-5 cells, miR-493-3p was universally underexpressed in B-ALL cell lines (Reh, RS4;11, NALM-6) and T-ALL cell line (MOLT-4) (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis showed the lowest miR-493-3p expression in RS4;11 cells (0.26 ± 0.05), followed by NALM-6 (0.27 ± 0.04), with relatively higher levels in Reh (0.55 ± 0.03) and MOLT-4 (0.58 ± 0.05). Conversely, DPY30 was significantly overexpressed in these leukemia cell lines (Fig. 4B), with the highest levels in RS4;11 (1.81 ± 0.07) and NALM-6 (1.75 ± 0.06), followed by Reh (1.45 ± 0.07) and MOLT-4 (1.36 ± 0.03). Although formal Pearson correlation analysis was not performed due to the small number of cell lines and potential technical variability, a clear inverse trend was observed: cell lines with lower miR-493-3p (RS4;11, NALM-6) exhibited higher DPY30 expression, while those with relatively higher miR-493-3p (MOLT-4, Reh) showed lower DPY30 levels. To further validate the observed dysregulation within the appropriate lymphoid context, we compared expression in ALL cell lines to normal primary lymphocytes. As shown in Figure S2, compared with primary healthy B cells, miR-493-3p was significantly underexpressed in all B-ALL cell lines (Reh, RS4;11, NALM-6) (P < 0.001, Fig. S2A). Similarly, its expression was markedly lower in the T-ALL cell line (MOLT-4) than in primary healthy T cells (P < 0.001) (Fig. S2B). Conversely, DPY30 was significantly overexpressed in all leukemia cell lines compared to their respective primary cell counterparts (P < 0.001) (Fig. S2C-D).

miR-493-3p directly binds to the 3’UTR of DPY30 and regulates its expression

For the dual-luciferase reporter assay, we selected the NALM-6 and RS4;11 cell lines as they represent two distinct, high-risk molecular subtypes of B-ALL and exhibited the most pronounced dysregulation of the miR-493-3p/DPY30 axis, making them ideal models for validating the direct targeting mechanism. Mechanistically, dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that miR-493-3p specifically inhibited the activity of the wild-type 3’-UTR of DPY30 (NALM-6: P < 0.01; RS4;11: P < 0.001) but had no effect on the mutant type (Fig. 4C-D). As shown in Fig. 4E, transfection with miR-493-3p mimic significantly increased miR-493-3p expression in RS4;11 and NALM-6 cells, while overexpression of DPY30 (achieved by transfection with a DPY30 expression vector) did not significantly alter miR-493-3p levels. Figure 4F demonstrates that miR-493-3p mimic markedly suppressed DPY30 mRNA expression in both cell lines. Transfection with the DPY30 overexpression plasmid effectively increased DPY30 mRNA levels and partially reversed the suppression induced by miR-493-3p mimic. Consistent with these findings, western blot analysis confirmed that miR-493-3p mimic downregulated DPY30 protein expression, whereas transfection with the DPY30 overexpression vector successfully restored DPY30 protein levels (Fig. S3).

miR-493-3p suppresses malignant phenotypes by regulating DPY30

Overexpression of miR-493-3p significantly inhibited cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 5A-B) and invasion ability (P < 0.001, Fig. 5C), while activating the apoptotic pathway, as evidenced by increased Cleaved caspase-3 activity (P < 0.001, Fig. 5D) and flow cytometry-determined apoptosis rate (P < 0.001, Fig. 5E). In functional rescue experiments, co-transfection with oe-DPY30 partially abolished the inhibitory effect of miR-493-3p overexpression on cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 5A-B). Similar trends were observed in invasion assays (Fig. 5C). Discussion

ALL is characterized by clonal proliferation of abnormal lymphoid precursor cells in the bone marrow, leading to disrupted normal hematopoiesis. As key regulators of gene expression, miRNAs play pivotal roles in hematopoietic cell differentiation and leukemogenesis [20, 21]. This study demonstrates that miR-493-3p is significantly downregulated in pediatric ALL patients and exhibits a distinctive correlation pattern. The inverse correlation between miR-493-3p levels and key clinical parameters (Table 1) also has important biological and prognostic implications. First, lower miR-493-3p was associated with higher bone marrow blasts and more severe leukocytosis—these are hallmarks of increased tumor burden and disease aggressiveness, suggesting miR-493-3p may act as a tumor suppressor: its downregulation could fail to inhibit the clonal expansion of leukemic cells, leading to elevated blast counts and WBC numbers [22]. Second, the significant reduction in platelet count in the low miR-493-3p group likely reflects impaired bone marrow hematopoiesis: excessive proliferation of leukemic blasts crowds out normal megakaryocytes (the precursor cells of platelets), reducing platelet production [23, 24]. This inverse association may indirectly indicate the degree of bone marrow infiltration by leukemic cells, with lower miR-493-3p and platelet counts potentially correlating with poorer treatment response. Third, the link between higher miR-493-3p and more low-risk cases (P = 0.001) or normal karyotypes (P = 0.035) further reinforces its value as a prognostic correlate of milder disease severity. Regarding BCR-ABL status, our data showed a trend toward higher positivity in the low miR-493-3p expression group (13/55, 23.6% vs. 5/48, 10.4%; P = 0.078), approaching but not reaching statistical significance. The near-significant difference raises the possibility that expanding the sample size could enhance statistical power to confirm this association—however, this remains speculative and requires validation in larger, multi-center cohorts.

Notably, unlike previous reports focused exclusively on B-ALL [14], we observed consistent downregulation of miR-493-3p across both major immunophenotypic subtypes (B-ALL and T-ALL), with no significant difference between in expression levels between them (P = 0.875). This pan-subtype expression pattern suggests that miR-493-3p may function as a broad-spectrum biomarker in pediatric ALL, potentially complementing existing markers particularly in T-ALL cases or those lacking established cytogenetic abnormalities.

From a translational perspective, serum miR-493-3p demonstrated considerable diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.881). While it cannot replace bone marrow examination—which remains essential for definitive diagnosis, immunophenotyping, and cytogenetic analysis [25, 26], it may serve as a valuable non-invasive adjunct for dynamic monitoring and long-term follow-up, especially in pediatric patients where repeated invasive procedures are challenging. Nonetheless, future studies are warranted to validate its utility in conjunction with conventional biomarkers and to explore its specific biological mechanisms.

The tumor-suppressive function of miR-493-3p has been well-characterized in solid tumors, where it targets distinct pathways across cancer types—such as being sponged by LINC01605 in laryngeal carcinoma [27], inducing apoptosis via AKT2/E2F5 in ovarian cancer [11], and suppressing Wnt/β-catenin signaling through DKK1 in gastric cancer [28]. While miRNAs are widely recognized as key regulators in leukemia—modulating pathogenesis, drug response, and prognosis in both AML and ALL, as highlighted in recent reviews [29–31]—the role of miR-493-3p in ALL remains poorly understood. This study provides the first investigation of miR-493-3p’s role in pediatric ALL, demonstrating that it directly targets DPY30 and functionally inhibits leukemic cell proliferation and invasion while promoting apoptosis. Our dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed direct binding to the 3’UTR of DPY30 and the strong negative correlation (r=−0.755) between serum miR-493-3p and DPY30 levels in patients further supports its clinical relevance. This is consistent with DPY30 regulatory mechanisms in ovarian cancer [32], where DPY30 reconstitution reverses the tumor-suppressive effects of miR-493-3p in both cancer types, suggesting cross-cancer conservation of this axis. As an integral component of the SET1/MLL histone methyltransferase complex, DPY30 facilitates H3K4me3 deposition at promoter regions of key oncogenes [33]. In the context of MLL-rearranged leukemia, DPY30 is critical for maintaining open chromatin states and sustaining the expression of leukemogenic genes such as HOXA9, MEIS1 [34]. This epigenetic function promotes self-renewal and impedes differentiation of leukemic stem cells, thereby contributing to blast accumulation and disease progression—helping to explain the correlation between high DPY30 levels (low miR-493-3p) and increased blast percentage observed in our cohort. The strong inverse correlation between serum miR-493-3p and DPY30 expression (r = − 0.755) further supports its clinical relevance. Notably, the most pronounced suppression of miR-493-3p and concurrent elevation of DPY30 were observed in RS4;11 and NALM-6 cell lines, which represent high-risk genetic subtypes of B-ALL (MLL-AF4 rearrangement and hyperdiploidy/TP53 alteration, respectively), suggesting that epigenetic silencing of miR-493-3p may be linked to aggressive molecular subtypes.

The consistent downregulation of miR-493-3p across ALL subtypes and its strong association with established high-risk features support its potential utility as an adjunct biomarker for refining risk stratification systems. For instance, integrating serum miR-493-3p levels with existing clinical and genetic markers could improve early identification of high-risk patients who might benefit from treatment intensification or novel therapies [35]. Moreover, the miR-493-3p/DPY30 axis represents a promising therapeutic target, especially in high-risk subgroups such as MLL-rearranged leukemia. Targeting this axis—using synthetic miR-493-3p mimics or small molecule inhibitors disrupting DPY30–MLL interactions—may help restore normal epigenetic regulation and reverse treatment resistance, consistent with emerging efforts to target epigenetic regulators in leukemia [29–31].

While these implications are encouraging, certain limitations of our study should be considered when evaluating clinical translatability. The absence of molecular subtyping for entities such as Ph-like ALL may obscure subtype-specific regulatory patterns of miR-493-3p. Furthermore, the precise apoptotic network downstream of DPY30-mediated H3K4me3 remains incompletely elucidated. Finally, in vivo validation is necessary to substantiate the therapeutic relevance of this axis. Future studies should prioritize evaluating miR-493-3p mimics in patient-derived xenograft models, particularly within defined genetic subtypes, and explore targeted delivery systems to improve specificity and efficacy of potential miRNA-based therapies.

In conclusion, this study first confirms pan-subtype downregulation of miR-493-3p in pediatric ALL, which inhibits malignant phenotypes of leukemic cells by targeting the epigenetic regulator DPY30, providing new molecular targets for precision diagnosis and treatment of pediatric ALL.
