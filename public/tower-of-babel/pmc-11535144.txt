Unraveling the role of heavy metals xenobiotics in cancer: a critical review
Authors: Sourav Pal, Sayed Mohammed Firdous
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer is a multifaceted disease characterized by the gradual accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations within cells, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and invasive behavior. The intricate interplay between environmental factors, such as exposure to carcinogens, and the molecular cascades governing cell growth, differentiation, and survival contributes to cancer’s development and progression. This review offers a comprehensive overview of key molecular targets and their roles in cancer development. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors are implicated in various cancers due to their role in regulating lipid metabolism, inflammation, and cell proliferation. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 protects cells from oxidative damage but can also promote tumor cell survival. Cytochrome P450 1B1 metabolizes exogenous and endogenous substances, and its increased expression is observed in several cancers. The constitutive androstane receptor regulates gene expression, and its dysregulation can lead to liver cancer. Transforming growth factor-beta 2 is involved in the development and progression of various cancers by dysregulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Chelation treatment has been investigated for removing heavy metals, while genetically altered immune cells show promise in treating specific cancers. Metal–organic frameworks and fibronectin targeting represent new directions in cancer treatment. While some heavy metals, such as arsenic, chromium, nickel, and cadmium, are known to have carcinogenic properties, others, like zinc, Copper, gold, bismuth, and silver, have many uses that highlight their potential as effective cancer control tactics. Introduction

Cancer is a complicated disease characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation and tissue infection [1]. Researchers have discovered many molecular mechanisms and signaling cascades that cause cancer in recent years. This has revealed biomarkers and key molecular players in cancer formation. Cancer is a significant worldwide public health issue, with a considerable influence on mortality rates. In the year 2020, cancer accounted for about 10 million deaths, representing approximately one-sixth of the total documented fatalities [2]. According to available data, the global mortality rate attributed to diverse types of cancer was around 9.6 million individuals in the year 2017 [3]. During the period of 2013–2017, the cancer mortality rate, which is determined by the number of cancer-related deaths per 100,000 individuals yearly, was recorded as 158.3 for both males and females [4]. In 2020, the prevalent types of cancer in terms of incidence encompassed breast cancer, lung cancer, colon and rectal cancer, prostate cancer, non-melanoma skin cancer, and stomach cancer [5]. In 2024, the American Cancer Society projects approximately 2,001,140 new cancer cases and 611,720 cancer deaths in the United States [6]. Research conducted in the United States, Bangladesh, Chile, Argentina, and Taiwan revealed that the consumption of water contaminated to a maximum of 10 μg/L arsenic resulted in an additional 4.51 cases of lung cancer per 100,000 people [7]. Globally, inorganic arsenic in food is responsible for 9129 to 119,176 cases of bladder cancer, 11,844 to 121,442 cases of lung cancer, and 10,729 to 110,015 cases of skin cancer, according to the study [8]. A prospective mortality study of INCO’s Ontario workforce from 1950 to 1976 centered on the extraction and refining of nickel and Cu. The exposure of 54,000 males to industrial heavy metals poses significant long-term health risks, as evidenced by the 5283 fatalities [9].

Xenobiotics refer to exogenous substances that infiltrate the human body as a result of contact with many environmental origins, including but not limited to pollutants, poisons, and toxins [10]. Certain xenobiotics have been associated with the development of cancer, and their modes of action entail interactions with distinct biological cascades. Several important cascades are involved in cellular processes, including Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), Cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily B member 1 (CYP1B1), Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR), Tumor growth factor β (TGF-β), and Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) [11]. The induction of oxidative assault by xenobiotics is a crucial factor in the development of cancer. Several types of heavy metals, including arsenic, chromium, nickel, and cadmium, together with substances like aflatoxin, have been found to play a role in causing oxidative damage, DNA alterations, and the development of tumors. Various forms of cancer, such as skin cancer, lung cancer, urethral carcinoma, kidney carcinoma, and gastric cancer, have been linked to exposure to xenobiotics [12].

The comprehension of the molecular mechanisms that underlie the initiation and advancement of cancer holds notable significance, as it provides opportunities for the implementation of preventative measures and the development of groundbreaking therapeutic approaches. In response to the detrimental impacts of xenobiotics, various therapeutic approaches have been developed, including Chelation therapy [13], CAR-T Cell Therapy [14], immunotherapy utilizing genetically modified T cells to selectively eliminate cancer cells, and the MOFs Technique [15]. The therapeutic applications of Fibronectin, including medication transport and the suppression of cell adhesion, exhibit significant potential for the field of cancer therapy. Hence, this review focuses on the molecular mechanism implicated in the development of cancer produced by heavy metals, as well as the current techniques employed to mitigate their harmful effects.

Xenobiotics in cancer promotion

Heavy metals are well recognized as one of the most perilous categories of environmental xenobiotics [16]. Heavy metals have a high level of resistance to environmental degradation and have the ability to accumulate within living organisms. This accumulation can result in the generation of free radicals and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to toxicity in both animals and humans. Elevated quantities of heavy metals, including Cu, zinc, lead, cadmium, and chromium, have been detected in the soil of agricultural areas [17]. The presence of heavy metal pollution in terrestrial ecosystems has the potential to directly impact plants by offering an elemental defense mechanism. Identifying molecular biomarkers that are targeted by xenobiotics can provide insight into the mechanisms underlying their carcinogenic effects. One example of a xenobiotic target molecular biomarker in cancer is the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [18]. Heavy metals, such as arsenite, can induce the activation of the AhR, resulting in its translocation into the nucleus and subsequent binding to the promoter region of the Cyp1a1 gene [19]. This activation may be attributed to the potential disruption of the connection between AhR and heat shock protein 90 (HSP90). Furthermore, it has been documented that the molecular chaperone HSP90 is capable of activating AhR. The activation of the AhR by heavy metal xenobiotics is mediated through either direct binding to AhR or by interfering with the interaction between AhR and HSP90 [20]. This elucidates the intricate relationship between environmental contaminants and cellular regulatory mechanisms. Abnormal expression and activation of the AhR have been observed in a range of malignancies, encompassing leukemia, lymphoma, glioblastoma, ovarian, lung, liver, and head and neck cancers [21]. The inhibition of AhR represents a potentially fruitful approach for cancer treatment, as it has been shown to enhance the sensitivity of tumors to chemotherapy, as seen by the effects observed with doxorubicin [22].

Xenobiotics such as benzo[a]pyrene can directly cause DNA assault, which triggers the activation of checkpoint kinases and DNA repair cascades. However, if the impairment is too severe, it can lead to genetic mutations and chromosomal abnormalities that promote cancer development [23]. Finally, the epigenetic machinery is also a target for xenobiotics in cancer development [24]. Xenobiotics such as bisphenol A and phthalates can alter DNA methylation and histone modification patterns, which can result in the dysregulation of gene expression and the promotion of cancer development [25].

Molecular cascades of xenobiotics-induced cancer

Xenobiotics have been shown to cause cancer by interfering with normal cellular processes. The activation of receptors like PPARs, aAhR, and CAR are part of the molecular processes that are implicated in xenobiotic-induced cancer [26]. These receptors can increase tumor growth by stimulating cell proliferation. Cyclin D1 and other genes involved in cell cycle progression are upregulated in response to PPAR activation. By stimulating the aAhR cascade, CYP1B1 aids tumor development [27]. Chemotherapy medications may be metabolized and eliminated more quickly if CAR is used to stimulate the production of drug-metabolizing enzymes like cytochrome P450 and uridine nucleotide transferases [28].

PPARs

PPARs are a group of nuclear receptors that have significant regulatory functions in the biotransformation of lipids and glucose. However, recent studies have also implicated PPARs in the pathogenesis of cancer, particularly in tumor progression [29]. The molecular mechanism underlying PPAR-mediated tumor progression involves several key processes. One mechanism by which PPARs promote tumor progression is through the expression of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) cascade. EMT refers to the phenotypic transformation of epithelial cells, wherein they acquire mesenchymal traits such as enhanced motility and invasiveness. The activation of PPAR has been observed to initiate the process of EMT through the regulation of gene expression related to cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cytoskeletal architecture [30].

PPARs can also promote tumor progression by regulating angiogenesis. Angiogenesis is a critical process for tumor growth and metastasis, as it supplies the tumor with nutrients and oxygen. By increasing the synthesis of VEGF and other substances that encourage angiogenesis, PPAR has been proven to induce vascular development [29]. Another mechanism by which PPARs promote tumor progression is through modulation of the immune response. PPARs possess the ability to modulate the transcriptional activity of genes associated with the activation and differentiation of immune cells, including T-helper 1 (Th1) and T-helper 2 (Th2) cytokines [31]. PPARs possess the ability to modulate the production of anti-apoptotic genes, including Bcl-2, hence exerting regulatory control over cell survival [32]. Finally, PPARs can also regulate tumor progression by modulating the activity of other signaling cascades, such as the Wnt/β-catenin cascade. As an illustration, it has been observed that di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate exhibits PPAR activation properties in rodent models, but such effects have not been observed in human cell lines [33].

CYP1B1

It is a member of the cytochrome P450 superfamily of enzymes and plays a crucial role in the biotransformation of xenobiotics and endogenous compounds. Overexpressed CYP1B1 has been linked to tumor progression in several investigations conducted in recent years. The molecular mechanism underlying CYP1B1-mediated tumor progression involves several key processes [34]. One mechanism by which CYP1B1 promotes tumor progression is through the activation of the aAhR cascade. CYP1B1 can metabolize PAHs found in tobacco smoke and other environmental pollutants to generate reactive intermediates that can activate the AhR cascade. AhR cascade has been associated with various cellular processes that are implicated in the growth of tumors. These processes include enhanced cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and immunological suppression [35].

CYP1B1 can also promote tumor progression by regulating the metabolism of steroid hormones, such as estrogens. CYP1B1 can metabolize estradiol to generate 4-hydroxyestradiol, which can promote cell proliferation and angiogenesis by the expression of PI3K/AKT signaling cascades. CYP1B1 can also metabolize androgen to generate metabolites that activate the androgen receptor, which plays a crucial role in prostate cancer progression [36]. Another mechanism by which CYP1B1 promotes tumor progression is through the regulation of the cell cycle. CYP1B1 can regulate the expression of genes involved in cell cycle progression, such as cyclin D1, cyclin E, and CDK2, leading to increased cell proliferation [37]. CYP1B1 can also inhibit apoptosis by regulating the expression of anti-apoptotic genes, such as Bcl-2, and surviving. The expression of genes associated with the Wnt/β-catenin cascade can be regulated by CYP1B1, resulting in enhanced tumor development and metastasis. Finally, CYP1B1 can promote tumor progression by regulating the immune response (Fig. 1). CYP1B1 can modulate the activity of immune cells, such as dendritic cells and T-cells, by regulating their cytokine secretion and proliferation [38].

CYP1B1-mediated tumor progression. CYP1B1 facilitates the metabolic transformation of PAHs, resulting in the production of metabolites that have affinity for the AhR. The process of phosphorylation of AhR has been observed to result in immunological suppression, upregulation of growth factor receptors, and production of oxidative assault. These effects contribute to the promotion of angiogenesis and cell proliferation. Furthermore, it has been observed that CYP1B1 plays a crucial role in the metabolic conversion of estradiol to 4-hydroxyestradiol, which then triggers various signaling cascades such as PI3K, Akt, and mTOR. These signaling cascades are known to facilitate processes like angiogenesis and cell proliferation

CAR

CAR, alternatively referred to as NR1I3, is a constituent of the nuclear receptor superfamily, which encompasses transcription factors responsible for modulating the expression of genes implicated in drug metabolism and the maintenance of energy balance [39]. Recent research findings have indicated that CAR executes a significant role in the development of cancer, specifically in facilitating tumor proliferation and conferring resistance to chemotherapy treatments. The molecular mechanism underlying CAR-mediated tumor progression involves several key processes. One mechanism by which CAR promotes tumor progression is through the regulation of drug metabolism. CAR can activate the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes, such as CYPs and UGTs, leading to increased metabolism and elimination of chemotherapeutic drugs. This can result in reduced efficacy of chemotherapy and the development of drug resistance [40].

The role of CAR in tumor growth can be facilitated through its regulation of endogenous chemical metabolism. As an illustration, the CAR can induce the transcription of genes associated with the metabolic cascades of bile acids. These molecules have been linked to the etiology and advancement of colon and liver malignancies [41]. CAR can also regulate the biotransformation of fatty acids and glucose, which are important for energy metabolism in cancer cells. Another mechanism by which CAR promotes tumor progression is through the regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis. CAR can activate the expression of genes involved in cell cycle progressions, such as cyclin D1 and CDK4, leading to increased cell proliferation [42]. By upregulating anti-apoptotic genes like Bcl-2 and Bcl-Xl levels of anti-apoptotic genes like Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, CAR can also prevent cells from committing suicide. CAR can also promote tumor progression by modulating the activity of other signaling cascades. For instance, CAR can trigger cell proliferation and survival-regulating the Wnt/-catenin cascade. The Akt/mTOR cascade, which controls cell growth and metabolism, can be activated by CAR as well [43]. CAR has the ability to modulate the immune response within the tumor microenvironment. It can induce the transcriptional activation of genes associated with the recruitment and differentiation processes of immune cells, including T-cells and natural killer cells [44].

TGF-β

TGF-β is a cytokine that plays a crucial role in the regulation of cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. Dysregulation of TGF-β2 signaling has been implicated in the development and progression of cancer [45]. In this portion, we will discuss the molecular mechanism of TGF-β2-mediated SAB-β-Gal and SM22 in tumor progression in the pathogenesis of cancer. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) serves as a biomarker for cellular senescence, a phenomenon characterized by permanent growth cessation in response to diverse assault stimuli [46]. The induction of senescence in both normal and cancer cells has been demonstrated through the overexpression of CDKIs, p16INK4a, and p21CIP1/WAF1 by TGF-β [47]. In addition, TGF-β2 can induce the expression of SA-β-Gal by upregulating lysosomal biogenesis and secretion [48]. Senescence induced by TGF-β2 can have both tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting effects, depending on the context and timing of TGF-β2 signaling. SM22 is a marker of smooth muscle cells, which are important components of blood vessels and other tissues. TGF-β2 has been shown to promote the differentiation of smooth muscle cells and the formation of blood vessels [49].

In addition, TGF-β2 can elicit the upregulation of SM22 gene expression in several cellular populations, including malignant cells. The involvement of SM22 has been identified in the modulation of cellular migration and invasion, as well as in the pathogenesis and advancement of diverse malignancies, such as breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers [50]. The molecular mechanism underlying TGF-β2-mediated SAB-β-Gal and SM22 in tumor progression involves several key processes (Fig. 2). First, TGF-β can express the Smad signaling cascade, which involves the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of Smad2/3. Subsequently, Smad2/3 can engage with Smad4 and form complexes that can specifically attach to DNA sequences located inside the promoter regions of target genes. These target genes encompass CDKIs and SM22. Second, TGF-β can activate non-Smad signalling cascades, such as the MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and JNK cascades, which can also regulate the expression of CDKIs and SM22. Third, TGF-β can induce the expression of microRNAs, which can target and regulate the expression of CDKIs and SM22 [51].

TGF-β2-mediated CDKIs and SM22 regulation in tumor progression. The binding of activated TGF-β to its receptor TβR-I triggers a series of phosphorylation processes that involve SMAD2 and SMAD3. This leads to the creation of the R-SMADs-co-SMAD complex, which includes SMAD4. The activation of this intricate mechanism results in the upregulation of CDKIs and SM22. Simultaneously, the signaling molecules Wnt, FGF, Notch ligands, and TGF-β initiate the activation of PI3K, Ras, and MMPs, thereby initiating a cascade of events involving Akt, P38, MAPK, Raf, MEK, and ERK1/2. Ultimately, this signaling cascade leads to the production of CDKIs and SM22. miRNAs that bind to TβR-II have been found to enhance the expression of CDKI and SM22 through the action of miR-200 and miR-211. This mechanism contributes to the promotion of cell proliferation, chemoresistance, and the formation and progression of cancer

In tumor cells, TGF-β facilitates proliferation by activating growth and survival pathways within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [52]. Additionally, TGF-β disrupts tight junctions that are essential for maintaining epithelial integrity, thereby promoting EMT. During EMT, epithelial cells lose their polarity and adhesion characteristics, acquiring migratory and invasive properties associated with mesenchymal cells [53]. This transition is crucial for cancer metastasis, as it enables cells to detach from the primary tumor, invade adjacent tissues, and penetrate the extracellular matrix. These alterations significantly enhance cancer cell motility and invasion, thereby promoting cancer progression.

Nrf2

The transcription factor known as Nrf2 is of significant importance in the control of cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative and xenobiotic assaults. Dysregulation of Nrf2 signaling has been implicated in the development and progression of cancer. In this portion, we will discuss the molecular mechanism of Nrf2-mediated tumor progression in the pathogenesis of cancer. Nrf2 exerts a crucial role in the regulation of several genes associated with the antioxidant response, detoxification processes, and anti-inflammatory cascades [54]. In typical physiological circumstances, the activity of Nrf2 is suppressed by its cytosolic inhibitor, known as Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), which promotes the destruction of Nrf2 via the ubiquitin–proteasome system. Upon encountering oxidative assault or exposure to electrophilic chemicals, the Nrf2 undergoes dissociation from Keap1 and subsequently relocates to the nucleus. Within the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to specific areas known as antioxidant response elements (AREs) located in the promoter regions of target genes. Consequently, leads to the expression of transcription for these target genes [55].

In cancer, Nrf2 can be overexpressed or constitutively activated, leading to the upregulation of genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, and drug resistance [56]. Nrf2 has been shown to promote tumor growth and metastasis in various types of cancer, including lung, breast, liver, and pancreatic cancer. Initially, it ought to be clarified that Nrf2 possesses the ability to modulate the transcriptional activity of genes implicated in the control of cellular cycle advancement, including cyclin D1, cyclin E1, and CDK4/6. Nrf2 can suppress the transcription of genes associated with programmed cell death, including Bax and caspase-3, while simultaneously enhancing the transcription of genes that promote cell survival, such as Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 [57]. Furthermore, the transcription factor Nrf2 can modulate the transcription of genes that play a crucial role in drug metabolism and detoxification processes. These genes include glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), UGTs, and multidrug resistance-associated proteins (MRPs). The overexpression of these genes may contribute to the emergence of drug resistance in cancer cells. Third, Nrf2 may influence the production of genes including IL-6, IL-8, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) that are implicated in inflammation and immune evasion [58]. The overexpression of these genes has the potential to facilitate the progression of tumors and the spread of cancer cells by augmenting processes such as angiogenesis, invasion, and immune evasion. Understanding the molecular mechanism underlying Nrf2-mediated tumor progression is important for identifying potential targets for cancer prevention and treatment (Fig. 3).

Nrf2-mediated cellular proliferation and immune evation. ROS traverse the phospholipid bilayer, resulting in the dissociation of Nrf2 from Keap1. Following this, the nuclear translocation of Nrf2 occurs, thereby triggering the creation of crucial regulatory proteins like Bax, Bcl2, caspase 3, cyclin D1, E1, and CCDK4/6. Moreover, the Nrf2 protein regulates the transcription of GSTs and USTs, which in turn promote the expression of MRPs. Additionally, Nrf2 triggers the production of IL-6, IL-8, and PD-L1, hence playing a role in the processes of inflammation and immune evasion

Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm, bound to its repressor Keap1. Upon oxidative stress or exposure to electrophiles, this interaction is disrupted, leading to Nrf2 stabilization and accumulation [59]. Nrf2 then translocates to the nucleus, where it forms heterodimers with small musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma proteins. This complex binds to AREs in the promoters of target genes, initiating their transcription. Once activated, Nrf2 induces a range of cytoprotective genes, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), which degrades heme into biliverdin and other byproducts that mitigate oxidative damage [60]. It also regulates NAD(P)H oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), essential for detoxifying reactive quinones, and GSTs, which aid in detoxifying harmful substances by conjugating glutathione. By upregulating these antioxidants, Nrf2 helps cells neutralize ROS, reducing the oxidative damage caused by platinum-based chemotherapeutics, such as cisplatin. This antioxidant response allows cancer cells to resist chemotherapy. Nrf2 also promotes metabolic reprogramming, favoring glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis, which provides energy for cell proliferation during stress. Additionally, Nrf2 inhibits apoptosis by regulating anti-apoptotic factors, allowing cancer cells to evade chemotherapy-induced cell death and contributing to chemoresistance [61].

Xenobiotic-induced oxidative assault and carcinogenesis

Lead, arsenic, and cadmium are heavy metals known for their toxicity, which can result in the initiation of oxidative assault and subsequent manifestation of many health complications. Exposure to lead has been found to be correlated with impairments in several physiological systems, such as the neurological system, cardiovascular system, and reproductive systems. The influence of lead on oxidative assault is evident in both the brain and liver, as evidenced by a positive correlation between greater lead concentrations and elevated levels of lipid peroxidation [62]. The heme synthesis cascade is impeded by the presence of lead, which interferes with the functioning of enzymes such as δ-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase and ferrochelatase [63]. Consequently, this disruption results in the production of ROS. Arsenic, an extensively researched foreign substance metal, induces oxidative assault through the generation of ROS, resulting in cellular harm and the initiation of oxidative-responsive signaling cascades [64].

Several investigations have identified the presence of superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals in several cell types, including HEL30 cells, NB4 cells, and CHOK1 cells when exposed to arsenic [65]. Superoxide is observed as the principal species that is generated by arsenic, initiating a cascade phenomenon that leads to the production of other ROS through dismutation and Fenton reactions. In contrast, cadmium does not directly produce free radicals that are linked to the occurrence of oxidative assault. However, it can be inferred that it indirectly results in the generation of superoxide, hydroxyl radicals, and nitric oxide radicals. Cadmium has the ability to stimulate the generation of hydrogen peroxide, a non-radical compound [66]. This hydrogen peroxide then acts as a reservoir of free radicals via a process known as Fenton chemistry. The indirect mechanism of cadmium involves the displacement of iron and Cu ions within cellular proteins, resulting in the liberation of unbound iron or Cu ions. These ions play an active role in the process of oxidative assault through Fenton reactions, hence adding to the carcinogenic consequences of cadmium [67].

ROS are highly reactive molecules that can assault cellular components, including DNA, proteins, and lipids. The molecular mechanism underlying ROS-mediated pathogenesis of cell proliferation and impaired DNA repair involves several key processes [68]. One mechanism by which ROS promotes cell proliferation is through the expression of the MAPK signaling cascade [69]. ROS-induced oxidative assault can activate upstream kinases, such as Raf-1 and MEK, which in turn activate downstream kinases, such as ERK1/2, JNK, and p38 MAPK105. Subsequently, these kinases can induce cellular proliferation through the activation of transcription factors and facilitation of gene expression related to the advancement of the cell cycle [70].

Another mechanism by which ROS promotes cell proliferation is through the stabilization of the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) protein, activated in response to hypoxia [71]. HIF-1α is responsible for the regulation of gene expression related to crucial biological processes such as angiogenesis, glucose biotransformation, and cell survival. ROS-induced oxidative assault can stabilize HIF-1α, leading to increased cell proliferation and tumor growth. ROS can also impair DNA repair mechanisms by damaging DNA and interfering with the function of key repair enzymes. ROS can instigate damage to DNA, encompassing single-strand breaks, double-strand breaks, and base alterations. These detrimental effects have the potential to create genetic mutations and chromosomal abnormalities [72]. Furthermore, it has been observed that ROS can directly hinder the activity of DNA repair enzymes, specifically those involved in the base excision repair (BER) cascade. This hindrance occurs by oxidizing crucial cysteine residues in these enzymes [73].

ROS can also promote DNA methylation, which can lead to epigenetic changes that impair DNA repair. ROS-induced oxidative assault can promote the formation of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), which can then be converted to 5-methylcytosine (5mC) by the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) enzyme [74]. Increased DNA methylation can impair DNA repair by inhibiting the binding of repair enzymes to assaulted DNA. In summary, ROS-induced oxidative assault can promote cell proliferation and impair DNA repair mechanisms by activating MAPK signaling cascades, stabilizing HIF-1α, damaging DNA interfering with the function of key repair enzymes, and promoting DNA methylation. These mechanisms contribute to the pathogenesis of cancer and other diseases associated with ROS-induced oxidative assault (Fig. 4).

Molecular artifacts induced by ROS, ranging from DNA damage, DNA methylation, angiogenesis, and cellular proliferation. When xenobiotics are encountered, mitochondria produce ROS by converting O2 into H2O2, resulting in the occurrence of oxidative stress. The oxidative attack leads to the oxidation of cysteine residues present in the BER mechanism, thereby limiting the activity of BER and causing damage to DNA. Furthermore, the presence of xenobiotics, such as 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, has a role in the process of DNA methylation. The activation of the Raf1, MEK, ERK1/2, P38, and MAPK cascade is initiated by xenobiotic-induced oxidative stress, resulting in the destabilization of HIF-α. These molecular processes collectively facilitate the formation of new blood vessels and aberrant cell proliferation

The effect of xenobiotics including arsenic, chromium, nickel, cadmium, and aflatoxin on oxidative assault and carcinogenesis at the molecular level. These toxic chemicals can cause an increase in ROS, which then attack cells via oxidative assault. The xenobiotics cause DNA damage, mutations, and genomic instability by upsetting the cellular redox equilibrium and weakening the antioxidant defense mechanisms [75]. To reduce cancer risk from occupational and environmental carcinogens, proper safety measures and laws are required.

Heavy metal xenobiotics induced cancer: ROS mediated mechanisms

Various heavy metal xenobiotics and their ROS-mediated mechanisms in cancer pathogenesis are explained below;

Arsenic

Arsenic, a hazardous metalloid, exhibits widespread distribution in the environment. The act of being exposed to arsenic has been linked to an elevated susceptibility to many forms of cancers. The molecular mechanism underlying arsenic-induced carcinogenesis is complex and involves multiple cellular processes. One of the key mechanisms by which arsenic promotes carcinogenesis is through the generation of oxidative assault. Arsenic can stimulate the expression of ROS and RNS, which can assault cellular macromolecules, including DNA, proteins, and lipids [76]. Arsenic-induced oxidative assault can lead to DNA assault and mutations, consequently the development of cancer. In addition to its role in generating oxidative assault, arsenic can also disrupt cellular signaling cascades that are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis [77].

Arsenic can activate various signaling cascades, including the MAPK, the PI3K/Akt, and the NF-κB cascades. Arsenic can also interfere with DNA repair mechanisms, which can increase the accumulation of DNA assault and mutations [78]. The inhibitory effects of arsenic on the repair of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) have been demonstrated through its interference with the recruitment of repair proteins, including ATM and DNA-PK, to the specific locations of DSBs [79]. Moreover, arsenic can induce epigenetic changes that can alter gene expression and contribute to the development of cancer. The induction of DNA methylation and histone changes by arsenic has been demonstrated, resulting in the suppression of tumor suppressor genes and the stimulation of oncogenes.

Chromium

Chromium is a metallic element that finds extensive application in various industrial processes, and it is recognized as a well-established carcinogenic agent. The presence of chromium has been linked to an elevated susceptibility to lung cancer, nasal cancer, and prostate cancer. The molecular mechanisms underlying chromium-induced carcinogenesis are complex and involve multiple cellular processes. One of the key mechanisms by which chromium promotes carcinogenesis is through the generation of oxidative assault [80]. Chromium can induce the production of ROS, which can cause assault on cellular macromolecules, including DNA, proteins, and lipids. Chromium-induced oxidative assault can lead to DNA assault and mutations, which can ultimately lead to the development of cancer. Chromium can also interfere with DNA repair mechanisms, which can increase the accumulation of DNA assault and mutations [81].

Chromium has been shown to inhibit the repair of DSBs by interfering with the recruitment of repair proteins, such as ATM and DNA-PK, to the sites of DSBs [82]. Moreover, chromium can interfere with cellular signaling cascades that are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Chromium can activate various signaling cascades, including MAPK, NF-κβ, and IGF-1R cascades. Dysregulation of these cascades can lead to increased cell proliferation and decreased apoptosis, which can promote the development of cancer. Chromium can also induce epigenetic changes that can alter gene expression and contribute to the development of cancer [83].

Nickel

Nickel is a carcinogenic metal that has been linked to the development of various cancers. The molecular mechanism underlying nickel-induced carcinogenesis is complex and involves multiple cellular processes. One of the key mechanisms by which nickel promotes carcinogenesis is through the induction of oxidative assault. Nickel can stimulate the production of ROS, which can assault cellular macromolecules, including DNA, proteins, and lipids [84]. Nickel-induced oxidative assault can lead to DNA assault and mutations, which can ultimately lead to the development of cancer. It can also interfere with cellular signaling cascades that are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Nickel can activate various signaling cascades, including the PI3K/Akt and MAPK. Dysregulation of the processes involved in cell proliferation, survival, and death has been linked to carcinogenesis. Moreover, nickel exposure has been shown to induce the expression of the tumor suppressor gene p16Ink4a, which can promote cellular senescence and limit the proliferation of assault cells [85]. However, prolonged exposure to nickel can lead to the inactivation of p16Ink4a and promote the proliferation of assault cells, leading to the development of cancer. Nickel can also interfere with DNA repair mechanisms, which can increase the accumulation of DNA assault and mutations. Nickel has been shown to inhibit the repair of DSBs by interfering with the recruitment of repair proteins, such as ATM and DNA-PK, to the sites of DSBs [86].

Cadmium

Cadmium exposure has been shown to induce the production of IL-6 and IL-8, which are pro-inflammatory cytokines. IL-6 and IL-8 can activate various signaling cascades, including the NF-κβ and the MAPK cascades, leading to the expression of transcription factors that regulate cell proliferation and survival. Moreover, IL-6 and IL-8 can induce the production of ROS, which can further enhance the activation of these cascades and contribute to the development of cancer [87]. Cadmium-induced oxidative assault can also lead to the activation of the Fanconianemia (FA) cascade. The FA cascade is involved in the repair of DNA inter-strand cross-links (ICLs), which are a type of DNA assault that can arise from exposure to various genotoxic agents, including cadmium [88].

The activation of the FA cascades can promote cell survival and prevent apoptosis, which can contribute to the development of cancer. Furthermore, cadmium exposure has been shown to interfere with the function of the FANCD2 protein, a key component of the FA cascade. Cadmium can inhibit the monoubiquitination of FANCD2, which is required for its activation and recruitment to the sites of ICLs. The inhibition of FANCD2 monoubiquitination by cadmium can impair the repair of ICLs and increase the accumulation of DNA assault, consequently the development of cancer [89]. Cancer associated with xenobiotic exposure

Several types of cancer have been related to xenobiotics such as PAHs, cadmium, arsenic, nickel, and radon [98]. Recognizing these connections highlights the need to reduce exposure to environmental toxins as a means of cancer prevention.

Skin cancer

Arsenic exposure is linked to an increased risk of developing squamous cell carcinoma and basal cell carcinoma of the skin [99]. When paired with other risk factors like sun exposure, arsenic has a greater impact on various subtypes of skin cancer than it does on melanoma [100]. Arsenic exposure has been linked to skin lesions, and this association appears to be strongly dose-dependent. Curiously, less than 30% of arsenic exposure sufferers ever show signs of skin lesions, even when exposed to relatively high amounts for long periods of time. The study found strong evidence linking certain gene variants to an elevated risk of getting skin cancer [101]. Potential risk factors included genotypes like EPHX1 Tyr113His, XPD C156A, and GSTT1 null. Those who possessed all three risk variants for EPHX1 Tyr113His, XPD C156A, and GSTs had a 400% higher risk of developing skin cancer than those who have one or fewer of these genetic variations [102].

Lung cancer

Lung cancer (LC) is a complicated ailment that is influenced by a variety of factors, including genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, age, and sex [103]. The role of environmental carcinogens in the development of lung cancer is dependent on the functional activity of biotransformation enzymes and the capacity for DNA repair. The GSTM1 polymorphism and LC risk have been studied extensively, with inconsistent results. In laryngeal cancer and certain northeast Indian tribes, the absence of the GSTM1 gene may reduce vulnerability to LC, according to previous studies [104]. However, GSTM1 deletion has been linked to a higher risk of lung cancer in Mongols, Chinese, North Indians, and South [105]. Coal miners are confronted with a multifaceted environmental exposure that encompasses radiochemical agents, such as radon, as well as particles and carcinogenic compounds like PAHs. These exposures have been linked to the development of LC [106]. The study posited that the involvement of the GSTM1 enzyme in the metabolic process of PAH epoxides, which are produced as a result of tobacco smoking, could potentially influence the risk of developing lung cancer [107].

Urethral carcinoma

Urothelial carcinoma, a cancer that is frequently encountered, has been linked to prolonged exposure to heavy metals [108]. In a study employing quantitative proteomics, the processes connecting heavy metal exposure, specifically arsenic (As) and cadmium (Cd), to bladder cancer (BLCA) were investigated. The PML protein is a primary area of investigation in this work since it has been observed to exhibit down-regulation in both cell models exposed to heavy metals and tissue specimens from patients with BLCA [109]. The tumor suppressor known as PML is of significant importance in the context of BLCA, as its overexpression has been observed to effectively inhibit tumor growth and trigger apoptosis in BLCA cells. Enhanced tumor formation is correlated with the loss of PML, which facilitates cell survival via autophagy under times of assault [110]. Significantly, exposure to As for a short duration results in a down-regulation of PML in bladder cell lines, with comparable outcomes seen in tests involving long-term exposure to arsenic [111]. The finding denotes a possible correlation between PML and As-related processes in bladder cancer. It also found a link between heavy metals and Nrf2, an antioxidant system component. PML deficiency increases ROS, Nrf2 activity, and Nrf2 downstream target genes, which include antioxidant proteins [112]. Arsenic exposure decreases SOD2 expression, indicating the importance of antioxidant systems in heavy metal oxidative assault [113].

Kidney carcinoma

Nickel chloride exposure is shown to lead to a concentration-dependent upregulation of the JMJD3 gene, suggesting a potential mechanism by which nickel may contribute to carcinogenesis [114]. Several study highlights the importance of understanding epigenetic alterations in the context of kidney cancer, specifically focusing on changes in histone methylation. H3K27me3, a modification of histone H3 at lysine 27, is known to be involved in several types of cancer, including gastric, colon, pancreatic, breast, ovarian, prostate, and kidney cancers [115]. Histone methylation, as an epigenetic mechanism, plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression, controlling the cell cycle, and maintaining genome stability. Aberrations in histone methylation are closely associated with the formation of tumors [116]. Histone methylation is a reversible process, dynamically regulated by histone methyltransferases and demethylases, whose mutations or abnormal expressions are frequently observed in cancer. The connection between kidney cancer and the H3K9 demethylase JMJD1A, emphasizing the importance of studying histone methylation in relation to cancer development [117]. JMJD3 has been identified as a specific demethylase for histone H3 lysine-27 tri/dimethylation (H3K27me3/2) in recent years. JMJD3 is essential for regulating gene transcription and cell differentiation [118]. Changes in JMJD3 expression are frequently observed in human malignancies, such as kidney cancer. The upregulation of JMJD3 in kidney malignant tissue suggests that it may play a role in the development of kidney cancer [119]. In conclusion, this sheds light on the intricate interplay between xenobiotic exposure, histone methylation, and kidney carcinoma. The concentration-dependent upregulation of JMJD3 by nickel chloride offers valuable insights into a potential molecular mechanism by which xenobiotics can contribute to cancer [120].

Gastric cancer

Based on multiple research, it has been determined that there exists a correlation between xenobiotics, specifically heavy metals such as Cd2+ and Pb2+, and their capacity to initiate stomach cancer. Exposure to heavy metals has been found to adversely affect the structural integrity of the stomach mucosa and its protective activities, hence initiating a series of events that heighten the susceptibility to gastric cancer [121]. The substitution of Ca2+ with Cd2+ hampers the functionality of E-cadherin, an essential protein involved in the preservation of intercellular connections [122]. Consequently, the integrity of cell junctions is impaired, leading to a substantial influence on the stomach mucosal barrier. The disruption is characterized by a decrease in the thickness of mucus, a reduction in the volume of mucous, and a decline in basal acid output. Moreover, the presence of heavy metals results in an elevation in lipid peroxidation byproducts, hence exacerbating the detrimental effects on the stomach mucosa’s well-being. The rats that were subjected to CdCl2 demonstrated notable elevations in the concentrations of Cd in both their blood and mucosa [123]. Concurrently, they experienced decreases in mucus thickness, mucous content, and basal acid output. The alterations in the stomach mucosa lead to a disruption of the gastric barrier, hence increasing its susceptibility to ulcer formation [124]. In addition, it has been observed that Cd exerts an influence on the proper functioning of E-cadherin, hence disrupting tight cell junctions. This disruption subsequently leads to the activation of oncogenes via the β-catenin-mediated cascade [125]. The aforementioned factors play a crucial role in the constitution of the gastric mucosal barrier, and their diminishment contributes to the susceptibility of the gastric mucosa, hence augmenting the likelihood of cancer progression. Additionally, the research examines the involvement of Pb2+ in the process of carcinogenesis. Exposure to Pb2+ has been seen to stimulate the upregulation of IL-8 expression in gastric cancer cells. The involvement of AP-1 and ERK is of utmost importance in the signal transduction cascades responsible for the activation of the IL-8 gene in gastric cancer cells stimulated by Pb2+. The activation of the EGFR by Pb2+ results in the phosphorylation of ERK-1/2, leading to the subsequent activation of AP-1 [126]. This series of events increases the likelihood of tumor formation.

The investigation of these tactics sheds light on their potential to tackle the complex obstacles presented by cancer, hence highlighting the continuous pursuit of novel and efficient therapies.

Therapeutic agents and approaches in cancer management

Chelation treatment

It is a significant therapeutic approach utilized to manage the adverse consequences of heavy metal-induced toxicity, providing a method to alleviate the detrimental impacts caused by these toxic compounds. Various heavy metal ions exhibit different affinities for specific donor atoms present in ligands [127]. The variations in affinity observed play a fundamental role in the process of selecting chelating agents.

Numerous chelating agents have been discovered and implemented in the realm of clinical practise. The compound known as 2,3-Dimercapto-Propanesulphonate (DMPS) is being discussed. The aforementioned substance, which is soluble in water, is employed for the purpose of mitigating the toxic effects of arsenic and mercury [128]. Dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) is a chemical compound commonly used in academic and scientific research. DMSA, often referred to as succimer, is composed of two carboxylic and two thiol groups, rendering it efficacious in the management of lead, mercury, and arsenic toxicity [129]. The chelating agent under consideration is a lipophilic C5-branched chain alkyl monoester of DMSA, which possesses the ability to traverse cell membranes and reach intracellular compartments. Recent studies have shown evidence of the effectiveness of this method in the process of chelation for the removal of arsenic, lead, cadmium, and mercury [130]. Both MchDMSA and MmDMSA are lipophilic compounds. MchDMSA is characterized by a cyclic carbon chain that is analogous to DMSA, while MmDMSA features a straight and branched chain methyl group that is analogous to DMSA. These chemicals have been shown to reduce cadmium and arsenic levels in living creatures [131]. Treatment with DMSA and α-lipoic acid together has been shown to reduce oxidative assault in lead poisoning patients. Additionally, CaNa2EDTA with zinc improves chelation. Chelation therapy for chronic arsenic toxicity with MiADMSA and antioxidants, especially thiol functional groups, has shown promising results [132].

CAR-T cell therapy

The utilization of CAR-T cell therapy in the context of cancer treatment, specifically emphasizing the advancements made in developing MET-specific CAR-T cells to effectively target hepatocellular carcinoma that express the MET protein [133]. The findings of the study demonstrate that MET-CAR-T cells exhibit proficient recognition and eradication of HCC cells, irrespective of the level of activity within the MET signaling cascade, only relying on the total expression of MET. CD28ζ indicates improved activation of effector T cell activities and exhibits greater anti-tumor activity in comparison to MET-CAR.4-1BBζ [134]. In order to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of MET-CAR-T cells, it is crucial to improve the structural design of the CAR construct and employ tactics to mitigate the fatigue of CAR-T cells caused by the tumor microenvironment.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) technique

MOFs, which belong to a category of porous materials, are utilized for the purpose of drug delivery in the context of cancer therapy [135]. The utilization of radiotherapy expedites the process of tumor ablation and mitigates the occurrence of lung metastases. MOFs have been utilized in the field of bone cancer therapy to enhance the effectiveness of radiotherapy and prevent the occurrence of lung metastases. The utilization of MOFs as drug delivery carriers has emerged as a promising strategy for targeted therapy in oral cancer treatment [136]. The proposed methodology involves the integration of phototherapy and immunotherapy technques utilising MOFs, hence yielding an enhanced immune response against cancerous tumors [137]. The application of nanoscale MOFs is employed in the treatment of head and neck cancer that is resistant to conventional therapies, resulting in a notable level of efficacy.

Therapeutic potential of fibronectin

Fibronectin (FN) has garnered considerable attention in the field of cancer research owing to its possible multifaceted involvement in cancer pathobiology [138]. Several research has put forth the notion that FN may serve as a tumor suppressor, hindering the first stages of tumor formation. However, contrasting evidence suggests that FN is associated with cancer spread and a worse prognosis when found within tumor cells. In particular, the suppression of α5 and β1 integrins, which play a crucial role in facilitating cell adhesion mediated by FN, has been shown to decrease the adherence of ovarian tumor cells in a three-dimensional model that incorporates primary cells and extracellular matrix constituents [139]. Additionally, the findings from various search results indicate a plausible correlation between protease inhibitors and FN in the context of cancer spread [138].

Defending against cancer with the symphony of heavy metals

The convoluted connection between heavy metals, especially Zn and Cu, has recently come under scrutiny in the field of cancer detection and prevention, particularly in relation to prostate cancer. Deficits in zinc, an important trace metal that is involved in many physiological processes, have been linked to negative health outcomes [151]. Nevertheless, zinc’s association with prostate cancer is multifaceted. A number of studies have shown that zinc may help prevent prostate cancer, while others have raised concerns about side effects, especially at high dosages. The ZnT and ZIP transporter families are essential for maintaining zinc homeostasis within cells. Two proteins, ZIP (SLC39A) and ZnT (SLC30A), are involved in the transport of zinc ions into the cytoplasm and zinc efflux into organelles or intercellular spaces, respectively. Important for metal transport across membranes are ZIP proteins, which have 14 subtypes (ZIP1–14) [152]. Important functions in zinc control are performed by these transporters, which are expressed either ubiquitously or selectively in different tissues. Specifically, a clinical trial that included nearly 700 men with prostate cancer found that adequate zinc absorption was associated with a decreased risk of prostate cancer [153]. Multiple studies have explored the link between zinc supplements and prostate cancer. A US case–control study found zinc reduced cancer risk, showing a dose–response effect. However, the VITAL study showed over 15 mg daily decreased advanced cancer risk, while over 100 mg daily or 10 + years of use increased risk. Further research is needed to clarify zinc’s role [154].

On the opposite, Cu, another vital trace metal, has been linked to the advancement of cancer. In patients with prostate cancer, especially moderate-high severity tumors, elevated Cu levels have been seen, both in the blood and within the tumor itself. Research into hCtr1 as a therapeutic target has focused on its role in regulating Cu influx [144]. Scientific investigations have demonstrated that hCtr1 knockdown inhibits tumor growth in animal xenografts and decreases the proliferation of cultured prostate cancer cells [155]. Cu triggers apoptosis by activating caspase 3 and causing stress in the ER, which in turn inhibits mitochondrial and ER apoptotic cascades. Cu also has an effect on autophagy, the adaptive process that happens before cell death. The process of protective autophagy is initiated through AMPK-mTOR signaling, which involves the overexpression of AMPK and the downregulation of mTOR. Changes in autophagy-related proteins, including ATG5-12, ATG7, ATG3, and Beclin1, are involved in Cu-induced autophagy [156]. By controlling the rate of autophagy flow, Cu NPs can affect cell fate. The autophagy flux is inhibited by Cu-based nano-alloys such as Au@Cu2–xSe NPs, which change the pH of the lysosome. A potential approach to treating Cu-dependent cancers is to combine Cu nanoparticles with autophagy inhibition; this would shed light on the complex relationship between autophagy, cancer treatment, and Cu. However, due to the cytotoxic effects of excess Cu, Cu ionophores like disulfiram are being investigated as possible treatments for prostate cancer. Researchers found that adding copper to disulfiram dramatically slowed tumor growth and caused prostate cancer cells to die. The specific toxicity of Cu complexes generated from bis(thiosemicarbazonato) against cancer cells provides another promising direction for future therapeutic development [157].

There is evidence that gold complexes, such as gold nanoparticles, can cause cancer cells to die by apoptotic and non-apoptotic means. The p53 cascade is one of the important signaling molecules and cascades for gold-induced cell death [158]. In this cascade, p53 is activated by gold complexes, which in turn increase ROS levels and p21 expression, which ultimately leads to cell death. Further, gold complexes are very effective TrxR inhibitors, which means they slow the proliferation of cancer cells by interfering with the thioredoxin redox cascade and, in the end, killing the cells [159]. Another channel that gold complexes use to regulate cellular reactions to oxidative assault and cause apoptosis in cancer cells is the ASK1-p38-MAPK signaling axis [160]. Further, mitochondrial malfunction, including free radical production and mitochondrial membrane potential disturbance, is linked to gold-induced cell death. Researchers Coulter et al. investigated the potential of maytansine analogue DM1 (MTC-100038) in combination with ultrasmall gold core nanoparticles (2 nm) to treat hepatocellular cancer in morin [161].

In the field of biomedicine, Silver (Ag) nanoparticles have been combined with photoacoustic imaging to create a new method for identifying and validating the nanoparticles’ intended role in cancerous tissues. In vivo tumor assessment using foliate-modified Ag nanoparticles, which improved photoacoustic contrast with normal tissues, showed promise. A composite substrate of high-sensitivity thermally annealed silver nanoparticles and porous silicon bragg mirror (AgNPs/PSB) was used to improve serum Raman spectroscopy (SERS) by Yang et al. (2023) [162]. With more pronounced spectral peaks than conventional Raman spectroscopy, this method shows promise for identifying new breast cancer biomarkers. A machine learning algorithm and improved SERS substrate distinguished breast cancer patients from healthy controls. Noorbazargan et al. (2021) found that silver nanoparticles (9.87 µg/mL) induced nuclear fragmentation, apoptosis, and elevated p53 expression in lung cancer cells, suggesting therapeutic potential [163].

Conclusion

Lead, arsenic, and cadmium are examples of heavy metals that are particularly carcinogenic because of the oxidative assault they cause and the cellular cascades they can disrupt. Heavy metals can have deleterious effects on the human body, although chelating agents like DMSA, DMPS, MiADMSA, MchDMSA, and MmDMSA have shown they can remove these metals from the body. CAR-T cell modification to specifically target heavy metal-related malignancies is a cutting-edge approach to cancer treatment that shows promise for improving outcomes. In the case of heavy metal-induced cancers, MOFs have shown great promise as efficient drug delivery vehicles, opening the door to potentially game-changing advances in treatment strategies. Finding FN to be a potential treatment for cancer opens up new doors. Heavy metal-induced cancers may be combatted with strategies that particularly target FN, such as preventing tumor cells from adhering to FN or reducing FN production in the extracellular matrix. Cancer pathogenesis research has also focused on several other biomarkers, such as ROS, PPARs, CYP1B1, CAR, TGFβ2, Nrf2, ALK, and EZH2. Early warning symptoms of cancer can be found in alterations to these processes, which may be genetic, epigenetic, proteomic, or metabolic in nature. The effectiveness of chelation treatment is limited by hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypotension. CAR-T cell immunotherapy, which is beneficial, faces toxicity issues such as cytokine release syndrome and off-target effects. MOFs, which could be drugs carriers, become unstable in physiological conditions and produce harmful metal ions. Fibronectin, an essential extracellular matrix component, has limitations that promote cancer development and inflammation. These limitations highlight the need to develop and optimize these therapeutic techniques to increase clinical safety and efficacy. The development of effective cancer treatments that can target cancer caused by heavy metals relies on a thorough understanding of these biological cascades. Last but not least, heavy metals such as zinc, Cu, gold, bismuth, and silver have several uses that show they could be good ways to manage cancer. The plethora of applications that these heavy metals provide, from improved diagnostic tools to tailored drug administration, bode well for the future of cancer treatment.
