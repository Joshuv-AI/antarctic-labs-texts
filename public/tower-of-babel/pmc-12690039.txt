Is It Getting Better? An Evaluation of Two Successive Generations of ChatGPT in Answering Specialized Vascular Surgery Questions
Authors: Dongjin Suh, Quang Le, Leana Dogbe, Kedar Lavingia, Michael Amendola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeLarge language models (LLMs) can generate clinically relevant text; however, their performance in highly specialized medical domains remains uncertain. This study evaluated ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4 (OpenAI) using vascular surgery board–style questions from the Vascular Education and Self-Assessment Program, version 4 (VESAP4) and compared the two public model versions (June and November 2023).Materials and MethodsAll non-image VESAP4 questions (n=384) were presented independently three times to each model version (ChatGPT-3.5 June/November; ChatGPT-4, June/November). Outcomes included accuracy (proportion correct), consistency (same option letter across all three attempts and “consistently correct”), explanation length (word count), and modes of failure classified for a pre-specified index attempt using a multi-label taxonomy, with independent dual review and consensus. Accuracy and consistency were reported with 95% confidence intervals, and between-condition differences were compared using the chi-square (proportions) and Welch t-test (word count).ResultsAccuracy was 47.9% and 46.5% for ChatGPT-3.5 (June/November) and 62.4% and 63.8% for ChatGPT-4, respectively. No significant within-model improvement occurred between June and November, whereas ChatGPT-4 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5 in both months (P<0.0001). For consistency, ChatGPT-3.5 increased in “same-letter” (55.5% to 65.6%; P=0.004) with no change in “consistently correct” (40.6% to 40.4%; P=0.94). ChatGPT-4 decreased in “same-letter” (90.1% to 79.7%; P<0.0001) with stable “consistently correct” (60.4% to 58.6%; P=0.61). Between the models, ChatGPT-4 exceeded ChatGPT-3.5 on both consistency metrics in June and November (all P<0.0001). Explanation length shifted within models: ChatGPT-3.5 produced shorter responses in November compared with June (105.4±1.0 vs. 164.2±1.5 words; P<0.0001), whereas ChatGPT-4 produced longer responses (282.0±1.3 vs. 120.0±1.2 words; P<0.0001). Performance across VESAP4 subsections was higher for Vascular Medicine and Radiological Imaging/Radiation Safety, and lower for Dialysis Access Management. The modes of failure were predominantly due to external information retrieval errors for ChatGPT-3.5 and logical errors for ChatGPT-4.ConclusionChatGPT-4 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5 on vascular surgery board–style questions yet achieved only moderate accuracy. Version updates did not consistently improve the performance in this specialized domain, emphasizing the complexity of decision-making in vascular surgery and the current limitations of LLMs in surgical education.

INTRODUCTION

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), specifically large language models (LLMs), have demonstrated notable potential to transform various industries, and healthcare is no exception. Trained on vast amounts of multimodal data, these models can proficiently predict logical phrases in response to prompts that mimic human responses [1,2]. Medical education has become a key area of interest for researchers exploring the applications of AI. One of the first and most widely acclaimed LLMs is OpenAI’s series of generative pretrained transformer models (ChatGPT models) [3]. ChatGPT has been used to answer the questions from the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) and specialized knowledge assessments in ophthalmology, obstetrics and gynecology, and surgery [4-7]. Such assessments have confirmed the ability of these models not only to pass medical examinations but also to demonstrate robust performance in answering frequently asked clinical questions [4,8].

However, significant questions remain regarding the application of this technology in medical education. While LLMs can generate highly plausible, logically consistent responses, they are prone to producing false but convincing information—commonly referred to as “hallucinations”—under circumstances such as insufficient training data, a tendency toward fluency over accuracy, or imprecise or unclear input [9]. Despite recent advances, the stability and reproducibility of LLM-generated outputs in specialized medical contexts remain unclear.

To address this gap, we examined the ability of two of the most popular LLM versions of ChatGPT to answer high-level vascular surgery test questions from the Vascular Education and Self-Assessment Program (VESAP), which embodies the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS)’s threshold knowledge for practicing vascular surgeons. Vascular surgery provides a particularly rigorous environment for evaluating LLM performance. First, many decisions hinge on quantitative thresholds (e.g., carotid duplex velocity criteria, aortic diameter cutoffs, and ankle-brachial index/toe-brachial index values) and modality tradeoffs (open, endovascular, or medical therapy). Second, case vignettes frequently require multi-domain integration across vascular medicine, imaging interpretation, perioperative risk, and device selection, with highly specialized terminology. Third, distractors in board-style items are designed to probe logical consistency and error-prone shortcuts, providing a practical lens into the types of reasoning failures that LLMs may exhibit. As such, we considered VESAP questions to be an appropriate and rigorous platform for stress-testing models that may perform well on broader medical knowledge assessments such as the USMLE.

By evaluating the performance of two successive ChatGPT releases (ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4) on VESAP, we aimed to determine whether incremental model updates led to meaningful performance improvement in this specialized domain. We compared accuracy, consistency, and explanation length, and categorized modes of failure using a predefined multi-label taxonomy. Additionally, VESAP subsections were analyzed to identify topic-level patterns and domains in which performance was relatively stronger or weaker.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1) Institutional review board

This study did not involve human subjects or identifiable medical information. As such, it was not subject to review by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating institutions, in accordance with their respective guidance.

2) Vascular education and self-assessment program

The VESAP is a repository of vascular surgery–specific multiple-choice test questions developed by the SVS for continuing medical education among practicing vascular surgeons, as well as for trainee instruction. The most recent iteration, VESAP6, was released in 2024 following the retirement of VESAP5 in 2020. Board-certified vascular surgeons served as volunteer item writers, with subsequent peer reviews and editorial oversight by SVS.

We obtained explicit permission from the SVS to use VESAP4 in this study. The questions were organized into ten subsections representing the breadth of vascular surgery: aortoiliac arterial disease, cerebrovascular disease, dialysis access management, upper extremity vascular disease, lower extremity vascular disease, renal and mesenteric vascular disease, venous and lymphatic disease, vascular medicine, vascular laboratory, and radiological imaging/radiation safety. Each question was presented in a clinical vignette format with multiple-choice responses (e.g., “What is the appropriate course of management for a 74-year-old male with asymptomatic left carotid stenosis and an occluded right internal carotid artery (ICA) presented with a left ICA peak systolic velocity of 210 cm/s and an end-diastolic velocity of 75 cm/s”). Questions that included radiographic images were excluded from analysis, leaving a final set of 384 questions for evaluation.

3) Generative AI models

Two LLMs from OpenAI—ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4—were evaluated in this study. For each model, two release versions were used: the 06/13/2023 release (June version: gpt-3.5-turbo-0613, gpt-4-0613) and the 11/06/2023 release (November version: gpt-3.5-turbo-1106, gpt-4-1106). An update to these models, which expanded their capacities, was released in November 2023 [10]. Notably, the November version of ChatGPT-4 included knowledge updated to April 2023, whereas the other versions (ChatGPT-3.5 June/November 2023 and ChatGPT-4 June 2023) were trained on data available up to September 2021. To emulate the day-to-day use of these two LLMs by vascular surgery trainees and surgeons, modifiable parameters of each model—such as temperature (creativity), maximum tokens (output length), and frequency/presence penalties (repetition control)—were left at platform defaults.

4) Accessing LLMs

Each of the 384 VESAP4 questions was submitted to every model version in the standard format. The following instruction first conditioned the model: “You are a helpful assistant answering vascular surgery test questions.” Each question was then phrased as: “[Question Stem] What is the correct answer choice among A. [Choice A] B. [Choice B] C. [Choice C] D. [Choice D] E. [Choice E]? Provide a brief explanation.” The official OpenAI application programming interface (API) was used with a custom Python 3.11 script (Python Software Foundation). Each item was queried three times per model version (triplicate attempts). Responses from the June 2023 versions were collected in October 2023, and responses from the November 2023 versions were collected in December 2023.

5) Evaluation and analysis

The primary outcomes were accuracy (proportion of correct responses per condition–pooled from three independent attempts per model and version; n=1,152 attempts); consistency, defined as same-letter consistency (identical option across all three attempts) and consistent correctness (all three attempts correct), calculated per condition over 384 questions; and explanation length (word count per attempt).

Two independent reviewers (MA and KL), blinded to the model and version, reviewed the incorrect responses and categorized them into pre-specified modes of failure: internal information retrieval error, external information retrieval error, logical error, mathematical error, and question stem ambiguity.

Internal information referred to data contained within the question stem or answer choices. External information referred to content requiring retrieval from sources outside the prompt. A logical error was defined as a flaw in reasoning or explanation, and a mathematical error was defined as a calculation mistake. Finally, question stem ambiguity was defined as incorrect answers not attributed to any other cause and was perceived as a suboptimal question stem presentation.

Discrepancies were resolved by consensus through discussions between the two reviewers. For the characterization of failure modes, incorrect responses from the index attempts per model and version (first of the three attempts) were analyzed to avoid repeated counting of the same item across attempts. Therefore, the denominator corresponded to the number of incorrect responses during the index attempt. Item-level identifiers were not retained, and only aggregate counts by category were reported. Incorrect answers were allowed for multiple failure modes. Thus, the failure modes for each model and version exceeded 100%.

6) Statistical analysis

In compliance with the SVS data minimization agreement, only aggregate counts were retained, and item-level pairing was unavailable. Therefore, we reported point estimates with Wilson 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the proportions. Between-condition differences (June vs. November within a model; GPT-3.5 vs. GPT-4 within a month) were compared using the chi-square test on pooled counts, as item-paired tests (e.g., McNemar) were not feasible; these comparisons were presented as sensitivity analyses. Welch t-test was used for explanation length. A two-sided α of 0.05 was used. Analyses were performed using Python version 3.11.

RESULTS

1) Model accuracy assessment

Across three independent attempts for each of the 384 questions (1,152 attempts per model version), accuracy was 47.9% (179/384, 193/384, 180/384; 552/1,152) for ChatGPT-3.5 (June), 46.5% (178/384, 177/384, 181/384; 536/1,152) for ChatGPT-3.5 (November), 62.4% (242/384, 238/384, 239/384; 719/1,152) for ChatGPT-4 (June), and 63.8% (247/384, 247/384, 241/384; 735/1,152) for ChatGPT-4 (November).

The Wilson 95% CIs were 45.0-50.8, 43.7-49.4, 59.6-65.2, and 61.0-66.5, respectively. Within-model comparisons between June and November were not significant for ChatGPT-3.5 (P=0.50) or ChatGPT-4 (P=0.49; Table 1). In the cross-sectional comparisons, ChatGPT-4 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5 in both months: June (62.4% vs. 47.9%; P<0.0001) and November (63.8% vs. 46.5%; P<0.0001).

Performance across the subsections of VESAP4 varied. Vascular Medicine and Radiological Imaging/Radiation Safety tended to consistently rank among the highest-performing sections, whereas Dialysis Access Management frequently ranked among the lowest across model versions (Table 2).

2) Model consistency assessment

Consistency was assessed using two measures: same-letter consistency (selection of the same options across all three attempts) and consistent correctness (all three attempts correct). For ChatGPT-3.5, same-letter consistency increased from 213 of 384 (55.5%; Wilson 95% CI, 50.5-60.4) in June to 252 of 384 (65.6%; 60.7-70.2) in November (P=0.004), while consistent correctness remained unchanged (156 of 384 [40.6%; 35.8-45.6] vs. 155 of 384 [40.4%; 35.6-45.3]; P=0.941).

For ChatGPT-4, same-letter consistency decreased from 346 of 384 (90.1%; 86.7-92.7) in June to 306 of 384 (79.7%; 75.4-83.4) in November (P<0.001), whereas consistent correctness was similar (232 of 384 [60.4%; 55.4-65.2] vs. 225 of 384 [58.6%; 53.6-63.4]; P=0.607). Between the two models, ChatGPT-4 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5 on both metrics in both June and November (all P<0.0001) (Table 3).

3) Word count

The mean explanation length across the pooled triplicate attempts for the 384 questions differed significantly between models. ChatGPT-3.5 produced shorter explanations in November compared with June (105.4±1.0 vs. 164.2±1.5 words; P<0.0001), whereas ChatGPT-4 produced longer explanations (282.0±1.3 vs. 120.0±1.2; P<0.0001). Cross-sectionally, ChatGPT-4 generated shorter explanations than ChatGPT-3.5 in June, but longer explanations in November (both P<0.0001). For reference, the mean length of VESAP item explanations was 122.8 words (Table 4).

4) Modes of failure

Modes of failure were characterized using a pre-specified index attempt (the first of three) for each model version to avoid duplicate counting. The denominator represented the number of incorrect responses in that attempt (e.g., ChatGPT-3.5 June: 384−179=205). Multi-label coding was then performed (Table 5).

For ChatGPT-3.5 June, the most frequent failure mode was external information retrieval error (137/205, 66.8%), followed by logical error (67/205, 32.7%), internal information retrieval error (14/205, 6.8%), and question stem ambiguity (10/205, 4.9%); no mathematical errors were identified. For ChatGPT-3.5 November, the distribution was similar: external information retrieval error, 153/206 (74.3%); logical error, 67/206 (32.5%); internal information retrieval error, 6/206 (2.9%); and question stem ambiguity, 5/206 (2.4%); no mathematical errors were identified.

For ChatGPT-4, logical errors were predominant. In the June version, logical errors accounted for 79/142 (55.6%) and external information retrieval errors accounted for 62/142 (43.7%); none were coded as internal information retrieval errors, mathematical errors, or question-stem ambiguities. In the November version, logical errors comprised 123/137 (89.8%), external information retrieval errors accounted for 54/137 (39.4%), and question stem ambiguity accounted for 1/137 (0.7%); again, no internal information retrieval or mathematical errors were observed.

DISCUSSION

In this study, two widely available LLMs were evaluated using a highly specialized vascular surgery question bank. ChatGPT-4 outperformed ChatGPT-3.5, yet both achieved only moderate accuracy (≈48% for ChatGPT-3.5 and ≈62%-64% for ChatGPT-4), and version updates from June to November did not significantly improve accuracy. Descriptive analysis showed that output consistency also shifted: ChatGPT-3.5 became more consistent in selecting the same option, whereas ChatGPT-4 became less consistent, with minimal change in the proportion of “consistently correct” responses for either model. The modes of failure differed by model, with ChatGPT-3.5 being more often limited by external information retrieval and ChatGPT-4 by logical errors.

Our results align with previous research, which has found that ChatGPT-4 is superior to ChatGPT-3.5 in accurately answering medical test questions. For multiple-choice questions, Rizzo et al. [11] reported accuracy rates of approximately 50% for ChatGPT-3.5 and 60%-70% for ChatGPT-4 in a set of orthopedic in-service exam questions, and Oh et al. [12] noted a 46.8% correct response rate for ChatGPT-3.5, and 76.4% for ChatGPT-4 for the Korean general surgery board exam. Similarly, Beaulieu-Jones et al. [7] reported accuracy rates of up to 70% on multiple-choice questions in medical residents’ surgical knowledge assessments.

Our findings further support these observations, indicating that ChatGPT-4 generally outperforms ChatGPT-3.5 in medical test questions. However, the accuracy rates in our study were lower than those reported in other contexts, likely because of the complexity of the VESAP questions. This is possibly because the VESAP questions are intended for surgical subspecialty trainees with specific knowledge that is not as readily available as it is in other specialties. For example, both models performed relatively well in Vascular Medicine and Radiological Imaging/Radiation Safety—topics relevant to multiple specialties such as radiology, nuclear medicine, interventional radiology, cardiology, and interventional cardiology—and across multiple occupations such as X-ray technicians and surgical technologists. This broad applicability likely contributes to their higher performance in this domain. In contrast, accuracy was notably lower for questions on Dialysis Access Management, which is unique to vascular surgery. This contrast highlights the depth and specificity of knowledge required in vascular surgery and underscores the limitations of general-use LLMs in addressing highly specialized topics without additional fine-tuning or training on domain-specific datasets. Despite advances in model performance in other fields, no significant accuracy improvement was found between the June and November versions of either model and no consistent trend could be identified. Given aggregate-only analyses, small version-to-version differences may have been difficult to detect. Future work with item-level pairing and larger question pools may be needed to detect subtle version differences.

Although LLM technology continues to evolve rapidly, newer versions can demonstrate both progress and regression—improvements in some areas (e.g., accuracy in ChatGPT-4 compared with ChatGPT-3.5) and declines in others (e.g., consistency for ChatGPT-4). This finding is not unique to the present study. For example, several authors have reported that ChatGPT has strong concordance, with Kung et al. [9] and Long et al. [13] noting a high concordance rate of 92.06%. However, Beaulieu-Jones et al. [7] found that, on a repeat query, the answers selected by ChatGPT varied in 36.4% of the questions. Wu et al. [14] reported intermediate consistency (82.3%) for epilepsy-related questions. While these findings demonstrate that advancements in LLMs may not always translate into improved performance in specific use cases such as VESAP, its use to supplement medical education in a more predictive manner should continue to be explored. For example, Holderried et al. [15] provided a framework for implementing LLMs to build interactive clinical scenarios and help students practice history-taking skills. This framework provided plausible responses in 98% of the cases, with strongly positive feedback from student users. Therefore, future models should be evaluated for potential improvements in consistency or accuracy when VESAP questions are presented in an interactive format, where subsequent prompts can be further tailored based on the reason for an incorrect response. Additionally, divergence between output consistency and correctness (e.g., rising same-letter consistency for ChatGPT-3.5 without a gain in ‘consistently correct’) suggests that stability of output should not be conflated with accuracy in specialized domains.

Given the modest accuracy of both models, several ethical considerations arise. If their limitations are not widely recognized in the medical community, referring physicians or trainees using such models to advise patients on vascular surgery issues may be misled. The current iterations of LLMs dynamically use architectures that incorporate active internet search capabilities. Regardless, our work indicates a potential bias in LLMs, such as ChatGPT, which are trained and adjusted based on datasets that may or may not include specialized medical and/or surgical knowledge. This has implications for novice learners (e.g., medical students or junior residents). General-purpose LLMs can simulate clinical reasoning but still produce confident, incorrect explanations, particularly in domains where we observed lower accuracy (e.g., Dialysis Access Management). Interface disclaimers are common; however, they do not reliably prevent automation bias or anchoring when responses are fluent and definitive [16]. Without expert oversight, learners may adopt incorrect rationales, especially when errors arise from logical faults (more common with ChatGPT-4 in our study) rather than from obvious knowledge gaps.

In educational settings, LLM use should be limited to formative activities (e.g., explanation critique and distractor analysis) and should remain under faculty supervision, with explicit correction of misconceptions. Other strategies to mitigate the risk of misinformation include prompts that require source-backed answers (e.g., SVS practice guidelines or regional practice guidelines). Where feasible, pairing general-purpose models with domain-curated retrieval (e.g., guideline-constrained context) may also reduce external information errors.

This study has several limitations. First, we used VESAP version 4 for analysis, which was retired in 2020. Updates in vascular surgery knowledge and board question design since then may have contributed to incorrect answers and may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future research should incorporate more recent resources, such as VESAP6, released in 2024. Second, there are no established benchmarks or thresholds for evaluating LLM performance in medical education, although comparisons with existing literature allowed us to contextualize our findings. Third, since the initiation of this project, numerous other LLMs have been released, including open-source models such as Meta’s LLaMA, Google’s Gemini, and Anthropic’s Claude. Comparative studies among these emerging models could help determine whether they offer superior performance in specialized domains, such as vascular surgery. Fourth, response length does not necessarily reflect quality; therefore, future studies should include blinded, rubric-based assessments of each response to evaluate explanation quality. Fifth, the current study’s analyses of LLM responses were limited to aggregate data and therefore lacked item-level pairing. As a result, we could not apply paired analyses (e.g., McNemar) or compute inter-rater reliability metrics (e.g., Cohen κ). Finally, image-based questions were excluded, which limited the applicability of our findings to imaging-dependent tasks.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to a broader understanding of AI in medical education. It establishes a specialty-specific baseline for longitudinal benchmarking by evaluating successive releases of widely used models in an official vascular surgery question bank. Furthermore, it distinguishes domain-specific accuracy from consistency, showing that version updates (e.g., June vs. November) may change output behavior without yielding paired gains in correctness. Finally, the modes of failure identified in this study provide a basis for evaluating the domain-specific vascular surgery knowledge in future models. Future investigations should incorporate updated protocols that address the aforementioned limitations as part of ongoing evaluations of LLM use in vascular surgery.

CONCLUSION

ChatGPT-4 outperforms ChatGPT-3.5 on vascular surgery board–style questions but achieved only moderate accuracy, and version updates did not meaningfully improve performance. Changes in consistency did not translate into higher correctness, and error profiling revealed model-specific limitations (external information retrieval versus logical reasoning). These results establish a specialty-specific baseline for longitudinal benchmarking and highlight the need for domain adaptation, expert oversight, and transparent reporting before LLMs can be used for vascular surgery education or decision support.
