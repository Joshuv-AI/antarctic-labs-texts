Ceci N’est Pas un Drone: Investigating the Impact of Design Representation on Design Decision Making When Using GenAI
Zeda Xu, Nikolas Martelaro, Christopher McComb

Abstract
With generative AI-powered design tools, designers and engineers can efficiently generate large numbers of design ideas. However, efficient exploration of these ideas requires designers to select a smaller group of potential solutions for further development. Therefore, the ability to judge and evaluate designs is critical for the successful use of generative design tools. Different design representation modalities can potentially affect designers’ judgments. This work investigates how different design modalities, including visual rendering, numerical performance data, and a combination of both, affect designers’ design selections from AI-generated design concepts for Uncrewed Aerial Vehicles. We found that different design modalities do affect designers’ choices. Unexpectedly, we found that providing only numerical design performance data can lead to the best ability to select optimal designs. We also found that participants prefer visually conventional designs with axis-symmetry. The findings of this work provide insights into the interaction between human users and generative design systems.

It may be critical to have design renderings and visualization presented for engineers to make accurate judgments. Nevertheless, it is also possible that the presence of design visualization introduces unwanted bias or design fixations, leading to sub-optimal design decisions.

That leads to our central research question:
Do different design modalities affect human decision-making behavior and their ability to make optimal selections when presented with AI-generated design solutions?
So far, there is insufficient evidence in the design and AI research community to answer this question or to explicitly and systematically examine the impact of design modalities on design decision-making. We hypothesize that different design modalities can affect engineers’ decision-making. We also hypothesize that using a design visualization (geometric representations of physical products) as a representation modality can negatively impact engineers’ ability to identify optimal and novel design solutions.

To test our hypotheses, we designed and conducted a within-subjects experiment across three studies targeting different populations to study whether and how different types of design representations, including design visualizations and numerical design performance data, affect engineers’ design choices and their ability to choose the optimal designs in a list of AI-generated design ideas. These three studies progressively build on one another to address emergent phenomena observed. During a single study, the participants individually considered three UAV (uncrewed aerial vehicles) design problems, where they were asked to select the most optimal drone design from the provided list of design options and briefly explain their choice. For the first study, we targeted drone hobbyists and drone pilots at a U.S. research university. During the first study, we observed that the number of design options provided could affect the participants’ choices. To further investigate the issue, we conducted the second study with STEM students at a U.S. research university through an engineering design related course. For the third study, we recruited aerospace engineers and designers from a large aerospace company in order to examine potential expert-novice differences. Combined, these experiments investigate whether and how different types of design representations affect engineers’ decision-making when working with generative-AI design tools.

Our results confirm both of our hypotheses. We found that different design modalities do affect engineers’ decision-making when using AI-powered generative design tools. Specifically, in this study, designers provided with numerical design performance data only lead to the best ability to select optimal designs, while only seeing design renderings provides marginal help in selecting optimal designs. Unexpectedly, when participants saw both the numerical design performance data and the design rendering, they performed more poorly compared to when they saw the numerical design performance data alone. However, we found that the participants prefer the best-performing designs
as long as
those designs possess traditional and symmetrical appearances. We also found in this study that the number of design options provided affects people’s ability to choose optimal designs, and a large number of design options can overwhelm engineers and lead to suboptimal design choices.

This paper contributes empirical evidence on how design representation modalities affect designers’ decisions when using AI-powered generative design tools, specifically through:

•

Empirical findings from three studies with drone hobbyists, STEM students, and aerospace professionals, showing that different representation modalities (visual rendering, numerical data, and visual rendering + numerical data) affect design choices with generative design tools.

•

Evidence suggesting that presenting only numerical performance data leads to the most accurate identification of optimal designs, while adding visual renderings can reduce accuracy. Also, a larger number of design choices overwhelms participants, reducing selection accuracy.

•

Analysis of human heuristics and preferences, revealing that designers prefer conventional, symmetrical designs with good performance.

3
Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we designed and conducted a within-subjects experiment across 3 populations to study whether and how different types of design representations, including design visualizations and numerical design performance data, affect engineers’ design choices and their ability to choose the optimal designs in a list of AI-generated design ideas.

3.1
Participants

In total, 160 participants were recruited, with 156 college students and 4 aerospace industry professionals. Out of the 156 recruited college students, 29 of them participated in study 1 as self-identified drone pilots and drone hobbyists from a U.S. research university. The other 127 of the college student participants in study 2 are from a sophomore-level engineering design course in the Department of Mechanical Engineering at the same U.S. research university. Participation was voluntary, and the participants were compensated with a $10 Amazon gift card. Participants recruited from the design course are also compensated with course credits. The 4 aerospace industry participants in study 3 were recruited from a major aerospace company. The ethnicity, age, and gender of the participants did not affect the recruitment process. All participants were over the age of 18 when recruited.

•

Among the 29 drone pilots and drone hobbyists participants in study 1, 11 identified as women, and 18 identified as men. Also, 7 drone hobbyist participants identified as White, 1 identified as Black or African American, 17 identified as Asian, 3 identified as other, and 1 preferred not to disclose their ethnicity.

•

Among the 127 STEM student participants in study 2, 62 identified as women, 57 identified as men, 2 identified as non-binary or third gender, 1 identified as other, and 5 preferred not to identify themselves. Also, 55 STEM student participants identified as White, 8 identified as Black or African American, 35 identified as Asian, 1 identified as Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, 20 identified as other, and 8 preferred not to disclose their ethnicity.

•

Among the 4 professional participants in study 3, all 4 of them identified as men. Also, 3 professional participants identified as White, and 1 identified as Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander.

3.2
Study Design

The university ethics review board approves human-subjects research, and they approved this project. Each of the three studies consisted of one online survey. In the survey, participants individually consider three UAV (uncrewed aerial vehicles) design problems. The design problems are similar in terms of difficulty and design scope, but with different design requirements (e.g., low cost versus low maintenance). A detailed problem description and design requirements for each design problem are provided in Appendix A.

The UAV designs used in the survey are from the AircraftVerse dataset developed by SRI International and Southwest Research Institute
21
. The UAV designs in this dataset are created using a proprietary generative AI system developed by SRI International
21
with real-world, off-the-shelf components with accurate physical properties. All the UAV designs used in this study are simulated for flight performance using a simulator developed for this work with the help of SRI International and Southwest Research Institute. We then documented the performance of each UAV design when conducting the tasks described in the design problems, enabling us to numerically determine the level of optimality of the design solutions presented to the participants.

The participants are asked to consider three design problems about UAVs with different design requirements. The three design problems are accompanied by three sets of design solutions. For each problem, they are tasked with selecting the best drone design and are also allowed to share the reasoning for their selection. The number of design solution options is 2, 8, and 16 for the three design problems, respectively. The 4-option case is used in training. The three sets of design solutions are the same for each experimental condition, but with different modalities, and are presented in random order. For each design problem, the three sets of design solutions are each presented by either 1) visual design rendering, 2) numerical design performance data with data visualization, or 3) visual design rendering plus numerical performance data and data visualization. The visual design rendering is presented with interactive 3D renderings of the UAV designs. The numerical design performance data is presented as tables, along with spider plots, which is a common practice in the aerospace industry. Figure

3.3
Procedure

The participants in all three studies completed the study using an online survey tool (Qualtrics). After giving consent, the participants begin the study and are instructed to consider three design problems about UAVs with different design requirements. For each design problem, the design options are shown in three different types of design representation modalities, namely, visual rendering, numerical performance data with data visualization, and visual rendering with numerical performance data and data visualization. The participants are informed that the designs are automatically generated by an AI system and that we need their expertise to evaluate and determine the feasibility of these solutions. The participants are also informed that the designs are tested in an advanced simulator, but they still need to utilize their engineering experience and knowledge to evaluate the designs, considering real-world scenarios. The study then asks the participant to choose the most optimal drone design from the provided list of design options (see Appendix B) with respect to several design restrictions. For each design selection, the survey also asks the participants to briefly explain their choice in the form of a textual response.

3.4
Measurement and Data Analysis

In this section, we outline the metrics and analytical procedures used to evaluate participants’ decision-making across experimental conditions. The analysis incorporates different quantitative measures, capturing the variability and optimality of participants’ design selections as well as the thematic content of their written explanations. Together, these measures provide a comprehensive view of participants’ performance, enabling assessment of both the outcomes of their choices and the underlying reasoning processes.

Multiple Choice Question Response Entropy

We first calculated the Shannon entropy of the choices for each design problem. A smaller entropy value corresponds to greater order and less randomness in the participants’ choices, suggesting the participants are making similar decisions instead of random choices. The entropy is calculated as

5
Discussion

5.1
Design modalities affect design decision making

We found that different design modalities appear to affect engineers’ decision-making, and that only using a visual design rendering as a design modality can negatively impact engineers’ ability to identify the optimal design solutions, when using AI-powered generative design tools.

The results of the entropy tests suggest that all three tested modalities can provide some extent of design information, and participants are making informed design decisions, resulting in non-random selections and non-uniform distributions. From the distribution of participants’ answers, there are differences between showing visual design rendering, numerical performance data, and visual design rendering with numerical performance data. The design modality affects participants’ design decision-making process and leads to different results.

The difference between different design modalities is more noticeable when looking at the participants’ choice of design for consecutive questions. For all three participant groups, most participants’ design choices changed when the design modality changed from visual rendering to numerical performance data. Despite the inconsistency across design problems, a significant portion of participants’ design choices also changed when the design modality changed from numerical performance data to the mixed modality with both visual rendering and numerical performance data. Since the design options are the same across all three modalities, the changes in participants’ choice when facing different design modalities indicate that the design modalities did affect participants’ decision-making. The participants change their minds and possibly select based on different factors when presented with different design modalities, even though the options are the same.

The difference between design modalities is also noticeable when looking at the participants’ ability to select the optimal designs. The three design modalities showed significantly different levels of benefits for selecting the Pareto-TOPSIS optimal design.

Overall, the numerical performance data of the designs gives the most helpful and accurate information for finding the optimal design options. The participants were able to find the optimal designs with much higher accuracy when shown only numerical performance data, compared to the other two modalities. This also shows people’s ability to read and interpret design information from data and spider plots, even though the spider plots face some criticism, including inconsistent areas and shapes caused by axis ordering, misleading area size, and deceptive importance of irrelevant options due to dimension normalization, from the data visualization research community
32
;
37
;
29
;
1
.

The results also suggest that visual renderings of the designs alone might provide the least helpful design information for determining the optimality of the given designs. Participants’ accuracy in selecting optimal designs is quite low. It might suggest that the participants gained little information from the visual rendering of the design alone, or that the visual renderings alone provide distorted design information and lead the participants to inaccurate conclusions (e.g., the propellers can appear smaller if the body of the UAV is larger). However, an alternative explanation is that the participants gained additional information from the visual renderings that are not captured by the performance data and our simulator (e.g., a design might not be easily serviceable, or it might not perform well in cross-winds). Therefore, the difference in accuracy in selecting the optimal designs for the visual rendering condition might suggest that engineers and designers can utilize human heuristics and their design experiences to gain insights that are not easily captured by current computational tools.

Interestingly, participant accuracy decreased when both the visual rendering and the numerical performance data were provided, compared to the numerical performance data only condition. It seems that the addition of visual information changed the participants’ minds, and they then made selections based on other factors. Participants’ textual responses suggest that they are considering and utilizing factors not explicitly shown with the numerical data. For example, in design problem 2 with 8 options, one of the aerospace professional participants (Study 3, P#01) in this study recognized the Pareto-TOPSIS optimal design based on the numerical performance data, but also pointed out the potential design flaws and infeasibility for real-life operations of that design. They then chose the designs with high reliability, stability, and practicality, which were factors they inferred from the visual rendering. Similar to the findings in the visual rendering condition, it is possible that the participants are getting additional information from the rendering that isn’t captured by the performance data. It could also mean the visual renderings are biasing people’s perceptions or causing design fixations. Future work should investigate further whether the addition of visual rendering is positively or negatively impacting designers’ decision-making.

5.2
Designers prefer conventional designs with good performance

In this study, participants showed clear preferences for conventional designs. Drone designs with conventional layouts and features have a significantly higher pick rate, namely, designs that are axis-symmetric and in which all propellers and the main body sit on one plane. Across all three participant pools, the participants showed a strong preference for conventional designs when they were provided with only visual renderings. Also, fewer participants picked the unusual designs when visual renderings became available, after transitioning from numerical performance data to the mixed modality.

Moreover, there is a significant difference in pick rates for designs with conventional features (axisymmetric and one-plane) versus designs with atypical features (non-axisymmetric, non-planar, or off-plane) for the visual rendering only condition (
t(24)=3.103,p=0.014,\text{Cohen's }d=1.458
). Participants picked more conventional axisymmetric and one-plane designs significantly more often than the other designs. Furthermore, the axisymmetricity seems to be the dominant factor, as there is a significant difference in pick rates for axisymmetric designs versus non-axisymmetric designs for the visual rendering only condition (
t(24)=2.512,p=0.023,\text{Cohen's }d=0.866
).
Participants prefer axisymmetric designs.
In addition, we found that a Rod Length to Propeller Area Ratio of 5 is a critical divider for participants’ preferences. The pick rates for designs with Rod Length to Propeller Area Ratio greater than 5 are significantly higher than those with Rod Length to Propeller Area Ratio less than 5 (
t(24)=2.840,p=0.017,\text{Cohen's }d=1.209
).

Looking at the changes in participants’ choice of design when the design modality changed from numerical performance data to the mixed modality with both visual rendering and numerical performance data, a noticeable number of participants moved away from the "unorthodox" designs with better performance, to pick designs with more conventional design features. An example is in design problem 1, where 55 out of 155 participants who chose the "unorthodox" design (design 18393) when shown only numerical performance data moved away and chose the more conventional design 16875 when presented with both visual rendering and numerical performance data (Figure
26
). Similarly, 21 and 14 out of 38 and 66 participants, who chose the more unusual design 16763 and design 20985, two of the most picked designs, when provided only numerical performance data, chose the more conventional design 15317 instead when presented with both visual rendering and numerical performance data (Figure
27
). These changes in participants’ choice of design when the design modality changed from numerical performance data to visual rendering + numerical performance data clearly show participants’ preference for conventional design features, despite the "unorthodox" designs having better performance data.

Participants’ textual responses for their reasons for the design choices also reflect their preferences for axis-symmetric and "reasonable-looking" designs. One participant in Study 2A said they “feel that symmetrical designs have a big impact on the hover time, so asymmetrical design D, design E, and design H were immediately ruled out” [Study 2A, P#57]. Participant #54 in Study 2B explained their design choice as “the rendering seems reasonable”. Another participant explained their preference for symmetrical design, as “symmetrical design would make manufacturing easier” [Study 2A, P#26]. Reliability can be another factor that drives the preference for symmetry, as one participant said “Design D combines symmetry with rotors close to the center of mass to create a reliable design” [Study 2A, P#18].

One participant in Study 2B clearly showed their preferences for "reasonable-looking" and good-performing design in their textual response, as they state “among the designs that look reasonable, Design K has the best statistics with a lower cost, and would probably work best for this scenario” [Study 2B, P#27]. They also doubted the designs with unconventional features despite the better performance data, saying “while Design C has the best statistics, I don’t think it would actually work in real life” [Study 2B, P#27]. Interestingly, there are also participants who have higher faith in the numerical data, “even though A looks strange, it outperforms B in every metric. There is a chance that its unorthodox design lends it some unique advantages” [Study 2B, P#08].

Overall, participants prioritize designs with conventional features, and they prefer the better-performing designs among those with conventional features.
Such design heuristics can be helpful for participants to identify the good performers, considering both simulation results and real-life scenarios. However, it can also mean that the participants are unknowingly or unintentionally omitting good-performing designs with atypical design features, leading to potential design fixation.

However, it is important to keep in mind that the performance data and the simulator used in this work may be inaccurate. Some real-life factors can be neglected, leading to an inaccurate evaluation of the drone design performance in real-life scenarios. The participants, however, may have considered these factors, which may have informed their design choice. This is further discussed in the limitations and future works subsection. Also, it is possible that the participants are aware of this imperfection during the studies, and therefore have less faith in the numerical performance data, leading to the preferences for conventional designs seen here.

5.3
Designer’s judgment ability is weakened when presented with a large number of options

From the two-way ANOVA test results on the interaction effect between the design modalities and the number of design options, we found that the number of design options provided can affect the participants’ accuracy in identifying the optimal designs. Since all three design problems have a single most optimal design (based on Pareto-TOPSIS), we compared the participants’ accuracy in selecting the optimal design across design problems with different numbers of design options. We found that, in all three modality conditions, the participants’ accuracy in selecting the optimal design decreases in the design problems with 8 or 16 design options, compared to the problems with only 2 options. This may suggest that with more potential design solutions provided, participants’ ability to determine the optimal design is weakened.

One possibility is that with fewer options shown, participants’ mental capacity is large enough to examine solutions and compare them in pairs throughout and simultaneously, leading to more comprehensive design interpretations. When a large number of candidate solutions are provided, e.g., with 8 or 16 options, the amount of design information that needs to be considered exceeds the mental capacity of the participants. The participants might be overwhelmed with potential options and the pair-wise comparisons when facing complex and challenging design requirements. Their abilities and willingness to explore more designs or more novel designs can be hindered. This finding resonates with research in working memory, which suggests that the capacity of human working memory is limited at a given time and is typically limited to 4 objects for
visual working memory and short-term memory

57
;
23
, although the classic research in working memory by George Armitage Miller argued a larger 7 objects with a range of plus or minus 2
63
. Future research should investigate this issue further, along with a more explicit examination of the impact of the number of design options offered on the designer’s decision-making.

5.4
Aerospace professionals might have better judgment abilities

Despite the small group size, the aerospace professionals who participated in this study might have shown a slight tendency toward superior ability to identify better designs.

Comparing the accuracy of selecting the optimal designs in the numerical performance data condition, the aerospace professionals have a higher accuracy on the design question with 8 design options, compared to the student population. Also, the aerospace professionals in this study showed a slightly stronger ability to identify the better design with only numerical performance data, on the design question with 2 design options. Interestingly, their ability to identify the better design with mixed modality (visual rendering + numerical data) is much inferior in comparison, on all three design questions.

Limited by the small sample size, we cannot conclusively determine the impact of participants’ technical and work backgrounds. However, our results might suggest that the technical and work backgrounds could affect participants’ preferences and decision-making when facing AI-generated designs. Future studies should further investigate the effect of technical background and field experience on designers’ ability to identify better designs.

5.5
Limitations and future works

This work faces several limitations. Firstly, the optimality of the design solutions in this work is evaluated with a physics-based simulator. Such a simulator is accurate regarding the physics phenomena and drone design features that are incorporated in the simulator. However, since no simulator considers all factors and scenarios that may occur in real life, many important factors can be neglected in the simulation results for real-life applications. Such imperfection can lead to an inaccurate evaluation of the drone design performance in real-life scenarios. Therefore, the simulation results used in this work and the performance evaluated for each drone design may not be perfectly accurate under the tasks described in the design problems. That means our evaluation of participants’ ability to select the optimal designs can be distorted. It is possible that certain participants conducted a more thorough and realistic evaluation of the designs than the simulator, but their design choice is deemed inferior in this study. This might lead to a seemingly poorer ability to select the optimal designs, while in reality, it is quite the opposite.

Secondly, this study only examined a very specific design problem with relatively high requirements for domain knowledge. Participants with prior UAV design, manufacturing, or flying experience will likely have a more comprehensive heuristic and can demonstrate a stronger ability to evaluate the UAV designs compared to those who don’t, potentially affecting the results. Future work should further investigate the impact of design modalities on more general design problems and other technical domains.

Thirdly, most participants in this study are current college students. Though it is a representative group to demonstrate the impact of different design modalities and experiment conditions, the participants may lack the field experience for some of the realistic technical challenges reflected in the design problems, which sometimes can only be obtained through years of industry-specific work experience.

Another limitation in this work lies in the relatively short length of the design decision-making process simulated here. Even though no hard limit was imposed, most participants finished the study within 40 minutes. Such a time span may not fully and accurately represent the extensive nature of real-world design challenges.

Furthermore, the numerical design performance data is presented along with spider plots, which are regarded as common practice in the aerospace industry. Since extensive research has been done to show that different styles of data visualization can affect the interpretation of visual information, future work should also examine the impact of different design visualization methods on design decision-making
73
;
22
;
66
.

Moreover, in this work, we observed that the number of design options provided in the study can alter participants’ ability to identify the optimal designs. It is worth investigating the impact of the number of design options on people’s design decision-making more directly in future studies. Besides, this work focuses more on the design decision-making results rather than the design decision-making process. The heuristics and reasoning behind the decisions made in the work under different conditions are briefly examined. Future work should further and more comprehensively study the influence of different design modalities on designers’ decision-making behavior and rationale.

Conclusion

This work examines the impact of different design modalities on the design decision-making process of engineers and designers. More specifically, we investigate whether design visualization affects engineers’ and designers’ ability to select the optimal designs from a list of AI-generated design ideas. We found that different design modalities do affect engineers’ decision-making when using AI-powered generative design tools. For the participants in this study, providing only the numerical design performance data leads to the best accuracy in selecting the most optimal design, while only seeing design renderings provides marginal help in selecting optimal designs. We found that presenting both the numerical design performance data and the design rendering results in worse accuracy compared to seeing the numerical performance data alone, suggesting that engineers can leverage design heuristics when providing the design visualization, or, alternatively, the presence of design visualization can induce design biases and fixations. In addition, we found that the participants generally prefer the best-performing designs
as long as
those designs possess traditional and symmetrical appearances. Also, we note that the number of design options provided affects people’s ability to choose optimal designs in this study, and a large number of design options can overwhelm engineers and lead to suboptimal design choices. This work deepens our understanding of how people interact with generative AI design systems, and can guide the future development and implementation of design comparison tools used with generative AI systems.

Design Problem

Imagine you are a lead design engineer working in an engineering consulting company designing UAVs tailored to customer needs. Your team uses an AI-powered automatic design generation system to help ideate and create initial design solutions. These solutions have been tested in a newly developed computer simulation environment. Your simulation teams assure you that the simulator is the best in the business and that the simulated performance data is accurate. However, as an engineer, you still need to use your expertise and engineering knowledge to inspect and evaluate the designs while considering real-world scenarios and pick the best design for further physical testing and validation before delivering it to the customers.

Here is your task:

Pennsylvania’s fire department is using UAVs to monitor wildfires. The UAV should have great hover time (maintain target position with minimal deviation) and carry at least 10 kg (22.0 lbs) of monitoring equipment, including RGB cameras, IR cameras, and other sensors. The UAV must rise to a designated height at a fast vertical lift speed. Without sacrificing hover time, more carrying capacity is desirable for carrying more equipment for better coverage. The UAV must also fly steadily and sustain cross-wind and other potential environmental hazards for safety reasons. There is no requirement for maximum travel distance and travel speed.

Important Design Information:
The best design refers to the design you deem most optimal considering all factors.
Hover time is not air time. Hover time means the UAV is hovering at the target position in mid-air.
The designs are not presented in any particular order. There is no correlation between the designs’ order and their performance. The designs are only presented in the order in which they are generated by the AI.
This is an AI-generative system. The design may or may not work in real life.
Please use your engineering knowledge and judgment. Consider all factors, including external ones, that may not have been considered and simulated by the AI.
