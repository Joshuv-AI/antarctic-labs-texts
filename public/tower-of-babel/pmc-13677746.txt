Dynamical signatures of conventional and asymptotic quantum many-body scars on a trapped ion simulator
Authors: Leonard Logarić, John Goold, Shane Dooley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the promising applications of digital quantum processors is the simulation of many-body quantum systems. They have been already used to investigate several ergodicity violating mechanisms, such as many-body localisation, Hilbert space fragmentation and quantum many-body scars (QMBS). In addition to conventional QMBS, a recently discovered mechanism for ergodicity violation are the so-called asymptotic quantum many-body scars (AQMBS). These become more stable as system size is increased, leading to progressively longer thermalisation timescales. In this work, we show a connection between gapless excitations of certain “reference” Hamiltonians and AQMBS in a related set of Hamiltonian and circuit models. We use this connection to construct a 2-local model hosting both conventional and asymptotic scars. By exploiting the structure of the AQMBS states and the all-to-all connectivity of the Quantinuum H1-1 quantum processor, we prepare these states in logarithmic circuit depth, and probe their thermalisation under Floquet circuit dynamics. Performing simulations with up to 418 entangling ZZ gates, we find slower thermalisation times as the system size is increased, providing experimental signatures of AQMBS.Subject terms: Quantum simulation, Theoretical physics

Introduction

Rapid advances in the laboratory have made it possible to experimentally explore the unitary dynamics of many-body quantum systems. In particular, state-of-the-art quantum simulators, including platforms based on Rydberg atoms1, superconducting circuits2 and trapped ions3, now routinely achieve high-fidelity preparation of entangled states, programmable interactions, and dynamics with relatively large circuit depth. These capabilities make it possible to investigate fundamental questions, such as the mechanisms responsible for thermalisation, or failure to thermalise, in isolated many-body systems4–7.

A phenomenon which has garnered significant attention is weak ergodicity breaking, in which a non-integrable system thermalises for almost all initial states but exhibits long-lived nonthermal dynamics for a restricted set of initial states. This is due to the presence of quantum many-body scars (QMBS): a small number of atypical eigenstates embedded within an otherwise thermalising many-body spectrum8–12. When an initial state overlaps significantly with these anomalous eigenstates, the ensuing time evolution shows dynamical signatures of the QMBS that can be measured experimentally, such as long-lived coherent oscillations or anomalously slow decay of observables13–17.

Very recently, the existence of so-called asymptotic QMBS (AQMBS) was also proven theoretically18. These are special states, which are not energy eigenstates of a system at finite size, but have a vanishing energy variance in the thermodynamic limit, and are non-thermal from the perspective of the eigenstate thermalisation hypothesis (ETH). Due to these properties, they emerge as QMBS only in the thermodynamic limit. As a consequence, AQMBS have the unusual dynamical property that they have relaxation times that diverge with increasing system size.

Both QMBS and AQMBS have been shown theoretically to arise in a wide variety of many-body models19,20. Nevertheless, experimental realisations of QMBS remain confined to a narrow class of systems13–17, most notably those effectively described by PXP-like Hamiltonians13–16, while no experiment to date has reported observations of AQMBS. Bridging this gap between theory and experiment is challenging because many known constructions of QMBS rely either on local Hilbert spaces of dimension greater than two (such as spin-1 models including the XY magnet11,21–23) or on interaction terms that extend beyond simple two-body couplings (such as the multi-body constraints underlying the PXP-like models8,16,24). By contrast, the most widely available ingredients across current quantum simulation platforms are local Hilbert spaces of dimension two (i.e., qubits) with two-body interactions. An experimental protocol for realising a single conventional QMBS in such models has been proposed in ref. 25.

In this work, we outline a general theoretical construction for models with QMBS and AQMBS as a deformation of a reference Hamiltonian. Using this construction, we introduce a model on a chain of qubits with experimentally realisable nearest-neighbour two-body interactions that hosts both QMBS and AQMBS. The model is parametrised by a complex variable g, and hosts a QMBS that is an edge-localised state: for ∣g∣ ≫ 1 it is confined to the left boundary of the chain, for ∣g∣ ≪ 1 it localises at the right boundary, and at the critical point ∣g∣ = 1 it becomes fully delocalised across the chain. At this critical point we also observe AQMBS, whose existence can be directly linked to gapless excitations of the reference Hamiltonian, following a connection first identified in ref. 19. A key advantage of our model is its straightforward implementation on current digital quantum simulators. We demonstrate this explicitly using the Quantinuum H1-1 trapped-ion quantum computer and probe the dynamics of the total magnetisation starting from several classes of initial states: conventional QMBS, non-QMBS, AQMBS, and local edge excitations. As expected, non-QMBS initial states exhibit rapid thermalisation, while dynamics initiated in a conventional QMBS display a markedly slower decay that is essentially independent of system size. By contrast, when the system is initialised in an AQMBS, the relaxation time increases with system size as the initial state approaches a stationary eigenstate, providing the first experimental evidence for AQMBS.

Results

General theoretical framework

In this section, we present a framework for identifying models that host conventional as well as asymptotic QMBS. Consider a system of N qudits (i.e., particles with Hilbert space dimension d), labelled n ∈ {0, 1, …, N − 1}, arranged on a D-dimensional lattice Λ. We also introduce a set of local interactions {H^Xn}n=0N−1, where each H^Xn is a Hermitian operator acting non-trivially only on a finite number of qudits in a local neighbourhood Xn ⊂ Λ of the qudit n. These local interactions serve as the fundamental building block for both circuit and time-independent Hamiltonian models: in the circuit setting, they generate local unitary gates U^Xn=exp(iH^Xn), which are composed into a many-body circuit unitary U^, while in the Hamiltonian setting they define a many-body Hamiltonian H^=∑nH^Xn.

Theorem 1

If the reference Hamiltonian H^+≡∑nP^Xn is frustration-free, that is, if a ground state ∣G⟩ is simultaneously a ground state of each individual projector P^Xn, then G remains an exact eigenstate of any corresponding circuit or Hamiltonian model constructed with the more general local interactions H^Xn=P^Xnh^XnP^Xn.

The proof is straightforward: A ground state G of the projectors obeys P^XnG=0, which implies that it is also a ground state of the reference Hamiltonian (H^+G=0), since it is non-negative (H^+≥0). It follows that H^XnG=P^Xnh^XnP^XnG=0 for any choice of h^Xn. Therefore, any many-body circuit or Hamiltonian constructed from the interactions H^Xn has G as an eigenstate. In such cases, G typically appears as a QMBS rather than as a ground state. In other words, as we deform the reference Hamiltonian H^+ into either the non-positive Hamiltonian H^ or the circuit model U^, the state G is transformed from a ground state to a QMBS.

Suppose that the reference Hamiltonian H^+ has gapless excitations, which become zero-energy ground states only asymptotically, in the thermodynamic limit. One might expect that such states become QMBS in the thermodynamic limit, i.e., are AQMBS, as was conjectured in ref. 29. This intuition is supported by the following result, which we prove in Supplementary Note I:

Theorem 2

If the reference Hamiltonian H^+=∑nP^Xn has positive-energy eigenstates ∣A⟩ for finite N that become zero-energy ground states in the thermodynamic limit H^+A→N→∞0 (e.g., gapless excitations), then the corresponding finite-depth circuit U^ or Hamiltonian model H^ constructed from the interactions H^Xn=P^Xnh^XnP^Xn will host AQMBS ∣A⟩.

In other words, the AQMBS A constructed in this way have an energy variance that decreases to zero in the thermodynamic limit (with respect to the Hamiltonian H^ or an effective Hamiltonian H^eff≡ilogU^ in the circuit case). While the conventional QMBS state G will be stationary for time evolution generated by the Hamiltonian H^ or the circuit U^ for any system size N, the AQMBS state A will approach a stationary state only as the system size N increases. More precisely, since the energy variance of A decreases with N, the fidelity relaxation time will increase due to the Mandelstam-Tamm bound18,30. As a consequence, the thermalisation of local observables, such as the total magnetisation, will be suppressed as the system size increases, which is an experimentally observable signature of AQMBS. Moreover, we show in Supplementary Note I that AP^XnA→N→∞0 for all n. This is a non-thermal expectation value of the local projector P^Xn, indicating that the ETH is violated by A in the thermodynamic limit, as it should for a genuine AQMBS.

Specifically, for our goal of constructing a model that is amenable to experiment on existing digital quantum simulators, we would like to find a reference Hamiltonian for a qubit system (d = 2) that is built exclusively from local two-qubit projector interactions P^Xn. We note that our general prescription complements prior work, presented in refs. 19,20,31, with differences to the present method discussed in Supplemetary Note II. We also note that the deformation P^Xn→P^Xnh^XnP^Xn can be related to Witten’s conjugation32,33. In the latter, the ground state(-s) G are required to remain ground state(-s) of the deformed model, which is satisfied provided that the operators h^Xn are positive semidefinite. However, for G to be QMBS in the middle of the energy spectrum, we generally desire h^Xn which are not positive semi-definite.

Model and experimental setup

Although Theorems 1 and 2 apply to both Hamiltonian and circuit models, throughout this work, we focus primarily on circuit models because of their convenience for experimental implementation on available digital quantum simulators. Experimental results

First, we would like to experimentally observe the product QMBS V=0⊗N on the trapped-ion simulator. Since it is an eigenstate of our Floquet unitary, in principle, it is a stationary state of the dynamics, and any observable evolving from that initial state will be stationary, thus failing to thermalise. The product QMBS V is easily prepared as an initial state on the trapped-ion device. In Fig. 2a, we plot the experimental data, corresponding to evolution of the total magnetisation ⟨M^Z(t)⟩≡∑n⟨Z^n(t)⟩ for this initial state, as a function of the number of Floquet cycles t for system sizes N = 8, 12, 16, 20. For comparison, we also show results for the initial product state 1⊗N, which rapidly decays towards ⟨M^Z(t)⟩≈0, consistent with each local subsystem thermalising to its infinite-temperature density matrix. The very slow decay from the QMBS initial state, compared to the rapid thermalisation from the non-QMBS initial state, is a clear signature of the conventional QMBS V=0⊗N, which persists in spite of the experimental errors. We note that increasing the system size leads to greater accumulated errors, primarily due to the increased number of entangling gates, which is observed in the slightly increased rate of decay of the magnetisation.

Next, we would like to observe dynamical signatures of the boundary QMBS ∣B(g)⟩ on the trapped-ion simulator. Instead of preparing the boundary QMBS as an initial state, we prepare L=1⊗0⊗(N−1), which is much easier experimentally and has a large overlap with the boundary QMBS when ∣g∣ ≫ 1 and a small overlap when ∣g∣ ≲ 1. Time-evolution starting from the initial state L should therefore have a large component that is approximately stationary when ∣g∣ ≫ 1, with the approximation improving as ∣g∣ increases. We therefore investigate how the site-resolved and total magnetisation evolve for different g values when evolving from the initial state L, with the results shown in Fig. 2b, d. We probe the dynamics for N = 20 qubits, g∈{23,1,32,2}, up to t = 10 time-steps. Figure 2(d) shows the experimental data corresponding to the local expectation values ⟨Z^n(t)⟩ as they evolve in space n and time t. For ∣g∣ ≲ 1 the excitation, initially localised at n = 0, spreads rapidly through the system. However, for g = 2 the excitation remains well localised, due to its large overlap with the boundary QMBS. Interestingly, at g = 1, where the initial state has a small overlap ∣⟨L∣B(g=1)⟩∣=1/N with the completely delocalised QMBS, we observe a strong signal along the “light-cone”, showing transport of the spin excitation close to the maximum possible velocity. In Fig. 2b we also show the evolution of the total magnetisation ⟨M^Z(t)⟩. The decay of the observable slows as g is increased, as a result of the increasing overlap with the stationary boundary QMBS. Taken together, the experimental results in Fig. 2b, d are strong evidence of the presence of the boundary QMBS in the Floquet circuit model.

Finally, to experimentally explore AQMBS in our model, we would like to be able to prepare Ak as an initial state on the trapped-ion device. While the product QMBS V and state L used to probe the boundary QMBS can be constructed almost trivially on any digital simulator, the preparation of the AQMBS requires a more complex state preparation procedure. Fortunately, because they are constrained to the single excitation subspace, Ak can be prepared in logarithmic circuit depth on devices with all-to-all connectivity. The state preparation protocol, for N = 8, is shown in Fig. 1c. This construction can be easily generalised to any N = 2k, requiring ~ N − 1 entangling gates and a depth of ~log2N=k. For N ≠ 2k, the state preparation circuit depth will be ~log2N. These state preparation circuits are described in more detail in Supplementary Note V, both for general N as well as the specific circuits deployed in our experiments.

In our experiment, we prepare the AQMBS A1, and observe the subsequent dynamics for a range of system sizes N ∈ {8, 12, 16, 20}, up to t = 10 time-steps. The total magnetisation is shown in Fig. 2c. For N = 8, 12 we see strong agreement between the classical simulation and experimental results. For greater system sizes, N = 16, 20, we see a more noticeable deviation due to greater accumulation of errors, as already observed for the V state. Despite the experimental errors, one can still observe slower thermalisation as the system size is increased, a distinct feature of AQMBS states. This should be contrasted with the lack of thermalisation for V and the rapid decay in the case of 1⊗N, both of which are independent of system size. As N is increased, the state A1 approaches a stationary state. This is, to the best of our knowledge, the first direct experimental evidence for AQMBS.

Discussion

To summarise, we first showed a direct relationship between gapless excitations in a frustration-free (or, alternatively, asymptotically frustration-free) reference Hamiltonian and the existence of AQMBS states in a related family of Hamiltonian and circuit models. Based on this, we constructed a 2-local model hosting both exact and asymptotic QMBS states. One of the QMBS states displays edge localisation and becomes fully delocalised over the system at ∣g∣ = 1. At this critical value, we also observe AQMBS states, corresponding to gapless excitations in the reference Hamiltonian, where the exact QMBS states are embedded as ground states.

The specific features of the model were deliberately constructed to make digital quantum simulation feasible. We exploited the freedom in the 2-local unitaries to find efficient gate decompositions, and the structure of the probed AQMBS state A1 to prepare it in logarithmic depth. Performing simulations starting from A1 at various system sizes on the Quantinuum H1-1 trapped ion simulator, we observe longer thermalisation timescales as N is increased. This is a direct experimental detection of anomalous thermalisation due to AQMBS states, which was made possible by the connectivity features and sufficiently low error rates of the used quantum processing unit. Furthermore, we also observed direct evidence of the product scar V and the edge localised scar state ∣B(g)⟩.

This work opens up several research pathways. We have considered a relatively simple model, with solely nearest neighbour interactions in one dimension, in order to facilitate the digital quantum simulation. However, we expect that the same arguments could be generalised to higher-order interactions of similar form, as well as to two or three spatial dimensions38, giving rise to a wide family of experimentally amenable models that could potentially host AQMBS states. Furthermore, for some sets of local generators, we can observe an exponential degeneracy in the middle of the spectrum, which might include additional QMBS states, as found in the spin-1 XY model39. On the experimental side, with more resources, it would also be possible and of great interest to investigate the fidelity decay or growth of Rényi entropies from AQMBS via randomised measurement techniques40,41. Finally, we have demonstrated the direct link between AQMBS states and gapless excitations in a relatively simple reference Hamiltonian. This connection may also be exploited in other candidate reference Hamiltonians such as the ferromagnetic Heisenberg model, Fredkin and Motzkin spin chains42–44, and the Rokhsar-Kivelson Hamiltonian45,46. The AQMBS in the deformed PVBS model have low entanglement entropy, consistent with the area-law behaviour expected for generic low-lying energy excitations47,48. However, the existence of AQMBS with volume-law entanglement remains an interesting possibility, particularly in light of recently identified atypical QMBS states with such entanglement properties49–52.

When running jobs on the device, we have used S = 100 shots for the initial states V and 1⊗N, corresponding to the results shown in Fig. 2a. For the initial state L, we have used S = 100 shots when performing simulations with g=12,1,32,2 and S = 70 shots for simulations with g=23. The decreased shot count for the latter value g is due to constraints on our access to the device. The obtained results are shown in Fig. 2b, d. Lastly, whenever starting in the AQMBS state A1, we have used S = 200 shots, with the obtained results shown in Fig. 2c. The error bars in Fig. 2a–c were obtained using σ/S, where σ is the sample standard deviation.

By default, the qubit register is always initialised in 0⊗N. Hence, when performing simulations for the QMBS state V=0⊗N, we do not have to perform any state preparation prior to the time evolution. The probed non-scar state 1⊗N can be prepared in a straightforward way, by applying the X^ (which is equivalent, up to a global phase, to U1q(θ = π, ϕ = 0)) operation on all of the qubits. Similarly, in order to prepare L=1⊗0⊗N−1, we apply X^ on the first qubit in the chain.

In order to decompose the 2-qubit unitaries that we want to execute on the device, U^e/o(g)=exp[iP^(g)h^e/oP^(g)], into the decomposition presented in Fig. 1 (d), we use pytket, version 2.7.0, obtaining the gate parameters displayed in Table 1. We will denote the unitaries corresponding to these decompositions by V^e/o(g) and define the gate infidelity as: r(U^,V^)=1−F(U^,V^),F(U^,V^)=Tr[U^V^†]2/d2, where d is the local Hilbert space dimension53–55. Using these, we find that the compilation errors for the used 2-qubit gates are r(U^e(g),V^e(g))=4×10−16 and r(U^o(g),V^o(g))=2×10−16, which are on the order of floating point precision. These errors are negligible in contrast to errors due to the gate infidelities, as reported in the Ion trap processor details.

Classical methods

The results presented in the main text, obtained via classical methods, were computed either by exact brute force methods implemented in Python’s numpy library56, for system sizes up to N = 12, whereas for greater system sizes, the Julia package ITensor was used57.
