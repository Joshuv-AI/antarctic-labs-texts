Complementary roles of the human perirhinal and the entorhinal cortex in the hippocampal retrieval of episodic memory
Authors: Ji Sun Kim, Jae-Min Seol, Choong-Hee Lee, Inah Lee, Sang Ah Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEpisodic memory involves the representation of memories shared across a global context as well as that of unique events that occurred within it. To examine how parahippocampal subregions support this balance, we had participants encounter multiple objects in each building of a virtual environment and later asked them to recall the building associated with each cued object during fMRI scanning. Across the MTL, objects from the same building showed greater representational similarity than those from different buildings, indicating the integration of contextually associated memories. This effect was observed only for task-relevant contexts (i.e., buildings but not corners). Higher similarity in the EC and lower similarity in the PrC were associated with performance, and integration in the EC mediated the role of CA3DG in memory retrieval. These findings extend current computational models of how the MTL represents context-dependent memories and demonstrate the complementary and opposing roles of the PrC and EC in supporting memory integration and differentiation.Subject areas: Health sciences

Introduction

Episodic memory allows us to form structured representations of our experiences by binding objects, places, and events into coherent memories. A hallmark of this system is its ability to form associations between events that occur within the same context (integration) while still differentiating across those unique events. These complementary processes are essential, for instance, in organizing memories of various objects encountered across different environments and require a coordinated activity of subregions in the medial temporal lobe (MTL). Among the MTL subregions, the hippocampus has been most widely studied for its role in binding elements of events within a spatiotemporal context.1,2,3,4 Its subfields provide complementary mechanisms to support this process: First, the dentate gyrus (DG) performs pattern separation to distinguish similar experiences,5,6,7,8,9 which is vital for object recognition and spatial memory. The CA3 subfield uses its sparse connectivity with the DG and recurrent network to support both pattern separation and completion,10,11,12,13 allowing it to form and retrieve object-place paired associative memories.13,14,15 Finally, CA1 integrates the inputs from CA3 and entorhinal cortex (EC), serving as a comparator and integrative hub that enables flexible retrieval and generalization.5,16,17

It has also been demonstrated that the parahippocampal subregions play a critical role in representing distinct types of information that guide hippocampus-dependent episodic memory both during encoding and retrieval.18,19,20,21 The parahippocampal gyrus encompasses several cytoarchitecturally distinct subregions: the perirhinal cortex (PrC), entorhinal cortex (EC), and parahippocampal cortex (PhC) — with the PhC referring to the posterior portion of the gyrus and considered the human analog of the postrhinal cortex described in animal models. According to classical models of MTL function, the PrC specializes in object (“what”) recognition, the PhC primarily processes spatial (“where”) information, and the two pathways converge in the EC before entering the hippocampus.22,23,24,25,26 Supporting this view, neurons in the EC have been shown to respond to both objects and spatial information,27,28 consistent with its proposed role in constructing integrated representations utilized by the hippocampus to support object-context associations and flexible retrieval. However, more recent evidence suggests that this two-stream model of episodic memory is not entirely accurate and that contextual information may already be represented in the PrC and PhC before being combined in the EC.29,30

While much of the literature has focused on the EC as a feedforward gateway to the hippocampus, anatomical studies have shown that the hippocampus also projects back to the EC, particularly to the deep layers.27,31,32,33 However, the functional significance of hippocampal output to the EC in human memory has been comparatively underexplored. Recent evidence suggests that hippocampal-entorhinal feedback supports object memory and memory-based decision making,34,35,36,37 pointing to a model in which associative computations are performed within the hippocampus and then expressed cortically via the EC during retrieval.

In contrast, the PrC, located between the sensory association cortex and the hippocampal formation, is well-positioned to support the differentiation of overlapping memories by representing fine-grained distinctions between objects.38,39,40,41,42,43 Beyond its role in object recognition, emerging evidence suggests that PrC incorporates contextual signals in representing content-specific information. Unlike other object-processing regions in the ventral visual stream, PrC activity is modulated by spatial context and goal-related task demands, suggesting that it flexibly bridges perceptual and mnemonic processes.30,44,45,46,47,48,49,50 These findings suggest that the PrC may play a key role in maintaining item specificity within context-rich episodic memories.

In the present study, we aimed to investigate how MTL subregions contribute to memory organization through complementary processes of integration and differentiation. We hypothesized that distinct parahippocampal regions support these computations—some by integrating across shared contexts, others by differentiating across objects—and that these signals interact within the hippocampal subfields to support episodic recall that preserves both spatially coherent and object-specific memories. Given previous studies showing that objects sharing a context (e.g., spatial or temporal) become similar in their neural activation patterns in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex,45,48,51,52,53,54,55,56,57,58 as well as studies suggesting that the PrC makes fine-grained distinctions among semantically or perceptually similar objects,59,60,61,62,63,64 we focused on the PrC and EC as our main regions of interest. Moreover, we aimed to gain insight into how associated memories in the hippocampus (e.g., CA3) might affect the subsequent representations of objects and contexts in upstream regions such as the PrC and EC.

To test this, we used a virtual navigation paradigm in which participants learned associations between objects and four different buildings in a plus-maze environment and later retrieved those associations during fMRI scanning.65 Within each building, objects were placed in four distinct corners, allowing us to further compare the importance of task relevant (i.e., buildings) vs. irrelevant (i.e., corners within a building) spatial contexts on such representations. Using fMRI pattern similarity analysis, we measured how neural similarity varied across MTL subregions as a function of shared context, both during object-cueing and active object-place response phases (Figures 1A–1C, see STAR Methods). Together, this approach enabled us to examine how the MTL organizes episodic memory by both integrating and differentiating across object-place associations.

Experimental design and behavior

(A) The experiment was conducted across two consecutive days. On Day 1, participants learned the association between object and place (building) and performed an object recognition task outside the scanner. Only those individuals who performed above 70% accuracy were brought back for Day 2. On Day 2, participants performed the retrieval task inside the scanner.

(B) The virtual environment comprises a plus-maze surrounded by different distal landmarks (green hill, farm, lake, and mountain) with four identical buildings at the end of each arm. The identical appearance of the buildings was intended to ensure that spatial differentiation among buildings could only be achieved through surrounding environmental cues, thereby maximally engaging hippocampal-dependent spatial navigation mechanisms. Each building consisted of four corners with five objects. The building walls were transparent; thus, distal landmarks were visible from each corner. N: North, E: East, S: South, W: West.

(C) The retrieval task consisted of an object-cueing phase, followed by an object-place response phase, ending with a spatial navigation phase in which participants simply drove themselves to the previously chosen building.

(D) The memory performance index was quantified as accuracy divided by RT. The resulting values were z-transformed, and the signs were inverted so that higher scores indicate better performance. The vertical dotted line indicates the average memory performance score across participants.

Results

Successful learning of object-place paired associations

On Day 1, all participants successfully learned to identify the old and new objects during the object recognition task with accuracy above chance level (84.6%, compared to 50% chance; t(21) = 23.352, p < 0.001). On Day 2, most participants successfully identified the target building when cued by an object with above-chance accuracy (58.1%, compared with 25% chance level; t(21) = 7.922, p < 0.001; see Figure S1 for average accuracy for each object, separately), within the average of 5.30 (standard deviation: 0.72) seconds, from which memory performance score was calculated (see STAR Methods; Figure 1D). Four subjects who scored below 30% accuracy in object-cued building retrieval were excluded from further analysis. All participants successfully navigated toward the previously selected building (94.1% against chance of 25%; t(21) = 42.308, p < 0.001; RT: 10.98 (standard deviation: 1.56) seconds). Thus, the neural activation pattern in this phase was used to extract stable building representations to test for context reinstatement during memory retrieval (see Figure 2).

Task-relevant contextual reinstatement during memory retrieval in the medial temporal lobe

Across all MTL regions (Figure 2A), a robust reinstatement of the associated building context (Figure 2B) was found during both the object-cueing phase (Figure 2C; PrC: t(21) = 11.196, p < 0.001; PhC: t(21) = 11.805, p < 0.001; EC: t(21) = 16.456, p < 0.001; CA1: t(21) = 17.810, p < 0.001; CA3DG: t(21) = 21.392, p < 0.001) and the object-place response phase (Figure 2D; PrC: t(21) = 11.737, p < 0.001; PhC: t(21) = 11.636, p < 0.001; EC: t(21) = 10.259, p < 0.001; CA1: t(21) = 14.989, p < 0.001; CA3DG: t(21) = 13.531, p < 0.001; see Figure S3 for a comparison between same vs. different context pattern similarity, separately). These results suggest that the building context was reinstated across the MTL subregions, starting from the object-cueing period when participants may have retrieved the associations. To test for possible effects of perceptual similarity, we repeated the analyses for regions in the visual cortex (lateral occipital cortex and cuneus). As expected, context reinstatement in the lateral occipital cortex and cuneus was not significant for both the object-cueing phase (lateral occipital cortex: t(21) = −0.819, p = 0.422; cuneus: t(21) = 0.587, p = 0.563) and object-place response phase (lateral occipital cortex: t(21) = −1.902, p = 0.071; cuneus: t(21) = −1.156, p = 0.261).

Memory integration occurs only across task-relevant spatial contexts

To determine whether representations of objects are integrated based on their shared spatial context, we defined the integration index as the difference in pattern similarity between objects associated with the same building and those associated with different buildings (Figure 3A). During the object-cueing phase, the PhC showed a marginally positive integration index (t(21) = 1.957, p = 0.064), while no other ROIs reached significance (Figure 3B). There was no significant difference in the integration index of the left and right hemispheres for all subregions (Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4). During the ensuing object-place response phase, in which participants actively retrieved the spatial context associated with the object, all MTL ROIs exhibited a significantly positive integration index (PrC: t(21) = 2.082, p = 0.050; PhC: t(21) = 3.007, p = 0.007; EC: t(21) = 3.858, p < 0.001; CA1: t(21) = 2.511, p = 0.020; CA3DG: t(21) = 3.850, p < 0.001; Figure 3C; see Figure S4 for a comparison between same vs. different context pattern similarity, separately). Again, there was no significant hemispheric difference for all subregions (Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4).

To assess whether contextual memory integration was modulated by task-relevance, we further compared the integration index across shared buildings (task-relevant) with shared corners (task-irrelevant). We found significantly higher levels of context-based integration for buildings than corners in the PhC (t(42) = 2.086, p = 0.043) and CA3DG (t(42) = 2.279, p = 0.027) during object-cueing, and in the PhC (t(42) = 2.692, p = 0.010), CA1 (t(42) = 2.314, p = 0.025), and CA3DG (t(42) = 3.211, p = 0.002) during the object-place response phase. Interestingly, during the object-cueing phase, the PrC (t(42) = −2.118, p = 0.046) and CA3DG (t(42) = −3.562, p = 0.002) showed a significantly negative integration index for shared objects from the same corner, possibly reflecting the separation of memory representations across different objects (Figures 3B and 3C; see Figure S5 for a comparison with integration index for corners of different buildings that shared common landmark views).

Finally, to determine whether trial-wise integration index predicted retrieval success, we performed binary generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) analyses comparing correct and incorrect trials based on the task-relevant building context. During object-cueing, pattern similarity significantly predicted correctness in the EC (β = 2.535, SE = 1.015, z = 2.498, p = 0.013), and CA1 (β = 3.246, SE = 1.150, z = 2.822, p = 0.005) and showed a trend in the PhC (β = 2.020, SE = 1.177, z = 1.716, p = 0.087), while effects in the PrC, and CA3DG were nonsignificant (PrC: β = −0.443, SE = 0.761, z = −0.583, p = 0.560; CA3DG: β = −0.374, SE = 1.027, z = −0.364, p = 0.716). During the object-place response phase, correctness was again significantly associated with pattern similarity in the EC (β = 2.078, SE = 1.001, z = 2.075, p = 0.038), PhC (β = 2.835, SE = 1.156, z = 2.453, p = 0.014), CA1 (β = 4.457, SE = 1.296, z = 3.440, p < 0.001), and CA3DG (β = 2.515, SE = 0.998, z = 2.519, p = 0.012), but not in the PrC (β = 0.229, SE = 0.761, z = −0.583, p = 0.560). These results indicate that MTL-based memory integration preferentially occurs for task-relevant spatial contexts and that trial-wise pattern similarity within the EC and hippocampal subfields predicts successful retrieval.

Opposing patterns in the perirhinal cortex and entorhinal cortex are associated with better memory

Building on the finding that the MTL selectively integrates task-relevant spatial contexts, we next asked whether variability in the degree of contextual integration was functionally linked to memory performance. During the object-cueing phase, we observed a functional dissociation between the parahippocampal regions. In the left PrC, stronger memory performance was linked to less integration (r = −0.485, p = 0.022; bilateral PrC: r = −0.392, p = 0.071), suggesting that this region may help discriminate across similar object memories. In the EC, on the other hand, higher integration was correlated with better memory (r = 0.491, p = 0.020), pointing to its role in representing global spatial contexts (Figure 4A). Despite not having significant differences between the left and right hemispheres, the effect was stronger in the left for the PrC and right for the EC (left vs. right: PrC: F(1,19) = 2.047, p = 0.169; EC: F(1,19) = 1.973, p = 0.176; Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4). A significant correlation between memory performance and difference in integration indices (i.e., contrast score) between the bilateral PrC and EC (r = 0.4484; p = 0.026; Figure 4C; Table 5) provided further evidence that the PrC and EC serve complementary and opposing roles in contextual episodic memory. No other significant correlations were observed in the remaining ROIs (PhC: r = −0.012, p = 0.654; CA1: r = −0.088, p = 0.697; CA3DG: r = 0.020, p = 0.914; Figure 4A; Table 5; see Figure S7 for correlation between memory performance and same vs. different context pattern similarity, separately).

In contrast, during the object-place response phase, integration indices based on the task-relevant building context were uniformly positive across all MTL regions and did not significantly correlate with memory performance (PrC: r = 0.028, p = 0.902; PhC: r = 0.100, p = 0.658; EC: r = −0.187, p = 0.406; CA1: r = 0.192, p = 0.392; CA3DG: r = −0.129, p = 0.579), suggesting that an increase in representational similarity based on task-related spatial context consistently occurred across participants during the active retrieval of object-place association (Figure 4B; Table 4).

To directly test the extent to which the functional dissociation across the parahippocampal subregions reflects their coordinated memory processing, we conducted a trial-wise functional connectivity analysis between the PrC and EC (Figure 4D).59 We found that individuals with lower functional connectivity between PrC and EC had better memory performance (r = −0.483, p = 0.023; Table 5), suggesting that the PrC and EC play complementary roles in episodic memory.

Entorhinal cortex mediates the effect of hippocampal contextual integration on memory retrieval

We next asked how parahippocampal activity patterns interact with subregions of the hippocampus proper. To answer this question, we examined the correlation between integration indices in the parahippocampal regions that showed significant correlations (EC, PrC) and the hippocampal subregions (CA3DG, CA1). A significantly positive correlation was found between integration indices in the EC and the CA3DG (r = 0.482, p = 0.023), suggesting that the EC and CA3DG may work together to support context integration during memory retrieval. No other significant correlations were found between the EC, PrC, and hippocampus (EC ∼ CA1: r = 0.181, p = 0.421; PrC ∼ CA1: r = 0.057, p = 0.802; PrC ∼ CA3DG: r = −0.169, p = 0.802).

To investigate the directional relationship between integration in the EC and CA3DG and their respective contributions to memory performance, we performed mediation analyses. We first tested the model in which integration in the EC influenced memory performance indirectly via its effect on CA3DG integration (Figure 5A). While EC integration significantly predicted greater CA3DG integration (a: β = 0.68, p = 0.015), CA3DG did not significantly predict memory performance (b: β = −5.95, p = 0.18), and the indirect effect was not statistically significant (a x b: β = −4.32, p = 0.33). In contrast, the reverse mediation model revealed a significant indirect pathway: Context-based integration in the CA3DG predicted greater integration in the EC (a: β = 0.355, p = 0.038), which in turn predicted better memory performance (b: β = 20.77, p = 0.004), resulting in a significant indirect effect of CA3DG on memory retrieval via its effect on context-based integration in the EC (a x b: β = 7.216, p = 0.010) (Figure 5B). These findings challenge the assumption that cortical reinstatement in EC drives hippocampal integration during memory retrieval. Instead, they suggest that contextual memory representations in the hippocampus, particularly in the CA3DG, support retrieval by exerting a downstream influence on the EC.

Discussion

In the current study, we investigated the influence of shared spatial contexts across objects on their neural representations across the MTL subregions. We found that the MTL broadly reflected a reinstatement of the relevant spatial context, starting from the object-cueing phase and into the object-place response phase. This context-dependent memory integration was selective to the task-relevant spatial context (i.e., building) and was strongest during the goal-based response phase.

A striking aspect of the present findings lies in the functional dissociation between the PrC and EC in relation to memory performance. During object cueing, before the virtual environment was visible, better memory performance was associated with greater integration in the EC but less integration (i.e., greater differentiation) in the PrC. Additionally, participants with better memory performance exhibited reduced functional connectivity between these two regions, suggesting their opposing processes across the parahippocampal cortex to support episodic memory.

Despite their anatomical proximity, the PrC and EC showed markedly dissimilar relationships with memory performance, highlighting the importance of resolving fine-scale anatomical distinctions within the MTL to reveal meaningful functional specialization that can be obscured when adjacent regions are analyzed in aggregate.66,67,68,69 Our findings align with the interpretation that the EC supports episodic memory by integrating memories of objects from a common context, whereas the PrC enhances representational specificity by increasing object-level differentiation, thereby minimizing confusion between them. These findings build on previous work showing that the EC encodes structural overlap across experiences,54,58 while the PrC supports the discrimination of overlapping or ambiguous object representations and supports feature-level specificity and context-sensitive object discrimination.38,39,40,41,42,59,60,61,62,63,64

The observed contrasting relationship between PrC-EC and memory performance further underscores the role of functional segregation, especially during the object-cueing phase when contextual information must be reconstructed from memory. One possible explanation is that maintaining both differentiated representations of objects in the PrC and integrated representations of shared spatial contexts in the EC supports stronger memory for the four buildings and their spatial contexts. This complementary coding strategy may be especially effective in our task because the buildings were visually identical and embedded within similar landmark views from within the buildings. In such a setting, visual cues alone were insufficient to distinguish between contexts. Therefore, keeping object representations distinct in the PrC may have helped reduce confusion between similar scenes, while their integration across shared contexts in the EC allowed participants to generalize across experiences and reconstruct coherent memories from partial cues. Notably, we did not observe reliable integration effects based on shared landmark views (Figure S5), suggesting that EC integration was not due to common visual features. Instead, the high perceptual similarity across the buildings themselves may have placed added demands on the MTL to differentiate components, such as specific placements of each object, to support accurate retrieval.

Our findings extend contemporary models of MTL organization, particularly those highlighting distributed functional specialization among its subregions, such as the dissociation between the “what” and “where” stream,22,24 and the posterior-medial versus anterior-temporal framework, which proposes two distinct cortical pathways to the hippocampus for object and spatial information.70,71 Our results support emerging perspectives that challenge the rigid “what” versus “where” functional divisions within the MTL by demonstrating that object representations in the PrC are modulated by spatial context, providing evidence that spatial and non-spatial information may converge earlier than traditionally assumed.29,30 Although there was no significant hemispheric difference overall, the PrC’s differentiation effect showed a modest left-hemisphere bias, potentially related to the fact that the objects used in our tasks were nameable everyday objects.41,42,72 Similarly, the EC’s integration index demonstrated a trend toward the right hemisphere, consistent with the right MTL’s role in spatial processing.73,74

The Binding of Item and Context (BIC) model23,25 posits an anterior-posterior functional gradient along the MTL, in which anterior regions such as the PrC primarily support item memory and familiarity, while posterior regions such as the PhC contribute to context and recollection processes. Our findings align with and extend this framework: the PrC primarily supports item-specific differentiation, the PhC codes contextual representations, and the hippocampus binds these elements for recollection. The EC, positioned at the interface between these streams, showed increased neural similarity during successful memory integration, and this similarity was mediated by hippocampal (CA3DG) integration during retrieval. Together, these findings align with and refine the BIC model, suggesting that successful episodic recollection arises from the coordinated binding of item and context information across distributed MTL subregions, with the hippocampus playing a central integrative role. However, as our study was not explicitly designed to separate recollection from familiarity, future work explicitly designed to disentangle recollection from familiarity at both behavioral and neural levels would be informative.

Beyond the parahippocampal regions, our findings offer new insights into the role of the hippocampus, particularly the CA3DG, in selecting and integrating task-relevant contextual information during retrieval. We observed strong context-based integration across the PhC, EC, and CA3DG, along with a significant correlation between CA3DG and EC integration. Notably, extensive prior work highlights complementary roles of DG and CA3: the DG supports pattern separation by orthogonalizing inputs, thereby enhancing fine object and spatial discrimination,5,6,7,8,9 whereas CA3, with its sparse connectivity with the DG, and recurrent collaterals forming an attractor-like network that facilitates autoassociative mechanisms, supports both pattern separation and completion, making it especially suited for constructing contextual memories.10,11,12,13,15,75 Anatomically, the DG receives input from the EC and projects via mossy fibers into CA3, such that DG-mediated separation may provide the differentiated inputs that the CA3 can complete into holistic contextual representations during retrieval when partial cues are provided.

Within this framework, our finding that associative memory representations in the CA3DG mediated integration in the EC, which in turn affected performance, implicates a feedback mechanism whereby hippocampal computations are reflected in the EC during episodic memory retrieval. Consistent with this view, prior work shows that CA3 place cells respond strongly to rich, complex contextual input,76 positioning CA3 as a key driver of holistic context reinstatement. Such hippocampal outputs may influence upstream cortical areas via “side-loops” from CA3DG back to the EC, thereby allowing memory-based signals to bias ongoing sensory processing.27,34,35,36,37 The CA3DG-EC correlation may thus reflect such side-loop mechanism, wherein DG provides discriminability, CA3 integrates and reinstates context, and the reactivated contextual representations propagate to the EC to support memory-guided retrieval. The absence of similar effects in the further downstream regions such as CA1 and subiculum may indicate that these critical context-dependent associative signals are strongest within the CA3DG and, perhaps, subsequently integrated with more generalized episodic memory representations farther along the hippocampal circuit. We note a methodological limitation of our study: our CA3DG ROI comprises both the CA3 subregion and the DG. While this limitation is inherent to the spatial resolution of our data, and is a common approach for many human hippocampal subfield studies,77,78,79,80,81,82,83,84 emerging techniques such as those enabled by 7T MRI may allow for more precise discrimination of hippocampal subfields.

We also observed a notable distinction between the object-cueing and object-place phases in terms of both the strength of integration and its relationship to memory performance. While integration based on spatial context was strong across MTL subregions during the explicit retrieval of object-place associations, all significant correlations with memory performance emerged during the earlier object-cueing phase. This suggests that successful memory performance depends on the reinstatement of contextual information even when the object was presented on its own, prior to the explicit retrieval response. This finding aligns with prior work showing that MTL subregions can reinstate contextual information when presented with partial cues of the original scene.55,82,85,86,87,88 Specifically, we propose that individual differences in performance in this task may be attributed to the balance between the spontaneous reinstatement of the spatial context via the EC and the discrimination of object-specific details via the PrC. An alternative possibility is that the object-place phase prompted more uniform retrieval strategies across participants due to its clear task demand, reducing variability and attenuating correlations with behavior. In contrast, the object-cueing phase required self-initiated contextual retrieval, introducing more variability in how spatial associations were accessed.

Importantly, it should be noted that the key brain-behavior relationships reported in this study were derived from individual differences in neural integration across participants, rather than from trial-wise variation within individuals. While the within-subject GLMM analyses revealed that trial-wise integration index within the EC and hippocampal subfields predicted retrieval success, our main findings reflect a correlation between integration index and overall memory performance at the participant level. Interestingly, this individual-differences approach revealed a significant relationship in the PrC that was not observed at the within-subject level. One plausible interpretation is that activity patterns in the PrC are dominated by ongoing object perception38,39,40,41,42,43 (rather than the evoked contextual memory), which is common to both correct and incorrect trials (i.e., because both involve the same perceptual processes), suggesting that contextual differentiation in the PrC may occur particularly in individuals who have truly engaged their neural representations of the objects according to their context.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of our task design is that our object-cueing phase served as a cueing period rather than an isolated measure of object representation. Thus, we cannot say whether object representations themselves were altered through their association with a particular spatial context. Future studies using tasks with an isolated object phase that is not followed by associated memory retrieval may be better suited to address whether and how object representations are modulated by contextual information.52,58,80,81

Another limitation of our study is that although we examined how memory is shaped by task-relevant versus task-irrelevant contexts by comparing integration within the same buildings with that of the same corners, these spatial contexts had an intrinsically hierarchical organization (i.e., corners were inside the buildings). Unlike the consistently high level of pattern similarity across objects associated with the same building, we found a significant negative integration score in the PrC and CA3DG for objects that shared the same corner, which may be attributable to its role in pattern separation between similar objects.89,90 Future studies contrasting conditions in which the task response switches between the building and the corner could provide insight as to the effects of spatial scale on object-place associations in the MTL, for instance, across the anterior-posterior axis represents fine-grained spatial details.91,92,93,94,95,96,97

Conclusions

Despite their anatomical proximity, neural activation patterns in the PrC and EC showed markedly distinct relationships with memory performance. These findings extend current computational models of the MTL by demonstrating that neural representations of both the PrC and EC are modulated by spatial context, albeit in dissociable ways. We believe that these findings provide both notable empirical and theoretical insights to understanding how context-dependent memory representations in the CA3 may influence upstream processes in the parahippocampal cortex during episodic memory retrieval.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

Thirty healthy young adults were recruited from Seoul National University and the local Seoul community. All participants were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no neurological conditions or cognitive dysfunction were reported. Informed consent was obtained in compliance with the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University. Participants were paid 40,000 KRW for participation and an additional 20,000 KRW if they completed the experiment successfully. Of the 30 participants, four were excluded due to technical issues with the VR software. Four subjects were excluded from the analysis due to low performance on the second session (below 30% correct performance in the object-place retrieval task). Analyses were performed on data from the remaining 22 participants (N = 22, 11 females, mean age = 23.36, age range 20–30). Method details

Experimental design

The experiment comprised two sessions conducted over two consecutive days, with a 24-hour interval between sessions (Figure 1A). Prior to the first session, participants completed a 5-10 minute VR navigation practice using a button box, with the duration depending on how quickly they could navigate smoothly along a circular path in a practice environment. The experimental environment was a large circular VR space (∼400m radius space) centered around a virtual plus-maze (40 × 40 m2), which was elevated ∼1.5 m above ground level. Transparent walls along the boundaries of the plus maze prevented participants from exploring outside the maze. The environment was surrounded by natural landmarks (green hill, farm, lake, and mountain) to act as distal cues during the task. Four visually identical buildings were located at the end of each arm of the plus-maze. Each building contained 4 corners with 5 objects each (20 objects per building, a total of 80 objects). The walls of the buildings were transparent, making different distal natural landmarks visible from each corner. The four buildings were deliberately designed to be visually identical and embedded within distinct distal landmarks to ensure that spatial differentiation among buildings could only be achieved through surrounding environmental cues (e.g., hill, lake, mountain, farm) rather than on visual differences between the buildings themselves, thereby maximally engaging hippocampal-dependent spatial navigation mechanisms. The VR environment was created using Unreal Development Kit (Epic Games, Cary, NC) (Figure 1B).

In the first session, participants were instructed to remember the objects and the buildings they were in. Participants visited each building twice in order of their preference, without visiting the same building consecutively. Each visit started in the center platform of the plus-maze and, participants traveled down an arm of their choice and entered the building. Upon entering a building, the participants were instructed to visually sample a set of five objects located in each corner, in a pseudorandomized order, for ten seconds. The corner the participants had to visit next was signaled with a green light on the ceiling of each corner. Once the green light turned off, participants turned around to look for the next green lamp. After visiting all four corners, participants exited the building and traveled back to the center platform, where they were disoriented for their next visit to another building to begin. Specifically, participants were relocated to one of the four corners of the center square platform, and their viewpoint was rotated to a randomly assigned orientation (e.g., North, South, East, West), ensuring that at the onset of the next visit, participants could not use their immediate memory of heading direction or prior movements to locate the next building.

After visiting each building twice, participants performed an object recognition task. Ten objects from each of the four buildings (40 objects in total) and 40 novel objects were shown in a pseudorandomized order against a grey background. When presented with an object, participants were asked to indicate whether the object was old or new by pressing a binary button. The first session was performed outside of the scanner.

In the second session that was conducted inside the scanner on the following day, participants performed an object-place associate retrieval task. To mitigate possible floor effects in memory performance after the 24-hour delay, participants were first allowed to visit each building once to review the object-place paired associations, prior to the task. This re-exposure provided an opportunity to prevent substantial forgetting, ensuring that participants were able to engage with the subsequent retrieval task effectively. Following this review, participants completed the object-place retrieval task, which was composed of different memory phases as described below.

In the first phase, each object was presented against a grey background. Participants were instructed to simply view the object and press a button upon recognizing it. This phase was designed to capture the neural representation of the object as a cue (referred to as the “object-cueing phase”). Importantly, this was not a traditional novelty detection or familiarity judgment task, as all objects presented had been previously learned. This design minimized the influence of additional, heterogeneous cognitive processes (e.g., novelty detection, familiarity judgment) while engaing the retrieval process that continued into the subsequent phase. Objects were presented for 11.2 seconds (4 TRs). Immediately following this, participants were teleported to the center of the plus-maze of the virtual environment, facing a random direction, and the second phase commenced without any delay. Thus, participants could immediately use the object cue to guide their navigation in the following response phase, minimizing retention interval effects such as memory decay, rehearsal, or working memory maintenance between phases.

In the second phase, once the trial began, participants were required to rotate at a fixed angular speed of 45°/sec at the maze center until they identified the building associated with the previously cued object by pressing a button (referred to as the “object-place response phase”). Each retrieval trial was followed by a spatial navigation phase that did not require memory of associated objects (referred to as the “spatial navigation phase”). In this phase, participants were first disoriented in their facing direction (i.e., rotated participants’ viewpoint to a random direction), then instructed to navigate to the arm of the building that they had previously selected during the object-place phase, irrespective of whether the building previously chosen was correct or incorrect in association with the cueing object. Without disorientation, participants would by default face the correct target location immediately after completing the previous phase, allowing them to reach the target building without engaging spatial memory processes. Once the participant entered the selected arm and reached its halfway point, they were relocated to the center of the maze and remained in a “black box” during an intertrial interval period lasting between 2.8 to 5.6 (1-2 TRs), before the onset of the next trial. The spatial navigation phase was later used to assess whether building representations were reinstated during memory retrieval by computing the pattern similarity with the memory phases (object-cueing, object-place phase) (Figure 1C; Video S1).

A total of 40 test trials were conducted in the scanner, using objects that had not been included in the object recognition task from the previous day. To maintain attentional engagement throughout the experiment, participants also completed interleaved non-memory control trials in which participants viewed a neutral object (i.e., a cone) and navigated to a target building explicitly indicated by a light cue positioned above the building entrance. These trials were not used for analysis.

fMRI data acquisition and image processing

MRI data were collected using a 3T Siemens Magnetom Trio scanner with a 32-channel head coil. To better dissociate functional contributions across the MTL, we employed an imaging protocol designed for high-resolution coverage of MTL subregions. Functional images were acquired using a gradient echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 2,800 ms; TE = 33 ms; flip angle = 75; interleave collection; 36 slices parallel to the hippocampal axis; field of view [FOV] = 180 mm; image matrix = 72 x 72; voxel size = 2.5 x 2.5 x 2.5 mm), and T1-weighted in-plane images in the identical slice prescriptions (TR = 1,200 ms; TE = 2.85 ms; flip angle = 9; field of view [FOV] = 180 mm; image matrix = 192 x 192 x 192) for image registration. Before functional data collection, three dummy volumes were discarded to allow for equilibration effects. Head motion was minimized using foam padding. In addition, a high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE structural image (TR = 1,900 ms; TE = 2.36 ms; field of view [FOV] = 256 mm; image matrix = 256 x 256 x 256 mm; voxel size = 1 x 1 x 1 mm) was also obtained. The VR stimuli were projected onto a screen using an LCD projector, and the subjects viewed the screen through mirrored goggles. All responses were collected using an MRI-compatible button box with four buttons. For each participant, an average of about 10 runs, ranging from 8 to 12 runs, were acquired.

fMRI preprocessing was carried out using the SPM12 toolbox (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/), through customized scripts. Slice acquisition timing was corrected by resampling all slices in time relative to the middle slice collected. Then, scans were realigned using rigid body motion correction to correct for head motion. Functional data were co-registered with the T1 structural image and segmented into different anatomical regions. All multi-voxel pattern analyses were performed in local space, with unsmoothed images.

Subregion demarcation

We defined anatomical region-of-interests (ROIs) for areas known to be involved in processing spatial information and episodic memory: the hippocampus (CA1, CA3DG), entorhinal cortex (EC), parahippocampal cortex (PhC), and perirhinal cortex (PrC). Images were processed using the Freesurfer software98,101 to generate anatomical ROIs, then resampled to the mean functional images of each subject. Although segmentation of hippocampal subfields ideally combines T1- and T2-weighted MRI for optimal anatomical delineation, this study only utilized T1-weighted images. Previous research has demonstrated the feasibility of segmenting major hippocampal subfields such as CA1, CA3DG, and subiculum using T1-weighted scans alone.81,102,103,104,105,106 T2-image is especially beneficial to distinguish the corno ammonia (CA) from the DG, and not to differentiate CA1 from the CA3DG.107,108 This study combined CA3 and DG into a single ROI (CA3DG), consistent with common approaches in functional MRI studies with standard resolution.77,78,79,80,81,82,83,84

Delineating the PrC is challenging for fMRI researchers due to its anatomical characteristics of being small and thin, and located deep in the temporal lobe, making it harder to distinguish from adjacent structures. Thus, each participant’s subfield segmentation was also manually inspected to ensure the accuracy of the segmentation protocol. The PrC (approximating Brodmann area 35) was further delineated following previously established anatomical guidelines.109,110,111 Specifically, the anterior boundary of the PrC was identified relative to the collateral sulcus through manual inspection. The PhC was defined to begin where the posterior boundary of the PrC ended. The EC (approximating Brodmann area 28), with its anterior border starting at the limen insulae and posterior border starting at the hippocampal head, was manually inspected and its anterior border further adjusted to ensure that it was located medial to the PrC, with no voxel overlap between the two regions (Figure S8). The resulting mean number of voxels in the ROIs was as follows: CA1 (100.727), CA3DG (65.409), PrC (44.220), PhC (84.614), EC (247.567). Mean temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) values, defined as the mean of the EPI time series divided by the standard deviation in every voxel of the ROIs, were calculated: CA1 (108.274), CA3DG (102.139), PrC (66.590), PhC (75.555), EC (72.680). We also included the lateral occipital cortex and the cuneus as control regions, applying the same ROI definition steps used for the MTL ROIs. The hippocampus was also segmented into its head, body, and tail for examining longitudinal axis effects (Figure S6).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Behavioral analysis

The behavioral analyses were conducted based on behavioral log files, which included locations, viewing perspectives, and timestamps for the task-relevant events within the virtual environment. Matlab (MathWorks, Waltham, MA) was used to analyze the response accuracy and reaction time (RT) for each phase of each trial. A trial was marked as correct if the participant viewed the correct building and pressed the button within 11.2 seconds (4 TR). RT was determined as the time spent from the beginning of a trial to the moment the participant pressed the button. To obtain a single measure that reflects both retrieval success and efficiency of the participant, memory performance was calculated as accuracy divided by RT.112 This combined measure was chosen because it captures both the success and speed of retrieval, thereby providing a more comprehensive index of episodic retrieval efficiency rather than accuracy alone. The resulting values were z-transformed, and the signs were inverted so that higher scores indicate better performance.

Multi-voxel pattern similarity analysis

Multivariate pattern similarity analyses were conducted on beta maps derived from unsmoothed functional data in each participant’s native space. To estimate trial-specific activation patterns, we employed a least squares separate (LSS) model approach,113 generating one beta map for each trial (n = 40). In each single-trial model, the first regressor modeled the trial of interest with a stick function, the second regressor modeled all remaining trials of the same phase, and the third regressor modeled all trials of the other phase. Six motion parameters were included as nuisance regressors to account for head motion. Onset times and durations were extracted from the recorded behavioral logs. Trial duration was defined as the interval between stimulus onset and the participant’s response, ensuring that the modeled activity reflected memory retrieval rather than continued visual exposure to the same building. Voxel-wise patterns of hemodynamic activation were separately extracted for each ROI using the single-trial beta images.

Across-phase pattern similarity analysis (context reinstatement)

To assess how strongly contextual representation was reactivated during retrieval, we measured the similarity of neural activity patterns between the retrieval phases (object and object-place phases) and the spatial navigation phase. Analysis was limited to trials in which participants correctly identified the building associated with the cued object. For each ROI, we computed Pearson’s correlation coefficients between trial-wise beta estimates across the phases to quantify neural pattern similarity. Critically, only correlations between different trials were included; correlations within the same trial were excluded to avoid confounds related to temporal autocorrelation or trial-specific noise. We then compared neural pattern similarity for trial pairs that involved the same building context (e.g., viewing an object from the north building and navigating toward the north building) versus trial pairs that involved different building contexts (e.g., viewing an object from the north building and navigating toward the west building). If memory retrieval reinstates the representation of the associated spatial context, neural activity during retrieval should resemble that of actual navigation to the same location.

We calculated a “context reinstatement score” by subtracting the average similarity for different-context pairs from that of same-context trial pairs. A higher value reflected stronger reactivation of the spatial context associated with the cued object. Because the building was not visually presented during retrieval, a significant context reinstatement score, evidenced by greater similarity between retrieval and navigation phases for same-context trials compared to different-context trials, indicates that a common contextual representation (i.e., the associated building) was reactivated from memory. All statistical tests for a priori defined ROIs are reported at nominal significance levels (uncorrected p-values), following established recommendations for hypothesis-driven neuroimaging analyses to maintain statistical power and minimize Type II error.45,114,115,116,117,118

Within-phase pattern similarity analysis (integration index)

We assessed context-based memory integration for both retrieval phases (object and object-place phase) by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficients between trial-wise beta estimates across trials, yielding a trial-by-trial similarity matrix for each MTL ROI, and computing an “integration index,” defined as the difference between the mean similarity of the same-context trials and the mean similarity of different-context trials. Analyses were restricted to trials in which participants correctly identified the building associated with the cued object, ensuring that context retrieval had occurred. Since contextual information was not explicitly presented during retrieval, any observed pattern similarity was assumed to reflect memory-driven reinstatement of contextual information, indicating context-based integration in memory representations. To examine hemispheric differences, paired t-tests compared left versus right integration indices, and multiple linear regression with contrast tests assessed whether left and right indices differentially predicted memory performance. Additionally, to assess whether trial-wise pattern similarity predicted retrieval success, we employed binary generalized mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with correctness (correct vs. incorrect) as the dependent variable, pattern similarity as the fixed effect, and random intercepts for participants to account for inter-individual variability.

We examined this integration index across two levels of spatial contexts - a task-relevant context and a task-irrelevant one. First, the task-relevant context was defined at the building level (since the task was to identify the building an object was in): pattern similarity between trials associated with the same building was contrasted with similarity between trials associated with different buildings. In addition, for comparison, the task-unrelated spatial context was defined as the specific corner of the building an object was in: pattern similarity between trials associated with the same corner of the same building was contrasted with similarity between trials associated with different corners of the same building. This allowed us to evaluate whether representational similarity could be extended to detailed spatial information that was not directly relevant to the memory task. To account for potential heteroscedasticity due to unequal trial numbers across the levels of spatial contexts, we used the Welch’s t-test for the between-condition comparisons, which does not assume equal variances and provides adjusted degrees of freedom for improved robustness. As an additional control, we also computed the integration index for corners of different buildings that shared common landmark views (since the walls of the buildings were transparent) (Figure S5).

The equations above describe the mediation analysis used to test the hypothesis that the relationship between integration indices and memory performance is mediated by intermediate neural activity. Y (dependent variable) denotes memory performance, X (independent variable) denotes the integration index of region A, and M (mediator) denotes the integration index of region B. The indirect effect of integration on memory via the mediator was estimated using 10,000 bootstrap samples, with statistical significance assessed via bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals on the product of path a (X → M) and path b (M → Y controlling for X). The direct effect (c’ path) was also evaluated. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Mediation analysis was conducted using the Multilevel Mediation and Moderation (M3) toolbox.93,94

Functional connectivity analysis (fMRI beta-series correlation)

Functional connectivity between regions was estimated using a beta-series correlation approach,119 in which time series of trial-wise activation estimates were correlated across ROIs. Single-trial beta estimates were derived using least squares separate (LSS) modeling approach113 as described above. We reasoned that this approach would maximize our ability to capture the variance explained by the object-cueing phase of each trial (our phase of interest) and distinguish this variance from the later phases of each trial (object-place phase and spatial navigation phase).

Based on prior multivariate pattern similarity analyses, we focused on two a priori ROIs: the PrC and EC. The parameter estimates were averaged per trial across all voxels and stacked to create a t-series for each ROI. Analyses were restricted to trials in which participants correctly identified the associated context (i.e., the building). Beta-series correlations were then computed by correlating the PrC and EC t-series using Pearson’s correlation for each participant. The Pearson’s r values were Fisher z-transformed and entered into the group-level analysis.

Object-intrinsic similarity

To ensure that the pattern similarity results are not derived from the similarity in intrinsic properties of the objects themselves, the visual and semantic similarity between the objects was calculated for all object pairs that appeared in the Day 2 retrieval task (Figure S2). Visual similarity was assessed by rendering each object as a standardized PNG image and computing pairwise similarity using the structural similarity index (SSIM), implemented via Matlab’s ssim function.120,121 As with the calculation of the integration index, average SSIM values were computed for object pairs from the same context (i.e., building and corner) and those from different contexts. Semantic similarity was estimated by first mapping each object to its corresponding word in Matlab’s Text Analytics Toolbox word embedding dictionary, based on the word2vec model. Each word was then converted into a 300-dimensional embedding vector, and the correlation between these vectors was used to quantify semantic similarity between object pairs.122,123
