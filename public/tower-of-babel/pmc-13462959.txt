Synergistic multiscale reinforcement of sand concrete incorporating steel fibers and granulated blast-furnace slag
Authors: Hanene Hafsi, Naoual Handel, Ibrahim M. H. Alshaikh, José Aguiar, Nassima Meftah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sand concrete is increasingly recognized as a resource-efficient cementitious material; however, its widespread adoption in load-bearing applications remains limited by relatively low mechanical strength and brittle behavior. This study investigates a multiscale reinforcement strategy combining macro-scale hooked-end steel fibers and micro-scale ground granulated blast furnace slag (GBFS) incorporated as an additive granular filler, aiming to simultaneously enhance strength, toughness, and crack resistance. Twelve mixtures were designed with systematic variation of steel fiber volume fraction (0%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5%) and GBFS filler-to-cement ratio (F/C = 66% and 80%). Mechanical properties, durability indicators, and microstructural characteristics were evaluated using standardized experimental protocols. The optimal combination (Mix 10: F/C = 80% GBFS filler with 0.5% steel fibers) produced increases in compressive, splitting tensile, and flexural strengths of 58.4%, 33.6%, and 25.1%, respectively, relative to the reference mixture. For this mixture, a Synergy Index exceeding 1.0 for all mechanical properties confirms a genuinely synergistic interaction beyond simple additivity. Fiber incorporation altered the failure mode from brittle to quasi-ductile, substantially improving energy dissipation capacity. Microstructural observations confirm that GBFS densifies the matrix, refines the fiber–matrix interfacial transition zone, and enhances fiber anchorage efficiency. Higher superplasticizer dosages were required to maintain workability, and certain durability parameters exhibited sensitivity to fiber content. These findings demonstrate the promising potential of the proposed composite system for structural sand concrete applications, subject to validation across diverse material sources and exposure conditions.

Introduction

Sand has emerged as a crucial natural resource, extensively utilized as a construction material, particularly in mortar and concrete1,2. Indeed, the construction industry around the world needs more sand than ever before because of rising living standards and urban growth3,4. This demand is explained by the fact that natural sand represents a principal component in most concrete formulations, typically accounting for roughly 30–40% of the total concrete volume. Beyond its fundamental volumetric contribution, sand concrete demonstrates several notable advantages including superior workability, enhanced interfacial bonding, and compatibility with heavily reinforced structural applications. It is important to distinguish sand concrete from conventional mortar. Although both materials lack coarse aggregates, sand concrete is specifically designed for structural applications following the SABLOCRETE National Methodology5, which employs granular compacity optimization through Caquot’s empirical law and the Modified Feret Law. In contrast, mortar is conventionally formulated for non-structural applications such as plastering and masonry bedding, without systematic optimization of the particle skeleton. The maximum aggregate size in sand concrete is limited to 5 mm, and its mix design is governed by packing density principles rather than by the bonding requirements that characterize mortar formulation. In regions where natural sand is locally and abundantly available, its use confers not only technical benefits but also significant economic advantages, positioning sand concrete as a cost-effective alternative to traditional concrete in such specific geographic and supply contexts3,6,7. The standard composition comprises a homogeneous mixture of sand, Portland cement, and water; however, its performance can be substantially modified and enhanced through the inclusion of supplementary materials acting as micro-scale fillers8 or fibrous reinforcements. These additives include fine pozzolanic materials (e.g., fly ash, metakaolin), fibrous reinforcements (e.g., glass fibers, polypropylene fibers), and various industrial by-products, which collectively enable the production of high-performance, durable, and sustainable concrete composites tailored for diverse structural and environmental demands.

The increasing use of industrial by-products in sand concrete production has promoted the development of environmentally sustainable construction materials. Beyond being a cost-effective alternative to conventional concrete in sand-abundant regions, sand concrete offers enhanced properties, including potential improvements in thermal and acoustic insulation, greater fire resistance, and reduced environmental impacts, collectively enhancing its technical, economic, and ecological value3,9.

The incorporation of ground granulated blast furnace slag (GBFS) into concrete has garnered considerable attention owing to its capacity to deliver enhanced technical performance alongside substantial environmental benefits10–13. Extensive studies have demonstrated that incorporating high proportions of GBFS into cementitious systems can reduce the embodied carbon of concrete by approximately 30–60%14–16. Within this framework, the utilization of GBFS as a granular micro-filler within optimized sand concrete matrices represents a promising strategy for advancing sustainable construction practices and supporting global initiatives aimed at decarbonizing the building sector, while maintaining or potentially enhancing concrete performance.

Slag is a residual material produced by metallurgical processes. To ensure terminological clarity, this study focuses exclusively on GBFS, which is obtained via rapid water cooling. The latent hydraulic properties of GBFS suggest it could be a valuable resource for the modern construction sector, provided that GBFS-based concretes are engineered for favorable performance. The rapid growth of the global economy and the rise of iron production have led to a significant increase in slag generation. While GBFS is widely valorized in industrialized regions, a significant portion of slag production in certain developing regions — including parts of North Africa — remains directed towards landfill disposal17, motivating local valorization initiatives such as the present study. The use of GBFS has been extensively studied either as an aggregate18,19 or as a supplementary cementitious material (SCM) incorporated as a partial cement replacement to exploit its pozzolanic reactivity20. However, there has been limited systematic investigation into its potential as an additive micro-scale granular filler — incorporated in addition to cement rather than as a substitute — to optimize the packing density of the particle skeleton in fiber-reinforced sand concrete matrices. It is hypothesized that such additive incorporation of GBFS as a fine filler may improve packing density within the microstructure while simultaneously enhancing the fiber–matrix interfacial transition zone, a mechanism which may represent a key aspect related to overall performance.

Despite the recognized operational and sustainability advantages of sand concrete, its broader structural application is constrained by intrinsic limitations in mechanical performance and long-term durability, particularly with respect to crack resistance, tensile capacity, and ductility. Consequently, the strategic incorporation of fiber reinforcement represents a robust approach to enhancing the structural integrity and serviceability of the cementitious matrix. Among the available reinforcement options, steel fibers are well documented for their ability to impart substantial post-cracking tensile strength, energy absorption, and crack-bridging capacity to concrete21,22. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of single-fiber systems is inherently limited, as individual fiber types alone may not adequately address the multi-scale cracking and damage mechanisms that develop in cement-based composites23.

It is important to acknowledge that the combined use of steel fibers and GBFS has been previously investigated in conventional concrete systems containing coarse aggregates17,24. These studies have demonstrated beneficial effects on mechanical properties and durability. However, sand concrete represents a fundamentally different cementitious matrix, characterized by a maximum aggregate size limited to 5 mm, resulting in a finer granular skeleton with higher specific surface area, a unique interfacial transition zone (ITZ) architecture where fiber–matrix interactions differ substantially from those in coarse-aggregate concrete, and distinct formulation requirements that cannot be directly extrapolated from conventional concrete mix design. Consequently, the optimization parameters, fiber–GBFS interaction mechanisms, and practical implementation considerations in sand concrete warrant dedicated investigation.

A critical distinction further separates the present study from previous work on fiber–GBFS combinations. Prior studies have largely incorporated GBFS as a supplementary cementitious material (SCM) partially replacing cement, whereas the present study — following the SABLOCRETE methodology5 and Achoura’s optimization framework25— incorporates GBFS as an additive granular filler to fill inter-granular voids and maximize matrix compacity. In this approach, the total fines content (cement + GBFS particles < 80 μm) is first determined by Caquot’s empirical law to achieve maximum granular packing. The filler-to-cement ratio (F/C) is then optimized according to the Modified Feret Law, based on the activity coefficient K of GBFS, while the cement content remains the primary hydraulic binder and GBFS acts simultaneously as a physical densifier and a latent hydraulic contributor. This dual physical–chemical role of GBFS, operating synergistically with macro-scale steel fiber reinforcement within a fine-aggregate matrix, constitutes the specific and novel focus of this investigation.

This study explores a synergistic multiscale reinforcement strategy in sand concrete by investigating the interaction between macro-scale hooked-end steel fibers and micro-scale GBFS used as a granular filler and latent hydraulic additive. In this system, steel fibers primarily function to control crack initiation and propagation, enhance post-cracking tensile capacity, and improve energy absorption, while fine GBFS particles act as a microstructural densifier, filling inter-granular voids within the sand skeleton, refining the pore structure, enhancing fiber–matrix interfacial bonding, and increasing fiber anchorage. Building on prior investigations into hybrid cementitious composites26, the present work systematically evaluates the combined effects of steel fibers (0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5% by volume) and GBFS (at filler-to-cement mass ratios of 66% and 80%, determined by the Modified Feret Law25 on the mechanical and durability performance of sand concrete.

The principal objectives of this study are threefold: (i) to identify optimal combinations of steel fiber content and GBFS filler dosage that achieve balanced enhancements in compressive strength, tensile capacity, flexural performance, and ductility; (ii) to evaluate the durability implications of these combinations, including water absorption characteristics and drying shrinkage behavior; and (iii) to elucidate the microstructural mechanisms underlying the observed synergistic effects, particularly the role of GBFS in refining the fiber–matrix interfacial transition zone. A review of the existing literature reveals that no prior study has systematically co-optimized GBFS filler content and steel fiber dosage within a sand concrete matrix designed according to granular compacity principles, thereby addressing a gap in the existing literature on multiscale reinforcement of fine-aggregate cementitious systems.

Concrete mix design

In this research, a combined approach of empirical and semi-empirical methods was used to develop an optimal sand concrete mix incorporating ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GBFS). The mix design was based on the granular packing principles outlined in the SABLOCRETE National Vision Plan5 and further refined using the optimization techniques proposed by Achoura25. for GBFS-based concrete. The procedure followed to prepare the sand concrete mixtures is as follows:

Granular skeleton

The first step consisted of developing a method to minimize the total void fraction in the solid skeleton, thereby maximizing the packing density of the sand concrete matrix. A significant limitation of conventional mix-design approaches, traditionally applied to standard concrete, is the reduced packing efficiency resulting from the fine particle size distribution of sand concrete, which typically has a maximum particle size of 5 mm. To overcome this limitation, the initial procedure focused on determining the total fines content (cement + GBFS particles < 80 μm) using Caquot’s empirical law “equation (1) “, which defines the optimal proportion of fines for achieving maximum compactness. Based on these findings, two specific F/C ratios were selected for the present study. F/C = 66% corresponds to a value close to the theoretical and experimental peak of the K₁ curve, where hydraulic efficiency is maximized. F/C = 80% represents the upper boundary of the favorable range, enabling investigation of a higher filler content while maintaining adequate hydraulic performance at all curing ages. These two ratios thus bracket the upper portion of the optimal range identified by Achoura25 for El-Hadjar GBFS, providing full scientific traceability for the mix design decisions adopted in this study.

Water dosage and workability adjustment

The theoretical water content for each mixture was initially calculated based on the specific surface area of the constituent materials using Caquot’s formula. However, the final water and superplasticizer dosages were determined experimentally to account for the high sensitivity of sand concrete workability to small variations in water content. For each mixture, the water dosage was adjusted to ensure consistent workability across all formulations. The target slump was set at 70 ± 10 mm, measured using the Abrams cone method (NF En 12350–2)35. The slump value was determined by measuring the height difference between the top surface of the concrete mixture and the top of the inverted Abrams cone, as illustrated in Fig. 3a.

Specimen preparation

A total of 432 specimens were prepared in this investigation, with 36 specimens per mixture. For compressive strength testing, three cylindrical specimens of 160 mm diameter and 320 mm height were tested at 7, 14, and 28 days. Splitting tensile strength and elastic modulus tests were conducted at 28 days using three cylindrical specimens per mixture. Additionally, flexural strength, capillary water absorption, water absorption by immersion, drying shrinkage, and weight loss tests were performed at 28 days on three prismatic specimens of 70 × 70 × 280 mm per mixture. The mixing procedure was as follows: cement and fine aggregates were dry-mixed for 2 min. For mixtures containing GBFS, the slag was added to the dry blend and mixed for an additional 1.5 min. The superplasticizer (SP) was then combined with the designated mixing water, with 70% of the solution added initially, followed by 2 min of mixing. The remaining water and SP were added, and mixing continued for 1.5 min to achieve a homogeneous paste. For fiber-reinforced mixtures, steel fibers were incorporated and blended for an additional 3–4 min until uniform distribution was obtained. Specimens were cast in steel moulds and covered with nylon sheets at ambient laboratory temperature for 24 h. After demoulding, the specimens were immersed in a water tank for curing durations of 7, 14, and 28 days, depending on the specific test.

Test method

Figure 3 illustrates the experimental setups used to assess the mechanical properties of the sand concrete mixtures. Fresh density was measured in accordance with NF En 12350–636 by determining the mass-to-volume ratio of freshly compacted concrete in cylindrical moulds (160 × 320 mm). Mechanical performance was evaluated through compressive, splitting tensile, and flexural strength tests. Compressive and splitting tensile strengths were determined on cylindrical specimens (160 × 320 mm) following NF En 12390–337 and NF En 12390–638, respectively, while flexural strength was measured on prismatic specimens (70 × 70 × 280 mm) in accordance with NF En 12390–539. Tests were conducted at 7, 14, and 28 days, using three specimens per mixture and age, with results reported as average values. The elastic response was characterized by measuring the secant modulus of elasticity in compression in accordance with NF En 12390–1340. Concrete homogeneity and internal quality were assessed using ultrasonic pulse velocity measurements performed according to NF En 12504-441 with a 54 kHz transducer and direct transmission mode. Durability-related properties were evaluated through capillary water absorption and total water absorption tests. Sorptivity was determined following Majhi and Nayak42 by sealing the specimen sides with epoxy resin, while total water absorption by immersion was conducted on prismatic specimens in accordance with NBN B 15–21543 after oven-drying at 105 °C to constant mass.

The shrinkage test was conducted on three prismatic specimens (70 × 70 × 280 mm) for each mix according to the NF P 18–42744. The test was carried out as follows: Immediately after demoulding, the specimens were stored in a climatic chamber under controlled conditions of 20 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity for the entire duration of the test. Dimensional changes and mass loss were recorded over time.

Microstructural characterization was performed using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) coupled with Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS). The specimens were obtained from fragments collected after compressive strength testing at 28 days from three selected concrete mixtures — namely the reference mixture without GBFS filler (Mix 1), and the GBFS filler-modified mixtures at F/C = 66% (Mix 5) and F/C = 80% (Mix 9). Prior to observation, specimen surfaces were prepared using the integrated metallization system of the SEM equipment. EDS analysis was conducted to identify the elemental composition of the hydration products present in the hardened matrix.

Results and discussion

Fresh concrete

Fresh density data, shown in Fig. 4, indicate that the reference sand concrete (Mix 1) exhibited the lowest density at 2,324 kg/m³. Incorporation of granulated blast-furnace slag (GBFS) enhanced the matrix density, as evidenced by Mix 5 (F/C = 66%), which reached 2,345 kg/m³. This increase is attributed to the fine GBFS particles filling inter-granular voids within the sand skeleton47. At the micro-scale, GBFS particles (Blaine fineness: 3,650 cm²/g) occupy the interstitial spaces between cement grains and sand particles that would otherwise be filled by water or air, thereby reducing the total void fraction of the granular skeleton. This mechanism is governed by Caquot’s compacity law, which states that the introduction of fine particles smaller than 80 μm into a granular system displaces free water from the voids, increasing the solid volume fraction and consequently the overall fresh density of the composite.

Fresh concrete density for all mix designs.

However, when the GBFS filler content was further increased from F/C = 66% in Mix 5 to F/C = 80% in Mix 9, the density slightly decreased to 2,340 kg/m³. This reduction can be explained by the difference in specific gravities of the binder components: cement (3.17 g/cm³) is denser than GBFS (2.80 g/cm³). Consequently, Mix 9 contains a lower cement content (284 kg/m³) combined with a higher GBFS content (227 kg/m³) compared to Mix 5 (311 kg/m³ cement, 205 kg/m³ GBFS), resulting in a higher proportion of the lighter GBFS in the total fines content, leading to a slight decrease in overall density despite enhanced packing. This result reveals a competition between two opposing mechanisms: the void-filling effect of GBFS, which tends to increase density, and the specific gravity dilution effect, which tends to decrease it. At F/C = 80%, the latter marginally prevails, suggesting that the optimal density is achieved at an intermediate filler content consistent with the theoretical peak of the K₁ curve at F/C = 68%. These results highlight the dual role of GBFS in sand concrete: as a fine filler improving particle packing and as a latent hydraulic contributor, with its lower specific gravity influencing net matrix density at high filler content levels.

The incorporation of hooked-end steel fibers as reinforcement resulted in a slight, nearly linear increase in the density of the sand concrete matrix. For instance, the average density of Mix 1 (0% fibers) was 2,324 kg/m³, which increased marginally to 2,327 kg/m³ for Mix 4 with a 1.5% fiber dosage. This increase is primarily attributable to the high density of steel (7.80 g/cm³). At the micro-scale, the introduction of steel fibers into the fresh matrix creates a three-dimensional network that partially disrupts the granular packing arrangement, generating localized voids at the fiber–matrix interfaces. However, at the fiber volume fractions investigated (0.5–1.5%), the mass contribution of the high-density steel fibers outweighs the density reduction associated with these interfacial voids, yielding only a minor net increase in unit weight. The observed variation between the maximum and minimum specimen densities was limited to 1.03%, suggesting a high degree of consistency in the fresh concrete mixtures.

The required dosage of superplasticizer (SP) was adjusted in order to maintain a consistent slump of 70 ± 10 mm after the modification to the concrete components, as illustrated in Fig. 5. As stated in the slump test results, the slump values for all mixtures were very similar. This resulted in the use of varying SP dosages for each individual mixture design, so that the comparison of hardened properties was not influenced by differing workability.

Fig. 5Superplasticizer dosages required for different concrete mixtures.

Superplasticizer dosages required for different concrete mixtures.

Experimental results indicated that increasing the dosages of steel fibers and GBFS filler significantly reduced the workability of the concrete mixtures. This reduction in flowability can be attributed to two main factors. First, the incorporation of fine GBFS particles and steel fibers increases the overall specific surface area, requiring a larger volume of binder paste to adequately coat all solid surfaces24,48. At the particle scale, the high Blaine fineness of GBFS (3,650 cm²/g) generates a significantly greater surface area per unit mass compared to cement, multiplying the number of particle–particle contact points and increasing inter-particle friction. This elevated surface energy demand reduces the amount of free water available for lubrication, directly decreasing mixture fluidity. Second, the interlocking or “knitting” action of the hooked-end steel fibers further restricts particle mobility, thereby impeding mixture flow49. The hooked geometry of the fiber ends generates mechanical interlocking between adjacent fibers and between fibers and the granular skeleton, creating a rigid network that opposes shear deformation of the fresh mixture. This effect is amplified at higher fiber volume fractions, where fiber–fiber contact frequency increases, progressively reducing the paste’s ability to flow freely between solid constituents.

To mitigate these effects and maintain consistent rheology, higher superplasticizer dosages were necessary. The polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer (Visco Crete-522 RMX) acts through steric repulsion: its long polymer chains adsorb onto particle surfaces and generate repulsive forces that overcome inter-particle attraction, restoring fluidity without increasing the water-to-cement ratio. At higher GBFS and fiber contents, a greater SP dosage is required to saturate the increased surface area and counteract the enhanced mechanical interlocking, explaining the progressive increase in SP demand observed across the mixture series (Table 3). This suggests that there is a trade-off between enhanced mechanical performance and greater complexity in implementing the resulting designs.

Hardened concrete properties

Compressive strength

Compressive strengths of all specimens at 7, 14, and 28 days are shown in Fig. 6. The reference sand concrete (Mix 1) exhibited moderate compressive strength, whereas Mix 10, containing 80% GBFS and 0.5% hooked-end steel fibers, achieved the highest strength among all tested mixtures. Mixtures incorporating fibers alone, such as Mix 2 and Mix 4, showed only minor improvements, with a maximum gain of 3.02% in Mix 3 (1% fibers), consistent with the primary role of steel fibers in enhancing post-cracking behaviour rather than peak compressive strength. However, fiber addition significantly altered failure modes: reference concrete failed in a sudden brittle manner, whereas fiber-reinforced mixtures exhibited progressive quasi-ductile fracture. Fibers bridge micro-cracks, preventing their coalescence into critical macro-cracks and mitigating explosive failure50. Fracture analysis confirmed that most fibers were oriented perpendicular to primary cracks, supporting their bridging action. These findings suggest that, while fibers marginally affect ultimate compressive strength, they markedly improve toughness and ductility, demonstrating the potential benefits of combining GBFS and steel fiber reinforcement in sand concrete under the investigated conditions.

The incorporation of GBFS filler exerted a markedly greater influence on compressive strength than fibers alone. Chemically, the reactive SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ in GBFS react with portlandite to generate secondary Calcium Silicate Hydrate (C–S–H) and Calcium Alumino-Silicate Hydrate (C–A–S–H) gels, refining the ITZ and progressively densifying the matrix17,51,52. Splitting tensile strength

Figure 8 illustrates the evolution of splitting tensile strength for the various concrete mixtures at curing ages of 7, 14, and 28 days. The test results demonstrate that the incorporation of fibers enhances splitting tensile strength by 8.66% at a fiber content of 1% (Mix 3). Beyond this dosage, splitting tensile strength decreases (Mix 4), most likely due to non-uniform fiber dispersion and the formation of fiber agglomerates, which generate localized weaknesses. The use of GBFS filler as the sole additive component increases splitting tensile strength by up to 22.74% for Mix 9, through the physical and chemical densification mechanisms discussed in Sect. 3.2.1. The highest increase occurs in Mix 10 (F/C = 80% GBFS + 0.5% fibers), exhibiting a 33.57% improvement relative to the control mixture.

Fig. 8Splitting tensile strength of different concretes at 7, 14, and 28 days.

Splitting tensile strength of different concretes at 7, 14, and 28 days.

Under splitting tensile loading, the failure mechanism differs fundamentally from compressive failure. A primary crack develops perpendicular to the applied diametral load, and the hooked-end fibers oriented across this crack plane develop tensile stresses through their anchorage zones, limiting crack mouth opening displacement and redistributing stress along the crack front. This crack-bridging action is particularly effective under splitting tensile loading because the crack plane is well-defined and fibers crossing it can develop their full pull-out resistance before the critical crack width is reached. However, excessive fiber content promotes agglomerate formation and associated interfacial voids, which act as preferential crack initiation sites under tensile loading, explaining the strength decrease in Mix 4 (1.5% fibers)56.

Statistical analysis of the splitting tensile strength data reveals coefficients of variation ranging from 7.05% to 8.85% across all mixtures and testing ages, reflecting reasonable experimental scatter consistent with the inherent variability expected in fiber-reinforced sand concrete. The relatively stable dispersion observed across all mixtures and ages suggests that the variability is primarily governed by the random orientation of steel fibers relative to the diametral crack plane, which influences their crack-bridging contribution under splitting tensile loading. These values confirm the statistical reliability of the observed strength enhancements and support the validity of the comparative analysis conducted across the tested combinations.

The highest SI value among all tested properties (SI = 1.22, Table 4) is observed for splitting tensile strength, confirming that the combined effect exceeds simple additivity. This result is physically consistent with the role of GBFS filler in reducing interfacial porosity, which increases the fiber–paste contact area, delays fiber debonding, and enables crack-bridging to persist to larger crack openings — an interaction that is more effective under tensile-dominated failure than under compressive loading.

Flexural strength

Figure 9 presents the flexural strength test results for each concrete mixture at curing ages of 7, 14, and 28 days. Overall, all mixtures containing fibers, GBFS filler, or a combination of both exhibited higher flexural strength values than the reference mixture (Mix 1) at all testing ages. Among the mixtures incorporating only fibers, Mix 3 (1% fiber content) achieved the greatest increase in flexural strength within the tested range. Hooked-end fibers crossing the developing flexural crack transfer tensile stresses across the crack faces through their anchorage zones, delaying crack mouth opening and increasing the load required to reach critical crack width. This mechanism allows fibers at successive crack front positions to engage sequentially, thereby increasing the overall energy absorption capacity of the composite57. At 28 days, flexural strength values were higher than those measured at 7 and 14 days, consistent with the strength development trends observed in previous sections.

These factors are intrinsic to fiber-reinforced systems and do not reflect inconsistencies in mix preparation, thereby confirming the overall reliability of the experimental measurements.

Elastic deformation modulus

The specimens were subjected to three loading–unloading cycles between a baseline stress and one-third of the ultimate compressive strength in order to obtain stabilized measurements. The results presented in Fig. 10 are generally consistent with the compressive strength trends, as a denser and mechanically stronger cementitious matrix is typically stiffer and therefore undergoes smaller deformations under a given stress level. The incorporation of F/C = 80% GBFS filler (Mix 9) produced the most pronounced increase in elastic modulus (+ 15.35%) among the tested mixtures. This enhancement can be attributed to substantial matrix densification and improvement of the ITZ, resulting from both the filling effect and latent hydraulic activity of the GBFS filler19, which together yield a significantly stiffer overall microstructure (Fig. 7e). At the micro-scale, the reduction in capillary porosity induced by GBFS filler increases the solid volume fraction available for stress transmission, directly increasing composite stiffness. Conversely, the addition of fibers alone (Mix 3) led to only a minor increase in stiffness (+ 1%). Although the elastic modulus of steel fibers (200 GPa) greatly exceeds that of the cement matrix, their relatively low volume fraction (0.5–1.5%) restricts their direct contribution to global composite stiffness. Their principal function is the control of microcracking, which contributes to the preservation of matrix integrity under loading rather than to an intrinsic increase in matrix stiffness. The experimental stiffness values showed minimal dispersion, as indicated by a maximum coefficients of variation (COV) of 4.98%, ensuring that the measurements are both consistent and reliable.

Microstructural analysis

Microstructural observations (Fig. 7), interpreted alongside the physicochemical properties of the GBFS filler (Tables 1 and 2), provide evidence for the multiscale mechanisms governing the mechanical performance of the investigated concretes. The reference mixture exhibits a loose and porous microstructure (Fig. 7a), consistent with its comparatively moderate strength.

In GBFS filler-modified concretes, performance enhancement arises from the synergistic contribution of both physical and chemical mechanisms. Owing to its high fineness (Blaine surface area of 3,650 cm²/g), GBFS acts as an ultra-fine filler, improving particle packing and reducing capillary porosity, as evidenced by the denser microstructures in Fig. 7c and e. This physical densification directly reduces the number of stress concentration sites available for micro-crack initiation, providing the microstructural basis for the compressive strength and stiffness improvements observed in GBFS filler-containing mixtures. Concurrently, the latent hydraulic activity of GBFS promotes secondary hydrate formation. The reactive oxides, primarily SiO₂ (36.80%) and Al₂O₃ (8.50%), react with portlandite to generate additional Calcium Silicate Hydrate (C–S–H) and Calcium Alumino-Silicate Hydrate (C–A–S–H) phases, leading to matrix densification and enhanced interfacial continuity. The progressive refinement of the ITZ through secondary C–S–H and C–A–S–H formation reduces the weakest zone of the microstructure, explaining the time-dependent strength gain observed across all mechanical properties and the enhanced fiber anchorage efficiency identified in the synergy analysis.

EDS analysis (Fig. 7f) indicates GBFS filler participation in hydration through the detection of magnesium within the hydrated matrix, suggesting chemical consumption rather than inert filling. This confirmation of chemical reactivity validates the latent hydraulic activity mechanism invoked to explain the superior mechanical and durability performance of high GBFS filler content mixtures, and demonstrates that GBFS functions as an active contributor to matrix consolidation rather than a passive granular filler. The combined filler effect and secondary hydrate formation result in a dense and homogeneous microstructure, providing a microstructural basis for the enhanced mechanical and durability performance observed in the concrete incorporating F/C = 80% GBFS filler under the tested conditions.

It should be noted that the present study relied on SEM-EDS observations to assess microstructural densification and hydration product composition. While SEM imaging provided direct evidence of the physical packing role of GBFS filler, and EDS analysis indicated partial chemical participation through the detection of magnesium within the hydrated matrix, a precise quantitative separation of the physical and chemical contributions was not achievable within the scope of this study. Future investigations incorporating XRD and TGA analyses would allow quantification of the GBFS reaction extent and a more rigorous distinction between the granular packing and latent hydraulic contributions to the observed performance enhancements.

Durability analysis

Capillary water absorption

Capillary absorption of water into concrete is one of the most reliable indicators of the concrete’s durability. Testing was performed on three prismatic specimens (70 × 70 × 280 mm) from each mixture. Sorptivity was measured by sealing the side faces of the specimens with epoxy to leave only the bottom face exposed to water according to the method developed by Majhi R and Nayak A42. The specimens were then positioned on supports in a tray containing a 20 mm deep constant level of water. The results show that the reference mixture (Mix 1) has a moderate capillary water absorption coefficient (Fig. 12). This is likely due to the low granular packing density of the mixture, which resulted in an interconnection of pores, indicated by the mixture’s relatively low apparent density (Table 2).

Fig. 12Capillary water absorption of different concrete.

Capillary water absorption of different concrete.

The use of F/C = 80% GBFS filler ratio in a concrete mix (Mix 9) resulted in the lowest capillary water absorption value among the tested mixes, with a reduction of 58.03% compared to Mix 1. This result, along with the performance of Mix 10 (80% GBFS + 0.5% fibers), which achieved a similar reduction, demonstrates that durability improvement is achievable when GBFS content is sufficiently high to ensure adequate matrix densification. The improved performance of these mixtures can be attributed to the densification of the concrete matrix. The use of fine GBFS filler particles filled the pores of the concrete matrix and the latent hydraulic activity of the fine GBFS filler particles contributed to improving the pore structure (Fig. 7e), drastically reducing the connectivity of the capillary network in the concrete matrix, and ultimately reducing the amount of water entering the concrete matrix through capillarity. Statistical analysis of the capillary absorption data revealed a maximum coefficient of variation of 5.94% across all tested mixtures, indicating low experimental scatter and confirming the reproducibility of the sorptivity measurements. This limited dispersion validates the statistical significance of the observed differences between mixtures, particularly the pronounced reduction achieved in Mix 9 and Mix 10 relative to the reference mixture.

It should be noted, however, that durability improvement was not uniform across all tested mixtures, and the results reveal a clear trade-off between fiber content and capillary performance. In cases where where excessive fibers were added to the concrete mix, the rate of capillary water absorption was negatively impacted. The mix containing F/C = 66% GBFS filler and 1.5% fibers (Mix 8) exhibited the highest level of water absorption among all of the mixes tested, with an increase of 212.77% in capillary absorption compared to Mix 1. This result is physically consistent and does not contradict the overall findings of the study. At a moderate GBFS content of 66%, matrix densification is insufficient to compensate for the interfacial micro-voids generated by the high fiber volume fraction of 1.5%. This observation is further supported by the ultrasonic pulse velocity results, where Mix 4 (1.5% fibers, no GBFS) also exhibited a significant velocity decrease of − 9.38%, confirming that excessive fiber content disrupts matrix continuity regardless of GBFS content. The presence of fine GBFS filler helps to fill in gaps that exist within the concrete matrix and thus reduces the overall porosity of the concrete (Fig. 7c), consistent with findings by Akyildiz58. However, the addition of a large quantity of fibers into the concrete matrix may lead to localized zones of defectiveness. These zones are often identified as micro-cracks or voids present between the fiber and the cement matrix, thereby forming a network of pathways that may facilitate capillary water absorption59. As a result, while fine GBFS filler reduces the overall porosity of the concrete matrix, the introduction of excessive fibers increases the potential for capillary connectivity60. These findings underscore the importance of simultaneously optimizing both GBFS content and fiber dosage to achieve durability benefits. The favorable combination identified in this study — Mix 10 (80% GBFS + 0.5% fibers) — demonstrates that adequate matrix densification at high GBFS content effectively counteracts the negative influence of fiber incorporation at moderate dosages, resulting in both mechanical and durability improvements relative to the reference mixture.

Water absorption by immersion

Figure 13 illustrates the complex behaviour of water absorption by immersion among the tested concrete mixtures. The reference mixture (Mix 1) exhibited a water absorption rate of 5.67%, attributable to the non-optimized porosity of its cement matrix (Fig. 7a). The low granular packing density in this mixture promotes interconnected capillary pores, which facilitates water ingress, as further reflected by the relatively low apparent density (Table 2).

Fig. 13Water absorption of concrete mixtures by immersion.

Water absorption of concrete mixtures by immersion.

The incorporation of F/C = 80% GBFS filler in Mix 9 markedly reduced water absorption to 3.40%, demonstrating the densification effect of GBFS filler on the cementitious matrix. The fine GBFS filler particles function as both physical fillers and latent hydraulic reactants, filling interstitial voids and generating secondary Calcium Silicate Hydrate (C–S–H) and Calcium Alumino-Silicate Hydrate (C–A–S–H) that refine the pore structure (Fig. 7e). This dual physical–chemical effect reduces capillary connectivity58, enhances matrix compactness, and effectively limits water transport, suggesting that matrix densification is the primary mechanism controlling water absorption in GBFS filler-modified concretes under the investigated conditions.

In contrast, mixtures combining high GBFS filler content with fiber reinforcement exhibited increased water absorption. The mixture containing F/C = 66% GBFS filler and 1.5% fibers (Mix 8) showed the highest absorption rate, approximately 7.29 ± 0.42%, indicating an increase of 28.57% in water-accessible porosity. First, the inherently porous, glassy GBFS filler particles possess significant internal pore volume and a high specific surface area, contributing to intrinsic water uptake. Second, high fiber content can induce localized interfacial voids and microcracks, particularly under conditions of reduced workability, thereby disrupting matrix continuity61. In these high-additive mixtures, the combined effects of fiber-induced defects and the water-absorbing nature of GBFS filler appear to outweigh the densification benefits, leading to higher overall water absorption. This underscores a critical trade-off in concrete design: while GBFS filler improves matrix density and durability, excessive fiber incorporation may compromise the integrity of the matrix, highlighting the need to optimize both fiber content and GBFS filler content to achieve a balance between mechanical performance and long-term durability within the parameter space investigated in this study. Statistical analysis of the water absorption by immersion data revealed a maximum coefficient of variation of 5.83% across all tested mixtures, indicating low experimental scatter and confirming the reproducibility of the measurements. This limited dispersion validates the statistical significance of the observed differences between mixtures, despite the competing physical mechanisms governing water absorption in the presence of both GBFS filler and steel fibers.

Drying shrinkage and weight loss

Shrinkage was measured on three 70 × 70 × 280 mm prismatic specimens per mix, in accordance with NF P 18–42744. After demoulding, the specimens were kept at 20 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity until testing. Drying shrinkage and weight loss were recorded at regular intervals over 39 days.

It should be noted that the shrinkage reductions reported at 2 days reflect early-age behavior only. Long-term reductions at 39 days range from 1.75% to 37.48%, consistent with published data for GBFS filler-modified cementitious systems17,51,52.

The results at 2 days (Fig. 14) showed substantial differences in the early-age shrinkage behavior of the tested mixtures compared with that of the reference (Mix 1). GBFS filler incorporation alone substantially reduced shrinkage: Mix 5 (F/C = 66%) and Mix 9 (F/C = 80%) achieved reductions of 67.85% and 66.36%, respectively. This improvement can be attributed to the filler effect of fine GBFS filler particles, which densify the cement paste by filling intergranular voids and reducing the connectivity of the capillary pore network, thereby minimizing moisture movement and associated volumetric changes17,19,51,52. At the micro-scale, the reduction in capillary pore connectivity limits the driving force for moisture transport from the interior to the surface of the specimen, directly reducing the volumetric contraction associated with early-age drying.

Fig. 14Shrinkage of different concrete mixtures.

Shrinkage of different concrete mixtures.

The most remarkable early-age performance was observed in the GBFS filler–fiber combinations: Mix 7 (F/C = 66% + 1% fiber) and Mix 8 (F/C = 66% + 1.5% fiber) achieved the highest reductions of 86.45% and 83.18%, respectively, indicating exceptional combined performance at moderate GBFS filler content. This result suggests that at F/C = 66%, the fiber network provides additional restraint against early-age volumetric contraction by mechanically opposing matrix deformation, while the GBFS filler simultaneously reduces capillary moisture transport. The combination of these two independent mechanisms — mechanical restraint and pore network densification — explains the superior early-age shrinkage reduction at moderate GBFS filler content. Steel fibers alone showed variable effects: Mix 3 (1% fibers) achieved a 62.62% reduction, Mix 2 (0.5% fibers) showed 39.91%, while Mix 4 (1.5% fibers) demonstrated only a 13.08% reduction. Among the other GBFS filler–fiber combinations, Mix 11 (80% GBFS + 1% fiber) demonstrated a modest 2.80% reduction, and Mix 6 (66% GBFS + 0.5% fiber) showed no change (0.0%). However, Mix 12 (80% GBFS + 1.5% fibers) exhibited a 6.54% increase, and Mix 10 (80% GBFS + 0.5% fibers) displayed a 29.91% increase. This counterintuitive early-age behavior of Mix 10 and Mix 12 can be attributed to the high internal specific surface area generated by the combination of fine GBFS filler particles and steel fibers, which increases the total water demand of the matrix and accelerates early moisture redistribution, temporarily amplifying volumetric changes before the densification effect of GBFS filler fully develops.

At 39 days (Fig. 14), all mixtures demonstrated reduced shrinkage compared to Mix 1, although the magnitudes varied considerably. Mix 3 achieved the best long-term performance with a 37.48% reduction, followed by Mix 8 at 26.27%, Mix 10 at 22.42%, Mix 11 and Mix 12 at 19.26% each, Mix 6 and Mix 9 at approximately 12.4%, Mix 2 at 9.98%, Mix 5 at 8.41%, Mix 7 at 6.13%, and Mix 4 at 1.75%. These sustained benefits in mixtures containing GBFS filler can be attributed to the combined effects of physical densification and progressive latent hydraulic reactions of GBFS filler, which further refine the pore structure and reduce capillary porosity, thereby minimizing the internal moisture gradients responsible for drying shrinkage17,51,52. The reversal of Mix 10 from an early-age increase to a long-term reduction of 22.42% confirms that the densification effect of GBFS filler progressively dominates over time as latent hydraulic reactions mature, ultimately producing a denser pore structure with reduced moisture transport capacity. The limited long-term performance of Mix 4 suggests that excessive fiber content may induce microcracking, which increases long-term deformations62,63.

The mass loss data (Fig. 15) corroborate the shrinkage observations. At 2 days, the GBFS filler–fiber combinations showed the best moisture retention: Mix 8, Mix 7, and Mix 6 achieved reductions of 2.72%, 2.63%, and 2.51%, respectively, compared to Mix 1. Mix 10 exhibited an anomalous 1.16% increase, consistent with its elevated early shrinkage rate.

Weight loss of different concrete mixtures.

At 39 days, Mix 11 demonstrated the lowest mass loss with a 4.33% reduction, followed by Mix 12 (3.47%), Mix 9 (1.49%), Mix 8 (1.23%), and Mix 7 (0.78%). These results suggest that a high GBFS filler content with moderate fiber dosages creates effective long-term moisture retention. Conversely, fiber-only mixtures showed minimal benefits: Mix 2 exhibited a 0.34% increase, while Mix 3 and Mix 4 showed negligible changes (0.02% and 0.08% reductions, respectively).

These findings illustrate the complex interplay among GBFS filler content, fiber dosage, and curing age across the tested combinations. At an early age, Mix 7 and Mix 8 showed optimal performance within the tested range, achieving over 83% shrinkage reduction. In the long term, Mix 3 demonstrated exceptional stability (37.48% reduction), while Mix 8 (26.27%) and Mix 10 (22.42%) provided the best GBFS filler–fiber combination performance. These results underscore the importance of considering both short-term and long-term behavior in mix design optimization under the tested conditions.

Conclusion

This study investigated a multiscale reinforcement strategy for sand concrete combining micro-scale GBFS granular filler and macro-scale hooked-end steel fibers, designed according to the SABLOCRETE methodology and optimized through the Modified Feret Law. The following conclusions are drawn within the scope of the investigated materials and conditions:

Performance enhancement was achieved through a dual-scale physical–chemical mechanism: at the micro-scale, GBFS filler particles fill inter-granular voids and generate secondary C–S–H and C–A–S–H phases through latent hydraulic activity, producing a dense and refined matrix; at the macro-scale, hooked-end steel fibers bridge developing cracks and enhance post-cracking energy absorption. The most significant aspect of fibers’ contribution to structural safety is their ability to change the structural failure mode from sudden brittle to ductile progressive behavior, thereby significantly enhancing energy absorption capability and overall structural resilience. This transition is attributed to the crack-bridging mechanism of hooked-end fibers, which is rendered more effective by the densified matrix provided by GBFS filler.

The most significant aspect of fibers’ contribution to structural safety is their ability to change the structural failure mode from sudden brittle to ductile progressive behavior, thereby significantly enhancing energy absorption capability and overall structural resilience. This transition is attributed to the crack-bridging mechanism of hooked-end fibers, which is rendered more effective by the densified matrix provided by GBFS filler.

Durability assessment revealed that the physical densification and pore structure refinement induced by GBFS filler at F/C = 80% significantly reduced capillary absorption and water absorption by immersion. However, a critical trade-off was identified: excessive fiber content at moderate GBFS filler levels disrupts matrix continuity, increasing capillary connectivity and offsetting the densification benefits, underscoring the necessity of simultaneous optimization of both parameters.

The use of GBFS as an additive granular micro-filler within the SABLOCRETE framework — rather than as a conventional cement replacement — constitutes the specific methodological innovation of this study. This approach exploits the dual physical–chemical role of GBFS to simultaneously optimize matrix compacity and latent hydraulic performance, a strategy that has not been previously applied in the context of fiber-reinforced sand concrete.

The findings of this study suggest that the proposed composite system represents a promising and sustainable approach for structural sand concrete applications in regions where natural coarse aggregates are scarce. However, validation across diverse material sources, extended durability assessment, and full-scale structural testing remain necessary before confident deployment in critical structural applications. In addition, although CO₂ reductions could not be quantified in this study, the high GBFS filler content used in this proof-of-concept (F/C = 80%) is expected to significantly reduce the overall environmental impact of the concrete and therefore may support the establishment of more sustainable infrastructure, as well as open up new opportunities for sustainable civil engineering projects.

Limitations and future work

While this study provides valuable insights into the synergistic effects of steel fibers and GBFS in sand concrete, several limitations should be acknowledged.
