The effect of electrical stimulation of the upper airway dilator muscles on blood pressure in healthy volunteers and patients with obstructive sleep apnoea: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zakaa S. Khaled, Abdullatif Alhuzaim, Joerg Steier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-term adherence to continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), the standard treatment for obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), is often poor. Electrical stimulation of upper airway dilator muscles through hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS) has emerged as an effective non-CPAP alternative. However, evidence regarding its effect on the cardiovascular system remains limited.MethodsA meta-analysis investigating the effect of HNS and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) on blood pressure was conducted (www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO identifier CRD42023429546). MEDLINE/PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection databases were searched up to 25 January 2024. Quality assessment was performed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias for Randomised Trials tool and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for nonrandomised studies.ResultsOut of 6260 eligible studies identified, eight underwent full-text screening. Four did not measure change in blood pressure and were excluded. Data from the remaining four studies (n=304) were pooled (three HNS studies (n=291) and one TENS study (n=13)). Patients were middle-aged (mean±sd age 59.3±13.1 years), overweight (mean±sd body mass index 28.7±3.8 kg·m−2), and had moderate-to-severe OSA (mean±sd apnoea–hypopnoea index 34.8±7.1 events·h−1). Electrical stimulation was associated with a trend towards reduction in systolic (−3.44, 95% CI −7.98–1.10 mmHg), diastolic (−3.41, 95% CI −8.10–1.27 mmHg), and mean arterial blood pressure (−5.69, 95% CI −13.66–2.28 mmHg).ConclusionA trend between electrical stimulation of upper airway dilator muscles and reduction in blood pressure was demonstrated. Further studies on normal subjects and patients with OSA are required to compare the effects on blood pressure to CPAP.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is one of the most common sleep-related breathing disorders, and is estimated to affect 13% of men and 6% of women [1]. It is characterised by repeated breath-holding during sleep caused by complete (apnoea) or partial collapse (hypopnoea) of the upper airway [2]. The respiratory events cause sleep fragmentation due to intermittent nocturnal hypoxia, high inspiratory pressures and arousal from sleep [2, 3]. The prevalence of OSA is increasing due to the rising number of patients affected by the worldwide obesity epidemic [1, 4].

OSA causes excessive daytime somnolence [2, 5], increased cardiovascular risks and adverse respiratory health outcomes [6–9], cognitive impairment [10], and it is associated with the metabolic syndrome [11] and premature death [12]. Treatment-resistant hypertension is one of the main cardiovascular risks associated with untreated OSA, and it poses a major disease burden due to its contribution to mortality [13–16].

The pathophysiology of OSA is multifaceted, involving sympathetic nervous system activation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, reduction in neuromuscular tone of the upper airway, and alterations in renal function and fluid balance [15, 17]. Recurrent episodes of apnoeas and hypopnoeas lead to physiological changes and contribute to systemic hypertension, by increasing pressor and cerebral vascular resistance responses to hypoxia [18].

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the first-line treatment for most cases of OSA [19]. While effective in restoring normal breathing and alleviating symptoms, long-term adherence to CPAP therapy remains limited [20–23]. This leads to reduced therapeutic efficacy to mitigate the neuropsychological and cardiovascular sequelae of OSA [16]. Alternative treatments are summarised as non-CPAP therapy; they include the use of mandibular advancement devices, which offer similar adherence rates to CPAP [21, 24], weight reduction through diet changes, pharmacological interventions and bariatric surgery [25]. More recently, electrical stimulation of the upper airway via hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS) obtained regulatory approval as a non-CPAP therapeutic intervention for eligible patients with OSA, demonstrating positive clinical outcomes [22, 26]. Noninvasive submental transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) has also shown promising results [23, 27], but has not yet been implemented in clinical practice [27, 28].

The studied effect of CPAP on blood pressure in patients with OSA suggests that treatment of the condition may improve blood pressure control [17, 29]. However, the evidence of the effect of HNS on blood pressure in patients with OSA remains of low quality [27]. This systematic review and subsequent meta-analysis was undertaken with the aim to comprehensively evaluate the effect of electrical stimulation of the upper airway on blood pressure in patients with OSA, and in healthy volunteers.

Methods

Trial registration and reporting

This systematic review was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO identifier CRD42023429546). It was conducted according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines [30].

Search strategy and selection criteria

The MEDLINE/PubMed and Web of Science Core Collection databases were searched from inception until 25 January 2024 to identify relevant studies. The following search terms were used: (electrical stimulation* OR transcutaneous electrical stimulation* OR hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS)* OR sleep apnoea* OR sleep apnea*) AND (hypertension* OR healthy* OR cardiopulmonary*). The literature search was performed independently by two reviewers (Z.S. Khaled and A. Alhuzaim). Studies assessed as eligible for inclusion were controlled trials or observational, prospective and retrospective studies (that were either clinical, cohort, physiological or randomised), looking at the effect of electrical stimulation (HNS or TENS) on the cardiovascular system in patients with OSA, as well as in healthy individuals. Studies that met the following participant characteristics were selected: adults (aged ≥18 years) of any gender, including patients with sleep apnoea (obstructive, central or mixed), patients with any type of sleep disordered breathing, patients with systemic hypertension, and healthy individuals. An English-only language restriction was also applied. Studies concerning children (up to the age of 18 years) and animal studies were excluded.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome measure was the effect of electrical stimulation (HNS or TENS) on systolic, diastolic and mean arterial blood pressure in patients with OSA, or in healthy individuals. This was measured as the mean difference in blood pressure (ΔBP) in mmHg, using blood pressure at baseline and blood pressure post-electrical stimulation.

Study selection

Following database searching, identified records were de-duplicated both manually and using EndNote 21 software (version 21.2; Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA). Studies were then arranged alphabetically by author surname on EndNote 21 and split between two reviewers (Z.S. Khaled and A. Alhuzaim), who independently screened titles and abstracts. Z.S. Khaled and A. Alhuzaim independently applied the inclusion criteria to all articles that were identified and retrieved for full-text review. Articles that met the inclusion criteria were then selected for meta-analysis. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and involvement of a third reviewer (J. Steier).

Risk-of-bias and quality assessment

The overall risk of bias for randomised controlled trials was assessed using version 2 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias for Randomised Trials (RoB 2) tool, developed by the Cochrane Bias Methods Group [31]. Risk was determined based on the highest risk of bias across any of the included domains, providing an overall score of either low risk, some concerns, or high risk. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for assessing the quality of nonrandomised studies in meta-analyses was used to perform a quality assessment of included cohort studies [32]. Each study was awarded up to a maximum of four stars in the selection category, two stars in the comparability category and three stars in the outcome category. Scores were then combined to give studies an overall score, up to a maximum of nine stars. Studies with a score of up to two stars were rated as poor quality, from three to six stars as fair quality, and seven to nine stars as high quality. Z.S. Khaled and A. Alhuzaim independently assessed the included studies for risk-of-bias and quality. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with J. Steier.

Data extraction and statistical methods

Two reviewers, Z.S. Khaled and A. Alhuzaim, independently extracted and then discussed the following information from the included studies: first author, publication year, study design, type of electrical stimulation, follow-up period, sample size, participant age, body mass index (BMI), apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) and blood pressure (systolic, diastolic and mean arterial) both at baseline and post-electrical stimulation, Δ BP, 95% confidence intervals, method of blood pressure measurement and p-values.

All calculations were performed by Z.S. Khaled using exact values from study reports and/or estimations, to avoid introducing rounding errors. Where multiple blood pressure readings were recorded, values that most closely matched the state of patients with OSA (e.g. supine, hypoxic) were used. Propensity score matched and intention-to-treat analysis patient groups were preferred for statistical analysis where these were provided in study reports, due to the more reliable estimates provided of true treatment effectiveness. Review Manager 5 software (version 5.4.1; The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) was used to perform a meta-analysis of the pooled data to look at the effect of electrical stimulation on systolic, diastolic and mean arterial blood pressure. A random-effect analysis model was used to assess the effect of electrical stimulation on blood pressure, as this model assumed the effects being estimated in the studies followed some type of distribution [33]. Chi-squared and I2 statistics were used to assess heterogeneity, and τ2 statistics were used to test for between-study variance. GraphPad Prism 9 software (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) was used to perform the Kruskal–Wallis test for study normality and the H-statistic, degrees of freedom and p-value are reported. Data are presented as mean±sd or 95% confidence intervals. Where mean, standard deviation or standard error values were not provided in manuscripts, they were estimated from other available values, including the median, interquartile range or 95% confidence intervals [33, 34]. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Search results and included studies

The initial database search yielded 7321 results; one record was identified through other sources. Following de-duplication, the remaining 6260 studies were screened by title and abstract. The full texts of eight articles were then assessed for eligibility. Four articles were excluded at this stage, as Δ BP was not measured as an outcome [35–38]. Four articles were found to meet the eligibility criteria and were included in the review for qualitative synthesis [39–42]. Of these studies, one was a randomised controlled trial [40], and three were cohort studies [39, 41, 42] (figure 1). Subsequently, data from the four studies (n=304) were pooled for a meta-analysis to assess the effect of electrical stimulation on blood pressure. This included three studies using HNS (n=291) and one using TENS (n=13).

Effect of electrical stimulation on blood pressure

In response to electrical stimulation of the upper airway, a trend towards a reduction of systolic, diastolic and mean arterial blood pressure was observed (figure 2). The pooled systolic Δ BP was −3.44 (95% CI −7.98–1.10) mmHg; the pooled diastolic Δ BP was −3.41 (95% CI −8.10–1.27) mmHg; and the pooled mean arterial Δ BP was −5.69 (95% CI −13.66–2.28) mmHg. Notably, the observed systolic, diastolic and mean arterial Δ BP in the study looking at the immediate effect of TENS were larger compared to the observed long-term changes in studies looking at the effect of HNS (table 2).

Risk-of-bias and quality assessment

This meta-analysis included a total of four studies measuring the effect of upper airway electrical stimulation on blood pressure. Different quality assessment tools were used to rate studies, as the included studies were of different study designs. Therefore, it was not possible to compare study quality and risk of bias directly across all studies. Subsequently, only within-design study quality is reported (tables 4 and 5). It was not possible to perform Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry to assess publication bias either, as the number of included studies was too small [33]. Nonetheless, the risk of bias was low and the quality of included studies was relatively high.

Discussion

TENS stimulation in the submental area in normal, healthy subjects has an immediate effect on the blood pressure, while there was a more modest long-term response in patients with moderate-to-severe OSA. In a pooled analysis, electrical stimulation of the upper airway dilator muscles has a trend towards blood pressure reduction, due to the relatively low number of included studies and subjects. Nonetheless, included studies were of relatively high quality and had a low risk of bias.

Clinical significance of findings

It has been established that lowering blood pressure positively impacts on life expectancy, and reduces the risk that systemic hypertension poses to health [13, 14]. OSA facilitates the pathophysiological pathways that lead to hypertension when left untreated, which may lead to adverse health outcomes [15–18]. Reduced quality of life and low treatment adherence to standard therapy indicate an urgent need to develop non-CPAP treatments to limit the disease burden of OSA [43], and indirectly modify the pathways that lead to hypertension. HNS has been recognised for its positive clinical outcomes [22, 26], indicating the potential for it to impact on long-term cardiovascular risks. HNS may particularly benefit cohorts of patients with OSA who are particularly non-adherent to CPAP, or who have other reasons why CPAP may be contraindicated (e.g. patients with learning disabilities and lower average life expectancy).

Approval of HNS by the United States Food and Drug Administration in 2014 marked a significant advancement in the development of non-CPAP therapeutic options for patients with OSA, and further evaluation of HNS was conducted by the United Kingdom National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in 2017 [26]. While HNS has demonstrated efficacy in treating moderate-to-severe OSA [22], evidence on the long-term effects of HNS on hypertension in patients with OSA remains limited. The CARDIOSA-12 study by Dedhia et al. [40] aimed to address this by using a double-blinded, sham-controlled randomised crossover trial to investigate the effect of HNS on cardiovascular health in patients with moderate-to-severe OSA. Their findings showed that the cardiovascular endpoints measured in patients (including mean 24-h systolic blood pressure) did not differ significantly between sham and active HNS therapy, which may, in part, be explained by a subtherapeutic treatment effect of the sham-control arm.

The European Respiratory Society guidelines on non-CPAP therapy do not recommend HNS as a first-line intervention in OSA, but recommend the use of HNS as a salvage treatment in patients with symptomatic OSA who cannot be treated with CPAP [43, 44]. A further factor to consider is the cost of HNS, which remains high, albeit cost-effective [45]. A transcutaneous approach to electrical stimulation of the upper airway dilator muscles may offer a more affordable way to develop new non-CPAP therapies for patients with OSA [27]. TENS machines are generally well tolerated by patients due to their noninvasive nature and limited side-effect profile, and can be titrated for patient comfort [23, 27, 46].

We identified two ways in which TENS and HNS may impact blood pressure, explained by the design of the included studies. In the single study investigating the effect of TENS, blood pressure was measured continuously in healthy volunteers, while awake and receiving ongoing electrical stimulation [39]. A reduction in blood pressure was observed due to the immediate effect of the current on baroreceptors. Conversely, in two of the three clinical studies using HNS [41, 42], an indirect and delayed effect on blood pressure was studied. Patients used HNS while sleeping, alleviating obstructive respiratory events at night, possibly impacting the sympathetic tone. Therefore, the reduction in blood pressure was delayed, and measured while patients were awake (and with electrical stimulation turned off). Our findings suggested that the immediate stimulation effect provided by TENS was more effective at lowering systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial blood pressure in comparison to the indirect and delayed response studied in patients with OSA using HNS. Further studies are required to explain the specific mechanisms by which electrical stimulation may impact baroreceptors and, potentially, lead to an augmented blood pressure effect.

In a review by Bradicich et al. [47], which looked at the cardiovascular effects of CPAP on patients with OSA, a reduction in blood pressure was mainly observed in uncontrolled and treatment-resistant hypertension. Since CPAP therapy is used as the first-line treatment for OSA and prior to the use of electrical stimulation, it is worth considering whether patients’ previous CPAP use may have impacted our findings, and how much of the observed effect in reduction of blood pressure can be attributed to electrical stimulation alone. However, a recent retrospective analysis of 63 patients with OSA at a single American private practice by Freeman et al. [48], showed statistically significant changes in overall antihypertensive medication requirements and in requirements of three or more than three medications with HNS use. A statistically significant improvement in mean arterial blood pressure was also observed: Δ BP was −8.4 (95% CI 5.8–11.4) mmHg, from 99.4 mmHg to 90.99 mmHg (p<0.0001). It is well-known that patients with OSA are strongly associated with having a “nondipper” profile (whereby a nocturnal blood pressure reduction is absent or significantly blunted in contrast to a normal physiological “dipper” pattern) [49–52], or refractory hypertension (uncontrolled hypertension despite the use of five or more antihypertensive agents of different classes) [53, 54]. Hence, the findings of this retrospective analysis are positive, and suggest that there may be a relationship between a reduction in blood pressure with HNS use, supporting the trends we observed in our meta-analysis. Nevertheless, it is important to note that patients in this cohort were predominantly older and of White ethnicity, limiting the extent to which these results may be generalised to other patient populations, given the high global prevalence of OSA. Additionally, lack of control for potential confounders such as previous CPAP exposure, exercise, changes to diet and/or stress means these other factors may have contributed to this noted improvement.

Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis that explored the effect of both types of electrical stimulation (HNS and TENS) on blood pressure in patients with OSA. This review summarised the published output of two English language databases. Consequently, studies available elsewhere and published in other languages which reported on the effect of HNS or TENS on blood pressure in normal subjects and patients with OSA may have been excluded without intention.

Due to the limited number of prior studies investigating the effect of electrical stimulation on blood pressure in OSA, our search criteria allowed for patients with any type of sleep apnoea (obstructive, central or mixed) to be included in our review, as well as healthy individuals. We acknowledge that different types of sleep apnoea have distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and may respond differently to electrical stimulation, but it is important to appreciate that the patient populations of all studies included in our final meta-analysis and from which we observed any patterns, included either patients with OSA, or healthy individuals. Therefore, any specific concerns regarding heterogeneity of the patient population due to the inclusion of patients with central or mixed sleep apnoea (which could contribute to variability in the outcomes and make it more difficult to generalise conclusions about the efficacy of electrical stimulation to treat OSA) could be discarded.

It was also difficult to compare the risk of bias and quality for the different study designs included, as these required assessment via multiple tools. The small number of included studies meant the estimated between-study variance indicated low precision, and we were unable to create funnel plots to assess publication bias [55]. Additionally, a Kruskal–Wallis test was only performed for one study [41], meaning normality could not be tested for the remaining three studies included.

Although there was a trend in the association of electrical stimulation and reduction in blood pressure, it is difficult to prove a causal link based on these findings alone. The results from the pooled meta-analyses have wide confidence intervals, and the limited data available, specifically on the effect of electrical stimulation on blood pressure, may have contributed to the lack of power for these comparisons. Heterogeneity for the effect of electrical stimulation on systolic, diastolic and mean arterial blood pressure was 60%, 85% and 86%, respectively. The moderate-to-high degree of heterogeneity suggests that variability between studies was less likely due to chance [56]. Calculation of mean arterial blood pressure (which may not have been performed uniformly between studies), or the values used to calculate the change in the blood pressure (Δ BP) are possible reasons for this. The method of calculating mean arterial blood pressure was only provided for one study [39], while in another study sham therapy was used for the calculation of baseline blood pressure [40]. In addition, values for the mean and standard deviation were not consistently provided between studies, which could have further contributed to the modest certainty of our findings.

Moreover, blood pressure measurements were not consistently standardised between studies, and it is likely that patients will have had differences between daytime and sleep-related blood pressure measurements. It is necessary to make such a distinction, as “nondipper” hypertension is associated with a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular events [52]. To stratify patients, 24-h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring offers greater insight beyond standard daytime readings by looking at sleep-related differences in hypertension, including deviations from physiological “dipping” and elevated nocturnal heart rate [57]. This positive predictive value may help in identifying patients with OSA who are likely to have better control of blood pressure following interventions, allowing for further optimisation in their management strategies. Only one study in our meta-analysis considered 24-h blood pressure, but did not identify patients as being “dippers” [40]. Patients with this profile may have responded differently to electrical stimulation, but since the studies included in our meta-analysis did not differentiate between the hypertension types of their included cohorts, this may limit the extent to which we were able to draw specific conclusions regarding a causal relationship between electrical stimulation use, and reduction in blood pressure in patients with OSA. As the effectiveness of HNS and TENS on blood pressure may also differ between these two hypertension groups, this could also be a possible reason for the variability seen in the results of our meta-analysis, and could also have been a potential confounder.

This further highlights the need to provide more reliable and standardised methods of blood pressure measurement when reporting results in future studies that investigate the effect of electrical stimulation in patients with OSA. Transparency into the methods used to calculate mean arterial blood pressure and Δ BP (or providing the values for these to be calculated) will enable more accurate and meaningful comparisons to be made between different study designs, and patients with different hypertension profiles. As mentioned previously, the time at which blood pressure was measured and stimulation was delivered is also highly relevant, and may help in explaining the clinical significance of future findings. If studies do not account for the hypertension profile of included patients, this may introduce variability to the results and will not allow more nuanced conclusions to be drawn regarding the effect of electrical stimulation on reducing blood pressure in patients with OSA.

There was a lack of standardised reporting in AHI values to measure sleep apnoea correction following the use of HNS or TENS across the studies included in our meta-analysis. Although three included studies provided baseline AHI values [40–42], post-treatment AHI values were not consistently reported. Changes in hypoxic burden such as oxygen desaturation index (ODI) or time spent with oxygen saturation <90% (t90) were not reported in any of the four included studies and could therefore not be pooled for further analysis. This limits our ability to determine whether the observed changes in blood pressure could be directly attributed to the improvements seen in the severity of sleep disordered breathing.

Recent evidence has questioned the utility of AHI as a single prognostic marker for cardiovascular risk in OSA, instead highlighting the importance of calculating the hypoxic burden using the ODI and t90 as better correlates of cardiovascular risk in patients with OSA [58]. Therefore, while our meta-analysis focused on blood pressure outcomes, our ability to assess the extent to which OSA severity was corrected with electrical stimulation was limited by the use of AHI to measure this. Thus, it is worth considering improvements in blood pressure in relation to reductions in the hypoxic index of patients with OSA following electrical stimulation in future studies, to better evaluate the physiological impact and therapeutic effectiveness of electrical stimulation. Addressing this may strengthen the clinical relevance of electrical stimulation in hypertensive OSA populations, by potentially providing important mechanistic insights into the cardiovascular benefits.

Conclusion

The results of our systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that electrical stimulation of the upper airway dilator muscles may cause a reduction in blood pressure, although this was a trend in the included studies. There may be a longer-lasting effect on blood pressure by alleviating nocturnal obstructive respiratory events in patients with OSA that leads to an overall lower sympathetic tone, but there seems to be an immediate effect of electrical current on the baroreceptor in awake subjects. Further well-designed studies are needed to evaluate the impact of electrical stimulation on blood pressure in patients with OSA to elucidate and refine its safe use, possibly as a clinical adjunct treatment.
