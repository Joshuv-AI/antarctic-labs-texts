Stability studies of cynaropicrin–major sesquiterpene lactone of Cynara cardunculus leaves
Authors: Daniela Rosa, Carlos Rial, José MG Molinillo, Alejandro Sampalo, Rosa M Varela, Francisco A Macías, Maria F Duarte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDCynaropicrin, the major sesquiterpene lactone in Cynara cardunculus leaves, exhibits phytotoxic activity and is a promising candidate for sustainable weed management. However, its environmental stability and the biological activity of its transformation products under agronomic and soil conditions remain poorly understood. This study investigated its stability under high temperature (50 °C) and pH (5 and 8), mimicking agronomic extremes, and under soil context.RESULTSCynaropicrin remained stable at 50 °C and pH 5 for up to 72 h but degraded extensively at pH 8, with an 80% concentration reduction over 7 days. Deacylcynaropicrin emerged as the major degradation product under alkaline conditions. In soil, complete degradation occurred within 7 days, producing dehydrocynaropicrin and a new compound, 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin. Bioassays demonstrated that both degradation products exhibited higher phytotoxic activity against P. oleracea than cynaropicrin and deacylcynaropicrin.CONCLUSIONThis research is the first to highlight cynaropicrin's stability, characterize the structures of its degradation products, and their phytotoxicity, showing the strong impact of environmental factors and microbes. The findings underscore its ecological importance and potential for sustainable weed control. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Weeds pose a significant challenge to global agriculture, competing with crops for essential resources, reducing yields, and contributing to substantial economic losses. Historically, synthetic herbicides have been the primary solution for weed management. However, their extensive use has led to environmental concerns, including soil and water contamination and the emergence of herbicide‐resistant weed species. 1 To address these challenges, allelopathy has gained interest as a potential approach for sustainable weed control. 2 , 3 Allelochemicals are naturally occurring bioactive compounds that influence the growth, survival, and reproduction of neighboring plants. These substances function as natural defense mechanisms, and in the context of phytotoxicity, allelochemicals can exhibit selectivity against weeds while offering an environmentally friendly alternative to synthetic herbicides. 2 Unlike conventional herbicides, allelochemicals degrade naturally, minimizing long‐term ecological impacts. 4

The efficacy of allelochemicals in soil is influenced by various environmental and biological factors, including climate conditions, soil properties, and interactions with microorganisms. Once released into the soil, these compounds undergo retention, transformation, and transport processes, which can alter their phytotoxic effects. In many cases, microbial degradation leads to the reduction of allelochemical activity due to structural modifications. 2 , 4 A study by Xiao et al. (2020) demonstrated that phenolic compounds derived from rice (Oryza sativa L.) initially inhibited the germination of lettuce (Lactuca sativa L.) seeds. However, as microbial activity degraded these compounds, their inhibitory effects diminished. 5 Conversely, certain degradation products may exhibit enhanced bioactivity compared to their parent compounds. The degradation of benzoxazinoids, including 2‐O‐d‐glucopyranosyl‐4‐hydroxy‐(2H)‐1,4‐benzoxazin‐3(4H)‐one (DIBOA‐Glc), 2,4‐dihydroxy‐(2H)‐1,4‐benzoxazin‐3(4H)‐one (DIBOA), and 2,4‐dihydroxy‐7‐methoxy‐(2H)‐1,4‐benzoxazin‐3(4H)‐one (DIMBOA), has been extensively studied, revealing that their transformation products, such as benzoxazolin‐2‐one (BOA), 6‐methoxybenzoxazolin‐2‐one (MBOA), 2‐aminophenoxazin‐3‐one (APO), and 2‐amino‐7‐methoxyphenoxazin‐3‐one (AMPO), exhibit stronger phytotoxic effects. 6 The bioactivity of these compounds has been evaluated against various plant species, including Triticum aestivum L., Allium cepa L., Lycopersicon esculentum Will., Lepidium sativum L., Lactuca sativa L., Lolium rigidum L., and Avena fatua L. Among these, APO demonstrated the highest phytotoxic potential, challenging the assumption that benzoxazinones such as DIBOA and DIMBOA are the primary active compounds. 7 , 8

Sesquiterpene lactones are a class of natural products with demonstrated phytotoxic activity, making them promising candidates for sustainable weed management. 3 , 9 , 10 , 11 These compounds are predominantly found in the Asteraceae family, including Cynara cardunculus L., where they occur in high concentrations. In C. cardunculus leaves, sesquiterpene lactones account for 94.5 g/kg of dry weight (DW), with cynaropicrin as the major constituent (87.5 g/kg DW). 12 Previous studies from our research team have reported the phytotoxic activity of sesquiterpene lactones from C. cardunculus leaves, both in purified form and as part of crude extracts, demonstrating their efficacy against a broad spectrum of weed species. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Our prior research evaluated the phytotoxic potential, and proposed a mode of action of C. cardunculus leaf extracts enriched in sesquiterpene lactones against P. oleracea in hydroponic conditions. These studies indicated that the compounds inhibited plant growth by reducing chlorophyll content and inducing oxidative stress, suggesting the potential of sesquiterpene lactone‐enriched fractions as bioherbicides for field applications. 16 To the best of our knowledge, there is an absence of available literature concerning the transformation processes of these compounds within soil matrices and the consequent phytotoxicity of their degradation products.

To address this knowledge gap, this study investigated the chemical stability of cynaropicrin under abiotic factors, specifically temperature and pH, as well as its transformation in soil. Given that cynaropicrin is the dominant sesquiterpene lactone in C. cardunculus leaves, its degradation profile was analyzed, and the resulting transformation products were identified. Additionally, the direct influence of standard agricultural soil on cynaropicrin degradation was also assessed. The phytotoxic activity of these degradation products was evaluated using a wheat coleoptile bioassay and against the common weed P. oleracea, thereby elucidating the impact of soil‐mediated transformation on the potential of cynaropicrin application. This study identified for the first time dehydrocynaropicrin and 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin as degradation products, the latter being a new compound reported for the first time in the literature and provided a comprehensive comparative assessment of the phytotoxic activities of cynaropicrin and its degradation products. The findings have significant implications for understanding the allelopathic potential of C. cardunculus and the further development of bioherbicides based on its bioactive compounds.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General experimental procedure

Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer Spectrum TWO IR spectrophotometer with major absorptions reported as wavenumbers (ν̃, cm − 1). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained at 400 MHz on Bruker spectrometers (Karlsruhe, Germany) using CDCl3 (Merck, Rahway, Union County, NJ, USA), as the solvent, with its residual peak as an internal reference (δ 7.26 ppm for 1H and δ 77.0 ppm for 13C). NOESY‐1D spectra were recorded at 500 MHz to determine the relative orientation of the stereocenters.

Cynaropicrin stability studies under abiotic conditions

For pH stability studies, two conditions were applied: pH 5 and pH 8, representing the typical acidic and alkaline extremes found in agricultural soils. In a different experiment, temperature stability was assessed at 50 °C, simulating the upper extreme found in crop conditions. 17 To achieve pH conditions were used acetic acid glacial 99.8% (Labchem, Barcelona, Spain) and sodium acetate anhydrous (99%, Labchem, Barcelona, Spain) (0.100 M and 0.160 M respectively) buffer adjusted for pH 5, and phosphate buffer [0.472 M of Na2HPO4·12H2O (98–102%, Labchem, Barcelona, Spain) and 0.013 M of NaH2PO4, (98%, Labchem, Barcelona, Spain)] for pH 8. The stability study was prepared with cynaropicrin initial concentration of 1 ppm and added in individual test tubes prepared for each time collection (0.5, 1, 24, 48 and 72 h) in triplicate. The test tubes were incubated at 25 °C and agitated at 440 rpm using a Heidolph tube shaker (Schwabach, Germany). The collected samples were filtered using PTFE filters (0.22 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, U.S.A) and analyzed using UPLC‐QTOF.

For temperature stability study, a hotplate with continuous stirring was used to maintain a silicone oil bath at 50 °C. The experimental conditions, including cynaropicrin concentration, incubation period, and sample collection times, remained the same as described above. The results are expressed as the ratio of cynaropicrin concentration at each time point to the initial concentration (C/C0). One‐way ANOVA was performed for statistical analysis, followed by Dunnett's test to compare time points against time zero (*: P < 0.05, **: P < 0.01, ***: P < 0.001).

Cynaropicrin stability study in soil

Blumenerde® universal substrate was used for cynaropicrin stability study in soil. Cynaropicrin (2.5 ppm in ultrapure water, 1.5 mL) was added to test tubes containing 0.4 g of soil. Three test tubes were incubated at 25 °C with agitation at 440 rpm for pre‐determined time points (0.5, 1, 24, 48, 72, and 168 h). After the incubation period, tubes were centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min and the supernatant collected. The collected samples were filtered using PTFE filters (0.22 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) and analyzed using UPLC‐QTOF. The results are presented as C/C0 ratios. One‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's test was used for statistical comparisons (*: P < 0.05, **: P < 0.01, ***: P < 0.001).

A portion of the soil was sterilized using an autoclave at 121 °C and a pressure of 2 atm for 22 min. The experiment described previously was repeated with this soil using a laminar flow hood to ensure sterile conditions.

Isolation of cynaropicrin degradation products

Degradation products were isolated in both cynaropicrin stability experiments. Regarding stability under abiotic factors, it was found that cynaropicrin concentration decreases at pH 8, and so, for degradation products assessment a scaled‐up experiment was conducted (24 tubes, C0 = 1 ppm). The solvents for isolation of compounds were HPLC‐grade. Methanol (MeOH), acetonitrile (ACN) and acetone were obtained from Fischer Chemicals (Geel, Belgium). Water was type I obtained from an Ultramatic system from Wasserlab (Barbatáin, Spain). A Strata™‐X 33 μm reverse phase column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) was used for sample preparation before isolation and purification. Thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) was performed using Silica Gel 60 RP‐18 F254S aluminum sheets (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Reverse‐phase chromatography (Strata™‐X column) was used with the mobile phases ultrapure water and methanol (60:40 v/v), ultrapure water and methanol (40:60 v/v), methanol and acetone, resulting in four fractions. Thin‐layer chromatography was used to identify degradation products onto the four fractions, being identified at ultrapure water and methanol (60:40 v/v) mobile phase resulting fraction. High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) Merck‐Hitachi D‐7000 system (Tokyo, Japan) with a refractive index detector (Elite LaChrom L‐2490; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) and a Gemini 5 μm C18 110 Å column (250 × 4.6 mm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) was used for the purification of degradation products of ultrapure water and methanol (60:40 v/v) fraction (18.1 mg) using isocratic mode with mobile phase of ultrapure water and acetonitrile (80:20 v/v) at 1 mL/min flow. The purity and the structures of the isolated compounds were confirmed by the 1H and 13C‐NMR spectra.

As for cynaropicrin degradation in standard soil, a higher scale of the experiment was also performed (24 tubes, 1.2 g of soil, 4.5 mL of cynaropicrin solution 10 ppm). After the incubation period, tubes were centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min and the supernatant collected. The residual soil pellet was added 4 mL of water, vortex stirred for 10 s and centrifuged at same conditions mentioned. The collected supernatant was filtered using Nylon filters (0.45 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, U.S.A). In order to recover as many degradation products as possible, the resulting pellet was resuspended in acetone, vortexed and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected, filtered and the acetone evaporated. The aqueous supernatant and acetone extract were used for isolation and purification of degradation product. Firstly, aqueous supernatant was purified using a Strata™‐X reverse phase column, and methanol as mobile phase A and acetone as mobile phase B, resulting in two fractions: Fraction A (methanol) and Fraction B (acetone). The acetone extract was resuspended in distilled water and then the same procedure applied, and the fractions obtained were combined with those obtained in the previous purification process. Isolation and purification of degradation products of Fraction A (49.3 mg) and Fraction B (28.9 mg) were made through HPLC in isocratic mode with mobile phase of ultrapure water and acetonitrile (75:25 v/v). The purity and the structures of the isolated compounds were confirmed by the 1H and 13C NMR and NOESY‐1D spectroscopy.

Sesquiterpene lactone quantification via UPLC‐QTOF

Ultra‐performance liquid chromatography‐quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (UPLC‐QTOF, Waters XEVO G2S QTOF, MA, USA) was performed using electrospray ionization in positive mode for molecular weight determination and quantification with 120 °C of source temperature, 450 °C of desolvation temperature, 850 L/h of gas desolvation flow, 20 L/h of gas cone flow and 40 V of cone voltage. The resolution in mass was 22 000 and the mass range 150–800 Da. The column chromatography was a Kinetex 1.7 μm F5 100 Å 100 × 2.1 mm (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, U.S.A) maintained at 65 °C. The solvents for UPLC‐MS/MS analysis were UHPLC/MS grade. Methanol (MeOH), acetonitrile (ACN), acetone and formic acid were obtained from Fischer Chemicals (Geel, Belgium). Water was type I obtained from an Ultramatic system from Wasserlab (Barbatáin, Spain). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water, 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (MeOH, 0.1% formic acid) and the flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min. The linear gradient system was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 30% B; 0.5–1 min, to 50% B; 1–2.5 min to 70% B; 2.5–3 min, to 95% B; 3–4 min to 30% B, 4–5 min, 30% B. The injection volume was 5 μL. Sesquiterpene lactones isolated and cynaropicrin standard were prepared in methanol at 1000 ppm and diluted with water from 10 000 to 1 ppb to perform the calibrations curves. Samples were filtered with 0.22 μm pore size membrane filters (Phenomenex, USA).

Wheat coleoptile bioassay

All experiments were carried out according to Rial et al. with some modifications. 13 Cynaropicrin and degradation products were tested at the concentrations of 10, 30, 100, 300 and 1000 μM, pre‐dissolved in 0.1% of dimethyl sulfoxide (99.9%, PanReac AppliChem, Barcelona, Spain) and diluted in phosphate–citrate buffer [K2HPO4·3H2O (99–102%, PanReac AppliChem, Barcelona, Spain); citric acid monohydrate (99.5%, Sigma Aldrich Co, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing 2% sucrose (99%, PanReac AppliChem, Barcelona, Spain)] at pH 5.6. The commercial herbicide Logran® (Syngenta, Switzerland) was used as positive control at same conditions and concentrations as above described. The measurement of coleoptile elongations was made by digitalization of their images and processed through Photomed Equipment Software. The results are displayed as a percentage deviation from the control. Zero denotes control, while positive values are associated with stimulation and negative values are associated with inhibition.

Phytotoxic activity against Portulaca oleracea

Cynaropicrin and degradation products phytotoxic activity was assessed against Portulaca oleracea (Cantueso Natural Seeds, Córdoba, Spain). Bioassay was conducted as previously described by Rial et al., with some modifications. 13 Briefly, cynaropicrin and degradation products were prepared at concentrations of 1000, 300, 100, 30, and 10 μM, pre‐dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (5 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide solution/mL buffer) and diluted in 2‐[N‐morpholino]ethanesulfonic acid (99.5%, Sigma Aldrich Co, St. Louis, MO, USA) (MES) buffer (pH 6). Pendimethalin (98%, Fisher Scientific, New Hampshire, EUA), active ingredient of Stomp®Aqua commercial herbicide, was used as positive control and applied at the same concentrations mentioned. Seeds were incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 8 days. Measurements of germination rate, root and shoot length were performed using a Fitomed system which allowed automatic data acquisition and statistical analysis using its associated software. Data were analyzed statistically using Welch's test, with significance fixed at 0.01 and 0.05. The results are displayed as a percentage deviation from the control. Zero denotes control, while positive values are associated with stimulation and negative values are associated with inhibition.

Statistical analysis

The IC50 values were determined by performing a non‐linear regression. Degradation constant rate (k), and time of half‐life were obtained adjusting the logarithm of concentration versus time to a linear regression. GraphPad Prism 5 package (San Diego, CA, USA) was used in both cases.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Cynaropicrin stability studies at different conditions of pH and temperature

The impact of pH and temperature as abiotic factors on the chemical stability of cynaropicrin was assessed. A temperature of 50 °C was selected as it represents an extreme agronomic condition exceeding typical agricultural temperatures. 17 Additionally, pH 5 and pH 8 were chosen to represent acidic and alkaline soils, respectively, commonly found in agricultural environments. 17 An incubation period of 72 h was established, with cynaropicrin concentration monitored at time points 0, 0.5, 1, 24, 48, and 72 h to evaluate the effects of these abiotic factors. The results of the stability studies under different temperature and pH conditions are presented in Figs 1 and 2, respectively.

Cynaropicrin stability curve at pH 5 and pH 8 over 72 h. Values are expressed as the ratio of cynaropicrin (1) concentration at each time point relative to the initial concentration (C/C0). C0 is 1 ppm. Statistical significance is indicated as *: P < 0.05, **: P < 0.01, ***: P < 0.001 compared with the initial concentration.

As shown in Fig. 1, cynaropicrin (1) remained stable at 50 °C over the 72‐h incubation period, with no statistically significant variations. Temperature is an abiotic factor known to influence compound degradation, as it can increase soil temperature and microbial activity, particularly in soils rich in organic matter. 4 This experiment investigated the thermal stability of cynaropicrin in water to determine whether temperature alone could influence its stability before interactions with soil properties occur. The results indicate that temperature alone did not significantly alter cynaropicrin stability, and no studies were found describing similar degradation mechanisms under agronomic thermal conditions. These findings are consistent with the profile of the sesquiterpene lactone eremantholide C, which showed stability under various temperature conditions (accelerated at 40 °C for 6 months and forced at 90 °C for 3 days). 18

In the pH stability study (Fig. 2), cynaropicrin (1) exhibited minimal degradation at pH 5, with a 7% concentration decrease at 0.5 h and a maximum reduction of 12% at 72 h, indicating high stability under acidic conditions. Nevertheless, we could not detect any degradation product in the solution. However, at pH 8, cynaropicrin (1) remained stable up to 24 h, after which degradation was observed, reaching a 38% reduction at 72 h. To further investigate this degradation, a scaled‐up experiment was conducted to isolate sufficient degradation product for structural characterization and phytotoxicity evaluation. HPLC purification confirmed that compound 2 was the primary degradation product of cynaropicrin (1).

Compound 2 1H‐NMR spectra (Figs S2.1–S2.6) showed some differences when compared with compound 1 1H‐NMR spectra (Figs S1.1–S1.6). The signals of H‐3′a, H‐3′b, and H‐4′ correspond to the hydroxymethacrylic group at C‐8 of compound 1 disappear, with a shielding of the signal assigned to H‐8 from δ 4.56 to δ 3.96. This indicates the saponification of the ester moiety bonded at C‐8 in this degradation compound, that was confirmed by its molecular formula obtained by mass spectrometry. Its 13C‐NMR spectrum (S2) supports this structure with the disappearance of the four carbons of the group at C‐8 of compound 1. All the spectroscopic data were identical to those described in the literature for deacylcynaropicrin (2) 19 (Fig. 3).

Chemical structures of degradation products from cynaropicrin pH 8 degradation experiment: cynaropicrin (1) and deacylcynaropicrin (2).

After purifying and identifying compound 2, its concentration profile was analyzed alongside compound 1. A new degradation curve was generated, extending the incubation period to 168 h (7 days) (Fig. 4).

Soil pH plays a crucial role in the degradation and transformation of phytotoxic compounds. 4 Studies indicate that soil pH affects the persistence and bioactivity of allelochemicals by influencing microbial degradation rates. For example, Galán‐Pérez et al. found that scopoletin was more stable in acidic soils but degraded more rapidly in alkaline soils, leading to a loss of phytotoxic activity in the latter. Consequently, phytotoxic activity was demonstrated in acidic soil but not in alkaline soil. 20 Similarly, cynaropicrin degradation at pH 8 resulted in the formation of deacylcynaropicrin (2), prompting further phytotoxic evaluations to determine activity changes.

Cynaropicrin stability in soil

The stability of cynaropicrin (1) in soil was assessed under standard agronomic conditions over a 168‐h incubation period. Samples were analyzed at 0, 0.5, 1, 24, 48, 72, and 168 h, and degradation products were isolated for identification. For a first evaluation of the stability of cynaropicrin and its degradation products, we used commercial soil to ensure controlled and well‐characterized conditions, facilitating the possibility of obtaining reproducible results. 21

Cynaropicrin remained stable for the first 0.5 h of incubation in standard soil (Fig. 6). A significant 14.2% reduction occurred after 1 h, and then gradually decreased. At 24, 48 and 72 h of incubation, the concentration decreased by 31.9, 35.5 and 44.6%, respectively. After 168 h cynaropicrin was below the quantification limit. Compound 1 degradation follows first‐order kinetics in soil. The degradation rate constant (k) in soil is 0.00755 ± 0.00095 h−1, with a corresponding half‐life (t 1/2) of 91.8 ± 12.3 h (Table 2), with a coefficient of variance of 0.94, similar than those found in analogous studies regarding degradation of other compounds in soil. 23 The faster degradation of compound 1 in soil suggests microbial involvement. 2 The transformation process is often associated with structural changes in allelochemicals in contact with soil, mainly due to pH, redox potential and microbial action. In this case, the standard soil used has in the composition 55% of organic matter, used by microorganisms for their survival, and so the microbial population present could be the responsible for the transformation of compound 1 into new compounds. 2

Stability profile of cynaropicrin (1) and the appearance of its degradation products after 168 h of incubation in standard soil: dehydrocynaropicrin (3) and 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin (4). Values are expressed as the ratio of compounds 1, 3 and 4 concentrations at each time point relative to the initial concentration of cynaropicrin (1) (C/C0). C0 is 2.5 ppm.

After verifying the decrease in compound 1 concentration in standard soil, a degradation study was performed on a larger scale. This scale‐up allowed us to obtain a greater amount of compound 1 degradation products, facilitating the determination of their chemical structures and phytotoxic activities. Purification by HPLC revealed that compound 1 degraded into two distinct compounds, designated as compounds 3 and 4. The chemical structures of these compounds were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 5).

Chemical structures of degradation products isolated from cynaropicrin (1) standard soil degradation experiments: dehydrocynaropicrin (3) and 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin (4).

The 1H‐NMR spectrum of degradation product 3 (Figs S3.1–3.6) showed similarities to compound 1, with the notable absence of the signal at δ 4.55 (H‐3) (compound 1 1H‐NMR spectrum, S1; compound 3 1H‐NMR spectrum, S3). This missing signal indicates the oxidation of the hydroxyl group at C‐3 to a carbonyl group. The presence of this ketone group was confirmed in the 13C‐NMR spectrum (Figs S3.1–3.6) by a signal at δ 203.5. Furthermore, the signals corresponding to δ 5.48 (H‐15a) and δ 5.36 (H‐15b) of compound 1 were deshielded to δ 6.34 and δ 6.00, respectively in compound 3. This downfield shift is associated with the formation of a carbonyl group at C‐3, resulting in conjugation of the double bond between C‐4 and C‐15 with the ketone. Based on this spectroscopic data, the structure of product 3 was assigned to dehydrocynaropicrin, that was described as natural product by Ingallina et al. (Fig. 5). 22

Comparison of the 1H‐NMR spectrum of compound 4 (Figs S4.1–S4.8; Table 3) with that of compound 1 (S1) revealed the absence of the signal at δ 4.55 (H‐3). As in compound 3, this is attributed to the loss of the hydrogen atom from the hydroxyl group at C‐3, leading to the formation of a ketone group. The presence of this ketone group was confirmed in the 13C‐NMR spectrum (S4) by a signal at δ 218.2, that shows correlation with a signal at δ 2.39 in the HMBC experiment. Additionally, the signals at δ 5.48 (H‐15a) and δ 5.36 (H‐15b) of compound 1 were not observed, indicating that the exocyclic double bond at C‐4 was hydrogenated to form a methyl group. This transformation is evidenced by a doublet integrating for three protons at δ 1.28 (H‐15) and a new signal at δ 2.37, corresponding to H‐4 in compound 4, correlated in the COSY experiment. To determine the stereochemistry of methyl 15, two NOE‐1D experiments were performed. Irradiation of the H‐6 signal resulted in a NOE enhancement of the H‐8 and H‐4 signals, whereas no enhancement of H‐6 was observed when H‐5 was irradiated, demonstrating that these protons have the same relative β‐orientation. This observation allowed for the assignment of the α‐orientation to methyl 15, which agrees with the W coupling observed between H‐4 and H‐2β (J = 1.2 Hz). The similar chemical shifts and coupling constants of the remaining signals, when compared to cynaropicrin, indicate that the stereochemistry at the other centers of the compound is identical to that of the starting material. All assignments were made using COSY, HSQC and HMBC experiments and are shown in Table 3. To the best of our knowledge, the structure of degradation compound 4, 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin, is new and was elucidated for the first time in the present study (Fig. 5). The chemical structure was corroborated by UPLC‐MS/MS, which indicated a molecular weight of 369.1357 g/mol for (C19H22O6Na) [M + Na]+.

Following the purification and identification of the degradation products, they were also identified in the stability profile of compound 1 (Fig. 6 and Table 4).

Based on the stability profile of compound 1 and the appearance of degradation products, compound 3 was observed after 0.5 h of incubation. After 24 h, its concentration was significantly higher (75 ppb) than at the initial time point, reaching a maximum of 162 ppb after 72 h (Table 4). Compound 3 was completely depleted by the end of the 168‐h incubation period. Compound 4 appeared after 48 h of incubation, reaching a maximum concentration of 851 ppb at 168 h. Regarding the conversion of compound 1 to compounds 3 and 4, after 72 h of incubation, compound 1 concentration decreased from 2375 to 1310 ppb (44.6% decrease), while compound 3 concentration was 162 ppb and compound 4 concentration was 425 ppb (24.7% of degradation products). This suggests that these two major compounds are the primary degradation products. The remaining 19.9% is likely attributable to minor degradation compounds that could not be isolated. The reduction in compound 1 concentration may be attributed to various soil characteristics that facilitate its transformation. 2 , 4 , 23 As demonstrated in this study, pH plays a significant role in the stability of cynaropicrin, promoting its degradation and conversion into compound 2 at pH 8. Subsequent analysis of the mixture pH, including compound 1 in standard soil, revealed a pH value of 5.1, at which compound 1 was shown to be stable (Fig. 2); hence, compound 2 was not detected. The identification of compounds 3 and 4 from compound 1 (cynaropicrin) transformation in a soil context indicates that the transformation may be mediated by soil microorganisms rather than pH‐related mechanisms. To verify microbial involvement, a resazurin assay was performed to assess metabolically active microorganisms in the soil. The results showed a marked increase in activity during the first 24 h compared to the initial measurement, as detailed in the Supporting Information (Figs S5, Fig S5.1).

The appearance of compound 3 is observed initially, and subsequently compound 4 begins to appear, with no increase in the concentration of 3 being observed after 48 h. This could indicate that 4 is probably produced from 3, such that a hypothetical degradation pathway would start from the oxidation of cynaropicrin (1) to afford 3 and a subsequent reduction to produce compound 4 (Scheme 1).

Proposed degradation pathway of cynaropicrin in soil.

A study on the degradation of the benzoxazinoid 2,4‐dihydroxy‐7‐methoxy‐(2H)‐1,4‐benzoxazin‐3(4H)‐one (DIMBOA) in soil was conducted by Macías et al. (2004) Compound 1 exhibits a longer half‐life compared to DIMBOA (t 1/2 ≈ 31 min) but a similar half‐life to DIBOA (t 1/2 ≈ 24–62 h) under similar experimental conditions. This suggests that cynaropicrin is more persistent in the environment than DIMBOA but has similar persistence to DIBOA. 6 The degradation of phytotoxic chemicals can result in the loss or enhancement of this activity, depending on the chemical structure of the compounds, as functional groups such as hydroxyl, methoxy, and carboxyl groups can undergo hydrolysis. 23

Consistent with previous studies on allelochemical interactions with soil, the degradation of allelochemicals into new compounds has been associated with soil microorganisms. 2 , 4 , 23 The persistence of the sesquiterpene lactone isoalantolactone in soil was investigated, and the compound disappeared completely after 90 days, most likely due to binding to humic material in the soil and microbial decomposition. 24 To confirm that the observed degradation was caused by the microorganisms present in the soil, the soil was sterilized using an autoclave at 121 °C and a pressure of 2 atm for 22 min. Subsequently, the experiment described previously was repeated but using a laminar flow hood to ensure sterile conditions. As can be observed in Fig. 7, the concentration of compound 1 remained stable throughout the experiment, which rules out the possibility that soil or abiotic conditions were responsible for the transformation of cynaropicrin into compounds 3 and 4.

Stability profile of cynaropicrin (1) over 168 h of incubation in sterilized standard soil. Values are expressed as the ratio of cynaropicrin (1) concentration at each time point relative to the initial concentration (C/C0). C0 is 2.5 ppm.

These results are in agreement with previous studies highlighting the central role of soil microbial communities in the transformation of bioactive compounds. In particular, research on Solanum rostratum litter demonstrated that allelopathic inhibition was significantly reduced in unsterilized soils compared to sterilized conditions, indicating that native microorganisms actively degrade or transform allelochemicals. This microbial activity was associated with shifts in metabolite profiles, including the reduction of phenolic compounds and the enrichment of microbial taxa known for their degradation potential. Similarly, the absence of cynaropicrin transformation in sterilized soil and its persistence under abiotic conditions in our study further support that its conversion into compounds 3 and 4 is predominantly mediated by soil microorganisms rather than physicochemical factors such as pH. 25 The effect of the degradation of compound 1 (cynaropicrin) and the phytotoxic activity of the degradation products is also evaluated in the present study.

With respect to seed germination, the inhibitory effect observed did not exceed 20% for any compound evaluated, except for compound 2 at a concentration of 1000 μM, which exhibited a germination inhibition of 27% (Fig. S6.1). Regarding root growth inhibition, compounds 3 and 4 were the most effective at 1000 μM, with inhibition percentages of 91 and 93%, respectively. At 300 μM, inhibition remained high, at 81 and 79%, respectively. Compound 3 continued to significantly inhibit root growth at 100 μM (54%), whereas compound 4 showed a marked reduction in phytotoxic activity to 27%. Compounds 1 and 2 exhibited strong inhibition effects at the highest concentrations tested, with inhibition percentages of 90% and 80% at 1000 μM, respectively, comparable to compounds 3 and 4. However, a more pronounced decline in activity was observed at lower concentrations, reaching 32% and 28%, respectively. Notably, compounds 1, 3, and 4 displayed phytotoxic activity similar to the commercial herbicide at 1000 μM. Regarding shoot growth inhibition, the phytotoxic effect was generally less pronounced across all evaluated compounds. Compound 3 was the most active, with 71% inhibition at 1000 μM and 56% at 300 μM. Compounds 1 and 4 exhibited similar phytotoxic activity at 1000 μM, with inhibition percentages of 61% and 65%, respectively. However, at 300 μM, compound 4 retained higher activity (39%) compared to compound 1 (7%). Compound 2 exhibited the lowest shoot inhibition effect, with a maximum inhibition percentage of 49% at 1000 μM. This study is the first to report the phytotoxic activity of these degradation products against P. oleracea.

The observed phytotoxic activity correlates with the chemical structure of the compounds. The transformation of compound 1 into compound 4 resulted in enhanced phytotoxic activity. Compound 3 demonstrated higher phytotoxicity than compound 1, likely due to the presence of a carbonyl group at C‐3 instead of a hydroxyl group. The hydroxymethacrylic group does not appear to significantly influence activity, as indicated by the similar activity levels of compound 1 and compound 2. However, this functional group may modulate other physicochemical properties such as solubility and bioavailability. The greater phytotoxic activity of dehydrocynaropicrin (compound 3) and 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin (compound 4) compared to cynaropicrin (compound 1) has significant ecological and agronomic implications. In the soil environment, cynaropicrin transformation could lead to a temporal shift in the herbicidal efficacy of Cynara cardunculus residues, with the degradation products playing a more prominent role in weed suppression over time. The persistence of the activity of the degradation compounds could be due to the fact that they maintain the α‐methylene‐γ‐lactone moiety in their structure, which is usually considered responsible for the activity of most sesquiterpene lactones. 26 Compound 3 presents an additional carbonyl group conjugated with a double bond, that could explain its higher activity. 27 This suggests that the allelopathic potential of C. cardunculus is not solely attributed to cynaropicrin but also to its dynamic transformation products in the soil.

CONCLUSION

Our research elucidates the degradation pathway of cynaropicrin under various abiotic and biotic conditions, revealing three degradation products with notable phytotoxic activity, being one of them new in the literature. The behavior of the sesquiterpene lactone cynaropicrin (1) under abiotic factors such as temperature and pH, as well as biotic interactions in standard soil, was investigated for the first time in this study. Cynaropicrin (1) remained stable at 50 °C and pH 5 over 72 h, indicating that extreme agricultural temperatures and acidic soil conditions do not significantly impact its stability. However, at pH 8, a 38% decrease in cynaropicrin concentration was observed after 72 h. As a result of this degradation, the study was extended to 168 h, identifying deacylcynaropicrin (compound 2) as the major degradation product. In soil, cynaropicrin underwent complete degradation within 168 h, yielding dehydrocynaropicrin (compound 3) and 4,15‐dihydro‐dehydrocynaropicrin (compound 4) as degradation products, with the latter being identified for the first time. The degradation of cynaropicrin in soil and at pH 8 followed first‐order kinetics. The accelerated degradation of cynaropicrin in soil compared to pH 8 alone suggests microbial involvement. The findings indicate that soil parameters, particularly microbial activity and alkaline pH, significantly influence cynaropicrin transformation, improving our understanding of its ecological impact in agricultural contexts. Regarding phytotoxicity, degradation products 3 and 4 exhibited higher phytotoxic activity than compounds 1 and 2, effectively inhibiting both root and shoot growth. These results suggest that soil‐mediated transformations not only alter the concentration of cynaropicrin but also generate degradation products with enhanced phytotoxic effects. This underscores the dynamic nature of cynaropicrin's allelopathic effects in the soil environment and highlights its potential for sustainable weed management strategies using Cynara cardunculus. This is the first study to investigate the stability of a sesquiterpene lactone through the characterization of its degradation products, their kinetics, and the evaluation of their phytotoxic activity. The research presented in this work addresses a relevant aspect of this family of compounds. These natural products are widely recognized in the literature for playing a fundamental role in the phytotoxic activity of many plants. Consequently, this study provides valuable insights for the development of plant‐based herbicides.

It is important to note that these degradation studies were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions. Further research under field conditions, considering factors such as varying soil types, moisture levels, and microbial community dynamics, is necessary to fully elucidate the environmental fate of cynaropicrin and its degradation products.
