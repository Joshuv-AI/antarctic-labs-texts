Quantum machine learning driven optimization of nutrient-hormone interactions for enhanced in vitro regeneration of common bean
Authors: Ramazan Katırcı, Muhammad Aasim, Farhan Aadil, Rehan Tariq Chohan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Common bean (Phaseoulus vulgarsis) is an important edible legume crop, but its improvement through modern biotechnological tools has been limited due to the lack of efficient and reproducible in vitro regeneration protocols. This bottleneck restricts the application of biotechnological applications like genetic transformation, genome editing, and crop improvement. To address the challenges, an experiment was designed to investigate the combined effect of KNO3 with Indole Butyric acid (IBA) and naphthalene acetic acid (NAA) on shoot proliferation using two different explants, followed by optimizing the results with quantum machine learning (QML) analysis. Results demonstrated enhanced shoot proliferation with increased KNO3 levels (5700 mg/L), with a shoot count of 6.44 from the shoot meristem explant in the presence of NAA. Whereas lower KNO3 concentration enlarged the shoot length. Results also revealed the impact of auxin-explant interaction, highlighting the significance of a tailored medium for in vitro regeneration. To strengthen prediction and optimization, classical and quantum machine learning (ML) algorithms were also used. A custom quantum circuit, utilizing RX, RZ, and Hadamard gates, was introduced to enhance classification accuracy using quantum superposition and entanglement principles. Results demonstrated the superior performance of a custom quantum circuit with an accuracy and F1 score of 83% and 84% respectively, for shoot counts. Whereas comparable performance was observed for shoot length classification. This hybrid computational approach not only reduced experimental uncertainty but also increased the nutrient-hormone optimization. These findings demonstrate the potential of hybrid quantum-classical approaches in plant biotechnology. The optimized in vitro system can facilitate downstream applications in plant biotechnology for breeding of legumes and other crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07555-x.

Introduction

Conventional plant breeding of economic crops is based on morphological traits, yield traits, genomics, and resilience to biotic and abiotic stress. In the modern era of molecular biology, biotechnology, and bioinformatics, these targets can be achieved in a short time with more precise and targeted breeding. Plant biotechnology is the mainstay of modern breeding, and the establishment of plant tissue culture protocol is the prerequisıte of the whole breeding program for employing biotechnological tools like genome editing, genetic transformation, or inducing haploid or double haploid plants. However, optimization of input factors plays a critical role, and manipulation of culture media can lead to establishing a desired protocol for further use in breeding programs [1]. In Plant tissue culture, Murashige and Skoog medium is [2] is the most recommended basal salt, containing multiple minerals and vitamins required for inducing organogenesis or somatic embryogenesis. In MS medium, Nitrogen (N) is the most abundant nutrient mineral available in the form of NH3NO3 (1650 mg/L) and KNO3 (1900 mg/L). Both N sources regulates the cell growth, metabolism, and culture viability which lead to organogenesis or embryogenesis [3]. The synergistic or antagonistic impact of plant growth regulators (cytokinin-auxin) and other factors, like explants, needs to be optimized according to species-specific requirements [4]. It is also well documented that enhanced N supplementation and source enhance plant stress tolerance by regulating the pH and ionic balance of the culture medium [5].

In plant tissue culture, data generated after experiments are subjected to classical statistical techniques like variance analysis and linear regression models. These techniques are insufficient for optimization due to sensitivity, complexity, and high probability. In recent years, the use of advanced computational models like design of experiment and machine learning (ML) models is increasing to overcome these issues by testing and optimizing single or multiple input variables simultaneously for a concerning output parameter [6]. Likewise, these models are also used for agricultural automation and precision farming, enhancing crop management and yield optimization [7–10]. Besides classical ML, Quantum computing has revolutionized computational capabilities, offering distinct advantages over traditional classical methods [11, 12]. Quantum Support Vector Machines (QSVMs), Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQCs), and Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs) [13, 14] have been employed in current quantum machine learning analysis. Quantum-based algorithms offer enhanced efficiency in information processing compared to classical methods due to employing fundamental quantum mechanical principles like superposition and entanglement [15]. Integration of hybrid classical and quantum methodologies has been documented and revealed the supremacy of QML over classical ML models [16, 17]. The hybrid quantum-classical approaches are efficient for displaying pattern recognition and optimization, especially in agricultural applications. A recent study revealed that the improved computational efficiency for specific problem categories by variational quantum algorithms has been documented [17, 18].

Application of quantum computing is prevailing despite being of relatively new technology in its initial phase of development and application in the real world. Application of QML in plant sciences and agricultural sciences has significantly improved data processing and analysis, particularly in high-dimensional data sets [19–21]. Recently, quantum-enhanced algorithms, including SVC with quantum kernel, quantum features, and Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC), optimized plant tissue culture data more precisely than classical ML models [22, 23]. For plant disease detection, Quantum Support Vector Classifier (QSVC) achieved high recall rates, indicating potential applications in agricultural sciences [24]. Optimization of electroplating processes using artificial intelligence methods has also demonstrated the applicability of quantum-classical hybrid approaches in agricultural technology development. Although QML is a state-of-the-art technique in its evolutionary place, its uses are encouraging, and a systematic analysis of the comparative performance of classical and quantum ML models were conducted in this study.

Although micropropagation of common bean has been reported previously, but with limited cussess and demnds an efficient and reproducible regeneration system. Therefore, this study aimed to integrate (i) nutrient-cytokinin-auxin interactions by analyzing with advanced statistical and computational analyses, (ii) employing hybrid hybrid quantum- classical ML models, and (iii) to enhance the efficacy of quantum algorithms through the design and implementation of advanced quantum circuits, subsequently comparing their performance with state-of-the-art classical/quantum algorithms. Additionally, contemporary innovations in quantum computing architectures and their profound implications for advancing agricultural data analysis methodologies were also explored.

Methodology

Experimental setup

The common bean Cv. Akman used in this study for in vitro regeneration has been procured from Sivas University of Science and Technology, Sivas, Türkiye. The experiment was performed at the Plant Biotechnology Laboratory, Faculty of Agricultural Sciences and Technologies, Sivas University of Science and Technology, Sivas, Türkiye. Uniform selected seeds were surface sterilized by following the protocol established in our laboratory [1] by using 3.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, w/v) for 15 min. The seeds were thereafter rinsed thrice with sterilized distilled water for 5 min. Surface-sterilized seeds were transferred to MS medium without any PGR (plant growth regulators) for germination to extract explants. Shoot meristem (SM) and cotyledonary node (CN) explants were isolated from 10–12-day-old seedlings. Both explants were cultured on regeneration medium containing an already optimized dose of 2.0 mg/L Benzylaminopurine (BAP). Medium was enriched with 0.1 mg/L Indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) or Naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), with a control group without any auxin. Three different concentrations of 1900, 3800, and 5700 mg/L of Potassium nitrate (KNO3) were used for investigating the impact of N. For preparing the medium with 3800 and 5700 mg/L KNO3, an extra dose of KNO3 was added to the culture medium, followed by adjusting the pH to 5.8. The explants were cultured in the growth room with 24 ± 2 °C temperature under a 16-hour light photoperiod equipped with white light-emitting diodes (LEDs), having a light intensity of 2000 lx. The basal medium was prepared as a standard protocol containing 30 g/L sucrose, 4.4 g/L MS (Duchefa Biochemie), and 6.5 g/L agar (Duchefa Biochemie) for gelling. Results generated at the end of the experiment were analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) and response surface regression analysis (RSRA) using the Minitab statistical program. Results were also computed with classical and quantum machine learning models.

Classical and quantum machine learning algorithms

The dataset utilized in this study comprised variations of KNO3, NAA, and IBA concentrations, along with explant type, as input features. The BAP variable was excluded due to its lack of variation across samples. Shoot count and shoot length values were binarized as output variables based on threshold values of 0.6 and 0.4, respectively. Specifically, values above these thresholds were classified as 1, and those below were classified as 0, enabling a binary classification approach to assess performance.

SVC, QSVC, PegasosQSCV, EstimatorQNN, SamplerQNN, VQC, and a custom hybrid model were employed to create the predictive models. The dataset was divided into 70% for training and 30% for testing using the train-test split method. To evaluate performance, metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score were calculated. The hyperparameters of each algorithm were not optimized; instead, default parameter values were used. The purpose of this study is to investigate the impact of a custom-designed circuit on the performance of the existing Qiskit framework. Specifically, ZZFeatureMap was used as the feature map, and TwoLocal was employed as the ansatz for the quantum circuits. By comparing the performance of the algorithms with their default settings, the study evaluates the effectiveness of the additional circuit design in improving model performance. For coding, the Python programming language was employed, while Scikit-learn and Qiskit libraries were employed for model development and evaluation.

Quantum support vector classifier (QSVC)

QSVC solves problems like SVM by using quantum kernels to map data into high-dimensional Hilbert spaces, but does so with quantum state representations like superposition and entanglement to create expressive decision boundaries. QSVC is more effective for datasets having intricate correlations and captures high-order dependencies due to quantum feature mapping. Feature mapping and quantum state preparation enable the model to outperform classical SVM in non-linear separability problems.

Pegasos quantum support vector classifier (PegasosQSCV)

PegasosQSCV is a stochastic sub-gradient algorithm used for optimizing margins and reducing computational complexity. Large feature dimensions, faster convergence, simplified iterative approach, and flexibility for non-linear problems make them suitable. High scalability, converging to the optimal solution with fewer iterations, are other advantages of the PegasosQSCV model.

Variational quantum classifier (VQC)

The Variational Quantum Classifier (VQC) is a classification model based on parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) to optimize circuit parameters. The VQC encodes classical data into quantum states through feature maps, and transforms the input data into quantum representations, followed by transforming the states using parameterized gates, and is optimized to minimize a loss function. The VQC captures data relationships, enables non-linear decision boundaries, adjusts layers and parameters, and accelerates learning through quantum parallelism.

Custom model (CM)

The quantum circuit is composed of multiple layers for data encoding, processing, and classification (Fig. 1), used in this study. It comprises three different layers named ZZFeatureMAP Layer, TwoLocal Layer, and Last Layer. The first (ZZFeatureMap) layer is used in QML applications to encode input data into quantum states through parametrized transformations with the help of RZ and CNOT gates. These gates are used for introducing entanglement and effective correlations within the input data. The second (TwoLocal) layer is the primary component of the ansatz structure, and it combines parameterized single-qubit rotations (RZ and RX gates) with two-qubit entangling gates (CNOT or CZ gates). This layer allows the circuit to process data through optimization and parameter adjustments, making it adaptable and suitable for machine learning tasks. The third (Last_Layer) layer is a novel addition that integrates outputs from the ansatz and processes them through an auxiliary qubit, optimizing amplitude values of 0 or 1. Figure 2 illustrates the Last_Layer with the sequences of RX, RZ, and Hadamard (H) gates applied to the qubits. The circuit uses different gates for different purposes (RX gates: initializing qubits, entangling gates like CZ or CNOT to establish correlations; RZ gates: for phase shifts, and Hadamard gates: for quantum parallelism. In this study, the innovative custom design was evaluated and compared with other quantum circuits.

Results

Shoot proliferation and growth analysis

The study presents the variable impact of KNO3, IBA, and NAA concentration on shoot proliferation and shoot elongation for both explants. ANOVA analysis is presented in Table 1 and revealed a statistically significant impact at p < 0.01 for both parameters. Increase of KNO3 without auxins resulted in a parallel increase in shoot count with a maximum at 5700 mg/L for NS explant (5.733). A similar trend was also observed for KNO3 with IBA (5.467) and NAA (6.443). For the CN explant, variable shoot count was observed, and maximum shoot count was recorded as 4.353 from 5700 mg/L KNO3 without auxin and 2.93 from 5700 mg/L KNO3 with 0.1 mg/L IBA. On the contrary, supplementation of 3800 mg/L KNO3 with 0.1 mg/L NAA yielded a maximum shoot count of 5.177. The study found that shoot length was consistently highest at 1900 mg/L KNO3, regardless of the explant type or auxin. The maximum shoot length achieved for NS explants was recorded as 3.460 cm without auxins, 3.627 cm with 0.1 mg/L NAA, and 3.333 cm with 0.1 mg/L IBA. Similarly, the highest shoot length for CN explants was recorded as 2.333 cm without auxins, 1.960 cm with 0.1 mg/L IBA, and 1.920 cm with 0.1 mg/L NAA. Results revealed that higher KNO3 levels are more effective for shoot proliferation, while lower concentrations are favorable for elongation.

Results of RSRA found that both linear and quadratic effects of KNO3 and IBA had a significant impact on shoot count and shoot length (Table 2). The linear effects were strongest for KNO3 (p < 0.01), while NAA significantly influenced shoot count (p < 0.01) but had no impact on shoot length. On the contrary, IBA was not significant for shoot count but marginally non-significant for shoot length. Explant type was highly significant for both shoot count and length. Two-way interactions revealed that KNO3 × IBA and KNO3 × Explant were significant for both shoot count (p < 0.01) and shoot length (p < 0.01), while IBA × Explant was non-significant for shoot count but significant for shoot length (p < 0.05). Whereas interactions of KNO3 × NAA and NAA × explant were non-significant. Results were further confirmed by Pareto chart and normal plot analysis (Fig. 3a-d). Results revealed that KNO3 is the most significant factor for both shoot counts and shoot length. Results illustrated the significance of KNO3×explant, NAA, explant, and KNO3×IBA factor for shoot length, as all these were statistically significant (Fig. 3a). Normal plot analysis of shoot length revealed the positive correlation for KNO3 and KNO3×IBA, while KNO3×explant, NAA, and explant exhibited negative correlation (Fig. 3b). Results of the Pareto chart of shoot length revealed the significance of KNO3, explant, KNO3×explant, KNO3×IBA, and IBA×explant (Fig. 3c). Analysis of the normal plot illustrated the negative impact of KNO3 and explant on shoot length, while the combination of KNO3×explant, KNO3×IBA, and IBA×explant led to enhanced shoot length (Fig. 3d).

Quantum machine learning analysis

Table 3 indicates the performance evaluation of the proposed quantum circuit alongside several classical and quantum machine learning models. The evaluation is based on key metrics such as accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall for both test and training datasets. The results highlight the comparative performance of these methods for shoot counts and shoot length classification. Performance evaluation of the proposed circuit was conducted by comparing its results with several classical and quantum machine learning models on test and training datasets. For shoot counts, the proposed custom model achieved the highest test accuracy (0.83) and F1 score (0.84), outperforming classical methods like SVC (0.67) and all quantum models, including QSVC, PegasosQSVC, EstimatorQNN, SamplerQNN, and VQC, which demonstrated significantly lower test accuracies ranging between 0.33 and 0.5. Similarly, the custom model again achieved the highest test accuracy (0.83) and F1 score (0.81), comparable to PegasosQSVC (0.83 accuracy) but superior to other quantum and classical approaches for shoot length. Training data results indicate that models like SVC, PegasosQSVC, VQC, and the proposed custom model achieved perfect accuracy (1.0) and F1 scores (1.0) for shoot count, suggesting potential overfitting. Quantum models like QSVC (0.92) and SamplerQNN (0.67) exhibited relatively lower but still competitive training performances. For shoot length, the proposed custom model again achieved perfect training scores (1.0), highlighting its ability to fit training data effectively, though overfitting may remain a concern.

Discussion

Optimization of multiple culture conditions, especially for recalcitrant crops like common bean, is highly critical. Results demonstrated the significant role of KNO3 on shoot count and length. Its linear and quadratic effects were statistically significant, confirming its role in nitrogen metabolism essential for shoot development [25]. Supplementation of 5700 mg/L KNO3 was highly significant, indicating the possibility of further increase of shoot counts and shoot length with an increase in KNO3 concentration. Results revealed that NAA with KNO3 significantly increased shoot count in CN explants, indicating its role in cell division during early shoot initiation. However, NAA had no significant impact on shoot length, suggesting its limited role in elongation. IBA, on the other hand, had a marginally non-significant effect on shoot length and shoot count. Comparison of explants illustrated the better performance of NS explants compared to CN explants, possibly due to young meristematic cells, higher metabolic activity, and hormone levels. The interaction between KNO3 and explant type further emphasized the need for optimizing nutrient and hormonal concentrations. Shoot elongation processes were more sensitive to auxin-explant interaction, and KNO3 × NAA and NAA × explant exerted a minor impact of NAA on shoot elongation. Potassium nitrate (KNO₃) is a source of N in the MS medium and regulates the shoot counts, but it was dependent on other factors like explant and type of auxin [4].

The robustness of observed biological parameters was subsequently validated through statistical modeling, like Response Surface Regression Analysis (RSRA) for identifying critical parameters [26]. The analysis revealed KNO3 as the most influential factor, and high concentrations led to maximum shoot count and lower concentrations promoted shoot elongation. The results were confirmed by Pareto chart analysis that ranked the standardized effects of factors and interactions to identify the most significant factors that regulated the shoot count and shoot length. Whereas normal plots illustrated the linear trend and confirmed the normal distribution aligned with the diagonal line [27]. The normal plot analysis further confirmed the relationships and exhibited positive correlations between KNO3 and shoot count and length, while negative correlations were observed between explant type and NAA, suggesting inhibitory effects in certain conditions. The integration of ANOVA, RSRA, and chart analyses provided a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing shoot development. The interactions between KNO3 and auxins, particularly IBA, and explant-specific responses underscore the need for customized protocols. Both Pareto and normal plots confirm the statistical significance of key factors, highlight the proportional or inverse relationships of factors with response variables, and validate the robustness and predictive power of the RSRA model.

Although statistical approaches provided robust validation of in vitro regeneration parameters, their predictive capacity remains limited and demands more sophisticated and modern computational models like machine learning and Quantum computing for enhanced predictive accuracy and to reduce experimental uncertainty. For this purpose, advanced computational tools like custom-designed quantum circuits were applied to achieve superior classification and optimization of the outcomes. The high accuracy and F1 scores for shoot length by the custom model demonstrated the superiority of the model, indicating its effective use of quantum principles and integration of classical techniques. However, models like SVC, PegasosQSVC, and VQC exhibited overfitting in the training data analysis. Although perfect training scores were recorded by these models, the relatively low test performance of these models indicated the need for implementing more regularization. Quantum models, EstimatorQNN and SamplerQNN, also showed weaker performance. Several optimizers like SLSQP, BFGS, L-BFGS-B, CG, and TNC were evaluated for optimizing a custom quantum circuit, but they often converged prematurely, resulting in local minima and inadequate loss reduction. The COBYLA optimizer outperformed these methods by effectively minimizing the loss function and achieving optimal parameter configurations. COBYLA’s derivative-free approach proved advantageous for non-linear and constrained optimization problems common in quantum machine learning. Unlike gradient-based methods such as BFGS and CG, which struggled with the non-smooth objective function, COBYLA successfully navigated these challenges, demonstrating its robustness in optimizing quantum circuit parameters.

The proposed quantum circuit (Fig. 2) employs RX and RZ gates, which are trained using an optimizer (COBYLA), and Hadamard (H) gates strategically placed at the input and output of the auxiliary qubit (q4). The RX gates applied to q0, q1, q2, and q3 act as rotation gates, initializing the states of the qubits in a parametrized manner. These rotations effectively prepare the qubits in superposition states, enabling the creation of interference patterns critical for quantum processing. The RZ gates are applied to q4, conditioned on the states of the upper qubits through controlled operations. These gates introduce phase shifts that modify the relative phases between quantum states. However, phase changes alone do not influence the amplitude of q4. To manipulate the amplitudes, the Hadamard gates at the input and output of q4 are essential. The initial Hadamard gate places q4 into a superposition state, allowing subsequent phase shifts from the RZ gates to translate into amplitude variations when the final Hadamard gate is applied. The RX gates are essential for controlled RZ operations, as they rotate qubits into non-zero amplitude states in the |1⟩ state, enabling the controlled operations to be effective. Without these rotations, qubits would remain in the |0⟩ state, rendering the controlled RZ gates inactive. The optimizer trains the RX and RZ parameters, fine-tuning rotations and phase shifts to optimize the circuit’s performance for classification tasks, ensuring iterative adjustment to minimize classification errors.

The provided objective function plays a central role in optimizing the parameters of the quantum circuit. The cost function minimizes the binary cross-entropy loss, which measures how well the predicted probabilities align with actual labels. The objective function calculates prediction probabilities using the predict function, assigning predictions to class 0 or 1 based on probability exceeding 0.5. Binary cross-entropy is computed to measure alignment between predictions and actual labels. Iterative updates fine-tune RX and RZ parameters, and checkpoints save parameter values when loss improves. This approach has several advantages, including penalizing confident but incorrect predictions, allowing for fine-tuning, and indexed display of predictions for debugging and performance monitoring. In classification, the objective function and circuit design work together to create parametrized states and phase shifts. The Hadamard gates in the auxiliary qubit channel enhance the accuracy by transforming phase shifts into measurable amplitudes. The proposed quantum circuit in this study optimizes parameters iteratively, demonstrating the superiority of hybrid quantum-classical approaches in classification tasks. The prediction errors were minimized by adjusting RX and RZ gate parameters. Amplitude and phase manipulation, Hadamard and RZ gates, and RX rotations used in this study revealed a robust framework for diverse machine learning tasks, confirmed by superior performance on training and test datasets.

Conclusion

The study highlights the successful integration of classification-based quantum machine learning algorithms for agricultural data analysis, generated under in vitro conditions for shoot counts and shoot length of common bean. By combining nutrient-auxin interaction with a custom-designed quantum circuit, relatively higher accuracy and predictive accuracy than classical ML models were recorded. The work highlights the potential of QML to improve the optimization of in vitro regeneration. Although integration of QML in plant biotechnology is an innovative approach, very limited work has been documented due to a lack of expertise, access, and complexity of the system. However, its application in precision agriculture is increasing gradually due to the QML’s superior ability to capture nonlinear and complex patterns and interactions in the biological data, and it can guide decision-making at the field level. The proposed custom quantum circuit architecture can be scaled to handle high-dimensional datasets in precision agriculture.
