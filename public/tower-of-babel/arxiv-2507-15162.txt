Designing User-Centric Metrics for Evaluation of Counterfactual Explanations
Firdaus Ahmed Choudhury, Ethan Leicht, Jude Ethan Bislig

Abstract
Machine learning-based decision models are increasingly being used to make decisions that greatly impact people’s lives, but their opaque nature leaves the end user without a clear understanding of why a decision was made. Counterfactual Explanations (CFEs) have grown in popularity as a means of offering actionable guidance by identifying the minimum changes in feature values required to flip a model’s prediction to something more desirable for the end user. Unfortunately, most prior research in counterfactual explanation relies on artificial evaluation metrics, such as proximity, which may potentially overlook end-user preferences and constraints, e.g., the user’s perception of the amount of effort needed to make certain feature changes may differ from that of the model designer. To address this research gap, this paper makes three novel contributions. First, we conduct a pilot study with 20 crowd-workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk to experimentally validate the alignment of existing CF evaluation metrics with real-world preferences of end users. Results show that user-preferred CFEs matched those based on proximity in only 63.81% of cases, which highlights the lack of applicability of these metrics in real-world settings. Second, inspired by the need to design a user-inspired evaluation metric for CFEs, we conduct a more detailed two-day user study with 41 participants facing realistic credit application scenarios to find experimental support for/against three intuitive hypotheses that may explain how end users evaluate counterfactual explanations. Third, based on our results from this second user study, we propose the Acceptability & Weighted Proximity (AWP) model, a novel user-centric two-stage model that describes one possible mechanism used by end users to evaluate and choose CFEs of their choice. Our experimental results show that AWP predicts user-preferred CFEs with 84.37% accuracy. Our study provides the first human-centered validation for personalized cost models in CFE generation and highlights the need for adaptive, user-centered evaluation metrics.



Introduction

Advances in Machine Learning (ML) have led to the deployment of predictive models in various aspects of human life, from approving credit applications
(
Faheem 2021
)
to job recruitment
(
Al-Alawi et al. 2021
)
. However, these advancements come at the cost of transparency, as complex black-box ML models provide little to no insight about how they arrive at their decisions
(
Herse et al. 2018
)
. As such, there is a growing demand not only from end users
(
Binns et al. 2018
)
but also from regulatory frameworks like the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) for increased transparency and accountability in AI/ML based decision support systems
(
Goodman and Flaxman 2017
)
.

To this end, counterfactual explanations (CFEs) have emerged as a promising approach for providing end users with intuitive, actionable insights into the decision-making process of black-box ML models
(
Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell 2018
)
. More formally, CFEs provide post-hoc transparency for the predictions returned by a black-box ML model on an input point,
x

x
into a counterfactual example,
x’
, a new artificially constructed data point which achieves a more desirable prediction outcome from the ML model. The recent rise in popularity around CFEs is in part due to their potential to provide actionable recourse to end users who have received adverse decisions from automated ML-based decision systems
(
Karimi, Schölkopf, and Valera 2021
)
. For example, a loan applicant who is denied credit by a bank’s ML model might be shown that increasing their income by $4,000 and reducing their amount requested by $1,000 would result in their loan application getting approved by the ML model.

Unfortunately, existing algorithms for generating CFEs suffer from a key bottleneck: they rely on artificial evaluation metrics such as proximity, sparsity, etc., to evaluate the quality of generated recourses

Related Work

Counterfactual Explanations.
Within the Explainable AI (XAI) community, CFEs have gained recognition for their ability to illustrate how black-box ML models arrive at their predictions
(
Byrne 2019
;
Verma et al. 2024
;
Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell 2018
)
.
A counterfactual method for data point
x
in model
f(.)
aims to find a minimally different point
x^{\prime}
, where (1) the distance between
x
and
x^{\prime}
is minimized on a particular distance metric, typically the
L_{1}
norm
(
Karimi et al. 2020
)
(referred to as the proximity objective in CFE algorithms), and (2) their model predictions are different, i.e.,
f(x)\neq{}f(x^{\prime})

(
Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell 2018
)
(referred to as the validity objective in CFE algorithms). As CFEs identify the smallest set of changes needed to alter a prediction, they naturally align with the goals of algorithmic recourse: to help users understand the cause behind unfavorable decisions they received from a predictive model and subsequently provide them with actionable steps they can take to overturn those decisions.
(
Ustun, Spangher, and Liu 2019
;
Tominaga, Yamashita, and Kurashima 2024
)
.

Algorithms for CFE Generation.

Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell 2018
proposed Vanilla-CF, which jointly optimizes CFEs on the combined loss of validity (measuring desirable change in prediction) and proximity (measuring cost of change needed to convert input
x
into CFE
x^{\prime}
) via gradient descent. Building on this foundational framework, subsequent methods have come up with additional objectives beyond proximity, such as diversity
(
Mothilal, Sharma, and Tan 2020
)
; feasibility
(
Mahajan, Tan, and Sharma 2020
)
; and sparsity
(
Keane et al. 2021
)
. Unfortunately, most of the loss functions optimized by these algorithms rely on these artificial loss functions and evaluation metrics (e.g., proximity, sparsity, diversity, etc.) to find the optimal CFE.

However, recent work has shown that these artificial metrics fail to account for human preferences
(
Tominaga, Yamashita, and Kurashima 2024
)
. As a result, there is a growing recognition that generating realistic algorithmic recourses requires evaluation metrics to move beyond model-centric methods and incorporate user-centered perspectives
(
Verma et al. 2024
)
. In contrast, our work evaluates counterfactuals using a weighted proximity metric, where the weights, learned through a Bradley-Terry model, represent the end user’s perceived costs of changing specific features
(
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
)
. Incorporating these personal costs allows us to generate recourses that better reflect each user’s unique preferences.

Generating Human-Centric CFEs.

Recent work on generating CFEs has begun to shift from purely model-driven generation techniques to methods that actively consider how real users perceive and interact with recourses.
Mahajan, Tan, and Sharma 2020
contributes to this line of work by proposing constraints on features using causal graphs to generate causally valid counterfactuals. Similarly, Actionable REcourse Summaries (ARES)
(
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2020
)
is a novel framework that generates interpretable summaries of recourses for population groups. Although their work helps detect biases in counterfactual models against specific population sub-groups, it does not deal with generating personalized recourses. In another study,
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
propose learning individual feature costs using pairwise comparisons fitted via the Bradley-Terry model
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)
. However, this approach has only been tested using simulations. Our work builds on their idea and provides the first empirical validation of their approach through a human user study.

Other studies, such as GAM Coach
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, introduced an interactive interface that allowed users to edit feature preferences and define difficulty levels and value ranges to personalize recourses. While powerful, a significant drawback of their interface was that it relies on a glass-box model for its ability to give users complete control over feature values. The authors themselves acknowledge that the transparent nature raises many security concerns, such as the risk of users gaming the system or being targeted by model inversion attacks
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
. Due to its limited generalizability and lack of applicability in black box models, we do not use GAM Coach as a baseline. User Feedback-Based Counterfactual Explanations (UFCE)
(
Suffian, Alonso-Moral, and Bogliolo 2024
)
is a human-in-the-loop framework that attempts to incorporate user preferences in CFE generation by collecting binary feedback on full recourses and optimizing a generator using Mutual Information (MI). However, UFCE focuses on learning general patterns of which types of recourses are preferred, rather than capturing why a certain recourse is preferred over another. Although it allows users to impose constraints by specifying which features can be changed and within what ranges, it does not learn feature-specific costs or preferences. In contrast, our approach uses pairwise comparisons to capture feature-level preferences and generate more nuanced and personalized recourses.

User Centric Evaluation of Algorithmic Recourses

A related but distinct body of research emphasizes the importance of empirically validating counterfactuals with real users.
Keane et al. 2021
(2021) critically examined gaps in the evaluation of counterfactual methods. They highlighted that only 21% of studies included user evaluations for specific counterfactual methods, with just 7% conducting comparative user studies to benchmark different approaches. In response to this gap, there have recently been some studies focused on user-centered evaluations in counterfactual research. For instance,
Warren, Byrne, and Keane 2023
(2023 conducted two large-scale controlled experiments with over 330 participants, finding that CFEs improved predictive accuracy more than causal or no explanations. They also showed that explanations using purely categorical feature changes were easier for users to understand than mixed-type explanations, adding further empirical support for human-aligned counterfactual design.
Tominaga, Yamashita, and Kurashima 2024
(2024) conducted an empirical user study showing that counterfactuals optimized on technical objectives such as proximity and sparsity often misalign with user preferences. Their findings reinforce the argument that strong results on simulated benchmarks do not always translate to acceptable recommendations in real-world contexts. These results highlight the need for human-centered evaluations to meaningfully assess the utility of counterfactual methods. Our work stems from this need to conduct user evaluations that not only validate a proposed counterfactual method but also uncover a richer understanding of the way users reason about and choose between alternative recourses.

Methodology

In this section, we describe our methodology for generating and evaluating counterfactual recourses, which serves as a foundation for designing and running our user studies. First, we outline our approach to recourse generation, in which we: (i) explain our choice of using a decision tree classifier (trained on a synthetic credit application dataset) as the ML model for which CFEs need to be generated; followed by (ii) discussing how CFEs are generated for this decision tree classifier. (iii), we introduce the distance metrics used to quantify recourse proximity, including standard and personalized formulations. (iv) Finally, we discuss how feature-level weights are learned from participant responses using the Bradley-Terry model. These weights are integrated into our weighted proximity metric to generate personalized counterfactuals, which are later compared against traditional proximity-based recourses using user evaluations.

Recourse Generation

Our process of generating counterfactual recourses follows a structured pipeline involving (1) synthetic data creation, (2) loan decision modeling, (3) recourse generation, and (4) learning preference-based weighting for individual users.

Synthetic Dataset Creation:

For this paper, we constructed a synthetic dataset simulating loan application outcomes in a binary classification setting (approved vs. rejected). While several real-world datasets exist for credit decision modeling
(
Hofmann 1994
;
FICO 2018
)
, we deliberately opted for a synthetic dataset to maintain greater experimental control. Real-world credit application datasets often include high-dimensional, noisy, and domain-specific features that can introduce confounding factors and increase the cognitive load on study participants. Since our primary objective was to evaluate how lay users perceive and compare counterfactual recourses, it was essential to minimize such complexity. A simplified, controlled feature space allowed participants to focus on the core trade-offs presented by each counterfactual explanation without being overwhelmed by extraneous details.

Each entry in our synthetic dataset represents a loan application and includes a mix of five continuous and categorical features commonly used in lending decisions:
income
,
credit score
,
employment status
,
education level
, and the
loan amount
requested. Feature values were independently sampled from predefined, realistic ranges that reflect plausible applicant profiles. These data points were labeled using a rule-based scoring function modeled on typical lending criteria. To better emulate real-world variability, we implemented multiple rule sets, each capturing a different combination of feature thresholds and decision logic for loan approval or rejection. Due to space constraints, full details of the sampling and labeling procedure are provided in the Appendix.

Training the Prediction Model:

Next, we trained a decision tree classifier on our synthetic dataset to predict whether a loan application would be approved or rejected. This model served as the basis for generating CFEs, which were later presented to study participants.

Note that while more complex models such as neural networks might offer higher predictive accuracy, we deliberately chose decision trees for their transparency, interpretability, and rule-based structure. These characteristics made them a better fit for our study, where the goal was to evaluate how users perceive and reason about counterfactual recourses. In contrast, neural networks function as black-box models and would have required the use of sophisticated, often opaque CFE generation algorithms (see Section
Related Work
). While such models might have produced technically stronger recourses, they would have significantly limited our ability to control the types of counterfactuals shown to participants and made it more difficult to study user preferences about CFEs in a controlled setting. In contrast, using decision trees as our classifier enabled us to use simple rule-based methods to generate CFEs with full transparency and control, which was essential for maintaining experimental rigor and interpretability (we explain our rule-based methods for generating CFEs below).

Our decision tree model was trained by using a standard 80/20 train-test split on our synthetic dataset, and achieved 99.15% accuracy on the test set, confirming that the feature-label relationships encoded by the rule sets were effectively captured. Due to space constraints, exact details of our training procedure are provided in the Appendix.

Counterfactual Generation:

Unlike most black-box ML models, decision trees provide an explicit rule-based mapping between input features and model prediction outcomes, which allows us to trace the exact
decision path
for any given data point, i.e., the sequence of feature-based splits from the root node to the leaf node reached by that data point. Let
L
denote a leaf node in our decision tree, and let
\phi(L)\in\{\text{accept},\text{reject}\}
denote the binary label assigned to all data points that reach leaf
L
in the decision tree.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, our strategy for generating CFEs for our decision tree classifier leverages a key structural insight: for each rejected loan application (data point)
x
that reaches leaf node
L
(with
\phi(L)=\text{reject}
), we identify counterfactuals by finding paths to alternate leaf nodes
L^{\prime}
where
\phi(L^{\prime})=\text{accept}
. Each such path defines a set of feature changes required to shift the prediction of the decision tree from rejection to approval. For example, as shown in Figure
1
, a counterfactual example
x^{\prime}_{1}
can be constructed for input
x
by following the feature modifications suggested by the two edges on the path between
x
and
x^{\prime}_{1}
. In this way, we convert the CFE generation problem for a decision tree classifier into a shortest path finding problem between two leaf nodes that contain opposite labels, which is then solved using off-the-shelf implementations of the Floyd-Warshall algorithm
(
Hougardy 2010
)
.

Using this simple rule-based algorithm, we individually generate CFEs for all instances in our test set classified as ‘rejected’ by our decision-tree based loan prediction model. In particular, for each ‘rejected’ instance
x
, we extract a set of top-15 CFEs, each corresponding to a distinct accepting leaf node
L^{\prime}_{i}
(i.e.,
\phi(L^{\prime}_{i})=\text{accept}
for all
i\in{1,\ldots,K}
). These 15 candidate CFEs are ranked in increasing order of proximity to the input data point
x
.

Finally, to ensure the realism and interpretability of generated recourses, we discard CFEs that suggest implausible or counterintuitive transformations. For example, continuous features such as
income
and
credit score
are only allowed to increase in a valid recourse, reflecting feasible, forward-looking improvements in creditworthiness. Thus, for each point
x
in the test set, we continue generating candidate CFEs until we identify
K
such realistic and semantically meaningful CFEs for each rejected instance
x
.

Evaluating Generated Counterfactuals.
To evaluate the quality of CFEs generated in the previous step, we employ two primary evaluation metrics:

•

Proximity
is the most widely used evaluation metric in prior CFE literature
(
Wachter, Mittelstadt, and Russell 2018
;
Mothilal, Sharma, and Tan 2020
;
Karimi et al. 2020
)
, and is central to the functioning of most CFE generation algorithms. Intuitively, the proximity of a counterfactual example
x^{\prime}
(in relation to the input data point
x
) measures the cumulative cost of changes required to convert
x
to
x^{\prime}
. It is most commonly operationalized as the normalized
L_{1}
distance between
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

\text{Prox}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\frac{|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|}{\text{Range}_{i}}

•

Weighted Proximity
We also utilize a user-specific weighted proximity metric, which is calculated by assigning a distinct feature weight
w_{i}
to feature
i
that reflects its perceived cost of modification to that user. Larger (smaller) values of
w_{i}
correspond to a greater (and lesser) perceived difficulty in modifying that feature value. For a given user with weights
w_{i}
for each feature
i
, the weighted proximity calculates the weighted cost of change (for that user) required to convert input
x
to CFE
x^{\prime}
. We operationalize this as the (normalized) weighted
L_{1}
distance between
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

\text{WeightedProx}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\cdot\frac{|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|}{\text{Range}_{i}}

The standard proximity metric implicitly assumes that all feature changes are equally feasible for a user, e.g., modifying one’s income is as easy as adjusting the loan amount requested. In practice, however, users may perceive different features as varying widely in difficulty, and these perceptions can differ significantly between individuals. By incorporating user-specific feature weights, the weighted proximity metric offers a more personalized and realistic assessment of recourse feasibility, aligning evaluation with each user’s individual preferences and constraints.

Learning User-Specific Feature Weights.

In order to learn individual feature weights
w_{i}
for a user,
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
have proposed using pairwise comparison data (collected from users), in which each data point corresponds to a pair of possible CFEs
x^{\prime}_{1}
and
x^{\prime}_{2}
(for input point
x
) which are presented to a user who is asked to select
their preferred CFE. Using simulated pairwise comparison data,
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
show to fit a Bradley-Terry model, which is a probabilistic framework used to model the likelihood of pairwise outcomes by estimating a strength parameter
\beta
for each item (feature) in a set based on the observed outcomes
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)
. Finally,
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
show that the additive complement of the inferred
\beta_{i}
parameters (in the learned Bradley-Terry model) can be used to accurately represent individual user-specific feature weights, i.e.,
w_{i}=-\beta_{i}
.

In our work, we collect pairwise comparison data from each of our study participants separately, which enables us to fit separate Bradley-Terry models for each participant (utilizing the approach designed by
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
). As a result, we are able to learn individual-specific feature weights for each of our study participants. These individual-specific personalized weights allow for the generation of CFEs that are tailored to each individual’s preferences and willingness to act.

User Validation of CFE Metrics

As discussed in Section
Related Work
, the field of counterfactual explanations has largely developed around mathematically convenient, but often artificial evaluation metrics such as proximity and sparsity. Despite their widespread use, these metrics have rarely been subjected to systematic validation from the perspective of real users. To date, limited attention has been paid to understanding whether these metrics genuinely reflect how people perceive, evaluate, and choose between counterfactual recourses.

To the best of our knowledge, the recent study by
Tominaga, Yamashita, and Kurashima 2024
is the only prior work that empirically examines user acceptance of CFEs optimized for conventional metrics like proximity. Their findings suggest that users’ willingness to accept a recourse does not reliably correlate with its cost of change, thereby casting doubt on the foundational assumptions behind these metrics. However, their study does not investigate more nuanced or personalized evaluation functions (such as user-specific weighted proximity), which may better capture the diverse constraints and preferences individuals bring to recourse decisions.

Pilot Study Design.
Motivated by the desire to address this research gap, we conducted an initial pilot user study to investigate the following question:
Are users more likely to accept CFEs that optimize standard proximity (or sparsity) vs those that optimize weighted proximity?
To explore this question, we designed a survey containing 20 CFE scenarios. Each scenario presented a rejected loan application alongside two alternative CFEs offering different recourse paths. The survey was administered to 20 participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), each of whom received $3 USD as compensation. Participants first received a brief explanation of counterfactual explanations and instructions for the task, and were then directed to a Qualtrics form, where they were presented with the 20 CFE scenarios one-by-one. All our user study protocols were approved by an Institutional Review Board at the host institution.

The 20 CFE scenarios used in the survey were selected through a multi-step process. First, as described in Section
Methodology
, we used our shortest-path algorithm to generate a top-
15
set of CFEs
{x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{15}}
for each rejected instance
x
in the test set. Next, we examined all possible CFE pairs
(x^{\prime}_{i},x^{\prime}_{j})
from this set and evaluated whether a scenario featuring
x
with alternatives
x^{\prime}_{i}
and
x^{\prime}_{j}
should be included. Our inclusion criterion was specifically designed to expose trade-offs between the two evaluation metrics: a CFE pair was included if, without loss of generality,
x^{\prime}_{i}
had a lower (better) proximity score than
x^{\prime}_{j}
, while
x^{\prime}_{j}
had a lower (better) weighted proximity score than
x^{\prime}_{i}
. Weighted proximity was computed using a fixed set of author-defined weights that reflected the perceived difficulty of modifying each feature. Pairs not satisfying this divergence in metric rankings were excluded from consideration. Finally, we randomly selected 20 such qualifying CFE scenarios to populate the survey. An example of a survey question is provided in Figure
7
in the Appendix.

For each of the CFE scenarios presented to a pilot study participant, they were asked to select which recourse (out of
x^{\prime}_{i}
and
x^{\prime}_{j}
) was more acceptable to them. In addition, for each scenario, they were asked to give a brief reason as to why they picked this recourse over the other.

Pilot Study Results.
In the pilot study, participants were shown 20 CFE scenarios related to the standard versus weighted proximity hypothesis. On average, participants selected the recourse with lower weighted proximity in only 36.29% of the cases.

Observation 1
.

This low selection rate raises concern about the suitability of weighted proximity (when computed using fixed global weights) as a reliable proxy for user preferences.

However, when we analyzed the open-ended explanations provided by pilot study participants justifying their choice of preferred CFE, it became evident that their aversion to low-weighted proximity recourses was driven by a mismatch between their perceived cost of feature changes and the global weights we had set. For example, P6 expressed that they “would prefer to make a change in employment type (from Private sector to Government sector) rather than reducing the amount [they are] requesting,” a view not aligned with the global weights that we had set.

Observation 2
.

Fixed feature weights fail to capture individual users’ subjective notions for the feasibility of change, leading to a misalignment between weighted proximity (using fixed feature weights) and actual user preferences.

Additionally, it was also evident from these explanations that different participants showed a large variation in preference rankings between each other. For example, P7 ranked their preference of changing features as loan amount
<
credit score
<
income
<
employment type
<
education, whereas P20 ranked their preference as credit score
<
education
<
employment type
<
income
<
loan amount.

Observation 3
.

Participants exhibited highly individualized notions of feature change difficulty, suggesting that each user should have their own personalized feature weights. These qualitative findings seem to suggest that people’s acceptability of weighted proximity as a metric should only be measured when personalized feature costs are learned for individual users (which was not done in this initial pilot study).

Despite the fact that we probably used weights that were not aligned with each user’s personalized weights, standard proximity-based recourses were only chosen in about 63% of the cases. Similarly, standard sparsity-based recourses were only chosen in about 69% of the cases.

Observation 4
.

Unweighted proximity and sparsity fail to sufficiently capture user preferences.

In addition, participants’ explanations for their choice of preferred recourse often revealed another interesting observation: in several cases, participants selected recourse A not because it was particularly desirable, but because the alternative, recourse B, was deemed unacceptable - typically due to it requiring the participant to modify feature values to implausibly large levels.

Observation 5
.

Users are willing to consider a recourse as acceptable as long as its suggested feature modifications remain below some threshold.

Two-Day User Study to Understand User Preferences

Our pilot study results revealed a notable misalignment between widely used evaluation metrics (such as proximity and sparsity) and actual user preferences. Even in a simplified setting, when confronted with a binary choice between selecting a CFE with better weighted proximity (which was calculated using potentially wrong and non-personalized feature weights) or a CFE with better proximity (or sparsity), only 63% (and 69%) of users selected CFEs with lower proximity (and sparsity), respectively. These results underscore the limitations of current CFE evaluation metrics in capturing what users truly value in counterfactual explanations, and point to the need for more realistic, user-centered evaluation metrics.

In this paper, we take a first step in this direction by proposing AWP, a novel user-centric two-stage model that can accurately predict which counterfactual explanations would be most preferred by an end user. As shown in our experimental results in Section
Acceptability & Weighted Proximity Model
, AWP achieves a 34% improvement in alignment with real user preferences (over existing metrics like proximity) by achieving an 84.37% predictive accuracy at identifying the CFE most preferred by a given end user.

However, in order to design a truly user-centric AWP evaluation metric, it is crucial to systematically understand and analyze first-order principles that may be used by end users in choosing a preferred counterfactual. A deeper understanding of these first-order principles can then be harnessed in the design and development of AWP. To this end, we conduct a detailed two-part, semi-structured interview-based study with 43 participants. The primary goal of this study was to evaluate four research hypotheses, each reflecting a distinct first-order principle that may influence user preferences over CFEs. These hypotheses were informed by both quantitative patterns and qualitative insights observed in our initial 20-user pilot study.

Specifically, Observations 2 and 3 (Section
User Validation of CFE Metrics
) highlighted that users often disagreed with the fixed feature weights used in computing weighted proximity during the pilot, suggesting the need for personalized feature weights. Meanwhile, Observation 5 (Section
User Validation of CFE Metrics
) revealed that users frequently rejected CFEs when the proposed change to a feature exceeded what they personally considered an acceptable or plausible shift, indicating the presence of feature-specific acceptability thresholds. These observations motivated the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1
.

Users prefer counterfactual explanations that minimize weighted proximity, where weights
w_{i}
are personalized to reflect the individual user’s perceived difficulty of modifying each feature
i
.

Hypothesis 2
.

Users possess a set of feature-specific thresholds of acceptability (
\alpha_{i}
for feature
i
); they are likely to reject any counterfactual explanation that recommends changing the value of feature
i
beyond their individual threshold
\alpha_{i}
.

Prior research in cognitive psychology
(
Tversky and Kahneman 1974
)
suggests that individuals often rely on heuristic processing, particularly when faced with complex or cognitively demanding decisions. Drawing on this insight, we hypothesize that users may prefer counterfactual explanations with rounded feature values (e.g., income of $5500) over those with precise but cognitively taxing values (e.g., income of $5257.29), even when the latter offer objectively smaller changes (with better weighted proximity). For instance, a user earning $5000/month may find it more intuitive and actionable to increase their income to $5500 rather than to an exact unrounded figure like $5257.29. Although the unrounded change may result in a lower weighted proximity (under any plausible weight configuration), the increased cognitive burden associated with processing and interpreting the unrounded feature value may make it less desirable. This intuition motivates our third hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3
.

Users prefer counterfactual explanations that involve rounded feature value changes over those that suggest highly precise, unrounded values, even when the latter result in lower weighted proximity.

User Study Design.
To validate our three hypotheses, we conducted an in-depth user study to explore how individuals evaluate and choose between counterfactual recourses. Each participant took part in two one-on-one interview sessions, each lasting approximately one hour and conducted via Zoom. As discussed below, this two-session format enabled us to learn personalized feature weights for each participant separately, which was necessary to validate Hypothesis 1. A schematic of our user study workflow is shown in Figure
2
.

Participants were recruited using a combination of convenience and snowball sampling methods, including targeted recruitment emails periodically shared in student listservs at a public research university in the United States, personal outreach, and social media posts. Additionally, we also encouraged the first set of interested prospective participants (who replied to our recruitment messages) to share the study invitation within their personal and professional networks to increase reach and diversity. Eligibility was restricted to individuals aged 18 years or older and currently residing in the United States. Through these efforts, a total of 43 participants were recruited, of whom 41 completed both sessions; the two incomplete cases were excluded from further analysis. Each participant was given a $30 USD Amazon.com gift card as compensation for their participation.

Each of the two sessions was structured to serve a distinct purpose: the first session focused on eliciting participants preferences through scenario-based pairwise comparisons of counterfactual explanations, which could then be used to learn personalized feature weights for each user separately. Once this was accomplished, the second session focused on finding evidence for/against our three hypotheses by presenting users with CFEs tailored to their personalized feature weights. All sessions were conducted with informed consent, recorded with permission, and transcribed to facilitate qualitative analysis of user preferences.

Session 1: Collecting Pairwise Comparison Data.
The first session was designed to elicit personalized preferences for feature changes by asking participants to imagine themselves in realistic credit rejection scenarios and choose between two alternative recourses. For each scenario, they were instructed to pick the recourse that they would be more willing to act upon (in order to get their loan approved).

Each participant was asked to pick their preferred recourse in 25 different credit rejection scenarios (each of which contained two alternative recourses) that spanned a diverse set of feature change combinations. For each credit rejection scenario, the pair of alternative recourses presented to the participant was carefully constructed to reflect a deliberate trade-off: one recourse in each pair had a lower raw (unweighted) proximity, while the other had a lower weighted proximity based on globally defined feature rankings that we derived from our pilot study. This design ensured that the two options emphasized different strengths, e.g., if counterfactual A had lower raw proximity, then counterfactual B had lower global weighted proximity, and vice versa. These contrasts enabled us to isolate which evaluation metric was preferred by our study participants.

Using this pairwise comparison data, we leveraged the approach in
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
to fit a Bradley-Terry model for each participant to learn their personalized feature weights
w_{i}
for each feature
i
. In addition, to complement this quantitative choice data, we also asked participants to explain the reasoning behind each of their selections, which gave us qualitative insights into how they perceived feasibility, effort, and desirability.

Session 2: Validating Hypotheses.
The second session was designed to find evidence for/against the three hypotheses outlined above. To this end, each (of our 41) returning participant was presented with a unique survey containing a new set of credit rejection scenarios that were tailored according to their personalized feature weights learned during Session 1.

To validate Hypothesis 1, we randomly sampled 15 data points (corresponding to rejected loan applications) from our synthetic dataset. Analogous to our CFE scenario generation procedure in Section
Methodology
, for each of these 15 points, our goal was to select a CFE scenario, i.e., identify two alternative CFEs such that one of them achieved lower raw proximity, whereas the other achieved lower weighted proximity. This time, however, the weighted proximity calculations leveraged the user-specific feature weights learned by fitting a Bradley-Terry model on the pairwise comparison data gathered from each user during the first session. By following a process similar to the one described in Section
Methodology
, we generated 15 CFE scenarios tailored for each participant, and asked them to pick their preferred CFE in each scenario, in addition to a brief explanation justifying their choice. Care was taken to ensure that the two recourses in each CFE scenario differed in at least one feature to ensure meaningful comparison.

Figure 4:
Flowchart describing our iterative probing strategy to validate Hypothesis 2.
Green cells correspond to decision points at which at least one (or more) feature-specific acceptability thresholds for the user can be inferred.

To validate Hypothesis 2, we incorporated an additional set of 10 CFE scenarios designed to probe participants’ feature-specific thresholds of acceptability. These scenarios were used to initiate one-on-one, interactive discussions during the session. As shown in Figure
4
, for each scenario, participants were first asked to choose between two distinct recourses (e.g., Recourse A and Recourse B). Once a preference was expressed, e.g., if a participant chose Recourse A, which required increasing income from $5000 to $5500 over Recourse B, which involved lowering the requested loan amount from $10,000 to $9000, the interviewer initiated a sequence of follow-up comparisons.

In each follow-up, Recourse A was incrementally modified by increasing the magnitude of change in the same feature (e.g., asking for a new income of $6000, then $6500, and so on), while Recourse B remained unchanged. At each step, participants were asked whether they still preferred the updated version of A (denoted A’) or whether they would now prefer B, or instead reject both options altogether. This iterative process continued until the participant either switched their preference to B or indicated that neither recourse was acceptable.

These pivots were used to infer feature-specific acceptability thresholds. If a participant ultimately rejected both recourses, this was taken as evidence that the feature change in A’ had surpassed their personal threshold, and that Recourse B had never been within their acceptable range. Conversely, the last instance in which A’ was still preferred was interpreted as lying just below the user’s threshold of acceptability for that particular feature. For example, if a participant continued to accept A’ up to an income change of $8000 but rejected A’ at $8500, we infer that their income acceptability threshold lies between $8000 and $8500.

Conversely, if the participant eventually pivoted to preferring Recourse B over the progressively modified versions of A, the interviewer initiated a second sequence of follow-up comparisons by incrementally increasing the magnitude of change in the feature modified by Recourse B. As before, the participant was repeatedly asked to choose between the updated version of B (denoted B’) and the last accepted version of A. This process continued until the participant either reverted to preferring A or rejected both options. In doing so, we were able to identify an approximate acceptability threshold for the feature modified in Recourse B, based on the point at which the participant’s preference shifted or their tolerance was exceeded. This two-sided probing procedure allowed us to establish the existence of personalized, feature-specific thresholds for multiple attributes, capturing how much change a participant was realistically willing to accept in any given feature. Note that during this discussion, in addition to recording points at which participant pivoted from A to B (or vice versa), we also corroborated their reasons for pivoting to ensure that they were indeed pivoting because the amount of change recommended by a particular recourse exceeded their threshold of acceptability.

Finally, to validate Hypothesis 3, we included an additional set of 10 CFE scenarios designed to assess the impact of potential cognitive overload (caused by uninterpretable and non-actionable feature changes suggested by some recourses) on user preferences. Each of these 10 scenarios presented participants with two recourses that were functionally equivalent in terms of the recommended (feature) changes, but differed in how those changes were expressed. Specifically, one recourse used precisely calculated (or unrounded) continuous feature values (e.g., increasing the income from $5000 USD to $5257.29 USD), while the other recourse presented a rounded version of the same change (e.g., to $5500 USD). These comparisons enabled us to isolate the effect of rounding on user preferences, which enables us to find evidence for/against Hypothesis 3.

In total, each participant was shown a set of 35 tailored CFE scenarios during the second session (some participants were shown fewer if their interview exceeded an hour).

User Study Results.
Our results from the second session can be divided into three main findings:

A. Preference for ‘Personalized’ Weighted Proximity:

On average, participants selected recourses optimized on their personalized feature weights 80.2% of the time, demonstrating a strong preference for individualized counterfactuals with lower weighted proximity as compared to counterfactuals with lower raw proximity. In particular, the right side of Figure
3
shows that among our 41 participants, over one-third (14 participants) selected CFEs with lower weighted proximity in over 90% of scenarios. Three-fourths (31 out of 41 participants) chose CFEs with lower weighted proximity in at least 70% of scenarios. Only two participants preferred unweighted proximity-based recourses in the majority of scenarios - in particular, these two participants chose CFEs with lower weighted proximity 40% of the time.

This result provides strong empirical support for Hypothesis 1, which establishes weighted proximity as an evaluation metric that is closely aligned with actual user preferences. Note that even though weighted proximity has been discussed by researchers for a long time, no existing work has focused on validating it with real users. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first work that provides an end user driven validation of the effectiveness of weighted proximity as an evaluation metric for CFEs. Additionally, our work provides the first-ever empirical validation of the method proposed by
Rawal and Lakkaraju 2024
to learn feature-specific weights with real users.

B. Strong Evidence for Acceptability Thresholds:

Our iterative probing revealed that participants had a limit for every feature beyond which they either pivoted to the alternative recourse or outright rejected both. Participants frequently pivoted away from a preferred recourse once a single feature’s change exceeded their perceived limit. These decisions were not arbitrary; participants explicitly cited feasibility concerns. For instance, P11 said that ”[they would not] pick any credit score change above 780 because to cross that point would require years of on-time payments.” Another participant talked about how they ”can’t realistically achieve income increases more than 20% because they would need to change their job to get that increase.”

Comments like these confirm that perceived feasibility, not just numerical distance, drives the acceptability of a recourse. Figure

. These histograms provide empirical evidence of the existence of feature-specific acceptability thresholds.

Note that these feature thresholds do not need to be learned. In real-world applications, the user usually applies for a recourse from a known baseline and can specify their own limits, which can be incorporated as a hard constraint in the recourse generation process. This ensures that generateed recourses are aligned with the end user’s acceptability thresholds.

C. Limited Impact of Rounding:
As mentioned before, we presented participants with CFEs that were functionally equivalent, but differed only in whether continuous features were shown as rounded (e.g., $5800) or precise (e.g., $5766.01) values. On average, participants picked recourses with precise continuous values in 49.26% of scenarios, and chose rounded values in 50.64% of cases.
When rounded options were chosen, participants’ justifications made it clear that rounding itself was not the deciding factor. Instead, choices were guided by the perceived ease of achieving the change or its potential long-term benefits (e.g., a higher credit score improving creditworthiness). A common theme among the participants was that they ”prefer better numbers rather than nicer looking numbers”. Thus, rounded recourses were accepted only when they aligned with participants’ goals, not because they appeared cleaner or more interpretable. As a result, we find strong evidence against Hypothesis 3, which seems to suggest that either users did not experience sufficient cognitive overload with our CFE scenarios (built around our synthetic 5-feature dataset) or that users’ cognitive overload was not sufficiently relieved by our use of the rounding-based heuristic.

Thus, findings from our user study reveals strong evidence in support of Hypothesis 1 and 2, whereas we observe strong evidence against Hypothesis 3. As mentioned earlier, these hypotheses correspond to (potential) first-order principles that may be leveraged by end users in choosing their most preferred recourse. Next, we harness our user study findings to develop the Acceptability & Weighted Proximity (AWP) model, a novel two-stage model that explains one possible mechanism used by participants in choosing a preferred counterfactual.

Acceptability & Weighted Proximity Model

The Acceptability & Weighted Proximity (AWP) model takes as input four things: (i) the original data point which gets an unfavorable prediction from a black-box ML model; (ii) a set of alternative recourses that are presented to a user, each of which suggests a distinct set of feature changes that can result in a more favorable ML model prediction; (iii) the personalized feature-specific weights
w_{i}
for that user; and (iv) the feature-specific acceptability thresholds
\alpha_{i}
for that user. Based on these four inputs, the AWP model predicts which recourse (out of the input set of alternative recourses) would be most preferred by the user.

According to the Acceptability & Weighted Proximity (AWP) model, end users utilize the following two-stage decision process when selecting between counterfactual recourses:

Stage 1 – Feasibility Filtering:
For each of the provided alternative recourses, the end user first assesses whether the changes suggested by that recourse are “acceptable” to the user or not. This is done by ensuring that for each feature, the changes suggested (by the recourse) to the value of feature
i
do not exceed
\alpha_{i}
, the user’s acceptability threshold for feature
i
. If a recourse is found to be “unacceptable” by the user, it is rejected and filtered out from further analysis in the AWP model. Note that this first stage in the AWP model is motivated by the strong empirical evidence found in favor of Hypothesis 2.

Stage 2 – Weighted Comparison:
The second stage of the AWP model starts with a list of all the recourses that passed the initial feasibility screening. In this stage, users simply select the recourse with the lowest personalized weighted proximity among all remaining recourses (calculated using the input user-specific feature weights). Note that this second stage in the AWP model is motivated by the strong empirical evidence found in favor of Hypothesis 1.

Predictive Accuracy Results with AWP Model.
Finally, as a means to validate the ability of the AWP model to effectively predict user preferences, we utilize our existing user study data (with 41 participants) to conduct a retrospective analysis of the predictive accuracy achieved by AWP in predicting users’ most preferred recourse.

For this analysis, we considered all CFE scenarios presented to participants during the second session, and categorized them into three bins: (i) scenarios in which participants rejected both recourses from the outset - corresponding to cases where both recourses were deemed unacceptable by the user in Stage 1 of AWP; (ii) scenarios where participants initially preferred one recourse but later pivoted to rejecting both options (after their preferred recourse was incrementally modified by the interviewer) - corresponding to cases where the preferred recourse was initially acceptable; however, the alternative recourse was unacceptable at the outset, hence it was filtered out in Stage 1 of AWP; and (iii) scenarios in which participants initially preferred one recourse, but after threshold for acceptability was breached, they pivoted to preferring the alternative recourse. Only this third category of CFE scenarios represents cases where both alternative recourses were within the user’s acceptability thresholds, and thus, passed Stage 1 of the AWP model.

To compute the predictive accuracy of the AWP model, we restricted our evaluation to this third category of CFE scenarios. This is because the first and second categories corresponded to scenarios where, after Stage 1 of the AWP model, there were either no acceptable recourses (first category) or a single acceptable recourse (second category) that was available for Stage 2 of AWP. In either of these cases, the user’s choice in Stage 2 would be trivial, as there would be at most one acceptable recourse, which would have to be picked by the user as per AWP.

For each scenario in this third category, we assessed whether the participant ultimately selected the recourse with the lower personalized weighted proximity, as calculated using the feature weights inferred for that participant from their Session 1 data (as selecting such a recourse would be consistent with Stage 2 of the AWP model). Across a total of 369 CFE scenarios presented during Session 2, 32 scenarios contained alternative recourses that were both acceptable to the user. In 84.4% of these cases, participants chose the recourse with the lower personalized weighted proximity, yielding a predictive accuracy of 84.4% for the AWP model.

To the best of our knowledge, AWP is a first-of-its-kind model for predicting user preferences with respect to evaluating the quality of counterfactual explanations. AWP has been derived using first-order principles that are validated using extensive human participant data. However, we acknowledge that since AWP was derived from the same study data used in this evaluation, this result may partially reflect overfitting to the observed behavioral patterns. A more rigorous, prospective validation on independent data will be necessary to confirm the generalizability of AWP as a user preference model—an important direction for future work.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations that need to be acknowledged. Our relatively small sample size of 61 participants, limited to people residing in the United States, may limit the generalizability of our findings. While our study offers promising results, it needs to be validated across more diverse populations. Secondly, our user study focuses only on recourses for credit rejection scenarios, which restricts the applicability of our results to a single domain. Preferences may differ across different decision-making tasks. In addition, in an effort to maintain tight control over our experimental settings, our paper relies on a “well-behaved” synthetic credit dataset and a glass-box decision tree classifier, and future efforts should consider extending this work to messier, real-world datasets and state-of-the-art models. In addition, the applicability of the AWP framework should be tested across various domains to test its robustness. Finally, future work should focus on validating the ability of frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model to capture user preferences in different domains.

Conclusion

Counterfactual
Explanations have grown in popularity as a means
of offering actionable recourse in a wide variety of domains. Unfortunately, most prior research in counterfactual explanation relies on
artificial
evaluation metrics, such as proximity, which may potentially overlook end-user preferences and constraints. To address this research gap, this paper makes three novel contributions. First, we conduct a pilot study with 20 crowd-workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk to experimentally validate the alignment of existing CF evaluation metrics with the real-world preferences of end users. Results show that user-preferred CFEs matched those based on proximity in only 63.81% of cases, which highlights the lack of applicability of these metrics in real-world settings. Second, inspired by the need to design a user-inspired evaluation metric for CFEs, we conduct a more detailed two-day user study with 41 participants facing realistic credit application scenarios to find experimental support for/against three intuitive hypotheses that may explain how end users evaluate counterfactual explanations. Third, based on our results from this second user study, we propose the Acceptability & Weighted Proximity (AWP) model, a novel user-centric two-stage model that describes one possible mechanism used by end users to evaluate and choose CFEs of their choice. Our experimental results show that
AWP
predicts user-preferred CFEs with 84.37% accuracy. Our study provides the first human-centered validation for personalized cost models in CFE generation and highlights the need for adaptive, user-centered evaluation metrics.

Synthetic Dataset generation

We began by synthesizing a dataset full of loan application profiles. We decided upon five basic features that would be included in each loan profile. To ensure the profiles we generated had some resemblance to reality in the United States, the distribution of values was carefully selected for each feature.

In 2023, the median personal income in the United States was approximately $42,000, and most of the total wealth is held by a small percentage of people. The log-normal distribution with a mean of $42,000 and a standard deviation of 0.8 provides a good approximation of the income distribution with an accurate median and mean. Incomes were excluded below $10,000 and above $500,000 to avoid extreme outliers.

Appendix B
Loan Decision

The next step was to develop an algorithm that our fictional bank will use to decide which profiles should be approved for their loan and which should be denied. The result was a
multi-step approach.

First, income and amount requested were transformed into a new feature called
income:amount-requested, which is simply the individual’s income divided by their credit score, or the ratio between the two features. This new feature was more concise, as it replaced two features with one. It represents how much money the user is requesting relative to how much they make, which is something a bank is likely to consider in its decision.

The two continuous features, credit score and income:amount-requested were then normalized to a range of 0 to 1 using the min-max formula. Let x be a single value for a feature
and x be the entire feature vector.

The two categorical features, employment type and education type, were mapped to numerical values in the range of 0 to 1, representing each category’s desirability score from the perspective of the bank. A higher desirability score means the category is more favorable for loan approval.

The rationale here is mostly intuitive. Higher levels of education are assigned higher desirability scores. An individual who is more educated is more likely to pay back their loan. Employment types are assigned desirability scores based upon their level of job security. An individual at higher risk of termination or business failure is less likely to pay back their loan.

With all values falling in a normalized range, we next assigned each of the four features a weight. The higher a feature’s weight, the more consequential it is to the bank’s decision. The weights are all positive and sum to 1.

Much like the categorical features’ desirability scores, feature weights were determined intuitively. When granting loans, banks are generally most concerned with an applicant’s credit
score, as the very purpose of the metric is to indicate creditworthiness. The applicant’s income relative to their loan request is of the next highest import, as it suggests whether they can pay back what they take out. Education type and employment type are secondary factors that provide a snapshot of the applicant’s background, work ethic, and job security.

For every profile, each feature value was multiplied by the feature’s weight and summed together to obtain the profile’s overall desirability score. The higher a profile’s desirability score, the more likely the bank deems it that the applicant will pay back their loan.

Finally, all 100,000 loan profiles were sorted in ascending order of desirability score and assigned a binary digit. The first 50,000 were assigned a zero for denial, and the remaining 50,000 were assigned a one for approval. Each decision digit was then appended to the original, unmodified profile, with no additional information regarding how the decision was made.
