KAP1 SUMOylates and stabilizes SR-A to facilitate glycated LDL transcytosis and accelerate atherosclerosis
Authors: Meng Shu, Wenzhuo Cheng, Fangyang Yu, Liyin Zhang, Li Wang, Yan Shu, Ruonan Wang, Baorui Xue, Si Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerosis (AS)-associated cardiovascular disease is the main cause of global mortality. The excessive retention of glycated low-density lipoprotein (G-LDL) under the vascular endothelium promotes AS. In addition, G-LDL supports a role in promoting the expression of scavenger receptor A (SR-A), increasing SR-A-mediated transcytosis of G-LDL in endothelial cells (ECs), consequently accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis. However, the underlying mechanism used by G-LDL to promote SR-A expression has not been elucidated, thus representing the aim of this work.MethodsThe protein–protein interaction of the E3 SUMO ligase KRAB structural domain-associated protein 1 (KAP1) and SR-A were confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)-based immunoblotting and immunofluorescence in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). G-LDL uptake and transcytosis in KAP1-silencing or overexpressing HUVECs were assessed. The effect of KAP1 on de-ubiquitination and SUMOylation of SR-A was determined by co-IP-based immunoblotting. The role of KAP1 on G-LDL-induced atherosclerosis was tested by adenovirus-mediated knockdown in ApoE−/− mice.ResultsKAP1 was identified as an enhancer of SR-A, promoting its expression. KAP1 bound to SR-A and promoted SUMO1 modification of the SR-A lysine (K)22, which hampers K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of SR-A. KAP1 deficiency attenuated G-LDL-induced SR-A activation both in vitro and in vivo, reduced aortic G-LDL retention, and consequently, atherosclerotic vulnerable plaque formation in murine models.ConclusionsThis study identifies a SUMOylation–ubiquitination crosstalk that governs SR-A stability, revealing KAP1 as a key molecular switch controlling SR-A turnover in endothelial cells. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for how G-LDL accelerates atherosclerosis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00954-4.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS)-associated cardiovascular diseases, which include coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, and peripheral arterial disease, are the primary cause of global morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Therefore, a better understanding of AS pathogenesis is of great significance for improving its treatment [3].

The “response-to-retention hypothesis” has recently gained a lot of attention. It emphasizes the retention of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), especially modified LDL, commonly represented by oxidized LDL (Ox-LDL) and glycated LDL (G-LDL), in the vascular endothelium, being the main factor involved in the pathogenesis and progression of AS [4, 5]. The promotion of AS by Ox-LDL has been widely reported [6–8], but the role of G-LDL in AS, as well as the onset and development mechanisms, are unclear.

Persistent hyperglycemia in patients with diabetes mellitus induces the generation of G-LDL from native LDL (N-LDL) through the Maillard reaction, with G-LDL being closely associated to the development of AS [9, 10]. Scavenger receptor A (SR-A) is involved in the uptake of a variety of modified LDL [11]. While other scavenger receptors such as CD36 and LOX-1 participate in modified LDL uptake in macrophages and smooth muscle cells [12], CD36 is predominantly expressed on microvascular endothelial cells and expressed at low levels in venous endothelium [13]. It is noteworthy that SR‑A itself exhibits cell‑type‑specific functions, e.g., it contributes to foam cell formation in macrophages, whereas our previous study demonstrated that G-LDL stimulation uniquely triggers SR-A protein expression, increasing G-LDL penetration and AS development [14]. Moreover, the stability of SR-A is greatly enhanced by G-LDL, but the specific molecular mechanisms used by G-LDL to extend the half-life of SR-A and inhibit its degradation rate have not been fully elucidated.

The presence of multiple protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) (including ubiquitination, acetylation, and ubiquitination-like) in the cell affects protein stability and, consequently, protein degradation [15–17]. The ubiquitin–proteasome system and autophagy–lysosome system are the two main routes performing substrate protein degradation in the cell [18]. In contrast, the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) protein covalently conjugated modification, called SUMOylation, is different from the aforementioned mechanisms. Indeed, SUMO molecules compete with ubiquitin molecules for lysine sites on target proteins, thereby preventing the ubiquitination of target proteins [19]. SUMO modifications provide a platform for enhanced protein–protein interactions. The results of SUMOylation include changes in protein cellular localization, protein activity, or protein stability [20, 21]. In addition, SUMO degrades proteins by binding to ubiquitin through SUMO-targeted ubiquitin ligase (STUbL) [22]. Twenty years of research finally demonstrated that SUMOylation is involved in vital biological pathways [23, 24]. Therefore, SUMO enzymes could be used as targets to develop drugs involved in the treatment of human diseases.

Growing evidence suggests that the E3 SUMO ligase Kruppel-associated box (KRAB)-associated protein-1 (KAP1) mediates signal transduction by working as a SUMO E3 ligase or signaling scaffold protein, which allows it to function independently of gene regulation [25]. KAP1 is abundantly expressed in atherosclerotic plaque tissue [26], and its knockdown reduces vascular endothelial dysfunction induced by ox-LDL [27]; thus, KAP1 might be a pivotal checkpoint involved in the regulation of AS. However, its contribution to G-LDL-driven atherosclerosis, a hallmark of diabetic vascular complications, remains unexplored. In this study, we tested the hypothesis that KAP1-mediated SUMOylation stabilizes SR-A and promotes G-LDL transcytosis, with mechanistic aims including defining the KAP1-SR-A interaction, mapping the SUMOylation site, and validating the pathway in vivo.

This study demonstrated that the E3 SUMO ligase KAP1 stabilized SR-A expression by promoting SR-A SUMOylation and enhancing subsequent G-LDL transcytosis. A mouse model of AS was successfully established by feeding ApoE−/− mice with a high-fat diet (HFD) and the results revealed that the knockdown of KAP1 in ECs mediated by a recombinant adeno-associated viral vector (rAAV) inhibited the stimulatory effect of G-LDL on AS in vivo. Thus, this research revealed a significant role of KAP1 in the pathogenesis of diabetic AS, shedding light on its potential as a therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; 8000, ScienCell, CA, USA) and human embryonic kidney (HEK293T; ZQ0033, Zhongqiaoxinzhou Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) cells were cultured in DMEM (SH30022.01, HyClone, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (G8001, Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) (0503, ScienCell, CA, USA) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Cells were used at passages 6–8. Cells were authenticated by STR profiling and confirmed to be Mycoplasma‑free (Mycoplasma Detection Kit, G1900, Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China).

Reagents and antibodies

LDL (YB-001) was obtained from Yiyuan Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). FITC (46950) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Beijing, China). Alexa-Fluor 555 (AF555) dye was purchased from Thermo Fisher (Beijing, China). Anti-HA-tag (no. 51064-2-AP), anti-Flag-tag (no. 66008-4-Ig), anti-MYC-tag (no. 16286-1-AP), and anti-KAP1 (no. 15202-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Protein A/G agarose (no. sc-2002), used for IP, and anti-SR-A (no. sc-166184) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Anti-SUMO1 (no. 4930), anti-SUMO-2/3 (18H8) (no. 4971) and anti-Rab11 (no. 5589) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (CST; MA, USA). Anti-ubiquitin (no. A19686) was purchased from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). The secondary antibodies HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (no. A21010) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (no. A21020) were purchased from Abbkine (CA, USA). DAPI, Alexa-Fluor-488-tagged and Alexa-Fluor-594-tagged secondary antibodies were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Cycloheximide (CHX; HY-12320), chloroquine (CQ; HY-17589A), MG132 (HY-13259), and Lactacystin (HY-16594) were obtained from MedChem Express (New Jersey, USA).

All primary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:1000 for immunoblotting, 1:200 for immunoprecipitation, and 1:100 for immunofluorescence staining. All secondary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:10,000 for immunoblotting and 1:500 for immunofluorescence staining.

Preparation of G-LDL

Native and glycated LDL (N-LDL and G-LDL) were prepared by incubating 2 mg protein/ml LDL without and with freshly prepared 50 mmol/L d-(+)-glucose for 7 days at 37 °C, pH 7.4, under nitrogen conditions, as previously described [28]. Dialysis was subsequently used to remove the excess glucose. The BCA Protein Quantification Kit (no. 23228, Thermo Fisher) was used to quantify LDL protein.

G-LDL labeling with FITC and AF555

FITC and AF555 were weighed in the dark and dissolved in PBS. Then, 5 mg of each LDL isoform was mixed with 1 mg FITC/AF555 and subsequently incubated in a water bath at room temperature, shielded from light, for 2 h. The incubated fluorescently labeled LDL suspension was transferred to a dialysis bag and placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C in the dark, stirring for 72 h. PBS was changed every 24 h to remove unbound FITC or AF555.

G- LDL transcytosis

G-LDL transcytosis was determined using an in vitro model established as previously described [29, 30]. Briefly, HUVECs were seeded onto 24-well Transwell inserts at a density of 2 × 105 cells per insert to evaluate the transfer of FITC-labeled LDLs to the lower compartment. The medium was changed every other day. The integrity of the monolayer was confirmed before performing permeability investigations by measuring transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) using a Millicell-ERS voltmeter (Millipore) (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). The monolayers were washed three times with PBS before the transcytosis experiments. Next, the apical side was treated with 100 μg/ml FITC-labeled LDLs, while the basolateral side was treated with 600 μl PBS. The sample was collected from the lower chamber, uncoupled FITC was removed by dialysis against PBS for 3 h at 4 °C, and transcytosis of LDLs was identified. The signal strength of the bound FITC was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (TECAN) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 490 nm and 520 nm, respectively. The background fluorescence of the serum-free medium (Opti-MEM, Gibco) was measured and subtracted from the estimated FITC fluorescence intensity to accurately assess the transcellular transit of LDLs. Intact LDL particles in the basolateral medium were further quantified by ApoB ELISA (3715-1H-20, MabTech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Confocal imaging analysis of G-LDL uptake

HUVECs were subjected to the prescribed pretreatments and were incubated with 100 μg/ml AF555-labeled LDLs for 24 h. After a gently wash with PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and the nuclei were labeled with DAPI. Images of the stained cells were taken using an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed from three independent fields of view from each biological replication. These intensities were normalized against the count of viable cells using the ImageJ program, thus quantifying LDL uptake by the HUVECs.

Flow cytometry

HUVECs were seeded into a 12-well culture plate and grown until confluence. After the appropriate treatment, HUVECs were cultured in Opti-MEM (Gibco) containing AF555-labeled LDLs (100 μg/ml) for 3 h, and the mean fluorescence intensity of the cells was measured by flow cytometry (Mindray) to quantify the uptake of AF555-LDLs. Unlabeled LDLs were used as controls, to help establish compensation settings and gating parameters. The acquired data were processed and analyzed using FlowJo Software, where forward scatter (FSC)/side scatter (SSC) plots comprising a total of 10,000 events were generated for analysis.

Western blotting

Cells were homogenized using ice-cold RIPA buffer fortified with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails sourced from Beyotime, following the prescribed pretreatment. Equivalent amounts of protein lysate were loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and subjected to electrophoresis to separate the proteins, which were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Millipore). The membrane was subsequently blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h at room temperature, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, and followed by treatment with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abbkine). Labeled proteins were visualized by chemiluminescence (Millipore).

Plasmid construction and transfection

The plasmids expressing MYC-KAP1, HA-Ub wild-type (WT), mutant K48, mutant K63, HA-SUMO1, KAP1 mutant (KAP1-C65/68A), Flag-SR-A-WT, Flag-SR-A-K22R, Flag-SR-A-K77R, Flag-SR-A-K230R, and Flag-SR-A-K241R were purchased from Miao Ling Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Sample loading sequence and ratio (1.0 μg DNA: 2–3 μl Lipo 2000) were as indicated in the manufacturer’s instructions for Lipo 2000 transfection reagent (no. 11668-019, Invitrogen, CA, USA).

RNA interference

HUVECs were cultured until reaching 80% confluence. Next, the cells were transiently transfected with a scrambled small interfering RNA (siRNA) (si-Ctrl) or siRNA targeting KAP1 (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) using a HiPerFect Transfection Reagent (no. 301,705, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Whole-cell extract was harvested by incubating the cells in immunoprecipitation lysis buffer supplemented with a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime) for 20 min on ice, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The control IgG antibody or a specific antibody was added to the cell lysate and gently placed under rotation at 4 °C overnight. Then, pre-washed A/G agarose beads (Santa Cruz) were added, and incubation was continued for 2 h at room temperature. After elution, the bead-bound proteins were resuspended in 2× SDS–PAGE loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and detected by western blot.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, China), and the purity and concentration were assessed at 260:280 nm. An equal amount of RNA was used for the synthesis of cDNA, which was subjected to RT-qPCR. The primers (Servicebio Technology) used were the following:

SR-A: Forward: GGGATACCCAGGTGTTCAAGC.

Reverse: TGTGAACAGGCTCTTGTCCCC.

β-actin: Forward: CACCCAGCACAATGAAGATCAAGAT.

Reverse: CCAGTTTTTAAATCCTGAGTCAAGC.

Immunofluorescence assay

HUVECs were fixed and permeabilized with 4% PFA and 0.5% Triton X-100. Cells were washed with PBS, blocked with Immunol Staining Blocking Buffer (Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C, treated with anti-Flag (mouse) and anti-KAP1(rabbit) or anti-SR-A (mouse) and anti-SUMO1 (rabbit) diluted in Immunol Staining Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, cells were treated with secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 and anti-mouse conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594) for 1 h and subsequently with DAPI for 5 min in the dark (37 °C). Finally, the staining results were evaluated using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Bioinformatic analysis

SUMOplot (Abgent) (http://www.abgent.com/sumoplot/), JASSA (http://www.jassa.fr), and GPS-SUMO (http://sumosp.biocuckoo.org) were used to perform bioinformatics analysis of the potential SUMO-binding consensus motifs of SR-A, and the SUMOylation sites in SR-A were predicted and scored.

Analysis of AS in mice

A total of 35 male ApoE−/− mice at 5 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g, were purchased from Moubaili Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Mice were housed in a controlled environment with a 12:12-h light/dark cycle with free access to water and food and subjected to 1 week of acclimatization before being used for experimental purposes. Next, mice were randomly divided into two groups by body mass at 6 weeks of age and treated with empty virus (rAAV9-ICAM2-GFP-sh-Ctrl; sh-Ctrl) or endothelial-specific KAP1 knockdown adeno-associated virus (rAAV9-ICAM2-GFP-sh-KAP1; sh-KAP1) [31, 32]. AAV9 vectors (Weizhen Biotechnology, Shandong, China) were administered at 5 × 1011 vg/mouse, and transduction efficiency was confirmed by GFP expression at 4 weeks after injection (Supplementary Fig. 2). Sample size (n = 8 per group) was determined on the basis of power analysis to ensure adequate power to detect a significant difference. Randomization was performed by body weight, and group allocation was blinded to the investigator during treatment and subsequent analyses. Mice were further randomized into two subgroups 4 weeks after injection: the N-LDL group and the G-LDL group, treated with an intravenous injection of N-LDL and G-LDL respectively, at a dosage of 50 μg/mouse twice weekly for 12 weeks. Mice were anesthetized and euthanized at 22 weeks of age, and whole arteries were collected. The heart and aorta were isolated and fixed with 4% PFA for 24 h. Excised aortic arches were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE), and the entire aorta was stained with Oil red O. The heart was embedded in an optimal cutting temperature compound and frozen at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Serial sections of the embedded aortic roots mentioned above were cut at 5-μm intervals until all three leaflets became visible. These sections were stained with Masson and Oil red O for histological evaluation.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Sections from the aortic tissue of mice were processed as described in the previous section and stained with antibodies specific for SR-A (1:50, sc-166184, Santa Cruz), KAP1 (1:100, 15202-1-AP, Proteintech), SUMO1 (1:50, no. 4930, CST), SUMO2/3 (1:50, A5066, ABclonal), and ubiquitin (1:50, A19686, ABclonal).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Data normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and equality of variances was evaluated by Levene’s test; the assumptions for parametric tests were satisfied. The unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare two groups, and the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons to control type I error. All experiments were carried out in triplicate and the results are shown as mean ± SEM. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Uptake of G-LDL by ECs is an intermediate step in G-LDL transport. Thus, the intracellular concentration of G-LDL in HUVECs reflects the activity of G-LDL endocytosis. Figure 2C and D shows that cells contain small, independent, and discrete vesicles after HUVECs were incubated with fluorescently labeled G-LDL. The fluorescence intensity of G-LDL increased in HUVECs with KAP1 overexpression, and the mean fluorescence intensity (representing the level of AF555-G-LDL uptake) was significantly increased, thus indicating increased G-LDL uptake (Fig. 2E, F). In contrast, KAP1 siRNA transfection significantly decreased the fluorescence intensity, indicating a reduced G-LDL uptake (Fig. 2I–L). Interestingly, N-LDL uptake was not significantly altered by either KAP1 overexpression or knockdown. Taken together, these results suggested that KAP1 may positively regulate G-LDL transcytosis and uptake. Orthogonal validation using ApoB ELISA further supported that KAP1 knockdown significantly reduced intact G‑LDL transcytosis, whereas KAP1 overexpression significantly increased it (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). To assess specificity, we further examined LOX-1 expression upon KAP1 knockdown; KAP1 siRNA did not alter LOX-1 levels (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B), consistent with selective regulation of SR-A.

KAP1 increases SR-A protein stability

Our previous studies showed that SR-A protein is degraded in a time-dependent manner after the inhibition of cellular protein synthesis by CHX, and that the half-life of SR-A is prolonged by G-LDL (Fig. 3A, B) [14]. The discovery of the interaction between SR-A and KAP1 led to the question of whether SRA was a substrate for the E3 SUMO ligase KAP1. Therefore, the potential ability of KAP1 to regulate SR-A protein expression by regulating its stability was investigated. HUVECs were transfected with SR-A ± KAP1 for 48 h and then treated with CHX. The expression of SR-A protein was detected by western blot at different time points. KAP1 overexpression prolonged the half-life of SR-A from 5.89 h to 10.04 h (Fig. 3C, D), whereas KAP1 knockdown had the opposite effect, the half-life of endogenous SR-A protein decreased from 6.47 h to 4.13 h (Fig. 3E, F). Next, the consequence of KAP1 inhibition in inducing SR-A downregulation in the presence of G-LDL was assessed according to the lipid uptake mediated by KAP1 by promoting SR-A stability. G-LDL significantly increased SR-A expression, while this increase was suppressed by KAP1 siRNA (Fig. 4G, H). The transcriptional level was also assessed in addition to the translational level. Despite the transcriptional regulatory function of KAP1[33], KAP1 deficiency did not alter SR-A transcript levels (Fig. 4I).

Ubiquitination is essential for protein degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. K48 and K63 are the primary forms of polyubiquitination [34]. K48-linked polyubiquitination leads to proteasome-mediated protein degradation, while K63-linked polyubiquitination is involved in signal regulation and in the activation of several signaling pathways [35]. The de-ubiquitination process of KAP1 on SR-A was investigated using HA-tagged wild-type ubiquitin to construct mutant vectors K48 and K63, in which arginine replaced lysine residues in both ubiquitin mutant vectors except for positions 48 and 63. HEK293T cells were transfected with the MYC-KAP1 plasmid along with the HA-Ub-K48 or HA-Ub-K63 plasmid. The results showed that KAP1 overexpression suppressed both the polyubiquitination of SR-A and the ubiquitination of K48 junctions (Fig. 4J, K). These findings indicated that KAP1 inhibited K48-linked ubiquitination, thereby inhibiting SR-A degradation.

KAP1 promotes SUMOylation of SR-A

Since KAP1 is a SUMO E3 ligase and SUMOylation competes for binding to the ubiquitination site, the potential ability of KAP1 to SUMOylate SR-A was assessed.

G-LDL promoted the binding of SR-A to SUMO1, and KAP1 siRNA inhibited G-LDL-induced SUMOylation of SR-A, as shown in Fig. 5A–C. Notably, KAP1 siRNA inhibited the basal levels of SUMO1-modified SR-A and did not affect the basal levels of SUMO2/3 modification. SUMO1 conjugation regulates physiological processes by virtually binding to substrate proteins, whereas SUMO2 and SUMO3 are widely expressed in free forms and often appear under stress response [36]. SUMO1 unbound pool is smaller compared with SUMO2 and SUMO3 [37]. Therefore, the subsequent investigation of this work focused on SUMO-1 modification. A SUMO ligase-deficient KAP1 mutant (KAP1-C65/68A) failed to enhance SR-A SUMOylation or increase SR-A protein levels, indicating that the SUMO E3 ligase activity of KAP1 plays an important role in SR-A stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 6A). In most cases, SUMO-modified proteins contain the specific core consensus motif Ψ-K-x-D/E, where Ψ is a hydrophobic residue, K is the lysine bound to SUMO, x is any amino acid, and D or E is an acidic residue, thus allowing the noncovalent binding of proteins to SUMO proteins [38, 39]. Therefore, bioinformatics analysis using three independent computational programs [SUMOplot analysis program (Abgent), JASSA, and GPS-SUMO] revealed five potential SUMO-binding common motifs for SR-A (Fig. 5D). Next, the conservation of this motif was evaluated in different organisms, revealing that four of them were evolutionarily conserved from mouse to human (red box) (Fig. 5E). The SR-A mutant with lysine (K) replaced by arginine (R) in the predicted SUMOylation site was constructed and used for in vivo SUMOylation experiments. Reduced SUMOylation in the presence of the SR-A mutant was observed in the K22R mutant, suggesting that K22 was a critical SUMOylation site for SR-A (Fig. 5F, G). Furthermore, the K22R mutation abolished SR-A stabilization and reduced G-LDL transcytosis, and KAP1 overexpression failed to rescue these defects, indicating that K22 plays an essential role in SR-A stability and function (Supplementary Fig. 6B–D).

Taken together, these results further supported that KAP1 competitively inhibited the ubiquitin–proteasome degradation of SR-A by SUMOylation, thereby stabilizing its expression.

KAP1 knockdown suppresses the formation of atherosclerotic lesions in vivo

Since KAP1 worked as an inhibitor of SR-A ubiquitination degradation and exerted potent protective effects against G-LDL transcytosis in vitro, the influence of KAP1 deficiency in ApoE−/− mice was investigated. Adeno-associated viral vectors with the ICAM2 promoter for EC-specific expression against KAP1 were constructed and delivered into mice through the tail vein, and N-LDL or G-LDL was injected after 4 weeks to determine the intervention potency (Fig. 6A). The results showed that KAP1, SUMO1, and SR-A expression was increased in the aortae of AS mice treated with G-LD, and these effects were reversed by rAAV9-sh-KAP1, suggesting that SUMOylation of SR-A was inhibited (Fig. 6B). In addition, the area of atherosclerotic lesions was significantly larger in the G-LDL group than in the N-LDL group (Fig. 6C, D). Similarly, AF555-LDL retention in vivo was measured, showing that the fluorescence intensity of the G-LDL group was higher than that of the N-LDL group (Supplementary Fig. 8A–C). Increased fibrosis and lipid deposition was also found in the subendothelial space in the G-LDL group than in the N-LDL group (Fig. 6E–L). rAAV9-sh-KAP1 significantly inhibited these effects, indicating that KAP1 deficiency inhibited G-LDL-induced AS. Discussion

Subcutaneous deposition of lipoproteins in the endothelium represents the main pathogenic factor in AS, largely contributing to chronic metabolic disorders. Various modified LDLs are involved in the development of AS. Plasma Ox-LDL levels are significantly elevated in patients with AS and are considered as an indicator for early screening and diagnosis of coronary heart disease [40]. G-LDL concentrations are significantly higher in diabetic AS patients than in healthy controls, although serum LDL concentrations are not high [9]. Our previous study found that the transcellular transport of G-LDL is faster than that of N-LDL, and the transcytosis of G-LDL is mainly mediated by SR-A [14]. G-LDL accelerates the progression of AS by increasing the stability of SR-A to promote its expression and increasing SR-A-mediated cell-penetration of G-LDL in ECs. However, the underlying mechanism used by G-LDL to promote SR-A expression is not fully elucidated. This cell‑type‑specific function of SR‑A in endothelial cells contrasts with its role in macrophages, providing a framework to reconcile conflicting reports in literature.

Various types of PTMs are involved in the regulation of protein folding, stability, localization, and functional activities [41, 42]. SUMOylation is one type of PTM; it is a modification that facilitates or prevents the coupling of SUMO molecules to the lysine residues of target proteins. SUMOylation sites of substrate proteins overlap with ubiquitination sites, resulting in an antagonistic effect of SUMOylation [43–45]. However, the physiological function of protein ubiquitination is mainly to degrade aberrant proteins, while the SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation process is mainly involved in the stabilization, subcellular localization, metabolism, and transcription of substrate proteins [46, 47]. SUMO1 modification normally blocks the ubiquitination of its substrate [48]. Both SUMO1 and ubiquitin bind to the lysine (K) residue of the substrate, resulting in a competitive inhibition; it is therefore generally believed that SUMO1 modification stabilizes conjugated proteins. For example, SUMO-1 modification protects IκBα from ubiquitin-dependent degradation [49]. SUMO1-modified VP1 stabilizes the protein by antagonizing K48-linked ubiquitination [46]. SUMO1 modification of Smad4 prevents its polyubiquitination and degradation [50]. SUMO1-modified cGAS and STING are resistant to ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation during viral infection [51]. As exemplified above, SUMO1 modification stabilizes target proteins. While our study focuses on KAP1‑mediated SUMOylation as a key mechanism for SR‑A stabilization, we acknowledge that other post‑translational modifications, such as phosphorylation or acetylation, may also influence SR‑A stability, and transcriptional regulation cannot be entirely excluded.

In the present study, a SUMOylation modification pathway associated with SR-A degradation was found, revealing the relevance and interaction of SR-A SUMOylation and ubiquitination in G-LDL stimulation. In this pathway, SUMO1 modified SR-A through the SUMO E3 ligase KAP1, which competed with ubiquitin molecules in the binding of modified sites. Thus, the K48-linked ubiquitin–proteasome pathway degradation of SR-A was inhibited. G-LDL promoted KAP1 expression, possibly through receptor for advanced glycation endproducts (RAGE)‑dependent signaling pathways, as G‑LDL is a known RAGE ligand [52]. However, the precise molecular link remains to be determined. KAP1 also mediated the increase in SUMOylation modification of SR-A. This PTM crosstalk stabilizes SR-A, thereby mediating increased G-LDL transcytosis. Therefore, the inhibition of the G-LDL/SR-A signaling pathway by increasing proteasome-mediated SR-A degradation might be a potential target in the treatment of AS-related cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, the AS model was obtained using ApoE−/− mice fed with a HFD. The atherosclerotic plaque area in the aorta was increased in these mice treated with G-LDL, but this effect was mitigated by pretreatment with rAAV9-sh-KAP1. These results indicated that KAP1 knockdown inhibited G-LDL transcytosis and improved AS. Thus, pharmacological inhibition of KAP1 could disrupt the G-LDL/SR-A axis, offering a precision medicine approach for diabetic AS.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our conclusions regarding KAP1‑mediated SUMOylation are based on overexpression systems; other post‑translational modifications or regulatory pathways may also contribute to SR‑A stability. Second, while HUVECs are a widely used model system for endothelial transcytosis studies, they do not constitute a full physiological substitute for human arterial endothelium; similarly, our in vivo G‑LDL administration may not fully recapitulate endogenous diabetic conditions. Third, validation in human atherosclerotic tissues is lacking and will be addressed in future studies. In addition, only male mice were used in this study; whether the KAP1-SR-A pathway functions similarly in females remains to be investigated, given the known sex differences in atherosclerosis.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings suggest that KAP1 deficiency is associated with reduced AS onset and progression in our experimental model, possibly by inhibiting the SUMOylation modification of SR-A to promote its degradation by the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway and reducing the infiltration of G-LDL in the subendothelium of the vasculature. Thus, our study provides mechanistic insights into the control of the pro-atherogenic effects mediated by G-LDL/SR-A using strategies targeting KAP1 clearance. While these findings provide mechanistic insights into the KAP1–SR‑A axis in G-LDL‑induced atherosclerosis, further investigation is needed to explore their implications, and the preclinical nature of this work should be emphasized.
