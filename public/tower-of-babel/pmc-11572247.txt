Case characteristics and surgical efficacy in elderly patients over 65 years of age with cervical spinal cord injury without fracture and dislocation: a retrospective study
Authors: Ningning Feng, Luchun Xu, Xing Yu, Jianbin Guan, He Zhao, Wenhao Li, Ziye Qiu, Guozheng Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe outcomes of surgical and conservative treatment in elderly patients with cervical spinal cord injury without fracture and dislocation (CSCIWFD) were evaluated over medium- and long-term follow-up periods and the case characteristics were analysed.MethodsData from 39 elderly patients over 65 years of age with CSCIWFD who were admitted to our hospital between January 2010 and June 2022 were retrospectively analysed. Neurological recovery was assessed using the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grading criteria and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score at admission, after surgery, and at the final follow-up. Post-injury imaging data were used to evaluate potential underlying lesions and injury types. In the surgical treatment group, the sagittal and transverse diameters of the cervical spinal canal were measured using magnetic resonance imaging at admission and the final follow-up to effectively assess spinal cord decompression.ResultsAt the final follow-up, the JOA score of the 21 patients who underwent surgical treatment was significantly higher than that of the patients who received conservative treatment (P < 0.001). Additionally, the ASIA grading showed a significant improvement compared to the conservative treatment group (P < 0.003). The sagittal and transverse diameters of the cervical canal in the surgical treatment group were significantly larger at the final follow-up than at admission, indicating surgical decompression (P < 0.001).ConclusionElderly men are the most commonly affected group with CSCIWFD, typically presenting with a history of low-energy trauma. For these patients, active surgical management is recommended following the stress period, assuming their physical condition is suitable. Our results suggest that even in patients with prolonged injury, improvement in neurologic and motor function may be superior to conservative treatment after thorough decompression surgery.Keywords: Cervical spinal cord injury without fracture and dislocation, Elderly patients, Surgical treatment, Operation time

Introduction

Cervical spinal cord injury without fracture and dislocation (CSCIWFD) describes patients who exhibit clinical symptoms of cervical spinal cord and nerve injury, yet show no abnormalities such as fractures or dislocations on radiographs, computed tomography (CT) scans, or other radiological examinations [1]. This condition, also known as spinal cord injury without radiological abnormality, was first identified by American scholar Pang [2] in 1982 and was classified as a distinct type of cervical spinal cord injury. Children and adults are the main populations with the incidence of CSCIWFD. With the ageing population and the widespread use of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in clinical practice, CSCIWFD has become increasingly recognised. Studies both domestically and internationally indicate that CSCIWFD constitutes approximately 12–16% of such injuries, with falls and traffic accidents as the main causes of injury [3–5]. CSCIWFD tends to develop in middle-aged and older people over 45 years of age [6]. Unlike children’s CSCIWFD, the pathogenesis of adult CSCIWFD is complex, with the prevailing view suggesting that cervical degeneration reduces the reserve space of the spinal cord, making it susceptible to injury from minor trauma [7]. Spinal stenosis significantly reduces the compensatory space of the cervical cord. Thus, even low-energy impacts can cause severe damage due to insufficient cushioning space [8]. The pathological mechanisms, prevention, and treatment of CSCIWFD have been key areas of clinical research. This study retrospectively analysed the clinical data of elderly patients diagnosed with CSCIWFD from January 2010 to June 2022, summarising their case characteristics. By examining medium- and long-term follow-up data, the efficacy of surgical and conservative treatments for elderly patients with CSCIWFD was compared and the ideal timing for surgical intervention was explored.

Materials and methods

Objective of the study

Data on patients aged ≥ 65 years with CSCIWFD who were admitted to our department from January 2010 to June 2022 were retrospectively collected. Thirty-nine patients were included in the study, comprising 25 males and 14 females (Fig. 1). The average age was 70.13 ± 4.46 years, with an age range of 65 to 81 years. All patients had varying degrees of trauma history before injury, with 34 cases resulting from falls and five from traffic accidents. All patients had a history of trauma of varying degrees prior to injury, with 34 cases caused by falls and 5 by minor motor vehicle collision. Based on the patient’s clinical presentation, imaging findings, and overall health status, the decision whether to proceed with surgery is made.For middle-aged and elderly patients with rapid deterioration of spinal cord function after admission, due to unstable physiological state in the acute phase and high surgical risk, we choose to postpone surgery in time after the patient’s condition recovers. In addition, we also perform surgical treatment for patients who do not have severe injuries to begin with and whose neurological symptoms progress slowly, but who do not improve over time and who are able to undergo surgical treatment.Such selection criteria ensure that patients receive individualized treatment, minimize surgical risks, and ensure that necessary interventions are made in appropriate circumstances. Of these patients, 21 underwent surgical treatment, with the time from injury to surgical decompression ranging from 3 days to 3 months. Specifically, five patients were operated on within 3 days to 1 week after injury, three within 1 week to 2 weeks, three within 2 weeks to 1 month, seven within 1 to 2 months, and three within 2 to 3 months. The remaining 18 patients chose to be treated conservatively due to factors such as concomitant more severe underlying diseases that could not tolerate surgery.

Participants flow chart of this study

Imaging data

The 39 patients included in the study underwent cervical radiography, CT, and MRI after their injuries. Based on these imaging examinations, the potential underlying lesions were evaluated and the MRI findings after injury were classified. For the surgical treatment group, the sagittal and transverse diameters of the cervical canal were measured using MRI at admission and the final follow-up.

Treatment methods

Based on the patient’s trauma history and clinical signs and symptoms, combined with cervical radiography, CT, and MRI examinations, the diagnosis of CSCIWFD was established. Among the 39 patients in the study, 21 underwent surgical treatment: 16 underwent anterior cervical discectomy with decompression and fusion, four underwent posterior unilateral expansive open-door laminoplasty, and one underwent combined anterior and posterior surgery. After surgery, all patients were treated with methylprednisolone or dexamethasone to control spinal cord edema, while mannitol was used to further reduce the edema, and neurotrophic drugs such as mecobalamine were given to promote neurological recovery. After getting out of bed, patients were instructed to wear neck brace for 12 weeks. The remaining 18 patients received conservative treatment due to factors such as mild spinal cord compression, age, and underlying health conditions. Conservatively treated patients were required to wear an appropriate neck brace for 12 weeks to maintain spinal stability and prevent secondary injury. These patients also received methylprednisolone or dexamethasone for shock treatment, mannitol to reduce spinal cord oedema, and neurotrophic drugs, such as methylcobalamin, as adjuvant therapy. In addition, patients in the conservative treatment group also received a 4-week physical therapy package that included methods such as thermal therapy and low-frequency electrotherapy to improve blood circulation, relieve pain and discomfort symptoms, and promote recovery.

Clinical efficacy and imaging evaluation

Japanese orthopaedic association (JOA) 17-point score method: [9]. JOA scores were recorded for all patients after admission, postoperatively, and at the final follow-up.

American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade: [10]. The degree of spinal cord injury was divided into grades A, B, C, D, and E based on the loss of motor and sensory function. All patients underwent ASIA grading on admission, postoperatively, and at the final follow-up.

Based on patients’ imaging examinations.The possible preoperative underlying lesions and types of injuries were assessed. In the surgical treatment group, the sagittal and transverse diameters of the cervical spinal canal at the time of admission and the last follow-up were measured [11]. The measurement method involved determining the sagittal diameter from the posterior edge of the most prominent part of the vertebral space to the most prominent part of the ligament of the laminae, and the transverse diameter was measured as the left-to-right width through the centre of the spinal cord [11] (Fig. 2).

Statistical methods

Analysis was performed using the SPSS 27.0 statistical software. Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. An independent sample t-test was used to compare the age, follow-up time, JOA scores at admission and the final follow-up between the surgical and conservative treatment groups. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The ASIA grade, sex, injury factors, injury segment, and results at admission and the final follow-up were analysed using the chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Clinical outcomes

The JOA scores of the 39 patients with CSCIWFD included in this study were recorded at admission, after surgery, and at the final follow-up. Their motor and sensory functions were graded according to the ASIA grading criteria (missing 1-year outcome scores were interpolated using the end-observation carry-over method, which has been validated in previous acute spinal cord injury studies) [12, 13]. During follow-up, the motor and sensory functions of the 21 patients who underwent surgical treatment gradually improved, with no cases of neurological deterioration or the need for revision surgery. Among the 18 patients who received conservative treatment, 13 experienced an improvement in nerve function compared to their condition before the injury, while five showed no significant improvement in sensory and motor functions.

JOA scores

In the surgical treatment group, the mean JOA score was 6.25 ± 1.98 at admission, 12.70 ± 1.81 after surgery, and 14.55 ± 1.64 at the final follow-up. In the conservative treatment group, the mean JOA score was 6.44 ± 0.76 at admission and 11.83 ± 0.93 at the final follow-up. There was no significant difference in the JOA scores between the two groups at admission (P > 0.05; Table 4); however, at the final follow-up, the JOA score in the surgical treatment group was significantly higher than that in the conservative treatment group (P < 0.001; Table 4).

Imaging results

Degree of cervical spine degeneration and the MRI type after injury

Upon examination of cervical MRIs after injury, all 39 patients included in the study exhibited varying degrees of cervical degenerative changes. In the surgical treatment group, this included nine cases of simple cervical disc herniation, seven cases of disc herniation combined with narrowing of the intervertebral space, two cases of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and three cases of posterior longitudinal ligament ossification. Similarly, in the conservative treatment group, these degenerative changes comprised seven cases of simple cervical disc herniation, five cases of disc herniation combined with narrowing of the intervertebral space, three cases of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and three cases of posterior longitudinal ligament ossification. There was no significant difference in the cervical degeneration type between the two groups (P > 0.05; Table 6). Furthermore, in the surgical treatment group, the MRI findings indicated 13 cases of oedema and eight cases of haemorrhage, whereas in the conservative treatment group, there were 13 cases of oedema and five cases of haemorrhage. No significant difference in terms of the MRI type after injury was observed between the two groups (P > 0.05; Table 6).

Typical cases

Xu XX, a 71-year-old female, was hospitalised due to “unsteady walking for more than 2 months following a fall”. The patient initially experienced unsteady walking, described as feeling like walking on cotton, after an inadvertent fall 2 months prior. The patient also had numbness in her hands and feet but was still able to walk slowly on her own. These symptoms were not given much attention, and she did not seek further treatment. One month ago, the patient fell again, after which her walking instability worsened, the sensation of walking on cotton intensified, and she experienced coldness in her limbs, weakness in her lower limbs, and an inability to walk independently. An MRI of the cervical spine revealed varying degrees of disc herniation at C3–6, dural sac compression, cervical spinal canal stenosis, and a partial high signal at the C3–4 spinal cord level. She was admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of “CSCIWFD”.

Discussion

CSCIWFD refers to a type of acute cervical cord injury that occurs without any associated fracture or dislocation due to neck trauma. This condition predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly individuals, with a significantly higher incidence in males compared to females [14–16]. In this retrospective study, 39 patients aged 65 to 81 years, with an average age of 70.13 ± 4.46 years, were analysed. The cohort included 25 male and 14 female patients. The higher number of male patients might be attributed to greater engagement in social and physical activities and more pronounced degenerative changes in the spine. The pathogenesis of CSCIWFD is primarily linked to degenerative changes in the cervical spine, which result in a reduced reserve space within the spinal canal. When trauma occurs, the already narrowed spinal canal further compresses the cervical spinal cord, leading to severe pincer-like injuries [17, 18]. Preoperative imaging in our study revealed varying degrees of cervical degenerative changes in all patients. Specifically, there were 16 cases of simple cervical disc herniation, 12 cases of disc herniation with intervertebral space narrowing, five cases of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and six cases of posterior longitudinal ligament ossification. These patients had a long history of cervical spine degeneration, and their bodies had adapted to prolonged spinal cord compression, allowing them to maintain normal function and daily activities without obvious abnormalities. However, relatively minor trauma that does not cause fractures or dislocations can result in significant spinal cord injuries in these patients. In contrast, such minor trauma typically does not affect younger individuals without degenerative disease. The most commonly affected segment in cervical spine injuries is C4–5, followed by C3–4 and C5–6 [19]. In our study, 92 operative segments were identified in 39 patients: 25 segments were C3–4 (27.17%), 35 segments were C4–5 (38.04%), 23 segments were C5–6 (25.00%), and nine segments were C6–7 (9.78%). The distribution of these injured segments indirectly suggests that degenerative changes in the cervical spine are a possible cause of CSCIWFD.

CSCIWFD is a cervical spinal cord injury caused by minor trauma, primarily characterised by incomplete spinal cord injury with mild primary injury and severe secondary injury [20]. Many patients experience varying degrees of neurological recovery after initial bed rest and dehydration therapy. As spinal cord oedema subsides, motor sensory functions begin to improve. However, natural recovery often plateaus or even deteriorates after a certain point [21]. During this period, patients are at an increased risk of secondary falls, as presented in the typical case. The management of CSCIWFD remains a highly debated topic within the spine community due to its highly variable clinical presentation, ranging from mild sensory deficits to significant loss, and the variable timeline for spontaneous neurological recovery, which can span from hours to months [15, 22–25]. With advancements in surgical techniques, the recommended treatment approach has increasingly favoured surgical intervention, particularly in patients with deteriorating neurological function and severe spinal stenosis. Surgical decompression and enlargement of the spinal canal can significantly reduce the risk of posterior spinal cord contusion, oedema, haemorrhage or ischaemic injury [26–28]. However, the clinical variability, nerve recovery patterns, and diverse sources of cervical cord compression in elderly patients with CSCIWFD mean that the surgical approach and timing remain controversial [29–31].

CSCIWFD can be treated with anterior cervical discectomy and fusion or posterior spinal canal enlargement plasty. The primary goal of surgery is to decompress and enlarge the spinal canal, thereby improving local haematology and microcirculation of the spinal cord to promote neurological recovery. Therefore, the degree and location of spinal cord compression are crucial factors in determining the surgical approach. The choice between anterior and posterior surgical routes should be based on the principle of accurately and effectively decompressing the compressed spinal cord. Generally, when compression originates from the anterior side of the spinal cord, such as with short-segment cervical disc herniation or posterior longitudinal ligament ossification, anterior decompression and internal fixation are considered viable options [32, 33]. For multi-level cervical disc herniation, continuous posterior longitudinal ligament ossification, or dorsal spinal cord compression, posterior decompression is more appropriate [28, 34, 35]. Within cases where both anterior and posterior compression of the spinal cord are present, neither purely anterior nor posterior surgery alone can achieve complete decompression. In such cases, a combination of anterior and posterior surgery is necessary to thoroughly decompress the spinal cord and maximise the improvement of the patient’s clinical symptoms [36, 37]. In this study, the 21 patients who underwent surgical treatment underwent different procedures based on their preoperative symptoms and the source of cervical cord compression. Specifically, 16 patients underwent anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, four underwent posterior cervical expansive open-door laminoplasty, and one underwent a combined anterior and posterior surgery. Postoperative imaging confirmed adequate surgical decompression. Medium- and long-term follow-ups revealed that patients in the surgical treatment group had significantly higher JOA scores at the final follow-up compared to those in the conservative treatment group. Their ASIA grading also showed significant improvement, and no cases of spinal cord function deterioration or revision surgery were observed during the follow-up period.

The timing of surgical decompression in CSCIWFD has been a subject of significant clinical debate, with limited and often conflicting studies available. Inoue [29] and Nori [7] selected 24 and 48 h after injury as the timing of surgery, respectively, while Nori [7] showed that surgery within 48 h had no positive effect on the recovery of spinal cord function. Qi [30], Zhou [38]and Zhao [39] selected 3–7 days after injury as the timing of surgery in patients with CSCIWFD. The studies by Qian [40] and Zhu [41] used 1 week after injury as the timing of surgery. Wang [31] and Zhang [42] used 3 months after injury as the timing of surgery. And the results of both studies showed no significant correlation between the timing of surgery (< 3 m) and neurologic recovery. The research results of Qi [30] and Badhiwala [43] showed that early surgical decompression (within 24 h of injury) can expedite the restoration of neurological function in patients with CSCIWFD and should be considered a neuroprotective treatment strategy. However, studies by Chikuda [44] and Aarabi [45] concluded that there is no significant difference in motor function recovery between early and delayed surgery. Ma [46] found through clinical studies that delayed surgery, when conditions permit, can better optimise the patient’s health status, reducing surgical complications and mortality. Given this context, it is believed that early and delayed surgeries for patients with CSCIWFD should be studied separately. Our clinical practice has shown that some patients with CSCIWFD, who do not exhibit severe neurological damage initially, are surgically only after their symptoms worsen over time. Therefore, patients with CSCIWFD often undergo a natural recovery process after injury, with only a few patients receiving surgical treatment within 24 h of the acute injury. In our study, cases with early surgery were excluded and only patients with CSCIWFD who underwent deferred surgery (3 days to 3 months after injury) were included. The focus is on evaluating the clinical outcomes of delayed surgery and conservative management to determine the optimal interventions and treatment strategies for patients with CSCIWFD treated after an initial period after injury.

The results of our study suggest that surgical treatment, even when performed long after the initial injury, yields better clinical outcomes compared to conservative treatment. This study focused on elderly individuals with CSCIWFD, all aged ≥ 65 years. These patients typically have poorer physical fitness and more underlying health conditions, making it challenging for them to safely survive the perioperative period given the dual trauma of the initial injury and the surgical intervention. In addition, CSCIWFD injuries are often located in the upper cervical spine, significantly affecting respiratory function. Prolonged use of ventilators after surgery can further complicate recovery. Therefore, when considering treatment options and the timing of surgery for elderly patients with CSCIWFD, active surgical management after the stress period is recommended, provided their physical conditions permit the same.

This study has some limitations. First of all, this is a retrospective study with a small number of included cases and is only a single-center study. Therefore, it is still necessary to increase the number of cases and carry out multi-center studies in order to improve the accuracy of research results. At the same time, due to the limitation of the type of study, some clinical data and imaging data of patients could not be completely evaluated. The follow-up data of such patients will be gradually improved in the future to improve data quality and reliability.

Conclusion

Elderly men are the most commonly affected group with CSCIWFD, typically presenting with a history of low-energy trauma. For these patients, active surgical management is recommended following the stress period, assuming their physical condition is suitable. Our results suggest that even in patients with prolonged injury, improvement in neurologic and motor function may be superior to conservative treatment after thorough decompression surgery.
