Conformationally Defined Alkaloidal Building Units for the Controlled Formation of Nanoscale Supramolecular Morphologies
Authors: Fumi Oki, Shunsuke Murakami, Tasuku Honda, Satoshi Yoshida, Saori Maki‐Yonekura, Jungmin Kang, Kensuke Tono, Koji Yonekura, Sota Sato, Hiroki Oguri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrecise control over nanoscale morphologies is essential for designing advanced supramolecular systems with programmed functions. Among various molecular motifs, diketopiperazine (DKP) stands out as a promising building block due to its robust hydrogen‐bonding capability. Inspired by natural products containing DKP units, we developed supramolecular assemblies based on bispyrrolidinoindoline (BPI) scaffolds fused with DKP rings. By exploiting the conformational tunability of BPI scaffolds arising from rotation around the C3a─3a′ bond, we achieved precise modulation of their three‐dimensional conformations and self‐assembly behavior through dual substituent and stereochemical modifications. Iodination at the C5/5′ positions refined conformational preferences and promoted the formation of highly ordered nano‐structures. Moreover, additional stereochemical modification at the C15/15′ positions successfully shifted the morphology from block‐like to fibrous assemblies. These findings underscore the pivotal roles of substituent and stereochemistry in controlling nanoscale morphology, positioning BPI‐based scaffolds as a versatile platform for supramolecular engineering.Keywords: chiral alkaloidal scaffolds, conformational control, natural products, programmable nanoscale morphologies, self‐assembly

Introduction

Self‐assembly is a cornerstone of supramolecular chemistry, representing a fundamental bottom‐up approach for constructing functional supramolecular architectures [1, 2, 3, 4]. In nature, biomolecules such as peptides and DNA exhibit remarkable self‐assembling abilities and inherent molecular chirality, inspiring versatile applications beyond biomaterials, including electronic materials [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. To rationally design advanced supramolecular systems with flexibly tailored and programmed functions, precise control and diversification of nanoscale morphologies—achieved through deliberate conformational and stereochemical design—are essential.

Among various molecular building units, amino acid derivatives have attracted considerable attention owing to their stereochemical diversity, tunable functionality, and rich noncovalent interactions. In particular, the cyclic dipeptide, 2,5‐diketopiperazine (DKP) has been recognized as a robust hydrogen‐bonding motif and serves as a versatile scaffold for constructing precisely organized supramolecular architectures [10, 11, 12]. Recent studies have shown that halogenation on aromatic rings in DKP induces modulation of three‐dimensional molecular structures and interaction modes, thereby diversifying their self‐assembled morphologies (Figure 1A) [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Furthermore, together with substituent effects, the stereochemical configuration of such dipeptides has been shown to direct the molecular packing mode and supramolecular organization [18, 19, 20, 21].

(A) Conceptual illustration of nanostructure control based on diketopiperazine (DKP) motifs. (B) Representative structures of BPI alkaloidal building units. (C) Schematic depiction of supramolecular morphology control enabled by three‐dimensional structural fine‐tuning of the BPI scaffold.

Bispyrrolidinoindoline (BPI) alkaloids, biosynthesized from tryptophan or tryptamine, are characterized by a C 2‐symmetric chiral framework, in which two segments are linked via a C3a─3a′ bond. Owing to free rotation around this central bond, BPI alkaloids exhibit notable conformational tunability [22]. Our previous study demonstrated that stereochemical variation at the C2/2′ positions of BPI‐based amphiphiles markedly influences their conformational and self‐assembly behavior [23]. This structural and stereochemical diversity presents a unique opportunity to systematically explore the interplay between stereochemistry, substitution, and supramolecular organization. Naturally occurring BPIs, several of which contain fused diketopiperazine (DKP) rings (such as (+)‐eurocristatine; Figure 1B), possess structural features that are well suited for supramolecular chemistry, yet their self‐assembly properties remain largely unexplored [24].

Inspired by these structural features, we herein report the design, synthesis, and self‐assembly behaviors of a series of DKP‐fused BPI building units (Figure 1C). By employing a dual approach of halogenation and stereochemical modification, the conformation of BPI scaffolds was finely tuned, enabling precise control over their self‐assembly behavior and nanoscale morphologies. Iodine substitution at the C5/5′ positions of DKPs induces conformational changes that lead to highly ordered self‐assemblies. Furthermore, variation in the stereochemistry at the C15/15′ positions modulates the nanostructures from block‐like to fibrous morphologies. Collectively, this study demonstrates that BPI scaffolds are promising molecular building units for supramolecular assembly, as precise control over their three‐dimensional structures enables deliberate manipulation of supramolecular morphology. These findings establish BPI scaffolds as a strategic platform for advancing supramolecular engineering.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Design and Synthesis of BPI‐Based Building Units

To construct highly ordered nanostructures, we employed DKP‐fused bispyrrolidinoindoline (BPI) scaffolds as building units. The BPI scaffold is a C 2‐symmetric dimeric framework comprising two pyrrolidinoindoline units connected via a C3a─3a′ bond. Owing to the rotational flexibility around this central bond, the BPI scaffold can adopt three distinct conformers (type‐I to ‐III), which are characterized by the dihedral angle between the two BPI aromatic rings (Figure 1C). Our previous study demonstrated that the conformational behavior of BPI scaffolds is highly sensitive to the stereochemical configuration and substituents on both the aliphatic and aromatic nitrogen atoms [23, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Notably, several naturally occurring BPI derivatives possess fused DKP rings, indicating a strong potential for hydrogen bond‐driven self‐assembly. Leveraging the structural features and conformational tunability of the BPI scaffold, we designed the DKP‐fused derivative 2‐(5‐H,15S) to deliberately control its self‐assembly behavior. Phenylalanine was chosen for DKP ring construction owing to its propensity to promote folded conformations stabilized by CH–π interactions [11, 13, 29]. By introducing stereochemical variation at the phenylalanine residue, we aimed to significantly alter the spatial orientation of the benzyl moiety, thereby reshaping the conformational landscape of the BPI scaffold and ultimately diversifying the morphology of the resulting supramolecular assemblies. To investigate control over conformational preferences and self‐assembly behavior through structural modifications—halogen substitution at the C5/5′ and stereochemical variation at the C15/15′—we synthesized the iodine‐substituted analogs 4‐(5‐I,15S) and 5‐(5‐I,15R).

The DKP‐fused BPI‐based building units 2–5 were synthesized from l‐tryptophan and l/d‐phenylalanine (Figure 2A). Utilizing the nickel‐catalyzed reductive dimerization, developed by our group [30], the BPI scaffold 6 was obtained from l‐tryptophan in five steps as a common intermediate. Subsequent removal of four Boc groups, followed by condensation with Fmoc‐l‐phenylalanine, furnished the DKP cyclization precursor 7 in 82% yield over two steps. Treatment with excess diethylamine enabled one‐pot, efficient removal of the Fmoc group and subsequent DKP formation, affording 2‐(5‐H,15S) in 67% yield. The stereoisomer 3‐(5‐H,15R) was prepared via a similar sequence, employing Fmoc‐d‐phenylalanine in the condensation step, and was obtained in 34% yield over three steps.

The site‐selective iodination at the C5/5′ positions of the common intermediate 6 was performed using N‐iodosuccinimide (NIS) and pyridinium p‐toluenesulfonate (PPTS), furnishing iodide 8 in 88% yield [27]. Following a similar procedure to that used for 2‐(5‐H,15S) and 3‐(5‐H,15R), 4‐(5‐I,15S) and 5‐(5‐I,15R) were synthesized in 51% and 40% yields over three steps, respectively.

Structural Evaluation of BPI Building Units

To reveal their three‐dimensional molecular structures, x‐ray crystallographic analyses were carried out for the C 2‐symmetric DKP‐fused BPI scaffolds 2–5 (Figure 2B–E) [31]. In the crystal state, compound 2‐(5‐H,15S) adopts a type‐I conformation, with both aromatic rings of the BPI scaffolds oriented on the same side (Figures 2B and S12). In this structure, the benzyl group of the phenylalanine residue points inward. In contrast, compound 3‐(5‐H,15R) adopts a type‐II conformation, in which the aromatic rings of the BPI core are oriented oppositely, indicating that stereochemistry at the phenylalanine residue alters the overall molecular geometry (Figures 2C and S12). Interestingly, stereo‐chemical variation (15S → 15R) induces outward folding of the benzyl group of the phenylalanine residue (Figure S5). It is well established that DKP frameworks frequently adopt folded conformations stabilized by CH–π interactions between the methylene or methine protons in DKP rings with adjacent aromatic rings [11, 13, 29]. In compound 3‐(5‐H,15R), similar intramolecular CH–π interactions between the C11/11′ C─H bonds of the DKP moiety and the phenyl ring of the phenylalanine side chain are likely responsible for the observed conformational change. This is supported by the short distance of 2.87 Å between the C11/11′ C–H sites and the phenyl ring, suggesting a clear CH–π interaction. Further evidence for the folded conformation of 3‐(5‐H,15R) is provided by its 1H NMR spectral analysis. The C11/11′ protons of 3‐(5‐H,15R) showed a significant upfield shift compared to those of 2‐(5‐H,15S) (Figure S45; 3.72 ppm → 2.10–2.00 ppm) likely due to the shielding effect induced by the ring current of the phenyl ring [32]. This suggests that 3‐(5‐H,15R) maintains a folded conformation due to the DKP C─H···π (phenyl) interactions in both the crystalline and solution states.

Conversely, 4‐(5‐I,15S) adopts a type‐III conformation, in which the aromatic rings of the BPI core are positioned in a nearly stacked arrangement (Figures 2D and S12). The crystal structure of 5‐(5‐I,15R) also confirms the type‐III conformation, while the characteristic folding around the phenylalanine unit remains similar to that observed in 3‐(5‐H,15R) (Figures 2E and S6, S12).

Taken together, these structural investigations demonstrate that stereochemical variation at the C15/15′ positions and halogen substitution at the C5/5′ positions enable precise tuning of the conformations of the C 2‐symmetric DKP‐fused BPI scaffolds.

Conformational Assessments of C 2‐symmetric DKP‐fused BPI Scaffolds

To further elucidate the conformational behavior of DKP‐fused BPI scaffolds 2–5, computational analysis and CD spectroscopy were performed (Figure 3) [22, 27, 28, 33]. X‐ray crystallographic analysis established the conformational types (type‐I–III) of 2–5 in the crystalline state (Figure 2).

Experimental circular dichroism (CD) spectra (c = 1 × 10−5 M) recorded in five solvents involving 1,4‐dioxane, THF, acetonitrile, methanol, and THF/ H2O (2:8) mixed solvent, and simulated CD spectra of conformer‐I, ‐II, and ‐III obtained at the TD‐CAM‐B3LYP/6‐31G(d)//CAM‐B3LYP/6‐31G(d) level of theory for (A) 2‐(5‐H,15S), (B) 3‐(5‐H,15R), and by TD‐CAM‐B3LYP/SDD//CAM‐B3LYP/SDD level of theory for (C) 4‐(5‐I,15S) and (D) 5‐(5‐I,15R).

These experimentally determined conformations were used as structural reference points for evaluating conformational preferences in solution by comparison of the experimental CD spectra with spectra simulated by time‐dependent density functional theory (TD‐DFT) calculations.

As a representative example, 3‐(5‐H,15R), whose x‐ray crystal structure indicates a type‐II conformation, exhibited CD spectra with characteristic negative Cotton effects at 305 and 251 nm and minimal solvent dependence. These spectral features agree with the TD‐DFT‐simulated spectra for both the type‐II and type‐I conformations (Figure 3B). In conjunction with the x‐ray crystallographic structure (Figure 2C), these results suggest that the type‐II conformation predominates in solution.

Similarly, 5‐(5‐I,15R), whose x‐ray crystal structure adopts a type‐III conformation, exhibited CD spectra with negative Cotton effects at 315 and 262 nm and minimal solvent dependence (Figure 3D). These features are consistent with the TD‐DFT‐simulated spectra for both type‐II and type‐III conformations. On the basis of its x‐ray crystal structure, the type‐III conformation appears to be preferentially adopted.

In contrast, both 2‐(5‐H,15S) and 4‐(5‐I,15S) exhibited pronounced solvent‐dependent changes in their CD spectra, indicating solvent‐dependent equilibria between two dominant conformational types in solution. The experimental CD spectra of 2‐(5‐H,15S) in aprotic solvents such as 1,4‐dioxane, THF, and acetonitrile exhibited negative Cotton effects at 305 and 250 nm, along with a relatively weak positive Cotton effect around 225 nm. This spectral profile resembles the TD‐DFT‐simulated spectra of type‐II conformations (Figure 3A), suggesting that these conformations predominate under these conditions. In contrast, in protic solvents such as methanol, the negative Cotton effect at 250 nm decreases while the positive signal around 225 nm becomes more intense, giving a spectral pattern that more closely matches the simulated type‐I profile. These results indicate that the population of the type‐I conformation increases in protic solvents consistent with the crystal structure of 2‐(5‐H,15S) (Figure 2B).

A similar solvent‐dependent trend was observed for 4‐(5‐I,15S). In aprotic solvents, the CD spectrum exhibited a negative Cotton effect at 262 nm together with a weak Cotton effect around 320 nm. These spectral features are consistent with the type‐III conformational model predicted by TD‐DFT calculations and with the type‐III conformation observed in the crystal structure (Figure 2D). In contrast, in protic media, the negative Cotton effect at 262 nm diminished, while a new positive Cotton effect appeared at 216 nm, indicating an increased contribution of the type‐I conformational state.

The difference in solvent‐dependent conformational flexibility among 2–5 is likely attributable to CH–π interactions. The dimeric scaffolds 3‐(5‐H,15R) and 5‐(5‐I,15R) exhibit CH–π interactions between the C11/11′ C–H sites and the phenyl ring (Figure 2C,E), thereby displaying relatively rigid conformational behavior. In contrast, 2‐(5‐H,15S) and 4‐(5‐I,15S) lack such interactions and exhibit greater conformational flexibility.

Collectively, these spectroscopic analyses of the four DKP‐fused BPI scaffolds 2–5 reveal that both stereochemical configuration at the C15/15′ and halogen‐substitution at the C5/5′ play pivotal roles in fine‐tuning the conformational landscape and enhancing the conformational rigidity of the dimeric scaffolds.

Morphological Characterization of Supramolecular Assemblies

Building upon insights into the conformational properties of the BPI scaffolds, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were performed on each BPI‐based building block powder to evaluate how structural features influence self‐assembly behavior and the resulting supramolecular morphologies (Figure 4). All powder samples were prepared by suspending the compounds in acetonitrile, allowing the suspensions to stand at room temperature, and collecting the resulting precipitates by filtration.

While SEM imaging of 2‐(5‐H,15S) showed no discernible nanoscale features (Figure 4A), the iodinated derivative 4‐(5‐I,15S) exhibited a well‐defined block‐like morphology, suggesting that iodine substitution induces conformational changes conductive to the formation of highly ordered supramolecular assemblies (Figure 4E).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of (A) 2‐(5‐H,15S), (C) 3‐(5‐H,15R), (E) 4‐(5‐I,15S), and (G) 5‐(5‐I,15R). Corresponding molecular packing structures of (B) 2‐(5‐H,15S), (D) 3‐(5‐H,15R), (F) 4‐(5‐I,15S), and (H) 5‐(5‐I,15R).

This morphological divergence can be reasonably attributed to differences in both molecular packing and intermolecular interactions. In the crystal structure of 2‐(5‐H,15S), the molecules adopt an alternating orientation with neighboring units rotated by approximately 90° while keeping the BPI aromatic rings aligned in the same direction, and a single‐point hydrogen bond is formed between the DKP rings (Figure 4B).

Conversely, in the packing structure of 4‐(5‐I,15S), an antiparallel arrangement is adopted, which minimizes the overall dipole moment by orienting neighboring molecules in opposite directions [34]. Indeed, the dipole moment of the iodine‐substituted 4‐(5‐I,15S), estimated by DFT calculations, was approximately twofold greater than that of 2‐(5‐H,15S) (Figures S34 and S36). Moreover, 4‐(5‐I,15S) displayed multiple hydrogen bonding interactions between the aromatic nitrogen atoms and DKP carbonyl moieties within each column (Figure 4F). In addition, two hydrogen bonds are formed between DKP rings of neighboring columns, with the adjacent column rotated by 90° relative to the original column (Figure S9).

Notably, the intermolecular distance between the DKP carbonyl group and the iodine atom in 4‐(5‐I,15S) measures 3.17 Å, suggesting it is sufficiently short to be indicative of halogen‐bond formation (C═O⋯I). Both hydrogen and halogen bonds are therefore likely to contribute to the stabilization of the rigid and linear packing arrangement, which is consistent with the observed block‐like morphology. Together, these results demonstrate that iodine substitution modulates molecular packing and strengthens intermolecular interactions, thereby promoting the formation of well‐ordered supramolecular architectures [35].

Following the evaluation of halogen substitution effects, the influence of stereochemical modification was evaluated. The unsubstituted 3‐(5‐H,15R) exhibited supramolecular assemblies lacking distinct morphological features, similar to its diastereomer 2‐(5‐H,15S) (Figure 4C). X‐ray crystallographic analysis of 3‐(5‐H,15R) revealed that its molecular packing is stabilized exclusively by single‐point hydrogen bonds, which is consistent with the observed lack of ordered morphology (Figure 4D).

In contrast, the halogen‐substituted diastereomeric pair 4‐(5‐I,15S) and 5‐(5‐I,15R) displayed pronounced morphological differences: 4‐(5‐I,15S) formed well‐defined block‐like assemblies, whereas 5‐(5‐I,15R) exhibited intertwined fibrous nanostructures (Figure 4E,G). This morphological change resulting from stereochemical variation can be rationalized by differences in the packing arrangements of the respective crystalline states. In the crystal packing, 5‐(5‐I,15R) assembled into a helical arrangement, in which the BPI aromatic rings of adjacent molecules were oriented in opposite directions. Each molecule is connected via a single intermolecular hydrogen bond between amide moieties (C13/13′ and N14/14′). Furthermore, an intramolecular hydrogen bond is formed between C16 and the aromatic amine in 5‐(5‐I,15R) (Figure S10), in contrast to 4‐(5‐I,15S), where the same carbonyl group participates in intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions. Additionally, no halogen‐bonding interactions were observed in the crystal packing of 5‐(5‐I,15R). Although 4‐(5‐I,15S) formed multiple interactions, including hydrogen and halogen bonds, 5‐(5‐I,15R) adopted a relatively flexible packing stabilized by fewer and weaker interactions, leading to a fibrous morphology. These observations underscore the importance of stereochemical configuration in dictating the resulting nanostructures. Taken together, halogen substitution and stereochemical modification enabled precise tuning of the three‐dimensional conformations of the BPI building units. These structural adjustments modulate intermolecular interactions, ultimately facilitating the formation of highly ordered supramolecular architectures and allowing for precise control over morphological features.

Investigation of Halogen‐Substituted BPIs

To investigate the influence of halogen identity on supramolecular architecture, chlorine‐ and bromine‐substituted derivatives (12–15) were also synthesized using procedures analogous to those employed for 4‐(5‐I,15S) and 5‐(5‐I,15R), [23] and their resulting supramolecular morphologies were examined by SEM (Figure 5). As with the iodide derivatives, both chlorine and bromine substitutions promoted the formation of ordered supramolecular structures, with stereochemical variation further modulating the resulting morphologies, spanning rigid, block‐like assemblies to flexible, fibrous architectures.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images illustrating supramolecular morphologies of chlorine‐ and bromine‐substituted BPI scaffolds 12–15.

For the chlorine‐substituted compounds 12‐(5‐Cl,15S) and 13‐(5‐Cl,15R), the observed morphological features closely resembled those of the iodinated analogues: 12‐(5‐Cl,15S) formed rigid, block‐like assemblies, whereas 13‐(5‐Cl,15R) produced flexible, fibrous structures. In contrast, the bromine‐substituted 14‐(5‐Br,15S) exhibited less well‐defined morphologies than 4‐(5‐I,15S) and 12‐(5‐Cl,15S). Notably, the self‐assembled structure of 15‐(5‐Br,15R) also adopted a fibrous morphology, closely resembling those of the chlorine‐ and iodine‐substituted BPIs, namely 5‐(5‐I,15R) and 13‐(5‐Cl,15R).

For the brominated derivatives 14 and 15, the less defined structures are presumed to arise from the weaker halogen‐bonding ability of bromine compared with iodine [36, 37]. In the case of the chlorine‐substituted derivatives 12 and 13, the observed morphology cannot be explained solely by the strength of halogen bonding. Because chlorine has a smaller atomic radius and weaker σ‐hole character than iodine, the intermolecular contact distances and packing geometries are expected to differ, which may lead to variations in supramolecular organization. However, since suitable single crystals for X‐ray analysis have not been obtained, a detailed discussion remains difficult at this stage.

Overall, these results confirm that halogen substitution at the C5/5′ positions plays a key role in the formation of well‐ordered supramolecular nanoarchitectures, although the magnitude of this effect depends on halogen identity.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated precise control over nanoscale morphologies in supramolecular assemblies derived from chiral alkaloidal building units. To promote self‐assembly via hydrogen bonding, we designed and synthesized BPI scaffolds incorporating fused DKP rings. Notably, halogen substitution at the C5/5′ positions and stereochemical modifications at the C15/15′ positions play pivotal roles in modulating the conformational behavior of the BPI core. Through this dual‐modulation strategy, we successfully tailored the BPI building units into three well‐defined conformational states of the C 2‐symmetric DKP‐fused BPI scaffolds (2: type‐I, 3: type‐II, 4 and 5: type‐III).

Halogen‐substituted derivatives 4‐(5‐I,15S) and 5‐(5‐I,15R) formed highly ordered supramolecular architectures, while the unsubstituted derivatives failed to form well‐defined nanoscale morphologies. These effects are attributed to the introduction of directional intermolecular interactions, including hydrogen‐ and halogen‐bonding interactions, induced by halogen substitution. Moreover, stereochemical modifications at the C15/15′ positions further modulated intermolecular hydrogen‐ and halogen‐bonding interactions, thereby amplifying morphological differences: 4‐(5‐I,15S) exhibited rigid, block‐like structures, while 5‐(5‐I,15R) showed flexible, fibrous assemblies.

Collectively, these results highlight that precise three‐dimensional structure modulation of chiral alkaloidal building units—achieved through cooperative substituent and stereochemical variation—critically dictates intermolecular interactions. This level of structural control enables the programmable formation of nanoscale supramolecular morphologies. Accordingly, this study establishes BPI scaffolds as versatile and strategically designable platforms for supramolecular engineering and provides a general framework for guiding the molecular design of functional self‐assembling systems. These insights offer design principles for the development of innovative supramolecular materials, highlighting the broader potential of three‐dimensional conformational control in advancing supramolecular chemistry.
