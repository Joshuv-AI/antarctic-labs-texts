Research Progress on Pathogenesis, Modern Interventions and Neuromodulatory Mechanisms of Acupuncture for Tinnitus
Authors: Jichao Xu, Wenhui Hu, Yueying Jiang, Weiwei Wang, Ling Li, Qingchang Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTinnitus refers to the subjective abnormal auditory perception occurring without external sound stimuli, characterized by prominent clinical heterogeneity and complicated pathophysiological mechanisms, which severely impair patients’ mental health and quality of life. Adopting a standardized literature retrieval procedure, this paper systematically sorts out the epidemiological characteristics, clinical classifications and diagnostic criteria of tinnitus, and deeply analyzes its dual pathological mechanisms involving peripheral auditory damage (hair cell injury, auditory nerve lesions) and abnormal central nervous system plasticity (auditory cortex remodeling, neurotransmitter imbalance, abnormal activation of the limbic system). It systematically summarizes mainstream Western medical interventions and critically evaluates the clinical efficacy and inherent research limitations of pharmacotherapy, sound therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation and other neuromodulation techniques. Meanwhile, it integrates the traditional theories, neuromodulatory mechanisms and available clinical trial evidence of acupuncture for tinnitus treatment. Existing basic and clinical studies indicate that acupuncture can alleviate tinnitus symptoms and regulate autonomic nervous disorders to a certain extent by improving cochlear microcirculation, upregulating the expression of inhibitory GABA neurotransmitters, and remodeling functional connectivity of the brain’s default mode network. Nevertheless, clinical trials on acupuncture are plagued by remarkable methodological flaws: acupoint prescriptions and acupuncture manipulation protocols are highly heterogeneous, outcome assessment indicators are inconsistent, most studies adopt single-center small-sample designs, long-term follow-up data exceeding one year are scarce, psychological placebo effects cannot be fully isolated, and efficacy reports vary widely across studies. At present, unified high-quality evidence is still lacking to confirm its stable clinical efficacy. As a narrative review, this paper abandons one-sided positive narratives and applies consistent evaluation criteria to balance the comparison of merits and drawbacks of all Chinese and Western therapeutic interventions. It fully discloses the procedures of literature retrieval, screening and synthesis, aiming to provide objective and reproducible theoretical and clinical references for standardized comprehensive diagnosis and treatment of tinnitus as well as interdisciplinary basic mechanistic research.Keywords: tinnitus, pathological mechanism, neural plasticity, therapeutic strategy, acupuncture and moxibustion, neural regulation, central nervous system

Introduction

Tinnitus refers to a subjective auditory perception occurring in the absence of external acoustic stimuli. It is frequently accompanied by anxiety, insomnia and other symptoms, severely impairing patients’ quality of life and evolving into a global public health challenge.1 Its pathophysiological mechanisms are extremely complex. It is widely accepted that tinnitus originates from compensatory dysfunction of the central nervous system triggered by peripheral auditory damage (such as cochlear hair cell injury), including auditory cortex reorganization, neurotransmitter imbalance, and abnormal activation of non-auditory brain regions such as the limbic system.2–4 Owing to the intricate mechanisms, no specific pharmacotherapy is available for clinical management. Current therapeutic strategies mainly include sound therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and neuromodulation techniques such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS).5,6 Against this backdrop, traditional therapies represented by acupuncture have attracted extensive attention due to their multi-target regulatory characteristics. Modern studies demonstrate that acupuncture can relieve tinnitus by improving cochlear microcirculation, modulating neurotransmitters, and remodeling brain functional networks.6–8

Although a certain body of research concerning acupuncture for tinnitus has been accumulated, notable deficiencies remain in the available clinical evidence, which have also been acknowledged by multiple published systematic reviews and clinical guidelines. Existing clinical trials of acupuncture share common limitations: prominent heterogeneity in acupoint prescriptions and acupuncture manipulation protocols, predominance of single-center small-sample designs, inconsistent outcome indicators for efficacy evaluation, and scarcity of long-term follow-up data. These obstacles hinder the formation of consistent and reliable evidence-based conclusions. Meanwhile, most existing reviews either separately elaborate the Western pathological mechanisms of tinnitus or simply summarize the response rates of acupuncture. They fail to systematically connect the complete pathological pathways from the periphery to the central nervous system, lack in-depth interpretation of the mechanisms by which acupuncture regulates neural plasticity, and omit horizontal comparison and integrated analysis of various Chinese and Western interventions. Significant research gaps and imbalanced evidence therefore persist in this field.

In view of the aforementioned unresolved scientific questions and drawbacks in current studies, the present review aims to systematically summarize advances regarding the pathogenesis and treatments of tinnitus, and further dissect the neurophysiological basis and clinical application of acupuncture. This work addresses the fragmentation and superficial mechanistic interpretations in existing literature, highlights the value of integrative analysis in this review, and provides systematic and objective theoretical evidence for constructing standardized comprehensive therapeutic regimens for tinnitus in clinical practice.

Methods

This study is a narrative review. To ensure comprehensive literature retrieval and reproducibility of the work, a standardized protocol for literature search and screening was formulated in advance. Chinese search terms included tinnitus, pathological mechanism, neural plasticity, neuromodulation and acupuncture, auditory cortex; English search terms were tinnitus, pathogenesis, neural plasticity, neuromodulation, acupuncture, auditory cortex. The searched databases covered PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang and VIP, with the retrieval time frame spanning from database establishment to April 2026. Inclusion criteria for literature: (1) Basic experiments, clinical trials, systematic reviews and meta-analyses focusing on the pathogenesis of tinnitus, various modern therapies, as well as the basic mechanisms and clinical efficacy of acupuncture; (2) Publications written in Chinese or English; (3) Complete data and standardized research design. Exclusion criteria: Single case reports, duplicate publications, conference abstracts, fragmented animal experimental studies lacking clinical reference value, and articles irrelevant to the neuromodulation of tinnitus and acupuncture mechanisms. Two researchers independently completed preliminary screening, full-text rescreening and data extraction. Discrepancies were resolved through group discussion to reach a consensus. Ultimately, 61 high-quality Chinese and English relevant papers were included. The existing research evidence was systematically integrated and summarized with critical comments.

Definition and Classification of Tinnitus

Tinnitus is defined as auditory perception without external stimuli, and its classification system can be divided into a variety of subtypes based on etiology, clinical signs and pathological mechanism. According to the diagnostic criteria of Meniere’s disease revised by AAO-HNS in 2015, tinnitus, a core symptom of Meniere’s disease, is often accompanied by vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss, and ear fullness.9 In addition, distinguishing between idiopathic tinnitus (without a clear cause) and secondary tinnitus (eg, vascular malformations and autoimmune diseases) is critical for clinical management. For example, Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) disease, an autoimmune disease, can involve the inner ear and cause tinnitus; its pathological mechanism is related to Th1 lymphocyte-mediated melanocyte attack.10

From the perspective of clinical features, tinnitus can be divided into two types: subjective and objective. Subjective tinnitus accounts for the vast majority of cases and can only be perceived by the patients themselves; objective tinnitus, such as vascular pulsatile tinnitus (PT), can be detected by auscultation or imaging. A prospective study of 11-year-old children showed a prevalence of 28.1% for any spontaneous tinnitus, with 3.1% of clinically significant tinnitus (ie, causing distress to life).11 The study further pointed out that the clinical significance of tinnitus was related to loudness and attack frequency but not to pitch or history length, suggesting that the pathological mechanism of tinnitus may involve central perceptual processing rather than simple peripheral damage.

Epidemiology

The global prevalence of tinnitus fluctuates significantly depending on the definition and study population. A systematic review of 39 studies from to 1980–2015 found that the overall prevalence ranged from 5.1% to 42.7%, with prevalence ranging from 11.9% to 30.3% for studies using the definition of “lasting more than 5 minutes”1 Age and sex are important factors, and most studies have shown that the prevalence of tinnitus increases with age and that the prevalence of tinnitus in men is slightly higher than that in women. For example, a case-control study based on 21,798 patients in Taiwan found that patients with sleep apnea had a significantly higher risk of tinnitus,12 suggesting that sleep disorders may be involved in tinnitus by affecting neurotransmitter balance or vascular regulation.

Attention should be paid to the prevalence of tinnitus in this population. In children, hearing loss caused by platinum-based chemotherapy is often accompanied by tinnitus; however, relevant research data are limited.13 In patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2), tinnitus is closely associated with bilateral vestibular schwannoma, with a mean morbidity age of 18–24 years, and almost all patients have bilateral lesions before the age of 30 years.14 In addition, after COVID-19 infection, approximately 18.6% of healthcare workers developed neuro-otological symptoms, of which tinnitus accounted for 43.5% and was significantly associated with fatigue, anosmia, and dysgeusia,15 suggesting that viral infection may affect the auditory system through inflammatory or vascular mechanisms.

Clinical Signs and Symptoms and Diagnostic Criteria

The clinical signs and symptoms of tinnitus are highly heterogeneous and are often accompanied by hearing loss, vertigo, anxiety, and sleep disorders. Tinnitus in patients with Meniere’s disease is usually low frequency, fluctuating, and associated with endolymphatic hydrops,16 while tinnitus in patients with acoustic neuroma is often unilateral, persistent, and associated with tumor size.17 In terms of diagnostic criteria, the AAO-HNS recommends a combination of history, hearing assessment, and imaging. For unilateral pulsatile tinnitus, vascular malformations such as dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVF), which are prevalent in approximately 18% of patients with pulsatile tinnitus,18 should be excluded.

Auxiliary examinations include audiological evaluation, imaging, and biomarker detection. Pure tone audiometry detects concomitant hearing loss, while the tinnitus match test determines the frequency and loudness of tinnitus.19 High-resolution CT and MRI can identify structural abnormalities such as sigmoid sinus wall defects and vascular rings.20 Biomarkers such as platelet markers (MPV decrease, PLT increase) may be used as auxiliary diagnostic indicators of subjective tinnitus,21 they still need to be verified using large samples. Pathological Mechanism of Tinnitus

The Role of Central Nervous System in Tinnitus

Abnormal plasticity of the central nervous system is key to the maintenance of tinnitus. The reorganization of the auditory cortex is characterized by frequency mapping changes; after noise exposure, the frequency response area of the auditory cortex in guinea pigs is enlarged and is related to tinnitus behavior.2 In addition, activation of the limbic system, such as the amygdala and hippocampus, is involved in tinnitus-related emotional responses; hippocampal volume is reduced in chronic tinnitus and is associated with cognitive decline.22

An imbalance in the neurotransmitter system also contributes to the central mechanism. Attenuation of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) -ergic inhibition is considered to be central; reduced GABA receptor expression in the rat inferior colliculus after salicylic acid treatment results in increased neuronal excitability.3 Meanwhile, hyperactivation of the glutamatergic system may mediate neuroplastic changes through NMDA receptors. For example, D-cycloserine can inhibit noise-induced Arc protein expression in the amygdala, suggesting that NMDA receptor antagonists may have therapeutic potential.23 The pathological mechanisms are illustrated in Figure 2.

Collectively, these interacting peripheral and central mechanisms underlie the generation, amplification, and persistence of tinnitus perception.

Relationship Between Peripheral Auditory System and Tinnitus

Peripheral hearing impairment is a common cause of tinnitus. Cochlear hair cell damage, such as noise exposure or drug ototoxicity, can lead to a reduction in afferent signals, which, in turn, triggers central gain compensation. For example, tinnitus occurs in approximately 67.1% of the children with platinum-induced hearing loss.13 In addition, acoustic neuropathies, such as acoustic neuroma, can cause tinnitus by mechanical compression or abnormal nerve conduction, which occurs in approximately 61.8%.17

Tinnitus can also be caused by middle ear diseases such as perforation of tympanic membrane and abnormal ossicular chain. A study on traumatic ossicular chain injury showed that the postoperative tinnitus improvement rate was approximately 66.7%,24 suggesting that structural repair can alleviate peripheral tinnitus. At the same time, eustachian tube dysfunction such as patency syndrome can lead to respiratory synchronous tinnitus, and tympanic membrane thickening can effectively improve symptoms,25 the mechanism of which may involve the restoration of middle ear pressure regulation.

Neurophysiological Mechanism of Tinnitus

The neurophysiological mechanism of tinnitus involves abnormal neuronal excitability, synchronous discharge, and changes in network connectivity. In animal models, spontaneous firing rates of inferior collicular neurons increased after noise exposure and were correlated with tinnitus behavior.26 Human studies have shown that auditory cortex alpha wave activity is abnormal in patients with tinnitus and is correlated with tinnitus loudness.27 In addition, disturbances in neural oscillations, such as enhanced gamma-wave synchrony, may be involved in the perceptual processing of tinnitus.28

Temporal dynamics of neural plasticity have also been studied, and after acute noise exposure, the expression of the Arc protein in the rat auditory cortex increased rapidly, suggesting early synaptic plasticity.23 In patients with chronic tinnitus, the default mode network has enhanced functional connectivity with the auditory network, which may reflect long-term neural adaptation.4 These findings provide targets for neuromodulatory therapies such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) to alleviate tinnitus by modulating cortical excitability.29

Treatment Strategy

Drug therapy is mainly aimed at the accompanying symptoms or the underlying causes of tinnitus. For Menier-related tinnitus, diuretics, such as hydrochlorothiazide, can reduce endolymphatic hydrops and improve vertigo symptoms in approximately 79% of patients, but the effect of hearing and tinnitus improvement is limited.30 Antidepressants, such as triptans, relieve migraine-associated tinnitus with improvement in approximately 68.8% of patients.31 In addition, EGb761, an extract of Ginkgo biloba leaves, alleviated noise-induced tinnitus, and 7/9 tinnitus rats were completely relieved,32 its clinical efficacy still needs to be verified in large samples.

Drugs targeting the neurotransmitter system, such as the NMDA receptor antagonist D-cycloserine, can inhibit noise-induced amygdala Arc protein expression,23 suggesting that it may alleviate tinnitus by modulating neuroplasticity. However, there are no FDA-approved drugs for tinnitus and most drug treatments are still in the experimental stage.

Sound therapy is the core method of tinnitus management and includes masking therapy, acclimatization therapy, and personalized sound therapy. Masking therapy masks tinnitus using external sounds, and symptoms improve in approximately 50% of patients.33 Acclimatization therapy, such as tinnitus retraining therapy (TRT), combined with counseling and low-level sound stimulation, can help patients adapt to tinnitus, with a long-term effectiveness rate of approximately 60%.5 Personalized sound therapy, such as notch music therapy, can be tailored to tinnitus frequency and has been shown to reduce tinnitus loudness.34

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) can significantly improve tinnitus by changing the cognitive and coping styles of patients with tinnitus. A systematic review has shown that CBT can reduce the tinnitus handicap inventory (THI) score by approximately 15 points,5 and the effect can last for more than 6 months. In addition, Internet-based CBT can improve treatment accessibility, and its efficacy is comparable to that of traditional CBT.35

Neuromodulation technology relieves tinnitus by regulating the central nervous system activity. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the left temporoparietal lobe reduces tinnitus loudness and improves symptoms in approximately 44.7% of patients.6 Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) combined with auditory cortical stimulation enhances efficacy, and the effect of multi-site stimulation is better than that of single-site stimulation.36 In addition, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) combined with notch music therapy significantly improved tinnitus, with 50% of patients experiencing symptom relief.37

Invasive neuromodulation, such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) targeting the inferior colliculus, can inhibit tinnitus-related neuronal activity, and animal models have shown that it can reverse noise-induced tinnitus behavior.38 However, the risk of invasive treatment is high, and it is only suitable for patients with refractory tinnitus (Figure 3).

Basic Theory of Acupuncture and Moxibustion Treatment of Tinnitus

As an important part of traditional Chinese medicine, the history of acupuncture can be traced back to the Neolithic Age, when people used stone needles to stimulate acupoints to relieve pain. Over time, the theory of acupuncture and moxibustion was gradually systematized. Meridian and acupoint theories were recorded in Huangdi Neijing, which laid a theoretical foundation for acupuncture and moxibustion treatment. In the Tang Dynasty, Sun Simiao’s Qianjin Yaofang described the specific methods of acupuncture treatment for tinnitus in detail, emphasizing the improvement of hearing by regulating the acupoints around the ear. Dacheng of Acupuncture and Moxibustion in the Song Dynasty further summarized the experience of acupuncture and moxibustion in the treatment of tinnitus, and put forward the theory that “the ear is the orifice of the kidney,” which holds that tinnitus is related to kidney deficiency and needs to be treated by tonifying the kidney acupoints. During the Ming and Qing Dynasties, the methods of acupuncture and moxibustion for tinnitus were more diversified, and a variety of acupuncture techniques and acupoint combinations appeared. Modern research shows that the historical evolution of acupuncture and moxibustion treatment for tinnitus reflects a deeper understanding of the etiology and pathogenesis of tinnitus in traditional Chinese medicine, from the initial empirical treatment to systematic treatment based on the meridian theory.39,40

In recent years, with the development of modern medicine, research on acupuncture and moxibustion for the treatment of tinnitus has gradually increased. For example, a quality assessment study of 3417 TCM case reports found that case reports of acupuncture for tinnitus had problems of poor reporting quality, such as unclear nature of the study and lack of abstracts.41 This suggests that, in future studies, it will be necessary to strengthen the standardized reporting of acupuncture treatment for tinnitus and improve the quality of research. In addition, as an important part of acupuncture and moxibustion, auricular therapy can be traced back to 2500 years. Initially, it was mainly used for bloodletting and cauterization. It was not until 1957 that the international scientific community recognized that the distribution of auricular points was similar to that of inverted fetuses, which promoted the systematic application of auricular therapy.39 This discovery provides a new idea for acupuncture treatment of tinnitus, which can improve tinnitus symptoms by stimulating the auricular acupoints to regulate systemic function.

Traditional Chinese medicine believes that tinnitus is related to many factors, including kidney deficiency, liver fire disturbance, phlegm-fire stagnation, qi stagnation, and blood stasis. The Yellow Emperor’s Canon of Internal Medicine points out that “the kidney opens in the ear,” and kidney deficiency is one of the main causes of tinnitus. Metabolomic analysis of modern research found that there were significant changes in the serum metabolic profile of tinnitus patients with kidney deficiency, which showed that glutamate, serotonin, orotic acid, and 8-oxoguanine were upregulated and taurine was downregulated. These metabolites are closely related to the calcium signaling pathway, GABA receptor signaling pathway, and purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis.42 This study provides a modern biological basis for the pathological mechanism of tinnitus in kidney deficiency type in traditional Chinese medicine.

Liver fire disturbances are also a common cause of tinnitus. Traditional Chinese medicine believes that emotional disorders and liver-qi stagnation can lead to liver-fire inflammation, disturb the clear orifices along the meridians, and cause tinnitus. A clinical study on liver-fire type tinnitus found that twirling and reducing acupuncture at Tinggong, Tinghui, Yifeng, and other acupoints could significantly reduce the severity of tinnitus and tinnitus handicap inventory (THI) scores, with an effective rate of 71.4%, which was better than physical therapy.43 This indicates that the treatment effect of tinnitus can be improved by using the corresponding acupuncture manipulation according to the different syndromes. In addition, phlegm-fire stagnation type tinnitus is mostly related to improper diet and endogenous phlegm-dampness, so it is necessary to resolve phlegm and reduce fire for treatment, and Fenglong, Neiting and other acupoints are commonly used. Tinnitus of qi stagnation and blood stasis type is related to the poor circulation of qi and blood, which needs to promote blood circulation and remove blood stasis, and use Xuehai, Geshu and other acupointsm44 (Figure 4).

Pathological Mechanism of Acupuncture and Moxibustion in Treating Tinnitus

The pathophysiology of tinnitus is complex and involves abnormalities of the peripheral and central nervous systems. Peripheral mechanisms include cochlear damage, hair cell dysfunction, and auditory neuropathy. For example, noise exposure can cause damage to cochlear hair cells, trigger abnormal nerve impulses, and produce tinnitus.26 The central mechanisms include auditory cortex remodeling, neural synchronization abnormalities, and limbic system activation. One study found functional connectivity abnormalities in the auditory cortex of patients with tinnitus, specifically altered connectivity in the default mode network and salience network.45 In addition, tinnitus is related to autonomic nervous system imbalance, inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and other factors.46

Acupuncture and moxibustion can affect the pathological mechanisms of tinnitus in many ways. First, acupuncture can improve local blood circulation, increase cochlear blood flow, and alleviate cochlear ischemia and hypoxia. An infrared thermography study found that acupuncture at TE3 and TE5 significantly reduced the bilateral ear temperature differences, suggesting improved cochlear circulation.7 Second, acupuncture modulates neurotransmitter release by increasing GABA levels and inhibiting hyperexcitable neural activity.42 In addition, acupuncture can inhibit the inflammatory response, reduce the level of pro-inflammatory factors, and protect cochlear hair cells.47

The neuromodulatory mechanism of acupuncture for tinnitus mainly includes the regulation of the central nervous system and balance of the autonomic nervous system. One fMRI study found that acupuncture reduced functional connectivity in the amygdala, particularly in the prefrontal and temporal lobes, in patients with tinnitus, thereby reducing tinnitus-related emotional responses.8 Another study found that acupuncture reduced the temporal variability of dynamic functional connectivity in patients with chronic tinnitus, especially the connectivity between the posterior cingulate gyrus and the angular gyrus, and improved the stability of brain networks.48 In addition, acupuncture modulates the autonomic nervous system to improve tinnitus symptoms by affecting heart rate variability.49

Clinical Practice of Acupuncture and Moxibustion in Treating Tinnitus

Clinical trials investigating acupuncture for tinnitus adopt diverse designs, including randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and case reports. A single-blind, sham-controlled randomized controlled trial involving 57 patients with chronic idiopathic tinnitus demonstrated that true acupuncture significantly improved Tinnitus Handicap Inventory (THI) scores.50 Another randomized controlled trial enrolling 101 patients with tinnitus due to flaming-up of liver fire compared the therapeutic effects of twisting reducing manipulation, needle retention, and physical therapy, and revealed that twisting reducing manipulation achieved the highest response rate.43 In addition, several trials adopted multi-arm designs to compare the efficacy of different acupuncture modalities, such as manual acupuncture, periauricular electroacupuncture, and distal electroacupuncture.51

The therapeutic efficacy of acupuncture for tinnitus varies depending on intervention modalities, syndrome patterns, patient populations and other factors. A Bayesian analysis comparing eight acupuncture-related therapies identified moxibustion combined with acupuncture as the most effective approach, followed by moxibustion plus electroacupuncture.52 Another systematic review and meta-analysis reported that acupuncture combined with Western medicine yielded a significantly higher effective rate than Western medicine alone for sudden sensorineural hearing loss accompanied by tinnitus.53 Moreover, differences exist in efficacy among different acupuncture manipulations; for instance, deep needling at periauricular acupoints produces superior outcomes compared with superficial needling.54

Individualized strategies for acupuncture treatment of tinnitus encompass treatment based on syndrome differentiation, disease differentiation, and body constitution differentiation. Syndrome differentiation guides the selection of acupoints and manipulations according to patients’ syndrome patterns: for example, Shenshu (BL23) and Taixi (KI3) are selected for tinnitus induced by kidney deficiency, while Taichong (LR3) and Xingjian (LR2) are adopted for tinnitus caused by liver fire disturbance.44 Treatment based on disease differentiation targets distinct subtypes of tinnitus; cervical tinnitus, for example, can be treated with acupuncture guided by the tendon theory.55 Treatment based on body constitution differentiation adjusts regimens according to individual constitutions: reinforcing manipulations are applied for patients with deficient constitution, whereas reducing manipulations are used for those with excessive constitution.56 Furthermore, combined adjuvant measures including psychological intervention and lifestyle modification can further enhance therapeutic outcomes.57

Controversies Over Acupuncture in Tinnitus Treatment

Although the aforementioned studies indicate the potential value of acupuncture, a critical appraisal of the supporting evidence base is warranted, with four major limitations summarized as follows. First, insufficient level of evidence. Most existing studies on acupuncture for tinnitus are small-sample, single-center trials, and large-scale confirmatory investigations remain scarce. According to the AAO-HNS Clinical Practice Guideline (2020) for tinnitus and multiple systematic reviews, the evidence grade for acupuncture is generally rated Level C (low quality) or Level D (very low quality).58 This stands in sharp contrast to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which holds Level A evidence: multiple large-scale meta-analyses have consistently demonstrated that CBT reduces Tinnitus Handicap Inventory (THI) scores by more than 15 points.5 Acupuncture researchers should acknowledge this disparity in evidence hierarchies, refrain from overstating “effectiveness”, and frame acupuncture as an investigational complementary therapy. Second, methodological dilemmas associated with sham acupuncture controls. The greatest methodological challenge in evaluating acupuncture efficacy lies in the infeasibility of true double-blinding. The sham acupuncture adopted in most trials (eg, superficial needling at non-acupoints or placebo needle shafts) is not an inert placebo. Skin penetration itself can activate deep tissue receptors and trigger endogenous opioid release and non-specific physiological responses.50 Consequently, when sham acupuncture controls are deployed, the between-group differences may be systematically underestimated, raising the risk of false-negative findings. Conversely, without sham acupuncture controls, positive outcomes can hardly be disentangled from placebo effects. Several positive RCTs43,51 lack rigorous sham acupuncture controls or adopt inadequate sample sizes; their observed therapeutic gains may merely reflect non-specific effects from a statistical perspective. Third, substantial heterogeneity of treatment protocols. Unlike modern neuromodulation techniques (eg, rTMS with relatively fixed stimulation targets and frequency parameters), acupuncture research suffers from profound protocol heterogeneity. Acupoint combinations (local points, distal syndrome-differentiated points, or a combination of both), acupuncture manipulations (twisting reducing manipulation, needle retention, electroacupuncture frequency), treatment frequency and course duration vary widely across studies.44,54 Such heterogeneity means that even if network meta-analyses suggest superiority for moxibustion,52 the included trials exhibit substantial clinical baseline imbalance, limiting the reliability of pooled estimates. More importantly, inconsistent protocols prevent the standardized generalization of any single positive finding and severely hinder clinical reproducibility. Fourth, logical leaps in mechanistic research. As outlined above, molecular observations from animal models and fMRI-based brain regional alterations8,23,42 remain exploratory hypotheses and have not been confirmed as direct causal effects of acupuncture. Directly extrapolating microscopic findings from animal experiments to explain clinical responses in human acupuncture trials constitutes a typical logical leap. To date, adequate evidence is lacking to verify whether the brain network modulation induced by acupuncture occurs independently of non-specific effects; relevant conclusions should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

Tinnitus is essentially a dynamic network disorder arising from the interaction between peripheral auditory damage and abnormal central neural plasticity. In terms of treatment, the evidence hierarchy of prevailing contemporary therapies is relatively clear: cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and sound therapy serve as the cornerstones of symptom management (Level A/B evidence), neuromodulation techniques provide alternative options for selected refractory patients (Level B/C evidence), whereas pharmacotherapy lacks sufficient evidence-based support at present. As a representative modality of traditional medicine, acupuncture exhibits theoretical advantages of multi-target regulation and preliminary exploratory clinical value in the comprehensive management of tinnitus. Nevertheless, it is essential to strictly distinguish “promising preliminary clues” from “validated clinical conclusions”. According to rigorous evidence grading from authoritative guidelines including the AAO-HNS, the independent therapeutic effects of acupuncture have not been substantiated by replicable high-quality randomized controlled trials. Its clinical role should currently be positioned as an exploratory adjunct intervention rather than an independent first-line treatment. Future breakthroughs rely on fundamental shifts in research paradigms. Methodologically, more rigorous sham acupuncture-controlled designs and large-scale multicenter validation are required. In terms of standardization, data-driven approaches should be adopted to screen core acupoints and parameters so as to substantially reduce the current high protocol heterogeneity. Regarding outcome evaluation, direct comparisons between acupuncture and modern gold-standard therapies should be conducted, instead of comparisons solely against blank controls. Only by critically acknowledging existing limitations and reconstructing acupuncture research with rigorous evidence-based frameworks can we scientifically define the genuine role of acupuncture within the overall treatment landscape of tinnitus and facilitate the transition from simple combination to organic integration of Chinese and Western medicine.
