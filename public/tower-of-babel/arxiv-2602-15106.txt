Topological superconductivity with emergent vortex lattice in twisted semiconductors
Daniele Guerci, Ahmed Abouelkomsan, Liang Fu

Abstract
The coexistence of superconductivity and fractional quantum anomalous Hall (FQAH) effect has recently been observed in twisted MoTe 2 and theoretically demonstrated in a model of repulsively interacting electrons under an emergent magnetic field arising from the layer pseudospin texture in moiré superlattice Guerci et al. 2025 . Here, we show that this superconducting state is a chiral f f -wave superconductor hosting an array of double vortices, which are induced by the emergent magnetic field with h / e h/e flux quanta per moiré unit cell. This superconducting vortex lattice state is topological and features Chern number − 1 / 2 -1/2 , giving rise to an half-integer thermal Hall conductance. Our theory provides a common mechanism and unified understanding of FQAH and topological superconductivity, with a rich phase diagram controlled by the spatial modulation of the emergent magnetic field.

I
Introduction

Magnetic fields induce a wealth of quantum phenomena in solids. Two paradigmatic examples are: (1) the fractional quantum Hall effect
Tsui et al. 1982
;
Laughlin 1983
, which arises when two-dimensional electron gases are subjected to strong external fields; (2) the Abrikosov vortex lattice in superconductors under a magnetic field

For example, quantum particles moving in a non-coplanar spin texture acquire a real-space Berry phase, equivalent to an emergent, magnetic field that is determined by the skyrmion density
Bruno et al. 2004
. The emergent magnetic field can give rise to flat Chern bands that mimic Landau levels
Paul et al. 2023
.

Indeed, we have demonstrated via numerically exact calculation and analytical theory

In this work, we elucidate the key properties of superconductivity in twisted TMDs and its relation to FCIs.
We establish the off-diagonal long-range order and determine the microscopic superconducting order parameter from exact diagonalization. Due to the presence of strong emergent magnetic field, the superconducting state is a vortex lattice with one vortex per unit cell, as shown in Fig.

We show that this exotic superconducting state, despite its nonperturbative nature arising from repulsive interaction, is smoothly connected to a chiral topological

We further study the coexistence and the relation of superconductivity and the FCI in twisted TMDs.
We identify two regimes sketched in Fig.

Our work highlights a key difference between twisted TMDs and Landau levels, i.e., between systems with emergent and real magnetic fields. While fractional quantum (anomalous) Hall states appear in both settings, superconductivity is found in twisted TMDs but not in the conventional lowest Landau level. As we show, this difference has a fundamental origin. In a two-dimensional electron gas, the presence of Galilean invariance forbids superconductivity under a uniform magnetic field that preserves continuous magnetic translation symmetry. In contrast, the emergent magnetic field in twisted TMDs is spatially modulated, and the broken Galilean invariance allows for superconductivity in the form of a vortex lattice locked to the moiré lattice.

The importance of Galilean invariance in relation to superconductivity under a magnetic field, or its lack thereof, has been missed in the hypothesized scenario of anyon superconductivity

The superconducting vortex lattice state in our microscopic study of twisted TMDs is a topological superconductor hosting a chiral Majorana edge mode, distinct from lattice models of the superconducting state of anyons

Pichler et al. 2025

II
Emergent magnetic field in twisted TMDs

We consider the continuum model of twisted TMDs
Wu et al. 2019
which describes valence band holes around the
K
and
K^{\prime}
valleys experiencing interlayer tunneling and intra-layer moiré potentials.
The Hamiltonian for holes in one valley involves two layers and is given by,

H_{0}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{p}}^{2}}{2m^{*}}\sigma_{0}+{\boldsymbol{J}}({\boldsymbol{r}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+V({\boldsymbol{r}})\sigma_{0},

(1)

where
\{\sigma_{0},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\}
are the identity and Pauli matrices in the layer pseudospin space.
The interlayer tunneling and intralayer moiré potentials are encoded in a spatially modulated Zeeman field
{\boldsymbol{J}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
and a periodic potential
V({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

To understand how the emergent magnetic field arises in twisted TMDs, we focus on the large
J
-limit of the Hamiltonian (
1
), also known as the adiabatic limit
Paul et al. 2023
;
Morales-Durán et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
;
Kolár et al. 2024
;
Li and Wu 2025
;
Onishi et al. 2026
. In such a limit, the layer pseudospin is locally aligned with the Zeeman field giving rise to an effective (one component) Hamiltonian:

\tilde{H}_{0}=\frac{({\boldsymbol{p}}-e{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}))^{2}}{2m^{*}}+\tilde{V}({\boldsymbol{r}}),

(2)

where
{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is a
U(1)
vector potential describing an
emergent
spatially modulated magnetic
B({\boldsymbol{r}})=\nabla\crossproduct{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.
Crucially, in this mapping, the flux enclosed per unit cell is proportional to the skyrmion winding number of the Zeeman field
{\boldsymbol{J}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
. For twisted TMDs, this corresponds to one flux quantum
h/e
per moiré unit cell.

This model Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{0}
supports narrow Chern bands over a wide parameter range
Paul et al. 2023
;
Morales-Durán et al. 2024
.
It reduces to the standard Landau level Hamiltonian in the limit of a uniform emergent magnetic field
B
and a constant scalar potential
\tilde{V}
.
More generally, the magnetic field and the potential are spatially varying with the periodicity of the underlying moiré lattice with lattice vectors
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1/2}
.
In this case, the emergent magnetic field experienced by the electrons takes the form:

B({\boldsymbol{r}})=B_{0}+\delta B(\bm{r}),

(3)

where the constant part of the magnetic field
B_{0}={h/(e|\bm{a}_{1}\times\bm{a}_{2}|)}
is fixed by the condition of one flux quantum
h/e
per unit cell
({\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2})
, and
\delta B({\boldsymbol{r}})
is periodic and has zero average over the unit cell.

III
Emergent vortex lattice

Regardless of microscopic details, we expect that in the presence of an emergent magnetic field, the superconducting state hosts an array of quantized vortices, such that the order parameter has a net vorticity of
2
per unit cell as sketched in Fig.
1
.
Here
2
is the number of superconducting flux quanta per unit cell in twisted TMDs, i.e,
2eB_{0}|\bm{a}_{1}\times\bm{a}_{2}|/h=2
.

The form of superconducting order parameter is constrained by lattice symmetry.
Due to the net magnetic flux, the standard translation operators
T_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}=e^{{\boldsymbol{R}}\cdot\nabla}
are replaced by the magnetic translation operators

M_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}=e^{ie\xi_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})/\hbar}e^{{\boldsymbol{R}}\cdot\nabla},

(4)

where
\xi_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is the magnetic phase determined by the requirement

\nabla\xi_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})={\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{r}})-{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{r}}+{\boldsymbol{R}}),

(5)

and
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is the vector potential of the uniform component of the magnetic field (
B_{0}=\nabla\times{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{r}})
).
Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) are dictated by gauge invariance.
Here,
M_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}}
and
M_{{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}}
commute with each other because of the condition of having one flux quantum per unit cell.

Since the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{0}
(
2
) commutes with the magnetic translation operators
[M_{{\boldsymbol{R}}},\tilde{H}_{0}]=0
, where
{\boldsymbol{R}}=n{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+m{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
is a lattice vector, the single-particle orbitals
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
can be labeled by the magnetic crystal momentum
{\boldsymbol{k}}
, such that
M_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{R}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
, or equivalently,

\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}+{\boldsymbol{R}})=e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{R}}}e^{-ie\xi_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})/\hbar}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}).

(6)

The latter relation implies that the magnetic orbitals
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
are quasiperiodic and that the net phase accumulated by a single-particle wave function upon encircling a unit cell is
\Phi_{e}=\oint d\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot e{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\ell})/\hbar=2\pi
.

Similarly, the superconducting order parameter
\Psi_{\rm pair}
, which describes the wavefunction of charge-
2e
pair, also transforms nontrivially under translations of the center-of-mass (CoM) coordinate
{\boldsymbol{X}}=({\boldsymbol{r}}+{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})/2
by a lattice vector
{\boldsymbol{R}}
.
Specifically,
\Psi_{\rm pair}
with zero CoM momentum transforms as:

\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{r}}+{\boldsymbol{R}},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{R}})=e^{-ie\frac{\xi_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})+\xi_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})}{\hbar}}\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}).

(7)

Importantly, the magnetic phase is twice the one acquired by the single particle wavefunction
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
(
6
), as expected since the Cooper pair carries twice the charge of a single particle.
Correspondingly, the superconducting order parameter acquires a net phase of
4\pi
upon transporting the center of mass of the pair around a single unit cell which has magnetic flux
h/e
:

\Phi_{\rm pair}=\oint d\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot 2e{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\ell})/\hbar=4\pi
.
More generally, in the presence of emergent magnetic field with one flux per unit cell, it follows from gauge invariance and magnetic translation symmetry as described above that the superconducting state is a vortex lattice, with a total vorticity
2N_{s}
on the torus.

IV
Superconductivity Requires Broken Galilean invariance

In the following, we present a general argument demonstrating that broken Galilean invariance is a necessary condition for superconductivity in magnetic fields.
This rules out the possibility of a translationally invariant superconducting state in a uniform magnetic field, but allows for superconducting states in moiré lattices that break continuous translation symmetry.

For a translationally invariant two-dimensional electron system with a parabolic energy dispersion,
the Hamiltonian in a uniform magnetic field takes the general form
H=\sum_{i}({\bm{p}}_{i}-e\bm{A}_{i})^{2}/2m+\sum_{i<j}U({\bm{r}}_{i}-{\bm{r}}_{j})
with
\bm{A}_{i}=(B\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{r}_{i})/2
, where
we assume that the interaction potential
U
only depends on relative particle coordinates. Note that
H
can be re-written in terms of the center-of-mass momentum
\bm{P}=\sum_{i}\bm{p}_{i}
and center-of-mass coordinate
\bm{R}_{0}=\sum_{i}\bm{r}_{i}/N
(
N
is particle number) and the remaining
N-1
relative coordinates and momenta:
H=(\bm{P}-Ne\bm{A})^{2}/2M+...
with
M=Nm
and
\bm{A}=(B\hat{\bm{z}}\times\bm{R}_{0})/2
.

Importantly, the center-of-mass variables
\bm{P}
and
\bm{R}_{0}
do not appear in the interaction term, or couple to other variables
Kohn 1961
. Furthermore, a uniform electric field
\bm{E}
only couples to the center-of-mass coordinate
\bm{R}_{0}
through
-(Ne)\bm{E}\cdot\bm{R}_{0}
, while the total charge current is
\bm{J}=(Ne)\bm{P}/M
.
Thus, this center-of-mass degree of freedom behaves as a single
free
“particle” of mass
M
and charge
Ne
under a perpendicular magnetic field
B
and electric field
\bm{E}
.
It follows from the equation of motion that in the stationary state, the center-of-mass particle moves in the transverse direction with a drift velocity of magnitude
v=E/B
. Therefore, the total current is
\bm{J}=Ne{\boldsymbol{v}}=(Ne/B)(\bm{E}\times\hat{\bm{z}})
. This implies that the DC conductivity is necessarily
\sigma_{xx}=0,\sigma_{xy}=\rho e/B
(
\rho
is particle density), in contrast to a superconductor defined by
\sigma_{xx}\rightarrow\infty,\sigma_{xy}=0
.

This conclusion holds true even in the presence of attractive interaction that leads to electron pairing. In the presence of a uniform magnetic field, charge-
2e
Cooper pairs are dispersionless, which frustrates condensation
Yang and Zhai 2008
;
Barlas and Yang 2011
. Superconductivity can only develop via the formation of a vortex lattice which spontaneously breaks translational symmetry, as in Abrisokov vortex lattice of conventional superconductors under a weak magnetic field. Moreover, a true zero-resistance state is only realized when the vortex lattice is pinned, consistent with our general conclusion that superconductivity in a magnetic field requires broken Galilean invariance.

In twisted TMDs, however, the emergent magnetic field (
3
) is spatially inhomogeneous and preserves only discrete magnetic translational symmetry. In this case, the center-of-mass degree of freedoms
\bm{P}
and
\bm{R}_{0}
are no longer decoupled from the other degrees of freedom, hence our previous derivation of the DC conductivity does not apply.
Importantly, even when the single-particle energy dispersion is completely flat, low-energy charged excitations in these systems are generally
dispersive

Abouelkomsan et al. 2020
;
Abouelkomsan et al. 2023
;
Schleith et al. 2025
;
Gonçalves et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Yan et al. 2025
.
Indeed, the emergent Fermi liquid state in twisted MoTe
2
at
2/3<\nu<1

Reddy and Fu 2023
arises from interaction-induced dispersion for
holes
and persists even in the limit of flat electron band
Guerci et al. 2025
.

XII
Discussion

Our work demonstrates the realization of a chiral
f
-wave superconductor hosting an
emergent
moiré vortex lattice in a purely repulsive electron model of twisted TMDs.
A crucial ingredient for superconductivity is the breaking of Galilean invariance, or continuous magnetic translation symmetry.
This can be achieved in several ways, including emergent inhomogeneous magnetic fields, as in our model, or externally imposed periodic potentials.
Notably, a similar chiral
f
-wave superconductor was recently reported in the lowest Landau level with a periodic potential
Wang and Zaletel 2025
.
In both cases, the superconducting state realizes a vortex lattice with pinned vortex cores, enabling a zero-resistance state and exhibiting nontrivial topological properties.

In our case, a key physical ingredient is the interaction-induced dispersion
Abouelkomsan et al. 2020
;
Abouelkomsan et al. 2023
generated from an otherwise flat Chern band.
This dynamically produces a Fermi surface, from which superconductivity emerges as an instability.
The robustness of the Fermi surface (see Figs.
4
(c)-(d)), together with the sensitivity to boundary conditions, provides conclusive evidence for a weak-pairing topological superconducting phase.

We have presented multiple evidence supporting the existence of superconductivity.
First, the evaluation of the 2RDM reveals in the superconducting regime a dominant eigenvalue at zero center-of-mass momentum that grows with system size, consistent with off-diagonal long-range order.
The associated eigenfunction exhibits chiral
f
-wave symmetry and defines the real-space superconducting order parameter, thereby enabling a direct characterization of the vortex lattice in real space.
The superconducting vortex lattice emerges from purely repulsive interactions in an inhomogeneous magnetic field carrying
h/e
flux quantum per unit cell.
This should be distinguished with other settings where an initially conventional superconductor becomes topological under an external magnetic field that induces a vortex-lattice structure
Mishmash et al. 2019
;
Chaudhary and MacDonald 2020
;
Shaffer et al. 2021
;
Antonenko et al. 2025a
;
Antonenko et al. 2025b
;
Qi et al. 2010
;
Zocher and Rosenow 2016
;
Kudo et al. 2025
;
Schirmer et al. 2022
;
Schirmer et al. 2024
.

Importantly, the superconducting ground state evolves adiabatically under an attractive interaction with chiral
f
-wave symmetry, implying adiabatic continuity to a chiral topological superconductor.
This adiabatic connection allows us to determine the topological properties of the ground state by mapping the strongly correlated problem onto an effective weakly interacting description within the Bogoliubov–de Gennes formalism.

The Chern number of the superconducting state with absolute value
|C|=1/2
reveals a single Majorana zero mode whose chirality opposes that of the Chern insulator at full filling (
\nu=1
) of the topological flat band.
This contrasts with recent theoretical works that predict an even number of Majorana zero modes
Shi and Senthil 2025a
;
Shi and Senthil 2025b
;
Nosov et al. 2025
;
Kim et al. 2025
;
Pichler et al. 2025
.
Interestingly, in contrast to single vortices in spinless chiral superconductors that can host an isolated Majorana zero mode
Cheng et al. 2009
;
Biswas 2013
;
Liu and Franz 2015
;
Murray and Vafek 2015
;
Gaggioli et al. 2025
, the double vortices found here are expected to have a gapped quasiparticle spectrum in the bulk, consistent with the large pairing gap found in our ED calculation.

Quite generally, our model supports superconductivity over a finite doping window whose extent depends on the modulation strength
\mathcal{K}
. For smaller
\mathcal{K}
, superconductivity appears in the vicinity of the fractional Chern insulator (FCI) at
\nu=2/3
, whereas for larger
\mathcal{K}
the superconducting region expands to include
\nu=2/3
and extends to lower fillings. Our numerics (Figs.
6
and
7
) are consistent with a first-order transition between the FCI and the superconducting phase as the strength of the quantum geometry is tuned.

Remarkably, our findings are consistent with recent experimental observations in
t
MoTe
2
in Ref.
Xu et al. 2025
of an anomalous hole-like Fermi Hall liquid for fillings above
\nu=2/3
and below
\nu=1
, which upon cooling evolves into a superconducting state over an extended filling range between
\nu\simeq 0.7
and
\nu\simeq 0.75
. The superconductor exists in the vicinity of an FCI at
\nu=2/3
, separated by a pronounced peak in the longitudinal resistivity. This is consistent with our findings at moderate magnetic field modulation, but speaks against the scenario of superconductivity arising from doping anyons to the
\nu=2/3
FCI.

We predict that the observed superconductor
Xu et al. 2025
is a chiral
f
-wave superconductor, which, due to the layer skyrmion winding
Wu et al. 2019
;
Morales-Durán et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2024
, features a strongly spatially modulated superconducting density with
h/e
vortices and circulating currents on the moiré lattice.
Since the vortices are strongly pinned at the maxima of the emergent magnetic field, they do not induce dissipation; the superconducting state retains zero resistivity for currents below the depinning threshold.
We propose real-space imaging to resolve the spatial modulation of the superconducting density and to detect Majorana zero modes.

Looking ahead, an interesting future direction is to employ methods that are able to reach larger system sizes
Li et al. 2025a
. Another promising direction is to formulate variational wavefunctions that can simultaneously capture both the FCI and the superconductor
Abouelkomsan et al. 2025
;
Abouelkomsan and Fu 2026
.
Our model provides a concrete starting point to microscopically investigate the proximity effect of fractionalization and superconductivity
Clarke et al. 2013
in a realistic setting.

To conclude, our work demonstrates that the spatial modulation of the emergent magnetic field enables and controls the coexistence of superconductivity and fractionalization in twisted TMDs.
A uniform field induces fractional quantum anomalous Hall states, whereas a superconducting vortex lattice arises when the emergent field forms a periodic array of
h/e
flux tubes, with each vortex carrying twice the vorticity of Abrikosov vortex. This establishes a unified setting and common mechanism for fractional quantum Hall physics and topological superconductivity—phases traditionally regarded as incompatible.

Appendix A
Single particle wavefunction and many-body Hamiltonian

Our model is obtained by projecting the two-body interaction onto the basis of ideal Chern wavefunctions
Wang et al. 2021
, which, in the symmetric gauge,
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{0}({\boldsymbol{r}})=B_{0}(\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\times{\boldsymbol{r}})/2
, take the form

\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,e^{-K({\boldsymbol{r}})}\,\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is a normalization factor,
K({\boldsymbol{r}})
the Kähler potential and
\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
the lowest Landau level on a torus.
These wavefunctions arise as zero-mode solutions of the adiabatic Hamiltonian (
2
) in the so-called Aharonov–Casher limit
Shi et al. 2024
, where the problem reduces to that of a Dirac particle in a spatially modulated magnetic field
Aharonov and Casher 1979
;
Dubrovin and Novikov 1980
;
Dong et al. 2022
.
We consider a magnetic field
B({\boldsymbol{r}})
with maxima on a triangular lattice, as shown in the main text.

The wavefunction satisfies the
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-space boundary conditions:
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{G}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=\eta_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}e^{i\ell^{2}_{B}({\boldsymbol{G}}\times{\boldsymbol{k}})/2}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}),

where
\eta_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}
is the signature of
{\boldsymbol{G}}
(i.e. 1 if
{\boldsymbol{G}}/2
is a reciprocal lattice vector and
-1
otherwise).
Similarly, in real space we have:
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}+{\boldsymbol{R}})=\eta_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{R}}}e^{i\frac{{\boldsymbol{R}}\times{\boldsymbol{r}}}{2\ell^{2}_{B}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}}),
where
{\boldsymbol{R}}=n{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+m{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
are lattice vectors and
\eta_{{\boldsymbol{R}}}
the signature of
{\boldsymbol{R}}
defined below Eq. (
9
).
Moreover, we have employed the symmetric gauge, where the lowest Landau wavefunction
\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
reads:

\begin{split}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})&=\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{z}{t_{1}}-\frac{k}{G_{2}},\omega\right)e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\bm{t}_{1}\,\frac{z}{t_{1}}}\\
&e^{\frac{\pi}{2\imaginary\omega}\left(\frac{z^{2}}{t^{2}_{1}}-\left|\frac{z}{t_{1}}\right|^{2}\right)}e^{\frac{\pi}{2\imaginary\omega}\left(\frac{k^{2}}{G^{2}_{2}}-\left|\frac{\tilde{k}}{G_{2}}\right|^{2}\right)},\end{split}

(2)

where
z=x+iy
,
k=k_{x}+ik_{y}
, and in complex notation
t_{1}=-ia
,
G_{2}=4\pi/\sqrt{3}a
,
t_{2}/t_{1}=\omega
,
\omega=e^{2i\pi/3}
,
G_{2}=it_{1}/\ell^{2}_{B}
and
G_{1}=-\omega G_{2}
. In Eq. (
2
), we have introduced the Jacobi
\vartheta_{1}
function:

\vartheta_{1}(z,\omega)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\pi\omega(n+1/2)^{2}}e^{2i\pi(z-1/2)(n+1/2)}.

(3)

Notice that the single-particle wavefunction satisfies the
C_{6}
symmetry relation
\psi_{C_{6}{\boldsymbol{k}}}(C_{6}{\boldsymbol{r}})=e^{i\pi/3}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

2RDM spectrum (a) and leading eigenfunction (b) for

N_{s}=27
and
N=16
particles.

Due to the complete flatness of the Chern band, the projected Hamiltonian consists only of two-body
V({\boldsymbol{q}})
interactions:

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2A}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}V({{\boldsymbol{q}}}):\bar{\rho}({{\boldsymbol{q}}})\bar{\rho}({-{\boldsymbol{q}}}):,

(4)

where
\bar{\rho}({{\boldsymbol{q}}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\rm BZ}\langle u_{\boldsymbol{k}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\rangle c^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}
is the projected density operator with
c^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
creating a particle at momentum
\bm{k}
in the flat band with wavefunction
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
(
1
),
``:\,:"
denotes normal ordering with respect to the empty band, and
A=|{\boldsymbol{L}}_{1}\times{\boldsymbol{L}}_{2}|
is the area of the system spanned by the vectors
({\boldsymbol{L}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{L}}_{2})
.

Finally, we define the wavefunction overlaps
\Lambda_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{G}}}=\langle u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}|u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{G}}}\rangle
appearing in the projected density operator in Eq. (
4
):

\Lambda_{{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{G}}}=\int_{\rm UC}\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{r}}}{|{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}\times{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}|}e^{-i{\boldsymbol{G}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}u^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{r}}),

(5)

where
{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}\in\rm BZ
and
u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=e^{-i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{r}}}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

Appendix B
Spectrum of
\rho^{(2)}
and condensate wavefunction

To begin, we recall that, following the definition in the main text,
\rho^{(2)}
is positive semidefinite and satisfies
\Tr\rho^{(2)}=N(N-1)
, where
N
denotes the number of particles.

Fig.
S1
(a) shows the spectrum of the 2RDM for
N_{s}=27
and
N=16
particles, corresponding to two-hole doping away from filling
\nu=2/3
.
The spectrum exhibits a dominant eigenvalue in the momentum sector
{\boldsymbol{Q}}=0
, clearly separated from the rest.
The associated eigenfunction
\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})
, shown in Fig.
S1
(b), displays the chiral
f-if
symmetry.

From the 2RDM, we extract the condensate wavefunction corresponding to the largest eigenvalue.
This can be readily obtained starting from the definition
Yang 1962
:

\rho^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{2};{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{2},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{1})=\langle\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{1})\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{2})\hat{\Psi}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1})\hat{\Psi}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{2})\rangle,

(6)

where
\hat{\Psi}({\boldsymbol{r}})
is the Fermi field operator.
Expanding
\hat{\Psi}({\boldsymbol{r}})
in the flat Cher band basis, we find:

\begin{split}\rho^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{2};{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{2},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{1})&=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{k}}_{4}\in\rm BZ}\psi^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{4}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{1})\psi^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{3}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{2})\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1})\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{2})\langle c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{4}}^{\dagger}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{3}}^{\dagger}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2}}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{k}}_{4}\in\rm BZ}\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{1}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1})\chi_{n({\boldsymbol{k}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2})}\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{2}}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{2})\,\psi^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{3}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{2})\chi^{*}_{n({\boldsymbol{k}}_{3},{\boldsymbol{k}}_{4})}\psi^{*}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}_{4}}({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{1})\\
&=\lambda_{0}\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{r}}_{2})\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{2}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}_{1})^{*}+\cdots.\end{split}

(7)

In the final line, we extract the contribution arising from the leading eigenvector, namely the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
. The corresponding wavefunction
\Psi_{\rm pair}
(
12
) describes the pair wavefunction which is macroscopically occupied in the superconducting ground state.

B.1
Momentum space properties of the superconducting order parameter

The leading eigenfunction is odd, symmetric under
C_{3}
rotations:

\chi_{0}(C_{3}{\boldsymbol{k}},-C_{3}{\boldsymbol{k}})=\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}}).

(8)

Moreover, in Chern bands, the pair wavefunction carries a momentum-space Berry flux equal to twice that of the underlying single particle wavefunction.
This implies the momentum-space boundary condition, which, in the symmetric gauge, is given by:

\Delta({\boldsymbol{k}})=e^{i\ell^{2}_{B}({\boldsymbol{G}}\times{\boldsymbol{k}})}\Delta({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{G}}).

(9)

The same boundary condition is satisfied by
\chi_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})
.

As a consequence of Eq. (
9
), there are six unpaired momenta at which the pairing function vanishes, located at
\gamma
,
\kappa
, and
\kappa^{\prime}
, as well as at the three different
m
points.
For
f\!-\!if
pairing, the gap function exhibits a cubic zero at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
, corresponding to angular momentum
-3
. In addition, we find simple zeros at
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
with angular momentum
-1
, and at the three
m
points with angular momentum
+1
.
The total vorticity is therefore
-2
, consistent with the
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-space Berry flux enclosed by the superconducting gap upon transport along the boundary of the Brillouin zone, Eq. (
9
).

B.2
Real-space properties of the pair wavefunction

The pair wavefunction
\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})
depends separately on
{\boldsymbol{r}}
and
{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}
, varying on the moiré length scale. We first discuss its dependence on the relative coordinate
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}
, keeping the center-of-mass coordinate
{\boldsymbol{X}}=({\boldsymbol{r}}+{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})/2
fixed;
the properties of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
are discussed in the next section.

As a function of the relative coordinate
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, at short distances (
{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow 0
), the wavefunction
\Psi_{\rm pair}
vanishes as
\Psi_{\rm pair}\sim z^{3}
, where
z
denotes the holomorphic relative coordinate.
However, an exception occurs at the special points
{\boldsymbol{X}}=n{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+m{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
, where the center of mass lies exactly at the double vortices of the vortex lattice
(Fig.
3
(d)).
In such case, the relative wave function vanishes instead as
z^{5}
.
This enhanced nodal order arises from the combined effect of the microscopic chiral
f\!-\!if
pairing symmetry, which contributes a cubic zero, and the double vortices located at
{\boldsymbol{X}}=n{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+m{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
.

B.3
Vortex Lattice Structure and Center-of-Mass Dependence

To extract the dependence on the center of mass, we “integrate out” the relative coordinate
{\boldsymbol{x}}
:

\psi({\boldsymbol{X}})=\int d^{2}{\boldsymbol{x}}F({\boldsymbol{x}})\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{x}}/2,{\boldsymbol{X}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}/2),

(10)

where
F({\boldsymbol{x}})
is odd
F(-{\boldsymbol{x}})=-F({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
This condition on
F({\boldsymbol{x}})
follow from the antisymmetry of the pair wavefunction,
\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{X}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}/2,{\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{x}}/2)=-\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{x}}/2,{\boldsymbol{X}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}/2)
.
Moreover, we require that
F({\boldsymbol{x}})
reproduce the short-distance
C_{3}
-symmetric properties of
\Psi_{\rm pair}
.
We readily realize that, in the symmetric gauge (
9
),
\psi({\boldsymbol{X}})
satisfies the magnetic boundary conditions:

\psi({\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{R}})=e^{i{\boldsymbol{R}}\times{\boldsymbol{X}}/\ell^{2}_{B}}\psi({\boldsymbol{X}}),

(11)

where
{\boldsymbol{R}}
is a lattice vector.

In the following, we prove that
\psi({\boldsymbol{X}})
hosts a vortex lattice of double vortices pinned at
{\boldsymbol{R}}=n{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+m{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
.
To this aim, we first notice that:

\begin{split}&\Psi_{\rm pair}({\boldsymbol{X}}+C_{3}{\boldsymbol{x}}/2,{\boldsymbol{X}}-C_{3}{\boldsymbol{x}}/2)=\omega^{2}\\
&\Psi_{\rm pair}(C^{-1}_{3}{\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{x}}/2,C^{-1}_{3}{\boldsymbol{X}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}/2),\end{split}

(12)

where in the last step we utilized the
C_{3}
symmetry in Eq. (
8
).
Furthermore, we have used the three-fold rotational symmetry around
z
of the wavefunction
\psi_{C_{3}{\boldsymbol{k}}}(C_{3}{\boldsymbol{r}})=\omega\psi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

From Eq. (
12
), follows that:

\psi(C_{3}{\boldsymbol{X}})=\omega^{2}\psi({\boldsymbol{X}}).

(13)

Consequently, we have
\psi(0)=\omega^{2}\psi(0)
, which immediately implies
\psi(0)=0
.
The combination of flux conservation—requiring two superconducting flux quanta per unit cell—and the symmetry relation (
13
) implies
\psi({\boldsymbol{X}}\to 0)\sim(X+iY)^{2}
.
As a consequence of discrete magnetic translation symmetry, the double vortices form a vortex lattice.

Appendix C
Form factors of the attractive interaction

To establish the adiabatic connection of the chiral
f-if
superconductor with the weak-pairing regime, we explore the evolution of the many-body ground state under the application of an attractive interaction (
13
).

The chirality
m=-
, characterized by the form factor
\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, is constructed to generate two-particle bound states with the same symmetries and boundary conditions of the leading eigenfunction of the 2RDM, namely
\chi_{0({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})}
.
Accordingly,
\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is required to satisfy three conditions. First, it must be invariant under
C_{3}
rotations,
\mathcal{F}_{-}(C_{3}{\boldsymbol{k}})=\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, and odd under inversion
\mathcal{F}_{-}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})=-\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})
. Second, it exhibits small-momentum behavior,
\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})\sim(k_{x}-ik_{y})^{3}
. Finally, it obeys the momentum-space boundary condition inherited from the flat Chern band wavefunction:

\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})=e^{i\ell^{2}_{B}({\boldsymbol{G}}\times{\boldsymbol{k}})}\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{G}}),

(14)

for any reciprocal lattice vector
{\boldsymbol{G}}
.

A function that satisfies these conditions is expressed as follows:

\mathcal{F}^{*}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\varphi({\boldsymbol{k}})\left[\vartheta_{1}\left(\frac{k}{G_{2}},\omega\right)e^{\frac{\pi}{2\imaginary\omega}\left[\left(\frac{k}{G_{2}}\right)^{2}-\left|\frac{k}{G_{2}}\right|\right]}\right]^{2},\quad\varphi({\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}e^{-\ell^{2}_{B}|{\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{G}}|^{2}/2}a(k+G)_{+},

(15)

where
\vartheta_{1}
is the Jacobi Theta function (
3
) and
\varphi({\boldsymbol{k}})
is periodic (
\varphi({\boldsymbol{k}}+{\boldsymbol{G}})=\varphi({\boldsymbol{k}})
) and behaves like
p+ip
for small
{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
Finally, the form factor with opposite chirality is defined as
\mathcal{F}_{+}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\mathcal{F}_{-}^{*}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.

C.1
Adiabatic evolution under
\delta H_{m}

We examine the 2RDM spectrum as a function of pairing strength
g
, with
g=0
as the unperturbed regime. Its evolution depends sensitively on the small-momentum chirality of the form factor,
\mathcal{F}_{\pm}({\boldsymbol{k}})\sim(k_{x}\pm ik_{y})^{3}
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\to 0
, leading to qualitatively distinct behaviors.

For
f\!-\!if

(m=-)
, aligned with the chirality of
\chi_{0({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})}
at
g=0
, the many-body gap grows linearly with
g
, and the leading eigenvalue of the 2RDM increases correspondingly, indicating a progressive increase of the superfluid fraction (Fig.
S2
(a)).
Fig.
S2
(b) and Fig.
S2
(c) show the momentum space structure of the eigenfunction
\chi_{0({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})}
obtained for
g/V_{1}=1
and for
g/V_{1}=10
and chirality
f\!-\!if
.
Increasing the coupling strength
g
does not change the
f\!-\!if
character of the leading eigenfunction; however, it enhances the absolute value of
\chi_{0({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})}
away from the Fermi surface.

Conversely, for
f\!+\!if

(m=+)
, with chirality opposite to that of
\chi_{0({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{k}})}
at
g=0
, the leading eigenvalue shown in Fig.
S2
(d) initially decreases slightly as
g
increases, then exhibits a sharp jump around
g/V_{1}\sim 5
, coinciding with the level crossing in the many-body ground state. Increasing
g
from
g/V_{1}=1
(Fig.
S2
(e)) to
g/V_{1}=10
(Fig.
S2
(f)) drives a change in the chirality of the leading eigenvector from
f\!-\!if
to
f\!+\!if
. This is consistent with a quantum phase transition between two superconducting states of opposite chirality.

C.2
BdG formalism

Figure S3:
Panels (a) and (b) show the pairing gap and the chemical potential at self-consistency as a function of the filling factor

v=V/(\hbar\omega_{c})=-0.2

Exploiting the adiabatic connectivity of our model to electrons with
f\!-\!if
attraction, we compute the Chern number of the superconducting state within the Bogoliubov-de Gennes (BdG) framework.
Without losing generality,
\tilde{H}_{0}({\boldsymbol{r}})
[Eq. (
2
)] realizes one flux quantum per unit cell and an interaction-induced dispersion maximized at
{\boldsymbol{k}}=0

Guerci et al. 2025
. The simplest single-particle Hamiltonian satisfying these requirements reads

\tilde{H}_{0}=\frac{({\boldsymbol{p}}-e{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}}))^{2}}{2m^{*}}+V\sum_{j=1}^{3}\cos(\b_j\cdot\r),

(16)

with
V<0
and uniform magnetic field
B_{0}=\nabla\times{\boldsymbol{A}}({\boldsymbol{r}})
.

For the interacting part, we assume that pairing gap
\Delta({\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})
develops exclusively within the lowest Chern band, corresponding to an effective attraction smaller than the band gap
Akera et al. 1991
;
MacDonald et al. 1992
;
Rasolt and Tesanović 1992
.
This assumption is motivated by our band-projected ED results, which show that superconductivity develops entirely within a single Chern band.
We further take
\Delta({\boldsymbol{r}},{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})
to arise from the attractive interaction with strength
g
in Eq. (
13
) with
f\!-\!if
symmetry, for which we have previously established adiabatic continuity.
The resulting BdG Hamiltonian (
15
) is solved self-consistently, as detailed below.

We begin by projecting
\tilde{H}_{0}
(
16
) onto the Landau-level basis
{|\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}n}\rangle}
.
Measuring energies in unit of
\hbar\omega_{c}
, we obtain:

\tilde{H}_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\mathbf{1}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)+v\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{I({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j})+I({\boldsymbol{k}},-{\boldsymbol{b}}_{j})}{2},

(17)

where
v=V/\hbar\omega_{c}
where the energy is expressed in unit of
\hbar\omega_{c}
, and
H_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is a matrix in the Landau level index
n
.
The matrix elements
I_{nn^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{G}})
is obtained employing magnetic translation algebra:

I_{nn^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{G}})=\matrixelement{\phi_{\k n}}{e^{-i\G\cdot\r}}{\phi_{\k n'}}=\eta_{{\boldsymbol{G}}}e^{i\ell^{2}_{B}({\boldsymbol{k}}\times{\boldsymbol{G}})}e^{-\ell^{2}_{B}|{\boldsymbol{G}}|^{2}/4}J_{nn^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\ell_{B}G_{+}}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{\ell_{B}G_{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(18)

where

\begin{split}&J_{nn^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\ell_{B}G_{+}}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{\ell_{B}G_{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{n!}{n^{\prime}!}}\left(-\frac{\ell_{B}G_{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{n^{\prime}-n}L_{n}^{(n^{\prime}-n)}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}_{B}|{\boldsymbol{G}}|^{2}}{2}\right),\quad\text{if}\,n^{\prime}\geq n,\\
&J_{nn^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\ell_{B}G_{+}}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{\ell_{B}G_{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{n^{\prime}!}{n!}}\left(\frac{\ell_{B}G_{+}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{n-n^{\prime}}L_{n^{\prime}}^{(n-n^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}_{B}|{\boldsymbol{G}}|^{2}}{2}\right),\quad\text{if}\,n>n^{\prime},\end{split}

(19)

where
L^{(n)}_{m}(x)
is the generalized Laguerre polynomials.
We emphasize that the size of the matrix in Eq. (
17
) scales with the number of Landau levels retained in the calculation,
N_{\rm LL}
. Since we are interested only in the lowest Landau level band, we set
N_{\rm LL}=5
in our calculations.

Within the mean-field approximation, in the basis
\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=(c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}0},\cdots,\,c^{\dagger}_{-{\boldsymbol{k}}0},\cdots)^{T}
with
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}n}
annihilating a fermion in
n
-th Landau level at momentum
{\boldsymbol{k}}
, the BdG Hamiltonian takes the form:

\hat{H}_{\rm BdG}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\rm BZ}\Phi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\begin{pmatrix}H_{0}({\boldsymbol{k}})-\mu&\Delta({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\Delta({\boldsymbol{k}})^{\dagger}&-H_{0}^{T}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})+\mu\end{pmatrix}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},

(20)

where we have introduced the chemical potential
\mu
and the pairing term:

\Delta({\boldsymbol{k}})_{nn^{\prime}}=-g\Delta\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{n0}.

(21)

In the latter expression, the value of
\mu
and
\Delta
are obtained by solving the self-consistency equations:

\begin{split}&\Delta=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\rm BZ}\mathcal{F}^{*}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}})\langle c_{-{\boldsymbol{k}}0}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}0}\rangle,\\
&N=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\rm BZ}\sum_{n}\langle c^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}n}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}n}\rangle,\end{split}

(22)

where
N
is the particle number. Fig.
S3
(a) and
S3
(b) shows the evolution
\Delta
and
\mu
obtained solving the self-consistency equations (
22
).
We emphasize that increasing the filling factor
\nu
suppresses the pairing strength, since the magnitude of the pairing function on the hole-like Fermi surface scales as the cube of the Fermi momentum,
|\mathcal{F}_{-}({\boldsymbol{k}}_{F})|\propto|k_{F}|^{3}
, with
k_{F}^{2}
proportional to the hole filling,
k_{F}^{2}\propto 1-\nu
(
\nu\leq 1)
.

To obtain the Chern number of the superconducting ground state, we first recall the eigenstates of the BdG Hamiltonian:

|\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}U_{0\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})|\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}0}\rangle,\cdots,V_{0\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})|\phi^{*}_{-{\boldsymbol{k}}n}\rangle,\cdots\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

(23)

The Berry connection is given by:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})=-i\langle\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha}|\partial_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha}\rangle=\mathcal{A}_{\rm LL}({\boldsymbol{k}})Q_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
&-i\sum_{n}\left[U^{*}_{n\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})\partial_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}U_{n\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})+V^{*}_{n\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})\partial_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}V_{n\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right],\end{split}

(24)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\rm LL}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is the Berry connection of Landau levels and we have introduced the quantity:

Q_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{n}\left[|U_{n\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})|^{2}-|V_{n\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})|^{2}\right].

(25)

Q_{\alpha}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is constant away from zero energy, taking the value
+1
for electron-like bands and
-1
for hole-like ones. Finally, the BdG Chern number
C
is given by:

2C=\sum_{\alpha}^{E_{\alpha}<0}C_{\alpha}+N_{\rm LL},

(26)

where
N_{\rm LL}
is the number of Landau levels included in the calculation and the sum extends to the BdG states with negative energy.
This definition guarantees the Chern number is measured with respect to the vacuum which is topologically trivial.
Moreover, the factor
2
in Eq. (
26
) reflects that, in our convention, the Chern number counts complex fermions and, therefore, each Majorana mode corresponds to a half-integer contribution.
