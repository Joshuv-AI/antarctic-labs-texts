Abstract
. Discretizing raw features into bucketized attribute representations is a popular step before sharing a dataset. It is, however, evident that this step can cause significant bias in data and amplify unfairness in downstream tasks. In this paper, we address this issue by introducing the unbiased binning problem that, given an attribute to bucketize, finds its closest discretization to equal-size binning that satisfies group parity across different buckets. Defining a small set of boundary candidates, we prove that unbiased binning must select its boundaries from this set. We then develop an efficient dynamic programming algorithm on top of the boundary candidates to solve the unbiased binning problem. Finding an unbiased binning may sometimes result in a high price of fairness, or it may not even exist, especially when group values follow different distributions. Considering that a small bias in the group ratios may be tolerable in such settings, we introduce the ε \varepsilon -biased binning problem that bounds the group disparities across buckets to a small value ε \varepsilon . We first develop a dynamic programming solution 𝖣𝖯 \mathsf{DP} that finds the optimal binning in quadratic time. The 𝖣𝖯 \mathsf{DP} algorithm, while polynomial, does not scale to very large settings. Therefore, we propose a practically scalable algorithm, based on local search ( 𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{LS} ), for ε \varepsilon -biased binning. The key component of the 𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{LS} algorithm is a divide-and-conquer ( 𝖣 & 𝖢 \mathsf{D\&C} ) algorithm that finds a near-optimal solution for the problem in near-linear time. We prove that 𝖣 & 𝖢 \mathsf{D\&C} finds a valid solution for the problem unless none exists. The 𝖫𝖲 \mathsf{LS} algorithm then initiates a local search, using the 𝖣 & 𝖢 \mathsf{D\&C} solution as the upper bound, to find the optimal solution. In addition to theoretical analysis, we perform extensive experiments using real-world and synthetic datasets to validate our problem and evaluate our algorithms. Besides confirming the efficiency of the algorithms, our experiments verify that while fairness-unaware binning can generate biased attribute representations, this bias can be significantly reduced at a negligible price of fairness.



1.
Introduction

For various reasons such as augmenting privacy preservation and minimizing the impact of noise
(
Mironchyk and Tchistiakov 2017
)
, attribute binning (also known as bucketization) is widely applied in domains such as credit scoring
(
Oliveira et al. 2008
;
Zeng 2014
)
, where the raw attribute values are partitioned into discrete intervals, known as bins or buckets
1
1

1

We use the terms “bin” and “bucket” interchangeably.
, each representing a category of values.
The original attribute values that fall into a given interval are replaced by the bin value.
Equal-size (aka. equal-frequency) binning, in particular, ensures an equal number of tuples fall inside each bucket
(
Kotsiantis and Kanellopoulos 2006
;
Boulle 2005
)
.

Despite its conceptual simplicity and computational appeal, the process of attribute bucketization frequently induces unintended biases, thereby amplifying preexisting disparities embedded within the dataset.
To further clarify this issue, let us present the following motivation example.

Example 0.

Consider a credit scoring application that stratifies individuals based on income.
Income disparities across different demographic groups, when combined with binning, may inadvertently cluster marginalized populations predominantly into lower-tier buckets.
Such biased attributes can propagate biases throughout the data analytics pipeline, further exacerbating unfairnesses within downstream decisions.

This issue becomes critically important in sensitive domains such as finance, healthcare, employment screening, and criminal justice, wherein biased data transformations can yield discriminatory and inequitable outcomes
(
Angwin et al. 2022
;
Barocas and Selbst 2016
)
. For example, diagnostic healthcare models trained on bucketized attributes exhibiting demographic bias might disproportionately recommend preventive care measures to certain populations while neglecting others
(
Obermeyer et al. 2019
)
. Similarly, credit scoring systems employing improperly bucketized financial attributes may lead to unfair loan application denials, particularly disadvantaging marginalized communities
(
Hurley and Adebayo 2016
)
.

Conversely, despite its substantial implications, this problem has been overlooked by the literature, while prior research has predominantly concentrated on training fair models using potentially biased attributes.

Therefore, towards filling this gap, in this paper we introduce the
unbiased binning
problem, wherein the goal is to ensure demographic group parity across buckets, while minimally deviating from equal-size bucketization.
In order to efficiently solve the problem, we define “boundary candidates”, a small set of possible boundaries, and prove that the optimal unbiased binning must draw the bucket boundaries only from this set. We develop an efficient dynamic programming approach on top of the boundary candidates that finds the optimal unbiased binning.

Due to several reasons, such as distribution mean differences between various groups, unbiased binning may cause a high price of fairness, significantly deviating from the equal-size binning, and sometimes, an unbiased binning may not even exist.
On the other hand, a small amount of bias might be tolerable in binning in such settings.
Therefore, we next introduce the problem of
\varepsilon
-biased binning
that bounds the group-disparity between the buckets to
\varepsilon
.
Our proposed solution for unbiased binning is not applicable for
\varepsilon
-biased binning, since the concept of boundary candidates does not extend to the new setting.
To address this issue, we introduce a matrix that specifies the set of possible
\varepsilon
-biased buckets, and use it to extend our dynamic programming (
\mathsf{DP}
) approach for
\varepsilon
-biased binning.
Our DP solution, however, is not scalable to very large settings since it has a quadratic time complexity.

3.
Unbiased Binning Algorithm

There are
n\choose k
possible ways to partition an attribute
x
into
k
buckets.
Checking if a
k
-binning is unbiased and computing the objective value takes
O(n)
time.
As a result, the brute-force approach for solving Problem
1
has a time complexity of
O\big(n{n\choose k}\big)
.
Alternatively, one can model the problem as an Integer Programming (IP) problem and use an existing solver to solve the problem. This approach also is not practical, since IP
\in
NP
-complete.

Therefore, in this section, we instead develop an efficient algorithm for the unbiased binning problem (Problem
1
).
Our algorithm has two sequential steps, where in step 1, a set of “boundary candidates” is identified. In step 2, we use the boundary candidates and develop a dynamic programming (DP) algorithm that quickly finds the optimal unbiased binning.
Without loss of generality, to simplify the explanations, in the following, we first consider the binary-group cases (e.g.,
male/female
).
We then extend our approach to non-binary grouping in Section
3.2.3
.

6.
Related Work

The increasing deployment of data-driven systems in socially impactful domains has led to a surge of research on algorithmic fairness. Seminal studies have shown that ML models often inherit or amplify biases present in training data, resulting in disparate impacts across demographic groups
(
Barocas and Selbst 2016
;
Barocas et al. 2023
;
Mehrabi et al. 2021
)
. While the major focus of the literature has been on fairness at the model level, there is a growing understanding that upstream data management operations can be a critical source of bias
(
Jagadish et al. 2022
)
.

In response, the research under the umbrella of responsible data management
(
Stoyanovich et al. 2020
)
has been dedicated to identifying and mitigating bias across the data analytics pipeline.
Some of the work in this area includes causal data repair techniques for fairness
(
Salimi et al. 2020
;
Salimi et al. 2019
)
,
identifying and tuning problematic data slices
(
Tae and Whang 2021
)
,
fairness-aware query adjustment
(
Roy et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Shetiya et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Accinelli et al. 2020
)
,
Coverage-based approaches for mitigating representation bias
(
Shahbazi et al. 2023b
)
,
label repair for individual fairness
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
,
mining the minority groups in data
(
Dehghankar and Asudeh 2025
)
,
fair and private data generation
(
Pujol et al. 2023
)
,
and fairness-aware
data imputation
(
Schelter et al. 2021
;
Zhu and Salimi 2024
)
,
entity matching
(
Shahbazi et al. 2023a
;
Shahbazi et al. 2024b
;
Moslemi and Milani 2024
)
,
data cleaning
(
Tae et al. 2019
)
,
data integration
(
Nargesian et al. 2022
;
Chang et al. 2024
)
,
data augmentation
(
Sharma et al. 2020
;
Erfanian et al. 2024
)
,
and many more.
Within the related work, FairHash
(
Shahbazi et al. 2024a
)
shares some high-level similarities with our problem since it aims to find a hash map that guarantees uniform distribution at the group-level across hash buckets. However, despite its high level of similarity, the objective of this paper is completely different from ours: given a set of attributes, FairHash finds a hash function as the combination of the attributes such that an equal-size binning on the hash values satisfies equal-group ratios.
Our problem is different, though: given an attribute where equal-size binning is biased, we want to partition it into unbiased bins.
As a result, the algorithms (based on ranking and necklace splitting) proposed in
(
Shahbazi et al. 2024a
)
cannot be adapted for unbiased binning.

Attribute binning is a ubiquitous data preprocessing step, especially in settings where privacy preservation, interpretability, or noise reduction is desired
(
Mironchyk and Tchistiakov 2017
;
Oliveira et al. 2008
;
Zeng 2014
)
. Techniques such as equal-width and equal-frequency binning have long been standard tools for transforming continuous features into categorical ones
(
Kotsiantis and Kanellopoulos 2006
;
Boulle 2005
)
.
A variety of methods for attribute discretization have been proposed in the literature, with several surveys offering comprehensive overviews
(
Kotsiantis and Kanellopoulos 2006
)
.
Even though improper binning can magnify machine bias in downstream data analytics tasks, to the best of our knowledge, this paper is the
first
to introduce and study the problem of unbiased binning to address such issues.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, underscoring the impact of attribute binning on amplifying bias in data-driven decision systems, we introduced the unbiased binning problem to achieve group-parity across discretized buckets.
Also recognizing practical limitations of unbiased binning, we offered
\varepsilon
-biased binning for settings where a small bias in the group ratios may be tolerable.
We proposed various solutions for our problems and conducted extensive experiments on real-world benchmarks and synthetic datasets to evaluate them.
Besides their theoretical guarantees, our experiments also confirmed the efficiency, effectiveness, and scalability of our algorithms.

As the first paper introducing unbiased binning, we defined our problems based on equal-size binning. Extending the scope of the problem to various attribute discretization techniques, including learning-based approaches, is an interesting direction that we consider for our future work.
