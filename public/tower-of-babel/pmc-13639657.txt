Topochemical synthesis of ternary metal sulfides including new LaAgS2
Authors: Glen R. Hebberd, Toby J. Blundell, Leon Bowen, Stewart J. Clark, Emma E. McCabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ternary sulfides are increasingly sought after for applications as battery materials, photovoltaics and photocatalysts as a result of their small band gaps and good electrical conductivities but their synthesis is often more challenging than that of oxides. We report here the low temperature synthesis of new sulfide LaAgS2 by an anionic redox topochemical reaction, alongside the synthesis of LaCuS2 by mechanochemical topochemical reaction. Our computational work indicates that reaction enthalpies to form LaAgS2, with structure closely related to that of LaCuS2, are relatively small, consistent with our synthetic work. Structural analysis suggests this is due to the larger size of the Ag+ ion compared with Cu+. Diffuse reflectance measurements indicate that both sulfides have band gaps ∼2.1(2) eV.

Introduction

Metal sulfides, often with smaller band gaps and higher electrical conductivities than their oxide analogues, are increasingly sought after for applications in energy storage and conversion, for example as electrodes1 or electrolytes in batteries,2,3 in supercapacitors,1,4 as photovoltaics5,6 and as photocatalysts.7–10 Their synthesis is therefore very topical10–12 and low temperature synthesis routes, which are likely to have lower energy costs and give products with smaller particle sizes,1,2,7 are particularly important.

Sasaki et al. have demonstrated that topochemical reactions involving anionic redox provide an exciting low-temperature route to synthesise both thermodynamically stable phases,13,14 as well as metastable phases.15 Examples include the oxidation and insertion of Cu into La2O2S2 to give the transparent p-type semiconductor LaCuOS.13,14 (LaCuOS is typically prepared by solid state reaction of oxide and sulfide reagents at temperatures ≥1000 °C,16,17 but interestingly, it was first prepared by oxidation of LaCuS2.18) The oxysulfide precursor La2O2S2 is composed of (001) fluorite-like [La2O2]2+ layers separated by layers of (S2)2− dimers. It is these (S2)2− dimers that are reduced by the metal reactant species which cleaves the S–S bonds to form a metal sulfide layer.13,14 The low temperature at which these reactions can be carried out means that structural rearrangements of the (S2)2− – containing precursor are limited, constraining the product to retain some structural features of the precursor. These topochemical anionic redox reactions can also proceed via de-intercalation of S2−, e.g. La2O2S2 + 2A → La2O2S + A2S (A = Na, K, Rb). Importantly, the oA-polymorph of La2O2S formed by this topochemical reaction retains the (001) fluorite-like [La2O2]2+ layers of the precursor and is metastable to the hexagonal hP thermodynamically-stable polymorph.19,20 (oA and hP refer to A-centred orthorhombic and primitive hexagonal unit cells, respectively, using Pearson notation).

In addition to the oxychalcogenides Ln2O2Q2 (Q = S, Se), binary chalcogenides such as BaS2 and BaS3 have also been shown to undergo topochemical anionic redox reactions.21 LaS2, containing (S2)2− dimers, is also an ideal precursor from which to carry out anionic-redox topochemical reactions to target LaMS2 phases (M = monovalent cation). Its structure is composed of rocksalt-like La3+ S2− blocks separated by layers of (S2)2− dimers. The stacking sequence and the orientation of the (S2)2− dimers gives rise to a variety of polymorphs (Fig. 1) including monoclinic (P21/c) α-LaS2 which may be the kinetic product, and orthorhombic (Pnma or Pn21a) β-LaS2 which is thought to be more thermodynamically stable.22–26

Illustration of the crystal structures for (a) α-LaS2, (b) β-LaS2 and (c) γ-LaS2 which does not have obvious (S2)2− dimers. La3+, S2− and (S2)2− sites are shown in green, yellow and orange, respectively. The stacking sequences AAA and ABA are highlighted for the α and β polymorphs.

The ternary lanthanide copper sulfides LnCuS2 are p-type semiconductors with band gaps in the range 2.2–2.5 eV.27,28 Several phases including SmCuS2 and YbCuS2 show interesting magnetic behaviour at low temperatures.29–31 A variety of structures have been reported for this series, all comprising CuS4 tetrahedra, joined by edges, corners, or both, with Ln coordination number decreasing from 7 for larger lanthanides, to 6 for the smaller lanthanides.32–35 The main LnCuS2 structures are summarised in the SI. These materials are typically prepared by high temperature (T ≥ 800 °C) solid state reactions27,34,36–39 and we describe here new low-temperature synthesis routes to prepare LaCuS2 including low-temperature topochemical reactions as well as analogous topochemical reactions carried out by ball milling (“mechanochemical topochemical” or MT reactions).

This success led us to investigate the silver analogue LaAgS2. Several LnAgS2 phases (Ln = Sm–Yb) are known,40–42 and reports of their photocatalytic activity43 will no doubt lead to further investigation of possible synthetic routes.44 LaAgS2, containing the largest lanthanide ion, has not been reported. The structures of LnAgS2 phases are quite different from the LnCuS2 analogues, with the larger Ag+ cation sitting in 5- or 6-coordinate sites.40–42 We report here the successful preparation of LaAgS2 with structure closely related to that of LaCuS2, as a result of the low-temperature topochemical synthesis method.

Methods

Synthetic procedures

Precursors Cu2S and Ag2S were synthesised by solid state reaction of stoichiometric amounts of Cu, Ag (Alfa Aesar, APS 1.3–3.2 micron, 99.9%), and S (Alfa Aesar, −325 mesh, ≥99.5%) at reaction temperatures of 600 °C and 250 °C, respectively, in evacuated, sealed quartz ampoules for 24 hours (see SI).

High temperature synthesis reactions (HT) were carried out by grinding together stoichiometric quantities of La2S3 (Strem Chemicals, 99.9%-La), S (Alfa Aesar, −325 mesh, ≥99.5%), Cu2S, or Ag2S as appropriate. All preparation and grinding steps were carried out in an N2-filled glovebox with sample mixes placed in alumina crucibles in quartz ampules which were evacuated to <10−2 bar, and flame sealed. The reaction ampoules were then heated at 2 °C min−1 to 400 °C, 0.5 °C min−1 to 600 °C, then 1 °C min−1 to the reaction temperature for the high temperature (HT) synthesis route.22,23 HT reaction of La2S3 and S to form LaS2 was carried out at 750–820° C for up to 8 days as reported previously.22,23 The HT reaction of La2S3 and Cu2S to form LaCuS2 was carried out at 900 °C for 3 days. The HT reaction of La2S3 and Ag2S to form LaAgS2 was attempted at 800 °C for 3 days.

Low temperature topochemical (LTT) reactions were also carried out from pre-synthesised LaS2 with Cu or Ag metal in evacuated, sealed quartz ampoules: LTT reaction of LaS2 with Cu to form LaCuS2 was carried out at 400 °C for 2 × 24 hours with grinding between heatings; LTT reaction of LaS2 with Ag to form LaAgS2 was carried out at 300–500 °C for 24 hours.

Mechanochemical topochemical (MT) reactions were also explored, using a Fritsch Pulverisette 6 planetary ball mill fitted with an 80 ml ZrO2 milling jar loaded with 24 × 10 mm ZrO2 milling balls. The reactant powders were placed into the milling jar under an N2 atmosphere, then clamped tightly shut. A total powder mass in the range of 0.7–2.0 g was sufficient for the ball milled reactions and subsequent characterisation steps (giving ball-to-powder ratios between 35 and 105). These mechanochemical reactions were undertaken at 550 rpm for one hour (composed of 5–10 min of milling time followed by 2–3 min pauses). Synthesis of LaS2 by this MT method was carried out with 10 mol% excess of sulfur to account for volatilisation.

Reactions were monitored using X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD), with data collected using a Rigaku Miniflex with Cu Ka radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å, 40 kV, 15 mA) and a D/teX Ultra detector. Rietveld refinements45 were carried out using the TopasAcademic software.46,47 Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) measurements were made using a Jeol 2100F transmission electron microscope operating at 200 keV. The sample was deposited onto a holey carbon grid. This was mounted in a double-tilt sample holder. EDX data were collected with an Oxford Instruments X-Max 65T silicon drift detector.

Crystal growth

Twinned single crystals of LaAgS2 were grown from the LaAgS2 powder prepared by LTT reactions in a CsCl flux (a 1 : 1 molar ratio) within an evacuated, sealed quartz ampule, cooling from 800 °C at a rate of 2 °C hour−1. The flux was subsequently removed by washing with deionised water. Crystals were viewed under an optical microscope fitted with a cross-polariser to identify specimens suitable for single crystal X-ray analysis. The X-ray single crystal data for LaAgS2 were collected at 120.0(2) K using Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) on a Bruker D8Venture diffractometer with a Photon III MM C7 CPAD detector, IμS-III-microsource, focusing mirrors equipped with a Cryostream (Oxford Cryosystems 700+) open-flow nitrogen cryostat. The structure was solved using Olex248 with the ShelXT49 structure solution program using Intrinsic Phasing and refined with Olex2.refine50 refinement package using Least Squares minimization on F2. All atoms were refined with anisotropic atomic displacement parameters. Crystal data and parameters of refinement are listed in Table 1. Crystallographic data for the structure have been deposited with the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre with deposition number CCDC 2580779.

Electronic structure calculations

Electronic structure calculations were performed using ab initio density functional theory (DFT) with the plane-wave pseudopotential method implemented in the CASTEP code.51 The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)52 exchange–correlation functional with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 1150 eV was used. The k-point spacing was decided by a Monkhorst–Pack grid53 to give convergence of the total energy to a tolerance of ≤1 meV per atom. Geometry optimisations were converged to tolerances of: total energy (2 × 10−5 eV per atom), maximum ionic force (5 × 10−2 eV Å−1), maximum ionic displacement (1 × 10−3 Å), and maximum stress component (0.1 GPa). Calculations for LaAgS2 were carried out assuming a fully ordered model with Ag+ ions fully occupying a single 4e site.

Results

Lanthanum disulfide LaS2

The layered precursor LaS2 was synthesised by a high temperature solid state reaction as described previously.22,23 Reaction at 750 °C gave a mix of ∼32.7(4)% by weight of α-LaS2, 27.9(5)% by weight of β-LaS2 and unreacted sulfur (see SI). LaS2 was also prepared by ball milling La2S3 and S (in N2(g) atmosphere) to give a sample of LaS2 (probably a mix of α and β polymorphs) and a trace of La3S4 (Fig. 2a). Annealing this sample at 800 °C gave a sample with some sharper diffraction peaks, Fig. 2b, which was fitted by a stoichiometric phase of α-LaS2 with some broadening of hk0 reflections.54

X-ray wavelength is Cu Kα, 1.5418 Å.

Lanthanum copper disulfide LaCuS2

High temperature solid state reactions can be used to synthesise LaCuS236,37,55 and our attempts at high temperature synthesis produced the desired phase in the presence of La2S3 and other impurity phases (see SI). We were also able to synthesise LaCuS2 by mechanochemical topochemical (MT) reaction: LaS2(s) (prepared by ball milling, see above) was ball milled with Cu(s) in a N2(g) atmosphere to produce LaCuS2(s) (∼98% by weight) with a trace of Cu(s) also detected by XRPD (Fig. 3a). Further work to optimise the milling process may lead to improved sample purity, although we note that the purity of the LaS2 precursor (and exposure to air, e.g. during transfers in and out of the glovebox) may have limited our MT LaCuS2 sample purity. Similarly, a lower purity sample of LaCuS2 was also prepared by a low temperature topochemical (LTT) reaction: LaS2(s) was reacted with Cu(s) (see Methods section) to produce LaCuS2(s) (∼70% by weight) with some unreacted α-LaS2 and Cu(s) (Fig. 3b). Pelletising the reaction mixture, and further heating may improve sample purity. The model refined for LaCuS2 was consistent with the monoclinic (P21/c) structure reported by Strobel et al.38 and Julien-Pouzol and Guittard.55

Diffuse reflectance measurements on LaCuS2 prepared by MT reaction showed an increase in absorption for energy E ≥ ∼2 eV and a Tauc plot56 (Fig. 4a), assuming a direct band gap,57 indicated an optical band gap of ∼2.1(2) eV, consistent with its dark red-brown colour and similar to values measured for La1−xYxCuS2.58

Tauc plots from diffuse reflectance measurements for (a) LaCuS2 and (b) LaAgS2 giving estimated optical direct band gaps of ∼2.1(2) eV for both materials.

Lanthanum silver disulfide LaAgS2

To the best of our knowledge, LaAgS2 has not yet been reported, and our attempts to synthesise LaAgS2 by high temperature reaction between La2S3 and Ag2S were not very successful leading to multiphase samples (see SI). We therefore attempted LTT reactions to synthesise LaAgS2: XRPD data (Fig. 5a) indicated that a phase with a similar unit cell to LaCuS2 was observed for reactions between LaS2 and Ag at 300–500 °C. The relatively low reaction temperature results in samples that are not well annealed and have broad peaks in the XRPD data. The amount of unreacted Ag in the reaction mixture (with peak in XRPD data at ∼37° 2θ) decreases with increasing reaction temperature, but otherwise the XRPD patterns change very little with increasing reaction temperature. EDX analysis (SI) indicate a fairly homogeneous distribution of La, Ag and S (close to the expected ratios) for much of the sample, but with some particles of unreacted Ag. Introducing further heating steps or longer reaction times, and pelletising the reaction mixture, may improve sample purity, as long as care is taken to prevent air-exposure of the reagents. Rietveld refinement (Fig. 5b) using these data was consistent with the monoclinic (P21/c) structure reported for LaCuS2 by Strobel et al.38 and Julien-Pouzol and Guittard.55

The low reaction temperature is not sufficient to anneal the sample and so peaks in XRPD data are broadened (due to the small particle size), and these data are not suitable to confirm the crystal structure of this new material. Therefore single crystals of LaAgS2, in the form of black platelets, were grown from LaAgS2 powder prepared by LTT reactions in a CsCl flux, similar to the crystal growth reported for LaCuS2.38 These crystals were found to be air-stable up to at least 800 °C. Analysis of single crystal XRD data revealed the crystal to be a three-component twin, but a structural model (Fig. 6 and Table 1, with atomic displacement parameters constrained to be equal for the two partially-occupied Ag sites to reduce correlations with site occupancies) was determined and refined. (It was found to be consistent with that refined from XRPD data.) This single crystal XRD analysis allowed the crystal structure to be investigated more carefully which suggested some disorder of the Ag+ cations over two closely spaced 4e sites.

Illustration of crystal structures of (a) LaCuS2 and (b) LaAgS2 prepared here; La, Cu, Ag(1), Ag(2) and S sites are shown in green, blue, black, grey and yellow, with LaS7, CuS4 and AgS5 polyhedra are shown in green, blue, and grey, respectively.

Diffuse reflectance measurements on LaAgS2 prepared by LTT reaction showed a similar spectrum to LaCuS2 and again, a Tauc plot56 (Fig. 4b) assuming a direct band gap57 indicated an optical band gap of ∼2.1(2) eV.

Electronic structure calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) was used to calculate formation energies for LaCuS2 and LaAgS2 from both binary sulfide reagents (reaction 1, akin to a high temperature reaction route), and from LaS2 and the metal (reaction 2, akin to a topochemical reaction involving anionic redox), Fig. 7. These calculations suggest that LaCuS2 is the thermodynamic product, consistent with its synthesis by high temperature reaction, and by low-temperature and mechanochemical topochemical reactions (Fig. 3). Reactions 1 and 2 to form LaAgS2 are only marginally enthalpically favourable, reaction 2 slightly more so, consistent with the greater success of our topochemical synthesis reactions (akin to reaction 2) compared with the high temperature reaction (more akin to reaction 1). The greater challenge in preparing this monoclinic phase of LaAgS2 compared with the copper analogue presumably results from the difficulty of introducing the relatively large Ag+ ion into a small site; indeed the only other ternary La–Ag–S phase reported,35 AgLa5S8, has Ag+ and La3+ ions disordered over 8-coordinate sites (with Ag–S bond distances of ∼3.01 Å).59 This results in LaAgS2 being less stable than LaCuS2 (calculated ΔHf = −5.4 eV for LaAgS2 and −5.6 eV for LaCuS2).

Scheme illustrating possible synthesis reactions to form LaMS2 (M = Cu, Ag): reaction 1 is the high temperature solid state reaction, and reaction 2 is the topochemical reaction involving anionic redox.

The electronic density of states (Fig. 8) reveals a clear difference between LaCuS2 and LaAgS2 with the latter having a larger calculated band gap (1.57 eV) than LaCuS2 (1.07 eV). Although both values are smaller than the experimentally determined optical band gaps (Fig. 4), this behaviour is entirely consistent with the well-known tendency of semi-local PBE density functional theory to underestimate band gaps.52 The calculations nevertheless reproduce the correct qualitative trend across the series, indicating that replacing Cu by Ag increases the band gap. A more quantitative description could be obtained using a higher-rung exchange–correlation functional, such as rSCAN60 or a screened hybrid functional (e.g. HSE06),61 although these approaches are computationally more demanding. The projected density of states shows that the valence-band maximum is dominated by hybridised S 3p and Cu 3d states and the conduction-band minimum is primarily La 5d in character. The larger band gap in LaAgS2 therefore reflects the modified interaction between the Ag 4d and S 3p states.

Projected density of states for (a) LaCuS2 and (b) LaAgS2. Calculations were carried out using PBE. It should be noted that PBE tends to underestimate bandgap values in semiconductors.

Discussion

Our study focuses on the synthesis of ternary sulfides LaMS2 (M = Cu, Ag) using the precursor LaS2. LaS2 was first prepared by high temperature solid state reactions22–24 but research into synthesis methods for this optoelectronic material are ongoing62 with reports of synthesis by metathesis reactions,25,63 microwave-assisted metathesis reactions,64 hydrothermal methods,65 and most recently, as colloidal nanocrystals from LaI3 in oleylamine.66 We can add our synthesis by ball milling to this list. It would be interesting to explore whether this route could be applied to other lanthanide dichalcogenides.67 Annealing our ball-milled samples gave a diffraction pattern with some sharp diffraction peaks, alongside other more broad peaks. It would be interesting to explore, perhaps using electron microscopy, the microstructure of the sample and the ratio of α and β polymorphs in the sample.

LaCuS2 has typically been synthesised by high temperature solid state reactions (T ≥ 800 °C),36–38 although recently LaCuS2 has also been prepared by a sol–gel route for photocatalysis applications.68 Sasaki et al. demonstrated that the ternary selenide LaCuSe2 could be prepared by LTT reaction of LaSe2 with Cu,13 and it is pleasing to extend this method to the ternary sulfide LaCuS2 in this work. To date, these anionic redox topochemical reactions are typically carried out by heating the precursor and transition metal element to low temperatures in an evacuated, sealed, quartz tube13,21 but Sasaki et al. have shown that analogous reactions can be performed by ball milling,14 as we have demonstrated here to prepare LaCuS2 (Fig. 3a).

Our synthesis of LaAgS2 by this LTT method (Fig. 5) highlights the importance of topochemical anionic redox reactions for preparing metastable phases.15,19,20 To the best of our knowledge, LaAgS2 has not been reported, and our attempts to synthesise it by conventional high temperature solid state reactions gave mixed-phase samples containing the possibility of only ∼9.5(2)% (by weight) of LaAgS2. However, several LnAgS2 phases with smaller lanthanide cations are known, including those of AgGdS2-type structure (P21 symmetry), AgYbS2-type structure (I41md or I41/amd symmetry), and NaCl-type structure (Fm3̄m symmetry), as summarised in Fig. 9. Unlike the LnCuS2 structures built from corner and-/or edge-linked CuS4 tetrahedra (see ref. 32–35 SI and LaCuS2 illustrated in Fig. 6a), the reported LnAgS2 structures, containing the larger Ag+ ions (4-coordinate ionic radii of Cu+ and Ag+ are 0.60 Å and 1.00 Å, respectively),69 are composed of 6-coordinate Ln3+ sites built around 5- or 6-coordinate Ag+ sites40–42 (Fig. 9). The anionic redox topochemical reaction can be considered in terms of a “zipper” mechanism with metals being oxidised to cations as they break apart the (S2)2− dimers which get reduced.15 The layers of (S2)2− dimers in the α and β polymorphs of LaS2 (Fig. 1) lend themselves to give 4-coordinate sites for the oxidised M+ ions (see LaCuS2 structure illustrated in Fig. 6a). Hence our LTT synthesis of LaAgS2, with the low reaction temperature likely allowing kinetics to play a role, results in a structure very similar to that of LaCuS2, despite the much larger size of the Ag+ ion. The MS4 sites in LaAgS2 are quite distorted, closer to elongated trigonal bipyramidal sites which are only corner-linked (and not edge-linked as in LaCuS2). The Ag+ ions are slightly displaced from the trigonal plane of these sites, moving towards the (approximately) tetrahedrally coordinated site occupied by Cu+, but being larger, Ag+ ions cannot occupy the smaller tetrahedral site. This splitting of the of Ag+ sites is similar to the Cu+ displacements described by the chalcohalide CuBiSeCl2 and could contribute to low thermal conductivity.70 The lower-symmetry coordination environment observed here for Ag+ is similar to that expected for ns2 lone pair cations such as Sn2+, Pb2+, Sb3+. We therefore attempted LTT reactions between Sn and LaS2 (assuming half-occupancy of the cation sites by divalent Sn2+ for charge balance) but these were not successful and instead resulted in S-deintercalation, consistent with other reported attempts at anionic redox reactions involving M2+ cations.71,72 It would be interesting to explore similar LTT reactions with other M+ ions; reaction with Tl+ might allow the influence of the second-order Jahn–Teller effect for ns2 ions to be investigated. The elongated Ag+ sites in LaAgS2 are accommodated by the greater monoclinic distortion (β ≫ 90° for LaAgS2) and flattening of the La–S layers compared with LaCuS2. It would be interesting to see whether P21/c LaAgS2-like polymorphs could be prepared by similar topochemical reactions for other smaller lanthanides.

Conclusions

The transparent p-type semiconductor LaCuOS was first prepared by topochemical oxidation of LaCuS218 and we report here the synthesis of this ternary sulfide LaCuS2 by low temperature topochemical reaction involving anionic redox, exploiting the reactive (S2)2− dimers in the layered LaS2 precursor. This topochemical approach also enabled the synthesis of the new ternary sulfide LaAgS2. DFT calculations indicate that the synthesis of monoclinic LaAgS2 has relatively small thermodynamic stability and it is likely that kinetics play a role in this topochemical synthesis route. This results in a monoclinic structure for LaAgS2 which is closely related to that of LaCuS2, despite the larger size of the Ag+ cation. Diffuse reflectance measurements indicate that both materials are semiconductors with band gaps ∼2.1(2) eV, highlighting the potential of these materials for optoelectronic and photocatalytic applications. These findings highlight anionic redox topochemistry as a powerful strategy for preparing chalcogenides. It would be interesting to extend this work to explore whether new polymorphs of other ternary sulfides (including other LnAgS2 phases with smaller lanthanide cations, and Bi-containing ABiQ2 chalcogenides6) could be synthesised by similar anionic redox topochemical routes.
