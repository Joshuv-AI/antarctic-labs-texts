SPINNs - Deep learning framework for approximation of stochastic differential equations
Marcin Baranek, Paweł Przybyłowicz

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the SPINNs (stochastic physics-informed neural networks) in a systematic manner. This provides a mathematical framework for approximating the solution of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) driven by Lévy noise using artificial neural networks. Key words: artificial neural networks, stochastic differential equations, stochastic physics-informed neural networks (SPINNs), Lévy process MSC 2010: 65C30, 68Q25



1.
Introduction

We consider the following stochastic differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\,{\mathrm{d}}X(t)=a(t,X(t-))\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma\,{\mathrm{d}}L(t),\ t\in[0,T]},\\
X(0)=x_{0},\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, the drift coefficient
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to be at least continuous,
(L(t))_{t\geq 0}
denotes an
m
-dimensional Lévy process, and
X(t-)=\lim\limits_{s\to t,s<t}X(s)
, see
[
1
]
. We investigate a non-standard approach to approximating solutions of such equations. In particular, we propose a novel deep learning-based methodology that enables artificial neural networks to learn and replicate the trajectories of the underlying SDE. Hence, the main question we pose in this paper is as follows:

Can artificial neural networks learn the stochastic dynamics given by the SDE (
1
)?

We know the answer is positive for deterministic ODEs and PDEs, see, for example,
[
3
]
,
[
7
]
. However, learning stochastic dynamics appears to be a much more involved task. The main problem is that artificial neural networks are deterministic functions. Therefore, we must be precise about exactly what ANNs are approximating when learning solutions to SDEs. The direct approach, known from the ODE case, is not possible for SDE. In our approach, we will use the fact that the solution
X
lives on the path space
D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
- the Skorokhod space - and can be expressed as a deterministic functional
\Psi
of the trajectories of the driving process
L
, see
[
11
]
. This fact allows us to define a suitable loss function for artificial neural network of a feedforward type. After minimizing the loss (i.e., training), we obtain the artificial neural network that approximates the function
\Psi
. As a byproduct, we get a new approximation algorithm for the SDEs (
1
) driven by the additive Lévy noise.

One of the first attempts in that direction (solving SDEs via artificial neural networks) was
[
18
]
. The thesis
[
18
]
introduces a neural network-based discretization of the Wong–Zakai approximation for solving SDEs. In this approach, the Brownian motion is replaced by a smooth interpolation, transforming the SDE into a deterministic ODE system. A deep neural network is trained to approximate the ODE solutions by minimizing a loss function reflecting the SDE’s drift–diffusion dynamics. Once trained, the model can generate approximate trajectories of the underlying stochastic process, offering a data-driven alternative to classical numerical schemes.
Numerical experiments show that while the approach successfully reproduces pathwise dynamics, it currently underperforms classical schemes such as Euler–Maruyama and Milstein in terms of convergence rate and computational efficiency, indicating the need for further methodological refinement. In
[
9
]
, the proposed method solves stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) by combining the Wiener chaos expansion with neural network approximation. The Wiener chaos expansion of the solution is first truncated and deterministic or random neural networks replace the propagators (its deterministic coefficient functions). Deterministic networks are fully trained, while random networks use fixed random hidden-layer parameters, requiring only the linear output layer to be optimized. By leveraging the universal approximation property of neural networks, this framework provides theoretical convergence guarantees and practical algorithms for approximating a wide class of SPDEs. Finally, the article
[
17
]
introduces SPINODE, a stochastic physics-informed neural ordinary differential equation framework designed to learn unknown hidden physics terms inside stochastic differential equations from trajectory data. It represents the hidden physics via neural networks and propagates uncertainty through the known SDE structure to obtain deterministic ODEs governing the time evolution of statistical moments (e.g., means and covariances). Using ODE solvers within the neural ODE paradigm and training via moment-matching with mini-batch gradient descent and adjoint sensitivity, the method fits the neural networks to data-estimated moments. They conclude that SPINODE is flexible and scalable for multivariate stochastic systems with multiplicative noise, but note that learning requires many repeated trajectories.

In this paper, we propose a different approach to that known from
[
18
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
17
]
. Namely, instead of replacing Brownian motion
W
(or, in our case the Lévy process) with some approximation, we transform our semimartingale equation (
1
) into a suitable random ODE, with a noise process embedded in the right-hand side function. This methodology allows us to cover the SDEs driven with additive Lévy noise and is more direct than the method proposed in the papers mentioned above. Compared to the existing literature, we extend the PINN approach (see, for example,
[
13
]
,
[
7
]
) to define its stochastic counterpart, called
stochastic physics-informed neural networks (SPINNs)
. We provide a suitable theoretical framework and we present results of numerical experiments.

2.
Preliminaries

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\|x\|
we mean the Euclidean norm, while for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
by
\|A\|
we mean the Frobenius norm. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probabilistic space, and let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration on that space that satisfies the usual conditions, see
[
12
]
. We set
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}=\sigma\Bigl(\bigcup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}\Bigr)
. For two sub-
\sigma
-fields
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2}
of
\mathcal{F}
we define
\mathcal{A}_{1}\vee\mathcal{A}_{2}=\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2})
. We assume that
(L(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is an
m
-dimensional Lévy process on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, see Section 4, Chapter I in
[
12
]
. By
\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
we denote the space of all
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued stochastic processes
(y(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
that are adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, have a.s. continuous trajectories with
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
-adapted and càdlàg derivative processes
(y^{\prime}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
, and
\max\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}\|y(0)\|^{2},\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y(t)\|^{2}dt,\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}dt\Bigr\}<+\infty
.

We impose the classical assumptions for the drift coefficient
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

(A1)

a\in C\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)

(A2)

there exists
L\in[0,+\infty)
such that for all
t\in[0,T],y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\|a(t,y_{1})-a(t,y_{2})\|\leq L\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|.

(2)

Since the Lévy process
(L(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a semimartingale (see Corollary at page 55 in
[
12
]
), by Theorem 6, page 255 in
[
12
]
, under the assumptions (A1), (A2) the equation (
1
) has a unique strong solution
X
. Moreover, since
X
has càdlàg paths, we get for all
t\in[0,T]

\int\limits_{0}^{t}a(s,X(s-))ds=\int\limits_{0}^{t}a(s,X(s))ds\quad a.s.

(3)

Hence, instead of (
1
) in the rest of the paper we will be considering

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\,{\mathrm{d}}X(t)=a(t,X(t))\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma\,{\mathrm{d}}L(t),\ t\in[0,T]},\\
X(0)=x_{0},\end{array}\right.

(4)

Remark 1
.

We perform our analysis under the standard assumptions (A1),(A2). We stress, however, that our approach can be used for SDEs (
1
) with, for example, discontinuous drift or with only local Lipschitz continuous drift.

3.
Transforming SDE to RODE

We introduce the process

Y(t)=X(t)-\sigma L(t),\ t\in[0,T],

(5)

and by (
4
) with probability one for all
t\in[0,T]

Y(t)=x_{0}+\int\limits_{0}^{t}a(s,Y(s)+\sigma L(s))ds.

(6)

Hence, the stochastic process
(Y(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
solves the following random ordinary differential equation (RODE)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\forall_{t\in[0,T)}Y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,Y(t),L(t)),Y(0)=x_{0}\Bigr)=1},

(7)

with

f(t,y,w)=a(t,y+\sigma w),\quad(t,y,w)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},

and where for
t\in[0,T)
by
Y^{\prime}(t)
we mean the right-hand side derivative. The value of
Y^{\prime}(T)
can be defined arbitrarily. Here we take
Y^{\prime}(T):=Y^{\prime}(T-)
a.s., since
Y^{\prime}(T-)=f(T,Y(T),L(T-))
is a.s. finite. The process
(Y(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
is adapted, has continuous trajectories, the derivative process
(Y^{\prime}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
has càdlàg paths, and is also adapted. Note that the trajectories of
Y
are, in general, of higher smoothness than the trajectories of
X
. For example, if
L
is the Wiener process, then the trajectories of
X
are nowhere differentiable, while the trajectories of
Y
are even continuously differentiable on
[0,T]
a.s.

The proof of the following fact is straightforward and therefore omitted.

Fact 1
.

Under the assumptions (A1), (A2) we have that
f\in C([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and for all
t\in[0,T]
,
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\|f(t,y_{1},w_{1})-f(t,y_{2},w_{2})\|\leq C_{1}\Bigl(\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|+\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|\Bigr),

(8)

and for all
t\in[0,T]
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\|f(t,y,w)\|\leq C_{2}(1+\|y\|+\|w\|),

(9)

where

\displaystyle C_{1}=L\max\{1,\|\sigma\|\},

(10)

\displaystyle C_{2}=\max\Bigl\{\sup\limits_{0\leq t\leq T}\|f(t,0,0)\|,L\max\{1,\|\sigma\|\}\Bigr\}.

(11)

From Theorem 3.1 in
[
11
]
and Fact
1
, we get that there exists a Skorokhod measurable function
\Psi:D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})\to D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

Y=\Psi(L),

(12)

where
Y
is the unique solution to (
7
). This give us a pathwise representation of the solution
Y
on the path space, i.e.:

Y(\omega)=\Psi(L(\omega)),

(13)

where, for a fixed
\omega\in\Omega
,
L(\omega)\in D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})
is a fixed trajectory of the Lévy process
L
. Moreover, from (
5
) we have that

X(\omega)=\bar{\Psi}(L(\omega)):=\Psi(L(\omega))+\sigma L(\omega),

(14)

where
\bar{\Psi}:D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{m})\to D([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
is also Skorokhod measurable. Therefore,

the main idea is to use a deep neural network
\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot)
, which input will be a finite approximation of trajectories of the Lévy process
L
, to approximate
\Psi
.

In this paper, we focus on the feedforward architecture for
\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot)
. However, other approaches are also possible. (This will be a topic of our future work.)

4.
Reformulation of solving SDE as a stochastic optimization problem

4.1.
Two loss functions

Under assumption (A1), we define the nonlinear functional

\mathcal{\bar{L}}:\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\to[0,+\infty],

(15)

by

\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[H(y,\tau,L)\Bigr],\ y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right),

(16)

where

H(y,\tau,L)=\|x_{0}-y(0)\|^{2}+T\cdot\|y^{\prime}(\tau)-f(\tau,y(\tau),L(\tau))\|^{2},

(17)

and the random variable
\tau\sim U(0,T)
, defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, is independent
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. We refer to
\mathcal{\bar{L}}
as the
theoretical loss function
.

Proposition 1
.

Under assumption (A1) the following holds for all
y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
:

(i)

\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)=\mathbb{E}\|x_{0}-y(0)\|^{2}+\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}dt,

(ii)

\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)=0=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\mathcal{\bar{L}}(u)\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\forall_{t\in[0,T)}\ y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,y(t),L(t)),\ y(0)=x_{0}\Bigr)=1.

Proof.

For any
y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
, by the freezing lemma (see, for example, Lemma 4.1 in
[
2
]
) and by the fact that the
\sigma
-fields
\sigma(\tau)
,
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
are independent, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|y^{\prime}(\tau)-f(\tau,y(\tau),L(\tau))\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\|y^{\prime}(\tau)-f(\tau,y(\tau),L(\tau))\|^{2}\ |\ \sigma(\tau)\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}\ |\ \sigma(\tau)\Bigr)\Bigl|_{t=\tau}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl|_{t=\tau}\Bigr]=\frac{1}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}\Bigr)dt,

(18)

which ends the proof of (i).

We now show (ii). Let us fix an arbitrary
y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
.

(’
\Leftarrow
’) If the process
y
solves (
7
) then directly from (i) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)=\mathbb{E}\|x_{0}-y(0)\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}dt=0,

(19)

and

\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\mathcal{\bar{L}}(u)\geq 0=\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)\geq\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\mathcal{\bar{L}}(u).

(20)

(’
\Rightarrow
’) If
\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)=0=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\mathcal{\bar{L}}(u)
then by (i) we get that
\mathbb{E}\|y(0)-x_{0}\|^{2}=0
and

\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}dt\Bigr)=0.

(21)

This in turn imply
\mathbb{P}(y(0)=x_{0})=1
and

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2}dt=0\Bigr)=1.

(22)

Therefore, by Lemma
2

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\forall_{t\in[0,T)}\ y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,y(t),L(t))\Bigr)=1,

(23)

since trajectories of the nonnegative process
(\|y^{\prime}(t)-f(t,y(t),L(t))\|^{2})_{t\in[0,T]}
are càdlàg.
∎

Below, we state sufficient conditions for the finiteness of
\mathcal{\bar{L}}.

Lemma 1
.

Assume that the drift coefficient
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies (A1), (A2).

(a)

If
\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|L(t)\|^{2}dt<+\infty
, then for all
y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
it holds

0\leq\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)<+\infty.

(24)

(b)

If
\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|L(t)\|^{2}dt=+\infty
and, in addition,

(A3)\ \exists_{D_{0}\in[0,+\infty)}:\forall_{(t,z)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \|a(t,z)\|\leq D_{0}

then for all
y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
it holds

0\leq\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)<+\infty.

(25)

Proof.

Fix
y\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
. We have with probability one that

0\leq H(y,\tau,L)\leq 2\|x_{0}\|^{2}+2\|y(0)\|^{2}+2T\|y^{\prime}(\tau)\|^{2}+2T\|f(\tau,y(\tau),L(\tau))\|.

(26)

By (A1), (A2) we get

H(y,\tau,L)\leq\max\{2,2T,6TC_{2}^{2}\}\Bigl(\|x_{0}\|^{2}+\|y(0)\|^{2}+\|y(\tau)\|^{2}+\|y^{\prime}(\tau)\|^{2}+\|L(\tau)\|^{2}\Bigr).

(27)

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)\leq\max\{2,2T,6TC_{2}^{2}\}\Bigl(\|x_{0}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|y(0)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|y(t)\|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|y^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}dt+\frac{1}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|L(t)\|^{2}dt\Bigr)<+\infty.

(28)

If
\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|L(t)\|^{2}dt=+\infty
then by the additional assumption (A3) we obtain

\mathcal{\bar{L}}(y)\leq 2\max\{1,T,TD_{0}\}\Bigl(1+\|x_{0}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|y(0)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\|y^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}dt\Bigr)<+\infty.

(29)

∎

By Proposition
1
, Lemma
1
, and Corollary
1
, we arrive at the following result for the theoretical loss function
\mathcal{\bar{L}}
.

Theorem 1
.

(i)

If
\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|L(t)\|^{2}<+\infty
and the assumptions (A1), (A2) hold then the unique solution
(Y(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
of (

) is the global minimizer of
\mathcal{\bar{L}}
in
\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|L(t)\|^{2}=+\infty
and the assumptions (A1), (A2), (A3) hold then the unique solution
(Y(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
of (
7
) is the global minimizer of
\mathcal{\bar{L}}
in
\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
.

By the results above, we can express the solution to (
7
) in terms of finding a global minimizer of
\mathcal{\bar{L}}
. Note, however, that the corresponding minimization problem

\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\left([0,T]\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)}\mathcal{\bar{L}}(u),

(30)

is infinite-dimensional. Hence, for the practical implementation, instead of considering (
30
), we are aiming at finite-dimensional minimization

\inf\limits_{\mathrm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{w})=\inf\limits_{\mathrm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[H_{n}(\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot),\tau,L)\Bigr],

(31)

where

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot),\tau,L)=\|\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},0,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})-x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+T\cdot\Bigl\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},t,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigl|_{t=\tau}-f(\tau,\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\tau,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}),\tilde{L}_{n}(\tau))\Bigl\|^{2},

(32)

the function
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the feedforward artificial neural network (see, for example,
[
7
]
), and

\Delta_{n}=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}=T\},

\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}=\{L(t_{1}),L(t_{2}),\ldots,L(t_{n})\},

and
\tilde{L}_{n}
is the Lévy bridge approximation of
L
, i.e.: the process
L
conditioned on
\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}
, see
[
5
]
. We refer to
\mathcal{L}
as to
empirical loss function
.

The use of Lévy bridges is justified by the fact that for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(R^{m})

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(L(\tau)\in A\ |\ \sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\vee\sigma(\tau)\Bigr)=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(L(t)\in A\ |\ \sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigr)\Bigl|_{t=\tau}

(33)

since
\tau
,
L
are independent. Moreover, we have

Fact 2
.

Let

F_{(\mathrm{w},t,y)}(z)=\Bigl\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},t,y)-f(t,\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},t,y),z)\Bigl\|^{2},

(34)

for all
(\mathrm{w},t,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[F_{(\mathrm{w},\tau,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})}(L(\tau))\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl[F_{(\mathrm{w},t,y)}(L(t))\ |\ \sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigr]\Bigl|_{t=\tau,y=\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}}\Biggr],

(35)

and the Lévy process
L
conditioned on
\sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})
is the Lévy bridge.

Proof.

By the properties of conditional expectation and the fact that
\sigma(\tau)
and
\sigma(L(t))\vee\sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})
are independent
\sigma
-fields, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[F_{(\mathrm{w},\tau,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})}(L(\tau))\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl(F_{(\mathrm{w},\tau,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})}(L(\tau))\ \Bigl|\ \sigma(\tau)\vee\sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl(F_{(\mathrm{w},t,y)}(L(t))\ \Bigl|\ \sigma(\tau)\vee\sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigr)\Bigl|_{t=\tau,y=\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}}\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl[F_{(\mathrm{w},t,y)}(L(t))\ |\ \sigma(\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigr]\Bigl|_{t=\tau,y=\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}}\Biggr].

(36)

∎

4.2.
SGD algorithm

Applying the Robbins-Monro stochastic approximation algorithm
[
14
]
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}^{k+1}=\mathrm{w}^{k}-\eta_{k}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{w}}H_{n}(\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot),\tau_{k},L_{k})\Bigl|_{\mathrm{w}=\mathrm{w}^{k}}

(37)

for
k=0,1,2,\ldots
, where
(\tau_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is an iid sequence from
U(0,T)
,
(L_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence of independent Lévy processes (that is also independent of
(\tau_{k})_{k\geq 0}
), and we impose the following assumption on the sequence of learning rates
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

\sum\limits_{k\geq 0}\eta_{k}=+\infty,\ \sum\limits_{k\geq 0}\eta^{2}_{k}<+\infty.

After training the neural network and obtaining
\textrm{w}^{*}
, we define the approximation to
X
as

\bar{X}_{n}(t,\omega)=\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w}^{*},t,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}(\omega))+\sigma L(t,\omega),\quad t\in[0,T],\omega\in\Omega,

(38)

where

\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w}^{*},t,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}(\omega))\approx(\Psi(L(\omega)))(t).

(39)

The resulting scheme, together with the above derivations, introduces the so-called
Stochastic Physics-Informed Neural Networks
(SPINNs). Note that when
\sigma=0
, then we recover the classical PINNs, see
[
7
]
. The main differences between the derivation of SPINNs and PINNs are the use of the universal representation theorem that gives us the existence of the function
\Psi
in (
12
), and the construction of the loss function for the artificial neural network
\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot)
that, after minimization, allows us to approximate the function
\Psi
. It was not the case in the classical PINNs.

Remark 2
.

The results presented in this paper can be applied directly, under the assumptions (A1), (A2), to the SDEs (
1
) driven by the Lévy process
L
of a finite Lévy measure. In this case,
L
can be, for example, a Wiener process, a compound Poisson process, or their linear combination. If the drift coefficient
a
satisfies (A1), (A2), (A3), then we can consider the case when, for example,
L
is the Cauchy process.

Remark 3
.

There is another option of approximating the loss
\mathcal{\bar{L}}
. Namely, we can take

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{n}(\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},\cdot),L)=\|\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},0,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})-x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{T}{n}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}\Bigl\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},t,\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}})\Bigl|_{t=t_{j}}-f(t_{j},\mathcal{N}(\mathrm{w},t_{j},\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}),L(t_{j}))\Bigl\|^{2}.

(40)

Such a loss function can be used in a more general case when
L
is a cadlag semimartingale, since for such a
\tilde{H}_{n}
we do not need to know the conditional law
L(t)
conditioned on
\bar{L}_{\Delta_{n}}
. However, under such an approach, we cannot use a compact form SGD (
4.2
).

4.3.
Some insight into the multiplicative noise case

Our diffusion removal transformation is only suitable for the SDEs with the additive noise as in (
1
) and cannot be directly applied to the SDEs with the multiplicative noise. In the scalar case and
L=W
being a scalar Wiener process let us consider the SDE

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\,{\mathrm{d}}X(t)=a(t,X(t))\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma X(t)dW(t),\ t\in[0,T]},\\
X(0)=x_{0}>0,\end{array}\right.

(41)

To transform the above equations into the RODE of the form (
7
) we proceed as follows. First, consider the SDE

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\,{\mathrm{d}}Z(t)=(e^{-Z(t)}a(t,e^{Z(t)})-\sigma^{2}/2)\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\sigma dW(t),\ t\in[0,T]},\\
Z(0)=\ln(x_{0}),\end{array}\right.

(42)

and then use the transformation (
5
), i.e.,
Y(t)=Z(t)-\sigma W(t)
, to transform the SDE (
42
) to the RODE

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\forall_{t\in[0,T)}Y^{\prime}(t)=f(t,Y(t),W(t)),Y(0)=\ln(x_{0})\Bigr)=1},

(43)

where

f(t,y,w)=e^{-(y+\sigma w)}a(t,e^{y+\sigma w})-\sigma^{2}/2,\quad(t,y,w)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then, we can apply our SPINN method to approximate
Y
. Since
X(t)=e^{Z(t)}
, what can be verified by the Itô formula, finally, we get the approximation of
X
.

Note that both transformations are the particular case of the well-known Doss-Sussman transformation, see
[
4
]
,
[
16
]
. In our future work we investigate the possibility of applying the D-S transformation to the multidimensional SDEs with multiplicative noise to extend the SPINNs approach derived in this paper.

(i)

(46)

\mathbb{P}\Biggl(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|Y^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}dt\leq C_{4}\Bigl(1+\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|L(t)\|^{2}dt\Bigr)<+\infty\Biggr)=1,

(47)

where

C_{3}=e^{C_{2}T}(1+\|x_{0}\|)\max\{1,C_{2},C_{2}T\},

(48)

(ii)

Under assumptions (A1), (A2), (A3) the following hold:

(50)

Proof.

We have with probability one for all

\displaystyle\|Y(t)\|\leq\|x_{0}\|+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\|f(s,Y(s),L(s))\|ds\leq\|x_{0}\|+C_{2}\int\limits_{0}^{t}(1+\|Y(s)\|+\|L(s)\|)ds

\displaystyle\leq\max\{1,C_{2},C_{2}T\}\Bigl(1+\|x_{0}\|+\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|L(s)\|ds\Bigr)+C_{2}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\|Y(s)\|ds.

(52)

Since almost all trajectories of

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|L(s)\|^{2}ds<+\infty\Bigr)=1

(53)

almost surely. This gives (

Lemma 2
.

Let
f:[0,T]\to[0,+\infty)
be càdlàg and
\int_{0}^{T}f(t)dt=0
. Then
f(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T)
.

Proof.

Fix any
t_{0}\in[0,T)
. Suppose for contradiction that
f(t_{0})>0
.
Since
f
is right-continuous at
t_{0}
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)\cap[0,T]

|f(t)-f(t_{0})|<\frac{f(t_{0})}{2},

and hence
f(t)>\frac{f(t_{0})}{2}>0
, for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)\cap[0,T]
.
Then

\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt\;\geq\;\int\limits_{[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)\cap[0,T]}f(t)dt\geq\min\{\delta,T-t_{0}\}\frac{f(t_{0})}{2}>0,

contradicting the assumption that
\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\,dt=0
.
Hence
f(t_{0})=0
. Since
t_{0}\in[0,T)
was arbitrary, we conclude that
f(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T)
, which ends the proof.
∎

7.
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we have introduced the SPINNs, the extension of the classical PINNs. Our approach allows us to solve SDEs (
1
), driven by an additive Lévy process, with artificial neural networks. The derivation performed within this paper shows that there is a rich mathematical structure behind SPINNs, and throughout mathematical investigations should be done in order to state rigorously convergence theorems, etc. In our future work, we plan to address some of these problems and also consider the SDEs with multiplicative noise case.
