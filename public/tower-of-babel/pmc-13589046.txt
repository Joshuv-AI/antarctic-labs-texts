Mechanistic understanding and comprehensive insights into green-synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles and its applications
Authors: Abdullahi Ahmad Bundi, Iram Jahan, Vikash Lodhi, Harshit Mani Tripathi, Araish Fatima, Yamini, Shailendra S Gurav, Sushant Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen synthesis offers a sustainable and biocompatible alternative to conventional chemical methods for Cerium oxide nanoparticle (CNPs) synthesis. It minimizes toxic waste and environmental hazards while improving functional performance. Chemical routes often require high temperatures, strong reducing agents and toxic solvents whereas green synthesis yields nanoparticles with smaller sizes, controlled morphologies, and bioactive surface coatings derived from polyphenols, sugars, and proteins. Phytochemicals including polyphenols, sugars, and proteins not only facilitate cerium oxide nanoparticle nucleation but also promote oxygen vacancy formation and modulate the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox coupling, which is a key to the catalytic, antioxidant, and free radical-scavenging activities of CNPs. Recent advances have shifted to green CeO₂ synthesis toward a phytochemical-directed defect engineering approach, allowing precise tuning of surface chemistry, redox states, and oxygen vacancy density through plant-derived ligands and optimized synthesis conditions. Green synthesized CNPs shows enhanced colloidal stability, improved biocompatibility, and reduced cytotoxicity due to the presence of biogenic capping layers. These features expand through the applications in catalysis, environmental remediation, biosensing, drug delivery, radioprotection, and biomedical imaging. Emerging applications include sonocatalytic wastewater treatment, ultrasensitive biosensors for food safety detection, and chitosan-CNPs nanocomposites for accelerated wound healing. Despite these promising advances, challenges related to reproducibility, size uniformity, purification and regulatory approval remain critical barriers for large-scale biomedical and industrial applications. Presently, this review focuses majorly on green routes of Cerium oxide nanoparticles synthesis and its relevant biological applications. Additionally, it discusses their physicochemical and toxicological evaluations, and life cycle assessment to ensure safe and scalable translation.Clinical Trial Number Declaration- Not Applicable. Introduction

Nanotechnology has emerged as one of the most transformative scientific achievements of the 21st century, integrating principles from physics, chemistry, biology, materials science, and engineering. This revolutionary field is defined as the science, engineering, and technology conducted at the nanoscale (1 to 100 nm), where unique phenomena enable novel applications that are not achievable with bulk materials [1]. At this scale, materials exhibit distinctive optical, electronic, catalytic, and biological properties due to their bulk counterparts. To put this scale into perspective, a single human hair is approximately 60,000 nm thick, while the DNA double helix is nearly 1 nm, illustrating the dramatic size reduction associated with nanoscale materials [2].

Nanoparticles, defined as particles with at least one dimension at the nanoscale, exhibit several unique characteristics that distinguish them from their bulk counterparts. These include a high volume-to-surface ratio, which enhances reactivity; surface plasmon resonance effects, crucial for optical applications; altered physical properties, such as melting point and surface energy; and easy surface functionalization. The defining feature at the nanoscale is the very large surface-to-volume ratio, meaning that no atom is very far from a surface or interface, and the behavior of atoms at these higher-energy sites significantly influences material properties [3]. The exceptional properties of nanoparticles have led to their widespread application across various sectors, including environment, agriculture, food, biotechnology, biomedicine, and pharmaceuticals. In the biomedical field, nanoparticles have attracted particular attention due to their ability to cross biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier and the placenta, enabling targeted drug delivery and cellular interactions [4].

Cerium oxide nanoparticles (CNPs), also known as CeO₂, represent one of the most promising metal oxide nanobiomaterials in contemporary nanotechnology. Cerium, the most abundant rare earth element with atomic number 58, exhibit two stable oxidation states: Ce³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺. This unique characteristic enables cerium oxide to exist in different oxide forms, primarily CeO₂ (Ce⁴⁺) and Ce₂O₃ (Ce³⁺), and to undergo reversible redox cycling between these states [5]. Structurally, CNPs possesses a cubic fluorite crystal structure, where eight oxygen anions surround each cerium atom, and each oxygen atom is located at a tetrahedral site. This crystal structure provides excellent catalytic properties and thermal stability due to its high structural order [6].

One of the most significant properties of CNPs is their enzyme-mimetic activity, particularly their ability to function as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase mimetics. These properties enable CeO₂ to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protect cells from oxidative stress, making them exceptional candidates for therapeutic applications. The nanoparticles can rapidly transition between different oxidation states, a property that makes them useful as catalysts, oxidizing agents, and reducing agents [7]. The size of CNPs significantly affects their properties and biological activity. As particle size decreases, the concentration of surface oxygen vacancies increases, leading to a higher Ce³⁺ content and a larger lattice constant. This size-dependent behaviour is crucial for their catalytic effect and bioactivity. Smaller CNPs are more easily phagocytosed by cells, potentially increasing their efficacy in targeted therapy [8].

In addition to their catalytic properties, CNPs exhibit unique optical and electronic properties, including a bandgap of approximately 3.2 eV, making them excellent UV radiation absorbers. Their magnetic properties make them useful in medical imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The large specific surface area of CNPs increases contact area with other substances, enhancing their reactivity and catalytic properties [9].

Despite these advantages, the conventional synthesis of CNPs through physical and chemical methods has been associated with significant environmental and safety concerns. This includes co-precipitation, hydrothermal synthesis, sol-gel processing, and thermal decomposition, which require high temperatures (300–800 °C), strong reducing agents such as sodium borohydride or hydrazine, toxic solvents such as dimethylformamide, ethylene glycol, and energy-intensive conditions. These methods are typically energy-intensive, generate toxic byproducts, and involve complex operational procedures, thereby limiting their sustainability and large-scale application [10].

In response to these limitations and growing environmental awareness, green synthesis has emerged as a sustainable alternative for nanoparticle production. Green synthesis, also known as biological synthesis or biosynthesis, is an eco-friendly approach that uses natural biological systems, including bacteria, fungi, algae, and plants, to produce nanoparticles [11]. This methodology aligns with the principles of environmental sustainability by employing non-toxic, biodegradable materials and minimizing ecological impact [12]. Unlike conventional methods that rely on toxic reducing agents and stabilisers, green synthesis harnesses the power of renewable biological resources that act as both reducing and stabilizing agents [13]. This approach eliminates the need for chemical additives while providing natural capping agents that enhance nanoparticle stability [14].

Among various biological routes, Plant-mediated synthesis has gained particular attention due to availability, simplicity, scalability, and cost-effectiveness [15]. Plant extracts contain various bioactive compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolic compounds, tannins, and alcohols that possess the capability to reduce metallic ions to nanoparticles with decent stability [16] (Fig. 1). These biomolecules not only accelerate the reduction of metal ions but also impart natural surface functionalization that enhances nanoparticle stability and biological compatibility. Compared with microbial synthesis, plant-mediated synthesis eliminates the need for sterile culture conditions and complex microbial maintenance, making it more suitable for rapid and large-scale nanoparticle production [17].

Recent advancements between 2025 and 2026 have further refined plant-mediated green synthesis into a more controlled process often described as phytochemical-directed defect engineering [18]. In this strategy, the intrinsic chemical composition of plant extracts influences oxygen vacancy formation and modulates the Ce3+/ Ce4+ redox balance, thereby tailoring the catalytic and biological activity of CNPs. Green-synthesised nano-ceria frequently demonstrate enhanced antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities compared with chemically synthesized counterparts due to the presence of bioactive phytochemical coating [19]. Moreover, natural capping agents derived from plant extracts provide better particle stabilization and prevent agglomeration more effectively than synthetic stabilizers [20].

Therefore, the objective of this review is to provide a comprehensive overview of the green synthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles and the fundamental mechanisms governing their formation and functionality. This article discusses the crystal structure, electronic properties, and redox properties of CeO₂, analyzing green synthesis methodologies through plant extracts, microorganisms, and biomolecules. Furthermore, the review compares physicochemical properties and functional performance of green synthesized CeO₂ and explores their emerging applications in catalysis, environmental remediation, biosensing, and biomedical technologies. By integrating mechanistic insights with recent technological advancements, this work aims to contribute to the development of sustainable nanotechnology while preserving the exceptional properties that make CNPs highly valuable across multiple scientific disciplines.

Structural and chemical properties of CNPs

Crystal structure and electronic properties of CNPs

Cerium oxide has a fluorite crystal structure (CaF2-type) with a cubic, face-centred cubic lattice. In this arrangement, Ce4+ cations occupy the face-centred cubic positions while O2− anions remain in the tetrahedral interstitial sites, forming a cubic unit cell with lattice structure (a = 5.411 A°). Each cerium ion is coordinated by eight oxygen atoms in a cubic structure, and these oxygen atom is tetrahedral arranged with four cerium ions. This symmetry provides structural stability and helps in rapid oxygen ion mobility through the structure [21, 22].

Cerium (atomic number 58) has an electronic configuration of [Xe]4f15d16s2, which allows it to have several oxidation states. In CeO₂, cerium is mainly found in the form of Ce4+ ([Xe]4f0), but easily reduces into Ce3+ ([Xe]4f1) when reducing conditions are experienced or when the temperature is high. This easy redox cycling between Ce4 + and Ce3+ is the basis of the catalytic a 2nd biological processes of ceria. Cerium 4f electronic states are close to the Fermi level, making the process of electron transfer possible. The stoichiometric CeO₂ band structure has a wide bandgap (3.0-3.6 eV) between the O2p valence band and the Cerium 4f conduction band and is therefore a wide-bandgap semiconductor [23, 24].

At the nanoscale, quantum confinement processes and surface-to-volume ratios have a strong impact on electronic properties. Nanoparticles less than 10 nm have blue-shifted optical absorption edges, disturbed Ce3+/Ce4+ ratios and increased surface reactivity compared to bulk ceria [25]. The increased surface atom density provides coordinately unsaturated sites and defect sites in the bandgap that act as catalytic reaction and radical scavenging active centres [26].

Redox chemistry and oxygen vacancy formation

The remarkable functional characteristics of the CeO₂ nanoparticle are based on the special redox chemistry and oxygen storage capacity. The reversible redox cycle of Ce4+/Ce3+ is associated with the formation and disappearance of oxygen vacancies (VO) based on KrogerVink equation, which contains the sum of three equations such as Oxygen vacancy formation, Reduction of CeO₂ and oxidation of reduced ceria (Fig. 2). This balance is changeable by temperature, oxygen partial pressure and the addition or removal of reducing or oxidizing agents. In reducing conditions (low pO2, presence of reducing agents, high temperature), the electrons are added to the Ce4+ ion, and the ion transforms into Ce3+, at the same time leaving the oxygen vacancies formed to charge neutrality [27]. On the other hand, in the presence of oxidizing agents (high pO2), Ce3+ ions lose electrons and reduce to Ce4+, and oxygen vacancies are replaced by atmospheric air or dissolved oxygen. This redox cycling is reversible so that CeO₂ can act as an oxygen buffer; the oxygen is stored and released depending on the environmental conditions. Oxygen storage capacity (OSC) is measured as the amount of oxygen that can be reversibly exchanged per unit mass or mole of ceria, which is normally 200–800 mmol oxygen per gram of nanoparticles against 50–150 mmol O2/g of bulk ceria [28]. Oxygen vacancies are the main catalytic reaction sites, radical scavenging, and biological interaction sites. These defects produce localized electronic states in the bandgap that simplify the movement of electrons and reduce activation energies of redox reactions [29]. The oxygen vacancy concentration increases with the reduction of the particle size since there is increased surface energy and thermodynamic driving forces for the formation of defects at surfaces and interfaces. Ce3+ concentrations reaching 40% of total cerium in nanoparticles below 5 nm can be reached, as compared to less than 5% of total cerium in bulk ceria [30]. Ce3+ and Oxygen vacancies are distributed unevenly in space and are mainly concentrated at the surface, edges, and corners of the surface where the coordination number is minimized [31]. The energies of oxygen vacancy formation at various crystallographic facets are different: the surface of oxygen vacancy is usually more concentrated at the {100} and the {110} facets, as compared to the more stable surface, the {111} one. This is facet-dependent chemistry, which affects catalytic selectivity and biological activity [32].

Green synthesis methodologies of CNPs

Green synthesis represents a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical and physical methods for the production of CNPs. Unlike traditional routes that require high temperatures, toxic solvents, and strong reducing agents, green synthesis utilizes plants, microorganisms, and purified biomolecules as natural reducing and stabilizing agents. These biological components contain diverse functional groups capable of reducing cerium ions and stabilizing the resulting nanoparticles, so that the produced particles have improved biocompatibility, reduced environmental impact, and enhanced surface functionality.

Plant-mediated synthesis of CNPs

Plant-mediated synthesis is the most widely studied and promising approach in green synthesis methodologies for cerium oxide nanoparticles, offering an environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional chemical and physical methods. This biological approach leverages the inherent reducing and stabilizing capabilities of phytochemicals present in various plant tissues to produce CNPs with enhanced biocompatibility and functional properties. The major components of plants, such as polyphenols and flavonoids, include quercetin, catechin, gallic acid, caffeic acid, and epigallocatechin gallate, which have a series of hydroxyl (-OH) functional groups that are connected to aromatic rings [33]. These hydroxyl groups are oxidized to quinones, which give away electrons that reduce Ce4+ to Ce3+ and consequently nucleate CNPs. The oxidized polyphenol is adsorbed onto nanoparticle surfaces, thus offering both steric and electrostatic stabilization [34]. Terpenoids like Monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and triterpenes contain different functional groups (hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl), which are engaged in the processes of metal coordination and reduction. Terpenoids provide morphological regulation by selective adsorption to certain crystallographic faces, too [35]. Alkaloids such as Nitrogen-based heterocyclic alkaloids, including caffeine, nicotine, and berberine, are capable of chelating metal ions and rearranging the reduction rate. Nitrogen atoms have lone pair electrons that enable them to coordinate with cerium ions [36]. Proteins and amino acids also mediate the synthesis. Enzymatic proteins (oxidoreductases, peroxidases) make reactions necessary in electron transfer, and structural proteins give templating effects. Amino acids containing thiol (-SH), amine (-NH2), and carboxyl (-COOH) functional groups attach to the surfaces of the nanoparticles, inhibiting aggregation [37]. Carbohydrates like polysaccharides (cellulose, starch, pectin) play the role of stabilizing agents, primarily due to hydrogen bonding and steric hindrance, although reducing sugars (glucose, fructose) may also be involved in reducing metals [38].

Synthesis procedure

Preparation of extracts. Fresh or dried plant material is washed and cut into small pieces, then boiled or refluxed in deionized water or ethanol (usually 5–20 g plant material/100 mL solvent) for 15–60 min. After the extraction, the extract is filtered to eliminate the particulate matter to obtain a bioactive solution. Cerium (III) nitrate hexahydrate [Ce(NO3).36H2O] or cerium(III) chloride heptahydrate (CeCl3.7H2O) is dissolved in deionized water in the range of 1-100 mM. The rate of addition is made to the cerium salt solution at volumes of 1:1–1:10 (extract: precursor). The mixture will react under room temperature or 100 °C, 1–24 h, and the pH of the mixture will be adjusted to alkaline conditions (pH 8–12) with the help of NaOH or NH4OH, which will increase the reaction rate. The colour of the solution turns colourless or pale yellow, which becomes yellow-orange or brown, and this is the indication of nanoparticle formation. The nanoparticles are centrifuged (8000-15 000 rpm, 15–30 min), washed with deionized water and ethanol several times to get rid of the unreacted precursors and surplus biomolecules and dried at 60–100 °C or lyophilized. It can also be calcined by heating at 300–600 °C for 2–4 h in air to eliminate the remaining organic matter, enhance crystallinity, and control the concentration of oxygen vacancies. Calcination can, however, remove desirable bioactive surface coatings [39].

Leaf extracts constitute the predominant approach to synthesis, with research evolution progressing from empirical optimization to systematic mechanistic investigation. According to Singh et al. (2020), plant-mediated biosynthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles enhances their biocompatibility and pharmacological potential, aligning with the idea that bio-inspired nanoparticles exhibit improved properties due to their nano dimension and interactions with biological compounds, thereby establishing the theoretical foundation for subsequent optimization studies [40]. Early investigations by Arumugam et al. (2015) achieved ultrasmall particles (5 ± 1 nm) using Gloriosa superba L. extract but provided limited optimization of synthesis parameters [41]. In contrast, recent studies by Hasan et al. (2024) demonstrate sophisticated optimization approaches, reporting that “systematic parameter screening involving extract concentration (1–10 g/L), pH range (4–10), temperature (25–80°C), and precursor concentration (0.5-5 mM) enabled identification of optimal conditions maximizing yield (89%) while minimizing size distribution (CV: 12%).“ [42]. This methodological evolution reflects broader trends in green synthesis research, transitioning from descriptive studies to quantitative optimization frameworks (Figure 3).

Evolution of plant-mediated synthesis

In the initial work, the major interest was in proving feasibility. Indicatively, Arumugam et al. (2015) made ultrasmall CNPs (approximately 5 nm) by using Gloriosa superba leaf extract, although this was not systematically optimized in terms of reaction parameters [41]. Other more recent studies are indicative of a shift to quantitative optimization and understanding of mechanics. Hasan et al. (2024) used systematic parameter screening and different extract concentrations, pH, temperature, and precursor concentration to obtain high yield (89) and a narrow particle size distribution [42]. Nevertheless, there is still a significant variation in particle size. Some of the researches have indicated nanoparticles as small as 5 nm, whereas other related studies have indicated researches produce 15–45 nm under the same procedure, implying that plant-specific phytochemical composition has a significant effect on the nucleation and growth processes [43]. As an example, Hkiri produced nanoparticles of CeO₂ with a range of 4–15 nm in size as a result of the reducing and stabilizing nature of flavonoids and alkaloids, owing to the use of Portulaca oleracea extract [44]. Likewise, phytochemical diversity, especially polyphenols and alkaloids, is important to define the morphology and size distribution of nanoparticles [45].

Recent studies have also been extended to non-foliar tissues of plants and farm wastes to facilitate circular economy approaches. An example of this is the successful synthesis of nanoparticles using fruit peels and seeds, which allows the use of waste to generate useful nanomaterials, and in the process, reduces the volume of waste. Microbial based synthesis of CNPs

Microbial synthesis represents an emerging area with significant potential for controlled production and industrial scaling, though current research remains limited compared to plant-mediated approaches. Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria have been used in the synthesis of CNPs. The bacteria release enzymes (nitrate reductases, NADH-dependent reductases), and metabolites (organic acids, amino acids, peptides) which decrease Ce3+/Ce4+ and stabilize nanoparticles [49]. Extracellular expression is normally favored because it makes down-stream purification easier. They can be examples of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, and Lactobacillus species. The syntheses of bacteria usually produce nanoparticles with a diameter of 5–50 nm, usually in form of a sphere or irregular shape [50]. Fungi have the benefits of increased production of biomass, increased resistance to metal ion concentration, and release of greater amounts of extracellular proteins and enzymes. Fungal cell walls contain chitin, glucans and protein cell walls and offer a lot of binding sites to the metal ions. Both fungi (Aspergillus, Fusarium, Penicillium) and yeasts (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Candida) have been studied. The synthesis of fungi tends to generate bigger nanoparticles (20–100 nm) with varying morphologies such as spheres, cubes, and rods [51]. The microalgae and macroalgae (seaweeds) have polysaccharides (alginates, carrageenans, fucoidans), proteins, and pigments (chlorophylls, carotenoids) which enable the synthesis of nanoparticles [52]. Algae also has the added advantage of growing photosynthetically which lowers the cost of cultivation. CNPs has been produced using species like Spirulina platensis, Chlorella vulgaris and Sargassum [53]. Microorganisms can be grown in the right growth media (bacteria in nutrient broth, fungi in potato dextrose broth, algae in BG-11 medium, and so on) and cultured under optimal conditions (temperature, pH, aeration) to reach either exponential or stationary growth. In the case of extracellular synthesis, the cells are centrifuged and the cell free supernatant of secreted enzymes and metabolites are harvested. In the case of intracellular synthesis, the biomass is employed as such. Mixing of the supernatant or biomass with cerium salt solution (1–10 mM) at 25–37 °C agitation of between 24 and 96 h with pH adjustment to alkalinity and then nanoparticles are centrifugally separated, cleaned and dried with a lot of washing to eliminate cellular debris and media components [54].

Microbial synthesis would have benefits such as scalability due to fermentation technology, there can be possibility of continuous production and genetic engineering can be done to improve the efficiency of the synthesis. Disadvantages however include increased synthesis times (days as compared to hours to plant extracts), the possibility of contamination by endotoxins (in the case of bacterial synthesis), and the requirement of sterile conditions [55]. There is also an established foundational protocols for the synthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles using the thermophilic fungus Humicola sp. Cultures were maintained and fermentation performed at pH 9 and 50 °C, with shaking at 200 rpm for 96 h [56].

The extracellular synthesis mechanism relies on the interaction between fungal secreted proteins and aqueous cerium(III) nitrate, resulting in stable, monodisperse CNPs (average diameter ~ 16 nm) exhibiting mixed cerium oxidation states (Ce³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺), as confirmed by XPS. Protein capping of nanoparticles conferred dispersibility and stability. Complete characterization was achieved via UV-Vis, photoluminescence, TEM, FTIR, XRD, and XPS technique.

The extracellular biosynthesis of CNPs using the plant pathogenic fungus F. solani, yielding spherical particles ranging from 20 to 30 nm with a cubic fluorite structure. The study demonstrated that bio-organic molecules (including proteins, heterocyclic compounds, and amines) present in the fungal supernatant act as capping and stabilizing agents, preventing agglomeration through electrostatic interactions [57]. Biosynthesis occurred at ambient conditions (pH 6.8), with calcination at 400 °C required for crystallization. The nanoparticles exhibited significant antibacterial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, with the highest efficacy against P. aeruginosa, and demonstrated anti-biofilm properties confirmed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Table 2). Bacterial synthesis remains the least developed area of green synthesis approaches, with limited quantitative data available in the current literature.

Significant discrepancies exist in reported particle sizes for similar synthesis conditions across different research groups. For example, Azadirachta indica synthesis reports range from 8 nm (optimized conditions) to 45 nm (basic protocols), indicating methodology-dependent outcomes that are not adequately addressed in the current literature. For this challenge, some studies proved that standardized protocols and comparative methodologies are essential to enable reproducible synthesis across different research groups and highlighted critical standardization gaps that require systematic attention [58]. A performance comparison reveals a 3-fold variation in photocatalytic efficiency across similar synthesis approaches, attributed to differences in testing conditions, dye selection, and catalyst loading. This variability indicates the need for standardized testing protocols enabling meaningful performance comparison.

Biomolecules –assisted green synthesis of CNPs

In biomolecule-assisted synthesis, purified or semi-purified biological molecules are used as reducing and stabilizing reagents, which allows a higher level of control and reproducibility than natural plant extracts or microbial cultures. This method allows investigating the relationships between structures and activities systematically and explaining mechanisms [59, 60]. Amino acids, including L-cysteine, L-tyrosine, L-tryptophan, and L-histidine, have functional groups (thiol, phenolic hydroxyl, indole, imidazole), which are able to coordinate metals and reduce them. Peptides and proteins (bovine serum albumin, gelatin, lysozyme) offer numerous sites of binding and templates. Nanoparticles can be formed by other particles dependent on the isoelectric point and secondary structure [61, 62]. The polysaccharides that are used mainly as stabilizing agents are chitosan, starch, cellulose, dextran, and alginate based on hydrogen bonding and steric forces. A few polysaccharides having reducing end groups (such as reducing sugars produced in the hydrolysis of starch) may also be used in a metal reduction reaction. Nanoparticles that have polysaccharides as the stabilizer display high biocompatibility and biodegradability [63, 64]. Vitamin also help in green mediated synthesis such as the reducing agent Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) commonly utilized in the green synthesis. The oxidation of it to dehydroascorbic acid supplies electrons to reduce Ce4+. Other vitamins such as thiamine (B1), riboflavin (B2) and a-tocopherol (vitamin E) have been studied [65, 66]. Organic acid like citric acid, tartaric acid and oxalic acid serve as chelating agents that promote complexion of cerium ions, regulate rates of hydrolysis and stabilize nanoparticles by coordination with carboxylates. The acids are especially applicable in pH-controlled synthesis [67]. Enzymes like Oxidoreductase enzymes (glucose oxidase, laccase, peroxidase) are the most selective and efficient enzymes in oxidoreductase reactions. Synthesis by means of enzymes is carried out under mild conditions (physiological temperature and pH) and yield highly uniform nanoparticles. Large-scale applications are, however, limited by the cost and stability of enzymes [68]. The possibility of systematic optimization of reaction parameters (concentration, pH, temperature, time) by biomolecule-assisted synthesis and mechanistic studies by spectroscopic and computational methods has become available. The clear chemical formulas of biomolecules that have been purified permit us to design synthesis regimens in a rational manner, according to applications [69].

Comparison between green synthesis and chemical method of CNPs

The chemical synthesis is carried out by reducing the precursors in a soluble organic or aqueous medium with a strong synthetic reducing agent for the metal. Precise control of pH is continuously needed and sometimes various capping agents such as PVP are required for the process to prevent nanoparticle agglomeration [70]. These processes are very effective and yield reproducible product amounts, but may leave traces of contaminating toxic precursors on their surfaces, which prevents their unprocessed application in biomedical applications without costly and extensive purification. In contrast, Green synthesis combines the two steps of reduction and stabilization in a “one pot” reaction [71]. Biological extracts have a complex composition of antioxidants, sugar, proteins, and polyphenols that act as both reducing agents and capping agents. Such a biogenic capping layer can also provide intrinsic biological properties, such as increased antimicrobial, antioxidant, or anti-inflammatory activity and properties [72]. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) produced with “Miswak” (Salvadora persica L.) extract showed antibacterial activity equal to or higher than chemically synthesized AgNPs because of the synergism of the biofilm of bioactive phytochemicals that form the coating [73]. The industrial potential of green routes is emphasized by quantitative assessments. In fact, as per studies, green synthesis can result in a reduction of energy consumption by 30% and cost savings up to 40% compared to conventional chemical processes [74]. Besides, some reports indicate an improvement of the production output by 50% using the biomass route [74]. The environmental benefit of these approaches is that they eliminate hazardous waste streams and, according to the principles of green chemistry, have a significantly reduced carbon footprint.

Colloidal stability and biocompatibility are better in green-synthesized NPs compared to chemical-synthesized ones [75]. Chemical-synthesized NPs are crystallized with higher crystallinity and lower polydispersity. This intrinsic stability is believed to be due to the complex biological capping agents that offer multi-functional surface properties, suitable for drug delivery, wound healing and in vivo diagnostics [76]. Phytochemical-directed defect engineering of CNPs

Defect engineering directed by phytochemicals is one major step towards green synthesis of CNPs, from empirical biosynthesis to rational materials design. This strategy capitalizes on the natural features of plant-based phytochemicals, specifically, polyphenols, to regulate the appearance of certain defects structures within the CeO₂ lattice [77]. These defect structures are mainly surface oxygen vacancies and Ceria pre-eminent sites, play a key role in improving the catalytic and antioxidant activities of CeO₂ [78]. It is a selective coordination of phytochemicals, including ortho-diphenolic catechols, flavonols, and tannins, with Ce3+ ions at the nucleation and growth stages of synthetic of CeO₂ [79]. These phytochemicals are reducing and capping agents, which have an effect on the Ce3+ /Ce4+ which, trapping Ce3+-rich surface domains. This causes the development of non-stoichiometric CeO₂. In which an increased concentration of Ce exists [80]. There is an existing ionic alignment balancing the charge of oxygen vacancies (V). These oxygen vacancies are directly associated with the superior redox activity and catalytic properties of CeO₂. The easy exchange between Ce3+ and Ce4+, defines oxidation states that form the basis of how ceria can function as an oxygen buffer and replicate the activity of antioxidant enzymes. The defect architecture achieved through phytochemical-directed synthesis is hierarchically programmable [81]. The phytochemical identity selected has an effect on the nature of the vacancy that is formed (isolated or clustered). Indicatively, experiments with coffee husk extract that is abundant in polyphenols have been considered to produce CeO₂ with increased levels of oxygen vacancy and improved radical-scavenging functions [82]. In like manner, extracts of Terminalia arjuna present higher stability of green CeO₂ and improved antioxidant functions than chemically synthesized CeO₂. Phytochemical sources such as Centella asiatica, Phoenix dactylifera, Coriandrum sativum, Ocimum tenuiflorum, Camellia sinensis, Hibiscus flower, Polygonum bistorta Linn, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, Ginger (Zingiber officinale), Portulaca oleracea, Mentha royleana and Cannabis sativa L. have all been effectively utilized in the green synthesis of CeO₂, showing the wide variety of phytochemicals [83]. The concentration of the plant extract is also involved in the regulation of the density of oxygen vacancies, which can be measured using such methods as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) by evaluating the Cerium. Electron paramagnetic resonance at g = 1.96 also gives an indication of the presence of oxygen vacancies [84]. Moreover, thermal treatment or rather the calcification temperature after the synthesis, is also a crucial parameter that can be used to fine-tune the defect structure. Moderate heating, usually ranging between 300 °C and 450 °C, enhances the crystallinity level of the CeO₂ but still maintains a substantial density of defect sites [85]. As an example, the surface and bulk oxygen vacancies in ceria nanopolyhedrons calcined at 800 °C (P-800) and ceria nanorods calcined at 800 °C (R-800) had a higher concentration than that of the ceria nanopolyhedrons calcined at 500 °C (P-500 or R-500). The combination of crystallinity and defect density is maximized by this balance [86]. Nonetheless, calcinations above 500 °C could induce high oxygen vacancy healing since lattice oxygen diffusion and coalescence of particles can induce a decrease in the oxygen vacancy density and this will reduce the catalytic and antioxidant properties of the CeO₂. Vacancy distributions may be mapped and characterized using positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). The improved functional properties are achieved through phytochemical-directed engineering to give the enhanced defect structures. As an illustration, defect-engineered CeO₂ have been found to be highly-superoxide dismutase (SOD)-mimetic and oxidation turnover frequency of carbon monoxide (CO) in specific cases than CeO₂ prepared using traditional chemical techniques [87]. The efficiency of this method of synthesis is evident by direct correlation between functional superiority and specific phytochemical-induced defect fingerprints. These defects can be controlled, and so it is possible to tailor antioxidant enzyme-like activities and mimic either catalase (CAT) or superoxide dismutase (SOD) ratio and environmental pH. Strategic defect engineering of CeO₂. Green synthesis will be of significant importance to a wide range of applications due to the switching capability of Cerium in CeO₂. Defect-engineered CeO₂ may show pH-responsive redox switching, which allows the selective pro-oxidative action in the acidic tumor microenvironment to be facilitated during chemodynamic therapy without interfering with its antioxidant activity at neutral pH, which demonstrates the dual programmability of pH. The application of green synthesis technologies decreases toxicity issues that are related to chemical-based routes based on chemicals. The high content of oxygen vacancies on defect-engineered nano-ceria is vital in enhancing catalytic activity in different reactions. The vacancies serve as active adsorption sites and redox reagents, which can be used in environmental remediation, including the degradation of pollutants and CO oxidation. The photocatalytic capabilities of defect-engineered ceria ensure that it is useful in degrading toxic dyes and other environmental contaminants.

Mechanism of green synthesis of CNPs

The mechanism of green synthesis of ceria can be understood through its reduction mechanism, nucleation and growth dynamics and surface functionalization with stabilization.

Green synthesis via reduction mechanism

The processes of reducing Ce4+ to Ce3+ and forming CNPs in the process of green synthesis are a multi-step process which mediated by phytochemicals or microbial metabolites. These mechanisms are important to understand so as to optimize and control the properties of nanoparticles rationally [88]. Polyphenol compounds are the major reducing reagents in the majority of the plant extract-mediated syntheses. The process entails oxidation of the phenolic hydroxyl groups to quinones, and the electron transfer to metal ions simultaneously. In the case of a generic polyphenol that has two neighbouring hydroxyl groups (catechol moiety), the oxidation occurs; the emitted electrons oxidize Ce4+ ions in the starting salt [89]. When the pH exceeds 8, the Ce3+ ions are hydrolyzed and oxidized to produce cerium hydroxide, and later, CeO₂.In other cases, dissolved oxygen direct oxidizes Ce3+.

Polyphenols containing many hydroxyl groups (e.g. gallic acid has three OH groups) give more electrons per molecule and are more effective at reduced [90]. Flavonoids which are a subgroup of polyphenols, and have a typical C6-C3-C6 structure, also have comparable reduction mechanisms. The major sites of donation of electrons are the B-ring catechol and the C-ring keto-enol systems [91]. Flavonoids like quercetin and kaempferol are tautomerized and oxidized to give reactive intermediates that reduce cerium ions and at the same time, adsorbed onto the surface of nanoparticles to provide stabilization [92]. proteins and amino acids also play a part in reduction in several ways. Thiol side chains (-SH) of cysteine residues are strong reducing agents, which oxidize to disulfides (-S-S-) and release electrons. The phenolic and indole groups on the residues of tryptophan and tyrosine respectively are oxidized like polyphenols [93]. Also, peptide bonds may be involved in metal coordination, and enzymatic proteins can be used to catalyze redox reactions via their active sites [94]. The groups of sugars (glucose, fructose, maltose) contain free aldehyde or ketones groups that oxidize to produce carboxylic acids (e.g., glucose to gluconic acid), and this provides electrons to reduce metals [95]. The mechanism is of special relevance in the microbial synthesis when glucose is a widespread carbon source. Generally, polysaccharides do not contain reducing groups except when they have reducing end groups or they are hydrolyzed to release monosaccharides [96]. The reduction of metal ions by specialized enzymes is used by microorganisms in their detoxification and energy metabolism pathways. Ce4+ can also be reduced to Ce3+ by nitrate reductases that reduce NO3- to NO2- as a reaction. NADH dependent reductases move electrons out of NADH (generated in glycolysis and the citric acid cycle) to metal ions. Extracellular electron transfer may also be through cytochrome proteins of the electron transport chain [97]. Stable metal ions are chelated by the secretion of organic acids (citric acid, oxalic acid, malic acid) by fungi and bacteria regulating the pH and promoting hydrolysis and precipitation [98] (Figure 5).

Nucleation and growth dynamics of cerium oxide nanoparticle

Following precursor reduction and hydrolysis, nanoparticle formation proceeds through nucleation and growth processes through crystallization principles and modification through biomolecular interactions. After the deionization of cerium and formation of CeO₂ molecular species upto the level of supersaturation, homogeneous nucleation takes place [99]. CeO₂ molecules form small clusters, which form nuclei that are thermodynamically unstable below a critical radius (rc) with a lot of surface energy [100]. where γ is the energy on the surface, Vm is the molar volume, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, and S is the ratio of the super saturation. Approximately bigger than rc nuclei increase spontaneously and smaller clusters disintegrate [101]. The rate of nucleation is dependent on the level of super saturation, temperature and the availability of heterogeneous nucleation sites (aggregates of biomolecules, surfaces in containers) [102]. In green synthesis, biomolecules impact nucleation in various ways first, functional groups in biomolecules chelate cerium ions and reduce their concentration in solution; these lower the energy barrier to the formation of a nucleus and slow down the rate of super saturation, second biomolecule aggregates at heterogeneous nucleation sites, and then pH modulation by organic acids impacts the hydrolysis kinetics and supersaturation [103]. When stable nuclei are created, its growth occurs in two major ways: diffusion-limited and reaction-limited. CeO₂ molecules in the bulk solution are allowed to diffuse to the surface of the nanoparticle and embedded into the crystal structure [104] The rate of growth is regulated by the concentration gradient and diffusion coefficient. The given mechanism usually results in the creation of spherical nanoparticles as those with quite narrow size distributions [105]. The rate of growth is determined by surface reactions (adsorption, surface diffusion, incorporation into lattice) slower than bulk diffusion. The result of this mechanism is anisotropic growth and faceted morphologies when other crystallographic facets have differing reaction rates [106] (Figure 6). During the growth process, biomolecules are adsorbed to the surfaces of nanoparticles and regulate crystal development through facet binding and capping effects. Relative growth is also regulated by selective adsorption on individual crystallographic facets (e.g. relative binding to a particular crystallographic face, such as to a {100} face rather than a {111} face). Increased biomolecule concentrations give high surface coverage, which lowers growth rates and results in small nanoparticles. This is similar to the conventional method of surfactant-directed synthesis [107, 108].

Plant extract, such as polyphenols attacked on cerium peripheral bond and form cerium-polyphenols complex which in turns an intermediate stage form. This intermediate readily form a cubic fluorite structure of cerium oxide nanoparticles on dehydration and nucleation.

Later stages of synthesis involve Ostwald ripening, where larger nanoparticles grow at the expense of smaller ones because of the size-dependent solubility of the Gibbs-Thomson equation. Particles with larger surface energy dissolve faster and the released species is recrystallized on other particles with lower surface energy [109]. Ostwald ripening causes size distribution to be broadened with time. Ripping can be prevented through biomolecule capping layers, which lower the difference in surface energies and sterically impede dissolution and re-deposition [110]. Aggregation and stabilization is due to attractive van der Waals forces and repulsive electrostatic or steric forces between nanoparticles in a solution. A balance between these forces, which is explained by the DLVO (Derjaguin-Landau-Verwey-Overbeek) theory, is what defines the stability in the colloid. Biomolecules bring about stabilization by electrostatic stabilization. Ionizable functional groups (carboxyl, amine, phosphate) on adsorbed biomolecules form a charged surface layer and electrical double layer, which repels aggregation by producing repulsive forces [111]. The surface charge is expressed as the zeta potential (z), and normally the zeta potential of the green-synthesized CNPs average to -20 to -50 mV, which is a good indicator of colloidal stability [112]. Large biomolecules (proteins, polysaccharides) are able to provide a physical barrier around nanoparticles. The osmotic repulsion and elastic compression occur during the approach of two particles as a result of overlapping the adsorbed layers which inhibit the direct contact and aggregation. Steric stabilization is especially good in high ionic strength media where it is screened [113, 114]. Therefore, the mechanistic pathway of green synthesis encompasses not only precursor reduction but also nucleation, crystal growth, and colloidal stabilization, collectively determining nanoparticle size, morphology, and long-term stability [115, 116].

Molecular-level reaction chemistry of Green-synthesized CNPs

Molecular-level chemistry underlying ceria formation, particularly coordination interactions, cerium speciation, and sequential condensation reactions that establish the cerium oxide lattice. Green synthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles includes coordination chemistry and redox transformations of cerium precursors. Cerium is unique among lanthanides because of its accessible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple, which shows its catalytic and biomedical relevance [117]. At the molecular level, dissolved cerium ions initially undergo coordination with biogenic ligands derived from plant or microbial systems [118]. These ligands commonly contain hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amine groups capable of chelating Ce3+ or Ce4+.ions and generating coordination complexes [119]. The delivery of Ce3+ ions in solution is often followed by coordination events of biogenic ligands [120]. These biomolecule-derived ligands are generally functionalized, typically containing functional groups such as hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), and amine (-NH2) groups [121]. These functional groups are chelating agents and create stable complexes with the Ce3+ ions [122]. Chelation play an important role in the stabilization of the cerium ions in aqueous environment and the control of the hydrolysis rate of the ion, thus regulating the nucleation and growth of nanoparticles [123]. As an example, high magnetic fields have the ability to increase the cerium loading capacity of peptide cerium(III) chelates meaning that coordination bonds are important [124]. The oxidation of Ce3+ is mainly electrostatic in nature [125, 126]. The involvement of Ce3+ is of special interest, which in most cases is associated with the reactivity of ceria nanomaterials in numerous applications [127]. Oxidation of dissolved Ce3+ can be driven by different factors such as the oxygen in the air or the mild oxidating agents within the medium of reaction [128]. In green synthesis, biomolecules found in plant extracts have the potential to serve as reducing and capping reagents to promote the development and stability of CNPs [129]. It has been demonstrated that the concentration of Ce3+ and Ce4+ may be accurately measured by experimental methods like the X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) and the X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) [130]. Hydrolysis of coordinated cerium species constitutes the next molecular transformation leading oxide network formation [131]. The formation of the Ce-O-Ce network, typical of ceria, is also catalyzed by a series of hydrolysis, olation, and oxolation reactions. The hydrolysis of the cerium ions with water results in the creation of aquo and hydroxo species. Tetravalent cerium (Ce4+) is reported to hydrolyze easier as compared to trivalent cerium (Ce3+) [132]. The hydrolysis process in general may be depicted as the substitution of ligands or water molecules already attached to the cerium ion with hydroxide ions [133]. An example is the hydrothermal synthesis of cubic CNPs, in which the homogeneous precipitation is commonly prepared by the addition of acetate ions, which affect the morphology of nanoparticles [134]. After hydrolysis, olation reactions take place and the metal-hydroxide species condense to form polymeric structures by formation of M-O-M bridges via µ2-OH complex. It involves the removal of water molecules between two hydroxyl groups attached to various metal centers. They are then oxidized by the formation of µ2-O bridges which are more stable than the µ2-OH bridges through a process called oxolation to create a strong Ce-O-Ce network [135]. It is a similar process to the sol-gel chemistry, extensively utilized in the synthesis of inorganic materials, where reactions formed by hydrolysis and condensation construct the inorganic backbone [136]. These processes are strongly affected by the pH and temperature of reaction medium, which has an impact on the size, morphology and crystallinity of the product CNPs. As an example, a layered phase, Ce-I, having a proposed formula CeIV(OH)2(CO3)x.nH2O, has been found to be an intermediate in the assembly of CNPs from cerium ammonium nitrate [137]. Hydrothermal conditions can be used to convert cerium oxalate precursors to CeO₂, shows the evidence of the significance of water coordination modes and ligand effect [138]. The precursor and the synthesis method, e.g. microwave combustion or sol-gel, also influences the end result of the CNPs [139]. The biogenic ligands have chelating groups which can be occasionally displaced or converted, especially upon heating or aging, as these polynuclear intermediates increase in size, and it is possible to grow the fluorite-structured CeO2 crystal [140]. This is a structural change of Ce(OH)3 to nanoscale CeO₂ often involves an oxyhydroxide intermediate phase [141]. The regulation of these crystallization processes including the co-precipitation synthesis of cerium carbonate, can produce unique self-assembled spherical cerium oxide particles with superior performances [142]. These consecutive reactions and close regulation of synthesis conditions ensure the final morphology and size of the CNPs. As an example, researchers have managed to use Spirulina platensis extract to synthesize crystalline and stable ceria nanoparticles, indicating that green synthesis is effective to regulate such processes [143]. Synthesis of zinc-doped cerium oxide nanoparticles by the use of Tulsi leaf extract as a reducing agent is also a good example of how plant extracts can be used to produce nanoparticles with specific morphology and properties [144]. Moreover, terminating with the green synthesis of CeO₂ and CeO₂ extracts of Terminalia arjuna, the latter employing biomolecules in catalyzing such intricate chemical reactions is more advantageous than chemically synthesized CeO₂ since the CeO₂ is found to have enhanced stability and antioxidant properties to conduct the reaction [145].All in all, coordination chemistry and redox reactions of cerium precursors in the process of green synthesis play a vital role in dictating the synthesis of CNPs with specific properties [146]. The interactions between the biogenic chelating agents, the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple, sequential hydrolysis, olation, and oxolation reactions determine the final structure, morphology and functionality of the resulting ceria nanomaterials.

Parameters for green synthesis

The mechanism of green synthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles represents a complex multi-step process involving the coordinated action of various bioactive compounds present in plant extracts and microbial systems. Understanding these mechanistic pathways is crucial for optimizing synthesis parameters is crucial for achieving controlled nanoparticle properties for specific applications. This includes critical parameters such as preparation methods, concentration and time, pH and temperature control that govern green synthesis processes and their influence on the final nanoparticle characteristics.

Extract preparation methods

Extract preparation is a fundamental parameter that determines the availability and concentration of bioactive compounds responsible for nanoparticle synthesis and stabilization. The choice between fresh and dried plant materials significantly affects the phytochemical composition and the outcomes of synthesis [147]. Ahmed et al. utilized fresh Abelmoschus esculentus material to preserve bioactive compounds, resulting in nanoparticles with enhanced wound-healing and anticancer activities, while other studies employ dried materials for standardization advantages. A comparative analysis reveals trade-offs between phytochemical preservation and process standardization [148]. On the other hand, some studies employed controlled drying procedures to maintain phytochemical integrity while enabling reproducible extract preparation for Melastoma sp. synthesis, representing a compromise approach balancing preservation with standardization. Limited comparative studies evaluate the effects of fresh vs. dried material on synthesis outcomes. This represents a critical research gap requiring systematic investigation across multiple plant species and applications [149].

Concentration and time optimization

The optimization of extract concentration and reaction time is an interconnected set of parameters that requires systematic investigation to achieve reproducible synthesis with the desired characteristics. A concentration-optimization study reveals significant advancement in methodological sophistication. They demonstrated the effects of extract concentration and reaction time on nanoparticle formation in the synthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles using Melastoma sp. leaf extract [150]. Additionally, it is the only paper that looked into how optimization of extract concentration and reaction time affects cerium oxide nanoparticle formation. The current literature provides insufficient mechanistic understanding of time-dependent synthesis processes, including nucleation kinetics, growth mechanisms, and stabilization dynamics.

pH control and effects

pH represents the most critical parameter influencing green synthesis outcomes, directly affecting ionization state of bioactive compounds, the stability of cerium precursors, and the nucleation kinetics of CeO₂ formation. The pH optimization requires careful consideration of multiple interconnected factors that collectively determine synthesis success and nanoparticle quality [151].

Analysis of pH optimization reveals significant methodological advancement. Early investigations employed empirical pH screening without mechanistic understanding, whereas recent studies integrate systematic pH optimization with phytochemical analysis and mechanistic modelling. Hasan et al. (2024) reported optimized synthesis parameters for Melastoma sp.-mediated CNPs, demonstrating that optimal conditions at pH 7, using 5 g/150 mL leaf extract concentration with 3 hours’ reaction time, yielded spherical morphology with particle size of 16 nm. This represents the first systematic parameter optimization study using Melastoma sp. leaf extract as a capping agent for cerium oxide nanoparticle synthesis. The mechanistic understanding of pH effects has evolved significantly [152]. Researchers observed systematic pH-dependent size control, noting that increasing synthesis pH results in particle size reduction, with nanoparticles ranging between 10 and 20 nm exhibiting spheroidal morphology, establishing the foundation for subsequent mechanistic investigations [153].

Conflicting results across different research groups reveal significant challenges with the transferability of pH optimization. While Hasan et al. (2024) achieved optimal results at pH 7 for Melastoma sp., Darroudi et al. (2014) employed pH 10 conditions using ammonium hydroxide precipitation in starch-mediated synthesis, demonstrating that alkaline conditions facilitate rapid precipitation while starch molecules terminate particle growth and provide stabilization. This discrepancy in the optimal pH range for synthesis (7 vs. 10) indicates plant-specific optimization requirements that current mechanistic models do not adequately address. Choudary et al. (2024) partially addressed this limitation, proved that pH affects the protonation state of functional groups in plant extracts, directly modulating their electron-donating capacity and coordination behavior with cerium ions. Despite extensive pH-optimization studies, the fundamental mechanistic understanding remains limited. Studies also identified this critical gap, noting that specific roles played by individual phytochemicals in pH-dependent synthesis processes remain poorly understood, limiting rational optimization capabilities [154]. The proton-coupled electron transfer mechanisms governing the reduction of Ce4+ to Ce³⁺ require systematic investigation across a different pH range and plant sources. The current literature provides insufficient quantitative data on reduction kinetics, activation energies, and the thermodynamic parameters governing pH-dependent synthesis outcomes [155].

Temperature and calcination conditions

Temperature control represents a crucial parameter balancing reaction kinetics with the preservation of bioactive compounds while enabling complete conversion to crystalline CeO₂ phases. Early studies employed trial-and-error temperature screening (ambient to 100 °C), while contemporary research integrates thermal analysis with mechanistic modeling for systematic optimization. Miri et al. (2021) demonstrated successful ambient temperature synthesis using banana peel extract, achieving “nanoparticles with sizes ranging from 4–13 nm while maintaining excellent biocompatibility with non-toxic behavior below 500 µg/mL concentrations.” This ambient-temperature approach represents an energy reduction of 60–80% compared to conventional chemical synthesis, which requires 300–800 °C [156] (Figure 7).

Applications of green-synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles

Catalytic and photocatalytic applications

Overall, the catalytic and photocatalytic potential of green-synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO₂ NPs) is a significant step towards sustainable environmental cleanup, which leverages the special redox capabilities of ceria [160]. The reversible reduction and oxidation between Ce³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺ is the key mechanism that underlies these applications, allowing the formation of oxygen vacancies and efficient storage and release of oxygen [161]. This redox activity makes CeO₂ NPs effective catalysts for different kinds of chemical reactions such as the degradation of organic pollutants, oxidation of CO, and sonocatalytic processes [162]. Recent literature has focused on green synthesis, whereby plant extracts or microbial products are used to reduce and stabilize nanoparticles, which are more environmentally friendly and improve the catalytic activity of the materials by surface functionalizing them with natural compounds [163]. A key application in the dye degradation field is using green synthesized CeO₂ NPs for the treatment of industrial wastewater polluted with hazardous organic dyes. The cube fluorite crystal structure CeO₂ NPs produced from the Saraca asoca leaf extract, for example, have a mean particle size of 15.48 nm and efficiently decompose Rhodamine B under the UV light irradiation [164]. Likewise, the CeO₂ NPs produced through the green technique with the leaf extract of Camphora exhibits a sharp absorption band at 352 nm and a minimum band gap. The energy gap of 3.52 eV suggest that the material has high photocatalytic activity for dye degradation [165]. This activity is further increased by integrating the dopants; the synergistic photocatalytic activity of silver-doped CeO₂ NPs prepared using Syzygium cumini leaf extract is observed against cationic dyes, where the enhanced surface area and redox properties of the NPs are responsible for enhanced activity [166]. Apart from this, ethylene glycol (EG) assisted synthesis of CeO₂ NPs has been reported to decrease the average particle size of CeO₂ NPs from 15 to 9 nm that enhance the surface area and increases the number of oxygen vacancies in the CeO₂ structure leading to better adsorption and photocatalytic degradation of Acid Orange 7 dye [167]. The results highlight the need for structural tuning to achieve optimal photocatalytic activity of CeO₂ NPs for particular targets of pollutant to ensure maximum efficiency. Beyond photo-catalysis Carbon monoxide (CO) oxidation is a crucial reaction for reducing the emissions from automotive vehicles and is also one of the reactions where CeO₂ NPs play an important role. The oxygen defects in the ceria lattice can adsorb and activate the CO molecules, which allows the conversion of CO molecules into CO2 through Mars-van Krevelen mechanism [168]. The recent studies of CeO₂ NPs show their multifunctional catalytic properties with high efficiency in the photodegradation of dangerous pollutants like nitrobenzene and methyl red and with an excellent antioxidant activity [169]. The dual function implies that CeO₂ NPs can potentially be used as multi-purpose catalysts for environmental matrices containing multiple pollutants. In addition, the green synthesis methods, like using Cleome gynandra seed extract, introduce an additional enhancement to the electrochemical properties of CeO₂ NPs, which are suitable for hybrid applications where both photocatalytic degradation and electrochemical sensing/energy storage are required [170]. The green-synthesized CeO₂ NPs are now applied in a new field of research known as sonocatalysis, which provides a promising alternative technology to the photocatalytic systems. The formation of cavitation bubbles by applying ultrasonic energy results in very high temperatures and pressures at the bubble center and activates the CeO₂ NPs as a source of ROS [159]. Some of the ROS, such as hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions are able to effectively reduce the organic pollutants in the dark, which overcomes some of the limitations of traditional photocatalysis. Recent work has optimized reaction conditions for the photo-induced degradation of methylene blue using Response Surface Methodology (RSM-CCD model). They applied green synthesized rare earth metal oxide CeO₂ NPs to photoreduction of and CO2 with good precision of catalytic efficiency [171]. The photocatalytic and sonocatalytic activity of CeO₂ NPs demonstrates their versatility and durability in environmental cleanup applications. The use of green-synthesized CeO₂ NPs in wastewater treatment can be further extended to heavy metals and other persistent organic pollutants (POPs) removal. The surface area and surface hydroxyl groups of biosynthesized CeO₂ NPs are high, which enables them to have a large number of adsorption active sites, thereby removing toxic ions from the water [172]. Comparative studies of chemically synthesized CeO₂ NPs and green-synthesized CeO₂ NPs show that the latter have better dispersion stability and less toxicity, which can be traced to the natural capping agent of derived from plants antioxidants [173]. These biomolecules neither stabilize the nanoparticles, but also impart extra functionality including natural antimicrobial activity that works synergistically with the intrinsic catalytic ability of the ceria core [174].There are still a number of problems that need to be addressed for instance, standardization of green synthesis conditions to batch to batch uniformity and control of particle size and morphology very important parameters for large scale applications in industry [175].The detailed mechanistic interactions between the green capped CeO₂ NPs with different pollutants and/or optimization of their performance in the complex environmental matrices are future research directions. Future research on doped and composite CeO₂ materials, including those with Cd doping and Ag coating, can lead to further advancements in catalytic efficiency and selectivity, For instance, NPs involving multi-element doping such as Cd in CeO₂ and Ag on CeO₂ have been prepared using Cymbopogon citratus leaf extract, and exhibited superior activity for the reduction of nitrophenols and photocatalytic degradation of pollutants [176]. In conclusion, the combination of green synthesis and advanced catalytic engineering renders CeO₂ NPs invaluable allies in the fight for sustainable and efficient environmental remediation solutions, providing promising avenues for solving key challenges in water purification and air quality control (Figure 8) [177].

Environmental remediation

Green synthesized nanomaterials for environmental remediation are one of the major breakthroughs in the solution of the global water contamination problem especially in the case of heavy metals, fluoride, uranium, organic pollutants etc. Recent literature has pointed to the superior efficacy of specialized adsorbents like, however, cerium oxide nanoparticles have great promise in these fields for their redox-active surface and high oxygen storage capacity. All these materials are expected to be used for specific contaminant removal: Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs), Covalent Organic Frameworks, and modified biochars [178]. Remediation of heavy metals, fluoride and uranium needs various mechanistic approaches that vary from electrostatic attraction, ion exchange to complexation and surface precipitation, depending on the specific physicochemical properties of the adsorbent and the pollutants to be removed [179]. Heavy metal pollution remains a constant environmental issue, with sources such as pollution from industry, mining operations, and agricultural practices, thus requiring effective remediation measures [180]. The adsorption method is a well-known process for heavy metal removal because of its simplicity, low cost and high efficiency [181]. Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs) are a new class of adsorbents that have been shown to be highly porous with large surface area and tunable chemical functionality, making them promising adsorbents for heavy metals [181]. MOF composites, like MOF-2 based on Al2O3, have been recently studied due to their superior stability and adsorption ability for heavy metals that results from the synergic effect between the MOF organic linker and the inorganic node [182]. Further, the green synthesis methods, which use waste materials like date palm waste derived, activated carbon, provide environmentally friendly solutions to the removal of Cd, Pb, Cr and Zn from aqueous solutions [183]. The mass transfer mechanisms in such systems and the potential of maximizing adsorption capacity depend critically on the adsorption kinetics and isotherm models, such as Langmuir and Freundlich equations [184]. Ground water pollution caused by fluoride is an important health problem and is responsible for the use of the advanced defluoridation technologies because of severe health risks such as dental and skeletal fluorosis [184]. Fluoride is highly removed by the metal-based adsorbents, especially those which contain aluminium, magnesium and lanthanum; [185]. Recent developments in MOFs have witnessed great potential for this direction, and structural modifications to improve the stability and adsorption capability of MOFs have been focused [186]. MOFs based on MOF(Zr) and MOF(Ce) composite adsorbents have been developed for the purpose of removing excess fluoride from water, for example, due to synergistic effect of the zirconium and cerium nodes, the adsorption kinetics and capacity have been improved [187]. In addition, biochars can be modified and incorporated with other additives such as MgO/, FeOOH or FeOCl biochar For fluoride remediation, composites provide efficient and low cost solutions: magnesium components, and the formation of stable fluoride complexes, allow chemical adsorption to take place [188].Optimization of such materials requires careful control of the synthesis conditions in order to maximize the availability of active sites and minimize the competitive adsorption of other anions [187]. Uranium contamination occurs in nature and is a consequence of nuclear fuel cycle activities and poses particular problems because of its radioactivity and chemical toxicity [187]. The adsorption with advanced materials like MOFs, COFs, and Functionalized MXenes has been paid much attention for the removal of uranium [189]. Recent developments show the use of Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs) containing phosphonate groups, including TpDBD-PHos, that display excellent adsorption performance towards uranium owing to the strong uranium-adsorbent interactions between the phosphonate group and uranyl ion. The materials exhibited high removal efficiencies even in complex environmental matrices like real rain water samples, which can be considered as a potential application [190]. Moreover, the amidoxime-functionalized MXene beads have been designed to perform the efficient removal of uranium ions from the wastewater containing high fluoride concentration, demonstrating a dual removal capability for multiple pollutants [191]. In addition, biochar from raw material such as Citrullus lanatus seed and modified by magnetic nanoparticles will be a sustainable and efficient material for uranium remediation [192]. Removal of organic pollutants, in addition to the removal of heavy metal and radionuclides, is also dealt with by multifunctional materials which possess catalytic/photocatalytic adsorption properties [193]. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) such as photocatalysis and piezocatalysis are methods in which reaction between the reactive oxygen species and the persistent organic pollutants (POPs) leads to mineralization [194]. MIPs and nanocomposites are being investigated for the capacity to target and destroy specific pollutants selectively, while preserving structural integrity [195]. These degradation processes can be combined with adsorption processes to provide all-in-one solutions for wastewater treatment for both organic and inorganic contaminants [196]. Mesoporous MOF composites can be used effectively to remove uranium, methyl orange, and methylene blue. The aqueous solution of these dyes was used to stain blood cells, which further confirmed their versatility in treating complex industrial effluents such as Congo red dyes [197]. Although significant progress has been made in the development of adsorbent materials, there are still some potential challenges to overcome in order to use them more widely. Preserving MOFs and COFs in aqueous environments, especially in acidic or basic conditions, is still a challenge to further improve the stability of MOFs and COFs. In addition, the regeneration and reuse of adsorbents are essential for economic viability and environmental sustainability, with the development of any materials with high desorption efficiency and with a minimum loss of capacity [198]. The scalability for the green synthesis of these advanced materials also need to be tackled to address the industrial requirements [199]. Optimization of the structural properties of adsorbents, improvement of selectivity with regard to particular contaminants, and the integration of them into continuous flow systems for practical wastewater treatment applications should be the object of future research [200]. The exploration and development of materials with hybrid characteristics which combine the benefits of various classes of adsorbents, e.g. MOF–Biochar composites, appears to have great potential for the remediation performance.

Bio-sensing and diagnostic applications

The incorporation of green synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO₂ NPs) into biosensing and diagnostic platforms marks a pioneering advancement in the fields of analytical chemistry and biomedical engineering. The peculiar electrochemical properties of CeO₂ are associated with the reversible Ce3+/Ce4 + redox couple and the related oxygen vacancies and can be exploited to make ultrasensitive detection devices [201]. The high catalytic activity, large surface area and rapid electron transfer kinetics are essential for the improvement of sensitivity and selectivity of electrochemical biosensors, and these nanoparticles are good electrode modifiers [202]. The recent progress has shown that green synthesis methods greatly enhances the biocompatibility of these nanomaterials, and also introduces natural capping agents to further functionalize the surface of the sensor, which enhances the stability and decreases the non-specific binding in complex biological matrices [203]. CeO₂ NPs have been widely used in the field of electrochemical biosensors for the detection of small biomolecules and disease markers. CeO₂ nanocubes synthesized by co-precipitation have been used for the selective and simultaneous determination of dopamine in the presence of interfering species e.g., uric acid and ascorbic acid. Separate oxidation peaks can be resolved by the different electrocatalytic activity of CeO₂, which allows the accurate quantitation of multiple analytes even in physiological conditions in which multiple analytes occur simultaneously. Moreover, novel composite materials like AMNFs@ZIF-67 nano composites extended the detection limit of specific cancer biomarkers, such as HER2, which further showed promise for clinical diagnostics using CeO₂ based hybrids [204]. While the majority of recent research in the field of HER2 detection has been based on ZIF-67 composites, the principles underlying metal-oxide nanostructure application for signal amplification are directly transferable to the CeO₂-based systems and reflect a general trend in the design of ultrasensitive immunosensors [205].

The green synthesis of CeO₂ NPs can be further expanded in the field of food safety monitoring, where timely and accurate identification of food contaminants is crucial. CeO₂ NPs are used to chemically modify electrochemical sensors that can be used for the detection of pesticides, heavy metals, and foodborne pathogens in a portable and cost-effective way, as opposed to the use of traditional chromatographic methods. These nanoparticles have a high surface-to-volume ratio, which makes them more effective at binding target analytes, and their redox activity makes it easier to convert these analytes into detectable signals. The fact that plant-derived materials (e.g., Polygonum bistorta) can be used as phytochemical capping agents to synthesize CeO₂ NPs could also account for the specific recognition of organic pollutants, but more studies are required to elucidate these molecular interactions [206].New models of ultrasensitive detection systems based on CeO₂ NPs are increasingly being based on hybrid architectures, where the synergistic effects of combining the catalytic properties of ceria with other nanomaterials are utilized. The effective surface area of the working electrode can be enhanced and the charge transfer resistance can be greatly diminished by incorporating CeO₂ with conductive polymers or carbonaceous materials [207]. In early-stage disease diagnosis, where the detection of low-abundance biomarkers is crucial, this approach will be especially useful for signal amplification. The mimicry of enzyme functions by CeO₂ NPs, like peroxidase, also presents opportunities for dual-mode colorimetric and electrochemical sensor development, which offers a great platform for point-of-care diagnostics. The characteristic switchable redox state of cerium makes it possible to dynamically react to variations in the local environment (e.g., pH, oxidative stress) and, for this reason, these nanoparticles are suitable for smart sensing applications, where the real-time monitoring of physiological parameters is required [204].

Although significant progress has been made, there are still some challenges to be addressed before the large-scale implementation of green-synthesized CeO₂ NP-based biosensors. For commercial application, the established protocols for the green synthesis need to be standardized to ensure batch-to-batch reproducibility in terms of particle size, morphology and surface chemistry. The characteristics of the nanoparticles generated can also vary depending on the variability in the composition of the plant extract, which may impact the performance and reliability of the sensors, Furthermore, knowledge of the stability of the bio-capping layers in operational conditions is important for the assurance of the stability of these sensors employed in field applications. The detailed mechanism of the effect of green capping agents on electrochemical behavior of CeO₂ NPs needs to be further understood and further investigation of capping agents derived from biological sources and CeO₂ NPs combinations is recommended to achieve more sensitive and selective sensors [208]. The prospect of multifunctional CeO₂-based nanocomposites for next-generation diagnostic tools with high sensitivity, environmental-friendly and economical is very promising.

Biomedical applications

This property of native antioxidant enzymes mimicry is the basis of the fundamental applications of CeO₂ NPs in biomedical applications, which are attributed to the reversible redox cycling between Ce3⁺/Ce4⁺, and the generation of oxygen vacancies on the surface of the CeO₂ NPs [209]. The intrinsic redox activity of CeO₂ NPs makes them very effective scavengers of ROS, an important pathological process involved in many diseases such as cancer, chronic inflammation, and impaired wound healing [210]. These green synthesis protocols, which use plant extracts or microbial products as reducing and capping agents, also nutralize the surface functionalization of these nanoparticles increasing their biocompatibility and therapeutic effects, especially due to the reduced potential cytotoxicity when compared to chemically synthesized nanoparticles [211]. The antioxidant activity of CeO₂ NPs is mainly due to their enzyme-mimetic properties, such as the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase mimetics, that allow them to transform superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide into less harmful molecules [212]. Recent research, however, using the method of self-combustion synthesis has shown that CeO₂ NPs have an impressive antioxidant activity that is directly related to the concentration of surface oxygen vacancies [213]. This ROS-scavenging ability also includes radioprotection by which CeO₂ NPs are found to be effective at protecting healthy tissues from damage caused by free radicals formed during the exposure to ionizing radiation [214]. The key ions in this respect are Ce³⁺ which actively involves in the electron transfer processes necessary for radical neutralization and maintain cell integrity under oxidative stress conditions. Variants obtained by green synthesis, including variants obtained using the extract of Abelmoschus esculentus, have been specifically assessed for antioxidant activity, demonstrating high antioxidant activity against free radicals, which makes them suitable for use in protection therapeutic regimes, CeO₂ NPs produced from A. esculentus extract have been shown to possess potent wound healing properties, which are believed to be mediated by their ability to stimulate cell proliferation and migration and regulate inflammation [215]. In the field of wound healing, CeO₂ NPs exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which, when combined, help to heal wounds more quickly. A chronic wound may be inflamed and have a high ROS level, slowing the healing process [211]. CeO₂ NPs counteract this by decreasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative damage in the wound bed. The green synthesis approach makes it possible to obtain nanoparticles that are functionalized with bioactive phytochemicals that can additionally influence the inflammatory environment, improving the therapeutic effect. Green synthesized CeO₂ NPs exert antimicrobial action by several mechanisms such as disruption of the bacterial cell wall, inhibition of important enzymatic activities, and generation of ROS, which results in oxidative stress in microbial cells]. NPs prepared using the various plant extracts like Polygonum bistorta and Cleome gynandra have been found to have strong antibacterial activity against Gram positive and Gram negative bacteria [216]. The ability of these nano particles to interact with the bacterial cell wall because of the small size of the particles and high surface area results in the structural damage of the bacterial cell wall and cell lysis [217]. Additionally, the catalytic formation of ROS with oxygen vacancies boosts the antimicrobial activity without the need for high nanomaterials concentration. CeO₂ NPs also exhibit selective cytotoxicity towards cancer cells, which is mainly attributed to the acidic tumour microenvironment [218]. The ability of the lower pH in tumour tissues to promote the Ce³+ state, which can serve as a pro-oxidant, leading to ROS production, DNA fragmentation and mitochondrial dysfunction and thereby trigger apoptosis in tumour cells, has been shown. On the other hand, CeO₂ NPs are anti-oxidants in a normal physiological pH and can protect healthy cells from oxidative damages. Some recent developments include the synthesis of folate linked chitosan-coated CeO₂ NPs using ananas leaves extract with improved selective anticancer bioactivity against human MCF-7 breast cancer cells [219]. Furthermore, hemp leaf extract-mediated CeO₂ NPs have been studied as anticancer agents, which shows a considerable inhibitory effect on cancer cell proliferation. Even the combination of CeO₂ NPs with composite structures like magnetic multi-walled carbon nanotubes can increase their targeting ability and therapeutic index in cancer therapy [220]. Due to their high surface area and tunable surface chemistry, CeO₂ NPs are promising candidates for drug delivery applications, Various biomolecules can be attached to the green-synthesized CeO₂ NPs for enhancing their stability, biocompatibility, and targeting specificity. For example, the chitosan coating of CeO₂ NPs not only stabilizes the nanoparticles, but also gives them extra mucoadhesive properties, enabling prolonged drug release [221] (Figure 9). CeO₂ NPs can release therapeutic agents in a controlled fashion in response to the oxidative status of the target tissue, enabling precision medicine approaches. Although further research is required to optimize their pharmacokinetic profiles, recent reviews point towards the potential of these nanoparticles to become multifunctional platforms combining drug delivery, diagnostic imaging and therapeutic monitoring. This thorough assessment of green-NPs of CeO₂ in these biomedical applications highlights their versatility and therapeutic potential in the clinic. The synergistic effect of the intrinsic physicochemical properties of ceria with the positive effect of green synthesis capping agents provides a powerful platform to develop next generation therapeutics. To ensure safety and efficacy in human applications, further research is required to elucidate the long-term biodistribution and clearance mechanisms of the nanoparticles.

Environmental and biological implications of green-synthesized CeO₂ NPs

The environmental and biological implications of green-synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles represent critical considerations for their sustainable development and practical implementation. Understanding the biocompatibility profiles, environmental behaviour, and ecosystem effects is essential to ensuring safe applications while maximizing the benefits of these eco-friendly nanomaterials. This section examines the biological and environmental aspects of green-synthesized CNPs, providing insights into their safety profiles and ecological impact.

Biocompatibility and toxicity aspects of green synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticle

The biocompatibility of green-synthesized CNPs represents a fundamental advantage over conventionally synthesized counterparts, primarily attributed to the presence of bioactive organic coronas that enhance cellular compatibility and reduce cytotoxic responses. Systematic evaluation reveals remarkable improvement in safety profiles over the research period [222] (Figure 10).

Diagrammatic illustration of plant- and microbe-derived cerium nanoparticles (CNPs) and their biological safety features and environmental applications

Despite widespread claims of enhanced biocompatibility, systematic analysis reveals significant methodological inconsistencies across studies. Mahmoodi et al. (2023) acknowledged this variability, stating that “cytotoxicity evaluation on CT26 cell lines revealed no toxicity in tested concentration ranges [223]. Darroudi et al. (2014) reported non-toxic effects at concentrations below 25 mg/mL on Neuro2A cells using honey-mediated synthesis, representing a higher toxicity threshold compared to other studies [224]. The differences in the observed cytotoxicity and biocompatibility studies can be explained by differences in the physicochemical properties of CeO₂ nanoparticles and experimental models used. The ratio of Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ and the associated oxygen-vacancy density are some of the most affective [225]. The surface Ce³⁺ sites are usually enriched in nanoparticles with superior redox cycling and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-mimetic activity, which allow them to efficiently scavenge the reactive oxygen species (ROS) and to have superior cytoprotective effects. Too many defects or changes in redox however can lead to pro-oxidant activity which can lead to greater oxidative stress and cell damage. Therefore, slight differences in synthesis technique, which change the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio can have an impact on biological results [225]. The size of the particles also has a significant influence on toxicity profiles. Smaller nanoparticles have larger surface area-to-volume ratios, more oxygen vacancies, and higher rates of cell uptake which lead to higher levels of biological reactivity. These properties can enhance antioxidant and therapeutic activity, but may also lead to dose-dependent cytotoxicity or intracellular accumulation. The differences are further exacerbated by surface functionalization [226]. A phytochemical, protein, polysaccharide or any other biogenic molecule is generally used to coat CNPs, thereby enhancing colloidal stability, minimizing aggregation, and regulating the interaction of CNPs with cells [227]. Often the bioactive surface coronas increase biocompatibility compared to chemically synthesized nanoparticles without natural capping layers. Furthermore, there are many other factors that influence the divergent toxicity levels reported in the literature, such as cell type, uptake mechanisms, metabolic rate, antioxidant defense ability, exposure time, and assay methodology. Thus, direct comparison between different studies is dependent on the careful consideration of the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles and experimental conditions. While in vitro toxicity studies have been conducted the majority of research is focused on this area, there is some promising in vivo research on the safety and therapeutic potential of cerium oxide nanoparticles. Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, radioprotective, and wound healing effects have been shown in animal studies, with several studies finding that nanoparticle administration led to decreased oxidative stress and tissue damage [228]. In animal models, accelerated wound closure, enhanced collagen deposition and enhanced tissue regeneration have been demonstrated by chitosan–CeO₂ nanocomposites and other biocompatible formulations [229]. However, in vivo responses are very dose-dependent in terms of the route of administration, particle size, surface chemistry and bio-distribution. Some studies have reported accumulation in organs like liver, spleen and kidneys, and thus, long-term toxicological research, pharmacokinetic studies and standardization of safety assessments procedures are required before general clinical translation. Overall, the existing evidences indicate that the CNPs obtained by green synthesis have good biocompatibility, but further in vivo studies are required to assess their potential therapeutic applications and long-term safety.

Eco-friendly degradation and biodegradability

The biodegradation behavior of green-synthesized CNPs represents a critical environmental consideration distinguishing these materials from conventional chemical synthesis approaches. Biodegradation processes involve complex interactions among nanoparticles, environmental factors, and biological systems that determine their long-term environmental fate and safety.

Naiel et al. (2022) provided a comprehensive review demonstrating strong qualitative relationships between synthesis methodology and the environmental fate of cerium oxide nanoparticles, emphasizing how synthesis parameters critically influence physicochemical characteristics that determine environmental behavior and bioavailability, though primarily through descriptive rather than quantitative analytical frameworks [230]. Samuel et al. (2022) emphasized that “biogenic synthesis produces more environmentally compatible materials.” Limited studies evaluate long-term environmental fate across different synthesis approaches [231]. Kulkarni et al. (2023) acknowledged this critical gap, noting that “molecular mechanisms, degradation pathways, kinetics, and biodistribution are often missing in published literature,” representing priority research areas requiring systematic investigation [232].

Agricultural applications demonstrate positive environmental applications with quantitative benefits assessment. Latif et al. (2024) achieved breakthrough results in “mitigating chromium toxicity in maize plants, showing significant potential for agricultural remediation applications” with foliar application of “100 mg/L CNPs resulting in improved plant growth parameters while reducing chromium content in roots by 70%.“ [233]. Korotkova et al. (2019) provided quantitative growth enhancement data, demonstrating that “biosynthesized CeO₂ particles stimulated plant growth by 5-11.4% relative to control samples while increasing leaf length and root development,” indicating beneficial agricultural applications with measurable performance metrics [234]. Limited studies have evaluated soil environmental behavior, including interactions among soil organic matter, mineral components, and soil microorganisms.

Impact on human health and ecosystems

The impact of green-synthesized CNPs on human health and ecosystems involves multifaceted considerations, including direct exposure effects, ecosystem-level interactions, and long-term environmental consequences. Tumkur et al. (2021) established a comprehensive exposure framework, noting that “CeO₂ nanomaterials are widely used, resulting in environmental release and human exposure primarily through inhalation pathways,” and that a quantitative assessment showed respiratory exposure is the primary concern [235].

Therapeutic applications demonstrate remarkable advancement, substantial clinical progress, demonstrating significant improvements in wound-healing progression when cerium oxide nanoparticles were incorporated into chitosan hydrogel membranes as wound dressing materials. Cancer therapy applications show exceptional promise, with systematic advances in efficacy and safety. Taherzadeh et al. (2023) demonstrated significant cytotoxicity of green-synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles against PC3 prostate cancer cells, with an IC₅₀ of 113.6 µg/mL in vitro. The study showed that nanoparticles effectively regulated cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration by modulating key genes (c-Myc, cyclin D1, p21, Bcl-2, Bax, MMP-2, and MMP-9), suggesting potential for targeted cancer therapy development, pending further preclinical and clinical validation [236]. hepato-neuroprotective effects through anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic mechanisms” via molecular docking studies revealing shows high binding affinity interactions with key neurological targets, supporting therapeutic applications in neurodegenerative diseases, with quantified binding energies ranging from − 8.5 to -12.3 kcal/mol.

Green-synthesized cerium oxide nanoparticles demonstrate remarkable biocompatibility and environmental compatibility, surpassing those of conventional chemical synthesis approaches. The biocompatibility profiles indicate excellent safety margins, supporting applications across the biomedical, cosmetic, and agricultural sectors, while environmental behavior studies reveal enhanced biodegradability and reduced ecological impact [237]. The positive therapeutic applications demonstrate significant health benefits, including wound healing, cancer therapy, and neuroprotection, which support continued development. However, critical research gaps remain in long-term environmental fate studies, standardized assessment protocols, and regulatory framework development that require attention for responsible technology implementation and widespread practical application [238].

Challenges and future prospects

The field of green synthesis of cerium oxide nanoparticles, while demonstrating remarkable progress and potential, faces several critical challenges that must be addressed to achieve widespread practical implementation and commercial viability. The environmental and biological implications of green-synthesized CNPs represent fundamental considerations that influence their development trajectory and practical applications across multiple sectors [239]. These challenges span technical, economic, and regulatory dimensions, requiring comprehensive solutions that maintain the environmental and functional advantages of green synthesis approaches while addressing practical limitations that currently constrain broader adoption and industrial scaling.

Limitations in green synthesis techniques

Green synthesis methodologies for CNPs face fundamental limitations that constrain broader application and industrial adoption despite their environmental advantages and enhanced biocompatibility. Persistent challenges in standardization, mechanistic understanding, and quality control have shown limited improvement in the literature analyzed, indicating the need for systematic attention and strategic intervention.

Standardization challenge represents the most persistent limitation across the entire analysis. There is several key challenges in plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis, emphasizing the critical need for standardized extraction methods to ensure consistent and reproducible synthesis. In some study, it is highlighted that scaling up production while maintaining quality and stability remains a significant consideration, and stressed that protocol standardization, along with interdisciplinary collaboration and method optimization, is essential to overcome current limitations in the field [240]. Variability in phytochemical composition represents a particularly intractable challenge. Mechanistic understanding limitations persist as fundamental constraints despite extensive research efforts. Early studies provided basic mechanistic frameworks without quantitative modelling, while recent investigations employ sophisticated analytical techniques but still lack comprehensive mechanistic understanding [241]. This limitation manifests in continued reliance on empirical optimization rather than predictive synthesis design, limiting rational development and troubleshooting capabilities. Despite the use of advanced characterization techniques, including XPS, Raman spectroscopy, and surface area analysis, in recent studies, fundamental mechanistic questions remain unanswered. The specific roles of individual phytochemicals, their synergistic interactions, and their individual contributions to synthesis outcomes require systematic investigation using approaches currently absent from green synthesis research.

Scale-up challenges and industrial feasibility

Green nanotechnology, which utilizes biological materials for nanoparticle synthesis, offers numerous advantages over traditional chemical methods, including environmental sustainability and enhanced biocompatibility. However, significant challenges remain in scaling these methods for industrial applications. Achieving consistent quality across different production batches is crucial for industrial applications. Variability in raw materials and synthesis conditions can lead to inconsistencies in nanoparticle properties, necessitating robust standardization protocols. Scaling up green synthesis processes involves optimizing reaction parameters and reactor designs. Challenges such as heat transfer limitations and mixing efficiency must be addressed to enhance production efficiency. Continuous-flow reactors and microfluidic systems are potential solutions but require further development. Post-synthesis purification of nanoparticles from biological components presents a significant hurdle. Ensuring the removal of any residual biological materials while maintaining nanoparticle integrity is essential for their safe application.

To overcome these challenges, future research should focus on: developing standardized synthesis protocols to ensure reproducibility and reliability in nanoparticle production; exploring advanced reactor designs that enhance scalability while maintaining the eco-friendly nature of the synthesis process; and encouraging collaborations among biologists, chemists, and engineers to innovate solutions that address the technical challenges associated with green synthesis.

Conclusion

Green-synthesized CNPs represent a sustainable and biocompatible alternative to chemical synthesis. This eco-friendly approach not only minimizes the use of hazardous reagents and energy-intensive conditions but also enhances the physicochemical and biological functionalities of CNPs. The presence of biogenic surface capping agents improves colloidal stability, promotes oxygen vacancy formation, and optimizes the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox balance, thereby enhancing other functionalities. In the future, integrating machine learning for predictive optimization of nanoparticle size and morphology, utilizing agricultural waste as a low-cost, high-yield phytochemical feedstock, and following green–chemical approaches can effectively improve the reproducibility and scalability of nanoparticles. Moreover, long-term ecotoxicology and human exposure studies guide regulatory compliance. Collectively, these strategies enable antioxidant, catalytic, and antimicrobial functionalities with reduced environmental footprints. Though standardization, mechanistic understanding, and industrial-scale production remain challenging, the continued integration of plant- and microbial-based production holds significant promise for biomedical, environmental, and industrial applications.*******
