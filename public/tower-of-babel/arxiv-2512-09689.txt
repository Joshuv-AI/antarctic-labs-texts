Regularity and pointwise convergence for dispersive equations on Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type
Utsav Dewan, Sanjoy Pusti

Abstract
. In this article, we first prove that for general dispersive equations on Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type 𝕏 = U / K \mathbb{X}=U/K , of rank 1 1 and 2 2 , the Sobolev regularity threshold α > 1 / 2 \alpha>1/2 for the initial data, is sufficient to obtain pointwise convergence of the solution a.e. on 𝕏 \mathbb{X} . We next focus on K K -biinvariant initial data for certain special cases of rank 1 1 , depending on geometric and topological considerations, and prove that the sufficiency of the regularity threshold can be improved down to α > 1 / 3 \alpha>1/3 , whereas the phenomenon fails for α < 1 / 4 \alpha<1/4 for the Schrödinger equation. We also obtain the same results for other dispersive equations: the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation, also known as the fourth order Wave equation, by a novel transference principle, which seems to be new even for the circle 𝕋 ≅ S ​ O ​ ( 2 ) \mathbb{T}\cong SO(2) and may be of independent interest. Our arguments involve harmonic analysis arising from the representation theory of compact semi-simple Lie groups and also number theory.

1.
Introduction

One of the most celebrated problems in Euclidean Harmonic analysis is the Carleson’s problem: determining the optimal regularity of the initial condition
f
of the
Schrödinger equation given by

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u=0\>,\>\>\>&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\>,\\
u(\cdot,0)=f\>,&\text{ on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\>,\end{cases}

in terms of the index
\alpha
such that for all
f
belonging to the inhomogeneous Sobolev space
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the solution
u
of (
1.1
) converges pointwise to
f
a.e., that is,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0+}u(x,t)=f(x)\>,\>\>\text{ almost everywhere }.

In 1980, Carleson studied this problem for
d=1
in
[
Car80
]
and obtained the sufficient condition
\alpha\geq 1/4
for the pointwise convergence (
1.2
) to hold. In 1982, Dahlberg-Kenig
[
DK82
]
showed that
\alpha\geq 1/4
is also necessary. This completely solved the problem in dimension one and subsequently, posed the question in higher dimensions.

In 1983, Cowling
[
Cow83
]
studied the problem for a general class of self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}(X)
for a measure space
X
and obtained
\alpha>1
, to be a sufficient condition for the associated Schrödinger operator. For
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in 1987, Sjölin
[
Sjö87
]
improved the bound down to
\alpha>1/2
, by means of a local smoothing effect. This improvement was also independently obtained by Vega
[
Veg88
]
in 1988. Then developing decoupling techniques, continuous improvements were made by several mathematicians
[
Bou92
,
MVV96
,
TV00
,
Lee06
]
. These attempts reached fruition recently in 2017, when Du-Guth-Li
[
DGL17
]
obtained the bound
\alpha>1/3
, for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by using polynomial partitioning and decoupling. Then in 2019, Du-Zhang
[
DZ19
]
obtained the bound
\alpha>d/2(d+1)
, for dimensions
d\geq 3
, by using decoupling and induction on scales. This bound is sharp except at the endpoint
\alpha=d/2(d+1)
, due to a counterexample by Bourgain
[
Bou16
]
. Thus in the setting of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Carleson’s problem has been almost fully resolved.

In the case of periodic initial data, that is on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, much less is known however. In dimension one, employing Strichartz estimates, Moyua-Vega showed in
[
MV08
]
that the bound
\alpha>1/3
is sufficient, whereas for
\alpha<1/4
the pointwise convergence fails. For
d\geq 2
, extending the above ideas along with the improved Strichartz estimates obtained in
[
BD15
]
, Compaan-Lucà-Staffilani showed in
[
CLS21
]
that the bound
\alpha>d/(d+2)
is sufficient, whereas for
\alpha<d/2(d+1)
the pointwise convergence fails.

For compact non-Euclidean Riemannian manifolds even less is known. In
[
WZ19
]
, Wang-Zhang showed that on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, the bound
\alpha>1/2
is sufficient. Moreover, for lower dimensional connected, compact Riemannian manifolds without boundary, they proved the sufficiency of the bound

(1.3)

\alpha>\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}&\text{ if }d=2\\
\frac{9}{10}&\text{ if }d=3\>.\end{cases}

In addition, if all geodesics are closed with a common period, that is, on a Zoll manifold, they could improve (
1.3
) down to

(1.4)

\alpha>\begin{cases}\frac{5}{8}&\text{ if }d=2\\
\frac{3}{4}&\text{ if }d=3\>.\end{cases}

The most well-known examples of Zoll manifolds are given by the class of rank one Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type. Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type are homogeneous spaces
\mathbb{X}=U/K
, where
U
is a connected, simply connected, compact, semi-simple Lie group and
K
is a closed subgroup with the property that
U^{\theta}_{0}\subset K\subset U^{\theta}
for an involution
\theta
of
U
. Here
U^{\theta}
denotes the subgroup of
\theta
-fixed points and
U^{\theta}_{0}
its identity component. In the special case when rank
(\mathbb{X})=1
, one has a well-known classification of such spaces
[
Wan52
]
:

•

the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}=SO(d+1)/SO(d)
,
d=1,2,3,\dots
;

•

the real projective space
P^{d}(\mathbb{R})=SO(d+1)/O(d)
,
d=2,3,4,\dots
;

•

the complex projective space
P^{d}(\mathbb{C})=SU(l+1)/S(U(l)\times U(1))
,
d=4,6,8,\dots
and
l=d/2
;

•

the quarternionic projective space
P^{d}(\mathbb{H})=Sp(l+1)/Sp(l)\times Sp(1)
,
d=8,12,16,\dots
and
l=d/4
;

•

the Cayley projective plane
P^{16}(Cay)
.

Here
d
denotes the real dimension of any of these spaces,
O(d),\>U(d),\>Sp(d)
denote the orthogonal, unitary and symplectic groups of order
d
and
S(\cdot)
denotes the formation of a subgroup of matrices of unit determinant. The Riemannian volume measure on
\mathbb{X}
is induced by the normalized Haar measure on
U
. For the notion of the rank of a symmetric space and other unexplained terminologies, we refer the reader to Section
2
.

In this article, in the setting of Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type
\mathbb{X}
, we will study the pointwise convergence of solutions of dispersive equations

(1.5)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\psi\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)u=0\>,\>\>\>&(x,t)\in\mathbb{X}\times[0,2\pi)\>,\\
u(\cdot,0)=f\>,&\text{ on }\mathbb{X}\>,\end{cases}

where
\psi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies suitable conditions and
\Delta
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{X}
with non-positive, discrete
L^{2}
-spectrum. We note that for
\psi(r)=r^{a}
with
a>1
, we recover the fractional Schrödinger equations with convex phase and moreover,
a=2
corresponds to the Schrödinger equation. Some other examples are given by
\psi(r):=r\sqrt{1+r^{2}}
and
\psi(r):=\sqrt{1+r^{4}}
corresponding respectively to the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation, also known as the fourth order Wave equation (for more details see
[
FG96
,
GPW08
]
), which often appear in Mathematical Physics.

To quantify the regularity of the initial data, we consider the inhomogeneous Sobolev spaces:

(1.6)

H^{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{X}\right):=\left\{f\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{X}\right)\mid\left(I-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}f\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{X}\right)\right\}\>,\>\alpha\geq 0\>.

Our first result yields a sufficiency bound on the regularity of the initial data for general dispersive equations:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a Riemannian symmetric space of compact type of rank
1
or
2
. For dispersive equations (
1.5
), the pointwise convergence of the solution to its initial data,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0+}u(x,t)=f(x)\>,

holds for almost every
x\in\mathbb{X}
, whenever
f\in H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})
with
\alpha>1/2
.

Remark 1.2
.

(1)

For the Schrödinger equation,

(a)

in the generality of Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type of rank
1
or
2
, Theorem
1.1
improves the best known bound until now,
\alpha>1
(due to Cowling
[
Cow83
]
) down to
\alpha>1/2
;

(b)

in the special case of Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type of rank
1
, Theorem
1.1
improves the bounds for lower dimensional Zoll manifolds given by (
1.4
)
[
WZ19
, Theorem 6.5]
.

(2)

For the fractional Schrödinger equations with convex phase
\psi(r)=r^{a}
,
a>1
, in the generality of Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type of rank
1
or
2
, Theorem
1.1
improves the best known bound until now,
\alpha>a/2
(due to Cowling
[
Cow83
]
) down to
\alpha>1/2
.

Remark 1.3
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the key idea is to utilize the concentration of the discrete
L^{2}
spectrum of
\Delta
. In the rank one case, this follows just from the identification of the highest weight lattice as the set of non-negative integers and the growth of the eigenvalues (see Remark
2.1
). In the rank two case however, similar growth estimates require more work (see Lemma
3.2
) and we also need the asymptotics on the number of lattice points on circles in terms of their radii (see Lemma
2.3
).

Any element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{X})
is naturally identified with an element of
L^{2}(U)
which is right
K
-invariant. We now focus our attention to more symmetric initial data, namely elements of
L^{2}(U)
which are
K
-biinvariant, that is, both left and right
K
-invariant. In the special case when rank
(\mathbb{X})=1
, the algebraic notion of
K
-biinvariance has an interesting connection with the geometry of the two-point homogeneous spaces
\mathbb{X}
. For a fixed origin
o\in\mathbb{X}
,
U
can be identified as the maximal connected group of isometries of
\mathbb{X}
and
K
as the stabilizer of
o
in
U
. Moreover, by the two-point homogenity,
K
fixes
o
and acts transitively on the set of points at a given distance from
o
. Thus
K
-biinvariant functions on
U
can be identified with functions on
\mathbb{X}
radial around
o
, that is functions
f
on
\mathbb{X}
such that
f(x)
only depends on the geodesic distance of
x
from
o
. Henceforth, we will refer to such functions simply as radial functions.

Due to the extra symmetry, one may expect an improvement of the sufficiency bound while specializing to
K
-biinvariant initial data, compared to the general case treated in Theorem
1.1
. This was recently studied for the Schrödinger equation on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
by Chen-Duong-Lee-Yan, where they proved the sufficiency of the bound
\alpha>1/3
for such functions
[
CDLY22
, Theorem 1.3]
. Our next result generalizes their result to other rank one Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type not only for the Schrödinger equation but also for other PDEs such as the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{S}^{d},\>P^{d}(\mathbb{R}),\>P^{4}(\mathbb{C}),\>P^{8}(\mathbb{H})
or
P^{16}(Cay)
. For the Schrödinger equation, the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation, the pointwise convergence of the solution to its initial data,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0+}u(x,t)=f(x)\>,

holds for almost every
x\in\mathbb{X}
, whenever
f\in H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})
is
K
-biinvariant with
\alpha>1/3
.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.4
yield sufficient conditions on the regularity threshold to guarantee pointwise convergence almost everywhere, which by standard arguments (for instance see the proof of Theorem 5 of
[
Sjö87
]
) boils down to obtaining
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{X})
(for some
p\geq 1
) boundedness results for the maximal function:

(1.7)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

for some
C>0
. Hence to put the above results in the retrospect of the Carleson’s problem, we present the following negative result which again generalizes the related result for the Schrödinger equation on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}

[
CDLY22
, Theorem 1.3]
by Chen et. al.:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{S}^{d},\>P^{d}(\mathbb{C}),\>P^{d}(\mathbb{H})
or
P^{16}(Cay)
. Then for the Schrödinger equation, the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation, the maximal estimate (
1.7
) fails for (any
p\geq 1
) the class of
K
-biinvariant functions in
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})
if
\alpha<1/4
.

Remark 1.6
.

We now briefly mention the key ideas for the proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
:

(1)

For the Schrödinger equation, we follow the general outline of the arguments employed by Chen et. al. on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
:

(a)

In the proof of Theorem
1.4
, the maximal function is estimated near the poles by the Sobolev embedding (see Lemma
5.4
). Then away from the poles, by expanding the Jacobi polynomials, the problem is reduced to a maximal estimate on
\mathbb{T}
. This is then obtained by adapting the number-theoretic ideas of Bourgain
[
Bou93
]
and Moyua-Vega
[
MV08
]
, relating the maximal estimate to a Strichartz estimate (see Lemmata
5.1
and
5.2
).

(b)

Following ideas of Moyua-Vega
[
MV08
]
, the proof of Theorem
1.5
uses quadratic Gauss sum evaluations to obtain the desired rate of divergence on a suitable set. Then using asymptotics of arithmetic functions, we see that the set is suitably large (see Lemma
6.1
).

(2)

The above mentioned number theoretic techniques are special for the case of the Schrödinger equation, as they do not seem to have a straightforward extension to other dispersive equations such as the Boussinesq equation or the Beam equation. Instead, we obtain Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
for the other two equations by means of a novel transference principle (see Theorem
1.9
), which seems to be new even for
\mathbb{T}\cong SO(2)
and may be of independent interest. We now explain the transference principle:

Definition 1.7
.

Let us consider two dispersive equations of the form (
1.5
) corresponding to
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
.

(i)

They are called
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
-
transferrable
if given that for some
\alpha_{0}>0
and some
p\in[1,\infty]
, the maximal estimate

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\leq C(\alpha){\|f\|}_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

holds for some
C(\alpha)>0
, all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
and all
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
, it follows that the maximal estimate

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\leq C^{\prime}(\alpha){\|f\|}_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

also holds for some
C^{\prime}(\alpha)>0
, all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
and all
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
.

(ii)

If the two equations are both
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
-transferrable as well as
(\psi_{2},\psi_{1})
-transferrable, then they are simply called
transferrable
.

(iii)

If there exist
R>0
and
C>0
, such that the phase functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
satisfy

\left|\psi_{1}(r)-\psi_{2}(r)\right|\leq C\>,\text{ for all }r>R\>,

then the equations are said to be
of comparable oscillation
.

Remark 1.8
.

Point (iii) of Definition
1.7
means that in high frequency, both the phase functions are within bounded error. Let us now consider the equations mentioned before. The phase functions for the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation are given respectively as,

\psi_{1}(r)=r\sqrt{1+r^{2}}\>,\text{ and }\psi_{2}(r)=\sqrt{1+r^{4}}\>.

For
r
large,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(r)=r\left(r+\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})\right)=r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(1)\>,

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(r)=r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(1)\>,

and thus both are of comparable oscillation to the Schrödinger equation
(\psi(r)=r^{2})
.

The notion of
comparable oscillation
defines an equivalence relation. Our next result presents the phenomenon that all the equations in the same equivalence class ‘behave similarly’:

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
be continuous real-valued functions on
[0,\infty)
. If the dispersive equations corresponding to
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are of comparable oscillation, then they are also transferrable.

, we fix our notations, recall the essential preliminaries about Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type and harmonic analysis thereon and also some number theoretic facts. We prove Theorems
1.1
,
1.9
,
1.4
and
1.5
in Sections
3
,
4
,
5
and
6
respectively. Finally, we conclude by posing some open problems in Section
7
.

Some notations

Throughout this article

will be used to denote positive constants whose values may change on each occurrence.
\mathbb{N}
will denote the set of positive integers. Two positive functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
will satisfy,

•

f_{1}\lesssim f_{2}
if there exists
C\geq 1
such that
f_{1}\leq Cf_{2}
;

•

f_{1}\gtrsim f_{2}
if there exists
C\geq 1
such that
Cf_{1}\geq f_{2}
;

•

f_{1}\asymp f_{2}
if there exists
C\geq 1
such that
\frac{1}{C}f_{1}\leq f_{2}\leq Cf_{1}
;

•

f_{1}=o(f_{2})
if
\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}}\to 0

The notation
\lesssim_{\varepsilon}
will also be used to denote the dependence of the parameter
\varepsilon
. In addition, if

is complex-valued, we will write,

•

f_{1}=\mathcal{O}(f_{2})

|f_{1}|\lesssim f_{2}
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}

\lfloor x\rfloor

. For a set
A
, the notation

.

2.2.
Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type and harmonic analysis thereon

In this subsection, we briefly recall some definitions and results on Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type. The relevant information can be found in
[
Hel78
,
Hel08
,
Hel00
,
ÓS08
]
.

Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type are homogeneous spaces
\mathbb{X}=U/K
, where
U
is a connected, simply connected, compact, semi-simple Lie group and
K
is a closed subgroup with the property that
U^{\theta}_{0}\subset K\subset U^{\theta}
for an involution
\theta
of
U
. Here
U^{\theta}
denotes the subgroup of
\theta
-fixed points and
U^{\theta}_{0}
its identity component.

Let
\mathfrak{u}
be the Lie algebra of
U
and
\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{k}+\mathfrak{q}
be the Cartan decomposition corresponding to
\theta
, where
\mathfrak{k}
is the Lie algebra of
K
and
\mathfrak{q}
can be identified with the tangent space at the origin
T_{o}\mathbb{X}
where
o=eK
. The Killing form being negative definite on
\mathfrak{u}
induces an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathfrak{u}
. Then
\mathfrak{k}
and
\mathfrak{q}
become orthogonal subspaces. We assume that the normalized Riemannian metric agrees with
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathfrak{q}=T_{o}\mathbb{X}
.

\mathbb{X}
is equipped with the push-forward measure of the normalized Haar measure
du
of
U
. Let
(\delta,V_{\delta})
be an irreducible unitary representation of
U
and
V^{K}_{\delta}
be the space of vectors
v\in V_{\delta}
fixed under
\delta(K)
. Let
\widehat{U}_{K}
denote the collection of equivalence classes of irreducible, unitary representations
\delta
of
U
such that
V^{K}_{\delta}\neq 0
. Then from the Peter-Weyl theory of
U
, we can obtain the decomposition,

(2.1)

L^{2}(\mathbb{X})=\bigoplus_{\delta\in\widehat{U}_{K}}H_{\delta}(\mathbb{X})\>,

where

H_{\delta}(\mathbb{X}):=\left\{\langle\delta(u){\bf e},v\rangle\mid v\in V_{\delta}\right\}\>,

and
{\bf e}
is the unique unit vector spanning
V^{K}_{\delta}
.

We now obtain a more explicit realization of
\widehat{U}_{K}
in terms of the highest weight lattice of the underlying root system. Let
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{q}
be a maximal abelian subspace,
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
its dual and
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{u}
be a Cartan subalgebra containing
\mathfrak{a}
. Then
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{m}\oplus\mathfrak{a}
, where
\mathfrak{h}_{m}=\mathfrak{h}\cap\mathfrak{k}
. The dimension of
\mathfrak{a}
is the rank of
\mathbb{X}
. By duality, we can equip
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
with an inner product, which is essentially induced by the Killing form. We denote by
\Sigma
the set of non-zero restrictions to
\mathfrak{a}
of roots of
\mathfrak{u}
with respect to
\mathfrak{h}
. We fix a set of positive restricted roots
\Sigma^{+}\subset\Sigma
and by
\Sigma^{+}_{s}
denote the corresponding set of positive simple roots. We will denote by
\rho
, the half-sum of the roots in
\Sigma^{+}
counted with multiplicity. The set
\Sigma^{+}_{s}
forms a basis of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. If rank
(\mathbb{X})=l
and
\Sigma^{+}_{s}=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{l}\}
, then
[
Hel78
, Chap. VII, Lemma 2.18]

(2.2)

\langle\beta_{j},\beta_{k}\rangle\leq 0\>,\>\forall 1\leq j,k\leq l\>.

Let
\Lambda^{+}(\mathfrak{h})
denote the set of dominant integral linear functionals on
\mathfrak{h}
. On the other hand let
\Lambda^{+}(U)\subset\Lambda^{+}(\mathfrak{h})
denote the set of highest weights of irreducible representations of
U
. Since our
U
is simply-connected we have
\Lambda^{+}(U)=\Lambda^{+}(\mathfrak{h})
. Let
\Lambda^{+}(U/K)
denote the subset of
\Lambda^{+}(U)
corresponding to
\widehat{U}_{K}
. By
[
Hel00
, Theorem 4.1, p. 535]
, we have the following identification of
\Lambda^{+}(U/K)
: let
\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U)
, then
\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)
if and only if
\mu|_{\mathfrak{h}_{m}}=0
and its restriction to
\mathfrak{a}
(that is, as an element of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
) satisfies

\frac{\langle\mu,\beta\rangle}{\langle\beta,\beta\rangle}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\>,\>\>\beta\in\Sigma^{+}\>.

From here on we will work with the restriction of
\mu
to
\mathfrak{a}
. Thus in view of the above identification, we can rewrite the decomposition (
2.1
), by an abuse of notation as,

L^{2}(\mathbb{X})=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}H_{\mu}(\mathbb{X})\>.

The above decomposition can also be viewed as the eigenspace decomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the Riemannian manifold
\mathbb{X}
. By
[
Pro07
, Lemma 1, Section 6.6, p. 367]
, for any
f\in H_{\mu}(\mathbb{X})
,

\Delta f=-\lambda^{2}_{\mu}f\>,

where

(2.3)

\lambda^{2}_{\mu}=\langle\mu,\mu\rangle+2\langle\mu,\rho\rangle\>,\>\>\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)\>.

Let
d_{\mu}:=dim\left(H_{\mu}(\mathbb{X})\right)
and
\{Y_{\mu,j}\}_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}
be an orthonormal basis of
H_{\mu}(\mathbb{X})
. Thus for
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
, the Fourier series of
f
is given by,

(2.4)

f=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\>Y_{\mu,j}\>,

where the Fourier coefficients are given by,

\widehat{f}(\mu,j)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}f(x)\>\overline{Y_{\mu,j}(x)}\>dx\>,

and the series converges uniformly and absolutely.

By the Plancherel identity, the Sobolev norms for the Sobolev spaces defined in (
1.6
) are given by,

. The following expansion of the Jacobi polynomials will be relevant for us:

.

[

, Theorem 8.21.13]

Let

and

. Then we have, for

\|Z_{n}(o,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(X)}=d^{\frac{1}{2}}_{n}\>.

We note that by the Cartan decomposition and the transitive action of

, from (

When
f
is also

for some

\|\tilde{Z}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim{\left(1+n^{2}\right)}^{\frac{d-1}{4}}\>,\>\>n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\>.

2.3.
Number theoretic facts

In this subsection, we recall some results in number theory, which will be crucial for us. All of this can be found in the classical text
[
HW75
]
.

We first see the following result which gives a bound on the number of ways a natural number can be written as a sum of two squares of integers:

Lemma 2.3
.

[
HW75
, Theorem 338]
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

r_{2}(n):=\left\{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2}=n\right\}\>.

Then

\#r_{2}(n)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}(1+n)^{\varepsilon}\>,\>\>\forall\varepsilon>0\>.

Next we look at some fundamental arithmetic functions, that is, functions defined on
\mathbb{N}
. The Euler’s totient function
\varphi
is defined as
\varphi(n):=
the number of positive integers less than and prime to
n
. The Möbius function
\mu
is defined as,

\mu(n):=\begin{cases}1\>\>\>&\text{if }n=1\>,\\
{(-1)}^{j}\>\>\>&\text{if }n\text{ is the product of }j\text{ distinct prime factors}\>,\\
0\>\>\>&\text{if }n\text{ has a squared factor }\>.\end{cases}

The following is the fundamental identity satisfied by the Möbius function
[
HW75
, Theorem 263]
:

(2.16)

\sum_{m|n}\mu(m)=\begin{cases}1\>\>\>&\text{ if }n=1\>,\\
0\>\>\>&\text{ if }n>1\>.\end{cases}

The functions
\varphi
and
\mu
are related via the Möbius inversion
[
HW75
, Eqn. 16.3.1]
:

(2.17)

\frac{\varphi(n)}{n}=\sum_{m|n}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}\>.

The divisor function is defined as
d(n):=
the number of divisors of
n
including
1
and
n
.
We have the following asymptotics of the functions
\varphi
and
d

[
HW75
, Theorems 315 and 327]
: for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(2.18)

d(n)\lesssim_{\varepsilon}n^{\varepsilon}\text{ and }\varphi(n)\gtrsim_{\varepsilon}n^{1-\varepsilon}\>,\text{ as }n\to\infty\>.

3.
Sufficiency of
\alpha>1/2
on symmetric spaces of rank
1
and
2

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
. We first start off with a linear algebraic result:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\left(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\right)
be a 2-dimensional real inner product space with a basis
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
. Let

\xi_{j}(v):=\frac{\langle v,v_{j}\rangle}{\langle v_{j},v_{j}\rangle}\>,\>v\in V\>,\>j=1,2\>.

Then for any
v\in V
,

\langle v,v\rangle=\frac{\xi_{1}(v)^{2}\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle^{2}\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle+\xi_{2}(v)^{2}\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle^{2}\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle-2\xi_{1}(v)\xi_{2}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle-\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle^{2}}\>.

Proof.

We first choose and fix
v\in V
. There exist
c_{1}(v),c_{2}(v)\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(3.1)

v=c_{1}(v)v_{1}+c_{2}(v)v_{2}\>.

Thus we have,

(3.2)

\langle v,v\rangle=c_{1}(v)^{2}\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle+2c_{1}(v)c_{2}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle+c_{2}(v)^{2}\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle\>.

From (
3.1
), we also have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle v,v_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle+c_{2}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\>,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\langle v,v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle+c_{2}(v)\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle\>.

Thus combining (
3.2
)-(
3.4
) and invoking the definition of
\xi_{j},\>j=1,2
, we get

(3.5)

\langle v,v\rangle=c_{1}(v)\xi_{1}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle+c_{2}(v)\xi_{2}(v)\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle\>.

Next solving the equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle c_{1}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\xi_{1}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle-\xi_{2}(v)\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle-\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle^{2}}\>,

(3.7)

\displaystyle c_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\xi_{2}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle-\xi_{1}(v)\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle}{\langle v_{1},v_{1}\rangle\langle v_{2},v_{2}\rangle-\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle^{2}}\>.

The result now follows by plugging (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) in (
3.5
).
∎

As an application of Lemma
3.1
, we obtain the following asymptotic lower bound for eigenvalues of
-\Delta
for rank
2
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a Riemannian symmetric space of compact type of rank
2
. Then the eigenvalues of
-\Delta
satisfy

\lambda_{\mu}^{2}\gtrsim n_{1}(\mu)^{2}+n_{2}(\mu)^{2}\>,\>\>\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)\>,

where the implicit constant only depends on the intrinsic geometry of
\mathbb{X}
and the coefficients
n_{j}
are defined in terms of the inner product induced by the Killing form as,

n_{j}(\mu):=\frac{\langle\mu,\beta_{j}\rangle}{\langle\beta_{j},\beta_{j}\rangle}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\>,\>\>j=1,2,\>\text{ where }\>\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}=\Sigma^{+}_{s}\>.

Proof.

For
\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)
, we have by (
2.3
),

\lambda^{2}_{\mu}=\langle\mu,\mu\rangle+2\langle\mu,\rho\rangle\geq\langle\mu,\mu\rangle\>.

Now
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}
forms a basis of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
where
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\}=\Sigma^{+}_{s}
. Then by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\langle\mu,\mu\rangle=\frac{n_{1}(\mu)^{2}\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle^{2}\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle+n_{2}(\mu)^{2}\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle^{2}\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle-2n_{1}(\mu)n_{2}(\mu)\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle}{\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle-\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle^{2}}\>.

Now by (
2.2
), as
\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle\leq 0
, we have

\langle\mu,\mu\rangle\geq n_{1}(\mu)^{2}\frac{\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle^{2}\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle}{\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle-\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle^{2}}+n_{2}(\mu)^{2}\frac{\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle^{2}\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle}{\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{1}\rangle\langle\beta_{2},\beta_{2}\rangle-\langle\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle^{2}}\>.

This completes the proof of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

We are now in a position to present the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It suffices to prove the maximal estimate

(3.8)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

for all
\alpha>1/2
and all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
. To prove (
3.8
), it suffices to consider its linearization,

T_{\psi}f(x):=e^{-it(x)\psi\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f(x)\>,\>\>x\in\mathbb{X}\>,

where
t(\cdot):\mathbb{X}\to[0,2\pi)
is a measurable function and obtain the boundedness result

(3.9)

\left\|T_{\psi}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

for all
\alpha>1/2
and all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
. For
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
, by the expression (
2.4
), we have

T_{\psi}f(x)=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}e^{-it(x)\psi\left(\lambda_{\mu}\right)}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\>Y_{\mu,j}(x)\>,\>\>x\in\mathbb{X}\>.

Thus

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\psi}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}e^{-it(\cdot)\psi\left(\lambda_{\mu}\right)}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\>Y_{\mu,j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\>Y_{\mu,j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Now by the Plancherel theorem,

\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\>Y_{\mu,j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}=\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\left|\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\>.

Then by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have for any
\alpha\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\left|\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\left({(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\left|\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mu}}\left|\widehat{f}(\mu,j)\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Thus to prove (
3.9
), it suffices to prove that

(3.10)

\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\alpha}<\infty\>,\>\>\text{ for }\alpha>1/2\>.

Now in the case when rank
(\mathbb{X})=1
,
\Lambda^{+}(U/K)=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and by Remark
2.1
, the eigenvalues satisfy
\lambda^{2}_{n}\gtrsim n^{2}\>,\>n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, with the implicit constant only depending on the intrinsic geometry of
\mathbb{X}
. Thus we get that

\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\alpha}\lesssim 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2\alpha}}\>,

and the last sum converges if
\alpha>1/2
. This yields (
3.10
) when rank
(\mathbb{X})=1
.

For the case when rank
(\mathbb{X})=2
, by Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}\frac{1}{{(1+n_{1}(\mu)^{2}+n_{2}(\mu)^{2})}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\left(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\right)^{2}}\frac{1}{{(1+n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2})}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\left(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\right)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\int_{{(n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2})}^{1/2}-1}^{{(n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2})}^{1/2}}\frac{dr}{{(1+r^{2})}^{\alpha}}\>.

We now consider for
r>0
, the set

\mathcal{N}^{+}_{r}:=\left\{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\left(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\right)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\mid{(n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2})}^{1/2}=r\right\}\>.

Now if
r^{2}
is not an integer, then
\mathcal{N}^{+}_{r}=\emptyset
. Thus by Lemma
2.3
, we get for all
r>0
,

(3.11)

\#\mathcal{N}^{+}_{r}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}(1+r^{2})^{\varepsilon}\>,\>\>\forall\varepsilon>0\>.

Hence by Fubini-Tonelli and the estimate (
3.11
), we have for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\left(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\right)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\int_{{(n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2})}^{1/2}-1}^{{(n^{2}_{1}+n^{2}_{2})}^{1/2}}\frac{dr}{{(1+r^{2})}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathcal{N}^{+}_{r}}\frac{dr}{{(1+r^{2})}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\#\mathcal{N}^{+}_{r}\right)dr}{{(1+r^{2})}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{{(1+r^{2})}^{\alpha-\varepsilon}}\>.

The last integral converges if
2(\alpha-\varepsilon)>1
, that is, for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
, given any
\varepsilon>0
. This implies that for
\alpha>1/2
, the sum
\sum_{\mu\in\Lambda^{+}(U/K)}{(1+\lambda^{2}_{\mu})}^{-\alpha}
converges. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

4.
The transference principle

From this point onwards, we will be working with
K
-biinvariant data on rank one Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type
\mathbb{X}
. In this section, we will prove the transference principle Theorem
1.9
, but first we see the following result on different scales of Sobolev norms:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
with spherical Fourier series
f=\displaystyle\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}
(as in (
2.14
)), for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for
0\leq\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\infty
, we have

\|f\|_{H^{\beta_{1}}(X)}\lesssim N^{-(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})}\>\|f\|_{H^{\beta_{2}}(X)}\>,

with the implicit constant depending only on the intrinsic geometry of
\mathbb{X}
.

Proof.

The result follows from a straightforward computation. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\beta_{1}}(X)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}{\left(1+\lambda^{2}_{n}\right)}^{\beta_{1}}\>{|a_{n}|}^{2}\>\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}{\left(1+\lambda^{2}_{n}\right)}^{-(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})}\>{\left(1+\lambda^{2}_{n}\right)}^{\beta_{2}}\>{|a_{n}|}^{2}\>\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})}_{n}\>{\left(1+\lambda^{2}_{n}\right)}^{\beta_{2}}\>{|a_{n}|}^{2}\>\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\>.

Now using Remark
2.1
, we get that

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})}_{n}\>{\left(1+\lambda^{2}_{n}\right)}^{\beta_{2}}\>{|a_{n}|}^{2}\>\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}n^{-2(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})}\>{\left(1+\lambda^{2}_{n}\right)}^{\beta_{2}}\>{|a_{n}|}^{2}\>\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N^{-(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})}\>\|f\|_{H^{\beta_{2}}(X)}\>.

∎

We now present the proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that for some
\alpha_{0}>0
and some
p\in[1,\infty]
, the maximal estimate

(4.1)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim{\|f\|}_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

holds for all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
and all
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
. We have to show that the maximal estimate

(4.2)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim{\|f\|}_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

also holds for all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
and all
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
.

By the hypothesis there exist positive constants
R
and
C
such that

(4.3)

\left|\psi_{1}(r)-\psi_{2}(r)\right|\leq C\>,\>\>r\in(R,\infty)\>.

Let us choose and fix
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
. Correspondingly, we have the spherical Fourier series (as in (
2.14
)),

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}\>.

Let
m_{0}
be the smallest positive integer such that
2^{m_{0}}>R
. We decompose
f
as follows,

f=f_{m_{0}}+\sum_{m=m_{0}+1}^{\infty}f_{m}\>,

where

f_{m_{0}}:=\sum_{n=0}^{2^{m_{0}+1}-1}a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}\>,\text{ and for }m\geq m_{0}+1,\>\>\>f_{m}:=\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}\>.

Invoking the above decomposition of
f
, we get that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m_{0}}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}+\sum_{m=m_{0}+1}^{\infty}\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Since
f
is
K
-biinvariant, so is each of its pieces
f_{m}
above, their propagations
e^{-it\psi_{j}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}
, for
j=1,2
and also the corresponding maximal functions.

By the
L^{\infty}
bound given in (
2.15
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have for all
t\in[0,2\pi)
, all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
,

\displaystyle\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m_{0}}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=0}^{2^{m_{0}+1}-1}e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{2^{m_{0}+1}-1}|a_{n}|\>\left|\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f_{m_{0}}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

from which it follows that

(4.5)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m_{0}}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Next for each
m\geq m_{0}+1
, we have for all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and all
t\in[0,2\pi)
,

\displaystyle\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}(\theta)-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}\left(e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}\right)a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}\left(1-e^{-it\left(\psi_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)-\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)\right)}\right)a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|\>.

Now using the smoothness of the exponential in time, we expand the exponential in the Taylor series to get,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}\left(1-e^{-it\left(\psi_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)-\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)\right)}\right)a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}\left[1-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left\{-it\left(\psi_{2}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)-\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)\right)\right\}^{j}}{j!}\right]a_{n}\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\left|\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\lambda_{n}\right)}\left[\psi_{2}(\lambda_{n})-\psi_{1}(\lambda_{n})\right]^{j}a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|\>.

For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\left\{2^{m},\dots,2^{m+1}-1\right\}
, setting
a_{j,n}:=\left[\psi_{2}(\lambda_{n})-\psi_{1}(\lambda_{n})\right]^{j}a_{n}
, we define,

g_{j,m}:=\sum_{n=2^{m}}^{2^{m+1}-1}a_{j,n}\tilde{Z}_{n}\;.

Then the computation above yields, for each
m\geq m_{0}+1
, for all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and all
t\in[0,2\pi)
,

\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}(\theta)-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}(\theta)\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}g_{j,m}(\theta)\right|\>,

which in turn implies

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}g_{j,m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, set
\alpha_{1}:=\alpha_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
\alpha_{2}:=\alpha_{0}+\varepsilon
. Then applying the assumption (
4.1
) and Lemma
4.1
, we get that

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}g_{j,m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim\|g_{j,m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\|g_{j,m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

which upon plugging in (
4.6
) yields,

(4.7)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}\|g_{j,m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Recalling the definition of
g_{j,m}
along with (
4.3
), we note that

\|g_{j,m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\leq C^{j}\>\|f_{m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

which upon plugging in (
4.7
) gives,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\|f_{m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{C^{j}}{j!}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\|f_{m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Hence for each
m\geq m_{0}+1
, applying (
4.1
) to
f_{m}
and combining it with (
4.8
), we get that

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}-e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}+\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{1}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\|f_{m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}+\|f_{m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{1}}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Then a further application of Lemma
4.1
gives

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\|f_{m}\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\leq 2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

which upon plugging in (
4.4
) and combined with (
4.5
) yields

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m_{0}}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}+\sum_{m=m_{0}+1}^{\infty}\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{-it\psi_{2}\left(\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)}f_{m}\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(1+\sum_{m=m_{0}+1}^{\infty}2^{-\frac{m\varepsilon}{2}}\right)\|f\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

As
\varepsilon>0
and
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
were arbitrarily chosen, (
4.2
) follows. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.9
.
∎

5.
Sufficiency of
\alpha>1/3
for
K
-biinvariant data

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.4
.

The unit circle
\mathbb{T}
will be identified with the interval
[0,2\pi)
equipped with the normalized Lebesgue measure. We first obtain the following variant of the Strichartz estimate on
\mathbb{T}
:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N\geq 1
be an integer and
\textsf{a}=\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a sequence. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{-it\lambda^{2}_{n}}e^{\pm in\theta}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\|\textsf{a}\|_{\ell^{2}}\>,

where
\lambda^{2}_{n}
is of the form appearing in Table
2
.

Proof.

By reflection
\theta\mapsto-\theta
, it is enough to consider
e^{-in\theta}
. As presented in (
2.6
), the general form of
\lambda^{2}_{n}
is given by,

\lambda^{2}_{n}=sn(sn+\sigma+\tau+1)\>,\>\>n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\>,

where
s=1
or
2
and the values of
\sigma
and
\tau
are given in Table
2
. Thus we note that while working in either of the cases, we have

\lambda^{2}_{n}=sn(sn+\ell)\>,\>\>n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\>,

for some fixed non-negative integer
\ell
. We now adapt the proof of
[
Bou93
, Proposition 2.36]
to our present setting. Via Plancherel theorem, the
L^{6}
-norm appearing in the statement of Lemma
5.1
can be written as,

\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{-itsn(sn+\ell)}e^{-in\theta}\right\|^{6}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}=C\sum_{u,v}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=u\\
sn_{1}(sn_{1}+\ell)+sn_{2}(sn_{2}+\ell)+sn_{3}(sn_{3}+\ell)=v\end{subarray}}a_{n_{1}}a_{n_{2}}a_{n_{3}}\right|^{2}\>,

for a positive constant
C
. We note that as
0\leq n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\leq N-1
, we have that
u\lesssim N
and
v\lesssim N^{2}
. Setting

r^{(\ell)}_{u,v}:=\#\left\{(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in[0,N]^{3}\mid\sum_{j=1}^{3}n_{j}=u,\sum_{j=1}^{3}sn_{j}(sn_{j}+\ell)=v\right\}\>,

we note by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=u\\
sn_{1}(sn_{1}+\ell)+sn_{2}(sn_{2}+\ell)+sn_{3}(sn_{3}+\ell)=v\end{subarray}}a_{n_{1}}a_{n_{2}}a_{n_{3}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v}r^{(\ell)}_{u,v}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=u\\
sn_{1}(sn_{1}+\ell)+sn_{2}(sn_{2}+\ell)+sn_{3}(sn_{3}+\ell)=v\end{subarray}}|a_{n_{1}}|^{2}\>|a_{n_{2}}|^{2}\>|a_{n_{3}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\max_{u,v}r^{(\ell)}_{u,v}\right)\|\textsf{a}\|^{6}_{\ell^{2}}\>.

Thus it suffices to prove that for all
u,v
as above and any
\varepsilon>0
,

r^{(\ell)}_{u,v}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\>.

Now for
\ell=0
, the proof of
[
Bou93
, Proposition 2.36]
yields

r^{(0)}_{u,v}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\>.

Then for
\ell\geq 1
, the same estimate follows from the simple observation that
r^{(\ell)}_{u,v}=r^{(0)}_{u,v-s\ell u}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
5.1
.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
5.1
, we obtain the following maximal estimate on
\mathbb{T}
:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
N\geq 1
be an integer and
\textsf{a}=\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N-1}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a sequence. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\left\|\sup_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{-it\lambda^{2}_{n}}e^{\pm in\theta}\right|\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{\frac{1}{3}+\varepsilon}\|\textsf{a}\|_{\ell^{2}}\>,

where
\lambda^{2}_{n}
is of the form appearing in Table
2
.

Proof.

While working in either of the cases as classified in Table
2
, we first consider the differential operator
D
on
\mathbb{T}
such that

D(e^{in\theta})=-\lambda^{2}_{|n|}\>e^{in\theta}\>,\>n\in\mathbb{Z},\>\theta\in\mathbb{T}\>.

Then we look at the solution of the corresponding Schrödinger equation:

(5.1)

\begin{cases}i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+Du=0\>,\>\>\>&(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}\>,\\
u(0,\cdot)=f\>,&\text{ on }\mathbb{T}\>,\end{cases}

with initial data

f(\theta):=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{\pm in\theta}\>,\>\>\theta\in\mathbb{T}\>.

We now adapt the proof of
[
MV08
, Proposition 1]
to our present setting. By an application of the Fundamental theorem of Calculus in time
t
, we have for any
(\theta,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}
,

|u(\theta,t)|^{6}=|f(\theta)|^{6}\>+\>3\int_{0}^{t}|u(\theta,s)|^{4}\>2\>\text{Re}\left(u(\theta,s)\overline{u_{s}(\theta,s)}\right)\>ds\>.

Thus taking the supremum in
t
and then integrating in
\theta
, it follows that

(5.2)

\left\|\sup_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{-it\lambda^{2}_{n}}e^{\pm in\theta}\right|\right\|^{6}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|f\|^{6}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T})}\>+\>6\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|u(\theta,s)|^{5}\>|u_{s}(\theta,s)|\>ds\>d\theta.

Now by the Sobolev embedding on
\mathbb{T}
, we have

(5.3)

\|f\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim N^{\frac{1}{3}}\>\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=N^{\frac{1}{3}}\>\|\textsf{a}\|_{\ell^{2}}\>.

For the other term, we apply Hölder’s inequality to get

(5.4)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|u(\theta,s)|^{5}\>|u_{s}(\theta,s)|\>ds\>d\theta\lesssim\|u\|^{5}_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}\>\|u_{s}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}\>.

Then by Lemma
5.1
, we get that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.5)

\|u\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}=\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_{n}e^{-it\lambda^{2}_{n}}e^{\pm in\theta}\right\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\|\textsf{a}\|_{\ell^{2}}\>.

To estimate
\|u_{t}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}
, we note that

u_{t}=ie^{itD}Df\>,

where
e^{itD}Df
is the solution of the Schrödinger equation (
5.1
) with initial data
Df
. Then applying Lemma
5.1
to
Df
and recalling Remark
2.1
, we obtain for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.6)

\|u_{t}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}N^{2+\varepsilon}\|\textsf{a}\|_{\ell^{2}}\>.

Thus combining (
5.2
)-(
5.6
), the result follows.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Lemma
5.1
can be viewed as a Strichartz estimate for even initial data on
\mathbb{T}
corresponding to the Schrödinger operator (
5.1
).

We will also require the following Sobolev embedding for
K
-biinvariant functions:

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a Riemannian symmetric space of compact type of rank
1
and dimension
d\geq 1
. Then for all
K
-biinvariant functions
f
and all
\varepsilon>0
, the following inequality holds:

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim_{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

Proof.

By density, it suffices to prove the inequality for
K
-biinvariant functions
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
. For such a function
f
, its spherical Fourier series (as in (
2.14
)) is of the form

f(\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\>,\>\>\theta\in[0,\pi]\>,

with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Performing the dyadic decomposition,

f=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=2^{m}-1}^{2^{m+1}-2}a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}\>,

we note that for any
\alpha\geq 0

\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\asymp\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{2\alpha m}\sum_{n=2^{m}-1}^{2^{m+1}-2}|a_{n}|^{2}\>.

Thus it suffices to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.7)

\|g_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim N^{\frac{d}{2}+\varepsilon}\|g_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

where

g_{N}:=\sum_{n=N-1}^{2(N-1)}a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}\>,\>N=2^{m}\>,\>m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\>,

as then by (
5.7
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\|g_{2^{m}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{(2^{m})}^{\frac{d}{2}+\varepsilon}\|g_{2^{m}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-m\varepsilon}\>{(2^{m})}^{\frac{d}{2}+2\varepsilon}\|g_{2^{m}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{-2m\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{2\left(\frac{d}{2}+2\varepsilon\right)m}\|g_{2^{m}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+2\varepsilon}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

The result would then follow as
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary.

Now (
5.7
) follows from the
L^{\infty}
bound (
2.15
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, as for all
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,

\displaystyle\left|g_{N}(\theta)\right|=\left|\sum_{n=N-1}^{2(N-1)}a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N-1}^{2(N-1)}\left|a_{n}\right|\>\left\|\tilde{Z}_{n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{n=N-1}^{2(N-1)}\left|a_{n}\right|\>{\left(1+n^{2}\right)}^{\frac{d-1}{4}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=N-1}^{2(N-1)}{\left(1+n^{2}\right)}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|g_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle N^{\frac{d}{2}}\>\|g_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{X})}\>.

This completes the proof of Lemma
5.4
.
∎

We now present the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

The proof is divided into two steps.

Step 1:
We first focus on the case of the Schrödinger equation. It suffices to prove the maximal estimate

(5.8)

\left\|\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{it\Delta}f\right|\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{X})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{X})}\>,

for all
\alpha>1/3
and all
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
.

The maximal estimate (
5.8
) for
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{S}^{d}
is proved in
[
CDLY22
, Theorem 1.3]
. So we will give the proof for the complementary cases:
\mathbb{X}=P^{d}(\mathbb{R}),\>P^{4}(\mathbb{C}),\>P^{8}(\mathbb{H})
and
P^{16}(Cay)
.

For a
K
-biinvariant
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{X})
, its spherical Fourier series is of the form

f(\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\>,\>\>\theta\in[0,\pi]\>,

with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then its Schrödinger propagation is also
K
-biinvariant and is given by,

e^{it\Delta}f(\theta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-it\lambda^{2}_{n}}\;a_{n}\>\tilde{Z}_{n}(\theta)\>,\>\>\theta\in[0,\pi]\>,

where
\lambda^{2}_{n}
are as classified in Table
2
. For notational convenience, we write the maximal function as,

S^{*}f(\theta):=\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{it\Delta}f(\theta)\right|\>,\>\>\theta\in[0,\pi]\>.

Then in view of (

In this section, we prove Theorem

. But we first see the following counting result, whose proof uses asymptotics of arithmetic functions:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be large and
\varepsilon>0
be small. Then there exists
C>0
independent of
N
such that the set

(6.1)

E_{N}:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\text{ odd }:\>\sqrt{N}\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{N}\\
p\text{ even }:\>2\varepsilon<\frac{2\pi p}{q}<\pi-2\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{2\pi p}{q}+\frac{\pi}{16N}\>,\>\frac{2\pi p}{q}+\frac{\pi}{8N}\right)\>,

has Lebesgue measure
|E_{N}|\geq C
.

Proof.

We first note that the intervals considered in the above union are either disjoint or identical. Indeed, let
(p_{1},q_{1})
and
(p_{2},q_{2})
be two pairs as in the definition (
6.1
) such that

\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}\leq\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\>.

Now if the corresponding intervals have non-empty intersection, then we must have

\frac{2\pi p_{2}}{q_{2}}+\frac{\pi}{16N}\leq\frac{2\pi p_{1}}{q_{1}}+\frac{\pi}{8N}\>,

which yields

p_{2}q_{1}-p_{1}q_{2}\leq\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{32N}\leq\frac{1}{8}\>.

Now since
p_{2}q_{1}-p_{1}q_{2}
is a non-negative integer, we must have
p_{2}q_{1}-p_{1}q_{2}=0
, that is,

\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}}=\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}}\>,

and hence the corresponding intervals are identical. Thus we get that

(6.2)

|E_{N}|\geq(\text{the number of intervals appearing in the definition of }E_{N})\times\frac{\pi}{16N}\>.

We now consider the pairs
(p,q)
such that
p=2p^{\prime}
with
\gcd(p^{\prime},q)=1
. Then the condition on
p
in the definition of
E_{N}
becomes

2\varepsilon<\frac{4\pi p^{\prime}}{q}<\pi-2\varepsilon\>,

that is,

\frac{\varepsilon q}{2\pi}<p^{\prime}<\frac{q}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon q}{2\pi}\>.

Now as

\left|\left(\frac{\varepsilon q}{2\pi},\frac{q}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon q}{2\pi}\right)\right|=\frac{q}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon q}{\pi}>\frac{q}{5}\>,\>\>\text{ for }\varepsilon<\frac{\pi}{20}\>\>,

for each odd
q
with
\sqrt{N}\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{N}
, we look at the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{I}_{q}
defined as,

\mathcal{I}_{q}:=\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\mid j\leq\frac{q}{5}\text{ and }\gcd(j,q)=1\right\}\>.

To proceed, we use the fundamental identity of the Möbius function given by (
2.16
) to write,

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{I}_{q}=\sum_{j\leq\frac{q}{5}}\>\>\sum_{m|\gcd(j,q)}\mu(m)=\sum_{m|q}\mu(m)\>\>\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq\frac{q}{5}\\
m|j\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m|q}\mu(m)\>\>\left\lfloor\frac{q}{5m}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m|q}\mu(m)\>\>\left(\frac{q}{5m}\>+\>\mathcal{O}(1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q}{5}\sum_{m|q}\frac{\mu(m)}{m}\>+\>\mathcal{O}\left(d(q)\right)\>,

where
d(q)
is the divisor function. Now by Möbius inversion (
2.17
), we have

\#\mathcal{I}_{q}=\frac{\varphi(q)}{5}\>+\>\mathcal{O}(d(q))\>,

which upon invoking the asymptotics of Euler’s totient function
\varphi
and the divisor function
d
given by (
2.18
), yields

\#\mathcal{I}_{q}\geq\frac{\varphi(q)}{6}\>,

for
q
sufficiently large. This is achieved by taking
N
sufficiently large. Now as
p=2p^{\prime}
with
\gcd(p^{\prime},q)=1
and
q
is odd, we have that
\gcd(p,q)=1
and hence each of the fractions
\frac{2\pi p}{q}
are unique. Thus it follows that,

(6.3)

\displaystyle\text{the number of intervals appearing in the definition of }E_{N}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sqrt{N}\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{N}\\
q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\#\mathcal{I}_{q}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sqrt{N}\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{N}\\
q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\varphi(q)\>.

This leads us to the following estimate, which by the Möbius inversion (
2.17
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq n\\
j\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\varphi(j)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq n\\
j\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\sum_{m|j}j\>\frac{\mu(m)}{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
m\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\mu(m)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq\frac{n}{m}\\
\ell\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\ell

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
m\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\mu(m){\left\lfloor\frac{\left(\frac{n}{m}+1\right)}{2}\right\rfloor}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
m\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\mu(m)\left\{\frac{\left(\frac{n}{m}+1\right)}{2}+\mathcal{O}(1)\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
m\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(n\sum_{m\leq n}\frac{1}{m}\right)+\mathcal{O}(n)\>.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq n\\
j\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\varphi(j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq n\\
m\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(n\log n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\frac{\zeta}{4}n^{2}\>,\text{ as }n\to\infty\>,

where
\zeta:=\displaystyle\sum_{m\text{ odd }}\frac{\mu(m)}{m^{2}}
, as the series converges absolutely. Hence for
N
sufficiently large,

(6.4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sqrt{N}\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{N}\\
q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\varphi(q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq 2\sqrt{N}\\
q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\varphi(q)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leq\sqrt{N}\\
q\text{ odd }\end{subarray}}\varphi(q)=(1+o(1))\frac{3\zeta}{4}N\>.

Thus combining (
6.2
)-(
6.4
), we obtain

|E_{N}|>\frac{\pi\zeta}{256}\>.

This completes the proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Thus for
\theta\in[\varepsilon,\pi-\varepsilon]
, the Schrödinger propagation of
f_{N}
is given by,

(6.6)

\displaystyle e^{it\Delta}f_{N}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{\sigma+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\right)^{\tau+\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-itn(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}\cos\left(n\theta+\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\log N)\>,

where

\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma+\tau+1)\theta-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\>.

We set,

S(t,\theta):=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-itn(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}\cos\left(n\theta+\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)\right)\>.

Now expanding the cosine in terms of exponentials, we write

(6.7)

S(t,\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\pm}S_{\pm}(t,\theta)\>e^{\pm i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)}\>,

where

S_{\pm}(t,\theta):=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-itn(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}\>e^{\pm in\theta}\>.

We now gradually obtain the divergence on the set
E_{N}
. First, we consider
t=\frac{2\pi}{q}
such that
q
is an odd integer with
\sqrt{N}\leq q\leq 2\sqrt{N}
. Then we have,

S_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-2\pi i\frac{n(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}{q}}\>e^{in\theta}\>.

We note that in our cases,
\sigma+\tau+1\in\mathbb{N}
and hence the function
n\mapsto e^{-2\pi i\frac{n(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}{q}}
is
q
-periodic. This enables us to decompose
S_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)
as follows,

(6.8)

\displaystyle S_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n=0}^{q\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor-1}\>+\>\sum_{n=q\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor}^{N-1}\right)e^{-2\pi i\frac{n(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}{q}}\>e^{in\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor-1}e^{i\ell q\theta}\right)\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)\>+\>\mathcal{O}(q)\>,

where

\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}e^{-2\pi i\frac{n(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}{q}}\>e^{in\theta}\>.

Next, if
\theta=\frac{2\pi p}{q}
for some integer
p
, then we get

\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}e^{-2\pi i\frac{n(n+\sigma+\tau+1)}{q}}\>e^{2\pi i\frac{np}{q}}=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}e^{-2\pi i\frac{n^{2}+n(\sigma+\tau+1-p)}{q}}\>.

Now completing the square in the numerator of the last expression modulo
q
and plugging in the evaluation of the complete Gauss sum, we get that

\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)=z_{q}\>\sqrt{q}\>e^{2\pi i\frac{r(\sigma+\tau+1-p)^{2}}{q}}\>,

where
r
is an integer such that
4r\equiv 1(\text{mod }q)
and
z_{q}
is a unimodular complex number as
q
is odd. Now choosing
p
to be even, we can utilize the property that
4r\equiv 1(\text{mod }q)
to get,

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{q}\>\sqrt{q}\>e^{2\pi i\frac{r(\sigma+\tau+1)^{2}+rp^{2}}{q}}\>e^{-2\pi i\frac{2r(\sigma+\tau+1)p}{q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{q}\>\sqrt{q}\>e^{2\pi i\frac{r(\sigma+\tau+1)^{2}+rp^{2}}{q}}\>e^{-i\frac{\pi(\sigma+\tau+1)p}{q}}\>.

Next, we consider
\theta=\frac{2\pi p}{q}+\xi
, where
p,q
are as before and
|\xi|\leq\pi/(8N)
. Then since the chordal metric on
S^{1}
is
1
-Lipschitz, it follows that

\left|\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)-\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}|e^{in\xi}-1|\leq|\xi|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}n\right)\leq\frac{q^{2}}{N}\>,

that is,

(6.10)

\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)=\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{q^{2}}{N}\right)\>.

Also,

\left|e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)}-e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}\left(\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)}\right|=\left|e^{\frac{i}{2}(\sigma+\tau+1)\xi}-1\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\>,

that is,

(6.11)

e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)}=e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}\left(\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\>.

Now combining (
6.8
)-(
6.11
), we get

S_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)\>e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)}=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor-1}e^{i\ell q\theta}\right)\mathscr{S}_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}\left(\frac{2\pi p}{q}\right)}+\mathcal{O}(q)\>.

Next plugging in the explicit expression (
6.9
) in the above, we further get,

S_{+}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)\>e^{i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)}=z_{q}\>\sqrt{q}\>e^{2\pi i\frac{r(\sigma+\tau+1)^{2}+rp^{2}}{q}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor-1}e^{i\ell q\xi}\right)e^{-i\frac{\left(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}\pi}+\mathcal{O}(q)\>.

Similarly, for the same values of
\theta
, we will get

S_{-}\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)\>e^{-i\kappa_{\sigma,\tau}(\theta)}=z_{q}\>\sqrt{q}\>e^{2\pi i\frac{r(\sigma+\tau+1)^{2}+rp^{2}}{q}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor-1}e^{-i\ell q\xi}\right)e^{i\frac{\left(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}\pi}+\mathcal{O}(q)\>.

From the above two estimates, we get back to
S\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)
in (
6.7
),

\left|S\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)\right|=\sqrt{q}\left|\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{N}{q}\right\rfloor-1}\cos\left(\frac{\left(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}\pi-\ell q\xi\right)\right|\>+\>\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\right)\>.

Now since in our cases
\frac{\left(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}=\frac{d-1}{4}
, with
d
even and
|\ell q\xi|\leq\pi/8
, the cosines in the sum are of the same sign and moreover, we get that there exists
C>0
such that each of the summands satisfy,

\left|\cos\left(\frac{\left(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}\pi-\ell q\xi\right)\right|\geq C\>.

And hence,

\left|S\left(\frac{2\pi}{q},\theta\right)\right|\gtrsim\sqrt{q}\times\frac{N}{q}-\sqrt{N}\gtrsim N^{3/4}\>.

Plugging this in (
6.6
), we get that in particular for
\theta\in E_{N}
,

\sup_{0\leq t<2\pi}\left|e^{it\Delta}f_{N}(\theta)\right|\gtrsim N^{3/4}\>,

that is, we get (
6.5
), which completes the proof for the Schrödinger equation.

Step 2:
By the transference principle (Theorem
1.9
), the results in Theorem
1.5
for the Boussinesq equation and the Beam equation follow from the result of the Schrödinger equation proved in Step
1
, in view of Remark
1.8
.

Indeed, for the sake of contradiction, let us assume that there exists
\alpha_{0}<1/4
for which the maximal estimate holds for Beam or Boussinesq. Then the maximal estimate also holds for all
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
. By the transference principle (Theorem
1.9
) and Remark
1.8
, the same must also be true for the Schrödinger equation, in particular, for all
\alpha\in(\alpha_{0},1/4)
which contradicts the result obtained in Step 1. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Our number theoretic arguments do not seem to work for
P^{d}(\mathbb{R})
, the only case of rank one Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type excluded from Theorem
1.5
. This can be attributed to the fact that by (
2.7
), only in the case of
P^{d}(\mathbb{R})
we have
s=2
in (
2.6
) and (
2.10
). The reason lies in the construction of
P^{d}(\mathbb{R})
from
\mathbb{S}^{d}
by identifying every pair of antipodal points and thus making it the only non simply-connected rank one Riemannian symmetric space of compact type of dimension
\geq 2
, with fundamental group
\pi_{1}\left(P^{d}(\mathbb{R})\right)=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

7.
Open problems

It would be interesting to pursue a complete solution to the Carleson’s problem on Riemannian symmetric spaces of compact type. It seems to be a difficult task however, given that it has not been achieved yet, even in the case of the circle
\mathbb{T}
. In this light, we pose the following problems:

(1)

Can Theorem
1.1
, that is, the sufficiency of the bound
\alpha>1/2
be extended to rank
\geq 3
, improving Cowling’s abstract result
[
Cow83
]
in the process?

(2)

As clarified in Remark
5.5
, due to the concentration of the antipodal manifold, our approach in the proof of Theorem
1.4
fails for the collection:
P^{d}(\mathbb{C})\>(d=6,8,\dots)
and
P^{d}(\mathbb{H})\>(d=12,16,\dots)
. Nevertheless, can the sufficiency of the bound
\alpha>1/3
be extended to all such spaces?

(3)

Can Theorem
1.5
, that is, the failure of
\alpha<1/4
be extended to
P^{d}(\mathbb{R}),\>d\geq 2
?

(4)

Finally, can the results appearing in this article be improved?
