BIS Versus End-tidal Anaesthetic Gas Concentration-guided General Anaesthesia for Prevention of Intraoperative Awareness in Elective Surgery: A Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Shiv Lal Soni, Goverdhan Dutt Puri, Kiran Jangra, Amitabh Dutta, Nitin Sethi, Shayam Charan Meena, Suman Arora, Kamal Kajal, Akhilesh Sharma, Narender Kaloria, Ajay Singh, Venkata Ganesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAwareness during general anaesthesia (GA) is a rare but serious complication, affecting 0.15%–0.2% of patients, with potential psychological consequences such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Bispectral index (BIS) monitoring, which uses electroencephalography (EEG)-derived metrics to assess anaesthesia depth, has shown promise in reducing intraoperative awareness, though its universal adoption remains limited due to mixed evidence.PurposeThis study aimed to compare BIS-guided and end-tidal anaesthetic gas concentration (ETAG)-guided techniques in a randomised controlled trial conducted in India, addressing gaps in existing literature, particularly among Asian populations.MethodsA total of 674 patients, aged 18–65 years, undergoing elective surgeries were randomised into two groups: BIS (n = 341) and ETAG (n = 333). The assessment of intraoperative awareness in both study groups was conducted using two distinct methods. Two independent interviewers, both blinded to the study conditions, carried out these assessments. The first method involved the modified Brice interview, while the second employed the Michigan Awareness Classification Instrument.ResultsAwareness incidence was 0.58% in the BIS group and 0.90% in the ETAG group (p = .63), with no statistically significant differences. Secondary outcomes, including time to eye opening, extubation, hospital stay duration and incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), were also comparable. Both methods provided equivalent anaesthetic depth and haemodynamic stability.ConclusionThis randomised trial found that neither BIS nor minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) demonstrated superiority in terms of intraoperative awareness. Future research should focus on high-risk populations, explore stringent BIS targets (e.g., BIS <50) and explore the cost-effectiveness of these monitoring techniques in various clinical settings.

Introduction

Awareness during general anaesthesia (GA) is a rare yet significant complication, affecting approximately 0.15%–0.2% of patients.1–3 Each year in the USA alone, around 20,000–40,000 individuals experience some form of awareness during surgery. 4 This phenomenon occurs when anaesthesia is insufficient, leaving the patient conscious of their surroundings or the surgical procedure itself. Such experiences can lead to profound cognitive and psychological effects, with some patients developing long-lasting conditions like post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).4–6

To combat this issue, the bispectral index (BIS) monitor, a processed electroencephalography (EEG) tool, has been increasingly adopted in operating rooms to gauge the depth of anaesthesia. By capturing and processing brain activity through a patented algorithm, BIS produces a number between 0 and 100, where 0 indicates total brain suppression and 100 denotes full wakefulness.7, 8 The ideal BIS range during surgery is typically between 40 and 60, ensuring a sufficient level of anaesthesia to prevent awareness while optimising anaesthetic dosing.9, 10

Despite its promising use, BIS monitoring has yet to become a universal standard for anaesthesia depth monitoring. While studies have demonstrated its potential to reduce anaesthesia awareness, some research, such as studies conducted by Avidan and Mashour, has yielded inconclusive results.9–14 Notably, much of the data supporting BIS’s efficacy comes from studies on Western Caucasian populations, leaving significant gaps in understanding how BIS performs in other demographic groups, particularly among Asian patients.15–19

Although some research has emerged from Asia, the data remain scattered and inconsistent. As of now, no comprehensive study has been conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of BIS in reducing anaesthesia awareness among Asian populations. 20 Given this gap, our aim is to carry out a prospective, randomised trial in India, focusing on whether BIS guidance in Asian patients aligns with the findings from Caucasian populations. This study could offer vital insights into the role of neurophysiological monitoring in minimising the risk of explicit recall during anaesthesia for Asian patients, contributing to more personalised and effective anaesthesia care.

Methods

A parallel group, randomised research design was used for this study. Following Institutional Ethics Committee approval, prospective CTRI registration (CTRI/2018/07/015093 [dated 27/07/2018]) and written informed consent, participants were recruited between August 2018 and December 2023. The initial plan was to recruit 2,000 participants; however, due to constraints imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, the trial was terminated. Both patients and analysts remained blinded to group assignments to maintain the study’s integrity and ensure unbiased outcomes.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study consisted of male and female participants aged between 18 and 65 years and of American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status I or II, scheduled to undergo GA for elective surgeries which were expected to last more than 30 min.

On the other hand, individuals were excluded if they had any uncompensated systemic comorbidities, were undergoing cardiac or neurosurgical procedures, obstetric surgeries or emergency surgeries. Patients with an anticipated difficult airway, a history of brain injury, EEG abnormalities, neuropsychiatric disorders or substance abuse involving opioids, alcohol, recreational drugs or benzodiazepines were also excluded. Further exclusion criteria were the presence of pacemakers or other electronic implants, obesity with a BMI greater than 32 kg/m2, and an allergy to adhesives.

Patients scheduled for elective surgery under GA were randomly assigned to one of two groups using a computer-generated random number table. Group I (BIS-guided group) had GA monitored and controlled to target a BIS between 40 and 60, while Group II (end-tidal anaesthetic gas concentration [ETAG]-guided group) had anaesthesia monitored and controlled through ETAG (ETAG or total minimum alveolar concentration [MAC] targeted between 0.8 and 1.2). Randomisation concealment was ensured by using opaque sealed envelopes labelled with alphabetic codes, with patient data slips attached to the envelopes prior to opening. Blinding was maintained, with the investigator, assessor and data analyst unaware of whether BIS or ETAG was used for anaesthesia monitoring. Subgroup analysis was conducted and reported specifically when gender equivalence was observed, defined as at least 40% representation of either sex in each group.

Anaesthesia Technique

The anaesthesia technique adhered to standard safety protocols, including monitoring end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2), pulse oximetry, electrocardiogram (EKG) and non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP). Induction of anaesthesia involved administering fentanyl (2 mcg/kg) and propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg), followed by a non-depolarising muscle relaxant (vecuronium, rocuronium or atracurium, as per the treating anaesthesiologist’s choice) to enable smooth tracheal intubation. GA was maintained using sevoflurane or desflurane combined with oxygen and nitrous oxide, aiming for an age-adjusted MAC of 0.8–1.2.

During the procedure, BIS scores and end-tidal agent concentrations were recorded at 5-min intervals. In the BIS group, the anaesthesiologist was blinded to the ETAG and MAC readings (parameter was deselected from display), while in the ETAG group, the BIS display was deselected/disabled. However, both ETAG and MAC data were reviewed post-study from the recorded trends in the monitor. MAC levels were adjusted to maintain haemodynamic stability within ±20% of baseline systolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure and heart rate. Deviations from this range were managed using vasopressors or vasodilators/beta-blockers.

At the conclusion of surgery, the inhalation agents were stopped, and any residual neuromuscular blockade was reversed with neostigmine and glycopyrrolate. Patients were extubated only when fully awake and capable of following commands, ensuring a smooth and safe emergence from anaesthesia.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the difference in the frequency of awareness between the two groups. The secondary outcomes included incidence of PTSD, time to eye opening upon verbal command, time to tracheal extubation after discontinuation of GA, hospital length of stay and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).

Assessment Parameters

The assessment of intraoperative awareness in both study groups was conducted using two distinct methods. Two independent interviewers, both blinded to the study conditions, carried out these assessments. The first method involved the modified Brice interview, while the second employed the Michigan Awareness Classification Instrument. 21 These interviews were conducted 24 h post-surgery, and follow-up assessments were done after 1 month, the latter being conducted via telephonic contact for convenience and consistency.

For any patients identified with possible or definite awareness during surgery, follow-up care was prioritised. The primary anaesthesiologist, in collaboration with a three-member Quality Assurance committee, took charge of these cases. Additionally, all patients with suspected or confirmed awareness were formally offered psychiatric care, ensuring comprehensive support for their mental and emotional well-being throughout the recovery process. This approach ensured that any instances of awareness were promptly addressed and managed with the highest standards of care. The quantitative assessment involved administering the modified Brice interview to participants from both groups 24 h after surgery and again after 1 month.

For the qualitative assessment, participants from both groups were evaluated using the Michigan Awareness Classification Instrument. 14 For participants with definite awareness, counselling sessions were offered to prevent long-term behavioural issues or PTSD.

Intra-reviewer observations were analysed using Fleiss’s kappa statistics for agreement. Additionally, intraoperative BIS scores and end-tidal inhalation agent concentrations were recorded every 5 min, alongside the time to awakening and tracheal extubation. Awakening time was measured from the discontinuation of anaesthesia to the patient’s first spontaneous eye opening and their ability to follow commands, while the time to tracheal extubation was noted from anaesthesia cessation to the actual extubation.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was estimated to detect a 0.04% difference in awareness incidence between the ETAG and BIS-guided groups, as reported in the study. 14 To achieve 80% power with a type-II error of 0.5, 1,800 patients were required. To mitigate potential dropouts, an additional 10% of participants were included, increasing the total sample size to 2,000 patients.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome, the difference in the frequency of awareness, was evaluated using a chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as either mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), depending on their distribution. Comparisons were conducted using the Welch t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Repeated measures were analysed using a linear mixed model with a random intercept at the subject level. Post hoc pairwise comparison p values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. Categorical data were expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using either the two-sample proportion test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, depending on suitability. A standardised difference exceeding 0.25 (absolute value) in baseline variables indicated significant imbalance. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, 2024).

Comparison of Incidence of Awareness and Other Characteristics Between Bispectral Index and ETAG Groups

Table 2 compares the incidence of awareness and other perioperative characteristics between the BIS group and the ETAG group, comprising 341 (51%) and 333 (49%) participants, respectively. The average length of hospital stay was slightly longer for BIS (6.01 days) versus ETAG (5.77 days), with a mean difference of 0.24 days (p = .13). Recovery metrics, including time to eye opening and time to extubation, were comparable between the groups, showing no significant differences. PONV occurred marginally more in the BIS group (12.02%) than ETAG (10.81%), but this was also not statistically significant. Additionally, BIS and MAC values at extubation and eye opening were nearly identical across groups, with all p values indicating no significant differences (p = .3–.7).

Analysis of Patients With Awareness and Without Awareness

Demographic, comorbidity and anaesthetic-technique characteristics were comparable between awareness and non-awareness patients (Supplementary Table 1). Two intraoperative variables were associated with postoperative awareness. First, anaesthesia maintenance duration, which closely tracks total surgical duration in this cohort, was substantially longer in awareness cases (median 295 min, IQR 110–340) than in non-awareness patients (median 115 min, IQR 85–145; Wilcoxon rank-sum p = .025). This represents a 2.6-fold difference in median exposure time and, at the individual case level, four of the five awareness patients underwent procedures lasting more than 110 min, with three exceeding 290 min. Second, any epoch of end-tidal MAC below 0.8 during maintenance was recorded in 2 of 5 awareness cases (40.0%) compared with 27 of 669 non-awareness patients (4.0%; Fisher’s exact p = .017; unadjusted odds ratio 16, 95% CI 1.3–142). No patient in the cohort recorded a BIS value above 60 at any 5-min sampling epoch during maintenance.

Per-patient depth profiles for the five awareness cases are presented in Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 2. Three of the five cases reached the upper portion of the recommended BIS range (55–58), but no patient exceeded BIS 60 at any recorded epoch. Two cases (Patient 2, BIS arm; Patient 32, MAC arm) showed brief but pronounced excursions in volatile depth (minimum MAC 0.2 and 0.3, respectively), accounting for the MAC <0.8 association observed in the aggregate analysis. Notably, two further awareness cases (Patient 24, MAC arm; Patient 470, MAC arm) experienced explicit recall despite intraoperative depth profiles that remained within nominally adequate ranges on both metrics (maximum BIS 54 and 52; minimum MAC 0.8 in both). Emergence values were appropriate in all five cases (BIS at eye-opening 89–93; BIS at extubation 92–98; MAC at eye-opening 0.2–0.5; MAC at extubation 0.2–0.3), indicating that recall was not attributable to delayed recovery of consciousness. Surgical case-mix in the awareness group (n = 1 type 3; n = 2 each types 4 and 5) was weighted towards longer, more complex procedures, congruent with the duration association.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial aimed to compare the incidence of intraoperative awareness between BIS-guided and ETAG-guided anaesthesia. The key finding revealed no statistically significant difference in awareness rates between groups, with 0.58% in the BIS group and 0.90% in the ETAG group. This suggests that both BIS and ETAG techniques are equally effective in preventing intraoperative awareness, making either method a viable option for anaesthesiologists. The overall incidence of awareness in the study was 0.74%, aligning with rates typically reported in the literature (0.1% –0.2%). 9 The slightly higher rate in the present study could be due to the specific patient population or the heightened sensitivity of our awareness detection methods. Another important finding was that there were no significant differences for haemodynamic parameters such as heart rate and mean arterial pressure or anaesthetic depth indicators (BIS or ETAG values) between groups. This suggests that both monitoring methods provided comparable levels of anaesthetic depth and haemodynamic stability throughout the surgeries.

The findings that secondary outcomes, such as time to eye opening, extubation time, hospital stay duration and incidence of PONV, did not differ significantly between groups are consistent with broader trends in the literature. However, the debate surrounding the superiority of these methods for preventing awareness remains unsettled. A study corroborates these findings, showing no significant difference in recovery profiles when comparing BIS-guided and ETAG-guided anaesthesia. 9 This aligns with the argument that the choice of monitoring method can be driven more by cost, availability and clinical expertise rather than clinical necessity in routine cases.

A trial suggested that BIS monitoring reduces the risk of awareness, particularly in high-risk populations. An 82% reduction in awareness was reported, making BIS an attractive option for cases where awareness is a significant concern. 12 In one study, similar findings were reported in their analysis of BIS use during intravenous anaesthesia, where it significantly reduced awareness. 22 However, its effectiveness was less evident for inhalation-based anaesthesia. 23 In contrast, the notion of BIS’s superiority was challenged, finding no significant difference in awareness prevention between BIS and ETAG-guided anaesthesia in high-risk surgical patients. 10 Their findings were supported by a subsequent meta-analysis, which concluded that neither technique had a significant edge over the other in terms of preventing awareness. 23

Some critiques have focused on methodological issues, such as variations in how intraoperative awareness is defined and measured. 21 It was highlighted that inconsistent protocols and reliance on patient-reported outcomes could introduce bias, further complicating direct comparisons between BIS and ETAG techniques. 10

Intraoperative awareness occurred in 0.7% of this cohort, at the upper end of contemporary estimates from non-cardiac surgery using structured Brice interviewing, but consistent with reports from settings where prolonged or higher-complexity procedures predominate. The absence of a statistically significant difference between MAC-guided and BIS-guided monitoring must be interpreted cautiously: with only five events, the comparison is profoundly underpowered, and the wide confidence interval around the odds ratio (0.18–19) is uninformative in either direction. The two signals that did emerge carry greater interpretive weight because they cohere with established mechanisms. Longer anaesthesia/surgical duration in awareness cases is consistent with both a longer at-risk window and a greater number of drug-titration decisions, including deliberate lightening of anaesthesia for haemodynamic reasons. Any episode of end-tidal volatile concentration below 0.8 MAC during maintenance was strongly associated with awareness (OR 16), reinforcing the well-established principle that brief lapses in pharmacological depth constitute a modifiable risk that vigilance and target-controlled delivery should be designed to prevent.

The description of the cases with awareness in this study offers two findings of clinical importance that the aggregate comparison obscures. First, no awareness case ever exceeded a BIS of 60, despite three cases reaching the upper margin of the recommended target range (55–58). This suggests that the conventional BIS ceiling of 60 may lack sensitivity in some patients, and that processed-EEG indices in the 55–60 band should not be regarded as unequivocally reassuring. Whether a more stringent ceiling, for example, BIS ≤55, would improve discrimination cannot be determined from these data; threshold-modification post hoc on five events would be statistically uninterpretable, and this question warrants prospective evaluation in adequately powered trials. Second, two awareness cases experienced explicit recall despite depth profiles that remained within nominally adequate ranges on both BIS (maximum 54 and 52) and end-tidal MAC (minimum 0.8 in both). This dissociation between summary depth indices and patient-reported recall, while infrequent, is consistent with the recognised limitations of processed EEG and MAC as proxies for the awareness phenotype: neither metric (i.e., their thresholds) directly measures the integrative neural correlates of consciousness, and inter-individual variability in EEG-consciousness coupling, idiosyncratic responses to specific volatile agents, and unmeasured contributions from concurrent opioid and neuromuscular blocking agents may all contribute to such discordant cases. Notably, both cases operated at the lower boundary of adequate depth throughout maintenance, and neither monitor provided any margin above its recommended threshold, a pattern that foreshadows the MAC <0.8 association discussed below and suggests that ‘adequate’ and ‘safe’ are not necessarily equivalent when depth is titrated to the edge of the recommended range. The clinical implication is that no single intraoperative monitor, in isolation, can be relied upon to exclude awareness in every patient, and that over-reliance on any single depth index, without corroboration from clinical vigilance and complementary monitoring, may contribute to missed lighter plane anaesthesia events.

Two mechanistically distinct pathways to awareness are suggested by the association with duration of anaesthesia and epochs with MAC <0.8. The duration association is consistent with a cumulative at-risk-time hypothesis: longer procedures increase the number of drug-titration decisions, the number of haemodynamic events prompting deliberate lightening of anaesthesia, and the number of transitions between surgical stimuli of differing intensity, each providing an opportunity for transient inadequate depth. Longer procedures are also disproportionately complex, involving more blood loss, greater fluid shifts and pharmacokinetic drift of both volatile and intravenous agents, all of which complicate depth maintenance. The median maintenance time of 295 min in awareness cases places them in the upper quintile of the cohort and reinforces that surgical complexity and duration are the operative construct. Duration should therefore be regarded as a proxy for a bundle of correlated risks rather than an independent causal factor, and clinically actionable interpretation lies in recognising prolonged, complex cases as a phenotype warranting heightened vigilance and, where possible, redundant (combination of BIS and ETAG) depth monitoring.

The MAC <0.8 finding operates on a shorter timescale and points to a more directly modifiable mechanism. End-tidal volatile concentration is the pharmacological substrate for hypnosis and immobility during volatile-based anaesthesia, and even transient reductions below 0.7 MAC have been associated with intraoperative awareness in registry data and in the B-Aware and B-Unaware trials.12, 24 The two awareness cases in whom MAC dropped to 0.2–0.3 experienced excursions well beyond routine titration variability and are consistent with deliberate lightening for haemodynamic reasons, vaporiser under-delivery or fresh-gas-flow-related dilution during long low-flow anaesthesia. The unadjusted odds ratio of 16 must be interpreted with two caveats: first, the confidence interval (1.3–142) is broad, reflecting the five-event denominator, and the point estimate is unstable; second, the association is confounded by duration, since longer cases provide more sampling epochs and therefore a greater opportunity for at least one epoch to fall below threshold. Notwithstanding these limitations, the convergence of a plausible mechanism, a large effect estimate, and consistency with prior literature supports MAC <0.8 as a candidate intraoperative marker for retrospective identification of at-risk cases and, prospectively, as an alarm threshold worth evaluating in future trials.

This is the first large randomised trial to compare BIS and ETAG/MAC-guided strategies in the Indian subcontinent. However, several limitations qualify the inferences. The trial was prematurely terminated before the planned 2,000 patients could be recruited. As a post hoc descriptive measure, conditional power at the time of trial cessation was 13% under the original design assumptions and 13% under the observed trend, indicating that the planned sample size was insufficient to detect the hypothesised effect had enrolment continued to completion; the trial is therefore best interpreted as inconclusive rather than negative. The trial included only ASA I–II patients and is single-centre in nature, which may limit generalisability. The multimorbid and the obese may have different awareness/anaesthetic requirement profiles. Future trials should plan to recruit enriched populations, that is, populations at high risk of awareness, when comparing BIS versus ETAG/MAC, explore stringent BIS thresholds and cost-effectiveness.

Conclusion

This randomised trial found that neither BIS nor MAC demonstrated superiority in terms of intraoperative awareness. Future research should focus on high-risk populations, explore stringent BIS targets and explore the cost-effectiveness of these monitoring techniques in various clinical settings.
