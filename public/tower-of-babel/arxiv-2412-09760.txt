Congruence-based Learning of Probabilistic Deterministic Finite Automata
M. Carrasco F. Mayr S. Yovine

Abstract
This work studies the question of learning probabilistic deterministic automata from language models. For this purpose, it focuses on analyzing the relations defined on algebraic structures over strings by equivalences and similarities on probability distributions. We introduce a congruence that extends the classical Myhill-Nerode congruence for formal languages. This new congruence is the basis for defining regularity over language models. We present an active learning algorithm that computes the quotient with respect to this congruence whenever the language model is regular. The paper also defines the notion of recognizability for language models and shows that it coincides with regularity for congruences. For relations which are not congruences, it shows that this is not the case. Finally, it discusses the impact of this result on learning in the context of language models.



A Preprint

1
Introduction

In the last few years, there has been a growing interest in trying to understand sequence processing neural networks via capturing their behavior with finite automata through active learning by means of adapting Angluin’s
\mathrm{L^{\ast}}
learning algorithm
[
1
]
.
Works like
[
16
,
12
,
8
]
deal with neural binary classifiers of finite sequences over finite alphabets with the aim to learning deterministic finite automata (DFA).
For neural language models, the goal is to learn a probabilistic deterministic finite automaton (PDFA)
[
14
]
. In this case, two approaches have been studied:
those which view neural networks as producing the probability of the input sequence
[
3
,
13
]
,
and those which consider a network to be an autoregressive model that outputs the next-symbol probability distribution
[
15
,
11
]
.
This paper focuses on the latter.

The algorithm proposed in
[
15
]
is based on a tolerance relation induced by the supremum distance between probability distributions in order to group states which have similar futures, in the sense that when continued by the same sequence they reach states that remain close to each other. This is achieved by clustering the observations obtained through querying the target neural language model with so-called
membership queries
. An important drawback of this approach is that the non-transitivity of the tolerance inherited from the distance implies that the clusters are not unique.
In contrast, the learning algorithm developed in
[
11
]
steps on equivalences over probability distributions in order to define a family of congruences over the set of sequences. The advantage of this is that a congruence being a transitive tolerance, it induces a unique partition of the set of observations got by membership queries, which allows avoiding the possibly arbitrary grouping choices made by the clustering method.
Indeed, this approach is aligned with the one behind
\mathrm{L^{\ast}}
whose cornerstone is the relation between regular languages and DFA induced by the Myhill-Nerode congruence.

The contribution of this paper is two-fold.
First, it studies the mathematical properties of the tolerance and congruence structures induced on the set of sequences by languages models for any similarity or equivalence relation over probability distributions.
Second, it steps on these properties to analyze the learning capabilities of these approaches provided the kind of relation they are based on.

To achieve this, the paper starts by reviewing similarities and equivalences commonly used in the literature for the analysis of language models (Section
2
).
It then shows that these similarities and equivalence relations induce tolerances and congruences over sequences, respectively, it proves several results about them, and it defines the quotient given by a congruence and the notion of regular language model (Section
3
).
Section
4
recalls the formal definition of PDFA, it introduces the concept of quotient PDFA, and it proves several results establishing the formal relations between language models, PDFA and their quotients.
Section
5
proposes the algorithm
\mathrm{L_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}}
which generalizes Angluin’s
\mathrm{L^{\ast}}
algorithm with the purpose of learning a quotient PDFA from a language model modulo an equivalence over distributions
\mathcal{E}
. Correctness and termination of
\mathrm{L_{\mathcal{E}}^{\ast}}
heavily rely on the properties of quotients proved before. It also defines the concept of recognizability of language models and proves that it coincides with regularity in the case of congruences.
Section
6
is devoted to the analysis of the feasibility of developing learning algorithms that rely on tolerances as opposed to congruences. The main result is that recognizability does not longer imply regularity. The impact of this fact in learning is discussed.
Section
7
summarizes the contributions.

2
Probability distributions

Let
\Sigma
be a finite
alphabet
and
\Sigma_{\$}\triangleq\Sigma\cup\{\$\}
, where
\$
is a special
terminal
symbol not in
\Sigma
.
A
probability distribution
over
\Sigma_{\$}
is a function
\delta:\Sigma_{\$}\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}}\delta(\sigma)=1
.
We denote
\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
the set of all probability distributions over
\Sigma_{\$}
.

2.1
Similarities

We call
similarity
, denoted
\mathcal{S}
, a reflexive and symmetric binary relation between distributions over
\Sigma_{\$}
. We write
\delta\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\delta^{\prime}
to indicate that
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
are related by
\mathcal{S}
.

A natural way of defining a similarity is by means of a function
{z}:\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})\times\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying:

1.

{z}
is symmetric in the sense that
{z}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})={z}(\delta^{\prime},\delta)
for all
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
;

2.

and
{z}(\delta,\delta)=0
for all
\delta\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
.

Such a function
{z}
induces a similarity via a threshold
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denoted
\approx_{({z},t)}
and defined as:

\displaystyle\delta\approx_{({z},t)}\delta

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}{z}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})\leq t

(1)

By choosing the function
{z}
one gets different examples of similarities appearing in the literature.
Before listing some of them, let us define
\mathit{rank}(\delta):\Sigma_{\$}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
to be the
ranking
of symbols
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}
induced by their probability
\delta(\sigma)
:

\displaystyle\mathit{rank}(\delta)(\sigma)

\displaystyle\triangleq\#\{\delta(\sigma^{\prime})\mid\delta(\sigma^{\prime})\geq\delta(\sigma),\,\sigma^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{\$}\}.

(2)

We assume
\mathit{rank}(\delta)
to be injective, or equivalently, that there are no ties. We can achieve this assuming
\Sigma_{\$}
to be equipped with an arbitrary ordering and break ranking ties using this ordering. For
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)\subseteq\Sigma_{\$}
, gives the set of top-
r
ranked symbols while forgetting their relative order:

\displaystyle\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)

\displaystyle\triangleq\{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}\mid\mathit{rank}(\delta)(\sigma)\leq r\}

(3)

The following are examples of similarities which are of interest in our context.

Variation Distance

corresponds to the choice of the infinity norm for
{z}
:

\displaystyle\mathit{vd}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}}|\delta(\sigma)-\delta^{\prime}(\sigma)|

(4)

This relation was used in
[
15
,
6
]
.

Support Difference Rate

A basic example of similarity is obtained by taking

\displaystyle\mathit{sdr}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\#\big(\supp(\delta)\oplus\supp(\delta^{\prime})\big)}{\#\Sigma_{\$}}\in[0,1]

(5)

where
\#
denotes cardinal,
\oplus
the symmetric difference between sets, and
\supp
the
support
of the distribution (set of elements with non-zero probability). The function
\mathit{sdr}
measures the proportion of symbols belonging to the support of one distribution but not to the other.

Word Error Rate

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the function:

\displaystyle\mathit{wer}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{2r}\#\big(\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)\oplus\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})\big)\in[0,1]

(6)

We obtain in this way the word error rate (WER), which measures the proportion of symbols which are
\mathit{top}_{r}
for one distribution but not for the other.
This relation was used in
[
15
]
with
r=1
.

Cumulative Gain

The previous example can also be obtained in a slightly different way by considering the cumulative gain with respect to a binary relevance measure. Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and define the relevance of symbol
\sigma
with respect to
\delta
as
\mathds{1}\big[\sigma\in\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)\big]
. Then the cumulative gain of
\delta^{\prime}
with respect to
\delta
for this relevance measure is given by:

\displaystyle\CG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})}\mathds{1}\big[\sigma\in\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)\big]

(7)

Since
\max_{\delta^{\prime}}\CG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)=r
, the normalized cumulative gain is
\frac{1}{r}\CG_{r}
. Then the previous example (Def.
6
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathit{wer}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\triangleq 1-\frac{\CG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)+\CG_{r}\left(\delta\mid\delta^{\prime}\right)}{2r}

(8)

Notice that
\CG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)
coincides with the cardinal of the intersection of the respective
\mathit{top}_{r}
symbols, which is symmetric. This symmetry breaks down in the general case of an arbitrary relevance measure or when considering a discounted cumulative gain as in the next example.

Discounted Cumulative Gain

Let us denote by
\sigma^{\delta}_{k}
the symbol whose ranking with respect to
\delta
equals
k
. That is
\mathit{rank}(\delta)\left(\sigma_{k}^{\delta}\right)=k
. Also, let
\mathit{rank}_{r}
be the function:

\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta)\triangleq\begin{cases}\mathit{rank}(\delta)&\text{if }\mathit{rank}(\delta)\leq r\\
r+1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(9)

In order to account for the specific ranking of symbols, we can modify the previous example by considering a measure of relevance related to the rank together with a discounted cumulative gain:

\displaystyle\DCG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{1}{\log_{2}(k+1)}\left[r-\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta)\left(\sigma_{k}^{\delta^{\prime}}\right)+1\right]

(10)

The discounting factor weights each ranking position so that the symbols that mostly account for the total sum are those appearing at the top of the ranking.
Since the maximum of
\DCG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)
is attained when
\delta^{\prime}=\delta
, the normalized discounted cumulative gain is given by:

\displaystyle\NDCG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left.\DCG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)\middle/\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{r-k+1}{\log_{2}(k+1)}\right.

(11)

Therefore we can consider the function:

\displaystyle\mathit{ndcg}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})\triangleq 1-\frac{\NDCG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)+\NDCG_{r}\left(\delta\mid\delta^{\prime}\right)}{2}

(12)

More generally, we can define the relevance of symbol
\sigma
with respect to a distribution
\delta
as
\delta(\sigma)
, and define the respective normalized discounted cumulative gain.
Such version of this similarity was used in
[
2
]
.

2.2
Equivalences

Let
\mathcal{E}
be an
equivalence
relation between distributions over
\Sigma_{\$}
.
For simplicity, we write
\delta=_{\mathcal{E}}\delta^{\prime}
to denote that
(\delta,\delta^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
.
We denote by
\left[\delta\right]_{\mathcal{E}}
the class of
\delta
and
\left[\cdot\right]_{\mathcal{E}}
the quotient map.
Several examples of equivalences are of interest. The following relations were used in
[
9
,
11
,
10
,
4
]
.

Quantization

Given a
quantization parameter

\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
,
\kappa\geq 1
, the quantization
interval

I_{\kappa}^{n}
, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq n<\kappa-1
, is the interval
\left[n\kappa^{-1},(n+1)\kappa^{-1}\right)
, and for
n=\kappa-1
, is the interval
\left[n\kappa^{-1},1\right]
.
For
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
:

\displaystyle\delta=_{\kappa}\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\textrm{for all}\ \sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}\ .\ (\delta(\sigma),\delta^{\prime}(\sigma))\in I_{\kappa}^{n}\times I_{\kappa}^{n}\ \textrm{for some}\ n

(13)

Rank

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and given distributions
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
, we define

\displaystyle\delta=_{\mathit{rank}_{r}}\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta)=\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})

(14)

Top

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
:

\displaystyle\delta=_{\mathit{top}_{r}}\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)=\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})

(15)

Support

For
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
:

\displaystyle\delta=_{\mathit{supeq}}\delta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\supp(\delta)=\supp(\delta^{\prime})

(16)

Combinations

One can combine two equivalence relations
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
by defining
\delta=_{\mathcal{E}}\delta^{\prime}
if and only if
\delta=_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}\delta^{\prime}
for
i=1,2
. For example,
=_{\kappa}
and
\mathit{top}_{r}
combined with
\mathit{supeq}
were used for analyzing constrained language models in
[
4
]
.

2.3
Properties

Intuitively, the examples of similarities and equivalences defined above suggest that they are related. We show here several results that formalize their relationship.

Proposition 1
.

For every
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
, if
\delta=_{\kappa}\delta^{\prime}
then
\delta\approx_{(\mathit{vd},\kappa^{-1})}\delta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Def.
13
,
\delta=_{\kappa}\delta^{\prime}
implies for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}
,

(\delta(\sigma),\delta^{\prime}(\sigma))\in I_{\kappa}^{n}\times I_{\kappa}^{n}
for some
n
.
Thus, by definition of
I_{\kappa}^{n}
,
|\delta(\sigma)-\delta^{\prime}(\sigma)|\leq\kappa^{-1}
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}
.
So,
\max_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{\$}}|\delta(\sigma)-\delta^{\prime}(\sigma)|\leq\kappa^{-1}
.
Hence, by Def.
4
,
\delta\approx_{(\mathit{vd},\kappa^{-1})}\delta^{\prime}
.
∎

In general, it is not true that
\approx_{({z},t)}
is an equivalence relation for
t>0
.
Nevertheless, when the function
{z}
is a pseudometric, meaning that it satisfies the triangle inequality
{z}(\delta,\delta^{\prime\prime})\leq{z}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})+{z}(\delta^{\prime},\delta^{\prime\prime})
, the relation
\approx_{({z},0)}
with threshold
t=0
is indeed an equivalence.
Clearly, this is the case for
\mathit{vd}
and
\mathit{sdr}
, which become
=
and
\mathit{supeq}
, respectively.
Moreover, the equivalence relations
\mathit{top}_{r}
and
\mathit{rank}_{r}
are related to the evaluation metrics WER and NDCG, sometimes used to compare language models
[
2
,
15
]
. This is made precise in the following propositions.

Proposition 2
.

For every
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
,

\delta=_{\mathit{top}_{r}}\delta^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathit{wer}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})=0
.

Proof.

From Def.
6
we have that
\mathit{wer}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})=0
is equivalent to
\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta)=\mathit{top}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

For every
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
we have
\delta=_{\mathit{rank}_{r}}\delta^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathit{ndcg}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})=0
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})=\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta)
. Then
\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta)\left(\sigma_{k}^{\delta^{\prime}}\right)=k
since
\sigma_{k}^{\delta^{\prime}}=\sigma_{k}^{\delta}
. Therefore by Def.
11
we have
\NDCG_{r}\left(\delta^{\prime}\mid\delta\right)=1
. Analogously we have
\NDCG_{r}\left(\delta\mid\delta^{\prime}\right)=1
. Thus, by Def.
12
,
\mathit{ndcg}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})=0
.

Let us prove that
\mathit{ndcg}_{r}(\delta,\delta^{\prime})=0
implies
\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta^{\prime})=\mathit{rank}_{r}(\delta)
. This amounts to showing that the function

\displaystyle S(R)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{r-R_{k}+1}{\log_{2}(k+1)}

(17)

defined over all sequences
R=\left(R_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{r}
of integers in
\{1,2,\ldots,r,r+1\}
with no repetitions except (possibly) for
r+1
, has a unique maximum at
R_{k}=k
. To prove this claim, first notice that if
R_{k}=r+1
for some
k
, then
r-R_{k}+1=0
and there is no contribution to the sum in Eq.
17
. So we can assume that
R
is a permutation of
\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
. Suppose that
R_{i}>R_{j}
for some indices
i<j
. Since
\log_{2}(i+1)<\log_{2}(j+1)
, swapping
R_{i}
and
R_{j}
yields a higher sum. Indeed, if
R^{\prime}
is the sequence with
R_{i}
and
R_{j}
swapped, then

S(R^{\prime})-S(R)=\left(R_{i}-R_{j}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\log_{2}(i+1)}-\frac{1}{\log_{2}(j+1)}\right]>0.

Therefore the maximum must satisfy
R_{i}<R_{j}
if
i<j
. That is
R_{k}=k
for all
k=1,\ldots,r
.
∎

3
Language models

A
language model
is a total function
\mathcal{M}:\Sigma^{\ast}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
that maps every string in
\Sigma^{\ast}
to a probability distribution over
\Sigma_{\$}
, where
\mathcal{M}(u)(\sigma)
is the probability of
u
to be continued by symbol
\sigma
.

Given a language model
\mathcal{M}
and a similarity relation
\mathcal{S}
on
\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
, we define the relation
\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}\times\Sigma^{\ast}
as follows:

\displaystyle u\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\forall w\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \mathcal{M}(uw)\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)

(18)

Actually,
\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}
is a
tolerance
relation
[
5
]
on the algebraic structure
(\Sigma^{\ast},F)
, where
F\triangleq\{f_{\sigma}:\Sigma^{\ast}\rightarrow\Sigma^{\ast}\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\}
such that
f_{\sigma}(u)\triangleq u\sigma
, i.e.,
f_{\sigma}
appends symbol
\sigma
to strings. That is,
\approxeq_{\mathcal{S}}
is a reflexive, symmetric and compatible relation:

Proposition 4
.

\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}
is a
tolerance
relation on
(\Sigma^{\ast},F)
.

Proof.

Reflexivity

Let
u\in\Sigma^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u)\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}(u)

\displaystyle\implies u\approxeq_{\mathcal{S}}u

\displaystyle\textrm{by reflexivity of }\approx_{\mathcal{S}}

Symmetry

Let
u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle u\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle\implies\forall w\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \mathcal{M}(uw)\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)

by Def.
18

\displaystyle\implies\forall w\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}(uw)

\displaystyle\textrm{by symmetry of }\approx_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\implies u^{\prime}\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}u

by Def.
18

Compatibility

Let
u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
such that
u\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}u^{\prime}
, and
\sigma\in\Sigma
.
Then, for all
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}((u\sigma)w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(u(\sigma w))

\displaystyle\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}(\sigma w))

by hypothesis and Def.
18

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}((u^{\prime}\sigma)w)

Hence,
u\sigma\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{S}}u^{\prime}\sigma
. ∎

The relation
\approx_{\mathcal{S}}
induces a reflexive and symmetric relation
\approxeq_{\mathcal{S}}
between language models as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}\approxeq_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\forall u\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \mathcal{M}_{1}(u)\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}_{2}(u)

(19)

However,
\mathcal{M}_{1}\approxeq_{\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{M}_{2}
does not imply that
\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}_{1}}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}_{\mathcal{S}}
are the same tolerance relation over
\Sigma^{\ast}
.
We will illustrate this with an example.

Example 1
.

Consider the alphabet
\Sigma=\{a\}
and the language models defined on
\Sigma^{\ast}
by

\mathcal{M}_{1}\left(a^{n}\right)\triangleq\begin{cases}\{a\mapsto 0.4,\$\mapsto 0.6\}&\text{if }n\in N_{1}\\
\{a\mapsto 0.6,\$\mapsto 0.4\}&\text{if }n\in N_{2}\end{cases}\quad\mathcal{M}_{2}\left(a^{n}\right)\triangleq\{a\mapsto 0.5,\$\mapsto 0.5\}\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N},

(20)

where
(N_{1},N_{2})
is a partition of
\mathbb{N}
.
We consider the similarity relation
\approx_{(\mathit{vd},t)}
on
\Delta\left(\Sigma_{\$}\right)
with
t=0.15
.
Then for any choice of partition
(N_{1},N_{2})
we have that
\mathcal{M}_{1}\approxeq_{(\mathit{vd},t)}\mathcal{M}_{2}
.
But by choosing appropriately the partition
(N_{1},N_{2})
we get different induced tolerance relations on
\Sigma^{\ast}
.
For instance, let
N_{1}=\{n_{k}\}_{k}
be a set with the property that the increments
n_{k+1}-n_{k}
are strictly increasing.
Then the words
a^{n_{k}}
, with
k\geq 1
, are pairwise non related for
\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}_{1}}_{(\mathit{vd},t)}
.
On the other hand in
\approxeq^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}_{(\mathit{vd},t)}
all words are related.

\blacksquare

Now, given a language model
\mathcal{M}
and an equivalence
\mathcal{E}
, we define the relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}\times\Sigma^{\ast}
as follows:

\displaystyle u\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\forall w\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \mathcal{M}(uw)=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)

(21)

Indeed,
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
is a
congruence
, that is, a transitive tolerance relation:

Proposition 5
.

\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
is a
congruence
on
(\Sigma^{\ast},F)
.

Proof.

For reflexivity, symmetry and compatibility the proof is similar to Prop.
4
.
Transitivity follows from the transitivity of
\mathcal{E}
. ∎

Equivalence
=_{\mathcal{E}}
also induces an equivalence relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{E}}
between language models as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}\equiv_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{\iff}}\forall u\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \mathcal{M}_{1}(u)=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{2}(u)

(22)

Indeed,
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
induce the same congruence over
\Sigma^{\ast}
.

Proposition 6
.

If
\mathcal{M}_{1}\equiv_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{2}
, then the congruences
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}_{1}}_{\mathcal{E}}
and
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}_{\mathcal{E}}
are the same.

Proof.

Let us show that
u\equiv^{\mathcal{M}_{1}}_{\mathcal{E}}u^{\prime}
implies
u\equiv^{\mathcal{M}_{2}}_{\mathcal{E}}u^{\prime}
, the other one follows by symmetry. Indeed, for all
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(uw)

\displaystyle=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{1}(uw)

by hypothesis and Def.
22

\displaystyle=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{1}(u^{\prime}w)

by Def.
21

\displaystyle=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{2}(u^{\prime}w)

by hypothesis and Def.
22

Then by transitivity
\mathcal{M}_{2}(uw)=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{2}(u^{\prime}w)
.
∎

Given the congruence
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
, we write
\left[\Sigma^{\ast}\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
for the partition of its equivalence classes and
\left[u\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
for the class of
u\in\Sigma^{\ast}
. We use the dot
\left[\cdot\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
for the quotient map
u\in\Sigma^{\ast}\mapsto\left[u\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}\in\left[\Sigma^{\ast}\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
.
Notice that
\left[\Sigma^{\ast}\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
is
countable
.

Definition 1
.

The congruence

4
Probabilistic Deterministic Finite Automata

A
probabilistic deterministic finite automaton
(PDFA)
[
14
]
over
\Sigma
, denoted
A
, is a tuple
(Q,q_{\mathrm{in}},\pi,\tau)
, where:

•

Q
is a finite set of states,

•

q_{\mathrm{in}}\in Q
is an initial state,

•

\pi:Q\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma_{\$})
maps each state to a probability distribution over
\Sigma_{\$}
, and

•

\tau:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow Q
is the transition function.

Both
\pi
and
\tau
are total functions.
We define
\tau^{\ast}
to be the extension of
\tau
to
\Sigma^{\ast}

\displaystyle\tau^{\ast}(q,\lambda)\triangleq q

(26a)

\displaystyle\tau^{\ast}(q,\sigma u)\triangleq\tau^{\ast}(\tau(q,\sigma),u)

(26b)

and
\pi^{\ast}
to be the extension of
\pi
to
\Sigma^{\ast}

\displaystyle\pi^{\ast}(q,u)

\displaystyle\triangleq\pi(\tau^{\ast}(q,u))

(27)

When the state is
q_{\mathrm{in}}
, we simply write
\tau^{\ast}(u)
and
\pi^{\ast}(u)
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that every state
q\in Q
is

, that is,
q=\tau^{\ast}(u)
for some string
u\in\Sigma^{\ast}
. Any such
u
is called an
access
string of

.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{A}(u)

\displaystyle\triangleq\pi^{\ast}(u)

\displaystyle\iff\forall w\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ \pi^{\ast}(\tau^{\ast}(u),w)\approx_{\mathcal{S}}\pi^{\ast}(\tau^{\ast}(u^{\prime}),w)

by Def.

\displaystyle\iff\tau^{\ast}(u)\approxeq^{A}_{\mathcal{S}}\tau^{\ast}(u^{\prime})

\forall u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ u\equiv^{\mathcal{M}_{A}}_{\mathcal{E}}u^{\prime}\iff\tau^{\ast}(u)\equiv^{A}_{\mathcal{E}}\tau^{\ast}(u^{\prime})

Analogous to Proposition

5.2
W
-equivalence

For any set of strings
W\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
, we define:

\displaystyle\forall u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}.\ u=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff\forall w\in W.\ \mathcal{M}(uw)=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)

(38)

It is straightforward to show that
=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W}
is an equivalence relation.
Notice that
=_{\mathcal{E}}^{\Sigma^{\ast}}
is
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
.
We denote
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\mathcal{E}}^{W}
the classes defined by the equivalence
=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W}
.

Recall that given two relations
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
on any set
X
,
R_{1}
is
finer
than
R_{2}
if only if for all
x,y\in X
,
xR_{1}y
implies
xR_{2}y
. It is also said that
R_{2}
is
coarser
than
R_{1}
.

Proposition 18
.

Let
W_{1},W_{2}\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
such that
W_{1}\subseteq W_{2}
.
Then
=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W_{2}}
is finer than
=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W_{1}}
.

Proof.

Let
u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle u=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W_{2}}u^{\prime}

\displaystyle\implies\forall w\in W_{2}.\ \mathcal{M}(uw)=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)

by Def.
38

\displaystyle\implies\forall w\in W_{1}.\ \mathcal{M}(uw)=_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}(u^{\prime}w)

\displaystyle\text{by}\ W_{1}\subseteq W_{2}

\displaystyle\implies u=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W_{1}}u^{\prime}

by Def.
38

Corollary 19
.

For all
W\subseteq\Sigma^{\ast}
,
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
is finer than
=_{\mathcal{E}}^{W}
. Moreover, if the quotient
\left[\Sigma^{\ast}\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
is finite and
\#\llbracket\Sigma^{\ast}\rrbracket^{W}_{\mathcal{E}}=\#\left[\Sigma^{\ast}\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
, then
=^{W}_{\mathcal{E}}
and
\equiv^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}
are the same.

Proof.

The first claim follows directly from Proposition
18
. The second follows since equality of cardinals implies that the map
\left[u\right]^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto\llbracket u\rrbracket^{W}_{\mathcal{E}}
is a bijection.
∎

7
Conclusions

The paper studied the problem of learning probabilistic deterministic finite automata from language models through the lens of tools given by the algebraic structures induced by similarities and equivalences, which provides a framework for understanding the foundations of algorithms proposed and implemented in the literature.
On one hand, it shows that relying on equivalences on distributions allows solving the problem using the same artifacts than for formal languages, which are derived from the fact that there is a canonically defined quotient.
On the other, it points out that algorithmic learning with tolerances is not yet well understood and requires further theoretical developments.
