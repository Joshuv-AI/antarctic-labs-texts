Pointwise ergodic theorem along primes of the form x 2 + n ​ y 2 x^{2}+ny^{2}
Jan Fornal

Abstract
. This paper resolves the question of pointwise convergence for ergodic averages of a single function along the set of polynomial values of primes of the form x 2 + n ​ y 2 x^{2}+ny^{2} . Following the influential paper of Bourgain [ Bou89 ] , we employ the Hardy-Littlewood circle method where major arc and minor arc estimates for the set of prime ideals constitute the main novelty of the paper. We also prove that our convergence results cannot be extended to class of L 1 L^{1} functions.



1.
Introduction

Pointwise ergodic theory dates back to the work of Birkhoff who proved the pointwise ergodic theorem for ordinary ergodic averages. Motivated by a question of Furstenberg, Bourgain started investigating subsequential ergodic averages. These take the form:

(1.1)

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k\leq M}f(T^{a_{k}}x).

More precisely, he published in Publications Mathématiques de l’IHÉS
[
Bou89
]
the highly creative and celebrated proof of the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P
be a polynomial with integer coefficients. Fix
q>1
, a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
. If
f\in L^{q}(X)
, then:

(1.2)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k\leq m}f(T^{P(k)}x)

converges for almost all
x\in X
.

In 1988, building off work of Bourgain, specifically the
L^{2}
formulation of the below result, Máté Wierdl in his paper
[
Wie88
]
managed to prove the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be the set of prime numbers. Fix
q>1
and suppose that
(X,\mu,T)
is measure-preserving system. If
f\in L^{q}(X)
, then:

(1.3)

\frac{1}{|\{p\in\mathbb{P}:p\leq m\}|}\sum_{p\leq m}f(T^{p}x)

converges for almost all
x\in X
.

In the previous two theorems, by a measure-preserving system, we mean a
\sigma
-finite measure space equipped with a measure-preserving transformation; throughout
(X,\mu,T)
will denote such a measure-preserving system. These two papers made a significant impact in the field of pointwise ergodic theory and in the author’s opinion they changed the general perception of this area of analysis. Later on, mathematicians gave plenty of modifications regarding convergence of nonstandard ergodic averages.
Our focus is the class of primes:

(1.4)

\mathbb{P}_{n}=\{p\in\mathbb{P}:\text{ there exists }u,v\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ so that }p=u^{2}+nv^{2}\},

where
n
is fixed for the rest of the paper. These primes have drawn the attention of various eminent mathematicians such as Fermat, Euler, Gauss, Hecke and Hilbert. They comply with various interesting principles coming from both analytic number theory and algebraic number theory. Using these primes, we will construct new types of ergodic averages and the main goal of this paper is to fully address the issue of pointwise convergence of these averages. The first result is qualitative:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n
be positive integer. Fix
q>1
, a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and a polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. If
f\in L^{q}(X)
, then:

(1.5)

A_{m}^{n}f(x):=A_{m}f(x)=\frac{1}{|\{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}:p\leq m\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\\
p\leq m\end{subarray}}f(T^{P(p)}x)

converges for almost all
x\in X
.

We further address quantitative convergence statistics for these averages, namely
r
-variation and the jump counting function, introduced by Lépingle in the context of martingales (
[
Lep76
]
) and imported to the ergodic-theoretic context by Bourgain (see for instance
[
Bou06
]
); we also address Bourgain’s oscillation seminorm. We will introduce the precise definitions of
r
-variation, the jump counting function and the oscillation seminorm in Section
7
. Much work has been devoted to addressing the interplay between these operators and classical questions in harmonic analysis and ergodic theory (see for instance
[
Cam+00
]
,
[
Zor15
]
or
[
Kra22
]
). Our next result proves that ergodic averages along primes of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
contribute another example to the aforementioned collection:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Fix
r>2,q>1
, a polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, a finite increasing sequence
I=(I_{j})_{j\in[1,M]}
and a positive real number
\lambda
. For all measurable functions
f
on a measure-preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, the following inequalities are satisfied:

(1.6)

\left\lVert\mathcal{V}^{r}\Bigl(A_{m}f(x):m\in\mathbb{Z}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{L^{q}(X)}\lesssim_{P,q,n}\frac{r}{r-2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{q}(X)}

(1.7)

\left\lVert\text{Osc}_{I}\Bigl(A_{m}f(x):m\in\mathbb{Z}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{L^{q}(X)}\lesssim_{P,q,n}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{q}(X)}

(1.8)

\left\lVert\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}\Bigl(A_{m}f(x):m\in\mathbb{Z}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{L^{q}(X)}\lesssim_{P,q,n}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{q}(X)}

It is worthwhile mentioning explicitly that in the second and third inequalities, the implied constants does not depend on the choice of sequence
I
nor the number
\lambda
, respectively.

Remark 1.5
.

Lemma
2.12
from
[
MSZ20b
]
together with interpolation methods justifies why (
1.6
) follows from (
1.8
). However, we will give the unified proof of Theorem
1.4
for all operators above.

The final result of our work addresses the
L^{1}
-endpoint. For the most popular nonconventional ergodic averages i.e. along primes and polynomial images, convergence was established only for functions in
L^{p}(X)
with
p>1
, in contrast to Birkhoff’s theorem. For a long time it was a serious question to investigate convergence only under the assumption that
f\in L^{1}
. In celebrated work, Buczolich and Mauldin (in
[
BM10
]
) established the
divergence
for averages of the form:

(1.9)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(T^{n^{d}}x)

for general
f\in L^{1}(X)
and
d=2
. They used Sawyer’s principle (more precisely, equation
(1)
from
[
Saw66
]
) regarding the link between weak-type maximal inequalities and almost everywhere convergence/divergence of ergodic averages for functions in
L^{1}(X)
. Later on, LaVictoire invented a more general construction for showing that certain ergodic averages diverge (see Theorem 3.1 in
[
LaV11
]
), using it to show that ergodic averages along primes and ergodic averages diverge (
1.9
) for
d\geq 3
. We will use that construction to justify the following:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. For every measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
, there exists a function
f\in L^{1}(X)
so that:

(1.10)

A_{m}f(x)=\frac{1}{|\{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}:p\leq m\}|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\\
p\leq m\end{subarray}}f(T^{p}x)

diverges for almost every
x\in X
.

,
[

The strategy for establishing quantitative and qualitative results in pointwise ergodic theory goes back to Bourgain, as he provided an adaptation of the
analytic-number-theoretic Hardy-Littlewood circle method
to accommodate the needs of pointwise ergodic theory. By the Caldéron’s transference principle, one may focus on the integer system
(\mathbb{Z},x\to x-1)
. In that context, the average with fixed scale can be rewritten as convolution with appropriate kernel. For instance, the expression on the integer system:

(1.11)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(T^{k}x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(x-k)

can be rewritten as:

(1.12)

\Bigl(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\delta_{k}\Bigr)\ast f(x)

In limit (i.e. when
m\to\infty
), these convolution kernels are convergent on the Fourier side to the Kronecker delta function,
\delta_{0}:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
, that evaluates to one on zero and zero everywhere else. In the case of polynomial averages:

(1.13)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(T^{P(k)}x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(x-P(k))

the analogous limit in Fourier space is equal to:

(1.14)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}\pmod{1}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\Biggl(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{b=1}^{q}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(b)}{q}\Bigr)\Biggr)\delta_{a/q}

For averages over primes weighted by the von Mangoldt function:

(1.15)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[m]}\Lambda(p)f(T^{p}x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}\cap[m]}\log pf(x-p)

these limits become:

(1.16)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}\pmod{1}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(q)}{\varphi(q)}\delta_{a/q},

where the quotient between Möbius function and Euler totient function
\frac{\mu(q)}{\varphi(q)}
arises after using Ramanujan formula for exponential sum of the form:

(1.17)

\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{b\in[q]:(b,q)=1}e\Bigl(\frac{b}{q}\Bigr)

For clarity, we recall that:

(1.18)

\varphi(a):=|\{b\in[a]:(b,a)=1\}|

and

(1.19)

\mu(a):=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\text{number of prime divisors of }a}&\text{when }a\text{ is squarefree}\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

In the simplest context we consider, i.e. for averages:

(1.20)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]}\log p\cdot f(T^{p}x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]}\log p\cdot f(x-p),

the behaviour of the spectrum looks almost the same: it is given by a linear combination of Kronecker delta functions along rational primes with coefficients that are slightly more sophisticated exponential sums:

(1.21)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{a}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}\pmod{1}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{S(a,q)}{2|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\delta_{a/q},

where
S(a,q),\varphi_{2}
and
q_{0}
are defined later (see respectively (
2.6
), (
2.3
) and (
2.5
)) and
\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))
is the ideal class group of the field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
(see Appendix
A
for details).

As the limit spectrum is supported on the set of all rationals, it was necessary for Bourgain to provide additional tools to tackle his problem, beyond those which give a distinguished role to the zero frequency, namely Bourgain’s multifrequency lemma and Bourgain’s superorthogonality method.
Naturally, Theorem
1.4
is strictly stronger than Theorem
1.3
. However, we still intend to cover both of these approaches.
In our context, the main significant challenge that arises is the investigation of the Fourier transform of the characteristic function of primes
\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[N]
when
N
goes to infinity. Crucially important in analysing these sums are the formulas derived from the theory of Hecke L-functions and Vaughan’s identity for general number fields.
Some of the number-theoretic computations are far simpler in the case when
n\equiv 1,2\pmod{4}
and
n
is squarefree. These are the cases when
n_{0}
(introduced later) is equal to
1
. Nevertheless, we didn’t want number theoretic complications to prevent us from fully resolving the above-raised questions for arbitrary positive
n
, so we accordingly cover all cases.

One can ask a similar question for arbitrary number fields: i.e. with a fixed number field
K
, determine whether a similar result holds for the set of primes
\mathbb{P}_{K}\subset\mathbb{P}
that split completely in
K
:

(1.22)

\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}_{K}\cap[m]|}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{K}\cap[m]}f(T^{p}x).

The answer is positive for Galois extensions over
\mathbb{Q}
(although the Galois condition is only used in equations (
3.7
) and (
3.9
)) and the proof is extremely analogous to the proof contained in this paper.

Across the paper, we will use the following notation:

(2)

(4)

2.2.
Essential arithmetic tools

The functions (
2.3
), (
2.6
) that were introduced above respect a few important properties:

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
\epsilon>0,q
,
a
that is coprime to
q
, and positive integer
k
, one has that:

(2.12)

\varphi_{2}(q)\gtrsim_{\epsilon}q^{2-\epsilon},

(2.13)

S(a,q)\lesssim_{\epsilon,d,n}q^{2-c_{d}+\epsilon},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(q_{0})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[q_{0}]:N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N(u+v\omega_{n}))}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(kq_{0})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[kq_{0}]:N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp kq_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N(u+v\omega_{n}))}{q}\Bigr),

where
c_{d}
is a certain constant depending on the degree of the polynomial
P
.

Proof.

From the Chinese remainder theorem, all functions on the left-hand size are almost multiplicative / preserve multiplicative behaviour. The easiest to justify will be why the second inequality is true.
It is enough to establish the same for
\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(q)
which is genuinely multiplicative (that is the variant of
\varphi_{2}
without dividing by two in case when
q>2
). We obtain:

(2.15)

\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(q)\geq\varphi(q)^{2}

from a couple of sentences:

(2.16)

\forall_{p\in\mathbb{P},\alpha>1}\qquad\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(p^{\alpha})=\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(p)p^{2\alpha-2}

(2.17)

\forall_{p\in\mathbb{P}}\qquad\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(p)\in\{p^{2}-1,p(p-1),(p-1)^{2}\}.

Let’s comment why the last inclusion is satisfied. Suppose that
p=2
, then
\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(p)
expresses the number of solutions for one of four congruences:

(2.18)

u^{2}+\epsilon_{0}uv+\epsilon_{1}v^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{2},

where
(\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
. The number of solutions is therefore either
1,2
or
3
. In the case when
p>2
, one is interested again about congruence:

(2.19)

u^{2}+(\omega_{n}+\overline{\omega}_{n})uv+\omega_{n}\overline{\omega}_{n}v^{2}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p},

or after a simple algebraic transformation:

(2.20)

u^{2}+tv^{2}\not\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}

for some
t\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. If
t
is equal to
0
, then naturally this has precisely
p(p-1)
solutions. In the case when
-t
is a non-quadratic residue modulo
p
, then
u^{2}+tv^{2}
is nonzero for precisely
p^{2}-1
choices of
(u,v)\in[p]^{2}
. Finally, when
-t
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
, then
u^{2}+tv^{2}
factorizes into a product of two linearly independent linear forms over
p
:

(2.21)

u^{2}+tv^{2}\equiv(u+rv)(u-rv)\pmod{p}

for some
r\neq 0
; this is nonzero for
(p-1)^{2}
pairs
(u,v)\in[p]^{2}
, finishing the proof of (
2.17
).
It is a bit harder to prove a similar fact for:

(2.22)

S(a,q)^{\prime}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[qn_{0}]:N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp qn_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N(u+v\omega_{n}))}{q}\Bigr).

In the previous sum, we pass to arithmetic progressions with respect to
n_{0}
, so our goal reduces to showing that:

(2.23)

\sum_{u,v\in[q]:F(u,v)\perp q}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)}{q}\Bigr)\lesssim_{d,\epsilon}q^{2-c_{d}+\epsilon},

where
F
is a degree
2
polynomial, monic with respect to the
u
variable and
Q
is a degree
2d
polynomial, so that the
u^{2d}
-coefficient is of order
O_{n,d}(1)
. If we denote this coefficient by
r_{2d}
, due to the Chinese remainder theorem, we need only show that:

(2.24)

T(a,p^{\alpha}):=\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]:F(u,v)\perp p}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\lesssim(r_{2d},p^{\alpha})^{c_{d}}p^{(2-c_{d})\alpha}.

After applying Fourier inversion:

(2.25)

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]:F(u,v)\perp p}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\mathbbm{1}_{F(u,v)\perp p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\Biggl(\frac{p-1}{p}-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{w=1}^{p-1}e\Bigl(\frac{wF(u,v)}{p}\Bigr)\Biggr),

and rearranging sums on the right-hand side, we obtain:

(2.26)

T(a,p^{\alpha})=\frac{p-1}{p}\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr)-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{w=1}^{p-1}\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)+wp^{\alpha-1}F(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr).

We consider three cases: if
d
is bigger than
1
, then the
u^{2d}
-coefficient of
aQ(u,v)+wp^{\alpha-1}F(u,v)
is
ar_{2d}
. Using Weyl’s estimate, we can estimate all
u,v
-indexed sums above by:

(2.27)

p^{\alpha}(p^{\alpha},r_{2d})\Bigl(\frac{p^{\alpha}}{(p^{\alpha},r_{2d})}\Bigr)^{1-c_{d}}=(p^{\alpha},r_{2d})^{c_{d}}p^{(2-c_{d})\alpha},

as the first factor contributes to every single
v
, and
(p^{\alpha},r_{2d})
arises when we split the
u
-range into intervals of length
\frac{p^{\alpha}}{(p^{\alpha},r_{2d})}
; this division is necessary for applying Weyl’s estimate.
The case when
d=1
and
p^{\alpha-1}
does not divide
r_{2d}
can be considered in exactly the same fashion, as it is enough to verify for
d=1
and
p^{\alpha-1}\nmid r_{2d}
that:

(2.28)

\sum_{u,v\in[p^{\alpha}]}e\Bigl(\frac{aQ(u,v)+wp^{\alpha-1}F(u,v)}{p^{\alpha}}\Bigr)\lesssim p^{2\alpha-c_{d}\alpha}(r_{2d},p^{\alpha})^{c_{d}}

for all
w\in[p-1]
but one. We know that for all
w
except one that we have:

(2.29)

(ar_{2d}+wp^{\alpha-1},p^{\alpha})=p^{\alpha-1}.

Weyl’s estimate in this case says that the left-hand side of (
2.28
) is bounded by:

(2.30)

p^{2\alpha-1}p^{1-c_{d}}=p^{2\alpha-c_{d}}=(p^{\alpha},p^{\alpha-1})^{c_{d}}p^{2\alpha-c_{d}\alpha}\leq p^{2\alpha-c_{d}\alpha}(r_{2d},p^{\alpha})^{c_{d}}.

It remains to justify (
2.28
) for
w=0
, however this is again the same reasoning as earlier.
In order to prove the third identity, suppose that
k=k_{1}k_{2}
where every prime divisor of
k_{1}
also divides
q_{0}
and
k_{2}\perp q_{0}
. Then:

(2.31)

\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(kq_{0})=k_{1}^{2}\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(k_{2})\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(q_{0}).

Also, in the numerator of the identity, we can extract the
k_{1}^{2}
factor:

(2.32)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[kq]:N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp k_{2}q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N(u+v\omega_{n}))}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[k_{2}q_{0}]\\
N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp k_{2}q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}\sum_{0<i,j<k_{1}}e\Bigl(\frac{a(P(N(u+ik_{2}q_{0}+(v+jk_{2}q_{0})\omega_{n})))}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[k_{2}q_{0}]\\
N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp k_{2}q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}\sum_{0<i,j<k_{1}}e\Bigl(\frac{a(P(N(u+v\omega_{n})))}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=k_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[k_{2}q_{0}]\\
N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp k_{2}q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{a(P(N(u+v\omega_{n})))}{q}\Bigr).

Now, fix
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
in
[q_{0}]
so that
N(u_{0}+v_{0}\omega_{n})
is coprime to
q_{0}
. In the right-hand side sum of the above equation, there are exactly
\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(k_{2})
elements
u_{1},v_{1}\pmod{k_{2}}
so that:
\newline
N(u_{0}+u_{1}q_{0}+(v_{0}+v_{1}q_{0})\omega_{n})
is coprime to
k_{2}
.
Therefore we end up with:

(2.33)

\displaystyle k_{1}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[k_{2}q_{0}]\\
N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp k_{2}q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N(u+v\omega_{n}))}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=k_{1}^{2}\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(k_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[q_{0}]\\
N(u+v\omega_{n})\perp q_{0}\\
n_{0}|v\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N(u+v\omega_{n}))}{q}\Bigr),

as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

One has the following inequalities as we sum the number theoretic functions along all small-norm ideals:

(2.34)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq x}1\lesssim_{n}x

(2.35)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{a})^{k}\lesssim_{n,k}x(\log x)^{2^{k}-1}.

Proof.

Naturally, all inequalities are still true for general number fields. These inequalities have well-known analogues for rational integers (see equation
1.80
from
[
IK21
]
), however for completeness of exposition we will discuss the proof here. The first one was already established in
[
Mar77
]
(Theorem
39
there). Therefore, we may focus on showing that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

(2.36)

\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{a})^{k}\lesssim_{k}x(\log x)^{2^{k}-1}.

The goal is to prove this via induction. For
k=0
this has been already done. For the inductive step, using the standard inequality
\tau(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})\leq\tau(\mathfrak{a})\tau(\mathfrak{b})
, we estimate:

(2.37)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{a})^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{b}\leq x}\Biggl(\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq\frac{x}{N\mathfrak{b}}}\tau(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})^{k-1}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{b}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{b})^{k-1}\Biggl(\sum_{\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq\frac{x}{N\mathfrak{b}}}\tau(\mathfrak{a})^{k-1}\Biggr)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{k}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{b}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{b})^{k-1}\frac{x}{N\mathfrak{b}}\Bigl(\log\frac{x}{N\mathfrak{b}}\Bigr)^{2^{k-1}-1}.

Therefore it suffices to verify that:

(2.38)

\sum_{\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{b}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{b})^{k-1}\frac{x}{N\mathfrak{b}}\lesssim_{k}x(\log x)^{2^{k-1}}.

Splitting the sum into dyadic intervals, we end up with:

(2.39)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{b}\leq x}\tau(\mathfrak{b})^{k-1}\frac{x}{N\mathfrak{b}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\leq\log_{2}x}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{b}\in[\frac{x}{2^{l+1}},\frac{x}{2^{l}}]}\tau(\mathfrak{b})^{k-1}2^{l+1}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{k}\sum_{l\leq\log_{2}x}\frac{x}{2^{l}}\Bigl(\log\frac{x}{2^{l}}\Bigr)^{2^{k-1}-1}2^{l+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l\leq\log_{2}x}x(\log x)^{2^{k-1}-1}

\displaystyle\lesssim x(\log x)^{2^{k-1}},

as desired.
∎

3.
Major arc analysis

Turning to our spectral analysis, suppose we are interested in
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
that is in
major arc
: there exists an irreducible fraction
\frac{a}{q}
so that

(3.1)

|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{(\log x)^{B}}{x^{d}}

with
q\leq(\log x)^{B}
. We will denote the set of these
\alpha
as
\mathcal{M}_{a/q,B}
and union of major arcs by:

(3.2)

\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}_{B}=\bigcup_{a/q:a\perp q,q\leq(\log x)^{B}}\mathcal{M}_{a/q,B},

suppressing the
x
-dependence.
Our goal is to establish the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

With
\alpha,a,q,x
as above, one has the following equality:

(3.3)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}e(\alpha P(p))\log p=\frac{xS(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\int_{0}^{1}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(xu)\Bigr)du+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))

where
R_{n}=2|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|
.

We begin the proof by stating the formula which encodes information regarding twisted character sums from Hecke L-functions:

Proposition 3.2
.

For every Hecke character from
I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q)
to
S^{1}
(see Appendix
A
for definitions):

(3.4)

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leq x}\chi(\mathfrak{p})\log N\mathfrak{p}=E(\chi)x+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))

where
E(\chi)
is
1
when
\chi
is a trivial character i.e. the image of
\chi
belongs to the set
\{0,1\}
, and
0
otherwise.

This fact can be derived by combining Theorem 5.33, Theorem 5.35 from
[
IK21
]
together with the Siegel zero upper bound coming from
[
Fog63
]
. With this in mind, we may now move to the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Proof.

Summing (
3.4
) over all Hecke characters times
\frac{1}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q)}
, and representatives
\mathfrak{a}
corresponding to principal ideal classes
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q)
in
I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q)
twisted by
e(\frac{aN\mathfrak{a}}{q})
, we get (for the brevity reasons the
c
constant in the exponential may vary from line to line):

(3.5)

\begin{gathered}\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{a})}{q}\Bigr)\sum_{\chi\in(I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0}))^{*}}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leq x}\frac{\chi(\mathfrak{p})\log N\mathfrak{p}\bar{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\\
=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{a})}{q}\Bigr)\sum_{\chi\in(I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0}))^{*}}\bar{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})\frac{(E(\chi)x+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})))}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\\
=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}\Biggl(e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{a})}{q}\Bigr)\frac{x}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))\Biggr)\\
=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{a})}{q}\Bigr)\frac{x}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})),\end{gathered}

since
q^{2}
can be incorporated into
O(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))
with no harm; above
G^{\ast}
denotes dual group to group
G
. If we rearrange the first two sums in the left-hand side, we end up with an inner sum that can be reduced using the orthogonality of Hecke characters:

(3.6)

\begin{gathered}\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{a})}{q}\Bigr)\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leq x}\sum_{\chi\in(I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}(qn_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0}))^{*}}\frac{\chi(\mathfrak{p})\log N\mathfrak{p}\bar{\chi}(\mathfrak{a})}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\\
=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}(q_{0})/\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{a})}{q}\Bigr)\sum_{\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leq x}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{p}\equiv\mathfrak{a}\pmod{\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{+}(q_{0})}}\log N\mathfrak{p}\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{p})}{q}\Bigr)\log N\mathfrak{p}+O_{n}(q),\end{gathered}

where last equality follows from the fact that double sum runs through the same set of prime ideals as the last sum (up to prime ideals containing
q_{0}
).
Recalling that
S(a,q)
is defined in (
2.6
), and combining formulas (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) yields:

(3.7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{p}:N\mathfrak{p}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}\end{subarray}}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{p})}{q}\Bigr)\log N\mathfrak{p}=S(a,q)\frac{x}{|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})).\\

We use the well-known fact that the prime ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}
either have norm
r^{2}
for a rational prime
r
, which occurs only when
\mathfrak{p}=(r)
, or
N\mathfrak{p}
is itself prime. If we sum the expression

(3.8)

e\Bigl(\frac{aP(N\mathfrak{p})}{q}\Bigr)\log N\mathfrak{p}

over prime ideals from the first case, we get the bound
x^{1/2}(\log x)
, which can be incorporated into
O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))
. In the sum above, we are summing over prime ideals belonging
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}
, therefore by the choice of
n_{0}
, one has that
N\mathfrak{p}
is of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
. This correspondence acts in two ways, i.e. every prime of the form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
can be written as the norm of a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}
for two different choices of
\mathfrak{p}
. Therefore:

(3.9)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}e\Bigl(\frac{aP(p)}{q}\Bigr)\log p=S(a,q)\frac{x}{2|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})).\\

If we denote
R_{n}:=2|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|
, then a standard summation by parts argument describes:

(3.10)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}e(\alpha P(p))\log p

for
\alpha
close to
\frac{a}{q}
. More precisely, we have:

(3.11)

\begin{gathered}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}e(\alpha P(p))\log p-\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\sum_{m\leq x}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(m)\Bigr)\\
=\sum_{m\leq x}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(m)\Bigr)\Biggl(e\Bigl(\frac{aP(m)}{q}\Bigr)\log m\mathbbm{1}_{m\in\mathbb{P}_{n}}-\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\Biggr)\\
=O_{B}\Bigl(x(1+x^{d}|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|)\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})\Bigr).\end{gathered}

In particular, for
\alpha
in the major arc surrounding
\frac{a}{q}
i.e. when
|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\leq\frac{(\log x)^{B}}{x^{d}}
, the error term is still of form
O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x}))
. Eventually we obtain that:

(3.12)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}e(\alpha P(p))\log p=\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\sum_{m\leq x}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(m)\Bigr)+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})).

We would now like to replace the exponential sum above with an appropriate oscillatory integral as in this case standard upper estimates on oscillatory integrals are more convenient than estimates on exponential sums. Observe that:

(3.13)

\bigg|e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(m)\Bigr)-\int_{m-1}^{m}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(y)\Bigr)dy\bigg|\leq 2\pi\bigg|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\bigg|\sup_{y\in[m,m+1]}|P^{\prime}(y)|

so by the triangle inequality and the Riemann summation we derive the following:

(3.14)

\bigg|\sum_{m\leq x}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(m)\Bigr)-\int_{0}^{x}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(y)\Bigr)dy\bigg|=O_{d}((\log x)^{B}),

as
m\to\sup_{y\in[m-1,m]}|P^{\prime}(y)|=O_{P}(m^{d-1})
. Incorporating the above formula into (
3.12
) and changing variables, we obtain:

(3.15)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}e(\alpha P(p))\log p=\frac{xS(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\int_{0}^{1}e\Bigl((\alpha-\frac{a}{q})P(xu)\Bigr)du+O_{B}(x\exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})),

finishing the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

4.
Minor arc analysis

Our goal in this section will be showing the following proposition:

Theorem 4.1
.

For any
A\gg 1
there exists bigger
B\gg A
, so that whenever
\alpha\in m_{B}:=\mathbb{T}\backslash\mathcal{M}_{B}
, we have:

(4.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{n}:N\mathfrak{n}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\mathfrak{n})e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{n}))\lesssim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}.

The argument from earlier (
3.9
) and the above inequality will suffice to yield:

(4.2)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[x]}\Lambda(p)e(\alpha P(p))\lesssim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}.

Before we start discussing the proof of
4.1
, let us recall Vaughan’s identity for general number fields, which will be of crucial importance to the below line of reasoning:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Lambda(\mathfrak{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{n}\\
N\mathfrak{d}\leq V\end{subarray}}\mu(\mathfrak{d})\log N\mathfrak{a}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{n}\\
N\mathfrak{m}\leq U\\
N\mathfrak{d}\leq V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\mathfrak{m})\mu(\mathfrak{d})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{n}\\
N\mathfrak{m}>U\\
N\mathfrak{d}>V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\mathfrak{m})\mu(\mathfrak{d})

as long as the norm of
\mathfrak{n}
exceeds
U
. For context, we recall Vaughan’s identity for integers, which presents as follows:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Lambda(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}da=n\\
d\leq V\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\log a-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mda=n\\
m\leq U\\
d\leq V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mu(d)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mda=n\\
m>U\\
d>V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(m)\mu(d)

whenever
n>U
. In the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we will still require to interpret (
4.3
) in the language of numbers instead of ideals.

Proof of
4.1
.

For convenience we introduce the notation
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}^{n_{0}}=\mathfrak{P}
. We twist the left-hand side of (
4.3
) by
e(\alpha N\mathfrak{n})
and sum over all principal ideals with norm at most
x
. Therefore:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{n}:N\mathfrak{n}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{P}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\mathfrak{n})e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{n}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{n}:N\mathfrak{n}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{P}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{n}\\
N\mathfrak{d}\leq V\end{subarray}}\mu(\mathfrak{d})\log(N\mathfrak{a})e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{n}))-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{n}:N\mathfrak{n}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{P}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{n}\\
N\mathfrak{m}\leq U\\
N\mathfrak{d}\leq V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\mathfrak{m})\mu(\mathfrak{d})e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{n}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{n}:N\mathfrak{n}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{n}\in\mathfrak{P}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{n}\\
N\mathfrak{m}>U\\
N\mathfrak{d}>V\end{subarray}}\Lambda(\mathfrak{m})\mu(\mathfrak{d})e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{n}))+O(U)=:S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}+O(U).

Set
U=V=x^{2/5}
; we show that every double sum on the right-hand side is of form:

(4.6)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq R\\
N\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\in\mathfrak{P}\end{subarray}}x_{\mathfrak{a}}y_{\mathfrak{b}}e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}))

where:

(A)

For every two ideals
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}
, one has that
|x_{\mathfrak{a}}|\leq\tau(\mathfrak{a})
and
|y_{\mathfrak{b}}|\leq\log N\mathfrak{b}
.

(B)

R
does not exceed
x^{9/10}
.

For the respective sums on the right-hand side of (
4.5
), one can just make the following assignments:

(1)

In the case of
S_{1}
, take
x_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mu(\mathfrak{a})
and
y_{\mathfrak{b}}=\log(N\mathfrak{b})
;

(2)

In the case of
S_{2}
, take
x_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mu(\mathfrak{a})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{N\mathfrak{a}<V}
and
y_{\mathfrak{b}}=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}|\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{m}\leq U}\Lambda(\mathfrak{m})
;

(3)

In the case of
S_{3}
, take
x_{\mathfrak{a}}=\sum_{\mathfrak{d}|\mathfrak{a}:N\mathfrak{d}>V}\mu(\mathfrak{d})
and
y_{\mathfrak{b}}=\Lambda(\mathfrak{b})\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{N\mathfrak{b}>U}
.

Therefore; our focus moves to showing that under Properties
(A)
and
(B)
from above list we have:

(4.7)

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq R\\
N\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\in\mathfrak{P}\end{subarray}}x_{\mathfrak{a}}y_{\mathfrak{b}}e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}))\bigg|\lesssim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}.

Fix a representative
\mathfrak{a}_{0}\in I_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}/\mathfrak{P}
and its inverse
\mathfrak{b}_{0}
, due to finiteness of the class group, we will prove only that:

(4.8)

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}:N\mathfrak{a}\leq R\\
N\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}\leq x\\
\mathfrak{a}\equiv\mathfrak{a}_{0},\mathfrak{b}\equiv\mathfrak{b}_{0}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}\end{subarray}}x_{\mathfrak{a}}y_{\mathfrak{b}}e(\alpha P(N\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}))\bigg|\lesssim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}.

Grouping ideals with the same norm:

(4.9)

\displaystyle z_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\equiv\mathfrak{a}_{0}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}:N\mathfrak{a}=c}x_{\mathfrak{a}},

(4.10)

\displaystyle t_{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\equiv\mathfrak{b}_{0}\pmod{\mathfrak{P}}:N\mathfrak{b}=d}y_{\mathfrak{b}},

we can reduce the task of proving (
4.8
) to estimating

(4.11)

\bigg|\sum_{a,b\geq 0:a\leq R,b\leq S,ab\leq x}z_{a}t_{b}e(\alpha P(ab))\bigg|\lesssim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}},

where:

(1)

The numbers
z_{a}
and
t_{b}
respect the bounds:

(4.12)

|z_{a}|\lesssim_{n}\tau^{2}(a)\leq\tau^{4}(a)

and

(4.13)

|t_{b}|\lesssim_{n}(\log b+O_{n}(1))\tau(b)\leq(\log b+O_{n}(1))\tau^{3}(b).

These inequalities come from the fact that there are at most
2
prime ideal factors occuring in factorization of ideal
(p)\subset\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})}
for
p\in\mathbb{P}
.

So, suppose that
k
is chosen so that
2^{k}\sim(\log x)^{A+128}
; then the left-hand side expression under (
4.11
) obeys:

(4.14)

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 0:a\leq R\\
ab\leq x\end{subarray}}z_{a}t_{b}e(\alpha P_{2}(ab))\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 0:a\leq R\\
ab\leq x/2^{k}\end{subarray}}z_{a}t_{b}e(\alpha P_{2}(ab))\bigg|+

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 0:a\leq R\\
ab\in(x/2^{j},x/2^{j-1}]\end{subarray}}z_{a}t_{b}e(\alpha P_{2}(ab))\bigg|.

The first term on the right-hand side is
\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A+1}}
due to Lemma
2.2
in the field of rational numbers. On the other hand, if (
4.11
) is not satisfied, then by the pigeonhole principle, we extract some
j\leq k
so that:

(4.15)

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 0:a\leq R\\
ab\in(x/2^{j},x/2^{j-1}]\end{subarray}}z_{a}t_{b}e(\alpha P_{2}(ab))\bigg|\gtrsim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A+1}}.

Due to further dyadic pigeonholing, there is
M\leq R
such that:

(4.16)

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 0:a\sim M\\
ab\in(x/2^{j},x/2^{j-1}]\end{subarray}}z_{a}t_{b}e(\alpha P_{2}(ab))\bigg|\gtrsim_{n}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A+2}}.

With this lower bound in mind, Proposition 2.2 from
[
MS21
]
is satisfied with
H=N=\frac{x}{2^{j}}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{(\log x)^{A+35}}
.
In particular, if
r_{d}
is the leading coefficient of
P_{2}
, then there exists
q\leq\delta^{-O_{d}(1)}\leq(\log x)^{O_{d,A}(1)}
, so that:

(4.17)

\left\lVert qdr_{d}\alpha\right\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\leq\delta^{-O_{d}(1)}\frac{2^{jd}}{x^{d}}\leq\frac{(\log x)^{O_{d,A}(1)}}{x^{d}},

as both
2^{jd}
and
\delta^{-O_{d}(1)}
have logarithmic size. Furthermore,
r_{d}
is of order
O_{P,n}(1)
, so
\alpha
must lie inside a major arc around a fraction with denominator of size
(\log x)^{O_{d,A}(1)}
and
\frac{(\log x)^{O_{d,A}(1)}}{x}
close to it. Putting
B\gg O_{d,A}(1)
gives us contradiction with the fact that we are in minor arc. Therefore (
4.11
) and in consequence Theorem
4.1
are both satisfied.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.3
- first part

With these preliminaries in hand, we now move to showing Theorem
1.3
.
We discuss a few standard reductions:

(1)

Adding weights to the
A_{m}
averages i.e.

(5.1)

A_{m}^{\prime}f(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\leq m:p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}}\log(p)f(T^{P(p)}x)

will not affect the convergence theorem (see Lemma 1 from
[
Wie88
]
). To get the sum of weights equal to
1
, we would need the weights to be multiplied by
R_{n}=2|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|
, however for clarity and correctness of the argument that gives no advantage.

(2)

Bourgain in
[
Bou06
]
gave a procedure to yield a pointwise convergence theorem like Theorem
1.2
from a maximal ergodic inequality and orthogonality (he proved a weaker special case of the Theorem
1.4
). That procedure works in our case as well, therefore for now, we will just focus on showing that:

(5.2)

\left\lVert\sup_{m}|A_{m}^{\prime}f|\right\rVert_{L^{p}(X)}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(X)}.

(3)

A standard transference principle of Calderon (see pages 86-88 from
[
Kra22
]
) says that the only system for which we need to prove (
5.2
) is
(\mathbb{Z},S)
where
S
maps
x\in\mathbb{Z}
to
x-1
.
In the case of the integers, applying the weighted average operator
A_{m}^{\prime}
to the function
f
is the same as convolving it with
K_{m}
where:

(5.3)

K_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]}\mathbbm{1}_{x=P(p)}\log p

(4)

We can restrict to the case
f\geq 0
, and restrict our set of times to powers of
2
, so it suffices to prove:

(5.4)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}\bigg|A_{m}^{\prime}f\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

Define now
L_{m}^{\prime}
and
v_{m},\varphi_{s}
as follows:

(5.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{L_{m}^{\prime}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{2^{s}\leq(\log m)^{B}}\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:(a,q)=1}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr),

\displaystyle v_{m}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}e(\alpha P(mu))du,

\displaystyle\varphi_{s}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\varphi(2^{s}\alpha)

for a smooth mollifier
\varphi
satisfying:

(5.6)

\mathbbm{1}_{[-1/4,1/4]}\leq\varphi\leq\mathbbm{1}_{[-1/2,1/2]},

R_{n}=2|\text{Cl}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n}))|
as above, and
B
comes from our particular choice in Theorem
4.1
.
We can group the fractions by denominator size to form operators
L_{m,s}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
which respect:

(1)

L_{m}^{\prime}
is the sum of
(L_{m,s})_{s\leq B\log_{2}\log m}
;

(2)

On the Fourier side
L_{m,s}
has the expansion:

(5.7)

\widehat{L_{m,s}}(\alpha)=\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:(a,q)=1}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr).

(3)

We also provide the operators
L_{m}

built with larger amount of increments
i.e.:

(5.8)

L_{m}=\sum_{2^{s}\leq\sqrt{m}/16}L_{m,s}.

The operators
L_{m}^{\prime}
are more convenient to use for the approximations in Fourier space we are about to introduce, but
(L_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
will be more convenient for Bourgain’s
superorthogonality
approach.

5.1.
Providing a Fourier approximant for the kernels
K_{m}

Our objective for this subsection is to prove that:

(5.9)

\left\lVert\widehat{K_{m}}-\widehat{L_{m}^{\prime}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{n,B}\frac{1}{(\log m)^{A}}.

Remark 5.1
.

By Lemma
2.1
, we have that for any
s
:

(5.10)

\left\lVert L_{m,s}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim 2^{-c_{d}s}

Therefore, the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the Fourier transform of
\widehat{K_{m}}-\widehat{L_{m}}
will still be
O_{n,B}(\frac{1}{(\log m)^{A}})
as long as (
5.9
) is satisfied.

Proof.

We will mimic the proof of equation (22) from
[
Wie88
]
, but will include the argument to jusitfy the advantage of manipulating the scaling of
\varphi_{s}
as much as we wish.
Without loss of generality, assume that
m
is larger than some large constant depending on
B
.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
; we need to distinguish between major and minor arcs.

(1)

If, for some irreducible fraction
\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
,
\alpha
lies in the major arc around
\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
, then from Theorem
3.1
, we obtain:

(5.11)

\widehat{K_{m}}(\alpha)=\frac{S(a^{\prime},q^{\prime})}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q^{\prime}_{0})}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\Bigr)+O_{B}(\exp(-c\sqrt{\log m})).

For any other irreducible fraction
\frac{a}{q}
whose denominator is bounded by
(\log m)^{B}
, we have from
m\gg B
that:

(5.12)

\bigg|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\bigg|\geq\frac{1}{2(\log m)^{2B}}

so consequently
v_{m}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q})\lesssim\frac{(\log m)^{2B/d}}{m}
(by applying Lemma B.2 from
[
Kra22
]
).
Applying Lemma
2.1
we get that
\frac{S(a,q)}{\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\lesssim q^{-c_{d}}
. The supports of
\varphi_{6s}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q})
when
q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})
are disjoint so in effect:

(5.13)

\bigg|\widehat{L_{m}^{\prime}}(\alpha)-\frac{S(a^{\prime},q^{\prime})}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q^{\prime}_{0})}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\Bigr)\bigg|\lesssim\frac{(\log m)^{2B/d}}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{c_{d}s}}.

Now
\varphi_{6s}(\alpha-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}})
is
1
, as by the size of
m
we have:

(5.14)

\bigg|\alpha-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\bigg|<\frac{(\log m)^{B}}{m}<\frac{1}{4\cdot 2^{6B\log_{2}\log m}}\leq\frac{1}{4\cdot 2^{6s}}

Combining (
5.13
) and (
5.11
) yields (
5.9
) in this case.

(2)

If
\alpha
lies in minor arc, then from (
4.2
):

(5.15)

|\widehat{K_{m}}(\alpha)|\lesssim_{n}\frac{1}{(\log m)^{A}};

the same argument as we did for (
5.13
) shows that
\widehat{L_{m}^{\prime}}
is
O\Bigl(\frac{1}{(\log m)^{B}}\Bigr)
. To elaborate a bit more,
|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|
is always at least
\frac{(\log m)^{B}}{m^{d}}
on the minor arc, so
v_{m}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q})\lesssim\frac{1}{(\log m)^{B/d}}
, which is enough to make the argument from (
5.13
) work. Obviously, the difference between
\widehat{K_{m}}
and
\widehat{L_{m}^{\prime}}
also respects the polylogarithmic savings.

∎

6.
Bourgain’s multi-frequency result revisited

In the case when
p=2
, we will use a famous result of Bourgain for a maximal estimate on the family
(L_{m,s})_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}
with fixed
s
. The original version of that result states the following:

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that
\Theta:=\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}\}
is
1
-separated i.e.
|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}|>1
when
i
is different than
j
and
\widehat{\chi_{k}}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{[-1/2^{k+1},1/2^{k+1}]}(x)
.
Then the maximal sublinear operator:

(6.1)

\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni f\to\sup_{k\geq 1}\Bigg|\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\text{Mod}_{\theta_{n}}\chi_{k})\ast f\Bigg|\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

has norm at most
(\log N)^{2}
up to absolute constant.

Remark 6.2
.

(1)

It is obvious that
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
has norm at most
2N
(use for instance the triangle inequality together with the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function);

(2)

Applying a simple dilation argument, this theorem works also when for any distinct indices
i,j
one has
|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}|<t
and supremum in
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
varies on range
k\geq\log_{2}t+1
.

(3)

From Lemma 4.4 in
[
Bou89
]
one can justify that
\mathcal{M}_{\Theta}
is
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-bounded operator with norm
O((\log N)^{2})
.

The famous paper containing the proof of that proposition is
[
Bou89
]
(see especially Lemma 4.1.)
The new result that we endeavour to prove is as follows:

Proposition 6.3
.

For any
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
g\in l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, one has the inequality:

(6.2)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\geq 4^{s+4}}\bigg|\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\int_{\mathbb{T}}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\hat{f}(\alpha)e(j\alpha)d\alpha\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{2}_{j}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim s^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Remark 6.4
.

Applying the above proposition for
g
which on the Fourier side is given by:

(6.3)

\widehat{g}(\alpha)=\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\frac{S(a,q)}{\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}\varphi_{6(s-1)}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\widehat{f}(\alpha),

together with the estimates from Lemma
2.1
, gives for any
\epsilon>0
:

(6.4)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\geq 4^{s+4}}\bigg|L_{m,s}\ast f\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{\epsilon}2^{(\epsilon-c_{d})s}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Combining this inequality together with (
5.9
) gives an
l^{2}
-maximal inequality in (
5.2
).

Proof.

For any two irreducible fractions
\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}
and
\frac{a_{2}}{q_{2}}
with denominators in
[2^{s-1},2^{s})
, the supports of
\alpha\to\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}\Bigr)
and
\alpha\to\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigr)
are disjoint.
The proof consists of two steps, in the first we will try to replace
v_{m}
by
\chi_{\log_{2}m}
. Due to a standard Parseval’s-identity argument, the difference between the expressions:

(6.5)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\geq 4^{s+4}}\bigg|\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\int_{\mathbb{T}}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\hat{f}(\alpha)e(j\alpha)d\alpha\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{2}_{j}(\mathbb{Z})}

and

(6.6)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\geq 4^{s+4}}\bigg|\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\chi_{\log_{2}m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\hat{f}(\alpha)e(j\alpha)d\alpha\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{2}_{j}(\mathbb{Z})}

is bounded by:

(6.7)

\left\lVert\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\geq 4^{s+4}}\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\Bigg|\Bigl(v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)-\chi_{\log_{2}m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\Bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}.

Take some
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}
and suppose the nearest fraction to it is
\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
. Suppose that distance between them is
\delta
:

(1)

Due to inequality
v_{m}(\beta)\lesssim\frac{1}{m|\beta|^{1/d}}
, the contribution from scales
m
with
\frac{1}{m}<\delta^{1/d}
is:

(6.8)

\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{4}{2^{k}M\delta^{1/d}}=O\Bigl(\frac{1}{M\delta^{1/d}}\Bigr)=O(1)

where
M\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}
is the smallest number satisfying
\frac{1}{M}<\delta^{1/d}
. We need not even consider
\chi_{\log_{2}m}
as they all vanish at
\alpha^{\prime}-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
as long as
m\geq M
.

(2)

Around zero,
v_{m}
is estimated via
|v_{m}(\beta)-1|\leq O(m^{d}|\beta|)
(the inequality follows straight from definition of
v_{m}
). The geometric series kicks in again from justification that the contribution from small
m
is also
O(1)
.

Consequently, (
6.7
) is bounded by
O(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})})
, whereas the estimate

(6.9)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\geq 4^{s+4}}\bigg|\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s})}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\chi_{\log_{2}m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{6s}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\hat{f}(\alpha)e(j\alpha)d\alpha\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{2}_{j}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim s^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

follows from the remark after Proposition
6.1
.
∎

6.1.
The High-Low method and superorthogonality

In this final subsection, we will finish the proof of (
5.2
), using the High-Low method. The approach is almost identical to that presented in Chapter 7 of
[
Kra22
]
, still running through entire argument will be appropriate to justify why we have not decided to select any simpler method. This method originates from work of Bourgain
[
Bou89
]
.
For any
S>1
, we will construct two operators
\mathcal{L}_{S}
and
\mathcal{H}_{S}
acting on functions
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
where:

(6.10)

\begin{gathered}\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast f|\leq\mathcal{L}_{S}f+\mathcal{H}_{S}f\\
\forall_{p>1}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{S}f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{p}S^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\\
\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{S}f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim 2^{-\delta S}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\\
\end{gathered}

for an appropriate constant
\delta>0
.

Proposition 6.5
.

The inequalities from (
6.10
) are sufficient to establish the
l^{p}
-boundedness (
p\in(1,2)
) of the operator:

(6.11)

f\to\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast f|

Proof.

Due to the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem (see Theorem 1.8 from
[
Kra22
]
), it is enough to check:

(6.12)

\bigg|\Bigl\{\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast\mathbbm{1}_{E}|\geq\lambda\Bigr\}\bigg|\lesssim_{p}\frac{|E|}{\lambda^{p}}.

Whenever
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{100}
, we may use the already-established
l^{2}
-boundedness:

(6.13)

\bigg|\Bigl\{\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast\mathbbm{1}_{E}|\geq\lambda\Bigr\}\bigg|\lesssim\frac{|E|}{\lambda^{2}}\lesssim\frac{|E|}{\lambda^{p}}.

In the other case, we need to wrestle a bit more. If we set
r=\frac{1+p}{2}
, then:

(6.14)

\displaystyle\bigg|\Bigl\{\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast\mathbbm{1}_{E}|\geq\lambda\Bigr\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\Bigl\{\mathcal{L}_{S}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigr\}\bigg|+\bigg|\Bigl\{\mathcal{H}_{S}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigr\}\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\Bigl(\frac{2}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{r}S^{2r}|E|+\Bigl(\frac{2}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{2}2^{-2\delta S}|E|.

Plugging
S=\frac{(2-p)\log\frac{1}{\lambda}}{2\delta}
, we bound both expressions by:

(6.15)

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{2}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{2}2^{-2\delta S}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\lambda^{p}}

(6.16)

\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{2}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{r}S^{2r}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{2}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{r}\frac{(2-p)^{2r}\log^{2r}\frac{1}{\lambda}}{(2\delta)^{2r}}=O_{p,\delta}(\frac{1}{\lambda^{p}}),

as the logarithm grows slower than any power function.
∎

The key fact we will need is that the operators
K_{m}
satisfy a
partial
l^{p}
maximal inequality
, similar to those coming from polynomial kernels:

Lemma 6.6
.

For each
p>1
:

(6.17)

\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:J\leq\log m<2J}|K_{m}\ast f|\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{p}\log J\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

A telescoping argument which does not exploit the behaviour of
K_{m}
shows that above lemma is satisfied as long as we have:

Lemma 6.7
.

Suppose that
(m_{i})_{i=0}^{r}
is an increasing sequence of powers of
2
with all elements inside
[\exp(J)
,
\exp(2J)]
. Assume also that for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}
one has that
\frac{m_{i+1}}{m_{i}}\geq J^{C_{r}}
.
Then for any set of integers
F\subset\mathbb{Z}
together with pairwise disjoint subsets
F_{2},\ldots,F_{r}\subset F
we have:

(6.18)

\left\lVert(K_{m_{1}}-K_{m_{0}})\ast\Bigl(\prod_{i=2}^{r}K_{m_{i}}\ast\mathbbm{1}_{F_{i}}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim J^{-r}|F|^{1/2}.

Proof.

The proof was given for polynomial values in
[
Kra22
]
, we just want to check that in our setting everything goes more or less the same:

(1)

We select parameters
A_{0},(\mathbf{c}(i))_{i=1}^{r}
in the same way, here we also insist that the constant
A
from Theorem
4.1
is so large that
A\gg A_{0}
;

(2)

In our case, the
(\Omega_{m_{i}})_{i=0}^{r}
operators are defined on the Fourier side as:

(6.19)

\widehat{\Omega_{m_{i}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{q\leq J^{c(i)}}\sum_{a\in[q]:a\perp q}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{m_{i}}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi\Biggl(\frac{J^{A_{0}}}{3m_{i}}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\Biggr);

(3)

The inequalities:

(6.20)

\left\lVert\Omega_{m_{i}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim J^{2c(i)}

and

(6.21)

\left\lVert\widehat{K_{m_{i}}}-\widehat{\Omega_{m_{i}}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim J^{-c_{d}c(i)}

are still satisfied. In order to justify (
6.20
), one sees that number of terms in the double sum in (
6.19
) is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{s:2^{s}\leq J^{c(i)}}

\displaystyle\sum_{q\in[2^{s-1},2^{s}),a\in[q]:a\perp q}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{m_{i}}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\Bigl(\varphi_{6s}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q})-\varphi(\frac{J^{A_{0}}}{3m_{i}}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}))\Bigr)

(4)

We will also need, at the end of the argument for (

Proposition 6.8
.

Given a natural number

, define the periodic multiplier:

(6.32)

\mathbf{m}_{per}^{q}(\alpha)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{m}(\xi-\frac{a}{q}).

Then:

(6.33)

\left\lVert\mathbf{m}_{per}^{q}\right\rVert_{M^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathbf{m}\right\rVert_{M^{p}(\mathbb{R})}.

where the multiplier norms
M^{p}
are defined as follows:

(6.34)

\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{M^{p}(\mathbb{G})}=\sup_{f:\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{G})}=1}\left\lVert\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathbb{G}}(m\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}(f))\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{G})},\text{ where }\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{R}\text{ or }\mathbb{Z}.

Eventually we may move to the proof of (
5.2
) by establishing the High-Low decomposition of the operator
f\to\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast f|
. We will mimic the argument from
[
Kra22
]
.
Take some
C_{0}
and
C
with
1\ll C_{0}\ll C
and separate the range of supremum into two intervals:

(6.35)

\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}}|K_{m}\ast f|\leq\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m\leq 2^{2^{CS}}}|K_{m}\ast f|+\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}|K_{m}\ast f|.

The first component can be attached to the low part by virtue of Lemma
6.6
.
The second component is split further:

(6.36)

\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}|K_{m}\ast f|\leq\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}|L_{m}\ast f|+\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}|K_{m}\ast f-L_{m}\ast f|

The second sum has norm
O(2^{-S}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})})
after applying
5.1
for
A=2
and not-too-large

:

(6.37)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}|K_{m}\ast f-L_{m}\ast f|\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m>2^{2^{CS}}}\left\lVert K_{m}\ast f-L_{m}\ast f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}\left\lVert K_{m}\ast f-L_{m}\ast f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{n}\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}\frac{1}{(\log m)^{2}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-CS}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Now we use the triangle inequality for the third time to address the first term in (
6.36
):

(6.38)

\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}|L_{m}\ast f|\leq\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}\bigg|\sum_{s\leq S}L_{m,s}\ast f\bigg|+\sum_{s>S}\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>4^{s+4}}|L_{m,s}\ast f|.

Computations for the
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-norm of the second sum present as follows (we use (
6.4
)):

(6.39)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{s>S}\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>4^{s+4}}|L_{m,s}\ast f|\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s>S}\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>4^{s+4}}|L_{m,s}\ast f|\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{s>S}2^{-c_{d}s}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-c_{d}S}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

It remains to decompose:

(6.40)

\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}\bigg|\sum_{2^{s}\leq m}L_{m,s}\ast f\bigg|

Define
M_{m,S}
and

(6.41)

Q_{S}=\text{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,2^{S})

(6.42)

\widehat{M_{m,S}}(\alpha)=\sum_{a\in[Q_{S}]}\frac{S(a,Q_{S})}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(Q_{S,0})}v_{m}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{2^{C_{0}S}+2}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)

We now conclude the rest of argument where we obtain the low-high part decomposition of (
6.40

(1)

The reason why
\widehat{M_{m,S}}
is close to
\widehat{L_{m}}
around fractions with denominator at most

is that the exponential sum
\frac{S(a,Q_{S})}{\varphi_{2}(Q_{S,0})}
is invariant under reducing by
\text{gcd}(a,Q_{S})
. This is a consequence of the last identity from Lemma
2.1
. We use the information that
|\alpha-a/q|\gtrsim 2^{-2^{C_{0}S}}
to bound:

(6.43)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sup_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}\bigg|\Bigl(\sum_{s\leq S}L_{m,s}-M_{m,S}\Bigr)\ast f\bigg|\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}\left\lVert\Bigl(\sum_{s\leq S}L_{m,s}-M_{m,S}\Bigr)\ast f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Bigl(\sum_{s\leq S}L_{m,s}-M_{m,S}\Bigr)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:m>2^{2^{CS}}}

\displaystyle\frac{2^{2^{C_{0}S}/d}}{m^{1/d}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

where in the last inequality we have used Lemma B.2 from
[
Kra22
]
. The sum on the right-hand side respects exponential decay with respect to

is subsumed in the low part.

(2)

The fraction
\frac{S(a,Q_{S})}{\varphi_{2}(Q_{S,0})}
is a convex combination of exponential phases with integral coefficients (they are
N(u+v\omega_{n})
for integral
u,v
). Therefore Proposition
6.8
is applicable.

This concludes the proof of (

).

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

For the next section we focus on the issue of quantifying convergence; we shall begin by introducing the framework for Ionescu-Wainger theory.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\rho\in(0,1)
be a very small parameter (later on, we will fix this precisely) and assume that
N\geq 2^{R}
, where
R=\lfloor\frac{2}{\rho}\rfloor+1
. Set:

(7.1)

S_{\rho}(N)=\Bigl\{\prod_{p\leq N^{\rho/2}}p^{\lfloor\log N/\log p\rfloor}:p\in\mathbb{P}\Bigr\}\cup\bigcup_{p\in(N^{\rho/2},N]\cap\mathbb{P}}\Bigl\{p^{\lfloor\log N/\log p\rfloor}\Bigr\}.

The
N
-th set of Ionescu-Wainger frequencies with parameter
\rho
is:

(7.2)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\leq R}(N)

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\frac{a}{q}:a\leq q,

\displaystyle\text{ there are at most }R\text{ elements in }S_{\rho}(N)\text{ whose product is }q\Bigr\}

Below we list all the properties of this set that we will need below:

(A)

There exists an absolute constant
C_{0}
for which the following statement is true: all fractions with denominator at most
N
belong to
\Sigma_{\leq R}(N)
, and all elements of
\Sigma_{\leq R}(N)
are fractions with denominator at most
C_{0}^{R^{2}N^{\rho/2}}
.

(B)

For a multiplier
\mathbf{m}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
supported on sufficiently small interval (i.e.
[-\exp(-N^{\rho}),\\
\exp(-N^{\rho})]
), we introduce the
\leq N
-th Ionescu-Wainger multiplier with parameter
\rho
as:

(7.3)

\Pi_{\leq N}[\mathbf{m}](\beta)=\Pi_{\leq N}^{\rho}[\mathbf{m}](\beta)=\sum_{\theta\in\Sigma_{\leq R}(N)}\mathbf{m}(\beta-\theta).

This multiplier satisfies the following estimate for any
p\in(1,\infty)
:

(7.4)

\left\lVert\Pi_{\leq N}[\mathbf{m}]\right\rVert_{M^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{p,\rho}\left\lVert\mathbf{m}\right\rVert_{M^{p}(\mathbb{R})},

(C)

For fractions
a/q
we will denote by
\mathbf{h}(a/q)
its Ionescu-Wainger height, that is, the smallest dyadic
N
so that
a/q\in\Sigma_{\leq R}(N)

Apart from that, we will use a few results coming from discrete harmonic analysis:

(1)

The operators
\text{Osc}_{(I_{j})_{j\in[1,N]}}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})
,
\mathcal{V}^{r}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})
and
N_{\lambda}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})
denote the following:

(7.5)

\text{Osc}_{(I_{j})_{j\in[1,N]}}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sup_{i\in[I_{j},I_{j+1})}|f_{i}-f_{I_{j}}|^{2}(x)\Bigr)^{1/2}

(7.6)

\mathcal{V}^{r}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})=\sup_{j;n_{0}<\ldots<n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{j}|f_{i}-f_{i-1}|^{r}(x)\Bigr)^{1/r}

(7.7)

N_{\lambda}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})=\sup\{j:\text{ there exists }n_{0}<\ldots<n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}:\forall_{i\in[j]}|f_{n_{i}}(x)-f_{n_{i-1}}(x)|>\lambda\}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

where for each
n
,
f_{n}
are complex-valued functions with a
\sigma
-finite measure space
X
as their domain.
For any subsequence
r>2
,
I\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\lambda
one has pointwise control:

(7.8)

\text{Osc}_{I}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N}),\mathcal{V}^{r}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N}),\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})\leq\mathcal{V}^{2}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})

Additionally, by Lemma 2.5 from
[
MSZ20
]
, one has the so-called Rademacher-Menshov inequality:

(7.9)

\mathcal{V}^{2}(f_{n}(x):n\in[0,2^{m}-1])\leq\sqrt{2}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m-i}}\big|f_{2^{i}(j+1)}-f_{2^{i}j}\big|^{2}(x)\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(2)

Let
\mathcal{F}
represent the following set of operators that take sequences of functions
(f_{n}:X\to\mathbb{C})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
into single complex-valued functions with domain
X
:

(7.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\text{Osc}_{I}:I=(I_{n})_{n\in[1,N]}\text{ is finite subsequence in }\mathbb{N}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\cup\Bigl\{\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}:\lambda>0\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{\mathcal{V}^{r}:r>2\Bigr\}.

For an operator
U\in\mathcal{F}
, define the
r-factor
,
r(U)
, as follows:

(7.11)

r(U):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }U=\text{Osc}_{I}\text{ or }U=\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}\\
\frac{r}{r-2}&\text{ if }U=\mathcal{V}^{r}.\end{cases}

There are two basic properties that operators in
\mathcal{F}

(7.12)

Whenever

or
\mathcal{V}^{r}

and the implicit constant above is

, when

(7.14)

(3)

One can split the variation into a

(7.18)

\displaystyle N_{\lambda,\mathcal{A}}^{L}(f_{i})(x)=\sup\{r\in\mathbb{N}:\text{ there exist numbers }

,

Meanwhile, for the oscillation operator

\text{Osc}^{L}_{I,\mathcal{A}}(f_{n}(x):n\in\mathbb{N})=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sup_{A_{i}\in[J_{j},J_{j+1})}|f_{A_{i}}-f_{J_{j}}|^{2}(x)\Bigr)^{1/2}

\text{Osc}_{I}(f_{n})(x)\leq 5\Bigl(\mathcal{V}^{2,S}_{\mathcal{A}}(f_{i})(x)+\text{Osc}_{I,\mathcal{A}}^{L}(f_{i})(x)\Bigr).

We merge the data encoded in inequalities (

\frac{\lambda}{3}(N_{\lambda/3,\mathcal{A}}^{L})^{1/2}&\text{ if }U=\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}\\
\text{Osc}_{I,\mathcal{A}}^{L}&\text{ if }U=\text{Osc}_{I}.\\

We have the following inequality for all

(6)

\mathcal{F}

(7.25)

\begin{gathered}\left\lVert\mathcal{V}^{r}(\mathbb{E}_{N}f(x):N\in\mathbb{Z})\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{p}\frac{r}{r-2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\\
\forall_{\lambda>0}\left\lVert\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}_{N}f(x):N\in\mathbb{Z})\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\\
\forall_{I\in\mathbb{N}:|I|<\infty}\left\lVert\text{Osc}_{I}(\mathbb{E}_{N}f(x):N\in\mathbb{Z})\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\end{gathered}

were established as follows: the first in

The goal for this case is to specify precisely the form of Lépingle inequality that will be useful later. Namely, fix
t>0
and suppose that
M_{t}
is an averaging operator on functions
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
:

M_{t}f(x)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(x-P(t)).

Using Theorems
2.14

from

the following estimates hold

(7.27)

\begin{gathered}\left\lVert\mathcal{V}^{r}(M_{t}f(x):t>0)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{p}\frac{r}{r-2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\\
\forall_{\lambda>0}\left\lVert\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}(M_{t}f(x):t>0)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\\
\forall_{I\in\mathbb{N}:|I|<\infty}\left\lVert\text{Osc}_{I}(M_{t}f(x):t>0)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\end{gathered}

In all the inequalities from Theorem
1.4
we will begin by using partial summation as in the case of maximal inequality, we state the following proposition, which will be useful in these efforts:

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(w_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be non-negative sequences satisfying one of the following conditions:

(1)

The sequence
\Bigl(\frac{w_{n}^{\prime}}{w_{n}}\Bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
decreases monotonically;

(2)

The sequence
\Bigl(\frac{w_{n}^{\prime}}{w_{n}}\Bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
increases monotonically and:

(7.28)

C=\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{W_{N}w_{N}^{\prime}}{W_{N}^{\prime}w_{n}}<\infty

where
W_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}
and
W_{N}^{\prime}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}^{\prime}
.

Then, for any sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of complex numbers, any finite subsequence
I=(i_{j})_{j\leq M}
of natural numbers, any real number
\lambda>0
, and any
r>2
:

(7.29)

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{V}^{r}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}a_{n}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\lesssim\mathcal{V}^{r}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}^{\prime}a_{n}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\\
\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}a_{n}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{j}C_{j}D_{j}\lambda N_{D_{j}\lambda}^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}^{\prime}a_{n}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\\
\text{Osc}_{I}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}a_{n}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{j}C_{j}\text{Osc}_{I_{j}}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}^{\prime}a_{n}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\end{gathered}

where:

(1)

The implied constant in first inequality in (
7.29
) depends only on
C
;

(2)

In the second and third inequalities, the set of indexes
j
used for the outer summation might be countably infinite, but the sum
\sum_{j}C_{j}
is bounded by
O_{C}(1)
;

(3)

The sequences
(I_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}},(C_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(D_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are only determined by the sequences
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\newline
(w_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and do not rely on the choice of the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The first inequality from (
7.29
) was already stated in Proposition 5.1 from
[
MTZ17
]
. The second and the third inequalities can be proved in a similar manner, still we will discuss the proof of the third inequality:

Proof.

Following Lemma 2 from
[
MTZ17
]
, we introduce a double-indexed sequence
(\lambda_{n}^{k})_{n,k\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-negative real numbers such that for every
k
one has:

(7.30)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{k}=\Lambda<\infty.

Furthermore assume that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence:

(7.31)

k\to\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda_{n}^{k}

is decreasing. We will firstly show that there are finite sequences
(I_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
together with constants
C_{j}
with
\sum_{j}C_{j}=\Lambda
so that:

(7.32)

\text{Osc}_{I}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{k}a_{n}:k\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{j}C_{j}\text{Osc}_{I_{j}}\Bigl(a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define a function
N_{k}:[0,\Lambda]\to\mathbb{N}
by:

(7.33)

N_{k}(t)=\inf\{N\in\mathbb{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{k}>t\}

and
I_{n}^{k}=\{t\in[0,\Lambda]:N_{k}(t)=n\}
. Following page 14 of
[
MTZ17
]
, one gets that:

(7.34)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{k}a_{n}=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}a_{N_{k}(t)}dt.

Using this identity, we compute:

(7.35)

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\sup_{k_{j}\in[i_{j},i_{j+1})}\bigg|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\lambda_{n}^{k_{j}}-\lambda_{n}^{i_{j}})a_{n}\bigg|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\sup_{k_{j}\in[i_{j},i_{j+1})}\bigg|\int_{0}^{\Lambda}a_{N_{k_{j}}(t)}-a_{N_{i_{j}}(t)}dt\bigg|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\sup_{k_{j}\in[i_{j},i_{j+1})}\big|a_{N_{k_{j}}(t)}-a_{N_{i_{j}}(t)}\big|dt\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\sup_{k_{j}\in[i_{j},i_{j+1})}\big|a_{N_{k_{j}}(t)}-a_{N_{i_{j}}(t)}\big|^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}dt.

Due to the fact that
k\to\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lambda_{n}^{k}
is decreasing, one knows that
N_{k}(t)
is non-decreasing, therefore the right-hand side of (
7.35
) can be estimated by:

(7.36)

\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\sup_{k\in[N_{i_{j}}(t),N_{i_{j+1}}(t))}\big|a_{k}-a_{N_{i_{j}}(t)}\big|^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}dt.\\

There are countably many sequences of the form
(N_{i_{1}}(t),\ldots,N_{i_{M}}(t))
when
t
varies in the interval
[0,\Lambda]
, therefore (
7.32
) is satisfied indeed.
In a similar fashion to (
7.32
), we would like to verify now that:

(7.37)

\lambda N_{\lambda}^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{k}a_{n}:k\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}10\Lambda\frac{3^{j/2}}{2^{j}}\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{10\Lambda}N_{\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{10\Lambda}}^{1/2}(a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}).

Take a sequence
(k_{i})_{i=0}^{m}
so that for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
one has:

(7.38)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{k_{i+1}}a_{n}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}^{k_{i}}a_{n}>\lambda

In particular, we obtain that:

(7.39)

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}|a_{N_{k_{i+1}}(t)}-a_{N_{k_{i}}(t)}|dt>m\lambda,

where we have used (
7.34
). Pick an arbitrary
t
, we claim that there exists a nonnegative integer
j
so that:

(7.40)

u_{j}(t):=\bigg|\bigl\{i\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}:|a_{N_{k_{i+1}}(t)}-a_{N_{k_{i}}(t)}|>\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{5\Lambda}\bigr\}\bigg|\geq\frac{m}{3^{j}}

If that is not the case then with
u_{-1}(t):=m
one gets that:

(7.41)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}|a_{N_{k_{i+1}}(t)}-a_{N_{k_{i}}(t)}|dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(u_{j-1}(t)-u_{j}(t))\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{5\Lambda}dt

\displaystyle\leq m\frac{\lambda}{5}+\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}u_{j}(t)\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{5\Lambda}dt.

Since we assume that
u_{j}(t)
is everywhere bounded by
\frac{m}{3^{j}}
, by combining (
7.39
) and (
7.41
), we derive the inequality:

(7.42)

m\lambda<\frac{m\lambda}{5}+m\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{j}}{5\cdot 3^{j}}=\frac{4m\lambda}{5}

clearly giving contradiction, unless
m=0
, in which case
N_{\lambda}=0
. Now, due to (
7.40
), we obtain that:

(7.43)

N_{\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{10\Lambda}}(a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N})\geq\frac{m}{3^{j}}

and also:

(7.44)

\lambda m^{1/2}\leq 10\Lambda\frac{3^{j/2}}{2^{j}}\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{10\Lambda}N_{\frac{2^{j}\lambda}{10\Lambda}}^{1/2}(a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}).

Taking a supremum in
m
, one arrives at (
7.37
).
Suppose now that the sequence
\Bigl(\frac{w_{n}^{\prime}}{w_{n}}\Bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
increases monotonically. Then for:

(7.45)

\lambda_{n}^{k}:=\begin{cases}\frac{W_{n}}{W_{k}^{\prime}}(\frac{w_{n}^{\prime}}{w_{n}}-\frac{w_{n+1}^{\prime}}{w_{n+1}})&\text{ if }n\in[1,k)\\
\frac{W_{k}}{W_{k}^{\prime}}\frac{w_{k}^{\prime}}{w_{k}}&\text{ if }n=k\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

(7.46)

A_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}a_{n},\qquad A_{N}^{\prime}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}^{\prime}a_{n}

and also:

(7.47)

\tilde{\lambda}_{n}^{k}=\begin{cases}-\lambda_{n}^{k}&\text{ if }n\in[1,k)\\
2C-\lambda_{k}^{k}&\text{ if }n=k\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

we see that
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}^{k}
are nonnegative and by the partial summation:

(7.48)

A_{k}^{\prime}=2CA_{k}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\lambda}_{n}^{k}A_{n}.

Similarly to page 15 of
[
MTZ17
]
, one sees that for any positive integer
N
:
k\to(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tilde{\lambda}_{n}^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing with:

(7.49)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\lambda}_{n}^{k}=2C-1.

Therefore, using (
7.32
), one gets that:

(7.50)

\displaystyle\text{Osc}_{I}(A_{N}^{\prime}:N\in\mathbb{N})

\displaystyle\leq 2C\text{Osc}_{I}(A_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N})+\text{Osc}_{I}\Bigl(\sum_{n=1}^{k}\tilde{\lambda}_{n}^{k}A_{n}:k\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 2C\text{Osc}_{I}(A_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N})+\sum_{j}C_{j}\text{Osc}_{I_{j}}\Bigl(A_{N}:N\in\mathbb{N}\Bigr)

as we wanted. A simpler argument works with the jump counting operator (where we use (
7.37
)) and when
(\frac{w_{n}^{\prime}}{w_{n}})
decreases monotonically, where we maintain the same definitions as (
7.45
) and (
7.46
).
∎

The proof of Theorem
1.4
is almost the same as the proof of rapid convergence for polynomial ergodic averages given in Chapter 8 of
[
Kra22
]
. We suggest strongly that reader first look at what is happening there, before jumping into the proof below. We put emphasis on all the aspects where the reasoning goes differently, but we will unify the approach for all operators in the family
\mathcal{F}
:

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We proceed in a sequence of steps.

Step 1:
Attaching the von Mangoldt weight to the ergodic average.
Fix an operator
U
from the family
\mathcal{F}
. Similar to what we did for the maximal ergodic theorem, for the sake of circle method, we would like to investigate the analogous inequality for the von Mangoldt weighted averages.
Applying Proposition
7.2
with
w_{n}^{\prime}=\log n
,
w_{n}=1
and
a_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}f(x-n)
, we reduce our goal to showing that:

(7.51)

\left\lVert U(A_{N}^{\prime}f(x):N\in\mathbb{N})\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{n,p}r(U)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

We will use the sequence of variables
\rho,A_{0},\epsilon
so that we fix the order:

(7.52)

\rho^{-1}\gg A_{0}\gg B\gg A\gg\epsilon^{-1}\gg\theta_{p}\gg 1,

with
A,B
as in
4.1
and
\theta_{p}
suitable for interpolation arguments.

Step 2:
Sparsifying the sequence of indexes in the variational operator.
We use the splitting into long and short variation as in (
7.24
), in this concrete situation we take sequence
\mathcal{B}=(\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor)_{k=1}^{\infty}
. Then Theorem
1.4
will follow from the two inequalities:

(7.53)

\left\lVert U^{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(A_{N}^{\prime}f(x):N\in\mathbb{N})\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{\rho,n,p}r(U)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

and

(7.54)

\left\lVert\mathcal{V}^{2,S}_{\mathcal{B}}(A_{N}^{\prime}f(x):N\in\mathbb{N})\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{\rho,n,p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

The sequence
\mathcal{B}
is sufficiently dense for showing the second statement:

(7.55)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{V}^{2,S}_{\mathcal{B}}(A_{N}^{\prime}f(x):N\in\mathbb{N})\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sup_{\lfloor 2^{n^{\epsilon}}\rfloor\leq n_{1}<\ldots<n_{t}<\lfloor 2^{(n+1)^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}|A_{n_{k+1}}^{\prime}f-A_{n_{k}}^{\prime}f|\Bigr)^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}(x)\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sum_{j=\lfloor 2^{n^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}^{\lfloor 2^{(n+1)^{\epsilon}}\rfloor-1}|A_{j+1}^{\prime}f-A_{j}^{\prime}f|\Bigr)^{2}\Biggr)^{1/2}(x)\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sum_{j=\lfloor 2^{n^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}^{\lfloor 2^{(n+1)^{\epsilon}}\rfloor-1}\left\lVert A_{j+1}^{\prime}f-A_{j}^{\prime}f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\Bigr)^{\min(2,p)}\Biggr)^{1/\min(2,p)}

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\sum_{j=\lfloor 2^{n^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}^{\lfloor 2^{(n+1)^{\epsilon}}\rfloor-1}\frac{1+\log(j+1)}{j+1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\Bigr)^{\min(2,p)}\Biggr)^{1/\min(2,p)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\Biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{\min(2,p)(2\epsilon-1)}\Biggr)^{1/2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

From now on, we endeavour to establish (
7.53
).

Step 3:
Addressing the contribution from the minor arcs.
First, we will reduce (
7.53
) to the same statement for a sequence of operators precomposed with Ionescu-Wainger multipliers:

(7.56)

\left\lVert U\Biggl(A_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}^{\prime}\Bigl(\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m})^{\vee}\ast f\Bigr)(x):k\in\mathbb{N}\Biggr)\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})},

where the multiplier
\mathbf{m}
is
\varphi_{d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}}(\beta)
with
d^{\prime}=\frac{2d-1}{2}
, where
d
is the degree of the polynomial
P
.
In both inequalities (
7.53
) and (
7.56
) one may restrict the
k
-range only to integers bigger than
O_{\rho}(1)
. The support of
\mathbf{m}
is in the interval
[-2^{-d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}},2^{-d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}}]
, which in turn is inside the interval
[-\exp(-k^{A_{0}\rho}),\exp(-k^{A_{0}\rho})]
due to (
7.52
). This means that conditions of (
7.4
) are satisfied for
\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m})
. In order to show the reduction stated earlier in this step, observe that:

(7.57)

\left\lVert A_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}^{\prime}\Bigl(f-\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m})^{\vee}\ast f\Bigr)(x)\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{n,\rho,p}k^{-2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}

as we can employ (
7.13
) together with (
7.12
). Inequality (
7.57
) is proved using interpolation methods, which we now describe. Due to (
7.4
), one has that:

(7.58)

\left\lVert A_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}^{\prime}\Bigl(f-\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m})^{\vee}\ast f\Bigr)(x)\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{\rho,p}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

On the other hand, the supremum of:

(7.59)

|\widehat{K_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}}\cdot(1-\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m}))|

on the torus is smaller than as the supremum of
\widehat{K_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}}
outside of the union:

\mathcal{R}_{k^{A_{0}}}:=\bigcup_{a/q:a\perp q,q\leq k^{A_{0}}}\Bigl[\frac{a}{q}-2^{-d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}-2},\frac{a}{q}+2^{-d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}-2}\Bigr].

m_{B}\subset\mathbb{T}\backslash\mathcal{R}_{k^{A_{0}}},

Eventually, (

Step 4:
Taking advantage of major arc behaviour given in Theorem
3.1

(7.63)

\left\lVert U((M_{k}^{(1)})^{\vee}\ast f(x):k\in\mathbb{N})\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{n,p}r(U)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})},

where:

(7.64)

M_{k}^{(1)}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathbf{h}(a/q)\leq k^{A_{0}}}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}).

Thanks to
7.13
and (
7.12
), it is sufficient for that reduction to get an appropriate bound for:

(7.65)

\left\lVert\Bigl((M_{k}^{(1)})^{\vee}-K_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}\ast\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m})^{\vee}\Bigr)\ast f(x)\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Since the size of
k^{A_{0}}
-th Ionescu-Wainger set is

and

,
\varphi(2^{d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}})^{\vee}

(7.66)

O_{\rho,p}(2^{k^{A_{0}\rho}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}).

We contrast this with
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-methods. Namely, the identity from Theorem
3.1
implies that:

(7.67)

\left\lVert\Bigl((M_{k}^{(1)})^{\vee}-K_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}\ast\Pi_{\leq k^{A_{0}}}(\mathbf{m})^{\vee}\Bigr)\ast f(x)\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{B}\exp(-ck^{\epsilon/2}),

so (
7.52
) and interpolation implies that (

) is at most

, which is sufficient for this reduction.

Step 5:
Oscillatory integral estimates allows us to pass to the multiplier with support not depending on
k
.
The following reduction uses again the aforementioned reference and is constructed so that the Ionescu-Wainger operator pops up:

(7.68)

\left\lVert U((M_{k}^{(2)})^{\vee}\ast f(x):k\in\mathbb{N})\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{n,p}r(U)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})},

where:

(7.69)

M_{k}^{(2)}(\alpha)=\sum_{s:2^{s}\leq k^{A_{0}}}\sum_{\mathbf{h}(a/q)=2^{s}}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\varphi_{2^{d^{\prime}\epsilon s/A_{0}}}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}).

Indeed, assuming (
7.68
), we may use (
7.13
) and (
7.12
) to show that:

(7.70)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert(M_{k}^{(1)}-M_{k}^{(2)})^{\vee}\ast f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{n,p,\rho}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

We use strong
l^{2}
estimates and acceptable
l^{p}
estimates to establish (
7.68
).
Note that
M_{k}^{(1)}-M_{k}^{(2)}
is the same as:

(7.71)

\sum_{s:2^{s}\leq k^{A_{0}}}\sum_{\mathbf{h}(a/q)=2^{s}}\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}v_{\lfloor 2^{k^{\epsilon}}\rfloor}\Bigl(\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\Bigr)\Bigl(\varphi_{d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q})-\varphi_{2^{d^{\prime}\epsilon s/A_{0}}}(\alpha-\frac{a}{q})\Bigr).

For the

, and the third equality from Lemma

is satisfied, namely

(7.72)

\frac{S(a,q)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}

is essentially a convex combination of phases. We conclude from (

) that the above multiplier satisfies:

(7.73)

\left\lVert(M_{k}^{(1)}-M_{k}^{(2)})^{\vee}\ast f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim_{p,\rho}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{p}(\mathbb{Z})}.

We now consider what is happening in the

-situation. More precisely, one has that for fixed

, there is only one term inside (

(7.74)

|\alpha-\frac{a}{q}|\gtrsim 2^{-d^{\prime}k^{\epsilon}},

so the oscillatory integral

(due to Lemma B.2. from
[

]

2^{-\frac{1}{2d}k^{\epsilon}}
, so:

(7.75)

\left\lVert(M_{k}^{(1)}-M_{k}^{(2)})^{\vee}\ast f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\lesssim 2^{-\frac{1}{2d}k^{\epsilon}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}.

Interpolating this inequality together with (

).

In order to deduce (

M_{k}^{(2)}=\sum_{s:2^{s}\leq k^{A_{0}}}M_{k,s}

Step 7:
Showing that Weyl sum estimates imply the exponential saving for the arithmetic factor.
We first discuss how to prove:

\displaystyle T_{s}(\alpha)

The Rademacher-Menshov is used to verifying the small scale case.

\left\lVert\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\big|((v_{t_{i+1}}-v_{t_{i}}))^{\vee}\ast f\big|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}

Recall at the beginning that action of

8.
Divergence in
L^{1}

Due to Sawyer’s principle, we want to show that for arbitrary
C
, one has a measure preserving system
(X,\mu,T)
and function
f\in L^{1}(X)
, so that:

(8.1)

\left\lVert\sup_{M\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg|\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[M]|}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[M]}f(T^{p}x)\bigg|\right\rVert_{L^{1,\infty}(X)}>C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{1}}.

We want to use the aforementioned Theorem 3.1. of
[
LaV11
]
. For this reason, let us justify that conditions
(3.1)-(3.5)
from LaVictoire’s work are satisfied, this will be sufficient to show that the investigated sequence does not satisfy the
L^{1}\to L^{1,\infty}
bounds. In the first two cases one needs to provide two sequences
(p_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
so that density of residues coming from the sequence of primes of form
x^{2}+ny^{2}
tends with
j\to\infty
respectively to
0
and
1
, respectively. Conditions
(3.3)
and
(3.4)
are the most technical, they more or less describe how residues modulo
q_{j}
and their sums are separated when
j\to\infty
. Nevertheless, it is known from the work of LaVictoire that when the limiting behaviour of the differences between consecutive residues is Poisson, then one gets
(3.3)
and
(3.4)
for free. Finally, the last condition says that all residues that are hit by
\mathbb{P}_{n}
are hit sufficiently often.

So, pick
(p_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
to be an increasing sequence of primes such that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
:
\Bigl(\frac{-n}{p_{j}}\Bigr)=1
, and choose
(q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
to be highly composite so that:

(1)

For every
j
,
q_{j}
is odd, squarefree and
-n
is a quadratic residue that is coprime with respect to every prime divisor of
q_{j}
;

(2)

The sequence
q_{j}^{-1}\varphi(q_{j})
converges to
0
as
j\to\infty
.

Next, for every
Q
so that all prime divisors
p
of
Q
satisfy
\Bigl(\frac{-n}{p}\Bigr)=1
, let
\Lambda_{Q}
represent the set of coprime residues with respect to
Q
. For
(3.1)
we use the fact that:

(8.2)

\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}:(\frac{-n}{p})=1}\frac{p-1}{p}=0,

due to the Chebotarev density theorem for
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-n})
.
(3.2)
is coming from the convergence
\frac{\varphi(p)}{p}\to 1
for primes, meanwhile
(3.3)
and
(3.4)
follow from the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\Lambda_{q_{j}}^{\gamma})
converges to the Poisson distribution with respect to
\gamma
as
j\to\infty
(see Lemma
3.2.
and the equation above it in
[
LaV11
]
). Originally, this fact was established by Hooley in
[
Hoo65
]
, we mimic LaVictoire there in applying Hooley’s work. Ultimately, the only thing that requires justification is the inequality:

(8.3)

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]|}\bigg|\{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]:p\equiv a\pmod{Q}\}\bigg|\gtrsim\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{Q}|}

whenever
Q
is a product of distinct elements from the sequences
(p_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
A standard partial summation argument allows us to obtain (
3.9
) from the following:

(8.4)

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]}e\Bigl(\frac{ap}{q}\Bigr)=S(a,q)\frac{\text{li}(m)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}+O(m\exp(-c\sqrt{\log m})),\\

where
\text{li}(m)
represents the logarithmic integral:

(8.5)

\int_{2}^{m}\frac{1}{\log t}dt.

Using Fourier inversion, we compute the size of the set:

(8.6)

\displaystyle\bigg|\{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]:p\equiv b\pmod{Q}\}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]}\sum_{a\pmod{Q}}e\Bigl(\frac{a(p-b)}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{a\pmod{Q}}e\Bigl(\frac{-ba}{q}\Bigr)\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}\cap[m]}e\Bigl(\frac{ap}{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{a\pmod{Q}}S(a,q)e\Bigl(\frac{-ba}{q}\Bigr)\frac{\text{li}(m)}{R_{n}\varphi_{2}(q_{0})}

\displaystyle+O(m\exp(-c\sqrt{\log m})).

The later average:

(8.7)

\frac{1}{Q}\sum_{a\pmod{Q}}S(a,q)e\Bigl(\frac{-ba}{q}\Bigr)

is the same as

(8.8)

\bigg|\{x,y\in[Q]:x^{2}+ny^{2}\perp Q,x^{2}+ny^{2}\equiv b\pmod{Q}\}\bigg|.

If we manage to show that the value of this expression does not depend on
b
, then we will have shown (
8.3
). Since
Q
is product of distinct prime factors and our claim has a local nature (due to the Chinese remainder theorem), one need only show that for every odd prime
p
with
\Bigl(\frac{-n}{p}\Bigr)=1
, the quantity:

(8.9)

\bigg|\{x,y\in[p]:x^{2}+ny^{2}\perp p,x^{2}+ny^{2}\equiv b\pmod{p}\}\bigg|

does not depend on
b
, provided
b
is coprime to
p
. So, suppose that
-n\equiv w^{2}\pmod{p}
; we want to solve the congruence:

(8.10)

(x-wy)(x+wy)\equiv b\pmod{p}.

It is straightforward to see that the number of solutions (
8.10
) and thus (
8.9
) is precisely
p-1
. That means we have checked all the conditions to apply Theorem
1.6
at LaVictoire
[
LaV11
]
.

Appendix A
Introduction to algebraic number theory

In this appendix we provide a little dictionary of terms coming from algebraic number theory, which we believe will help to understand the paper for those whose background is more analytic:

(1)

The number field
K
is an extension of
\mathbb{Q}
so that
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}K
is finite. Classical examples of number fields are
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-l})
or
\mathbb{Q}(\exp(\frac{2\pi i}{l}))
for an integer
l
.

(2)

An element
a\in K
is called
integral
if it is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. One can prove that the set of integral elements of field
K
is closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication; we will denoted this ring by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

(3)

For a commutative ring
R
with identity, we define the
R
-module
M
to be an abelian group with respect to addition, equipped with multiplication
\cdot:R\times M\to M
so that for all
r,s\in R
and
x,y\in M
:

(A.1)

r\cdot(x+y)=r\cdot x+r\cdot y

(A.2)

(r+s)\cdot x=r\cdot x+s\cdot x

(A.3)

rs\cdot x=r\cdot(s\cdot x)

(A.4)

1\cdot x=x

(4)

A fractional ideal
is an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-submodule
J
of
K
such that there exists a non-zero
r\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
with
rJ\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
. We will denote the set of these ideals by
I_{K}
.

(5)

A prime ideal
is an ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subsetneq\mathcal{O}_{K}
so that for any two
a,b\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
with
ab\in\mathfrak{p}
, either
a
or
b
is an element of
\mathfrak{p}
.

(6)

The product of two fractional ideals
I
and
J
is defined as:

(A.5)

IJ=\{a_{1}b_{1}+\ldots+a_{k}b_{k}:\forall_{i}a_{i}\in I,b_{i}\in J\}.

(7)

By the above, the set of fractional ideals form a commutative semigroup; in our case, we are dealing with rings
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, so for every element
I
in
I_{\mathcal{O}_{K}}
one can find an inverse ideal
J
so that
IJ=\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

(8)

An important theorem in algebraic number theory says that ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
obey
unique factorization
, so that every ideal can be written as a product of prime ideals. Furthermore, every fractional ideal can be written uniquely as a product of prime ideals and their inverses.
This gives an analogy between the arithmetic situation for integers: prime ideals are to rings
\mathcal{O}_{K}
the analogues of primes in
\mathbb{Z}
, ideals correspond to integers and fractional ideals correspond to rational numbers (up to sign).

(9)

The norm of an ideal
\mathfrak{n}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
is simply the size of quotient group
\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{n}
. We will denote it by
N\mathfrak{n}
. This norm respects product
N(\mathfrak{n}\mathfrak{k})=N\mathfrak{n}N\mathfrak{k}
, therefore one can extend this definition to fractional ideals. Moreover for
a\in K\backslash\{0\}
, we define
Na=N((a))
, i.e. the norm of the element
a
is the norm of the principal ideal generated by
a
.

(10)

We will say that two fractional ideals are
coprime
when there is no joint prime ideal factor in the corresponding factorizations.

(11)

For a general number field
K
, ideals
I\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
may not be
principal
(i.e. generated by single element). The set of ideals are closed with respect to actions of multiplication and taking inverses, so the group of these ideals will be called
\mathcal{P}_{K}
. One of the main results in algebraic number theory says that the quotient group
I_{K}/\mathcal{P}_{K}=:\text{Cl}(K)
is finite.

(12)

Last but not least, by a Hecke character we will mean a function
\chi:I_{K}\to S^{1}
, and so that
\chi(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b})=\chi(\mathfrak{a})\chi(\mathfrak{b})
for all
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
in
I_{K}
so that for some
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\chi
maps the set of principal ideals with generators congruent to
1
modulo
q
(denote it
\mathcal{P}_{K}^{+}(q)
) to
1
.
