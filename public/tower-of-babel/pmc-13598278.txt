Blended Learning with Holographic Imaging Support in Urologic Oncology Education for International Medical Students: A Controlled Educational Study
Authors: Binbin Zhao, Xiaoyu Lu, Jianbin Bi, Lizhu Chen, Xin Jin, Mo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTeaching urologic oncology to international medical students is challenging because the content is anatomically complex, clinically applied, and often learned in a second-language environment. Introduction

In recent years, with the comprehensive implementation of the Belt and Road Initiative, China has continuously strengthened educational exchanges and collaboration with countries and regions along the routes, attracting a growing number of international students to pursue studies in China.1–3 However, despite the positive progress achieved in the education of international students in China, persistent problems and challenges remain.4–6 International medical students often encounter distinctive challenges in clinical education, particularly when learning highly specialized content in a second-language environment. These challenges may be amplified in topics that require both conceptual understanding and the ability to mentally construct complex three-dimensional anatomical relationships.7 In addition, current curricular arrangements for international students lack sufficient specificity, and the associated teaching systems lag behind the rapid development of international medical education.8,9 Therefore, developing scientific, student-centered improvements to educational models and effectively enhancing teaching quality—grounded in the characteristics of international students—10,11 has become both an essential requirement and an urgent priority for medical education for international students at this stage.12

Breakthroughs in information technology and artificial intelligence have brought profound changes and important opportunities for the advancement of medical education.13,14 In particular, blended learning, supported by an information-rich online learning environment and oriented toward personalized learning, has attracted extensive attention from scholars in China and internationally and has become an important direction in medical education reform.15–17 For international medical students, the value of blended learning may extend beyond flexibility of access alone. Online support allows learners to revisit materials at their own pace, reinforces key concepts through repeated exposure, and may reduce some of the linguistic pressure associated with one-time real-time classroom delivery. These characteristics are especially relevant in international medical education, where students often differ in language proficiency, prior educational background, and confidence in participating in classroom discussion. In this sense, blended learning may provide a more adaptable instructional framework for complex medical content than conventional teacher-centered delivery alone.

Urologic tumors exhibit substantial anatomical complexity and distinctive spatial relationships. The urinary system consists of the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra, and in males, also includes accessory structures such as the prostate. These organs differ significantly in anatomical location, histological composition, and physiological function, and are closely associated with major blood vessels, nerves, and adjacent organs. Consequently, tumor development and progression often involve intricate, multi-layered spatial relationships, placing high demands on precise anatomical localization and three-dimensional structural understanding. This complexity increases the challenges encountered in both clinical teaching and practice. Holographic imaging, an emerging approach to medical image visualization, can reprocess raw imaging data and use three-dimensional reconstruction to transform conventional two-dimensional images into high-definition holographic digital 3D anatomical structures with a wide color gamut and strong stereoscopic depth.18,19 Compared with topics that rely mainly on factual memorization, urologic oncology requires learners to integrate multiple levels of information, including tumor location, vascular relationships, adjacent structures, and the implications of these factors for clinical decision-making and operative strategy. In conventional classroom teaching, such relationships are often presented through static textbook figures or slide-based illustrations, which may not fully convey depth, orientation, or the spatial overlap of anatomical structures. As a result, students may acquire isolated pieces of knowledge without developing a sufficiently integrated understanding of how anatomical relationships influence clinical reasoning. When displayed through mixed-reality devices, reconstructed images can be overlaid on the physical environment to provide an intuitive three-dimensional view.20 At present, the application of holographic visualization in surgery is rapidly expanding, demonstrating clear advantages in multiple domains, including preoperative planning, surgical simulation, and skills training.21–26 Although holographic or mixed-reality tools are increasingly visible in medical education, simply introducing new technology does not by itself establish educational value. The more important question is whether such tools, when embedded in a coherent instructional design, improve the kinds of learning outcomes that are most relevant to the content being taught. For urologic oncology, these outcomes may include not only factual knowledge, but also spatial understanding and case-based reasoning. The potential educational value of holographic visualization may be understood in the context of the cognitive theory of multimedia learning and spatial mental-model development. Coordinated verbal and visual information may help learners organize complex anatomical relationships, while manipulable three-dimensional representations may facilitate the construction of spatial mental models for interpreting clinical cases. These theoretical perspectives provide a possible explanation for how holographic visualization may support learning.27–31 Therefore, developing innovative teaching strategies that combine immersive visualization technologies with flexible learning formats may help improve students’ comprehension of complex anatomical structures and clinical concepts.

However, whether the educational benefits of such an approach differ across types of learning outcomes remains unclear. To address this question, we evaluated a blended-learning intervention with holographic imaging support for international undergraduate medical students learning urologic oncology. Rather than examining only overall performance, we compared its effects on theoretical knowledge and the application of knowledge to case-analysis questions. We hypothesized that the intervention would show greater benefit for clinically applied and spatially demanding tasks, such as case analysis, than for factual theoretical recall.

Methods

Study Design

This was a single-center, parallel-group randomized controlled educational study conducted between April and June 2024 at China Medical University. The study was designed to compare a blended learning approach incorporating holographic imaging support with a conventional teaching approach in the urologic oncology module for international undergraduate medical students.

Setting and Participants

A total of 80 international undergraduate medical students from the 2021 cohort majoring in clinical medicine at China Medical University were included in this study. All participants had completed the General Surgery course and therefore met the prerequisite requirements for learning the urologic oncology module. Students were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the control group using a computer-generated allocation sequence prepared in Microsoft Excel, with 40 students in each group. In the intervention group, there were 24 males and 16 females, with a mean age of 22.40 ± 1.35 years and a prior surgery course score of 83.47 ± 7.01. In the control group, there were 23 males and 17 females, with a mean age of 22.18 ± 1.41 years and a prior surgery course score of 82.54 ± 7.22. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in age, sex, or prior academic performance (all P > 0.05), indicating baseline comparability. All students provided written informed consent before participation.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Students were included if they: (1) were full-time international undergraduate medical students enrolled in the clinical medicine program at the study institution; (2) had completed the required basic medical courses but had not received systematic teaching on the urologic oncology topics covered in this study; (3) were able to complete classroom learning, case analysis, theoretical assessment, and the learner-perception questionnaire in the language used in the course; (4) were able to participate in the complete teaching program and post-course assessment; and (5) voluntarily participated and provided written informed consent.

Students were excluded before randomization if they: (1) had previously completed the same or a highly similar urologic oncology course, holographic-imaging course, or related specialist training; (2) were unable to complete the teaching program because of suspension of study, exchange study, or another known reason; (3) were unable to use the study equipment despite reasonable support; or (4) declined participation or the use of their data for educational research.

Random Sampling and Randomization

Before randomization, eligible consenting students were identified using unique study numbers. Names, student identification numbers, previous grades, and other potentially influential information were not used during random selection. The candidate list was randomly ordered using computer-generated random numbers in Microsoft Excel, and the first 80 students in the randomized list were selected for inclusion.

The selected students were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention group or the control group. Randomization was stratified by sex. Within each sex stratum, the allocation sequence was generated in Microsoft Excel using randomly varying block sizes of 4 and 6. The block sizes and their order were not disclosed to the personnel responsible for participant enrolment or teaching. The sequence was generated and retained by a researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment, teaching, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis. The resulting allocation included 40 students in each group.

Sample-Size Calculation

The total post-module objective assessment score was specified as the primary outcome. A systematic review and meta-analysis of blended versus traditional learning in medical education reported a standardized mean difference of 0.73 for knowledge outcomes in the comparison of online blended learning with traditional learning.17 Considering the differences in participants, course content, instructional technology, and outcome assessment, a slightly more conservative anticipated effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.70 was used for the present study. With a two-sided α of 0.05, 80% power, and 1:1 allocation, 34 students per group (68 in total) were required for an independent-samples comparison. Allowing for approximately 10% attrition or missing primary-outcome data increased the required sample size to at least 76 students. Because the number of eligible students was sufficient, the target sample was rounded up to 80 to maintain equal allocation with 40 students per group and to provide a modest increase in statistical power.

Instructional Context

Both groups studied the urologic oncology chapter from Campbell-Walsh Urology (12th edition) over 9 class hours. Teaching in both groups was delivered by the same instructor, who had substantial teaching experience, was qualified to teach in English, and had received systematic training in holographic imaging technology. Before the study, the instructor reviewed the standardized lesson plan to maintain the intended content, duration, and sequence in both groups.

Intervention and Comparator

The same module content, course duration, and instructor were used in both groups; the principal pedagogical difference was the incorporation of holographic imaging into the blended learning process in the intervention group. The intervention was delivered across three phases: pre-class preview, in-class holographic imaging-supported teaching, and post-class review with exercises.

The intervention group received a blended learning approach in which holographic imaging was incorporated as the principal instructional enhancement. Rain Classroom was used as an online platform for distributing teaching materials, supporting preview, assigning exercises, and facilitating review. Before class, students could access teacher-prepared materials and preview QR-code-linked three-dimensional visualizations of tumors, vasculature, and adjacent organs (Supplementary Figure 1). Important anatomical structures were labeled in English to facilitate understanding (Figure 1). During face-to-face sessions, holographic imaging served as the key differentiating teaching feature. The instructor and students jointly explored reconstructed holographic models using HoloLens. The models could be manipulated through rotation, zooming, structure separation, selective display, and transparency adjustment. Surgical videos incorporating holographic navigation were also used to connect anatomical understanding with operative strategy. After each section, exercises were distributed through the platform, and students were able to review the materials after class.

English-labeled three-dimensional anatomical structures used in holographic imaging-supported teaching.

The control group received a conventional teacher-centered instructional approach without holographic imaging support. The same module content was delivered using standard teaching materials, including textbook-based explanation and slide-based presentation. Students previewed the textbook before class and reviewed the material independently after class.

The groups were taught at different times and in different classrooms and used separate online course spaces with different access permissions. Holographic models, three-dimensional reconstructions, HoloLens materials, holographic-navigation videos, and related QR codes were available only to the intervention group until completion of the primary-outcome assessment. Students were asked not to share group-specific materials or attend the other group’s sessions, and the instructor followed the prespecified teaching plans without presenting intervention-only resources to the control group. Because participants attended the same institution, informal communication outside class could not be completely excluded.

Outcome Measures

The total score on the immediate post-module objective assessment was the primary outcome. The case-analysis score and theoretical essay score were secondary objective outcomes, and responses to the six learner-perception items were exploratory secondary outcomes. The assessment was administered to both groups at the same time and place and comprised questions based on 10 typical urologic tumor cases (50 points) and five theoretical essay questions (50 points), for a total of 100 points. The assessment content was based on the course objectives. Case-analysis and essay responses were scored using the same prespecified analytic rubric for both groups. Answer scripts were identified only by study number so that the grader did not have access to student names or group allocation during scoring. The use of the same instructor for both teaching and grading helped maintain consistency in instructional delivery and scoring across the two groups.

Learner-Perception Questionnaire

After the objective assessment, both groups completed an anonymous investigator-developed questionnaire intended for local course evaluation. Its six items addressed preference for the teaching approach, perceived self-directed learning, learning interest, anatomical understanding and three-dimensional spatial imagination, teacher-student interaction, and support for pre-class preparation and post-class review. Each item used a three-level ordinal response (agree, neutral, disagree). The items were developed according to the local course objectives. The questionnaire was intended for local course evaluation and was not established as a formally validated psychometric scale; therefore, each item was analyzed separately and no composite score was calculated. All 80 questionnaires were returned.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed separately within each study group for each assessment outcome using the Jarque–Bera test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Because the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was not satisfied for any of the three assessment outcomes, between-group comparisons were performed using Welch’s independent-samples t test. When evidence of non-normality was identified, a Mann–Whitney U-test was additionally performed as a sensitivity analysis. For the objective assessment outcomes, mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as intervention minus control, and effect sizes were reported as Hedges’ g. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Fisher’s exact test was used for sex, and Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact tests were used for the three-category questionnaire items because of small expected cell counts. The six questionnaire items were analyzed separately, and Holm adjustment was applied to their P values to control the family-wise error rate. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The randomized controlled educational trial evaluated blended learning with holographic imaging support for teaching urologic oncology to international medical students. The intervention group achieved significantly higher overall course and case-analysis scores than the control group and reported more favorable perceptions across all six questionnaire items. The theoretical essay scores did not differ significantly between the groups. The between-group difference in the overall score was largely attributable to the case-analysis component, suggesting that the teaching approach may help students understand and apply knowledge involving complex spatial relationships in urologic oncology.

Given the large effect sizes observed for the case-analysis and overall scores, the normality and homogeneity-of-variance assumptions were examined further. The Jarque–Bera test indicated evidence of non-normality in the intervention-group case-analysis scores. Levene’s tests showed unequal variances for the theoretical essay, case-analysis, and overall scores. Welch’s independent-samples t tests were therefore used for all three between-group comparisons, and a Mann–Whitney U sensitivity analysis was performed for the case-analysis score. The sensitivity analysis led to the same conclusion as the Welch t test, indicating that the difference in case-analysis performance remained evident under different statistical approaches. Nevertheless, the trial involved a limited sample from a single center, and the large effect sizes require confirmation in larger investigations.

The educational value of holographic imaging support may be related to its capacity to present complex anatomical relationships intuitively. Learning urologic oncology requires students to integrate tumor location, surrounding vasculature, adjacent organs, and their relationships with operative strategies. Students in the intervention group could preview three-dimensional materials before class, examine and manipulate reconstructed models with HoloLens during class, and revisit the materials after class. Functions such as rotation, zooming, structure separation, selective display, and transparency adjustment allowed students to examine spatial relationships between tumors and surrounding structures from different perspectives. Repeated exposure and interactive exploration may have helped students develop more coherent spatial representations and use relevant knowledge more effectively when answering case-analysis questions. This proposed explanation is consistent with the cognitive theory of multimedia learning and the development of spatial mental models. Coordinated verbal and visual information can help learners organize complex material, while manipulable three-dimensional models provide intuitive support for understanding depth, orientation, and overlapping structures. Combining pre-class preparation, teacher-guided model exploration, and post-class review may have helped students progressively integrate anatomical, imaging, and clinical information. Cognitive load and changes in spatial mental models were not measured directly. The theoretical frameworks therefore provide a possible explanation for the observed results, and the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. The teaching approach may also be well suited to the learning needs of international medical students. When studying specialized medical subjects, international students must simultaneously process a second language, medical terminology, and complex anatomical relationships. Key anatomical structures were labeled in English, and the online platform provided materials that students could access repeatedly for pre-class preparation and post-class review at their own pace. Such an arrangement may have helped students gradually connect English terminology with anatomical structures and their clinical significance. Repeated access may also have contributed to the more favorable perceptions reported by the intervention group regarding learning interest, self-directed learning, teacher–student interaction, and the convenience of learning.

The learner-perception findings were generally consistent with the objective assessment results. After Holm adjustment, responses to all six items remained significantly more favorable in the intervention group, indicating that students generally valued the teaching approach and their learning experience. The investigators developed the questionnaire according to local course objectives, and the instrument has not undergone comprehensive psychometric validation. Each item was therefore analyzed separately, and no composite score was calculated. The questionnaire findings describe students’ perceptions of the teaching activities and complement the objective assessment results.

Previous studies and systematic reviews have suggested that blended learning may improve knowledge outcomes in medical education. Three-dimensional visualization, augmented reality, and mixed reality have also been applied in anatomical and clinical education. The educational trial extended these approaches to a urologic oncology course for international medical students and examined theoretical essay and case-analysis performance separately. Case analysis requires students to integrate anatomical, imaging, and clinical information. The improvement in case-analysis performance is consistent with the spatial learning support provided by interactive three-dimensional visualization. However, previous investigations have differed in learner characteristics, course content, educational technologies, and assessment instruments; their reported effect sizes should therefore be compared cautiously.

The intervention comprised a complete blended-learning program that included online preparation, Rain Classroom materials, classroom teaching, teacher-guided manipulation of holographic models, surgical videos incorporating holographic navigation, post-class exercises, and repeated access to learning materials. Holographic imaging served as the principal visual and interactive component of the program. The observed outcomes reflect the combined effect of the complete educational intervention, and the contribution of each component was not assessed separately. Implementation also requires mixed-reality equipment, three-dimensional models, an online platform, technical support, and instructor training. Equipment costs, technical maintenance, class size, and access to resources may influence wider adoption.

Several limitations should be considered: 1. The trial was conducted at a single center and included a relatively limited sample. The applicability of the findings to other institutions, courses, and student populations requires further evaluation. 2. A specific baseline assessment of urologic oncology knowledge was not conducted. Although the groups did not differ significantly in age, sex, or previous surgery course scores, those characteristics do not fully represent their knowledge of urologic oncology before the intervention. 3. The same instructor taught both groups and graded the assessments. The arrangement helped maintain consistency in teaching delivery and scoring standards, but the instructor was aware of the teaching approach used in each group, leaving the possibility of expectancy or grading bias. 4. The objective assessment and learner-perception questionnaire were developed according to local course objectives, and the questionnaire has not undergone comprehensive external validation. 5. The trial evaluated a complete educational package, and the independent contribution of each component was not examined. 6. All outcomes were assessed immediately after the module. Knowledge retention, subsequent clinical learning performance, and the durability of the educational effects were not evaluated. 7. Although the groups were taught at different times and in different classrooms, used separate online course spaces, and had different access permissions, informal communication between students at the same institution may still have resulted in some contamination between groups.

Despite these limitations, blended learning with holographic imaging support improved overall course scores and case-analysis performance among international medical students and was associated with more favorable learner perceptions. The Welch t test and Mann–Whitney U sensitivity analysis led to consistent conclusions, providing further support for the observed difference in case-analysis performance. By combining online preparation, face-to-face teaching, interactive three-dimensional visualization, and post-class review, the approach offers a potentially useful option for teaching spatially complex content in urologic oncology. Future multicenter investigations with larger samples, baseline and delayed assessments, and further validated outcome measures are needed to examine wider applicability and the durability of the educational effects.

Conclusions

In this single-center randomized controlled educational study, blended learning with holographic imaging support significantly improved overall course scores and performance on case-analysis questions among international medical students and was associated with more favorable evaluations of the learning experience, whereas theoretical essay scores did not differ significantly between the groups. These findings suggest that this teaching approach may be particularly useful for supporting the understanding and application of spatially complex knowledge in urologic oncology and may offer a promising option for teaching international medical students. Further multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate its wider applicability and the durability of its educational benefits.
