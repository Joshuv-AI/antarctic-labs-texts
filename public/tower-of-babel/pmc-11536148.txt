Maternal NO2 exposure and fetal growth restriction: Hypoxia transmission and lncRNAs-proinflammation-mediated abnormal hematopoiesis
Authors: Liyao Qin, Huifeng Yue, Zhihua Gong, Yuqiong Guo, Dan Li, Li Ma, Zhuoma YiXi, Jing He, Zhihong Li, Guangke Li, Wei Yan, Nan Sang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEpidemiological studies suggest a link between air pollution and adverse birth outcomes, though these findings are controversial. This study integrates the retrospective epidemiological analysis with toxicological experiments to confirm the causal relationship between maternal nitrogen dioxide (NO2) exposure and an increased risk of fetal growth restriction (FGR). By identifying critical windows, elucidating the maternal–fetal transmission pathway, and uncovering potential epigenetic mechanisms, our study establishes a comprehensive understanding of not only FGR progression from environmental exposure but also molecular targets for risk prevention and population intervention in clinical practice. Furthermore, our study offers valuable insights for local governments and municipalities to mitigate regional air pollution and reduce the health burden on children.

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) affects approximately 10 to 15% of pregnancies globally and impedes a fetus from reaching its genetic growth potential (1–3). This obstetric complication remains a critical concern in global public health due to its association with perinatal mortality, stillbirths (26% in preterm and 53% in term-born infants), neonatal morbidity and mortality (3, 4), and various adult diseases, such as hypertension, cardiovascular and renal disorders, neurological impairments, metabolic dysfunction, type 2 diabetes, and cancers (5, 6). Besides maternal factors, such as inadequate or severe malnutrition, chronic diseases, multiple births, genetic factors, and lifestyle habits (7), epidemiological studies have increasingly shown that maternal exposure to ambient air pollution during pregnancy markedly affects fetal growth and birth outcomes, especially the development of FGR (8). For example, Nobles et al. evaluated the relationship between air pollution and physician-diagnosed FGR and population-based small for gestational age (SGA), and found that among seven criteria air pollutants, NO2, SO2, and PM10 contributed significantly to FGR throughout preconception and pregnancy (9).

However, due to spatiotemporal differences and detection windows, current epidemiological studies are often confined to correlation analysis and obtain inconsistent fetal growth outcomes (9–11). Given the intricate process from structural changes in fetal tissues during pregnancy to functional impairment or recovery later in life, as well as the diversity of disease phenotypes, the developmental origin of FGR remains unclear, let alone toxic characteristics and pathogenic mechanisms. Moreover, due to the limitations in accurate tracking, localization, and quantitative detection of these changes, the precise pathways for toxicity transmission from mother to fetus remain unrevealed. Therefore, it is necessary to disentangle the relationship between air pollution exposure and FGR, and to further identify suspect targets for regional risk prevention and control of specific air pollutants.

Nitrogen dioxide (NO2), a well-known precursor for the formation of fine particulate matter and ozone (12, 13), has been considered a major airborne pollutant in both outdoor and indoor environments. The primary anthropogenic source of NO2 is fossil fuel combustion, particularly from vehicles (14). Urban areas, typically characterized by heavy traffic, experience severe ambient NO2 pollution. It’s reported that the air quality in only 23% of the assessed settlements meets the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for annual mean NO2 levels (15). In China, though rigorous air pollution control measures have been implemented to reduce the emission of NO2 from fossil fuel combustion in industries and transportation, there is still severe air pollution in some specific northern regions during winter (16). Generally, urban residents spend most of their time indoors. In this case, they are potentially exposed to high levels of NO2 produced by indoor combustion processes, including cooking and smoking, which account for 30 to 40% of NO2 exposure (17). Recent epidemiological studies conducted in Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, and China have reported the association between NO2 exposure and adverse fetal growth outcomes, such as low birth weight (LBW) and SGA (11, 18–22).

In this study, we first conducted a retrospective analysis of birth records from a coal-combustion city in northern China, and then established an animal model of maternal NO2 exposure to confirm its association with FGR. By combining phenotypic detection, histological observation, and molecular mechanism analysis, we clarified how maternal NO2 exposure induced abnormal hematopoietic development in the fetus during the critical developmental windows, aiming at unveiling the maternal–fetal transmission pathway and elucidating potential epigenetic mechanisms of FGR. The findings provide strong evidence for governments and local municipalities to develop and implement strategies to control source emissions of NO2, prevent the progression of pediatric diseases, and promote the well-being of children.

Results

Retrospectively Analyses of the Association between Air Pollution and FGR.

A total of 28,796 mother–infant pairs were enrolled in this study (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), and the main characteristics of the study population are shown in SI Appendix, Table S1. The mean birth weight of the infants was 3,326.84 ± 462.69 g, with 6.20% of the infants diagnosed with SGA. Maternal age, university degree, smoking, sex of infant, and gestational diabetes mellitus exhibited no significant association with SGA (P > 0.05). However, low maternal prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) (<18.5 kg/m2), absence of occupation, low family income per person (≤4,000), lack of folic acid supplementation, first gravidity, first parity, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) increased the risk of SGA. Given the association between air pollution and adverse pregnancy events (7), especially when the average concentrations of PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 in coal-combustion areas exceed the annual air quality standards recommended by the 2021 WHO air quality guidelines (SI Appendix, Table S2), we developed logistic regression models to evaluate the cumulative effects of maternal exposure to PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, and O3 on SGA.

As shown in Table 1, after adjusting the covariates, no significant associations between these pollutants and SGA were observed during the preconception, the first and third trimesters. However, during the second trimester, maternal exposure to PM10 [adjusted odds ratio (OR) 1.028, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.013 to 1.043], PM2.5 (adjusted OR 1.037, 95% CI: 1.009 to 1.065), NO2 (adjusted OR 1.075, 95% CI: 1.020 to 1.133), and SO2 (adjusted OR 1.039, 95% CI: 1.014 to 1.065) showed significant positive associations with increased risk of SGA, while exposure to O3 (adjusted OR 0.987, 95% CI: 0.976 to 0.999) exhibited a significant negative association with SGA. These results implicated that maternal exposure to PM10, PM2.5, NO2, and SO2 during the second trimester contributed to the development of FGR. Considering the highest risk (7.5%) of SGA per 10 μg/m3 increase in NO2 concentration, this study used NO2 as an example to elucidate the possible toxicological mechanisms underlying the maternal–fetal transmission and biological responses of air pollutants contributing to FGR.

Embryonic Growth Restriction in Mice Offspring Following Maternal NO2 Exposure.

To further elucidate the impact of maternal NO2 exposure on embryonic development and validate the induction of FGR phenotype, we set up a prenatal animal exposure model and evaluated fetal growth parameters at two developmental windows. As shown in Fig. 1 A and B, maternal NO2 exposure significantly reduced fetal weight and crown-rump length at embryonic day (E) 10.5 and E14.5. The results are in line with our previous studies in which maternal NO2 exposure significantly reduced fetal weight at E18.5 (23) and neonatal weight on postnatal day 1 (PND1) (24). In addition, we observed fetal resorption specifically at E10.5 (Fig. 1C, black arrow). Though there was no significant difference in the proportion of absorbed fetuses between the control and NO2-exposed groups, the proportion showed an upward trend following maternal exposure (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). These findings indicate that maternal NO2 exposure during pregnancy contributed to FGR, which is consistent with the epidemiological results obtained above.

Surprisingly, we found discernible pallor in embryos at E10.5, particularly in three essential hematopoietic organs, including the yolk sac (YS, *), aorta–gonad–mesonephros (AGM, black circle) (Fig. 1C), and fetal liver (FL, red circle) (Fig. 1D), suggesting that maternal NO2 exposure might disrupt the development of embryonic hematopoiesis, thereby affecting the growth and development of fetuses, ultimately leading to FGR.

Abnormal Embryonic Hematopoiesis in Response to Growth Restriction.

Hematopoiesis is a dynamic and highly tuned process with strong temporal and spatial specificity. In mice, the YS is the first hematopoietic site for primitive hematopoiesis starting at E7.5 and prodefinitive hematopoiesis starting at E8.25 (25). Before the formation of blood circulation at E10.5, the YS serves as the primary hematopoietic organ and generates erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMP) which maintain transient multilineage hematopoiesis and differentiation (25) (Fig. 2A). To investigate the influences of maternal NO2 exposure on embryonic hematopoietic development, we first observed the morphological and functional development of the YS at E10.5. As shown in Fig. 2B, the YS in the control group had readily visible blood vessels, while vascular branching decreased in the NO2-exposed group. In addition, the o-dianisidine staining showed a considerable reduction of hemoglobin (Hb) levels in the NO2-exposed group at E10.5, reflecting a decrease in erythropoiesis. Furthermore, we assessed the differentiation potential of EMPs in the YS by colony-forming unit (CFU) culture assay. As shown in Fig. 2C, the number of colony-forming unit-granulocyte and macrophage (CFU-GM) was markedly reduced in the NO2-exposed group, but not burst-forming unit-erythroid (BFU-E), suggesting that maternal NO2 exposure did not affect the differentiation of EMPs into erythrocytes but impaired their differentiation into myeloid cells.

Following the establishment of blood circulation, EMPs originating from the YS and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) arising from the AGM region begin migrating to the FL at E12.5, where EMPs and HSCs continue to proliferate and differentiate until they reach a peak at E14.5 (25, 26). During this period, the FL gradually becomes the major hematopoietic organ. Therefore, we further determined the hematopoietic development in the FL at E14.5. Here, we observed that the liver-to-body weight ratio decreased significantly in the NO2-exposed group (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, we detected the migration of EMPs and HSCs to the FL at E14.5 using the immunohistochemical staining of c-kit [a marker of EMPs and HSCs (27, 28)], and observed a significant reduction in the number of c-kit+ cells (Fig. 2E), suggesting that maternal NO2 exposure might reduce the number of embryonic EMPs and HSCs in the FL. Then, we performed in vitro CFU assays of FL cells to detect the differentiation potential of HSCs and EMPs, and found that the number of CFU-GM colonies significantly declined in the NO2-exposed group (Fig. 2F), which is similar to the YS. Since macrophages play an important role in promoting erythroid maturation, a process of transition from nucleated erythrocytes to nonnucleated erythrocytes (29), we further evaluated the effects of maternal NO2 exposure on erythroid maturation in the FL by observing Wright-Giemsa stained peripheral blood smears. The results indicate that maternal NO2 exposure remarkably decreased the ratio of enucleated erythrocytes (mature erythrocytes) to nucleated erythrocytes (immature erythrocytes, indicated by white arrow) (Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis revealed significantly decreased mRNA level of β-globin specifically expressed by definitive erythroid cells (Fig. 2H), suggesting that maternal NO2 exposure caused erythroid disorders. Taken together, these findings suggest that maternal NO2 exposure affected the differentiation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and inhibited the maturation of embryonic erythroid cells, thereby contributing to FGR.

Transcriptional Alterations Associated with Abnormal Embryonic Hematopoiesis.

The FL is the major hematopoietic organ during embryogenesis (30). To clarify the potential gene regulation associated with embryonic hematopoiesis in response to maternal NO2 exposure, we analyzed the transcriptional profile of the FL at E14.5 and identified a total of 370 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (P < 0.05, |fold change| > 1.5) (Fig. 3A, SI Appendix, Fig. S3, and Dataset S1). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (|NES| > 1.5, q < 0.05, Fig. 3B) showed that three proinflammatory signaling pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway, NF-kappaB signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway were significantly enriched. Among the 6 biological processes in Gene Ontology (GO) analyses, DEGs were enriched in two proinflammation- and immunity-related terms, including T cell activation (GO:0042110) and response to interleukin-17 (GO:0097396) (Fig. 3C and Dataset S2). In addition, consistent with GSEA, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation (Fig. 3D and Dataset S3) also confirmed that two proinflammatory signaling pathways (the TNF signaling pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway) were significantly enriched. Since the proinflammatory signaling pathways are required to establish the hematopoietic system during embryogenesis (30), these significantly enriched pathways are most likely to be involved in abnormal embryonic hematopoiesis caused by maternal NO2 exposure.

Based on the GSEA and KEGG analysis above, a total of 12 DEGs were involved in these two coenriched pathways (the TNF signaling pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway), 10 of which were down-regulated after maternal NO2 exposure (Fig. 3 E and F). qRT-PCR further validated the downregulation of these DEGs, except Fos in the FL (Fig. 3G). Notably, 83% of these genes in the YS displayed a similar expression trend to those observed in the FL (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Since proinflammatory cytokines play a crucial regulatory role in embryonic hematopoiesis (31), these down-regulated genes associated with the proinflammatory signaling pathways might play an important role in maternal NO2 exposure-induced erythroid disorders.

Long Noncoding RNA (LncRNA) Regulation of DEGs from Transcriptional Alterations.

LncRNAs play a crucial role in coordinating the development of erythrocytes (32). To clarify the potential lncRNA regulation of genes involved in abnormal embryonic hematopoiesis, we compared the lncRNA expression profile of the FL between the control and NO2-exposed groups at E14.5, and identified 91 differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) (q < 0.05) (Fig. 3A, SI Appendix, Fig. S5A, and Dataset S4). Then, we performed a coexpression analysis of these DElncRNAs with DEGs to elucidate their biological functions and uncovered 2,294 significantly coexpressed lncRNA-mRNA pairs (P < 0.01, |r| > 0.9, Dataset S5). Subsequently, GO and KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these DElncRNAs were primarily involved in terms including response to interleukin-17 (GO: 0097396), TNF signaling pathway (mmu04668), and IL-17 signaling pathway (mmu04657) (FDR < 0.05, SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and C). In accordance with those obtained from the mRNA expression profile, these results suggest that lncRNAs play a significant role in maternal NO2 exposure-induced abnormal fetal hematopoiesis by mediating the proinflammatory signaling pathways.

Various stimuli, such as TNF, IL-17, and IL-1β, can activate the canonical NF-κB pathway by transducing signals through specific cell surface receptors (cytokine receptors and the TNF receptor superfamily) to mediate proinflammatory responses (Fig. 4A) (33, 34). The GSEA of the TNF signaling pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway showed that the entire set of core genes, comprising 10 and 5 DEGs, respectively, were significantly down-regulated in the NO2-exposed group (Fig. 4B). Given the crucial role of proinflammatory cytokines in the development and differentiation of hematopoietic cells, the downregulation of these genes is of great significance. To determine lncRNAs modulating the expression of these genes, we conducted a coexpression analysis of DElncRNAs with these mRNAs and identified 52 significantly coexpressed lncRNA-mRNA pairs (P < 0.01, |r| > 0.9, Dataset S6). Notably, the top four lncRNAs that were coexpressed with the highest number of mRNAs were NONMMUT109311.1, NONMMUT025168.2, NONMMUT044528.2, and NONMMUT053442.2, and they were coexpressed with Cxcl2, Il1b, Cxcl10, Cxcl1, Ccl2, Icam1, Bcl3, Socs3, and Fos (Fig. 4C). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis of the YS at E10.5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) and the FL at E14.5 (Fig. 4D) confirmed the reduced expression of NONMMU T044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2 during hematopoietic development after maternal NO2 exposure.

To further explore the potential transcriptional regulation of gene expression by lncRNAs, we used ChIP-Atlas, a public bioinformatics resource (35, 36), to identify transcription factors (TFs) of their coexpressed mRNAs. Among the predicted 43 TFs, CCAAT/enhancer binding protein alpha (C/EBPα) was found to bind to all nine coexpressed genes (Fig. 4E). Chip-seq results showed that C/EBPα bound to the promoter regions of the top three hub genes (Il1b, Ccl2, and Cxcl10) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), and their interactions were further observed by the protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis (confidence level > 0.9) (Fig. 4F). Specifically, JASPAR database analyses showed that C/EBPα had 2, 2, and 1 binding sites to the Il1b, Ccl2, and Cxcl10 promoter regions, respectively (Fig. 4G), which was further confirmed by the ChIP analysis of the FL (Fig. 4H). Next, we used RPISeq to predict potential combinations of NONMMUT044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2 with C/EBPα. The results indicated a strong interaction between them, as evidenced by high scores predicted by both the Random Forests (RF) Classifier and Support Vector Machine (SVM) Classifier (Fig. 4I). This was further verified by the RIP experiment in the FL at E14.5, showing that NONMMUT044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2 specifically bound to C/EBPα (Fig. 4J). These results demonstrated that two lncRNAs (NONMMUT044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2) potentially regulated the expression of genes involved in impaired embryonic hematopoiesis through C/EBPα-mediated inflammatory signaling following maternal NO2 exposure.

LncRNA Responses from Maternal–Fetal Hypoxia Transmission.

To investigate the possible maternal–fetal transmission pathways by which maternal NO2 exposure induced abnormal embryonic hematopoiesis, we first measured the concentrations of nitrate (NO3−) and nitrite (NO2−), two major products of inhaled NO2 in maternal blood, and observed that the exposure markedly increased their levels at E14.5, especially the NO3− level (Fig. 5A). Moreover, we used 15NO2 to track the distribution of 15N, and found that the ratio of 15NO3− to total NO3− in maternal blood increased by 19.72%, 34.96%, and 30.06% at 2, 5, and 8 h following 15NO2 exposure at E14.5, respectively, reaching a peak at 5 h (Fig. 5B). It’s reported that NO2− in the blood can oxidize ferrous ion (Fe2+) in Hb to ferric iron (Fe3+), convert Hb to methemoglobin (MetHb), and reduce the oxygen-carrying capacity of Hb, thereby triggering hypoxia (37). Therefore, we further determined whether NO2 exposure affected MetHb levels in maternal blood. The result indicated that NO2 exposure markedly raised the level of MetHb (Fig. 5C), but did not affect the level of Hb and the numbers of white blood cells, red blood cells, and platelets (Fig. 5D), suggesting that maternal NO2 exposure resulted in acquired methemoglobinemia in pregnant mice but did not decrease Hb production through its by-products.

The placenta serves as the site of exchange between mother and fetus, where the fetus receives nutrients and O2 from the mother’s blood and expels metabolites and CO2 (38). The pimonidazole adducts staining revealed that the positive areas in the placenta labyrinthine (Fig. 5E), a symptom of placental hypoxia, significantly increased following maternal NO2 exposure. Then, we further investigated whether maternal hypoxia permeated fetal tissues, and found a significant hypoxia phenotype in the FL following maternal NO2 exposure (Fig. 5F), together with a significant upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (Hif1α) and Hif1β (Fig. 5G).

Since aberrant expression of lncRNAs is attributed to the hypoxic microenvironment (39), we hypothesized that NONMMUT044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2 might be hypoxia-responsive lncRNAs, particularly regulated by HIF-1α and HIF-1β. Predictions from the JASPAR database unveiled that HIF-1α had no binding sites to these two lncRNAs, but HIF-1β had two binding sites for the NONMMUT044528.2 promoter region with scores of 10.35 (#1) and 8.61 (#2) and two binding sites for the NONMMUT053442.2 promoter region with scores of 10.35 (#1) and 10.35 (#2) (Fig. 5H). ChIP assays confirmed the occupancy of HIF-1β in the upstream region of the NONMMUT044528.2 TSS #1 and NONMMUT0 53442.2 TSS #2 in the FL (Fig. 5I). In addition, with increasing expression of HIF-1β following maternal NO2 exposure, the expression of these two lncRNAs decreased, suggesting that HIF-1β might function as a transcriptional repressor for NONMMUT044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2. These results suggest that maternal NO2 exposure induced acquired methemoglobinemia and triggered a maternal–fetal hypoxia transmission, which in turn caused abnormal erythrocyte maturation through HIF-1β-lncRNAs (NONMMUT 044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2)- C/EBPα-proinflammatory signals in the FL.

Based on these findings, we proposed a possible toxicological mechanism underlying FGR caused by maternal NO2 exposure (Fig. 6). Briefly, NO2 exposure induces acquired methemoglobinemia through its by-products in pregnant mice, leading to hypoxia within the placenta and fetal hematopoietic organs. Then, hypoxia triggers a dysregulation of the HIF-1β-lncRNAs-C/EBPα-proinflammation axis, resulting in fetal hematopoietic dysfunction and ultimately contributing to FGR progression.

Discussion

The adverse effects of air pollution on fetal growth have been gradually recognized by researchers, even obstetricians. However, due to uncertainties in exposure assessment, confounding factors, selection biases, and different epidemiological models, recent studies yield inconsistent conclusions for specific pollutants. As for NO2, Lepeule et al. found a link between maternal NO2 exposure and low birth weight in France (40), and Stieb et al. revealed an association between NO2 exposure and SGA and term LBW in Canada (41). However, Laurent et al. found that term LBW is not significantly associated with NO2 in California (10), and Mariet et al. observed no association between NO2 and SGA in multiple pregnancies (11). In China, a retrospective cohort study conducted in Chongqing reveals that NO2 exposure during the first and second trimesters exhibits a significant relation to very low birth weight (18). Consistently, our retrospective analysis confirmed the correlation between maternal NO2 exposure during the second trimester and SGA. Importantly, we demonstrated the population-based results by setting up an animal exposure model and further elucidated the mechanisms underlying abnormal fetal development.

FGR, a condition linked to short- and long-term fetal morbidity, often coincides with placental insufficiency and is triggered by reduced placental blood flow, hypoxemia, and maternal undernutrition (42). Animal models have shed light on potential mechanisms contributing to FGR, such as alterations in placental volume and blood flow (43), epigenetic changes in placental and fetal tissues (44, 45), and changes in placental mitochondrial content (46). As a typical gaseous pollutant, upon inhalation, NO2 or its by-products can be retained longer within the lung and disseminated to extrapulmonary sites via the bloodstream (47). In the blood, NO2− and NO3− achieve their peak concentrations within 1 h of NO2 exposure and return to baseline levels within 1.5 to 2 h after exposure (48). To improve our preliminary study (23), here, we used ion chromatography and observed a significant increase in NO3− and NO2− levels in maternal blood at E14.5 following NO2 exposure, accompanied with elevated 15N-labeled nitrate levels. Notably, NO2− can mediate the oxidation of Fe2+ in Hb to Fe3+, resulting in the formation of MetHb (37). The increased MetHb levels, along with unchanged red and white blood cell counts in maternal blood following NO2 exposure, suggest that inhaled NO2 during pregnancy served as the source of exogenous nitrite and nitrate nitrogen, leading to the formation of MetHb and acquired methemoglobinemia.

In MetHb, the presence of Fe3+ induces allosteric modifications of Hb that promote irreversible oxygen binding and increase the affinity of Fe2+ for oxygen, thereby impeding the release of oxygen to tissues and resulting in hypoxia (49). Our study revealed that the placenta and FL exhibited hypoxia phenotype following maternal NO2 exposure, primarily evidenced by the increased positive areas in the placenta labyrinthine and significantly up-regulated expression of Hif1β at E14.5. Given the role of the placenta in providing oxygen and nutrients to the fetus and the contribution of placental hypoxia to the development of FGR (50), the aforementioned findings strongly suggest that exogenous NO2− and NO3− might be the critical factor for maternal NO2 exposure-induced FGR by causing acquired methemoglobinemia and inducing the symptoms of hypoxia which were transmitted from the placenta to fetal tissues.

Pallor is a characteristic clinical symptom of methemoglobinemia (51). Interestingly, we found that maternal NO2 exposure induced pallor in fetal organs, particularly in hematopoietic organs, such as the YS, AGM, and FL. During mouse embryogenesis, the YS is the first tissue to produce hematopoietic cells, and definitive erythroid, myeloid, and multipotential progenitors also originate at this site (25). Our present study revealed that maternal NO2 exposure decreased Hb levels and the number of myeloid colony in the YS at E10.5. The FL is another anatomical location for hematopoietic development, particularly for the expansion and differentiation of HSCs during early fetal life (52). Notably, we demonstrated that maternal NO2 exposure substantially decreased the population of embryonic EMPs and HSCs in the FL, reduced the differentiation potential of HSCs to myeloid cells, decreased the production of definitive erythroid cells, and inhibited the maturation of embryonic erythrocytes at E14.5. The FL circulation is closely related to fetal growth, and FGR is prevalent in fetuses with intrahepatic Umbilical-Porto-Systemic Venous Shunt (IHUPSVS) which leads to a decrease in the umbilical blood flow to the liver (53). Here, we found that maternal NO2 exposure led to hematopoietic dysfunction, especially abnormal FL hematopoiesis, which might be critical for FGR progression. Importantly, the developmental stages between E12 and E16 in mice correspond to the second trimester in humans (54), and the human FL becomes the major hematopoietic organ from gestational weeks 10 to 16 (55). These results indicate that maternal NO2 exposure initially caused maternal methemoglobinemia, which triggered hypoxia in the placenta and fetus and caused abnormal hematopoietic development in the YS and FL, finally leading to FGR. Especially, these results also indicate that the second trimester of pregnancy might be a sensitive developmental window for maternal NO2 exposure-induced FGR.

Then, we further explored possible mechanisms underlying abnormal HSPC differentiation and erythroid maturation. We found that DEGs in the FL were primarily involved in two proinflammatory signaling pathways, i.e., the TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways. Moreover, the hub genes (such as Cxcl2, Il1b, and Ccl2) were significantly down-regulated in both YS and FL following maternal NO2 exposure. Inflammation plays a critical role in activating HSPCs to proliferate and differentiate, signaling the specification of embryonic HSPCs, and maintaining adult HSPC homeostasis (56). In detail, proinflammatory mediators, including TNFα, IFNγ, IL-1β, PGE2, TLR4, and other chemokines, can regulate the specification, emergence, maintenance, and maturation of HSCs through the activation of NF-κB, Notch, and Jak/Stat1 (31, 56). Here, the downregulation of genes associated with TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways further supported that the deficiency of proinflammatory signals in hematopoietic organs might be a critical factor for impaired embryonic hematopoiesis. It is widely accepted that during embryonic development, the specification of definitive HSCs is facilitated by inflammatory signals primarily from myeloid cells (56). Among them, macrophages play a key role in the production of red blood cells by binding to erythroblasts, stimulating the maturation and enucleation of erythroblasts, and facilitating the clearance of dead cells (57, 58). Here, our results revealed that maternal NO2 exposure significantly reduced the differentiation of hematopoietic stem progenitor cells to myeloid cell populations in the YS and FL, thereby inhibiting erythroid maturation.

Growing evidence elucidates the critical involvement of lncRNAs in regulating the expression of genes associated with fetal hematopoiesis and mediating HSC differentiation (59). Moreover, lncRNAs also regulate innate immune and inflammatory responses and play an important role in myeloid and lymphatic function (60, 61). Our results identified a total of 91 significantly differentially expressed lncRNAs in the FL after maternal NO2 exposure. Among them, lncRNAs NONMMUT044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2 were significantly down-regulated and coexpressed with most of the genes involved in the proinflammatory signaling pathways. Specifically, they interacted with C/EBPα and inhibited the expression of three downstream genes (Cxcl2, Il1b, and Ccl2). It’s reported that aberrant expression of lncRNAs can be attributed to hypoxic microenvironment (39). HIF-1 can regulate hypoxia-responsive lncRNAs under hypoxic conditions, and alterations in the expression of lncRNAs can regulate the production of TFs and signal transduction (39, 62). In addition to hypoxia phenotype in the FL and increased Hif1β expression following maternal NO2 exposure, we further confirmed the interaction between HIF-1β and NONMMU T044528.2 and NONMMUT053442.2, suggesting that these two lncRNAs were down-regulated by activating HIF-1β in response to fetal hypoxia, consequently contributing to hematopoietic disorders and FGR by binding to C/EBPα and regulating its downstream genes.

In summary, we epidemiologically and toxicologically demonstrated that maternal NO2 exposure increased the risk of FGR, with the second trimester of pregnancy as the critical window. Mechanistically, we clarified that NO2 inhalation induced maternal methemoglobinemia and placenta-fetal hypoxia transmission, and fetal hypoxia further resulted in abnormal hematopoiesis through the HIF-1β-lncRNAs-C/EBPα-proinflammatory signaling pathway, ultimately contributing to FGR progression. This work provides experimental evidence for an increased risk of FGR following maternal NO2 exposure, and reveals that lncRNAs can serve as specific molecular targets for both risk prevention and population exposure intervention in clinical practice. Moreover, this work presents insights for local governments and municipalities to control regional air pollutants and reduce the health burden on children. However, some limitations need to be addressed in the future: 1) Population-based epidemiological evidence is still needed to elucidate the potential of lncRNAs-modulated proinflammation as the target for clinical implication. 2) The potential contribution of other pollutants, such as PM2.5, PM10, and SO2, should be considered.
