Development and validation of a frailty assessment model for elderly patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis
Authors: Yu Zhao, Ying Gao, Yi Li, Ya Tuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The number of elderly patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) in China is constantly increasing, with an upward trend. Meanwhile, the frailty of elderly patients undergoing MHD is closely associated with decreased quality of life, hospitalization, falls, disability, and mortality. To explore the influencing factors of frailty in elderly patients undergoing MHD, construct a frailty diagnosis model, and validate its efficacy, in order to provide a reference for clinical nursing practice. A total of 296 elderly patients receiving MHD treatment at 2 tertiary Class A hospitals in Hohhot from June to November 2024 were selected as research subjects. The basic inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥60 years who have undergone regular dialysis for ≥3 months. This model performed a classification diagnostic task, namely, using readily available clinical indicators to determine whether a current state of frailty exists. Patient assessments were conducted using the General Information Questionnaire, Charlson comorbidity index, Edmonton Frailty Scale, Social Support Rating Scale, Barthel index, Nutritional Risk Screening (Nutritional Risk Screening Tool 2002), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and Brief Depression Scale for the Elderly (Geriatric Depression Scale-15). Univariate analysis and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression analysis were employed to identify risk factors for frailty in elderly MHD patients, followed by multivariate logistic regression modeling to establish a frailty diagnosis model. Among the 296 elderly MHD patients, 189 cases (63.9%) exhibited frailty. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression analysis revealed that hemoglobin levels, serum albumin levels, serum creatinine levels, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Nutritional Risk Screening (Nutritional Risk Screening Tool 2002), Brief Depression Scale for the Elderly (Geriatric Depression Scale-15), Social Support Rating Scale, and Barthel index were independent influencing factors for frailty in elderly MHD patients. The model achieved an area under the curve of 0.899 (95% confidence interval: 0.855–0.942) with an optimal cutoff value of 0.719, demonstrating 89.6% sensitivity, 75.3% specificity, and a maximum Youden index of 0.672. External validation further confirmed the model’s robustness. The frailty diagnosis model for elderly patients undergoing MHD constructed in this study has good discriminatory efficacy and has certain clinical application value.

1. Introduction

End-stage renal disease occurs when chronic kidney disease progresses to its final phase, resulting in irreversible kidney damage that disrupts the body’s electrolyte, fluid, and metabolic balance.[1] Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), the primary clinical treatment for end-stage renal disease, not only filters out harmful substances from the bloodstream but also plays a vital role in supporting organ function and maintaining internal homeostasis, making it essential for kidney disease patients.[2] Frailty is an age-related syndrome characterized by diminished physiological reserves and multi-system dysfunction, particularly in the elderly, which increases physical vulnerability.[3] The frailty status in MHD patients is closely associated with reduced quality of life, hospitalization, falls, disability, and mortality rates,[4–6] acting as a significant independent factor.[7] Among hemodialysis patients, the 65 to 74 age group accounts for the highest proportion at 90.31%, with an increasing trend that highlights the unique frailty characteristics of elderly MHD patients.[8] The prevalence of frailty in long-term dialysis patients ranges from 14% to 73%, significantly higher than in other disease populations.[9–12] However, current research mainly focuses on the vascular access conditions and dialysis complications of the overall hemodialysis population, with little attention paid to the rapidly growing group of elderly MHD patients who are likely to be highly frail. Moreover, most existing studies conduct general screening for all patients, while targeted assessment of the frailty status of elderly MHD patients is relatively scarce. Therefore, based on the model previously established by others that covers all populations, including young and middle-aged individuals, this study develops a visual nomogram diagnostic model specifically focusing on the elderly MHD population in China and verifies it. It explores the relevant influencing factors of frailty in this group and provides a simple and objective assessment tool for clinical early intervention of the frailty status of elderly MHD patients, with the aim of comprehensively and timely improving the physical condition of patients.

2. Methods

2.1. Research subjects

2.1.1. Research object

Using convenience sampling, this study recruited MHD patients from 2 grade III-A hospitals (hospital A and hospital B) in Hohhot City between June and November 2024. Participants were divided into a training set and a test set at a 7:3 ratio. The criterion for handling missing data was questionnaires with an item missing rate exceeding 10% were deemed invalid and excluded from the analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hohhot First Hospital (approval number: IRB2024330). All participants and their family members signed the informed consent form and voluntarily participated in this study.

2.1.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for patients are age ≥60 years; meeting the diagnostic criteria for end-stage renal disease as outlined in the “Guidelines for Screening, Diagnosis, and Prevention of Chronic Kidney Disease”[13]; having received regular dialysis for ≥3 months; maintaining clear consciousness and cognitive function; and being able to complete the questionnaire with assistance from the investigator. Exclusion criteria are patients with acute life-threatening conditions such as cerebrovascular accidents or severe arrhythmias, and those with other malignancies or recurrent cases.

2.1.3. Sample size estimation

Based on the literature review and expert consultation, this study anticipated fewer than 10 factors to be included in the model. Following Riley et al’s sample size calculation method for model development,[14] the required sample size should be at least 10 times the number of independent variables. Meta-analysis results indicated that frailty prevalence in elderly MHD patients was approximately 60%. With 10% of invalid samples excluded, the calculated sample size was 10 × 10 ÷ 60% ÷ (1–10%) = 185 cases. Ultimately, 296 patients were included, divided into a training set and a test set in a 7:3 ratio.

2.2. Frailty criteria and classification

Patients were assessed using the Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS) and then grouped according to their scores. This scale was developed by Rolfson et al in 2006[15] and later translated into Chinese by Yang Liu, Bao Lihua, et al in 2016 to evaluate whether elderly patients (aged ≥60 years) in China were in a frail state.[16] Based on existing research in China’s elderly population, patients with EFS scores ranging from 0 to 5 were classified as non-frail groups, while those scoring ≥6 were categorized as frail groups.[17] The Cronbach alpha coefficient of the Chinese version of the EFS scale was 0.780, and in this study, the Cronbach alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.832.

2.3. Survey tools

2.3.1. General information questionnaire

Based on the literature review,[18–26] the possible risk factors for frailty in elderly patients undergoing MHD were summarized. Combined with expert opinions and clinical practice situations, a general information questionnaire for this study was independently compiled, which was divided into 3 dimensions: basic information, health information, and laboratory indicators. Basic information: age, gender, employment status, educational level, marital status, average monthly family income per person, living alone, self-assessed health condition, and exercise habits. Health status: body mass index (BMI), duration of dialysis, vascular access type, hemodialysis complications, smoking history, and alcohol consumption. Laboratory parameters: serum albumin, hemoglobin, serum creatinine, serum phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, and urea nitrogen clearance index.

2.3.2. Charlson comorbidity index

The Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) takes into account the patient’s age, the diseases they have, and their severity. Each item has a weight assigned to it, which is used to assess the coexistence of multiple diseases. After obtaining the patients’ informed consent, this study retrieved the patients’ comorbidity information from the hospital’s medical record system and used the Medlive system (MedLive Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to calculate the CCI score. The calculation was quick, simple, and cost-effective. The CCI index is divided into 2 scoring levels: 1 to 4 points and ≥5 points.

2.3.3. Social Support Rating Scale

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), developed by Xiao Yuan, was employed for evaluation, comprising 10 items assessed across 3 dimensions: objective support, subjective support, and support utilization.[27] The scoring system features 4 levels (12–66 points), with scores <33 indicating low social support, 33 to 45 representing average support, and >45 indicating high support. Higher scores reflect greater perceived social support. The scale demonstrated Cronbach alpha coefficients ranging from 0.825 to 0.896, with the present study’s coefficient being 0.751.

2.3.4. Barthel index

The Barthel index (developed by Dorothea Barthel and Florence Mahoney in 1965) assesses patients’ ability to perform daily activities,[28] with scoring levels as follows: level I: ≤40 points, level II: 41 to 60 points, level III: 61 to 99 points, and level IV: total score of 100 points, representing severe dependence, moderate dependence, mild dependence, and no dependence, respectively. The scale has a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.916, indicating good reliability. In this study, the Cronbach alpha coefficient was 0.865.

2.3.5. Nutritional Risk Screening Tool 2002

The Nutritional Risk Screening Tool 2002 (NRS-2002) was developed by the European Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition in 2002.[29] The total score ranges from 2 to 7 points. A score below 3 indicates normal nutrition, while a score of 3 or above indicates nutritional risk. Nutritional support care should be tailored to individual conditions.

2.3.6. Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al,[30] was employed to assess sleep quality. A comprehensive evaluation of patients’ sleep quality was conducted, with a PSQI score exceeding 7 indicating sleep disturbances. The scale demonstrated a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.796.

2.3.7. Simplified version of the Geriatric Depression Scale

Depression was assessed using the simplified version of the Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15), a streamlined adaptation of the standard GDS developed by Sheikh and Yesavage for easier administration. This version has gained widespread adoption among healthcare professionals.[31] A score of 8 or higher indicates depressive symptoms, with the Chinese version of GDS-15 demonstrating a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.793.[32]

2.4. Methods of data collection

Before the start of the research, the researchers conducted training for the investigators. Those who passed the assessment would then proceed with the investigation. General information, CCI, and so on of the patients were obtained through the electronic medical record system and the nursing record sheet. The EFS, SSRS, Barthel index, Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS-2002), PSQI, and the Brief Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) were investigated and collected using a one-on-one method.

2.5. Quality control

To ensure the completeness of the data, strict quality control is carried out: standardized training: conducted unified training for surveyors before the survey began, covering research objectives, informed consent procedures, and confidentiality commitments; consistent methodology: collected questionnaires through one-on-one interviews with standardized instructions, distributing them during designated waiting periods before and after dialysis sessions to maintain survey consistency and patient engagement; real-time verification: all surveys were conducted on-site, with immediate review of each item upon submission. Surveyors promptly communicated with patients to correct errors or complete missing entries; empathetic approach: used compassionate language and employed progressive questioning for sensitive topics (e.g., marital status, income) to build emotional trust; dual electronic entry and verification: ensured data authenticity through a 2-person verification process. During the questionnaire measurement process, the names of the research subjects will be replaced by numbers to ensure the anonymity of the patient information and to standardize the data recording.

2.6. Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk) and R software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Data meeting normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, with independent-samples t test for inter-group comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians and quartiles, with the Mann–Whitney U test for inter-group comparisons. Count data were described as frequencies and percentages, with the chi-square test or the Fisher exact probability method selected for inter-group comparisons.

Variable selection and model construction: the occurrence of frailty in elderly MHD patients was designated as the dependent variable. Initially, univariate analysis was employed to perform preliminary dimensionality reduction on a broad set of candidate variables. Subsequently, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied to identify independent risk factors. A final multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to establish a diagnostic tool for frailty in elderly MHD patients.

Internal validation and optimism correction: to evaluate and correct for potential optimistic bias generated by the aforementioned modeling procedures on the training dataset, bootstrap-based internal validation was performed on the complete training set. The specific methodology entailed 1000 bootstrap resamples, with the full workflow – “LASSO variable selection → logistic regression fitting” – replicated entirely within each resampled dataset. By comparing model performance between bootstrap samples and the original training set, an optimism estimate for each performance metric was computed. Ultimately, the discrimination and calibration metrics reported for the training set were bootstrap-corrected to provide a more reliable reflection of internal validity.

Test set validation: to assess the model’s generalizability, a holdout validation approach was adopted. The entire dataset was randomly divided into a training set and an independent test set at a 7:3 ratio. The test set was strictly withheld during all variable selection and model-building phases and did not participate in any modeling steps. Its sole purpose was to apply the preestablished model (trained solely on the training set) to evaluate discrimination (receiver operator characteristic curve [ROC] curve and area under the curve [AUC]), calibration (calibration curve, calibration intercept and slope, Hosmer-Lemeshow test), and clinical utility (decision curve analysis [DCA]) in new samples.

External validation: to further verify the model’s extrapolation potential, validation was conducted on a completely independent external cohort, with corresponding performance metrics reported. All statistical tests were conducted with a significance level of P < .05, indicating that the differences were statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Comparison of general clinical data

A total of 310 questionnaires were distributed at the beginning of the study. Among them, 14 were judged as invalid and excluded for the following reasons: contradictory information in the questionnaire (e.g., in the “Marital Status” item of the general information scale, the selecting “widowed,” but in the “Support and Care Received from Family Members” item of the SSRS, the option “spouse” was chosen); questionnaires with no significant differences in responses (for example, presenting as giving systematic and fixed responses on all items, such as choosing the “①” option on all items); and incomplete filling of the questionnaire information with a completeness rate of <90%. To ensure the reliability and completeness of the analysis data, any questionnaire that meets either of these criteria will be completely excluded. The data preprocessing (removal of invalid questionnaires) is completed before the data set is divided. Finally, 296 valid questionnaires were received, with an effective rate of 95.5%. Among the 296 patients meeting the criteria, 152 were male (51.35%), 144 were female (48.65%), and the average age was around 67 years old. The incidence of frailty in the training set was 65.7%, and the incidence in the test set was 59.6%.

When comparing the training set patients with the test set patients, no statistically significant differences were observed in age, BMI, employment status, educational level, marital status, living alone, alcohol consumption history, self-reported health status, physical activity, household monthly income, dialysis duration, vascular access, hypotension during dialysis, hemoglobin, serum albumin, urea nitrogen clearance index, serum phosphorus, serum creatinine, CCI, SSRS, Barthel index, Nutritional Risk Screening Score (NRS-2002), PSQI, and Simplified Version of the Geriatric Depression Scale (P > .05). However, gender (P = .027) and smoking history (P = .049) showed statistically significant differences, with Cramer V values of 0.128 and 0.114, respectively (see Table 1).

3.3. LASSO regression analysis to identify independent risk factors for frailty in elderly patients undergoing MHD

In the LASSO regression analysis, factors with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were selected as independent variables, with frailty occurrence in elderly MHD patients as the dependent variable. The λ selection process through 10-fold cross-validation is shown in Figure 1. The λ value with one standard error from the minimum mean square error (representing the most parsimonious model) was chosen. Based on this, the following variables were screened by LASSO regression (when λ = 0.0303) for subsequent modeling analysis: hemoglobin level, serum albumin level, serum creatinine, CCI, SSRS, Barthel index, Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS-2002), and Brief Depression Scale for the Elderly (GDS-15), as illustrated in Figure 2.

3.4. Construction and internal validation of a frailty diagnostic model for elderly MHD patients

The 8 independent risk factors were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The consistency evaluation used the Hosmer-Lemeshow test with χ2 = 7.99, P = .43 > 0.05; the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the model was 0.899 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.855–0.942), as shown in Figure 4. This model can well distinguish elderly MHD frail patients from non-frail patients, and the model has strong discrimination ability on the training set. At the optimal cutoff value of 0.719, the model’s sensitivity was 89.6%, specificity was 75.3%, and the maximum Youden index was 0.672. The corrected curve of the model (intercept = 0.000, slope = 1.000) generally trended in line with the ideal curve, indicating that the model’s goodness of fit on the training set was good, as shown in Figure 5. It should be noted that the reported calibration intercept and slope for the training set are the optimism-corrected values obtained via bootstrap resampling. As the training set serves as the reference for model fitting, the slope remains at 1 after correction, which is an inherent property of the bootstrap procedure and does not indicate overfitting. The overall calibration validity of the model was further confirmed by the aforementioned Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

4. Discussion

4.1. High incidence of frailty in elderly MHD patients

This study reported a frailty prevalence of 63.9% among elderly MHD patients, consistent with previous findings. In contrast, the prevalence among general adult MHD patients was 50%, significantly lower than that of the elderly patient group in this study.[34] The variation in frailty prevalence among elderly MHD patients may stem from differences in assessment tools, age group limitations, regional healthcare disparities, and lifestyle variations. These patients typically experience advanced chronic kidney disease with reduced glomerular filtration rate, severe metabolic disturbances, and toxin accumulation – pathological conditions that further increase frailty risk. Early identification of frailty enables clinicians to detect potential multi-system dysfunction and heightened susceptibility to stress factors in elderly patients. The prevalence of frailty rises with age, reflecting the heavier disease burden experienced by older adults. Therefore, clinicians should recognize the particularities of elderly patients when addressing vulnerable groups and conduct regular screenings for those of advanced age.

4.1.1. Regular screening of frailty occurrence in elderly MHD patients

DCA is used to evaluate the clinical net benefits of the model at different decision thresholds. In the internal validation, the model consistently provided stable net clinical benefits within a wide range of thresholds from 10% to 15%, indicating its good potential for clinical application. In the external validation, this effective threshold was further confirmed: when the threshold exceeded 13%, using the model to guide decision-making showed clear net benefits. Based on the results of both internal and external validations (with 13% falling within the 10%–15% range), 13% can be regarded as a more accurate and reliable clinical operational threshold after both internal and external verification. In clinical decision-making, this model is applicable for screening out elderly MHD patients with a relatively high probability of frailty (over 13%) and conducting more targeted frailty management. Based on this, in clinical practice, 13% can be used as the decision boundary point to regularly (e.g., every quarter or every half year) apply this nomogram to screen elderly MHD patients, identifying patients in a frail state, and for frail patients, an individualized management plan can be initiated, while for patients without frailty, routine health guidance can be provided and regular screening can continue.

Stratified management helps achieve efficient initial diversion of the workflow, concentrating valuable clinical resources and attention on the groups that can benefit most from the intervention and are truly in a frail state, thereby reasonably allocating medical resources and improving patient quality of life.

4.2. Independent risk factors for frailty in elderly MHD patients

4.2.1. Nutrient-related proteins and nutrient risk scores

The study demonstrated that hemoglobin (OR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.02–0.67, P < .05), serum albumin levels (OR = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.01–0.19, P < .05), and nutritional risk scores (NRS-2002; OR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.26–3.39, P < .05) were all independent risk factors for frailty in elderly patients undergoing MHD, collectively reflecting the impact of nutritional imbalance on the progression of frailty.

Low hemoglobin levels impair the body’s oxygen-carrying capacity, causing prolonged oxygen deprivation in organs and tissues. Patients develop persistent fatigue that cannot be relieved by rest, accompanied by declining physical performance that progressively leads to frailty. These findings align with earlier studies,[35,36] further underscoring the clinical importance of timely anemia correction. Therefore, in clinical practice, it is necessary to manage the patient’s hemoglobin level based on their specific condition in order to prevent and delay the occurrence of frailty.

Serum albumin serves as a key indicator of nutritional status in patients. Reduced serum albumin levels can exacerbate frailty through multiple mechanisms, consistent with previous research findings.[37] Globally, 23% to 54% of MHD patients experience malnutrition,[38] which is a primary modifiable factor contributing to frailty. Comorbid conditions such as medication side effects, inflammation, and diabetes history can all reduce appetite in elderly MHD patients.[39] Nutritional risks are further intensified by nutrient loss during dialysis and dietary restrictions. Elevated nutritional risk scores (NRS-2002) not only accelerate skeletal muscle wasting and atrophy but also compromise immune function, increasing infection risks. Given that the energy and protein intake of hemodialysis patients on dialysis days is often lower than that on non-dialysis days, the diet of these patients can be adjusted accordingly, for example, by including high-protein foods.[40]

4.2.2. CCI and physical activity capacity

Consistent with domestic and international research, comorbidities are the core contributing factors to frailty in elderly patients with MHD.[41] The cumulative effects of systemic functional impairments significantly weaken the body’s ability to maintain homeostasis. This pathological state of comorbidities increases the possibility of frailty, aligning with the concept of deficit accumulation in frailty.[42] Improving patient care experiences involves establishing dynamic condition monitoring plans to track key comorbidity indicators such as blood glucose and cardiovascular function while developing personalized health education systems. Out-of-hospital monitoring through information and communication technologies for remote patient care is increasingly proven to be an effective approach that meets healthcare demands while providing patient-centered services.[43] Utilizing telemedicine to facilitate self-assessment of symptoms can enhance patients’ management of comorbidities and potentially overcome established home care barriers, thereby improving overall care experiences.

The decline in physical activity capacity indirectly indicates that patients may be in a state of frailty, which is consistent with previous research findings.[44] Most patients in this study reported experiencing significant physical fatigue and frailty after dialysis treatment, requiring home rest for the day. As elderly MHD patients age, their bodily functions gradually deteriorate, leading to reduced physical fitness and mobility. This decline further affects muscle mass and strength, impairing daily living abilities and increasing the possibility of frailty.[45] Clinical work can provide elderly patients with exercise rehabilitation recommendations in order to reduce inflammatory markers, improve dialysis adequacy, improve physical activity level, and reduce patient fatigue and frailty.[46] A multicenter study suggests that implementing exercise rehabilitation programs can alleviate symptom burden in hemodialysis patients. During the initial 26-week program, exercise intensity and duration are monitored and personalized by sports specialists based on patients’ baseline physical functions, which can improve common symptoms and enhance quality of life.[47] A patient exercise log tracking system can be established during this process, using wearable devices to monitor adherence and dynamically adjust exercise plans.

4.2.3. Social support

Research demonstrates that social support levels significantly influence the development of frailty (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.57–0.86, P < .05). For elderly patients undergoing regular MHD, disease-related limitations restrict access to social support from colleagues and friends, making family companionship particularly crucial. Consistent with Yuan et al’s findings, patients with higher social support levels maintain more positive mental states, showing significantly improved treatment adherence and self-management capabilities, thereby forming a virtuous cycle in disease management.[48] Therefore, clinical practice should provide systematic guidance to caregivers and establish a collaborative model involving physicians, nurses, and families to implement personalized psychosocial rehabilitation plans. For elderly patients living alone, communities can create mutual assistance mechanisms by integrating resources from neighboring communities to organize collective activities such as health tea parties and interest groups. This establishes a tripartite social support network involving physicians, nurses, and communities, effectively compensating for the limitations of family support resources.

4.2.4. Blood creatinine

Serum creatinine level is a significant risk factor for frailty in elderly MHD patients (OR = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.00–0.27, P < .05). For the elderly MHD patients in this study who may have very little remaining kidney function, blood creatinine levels may no longer primarily reflect renal clearance capacity but instead serve as a surrogate indicator for evaluating nutritional status and skeletal muscle mass,[49] consistent with findings from Hanna et al’s study.[50] Decreased blood creatinine levels typically indicate inadequate food intake, reduced daily activity capacity, or muscle tissue loss, serving as objective markers of malnutrition and muscle atrophy. Therefore, clinical healthcare providers should emphasize the clinical significance of reduced blood creatinine in elderly MHD patients. Beyond active treatment of underlying conditions, professional dietary guidance should be provided, along with encouragement of personalized rehabilitation exercises to enhance physical fitness. Ensuring adequate daily protein and energy intake is crucial for improving nutritional status and addressing frailty in these patients.

4.2.5. Depressive state

Consistent with existing research,[51] patients with high GDS-15 depression scores are more prone to frailty (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.34–2.95, P < .05). This may be due to the disruption of daily life caused by long-term and regular dialysis treatment, along with the resulting economic and psychological stress. Elderly MHD patients often experience negative emotions, and the survey found that this is most common among patients in the early stages of dialysis. Depression can trigger chronic low-grade inflammation,[52] while also reducing patients’ self-management efficacy and participation in physical activities, increasing the likelihood of failure in diet and weight management. Therefore, community services and medical institutions can establish a multidimensional psychological support system to help patients adopt an optimistic attitude toward treatment. Providing psychological care and increasing patients’ hope for life can help them cooperate with treatment and face their later life with a positive attitude, effectively reducing the negative impacts of depression and disease factors, and improving frailty status.

4.3. Summary

This study constructed a frailty assessment model for elderly MHD patients in China based on objective data such as patients’ general information and disease data. Under the optimal cutoff value of 0.719, the model had a sensitivity of 89.6%, a specificity of 75.3%, and a maximum Youden index of 0.672. The internal validation results showed that the model had good discrimination ability, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.788–0.963). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test yielded χ2 = 14.52, P = .07 > .05, and the calibration curve (including calibration intercept = −0.014, slope = 0.908) showed a slight deviation, indicating that the model had acceptable calibration in the development population. The overall ability of the model to identify frail patients was good, and the clinical decision curve (DCA) results showed that when the threshold exceeded 13%, the net benefit of using this nomogram model to guide intervention decisions was superior to the “full treatment” or “no treatment” strategies, indicating that the model had good clinical net benefits. The model’s discrimination remains good when used externally, but there is a significant deviation in calibration. This indicates that the model must be recalibrated using its linear predictor (logit) before being applied to new populations for generalization. This can largely correct the deviations, and the model can still be used to identify frail patients.

In conclusion, the indicators in this model are comprehensive, and the discrimination ability is relatively stable. However, the calibration deviation indicates that its application in new environments requires caution, and it is not recommended to be used directly. This model can provide a direct reference for nurses to assess the physical condition of patients, but its potential deviations should be fully considered in clinical applications.

5. Limitations and prospects

In this study, multiple continuous variables such as hemoglobin, albumin, and creatinine were dichotomized based on fixed cutoff values. Although the clinical operation was simple, the rich information of continuous variables was lost, and the nonlinear relationship between the variables and frailty could not be captured. The cutoff values determined based on this study population may not be applicable in other populations with a larger environmental span, which would compromise the calibration and extrapolation of the model. Moreover, this study did not follow up on the trajectory of frailty status and the dynamic changes of influencing factors in elderly MHD patients.

Due to the excessive number of initial candidate variables and the limited sample size, a single-variable preliminary screening was conducted before modeling to reduce the dimensionality, which may affect the robustness of the model.

Due to limitations in time and funds, the 3 hospitals selected in this study originated from the same city, and the representativeness of the external validation sample was insufficient. At the same time, the calibration performance of the external validation set decreased.

Therefore, future research should prioritize the use of continuous variables (such as using restricted cubic splines) to construct the model, making the model information more complete and the generalization ability stronger. Second, as many cross-regional, cross-time multicenter large-sample studies should be carried out as much as possible, and prospective longitudinal studies should also be conducted to deeply explore the changing patterns of frailty status in elderly MHD patients and clarify the changes in influencing factors over time.
