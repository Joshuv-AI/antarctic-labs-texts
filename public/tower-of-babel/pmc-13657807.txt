Prognostic value of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 30 cohorts through June 30, 2026
Authors: Yunliang Lu, Biaoyou Chen, Jingfeng Ye, Xinyi Hu, Tingting Jiang, Zhe Zhang, Tingting Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious meta-analyses have linked an elevated pretreatment neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) to poor outcomes in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), but they included few recent cohorts and often combined adjusted and unadjusted estimates. We updated the evidence base and evaluated the consistency and certainty of the associations between pretreatment NLR and survival outcomes.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data from inception through June 30, 2026. Eligible reports were cohort studies of pathologically confirmed NPC that compared high versus low pretreatment NLR and reported multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), or locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS). Two reviewers independently selected studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Random-effects models, prespecified subgroup analyses, leave-one-out analyses, assessment of small-study effects, and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty assessment were used.ResultsThirty cohorts comprising 11,671 patients were included; NOS scores ranged from 7 to 9. Elevated pretreatment NLR was associated with inferior OS [27 cohorts; HR: 2.16, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.76–2.66] and PFS (18 cohorts; HR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.45–2.00). Furthermore, high NLR correlated with compromised DMFS (6 cohorts; HR: 1.61, 95% CI: 1.33–1.93) and LRFS (2 cohorts; HR: 4.82, 95% CI: 1.51–15.31). Heterogeneity was substantial for OS (I2=84%) and moderate for PFS (I2=57%). Egger’s tests indicated funnel-plot asymmetry for OS (P<0.001), but not PFS (P=0.26). GRADE certainty was very low for OS and PFS and low for DMFS and LRFS.ConclusionsElevated pretreatment NLR was associated with poorer survival outcomes in NPC. However, substantial heterogeneity, evidence of small-study effects for OS, and low to very low certainty of evidence preclude routine clinical implementation. Prospective validation using standardized sampling and cutoff methods is needed.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this meta-analysis of 30 cohorts [11,671 patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC)], elevated pretreatment neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was consistently associated with poorer overall survival (OS), progression-free survival, distant metastasis-free survival, and locoregional recurrence-free survival; however, heterogeneity was substantial for OS, and the certainty of evidence ranged from low to very low.

• Leave-one-out analyses did not materially change the direction of the pooled estimates, but this stability does not remove clinical heterogeneity or the possibility of small-study effects.

What is known and what is new?

• Earlier meta-analyses associated elevated NLR with poor NPC outcomes, but heterogeneity, variable cutoffs, and the mixing of adjusted and unadjusted estimates limited clinical interpretation.

• This update searches English- and Chinese-language databases, restricts quantitative synthesis to multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), and evaluates certainty using GRADE.

What are the implications, and what should change now?

• Pretreatment NLR may be considered a candidate adjunct to tumor-node-metastasis staging and Epstein-Barr virus DNA in future validated risk models.

• The current aggregate evidence does not support treatment intensification or surveillance changes based on NLR alone.

• Prospective studies should standardize sampling and cutoff derivation and test whether NLR adds prognostic value beyond established clinical and viral markers.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) exhibits a highly skewed geographical distribution, remaining predominantly endemic to southern China and Southeast Asia (1). Its pathogenesis is intrinsically linked to a complex interplay of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, genetic susceptibility, and environmental exposures, which collectively forge its unique, immune-dense tumor microenvironment (1-3). Despite the widespread implementation of intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and optimized systemic therapeutic regimens, a considerable proportion of patients still suffer from disease recalcitrance, manifesting as locoregional relapse or distant metastasis (4,5). Consequently, there is an unmet clinical need for accessible biomarkers that can complement the traditional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system and EBV status to refine risk stratification and personalize treatment intensity.

The management of NPC has evolved substantially toward more individualized risk stratification and treatment selection. The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)/Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging system, introduced in 2017, improved the prognostic relevance of the T and N classifications (6). More recently, the ninth version, launched for global application in January 2025, further refined prognostic stratification by incorporating advanced radiological extranodal extension into the N3 category, regrouping nonmetastatic disease into stages I–III, and subdividing metastatic disease according to metastatic burden (7). Importantly, the ninth version remains primarily anatomy-based; nonanatomical factors such as plasma EBV DNA have not yet been formally incorporated, although they represent important directions for future refinement (7). In parallel, systemic management of locoregionally advanced disease has increasingly favored induction chemotherapy followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy, although treatment benefits vary across risk groups and careful selection of patients at high risk of relapse remains essential (8). Plasma EBV DNA levels and their dynamic changes show promise for biomarker-guided treatment selection, but assay harmonization and standardized cutoff values are still required (9). Within this increasingly complex and risk-adapted treatment landscape, an inexpensive and readily available biomarker such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) may provide complementary prognostic information beyond anatomical staging.

Systemic inflammation is recognized as a fundamental hallmark of carcinogenesis and tumor progression. In the context of NPC, EBV-driven immune dysregulation and inflammatory signaling cascades can synergistically promote tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion (10). Consequently, systemic inflammation-based indices derived from routine peripheral blood tests, including the NLR, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and systemic immune-inflammation index, have emerged as compelling prognostic adjuncts (11-14).

Among these, the NLR offers a conceptually straightforward interpretation: neutrophilia reflects tumor-driven inflammatory activation and the expansion of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, fostering a microenvironment conducive to metastasis; conversely, lymphopenia indicates compromised antitumor immune surveillance and reduced treatment tolerance (15,16). Experimental and translational evidence in EBV-associated NPC corroborates the significance of this inflammatory crosstalk, highlighting the chemokine-driven recruitment of tumor-associated neutrophils and the subsequent exhaustion of cytotoxic lymphocytes (17,18). Therefore, pretreatment NLR serves as a composite barometer of tumor-host interactions, systemic inflammation, and immune competence.

Nevertheless, published NPC cohorts have reported markedly heterogeneous effect sizes, hindered by inconsistently defined NLR cutoffs, variable analytic strategies, and differing confounder adjustments. The clinical interpretation of NLR also diverges across distinct therapeutic settings, such as metastatic versus non-metastatic disease, and radiotherapy alone versus intensive chemoradiotherapy regimens. These differences in disease stage and treatment setting introduce additional clinical heterogeneity, further complicating the interpretation and comparison of NLR-related prognostic estimates across studies. While earlier meta-analyses published up to 2023 suggested that an elevated NLR confers worse overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) outcomes (11), the subsequent proliferation of new cohorts—particularly those incorporating contemporary immunotherapeutic regimens—necessitates an updated, methodologically transparent synthesis (19-22). From a translational perspective, NLR remains an attractive, universally available parameter for prognostic modeling, especially where advanced molecular testing is constrained (23). Because NLR is susceptible to transient physiological fluctuations (e.g., occult infection, stress, corticosteroid use), its clinical application demands standardized pretreatment sampling (24).

Accordingly, this meta-analysis aimed to quantify the association of pretreatment NLR with OS, PFS, distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS) using only multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs); to examine consistency across clinically relevant study-level characteristics; and to assess the certainty of evidence. The objective was to refine the magnitude, applicability, and limitations of an established association rather than to claim a novel prognostic relationship. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1021/rc) (25).

Methods

Protocol and literature search

The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251122974). PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data were searched from database inception through June 30, 2026. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms for “nasopharyngeal carcinoma” and “neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio” were combined. The date of the last search and the complete strategy for each database, including all limits, are provided in Table S1. Reference lists of eligible reports and relevant reviews were also screened.

Study selection

Eligibility was defined as follows: (I) population: patients with pathologically confirmed NPC; (II) exposure: pretreatment peripheral blood NLR measurements; (III) comparison: high versus low NLR based on a reported threshold; (IV) outcomes: OS, PFS, DMFS, or LRFS; (V) study design: an original cohort study reported in full text in English or Chinese. A study was eligible for quantitative synthesis only if it reported a multivariable-adjusted HR with a 95% confidence interval (CI) for at least one outcome; HRs reconstructed solely from Kaplan-Meier curves were not used because covariate adjustment could not be retained. Reviews, case reports, laboratory studies, conference abstracts without full data, and reports without an eligible adjusted estimate were excluded. For potentially overlapping populations, the most comprehensive or recent report was retained. Two investigators (Y.L. and B.C.) independently screened records and full texts; disagreements were resolved by consensus and, when necessary, senior-author adjudication.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (Y.L. and B.C.) independently extracted the first author, publication year, recruitment period, country or region, study design, sample size, disease setting, treatment regimen, NLR sampling time, cutoff value and derivation method, outcome definitions, follow-up, covariates included in the model, and adjusted HRs with 95% CIs. When several multivariable models were available for the same cohort and outcome, the model with the most comprehensive clinically relevant adjustment was selected. Risk of bias was appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (26). Scores of 7 to 9 were classified as high quality according to a prespecified convention (27), but this classification did not determine study inclusion or statistical weight; item-level judgments are reported in Table S2.

If a publication reported multiple independent cohorts, each cohort was treated as a separate dataset, provided that patient overlap was unlikely. Subcohorts in the included studies were primarily defined by the NLR cutoff corresponding to the extracted survival outcome. In addition, one study stratified by study center (Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center vs. The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University) (28). Potentially overlapping cohorts were not entered more than once in the same outcome synthesis.

Certainty of evidence assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated the certainty of evidence for each survival outcome using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach with disagreements resolved by consensus. Because the included evidence was observational, certainty was initially rated as low and then assessed for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Potential upgrading factors were a large magnitude of effect, a dose-response gradient, and plausible residual confounding that would reduce the observed effect. Overall certainty was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low; judgements and reasons for rating down or up are provided in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

HRs and corresponding 95% CIs were pooled by inverse-variance weighting on the logarithmic scale. Because true associations were expected to vary across population, treatment, and cutoff methods, a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was applied a priori. This model accommodates statistical between-study variability but does not resolve clinical heterogeneity; pooled estimates were therefore interpreted together with heterogeneity, subgroup, sensitivity, and certainty assessments.

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic (29). Prespecified exploratory subgroup analyses considered study design, age category, publication language, metastatic status, NLR cutoff category, and NOS score. Differences in within-subgroup I2 values were not interpreted as evidence of effect modification in the absence of a formal between-subgroup comparison. The influence of individual cohorts was examined using leave-one-out analyses.

Potential small-study effects were assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test for outcomes with at least 10 cohorts. Analyses were performed using Stata 15.0 and Review Manager 5.4; a two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. When funnel plot asymmetry was detected, Duval and Tweedie’s nonparametric trim-and-fill method was applied as a sensitivity analysis to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and recalculate the pooled effect under a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model. Estimates were analyzed on the log-HR scale and exponentiated for presentation. Because trim-and-fill relies on unverifiable assumptions, it was not interpreted as a correction for, or proof of the absence of, publication bias.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The searches identified 516 records. After screening, detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1), 30 eligible cohort studies comprising 11,671 patients were included; the contributing reports and cohort-level characteristics are listed in Table 1. Most cohorts were conducted in China (29/30), with only one study conducted outside China (33). Publication years ranged from 2012 to 2025, and NLR cutoffs varied from 1.886 to 6. NOS scores ranged from 7 to 9; item-level risk-of-bias assessments are presented in Table S2.

Prognostic Value of Pretreatment NLR

Across 27 cohorts, elevated pretreatment NLR was associated with poorer OS (HR: 2.16; 95% CI: 1.76–2.66; P<0.001, Figure 2A), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2=84%, P<0.001). Across 18 cohorts, elevated pretreatment NLR was associated with poorer PFS (HR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.45–2.00; P<0.001; Figure 2B), with moderate heterogeneity (I2=57%, P=0.001). Elevated pretreatment NLR was associated with inferior DMFS (6 cohorts; HR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.33–1.93; P<0.001, Figure 2C) and LRFS (2 cohorts; HR: 4.82; 95% CI: 1.51–15.31; P=0.008, Figure 2D). The LRFS estimate was based on only 2 cohorts and had a wide CI; both secondary-outcome findings therefore require cautious interpretation (37,48).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sequential omission of individual cohorts did not materially alter the direction or magnitude of the pooled estimates (Figure 3). Funnel plots (Figure 4) and Egger’s regression tests indicated small-study effects for OS (P<0.001), but not for PFS (P=0.26). Trim-and-fill analysis was therefore performed for OS only. After imputing five potentially missing studies, the pooled HR was 1.872 (95% CI: 1.534–2.285, Figure S1). This result was treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than a bias-corrected estimate. Analyses stratified by NOS scores (7, 8, and 9) showed the same direction of association, although NOS score did not explain the substantial heterogeneity.

GRADE assessment of evidence certainty

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE approach (Table S3). As all included studies were observational, the initial certainty of evidence was rated as low. The certainty was rated as very low for OS because of serious inconsistency and strongly suspected publication bias, despite an upgrade for the large magnitude of effect. Evidence for PFS was also rated as very low because of serious inconsistency. The certainty of evidence for DMFS remained low, with no serious concerns identified in the assessed domains. For LRFS, serious imprecision arising from the small number of studies and wide confidence intervals was offset by an upgrade for the large magnitude of effect, resulting in an overall low certainty rating.

Discussion

This update, with searches through June 2026, found that elevated pretreatment NLR was associated with poorer OS, PFS, DMFS, and LRFS across 30 eligible cohorts with NPC patients. However, certainty was very low for OS and PFS and low for DMFS and LRFS. The results therefore support a prognostic association but do not establish NLR as a clinically validated decision biomarker. The association is biologically plausible: neutrophilia may reflect pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive myeloid activity, whereas lymphopenia may indicate impaired antitumor immune surveillance (57-61). Mechanistic plausibility, however, cannot resolve confounding or heterogeneity in the observational evidence. Older cohorts were retained because the review question concerned the prognostic association across treatment settings rather than a treatment-specific effect in immunotherapy. Nevertheless, historical and contemporary cohorts should not be considered clinically interchangeable. Only a small number of included cohorts evaluated immune-checkpoint inhibitor settings; consequently, the pooled results should not be interpreted as evidence that NLR predicts benefit from immunotherapy or from any specific modern treatment regimen.

Pretreatment NLR is inexpensive and routinely available, and it may eventually complement TNM staging and plasma EBV DNA within validated multivariable models (62). The present evidence does not support using NLR alone to intensify treatment or surveillance. Several limitations constrain interpretation. Most cohorts were retrospective, and residual confounding by infection, corticosteroid exposure, comorbidity, nutritional status, and other inflammatory conditions is likely despite multivariable adjustment. Cutoff derivation was heterogeneous and frequently data driven, reducing transportability. The predominance of single-center Chinese cohorts limits generalizability and increases the possibility of regional overlap. Aggregate study-level data prevented harmonization of outcome definitions, covariates, sampling conditions, and cutoff methods. Furthermore, inconsistent covariate definitions and incomplete reporting across the primary studies resulted in a sparse study-level data matrix that precluded reliable multivariable meta-regression. Therefore, the subgroup analyses should be regarded as exploratory and cannot be considered to fully explain the residual heterogeneity. SinoMed and gray literature sources were not searched, so additional regional studies may have been missed. Finally, funnel-plot asymmetry for OS may indicate that the pooled association is inflated.

Future work should prioritize prospective, multicenter validation with standardized exclusion criteria for acute inflammation, prespecified blood-sampling windows, and externally validated cutoff procedures. Individual-participant-data meta-analyses could harmonize covariates and quantify incremental prognostic value beyond TNM stage and plasma EBV DNA using discrimination, calibration, and decision-curve analyses. Longitudinal NLR during induction chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, and immunotherapy should be studied separately from pretreatment NLR because dynamic measures address a different prognostic question (21).

The direction of association is consistent with previous meta-analyses. The contribution of this update is a larger and more recent evidence base, restriction to multivariable-adjusted HRs, inclusion of Chinese-language searches, and explicit GRADE assessment. Its clinical message is therefore a more precise delimitation of the association and its uncertainty, not a new claim of prognostic relevance. Research should now move beyond repeated association testing toward standardized sampling and cutoff derivation and formal assessment of incremental value beyond established staging and viral markers.

Conclusions

Elevated pretreatment NLR was associated with poorer survival outcomes in NPC, but substantial heterogeneity, possible small-study effects, and low to very low certainty of evidence limit clinical interpretation. NLR should not guide treatment or surveillance outside validated multivariable models. Standardized sampling, prespecified cutoff methods, and prospective confirmation of incremental value beyond plasma EBV DNA and anatomical staging are required.
