Improved electrical performance of PDMS and PEDOT: PSS composites with MWCNT and AgNP particles
Authors: S. Haghgooye Shafagh, I. Deen, M. Packirisamy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The fabrication of conductive polymer composites (CPCs) using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and poly(3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene): poly(4-styrene-sulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS) as a matrix with multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) as fillers was investigated to determine their potential for use in applications such as electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, sensors, and thermal switches/cut-offs. The effect of the fillers was investigated using MWCNTs of different sizes and including both MWCNTs and AgNPs at different ratios. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyzed the morphology of the CPCs showed that the inclusion of AgNPs in the PDMS matrix resulted in a particle size gradient, with larger particles at the bottom of the CPC, although SEM confirmed that there was no separation between PDMS/AgNP and bulk PDMS. SEM of the PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT composites showed an anisotropic structure, with MWCNTs randomly oriented and dispersed throughout the polymer. Electrical characterization showed that the fabrication method and the MWCNT diameter and content affect the conductivity, with MWCNTs of smaller diameters at 45 wt% in CPCs synthesized using ultrasonication having the highest conductivity at 121.21 S/m. The analysis of CPCs synthesized with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and glycerol was also shown to be feasible and improved the flexibility of the composite, and resulted in a conductivity of 378.97 S/m, ~ 26% higher than that reported in the literature. Finally, thermal characterization showed that PDMS and PEDOT: PSS composites exhibit the thermoelectric (TE) effect, where a change in temperature creates an electric potential and vice versa, with the temperature of PDMS composites rising from room temperature (22℃) to approximately 70℃ when a 5 V potential was applied. Under the same conditions, PDMS composites with 50 wt% MWCNTs rose from room temperature to 106℃, while composites with 30 wt% MWCNTs only rose to 65℃. and 50 wt% MWCNTs. The PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT composites also exhibited the TE effect, with the measured resistance varying with temperature. These results demonstrate that CPCs exhibit tunable properties that are suitable for use in various electrical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44291-025-00136-0.

Introduction

The development of novel types of polymer composites has been an ongoing research subject for many decades. The use of ablative polymer composites to protect surfaces in aerospace applications [1–3], fiber-reinforced polymer composites in the automotive industry [4, 5], polymer-impregnated concrete for construction [6, 7], and different materials and processes have been investigated as early as the 1960s. The end goal was to determine how polymer composites might be optimized for a diverse range of applications and industries.

Thanks to their unique properties, there is considerable interest in next-generation conductive polymer composites (CPCs) that can be employed as smart materials in applications such as sensors [8–11], optoelectronics [12], and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding [13–15]. CPCs are of particular interest for EMI shielding in devices used in commercial, industrial, military aerospace sectors [15]. While polymers are typically used for insulation, there is a need for electrically conductive polymer composites, as modern communication and electronic products require materials to have higher EMI shielding performance, and are lightweight, flexible, and reliable [16]. Although metals are typically used for their strength and conductivity, in low-strength applications, CPCs offer several advantages. Metals are generally heavy and have high associated costs, are difficult to process, and have poor corrosion resistance [17–21]. Replacing metallic components with more readily available organic materials offers alternatives that have good mechanical properties, such as flexibility [22–25] and shock absorbance [26, 27], as well as lower cost and ease of processing [24, 28], as devices can be fabricated using simple techniques such as roll-to-roll processing [29, 30], inkjet printing [31–33], and 3D printing [34–36].

A multitude of polymers have been investigated for their potential as CPCs, but two polymers of particular interest are polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) [37–39] and poly(3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene): poly(4-styrene-sulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS) [10, 40–43]. PDMS is non-conductive but flexible, whereas PEDOT: PSS is highly conductive and has excellent flexibility and the ability to adhere to different substrates. Using PDMS or PEDOT: PSS in a flexible matrix to which additives can be included, such as multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) [39, 44, 45], (see Fig. 1) or silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) [46–48], have been shown to result in a CPC with improved and tailored material properties.

The use of a suitable filler, or a combination of fillers, provides the opportunity to tune the properties of CPCs, as well as to adjust the permittivity, permeability, and thermal and electrical conductivity to obtain improved performance [13, 49]. A wide variety of fillers have been investigated, mainly consisting of conducting nanostructures such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, graphene oxide, metals (e.g., Au, Ag, and Pd), oxides (e.g., Fe2O3, MnO2, CuO), and insulating ceramic nanostructures [25, 40, 49–52], but CNTs remain one of the most common. CNTs have typically been used for electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding because of their high electrical conductivity [53, 54]. However, they have also been shown to reduce mechanical shock waves by up to a third [27], and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) have been used to create flexible devices, such as supercapacitors [55, 56] and sensors [57, 58]. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), another popular filler, have been used for EMI shielding [59] and in 3D printed devices [60], and have been successfully coupled with CNTs to form advanced CPCs [35, 59, 61–63].

In this study, we investigated the potential for developing flexible CPCs with tunable properties by adjusting the CPC filler composition. Of particular interest are CPCs that exhibit the thermoelectric (TE) effect, the phenomenon through which a change in temperature creates an electric potential, or an electric current causes a change in temperature [28, 64, 65], and can be used to fabricate thermal sensors and cut-offs. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, the combination of fillers with the base polymer has been extensively investigated in the literature, mainly CPCs with only one type of filler and in relatively low (roughly a third by weight) quantities. This research aims to develop CPCs that go beyond the amount reported in the literature by fabricating and characterizing CPCs composed of up to 50% (by weight) filler and incorporating a second filler to determine its effect on the properties of the CPC.

It is pertinent to note that no prior literature has reported the addition of CNTs in PDMS at concentrations exceeding 30 wt%. However, in our study, we surpassed this concentration by incorporating up to 50 wt%. When the CNT concentration was increased to 50 wt%, the curing time of the composite material was increased to six hours. This phenomenon represents a significant challenge that has not been addressed in previous studies that have limited CNT concentrations of up to 30 wt%.The novelty of our approach lies in stable processing and curing at such elevated concentrations, overcoming the trade-off between filler content vs. matrix integrity at elevated CNT concentrations. Through optimized ultrasonication-assisted mixing and controlled thermal curing, we have managed to fabricate a mechanically flexible nanocomposite that achieved a conductivity which is significantly higher than previously reported for PDMS-based CNT composites. This increased filler enabled deeper investigation of MWCNT behavior within PDMS, including quantifying its electro-thermal response and electrical properties, and demonstrates how MWCNT composites can be used for flexible electronic and thermal management applications.

Finally, as synthesis techniques have been found to affect the final properties of CPPs [49], different fabrication routes have been examined to optimize the incorporation of inorganic components into the conducting polymer.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of PDMS/MWCNT and PDMS/MWCNT/AgNP composites

A SYLGARD 184 Silicone Elastomer Kit (Dow Inc., USA) was used to prepare the PDMS molds by mixing the base elastomer and curing agent at a 10:1 ratio (by weight) using an ultrasonicator (Sonics & Materials, Inc., USA). It was placed in a desiccator hooked up to a vacuum pump (Welch, USA) to remove air bubbles within the mixture, then placed in a furnace at 60℃ for 20–30 min to cure. MWCNTs with an outer diameter (OD) of 8–15 nm and length of 10–15 μm (CNT15) and with OD of 10–30 nm and length of 10–30 μm (CNT30) were purchased (Cheap Tubes Inc., USA) and mixed within the PDMS.

To obtain a homogeneous distribution of MWCNTs inside the polymer and functionalize the MWCNTs, an optimized dispersion technique using isopropyl alcohol (IPA) in combination with ultrasonication was used [66]. Figure 1 shows the steps for fabricating the PDMS/MWCNT composite. After ultrasonication for 10 min, a small amount of silicon oil (MEP) was added to the solution and ultrasonicated for 10 min. PDMS was then added to the solution and ultrasonicated for 10 min. The samples were placed in a furnace at 70℃ for 30 min until the IPA vaporized.

Figure 2 shows the preparation method of the whole mixture, including the razor blade and peeling-off methods. Depending on the mold geometry, different shapes and sizes can be easily generated, and the final sample exhibits high flexibility and elasticity.

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) under 100 nm (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were combined with MWCNT to enhance the conductivity of the polymer. Mixtures incorporated 50 wt% filler (MWCNT and AgNP) and 50 wt% polymer (PDMS). By varying the MWCNT to AgNP ratio, four different compositions were obtained (Table 1).

To ensure homogenous distribution of AgNPs within the composite, two mixing methods were employed. In the first method, MWCNTs were added to IPA at 1:100 by weight and ultrasonicated for 10 min. MEP was added at a ratio of 1:5 to PDMS and ultrasonicated for 10 min. AgNPs were added, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for a final time of 2 min. The solution was then placed in a furnace at 70℃ for 30 min to evaporate the IPA. The second method differs from the first only in that when AgNPs were added, shear mixing was used instead of ultrasonication.

Fabrication of PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT composites

An improved dispersion process combining IPA and ultrasonication was employed to achieve a homogenous distribution of MWCNTs inside the polymer matrix as follows. MWCNTs were added to IPA at 1:100 (by mass) by measuring 0.05 g) in a glass beaker and adding 5 g of IPA. The solution was ultrasonicated (750 W, 40% power, pulse 10 s on, pulse 15 s off) for 10 min. A 1:2 (by volume) 1 g/mL aqueous PEDOT: PSS (Sigma-Aldrich) to the MWCNT + IPA mixture was obtained by adding 3.18 mL of PEDOT: PSS to the solution. The mixture was then ultrasonicated for 5 min. Finally, 0.2 mL Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) and 0.4 mL glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the PEDOT: PSS + MWCNT + IPA solution. The mixture was then ultrasonicated a final time for 3 min. The mixture was poured into a mold and placed in a furnace for 20 min at 60℃ for curing.

Mold patterns

Three different molds were designed using SolidWorks (Fig. 3). A Form 2 Stereolithography (SLA) 3D Printer (Formlabs Co., USA) was used to print the 3D model. The channels had a width of 2 mm and depth of 300 μm, and the mold walls were 2 mm in height.

Results and discussion

Scanning electron microscopy

Figure 9(a) shows a top-down view of the conductive line containing a mixture of PDMS, CNT15, and AgNPs. From the SEM image, larger AgNPs (white dots) can be seen to have sunk to the bottom (section i), smaller silver nanoparticles floated at the top (section ii), and section iii was the PDSM mold. A close-up of the PDMS/MWCNT/AgNP line (Fig. 9(b)) shows that section i contains larger AgNPs, section ii has smaller AgNPs, and section iii contains pure PDMS. Because fabrication takes place in an ambient atmosphere, oxidation can occur, which decreases conductivity. To determine whether AgNPs were oxidized within the sample, a chemical analysis was carried out. Elemental analysis (Table S1) shows that the oxygen level in the silver particles was the same throughout the sample, which indicates that no oxidation took place.

SEM images were taken of the PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT composites Fig. 10(a) and show its morphology. The images show that the MWCNTs in PEDOT: PSS are randomly oriented, and indicate that a conducting route is formed by the long MWCNTs inside the PEDOT: PSS polymer. Figure 10(b) shows the diameters of the MWCNTs in the composite. The outer diameter (OD) of the as-received MWCNTs was 8–15 nm, whereas that OD of the composite was 20–30 nm. This increase in diameter can be attributed to the PEDOT: PSS coating of the MWCNTs. It also shows that PEDOT: PSS can be used not only as a conductive solvent, but also as a conductive medium. Moreover, coating PEDOT: PSS on the outer wall of MWCNT leads to a reduction in agglomeration and a more homogenous mixture. In the PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT mixture, because the MWCNTs were embedded in PEDOT: PSS, MWCNTs and PEDOT: PSS functioned as the guest and host components, respectively [67].

Electrical properties of MWCNT composites

Using the measured resistance (see Table S2), calculated cross-sectional area, and known length between the probe tips, it was possible to calculate the conductivity of the PDMS/MWCNT composites using Eqs. (1) and (2) (see Table S3). Figure 12(a) shows that the resistance at 10 mm (R10) for both composites decreased with increasing MWCNT content. Both MWCNT sizes exhibit the highest resistance at 18 wt%. However, composites with CNT30 have a resistance several times higher (131.60 ± 26.22 kΩ) than the resistance of those with CNT15 (30.00 ± 3.59 kΩ). However, the resistance of both composites decreased to approximately 7 kΩ at 35 wt%, after which point there is only a slight decrease in resistance regardless of the amount of MWCNT. The results indicate that by increasing the MWCNT content in the polymer matrix, the resistance decreases exponentially. While CNT30 and CNT15 decreased at different rates, they both reached the same resistance at 35 wt%, at which point resistance remains constant.

Fig. 12(a) Measured resistance and (b) calculated conductivity of PDMS/MWCNT micro-lines (SD for n = 3, p < 0.05)

(a) Measured resistance and (b) calculated conductivity of PDMS/MWCNT micro-lines (SD for n = 3, p < 0.05)

The calculated conductivity (Fig. 12(a)) shows a linear increase with MWCNT content. There was a difference of approximately 15 S/m between the CNT15 and CNT30 composites, with CNT15 being more conductive. The highest conductivity is 94 S/m with CNT15, while the second highest was 86.20 S/m with CNT30, both at 50 wt% MWCNT content. This was attributed to the increasing aspect ratio of the fillers, which improved the conductive paths inside the polymer [40]. Previous work has shown that increasing the CNT content within a PDMS network increases conductivity [68]. However, the results reported in the literature indicate significantly higher conductivities. A conductivity of 285.7 S/m was achieved using the same mixing and preparation method but with the addition of aqueous gold chloride and silver nanoparticles to their solutions [13].

Additional composites were fabricated with CNT15, which showed the highest conductivity, using different mixing techniques. Composites were fabricated from a mixed solution using ultrasonication and shear mixing. Figure 13 shows the resistance of these samples (see Tables S4). With samples made with ultrasonication, increasing MWCNT content leads to a graducal increase in conductivity before reaching a plateau, with the lowest resistance at 3.66 kΩ giving a conductivity of 121.21 S/m at 45 wt% MWCNT. Increasing conductivity with MWCNT content has been previously reported [69, 70] and is attributed to the formation of a conductive network with increasing contact between the MWCNTs [69, 71]. The samples prepared via shear mixing showed a drop in resistance with MWCNT contents up to 35 wt% MWCNT, at which point resistance increased. This is attributed to the lack of force exerted on the MWCNT in the solution, which caused the dispersion of the MWCNTs, leading to poorer network connectivity within the composite.

As PEDOT: PSS is known for its flexibility and conductivity [72, 73], in addition to normal electrical measurements, a flexibility test was conducted on the composite material. The conductive line was subjected to bending and its resistance was measured. The resistance remained unaffected despite bending and no cracks were detected on the line. The results indicated that the composite material under stress exhibited considerable flexibility and did not inhibit its conductivity.

Dimensional impedance analysis of PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT composites

To ensure that the electrical properties did not change with size, three different lengths of PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT lines were fabricated via ultrasonication, incorporating CNT15, and analyzed. The width of the lines was set to 2 mm, and the height was 0.24 mm (verified with microscopic imaging). Additionally, a mold with a channel width of 1 mm was fabricated to determine whether the line width affected the impedance. A comparison of the results obtained (Table 2) shows that while the resistance is proportional to the dimensions of the PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT line, the conductivity remains constant, indicating that for large-sized samples (dimensions above 1 mm), the electrical properties are based solely on the material properties and are independent of the composite dimensions [74].

By using MWCNT/IPA + PEDOT: PSS + PEG + Glycerol, a conductivity of 378.97 S/m was attained, higher than what has previously been reported in the literature [13]. These findings are consistent with the results reported in the literature, which show conductivities between 100 and 300 S/m for 1 wt% MWCNT in PEDOT: PSS [13]. The use of PEG or plasticizers has been shown to reduce the mass transport resistance [75] or dissociate ion pairs into free ions [76] within polymer networks, which can account for the increased conductivity.

Thermal characterization

Thermal behaviour of PDMS and PEDOT: PSS composites

PDMS composites with different CNT15/AgNP ratios (C45S05 and C35S15) were tested. A 5 V potential was applied to the conductive lines by the DC power supply, and the temperature was measured by a thermocouple, which recorded the temperature in real time. Figure 14 shows the temperature profiles of these two micro-lines. The results show distinct heating, equilibrium, and cooling phases that have been observed in the literature for the PDMS/MWCNT constructs [77]. Before applying the voltage, the temperature was 23℃ (room temperature). When a voltage was applied, the temperature increased rapidly. In less than a minute the C45S05 sample averaged 66℃ and the C35S15 samples averaged 78℃. The temperature remained constant for the next 15 min, and as soon as the voltage was cut it decreased rapidly to room temperature. The rise in temperature is attributed to ohmic heating (also called Joule heating, electroheating, and electroconductive heating), wherein a current that passes through a material causes heating due to the transfer of energy from electrical to thermal, as an increasing number of electrons impinge upon the material [78], and has been used to cure PDMS/CNT composites in the past [68]. The difference in the maximum temperature between C35S15 and C45S05 is attributed to the difference in their conductivities: at room temperature, C45S05 has a conductivity of 121 S/m while C35S15 has one of 108.8 S/m.

Figure 15 shows the temperature profiles of micro-lines with 30 wt% and 50 wt% CNT15. Both samples were measured at room temperature (23℃). As soon as the 5 V potential was applied, the temperature increased to 106℃ and 65℃ for 30 wt% and 50 wt% MWCNTs, respectively. The temperature remained steady as the potential was held, and dropped immediately to 40 °C before slowly cooling to room temperature when it was removed. The rise in temperature is attributed to Joule heating in the line, and for higher resistances, the line reaches a higher temperature [77, 78].

In addition to the PDMS micro-lines, PEDOT: PSS channels were heated on a hot plate to determine the effect of temperature on the electrical performance. Figure 16 shows the channels filled with PEDOT: PSS/MWCNTs. The effective length of the resistance line remained constant at 80 mm for all the three channel types (straight, serpentine, and zigzag). A copper foil was embedded into the PDMS substrate to attach the probes and act as an electrode to measure the resistance of the PEDOT: PSS/MWCNT composites (R80, denoting an 80 mm distance between the measuring probes). Figure 17 shows that as temperature increased from 23 °C to 75 °C, the resistance of PEDOT: PSS composites decreased. In all cases, the resistance decreased linearly by approximately 75 Ω. The increase in resistance is attributed to the expansion of the polymer, which hinders electrons, as the conductive path is filled with gaps [79].

By increasing the temperature by 53℃, the resistance decreased for all the samples. The increase in temperature with increasing polymer conductivity has been previously shown in the literature [80–82] and is attributed to the increased movement of free electrons and better conductive paths throughout the sample [82].

The temperature of the polymer increased when applying a potential, indicating that the internal heat generation effect, or Joule heating, occurs within the sample. This is attributed to electrons colliding with the polymer lattice, transferring their kinetic energy as dissipated heat, and has been previously observed in conductive polymers [83–85].

Conclusion and future research

PDMS and PEDOT: PSS were used as base polymers for the CPCs. The maximum conductivity achieved with the PDMS was 121.21 S/m. Using PEDOT: PSS instead of PDMS, the conductivity reached 378.97 S/m. PEG and Glycerol were added to the mixture to improve the conductivity and flexibility and to achieve better attachment to the PDMS mold. SEM images showed that the conducting route was formed via MWCNTs inside the PEDOT: PSS matrix and that there was no agglomeration. The thermal characterization of the composites shows the polymer was sensitive to changes in temperature, and by applying a potential to the conductive lines, the temperature increased by approximately 40 °C owing to Joule heating, which can potentially be used to cure PDMS composites. Three different PEDOT: PSS line shapes were analyzed: straight, serpentine, and zigzag. Regardless of the shape, all materials showed similar electrical properties. Moreover, the FEA showed a match with the experimental results, indicating that the PDMS and PEDOT: PSS composites can be used to optimize the design process of electrical devices.
