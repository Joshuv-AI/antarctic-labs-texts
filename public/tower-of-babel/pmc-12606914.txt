FTO-engineered extracellular vesicles from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells ameliorate Staphylococcus aureus-induced osteomyelitis via m6A-dependent suppression of autophagy and pyroptosis
Authors: Muguo Song, Siyu Lu, Hao Tang, Kehan Lv, Junyi Li, Jian Shi, Yongqing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) ameliorate various orthopedic diseases through the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs). This study investigates the effects of FTO-overexpressing BM-MSCs-derived EVs (FTO-EVs) on osteocyte autophagy and pyroptosis in Staphylococcus aureus-associated osteomyelitis (OM).MethodsThe expression levels of N6-methyladenosine regulators were investigated in bone tissues and serum EVs obtained from patients with OM. Osteoblast MC3T3-E1 and osteocyte MLO-Y4 cells were induced with Staphylococcal Protein A (SpA) to simulate in vitro OM models, and murine OM models were established by bone drilling followed by local injection of Staphylococcus aureus suspension, both of which were treated with EVs.ResultsFat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) was downregulated in the bone tissues or serum EVs from OM patients compare to healthy control. Compared with negative control EVs, FTO-EVs significantly increased FTO expression, elevated cell viability, and inhibited cell death, ATG2A/ATG14-mediated autophagy and NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in SpA-induced MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells. Treatment with FTO-EVs significantly ameliorated bone damage in OM model mice, upregulated the expression of pro-survival proteins in bone tissue, while downregulating key markers of autophagy and pyroptosis. Mechanistically, FTO-EVs N6-methyladenosine dependently downregulated the expression of ATG2A, ATG14 and NLRP3 to modulate autophagy and pyroptosis in SpA-induced MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells.ConclusionsIn summary, this study confirms the role of BM-MSCs-derived FTO-EVs in inhibiting the progression of Staphylococcus aureus-related OM both in vivo and in vitro, providing promising therapeutic strategies for clinical treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00572-2.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Staphylococcus aureus, Extracellular vesicle, Osteomyelitis, Autophagy, Pyroptotic cell death

Introduction

Osteomyelitis (OM) is a destructive inflammatory condition of the bone caused by bacterial infection. Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most common causative agents of osteomyelitis, accounting for over 75% [1]. Through adhesion proteins, S. aureus binds to the bone matrix and forms biofilms on bone tissue or implant surfaces, leading to chronic infection and antibiotic resistance. Concurrently, it releases toxins that disrupt host cells, exacerbating inflammation and osteonecrosis [2, 3]. The pathogen can infect bone via hematogenous dissemination, direct contamination following open surgery or trauma [1]. Current therapeutic approaches primarily involve prolonged antibiotic regimens combined with surgical debridement of necrotic and infected tissues. However, these strategies exhibit significant limitations in efficacy, impose high financial costs, and create substantial socioeconomic burdens for families and healthcare systems [4]. Due to its robust biofilm-forming capacity, antibiotic selection pressure, and genetic mutations, S. aureus readily develops resistance, resulting in increased clinical treatment failure and recurrence risks [5]. Consequently, S. aureus-associated OM remains a formidable challenge in orthopedics and infectious disease management. A deeper exploration of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying OM is essential to develop novel therapeutic strategies for this disease.

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) are multipotent stem cells residing in adult bone marrow, characterized by their self-renewal capacity and ability to undergo extensive in vitro passaging [6, 7]. BM-MSCs mediate intercellular communication through the secretion of extracellular vesicles (EVs), thereby modulating the biological functions of target cells and regulating the progression of various orthopedic disorders. For example, both MSCs and MSCs-derived EVs are reported to participate in chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation in rheumatoid arthritis [8], and BM-MSCs-derived EVs have been demonstrated to mitigate the progression of osteoarthritis and osteomyelitis [9–11]. Furthermore, Lisa et al.. revealed that MSCs can deliver antimicrobial agents and immunomodulatory molecules via EVs, with cluster analysis highlighting their enrichment in biological processes linked to S. aureus infection [12]. Thus, BM-MSCs exhibit immense therapeutic potential in S. aureus-associated OM, although their precise mechanisms warrant further elucidation.

Epigenetic modifications, which reversibly regulate gene expression without altering DNA sequences through chemical modifications or physical structural changes, provide a molecular explanation for the interplay between environmental and genetic factors. N6-methyladenosine (m6A), a prevalent RNA modification in eukaryotes, has recently emerged as a key epigenetic regulator. Studies have demonstrated that RNA m6A modification is closely associated with OM progression: Shi et al.. reported that m6A regulators serve as diagnostic markers, subtype predictors, and indicators of immune infiltration in OM [13]. Zhang et al.. reported that m6A modification plays vital roles in osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation of BM-MSCs [14]. Gao et al.. discovered that methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) accelerates staphylococcal protein A (SpA)-induced OM progression by regulating microRNA expression in an m6A-dependent manner, collectively highlighting the critical role of m6A-related genes in OM pathogenesis [15]. As a pivotal m6A demethylase, fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) has garnered significant attention for its multifaceted functions, including ameliorating cellular inflammation, oxidative stress, and autophagy [16–20]. Notably, FTO has been shown to inhibit SpA-induced ferroptosis and modulate immune in OM progression [21, 22]. Therefore, this study isolated and purified EVs from BM-MSCs with high FTO expression to investigate their regulatory effects and mechanisms on OM progression.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

Bone tissue and blood samples were collected from the 920th Hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force during November 2023 to August 2024, which included healthy control (N = 6) and OM patient (N = 10). Healthy controls included patients undergoing fracture fixation or hip arthroplasty without evidence of bone infection, autoimmune disease, or osteoporosis. Control bone tissues were acquired from surgically excised fragments with no therapeutic value, pathologically confirmed as healthy. Patients with OM were confirmed to have S. aureus as the sole or primary pathogen in the infected bone lesions through microbiological culture, PCR, or genomic sequencing. Cases with co-infections involving other bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa) or fungi were excluded. All participants were signed informed consent forms, and the experiments in this project were approved by the ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Isolation and identification of BM-MSCs

Primary BM-MSCs were obtained from mouse bone marrow. In brief, three 6-week-old mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and their femurs and tibias were subsequently isolated. The bone marrow cavities were flushed with low-glucose DMEM medium containing 10% FBS to collect bone marrow cells. After filtration through a 70-µm cell strainer, the cells were centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 10 min. The cell pellet was then resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and seeded into culture dishes. Adherent BM-MSCs were obtained after 3–5 days of culture, and the cells were passaged several times to obtain highly purified BM-MSCs. The BM-MSCs were then subjected to osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation induction, and the multipotency of the isolated cells was confirmed by Alizarin Red, Oil Red O, and Alcian Blue staining assays, respectively.

Transfection

The overexpression vectors for YTH domain-containing family protein 1 (YTHDF1), FTO, autophagy related 2 A (ATG2A), ATG14, NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3), and METTL3, the knockdown vectors for YTHDF1, METTL3, and METTL14, and the corresponding negative control vectors were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. When the cell density reached approximately 80% or higher, the corresponding vectors were transfected into cells using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent kit (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 11668019).

EV sources

Peripheral blood-derived EVs: Peripheral blood samples were collected from clinical subjects using EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. BM-MSC-derived EVs: BM-MSCs were transfected with FTO overexpression vectors and negative control vectors, respectively. When cell confluence reached 80 ~ 90%, cultures were washed thrice with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and maintained in serum-free medium for 48 h. The supernatant was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris and dead cells.

Isolation and identification of EVs

The supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 2000 × g for 30 min to remove cell remnants and 10,000 × g for 60 min to remove small debris. Finally, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 90 min to collect the EVs pellet. The EVs pellet was resuspended in PBS for identification. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, 10 µL of the purified EVs sample was applied onto a 200-mesh carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to settle for 1 min. Excess liquid was carefully blotted away with filter paper. The sample was negatively stained by applying 10 µL of 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min, followed again by blotting to remove excess stain. The grid was then air-dried at room temperature for 5 min. Imaging was performed using a Hitachi HT-7700 transmission electron microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV, with images captured at a magnification of ×60,000. For nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), the purified EV sample was serially diluted in PBS (1:10, 1:100, 1:1000). The NanoFCM N30E instrument was calibrated using 100 nm silica beads (Fuji Silysia, Cat. No. NFS-0100) to ensure a particle size detection error of less than 5%. The optimal dilution ratio was determined by targeting an event rate of 200–400 events/s, specifically selecting a dilution yielding an event rate within the ideal range of 280 ± 50 events/sec. Data acquisition involved capturing at least 1,000 valid events, and particle size distribution was analyzed using the NanoFCM Professional V1.0 software. For Western Blot analysis, EVs proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0013B). The total protein concentration was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0398S). Subsequently, 20 µg of total protein per lane was loaded for Western blot.

Cell treatment

The mouse embryonic osteoblast precursor cells MC3T3-E1 (Stem Cell Technology, Cat. No. STM-CL-6033) and mouse osteoblastic cells MLO-Y4 (Stem Cell Technology, Cat. No. STM-CL-6502) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Cat. No. C11875500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Science Cell, Cat. No. 0025) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (ScienceCell, Cat. No. 0503) at 37 °C in an environment of 5% CO2, and the culture medium was changed 2–3 times per week. To simulate an in vitro model of OM, the cells were treated with 10 µg/mL SpA for 12 h. 20 µg/mL EVs were used for cell treatment [11, 23].

Uptake of PKH67-labeled EVs

BM-MSCs-derived EVs were fluorescently labeled using the PKH67 kit (Beyotime, Cat. No. C3635M). 100 µg of EVs were resuspended in diluent C, incubated with PKH67 dye (10 µM final concentration) for 5 min at room temperature, the reaction was terminated with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and unbound dye was removed via ultracentrifugation. Labeled EVs were then co-cultured with MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 for 24 h. After co-culture, cells were washed to remove unbound EVs, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and counterstained with DAPI for nuclei. EV-cell fusion was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Real-time qPCR

Bone tissue specimens (50 mg) were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen after surgical resection, mechanically pulverized under cryogenic conditions, and homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Sigma, Cat. No. 93289) at a ratio of 1 mL reagent for RNA extraction. The cell samples (1 × 10⁶ cells) were washed three times with PBS, followed by the addition of 1 mL of Trizol reagent for lysis and RNA release on ice for 15 min. For serum EVs, total RNA was extracted using the exoRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 77164), with ≥ 50 ng purified EV-RNA used as input. Subsequently, the total RNA was purified using chloroform-isopropanol method. The extracted total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using the Reverse Transcription PCR Kit (Roche, Cat. No. DL-RC-4913914001). Real-Time qPCR was then performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Cat. No. DL-RC-4913914001). The thermal cycling program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, annealing at 56℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72℃ for 1 min, with a final extension at 72℃ for 5 min. The primers were designed and synthesized by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd., and their sequence information is detailed in Table 1.

Western blot

Bone tissues (200 mg per specimen) underwent decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) with agitation at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by protein extraction using 1 mL RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, Cat. No. P0044), with the clarified lysate supernatant obtained by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Parallelly, cellular proteins were extracted by direct lysis in RIPA buffer (200 µL per 10⁶ cells) on ice for 30 min, with protein concentrations determined by BCA assay. Subsequently, the proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (ThermoFisher, Cat. No. 88518). The membrane was then blocked with 10% skim milk for 2 h. Afterward, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C on a shaker. Following washing, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The proteins were visualized by adding ECL chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher, Cat. No. 34577) and the images were captured using the MiniChemi 610 system. The antibodies used are detailed in Table 2.

CCK-8 assay

MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates and pre-cultured overnight. After the medium was changed, the cells were subjected to group-specific treatments for 12 h. Subsequently, 20 µL of CCK-8 reagent (0.5 mg/mL; Beyotime, Cat. No. C0039) was incubated with each well of cells for 1 h. The OD values of the cells at a wavelength of 450 nm were measured using a FC microplate reader.

Trypan blue staining

The MC3T31-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells that were subjected to grouped treatment were washed twice with PBS, followed by the addition of an appropriate amount of trypsin for digestion in the incubator. The cells were then dispersed by repeated pipetting. A volume of 0.5 mL of the cell suspension was mixed with an equal volume of 0.4% Trypan Blue solution (Solarbio, Cat. No. 72-57-1) at room temperature for 5 min. Under the microscope, dead cells were observed to be stained blue, while live cells remained transparent. The proportion of dead cells was then counted.

Immunofluorescence staining

The MC3T3-E1 cells cultured in vitro were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA for 5 min. Primary antibodies against GSDMD-N or LC3 (diluted at 1:800) were applied for overnight incubation at 4 °C. Afterward, cells were incubated with FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 65-6111) at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI. The fluorescent signal was observed and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18 in the cell culture supernatants were analyzed using IL-1β ELISA kits (Coibo Bio, Cat. No. CB10173-Mu) and IL-18 ELISA kits (Coibo Bio, Cat. No. CB10172-Mu), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The standard curves were plotted and the content of inflammatory factors was calculated.

OM animal models

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital. A hole was drilled on the surface of the femur using a bone drill, and 0.1 mL of Staphylococcus aureus suspension (concentration 1 × 10^7 colony-forming unit/mL) was injected into the bone marrow cavity using a micro-injector. The wound was then sutured. On days 5, 8, and 11 after modeling, 0.1 mL of EV suspension (100 µg/mL) was injected into the infected bone marrow cavity or around the abscess [26]. On day 14 after modeling, the mice were euthanized and the femoral tissues were harvested for analysis.

Micro-CT

All harvested samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4℃, and then scanned by Micro-CT Skyscan 1276 system with the following parameters: 60 kV voltage, 200 µA current, 0.25 mm Al filter, 205 ms integration time, and 6.05 μm isotropic voxel size. Density calibration was performed using a hydroxyapatite phantom (CaHA, Bruker). The anatomically defined VOI comprised distal femoral trabecular bone spanning 0.5–2.0 mm proximal to the growth plate (250 slices, 1.51 mm height). Quantitative analysis of bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, µm), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, µm), bone mineral density (BMD, mg HA/cm³) and structure model index (SMI) was performed using CTAn v1.20.3.0 software, following reconstruction in NRecon v1.7.4.2 with ring artifact correction set to 10 and beam hardening compensation at 30%, and 3D visualization via grayscale distance transformation in CTvox v3.3.0, with quality control enforcing rescanning when motion artifacts exceeded 5%.

Me-RIP-qPCR

The level of RNA m6A modification was analyzed using an RNA m6A Detection Kit (RiboBio, Cat. No. C11051-1). First, the total RNA extracted from MC3T3-E1 cells and MLO-Y4 cells was subjected to ultrasonic fragmentation. A 50 µL RNA sample was taken as the Input sample, and the rest was used for the IP group. Each group of RNA sample was incubated with 4 µg m6A or IgG antibodies at 4℃ for 4 h in a vertical shaker. Subsequently, protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4℃ for 1 h. After adsorption of the magnetic beads by the magnetic rack, 200 µL of elution buffer was added, and the RNA was extracted for Real-Time qPCR analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized using SPSS 18.0 and GraphPad Prism 8 software. Measurement data were presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation. First, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check whether all data were normally distributed. For data that were normally distributed, the independent samples t-test was used to compare differences between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences among multiple groups, followed by post-hoc analysis with Tukey’s test. For data that were not normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare differences among multiple groups. Each experiment was repeated at least three times, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The expression levels of m6A-related genes are closely associated with OM

To explore the regulatory patterns of m6A modification in OM, we first measured the expression of m6A modification-related genes. The gene expression heatmap showed that compared with healthy human bone tissue, the mRNA expression levels of METTL3 and YTHDF1 were upregulated, while the mRNA expression level of FTO was significantly downregulated in the tissues of OM patients (Fig. 1A). Subsequent Western Blot experiments also confirmed this point: compared with healthy human bone tissue, the protein expression levels of METTL3 and YTHDF1 were upregulated in clinical samples of OM, while FTO protein expression was significantly downregulated (Fig. 1B). In addition, 10 µg/mL SpA was used to stimulate mouse embryonic osteoblast precursor cells MC3T3-E1 and mouse osteoblast-like cells MLO-Y4 as an in vitro cell model of OM. Real-Time qPCR and Western Blot experimental results confirmed that SpA treatment significantly increased the mRNA and protein expression levels of METTL3 and YTHDF1 in MC3T3-E1 cells and MLO-Y4 cells, and decreased the mRNA and protein expression levels of FTO (Fig. 1C-D). Subsequently, these two types of cells were respectively transfected with FTO overexpression vectors, METTL3 knockdown vectors, and YTHDF1 knockdown vectors, and we found that overexpression of FTO, knockdown of METTL3 or YTHDF1 significantly increased the cell viability and decreased the cell death rate of MC3T3-E1 cells and MLO-Y4 cells (Fig. 1E-F). According to previous research reports, FTO can be transferred between cells in the form of EVs [27, 28], suggesting that FTO derived from EVs of the peripheral blood of patients may serve as a potential molecular target for disease diagnosis and prognosis. Therefore, we also collected and separated EVs derived from the peripheral blood of OM patients and healthy volunteers. The results showed that compared with serum EVs derived from healthy individuals, the protein expression level of FTO in serum EVs derived from OM patients was significantly downregulated, while the content of FTO mRNA remained unchanged (Fig. 1G-H), indicating that FTO protein from EVs might serve as a potential diagnostic target for OM.

BM-MSCs-derived FTO-EVs alleviate SpA-induced cell death

Given the critical association between low serum EVs-FTO levels and OM progression, we hypothesized that FTO delivery via MSCs-derived EVs might confer therapeutic benefits. BM-MSCs were initially isolated from mouse bone marrow, exhibiting spindle-shaped or elongated morphologies under light microscopy (Fig. 2A). Multilineage differentiation potential was confirmed through osteogenic (Alizarin Red S staining), adipogenic (Oil Red O staining), and chondrogenic (Alcian Blue staining) induction assays (Fig. 2A). Then, BM-MSCs were transfected with either FTO-overexpression or negative control vectors, and Real-Time qPCR analysis demonstrated significantly elevated FTO mRNA levels in FTO overexpression vectors-transfected BM-MSCs compared to controls (Fig. 2B). EVs were subsequently isolated from these cells, and designated as FTO-EVs and NC-EVs, respectively. EVs characterization revealed typical saucer-shaped vesicular structures by TEM, with comparable particle sizes between groups (NC-EVs: 98.5 ± 18.2 nm; FTO-EVs: 96.9 ± 15.9 nm; p > 0.05) as quantified by NTA (Fig. 2C). While both EV groups showed similar protein concentrations (Fig. 2D) and canonical EV markers (CD63/CD81) without Calnexin contamination (Fig. 2E), FTO-EVs exhibited markedly higher FTO protein expression versus NC-EVs (Fig. 2E), confirming successful engineering of FTO-enriched EVs.

In functional studies, PKH67-labeled EVs effectively fused with MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cell membranes (Fig. 3A). Co-culture with FTO-EVs, but not NC-EVs, significantly upregulated FTO protein (but not mRNA) levels in both cell types (Fig. 3B-C). In SpA-induced OM models by treatment with 10 µg/ml SpA for 12 h, FTO-EVs treatment notably attenuated SpA-mediated cytotoxicity, as evidenced by improved cell viability and reduced mortality (Fig. 3D-E), and upregulated cell cycle regulators (CDK2, CDK6, and Cyclin D1) (Fig. 3F). These findings collectively demonstrate that BM-MSC-derived FTO-EVs mitigate SpA-induced cellular damage in OM models in vitro.

FTO-EVs alleviate SpA-induced autophagy and pyroptosis

SpA has been implicated in triggering autophagy in affected cells in OM [29, 30]. In vitro, SpA treatment markedly upregulated ATG2A and ATG14 mRNA levels in MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells. However, co-culture with FTO-EVs significantly suppressed SpA-induced elevations in ATG2A and ATG14 transcripts compared to SpA alone or SpA + NC-EVs groups. (Fig. 4A-B). Consistently, SpA increased autophagic flux, as evidenced by elevated ATG2A, ATG14, and LC3B-II/I protein levels alongside reduced p62 expression in both cell types (Fig. 4C). Strikingly, FTO-EVs reversed these effects, downregulating ATG2A, ATG14, and LC3B-II/I while restoring p62 expression (Fig. 4C). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that FTO-EVs attenuated SpA-driven LC3 accumulation in MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 4D), collectively demonstrating the inhibitory role of FTO-EVs in SpA-induced autophagy.

FTO-EVs inhibit OM progression in animal models

To validate the effects of FTO-EVs on OM in vivo, we established a model of OM in mice and injected EVs into the bone joints of the mice. Micro-CT analysis demonstrated severe cortical bone damage with irregular surfaces in the Model group compared to Sham controls (Fig. 6A), evidenced by 41.0% reduction in bone volume fraction (BV/TV), 23.7% decrease in BMD, 106.7% increase in Tb.Sp, and structural deterioration (SMI surged to 2.54 ± 0.68). While NC-EVs provided limited improvement, FTO-EVs treatment potently reversed these alterations, restoring BV/TV (recovery rate = 75.4%), BMD (88.9%), and substantially ameliorating Tb.Sp (68.8%) and SMI (73.9%), collectively confirming its efficacy in promoting bone regeneration (Table 3). The subsequent histopathological staining experimental results demonstrated that compared with the Sham group, the Model group exhibited severe trabecular destruction with erosive edges and massive neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 6B). In contrast to the Model group, both NC-EVs and FTO-EVs effectively restored bone tissue morphology in mice. However, FTO-EVs demonstrated a significantly superior effect compared to NC-EVs in suppressing OM. Compared with the Sham group, the expression levels of autophagy-related proteins (ATG2A, ATG14, and NLRP3) and cell death-related proteins (Bax) in the Model group were significantly upregulated, while the expression levels of cell cycle-related proteins (CDK2 and CDK6) were significantly downregulated in lesion bone tissues (Fig. 6C). FTO-EVs significantly reduced the protein expression of ATG2A, ATG14, NLRP3, and Bax in bone tissue, while increasing the protein expression of CDK2 and CDK6. These results indicate that FTO-EVs alleviate the progression of OM in vivo by inhibiting autophagy, cell death, and pyroptosis.

FTO-EVs regulate the expression of autophagy- and pyroptosis-related genes in a m6A-dependent manner

M6A modification plays an important role in the regulation of gene expression. In addition to FTO, which acts as an m6A demethylase, our experiments also found that the expression of the m6A reader protein YTHDF1 was significantly upregulated in MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells stimulated by SpA (Fig. 7A). Moreover, the total m6A level in the cells (Fig. 7B) and the m6A enrichment levels of ATG2, ATG14, and NLRP3 were all significantly upregulated by SpA (Fig. 7C). However, FTO-EVs had no effect on the expression of YTHDF1 but significantly reduced the total m6A modification level and the m6A modification levels of ATG2, ATG14, and NLRP3 in SpA-treated cells. Subsequently, to investigate whether FTO-EVs regulate the expression levels of ATG2, ATG14, and NLRP3 genes in an m6A-dependent manner, MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells were transfected YTHDF1 knockdown and overexpression vectors, as well as METTL3 and METTL14 knockdown vectors, and co-cultured with EVs in vitro. Knockdown of YTHDF1 significantly reduced the mRNA and protein expression levels of ATG2A, ATG14, and NLRP3 in MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells (Fig. 7D-E). However, overexpression of YTHDF1 significantly promoted the expression levels of ATG2A, ATG14, and NLRP3, which was reversed by knockdown of METTL3 and METTL14 or treatment with FTO-EVs (Fig. 7D-E). This indicates that FTO-EVs m6A dependently downregulate the expression levels of autophagy and pyroptosis-related genes.

FTO-EVs inhibit SpA-induced autophagy by regulating the m6A-ATG2A/ATG14 axis

ATG2A and ATG14 are key genes involved in cellular autophagy, but further experimental verification is needed to determine whether the regulation of autophagy by FTO-EVs is mediated via the m6A-ATG2A/ATG14 axis. To this end, MC3T3-E1 and MLO-Y4 cells were transfected with overexpression vectors for ATG2A, ATG14, and METTL13, and these cells were treated with SpA and co-cultured with FTO-EVs. Real-Time qPCR confirmed the successful overexpression of these genes (Fig. 8A). Consistent with the above findings, FTO-EVs significantly inhibited SpA-induced autophagy in both cell lines. However, the overexpression of ATG2A, ATG14, and METTL13 markedly reversed the inhibitory effect of FTO-EVs on autophagic flux, as evidenced by increased LC3B-II/I protein ratios, enhanced LC3 fluorescence intensity, and reduced p62 protein expression, ultimately leading to decreased cellular viability (Fig. 8B-D). These results confirm that FTO-EVs suppress SpA-induced autophagy by inhibiting the m6A-ATG2A/ATG14 axis.

Furthermore, we evaluated the impact of FTO-EVs on bone formation markers and osteoclast regulatory signals under SpA-induced pathological conditions. In MC3T3-E1 cells, SpA significantly suppressed mRNA expression of key osteogenic markers, including runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and osteocalcin (OCN), and dysregulated the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL)/ osteoprotegerin (OPG) axis by inhibiting OPG secretion while promoting RANKL release (Fig. S1A-B). Notably, FTO-EVs treatment not only restored osteogenic transcription to near-baseline levels but also corrected the RANKL/OPG imbalance, shifting the ratio toward an anti-osteoclastic profile. Crucially, overexpression of METTL3 completely abolished these protective effects, reversing both the transcriptional activation of osteogenic genes and the normalization of RANKL/OPG secretion. These results demonstrate that FTO-EVs restore osteogenic gene expression and normalize RANKL/OPG balance through an m6A methylation-dependent mechanism.

Discussion

m6A modification extensively participates in the progression of various inflammatory diseases, cancers, and other pathologies by regulating gene expression. In our clinical study, we found that METTL3 and YTHDF1 expression was significantly unregulated in OM bone tissue, while FTO expression levels were markedly downregulated in both bone tissue and serum EVs of OM patients. These findings were further validated in SpA-induced osteoblast and osteocyte-like cell models, where FTO expression was downregulated by SpA treatment. This is consistent with previous findings: Gao et al.. confirmed that METTL3 accelerates SpA-induced OM progression by regulating downstream gene expression in an m6A-dependent manner [15]. Song et al.. discovered that FTO suppresses S. aureus-induced ferroptosis in BM-MSCs and alleviates osteopathological damage [21]. These results collectively demonstrate that m6A-regulated genes play a significant role in OM progression, and targeting these key molecules may reverse the initiation and development of OM.

Therefore, this study employed FTO-overexpressing BM-MSCs to isolate EVs for therapeutic applications in both in vitro and in vivo OM models. Our data suggested that FTO-EVs significantly alleviated SpA-induced cell death in MC3T3 and MLO-Y4 cells in vitro and mitigated bone damage in S. aureus-induced OM mouse models in vivo, while NC-EVs showed minimal therapeutic effects. EVs derived from MSCs exhibit dual therapeutic mechanisms in OM: directly exerting anti-inflammatory and tissue-repairing effects within the bone microenvironment, or serving as efficient drug delivery vehicles for targeted transport of therapeutic molecules [31, 32]. However, the therapeutic efficacy of natural EVs in disease treatment remains limited. Engineered EVs may represent a promising frontier, as evidenced by recent advancements [33]. Targeted modification of EVs cargo (e.g., overexpression of therapeutic proteins or delivery of functional RNAs) can synergistically enhance EV functionality and prolong therapeutic effects [34, 35]. For instance, a study has shown that EVs loaded with the antimicrobial peptide cathelicidin exhibit superior serum stability and antiviral activity [36]. A notable example involves hydrogel-encapsulated engineered EVs carrying bone morphogenetic protein-2 mRNA, which achieved sustained release of the molecule to promote bone regeneration [34]. Furthermore, EVs derived from allogeneic BM-MSCs demonstrate low immunogenicity and minimal rejection risk, highlighting their significant therapeutic advantages for clinical translation.

Autophagy and pyroptosis are two pivotal cellular processes that play essential roles in infection, inflammation, and immune regulation [37]. Both have been reported to contribute to the pathogenesis of OM through distinct mechanisms: Autophagy exhibits dual effects: it suppresses infection by clearing intracellular pathogens (e.g., S. aureus), yet excessive autophagy activation may induce osteocyte death and perpetuate the inflammatory microenvironment [29, 38]. Pyroptosis, a form of inflammatory programmed cell death mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome, is activated during infection. While it aids in pathogen clearance, dysregulated pyroptosis may paradoxically facilitate bacterial dissemination and exacerbate autophagy and death of host cells [30, 39, 40]. In the SpA-induced OM cellular model, we demonstrated that SpA treatment promoted autophagic flux by upregulating key autophagy-regulators (ATG2A, ATG12, and LC3B-II/I) and downregulating p62 expression, and activated pyroptosis via accelerating the expression of NLRP3 and the cleavage of caspase-1 and GSDMD, collectively accelerating OM progression. Mechanistically, FTO-EVs were shown to suppress autophagy and pyroptosis through demethylation-dependent downregulation of autophagy regulators (ATG2A/ATG14) and pyroptosis-associated molecules (NLRP3). Functional rescue experiments confirmed these targets as critical downstream mediators of FTO-EVs in modulating both pathways. Emerging studies indicate m6A modification regulates bone metabolism: m6A demethylase ALKBH5 promotes osteoblast differentiation by stabilizing Runx2 mRNA [41]; During osteoclastogenesis, m6A levels and methyltransferase METTL3 expression increases [42]; METTL3 knockdown suppresses osteoclast-specific genes, exacerbates iNOS/NO-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction by stabilizing Nos2 mRNA in a YTHDF1-dependent manner, further inhibiting differentiation [43]. As an m6A demethylase, FTO counteracts METTL3-driven hypermethylation, potentially promoting osteoblast differentiation and suppressing osteoclastogenesis. This established regulatory paradigm is corroborated by our findings that FTO-EVs upregulated osteogenic markers and inhibited osteoclast signals in SpA-stimulated MC3T3-E1 cells.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that FTO-enriched EVs derived from BM-MSCs significantly ameliorate SpA-induced autophagy and pyroptosis through an m6A demethylation-dependent mechanism, thereby attenuating S. aureus-associated OM. These results collectively provide a novel therapeutic strategy and theoretical foundation for targeting S. aureus-related OM. Although the local injection of FTO-EVs in our study demonstrated promising efficacy in treating osteomyelitic bone defects, this administration route may present limitations for the clinical translation to deep-seated or disseminated infections. The development of advanced delivery systems is therefore crucial to achieve efficient targeting. Among these, localized, sustained-release systems based on biocompatible hydrogels hold significant promise. For instance, encapsulating FTO-EVs within an injectable hydrogel (e.g., based on hyaluronic acid or collagen) could allow for direct application into the debrided bone cavity during surgery. This system would form a gel in situ at body temperature, creating a depot for the prolonged and localized release of FTO-EVs. This approach could maintain effective therapeutic concentrations at the infection site, thereby maximizing the osteogenic and anti-inflammatory benefits while minimizing systemic exposure. Future studies will focus on optimizing the loading efficiency, release kinetics, and efficacy of such delivery platforms in complex infectious environments.
