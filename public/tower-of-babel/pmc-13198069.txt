The ECG in Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Fatima M Ezzeddine, Samuel J Asirvatham
Published: 2026

The electrocardiogram (ECG) remains one of the most widely available diagnostic tools in cardiovascular medicine. In congenital heart disease (CHD), it reflects the underlying cardiac anatomy, surgical history, and evolving electrophysiologic substrates. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, we present a series of cases that underscore the value of the ECG in the evaluation and longitudinal care of patients with congenital cardiac conditions.

The case by Ader et al1 illustrates the importance of integrating ECG findings in patients with overlapping congenital substrates. The ECG demonstrates features attributable to both Ebstein’s anomaly and surgically repaired tetralogy of Fallot (ToF), including high-amplitude right bundle branch block (RBBB) with right ventricular hypertrophy, findings typical of repaired ToF but atypical of isolated Ebstein’s anomaly, where the functional right ventricle is often small (Figure 1). Concomitant features that were typical of Ebstein’s anomaly included tall, broad “Himalayan” P waves, first-degree atrioventricular block, and fragmented QRS complexes reflecting atrial enlargement and delayed conduction across the atrialized right ventricle. It is worth noting that in Ebstein’s anomaly, the main problem is highly disordered myocardial fibers in the atrialized portion of the ventricle that result in conduction delay (ie, late fragmentation), with no evidence of hypertrophy, whereas in repaired ToF, there is hypertrophy of the right ventricle with or without dilation and delay that is based on the degree of pulmonary regurgitation and the age of the patient. When both congenital entities coexist, there is a hypertrophied segment of the true right ventricular inflow and outflow tract with disorganized myocardium in the atrialized inlet portion, and whichever predominates determines the ECG features.

The coexistence of a Q-wave in lead V1 and RBBB is unusual because septal activation typically proceeds from left to right, generating an initial R-wave in lead V1. This atypical pattern can occur in conditions such as completely absent septum, extensive septal infarction, or significant counterclockwise cardiac rotation in the setting of suprasystemic right ventricular pressures, which reorients the septum parallel to lead V1. In this combined substrate of Ebstein’s anomaly and repaired ToF, a Q-wave in lead V1 despite the presence of RBBB reflects altered initial depolarization vectors that may result from distorted septal orientation, postsurgical changes from ToF repair, and right ventricular remodeling, particularly hypertrophy of the right ventricular outflow tract, which can dominate early vectors and shift them leftward.

The case by Cotter et al2 shows another example of ECG findings in a patient with overlapping congenital conditions. A young woman with a history of repaired ToF had an ECG pattern suggestive of dextrocardia, prompting further investigation that revealed the rare coexistence of ToF with situs inversus totalis. In dextrocardia, the diagnosis of right ventricular hypertrophy is facilitated by right-sided precordial lead placement, after which standard criteria of right ventricular hypertrophy—dominant right precordial R waves, right-axis deviation, and strain pattern—can be appropriately applied. Beyond its diagnostic value, the ECG in patients with ToF also serves as a longitudinal marker of right ventricular remodeling and arrhythmogenesis. This emphasizes the importance of serial ECG assessment in patients with repaired CHD, where evolving electrical patterns may reflect underlying hemodynamic and structural changes.

In the case by Borkey et al,3 the ECG serves as an early clue to an underlying structural disease in a pediatric patient with low right atrial rhythm, an absent right superior vena cava (SVC), and a persistent left SVC. Most patients with persistent left SVC retain a right-sided sinus node, but a minority, particularly in the presence of right atrial isomerism, can have a left-sided sinus node that manifests with a left-to-right atrial activation vector characterized by positive P waves in leads II, III, and aVF, with negative P waves in leads I and aVL.4 In this case of persistent left SVC with absent right SVC, the P-wave is negative in leads III and AVF and isoelectric in lead II, yet positive in lead I. These findings are consistent with a low right atrial focus, although junctional rhythm with retrograde atrial activation cannot be excluded.

Finally, the case by Zhang et al 5 broadens the scope of this discussion by highlighting a nontraditional congenital entity—a coronary artery fistula—manifesting in a patient presenting with chest pain and ventricular tachycardia. The ECG revealed diffuse T-wave inversion across the limb and precordial leads, reflecting a global repolarization abnormality. Although such findings often prompt evaluation for ischemia or cardiomyopathy, this case points to the importance of considering coronary steal physiology secondary to a large fistula in the differential diagnosis.

Across these cases, several unifying themes emerge. First, the ECG remains a bridge between cardiac anatomy and electrophysiology, particularly in CHD, where structural abnormalities and surgical interventions create unique electrical signatures that evolve in a largely predictable pattern. Second, the ECG provides important prognostic information, with features such as QRS complex fragmentation, conduction delay, and repolarization abnormalities serving as markers of arrhythmic risk. Third, unexpected ECG findings should prompt careful reconsideration of underlying anatomy, including the possibility of additional congenital anomalies or incomplete repair. Perhaps most importantly, these cases remind us that the ECG is not a static tool, but a dynamic one. In patients with CHD, many who now survive well into adulthood, the ECG serves as a valuable longitudinal tool for assessing cardiac remodeling, disease progression, and arrhythmic risk.

Despite being more than a century old, the ECG retains a unique role: it is immediate, accessible, and deeply informative. This series of cases challenges us to look beyond pattern recognition and instead integrate ECG findings with anatomy, physiology, and clinical context. In doing so, we can continue to unlock the full diagnostic and prognostic potential of this tool in the care of patients with CHD.
