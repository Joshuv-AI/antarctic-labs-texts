Quantum Wall States for Noise Mitigation and Eternal Purity Bounds Journal: Quantum Science and Technology
Miguel Casanova, Francesco Ticozzi

Abstract
The present work analyzes state-stabilization techniques for decoupling a subsystem from environmental interactions. The proposed framework uses analytical and numerical tools to find an approximate decoherence-free subspace (DFS) with enhanced passive noise isolation. Active state-stabilizing control on a subsystem mediating dominant environmental interactions, which we call wall subsystem, creates an effective quantum wall state. The proposed method controls only the wall subsystem, leaving the logical subsystem untouched. This simplifies logic operations in the protected subsystem, and makes it suitable for integration with other quantum information protection techniques, such as dynamical decoupling (DD). We demonstrated its effectiveness in enhancing the performance of selective or complete DD. Under suitable conditions, our method maintains system purity above a threshold for all times, achieving eternal purity preservation. Theoretical analysis links this behavior to the asymptotic spectrum of the Hamiltonian when the control gain grows unbounded.

Introduction

In the effort of creating quantum information processing devices able to move the technology past the NISQ era
[
1
]
, for many potential physical implementations the detrimental effect of noise still represents a crucial barrier. To avoid or mitigate this issue, several noise avoidance and noise suppression techniques have been proposed, supplementing efforts to engineer suitable physical layers.
The first class, which focuses on passive protection from errors by encoding information in decoherence-free subspaces and noiseless subsystems
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, offers the advantage of having the manipulation of the encoded information unhindered by the presence of control sequences, or correction protocols. However, the presence of a viable noiseless code is granted only in presence of symmetries in the (dominant) coupling between the system and the environment, and the information might need to be stored in some states that are highly entangled with respect to the physical subsystems decomposition, which might thus be hard to prepare.
The noise suppression methods, such as dynamical decoupling
[
5
]
and active error correction
[
6
,
7
]
, (see
[
8
]
and references therein for a more comprehensive review) target the effect of noise effects via symmetrization or undoing the information leakage from a given code by employing typically fast and strong control actions, which have to be intertwined with the control actions needed to implement the desired information processing
[
9
]
.

In this work, we offer a pathway toward the construction approximately noiseless codes by the use of active methods, where the controls limiting the effect of noise do not act directly on the encoded information. In this sense, the proposed method can be seen as a
hybrid
noise protection approach.
We proceed by first separating the degrees of freedom of the system of interest in two factor (virtual) subsystems: the
logical subsystem
, corresponding to the support of the encoded information, and the
wall subsystem
, mediating the leading interaction terms with the environment. The state and dynamics of the latter are then engineered to block such interactions by preparing and stabilizing a state that minimizes purity loss in the code subsystem, which we call the
wall state
.

The interaction between the system and its environment is assumed to be Hamiltonian, while the dynamics on the latter is allowed to include dissipative, time-homogenous dynamics in Lindblad form. That is, we consider a
structured
environment, which combines non-Markovian coupling with the system and is allowed to further interact with a memory-less reservoir, leading to Markovian dissipation terms.
The toolbox we develop includes:

1.

A numerical method, based on gradient-based Rimeannian optimization, for the optimal selection of the code and wall subsystem;

2.

A prescription for the selection of a special state of the interface subsystem, the
wall state
, to minimize purity loss on the code. This can be done either numerically or following an heuristic strategy, which is proved to be optimal in the qubit case;

3.

Multiple ways to actively stabilize the wall state and improve the lifetime of encoded information. We consider engineered Markovian dynamics in Lindblad form, zeno-inducing repeated measurements, and strong Hamiltonian driving.

In building our methodologies, we also show that points 1. and 2. above can be interpreted as a way to find and stabilize approximate decoherence-free subspace, see
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
and references therein.
From another viewpoint, the method can be seen as an alternative way to enforce a selective quantum Dynamical Decoupling (DD)
[
5
,
13
]
of the code subsystem. Two differences with typical dynamical decoupling scenarios are to be remarked: first, we do not aim to decouple the whole physical system of interest, but only a portion of it, whose dimension is fixed by the QIP task at hand; second, we are only aiming to suppress the leading term of the interaction, similar to what is done e.g. in
[
13
,
14
]
.
Given this limitation, in the simulation section of the paper we investigate the interplay between our method and standard DD, revealing how the performance of the latter can be improved by combining with our wall-state techniques if some resonance conditions are avoided. A third connection emerges with the approach developed in
[
15
]
, where pulsed sequences are used in non-Markovian open systems to effectively generate approximate fixed points, or
pointer states
. Our problem of interest can be seen as a generalization of the one considered there, where the target to be stabilized is not a single state but a whole subspace, or subsystem; the methods we use are also different, as we couple passive encodings with strong coherent or dissipative driving.

In addition, we show how stabilizing the wall state by strong Hamiltonian driving can lead to a remarkable feature, not exhibited by the other incoherent control methods: the purity of the code system can be preserved above a certain threshold for arbitrarily long times, giving rise to an effective
eternal purity bounds
. An explanation of the phenomenon in terms of the evolution of the coherences is provided, together with sufficient conditions for its emergence based on spectral gaps in the underlying Hamiltonian dynamics.

Some seminal ideas used in this work have been presented in
[
16
]
, in a simplified setting where the logical and interface (wall) subsystems were assumed to be known and many technical details and proofs were missing. We here generalize, advance, and provide formal proofs for the results of that work, developing a complete theory and a
systematic toolobox
to wall-state engineering. In particular (each item highlights a key improvement with respect to the previous work), we:

(i)

Pose the problem for a general finite-dimensional controlled system coupled to an environment, possibly structured and/or with Markovian dissipation, via a general interaction Hamiltonian (Section
2
; before we only considered a logical-wall subdivision of the system to be given);

(ii)

Compare and interpret the approach in the framework of decoherence-free subspaces (DFS), showing that
perfect wall states
are equivalent to DFSs for systems composed by
n
identical subsystems (Section
4
): in this sense, our general method can be seen as a way to find the best approximate DFS one can engineer in a given system;

(iii)

Develop a method for
finding
optimized logical-wall subsystem decomposition, including a critical regularization functional (Section
5
);

(iv)

Analyze in detail the purity dynamics, and propose optimization problems to select the wall states that minimizes the initial purity acceleration (Section
6
), with an analytical solution for the qubit case;

(v)

Demonstrate the performance of our wall state selection algorithms and different stabilization techniques in richer and more complex examples, including a
5 spin-1/2 chain
with three-body interactions and a
central spin model
(Section
8
);

(vi)

Compare and integrate the method with dynamical-decoupling techniques (Section
9
);

(vii)

Provide an in-depth analysis and spectral conditions that guarantee the emergence of
eternal purity preservation
, a remarkable phenomenon one can observe, under suitable conditions, while stabilizing the wall state with a strong Hamiltonian driving (Section
10
).

2
Setup

Consider a finite-dimensional quantum system with Hilbert space

{\cal H}={\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
, where
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
corresponds to
a controllable system of dimension
n_{\mathfrak{s}},
and
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
to the environment of dimension

n_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
We consider a decomposition of the system’s Hamiltonian into local and interaction terms, i.e.

H=H_{\mathfrak{s}}+H_{\mathfrak{e}}+H_{\mathfrak{se}},

(1)

where
H_{\mathfrak{s}}=\check{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}

and
H_{\mathfrak{s}}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes\check{H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
In addition, we consider cases in which the evolution of the environment subsystem
includes dissipative effects described by a Markovian master equation
[
17
,
18
]
. Therefore, the
state dynamics follows

\dot{\rho}(t)={\cal L}(\rho)=-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu[H,\rho(t)]+\sum_{m}{\cal D}_{L_{m}}(\rho),

(2)

where
{\cal D}_{L_{m}}(\rho)=L_{m}\rho(t)L_{m}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{m}^{\dagger}L_{m},\rho(t)\},
where
we only consider Lindblad operators of the following form,

L_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes L_{m}^{\mathfrak{e}}.

(3)

This compactly models a structured environment
\cal H_{\mathfrak{e}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{m}}
, where we traced out
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{m}}
,
an infinite-dimensional subsystem that
induces Markovian dynamics in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
and has no interaction with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
.
Under these assumptions, the evolution of
\rho_{\mathfrak{s}}=\tr_{\mathfrak{e}}(\rho)
becomes non unitary only due to its Hamiltonian coupling with the environment via
H_{\mathfrak{se}}.

The first task of our work is to identify the subsystem of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
subject to the dominant
terms of the interaction with
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
. Once this subsystem has been identified,
we can decompose
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
into such a subsystem
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}},
which we shall call the
wall subsystem
,
and its tensor complement
\cal H_{\mathfrak{l}}
the
logical subsystem
, defined such that
\cal H_{\mathfrak{s}}=H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}
.
This decomposition allows us to apply arbitrary control actions on
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
,
even dissipative ones, without directly affecting the information stored in the logical subsystem. We shall look for control actions such that the purity of the reduced state in
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
is maximized.

This setup allows for the implementation of quantum gates or other quantum information
processing protocols to
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
completely independently of the noise-suppressing
control protocol, which is applied to
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
instead, in what could be called a
decoupled decoupling
strategy.
In this way, we “sacrifice” the possibility of encoding quantum information
in
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
and instead transform its state as a control resource. A state optimized for noise protection will be
called a
wall state.

3
Motivating example: Ising chain

Consider as a toy model the one-dimensional Ising model of length
N
with longitudinal field.
Each of the spins in the chain has an associated Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{i}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2},i=1,...,N
,
whereas the whole chain has a Hilbert space
{\cal H}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}{\cal H}_{i}
.
Suppose that we aim to use the spins up to the
j
-th to store quantum information that we
want to preserve, whereas the spins starting from
j+1
are uncontrollable.
Therefore, we take the latter to act as
the environment, i.e.
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{j}{\cal H}_{i}
,

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}=\bigotimes_{i=j+1}^{N}{\cal H}_{i}
and
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
Let
\sigma_{x,y,z}
denote the Pauli matrices, and define

J_{i}^{z}=\mathds{1}^{\otimes i-1}\otimes\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes N-i}.

(4)

The system has the following Hamiltonian,

H=h\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{i}^{z}+g_{z}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}J_{i}^{z}J_{i+1}^{z},

(5)

which can then be decomposed into the following local and interaction terms

H_{\mathfrak{s}}=h\sum_{i=1}^{j}J_{i}^{z}+g_{z}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}J_{i}^{z}J_{i+1}^{z},

(6)

H_{\mathfrak{e}}=h\sum_{i=j+1}^{N}J_{i}^{z}+g_{z}\sum_{i=j+1}^{N-1}J_{i}^{z}J_{i+1}^{z},

(7)

H_{\mathfrak{se}}=g_{z}J_{j}^{z}J_{j+1}^{z}.

(8)

It is easy to see that for this model it suffices to use the
j
-th spin as wall subsystem in order to
decouple the two sides of the chain. More precisely, as above,

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}={\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
,
where
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{j-1}{\cal H}_{i}
and

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}={\cal H}_{j}
. Under this setup,
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
may
be perfectly decoupled from the environment just by setting the initial state of

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
equal to either
\lvert 0\rangle_{j}
or
\lvert 1\rangle_{j}
, where

J_{j}^{z}\lvert 0\rangle_{j}=\lvert 0\rangle_{j}
and
J_{j}^{z}\lvert 1\rangle=-\lvert 1\rangle
.
Let us fix
\lvert w\rangle=\lvert 0\rangle_{j}
, then we can verify that if

\rho(0)=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
, we have that

\rho(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHt}\rho(0)e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHt}=\tilde{\rho}_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)\otimes\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\otimes\tilde{\rho}_{\mathfrak{e}}(t),

(9)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathfrak{l}}=U_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}U_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)
,

\tilde{\rho}_{\mathfrak{e}}=U_{\mathfrak{e}}(t)\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}U_{\mathfrak{e}}(t)
and in turn

U_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)=(\prod_{i}^{j-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muhJ_{i}^{z}t})(\prod_{i}^{j-2}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mug_{z}J_{i}^{z}J_{i+1}^{z}})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mug_{z}J_{j-1}^{z}t}

(10)

and

U_{\mathfrak{e}}(t)=(\prod_{i=j+1}^{N}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muhJ_{i}^{z}t})(\prod_{i=j+1}^{N-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mug_{z}J_{i}^{z}J_{i+1}^{z}})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mug_{z}J_{j+1}^{z}t}.

(11)

In regards to the evolution of
\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert
, we have that it is static, since

U_{\mathfrak{w}}(t)\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert U_{\mathfrak{w}}(t)^{\dagger}=\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert
, where

U_{\mathfrak{w}}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(h+2g_{z})J_{j}^{z}t}.

(12)

It is clear that with this choice of initial state of the
j
-th spin, no correlations can occur
between any of the
i
-th and
k
-th spins, where
i<j
and
k>j
, since it would
need to happen via the
j
-th spin, which experiences no dynamics.

4
Ideal Case: Perfect Decoupling via Wall States

4.1
Perfect wall states

In the previous example, we saw how
with an appropriate choice of wall subsystem and wall state, one may perfectly
decouple the two subsystems without the need of any kind of additional control. In particular,
no control action is applied to the system where the information is stored, leaving it
fully available to other kinds of information processing controls.
This behavior motivates the following definition.

Definition 1
.

Consider a system with Hilbert space
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}

and Hamiltonian
H
. Let the
\cal H_{\mathfrak{w}}
partition be initialized to pure state
\lvert w\rangle

and the total initial state be factorized
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
Then
\lvert w\rangle
is said to be a
perfect wall state
if the reduced dynamics on the logical subsystem
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)=\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHt}\rho_{0}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHt})
are
unitary for all initial
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
.

In the Ising chain example the choice of wall subsystem and state is trivial, while in general this might not be the case. Assuming for now that we consider systems such that
all the interactions between the logical subsystem and the environment are mediated by a subsystem, the behavior of the example can be generalized as follows.

Proposition 1

(Perfect wall eigenstates)
.

Consider a system with Hilbert space
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}
with
Hamiltonian

H=H_{\mathfrak{l}}+H_{\mathfrak{w}}+H_{\mathfrak{e}}+H_{\mathfrak{lw}}+H_{\mathfrak{we}},

where

H_{\mathfrak{l}}=H_{L}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
,

H_{\mathfrak{e}}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{E}
,

H_{\mathfrak{w}}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{W}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
,

H_{\mathfrak{lw}}=\sum_{j}B_{j}\otimes C_{j}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
and

H_{\mathfrak{we}}=\sum_{j}\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes D_{j}\otimes E_{j}
. If there exists a representation such that all of the
H_{W}
,
C_{j}
and
D_{j}
operators share a common eiegenstate, such state is a perfect wall state.

Proof.

Denote the common eigenstate of the
H_{W}
,
C_{j}
and
D_{j}
operators as
\lvert w\rangle
.
Then, each of these operators has an eigen-decomposition of the following form:

X_{k}=\lambda_{w,k}\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert+\sum_{i\neq w}\lambda_{i,k}\Pi_{i,k}=\lambda_{w,k}\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert+\tilde{H}^{w^{\perp}}_{\mathfrak{w},k},

(13)

where
X_{k}\in\{\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}},H_{W},C_{j},D_{j},\forall j\}
, and

\tilde{H}^{w^{\perp}}_{\mathfrak{w},k}\lvert w\rangle=\Pi_{i,k}\lvert w\rangle=0,\forall i\neq w
, and

\Pi_{i,k}\Pi_{j,k}=0,\forall i\neq j
.
By substituting this decomposition back into the full Hamiltonian, we obtain

H=\sum_{i}\lambda_{w,i}\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{l},i}\otimes\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\otimes\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{e},i}+\sum_{k}\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{l},k}\otimes\tilde{H}^{w^{\perp}}_{\mathfrak{w},k}\otimes\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{e},k},

(14)

by collecting all the
\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert
terms together, and where

\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{l},k}\in\{\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}},H_{L},B_{j},\forall j\}
and

\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{e},k}\in\{\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}},H_{E},E_{j},\forall j\}
.
Since
\tilde{H}^{w^{\perp}}_{\mathfrak{w},k}\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert=0,\forall k
,
the two sums commute and we can write the evolution operator as

U=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHt}=U_{w}U_{w^{\perp}},

(15)

where
U_{w}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\sum_{i}\lambda_{w,i}\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{l},i}\otimes\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\otimes\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{e},i})t}
and

U_{w^{\perp}}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{l},k}\otimes\tilde{H}^{w^{\perp}}_{\mathfrak{w},k}\otimes\tilde{H}_{\mathfrak{e},k})t}
.
Then, it is easy to verify that

U_{w^{\perp}}\sum_{ik}\alpha_{ik}\lvert i\rangle\otimes\lvert w\rangle\otimes\lvert k\rangle=\sum_{ik}\beta_{ik}\lvert i\rangle\otimes\lvert w\rangle\otimes\lvert k\rangle,

(16)

U_{w}\sum_{ik}\alpha_{ik}\lvert i\rangle\otimes\lvert w\rangle\otimes\lvert k\rangle=\sum_{ik}\mu_{ik}\lvert i\rangle\otimes\lvert w\rangle\otimes\lvert k\rangle.

(17)

Therefore, the state
\lvert w\rangle
remains fixed. Finally, since there is no direct interaction between

\cal H_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
\cal H_{\mathfrak{e}}
, no entanglement can occur between these two subsystems
if the initial state is a product state such that
\tr_{\mathfrak{le}}(\rho)=\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert
.
∎

When a perfect wall state exists, we can interpret this passive information protection
in terms of a unitarily evolving subspace. This correspondence is
detailed in the next section.

4.2
Perfect walls and DFS

In many-body systems formed by a number of identical subsystems, the existence of a perfect wall state is equivalent to
the existence of a decoherence-free subspace. The latter are defined as follows
[
2
]
:

Definition 2
.

Consider a Hilbert space
\cal H
that may be decomposed as
\cal H\simeq H_{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus H_{\mathfrak{r}}
.
If any state
\rho
that is initialized with support only on
\cal H_{\mathfrak{q}}
evolves unitarily,
then
\cal H_{\mathfrak{q}}
is said to be a Decoherence-Free Subspace (DFS).

Proposition
1
is closely related to the following well-known characterization of DFS
[
19
,
20
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a bipartite system
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}
with Hamiltonian
H=H_{\mathfrak{s}}+H_{\mathfrak{e}}+H_{\mathfrak{se}}
.
Let
H_{\mathfrak{se}}=\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}
. If the following two conditions are satisfied, the system has a DFS

\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{q}}={\rm span}(\{\lvert\psi_{k}\rangle\}_{k})

such that
\cal H_{\mathfrak{s}}\simeq H_{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus H_{\mathfrak{r}}
(with unitary dynamics generated by
H_{\mathfrak{s}}
),

A_{i}\lvert\psi_{k}\rangle=\lambda_{i}\lvert\psi_{k}\rangle,\ \forall i,\lvert\psi_{k}\rangle,

(18)

{\rm supp}(H_{\mathfrak{s}}\lvert\psi\rangle)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{q}},\ \forall\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{q}},

(19)

i.e. if the operators
A_{i}
have a common (degenerate) eigenspace that is left invariant by
H_{\mathfrak{s}}
.

In the example from the previous section, we may identify
A_{1}=J^{z}_{j}
and

B_{1}=J^{z}_{j+1}
and verify that Theorem
1
confirms the existence of a DFS:
It suffices to check that the eigenspaces of
A_{1}
are left invariant by
H_{\mathfrak{s}}
.
We formalize the general connection between DFS and perfect wall states in the following result.

Proposition 2
.

If
{\rm dim}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}})={\rm dim}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}})n_{\mathfrak{w}},
for some integer
n_{\mathfrak{w}}
, then a perfect wall state exists if and only if a DFS exists.

Proof.

Given a system with Hilbert space
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}

with a DFS
\cal H_{\mathfrak{q}}
such that
\cal H\simeq(H_{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus H_{\mathfrak{r}})\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
and
{\rm dim}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{r}})=n_{\mathfrak{r}}=n_{\mathfrak{q}}(n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1)
,
where
n_{\mathfrak{q}}={\rm dim}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{q}})
and
n_{\mathfrak{w}}\in\mathbb{N}
.
In such a case,
\cal H_{\mathfrak{r}}
can be interpreted as
n_{\mathfrak{w}}
copies of
\cal H_{\mathfrak{q}}
,
i.e.
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{r}}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{q}}
.

In such a case, by choosing a basis
\{\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle\}\otimes\{\lvert\psi_{j}\rangle\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
,
such that
{\rm span}(\{\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle\otimes\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\}_{i})=\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{q}}
, we can identify

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}={\rm span}\{\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle,i=0,...,n_{\mathfrak{l}}-1\}
and

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}={\rm span}\{\lvert\psi_{i}\rangle,i=0,...,n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1\}
.

Under this setting, we see that

\cal H\simeq H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}\simeq H_{\mathfrak{q}}\oplus H_{\mathfrak{r}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}},

and that
\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle
is a perfect wall state. More precisely,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes{\rm span}(\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle)\simeq\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{q}}
.
This means that initializing the state
\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}
of
\cal H_{\mathfrak{w}}

as the perfect wall state
\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}\rvert
results in a system state
\rho_{\mathfrak{s}}

such that
\rho_{\mathfrak{s}}\in\cal H_{\mathfrak{q}}
.
Conversely, if we initialize the state of the system inside of
\cal H_{\mathfrak{q}}
and
then we trace out the logical system and the environment, we have
\tr_{\mathfrak{le}}(\rho)=\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}\rvert
.
∎

5
General case: Finding an optimal wall subsystem

Requiring that a perfect wall state (or a DFS) exists is too restrictive in realistic scenarios.
Therefore, in the following sections we propose a method to make these choices in general cases,
even when no perfect wall state exists, by means of Riemannian optimization.
This approach has been successfully used in a number of applications where optimization over fixed-dimension subspaces or unitary transformations is necessary, as done in e.g.
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

In order to treat general (approximate) cases, we first
search for a unitary
U=U_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
that
allows us to identify the (virtual) degrees of freedom that mediate the dominant
interaction with the environment. After an optimal
\hat{U}
is found, the controllable
subsystem can be decomposed into a logical and a wall subsystems,

{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{s}}\simeq{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
.
With this decomposition, we then look for a state of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
that optimally
minimizes the interaction between
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
In this section we propose an optimization problem to choose the optimal
\hat{U}
.
In light of the previous section, this can be seen as (the first step of) a method to
find approximate DFSs.
The next step shall be the selection of the wall step, which we will address later.

5.1
Optimal wall subsystem via Riemannian optimization

Let us denote the dimension of each subsystem
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{u}}
as
n_{\mathfrak{u}}
, where

\mathfrak{u}\in\{\mathfrak{l},\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{e}\}
.
In the same way, let
\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{u}}
be the identity matrix of dimension
n_{\mathfrak{u}}\times n_{\mathfrak{u}}
.
Similarly, let
\{\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{u}}\}|_{i=0}^{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}-1}
be
a basis of the space of Hermitian matrices of dimension
n_{\mathfrak{u}}\times n_{\mathfrak{u}}
,
such that
\sigma_{0}^{\mathfrak{u}}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{u}}/\sqrt{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}
and
\tr(\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{u}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{u}})=\delta_{ij}
.
Examples of such bases would be the Pauli or Gellmann matrices, including the identity
and normalized to one (instead of the usual normalization to two).
We then abbreviate the notation of tensor products of two or more basis elements as

\sigma_{ij}^{\mathfrak{uv}}=\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{u}}\otimes\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{v}}
.
Finally, we implicitly specify the dimensions of an operator by choosing the
indices. For example,
H_{\mathfrak{we}}
would stand for an operator of dimension
n_{\mathfrak{w}}n_{\mathfrak{e}}\times n_{\mathfrak{w}}n_{\mathfrak{e}}
.

The space of observables associated to Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{u}}
is denoted as

{\cal O}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{u}})
, whereas the convex set of density matrices is denoted
as
{\mathfrak{D}}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{u}})
. Since the set of pure density matrices is the
set of extremal points of
{\mathfrak{D}}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{u}})

[
24
]
,
we use
{\rm extr}({\mathfrak{D}}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{u}}))
to
denote it.

In order to identify the optimal choice of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
, we try to find
a unitary operator
U=U_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
, such that we can decompose
the system’s Hamiltonian as

U^{\dagger}HU=H_{\mathfrak{l}}+H_{\mathfrak{w}}+H_{\mathfrak{e}}+H_{\mathfrak{lw}}+H_{\mathfrak{we}}+\Delta

(20)

where
\Delta=H_{\mathfrak{le}}+H_{\mathfrak{lwe}}
.
The choice of
U_{\mathfrak{s}}
should be done such that it minimizes the norm of
\Delta
.
Let

h_{ijk}=\tr(\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}}H),

(21)

so that

H=\sum_{ijk}h_{ijk}\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}}.

(22)

Notice that we can then re-write
\Delta
selecting only the relevant basis elements as follows:

\Delta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tr(\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}}U^{\dagger}HU)\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}}.

(23)

Using the orthonormality of the basis, we can now write

\|\Delta\|^{2}=\tr(\Delta^{\dagger}\Delta)=\tr(\Delta^{2})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}|\tr(A_{ij}C_{k})|^{2}
, where
A_{ij}=U_{\mathfrak{s}}\sigma_{ij}^{\mathfrak{lw}}U_{\mathfrak{s}}^{\dagger}
and
C_{k}=\sum_{ab\geq 0}h_{abk}\sigma_{ab}^{\mathfrak{lw}}
. We can
then use this expression to define the following cost function

J(U_{\mathfrak{s}})=\|\Delta\|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}|\tr(A_{ij}C_{k})|^{2}

(24)

We then propose the following optimization problem
on the special unitary group
{\rm SU}(n_{\mathfrak{s}})
,

\hat{U}_{\mathfrak{s}}=\underset{U_{\mathfrak{s}}\in{\rm SU}(n_{\mathfrak{s}})}{\mathrm{argmin}}J(U_{\mathfrak{s}}).

(25)

Notice that, at this point, no minimization is done on the terms
H_{\mathfrak{lw}}
(nor
H_{\mathfrak{we}}
).
In the following subsection we introduce a regularization term to
limit any detrimental effect due to
H_{\mathfrak{lw}}
that may occur due to the transformation
U_{\mathfrak{s}}
.

When equipped with the metric
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(A^{\dagger}B)
,
the Lie group
{\rm SU}(n_{\mathfrak{s}})
becomes a Riemannian manifold.
This means that Problem (
25
) can be solved by
Riemannian gradient descent
[
25
]
.
The Riemannian gradient of (
24
) can be computed following the
steps outlined in

[
26
,
27
]
,
resulting in

{\rm grad}J(U_{\mathfrak{s}})=-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tr(A_{ij}C_{k})[A_{ij},C_{k}]U_{\mathfrak{s}},

(26)

where
U_{\mathfrak{s}}\in{\rm SU}(n_{\mathfrak{s}})
and
{\rm grad}J(U_{\mathfrak{s}})\in T_{U_{\mathfrak{s}}}{\rm SU}(n_{\mathfrak{s}})
.
Therefore, the exponential map solving the gradient flow

\dot{U}_{\mathfrak{s}}(t)=-{\rm grad}J(U_{\mathfrak{s}})
is the following,

U_{\mathfrak{s}}(t+\epsilon)={\rm exp}(-\Omega\epsilon)U_{\mathfrak{s}}(t)

(27)

where
\Omega=-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tr(A_{ij}C_{k})[A_{ij},C_{k}]
. More details on the implementation
of a numerical algorithm that solves this problem is given in
A
.

After settling on a solution
\hat{U}_{\mathfrak{s}}
and with
\hat{U}=\hat{U}_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
we use
\hat{U}
to perform a change of basis. We call the new frame the “
\hat{U}
-frame”,
and the Hamiltonian becomes

\begin{split}\hat{H}=&\hat{U}^{\dagger}H\hat{U}=\sum_{ijk}g_{ijk}\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}}\\
=&\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{l}}+\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{w}}+\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{e}}+\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}+\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{we}}+\hat{\Delta},\end{split}

(28)

where
g_{ijk}=\tr(\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}}\hat{H})
. Equivalently,
by defining
\hat{A}_{ij}=\hat{U}_{\mathfrak{s}}\sigma_{ij}^{\mathfrak{lw}}\hat{U}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{\dagger},

we have that
g_{ijk}=\tr((\hat{A}_{ij}\otimes\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}})H)
.

5.2
Regularization

The previous optimization problem allows us to find subsystem decompositions that
minimize the
direct
interaction between the logical subsystem and the environment.
However, these solutions are not unique, and some of them might lead to frames in which
the information loses coherence faster (in the absence of control) than in the original frame,
by increasing the interaction between
\cal{H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
\cal{H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
.
In order to promote solutions in which the latter is kept low (or even provide
a full decoupling when possible), a regularization term can be added to the cost function.
In particular, we add the squared norm of
H_{\mathfrak{lw}}
, resulting in the following regularized
cost function,

J_{\rm reg}(U_{\mathfrak{s}})=\|\Delta\|^{2}+\eta_{\rm reg}\|H_{\mathfrak{lw}}\|^{2},

(29)

where
\eta_{\rm reg}
is the regularization hyperparameter. Its Riemannian gradient is

\begin{split}{\rm grad}J_{\rm reg}(U_{s})=&-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tr(A_{ij}C_{k})[A_{ij},C_{k}]U_{\mathfrak{s}}-2\sum_{i,j>0}\tr(A_{ij}C_{0})[A_{ij},C_{0}]U_{\mathfrak{s}}.\end{split}

(30)

Then, the gradient flow is solved by the same exponential as before, substituting

\Omega
by
\Omega_{\rm reg}=-2(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k>0\\
j\geq 0\end{subarray}}\tr(A_{ij}C_{k})[A_{ij},C_{k}]+\sum_{i,j>0}\tr(A_{ij}C_{0})[A_{ij},C_{0}])
.
The introduction of the regularization is further motivated analytically and tested in the example
given in Section
8.1
.

6
General case: Purity dynamics and the choice of wall state

After finding a good choice of wall subsystem, we proceed to search for an
optimal choice of initial wall state. The purity variation is used as quality index.
That is, given an initial product state
\rho=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
we search for a state
\lvert w\rangle
that minimizes the initial decay of the purity of the logical subsystem
\cal H_{\mathfrak{l}}
.

6.1
Purity loss acceleration

We wish to study the dynamics of the purity

\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))=\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\rho(t))^{2})=\tr(\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)^{2})
of the state

\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}(t)=\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\rho(t))\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}})
,
where
\rho(t)\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\cal H})
. In particular, we are interested in the
initial dynamics of the purity when the initial state
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}

is a product state. Our goal will then be to find a way to manipulate these dynamics
in order to extend the time that the purity remains sufficiently close to one,
as a way to reduce information loss to the environment.
The first derivative of the purity is as follows,

\dot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)=2\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}({\cal L}(\rho))\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\rho)).

(31)

However, notice that if
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
,

\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}([\sigma_{ijk}^{\mathfrak{lwe}},\rho_{0}])=\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})[\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}},\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}],

(32)

and if
L_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes L_{m}^{\mathfrak{e}}
,
we have
\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}({\cal D}_{L_{m}}(\rho))=0.

This means that whenever the initial state is a product state, the initial
purity loss rate is equal to zero,

\begin{split}\dot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}=&-2\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\sum_{ijk}g_{ijk}\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})\tr([\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}},\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}]\rho_{l})=0.\end{split}

(33)

Therefore, this quantity is not suitable for our aims, and we resort to the second derivative, which has
the following general expression:

\begin{split}\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)=&2\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}({\cal L}\circ{\cal L}(\rho))\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\rho)+\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}({\cal L}(\rho))^{2}).\end{split}

(34)

Then, in order to find a function that depends only on the initial wall state
\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}
, we take the average of the initial
value of the second derivative
\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}
over all the initial pure logical states
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
and fix
the initial state of the environment
\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
This results in a function of
\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}
only, whose expression is given in Proposition
3
.

Proposition 3
.

Given a tripartite system with Hilbert space
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}

and factorized initial state
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
which follows Lindbladian dynamics with arbitrary Hamiltonian and noise operators
L_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes L^{\mathfrak{e}}_{m}
,
the second derivative of the purity
\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)
has the following average initial value over all the reduced states
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
,

\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}\rangle_{\mathfrak{l}}=4\nu_{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i>0\\
bcjk\geq 0\end{subarray}}g_{ibc}g_{ijk}(\tau_{c0}\tau_{0k}\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})-\tau_{ck}\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})),

(35)

where
\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{l}}}-\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{l}}(n_{\mathfrak{l}}+1)}
and

\tau_{ck}=\tr(\sigma^{\mathfrak{e}}_{c}\sigma^{\mathfrak{e}}_{k}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})
.

Proof.

The proof is given in
B
.
∎

6.2
Wall states minimizing the purity loss acceleration

A direct method to find a good choice of wall
state consists in solving an optimization problem that minimizes the
average initial purity loss acceleration (
35
).
In particular, we can parameterize
{\rm extr}({\cal O}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}))

as the complex-valued
(n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1)
-sphere,

S^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}(\mathbb{C})
and propose an optimization problem on it.

\lvert\hat{w}\rangle=\underset{\lvert w\rangle\in{\rm S}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}(\mathbb{C})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\Gamma_{1}(\lvert w\rangle),

(36)

where
\Gamma_{1}(\lvert w\rangle)=-(4\nu_{n})^{-1}\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}\rangle_{\mathfrak{l}}
.

Given a point
\lvert w\rangle\in S^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}(\mathbb{C})
and vectors

x,y
in the tangent space
T_{\lvert w\rangle}S^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}(\mathbb{C})
,
the complex sphere
S^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}(\mathbb{C})
equipped with the metric induced
by the inner product

\langle x,y\rangle_{\lvert w\rangle}=x^{\dagger}(\mathds{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert)y
is a Riemannian manifold.
Therefore, we can solve problem (
36
)
with the same algorithm used to solve
(
25
), but with the Riemannian gradient

\begin{split}{\rm grad}\Gamma_{1}(\lvert w\rangle)=&2\sum_{bcijk}g_{ibc}g_{ijk}(\tau_{c0}\tau_{0k}(2\langle\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rangle_{w}\langle\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rangle_{w}\lvert w\rangle-\langle\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rangle_{w}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\lvert w\rangle\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\ \ -\langle\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rangle_{w}\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\lvert w\rangle)-\tau_{ck}(\langle\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rangle_{w}\lvert w\rangle-\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\lvert w\rangle)),\end{split}

(37)

and exponential map

\lvert w(t+\epsilon)\rangle={\rm exp}((QX\langle w(t)\rvert-\lvert w(t)\rangle X^{\dagger}Q)\epsilon)\lvert w(t)\rangle,

(38)

where
Q=\mathds{1}-\lvert w(t)\rangle\langle w(t)\rvert/2
and
X=-{\rm grad}\Gamma_{1}(\lvert w(t)\rangle)
. More information on the implementation of
a numerical algorithm to solve this optimization problem is given in
A
.

6.3
Optimization with no direct LE interactions

The previous optimization problem becomes significantly simpler in the absence of direct Hamiltonian coupling between the logical subsystem and the environment.
In fact, assuming that
\hat{\Delta}=0
, the second derivative becomes

\begin{split}\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
\hat{\Delta}=0\end{subarray}}=&\frac{-4}{n_{\mathfrak{e}}}\sum_{abij>0}g_{ab0}g_{ij0}\tr(\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}){\rm Cov}_{\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}}(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}},\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}),\end{split}

(39)

where
{\rm Cov}_{\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}}(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}},\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}})=\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})-\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})
.

Notice that, as before, the terms with
a=0
and/or
i=0
do not contribute to the sum. However, now this is also
the case if
b=0,
and / or
j=0
. Therefore, the expression is simplified by restricting all four
indices to be greater than zero.
In other words, this expression is completely independent of all local Hamiltonian
terms and
\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{we}}
and depends only on the interaction term
\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}
.

Since the simplified initial purity loss acceleration depends only on a
single bipartite operator,
\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}
, we can take its
operator-Schmidt decomposition (OSD). This allows us to propose a choice
of wall state with an analytic expression by targeting the component of

\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}
with the highest singular value. In fact, the LW interaction Hamiltonian can be written as

\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}=\sum_{ij>0}g_{ij0}\sigma_{ij}^{\mathfrak{lw}}.

(40)

We can then arrange the coefficients into a matrix

G=[0]\oplus[g_{ij0}]_{ij>0}=[\tr(\sigma_{ij}^{\mathfrak{lw}}\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}})]_{ij\geq 0}
and
find its SVD
G=USV^{\dagger}
, where

U\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}\times n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}
,

V\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}^{2}\times n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}
,

S\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}\times n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}
and

n_{\mathfrak{u}}=\min\{n_{\mathfrak{l}},n_{\mathfrak{w}}\}
.
Due to the properties of the SVD,
U
and
V
are such that
U^{\dagger}U=V^{\dagger}V=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{u}}
,
and
S
is a diagonal matrix with semipositive entries ordered in non-increasing order, i.e.

S={\rm diag}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}})
where
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}
.
Additionally, because
\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}
is a purely interaction Hamiltonian (i.e. it does not contain any local term),
we have that for all
i>1
and
j<n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}
the entries
U_{in_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=U_{1j}=V_{in_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=V_{1j}=S_{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=0
, while

U_{1n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=V_{1n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=1
.

By defining new operators
C_{i}=\sum_{a\geq 1}U_{ai}\sigma_{a-1}^{\mathfrak{l}}
and
D_{j}=\sum_{b\geq 1}V_{bj}\sigma_{b-1}^{\mathfrak{w}}

we can rewrite the CW interaction Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}_{\mathfrak{lw}}=\sum_{i\geq 1}s_{i}C_{i}\otimes D_{i},

(41)

which is its OSD. Notice that due to the orthonormality of the columns of
U
and
V
,
the two sets of matrices
\{C_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{D_{i}\}_{i}
satisfy
\tr(C_{i}C_{j})=\delta_{ij}

and
\tr(D_{i}D_{j})=\delta_{ij}
, just as was the case for
\{\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\}_{i}

and
\{\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\}_{j}
.
We also have
\tr(C_{i})=0
and
\tr(D_{i})=0
for
i<n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}
and
C_{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=\sigma_{0}^{\mathfrak{l}}
and
D_{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}=\sigma_{0}^{\mathfrak{w}}
.

Writing
\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}
in terms of the OSD, we obtain

\begin{split}\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
\hat{\Delta}=0\end{subarray}}=&\frac{-4}{n_{\mathfrak{e}}}\sum_{ij}s_{i}s_{j}\tr(C_{i}C_{j}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-C_{i}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}C_{j}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}){\rm Cov}_{\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}}(D_{i},D_{j}),\end{split}

(42)

and its average over the pure states of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
is

\begin{split}\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
\hat{\Delta}=0\end{subarray}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{le}}=&-4\frac{\nu_{n}}{n_{\mathfrak{e}}}\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}{\rm Var}_{\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}}(D_{i}).\end{split}

(43)

Under these assumptions (no LE or no three-body interaction terms) the optimization problem (
36
) simplifies to:

\lvert\hat{w}\rangle=\underset{\lvert w\rangle\in{\rm S}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}(\mathbb{C})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle),

(44)

where
\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)=-(4\frac{\nu_{n}}{n_{\mathfrak{e}}})^{-1}\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=0\\
\hat{\Delta}=0\end{subarray}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{le}}
, i.e.

\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)=\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}{\rm Var}_{\lvert w\rangle}(D_{i}).

(45)

Problem (
44
) can also be considered as a simpler, yet approximate alternative to the general case.

We can solve problem (
44
)
with the same Riemannian gradient descent algorithm used to solve
(
36
), but with the following Riemannian gradient

\begin{split}{\rm grad}\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)=&\sum_{i}s_{i}^{2}(2(D_{i}^{2}\lvert w\rangle-\langle w\rvert D_{i}^{2}\lvert w\rangle\lvert w\rangle)-\\
&\qquad 4(\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i}\lvert w\rangle-(\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle)^{2}\lvert w\rangle)),\end{split}

(46)

and with
X=-{\rm grad}\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w(t)\rangle)
. See
A
for a more detailed
explanation of Riemannian gradient descent method.

, we can

In particular, the solution is given by the eigenstates of the dominant interaction term
D_{1}
,
i.e. wall states
\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
such that
D_{1}\lvert\hat{w}\rangle=\lambda\lvert\hat{w}\rangle

\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)

,

If
\Delta=0
and the chosen
\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
is a perfect wall state,
initializing the state of
\cal H_{\mathfrak{w}}
to
\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
would cause the logical subsystem

\cal H_{\mathfrak{l}}

from the rest of the system. However, if that is not the case, one could try different kinds

7
General Case: Stabilizing and Protecting the Wall State

After settling on a choice of wall subsystem (as in Section
5
) and wall state (as in Section
6
),
we want to improve the performance of the noise suppression protocol by stabilizing the wall state,
so that the optimization of the purity dynamics remains significant for longer times, with the purity
\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))

remaining close to one for longer times.

The first method to stabilize the initial state that we shall consider is
repeated measurements
:
If the frequency of the measurements is high enough, they induce a quantum Zeno effect

[
28
]
, slowing the drift of the wall state.
Next, we consider the addition of
engineered dissipation
terms to the wall, so that the desired wall state is approximately stabilized. Lastly, we will consider constant
strong Hamiltonian driving
as a
way to energetically decouple the wall subsystem from the other parts of the system. This last
approach, which also results in zeno-like dynamics, exhibits interesting phenomena, in which the purity oscillations may not just
slow down, but also shrink in amplitude with an increase of the control Hamiltonian amplitude. This phenomenon may, in certain cases, lead to
lower bounds on purity
\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))
that are valid for
at any time
. These might be seen as an approximate
eternal purity preservation
: see Figure
3c
, and Section
10
for an analytical treatment.
All these control methods are implemented and investigated numerically in the following subsections for prototypical examples.

7.1
Repeated measurements

We assume to be able to measure

\hat{\Pi}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
.
As such, if the measurement result is positive, the state
\rho
collapses to

\hat{\Pi}\rho\hat{\Pi}
, otherwise we obtain
(\mathds{1}-\hat{\Pi})\rho(\mathds{1}-\hat{\Pi})
.

\rho(t_{i}+\varepsilon)=\hat{\Pi}\rho(t_{i})\hat{\Pi}+\hat{\Pi}^{\perp}\rho(t_{i})\hat{\Pi}^{\perp},

(47)

where
\hat{\Pi}^{\perp}=(\mathds{1}-\hat{\Pi})
.
Measurements are performed with frequency
f
. We can then discretize the evolution as

\rho(t_{i+1})=\hat{\Pi}U_{1/f}\rho(t_{i})U_{1/f}^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}+\hat{\Pi}^{\perp}U_{1/f}\rho(t_{i})U_{1/f}^{\dagger}\hat{\Pi}^{\perp},

(48)

where
U_{1/f}=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\hat{H}/f)
and
t_{i}=i/f
. In the limit where

f\to\infty
, the system behaves according to the following effective Zeno
Hamiltonian
[
29
]
,

H_{Z}=\Pi H\Pi+\Pi^{\perp}H\Pi^{\perp}.

(49)

The resulting evolution operator is

e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muH_{Z}t}=\Pi e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\Pi H\Pi t}\Pi+\Pi^{\perp}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\Pi^{\perp}H\Pi^{\perp}t}\Pi^{\perp},

(50)

which clearly prevents any entanglement between the wall and the other partitions,
whenever the initial state has the form

\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{s}}\otimes\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
as it leaves the wall partition invariant.

7.2
Dissipation

If one could design dissipation terms acting on the wall subsystem by techniques such as
reservoir engineering and dissipative control
[
30
,
31
]
, one could implement dissipation operators such as

L_{i}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}^{\perp}_{i}\rvert\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}},

(51)

where
\{\lvert\hat{w}^{\perp}_{i}\rangle\}_{i}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}
are
n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1
mutually orthonormal
states such that
\langle\hat{w}\lvert\hat{w}^{\perp}_{i}\rangle=0,\forall i
. The state dynamics would then
be governed by

\dot{\rho}(t)=-\mathrm{i}[\hat{H},\rho(t)]+\eta\sum_{i}(L_{i}\rho L_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i},\rho\}).

(52)

One can prove
[
20
,
32
]
that the desired wall state

\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert
is the unique fixed point of the reduced dynamics
\tr_{\mathfrak{le}}(\rho(t)),
and hence it is asymtpotically stable. For increasing
\gamma,
the convergence becomes faster.

7.3
Strong Hamiltonian driving

Let there be a controllable Hamiltonian term
\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}

whose amplitude can be modulated via an input function
u(t)
. We are interested in studying the case where
u(t)=\kappa
is large. The driven
Hamiltonian would be the following

H_{\kappa}(t)=\hat{H}+u(t)\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}

(53)

H_{\kappa}=\hat{H}+\kappa\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}.

(54)

Assume also that
H_{u}
is nondegenerate. If
\kappa
is large enough, this kind of control
would consist of a detuning of the wall from the rest of the system, i.e. the eigenenergies
of
H_{\mathfrak{w}}
are taken away from any possible resonance with the eigenenergies of the
other Hamiltonian terms (except for any possible eigenenergy fixed at zero).

In particular, we want the control Hamiltonian to be of the form

H_{u}=\lambda_{\hat{w}}\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}\lambda_{\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}}\lvert\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rvert,

(55)

where
\lvert\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rangle
are
n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1
mutually orthonormal states such that
\langle\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\lvert\hat{w}\rangle=0
.
By the dynamical Zeno theorem
[
29
]
, we can then prove that in the limit
\kappa\to\infty
,
the dynamics of the system are governed by the following effective Hamiltonian

H_{\rm eff}=\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert\hat{H}\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}-1}\lvert\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rvert\hat{H}\lvert\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{w}^{\perp}_{j}\rvert.

(56)

Under this Hamiltonian, the wall state
\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
becomes a fixed point and therefore no entanglement can be formed
with the rest of the system.

8
Numerical Results

In this section, we provide a series of examples to demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. We start by considering two minimal three-qubit systems. This choice allows us to test the algorithms against a model with a simple known solution and highlight the importance of the regularization terms.
First, we introduce a toy Hamiltonian in order to test the algorithm to find the optimal subsystem
decomposition of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{s}}
independently of the choice of wall state. Next ,
we consider a three qubit Ising chain to test our method to choose the optimal wall state
and the stabilizing controls, independently of the choice of subsystem decomposition.

The whole method is then tested in two additional, richer examples. The first is a
spin lattice
with three-body interaction terms.
The other is a
central spin model,
in which a single 3/2-spin is decomposed
into logical and wall subsystems.

In all of the examples, we use a regularization hyperparameter of
\eta_{\rm reg}=0.01
and initialize
the environment to a thermal state with inverse temperature
\beta=0.01
,

\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}=\frac{e^{-H_{\mathfrak{e}}\beta}}{\tr(e^{-H_{\mathfrak{e}}\beta})}.

(57)

The starting point of the Riemannian gradient descent for subsystem decomposition is set to a random unitary,
whereas that for wall state optimization is set to
\lvert w_{0}\rangle=\mu(\lvert w_{D}\rangle+\lvert w_{r}\rangle/10)
, where
\lvert w_{D}\rangle

is an eigenstate of
D_{1}
,
\lvert w_{r}\rangle
is a random unit vector and
\mu
is a normalization constant.

9
Comparison and Integration with Dynamical Decoupling

A widespread and successful approach for the protection of quantum information is offered by quantum dynamical decoupling (DD) techniques
[
5
]
.
DD consists in actively symmetrizing the dynamics so that the leading orders in the coupling between the system and the environment are suppressed. This is done by the introduction of periodic control such that in the
Floquet (stroboscopic) picture, the system (approximately) follows a unitary evolution.
In this section, we wish to study how our quantum wall method and DD compare and complement each other.

In the following, the target system for the decoupling is considered to be a qubit.
In this case, an idealized universal zero-order DD pulse sequence, that is, a pulse sequence that effectively suppresses any interactions averaged in the pulse cycle time, is given by a sequence of four instantaneous unitary transformations
X,Z,X,Z
interspaced by free evolution on a given time
T/4.
Here
T
represents the length of the control cycle.
X,Z
are
\pi
-pulses along
the
x
and
z
axes, respectively:

X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},

(74)

Z=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(75)

The unitary evolution over a control cycle, including the free evolution, reads

\begin{split}U_{\rm DD}=&(Z\otimes\mathds{1})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHT/4}(X\otimes\mathds{1})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHT/4}(Z\otimes\mathds{1})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHT/4}(X\otimes\mathds{1})e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHT/4}.\end{split}

(76)

In some special cases, when the interaction terms are known and are all commuting, decoupling may be achieved by applying pulses along a single
axis. For instance, if the Hamiltonian is a pure dephasing Hamiltonian,
then we do not need to apply the pulses along the
z
axis, resulting in the
simplified sequence

U_{\rm DD}=Xe^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHT/2}Xe^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muHT/2}.

(77)

This is an instance of
selective decoupling
, where a simpler pulse sequence is tailored to suppress only the dominant error sources, rather than obtaining complete decoupling in some given order
[
13
]
.

The sequence (
76
) corresponds to an ideal case in which instantaneous
unbounded
\pi
-pulses are possible. A more realistic setting would be to
consider finite-amplitude pulses. More precisely, we consider the following
time-dependent Hamiltonian,

H_{\rm DD}=H+u_{x}(t)X+u_{z}(t)Z,

(78)

where

u_{x}(t)=\kappa(\Theta((t\ {\rm mod}\frac{T}{2})-\frac{T}{4}+\tau)-\Theta((t\ {\rm mod}\frac{T}{2})-\frac{T}{4})),

(79)

u_{z}(t)=u_{x}(t-T/4),

(80)

\Theta(t)
is the Heaviside function and
\kappa\tau=\pi/2
.
Equivalently, for the simplified sequence (
77
),
the corresponding pulses would be

u_{x}(t)=\kappa(\Theta((t\ {\rm mod}\frac{T}{2})-\frac{T}{2}+\tau)-\Theta((t\ {\rm mod}\frac{T}{2})-\frac{T}{2})),

(81)

u_{z}(t)=0.

(82)

In the following, we apply the DD to the spin lattice and the central spin models from the previous section
and study how it compares to our quantum wall method with strong Hamiltonian driving, as well as how they
can be used in tandem to further improve the protection of logical information stored in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
.

10
Eternally bounded purity

In the numerical exploration of the proposed methods, a remarkable phenomenon emerges, as illustrated in Figure
3c
:
under certain conditions
, the strong Hamiltonian driving exhibits a uniform lower bound on the decrease in purity. Not only that, but such a lower bound can be raised arbitrarily close to one by increasing the driving Hamiltonian gain. In contrast, all the other stabilization methods for the wall states only induce a reduced decrease rate for purity, which still approaches its minimum of 1/2 at later times.

We next proceed to characterize the conditions under which we have
the emergence of eternal lower bounds on the purity when strong Hamiltonian control is applied to the quantum wall.
In particular, we find (1) a time-independent lower bound for the purity in function of the initial state and the
system’s Hamiltonian (2) a series of conditions on the Hamiltonian so that the purity remains arbitrarily
close to
1
at every time
t
, if one suitably increases the strong Hamiltonian driving gain
\kappa
.

Consider a system
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}
with Hamiltonian

H_{\kappa}=H+\kappa\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{e}}
. Let
\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rangle
and
\lambda^{\kappa}_{i}

be the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
H_{\kappa}
, respectively. For the sake of simplicity, in this Section we do not consider any dissipative
term
L_{k}
: the calculations can be in principle extended to that case, but it would burden the notation and would require working with vectorized representation of the generators.
Therefore, the evolution of the system is purely Hamiltonian,

\begin{split}\rho(t)=&e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muH_{\kappa}t}\rho_{0}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muH_{\kappa}t}=\Big(\sum_{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{i}t}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rvert\Big)\rho_{0}\Big(\sum_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{j}t}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rvert\Big).\end{split}

(83)

Let us define the following instrumental quantities (with the explicit dependence on
\kappa
is omitted to lighten the notation):

\lambda_{abij}=\lambda^{\kappa}_{a}-\lambda^{\kappa}_{b}+\lambda^{\kappa}_{i}-\lambda^{\kappa}_{j},

(84)

\rho_{ij}=\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rvert\rho_{0}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rangle,

(85)

\tau_{ij}=\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rvert).

(86)

Then, the purity of the logical partition can be written as

\begin{split}\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))=&\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\rho(t))^{2})\\
=&\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}((\sum_{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{i}t}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rvert)\rho_{0}(\sum_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{j}t}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rvert))^{2})\\
=&\sum_{abij}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu(\lambda_{a}-\lambda_{b}+\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})t}\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{a}\rvert\rho_{0}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{b}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rvert\rho_{0}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rangle\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{a}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{b}\rvert)\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}(\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{i}\rangle\langle\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rvert))\\
=&\sum_{abij}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{abij}t}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})\\
=&\sum_{abij}\gamma_{abij}(t),\end{split}

(87)

where, we defined:

\gamma_{abij}(t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{abij}t}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij}).

(88)

Whenever
\lambda_{abij}=0

we have that
\gamma_{abij}(t)=\gamma_{abij}
is constant. Otherwise,

\gamma_{abij}(t)+\gamma_{baji}(t)=2\cos(\lambda_{abij}t)\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})
.
Let us define the set of indices for which the summands
\gamma_{abij}(t)
are not constant,

{\cal C}_{1}=\{(a,b,i,j)|\lambda_{abij}\neq 0\}.

(89)

This allows us to split the expression of the purity into two sums, one consisting only constant terms, and the other
being a superposition of oscillations

\begin{split}\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))=&\sum_{(a,b,i,j)\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})+\sum_{(a,b,i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{abij}t}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})\\
=&\bar{\gamma}+\vec{e}(t)^{\top}\vec{\rho},\end{split}

(90)

where
\bar{\gamma}=\sum_{(a,b,i,j)\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})
,

\vec{e}(t)=[e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{abij}t}]_{(a,b,i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}
and

\vec{\rho}=[\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})]_{(a,b,i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}
, where the last two quantities are vectors that have as many components as the elements of
{\cal C}_{1}.

The term
\bar{\gamma}
gives us the base value around which the purity oscillates, and the vector
\vec{\rho}

encodes the amplitudes of the individual oscillations, collected in
\vec{e}(t)
.

Ideally, we would like
\vec{\rho}=0
.
That would correspond to a perfectly preserved purity, i.e. a unitary evolution of the reduced
state
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
. If there exists a state
\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}=\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert

such that that is the case, independently of the initial states
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
, then it
is a perfect wall state, as the evolution of the reduced state
\rho_{\mathfrak{s}}
would be unitary independently
of its initial state and the state of the environment, which is precisely the statement of Definition
1
.
Then, by Proposition
2
we also know that a DFS exists.

In general, even if
\vec{\rho}\neq 0
, i.e. the system is not initialized in a DFS,
the bound given in the following proposition applies.

Proposition 5
.

Given a system with purity dynamics as described by (
90
),
then the following bound is satisfied for all time
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))\geq\bar{\gamma}-\|\vec{\rho}\|_{1}.

(91)

Proof.

It is easy to see that

\begin{split}\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho(t))=&\bar{\gamma}+\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\vec{e}(t)^{\top}\vec{\rho}\\
=&\bar{\gamma}+\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{(a,b,i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{abij}t}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})\\
\geq&\bar{\gamma}+\sum_{(a,b,i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}\min_{t\in\mathbb{R}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lambda_{abij}t}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})\\
=&\bar{\gamma}-\sum_{(a,b,i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}}|\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})|\\
=&\bar{\gamma}-\|\vec{\rho}\|_{1}.\end{split}

∎

At this point, we study the effect of the strong Hamiltonian driving on this bound.
We begin by introducing the following lemma, which will be useful to characterize conditions necessary
for the vector
\vec{\rho}
to vanish in the limit
\kappa\to\infty
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
H_{\kappa}
be the Hamiltonian of a tripartite system
{\cal H}
with a tunable parameter
\kappa
, such that

H_{\kappa}=H_{0}+\kappa\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1},

(92)

where
H_{u}
is non-degenerate. Then, its eigenstates
\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rangle
satisfy

\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\lvert\psi^{\kappa}_{j}\rangle=\sum_{ac}\alpha_{abc}^{(j)}\lvert a\rangle\lvert w_{b}\rangle\lvert c\rangle=\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle,

(93)

where
H_{u}\lvert w_{b}\rangle=\lambda^{u}_{b}\lvert w_{b}\rangle
. Namely, the eigenstates of
H_{\kappa}

converge to eigenstates of
\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}
, where the wall partition
is not entangled to any of the other two partitions.

Finally, the widths of the energy level splittings induced by
H_{0}
on
\kappa\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}
converge to

\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\langle\psi_{j}\rvert(H_{\kappa}-\kappa\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1})\lvert\psi_{j}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\psi}_{j}\rvert H_{0}\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle.

(94)

Proof.

Let
\{\lvert w_{b}\rangle\}_{b=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}}
be the eigenstates of
H_{u}
with corresponding eigenvalues

\{\lambda_{b}^{u}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{w}}}
. The eigenvalues are all distinct, since we are assuming

H_{u}
to be non-degenerate.

Cosider an arbitrary state
\lvert\psi\rangle
,
then

\begin{split}\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\frac{H\lvert\psi\rangle}{\|H\lvert\psi\rangle\|}=&\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\frac{H_{0}\lvert\psi\rangle+\kappa\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}\lvert\psi\rangle}{\|H_{0}\lvert\psi\rangle+\kappa\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}\lvert\psi\rangle\|}\\
=&\frac{\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}\lvert\psi\rangle}{\|\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}\lvert\psi\rangle\|}.\end{split}

(95)

This proves that indeed the eigenstates converge to those of
\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}
.
As
H_{u}
is nondegenerate, its eigenvectors
\{\lvert w_{b}\rangle\}
forms a basis of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
. Let
\{\lvert a\rangle\}_{a=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{l}}}

and
\{\lvert c\rangle\}_{c=1}^{n_{\mathfrak{e}}}
be bases of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}
and
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{e}}
, respectively.
Now express the state
\lvert\psi\rangle
in terms of the basis of
{\cal H}
formed from tensor products of the former,
i.e.
\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_{abc}\alpha_{abc}\lvert a\rangle\lvert w_{b}\rangle\lvert c\rangle
,

\begin{split}\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\frac{H\lvert\psi\rangle}{\|H\lvert\psi\rangle\|}=&\frac{\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}(\sum_{abc}\alpha_{abc}\lvert a\rangle\lvert w_{b}\rangle\lvert c\rangle)}{\|\mathds{1}\otimes H_{u}\otimes\mathds{1}(\sum_{abc}\alpha_{abc}\lvert a\rangle\lvert w_{b}\rangle\lvert c\rangle)\|}\\
=&\frac{\sum_{abc}\alpha_{abc}\lambda_{b}^{u}\lvert a\rangle\lvert w_{b}\rangle\lvert c\rangle}{\|\sum_{abc}\alpha_{abc}\lambda_{b}^{u}\lvert a\rangle\lvert w_{b}\rangle\lvert c\rangle\|}.\end{split}

(96)

But since the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{u}
are all distinct,
\lvert\psi\rangle
is an eigenstate
of
H
(in the limit
\kappa\to\infty
) if and only if the coefficients
\alpha_{abc}

are non-zero only for a single value of the index
b
, i.e. they have the form given by (
93
).
Finally, (
94
) can be verified by direct calculation.
∎

We split our study of
\vec{\rho}
into a characterization of the coefficients
\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}
and the traces
\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})
.
We begin by characterizing the cases for which

\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}=0
.
The terms
\rho_{ij}
involve the
initial state
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\rvert\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
where
\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
is an eigenstate of
H_{u}
. Then,
by Lemma
1
, we have that

\begin{split}\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\rho_{ij}=&\sum_{kl}\overline{\alpha_{k_{i}l_{i}}}\alpha_{k_{j}l_{j}}\langle k_{i}\rvert\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\lvert k_{j}\rangle\langle w_{i}\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\langle\hat{w}\lvert w_{j}\rangle\langle l_{i}\rvert\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}\lvert l_{j}\rangle,\end{split}

(97)

which is zero whenever
\lvert w_{i}\rangle\neq\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
or
\lvert w_{j}\rangle\neq\lvert\hat{w}\rangle
.
The following is the set of indices for which the product
\rho_{ab}\rho_{ij}
cannot be made
arbitrarily small by increasing
\kappa
,

{\cal K}_{1}=\{(a,b,i,j)|\lvert w_{a}\rangle=\lvert w_{b}\rangle=\lvert w_{i}\rangle=\lvert w_{j}\rangle=\lvert\hat{w}\rangle\}

(98)

Next, the trace
\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})
remains to be studied, where

\tau_{ij}
is purely dependent on the system’s
Hamiltonian.

\begin{split}\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})=&\sum_{k_{a}k_{b}l_{a}l_{b}p_{i}p_{j}q_{i}q_{j}}\alpha_{k_{a}l_{a}}\overline{\alpha_{k_{b}l_{b}}}\alpha_{p_{i}q_{i}}\overline{\alpha_{p_{j}q_{j}}}\langle k_{b}\lvert p_{i}\rangle\langle p_{j}\lvert k_{a}\rangle\langle w_{a}\lvert w_{b}\rangle\langle w_{i}\lvert w_{j}\rangle\langle l_{a}\lvert l_{b}\rangle\langle q_{i}\lvert q_{j}\rangle\\
=&\sum_{k_{a}k_{b}l_{a}l_{b}p_{i}p_{j}q_{i}q_{j}}\alpha_{k_{a}l_{a}}\overline{\alpha_{k_{b}l_{b}}}\alpha_{p_{i}q_{i}}\overline{\alpha_{p_{j}q_{j}}}(\langle k_{b}\rvert\otimes\langle p_{j}\rvert)(\lvert p_{i}\rangle\otimes\lvert k_{a}\rangle)\\
&\qquad(\langle w_{b}\rvert\langle l_{b}\rvert\otimes\langle w_{j}\rvert\langle q_{j}\rvert)(\lvert w_{a}\rangle\lvert l_{a}\rangle\otimes\lvert w_{i}\rangle\lvert q_{i}\rangle)\\
=&(\langle\tilde{\psi}_{b}\rvert\otimes\langle\tilde{\psi}_{j}\rvert){\rm SWAP}_{\mathfrak{ll}}(\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{a}\rangle\otimes\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{i}\rangle),\end{split}

(99)

where
{\rm SWAP}_{\mathfrak{ll}}(\lvert a\rangle\lvert b\rangle\lvert c\rangle\otimes\lvert i\rangle\lvert j\rangle\lvert k\rangle)=\lvert i\rangle\lvert b\rangle\lvert c\rangle\otimes\lvert a\rangle\lvert j\rangle\lvert k\rangle
.
We notice that the trace converges to zero whenever

{\rm SWAP}_{\mathfrak{ll}}(\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{a}\rangle\otimes\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{i}\rangle)
is orthogonal to

\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{b}\rangle\otimes\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle
. We now define the set of the indices
for which the trace
\tr(\tau_{ab}\tau_{ij})
cannot be made arbitrarily small by

Conclusion

The path towards the realization of robust, scalable devices for quantum information processing should arguably involve the use of multiple techniques aimed to producing suitable quantum hardware as well as mitigating the effect of noise in the device via protected encodings, correction protocols and noise suppression control. Being based on the system manipulation, rather than its physical building, multiple noise control methods can in principle be employed in synergy towards the needed noise suppression performance. However, the complexity of the resulting concatenated protocols and their implementation times can limit the resources available for the information processing task. We here focus on developing a method that can seamlessly used in combination with existing one, as the necessary control action does not directly affect the encoded information.

To this aim, the present work introduces and analyzes the potential of state-stabilization techniques towards the decoupling of a subsystem from environmental interaction.
The proposed framework relies on both analytical and numerical tools, which allow one to search for an (approximate) DFS, whose passive noise isolation properties can be enhanced by actively performing state-stabilizing control on a subsystem that
mediates the dominant interactions with the environment, creating an effective quantum wall state.
One notable advantage with respect to direct error-correcting and DD techniques is that the proposed method employs controls that act only on the wall subsystem and leave the
logical subsystem untouched. This setup allows for simpler implementations of logic operations in the protected subsystem.
It is also suitable for integration with other quantum information protection techniques, such as dynamical decoupling: in this sense, we have demonstrated in one example how our method can be used to enhance the performance of (selective or complete) dynamical decoupling.

When the state-stabilizing control is performed using strong Hamiltonian driving, we found that, under suitable conditions, the purity of the system remains
above a certain threshold
for all times
, achieving eternal purity preservation. This remarkable behavior is analyzed theoretically, and linked to the asymptotic spectrum of the Hamiltonian when the control gain grows unbounded, showing how this affects the oscillatory behavior of the density matrix elements. The sufficient conditions we obtain are tested on a simple three-qubit case, so that we can verify the existence of eternal purity preservation analytically and test the result and bounds numerically.

We believe that the selection and stabilization of wall states in interface subsystems can provide a simple yet effective tool for controlling decoherence. Its possibility of direct integration with other
noise mitigation protocols should make it a desirable technique in the design of new quantum devices. Future developments include refined heuristics in defining the interface subsystems and the dominant Hamiltonian interactions, and testing the approach with more realistic models, as well as with experimental systems.

Where the average is taken over

Proof.

Let
\rho=\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert

[

36
]

\mathrm{Dir}(1/2)

•

\langle\alpha_{i}^{4}\rangle=\frac{2}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}

•

Finally, we are able to compute the second quantity

\begin{split}\langle\rho_{jk}\rho_{cb}\rangle=&\langle\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{b}\alpha_{c}e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi_{j}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi_{k}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi_{b}+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\phi_{c}}\rangle\\
=&\langle\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}\alpha_{b}\alpha_{c}\rangle(\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}+\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc}-\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc})\\
=&\langle\alpha_{j}^{2}\alpha_{c}^{2}\rangle(\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}+\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc})(1-\delta_{jc})+\langle\alpha_{j}^{4}\rangle\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc}\\
=&\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}(\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}+\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc})(1-\delta_{jc})+\frac{2}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc}\\
=&\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}(\delta_{jk}\delta_{bc}+\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc}).\end{split}

(112)

∎

These results can now be used to compute the values of
\langle\tr(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{a}\rho)\rangle
and

\langle\tr(\sigma_{i}\rho\sigma_{a}\rho)\rangle
, which will be necessary to prove Proposition
3
.
The computation is given in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let

\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}-1}=\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}}\mathds{1}\}\cup\{\lambda^{s}_{jk}\}_{0\leq k<j\leq n_{\mathfrak{u}}-1}\cup\{\lambda^{a}_{jk}\}_{0\leq j<k\leq{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}-1}\cup\{\lambda^{d}_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n_{\mathfrak{u}}-1}
,
where
\lambda^{s}_{jk}
,
\lambda^{a}_{jk}
and
\lambda^{d}_{jk}
are the symmetric,
antisymmetric and diagonal generalized
n_{\mathfrak{u}}\times n_{\mathfrak{u}}
Gell-Mann matrices
[
37
]
(normalized to
1
), respectively, i.e.

\begin{split}\lambda^{s}_{jk}=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert+\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\\
\lambda^{a}_{jk}=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\\
\lambda^{d}_{k}=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lvert j\rangle\langle j\rvert-k\lvert k\rangle\langle k\rvert).\end{split}

(113)

Let in particular
\sigma_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}}\mathds{1}
. Then for
i,j>0
,

1.

\langle\tr(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{a}\rho)\rangle=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}\delta_{ia}
and

2.

\langle\tr(\sigma_{i}\rho\sigma_{a}\rho)\rangle=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\delta_{ia}
,

where the average is taken over all pure states
\rho\in\mathrm{extr}(\mathfrak{D}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathfrak{u}}))
.

Proof.

The first point is obtained as a direct application of Lemma
2
and of the orthonormality of
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i}
,

\langle\tr(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{a}\rho)\rangle=\tr(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{a}\langle\rho\rangle)=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}\tr(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{a}\mathds{1})=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}}\delta_{ia}.

(114)

The second point is proven by cases. First, let

\sigma_{i}=\lambda^{s}_{jk}
and
\sigma_{a}=\lambda^{s}_{bc}
, i.e.
j>k
and
b>c
, then

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{s}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{s}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{2}\langle\tr((\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert+\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\rho(\lvert b\rangle\langle c\rvert+\lvert c\rangle\langle b\rvert)\rho)\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{2}(\langle\rho_{kb}\rho_{cj}\rangle+\langle\rho_{jb}\rho_{ck}\rangle+\langle\rho_{kc}\rho_{bj}\rangle+\langle\rho_{jc}\rho_{bk}\rangle).\end{split}

(115)

By applying Lemma
2
, we know that the first and last terms of this sum are zero, since we have that
k\neq j
and
c\neq b
, whereas the middle two are equal, resulting in

\langle\tr(\lambda^{s}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{s}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc}.

(116)

If
\sigma_{i}=\lambda^{a}_{jk}
and
\sigma_{a}=\lambda^{a}_{bc}
, i.e.
j<k
and
b<c
, then

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{a}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{a}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{2}\langle\tr((\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\rho(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert b\rangle\langle c\rvert-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert c\rangle\langle b\rvert)\rho)\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{2}(-\langle\rho_{kb}\rho_{cj}\rangle+\langle\rho_{jb}\rho_{ck}\rangle+\langle\rho_{kc}\rho_{bj}\rangle-\langle\rho_{jc}\rho_{bk}\rangle).\end{split}

(117)

The same argument as in the previous case applies here, resulting in

\langle\tr(\lambda^{a}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{a}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\delta_{jb}\delta_{kc}.

(118)

If
\sigma_{i}=\lambda^{d}_{k}
and
\sigma_{a}=\lambda^{d}_{c}
, then

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{d}_{k}\rho\lambda^{d}_{c}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c(c+1)}}\\
&\langle\tr((\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lvert j\rangle\langle j\rvert-k\lvert k\rangle\langle k\rvert)\rho(\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\lvert b\rangle\langle b\rvert-c\lvert c\rangle\langle c\rvert)\rho)\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c(c+1)}}\\
&(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\langle\rho_{jb}\rho_{bj}\rangle-k\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\langle\rho_{kb}\rho_{bk}\rangle-c\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\langle\rho_{jc}\rho_{cj}\rangle+kc\langle\rho_{kc}\rho_{ck}\rangle)\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c(c+1)}}\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\\
&(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\delta_{jb}-k\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\delta_{kb}-c\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\delta_{jc}+kc\delta_{kc}).\end{split}

(119)

The first term of the sum is equal to
\min\{k,c\}
, whereas the addition of the second and third terms is equal to
-\min\{k,c\}(1-\delta_{kc})
.
Then, we have that

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{d}_{k}\rho\lambda^{d}_{c}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c(c+1)}}\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\\
&(\min\{k,c\}-\min\{k,c\}(1-\delta_{kc})+kc\delta_{kc})\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c(c+1)}}\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}(\min\{k,c\}+kc)\delta_{kc}\\
=&\frac{1}{k(k+1)}\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}(k+k^{2})\delta_{kc}\\
=&\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{u}}(n_{\mathfrak{u}}+1)}\delta_{kc}\end{split}

(120)

Now we have to prove that whenever
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{a}
are of different kinds, e.g. one is
\sigma_{i}
symmetric
and
\sigma_{a}
is diagonal, the average
\langle\tr(\sigma_{i}\rho\sigma_{a}\rho)\rangle
is zero.
If
\sigma_{i}=\lambda^{s}_{jk}
and
\sigma_{a}=\lambda^{a}_{bc}
, i.e.
j>k
and
b<c
, we have that

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{s}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{a}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{2}\langle\tr((\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert+\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\rho(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert b\rangle\langle c\rvert-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert c\rangle\langle b\rvert)\rho)\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\langle\rho_{kb}\rho_{cj}\rangle+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\langle\rho_{jb}\rho_{ck}\rangle-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\langle\rho_{kc}\rho_{bj}\rangle-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\langle\rho_{jc}\rho_{bk}\rangle).\end{split}

(121)

From the first case, we know that the first and last terms are zero, whereas the two in the middle were equal. However, now they appear
with opposite signs, making the entire expression equal to zero. Therefore,

\langle\tr(\lambda^{s}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{a}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=0.

(122)

If
\sigma_{i}=\lambda^{s}_{jk}
and
\sigma_{a}=\lambda^{d}_{c}
, i.e.
j>k
, we have that

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{s}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{d}_{c}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2c(c+1)}}\langle\tr((\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert+\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\rho(\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\lvert b\rangle\langle b\rvert-c\lvert c\rangle\langle c\rvert)\rho)\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2c(c+1)}}(\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\langle\rho_{kb}\rho_{bj}\rangle+\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\langle\rho_{jb}\rho_{bk}\rangle-c\langle\rho_{kc}\rho_{cj}\rangle-c\langle\rho_{jc}\rho_{ck}\rangle).\end{split}

(123)

Since
j\neq k
, by Lemma
2
we know that all four terms inside the parenthesis in the previous expression are equal to zero.
Therefore

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{s}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{d}_{bc}\rho)\rangle=&0.\end{split}

(124)

Finally, if
\sigma_{i}=\lambda^{a}_{jk}
and
\sigma_{a}=\lambda^{d}_{c}
, i.e.
j<k
, we have that

\begin{split}\langle\tr(\lambda^{a}_{jk}\rho\lambda^{d}_{c}\rho)\rangle=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2c(c+1)}}\langle\tr((\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert j\rangle\langle k\rvert-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\lvert k\rangle\langle j\rvert)\rho(\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\lvert b\rangle\langle b\rvert-c\lvert c\rangle\langle c\rvert)\rho)\rangle\\
=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2c(c+1)}}(\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\langle\rho_{kb}\rho_{bj}\rangle-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\sum_{b=0}^{c-1}\langle\rho_{jb}\rho_{bk}\rangle-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muc\langle\rho_{kc}\rho_{cj}\rangle+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muc\langle\rho_{jc}\rho_{ck}\rangle),\end{split}

(125)

which is is zero by the same argument as in the previous case. All other cases are automatically verified by the cyclicity of the trace.
∎

With these results in hand, we can now proceed to prove Proposition
3
.

Proposition
.

Given a tripartite system with Hilbert space
\cal H=H_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes H_{\mathfrak{e}}

and factorized initial state
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
,
which follows Lindbladian dynamics with arbitrary Hamiltonian and noise operators
L_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes L^{\mathfrak{e}}_{m}
,
the second derivative of the purity
\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)
has the following average initial value over all the reduced states
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
,

\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}\rangle_{\mathfrak{l}}=4\nu_{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i>0\\
bcjk\geq 0\end{subarray}}g_{ibc}g_{ijk}(\tau_{c0}\tau_{0k}\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})-\tau_{ck}\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})),

(126)

where
\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{l}}}-\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{l}}(n_{\mathfrak{l}}+1)}
and

\tau_{ck}=\tr(\sigma^{\mathfrak{e}}_{c}\sigma^{\mathfrak{e}}_{k}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})
.

Proof.

If
\rho_{0}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
, we have that

\begin{split}\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}([\sigma_{abc},[\sigma_{ijk},\rho_{0}]])\rho_{\mathfrak{l}})=&\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{c}^{\mathfrak{e}}\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})\tr(\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}})+\\
&\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\sigma_{c}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})\tr(\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}),\end{split}

(127)

and that

\begin{split}\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}([\sigma_{abc},\rho_{0}])\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}([\sigma_{ijk},\rho_{0}]))=&\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{c}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})\tr(\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})\\
&\qquad\tr(2\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}-\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}).\end{split}

(128)

Furthermore, if we also have

L_{m}=\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{l}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{w}}\otimes L_{m}^{\mathfrak{e}}
,
then

\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}({\cal D}_{L_{n}}\circ{\cal D}_{L_{m}}(\rho))=0,

(129)

\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}({\cal D}_{L_{m}}([\sigma_{ijk},\rho]))=0,

(130)

and

\tr(\tr_{\mathfrak{we}}([\sigma_{ijk},{\cal D}_{L_{m}}(\rho)]))=0.

(131)

Notice that the dissipative terms on the environment have no effect on the second derivative
of the purity
\gamma_{\mathfrak{l}}
. Using the previous expressions we may write the initial
purity loss acceleration as

\begin{split}\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}=&4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ai>0\\
bcjk\geq 0\end{subarray}}g_{abc}g_{ijk}\tr(\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}})\\
&\qquad(\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{c}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})\tr(\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})-\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{c}^{\mathfrak{e}}\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})).\end{split}

(132)

Notice that the terms with
a=0
or
i=0
do not contribute to this sum,
since the trace
\tr(\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}})
is zero in such cases, i.e.
the local Hamiltonians
H_{\mathfrak{w}}
,
H_{\mathfrak{e}}
and the
interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\mathfrak{we}}
do not contribute to the initial
purity loss acceleration.
Therefore we can remove them from the sum, as done above.
This is also the
case when either
b=0
or
j=0
, and at the same time either
c=0
or
k=0
, i.e. when
b=c=0
,
b=k=0
,
j=c=0
or
j=k=0
.
In order to find a function that depends only on
\rho_{\mathfrak{w}}
, we may consider the initial
state of the environment as part of the model of the system, i.e. we may fix an initial state

\rho_{\mathfrak{e}}
which is assumed to be known. Then, we may average the purity loss
acceleration over all the initial pure states of the information subsystem, i.e. we compute

\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}\rangle_{\mathfrak{l}}=\frac{1}{\nu_{\mathfrak{l}}}\int_{{\rm extr}({\cal D}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}))}\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}d\rho_{\mathfrak{l}},

(133)

where
\nu_{\mathfrak{l}}=\int_{{\rm extr}({\cal D}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}))}d\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
.

The computation of the average consists in solving integrals of the following kind:

I_{a,i}=\int_{{\rm extr}({\cal D}({\cal H}_{\mathfrak{l}}))}\tr(\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}-\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}})d\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}.

(134)

By linearity, we can split
I_{a,i}
into two integrals, such that

I_{a,i}=\nu_{\mathfrak{l}}(\langle\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{a}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\sigma_{i}^{\mathfrak{l}}\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}\rangle),

(135)

where we have also used the fact that for any pure state
\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}^{2}=\rho_{\mathfrak{l}}
.
This integral is then solved by applying the results of the previous lemma.

Finally, let
\tau_{ck}=\tr(\sigma_{c}^{\mathfrak{e}}\sigma_{k}^{\mathfrak{e}}\rho_{\mathfrak{e}})
and

\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{l}}}-\frac{1}{n_{\mathfrak{l}}(n_{\mathfrak{l}}+1)}
.
Putting all the terms together we obtain,

\langle\ddot{\gamma}_{\mathfrak{l}}(\rho)|_{t=0}\rangle_{\mathfrak{l}}=4\nu_{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i>0\\
bcjk\geq 0\end{subarray}}g_{ibc}g_{ijk}(\tau_{c0}\tau_{0k}\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\tr(\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})-\tau_{ck}\tr(\sigma_{b}^{\mathfrak{w}}\sigma_{j}^{\mathfrak{w}}\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})).

(136)

∎

Appendix C
Proof of Proposition
4

We begin by proving some useful properties of the operators
D_{i}
in the special case of
n_{\mathfrak{w}}=2
.
For larger wall subsystems, these properties do not apply,
but we can still use the eigenstates of
D_{1}
as the initial points of the gradient descent algorithm
to solve (
36
) or (
44
).

Lemma 4
.

Given a set of Hermitian operators
\{D_{i}\}_{i}
such that
\tr(D_{i}D_{j})=\delta_{ij}
and
\tr(D_{i})=0
,
let
\{\lambda_{k}^{i}\}_{k}
and
\{\lvert\psi_{k}^{i}\rangle\}_{k}
be their eigenvalues and eigenvectors, respectively,
i.e.
D_{i}\lvert\psi_{k}^{i}\rangle=\lambda_{k}^{i}\lvert\psi_{k}^{i}\rangle
. If they act on qubits, the following properties are
satisfied:

1.

\lambda_{1}^{i}=-\lambda_{2}^{i}

2.

\langle\psi_{k}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{k}^{i}\rangle=\lambda_{k}^{j}\delta_{ij}

3.

D_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}

4.

(\lambda_{k}^{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}

Proof.

Property (1) is a direct consequence of
\tr(D_{i})=\lambda_{1}^{i}+\lambda_{2}^{i}=0
.
Property (2) is also obtained by direct calculation. Assume
i\neq j
, then

\begin{split}\tr(D_{i}D_{j})=&\tr(D_{j}\lambda_{1}^{i}\lvert\psi_{1}^{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}^{i}\rvert+D_{j}\lambda_{2}^{i}\lvert\psi_{2}^{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}^{i}\rvert)\\
=&\lambda_{1}^{i}\langle\psi_{1}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{1}^{i}\rangle+\lambda_{2}^{i}\langle\psi_{2}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{2}^{i}\rangle\\
=&\lambda_{1}^{i}(\langle\psi_{1}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{1}^{i}\rangle-\langle\psi_{2}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{2}^{i}\rangle)=0.\end{split}

Therefore,
\langle\psi_{1}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{1}^{i}\rangle=\langle\psi_{2}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{2}^{i}\rangle
. But we also
have that
\tr(D_{i})=\langle\psi_{1}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{1}^{i}\rangle+\langle\psi_{2}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{2}^{i}\rangle=0
.
This implies that
\langle\psi_{1}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{1}^{i}\rangle=\langle\psi_{2}^{i}\rvert D_{j}\lvert\psi_{2}^{i}\rangle=0
whenever

i\neq j
. On the other hand, if
i=j
, we have that
\langle\psi_{k}^{i}\rvert D_{i}\lvert\psi_{k}^{i}\rangle=\lambda_{k}^{i}
.
Putting the two cases together proves property (2).

Finally, property (3) is a consequence of
D_{i}=D_{i}^{\dagger}
, property (1) and
\tr(D_{i}^{2})=1
.

∎

With these properties in hand, we have the tools necessary to prove Proposition
4
.

Proposition
.

Given
H_{\mathfrak{lw}}
in the OSD form as in (
41
),
n_{\mathfrak{w}}=2

and
s_{1}>s_{i}
for
i>1
, choosing
\lvert w\rangle\in\mathrm{eigenvectors}(D_{1})

minimizes
\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)
over
\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{eigenvectors}(D_{i})
,
and locally minimizes
\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)
over the set of pure states of
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}

Proof.

The Riemannian gradient (
46
) of
\Gamma_{2}(\lvert w\rangle)
is equal to zero if and only if

\begin{split}\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}(D_{i}^{2}-2\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i})\lvert w\rangle=\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}(\langle w\rvert D_{i}^{2}\lvert w\rangle-2(\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle)^{2})\lvert w\rangle,\end{split}

(137)

i.e. if
\lvert w\rangle
is an eigenstate of
\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}(D_{i}^{2}-2\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i})
.

Since
n_{\mathfrak{w}}=2
, i.e.
{\cal H}_{\mathfrak{w}}
is a qubit system, we can use the properties given in Lemma
4
.
By applying property 3 of lemma
4
, the previous expression simplifies to

\sum_{i=1}^{3}s^{2}_{i}\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i}\lvert w\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}s^{2}_{i}(\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle)^{2}\lvert w\rangle,

(138)

i.e.
\lvert w\rangle
must be an eigenstate of
\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i}
. Consider now property
2 of lemma
4
and
\lvert w\rangle=\lvert\psi_{k}^{j}\rangle
. We get that

\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i}=s^{2}_{j}\lambda_{k}^{j}D_{j}
, i.e. the eigenvectors
of each of the operators
D_{i}
are critical points of the function
\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})
for qubit walls.

In order to test if these critical points are minima of
\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})
, we can
compute the Riemannian Hessian at these points and check that it is positive definite.
The Euclidean Hessian is given by

\begin{split}\nabla^{2}\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})=&\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}(4D_{i}^{2}-8\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i}-8D_{i}\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert D_{i}).\end{split}

(139)

The Riemannian Hessian can be computed as

{\rm Hess}\,\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})[\vec{\epsilon}]=(\mathds{1}-\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert)\nabla^{2}\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})\vec{\epsilon}-\vec{\epsilon}\langle w\rvert(\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert\nabla\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})),

where
\vec{\epsilon}\in{\cal T}_{\lvert w\rangle}{\cal M}
, resulting in

\begin{split}{\rm Hess}\,\Gamma_{2}(\rho_{\mathfrak{w}})[\vec{\epsilon}]=&(\mathds{1}-\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert)\\
&(\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}(4D_{i}^{2}-8\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle D_{i}-8D_{i}\lvert w\rangle\langle w\rvert D_{i}))\vec{\epsilon}\\
&-\sum_{i}s^{2}_{i}(2\langle w\rvert D_{i}^{2}\lvert w\rangle-4(\langle w\rvert D_{i}\lvert w\rangle)^{2})\vec{\epsilon}.\end{split}

(140)

By imposing that the wall be a qubit system and
\lvert w\rangle
be an eigenstate
of
