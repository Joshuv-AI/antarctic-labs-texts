Survival improvement and widening social disadvantage-related disparities in osteosarcoma: a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Shilin Luo, Chuang Li, Leilei Tian, Rui Shi, Yi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on contemporary survival trends and social disadvantage-related disparities in osteosarcoma remains limited due to its rarity. According to the theory of fundamental causes, socially advantaged populations may benefit more from medical progress, potentially widening survival disparities over time. This study aimed to evaluate temporal trends in survival among patients with osteosarcoma and to examine whether survival gains and survival disparities differed across social disadvantage risk groups.MethodsPatients diagnosed with osteosarcoma between 2000 and 2019 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were evaluated across diagnostic periods using Kaplan-Meier methods and multivariable Cox regression. A social disadvantage risk score was constructed based on sex, race, household income, and residence, and patients were classified into low- and high-risk groups. Differences in survival gains and temporal changes in survival disparities between these groups were assessed.ResultsA total of 4,381 patients were included; most patients were younger than 40 years (72.1%) and had non-metastatic disease at diagnosis (57.9%). Compared with patients diagnosed in 2000–2004, those diagnosed in 2015–2019 had better OS [hazard ratio (HR) =0.79, 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.64–0.94, P=0.001] and CSS (HR =0.76, 95% CI: 0.63–0.92, P<0.001). Male sex, non-White race, lower income, and rural residence were associated with poorer survival. Based on these factors, 2,523 patients were classified as low social disadvantage risk and 1,858 as high social disadvantage risk. Survival gains were greater in the low-risk group than in the high-risk group (OS: P for interaction=0.002; CSS: P for interaction=0.004). CSS disparities between groups widened significantly over time (P for interaction=0.048), with a similar but non-significant trend for OS (P for interaction=0.10).ConclusionsSurvival among patients with osteosarcoma improved modestly between 2000 and 2019, but these gains were not equally distributed. Patients with lower social disadvantage risk experienced greater survival improvements, contributing to widening survival disparities. Efforts to improve equitable access to timely diagnosis, specialized sarcoma care, and supportive services are needed to reduce these disparities.Keywords: Osteosarcoma; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER); survival; improvement; disparities

Highlight box

Key findings

• Survival among patients with osteosarcoma improved modestly between 2000 and 2019. Based on sex, race, household income, and residence, a social disadvantage risk score effectively stratified patients into distinct prognostic groups; survival gains were greater in patients with lower social disadvantage risk, leading to widening social disadvantage-related survival disparities over time.

What is known and what is new?

• Osteosarcoma survival has shown limited improvement over recent decades, and socioeconomic and demographic disparities have been reported in cancer management and outcomes.

• Our study found that contemporary survival gains in osteosarcoma were not equally distributed across social disadvantage risk groups, and that unequal survival gains contributed to widening survival disparities over time.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Advances in osteosarcoma care should be accompanied by strategies to reduce barriers faced by disadvantaged populations, such as improving access to timely diagnosis, specialized sarcoma care, and supportive services.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor and predominantly occurs in children and adolescents (1). Although osteosarcoma is relatively rare overall, it accounts for a substantial proportion of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among children and adolescents (2,3). In terms of treatment, the introduction of multi-agent chemotherapy in the 1970s represented a major milestone in the management of osteosarcoma (4,5). Multi-agent chemotherapy combined with surgical resection is now the standard treatment for localized disease (6). However, therapeutic options for patients with advanced or metastatic osteosarcoma remain limited, and improving treatment outcomes remains a major challenge despite ongoing exploration of novel therapies (7,8). Importantly, compared with several common malignancies that have experienced major biologic breakthroughs over recent decades, progress in osteosarcoma has been relatively modest (5,9). Therefore, contemporary temporal gains in survival in osteosarcoma may be influenced not only by advances in tumor biology and treatment strategies but also by improvements in healthcare accessibility, supportive care, multidisciplinary management, and timely treatment delivery. Overall, long-term outcomes for patients with osteosarcoma have remained largely stagnant over an extended period (3,10). Previous studies have reported that long-term survival among patients with osteosarcoma did not show substantial improvement from the 1980s to the 2010s (3). However, the latest evidence on changes in survival outcomes remains limited.

Meanwhile, systematic disparities related to socioeconomic and social disadvantage exist in cancer management and outcomes. In general, patients with more favorable socioeconomic conditions are more likely to receive timely diagnosis and appropriate medical care, and therefore tend to achieve better clinical outcomes (11,12). Importantly, such disparities may not only manifest as static differences in survival but may also influence how the benefits of medical advances are distributed across populations. Individuals with more favorable socioeconomic conditions may experience greater improvements in survival as new diagnostic and therapeutic advances emerge (13,14). This concept is supported by the theory of fundamental causes, which proposes that individuals with greater access to social and economic resources are more capable of benefiting from medical innovations and healthcare advances, thereby perpetuating or even widening health disparities over time (15,16). In oncology, growing evidence suggests that survival gains associated with modern cancer management are not equally distributed across socioeconomic groups (11,13,14,17,18). In osteosarcoma, previous studies have suggested that treatment patterns and patient outcomes may also vary according to socioeconomic and demographic factors, including sex, race, income, and residence (19-22). However, most previous studies were cross-sectional analyses or focused primarily on static survival differences, rather than longitudinal changes in survival improvement and disparities over time. In addition, large population-based databases with long-term follow-up provide a valuable opportunity to evaluate temporal changes in survival and social disadvantage-related disparities at the population level. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether temporal gains in survival differ across social disadvantage groups or whether social disadvantage-related survival disparities have changed over time.

Therefore, based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, the present study aimed to systematically evaluated temporal trends in survival among patients diagnosed with osteosarcoma between 2000 and 2019 and further examined the association between socioeconomic and demographic disadvantage factors and patient outcomes. In addition, differences in survival improvement across social disadvantage risk groups were analyzed, and changes in social disadvantage-related survival disparities across diagnostic periods were assessed. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0524/rc).

Methods

Patient selection

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The SEER program of the National Cancer Institute collects population-based cancer incidence and survival data from multiple cancer registries in the U.S. and currently covers approximately half of the U.S. population (23). Data from the SEER-17 registries were used in this study (24). SEER registries use standardized procedures for case ascertainment, data collection, and follow-up. Vital status and follow-up time are updated through registry-based follow-up procedures and linkages with death records, which support population-based survival analyses (25).

Osteosarcoma cases were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) primary site codes (C40.0–C41.9) and histology codes (9180–9186 and 9192–9194). Patients diagnosed with osteosarcoma between 2000 and 2019 were included. The following patients were excluded: (I) osteosarcoma was not the only or first primary tumor, or the case was identified by autopsy or death certificate only; (II) missing variables of interest, including sex, race, household income, or residence; and (III) unknown cause of death or follow-up time. Study variables and outcomes

Socioeconomic and demographic disparity factors of interest included sex, race, median household income, and residence, which have been associated with cancer management and survival outcomes in previous studies (19-22). Median household income was dichotomized at 75,000 United States dollars (USD) with reference to the 2023 U.S. national median household income and the median income level of the included cohort, as the SEER county-level income variable was adjusted to 2023 U.S. dollars (26). Residence was categorized as urban or rural according to SEER county-level rural-urban classification (27). The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. CSS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death attributable to osteosarcoma, with deaths from other causes censored according to the SEER cause-specific death classification.

Statistical analysis

Patients were categorized into four groups according to year of diagnosis: 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. Kaplan-Meier methods were used to estimate OS and CSS across diagnostic periods. Multivariable Cox regression was used to evaluate changes in OS and CSS across diagnostic periods, adjusting for age, sex, race, median household income, residence, stage, grade, and treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). In addition, absolute improvements in OS and CSS rates at 12, 36, and 60 months for patients diagnosed in 2015–2019 compared with those diagnosed in 2000–2004 were calculated to describe the survival improvement over this period.

To assess the association between socioeconomic and demographic disparity factors and survival outcomes, multivariable Cox regression was first performed in the overall population to evaluate the effects of selected variables on OS and CSS, including sex, race, household income, and residence, adjusting for age, year of diagnosis, stage, grade, and treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). Based on these factors, a social disadvantage risk score was constructed to summarize the cumulative burden of adverse socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. This score was intended to provide a simple and clinically interpretable measure of cumulative social disadvantage. Specifically, each adverse factor associated with poorer survival was assigned one point, including male sex, non-White race, median household income below 75,000 USD, and rural residence, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 4. This equal-weight approach was used to reflect the number of adverse factors present in each patient and to enhance interpretability. Patients were then classified into low social disadvantage risk [0–1] and high social disadvantage risk [2–4] groups. Kaplan-Meier methods and multivariable Cox regression were used to validate survival differences between social disadvantage risk groups, adjusting for age, year of diagnosis, stage, grade, and treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy).

Finally, multivariable Cox regression was used to evaluate whether survival improvements differed across social disadvantage risk groups and whether survival disparities between these groups changed across diagnostic periods. These analyses were adjusted for age, year of diagnosis, stage, grade, and treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). Interaction was assessed on the multiplicative scale by including product terms between diagnostic period and social disadvantage risk group in the Cox regression, adjusting for age, stage, grade, and treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy). Differences in survival rates between high and low social disadvantage risk groups at 12, 36, and 60 months were also calculated across diagnostic periods. These absolute survival differences were used to describe changes in survival disparities on an absolute scale.

The proportional hazards assumption for Cox regression was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. No substantial violation of the proportional hazards assumption was observed. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

A total of 4,381 patients were included, including 1,032 patients diagnosed in 2000–2004, 1,081 in 2005–2009, 1,196 in 2010–2014, and 1,072 in 2015–2019. Most patients were aged 15–39 years (41.3%) and male (55.8%). The majority were White (75.5%), had a median household income >75,000 USD (52.7%), and resided in urban areas (90.6%). Most patients underwent local surgery (81.7%) and received chemotherapy (81.4%). Detailed baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Survival improvement by social disadvantage risk and temporal changes in disparities

As shown in Table 2, improvements in OS (P for interaction =0.002) and CSS (P for interaction =0.004) differed significantly across social disadvantage risk groups. Overall, survival improvement was greater among patients with low social disadvantage risk than among those with high social disadvantage risk (OS: 2015–2019 vs. 2000–2004, HR =0.74 vs. 0.81; CSS: HR =0.70 vs. 0.81). Figure 5 further illustrates changes in 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS and CSS rates across diagnostic periods in different social disadvantage risk groups. Overall, OS and CSS rates at each time point increased over time in both low-risk and high-risk patients (Figure 5A-5D). However, the magnitude of improvement was greater in the low social disadvantage risk group, compared with patients diagnosed in 2000–2004, those diagnosed in 2015–2019 in the low social disadvantage risk group showed increases of 4.0%, 5.2%, and 7.2% in the 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS rates, respectively, and increases of 4.8%, 6.5%, and 8.5% in the corresponding CSS rates. In contrast, the high social disadvantage risk group showed increases of 2.5%, 2.9%, and 3.5% in the 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS rates, and increases of 3.4%, 1.6%, and 1.4% in the corresponding CSS rates (Figure 5E).

Discussion

Because osteosarcoma is a relatively rare malignancy, studies examining temporal changes in survival among patients with osteosarcoma remain limited (10,28-30). Mirabello et al. analyzed SEER data from 1973 to 2004 and found that survival improved substantially between 1973 and 1993 but showed only limited improvement during the period from 1994 to 2004 (28). Similarly, Wu et al. investigated survival trends among osteosarcoma patients younger than 30 years between 1984 and 2013 and reported only modest improvements in survival over time (10). On the other hand, previous studies have suggested that treatment patterns and outcomes among patients with osteosarcoma may be influenced by socioeconomic factors (19-22). However, the relationship between survival improvement and socioeconomic disparities may be complex. The magnitude of survival improvement may differ across population subgroups with different socioeconomic characteristics. A recent study reported that improvements in survival for major cancers in the U.S. between 2000 and 2019 varied according to socioeconomic factors, with patients from more socially advantaged backgrounds experiencing greater survival gains (14). Conversely, if survival improvements differ across social disadvantage groups, such differences may further widen existing survival disparities (14). Nevertheless, systematic evidence on survival improvements among patients with osteosarcoma across different social disadvantage groups remains limited. In particular, it remains unclear whether survival improvements differ between social disadvantage groups and whether social disadvantage-related survival disparities have changed over time.

In the present study, we observed a trend toward improved survival among patients with osteosarcoma between 2000 and 2009, although the improvement did not reach statistical significance. This finding is consistent with previous studies, and may partly reflect the relatively small number of osteosarcoma cases, which limit the statistical power (3,10,31). At the same time, it may also indicate that major breakthroughs in osteosarcoma treatment were lacking during this period (4,5,7,32). Encouragingly, survival showed a more consistent improvement during 2010–2019, which may be attributable to the gradual refinement of existing treatment strategies and improvements in comprehensive cancer management (7,32,33). Male sex, non-White race, lower income, and rural residence have been widely recognized as unfavorable factors in the management and prognosis of most cancers, but their roles in osteosarcoma have been less frequently investigated (34-36). In this study, we confirmed that these factors were also associated with poorer survival among patients with osteosarcoma and constructed a social disadvantage risk score based on these variables, which demonstrated good discriminatory ability in stratifying patient survival. Similar to observations in many other cancers, further analyses showed that survival improvements were significantly greater among patients with low social disadvantage risk than among those with high social disadvantage risk (13,14). Although this finding may not be entirely unexpected, it remains noteworthy. From the perspective of the theory of fundamental causes, socially advantaged patients may be better positioned to convert medical progress into survival benefits because they have greater access to flexible resources, including specialized care, transportation, financial support, health literacy, and the ability to navigate complex treatment systems (15,16). This mechanism may be particularly relevant for osteosarcoma, because optimal management often requires timely referral, multidisciplinary evaluation, complex surgery, and systemic therapy at specialized sarcoma or high-volume orthopedic oncology centers (5,6). Prior studies have suggested that access to high-volume providers and specialized sarcoma centers may influence treatment delivery and outcomes in bone sarcoma (37).

A structural competency perspective further suggests that these disparities should not be interpreted solely as individual-level differences. Instead, they may reflect broader structural barriers, including the geographic concentration of expert sarcoma care in urban centers, transportation burden, insurance-related constraints, referral delays, and unequal access to supportive services. These structural mechanisms may help explain why improvements in cancer care are more readily translated into survival gains among socially advantaged patients, while disadvantaged patients may experience delayed or incomplete access to the same advances (11,12,38). Therefore, while efforts to improve osteosarcoma treatment should continue, attention should also be paid to inequalities in healthcare access among different socially disadvantaged groups. Specific strategies may include early referral pathways to specialized sarcoma centers, expanded patient navigation programs, transportation and lodging support for patients traveling to high-volume centers, insurance coverage optimization, and routine screening for social determinants of health in oncology practice (39,40).

To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically evaluate differences in survival improvement among patients with osteosarcoma across social disadvantage groups and to examine temporal changes in social disadvantage-related survival disparities. These findings provide new population-level evidence for understanding the evolution of survival improvement and survival inequalities in osteosarcoma. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce potential selection bias. In particular, 859 patients were excluded because of missing key variables. Because missing information may not occur completely at random and may be more common among socioeconomically disadvantaged patients, this exclusion may have led to underrepresentation of vulnerable populations and potential underestimation of social disadvantage-related disparities. Second, CSS was determined using the SEER cause-specific death classification, which relies on death certificate information and registry algorithms; therefore, potential misclassification of cause of death may have affected CSS estimates. Third, the SEER database lacks detailed treatment information, such as specific chemotherapy regimens and doses, and tumor size was unavailable or incompletely recorded across the entire study period, limiting further adjustment for these clinical factors. Fourth, although the social disadvantage risk score provided a simple and clinically interpretable measure of cumulative disadvantage, the equal-weight scoring approach did not account for differences in the magnitude of association across individual factors. Finally, because osteosarcoma is rare, the overall sample size remained relatively limited, which may reduce the statistical power of some analyses. Although the SEER database covers a large proportion of the U.S. population, the generalizability of the findings to other populations should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Between 2000 and 2019, survival among patients with osteosarcoma showed a modest overall improvement. However, these gains were not evenly distributed across social disadvantage risk groups. Patients with lower social disadvantage risk experienced greater survival gains, whereas those with higher social disadvantage risk showed more limited improvement, leading to widening social disadvantage-related survival disparities over time. These findings suggest that the benefits associated with advances in osteosarcoma care have not been equally shared across patient populations. Future efforts to improve osteosarcoma outcomes should also focus on disadvantaged populations to reduce social disadvantage-related survival disparities.
