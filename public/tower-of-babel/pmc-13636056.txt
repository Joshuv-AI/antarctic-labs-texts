Dose effect of bilateral transcranial magnetic stimulation for depression and anxiety in a real-world clinical setting
Authors: Noah Marchuck, Raneeka DeMoss, William C. Turner, Michelle Tobar, Ben W. Hunter, Alex O. Rothbaum, Sanne J.H. van Rooij
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive intervention for treatment-resistant depression (TRD). Comorbid anxiety is common in TRD patients. We examined the effect of single-dose versus high-dose bilateral TMS on depression and anxiety. Methods: Seventy-eight patients received 1 Hz right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) stimulation combined with left single-dose or high-dose iTBS to the left DLPFC at a nonprofit clinic. Depression and anxiety symptoms were measured at baseline, after 10 and 20 sessions, and final (30−36) TMS sessions. Results: Bilateral TMS resulted in a significant decrease in depression (F(3,70)= 9.670, p < .001; 74.4% response, 47.7% remission) and anxiety (F(3,70)= 4.916, p = 0.004, 52.6% response, 39.7% remission). The high-dose group showed a faster reduction in depression (F(3,70)= 2.875, p = 0.042), but not anxiety symptoms. There was no dose difference at the final session. Conclusion: Bilateral TMS improved depression and anxiety symptoms with a quicker reduction in depression symptoms with higher dosing, which highlights an important clinical consideration to reduce visits.

Background

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability [1], affecting 30.9% of U.S. adults [2]. Despite continued investment in antidepressant treatment options, novel treatment breakthroughs have been relatively few. Even with augmentation strategies, the efficacy of these options remains stagnant [3] with pharmacological antidepressant treatments, such as serotonin reuptake inhibitors, only effective in 30%-40% of individuals with MDD [4]. More than 50% of individuals with MDD meet criteria for a co-occurring anxiety disorder [5], which is associated with greater functional impairment and poorer treatment response [6], [7], [8]. Treatment-resistant depression (TRD), defined as MDD that persists following two pharmacological treatments [9], is more common for patients with co-occurring anxiety [10], [11], [12]. Intervention strategies targeting both depression and anxiety are therefore needed.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is an efficacious, non-invasive neuromodulation therapy that uses magnetic pulses and is FDA-approved for TRD [13], [14]. Standard TMS protocols target the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (lDLPFC) with 3000 10 Hz pulses for 37.5 min per session [13]. Intermittent theta burst (iTBS) is a quicker form of TMS in which 600 pulses are delivered in 3 min as triplets at 50 Hz repeated at 5 Hz with comparable antidepressant effects [15]. Higher doses of iTBS, such as delivering three times the pulses per session (i.e., 1800), have similarly shown antidepressive effects without added side effects [16], but little is known about how this change impacts symptom reduction across treatment.

There is no FDA-approved TMS protocol unilaterally targeting anxiety, though 1 Hz right (r)DLPFC TMS has been shown to reduce anxiety symptoms [17], [18]. Bilateral TMS protocols pairing iTBS to the lDLPFC with low frequency 1 Hz pulses to the rDLPFC are effective in reducing both depressive and co-occurring anxiety symptoms [19], [20], though little is known of the additive effects from combining 1 Hz rDLPFC with lDLPFC iTBS.

TMS is increasingly available across diverse clinical settings. In outpatient care, patients come to the treatment center only for TMS [21], [22], whereas in psychiatric or hospital settings TMS is delivered alongside intensive mental health treatments [23], [24]. Patients in intensive settings often experience greater comorbidity, symptom severity, and functional impairment [25], [26]. Although TMS has shown promise for reducing depression symptoms in both settings, differences in outcomes by setting remain largely unclear.

This naturalistic observational study examined patients receiving TMS at a non-profit psychiatric rehabilitation facility. The effect of a bilateral TMS protocol on depression and comorbid anxiety symptoms was investigated. TMS was delivered over 30–36 daily sessions and combined 1 Hz rDLPFC TMS with either single-dose or high-dose lDLPFC iTBS. TMS was delivered in outpatient (TMS only) or intensive treatment settings. We hypothesized that: (1) bilateral TMS would reduce depression and anxiety symptoms; (2) high-dose of iTBS would result in an earlier reduction in depression and anxiety symptoms compared to single-dose of iTBS; and (3) intensive patients would show a greater reduction in depression and anxiety symptoms than outpatients.

Methods

Sample

This was a retrospective chart review of patients (18–65 years old) with TRD who received TMS as intensive or outpatients between 01/2022–07/2025. All study procedures took place at a non-profit, non-emergent psychiatric rehabilitation facility located in the Southeastern United States. Intensive patients received evidence-based psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy at the facility and were referred by the program psychiatrists. Outpatients were referred by facility psychiatrists for TMS only. This project does not require IRB review because it is not human subjects research as defined in the federal regulations, determined through Emory IRB.

TMS procedures

Prior to beginning TMS, all patients completed an intake and clinical interviews with the TMS clinician and the psychiatrist to approve eligibility and safety for TMS for TRD. TMS was delivered using the MagVenture MagPro R30 with B70 coil to the DLPFC determined using a standardized Beam F3/F4 method for all patients. Bilateral TMS combining 600 1 Hz pulses to rDLPFC with iTBS to the lDLPFC was delivered. Two different doses of iTBS were delivered: single-dose iTBS (SD-iTBS; 600 pulses/session) and high-dose iTBS (HD-iTBS; 1800 pulses/session). Motor threshold (MT) was determined during the baseline session and defined as the minimal stimulation needed to trigger movement in the patient’s thumb on the contralateral side of the stimulation. Target intensity of iTBS and 1 Hz was defined as 120% of the MT. Patients included in the analyses received five sessions a week for six weeks (30 sessions), and an optional additional six sessions spread across three weeks to taper out in both protocols (supplemental CONSORT diagram, S1).

Measures

Age was calculated at the beginning of TMS treatment. Birth sex was defined as biological variable, independent from gender identity, and self-disclosed by the patient. Clinician-rated measures of depression and anxiety were administered at baseline, after 10 and 20 sessions, and at the final TMS session.

The Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) was used to measure depression symptom severity [27]. Depression remission was defined as a final MADRS score ≤ 9, and clinical response as 50% or greater reduction from baseline MADRS score [28]. The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) was used to measure anxiety symptom severity [29]. Anxiety remission was defined as a final HAM-A score ≤ 7, and clinical response as 50% or greater reduction from baseline HAM-A score [30]. Missing scores at sessions 10 or 20 were imputed using the average of the immediate previous and following session scores.

Statistical analysis

Repeated measures general linear models (GLM) were used to assess changes in depression and anxiety severity over the course of treatment (baseline, 10 sessions, 20 sessions, final session). Repeated measures GLM included TMS dose (SD-iTBS versus HD-iTBS) and setting (intensive versus outpatient delivery) as between subjects factors and controlled for age and birth sex. Posthoc tests were conducted for factors with significant interactions. Chi-square tests were conducted to examine differences in remission and clinical response rates by dose as well.

There was a significant interaction between time and dose for MADRS scores (F(3,70)= 2.875, p = 0.042). Post-hoc t-tests showed a significantly greater symptom reduction for the HDiTBS group (M=-4.83, SD=7.41) compared to the iTBS group (M=-1.20, SD=8.11) between sessions 10 and 20, t(76) = 2.051, p = 0.044. Between session 20 and final session, the iTBS group showed a reduction (M=-6.85, SD=6.92) that was marginally greater than the HDiTBS group (M=-3.32, SD=9.92), t(76) = -1.841, p = 0.070. There was no difference between baseline and session 10. After completing TMS, the groups did not significantly differ at final session (Table 1). There was no significant interaction observed with the setting.

The remission rate across groups was 47.7%, and clinical response rate across groups was 74.4%. Chi-square tests revealed no significant difference in remission or response rates by dose (χ2=0.892, p = 0.345; χ2=0.014, p = 0.904).

Change in anxiety

A significant effect of time on HAM-A scores was observed, such that anxiety symptoms decreased significantly over the course of TMS (F(3,70)= 4.916, p = 0.004; Fig. 1b).

There was a marginal, but not significant, interaction observed with dose for HAM-A scores (F(3,70)= 2.281, p = 0.087), with groups differing at Session 20 (Table 1). There was no significant interaction observed between setting and HAM-A scores.

Remission rate across groups was 39.7%, and clinical response rate across groups was 52.6%. Chi-square tests revealed no significant difference in remission or response rates by protocol (χ2=0.368, p = 0.544; χ2=0.176, p = 0.675).

Discussion

In this retrospective, naturalistic study, we observed the reduction of depression and anxiety symptoms over the course of bilateral TMS for TRD in a real-world clinical sample. The HD-iTBS group who received triple the dose of SD-iTBS showed a faster rate of reduction in depression symptoms compared to the SD-iTBS group, suggesting the effect of dose on depression recovery trajectory. Notably, by the end of treatment, there was no difference in depression or anxiety symptoms between the two dose groups. Finally, there was no significant difference in outcomes between outpatient and intensive patients.

We observed a significant effect of dose on depression symptom reduction trajectory with greater dose resulting in a more rapid decrease in depressive symptoms. This aligns with several randomized controlled trials [31], [32], [33], [34] and a recent real-world clinical study [35] in which a higher number of daily pulses was associated with a shorter time to depression recovery. For SD-iTBS, symptoms continued to improve after 20 sessions, which is in line with a recent registry study showing that anti-depressant responses were greatest in patients who received more than 30 sessions of lDLPFC TMS [36]. Using a naturalistic approach in an intensive care setting, our study supports prior trials and suggests that a higher dose may necessitate fewer TMS sessions than SD-iTBS. This is an important consideration for optimizing clinical care as a higher dose TMS protocol may be a more accessible alternative to standard TMS protocols [37].

TMS protocols targeting anxiety or related disorders have demonstrated efficacy with 1 Hz rDLPFC [38], [39], [40]. Here we observed a reduction in anxiety symptoms incorporating an abbreviated 1 Hz protocol supporting its potential in improving anxiety. HD-iTBS resulted in a lower HAM-A score at Session 20 only, such that patients reached the “mild anxiety” cutoff at a faster rate than SD-iTBS. This may suggest lDLPFC iTBS combined with 1 Hz rDLPFC works to decrease comorbid anxiety symptoms. However, the interaction between dose and HAM-A scores was not statistically significant, and these findings should be interpreted with caution. Randomized trials comparing left versus right or other distinct TMS protocols in patients with comorbid anxiety and depression are needed to determine potential impact of target selection and protocol specificity. Multiple TMS protocols for comorbid depression and anxiety have been successful in reducing symptoms including unilateral low frequency rDLPFC TMS [17], [41], [42], [43], continuous TBS to the rDLPFC [44], [45] and a bilateral protocol delivering high-frequency TMS to the lDLPFC and iTBS to the right [46]. A recent trial showed specific clinical effects on comorbid anxiety and depression for an anxiosomatic versus dysphoric target [22] suggesting target precision and protocol selection can be optimized to improve clinical efficacy.

We observed no effect of setting, suggesting that TMS in either setting results in clinically significant improvement. Given the large treatment effect observed, and underpowered setting distribution, larger studies may identify specific differences in setting that this naturalistic, observational study did not.

Limitations and future directions

This was a naturalistic, observational study including participants receiving TMS in an intensive and outpatient setting and we were unable to control for potential concurrent treatments received by outpatients in addition to TMS. Notably, we did not observe an effect of setting. Second, patients were not randomly assigned to the iTBS or HD-iTBS groups but were assigned a treatment protocol based on when they received treatment, with newer patients receiving HD-iTBS. Because clinicians were not blinded to the dose difference, a rater expectancy bias should be considered when interpreting the results. Notably, however, neither score differed by dose at final session. Third, because this was a retrospective review, we were unable to include a rDLPFC comparator arm and therefore cannot assert that the anxiolytic effect we observe is attributed to the 1 Hz rDLPFC stimulation alone. Future clinical trials are needed to address these limitations. Additionally, delayed clinical effects of TMS have been observed in previous studies [40], [47]. Given our findings on higher dose gains in depression with HD-iTBS, we plan to examine whether this additional time with reduced symptoms results in increased benefit from the intensive program, potentially leading to differential outcomes in the intensive program overall.

Conclusions

Our retrospective, naturalistic study showed that bilateral TMS improved depression and anxiety symptoms. This study supports the feasible and effective use of TMS in a real-world clinical setting, with multiple levels of care. The more rapid reduction in depression symptoms observed with higher dosing highlights an important consideration of increasing dose and reducing clinic visits. These results are encouraging, demonstrating that bilateral TMS is a promising treatment for depression and anxiety in a real-world setting treating some of the most severely impaired patients.
