The role of healthy diet literacy in promoting sustainable and healthy eating behaviors in Türkiye: implications for environmental health
Authors: Çağdaş Salih Meriç, Hacı Ömer Yılmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growing emphasis on sustainability in recent years has been largely driven by the pressing challenges of climate change. In this context, healthy diet literacy represents a critical approach to promoting the sustainability of the food system, which has far-reaching implications for both public and environmental health.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 495 university students. The questionnaire form consists of the nutritional habits and anthropometric measurements of university students, “e-Healthy Diet Literacy Questionnaire (e-HDLQ)”, and “Sustainable and Healthy Eating (SHE) Behaviors Scale”.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 19.26 ± 2.19 years, with a mean BMI of 22.40 ± 4.04 kg/m2. A one-unit increase in e-HDLQ was significantly associated with a 0.709 unit (95%CI: 0.289; 1.130) increase in SHE behaviors and demonstrated significant explanatory power (R2 = 0.022, p < 0.001). A one-unit increase in e-HDLQ was significantly associated with sub-dimensions of SHE behaviors: quality labels (β = 0.154, R2 = 0.013, p < 0.001), animal welfare (β = 0.146, R2 = 0.028, p < 0.001), meat reduction (β = 0.093, R2 = 0.026, p < 0.001), healthy and balanced diet (β = 0.119, R2 = 0.011, p < 0.05), local food (β = 0.091, R2 = 0.020, p < 0.001) and low-fat (β = 0.063, R2 = 0.008, p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe study’s outcomes underscore the need to design educational initiatives that connect nutrition literacy with sustainable and healthy nutritional behaviors. To enhance their effectiveness, such initiatives should be integrated into interdisciplinary academic frameworks, targeting individual health and environmental sustainability improvements.Trial registrationThis is cross-sectional research conducted on human participants but does not require intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25088-8.Keywords: Healthy diet literacy, Healthy eating behaviors, Sustainable nutrition, Sustainability

Background

While nutrition has always been a fundamental determinant of health, recent global challenges such as rapid population growth and climate change have highlighted the importance of the concept of sustainable nutrition (SN). According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the global food system is responsible for nearly one-third of total greenhouse gas emissions, while approximately 931 million tons of food are wasted annually worldwide [1, 2]. At the same time, unhealthy eating behaviors remain a major contributor to non-communicable diseases, which account for 74% of all global deaths [3]. These figures illustrate the magnitude of the issue and highlight the critical need for SN, defined as an accessible, affordable, reliable, equitable, and culturally acceptable dietary pattern with low environmental impact that promotes both individual and planetary health [4, 5].

The SN emphasizes the increased the consumption of plant-based foods, reducing meat consumption, and consuming seasonal, fresh, and local vegetables and fruits; also involves reducing overconsumption, food waste, and loss [6]. In Türkiye, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults has risen significantly in the last two decades, with nearly 60% of adults classified as overweight or obese [7]. Moreover, university students—a population undergoing lifestyle transitions and forming long-term eating habits—are particularly vulnerable to unhealthy dietary practices, including low fruit and vegetable consumption, frequent fast-food intake, and irregular meal patterns [8, 9]. These trends underline the importance of fostering sustainable and healthy eating (SHE) behaviors in this group.

The nutrition literacy (NL), an extension of health literacy, plays a vital role in the identification, promotion, and maintenance of SHE behaviors [10]. It encompasses the ability to comprehend and apply appropriate, adequate, and health-promoting nutritional practices, which is crucial for the prevention and management of nutrition-related health conditions [11, 12]. Studies among university students have shown that higher NL levels are positively associated with adherence to dietary guidelines, increased fruit and vegetable intake, and healthier food choices [13–15]. Nevertheless, evidence also suggests that many students exhibit inadequate NL levels, which may compromise their capacity to engage in SHE behaviors [16].

In today’s digital age, traditional NL alone is insufficient; young adults, particularly university students, are among the most active users of digital technologies, frequently relying on online sources for health and nutrition information [17]. However, the abundance of digital content brings challenges related to accuracy, credibility, and applicability. To address these issues, the concept of “digital healthy nutrition literacy” has emerged as an extension of NL, encompassing the skills required to critically access, evaluate, and use nutrition information obtained from digital platforms to develop healthier eating practices [18, 19]. Compared with traditional NL, this construct better reflects the realities of contemporary information-seeking behaviors, especially in populations with high digital engagement [12, 20].

Despite growing attention to NL and its relationship with dietary habits, evidence on digital healthy NL remains limited. While studies in different contexts have examined food and NL in university students [21, 22], to our knowledge, no research in Türkiye has specifically investigated the interplay between digital healthy NL and SHE behaviors in this population. Addressing this gap is crucial, as improving digital NL in university students may not only enhance their individual health outcomes but also contribute to broader public health and environmental sustainability goals.

Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the association between digital healthy NL and SHE behaviors among university students in Türkiye. This research is one of the pioneering studies on this subject. A further objective was to gain insights into this association with the aim of encouraging healthier eating habits within this population, which could potentially support better overall health outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Gaziantep University in the Anatolia Region of Türkiye.

Study population and sample

This cross-sectional study included overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2; n = 115), normal weight (BMI = 18.5–24.9 kg/m2; n = 308), and underweight (BMI ≤ 18.5 kg/m2; n = 72) individuals. The minimum sample size targeted to be reached in the study was calculated as 236 subjects with an effect size of 0.5, 95.0% power, and an α = 0.05 significance level through the G*Power 3.1.9.4 software program. Considering possible withdrawal and data loss, 495 participants were included in the study with the random sampling method.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria required participants to be university students aged 18–24 years, healthy, and volunteers. Additionally, they needed to consent by signing the Informed Voluntary Consent Form (IVCF).

Exclusion criteria

Those students who declined to participate in the study, aged < 18 years and > 24 years, diagnosed with chronic disease, and those on disease-related diets were excluded. Exclusion criteria were defined to ensure the homogeneity of the study population as such conditions which could directly influence NL and SHE behaviors, thereby acting as potential confounding factors (e.g., disease-specific dietary interventions, medical nutritional therapy or age-related differences).

Data collection

Prior to data collection, a pilot study was conducted to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire, and the final version was developed by refining or removing items that were found to be unclear. The research data were obtained through a questionnaire form (supplementary file) created by the researchers through a detailed review of current literature. Data collection was carried out following ethical approval during the spring semester of the 2024–2025 academic year at the Faculty of Health Sciences with university students. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in classrooms in order not to interfere with scheduled class hours. On each data collection day, one of the researchers was present in the classroom, explained the study objectives, provided standardized oral and written instructions on how to complete the questionnaire, and remained available to clarify questions without influencing responses. This systematic procedure was applied consistently until the planned sample size was achieved.

Data collection tools

Research data were collected through face-to-face interviews with each participant, using a survey form prepared by the researchers. The survey form included general information about the participants, sociodemographic characteristics (gender, income, residence, etc.), nutritional habits (main meals, snack meal, meal skipping, eating outside, salt in food, water consumption, etc.), physical activity (no, rarely, 1–2 times/w, 3–4 times/w, ≥ 5 times/w), anthropometric measurements (weight (kg), height (cm), BMI (kg/m2)), “e-Healthy Diet Literacy Questionnaire (e-HDLQ)”, and “Sustainable and Healthy Eating (SHE) Behaviors Scale”. Each instrument was analyzed using its original scoring system without normalization.

Descriptive information form

Developed by the researchers based on the existing literature, this form includes 17 statements that inquire about various descriptive characteristics of the participating students, such as age, gender, place of residence, sociodemographic factors, nutritional habits, and physical activity scores. In addition to sociodemographic data, sleep duration was assessed as an important lifestyle factor for a potential determinant of SHE behaviors and NL within this population [23, 24].

Anthropometric measurements

Body weight (kg) and height (cm) were measured by researchers. Anthropometric measurements were obtained following the completion of the questionnaire. Prior to data collection, participants were asked to attend the session in a fasting state to standardize the measurements. The procedure was conducted during a single visit: first, the questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews, and subsequently anthropometric measurements were performed by trained researchers on the same day.

The body weight of the participants was measured with the “TANITA BC401” device in the morning while fasting, after defecation, with light/thin clothes, without shoes, and in accordance with the measurement technique. Height was measured using a portable stadiometer with the feet together and the head in the Frankfort plane. The measurement was performed with a sensitivity of 0.1 cm (1 mm). Body Mass Index (BMI) was obtained by dividing body weight (kg) by height squared (m2) (BMI = kg/m2). BMI was assessed according to the WHO classification and classified into three categories: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.99 kg/m2), and overweight-obese (> 25.0 kg/m2).

E-Healthy diet literacy questionnaire (e-HDLQ)

The “e-Healthy Diet Literacy (e-HDL)” Questionnaire, which was first developed by Van Duong et al. in 2021 to measure the e-healthy NL levels of adults, consists of 5 factors and 15 items [25]. The validity and reliability of the “e-Healthy Diet Literacy Scale” in Turkish adults were made by Karahan Yılmaz et al. in 2023 and were adapted as the “e-Healthy Diet Literacy Questionnaire (e-HDLQ)” [26]. The e-HDLQ has 4 sub-dimensions, which are “Accessing”, “Understanding”, “Appraising,” and “Applying,” and 11 items. The total score of the e-HDLQ is obtained by summing the scores for all items. A higher total score reflects a higher level of e-healthy diet literacy among the participants.

Sustainable and healthy eating (SHE) behaviors scale

The Sustainable and Healthy Eating (SHE) Behaviors Scale is developed in accordance with the “Food and Agriculture Organization” ‘s (FAO) definition of the concept of a sustainable diet, the LiveWell approach, and the principles of SHE habits. This scale, first developed by Zakowska-Biemans et al. in 2019, consists of 8 factors and 34 questions [27]. The Turkish adaptation was made by Köksal et al. in 2022 and was adapted as the “SHE Behaviors” scale [28]. The Turkish adaptation of this scale consists of 7 factors and 32 questions in total. These 7 factors include “quality marks (local and organic), seasonal foods and avoiding food waste, animal health, reducing meat consumption, healthy and balanced nutrition, local food, and low fat”. An increase in the total score indicates an increase in the level of SHE behavior.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 25.0 statistical software suites and the following statistical methods were used in the analysis of the research data. In the evaluation of descriptive data, number (n), percentage (%), mean (x̄), and standard deviation (SD) are given in the tables. The normal distribution of numerical data variables “(histogram and probability graphs)” and “(Kolmogorov-Smirnov)” was evaluated with kurtosis and skewness coefficients, and the variables whose kurtosis and skewness values were in the range of (− 1.5) and (+ 1.5) were considered to have a normal distribution. For normally or nonnormally distributed data, “the Independent Samples t-test” or “Mann‒Whitney U test” was used to compare independent variables between groups. “Fisher Exact Chi-Square” was used for comparisons of categorized data within groups, and “One-Way ANOVA” and “post hoc Tukey tests” were used for comparisons of quantitative data within groups (Bonferroni correction). Path analysis was also applied to examine linear relationships (β and the standard error (S.E. of β)) between the determined dependent and independent variables. This analysis served as an effective tool for understanding and interpreting complex relationships between various variables. The linear regression model was evaluated using variables of seven factors of SHE behaviors and the relationship between healthy diet literacy, with p < 0.05 and p < 0.001 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations and consent to participate

The study was conducted according to the criteria set by the “Declaration of Helsinki”, and the study protocol was approved by Gaziantep Islamic Science and Technology University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 508.45.09; Protocol No: 2025/578). Additionally, institutional approval permission was obtained from the Deanship of Gaziantep University Faculty of Health Sciences. At the beginning of the research, all participants were informed in detail about the study and signed an Informed Voluntary Consent Form (IVCF).

Discussion

The growing emphasis on sustainability in recent years has been largely driven by the pressing challenges of climate change. In response, the United Nations established the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in 2015, emphasizing SHE and global food security as key priorities [29]. In this context, healthy diet literacy represents a critical approach to promoting the food system’s sustainability, which has far-reaching implications for both public and environmental health [30]. Healthy NL contributes to improved individual nutrition and well-being and equips individuals with an understanding of the environmental consequences of their dietary choices. As such, NL serves as a foundational element for the development of programs and policies aimed at disease prevention and the promotion of public health [31].

To our knowledge, this is the first research to assess the relationship between SHE behaviors and the e-healthy NL of university students in Türkiye. This research showed the increase in e-HDLQ was significantly associated with increases in SHE behaviors and all sub-dimensions (Table 4), suggesting a close link between digital NL and SHE dietary practices. Moreover, higher e-HDLQ scores were linked to lower fast-food consumption, greater preference for healthy snacks, increased consumption of local and seasonal foods, and reduced meat intake. These results highlight how NL may guide students toward more sustainable food choices by shaping their awareness of both personal health and environmental consequences. One possible explanation for the positive association is that snacks may not necessarily reflect unhealthy choices. Participants with higher NL and SHE behaviors might prefer healthier snack options, such as fruits, nuts, or whole-grain products, instead of energy-dense, nutrient-poor alternatives. Thus, increased frequency of snacking may reflect more conscious and health-oriented food selections rather than unhealthy eating habits. Similarly, the association with meat reduction and local food consumption reflects a broader understanding of sustainability, including environmental protection and food system resilience which directly aligns with the study’s objectives.

It was determined that students’ self-perception of healthy eating was significantly associated with both e-HDLQ and SHE behaviors. Participants with higher e-HDLQ and SHE behavior scores were less likely to consume fast food or eat outside (Table 3), supporting the view that healthy eating habits and adequate, balanced nutrition—both key components of NL—have been associated with more favorable indicators of SHE behaviors. This research showed that a one-unit increase in e-HDLQ was significantly associated with a 0.709 increase in SHE behaviors and demonstrated significant explanatory power (p < 0.001). This result is consistent with previous studies. Liao et al. identified a significant positive correlation between NL and SHE behaviors among university students [32]. Similarly, Lee et al. reported that students who formulated a healthy eating plan demonstrated higher NL scores and more favorable eating habits than their peers [33]. These results suggest that individuals who actively prioritize a healthy lifestyle are more likely to develop and sustain beneficial dietary behaviors, underscoring the value of interventions that incorporate NL into structured dietary planning. Previous studies have indicated that individuals’ eating behaviors are influenced by their levels of NL [34], with healthy eating patterns showing a positive association with NL competencies [35]. Placing a high priority on personal health has been found to positively affect dietary habits. Enhancing nutrition knowledge and engaging in the planning of balanced meals can significantly shape eating behaviors and contribute to improved overall health and well-being. Given the critical role of nutrition education in fostering healthy eating practices, the promotion of NL should be considered a key public health priority.

In the present research, healthy and balanced nutrition was significantly correlated with healthy diet literacy (Table 4). Food literacy has the potential to empower individuals to enhance their dietary behaviors, thereby fostering healthier eating patterns [36]. A recent systematic review, similarly emphasized that NL has a broad impact on dietary intake and suggested that enhancing NL may serve as an effective public health strategy to prevent excessive weight gain [37]. Within this framework of NL, the “knowing where food comes from” approach aims to encourage sustainable food consumption practices. Supporting this perspective, the present study demonstrated that each one-unit increase in e-healthy NL was significantly correlated with increases of 0.154, 0.146, 0.093, 0.119, 0.091, and 0.063 in the subdimensions of SHE behaviors, namely: “quality labels, animal welfare, meat reduction, healthy and balanced nutrition, local food, and low-fat consumption”, respectively (Table 4, Model 1). Accordingly, the total score of the e-HDLQ was found to be strongly and positively correlated with meat reduction (B = 0.093; p < 0.001), suggesting that improving NL may contribute to the enhancement of SHE behaviors.

Meat consumption plays a critical role in discussions surrounding sustainability, as it is among the most energy-intensive and environmentally detrimental food categories [38]. Environmental concerns related to meat consumption have intensified following reports from the FAO, which indicate that livestock farming is a major contributor to global greenhouse gas emissions (FAO, 2025). In light of these issues, plant-based dietary alternatives have gained prominence for their potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, protect biodiversity, enhance environmental sustainability, and lower diet-related mortality rates. There is increasing consensus that reducing meat consumption and transitioning toward plant-based diets is a key strategy to address pressing public health and environmental challenges. Notably, reduced meat intake is also associated with greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet (MD) [39]. The MD, characterized by an emphasis on cereals, fresh fruits and vegetables, and reduced intake of dairy products, eggs, meat, and processed foods, aligns closely with the FAO’s definition of a sustainable diet. A study found that higher levels of NL were positively associated with adherence to a MD-style dietary pattern [40]. Despite Türkiye’s geographical and cultural suitability for implementing the MD and relatively low meat consumption due to household income constraints, this dietary pattern is not widely adopted. A possible explanation, supported by prior research, suggests that barriers and enablers to reducing meat consumption include limited consumer awareness, difficulties in acquiring new culinary skills, transformations in institutional meal services, and the appeal of taste in plant-based meals [41].These insights are relevant to these results, as the observed association between higher NL and reduced meat consumption among students reflects similar patterns identified in adherence to the MD.

Understanding the factors that promote SHE behaviors also requires recognizing the value of traditional and local foods for human and environmental health. In this context, promoting indigenous and local food sources has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in improving NL and food security [42]. In the current research (Table 4, Model 1), local food consumption was significantly and positively correlated with e-HDLQ scores (B = 0.091; p < 0.001). Moreover, seasonal food consumption and minimizing food waste (Table 4) were associated with higher e-HDLQ scores (B = 0.044). This may reflect the tendency of individuals with greater healthy diet literacy to make more informed food choices and possess a deeper understanding of food selection and food security. Importantly, seasonal food consumption is not only a health-related behavior but also contributes significantly to environmental protection and food system resilience, making it a critical component of SN. These results further reinforce the central aim of the study by demonstrating how NL shapes sustainable consumption behaviors in practical and culturally relevant ways.

The present study identified a relationship between BMI, SHE behaviors, and e-HDLQ. The rising prevalence of obesity both in Türkiye and globally is a growing public health concern, not only due to its health implications but also because of its negative impact on environmental sustainability. Previous studies have indicated that obesity is linked to lower adherence to SHE behaviors, as well as reduced dietary sustainability [43, 44]. Research in current literature demonstrated a significant association between unhealthy eating behaviors—including poor nutritional habits, SHE behavior, and BMI [10, 45, 46]. A higher BMI was associated with lower scores on the SHE Behaviors Scale in this research. This finding is supported by our regression model, which indicates that a decrease in BMI corresponds with an increase in SHE Behaviors (Table 4). This supports the central aim of the study by indicating that BMI, as a health outcome, is meaningfully linked with both NL and SHE practices. While obesity remains a complex, multifactorial public health issue, the present results indicate that enhancing literacy could contribute not only to healthier dietary practices but also to more sustainable patterns of consumption.

Taken together, these results provide clear evidence that improving NL among university students can play a pivotal role in fostering SHE behaviors. Although the instruments used in this study capture distinct dimensions, their observed associations demonstrate the potential of literacy-focused interventions to simultaneously promote individual well-being and environmental sustainability. Future research should test targeted literacy-based programs and evaluate long-term effectiveness in shaping sustainable dietary behaviors among young adults.

Strengths and limitations

This research diverges from previous ones in two key aspects. First, it examines the relationship between NL and SHE behaviors specifically among university students, rather than the general population. Second, it investigates the association between SHE behaviors and BMI within this demographic. By employing standardized instruments to assess NL, SHE behaviors, and BMI, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of eating patterns among university students. These findings may highlight potential gaps in current educational curricula related to nutrition and underscore the need for targeted interventions to enhance NL in this population.

A notable limitation of this research is potential selection bias due to single-university recruitment, which limits generalizability to the broader population. This is primarily related to the young average age of the university students and the specific cultural characteristics of the sample. Moreover, students’ academic programs may have acted as a potential confounder influencing the observed associations. In addition, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, causality cannot be inferred from the results. It should be acknowledged, however, that the e-HDLQ and SHE Behaviors scales measure distinct but complementary dimensions—knowledge/literacy and behavioral practices. Therefore, the regression coefficients should not be interpreted as directly comparable in magnitude across instruments. Rather, they provide evidence of significant associations, indicating that higher NL levels are associated with more favorable SHE behaviors. Although Türkiye is geographically well-positioned to adopt the MD, actual dietary habits—particularly in the central and eastern regions—often diverge considerably from this model, with a heavier emphasis on meat consumption. This research was conducted in Gaziantep, a city characterized by a rich and complex culinary tradition influenced by substantial internal migration from eastern Türkiye. As a result, the local food culture reflects a blend of multiple regional dietary patterns. Furthermore, the participants were primarily drawn from the local population, which may further limit the applicability of the results to other regions with different dietary practices and cultural contexts.

Another limitation is that students with chronic diseases or those following therapeutic diets were excluded from the study. This may have biased the sample toward healthier participants and, consequently, led to an underestimation of challenges related to NL and SHE behaviors. In addition, as the data were collected through self-report, the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias cannot be excluded. The absence of a quantitative dietary assessment method (e.g., 24-hour recall or food frequency questionnaire) limited the ability to objectively evaluate diet quality. Although the culinary culture of the city where the research was conducted—heavily influenced by eastern regions—may be considered a limitation, the inclusion of university students from across Türkiye allows the results to offer a broader perspective on the country’s nutritional behaviors.

Conclusion

This research has provided valuable insights into the examined relationships. Firstly, it is noteworthy that e-HDLQ was significantly associated with SHE behaviors, suggesting a potential role in promoting SHE patterns. Secondly, the results suggest that higher e-HDLQ and SHE behavior scores reflect healthier dietary patterns, whereas increased BMI is linked to less favorable outcomes. These results highlight the role of NL—particularly healthy eating habits and adequate, balanced nutrition—as key determinants of sustainable and health-promoting dietary behaviors.

From a conceptual perspective, this research underscores the significant influence of individuals’ NL on SHE behaviors. This result highlights the necessity of expanding food and nutrition education beyond traditional health-related disciplines within educational institutions. Integrating such education across all academic fields can play a pivotal role in promoting sustainability among young adults, and fostering more conscious food consumption practices. Moreover, the research’s outcomes offer valuable insights for developing future educational materials, emphasizing the effectiveness of NL as a dimension of SHE behaviors.
