Genesis and Accumulation Modes of Coalbed Methane at the Eastern Margin of Ordos Basin, China: Evidence from Its Geochemistry
Authors: Zhanwei Li, Shida Chen, Dazhen Tang, Yuanhao Zhi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The genesis, occurrence, and accumulation of coalbed methane (CBM) are critical to the methane exploration and development. Combining the geological and geochemical data from CBM exploration wells and basin modeling, the genesis and accumulation characteristics of CBM in different regions and depths at the eastern margin of Ordos Basin were elucidated. Regional-scale gas content is controlled by depth and coal rank without turning depth occurs, but blocks perform variably. Three CBM genetic types, that is, secondary microbial, thermogenic, and mixed genesis, were identified based on data of gas components and isotopic composition. Under the influences of tectonic and hydrogeological conditions, secondary microbial gas is widely distributed in shallow coal seams (depth <1350 m) showing mixed gas characteristics, while secondary thermogenic gas dominates in deep coal seams. The extensive variations of carbon isotope of methane raised from desorption–diffusion induced by tectonic uplift, dissolution in flowing groundwater, secondary microbial gas generation, and rapid gas generation enhanced by magmatic thermal. Three CBM accumulation modes have been concluded depending on the depth and coal rank. The Baode mode is characterized by a high-ratio secondary microbial gas replenishment, typically occurring at lower ranks and shallow depths. The Linxing mode is distinguished by magmatic thermal influences, and the Daji mode features massive secondary thermogenetic gas associated with higher ranks and deeper depths. Every mode has the potential to form high-gas-content zones.

Introduction

Coalbed methane (CBM) is a low-carbon clean energy source, and its utilization has triple benefits of “energy, environmental protection, and safety”,1−5 which plays an important role in China’s energy demand security and the realization of carbon peak and neutrality goals.6 China is rich in CBM resources, ranking third globally.7 After over 40 years of exploration and development practice, great strides have been made in both geological theory and commercial development.8−11 However, the industrial scale of CBM in China still faces challenges, with below 3% of proven resources and less than 10 × 109 m3 of annual surface gas production.6,11

CBM is generally categorized into organic, inorganic, and mixed types via its soures and formation stages.12−17 Organic gas consists of microbial (biogenic) and thermogenic types.12 Microbial genesis can be further classified into primary and secondary microbial,18−20 while thermogenic genesis further divides into thermal degradation and cracking.21 The formation and evolution of CBM are controlled by the coupling of gaseous parent material, organic matter thermogenic evolution, tectonic–hydrodynamic conditions, and microbial degradation.12,16 Therefore, the occurrence of CBM reservoirs is characterized by the coexistence of microbial and thermogenic gases in several coal-bearing basins worldwide.21−28 Numerous examples confirmed in exploration practice, such as Upper Silesian Basin,24 Bowen Basin,26 San Juan Basin,18 Powder River Basin,27 Junggar Basin,29 and Fuxin Basin.30 The genesis and evolution processes of various genetic types of CBM manifest in discernible stages and patterns, resulting in significant differences in its geochemistry.1,31 Generally, the genesis of CBM can be identified by components and isotopic compositions.1,20,21 Initially, CBM genesis identification heavily depended on parameters of conventional natural gas, employing criteria to select relevant factors and delineate genetic boundaries.32−35 However, due to the constraints posed by a limited sample data set and restricted geographical range, the resulting application often led to contradictions and fallacies.15,36 The revised genetic diagrams, utilizing a compiled global database of over 20,000 gas samples,37 offer a more comprehensive and responsive approach to identify CBM genesis.

The genesis, occurrence, and accumulation patterns are crucial for strategically identifying favorable CBM regions and devising effective exploration and development methods. The eastern margin of Ordos Basin is one of the demonstrative CBM industrial bases in China.38 Currently, the depth interval for CBM exploration and production has reached beyond 2000 m, encompassing low-to-high-rank coal reservoirs.38,39 Previous research on CBM genesis and accumulation in the region was identified by the analysis of limited geochemical and geological data within shallow depth in specific blocks,28,40−47 which cannot fully reflect the distribution patterns both vertically and regionally. In this work, geological and geochemical data within 400–2500 m depth from 226 CBM exploration wells in various CBM blocks were comprehensively analyzed to elucidate CBM genesis and accumulation, considering variable factors including depth, coal rank, and magmatism. The findings are intended to offer guidance for CBM exploration in the near future.

Geological Setting

The Ordos Basin is situated at the west side of the North China Craton, which is a large-scale and multioverlapping basin developed on the top of the Paleozoic Craton (Figure 1a). The eastern margin of Ordos Basin displays a large and gently dipping monoclinic structure with five large faults, covering an area of over 4.5 × 104 km2 (Figure 1b).48,49 The tectonic deformation intensity in the north is comparatively weaker than that in the south. In the northern part, there is a prevalence of west-dipping monoclinic tectonics with few small faults, while the southern part exhibits more developed faults and folds (Figure 1c). Following the structural pattern, the study area has been segmented into several CBM blocks, including the Junger, Hequ, Baode, Linxing, Sanjiaobei, Sanjiao, Liulin, Shilou, Daji, Yanchuannan, and Hancheng blocks from north to south.

The study area underwent significant coal accumulation over the Middle Ordovician Majiagou Formation limestone,50 forming the Carboniferous–Permian (C–P) coal measures (Figure 2). The Taiyuan Formation and Shanxi Formation are the main coal-bearing strata of the C–P coal measures with more than 10 coal seams developed vertically. Two main minable coal seams are available for CBM exploration, including the No. 8 + 9 coal seam in the Taiyuan Formation and the No. 4 + 5 coal seam in the Shanxi Formation.51 The distribution of minable seams is relatively stable, mostly ranging between 10 and 15 m in thickness, and generally exhibits a trend of “north-thick and south-thin”.51 Moreover, the structural pattern has created depth variations: shallow in the east and deeper in the west, as well as shallow in the north and deeper in the south.51 According to the available exploration data, coal rank in the area ranges from subbituminous to anthracite (Figure 1c), and the distribution pattern of maximum vitrinite reflectance (Ro,max) resembles that of the corresponding depth of coal seams.51,52 Localized coal seams in the Linxing block were affected by magmatic intrusion resulting in coalification advance; hence, the Ro,max values vary in circular pattern surrounding the Zijinshan massif (abnormal high values over 4%), disrupting the regional trend.51

As the result of long-term tectonic uplift since the Late Early Cretaceous, the coal seams near the eastern basin boundary have become shallowly buried or exposed to the surface (Figure 1c). Generally, the coal seams are not hydraulically connected to upper and lower aquifers, unless faults interfere. Meteoric water was available to infiltrate into the main coal seams along the dip direction. Thus, the overall mineralization of coal seam water rises with an increased depth (from several hundred to over 300,000 mg/L), as well as hydrodynamic condition gradually weakened to stagnant.39,54

Materials and Methods

The geological and geochemical data sets (depth, Ro,max, gas content, gas component, and stable isotope composition) were compiled from 226 CBM exploration wells in the study area. Due to varying exploration degree in different CBM blocks, the quantity of available measured data differs widely. The data for the Linxing and Yanchuannan blocks were primarily obtained from publicly available sources,43−45 whereas the remaining blocks were sourced from PetroChina Coalbed Methane Company Limited records, some of which have been reported in previous studies.40−42,46,47 Note that not all types of data used in the study are available for every CBM exploration well due to limitations in sampling and testing. The collected data include 360 sets of coal seam depths, 260 sets of Ro,max, 358 sets of gas content, 275 sets of gas component, 236 sets of δ13C–CH4, 95 sets of δ13C–CO2, and 133 sets of δD-CH4. The number of samples referenced for each data type is as follows: 91, 80, 79, 94, 28, and 38, respectively.

The methods for aforementioned data measurement and selection are described below: (1) The depth of coal seam was determined from drilling and logging data, and the midseam depth was employed. (2) Measurement of Ro,max values for coal samples follows the China National Standard “Method of determining microscopically the reflectance of vitrinite in coal” (GB/T 6948-2008).55 The average value of multiple samples from the same coal seam was chosen as the representative value. (3) Gas content of coal core samples was obtained by the direct method in accordance with the China National Standard “Method of determining coalbed methane content” (GB/T 19559-2008, GB/T 19559-2021).56,57 The final gas content results were reported on an air-dried basis for removing impurities. The arithmetic average of the measured gas content values at comparable depths within the same coal seam in individual wells was selected for subsequent analysis. (4) Gas components of CBM samples (including coal core desorption gas and wellhead drainage gas) were analyzed using an Agilent 7890A series gas chromatograph according to the China National Standard “Gas chromatography for composition analysis of natural gas” (GB/T 13610-2014).58 The volume fractions of gas components detected were reported based on the air-deduction basis. (5) Stable carbon and hydrogen isotope compositions (δ13C–CH4, δ13C–CO2, and δD-CH4) of gas samples were determined using a MAT253 stable isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). More details of the experimental procedures for gas stable isotope measurement can be found in ref (59). The δ13C and δD values were reported in per mil (‰) units relative to the VPDB (Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite) scale and the VSMOW (Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water) scale, respectively. The precision of gas stable isotope measurement is ±0.5‰ for δ13C and ±1‰ for δD.

Numerical simulation was performed using the advanced petroleum system modeling technique PetroMod 16.0 (developed by Schlumberger Oil Field Service Company) to recover the burial–thermal evolution history of the C–P coal measures in the study area. Drilling and logging data of CBM wells provide fundamental input parameters required for basin modeling, such as depth, lithology, thickness, and geologic stratification. Geologic time scales of stratigraphic deposition and erosion were defined by combining the stratigraphic chart of Ordos Basin and region-specific geologic evolution features.51,60 Erosional thicknesses in critical geological periods were calculated using the mudstone acoustic time-difference method in comparison to previous results.61,62 Detailed procedures for determining the boundary conditions of the geologic models could be found in previous research.62 Measured Ro,max values were used as constraints to continuously optimize the established geologic models.

Results

Gas Content

Measured data from various CBM blocks in the study area reveal substantial variability in regional gas contents (0.05–37.16 m3/t), which exhibit a linearly correlation with increasing depth and Ro,max values (Figure 3). Excluding outliers resulting from high ash content, ultrahigh Ro,max, and excessive core lifting time, the performance varies from specific to different blocks. For example, gas contents of coal seams with a lower coal rank in the northern parts (Junger, Hequ, and Baode blocks) show an increasing trend followed by a decrease with increasing depth and reach peak value at ∼700–800 m (Figure 3a). In the Baode block, this variation ranges from 1.8 to 6.8 m3/t at a similar Ro,max of ∼0.75% (Figure 3b). Whereas in the central part dominated by medium rank (Linxing, Sanjiaobei, and Sanjiao blocks), most measured gas content varies consistently with the regional trend, but deep coal seams (1700–2100 m) vary considerably, with some values exceeding 30 m3/t. Medium–high-rank coals from the Liulin block and the southern area at depths <2000 m exhibit a turning depth of ∼1100–1200 m in measured gas content data. In contrast, a gas content trend in deep coal seams (>2000 m) in the Shilou and Daji blocks remains upward without reversal. Meanwhile, when comparing coal seams of similar rank, the gas content in the Shilou and Daji blocks is significantly higher than that observed in shallow to mid-deep seams in the Yanchuannan and Hancheng blocks (Figure 3b).

Gas Component

The statistical analysis of gas component data reveals that the primary constituents of the CBM are CH4, followed by N2 and CO2. Additionally, there is a relatively low concentration of C2+, which include C2H6 and long-chain hydrocarbons. The CH4 concentration spans from 11.65 to 99.68%, with concentrations predominantly exceeding 90% in medium–high-rank coals in the southern part and falling below 90% in low–medium rank coals in the northern part (Figure 4a). The distribution of the C2+ concentration ranges from 0 to 8.44%, concentrating predominantly below 0.5% (Figure 4b). Concerning non-hydrocarbon gases, N2 concentration ranges from 0 to 33.19%, mostly below 8%, with average predominantly below 5% (Figure 4c), showing a regional inverse correlation with CH4. CO2 concentration exhibits a slightly narrower range from 0 to 13.83%, with average values mostly lower than 5% (Figure 4d). From a regional perspective, the concentrations of CBM components show significant variability and these distributions tend to become more concentrated with increasing depth. Furthermore, the variable gas fraction indexes indicate the complexity of current CBM sources in their genesis and distribution. The dryness index (C1/C1–5) ranges from 0.86 to infinitesimally close to 1, with mostly concentrates above 0.99 (Figure 5a), indicating extremely dry gas. Conversely, the hydrocarbon index (C1/[C2+C3]) ranges from 5.8 to 9675 (with a single anomalous value of 47,330 in the Daji block), indicating a more dispersed distribution in shallower coal seams and deep seams in the Linxing block (Figure 5b). In addition, CO2–CH4 index, represented by CDMI (CO2/ [CO2+ CH4] × 100%), ranges from 0.17 to 39.6%, with concentration mostly below 5% (Figure 5c).

Gas Isotopic Composition

δ13C–CH4 and δD-CH4

As shown in Figure 6, the δ13C–CH4 and δD-CH4 values exhibit a broad range of variability. Specifically, δ13C–CH4 values range from −81.9 to −9.1‰, with the majority falling within the interval of −65 to −30‰, while δD-CH4 values vary between −259.4 and −155‰. Regionally, both δ13C–CH4 and δD-CH4 values displays a positive correlation with the depth and Ro,max. However, δ13C–CH4 values exhibit a more pronounced trend compared to the relatively discrete variations in δD-CH4 values.

δ13C–CH4 and δD-CH4 of coalbed methane relative to the corresponding depth and Ro,max of the coal seam at the eastern margin of Ordos Basin. (a) δ13C–CH4 versus depth; (b) δ13C–CH4 versus Ro,max; (c) δD-CH4 versus depth; (d) δD-CH4 versus Ro,max.

In distinct CBM blocks, δ13C–CH4 values within the Hequ block are lighter than −80‰ due to the shallow depth and low rank. In the Junger block, δ13C–CH4 progressively shifts toward positive values, ranging from −66.8 to −60.5‰ with increasing depth (Figure 6a,b). For the Baode block, the isotopic values of CH4 (δ13C–CH4, –61.8 to −45.7‰; δD-CH4, –259.4 to −231.6‰) change significantly with depth. Moreover, the range of isotopic values (δ13C–CH4, –65.7 to −34.7‰; δD-CH4, –259.2 to −201.2‰) for CH4 in the Linxing block becomes relatively wider due to complex impacts caused by the intrusion of the Zijinshan massif. Within the Liulin block, which is mainly composed of medium rank coal, δ13C–CH4 values vary from −62.1 to −42.3‰, with a mean value of −52.8‰ (Figure 6a,b).

δ13C–CO2

The δ13C–CO2 ranges from −29.6 to 14.6‰, representing a narrower range compared to the δ13C–CH4 distribution, which exhibit a more discrete distribution at shallower depths with lower Ro,max values (Figure 7). On the regional scale, the δ13C–CO2 values tend to become lighter with increasing depth and coal rank, but variations in the change pattern are evident between different blocks. For example, the complex variations observed with an increasing depth in the Baode block deviate from the regional trend (Figure 7a). Additionally, anomalously high δ13C–CO2 values are present in high-rank coals of the Yanchuannan and Hancheng blocks (Figure 7b).

Discussion

CBM Genesis at the Eastern Margin of Ordos Basin

Genetic Types of CBM

With the integrated interpretation of gas genetic diagrams, the origins of the CBM from different blocks in the study area are complex and diverse (Figure 8). The presence of microbial gas is notably significant in the northern part, primarily composing of low-to-medium-rank coals. In the Junger block, there is compelling evidence that CBM predominantly constitutes microbial gas, as inferred from the points in the primary microbial gas field (Figure 8a,b). However, the graphical results present a contradiction, suggesting that determining the main formation pathway remains inconclusive (Figure 8a,b,e). Gas samples from the Hequ block may consist of primary microbial gas generated through CO2 reduction, yet accurately defining whether mixing with early mature thermogenic gas occurs challenging due to limited sampling data (Figure 8a,e,f). For the Baode block, the data of CBM samples are scattered somewhat concentrically across the fields of primary microbial gas, early to midmature thermogenic gas, and secondary microbial gas (Figure 8). These points are distributed on both sides of the upper end element (−55 or −50 ‰) of δ13C–CH4 of primary microbial origin,37 demonstrating a potential mixing of primary microbial gas with early thermogenic gas (Figure 8a,b). Additionally, the initial mixture may have mixed with subsequent thermogenic gases, further complicated by superimposed late-stage biodegradation and the mixing of secondary microbial CH4 from the CO2 reduction (Figure 8e,f).

For the Linxing block, characterized as a magmatic intrusion impact zone, the complexity of CBM genesis is evident. For example, some gas samples are found outside the type III kerogen field but within the area of type II kerogen (Figure 8a). Additionally, the distribution pattern presents in the overlap of secondary microbial and thermogenic gas fields (Figure 8b–d), demonstrating possible effects of secondary microbial processes. Moreover, the data in the overlap of thermogenic and abiotic gas fields suggest potential effects associated with the mixing of abiotic gas from magmatic intrusion (Figure 8c,d,f).

In the higher-rank coals of the Daji, Yanchuannan, and Hancheng blocks, the CBM is predominantly derived from thermogenic processes and influenced by migration–diffusion. Some shallow seams exhibit blending with secondary microbial gas. The data points observed in the overlap of secondary microbial and thermogenic gas fields may be attributed to biodegradation or migration influences that contribute to the enrichment of CH4 (Figure 8a,b). Simultaneously, the presence within the overlap of thermogenic and abiotic gas fields implies that CBM might have undergone hydrothermal modification during the tectonothermal event in the Early Cretaceous.63 However, several data points deviate from the expected genetic fields, potentially stemming from an underestimation of the range of δ13C–CO2 and δD-CH4 values associated with thermogenic gas in the genetic diagrams.29,37

Origins of CO2 in CBM

There are two major sources of CO2: inorganic and organic.17 Organic CO2 genesis can be subdivided into microbial activity (microbial CO2) and organic matter thermal degradation (thermogenic CO2).17,24 Generally, the diagrams of δ13C–CO2 versus CO2 concentration and δ13C–CO2 versus CDMI can be used to discriminate CO2 origin.64,65

In Figure 9, a significant overlap in the CO2 genetic fields of gas samples suggests the complexity of the evolutionary process during coalification. Although several gas samples from the Junger, Baode, Linxing, and Yanchuannan blocks are situated within inorganic gas field (Figure 9a), low concentrations (<15%) suggest that such CO2 is still of organic origin associated with secondary methanogenesis after biodegradation. CO2 in the Junger and Hequ blocks primarily originates from microbial methanogenesis and decarboxylation processes during coalification, considering the shallow depth and low coal rank. The origin of CO2 in the Baode block is primarily associated with microbial methanogenesis due to a low coal rank and water intrusion. For the Linxing block, thermogenic CO2 is predominantly in the coal seams with the medium coal rank, mixed with CO2 derived from microbial methanogenesis-related in the shallow seams with lower rank. Additionally, CO2 generation from thermal decomposition of organic matter during the thermal degradation to the thermal cracking gas stage predominates in the Yanchuannan block and deep coal seams of the Daji block. Conversely, a notable proportion of the CO2 observed in the medium-to-high-rank coals from the Hancheng block is attributed to microbial activities associated with secondary methanogenesis. Overall, the available data indicate that the CO2 in coal seams is of organic origin, associated with microbial methanation or the thermal decomposition of organic matter.

Quantitative Evaluation of Different Genetic CBM

Given that most CBM blocks in the study area encompass both microbial and thermogenic gases, a quantitative assessment of their relative contributions becomes imperative for a more comprehensive understanding of CBM formation processes. Typically, the δ13C–CH4 end value of thermogenic CBM depends on the coal rank and can be calculated using empirical formulas proposed by ref (66). In contrast, there is no uniform guide number for the δ13C–CH4 end value of microbial CBM. For instance, ref (14) selected −70‰ after statistically analyzing the δ13C–CH4 values of conventional microbial gases worldwide, while refs (59) and (67) opted for the measured lightest value. In this study, the measured lightest δ13C–CH4 value in the study area (−81.9‰) was chosen as the end value for microbial CH4, referencing the genetic diagrams proposed by ref (37). Focusing solely on the mixing of thermogenic and microbial gases leading to carbon isotope fractionation, a binary mixing model was employed for the calculations.59,66

According to the calculation results (Table 1), the contribution of microbial gas exhibits regionally higher values in the northern and eastern portions, while lower values are observed in the western and southern areas, particularly in shallow coal seams. In the northern part, microbial gas predominates in the Junger and Hequ blocks, constituting an average contribution of 66 and 99%, respectively. In the Baode block, microbial gas serves as a significant supplement, with an average proportion of ∼43%, and over 60% of gas samples exceeding 40%, reaching up to ∼62%. In the Linxing block, the average proportion of microbial gas accounts for ∼30%, with one-third of gas samples exceeding 40%, reaching the highest value of ∼69% in shallow coal seams. Microbial degradation of the high-rank coal is relatively weak, which cannot support the substantial generation of secondary microbial gas generation.28 Therefore, within the central to southern parts dominated by medium to high coal rank, the contribution of microbial gas would be surpassed by thermogenic gas with increasing depth until dominance.

Note that the heterogeneity of coal seams and diverse factors affecting δ13C–CH4 of CBM introduce limitations to the calculation results for the study area. For instance, gas samples from the Hequ block were obtained from coals in the CBM oxidation zone, where methane oxidation cannot be ignored. Nevertheless, these results generally align with the identification results of the empirical genetic diagrams described above. CBM genesis in the study area is characterized by the dominance of microbial genesis in the northern part with low coal rank, transitioning to thermal genesis in the central to southern parts characterized by medium to high coal rank. Additionally, mixed genesis gas formed by secondary microbial gas replenishment prevails in shallow coal seams, while evolving into thermal genesis dominance in deep coal seams with a transition from thermal degradation gas to thermal cracking from north to south.

Differential Formation and Isotopic Responses of CBM

Formation Process of CBM

The variations in tectonic–thermal evolution processes and geological conditions for the C–P coal measures in the study area constitute a pivotal factor resulting in distinctions in CBM formation and evolution. The modeled burial–thermal evolution history revealed that the coal measures underwent modifications due to superimposed tectonic movements during the Indosinian, Yanshanian, and Himalayan periods following sedimentation (Figure 10). The resulting alterations in the depth of coal seams and geothermal conditions led to differences in CBM formation and evolution. Overall, the burial–thermal evolution degree displayed an obviously distribution trend, being weaker in the north and stronger in the south, with Linxing block that experiencing modification due to magmatic intrusion.28 The maximum historical depth was achieved during the Early Cretaceous, which determined the thermal evolution degree of coal, establishing the basic pattern of thermogenic gas generation. The dominant emplacement period of the Zijinshan massif matched the regional secondary rapid subsidence stage.68 The associated anomalously rapid heating further enhanced the rate and intensity of secondary thermogenic gas generation in the coal seams.28 Subsequent regional return uplift since the Late Early Cretaceous period led to decreasing temperature and pressure of the coal seams as depth shallowed, resulting in the termination of gas generation as thermal maturity ceased to rise. Simultaneously, the intrusion of meteoric water introduced methanogenic bacteria and nutrients, creating favorable conditions for the formation of secondary microbial gas within the suitable temperature range.12,24

Carbon Isotopic Fractionation

The carbon isotope signatures of CBM exhibit variability influenced by various factors, including mixing with secondary microbial gases, water dissolution, desorption/diffusion effects, CH4/CO2 isotope exchange reactions, and thermodynamic fractionation effects due to magma intrusion.16,18,28,37,44,69 The thermal maturity of coal organic matter represents a critical determinant of carbon isotope value at the initial stage of methanogenesis.16,44 The tectonic and hydrologic background of the study area resulted in commonalities and differences in the factors affecting the carbon isotope signatures of CBM.

During the initial coalification stage, CH4/CO2 exchange reactions occur at high concentrations of CH4 and CO2,28 but their impact on the lightening of δ13C–CH4 in the existing CBM of the study area is considered negligible. Because most primary microbial gas generated in the early stage dispersed due to poor preservation conditions during tectonic uplift;22 in the secondary deep burial stage of the C–P coal measures, partial magmatic thermometamorphism superimposed on deep burial metamorphism in the Linxing block enhanced the degradation/cracking rate of 12C-rich side-chain alkyls. This, coupled with accelerated gas diffusion rates, led to the lightening of δ13C–CH4 with increasing Ro,max.28

Moreover, long-term tectonic uplift since the Late Early Cretaceous has resulted in a reduction in depth and subsequent pressure leakage of coal seams, leading to CBM desorption–diffusion. Specifically, 12CH4 preferentially desorbed and diffused from deep to shallow seams, with some escaping gas being reabsorbed.28,44 This dynamic process has given rise to a δ13C–CH4 vertical zoning pattern in the current CBM. Consequently, 13C–CH4 becomes relatively enriched with increasing depth, a phenomenon that becomes more conspicuous as coal rank increases. In addition, the preservation conditions of CBM have been significantly influenced by tectonic activities subsequent to the deposition of the C–P coal measures. As tectonic deformation was strong in the east and weak in the west, shallow coal seams in the east were exposed or connected with the aquifers through faults, creating conditions for meteoric water recharge. The hydrogeological condition was the key factor controlling the accumulation of mixed genesis CBM reservoirs with low-to-medium-rank coals.12,20,65,70,71 In active hydrodynamic zones, flowing groundwater tended to carry away 13CH4 by preferential dissolution, overlaid the conversion of free and adsorbed gases. This prolonged process resulted in the long-term accumulation and subsequent depletion of 13CH4.69 In contrast, within inactive hydrodynamic zones, secondary microbial gas generated or migrated following coal seams uplift and mixing with thermogenic gas led to lightening of 13CH4, which tended to occur prominently in the lower coal rank in the northern part.40,46

CBM Accumulation Modes

Considering the genesis and distribution of CBM in the study area, three types of CBM accumulation modes were established, including the Baode mode supplemented by secondary microbial gas, the Linxing mode influenced by magmatic intrusion, and the Daji mode dominated by massive secondary thermogenic gas. The Baode mode applies to the northern part with lower coal metamorphism including the Jungar, Hequ, and Baode blocks. Conversely, the Daji mode is pertinent to the central to southern parts with higher coal rank, including the Shilou, Daji, Yanchuannan, and Hancheng blocks. Notwithstanding the fact that the Linxing mode highlights the impact of magmatic intrusion, the normal trend variation is still applicable to the neighboring areas, including the Sanjiaobei, Sanjiao, and Liulin blocks.

Baode Mode

The maximum historical depth of the C–P coal measures in the Baode block was relatively shallow (∼2200 m), and coalification was dominated by deep metamorphism (Figure 10). As the maximum historical temperature reached 100–120 °C, with average Ro,max at ∼0.8%, the gas generation peak stage of thermal degradation (Ro,max ∼1.1%) had not yet been reached.31 Thus, there was limited thermogenic gas accumulation during the brief gas generation period (∼12 Ma). The subsequent significant uplift led to the desorption and diffusion of part of adsorbed gas due to the rebound of the compaction degree and the continuous decrease of temperature and pressure. The free gas mostly escaped due to poor preservation conditions, and the originally saturated CBM reservoirs gradually converted to undersaturated.70 The supplementation of elevated secondary microbial gas then increased the gas content from ∼500 to 1100 m. Simultaneously, the high-gas-content zone also exhibits lighter δ13C–CH4 (Figure 12). The extreme conditions of deep coal seams, characterized by high temperature and salinity, were not conducive to the survival of microorganisms.40 Methanogenesis cannot be initiated due to insufficient dilution from meteoric water recharge.19 Thus, current CBM gradually transforms into predominantly thermogenic gas with increasing depth. Available data suggest that the contribution of secondary microbial gas at depths of less than 600 m is generally more than 50%. Mixed genetic gas predominates in deeper coal reserviors (600 m to ∼1300 m), with secondary microbial gas mostly accounting for ∼30 to 50% (Figure 13). Contribution of secondary microbial gas tends to decrease with depth and fluctuates depending on the tectonic and hydrological conditions.

Linxing Mode

The normal trend of coal rank in the Linxing block is generally in medium rank (Ro,max concentrates at 0.65–1% in shallow, 1–1.5% in deep). In specific areas, magma intrusion has resulted in coal rank jump.52 Shallow coal seams in the northeastern part are extremely similar to those of the Baode block.45 In the west-central part, deep coal seams with not much difference in maximum historical depths have undergone varying degrees of alteration at different distances from the Zijinshan massif. Magmatic intrusion promoted secondary thermogenic gas (thermal degradation gas to thermal cracking gas) generation to increase gas content, and the quantity was contingent upon the thermal maturity. Also, the tectonic damaging effects on preservation conditions diminish with increasing distance. Fo instances where tectonic modification was weak during later uplift processes, high-gas-content zones where exceeding regional saturated adsorbed gas content background can be formed under favorable preservation conditions (Figure 12). Overall, current CBM in shallow depths shows mixed genesis (<1350 m), secondary microbial gas replenishment shows an overall weakening trend with depth (Figure 13). Deep CBM is of thermal genesis, with secondary thermogenic gas generated by thermal decomposition of organic matter caused by magma intrusion, providing sufficient gas for CBM enrichment and accumulation.

Daji Mode

The maximum historical depth of the C–P coal measures in the Daji block varied significantly influenced by tectonic movements, while the amplitude of the tectonic uplift remained relatively consistent. Consequently, the Ro,max values exhibit an increasing trend with depth, where shallow seams generally have Ro,max values less than 2%, while deep seams predominantly surpass 2.5%. Moreover, the southeastern part of the Daji block experienced strong tectonic extrusion and possessed relatively complex geological conditions, contrasting with the tectonically stabilized northwest part characterized by simpler geological conditions. Shallow CBM is characterized by a mixed origin gas dominated by thermogenic gas (accounting for over 80%), and the existing data revealed that secondary microbial gas can be found at depths of <1250 m (Figure 13). Deep CBM is composed of secondary thermogenic gas generated during the periods of high to over thermal maturity, which not only resulted in higher gas content but also increased the proportion of in situ assigned free gas.39,72 Despite the impact of later tectonic uplift, the preservation conditions of deep seams remain relatively favorable due to larger depth, effective capping and sealing properties of surrounding rocks, and hydrodynamic lateral sealing. Therefore, high gas content is more prevalent in deep seams (Figure 12), averaging more than 20 m3, and has been proven by development practices.38,39
