Effects of Combined Intravenous and Topical Magnesium Sulfate Administration on Pain, Stress, and Functional Recovery After Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Prospective, Randomized, Blinded Controlled Trial
Authors: Qiuru Wang, Yinchan Wang, Jian Hu, Changjun Chen, Jing Yang, Pengde Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEvidence suggests that both intravenous and intraoperative topical application of magnesium sulfate can improve pain management after total knee arthroplasty (TKA). However, the optimal method of magnesium sulfate administration remains unclear, and no studies have evaluated the effect of combined intravenous and topical application on post-TKA pain management. This study aimed to assess the effects of combined intravenous and topical magnesium sulfate on pain, stress, and functional recovery following TKA.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 150 patients undergoing TKA were randomly assigned to one of three groups: the Intravenous Group (received intravenous magnesium sulfate intraoperatively), the Topical Group (received magnesium sulfate added to the local infiltration analgesic cocktail), and the Combined Group (received both intravenous and topical magnesium sulfate). The primary outcome was the resting pain score at 24 hours postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included pain scores at other time points, postoperative morphine consumption for rescue analgesia, postoperative knee range of motion, walking ability, hospital length of stay, intraoperative stress levels (intraoperative inhaled anesthetic consumption, incidence of intraoperative hypertension), postoperative stress levels (postoperative serum cortisol/ACTH levels), and complication rates.ResultsNo significant difference was found in the resting Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores at 24 hours postoperatively among the three groups. Compared to the Intravenous and Topical groups, the Combined Group showed significantly lower VAS scores at several time points on the day of surgery, although this did not reach the minimal clinically important difference. No significant differences were observed among the three groups for the other secondary outcomes.ConclusionCompared with intravenous or topical magnesium sulfate alone, combined intravenous and topical administration did not demonstrate a clinically significant advantage in postoperative pain management, stress response, or functional recovery. Further studies are warranted to determine the optimal route and regimen for perioperative magnesium sulfate administration in TKA.Keywords: local infiltration analgesia, intravenous administration, postoperative analgesia, randomized controlled trial, enhanced recovery

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is commonly associated with moderate-to-severe acute postoperative pain, with more than 60% of patients reported to experience severe pain after surgery.1–3 Inadequately controlled postoperative pain can impair sleep and quality of life and may increase the risks of thromboembolic events, infection, persistent postsurgical pain, and opioid dependence. These adverse effects may hinder early mobilization, delay functional recovery, and prolong hospitalization.4–6 Because TKA is primarily performed for patients with advanced knee osteoarthritis, effective management of pain and functional impairment is important throughout both the preoperative and postoperative periods. Recent randomized studies have shown that combining complementary interventions, such as manual therapy or knee taping, with supervised exercise may provide greater improvements in pain and physical function than exercise alone in patients with knee osteoarthritis.7,8 These findings further support the broader principle of integrating interventions with different mechanisms to optimize pain control and functional recovery.

Contemporary perioperative pain management for TKA therefore emphasizes multimodal analgesia, which combines pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions that act through different mechanisms.9–11 By targeting multiple pain pathways, multimodal regimens may improve analgesia while reducing opioid requirements and the adverse effects associated with reliance on a single analgesic agent.10,11 Magnesium sulfate has increasingly been investigated as an adjunct to multimodal analgesia following TKA.12–14 Magnesium ions exert analgesic and antihyperalgesic effects primarily through noncompetitive antagonism of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors and inhibition of calcium influx, thereby reducing neuronal hyperexcitability, central sensitization, and nociceptive signal transmission.13 Recent clinical studies and systematic reviews suggest that perioperative magnesium sulfate may improve early postoperative pain control and reduce analgesic requirements after TKA, although findings have not been entirely consistent across administration routes and study protocols.14–18

Previous studies have evaluated magnesium sulfate administered either intravenously during surgery15,16 or topically as an adjunct to the local infiltration analgesia (LIA) cocktail.17,18 Both approaches have shown potential analgesic benefits; however, the optimal route and regimen for perioperative magnesium sulfate administration after TKA remain uncertain. To our knowledge, no previous study has directly evaluated whether combining intravenous and topical magnesium sulfate provides additional benefits compared with either route alone. The rationale for evaluating a combined regimen is supported by experience with other perioperative medications. For example, combined intravenous and topical administration of tranexamic acid may reduce perioperative blood loss more effectively than either route alone,19,20 while combined systemic and periarticular corticosteroid administration may enhance postoperative anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects.21,22

We therefore hypothesized that combined intravenous and topical administration of magnesium sulfate would reduce postoperative pain and stress responses and improve functional recovery compared with either intravenous or topical administration alone. The primary objective of this randomized controlled trial was to compare the effects of these three magnesium sulfate administration strategies on postoperative pain after TKA. Secondary objectives were to evaluate their effects on perioperative stress responses, functional recovery, rescue analgesic consumption, length of hospital stay, and postoperative complications.

Materials and Methods

This randomized controlled trial received approval from the Clinical Trials and Biomedical Ethics Committee of our institution. The trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500107495) on August 12, 2025, prior to patient enrollment. The first participant was enrolled on August 25, 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All enrolled participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Patient Recruitment

Consecutive patients scheduled for primary unilateral TKA for knee osteoarthritis at our institution from August 2025 to February 2026 were screened. Inclusion criteria: age 18–80 years and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III. Exclusion criteria: (1) severe cardiac or pulmonary insufficiency; (2) abnormal hepatic or renal function (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] or aspartate aminotransferase [AST] >1.5 times the upper limit of normal, blood urea nitrogen [BUN] >8.3 mmol/L, or serum creatinine [Scr] >115 μmol/L); (3) poorly controlled diabetes mellitus with an associated risk of infection as judged by the investigator; (4) impaired consciousness or psychiatric disorders; (5) history of previous open knee surgery; (6) neuromuscular dysfunction affecting lower limb function; (7) severe coagulation disorders; (8) severe ligamentous instability not amenable to treatment with a ligament-retaining TKA prosthesis; (9) active systemic or local infection; (10) women who were planning pregnancy, pregnant, or lactating during the study period; (11) knee flexion contracture ≥30° or varus/valgus deformity ≥30°; (12) known allergy to any medication used in the study protocol; (13) opioid addiction; (14) language barriers or inability to complete the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain assessment; (15) any other condition deemed by the investigator to render the patient unsuitable for trial participation.

Randomization and Blinding

Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three treatment groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence created with Microsoft Excel. An independent researcher (Investigator 1), who was not involved in patient recruitment, treatment administration, outcome assessment, or data analysis, prepared sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing the group assignments. On the morning of surgery, a second independent researcher (Investigator 2) opened the corresponding envelope and informed the attending anesthesiologist of the assigned intervention.

Because preparation of the intravenous infusion and local infiltration analgesic solution differed according to group allocation, Investigator 2 and the attending anesthesiologist were necessarily aware of the treatment assignment. The attending anesthesiologist prepared the corresponding intravenous and local infiltration study medications in the central pharmacy before surgery. Neither Investigator 2 nor the attending anesthesiologist participated in postoperative outcome assessment, data collection, or statistical analysis.

The prepared study medications were provided to the surgical team without labels indicating their magnesium sulfate content. The surgeon administered the prepared medications according to the standardized study protocol but remained unaware of the treatment allocation. The patients, surgeon, postoperative outcome assessor (Investigator 3), and statistician (Investigator 4) remained blinded to group assignment throughout the study and until completion of the statistical analysis.

Surgery and Perioperative Management

Preemptive oral analgesia (celecoxib 200 mg and pregabalin 150 mg twice daily) was administered starting the day before surgery. All procedures were performed under standardized general anesthesia. Approximately 30 minutes before induction, an experienced anesthesiologist performed an adductor canal block according to a previously described technique.1

During induction, patients in the Intravenous and Combined groups received an intravenous loading dose of magnesium sulfate (40 mg/kg over 15 min), followed by a continuous infusion (10 mg/kg/h) until surgery end. The Topical group received a placebo saline infusion. All surgeries were performed by a single senior surgeon via a medial parapatellar approach using cemented prostheses, with a tourniquet (240 mmHg) applied throughout. No surgical drains were used.

Local infiltration analgesia (LIA) was performed intraoperatively. A 100 mL cocktail containing 0.2% ropivacaine, epinephrine (2.0 μg/mL), dexamethasone (0.1 mg/mL), and magnesium sulfate (2.5 mg/mL) was used for the Topical and Combined groups; the Intravenous group received the same cocktail without magnesium sulfate, as per prior description.23

Approximately 20 minutes before anticipated surgery end and prior to tourniquet release, intravenous sufentanil (5 μg), dexamethasone (10 mg), and tropisetron (5 mg) were administered for postoperative pain and nausea prophylaxis.

Postoperatively, oral celecoxib (200 mg) and pregabalin (150 mg) were given twice daily for pain control. Subcutaneous morphine (10 mg) was available as rescue analgesia for intolerable pain (resting VAS ≥4 or movement VAS ≥6).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the VAS pain score at rest 24 hours after surgery. Secondary outcomes included pain scores at other time points, postoperative consumption of morphine hydrochloride administered as rescue analgesia, knee range of motion, ambulation capacity, length of hospital stay, intraoperative and postoperative stress indicators, and incidence of complications.

All outcome measures were standardized, widely accepted clinical or laboratory assessments. Postoperative pain was evaluated using the validated and reliable Visual Analogue Scale (VAS),24 while the remaining outcomes consisted of objective clinical assessments or laboratory measurements that are routinely used in clinical practice and have been widely adopted in previous total knee arthroplasty studies.

Postoperative pain was assessed using a VAS,24 with scores ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (extreme pain). Assessments were conducted both at rest and during movement, with the latter defined as the pain level when the patient was instructed to flex the knee as much as possible. Measurements were recorded at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours postoperatively.

Knee range of motion was measured three times daily using a goniometer, with assessments spaced 6 hours apart; the highest value obtained each day was used for analysis. Ambulation ability was assessed on the mornings of postoperative days 1 and 2 by measuring the maximum distance each patient could walk in a single attempt without assistance.

Intraoperative stress-related parameters included the total consumption of inhaled anesthetics and the incidence of hypertensive episodes during the procedure. Postoperative stress was evaluated by measuring serum cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels on the mornings of postoperative days 1 and 2.25

Discharge criteria included adequate pain control with oral analgesics, the ability to independently ambulate more than 60 meters, capacity to climb stairs, and ability to transfer between positions without assistance.

The following postoperative complications were documented: cardiovascular events, neurological complications, electrolyte imbalances, nausea, vomiting, venous thrombotic events, falls, and wound-related issues (eg, wound oozing, delayed wound healing).

Sample Size Estimation

The primary endpoint of this study was the resting VAS pain score at 24 hours after surgery. Based on previous literature and clinical experience, a difference of 1.0 point in the VAS pain score was considered the minimal clinically important difference (MCID).

A pilot study involving 45 patients (15 patients per group, not included in the final trial analysis) was conducted to estimate the variability of the primary outcome and determine the expected effect size for sample size calculation. The preliminary resting VAS scores at 24 hours postoperatively were approximately 3.4±1.6, 3.3±1.6, and 2.9±1.6 in the Intravenous, Topical, and Combined groups, respectively. These pilot data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation solely for the purpose of estimating the standardized effect size required for the sample-size calculation based on a one-way ANOVA model. Based on these data, the standardized effect size (Cohen’s f) was estimated to be approximately 0.28.

Sample size calculation was performed using PASS 2023 based on a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) model for comparing three independent groups. The calculation assumed a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 90%, and an equal allocation ratio among the three groups (Intravenous Group: Topical Group: Combined Group = 1:1:1). The analysis indicated that a minimum of 45 patients per group was required. Considering a potential dropout rate of approximately 10%, the final sample size was increased to 50 patients per group, resulting in a total planned enrollment of 150 patients.

Statistical Analysis

All efficacy and safety analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle. All randomized participants were analyzed in the groups to which they were originally assigned. All 150 randomized participants completed the study, and complete data were available for the primary and secondary outcomes. Therefore, no missing-data imputation was required. Because there were no losses to follow-up or post-randomization exclusions, the intention-to-treat analysis population was identical to the population included in the final analysis.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Normality of distribution was assessed using histograms and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. For continuous variables that satisfied the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Variables that did not satisfy these assumptions, as well as ordinal data, were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with the Bonferroni correction was used to adjust the significance level for multiple comparisons among groups. Because resting and movement VAS pain scores were repeatedly assessed in the same participants at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours postoperatively, supplementary repeated-measures analyses were performed to evaluate differences in pain scores over the entire postoperative assessment period. Time was treated as the within-subject factor, and treatment group was treated as the between-subject factor. When an overall between-group difference was identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons among the Intravenous, Topical, and Combined groups were performed with adjustment for multiple comparisons. For categorical variables, the Pearson chi-square test was employed. Time to discharge was analyzed using survival analysis, with Kaplan–Meier curves compared using the Log rank test. Normally distributed continuous data are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed continuous data and ordinal data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data are summarized as counts and percentages. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For outcomes analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, the pairwise comparisons were performed, between-group effects were estimated using the Hodges–Lehmann location-shift estimate with the corresponding 95% confidence interval. For outcomes analyzed using one-way ANOVA, the pairwise effects were reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. Exact two-sided p-values were reported whenever available; p-values smaller than the numerical reporting limit of the statistical software were reported as p<0.001.

Secondary Outcomes

At individual postoperative time points, the Combined group had significantly lower resting VAS pain scores at 6 and 12 hours postoperatively than the Intravenous and Topical groups. The Combined group also had a significantly lower movement VAS pain score at 12 hours postoperatively (Tables 2 and 3).

Supplementary repeated-measures analysis demonstrated a significant main effect of treatment group on resting VAS pain scores across the postoperative assessment period (p<0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons showed no significant difference between the Intravenous and Topical groups (p=0.292). However, the Combined group had significantly lower overall resting VAS pain scores than both the Intravenous group (p<0.001) and the Topical group (p<0.001).

A significant main effect of treatment group was also observed for movement VAS pain scores across the postoperative assessment period (p=0.003). Post hoc pairwise comparisons showed no significant difference between the Intravenous and Topical groups (p=0.192). The Combined group had significantly lower overall movement VAS pain scores than the Intravenous group (p=0.035) and the Topical group (p=0.001).

No significant differences were observed among the three groups in terms of postoperative morphine hydrochloride consumption, knee range of motion, postoperative ambulation capacity, or length of hospital stay (Tables 4, 5 and Figure 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effects of combined intravenous and topical administration of magnesium sulfate on pain management after total knee arthroplasty (TKA). The main finding of this trial was that, although the combined regimen produced statistically significant reductions in some early postoperative VAS pain scores, these differences did not reach the prespecified threshold for clinical importance and therefore did not represent a clinically meaningful advantage over intravenous or topical magnesium sulfate alone. In addition, no clear difference was observed between intravenous and topical administration alone. Therefore, the present findings do not support the superiority of any one administration strategy, and further studies are needed to determine the optimal route and regimen.

Magnesium sulfate was initially administered intravenously as an adjunct for postoperative pain management following TKA. Approximately a decade ago, researchers began investigating intravenous magnesium sulfate after TKA, typically using a bolus dose of 40–50 mg/kg followed by a continuous infusion of 10–15 mg/kg/h.15,16,26,27 Several studies have reported that intraoperative intravenous magnesium sulfate may reduce postoperative pain and analgesic requirements, enhance functional recovery, and decrease the incidence of postoperative urinary retention.15,16,27 However, other studies have reported conflicting findings, with no significant improvement in postoperative pain outcomes.26 More recently, magnesium sulfate has been added to local infiltration analgesia (LIA) cocktails, commonly at a dose of 250 mg.17,18,23 Previous studies have suggested that adding magnesium sulfate to the analgesic mixture may prolong postoperative analgesia, reduce analgesic consumption, and alleviate early postoperative pain.17,18,23 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of five randomized controlled trials involving 432 patients also concluded that MgSO4 may be a useful adjunct to periarticular analgesic cocktails by improving early postoperative pain scores and reducing opioid consumption.28 Taken together, the available evidence suggests that both intravenous and topical magnesium sulfate may provide early postoperative analgesic benefits after TKA, although the optimal route remains uncertain.

Evidence from other perioperative interventions suggests that combined routes of administration may sometimes provide additional benefits compared with a single route. For example, combined intravenous and topical tranexamic acid may reduce perioperative blood loss more effectively than either route alone after TKA.19,20 Similarly, combined systemic and periarticular glucocorticoid administration may enhance anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects.21,22 On this basis, we hypothesized that combined intravenous and topical magnesium sulfate would further reduce postoperative pain and stress responses and improve functional recovery. The present randomized controlled trial was therefore conducted to evaluate this hypothesis.

Although patients in the Combined group had statistically lower resting VAS pain scores at 6 and 12 hours postoperatively and lower movement VAS pain scores at 12 hours than those in the single-route groups, the absolute between-group differences were less than 1 point. Supplementary repeated-measures analyses also demonstrated significant main effects of treatment group for both resting and movement VAS pain scores across the postoperative assessment period. However, statistical significance alone does not necessarily indicate a clinically meaningful benefit. Previous studies have suggested that a difference of at least 1 point in VAS pain score is required to reach the minimal clinically important difference (MCID).29,30 Because the observed between-group differences were below this threshold, their clinical relevance was limited despite statistical significance. Moreover, no significant difference was observed among the three groups in the prespecified primary outcome of resting VAS pain score at 24 hours postoperatively, and no significant differences were found in most other secondary outcomes. Taken together, these findings indicate that combined intravenous and topical magnesium sulfate administration produced statistically detectable reductions in some early postoperative pain scores but did not provide a clear clinically meaningful advantage over either route alone.

No significant differences were observed between the Intravenous and Topical groups for any of the measured outcomes. Although topical administration involved a lower total dose of magnesium sulfate, the present study was not designed to compare administration convenience, drug exposure, cost-effectiveness, or route-specific adverse effects. Therefore, our findings do not establish the superiority of topical administration over intravenous administration. The selection of the administration route should be individualized according to the perioperative analgesic protocol, patient characteristics, and clinical workflow. Further adequately powered comparative studies are required to determine whether either route offers clinically relevant advantages.

Beyond TKA, previous studies have shown that adding magnesium sulfate to local infiltration analgesic cocktails may prolong analgesia and improve pain control in other orthopedic procedures, including unicompartmental knee arthroplasty and total hip arthroplasty.31–33 However, these findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence that topical administration is superior to intravenous administration after TKA.

Although the present study provides preliminary comparative evidence, the optimal perioperative protocol for magnesium sulfate remains undefined. In previous studies of postoperative analgesia after TKA, magnesium sulfate has been administered not only intravenously and through local infiltration but also as an adjunct to peripheral nerve blocks, including the adductor canal block,12,34,35 and through epidural catheter infusion,36 with favorable analgesic outcomes reported in some studies. In the present trial, the combined regimen involved a higher total dose of magnesium sulfate than either single-route regimen. Because of concerns regarding possible dose-related adverse effects, magnesium sulfate was not added to the adductor canal block. Whether its addition to this or other peripheral nerve blocks could provide further analgesic benefit requires investigation.

Considerable variability also exists in the dosage and administration frequency of magnesium sulfate across previous studies.15–18,23,26,27 Because different doses, routes, and administration schedules may influence analgesic effectiveness, future studies should systematically compare different perioperative regimens. In this study, the incidence of postoperative complications, including potentially drug-related adverse events, did not differ significantly among the three groups. However, because the study was not specifically powered to evaluate uncommon adverse events, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of comparative safety. Future studies with larger sample sizes should further evaluate the dose-response relationship, optimal administration strategy, and safety profile of perioperative magnesium sulfate.

This study has several limitations. First, the follow-up period was limited to hospitalization; therefore, the long-term analgesic effects, functional outcomes, and safety of the different magnesium sulfate regimens could not be evaluated. Second, the sample size was estimated using pilot data and was based on the resting VAS pain score at 24 hours postoperatively, a commonly used outcome in studies of analgesia after joint arthroplasty.37 Although the sample size was adequate for the primary outcome, the study may have been underpowered to detect differences in secondary outcomes or uncommon adverse events. Third, this was a single-center study conducted within a specific perioperative multimodal analgesia protocol, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, although the patients, surgeon, outcome assessor, and statistician were blinded, the investigator responsible for allocation disclosure and the anesthesiologist who prepared the medications were aware of group assignment. Although these personnel were not involved in outcome assessment or statistical analysis, residual performance bias cannot be completely excluded. Future multicenter studies with longer follow-up, larger sample sizes, and strengthened allocation and blinding procedures are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Compared with intravenous or topical magnesium sulfate alone, combined intravenous and topical administration did not demonstrate a clinically significant advantage in postoperative pain management, stress response, or functional recovery. Further well-designed studies are warranted to determine the optimal route, dosage, and administration strategy of magnesium sulfate for perioperative pain management after TKA.
