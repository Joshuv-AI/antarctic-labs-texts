Comparing quantum channels using Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps: A physically meaningful approach
Arindam Mitra, Jatin Ghai

Abstract
In quantum technologies, quantum channels are essential elements for the transmission of quantum states. The action of a quantum channel usually introduces noise in the quantum state and thereby reduces the information contained in it. Concatenating a quantum channel with another quantum channel makes it more noisy and degrades its information and resource preservability. These are mathematically described by completely positive trace-preserving linear maps that represent the generic evolution of quantum systems. These are special cases of Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps. In this work, we demonstrate a physically meaningful way to compare a pair of quantum channels using Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps. More precisely, given a pair of quantum channels and an arbitrary unknown input state, we show that if the output state of one quantum channel from the pair can be obtained from the output statistics of the other channel from the pair using some quantum measurement, then the latter channel from the pair can be obtained from the former channel by concatenating it with a Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map. This relation between these two channels is a preorder, and we try to study its characterization in this work. We also illustrate the implications of our results for the incompatibility of quantum devices through an example.



I
Introduction

In quantum theory, quantum channels, i.e., completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) linear maps, are fundamental objects which provides the mathematical framework for describing all physically realizable transformations of quantum systems
Wilde 2013
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. They encompass how quantum systems evolve while interacting with environment
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Lidar 2020
, making them a cornerstone of quantum theory for quantum communication, information, processing and computation. The structure of quantum channels provides us with tools for the characterization of noise in relevant experimental scenarios, thus bridging them with the abstract quantum mechanics. When a quantum channel is post-processed with another channel, the resultant channel is noisier. Thus, its ability to communicate information, both classical and quantum, is degraded. This is reflected by a decrease in its classical and quantum capacity
Wilde 2013
.

On the other hand, compared to quantum channels, Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps have been less explored from a physical perspective. A basic requirement for a transformation in quantum theory is that it should map real-valued observables to real-valued observables. Thus, for any linear map describing these kinds of transformations, Hermitian-preservability is the weakest natural demand. Among Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving maps, CPTP linear maps represent the generic evolution of quantum systems. However, the physical implementability of Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps has also been a subject of research
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Parzygnat et al. 2024
;
Rossini et al. 2023
. Recently an algorithm has also been developed for the implementation of these general Hermitian preserving maps through exponentiation
Wei et al. 2024
. Besides, these positive maps are also useful for constructing witnesses
Peres 1996
;
Chruściński and Sarbicki 2014
;
Mallick et al. 2025
;
Huber and Sengupta 2014
;
Rodríguez-Rosario et al. 2008
;
Li et al. 2024
for entanglement detection. Thus, there is quite a bit of interest in exploring the transformations beyond the complete positivity as these conditions are deemed a bit restrictive, especially in quantum open system scenarios
Carteret et al. 2008
;
Dominy and Lidar 2016
;
Pastuszak et al. 2025
;
Rossini et al. 2023
;
Usha Devi et al. 2011
. From this context, the existence of quantum maps between CPTP linear maps and Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps has also been studied
Cao et al. 2023
. Motivated by this, in this work, we try to give a physical interpretation of Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps that may or may not be positive. We try to provide a physically meaningful way to compare two quantum channels using Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps. Given a pair of quantum channels and an arbitrary unknown input state, we show that if the output state of one quantum channel from the pair can be obtained from the output statistics of the other channel from the pair using some quantum measurement, then the latter channel from the pair can be obtained from the former channel by concatenating it with a Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map. Through an example, we illustrate that in such a scenario, the latter channel can’t always be obtained from the former through post-processing it with a quantum channel. Both of these two comparative relations are distinct and follow a hierarchy. We also discuss the implications of our results on the incompatibility of quantum devices.

This paper is arranged as follows. In Sec.
II
we discuss the preliminaries.
In Sec.
III
we present the main results of this work. More specifically, in Sec.
III.A
, we provide a physically meaningful full way to compare two quantum channels using Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps. In Sec.
III.B
we provide the hierarchy between the set of channels obtained from a given channel by post-processing it with Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps and the set of channels obtained by post-processing the same given channel with other quantum channels. The set of channels obtained by post-processing the same given channel with positive maps lies between these two sets. In Sec.
IV
, we summarize our results and discuss future directions.

II
Preliminaries

II.1
Quantum Measurements, Channels and Instruments

A quantum measurement constitutes a set of positive semi-definite matrices
M=\{M(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\}_{x\in\Omega_{m}}
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying the condition
\sum_{x\in\Omega_{M}}M(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
where
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
is the identity matrix on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. Here,
\Omega_{M}
is the set of outcomes for the measurement
M
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of all linear operators on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. In this work, we assume the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
to be finite. Each
M(x)
is called a POVM element of the measurement
M
. If we have
M^{2}(x)=M(x)\penalty\ \forall x\in\Omega_{M}
, then
M
is said to be a projective measurement. In this work, we will only consider measurements that have a finite number of outcomes. If measurement
M
is performed on a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of density matrices on
\mathcal{H}
, then the probability of obtaining the outcome
x
is given by
\text{Tr}[\rho M(x)]
. The set of all the measurements acting on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is denoted as
\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
.

A quantum channel
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is mathematically represented by a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. Physically, it transforms an arbitrary density matrix to another arbitrary density matrix. This description is known as the Schrodinger picture. For any linear map
\Phi:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
, the dual map
\Phi^{\dagger}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is defined through the equation

\text{Tr}[B^{\dagger}\Phi^{\dagger}(A)]=\text{Tr}[(\Phi(B))^{\dagger}A],

(1)

where
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
and
B\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
Watrous 2018
. The dual map of a quantum channel represents the action of that channel in the Heisenberg picture. We denote the set of all quantum channels with the input Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and the output Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
as
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. The composition of two arbitrary linear maps
\Lambda_{1}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
\Lambda_{2}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
is denoted as
\Lambda_{1}\circ\Lambda_{2}
where for an arbitrary
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, we have
\Lambda_{1}\circ\Lambda_{2}(\rho):=\Lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{2}(\rho))
. Clearly,
\Lambda_{1}\circ\Lambda_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. For two arbitrary quantum channels
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
and
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
if
\Lambda=\Theta\circ\Lambda^{\prime}
where
\Theta\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},\mathcal{K})
then we denote it as
\Lambda^{\prime}\succeq_{postproc}\Lambda
. Here, the relation
\succeq_{postproc}
is a preorder which is also known as
post-processing preorder

Heinosaari and Miyadera 2017a
. The action of a quantum channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
on a measurement
M=\{M(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\}
in the Heisenberg picture is denoted as
\Lambda^{\dagger}(M):=\{\Lambda^{\dagger}(M(x))\}
.

A linear map
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is said to be Hermitian-preserving if for arbitrary
X\in Herm(\mathcal{H})
we have
\Lambda(X)\in Herm(\mathcal{K})

. Here,
Herm(\mathcal{H})
is a set of Hermitian matrices in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Let us denote the set of all
Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps
with the input Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and the output Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
as
\mathscr{C}_{HP}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. Similar to the post-processing preorder, if a quantum channel
\Lambda_{1}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
is related to another quantum channel
\Lambda_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
through the relation
\Lambda_{2}=\Theta\circ\Lambda_{1}
, where
\Theta\in\mathscr{C}_{HP}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2})
, it is denoted as
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{HP}\Lambda_{2}
. It can be easily seen that the relation
\succeq_{HP}
is also a preorder.

A linear map is
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is called positive if for any
X\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
we have
\Lambda(X)\in\mathcal{L}_{+}(\mathcal{K})
, where
\mathcal{L}_{+}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of positive-semidefinite matrices in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
Watrous 2018
.
\mathscr{C}_{P}(\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathcal{H}})
denotes the set of all
positive trace-preserving linear maps
with the input Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and the output Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
. Similarly as above, if a quantum channel
\Lambda_{1}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
is related to another quantum channel
\Lambda_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
through the relation
\Lambda_{2}=\Theta\circ\Lambda_{1}
, where
\Theta\in\mathscr{C}_{P}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2})
, it is denoted as
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{P}\Lambda_{2}
. It can also be easily seen that the relation
\succeq_{P}
is also a preorder. It is worth mentioning that
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})\subset\mathscr{C}_{P}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})\subset\mathscr{C}_{HP}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
Watrous 2018
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

Both quantum measurements and quantum channels can be simultaneously generalized to the notion of quantum instruments. Mathematically, a quantum instrument
\mathbf{I}
is defined as a set of CP trace non-increasing maps, i.e.,
\mathbf{I}=\{\Phi_{x}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\}_{x\in\Omega_{\mathbf{I}}}
such that
\Phi=\sum_{x\in\Omega_{\mathbf{I}}}\Phi_{x}
is a quantum channel
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. For the instrument
\mathbf{I}
acting on a quantum state
\rho
, the classical output of the instrument is denoted by
x
, while its quantum output is given by
\frac{\Phi_{x}(\rho)}{\text{Tr}[\Phi_{x}(\rho)]}
. Both of these occur with probability
\text{Tr}[\Phi_{x}(\rho)]
. The set of all such quantum instruments with the input Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and the output Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
is denoted as
\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. A quantum instrument induces a unique measurement
M=\{M(x)\}
such that
\text{Tr}[\Phi_{x}(\rho)]=\text{Tr}[\rho M(x)]
for all
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
x\in\Omega_{\mathbf{I}}
. Moreover
M(x)=\Phi_{x}^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}})
through duality. For a given measurement, there exist many different instruments that implement it.

In the most basic sense of the words, the compatibility of a set of quantum devices means their ability to be performed simultaneously. In other words, there exists a joint device that reproduces the outcomes of all devices in that set. A set of devices are said to be compatible if such a joint device exists for them. Otherwise, the set is incompatible. In the context of quantum measurements and channels, the following three notions of compatibility have already been defined
Heinosaari et al. 2016
:

•

Measurement compatibility:
Two arbitrary measurements
M\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
and
N\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
are said to be compatible if there exists a joint measurement
G=\{G(x,y)\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})\}
with outcome set
\Omega_{M}\times\Omega_{N}
such that
Heinosaari et al. 2016

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\Omega_{N}}G(x,y)\qquad\forall x\in\Omega_{M},

(2)

\displaystyle N(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Omega_{M}}G(x,y)\qquad\forall y\in\Omega_{N}.

(3)

By performing the measurement
G
, the outcomes of both
M
and
N
can be obtained simultaneously. This notion is naturally generalized to an arbitrary number of measurements.

•

Channel compatibility:
Two channels
\Lambda_{1}:\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
and
\Lambda_{2}:\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
are said to be compatible if there exists a joint channel
\Lambda:\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that
Heinosaari and Miyadera 2017a
;
Heinosaari et al. 2016

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\circ\Lambda,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\circ\Lambda.

(4)

This definition of compatibility can also be naturally extended to an arbitrary set of channels.

•

Measurement-Channel compatibility:
A measurement
M=\{M(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
and a channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
are said to be compatible if there exists a quantum instrument
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011

\mathbf{I}=\{\Phi_{x}\}\in\mathscr{I}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
such that for any
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
we have
\text{Tr}[\Phi(\rho)]=\text{Tr}[M(x)\rho]\penalty\ \forall x
and
\sum_{x}\Phi_{x}=\Lambda
. Thus, by implementing the instrument
\mathbf{I}
, the measurement
M
and the channel
\Lambda
can be implemented simultaneously.

It is known that for a given quantum channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
there exists its Stinespring dilation
Stinespring 1955
;
Watrous 2018
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2010

(\mathcal{V},\overline{\mathcal{K}})
, where
\mathcal{V}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}\otimes\overline{\mathcal{K}}
is an isometry, such that

\displaystyle\Lambda=\text{Tr}_{\overline{K}}[\mathcal{V}\rho\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}],

(5)

for every
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Through this dilation we can define the
conjugate channel

\overline{\Lambda}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathcal{K}})
as

\displaystyle\overline{\Lambda}=\text{Tr}_{K}[\mathcal{V}\rho\mathcal{V}^{\dagger}].

(6)

Depending on the isometry, there exist multiple conjugate channels for a given quantum channel, and they are post-processing equivalent

;

Consider two quantum channels

II.2
Informationally complete measurements

An important class of measurements is the informationally complete measurements
D’Ariano et al. 2004
. It is a special kind of measurement whose output statistics uniquely determines the state of the quantum system. If
d
is the dimension of the system of Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then at least
d^{2}
POVM elements are required to form an informationally complete measurement. Any linear operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
can be expanded in terms of informationally complete measurement
M=\{M(x)\}_{x=1}^{N}
as

A=\sum_{i}^{N}c_{x}M(x)

(7)

where
N\geq d^{2}
. In other words, the POVM elements
\{M(x)\}
of an informationally complete measurement span
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. It is worth mentioning that even if the operator
A
is positive semi-definite, some of the expansion coefficients
\{c_{x}\}
in Eq. (
7
) can be negative. The POVM elements of any informationally complete measurement form an overcomplete spanning set in general. Thus, the expansion in the above Eq. (
7
) is not unique in general. An informationally complete measurement is said to be minimal if
N=d^{2}
,
i.e.
there are exactly
d^{2}
linearly independent POVM elements spanning the operator space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Thus, the expansion in Eq. (
7
) is unique for the POVM elements of minimal informationally complete measurement.

State reconstruction from informationally complete measurement

Any density matrix
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
can be expanded in terms of a minimal informationally complete measurement

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{y}^{d^{2}}c_{y}M(y).

(8)

Further, we can write

\displaystyle p(x):=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\rho M(x)]=\sum_{y}^{d^{2}}c_{y}\text{Tr}[M(x)M(y)]

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y}R(x,y)c_{y},

(10)

where

. We can rewrite the above expression in form of a matrix equation as

\displaystyle P=R.C,

where

\displaystyle R.\mathbf{v}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{y}R(x,y)v_{y}=0\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ x

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[M(x)\sum_{y}v_{y}M(y)]=0\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ x.

As we know that the set

\displaystyle\sum_{y}v_{y}M(y)=

\displaystyle 0.

Since the informationally complete measurement

Thus obtaining the probability distribution given in Eq. (

III.1
Informationally complete measurement, asymptotic power and Channel postprocessing

Definition 1
.

A channel
\Lambda_{1}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})

\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{2}(\rho)M_{2}(x)]=\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{1}(\rho)M_{1}(x)]\penalty\ \forall x\in\Omega_{M_{2}}.

(16)

\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}

\overline{M}_{2}(i)=M_{2}(i)\penalty\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\}\setminus j

\overline{M}(j)=M_{2}(j)+X
. Clearly,
\overline{M}_{2}(i)\geq 0\penalty\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\}
and
\sum^{d^{2}}_{i=1}M_{2}(i)=\sum_{i}M_{2}(i)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}
. Hence,
\overline{M}_{2}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
is a valid quantum measurement with
|\Omega_{\overline{M}_{2}}|=d^{2}
. Now, note that the set
\{M_{2}(1),\ldots M_{2}(d^{2})\}
is linearly independent. Consider
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d^{2}}\}
be an arbitrary set of complex numbers i.e.,
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbbm{C}\penalty\ \forall i
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\overline{M}_{2}(i)=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i,i\neq j}\alpha_{i}\overline{M}_{2}(i)+\alpha_{j}\overline{M}_{2}(j)=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i,i\neq j}\alpha_{i}M_{2}(i)+\alpha_{j}[M_{2}(j)+X]=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i,i\neq j}\alpha_{i}M_{2}(i)+\alpha_{j}[M_{2}(j)+\sum^{d^{2}}_{i=1}c_{i}M_{2}(i)]=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}c_{i})M_{2}(i)=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}c_{i})=0\penalty\ \forall i,

(19)

Let us define
\beta_{i}:=\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}c_{i}
. Note that
c_{j}>0
and therefore,
\beta_{j}=\alpha_{j}(1+c_{j})=0\Rightarrow\alpha_{j}=0
. As
\alpha_{j}=0
,
\beta_{i}=0\Rightarrow\alpha_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\}\setminus j
. Hence,
\alpha_{i}=0\forall i
. Therefore, the set
\{\overline{M}_{2}(1),\ldots\overline{M}_{2}(d^{2})\}
is linearly independent. Clearly, for an arbitrary matrix
Y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
and
Y=\sum^{d^{2}}_{i=1}c^{\prime}M_{2}(i)
, one can write
Y=\sum^{d^{2}}_{i=1}c^{\prime\prime}\overline{M}_{2}(i)
where
c^{\prime\prime}_{i}=c^{\prime}_{i}-\frac{c_{i}c^{\prime}_{j}}{1+c_{j}}\penalty\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\}\setminus j
and
c^{\prime\prime}_{j}=\frac{c^{\prime}_{j}}{1+c_{j}}
, i.e., the set
\{\overline{M}_{2}(1),\ldots,\overline{M}_{2}(d^{2})\}
is able to span
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
. Hence,
\overline{M}_{2}
is a minimal informationally complete measurement. Consider another set of matrices
\overline{M}_{1}=\{\overline{M}_{1}(1),\ldots,\overline{M}_{1}(d^{2})\}
such that
\overline{M}_{1}(i)=M_{1}(i)\penalty\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\}\setminus j
and
\overline{M}_{1}(j)=M_{1}(j)+\sum^{d^{2}+n}_{d^{2}+1}M_{1}(j)
. Clearly,
\overline{M}_{1}(i)\geq 0\penalty\ \forall i
and
\sum^{d^{2}}_{i=1}\overline{M}_{1}(i)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
. Therefore,
\overline{M_{1}}=\{\overline{M}_{1}(1),\ldots,\overline{M}_{1}(d^{2})\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
is a valid measurement. Then from linearity of
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
alongwith Eq.
16
, for all
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
we have

\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{2}(\rho)\overline{M}_{2}(i)]=\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{1}(\rho)\overline{M}_{1}(i)]\penalty\ \forall i\in\Omega_{\overline{M}_{2}}.

(20)

Conversely, if
\overline{M}_{2}
is minimal informationally complete in Eq.
18
then by definition it is informationally complete and therefore, from Definition
1
, we have
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
.
Hence, the proposition is proved.
∎

Next, we provide an equivalent characterization of the relation
\succeq_{asymp}
using Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

A channel
\Lambda_{1}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
is at least as powerful as
\Lambda_{2}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
in the asymptotic sense (i.e.,
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
) if and only if their exists a Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map
\Theta:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that
\Lambda_{2}=\Theta\circ\Lambda_{1}
.

Proof.

From Proposition
18
, we have a minimal informationally complete measurement
\overline{M}_{2}
and an arbitrary measurement
\overline{M}_{1}
such that Eq. (
18
) holds. In Heisenberg picture we have (similar to Eq. (
17
)), we have

\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(\overline{M}_{2}(x))=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(\overline{M}_{1}(x))\penalty\ \forall x\in\Omega_{\overline{M}_{2}}.

(21)

Note that the set
\{\overline{M}_{2}(x)\}^{d^{2}}_{x=1}
forms a basis of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
and consider a linear map
\Theta:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that

\displaystyle\Theta^{\dagger}[\overline{M}_{2}(x)]:=\overline{M}_{1}(x)\penalty\ \forall x\in\Omega_{\overline{M}_{2}}.

(22)

Therefore, for an arbitrary matrix
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that
X:=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\overline{M}_{2}(x)
,

\Theta^{\dagger}(X)=\Theta^{\dagger}(\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\overline{M}_{2}(x))=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\Theta^{\dagger}(\overline{M}_{2}(x))=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\overline{M}_{1}(x).

(23)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(X)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\overline{M}_{2}(x))=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(\overline{M}_{2}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(\overline{M}_{1}(x))=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\overline{M}_{1}(x))

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(\Theta^{\dagger}(X)).

(24)

Hence,
\Lambda^{\dagger}_{2}=\Lambda^{\dagger}_{1}\circ\Theta^{\dagger}
or equivalently
\Lambda_{2}=\Theta\circ\Lambda_{1}
. Now, we have to show that
\Theta^{\dagger}
is Hermitian-preserving unital map. Note that

\displaystyle\Theta^{\dagger}[X^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\Theta^{\dagger}[\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c^{*}_{x}\overline{M}^{\dagger}_{2}(x)]=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c^{*}_{x}\Theta^{\dagger}(\overline{M}^{\dagger}_{2}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c^{*}_{x}\Theta^{\dagger}(\overline{M}_{2}(x))=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c^{*}_{x}\overline{M}_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c^{*}_{x}\overline{M}^{\dagger}_{1}(x)=[\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}c_{x}\overline{M}_{1}(x)]^{\dagger}=[\Theta^{\dagger}(X)]^{\dagger}.

(25)

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\Theta^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}})=\Theta^{\dagger}(\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}\overline{M}_{2}(x))=\sum^{d^{2}}_{x=1}\overline{M}_{1}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}.

(26)

Hence,
\Theta^{\dagger}
is the Hermitian-preserving unital linear map and therefore,
\Theta
is the Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map.

Conversely, suppose
\Lambda_{2}=\Theta\circ\Lambda_{1}
where
\Theta
is a Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map. Then
\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}\circ\Theta^{\dagger}
. Therefore, for every
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
, there exists a
Y=\Theta^{\dagger}(X)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
such that
\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(X)=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(Y)
holds. Now, consider an arbitrary minimal informationally complete measurement
M^{\prime}_{2}=\{M^{\prime}_{2}(x)\}_{x=1}^{d^{2}}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
where
d=\dim(\mathcal{K}_{2})
. Then there exists a set of matrices
M^{\prime}_{1}=\{M^{\prime}_{1}(x)=\Theta^{\dagger}(M^{\prime}_{2}(x))\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
such that

\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(M^{\prime}_{2}(x))=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(M^{\prime}_{1}(x))\penalty\ \forall x,

(27)

holds. We can see that
M^{\prime}_{1}(x)=(M^{\prime}_{1}(x))^{\dagger}\penalty\ \forall x
as
\Theta^{\dagger}
is a Hermitian-preserving and
M^{\prime}_{1}(x)=\Theta^{\dagger}(M^{\prime}_{2}(x))\penalty\ \forall x
. Also,
\sum_{x}M^{\prime}_{1}(x)=\sum_{x}\Theta^{\dagger}(M^{\prime}_{2}(x))=\Theta^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}})=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
as
\Theta^{\dagger}
is unital. But all
M^{\prime}_{1}(x)
s may not be positive semi-definite. Now, for an arbitrary Hermitian matrix
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
, let
r_{A}:=\min\{\lambda\in\mathbbm{R}:\lambda\geq 0,0\leq A+\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\}
. Let
r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}:=\max_{x}r_{M^{\prime}_{1}(x)}
. Clearly, we have
0\leq r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}<\infty
. Now, consider the set of matrices
M_{1}=\{M_{1}(x)=\frac{M^{\prime}_{1}(x)+r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}}{1+d^{2}r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}}\}
. Clearly,
M_{1}(x)\geq 0\penalty\ \forall x
and
\sum_{x}M_{1}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
. Hence,
M_{1}
is a valid measurement. Consider another set of matrices
M_{2}=\{M_{2}(x)=\frac{M^{\prime}_{2}(x)+r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}}{1+d^{2}r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}}\}
. Clearly,
M_{2}(x)\geq 0\penalty\ \forall x
and
\sum_{x}M_{2}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}
. Hence,
M_{2}
is also a valid measurement. Then from the unitality and linearity of both
\Lambda^{\dagger}_{1}
and
\Lambda^{\dagger}_{2}
along with Eq. (
27
) we have

\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(M_{2}(x))=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}(M_{1}(x))\penalty\ \forall x.

(28)

Lastly, we have to prove that
M_{2}
in an informationally complete measurement. It is sufficient to show that the set of matrices
\{M_{2}(x)\}
is linearly independent. Note that as
M^{\prime}_{2}
is a minimal informationally complete measurement the set
\{M^{\prime}_{2}(x)\}
is linearly independent. For notational simplicity let
t:=\frac{1}{1+d^{2}r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}}
and therefore,
\frac{r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}}{1+d^{2}r_{M^{\prime}_{1}}}=\frac{(1-t)}{d^{2}}
. Clearly,
0<t\leq 1
. Consider a set of complex number
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d^{2}}\}
such that
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}M_{2}(x)=0
holds and let
\alpha:=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}M_{2}(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\Big(tM^{\prime}_{2}(x)+\frac{(1-t)}{d^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}\Big)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}(tM^{\prime}_{2}(x))+\frac{(1-t)\alpha}{d^{2}}\sum_{x}M^{\prime}_{2}(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Big(\alpha_{x}t+\frac{(1-t)\alpha}{d^{2}}\Big)M^{\prime}_{2}(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha_{x}t+\frac{(1-t)\alpha}{d^{2}}=0\penalty\ \forall x

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha_{x}=-\frac{(1-t)\alpha}{td^{2}}=:\beta\penalty\ \forall x,

(29)

where in the fifth line, we have used the fact that the set
\{M^{\prime}_{2}(x)\}
is linearly independent. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}M_{2}(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\beta\sum_{x}M_{2}(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\beta\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\beta=0.

(30)

Hence,
\alpha_{1}=\ldots=\alpha_{d^{2}}=\beta=0
. Hence,
M_{2}
is a minimal informationally complete measurement. Therefore, from Eq. (
27
), Eq. (
17
), we have
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
. Hence, the theorem is proved.

Remark 2
.

From Theorem
1
, we can conclude that
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}\Leftrightarrow\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{HP}\Lambda_{2}
. Hence, the relation
\succeq_{asymp}
is a preorder. Furthermore, we would like to mention that a very special case of Theorem
1
, where
\Lambda_{2}
is an identity channel, has been discussed in Sec. 2.2
of Ref.
Jiang et al. 2021
. Additionally, our approach is different from the approach in Ref.
Jiang et al. 2021
as informational complete measurements have not been discussed in that work.

.
Next, we give an example of such channels.

Example 1
.

Consider the identity channel
\Lambda_{1}=\mathbbm{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
and a completely dephasing quantum channel
\Lambda_{2}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
such that for an arbitrary quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
,
\Lambda_{1}(\rho)=\sum_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}\rho\ket{i}\bra{i}
where
\{\ket{i}\}
forms a orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Note that as
\Lambda_{2}=\Lambda_{2}\circ\Lambda_{1}
, we have
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{postproc}\Lambda_{2}
and therefore, we have
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
. Now, we show that
\Lambda_{2}\not\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{1}
. Consider an arbitrary measurement
M=\{M(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
. Then we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}^{\dagger}(M(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}M(x)\ket{i}\bra{i}\penalty\ \forall x

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}[\sum_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}M(x)\ket{i}\bra{i}]\penalty\ \forall x

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{1}^{\dagger}[M^{\prime}(x)]\penalty\ \forall x,

(31)

where
M^{\prime}=\{M^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}M(x)\ket{i}\bra{i}\}
is a valid measurment. Note that all POVM elements are diagonal in
\{\ket{i}\}
basis and therefore, it cannot be an informationally complete measurement (as the matrices
\ket{i}\bra{j}
for
i\neq j
can not be written as a linear combination of only diagonal matrices). Hence, in this case, there is no informationally complete measurement such that Eq. (
1
) is satisfied. Therefore,
\Lambda_{2}\not\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{1}
.

Now, an important question is whether
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{postproc}\Lambda_{2}
holds given that
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
holds. We show the negative result through an example. But before that we prove the following useful Lemma.

Lemma 1
.

If the measurement
M=\{M(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{1})^{d^{2}}_{x=1}
is a minimal informationally complete measurement then the measurement
M^{\prime}=\{M^{\prime}(x)=tM(x)+(1-t)\frac{\text{Tr}[M(x)]}{d}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\}^{d^{2}}_{x=1}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
is also informationally complete where
0<t\leq 1
and
d=\dim(\mathcal{K}_{1})
.

Proof.

Clearly, it suffices to show that the set
\{M^{\prime}(x)\}^{d^{2}}_{x=1}
is linearly independent. Consider a set of complex numbers
\{\alpha_{x}\}^{d^{2}}_{x=1}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}M^{\prime}(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\Big(tM(x)+(1-t)\frac{\text{Tr}[M(x)]}{d}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\Big)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}tM(x)+(1-t)\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\frac{\text{Tr}[M(y)]}{d}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}tM(x)+(1-t)\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\lambda_{y}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=0,

(32)

where
\lambda_{y}=\frac{\text{Tr}[M(y)]}{d}\forall\penalty\ y
. Therefore, from Eq.
32
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}tM(x)+\sum_{x}(1-t)(\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\lambda_{y})M(x)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{x}(\alpha_{x}t+(1-t)(\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\lambda_{y}))M(x)=0.

(33)

As the set
\{M(x)\}
is linearly independent, from Eq. (
33
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{x}t+(1-t)(\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\lambda_{y})=0\penalty\ \forall x

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha_{x}=-\frac{(1-t)}{t}(\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\lambda_{y})\penalty\ \forall x.

(34)

Hence, we have
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\ldots=\alpha_{d^{2}}=-\frac{(1-t)}{t}(\sum_{y}\alpha_{y}\lambda_{y})=:\alpha
(say).
Then we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}M^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle\alpha\sum_{x}M^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{x}=0\quad\forall x.

(35)

Hence, the set
\{M^{\prime}(x)\}^{d^{2}}_{x=1}
is linearly independent.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Given two quantum channels
\Lambda_{1}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{1})
and
\Lambda_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}_{2})
, the relation
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
is satisfied if and only if
ker(\Lambda_{1})\subseteq ker(\Lambda_{2})
.

Proof.

If
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
, then equivalently, we have

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{2}(\rho)M_{2}(x)]=\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{1}(\rho)M_{1}(x)],

(39)

for every
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Here,
M_{2}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
is a minimal informationally complete measurement and
M_{1}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
is another measurement.
Let us first assume
\rho\in ker(\Lambda_{1})

i.e

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\rho)=0.

(40)

This implies that

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{1}(\rho)M_{1}(x)]=\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{2}(\rho)M_{2}(x)]=0\quad\forall\penalty\ x,

(41)

As
M_{2}
is informationally complete

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{2}(\rho)M_{2}(x)]=0\penalty\ \forall x\Rightarrow\penalty\ \Lambda_{2}(\rho)=0.

(42)

Hence,

\displaystyle\rho\in ker(\Lambda_{1})\Rightarrow\rho\in ker(\Lambda_{2}).

(43)

Thus, we can conclude that

\displaystyle ker(\Lambda_{1})\subseteq ker(\Lambda_{2}).

(44)

Conversely, let
ker(\Lambda_{1})\subseteq ker(\Lambda_{2})
. Then, we can define a map
\Theta_{1}:Im(\Lambda_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\rho)):=\Lambda_{2}(\rho)\quad\forall\penalty\ \rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}).

(45)

Here, the symbol
Im
refers to the image of a linear map. Consider
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\Lambda_{1}(\rho)=\Lambda_{1}(\sigma)
. This implies

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\rho-\sigma)=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\rho-\sigma\in ker(\Lambda_{1}).

(46)

As
ker(\Lambda_{1})\subseteq ker(\Lambda_{2})
we have

\displaystyle\rho-\sigma\in ker(\Lambda_{2})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\rho)=\Lambda_{2}(\sigma).

(47)

Thus
\Lambda_{1}(\rho)=\Lambda_{1}(\sigma)\Rightarrow\Theta(\Lambda_{1}(\rho))=\Theta(\Lambda_{1}(\sigma))
. Hence, the map
\Theta_{1}
is well-defined.

For
\alpha\Lambda_{1}(\rho)+\beta\Lambda_{1}(\sigma)\in Im(\Lambda_{1})
where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbbm{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}(\alpha\Lambda_{1}(\rho)+\beta\Lambda_{1}(\sigma))=

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\alpha\rho+\beta\sigma))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\alpha\rho+\beta\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\Lambda_{2}(\rho)+\beta\Lambda_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\rho))+\beta\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\sigma)).

(48)

Thus,
\Theta_{1}
is a linear map.

Next, as we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}((\Lambda_{1}(\rho))^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\rho^{\dagger}))

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{2}(\rho^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=(\Lambda_{2}(\rho))^{\dagger}=(\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\rho)))^{\dagger}.

(49)

The linear map
\Theta_{1}
is Hermitian-preserving.

Also

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Theta(\Lambda_{1}(\rho))]=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{2}(\rho)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\rho]=\text{Tr}[\Lambda_{1}(\rho)].

(50)

Thus, the map
\Theta_{1}
is trace-preserving also.

Next, define another map
\Theta_{2}:Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
such that for all
Y\in Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}(Y):=Tr[Y]\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}}{dim(\mathcal{K}_{2})}.

(51)

We can see that it is linear, as for any
\alpha Y_{1}+\beta Y_{2}\in Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}
we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}(\alpha Y_{1}+\beta Y_{2})=\alpha\Theta_{2}(Y_{1})+\beta\Theta_{2}(Y_{2}).

(52)

Also as

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}(Y^{\dagger})=

\displaystyle(\text{Tr}[Y])^{*}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{2}}}{dim(\mathcal{K}_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Theta_{2}(Y))^{\dagger}.

(53)

The linear map
\Theta_{2}
is Hermitian-preserving.

It is also trace-preserving as

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Theta_{2}(Y)]=\text{Tr}[Y].

(54)

Now, as we know

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})=Im(\Lambda_{1})\oplus Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp},

(55)

an arbitrary
Z\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
can be written in a unique decomposition as

\displaystyle Z=X\oplus 0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}}+0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})}\oplus Y,

(56)

where
X\in Im(\Lambda_{1})
and
Y\in Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}
. Here,
0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}}
is the null matrix in the vector space
Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}
and
0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})}
is the null matrix in the vector space
Im(\Lambda_{1})
. Let us define another map
\Theta:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{2})
as

\displaystyle\Theta(Z):=\Theta_{1}(X)+\Theta_{2}(Y).

(57)

For
\alpha Z_{1}+\beta Z_{2}\in Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}
, where
Z_{1}=X_{1}\oplus 0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}}+0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})}\oplus Y_{1}
and
Z_{2}=X_{2}\oplus 0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}}+0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})}\oplus Y_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(\alpha Z_{1}+\beta Z_{2})=

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}(\alpha X_{1}+\beta X_{2})+\Theta_{2}(\alpha Y_{1}+\beta Y_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(\Theta_{1}(X_{1})+\Theta_{2}(Y_{1}))+\beta(\Theta_{1}(X_{2})+\Theta_{2}(Y_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\Theta(Z_{1})+\beta\Theta(Z_{2}).

(58)

Thus
\Theta
is a linear map.

Clearly, for an arbitrary
Z\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
(as in Eq. (
56
)) we have

\displaystyle\Theta(Z^{\dagger})=

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}(X^{\dagger})+\Theta_{2}(Y^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Theta_{1}(X))^{\dagger}+(\Theta_{2}(Y)^{\dagger}=\Theta(Z)^{\dagger}

(59)

and

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Theta(Z)]=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\Theta_{1}(X)]+\text{Tr}[\Theta_{2}(Y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[X]+\text{Tr}[Y]=\text{Tr}[Z].

(60)

Hence,
\Theta
is a Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map.
Now, as
\Lambda_{1}(\rho)\in Im(\Lambda_{1})
for an arbitrary
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(\Lambda_{1}(\rho))=\Theta_{1}(\Lambda_{1}(\rho))+\Theta_{2}(0_{Im(\Lambda_{1})^{\perp}})=\Lambda_{2}(\rho).

(61)

Hence, we have
\Lambda_{2}=\Theta\circ\Lambda_{1}
which in turn, by Theorem
1
implies
\Lambda_{1}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda_{2}
.
∎

.

From Definition
1
, Proposition
18
, Theorem
1
, and Theorem
2
, we obtain four equivalent definitions of the relation
\succeq_{asymp}
.

III.2
Hierarchy of the preorders

Consider a quantum channel
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
. Let us denote the set of all quantum channels obtained by concatenating it with Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps as
\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
. In other words

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda):=\{\Phi:\Phi\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathcal{H}}),\Lambda\succeq_{HP}\Phi\}.

(62)

It is worth mentioning that the set
\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
is a convex set. This can easily be seen by choosing two quantum channels
\Phi_{1}\in\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
and
\Phi_{2}\in\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
such that
\Phi_{1}=\Theta_{1}\circ\Lambda
and
\Phi_{2}=\Theta_{2}\circ\Lambda
. Here,
\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2}\in\mathscr{C}_{HP}(\mathcal{K},\overline{\mathcal{H}})
. By taking the convex combination of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
we can easily see that

\displaystyle\lambda\Phi_{1}+(1-\lambda)\Phi_{2}=

\displaystyle\lambda\Theta_{1}\circ\Lambda+(1-\lambda)\Theta_{2}\circ\Lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda\Theta_{1}+(1-\lambda)\Theta_{2})\circ\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda),

(63)

for
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
as
\lambda\Theta_{1}+(1-\lambda)\Theta_{2}\in\mathscr{C}_{HP}(\mathcal{K},\overline{\mathcal{H}})
. Hence
\lambda\Phi_{1}+(1-\lambda)\Phi_{2}\succeq_{HP}\Lambda
. Thus,
\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
forms a convex set.

From Definition

1
, we can also define the set of channels that are at least as powerful as the quantum channel
\Lambda
in the asymptotic sense as
\mathscr{C}^{asymp}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
such that

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{asymp}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)=\{\Phi:\Phi\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathcal{H}}),\Lambda\succeq_{asymp}\Phi\},

(64)

From Theorem
1
we get
\mathscr{C}^{asymp}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)=\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
.

Similarly, let us denote the set of all quantum channels obtained by concatenating
\Lambda
with positive maps as
\mathscr{C}^{P}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
. In other words

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{P}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)=\{\Phi:\Phi\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathcal{H}}),\Lambda\succeq_{P}\Phi\}.

(65)

Just like the previous case the set
\mathscr{C}^{P}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
is also a convex set.

For completeness, we also denote the set of all quantum channels obtained by post-processing it with another quantum channel as
\mathscr{C}^{CP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
such that

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{CP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)=\{\Phi:\Phi\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathcal{H}}),\Lambda\succeq_{postproc}\Phi\}

(66)

It is known that the set
\mathscr{C}^{CP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
is also convex.

It is easy to see that the above four sets of channels satisfy the following subset relations, as illustrated in Fig.
1
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{CP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{P}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{asymp}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)=\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda).

(67)

Figure 1:
For a given channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
,
\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
represents set of all quantum channels obtained by concatenating it with Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps,
\mathscr{C}^{P}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
represents set of all quantum channels obtained by concatenating it with positive maps and
\mathscr{C}^{CP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)
represents set of all quantum channels obtained by post-processing it with a quantum channels. They follow the subset relation:
\mathscr{C}^{CP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{P}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)\subseteq\mathscr{C}^{asymp}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)=\mathscr{C}^{HP}_{\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{H}}}(\Lambda)

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have tried to provide a physically meaningful way to compare two quantum channels using Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear maps. Whenever it is possible to determine the output state of one quantum channel
\Lambda_{2}
from the output statistics of another quantum channel
\Lambda_{1}
using some quantum measurement, we say that the quantum channel
\Lambda_{1}
is at least as powerful as the quantum channel
\Lambda_{2}
in the asymptotic sense. We have shown that it is equivalent to saying that
\Lambda_{2}
can be written as a concatenation of
\Lambda_{1}
with a Hermitian-preserving trace-preserving linear map, and this map need not to be positive either. We further show that it is possible that
\Lambda_{1}
can’t be post-processed from
\Lambda_{2}
even though the output statistics of the former can be obtained from the output statistics of the latter. Thus there exists a hierarchy between the preorders induced by these two relations. In between these two, there exists the prorder introduced by the relation between
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
, where
\Lambda_{2}
can be obtained by concatenating
\Lambda_{1}
with a positive linear map. We further show the implication of our results on quantum incompatibility. Our work opens up several avenues for future research direction.

We already know positive maps are important for constructing witnesses for entanglement detection. It would be interesting and worthwhile to see whether a similar physical meaning can also be given for positive linear maps in the context of the concatenation of quantum channels. Another direction to venture is how some information-theoretic and thermodynamic properties of two channels are related to each other, given that one of them is at least as powerful as the other in an asymptotic sense. It will also be interesting to explore how our study is helpful in quantifying the performance of some information-theoretic or thermodynamic tasks.
