Off-policy estimation with adaptively collected data: the power of online learning
Jeonghwan Lee and Cong Ma

Abstract
We consider estimation of a linear functional of the treatment effect using adaptively collected data. This task finds a variety of applications including the off-policy evaluation ( OPE ) in contextual bandits, and estimation of the average treatment effect ( ATE ) in causal inference. While a certain class of augmented inverse propensity weighting ( AIPW ) estimators enjoys desirable asymptotic properties including the semi-parametric efficiency, much less is known about their non-asymptotic theory with adaptively collected data. To fill in the gap, we first establish generic upper bounds on the mean-squared error of the class of AIPW estimators that crucially depends on a sequentially weighted error between the treatment effect and its estimates. Motivated by this, we also propose a general reduction scheme that allows one to produce a sequence of estimates for the treatment effect via online learning to minimize the sequentially weighted estimation error. To illustrate this, we provide three concrete instantiations in (i) the tabular case; (ii) the case of linear function approximation; and (iii) the case of general function approximation for the outcome model. We then provide a local minimax lower bound to show the instance-dependent optimality of the AIPW estimator using no-regret online learning algorithms.

Problem formulation

We formulate our problem with the language of contextual bandits: let
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{A}
, and
\mathbb{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
denote the
context space
, the
action space
, and the
outcome space
, respectively. Denote by
\mathbb{O}:=\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{Y}
the space of all possible context-action-outcome triples. In an adaptive experiment, we observe
n
samples
\left\{\left(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i}\right)\in\mathbb{O}:i\in[n]\right\}
produced by the following data generating procedure
[
26
,
68
]
: At each stage
i\in[n]
,

(i)

A context
X_{i}\in\mathbb{X}
is independently sampled from a fixed
context distribution

\Xi^{*}(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathbb{X})
;

(ii)

There exists a
behavioral policy

\Pi_{i}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{O}^{i-1}\to\Delta(\mathbb{A})
that selects the
i
-th action as
A_{i}\left|X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right.\sim\Pi_{i}^{*}\left(\cdot\left|X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right.\right)
, where
\mathbf{O}_{i}:=\left(X_{1},A_{1},Y_{1},\cdots,X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i}\right)\in\mathbb{O}^{i}
for
i\in[n]
. Since
\Pi_{i}^{*}\left(\cdot\left|X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right.\right)
may depend on previous observations,
\left\{\left(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
are no longer i.i.d.;

(iii)

Given a Markov kernel
\Gamma^{*}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{Y})
, we assume that the outcome is generated according to
Y_{i}\sim\Gamma^{*}\left(\cdot\left|X_{i},A_{i}\right.\right)
. Moreover, the conditional mean of the outcome
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{Y}
is specified as

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\left|X_{i},A_{i}\right.\right]=\int_{\mathbb{Y}}y\Gamma^{*}\left(\mathrm{d}y\left|X_{i},A_{i}\right.\right)=\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right),

where the function
\mu^{*}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is called the
treatment effect
(in the literature of causal inference) or the
reward function
(in bandit and
RL
literature). We note that the treatment effect
\mu^{*}
is not revealed to the statistician. We also define the conditional variance function
\sigma^{2}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[0,+\infty\right]
defined by
\sigma^{2}\left(x,a\right):=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left\{Y-\mu^{*}\left(X,A\right)\right\}^{2}\right|\left(X,A\right)=\left(x,a\right)\right]
, which is assumed to satisfy
\sigma^{2}(x,a)<+\infty
for all state-action pairs
(x,a)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}
.

We embark on our discussion after assuming the existence of
\sigma
-finite base measures
\lambda_{\mathbb{X}}(\cdot)
,
\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(\cdot)
, and
\lambda_{\mathbb{Y}}(\cdot)
over
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{A}
, and
\mathbb{Y}
, respectively, such that
\Xi^{*}(\cdot)\ll\lambda_{\mathbb{X}}(\cdot)
,
\Pi_{i}^{*}\left(\cdot\left|x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1}\right.\right)\ll\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(\cdot)
for every
\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1}\right)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{O}^{i-1}
and
i\in[n]
, and
\Gamma^{*}\left(\cdot\left|x,a\right.\right)\ll\lambda_{\mathbb{Y}}(\cdot)
for all state-action pairs
(x,a)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}
. The notation
\ll
stands for the
absolute continuity
between measures. Our main objective is to estimate the
off-policy value
for any given target evaluation function
g(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\begin{split}\tau^{*}=\tau\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left\langle g(X,\cdot),\mu^{*}(X,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right],\end{split}

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{I}^{*}:=\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)\in\mathbb{I}:=\Delta(\mathbb{X})\times\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{Y})\right)
refers to the ground-truth
problem instance
, and given any pair of functions
(f,g)\in\left(\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right)\times\left(\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right)
such that
fg\in\mathbb{L}^{1}\left(\mathbb{A},\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}\right)
, we define the inner product

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}:=\int_{\mathbb{A}}f(a)g(a)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(a).

In this paper, we assume that the propensity scores
\left\{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
are known to the statistician, where
\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1};\cdot\right):=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi_{i}^{*}\left(\cdot\left|x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1}\right.\right)}{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

As we mentioned earlier in Section
1
, the estimation problem of a linear functional of the treatment effect

turns out to be useful in both causal inference and

•

Estimation of average treatment effects
: Consider the binary action space
\mathbb{A}=\left\{0,1\right\}
equipped with the counting measure over

. The

(

) in our problem setting is defined as the linear functional

\begin{split}\textsf{ATE}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\mu^{*}\left(X,1\right)-\mu^{*}\left(X,0\right)\right].\end{split}

Once we take the target evaluation function as

, the

boils down to a particular case of the equation (
2.1
);

•

Off-policy evaluation for contextual bandits
: We assume that a

\Pi^{\textnormal{target}}(\cdot):\mathbb{X}\to\Delta(\mathbb{A})
is given such that
\Pi^{\textnormal{target}}\left(\cdot\left|x\right.\right)\ll\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(\cdot)
for every context

) corresponds to the value of the target policy

We conclude this section by introducing notations that will be useful in later sections: let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{I}}^{i}(\cdot)\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{O}^{i}\right)
denote the law of the sample trajectory

. Lastly, we define the
k

-norm
for

as

\left\|\varphi\right\|_{(k)}^{2}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\varphi^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]

(2.2)

for any function
\varphi(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, together with the
k
-th weighted
\ell_{2}
-space
by

\mathbb{L}_{(k)}^{2}:=\left\{\varphi(\cdot,\cdot)\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right):\left\|\varphi\right\|_{(k)}<+\infty\right\}.

3
A class of

The main objective of this section is to develop a

3.1
How can we reduce the variance of the
IPW
estimator?

Similar to the prior work
[
42
]
, we consider a class of two-stage estimators obtained from simple perturbations of the

f:=\left(f_{i}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{O}^{i-1}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}:i\in[n]\right)
of auxiliary functions, we consider the following

\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f}\left(\cdot\right):\mathbb{O}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f}\left(\mathbf{o}_{n}\right):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{g\left(x_{i},a_{i}\right)y_{i}}{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i-1};a_{i}\right)}-f_{i}\left(x_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i-1},a_{i}\right)+\left\langle f_{i}\left(x_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i-1},\cdot\right),\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i-1};\cdot\right)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right\}.

For each
i\in[n]
, let
\nu_{i}(\cdot)\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{O}^{i-1}\times\mathbb{A}\right)
denote the joint distribution of
\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1},A_{i}\right)
induced by the adaptive data collection scheme described in Section
2
. Then, we arrive at the following result whose proof is deferred to Appendix
B.1
:

Proposition 3.1
.

For any given collection
f:=\left(f_{i}\in L^{2}\left(\nu_{i}\right):i\in[n]\right)
of auxiliary deterministic functions, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)\right]=\tau\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)
. Furthermore, if

\begin{split}\left\langle f_{i}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1},\cdot\right),\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1};\cdot\right)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}=0,\ \forall\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1}\right)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{O}^{i-1}\end{split}

(3.1)

for each
i\in[n]
, then

\displaystyle n\cdot\textnormal{{Var}}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)\right]=\textnormal{{Var}}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left\langle g(X,\cdot),\mu^{*}(X,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right]+\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}

(3.2)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\left\{\frac{g\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}-\left\langle g\left(X_{i},\cdot\right),\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},\cdot\right)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}-f_{i}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}\right].

From the decomposition (
3.2
) of the variance of the perturbed

\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)
, one can observe that the only term which depends on the collection of auxiliary functions

is the third term.
More importantly, the third term is equal to zero if and only if

\begin{split}f_{i}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1},a\right)=f_{i}^{*}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1},a\right):=\frac{g\left(x,a\right)\mu^{*}\left(x,a\right)}{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1};a\right)}-\left\langle g(x,\cdot),\mu^{*}(x,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}.\end{split}

(3.3)

The collection of functions
f^{*}:=\left(f_{i}^{*}\in L^{2}\left(\nu_{i}\right):i\in[n]\right)
minimizing the third term in the right-hand side of the equation (

\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f^{*}}\left(\cdot\right):\mathbb{O}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

\begin{split}\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f^{*}}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\frac{g\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\left\{Y_{i}-\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}}{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}+\left\langle g\left(X_{i},\cdot\right),\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},\cdot\right)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right\},\end{split}

(3.4)

whose variance is given by

\begin{split}n\cdot\textsf{Var}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\hat{\tau}_{n}^{f^{*}}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)\right]=v_{*}^{2}:=\textsf{Var}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left\langle g(X,\cdot),\mu^{*}(X,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right]+\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}.\end{split}

estimators for dealing with estimation of the off-policy value (

). The main result of this section can be summarized as the following non-asymptotic upper bound on the mean-squared error (
MSE
) of Algorithm
1
:

Theorem 3.1

(Non-asymptotic upper bound on the
MSE
of the
AIPW
estimator)
.

Given any sequence of estimates
\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
for the treatment effect
\mu^{*}
, the
AIPW
estimator (
3.6
) has the
MSE
bounded above by

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\left\{\hat{\tau}_{n}^{\textnormal{{AIPW}}}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)-\tau\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right\}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}\left\{v_{*}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)-\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]\right\}.\end{split}

(3.8)

We notice that the non-asymptotic upper bound (
3.8
) on the
MSE
for the class of
AIPW
estimators (
3.6
) consists of two terms, both of which have natural interpretations. Here, the first term
v_{*}^{2}
corresponds to the optimal variance (
3.5
) achievable by the oracle estimator, and the second term

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)-\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]\end{split}

(3.9)

measures the average estimation error of the estimates
\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
of
\mu^{*}
. Of primary interest to us is a subsequent upper bounding argument based on the upper bound (
3.8
) on the
MSE
in the finite-sample regime: in particular, to minimize the right-hand side of the bound (
3.8
), one needs to choose a sequence of estimates
\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
which minimizes the second term (
3.9
).

3.4
Reduction to online non-parametric regression

We now focus on constructing a sequence of estimates
\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
for the treatment effect, and upper bounding the estimation error (
3.9
) in the
MSE
bound (
3.8
). To this end, we borrow ideas from the literature of online non-parametric regression
[
45
]
.

To begin with, we consider a turn-based game with
n
rounds between the learner and the environment: see Algorithm
2
for the details. Then, one can readily observe for any
\mu(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\left.l_{i}(\mu)\right|\left(\mathcal{H}_{i-1},X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right]=\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\left[\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)+\left\{\mu\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)-\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}\right].\end{split}

(3.10)

In the current turn-based game framework, our natural goal is to minimize the learner’s static regret against the
best fixed action in hindsight
belonging to a pre-specified function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\right)
:

\begin{split}\textsf{Regret}\left(n,\mathcal{F};\mathcal{A}\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\right\}-\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}(\mu):\mu\in\mathcal{F}\right\},\end{split}

(3.11)

where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the learner’s online non-parametric regression algorithm that returns a sequence of estimates
\left\{\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
for the treatment effect
\mu^{*}
. Then, one can prove the following oracle inequality that demystifies a relationship between the estimation problem of the off-policy value and the online non-parametric regression protocol. See Appendix
B.3
for the proof.

\mu^{*}

\mathcal{F}

4
Lower bounds: local minimax risk

We turn our attention to a local minimax lower bound for estimating the off-policy value
\tau^{*}=\tau\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)
. Here, we aim at establishing lower bounds that hold uniformly over all estimators that are permitted to know both the propensity scores
\left\{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
and the evaluation function
g
. We assume the existence of a constant
K\in\left[1,+\infty\right)
and
reference Markov policies

\left\{\overline{\Pi}_{i}:\mathbb{X}\to\Delta(\mathbb{A}):i\in[n]\right\}
such that
\overline{\Pi}_{i}\left(\left.\cdot\right|x\right)\ll\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(\cdot)
for
(x,i)\in\mathbb{X}\times[n]
, and

\frac{1}{K}\leq\frac{\overline{\pi}_{i}\left(x,a\right)}{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1};a\right)}\leq K

(4.1)

for every
\left(x,\mathbf{o}_{i-1},a\right)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{O}^{i-1}\times\mathbb{A}
, where
\overline{\pi}_{i}\left(x,\cdot\right):=\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\Pi}_{i}\left(\left.\cdot\right|x\right)}{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
for each context
x\in\mathbb{X}
. Proximity of behavioral policies to certain Markov policies is often assumed under adaptive data collection procedures. For instance, in
Theorem 1
of
[
67
]
, the authors assumed that the sequence of behavior policies is
eventually Markov
; see the equation (8) therein.

4.1
Instance-dependent local minimax lower bounds

For any problem instance
\mathcal{I}^{*}=\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)\in\mathbb{I}
and an error function
\delta:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we consider the following local neighborhoods:

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right):=\ &\left\{\Xi\in\Delta(\mathbb{X}):\textnormal{KL}\left(\Xi\left\|\Xi^{*}\right.\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}\right\};\\
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\left(\Gamma^{*}\right):=\ &\left\{\Gamma\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{Y})\right):\left|\mu(\Gamma)(x,a)-\mu\left(\Gamma^{*}\right)(x,a)\right|\leq\delta(x,a),\ \forall(x,a)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right\},\end{split}

where for any given
\Gamma:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{Y})
, let
\mu(\Gamma)(x,a):=\int_{\mathbb{Y}}y\Gamma\left(\left.\mathrm{d}y\right|x,a\right)
for each
(x,a)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}
. Our goal is to lower bound the following
local minimax risk
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right):=\inf_{\hat{\tau}_{n}(\cdot):\mathbb{O}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}}\left(\sup_{\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}}\left[\left\{\hat{\tau}_{n}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)-\tau\left(\mathcal{I}\right)\right\}^{2}\right]\right),\end{split}

(4.2)

where
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right):=\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)\times\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\left(\Gamma^{*}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{I}
. We now specify some assumptions necessary for lower bounding the local minimax risk (
4.2
). Prior to this, we introduce a new significant notation: given any random variable
Y\in\mathbb{L}^{4}\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
defined on the underlying probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)
, its
(2,4)
-moment ratio
is defined as
\left\|Y\right\|_{2\to 4}:=\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{4}\right]}}{\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{2}\right]}

Appendix A
Some elementary inequalities and their proofs

The following lemma plays a key role in the truncation arguments used in establishing our local minimax lower bounds. In particular, it enables us to make small modifications on a pair of probability dsitributions by conditioning on
good events
of each probability measure, without inducing an irregularly large change in the total variation distance.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\left(\mu,\nu\right)
be any pair of probability measures defined on a common sample space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F}\right)
. Let us consider any two events
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
B\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
\min\left\{\mu(A),\nu(B)\right\}\geq 1-\epsilon
for some
\epsilon\in\left[0,\frac{1}{4}\right]
. Then, the conditional distributions
\left(\mu|A\right)(\cdot)\in\Delta\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F}\right)
and
\left(\nu|B\right)(\cdot)\in\Delta\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F}\right)
defined by

\left(\mu|A\right)(E):=\frac{\mu\left(A\cap E\right)}{\mu(A)}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\left(\nu|B\right)(E):=\frac{\nu\left(B\cap E\right)}{\nu(B)}

for any event
E\in\mathcal{F}
, satisfy the bound

\left|\textnormal{{TV}}\left(\mu|A,\nu|B\right)-\textnormal{{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\right|\leq 2\epsilon.

(A.1)

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Due to the triangle inequality for the total variation (
TV
) distance, it follows that

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\leq\textsf{TV}\left(\mu,\mu|A\right)+\textsf{TV}\left(\mu|A,\nu|B\right)+\textsf{TV}\left(\nu|B,\nu\right),\end{split}

(A.2)

and

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mu|A,\nu|B\right)\leq\textsf{TV}\left(\mu|A,\mu\right)+\textsf{TV}\left(\mu,\nu\right)+\textsf{TV}\left(\nu,\nu|B\right).\end{split}

(A.3)

At this point, one can easily observe that

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mu,\mu|A\right)=\ &\sup\left\{\left|\mu(E)-\left(\mu|A\right)(E)\right|:E\in\mathcal{F}\right\}=\left(\mu|A\right)(A)-\mu(A)=1-\mu(A);\\
\textsf{TV}\left(\nu,\nu|B\right)=\ &\sup\left\{\left|\nu(E)-\left(\nu|B\right)(E)\right|:E\in\mathcal{F}\right\}=\left(\nu|B\right)(B)-\nu(B)=1-\nu(B).\end{split}

(A.4)

Putting the finding (
A.4
) into the inequalities (
A.2
) and (
A.3
), the assumptions
1-\mu(A)\leq\epsilon
and
1-\nu(B)\leq\epsilon
establish the desired result.

∎

\leq\ &\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\overline{\textnormal{Regret}}\left(n;\overline{\mathcal{A}}\right)\right]+\inf\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\left\{\mu\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)-\mu^{*}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}\right]:\mu\in\mathcal{F}\right\}.\end{split}

(B.24)

Hence, the desired result (
B.21
) on the
MSE
for the
AIPW
estimator (
3.6
) is a straightforward consequence of the inequality (
B.24
) by plugging it into the bound (
B.16
).

∎

Here, we remark that aside from the optimal variance
v_{*}^{2}
, the bound (
B.21
) shows two additional terms: (i) the expected regret relative to the number of rounds
n
, where the expectation is taken over
\mathbf{O}_{n}\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}^{n}(\cdot)
; and (ii) the approximation error term whose form is slightly different from the one
\inf\left\{\left\|\mu-\mu^{*}\right\|_{(n)}^{2}:\mu\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
appeared in the
MSE
bound (
3.13
) of Theorem
3.2
.

Non-asymptotic theory of online non-parametric regression

Before delving into the investigation of the modified regret (
B.20
), we briefly recap the main results in
[
45
]
that establishes a theoretical framework of online non-parametric regression. In contrast to most of the existing works on online regression, the authors do NOT start from an algorithm, but instead directly work with the minimax regret in
[
45
]
. We will be able to extract a (not necessarily efficient) algorithm after taking a closer inspection on the minimax regret. We use
\left\llangle\cdots\right\rrangle_{i=1}^{n}
to denote an interleaved application of the operators inside repeated over
n
rounds. With this notation in hand, the minimax regret of the online non-parametric regression problem for estimation of the treatment effect can be written as

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\left\llangle\sup_{\left(x_{i},a_{i}\right)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}}\inf_{\hat{y}_{i}\in\left[-L,L\right]}\sup_{y_{i}\in\left[-L,L\right]}\right\rrangle_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}\right)^{2}-\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\mu\left(x_{i},a_{i}\right)-y_{i}\right\}^{2}\right],\end{split}

(B.25)

where
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
is a pre-specified function class. One of the key tools in the study of estimators based on i.i.d. data is the
symmetrization technique

[
15
,
62
]
. Under the i.i.d. scenario, one can investigate the supremum of an empirical process conditionally on the data by introducing Rademacher random variables, which is NOT directly applicable given the adaptive nature of our main problem. Under the online prediction scenario, such a symmetrization technique becomes more subtle and it requires the notion of a binary tree, the smallest entity which captures the sequential nature of the problem in some sense. Towards achieving our goal in our problem, let us state some definitions.

Definition B.1
.

An
\mathbb{S}
-valued tree of depth n
(here,
\mathbb{S}
is any measurable state space) is defined as a rooted complete binary tree with nodes labeled by elements of the state space
\mathbb{S}
: the sequence
\mathbf{s}=\left(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{s}_{n}\right)
of labeling functions
\mathbf{s}_{i}(\cdot):\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{i-1}\to\mathbb{S}
which provides the labels of each node. Here,
\mathbf{s}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}
denotes the label for the
root of the tree
, while
\mathbf{s}_{i}
for
i\in\left\{2,3,\cdots,n\right\}
denotes the label of the node obtained by following the path of length
i-1
from the root, with
+1
indicating
right
and
-1
indicating
left
. A
path of length
n
is given by the sequence
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:n}=\left(\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{n}\right)\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}
. Given any measurable function
\phi(\cdot):\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\phi(\mathbf{s})
is an
\mathbb{R}
-valued tree of depth
n
with labeling functions
\left(\phi\circ\mathbf{s}_{i}\right)(\cdot):\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{i-1}\to\mathbb{R}
for level
i\in[n]
(or, in words, the evaluation of
\phi(\cdot):\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\phi(\mathbf{s})
on
\mathbf{s}
). Lastly, let
\textsf{Tree}\left(\mathbb{S},n\right)
denote the set of all
\mathbb{S}
-valued trees of depth
n
.

Here, one may think of the sequence of functions
\left\{\mathbf{s}_{i}(\cdot):i\in[n]\right\}
defined on the underlying sample space as a predictable stochastic process with respect to the dyadic filtration
\left\{\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:i}\right):i\in[n]\right\}
. Next, let us define the notion of a
sequential
\beta
-cover
quantifies one of the key complexity measures of a function class
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\right)
evaluated on the predictable process: the
sequential covering number
.

Definition B.2

(Sequential covering numbers
[
46
]
)
.

(i)

We define a random pseudo-metric between two
\mathbb{R}
-valued trees
\mathbf{u}=\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}:i\in[n]\right)
and
\mathbf{v}=\left(\mathbf{v}_{i}:i\in[n]\right)
of depth
n
defined as follows: for any
\left(p,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:n}\right)\in\left[1,+\infty\right]\times\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}
,

\begin{split}d_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:n}}^{p}\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right):=\begin{cases}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\mathbf{u}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:i-1}\right)-\mathbf{v}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:i-1}\right)\right|^{p}\right\}^{\frac{1}{p}}&\textnormal{if }1\leq p<+\infty;\\
\max\left\{\left|\mathbf{u}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:i-1}\right)-\mathbf{v}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:i-1}\right)\right|:i\in[n]\right\}&\textnormal{if }p=+\infty.\end{cases}\end{split}

(B.26)

(ii)

A set
V\subseteq\textsf{Tree}\left(\mathbb{R},n\right)
is called a
sequential
\beta
-cover with respect to the
l_{p}
-norm of
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\right)
on a given
\mathbb{S}
-valued tree
\mathbf{s}
of depth
n
, where
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
, if

\begin{split}\sup\left\{\inf\left\{d_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:n}}^{p}\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right):\mathbf{v}\in V\right\}:\left(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1:n}\right)\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{s})\times\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}\right\}\leq\beta,\end{split}

(B.27)

where
\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{s}):=\left\{g(\mathbf{s}):g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}\subseteq\textsf{Tree}\left(\mathbb{R},n\right)
;

(iii)

The
sequential
\beta
-covering number with respect to the
l_{p}
-norm of a function class
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}\right)
on an
\mathbb{S}
-valued tree
\mathbf{s}
of depth
n
, where
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
, is defined by

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(\beta,\mathcal{G},\mathbf{s}\right):=\min\left\{\left|V\right|:V\subseteq\textsf{Tree}\left(\mathbb{R},n\right)\textnormal{ is a sequential $\beta$-cover w.r.t. the $l_{p}$-norm of $\mathcal{G}$ on $\mathbf{s}$}\right\}.\end{split}

Here, we further define
\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(\beta,\mathcal{G},n\right):=\sup\left\{\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(\beta,\mathcal{G},\mathbf{s}\right):\mathbf{s}\in\textsf{Tree}\left(\mathbb{S},n\right)\right\}
to be the
maximal sequential
\beta
-covering number with respect to the
l_{p}
-norm of
\mathcal{G}
over
\mathbb{S}
-valued trees of depth
n
. Now, we will refer to
\log\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(\beta,\mathcal{G},n\right)
as the
sequential
\beta
-metric entropy of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to the
l_{p}
-norm
.

In particular, we investigate the behavior of the minimax regret
\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F})
for the case where the sequential metric entropy of the function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
with respect to the
l_{2}
-norm grows polynomially as the scale
\beta
decreases:

\begin{split}\log\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\beta,\mathcal{F},n\right)\sim\beta^{-p}\quad\textnormal{for }p\in\left(0,+\infty\right).\end{split}

(B.28)

Let us also consider the
parametric “
p=0
” case
when the sequential covering number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
l_{2}
-norm itself behaves as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\beta,\mathcal{F},n\right)\sim\beta^{-d}.\end{split}

(B.29)

For instance, the function class
\mathcal{F}:=\left\{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\right\}
for the linear regression problem in a bounded measurable subset
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the function
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}):=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, satisfies the condition (
B.29
). By employing the main results (in particular,
Theorem 2
) in
[
45
]
, one can establish the following conclusion:

Theorem B.2

(The rates of convergence of the minimax regret)
.

Given any function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
with sequential metric entropy growth
\log\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\beta,\mathcal{F},n\right)\leq\beta^{-p}
for
p\in\left(0,+\infty\right)
, it holds that

(i)

for
p\in\left(2,+\infty\right)
, the minimax regret (
B.25
) is bounded as

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\left(4+\frac{24}{p-2}\right)Ln^{1-\frac{1}{p}}.\end{split}

(B.30)

(ii)

for
p\in\left(0,2\right)
, the minimax regret (
B.25
) is bounded as

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\left(32L^{2}+4L+\frac{24L}{2-p}\right)n^{1-\frac{2}{p+2}}.\end{split}

(B.31)

(iii)

for
p=2
, the minimax regret (
B.25
) is bounded as

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\left(32L^{2}+4L+3\right)\sqrt{n}\log n.\end{split}

(B.32)

(iv)

for the parametric case (
B.29
), the minimax regret (
B.25
) is bounded as

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq\left(16L^{2}+4L+12\right)d\log n.\end{split}

(B.33)

(v)

if the function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
is a finite set, the minimax regret (
B.25
) is bounded as

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq 32L^{2}\log\left|\mathcal{F}\right|.\end{split}

(B.34)

It is shown in
[
45
]
that the upper bounds (i)–(iv) on the minimax regret (
B.25
) in Theorem
B.2
are
tight up to logarithmic factors
. See
Theorem 3
therein for further details.

Although Theorem
B.2
characterizes the rates of convergence of the minimax regret (
B.25
) under various scenarios
statistically
, its proof is
non-constructive
in the sense that the regret bounds therein are established without explicitly constructing an algorithm. To provide a general algorithmic framework for the problem of online non-parametric regression, we follow the abstract
relaxation recipe
proposed in
[
47
]
. It was shown therein that if one can find a sequence of mappings from the observed data to real numbers, often called a
relaxation
, satisfying some desirable conditions, then one can construct estimators based on such relaxations. To be specific, we search for a relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following two conditions:

Assumption 5

(Initial condition)
.

The relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\begin{split}\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:n},\mathbf{y}_{1:n}\right)\geq-\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\{y_{i}-\mu\left(x_{i},a_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}:\mu(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal{F}\right\},\end{split}

(B.35)

where
\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k}:=\left(\left(x_{i},a_{i}\right):i\in[k]\right)\in\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}
and
\mathbf{y}_{1:k}:=\left(y_{i}:i\in[k]\right)\in\left[-L,L\right]^{k}
for every
k\in[n]
.

Assumption 6

(Recursive admissibility condition)
.

The relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\begin{split}\inf_{\hat{y}_{k}\in\left[-L,L\right]}\sup_{y_{k}\in\left[-L,L\right]}\left\{\left(\hat{y}_{k}-y_{k}\right)^{2}+\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k},\mathbf{y}_{1:k}\right)\right\}\leq\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k-1},\mathbf{y}_{1:k-1}\right),\end{split}

(B.36)

for any
k\in[n]
and any
x_{k}\in\mathbb{X}
.

A relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies Assumptions
5
and
6
is said to be
admissible
. With an admissible relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
in hand, one can design an algorithm for the online non-parametric regression problem with the following associated regret bound (see Algorithm
5
for the detailed description):

\begin{split}\overline{\textsf{Regret}}\left(n,\mathcal{F};\textnormal{Alg. \ref{alg:generic_forecaster}}\right)=\ &\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{Y_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i}\left(\mathbf{O}_{i-1}\right)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}-\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{Y_{i}-\mu\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}^{2}:\mu\in\mathcal{F}\right\}\\
\leq\ &\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\varnothing,\varnothing\right).\end{split}

(B.37)

p=2
, it holds that

\begin{split}\overline{\textnormal{{Regret}}}\left(n,\mathcal{F};\textnormal{Algorithm \ref{alg:generic_forecaster}}\right)\leq\left(32L^{2}+4L+3\right)\sqrt{n}\log n.\end{split}

(B.48)

(iv)

for the parametric case (
B.29
), it holds that

\begin{split}\overline{\textnormal{{Regret}}}\left(n,\mathcal{F};\textnormal{Algorithm \ref{alg:generic_forecaster}}\right)\leq\left(16L^{2}+4L+12\right)d\log n.\end{split}

(B.49)

(v)

if the function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
is a finite set, it holds that

\begin{split}\overline{\textnormal{{Regret}}}\left(n,\mathcal{F};\textnormal{Algorithm \ref{alg:generic_forecaster}}\right)\leq 32L^{2}\log\left|\mathcal{F}\right|.\end{split}

(B.50)

Although Corollary
B.1
gives no-regret learning guarantees of Algorithm
5
with the admissible relaxation
\mathcal{R}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
defined by (
B.44
) for a number of function classes
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
, it is still NOT a practical algorithm because the relaxation
\mathcal{R}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
defined as (
B.44
) is not directly computable in general. To address this problem,
[
45
]
provided a generic schema for developing implementable online non-parametric regression algorithms. The schema can be described as follows:

(a)

Find a
computable relaxation

\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k},\mathbf{y}_{1:k}\right)\leq\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k},\mathbf{y}_{1:k}\right)\end{split}

for every
\left(k,\mathbf{x}_{1:n},\mathbf{a}_{1:n},\mathbf{y}_{1:n}\right)\in\left\{0,1,\cdots,n\right\}\times\mathbb{X}^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{n}
, and the function
y_{k}\in\left[-L,L\right]\mapsto\left(\hat{y}-y_{k}\right)^{2}+\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k}\right),\left(\mathbf{y}_{1:k-1},y_{k}\right)\right)\in\mathbb{R}
is convex for every
\left(\hat{y},\mathbf{x}_{1:n},\mathbf{a}_{1:n},\mathbf{y}_{1:k-1}\right)\in\left[-L,L\right]\times\mathbb{X}^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k-1}
and
k\in[n]
;

(b)

Next, we check the following condition:

\begin{split}&\sup_{\left(x_{k},a_{k},\mu_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\times\Delta\left(\left[-L,L\right]\right)}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{y_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{y_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\left[y_{k}\right]-y_{k}\right)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{y_{k}\sim\mu_{k}}\left[\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k},\mathbf{y}_{1:k}\right)\right]\right\}\\
\leq\ &\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\right)_{1:k-1},\mathbf{y}_{1:k-1}\right)\end{split}

for every
\left(\mathbf{x}_{1:k-1},\mathbf{a}_{1:k-1},\mathbf{y}_{1:k-1}\right)\in\mathbb{X}^{k-1}\times\mathbb{A}_{k-1}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k-1}
and
k\in[n]
;

(c)

Implement Algorithm
5
using the relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
as an input.

The authors proved that any computable relaxation
\textsf{Rel}_{n}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):\biguplus_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\right)^{k}\times\left[-L,L\right]^{k}\right\}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying conditions in (a) and (b) are admissible; see
Proposition 17
therein. Consequently, any online non-parametric regression algorithm produced by the above generic schema always satisfies the regret bound (
B.37
). Moreover, the authors established a practical online non-parametric regression algorithm with no-regret learning guarantees based on the above procedure for the finite function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\left(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\left[-L,L\right]\right)
and the online linear regression problem.

C.1
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Theorem

can be established by taking the following two lemmas collectively:

Lemma C.1
.

Under Assumption

, the local minimax risk over the class
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{2304}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{n}\textnormal{{Var}}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left\langle g(X,\cdot),\mu^{*}(X,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right],\end{split}

(C.1)

provided that

.

Lemma C.2
.

Under Assumption

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{8K^{4}}\cdot\frac{\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}}{n}.\end{split}

(C.2)

C.2
Proof of Lemma
C.1

The proof relies on Le Cam’s two-point method by taking the outcome kernel
\Gamma^{*}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\Delta(\mathbb{Y})
to be fixed, and perturbing the context distribution
\Xi^{*}(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathbb{X})
: we first construct a collection of context distributions
\left\{\Xi_{s}(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathbb{X}):s\in\left(0,+\infty\right)\right\}
. Later, we will choose the parameter
s>0
small enough so that
\Xi_{s}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)
and two distributions
\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}(\cdot)\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{O}^{n}\right)
and
\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}(\cdot)\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{O}^{n}\right)
are
indistinguishable
, but large enough such that the functional values
\tau\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)
and
\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)
are
well-separated
. Le Cam’s two-point lemma (the equation (15.14) in
[
62
]
) guarantees that the local minimax risk
\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)
is lower bounded as

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)\geq\ &\frac{1}{4}\left\{1-\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)\right\}\left\{\tau\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)-\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)\right\}^{2},\end{split}

(C.3)

provided that
\Xi_{s}\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)
.

First, we upper bound the total variation distance
\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)
. Thanks to the Pinsker-Csiszár-Kullback inequality, one has

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)}.\end{split}

(C.4)

One can reveal that the density function of the law
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{I}}^{n}=\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi,\Gamma\right)}^{n}\in\Delta\left(\mathbb{O}^{n}\right)
of the sample trajectory
\mathbf{O}_{n}
under the problem instance
\mathcal{I}=\left(\Xi,\Gamma\right)\in\mathbb{I}
with respect to the base measure
\left(\lambda_{\mathbb{X}}\otimes\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}\otimes\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}\right)^{\otimes n}
is given by

\begin{split}p_{\mathcal{I}}^{n}\left(\mathbf{o}_{n}\right)=p_{\left(\Xi,\Gamma\right)}^{n}\left(\mathbf{o}_{n}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\xi\left(x_{i}\right)\pi_{i}^{*}\left(x_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i-1};a_{i}\right)\gamma\left(\left.y_{i}\right|x_{i},a_{i}\right)\right\}.\end{split}

(C.5)

Using this fact, the
KL
-divergence
\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)
can be computed as

\begin{split}\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)=\ &\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}\left[\log\frac{p_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)}{p_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)}\right]\\
=\ &\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\xi_{s}\left(X_{i}\right)\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)\gamma^{*}\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\xi^{*}\left(X_{i}\right)\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)\gamma^{*}\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\right]\\
=\ &\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}\left[\log\frac{\xi_{s}\left(X_{i}\right)}{\xi^{*}\left(X_{i}\right)}\right]\\
=\ &n\cdot\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right).\end{split}

(C.6)

So if one can show that
\Xi_{s}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)
, then the equation (
C.6
) guarantees that

\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)=n\cdot\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)\leq 1,

which can be taken collectively with the bound (
C.4
) to produce the following conclusion:

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)}^{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.\end{split}

(C.7)

With the arguments thus far in place, it remains to construct a family
\left\{\Xi_{s}(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathbb{X}):s\in\left(0,+\infty\right)\right\}
and then choose a parameter
s>0
such that
\Xi_{s}\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)
and the functional values
\tau\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)
and
\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)
are well-separated. To this end, we consider the function
\tilde{h}(\cdot):\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\tilde{h}(x):=\begin{cases}h(x)&\textnormal{if }\left|h(x)\right|\leq 2H_{2\to 4}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right]};\\
\textsf{sign}\left(h(x)\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right]}&\textnormal{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
H_{2\to 4}\geq 1
, one can easily find that
\left|\tilde{h}(x)\right|\leq\left|h(x)\right|
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
. Now for each
s\in\left(0,+\infty\right)
, we define the
tilted probability measure

\Xi_{s}(\cdot)\in\Delta(\mathbb{X})
by

\begin{split}\xi_{s}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Xi_{s}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{X}}}(x):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}(s)}\xi^{*}(x)\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(x)\right),\ \forall x\in\mathbb{X},\end{split}

(C.8)

where
\mathcal{Z}(s):=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\xi^{*}(x)\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{X}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]
. At this point, we note for every
x\in\mathbb{X}
that

\begin{split}\exp\left(-s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\right)\leq\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(x)\right)\leq\exp\left(s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\right),\end{split}

(C.9)

which also immediately yields

\begin{split}\exp\left(-s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\right)\leq\mathcal{Z}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]\leq\exp\left(s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\right).\end{split}

(C.10)

Here, we choose
s=\frac{1}{4\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}\sqrt{n}}>0
. Then, it holds due to the fact
\left|\tilde{h}(x)\right|\leq 2H_{2\to 4}\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
that

\begin{split}s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}}{\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}}\leq\frac{H_{2\to 4}}{2\sqrt{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{8},\end{split}

(C.11)

where the step (a) follows due to the assumption that
n\geq 16H_{2\to 4}^{2}
. Now, it’s time to prove that
\Xi_{s}\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)
for the current choice of the parameter
s>0
. Due to Theorem 5 in
[
14
]
, it follows that

\begin{split}\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)\leq\log\left\{1+\chi^{2}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)\right\}\leq\chi^{2}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right).\end{split}

(C.12)

So it suffices to upper bound the
\chi^{2}
-divergence
\chi^{2}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)
. One can reveal that

\displaystyle\chi^{2}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)=\

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\frac{\xi_{s}(X)}{\xi^{*}(X)}\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}^{2}(s)}\textsf{Var}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}^{2}(s)}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left\{\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)-1\right\}^{2}\right]

(C.13)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(b)}}}{{\leq}}\

\displaystyle\exp\left(2s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\right)\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(2s\left|\tilde{h}(X)\right|\right)\cdot s^{2}\tilde{h}^{2}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(c)}}}{{\leq}}\

\displaystyle\exp\left(4s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\right)\cdot s^{2}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right],

where the step (b) makes use of the fact (
C.10
) together with the elementary bound
\left|\exp(u)-1\right|\leq|u|\exp\left(|u|\right)
,
\forall u\in\mathbb{R}
, and the step (c) follows from the fact
\left|\tilde{h}(x)\right|\leq\left|h(x)\right|
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{X}
. If we put
s=\frac{1}{4\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}\sqrt{n}}
into the bound (
C.13
), then we obtain from the fact
s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{8}
together with the basic inequality (
C.12
) that

\begin{split}\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)\leq\chi^{2}\left(\left.\Xi_{s}\right\|\Xi^{*}\right)\leq 2s^{2}\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}^{2}=\frac{1}{8n},\end{split}

(C.14)

which implies
\Xi_{s}\in\mathcal{N}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)
for the choice of the parameter
s=\frac{1}{4\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}\sqrt{n}}>0
. Therefore, the upper bound on the total variation distance (
C.7
) turns out to be valid.

Next, we lower bound the gap between the functional values
\tau\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)
and
\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)
. It holds that

\begin{split}\tau\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)-\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)=\ &\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi_{s}}\left[\left\langle g(X,\cdot),\mu^{*}(X,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right]-\tau\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\\
=\ &\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}(s)}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\xi^{*}(x)\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(x)\right)\underbrace{\left\{\left\langle g(x,\cdot),\mu^{*}(x,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}-\tau\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right\}}_{=\ h(x)}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{X}}(x)\\
=\ &\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}(s)}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h(X)\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]\\
=\ &\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h(X)\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]}.\end{split}

(C.15)

Since
s\left\|\tilde{h}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{8}
, we have
s\tilde{h}(X)\in\left[-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right]
and therefore the simple inequality

\left|\exp(u)-1-u\right|\leq u^{2},\ \forall u\in\left[-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right],

implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h(X)\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(d)}}}{{\geq}}\

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h(X)\right]}_{=\ 0}+s\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left|h(X)\right|\left|\tilde{h}(X)\right|\right]-s^{2}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left|h(X)\right|\tilde{h}^{2}(X)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(e)}}}{{\geq}}\

\displaystyle s\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\tilde{h}^{2}(X)\right]-s^{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right]}\underbrace{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{4}(X)\right]}}_{=\ H_{2\to 4}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right]}

(C.16)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(f)}}}{{\geq}}\

\displaystyle\frac{s}{2}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right]-s^{2}H_{2\to 4}\left(\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[h^{2}(X)\right]\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}}{8}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{H_{2\to 4}}{2n}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(g)}}}{{\geq}}\

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}}{16\sqrt{n}},

where the step (d) follows due to the fact that
\textsf{sign}\left(h(x)\right)=\textsf{sign}\left(\tilde{h}(x)\right)
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{X}
, the step (e) makes use of the property that
\left|\tilde{h}(x)\right|\leq\left|h(x)\right|
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{X}
, together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the step (f) follows due to Lemma 7 in
[
42
]
, and the step (g) utilizes the assumption that
n\geq 16H_{2\to 4}^{2}
. Putting the lower bound (
C.16
) into the equation (
C.15
) yields

\begin{split}\tau\left(\Xi_{s},\Gamma^{*}\right)-\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma^{*}\right)\geq\frac{\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}}{16\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(h)}}}{{\geq}}\frac{\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Xi^{*}\right)}}{24\sqrt{n}},\end{split}

(C.17)

where the step (h) holds since
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\exp\left(s\tilde{h}(X)\right)\right]\leq\frac{3}{2}
, which follows from the fact
\left|s\tilde{h}(X)\right|\leq\frac{1}{8}
. Finally, by taking three pieces (
C.3
), (
C.7
), and (
C.17
) collectively, one completes the proof of Lemma
C.1
.

C.3
Proof of Lemma
C.2

The proof of Lemma
C.2
is also heavily relies on Le Cam’s two-point method. For
(i,s,z)\in[n]\times\left(0,+\infty\right)\times\left\{\pm 1\right\}
, we consider the function
\mu_{i}(zs)(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\begin{split}\mu_{i}(zs)(x,a):=\mu^{*}(x,a)+\frac{zsg(x,a)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}(x,a)}\sigma^{2}(x,a),\ \forall(x,a)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}.\end{split}

(C.18)

Also, we define the perturbed outcome kernel
\Gamma_{i}(zs)(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{Y}
as

\Gamma_{i}(zs)\left(\left.\cdot\right|x,a\right):=\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{i}(zs)(x,a),\sigma^{2}(x,a)\right),\ \forall(x,a)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}.

Then, due to Le Cam’s two-point lemma, the local minimax risk over the class
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)
can be lower bounded by

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\left\{1-\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)\right\}\left\{\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)-\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)\right\}^{2},\end{split}

(C.19)

provided that
\Gamma_{i}(zs)\in\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\left(\Gamma^{*}\right)
for
z\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}
.

We first derive an upper bound on the total variation distance
\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)
. The Pinsker-Csiszár-Kullback inequality implies that

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)}.\end{split}

(C.20)

The
KL
-divergence
\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)
can be computed as

\begin{split}&\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)\\
=\ &\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\log\frac{p_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)}{p_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\left(\mathbf{O}_{n}\right)}\right]\\
=\ &\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\xi^{*}\left(X_{i}\right)\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)\gamma_{i}(s)\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\xi^{*}\left(X_{i}\right)\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)\gamma_{i}(-s)\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\right]\\
=\ &\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\log\frac{\gamma_{i}(s)\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\gamma_{i}(-s)\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\right].\end{split}

(C.21)

Note that

\begin{split}\log\frac{\gamma_{i}(s)\left(\left.y\right|x,a\right)}{\gamma_{i}(-s)\left(\left.y\right|x,a\right)}=\ &-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}(x,a)}\left[\left\{y-\mu_{i}(s)(x,a)\right\}^{2}-\left\{y-\mu_{i}(-s)(x,a)\right\}^{2}\right]\\
=\ &\frac{sg(x,a)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}(x,a)}\left\{2y-\mu_{i}(s)(x,a)-\mu_{i}(-s)(x,a)\right\}.\end{split}

(C.22)

By utilizing the fact (
C.22
), one can obtain from the equation (
C.21
) that

\displaystyle\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\left.\frac{sg\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\left\{2Y_{i}-\mu_{i}(s)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)-\mu_{i}(-s)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}\right|\left(X_{i},A_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i-1}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\frac{sg\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\left\{\mu_{i}(s)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)-\mu_{i}(-s)\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 2s^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\right]

(C.23)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 2s^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(a)}}}{{\leq}}\

\displaystyle 2K^{2}s^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 2K^{2}s^{2}n\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2},

where the step (a) follows from the assumption (
4.1
). If we put
s=\frac{1}{2K\sqrt{n}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}}
into the inequality (
C.23
), it follows that
\textsf{KL}\left(\left.\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n}\right\|\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Hence, by combining this conclusion together with the basic inequality (
C.20
), we arrive at

\begin{split}\textsf{TV}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)}^{n},\mathbb{P}_{\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)}^{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

(C.24)

At this point, we should note for every
(i,z,x,a)\in[n]\times\left\{\pm 1\right\}\times\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{A}
that

\begin{split}\left|\mu^{*}(x,a)-\mu_{i}(sz)(x,a)\right|=\ &\frac{s\left|g(x,a)\right|\sigma^{2}(x,a)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}(x,a)}\\
=\ &\frac{1}{2\sqrt{K}}\cdot\frac{\left|g(x,a)\right|\sigma^{2}(x,a)}{\sqrt{n}\overline{\pi}_{i}(x,a)\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(b)}}}{{\leq}}\ &\frac{\delta(x,a)}{2\sqrt{K}}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(c)}}}{{\leq}}\ &\delta(x,a),\end{split}

(C.25)

where the step (b) holds due to Assumption
4
, and the step (c) utilizes the fact that
K\geq 1
, which establishes that
\Gamma_{i}(zs)\in\mathcal{N}_{\delta}\left(\Gamma^{*}\right)
for
z\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}
and thus the local minimax lower bound (
C.19
) turns out to be valid.

Next, it’s time to aim at establishing a lower bound on the gap between the functional values
\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)
and
\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)
. One can observe that

\begin{split}\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(s)\right)-\tau\left(\Xi^{*},\Gamma_{i}(-s)\right)=\ &\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Xi^{*}}\left[\left\langle g(X,\cdot),\mu_{i}(s)(X,\cdot)-\mu_{i}(-s)(X,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}}\right]\\
=\ &2s\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{A}}\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},a\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},a\right)}{\overline{\pi}_{i}\left(X_{i},a\right)}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(a)\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(d)}}}{{\geq}}\ &\frac{2s}{K}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{A}}\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},a\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},a\right)}{\pi_{i}^{*}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};a\right)}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{\mathbb{A}}(a)\right]\\
=\ &\frac{2s}{K}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]\\
=\ &\frac{1}{K^{2}\sqrt{n}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right],\end{split}

(C.26)

where the step (d) holds by the assumption (
4.1
). Taking three pieces (
C.19
), (
C.24
), and (
C.26
) collectively, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{8K^{4}n\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]\right)^{2}\end{split}

(C.27)

for all
i\in[n]
. Hence, one can conclude by taking an average of the local minimax lower bound (
C.27
) over
i\in[n]
that

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)=\ &\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{n}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\delta}\left(\mathcal{I}^{*}\right)\right)\\
\geq\ &\frac{1}{8K^{4}n^{2}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]\right)^{2}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{(e)}}}{{\geq}}\ &\frac{1}{8K^{4}n^{3}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{I}^{*}}\left[\frac{g^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)\sigma^{2}\left(X_{i},A_{i}\right)}{\left(\pi_{i}^{*}\right)^{2}\left(X_{i},\mathbf{O}_{i-1};A_{i}\right)}\right]\right)^{2}}_{=\ n^{2}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{4}}\\
=\ &\frac{1}{8K^{4}}\cdot\frac{\left\|\sigma\right\|_{(n)}^{2}}{n},\end{split}

(C.28)

where the step (e) makes use of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
