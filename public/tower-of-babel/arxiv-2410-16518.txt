Abstract
The applications of the partial fraction decomposition in control and systems engineering are several. In this letter, we propose a new interpretation of residues in the partial fraction decomposition, which is employed for the following purposes: to address the pole sensitivity problem, namely to study the speed of variation of the system poles when the control parameter changes and when the system is subject to parameters variations, as well as to propose a new algorithm for the construction of the root locus. The new algorithm is proven to be more efficient in terms of execution time than the dedicated MATLAB function, while providing the same output results.

I
Introduction

The partial fraction decomposition technique is a powerful calculus tool
in the field of control and systems engineering, which allows to break down a rational function into a sum of simpler fractions. This method is particularly useful to integrate rational functions and to solve differential equations, and
finds many different applications. As an example, it is employed in
[
1
]
to decompose the transfer function of infinite impulse response filters into the sum of fractions, which is ultimately used to perform the optimal design of the filters. In
[
2
]
, the partial fraction decomposition concept is used as a basis for an adaptive Antoulas-Anderson algorithm, which allows to derive an approximation of the transfer function starting from the data acquisitions of the frequency response.
Other uses of the partial fraction expansion include algorithms to compute the storage function in lossless systems
[
3
]
, and the calculation of the Padè approximation of transfer functions via a Lanczos process
[
4
]
.

Many different methods have been developed over the years for the computation of the residues
[
5
]
, offering different pros and cons with respect to each other. Out of these methods, the so-called cover-up method is the one which is typically employed in control theory
[
6
]
.

However, the actual meaning of residues has not been fully investigated in the literature yet. In this letter, we investigate the meaning of residues in detail, finding a remarkable result: given an open loop system described by the transfer function
G(s)
, from which a linearly controlled feedback system can be constructed to obtain the closed-loop transfer function
G_{0}(s)
as illustrated in Fig.
1
, the residues of the partial fraction decomposition of
G_{0}(s)
taken with opposite signs are exactly the speed of variation of the poles of the closed-loop system as a function of the control parameter
K
.
This result introduces different potential applications, out of which we propose three in this letter: 1) The pole sensitivity analysis of the feedback system to variations of the control parameter
K
, that is the study of how fast the system poles move in the complex plane as a function of the control parameter
K
, which represents an extension of the root locus method
[
7
,
8
]
. The root locus method is known to be widely employed both in classical automatic control as well as in more advanced control theory applications, as in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
where it is applied to multi-agent systems. Its applications also extend to other engineering fields, such as the use of the root locus for the analysis and control design of power systems
[
11
]
, and the optimal design of delta–sigma modulators
[
12
]
, for instance.

[formula]

Fig. 1:
Linearly controlled feedback system.

However, the branches of the root locus simply give information about the location of the poles as a function of the control parameter
K
, without giving any specific information about the speed of variation of the poles themselves. In order to derive the latter piece of information, the derivative of the root locus branches should be computed, which can lead to inaccuracy since the derivative is computed numerically. The analysis proposed in this letter allows to determine a very simple analytical and exact way of computing the speed of variation of the poles in the complex plane, which coincide with the residues of the partial fraction decomposition of the closed-loop transfer function taken with opposite signs.
2) Based on the performed analysis, we propose a new algorithm for the derivation of the root locus. The derivation of the root locus is normally performed using the tools available from computer aided control system design software, such as the
rlocus
MATLAB function for example
[
13
]
.
The algorithm proposed in this letter is proven to work more efficiently than the
rlocus
MATLAB function in terms of execution time, while providing the same output results.
3) The pole sensitivity analysis of the system when subject to parameters variations. The three previous applications have then been applied to different dynamical system case studies.

The remainder of this letter is structured as follows. The definition of pole sensitivity is given in Sec.
II
, and investigated in Sec.
III
for a feedback system. The new algorithm for the root locus calculation is proposed in Sec.
IV
, while Sec.
V
addresses the pole sensitivity analysis of a system subject to parameters variations. The conclusions are given in Sec.
VI
.

II
Poles Sensitivity of a Dynamical System

Definition 1

We define the term pole sensitivity of a dynamical system as the study of how fast
the poles of a dynamical system move in the complex plane as the system’s parameters change.

In this letter, the following types of
pole sensitivity
will be considered:
1) pole sensitivity of a linearly controlled feedback system.
2) pole sensitivity of a dynamical system to its own parameters.

III
Pole sensitivity of a linearly controlled feedback system

Reference is made to the feedback system of Fig.
1
, where
K
is a proportional controller and
G(s)
is the rational transfer function of the considered dynamical system:

G(s)=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)}=\frac{N(s)}{(s-p_{1})\,(s-p_{2})\,\ldots\,(s-p_{n})},

(1)

where
p_{j}
are the poles of function
G(s)
, for
j\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n\}
. In the case of simple poles, the function
G(s)
can always be decomposed into partial fractions as follows:

G(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{k}_{j}}{s-p_{j}},

where
\bar{k}_{j}
are the residues of function
G(s)
at the poles
s=p_{j}
:

\bar{k}_{j}=\frac{N(p_{j})}{\prod_{h=1,h\not=j}^{n}(p_{j}-p_{h})}.

(2)

Note: the residues
\bar{k}_{j}
are real numbers if pole
p_{j}
is real, and are complex numbers if pole
p_{j}
is complex. It is a well known result
[
6
]
that the poles of the feedback system in Fig.
1
are the poles of the following closed-loop transfer function
G_{0}(s)
:

G_{0}(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{R(s)}=\frac{N(s)}{D(s)+K\,N(s)}=\frac{N(s)}{\Delta(s,K)},

(3)

namely the roots of the following characteristic equation:

\Delta(s,K)=D(s)+KN(s)=0,

(4)

where
N(s)
and
D(s)
are the numerator and the denominator of function
G(s)
in Fig.
1
.

III-A

Root locus and pole sensitivity to parameter
K
.

The movement of the poles of the feedback system
G_{0}(s)
in the complex plane when the parameter
K
ranges from
0
to
\infty
(or from
0
to
-\infty
)
is well described by the root locus. However, the root locus
does not clearly solve the pole sensitivity problem in Definition
1
, namely it does not
show the velocity
\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}
of the poles
p_{j}
with respect to
K
, that is how fast the poles
p_{j}
move in the complex plane when the control parameter changes from
K
to
K+dK
, where
dK
is an infinitesimal increment of
K
. The pole sensitivity problem can be addressed using the following theorem:

Theorem 1

(Simple poles) The velocity
\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}
in the complex plane of the poles
p_{j}
of the feedback system shown in Fig.
1
, in the case of simple poles
p_{j}
and when the parameter
K
changes from
K
to
K+dK
, is the following:

\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}=-\bar{k}_{j},\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{ for }j\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n\},

(5)

where
\bar{k}_{j}
defined in (
2
) are the residues associated with the poles
p_{j}
of the function
G_{0}(s)
defined in (
3
).

Proof.
Using (
1
) and replacing
K
with
K+dK
, the characteristic equation (
4
) can be rewritten as follows:

\Delta(s,K)+dK\,N(s)=0,

(6)

where
\Delta(s,K)
is the characteristic equation of the feedback system
G_{0}(s)
.
When
dK=0
, the roots
p_{h}
of the characteristic equation (
6
) are equal to the poles
p_{j}
of the feedback system in Fig.
1
for the given parameter
K
. When
dK\neq 0
, the new roots
\bar{p}_{h}
of the characteristic equation (
6
) can be expressed as
\bar{p}_{h}=p_{h}+dp_{h}
, where
dp_{h}
is the infinitesimal distance of the new root
\bar{p}_{h}
from the previous root
p_{h}
. Therefore, when
dK\not=0
, the characteristic equation (
6
) can be expressed as follows:

\Delta(s,K)+dK\,N(s)=\prod_{h=1}^{n}(s-p_{h}-dp_{h})=0.

(7)

The relation (
7
) holds
\forall s\in\mathbb{C}
.
Substituting
s=p_{j}
in (
7
), for
j\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n\}
,
yields:

\underbrace{\Delta(p_{j},K)}_{0}+dKN(p_{j}\!)\!=\!-dp_{j}\!\!\prod_{h=1,h\not=j}^{n}\!\!(p_{j}-p_{h}-dp_{h}),

(8)

where
\Delta(p_{j},K)=0

because
p_{j}
is a pole of the function
G_{0}(s)
.
Letting the infinitesimal quantities
dp_{h}
,
dp_{j}
and
dK
tend to zero, from (
8
) and using the definition of residues
\bar{k}_{j}
given in (
2
), it follows that:

\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}=-\frac{N(p_{j})}{\prod_{h=1,h\not=j}^{n}(p_{j}-p_{h})}=-\bar{k}_{j},

which is the relation (
5
) given in Throrem
1
. Relation (
5
) can be interpreted as follows: the velocities
\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}
in the complex plane of the roots
p_{j}
of the characteristic equation (
4
) when the parameter
K
changes from
K
to
K+dK
are given by the residues
\bar{k}_{j}
of the function
G_{0}(s)
at the points
s=p_{j}
taken with opposite signs.

\hfill\Box

Property 1

(Multiple poles) The modulus of the velocity
\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}

of the poles
p_{j}
of the feedback system
G_{0}(s)
in (
3
) in correspondence of poles
p_{j}
with multiplicity
r_{j}(p_{j})>1

is infinite:

\left|\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}\right|=\infty\hskip 34.1433pt\mbox{ if }\hskip 5.69054ptr_{j}(p_{j})>1.

(9)

Proof.
Relation (
7
) holds true for poles
p_{j}
with multiplicity
r_{j}(p_{j})>1
as well. Substituting
s=p_{j}
in (
7
), where
p_{j}
is a pole of function
G_{0}(s)
with multiplicity
r_{j}(p_{j})>1
, and recalling that
D(p_{j}\!)\!+\!KN(p_{j}\!)=0
from (
4
), yields:

dKN(p_{j}\!)=(-1)^{r_{j}}\!\!\!\!\prod_{h=1,p_{h}=p_{j}}^{n}\!\!\!\!dp_{h}\prod_{h=1,p_{h}\not=p_{j}}^{n}\!\!\!\!(p_{j}-p_{h}-dp_{h}).

(10)

In the vicinity of a multiple pole
p_{j}
, the differentials
dp_{h}
are a balanced set of vectors, with the same modulus
|dp_{j}|
, satisfying the following relation:

\prod_{h=1,p_{h}=p_{j}}^{n}\!\!\!\!dp_{h}=(-1)^{r_{j}-1}|dp_{j}|^{r_{j}}.

(11)

Substituting (
11
) in (
10
) and letting the infinitesimal quantities
dp_{h}
,
dp_{j}
and
dK
tend to zero, yields:

\frac{|dp_{j}|^{r_{j}}}{dK}=-\frac{N(p_{j})}{\prod_{h=1,p_{h}\not=p_{j}}^{n}\!(p_{j}-p_{h})}=-\bar{k}_{j,r_{j}},

(12)

where
\bar{k}_{j,r_{j}}
is a constant defined as follows:

\!\!\bar{k}_{j,r_{j}}\!=\!\left.G_{0}(s)(s-p_{j})^{r_{j}}\right|_{s=p_{j}}\!=\!\frac{N(p_{j})}{\prod_{h=1,p_{h}\not=p_{j}}^{n}\!(p_{j}-p_{h})}.

(13)

Dividing both sides of (
12
) by
|dK|^{r_{j}-1}
and taking the modulus yields:

\left|\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}\right|=\left(\frac{|\bar{k}_{j,r_{j}}|}{|dK|^{r_{j}-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r_{j}}}.

(14)

Relation (
13
) shows that, when
dK\rightarrow 0
, the modulus of the velocity
\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}
tends to infinity if the multiplicity
r_{j}(p_{j})
of pole
p_{j}
is grater that 1, as stated in (
9
). Relation (
14
) can be rewritten in logarithmic scales as follows:

\left|\frac{dp_{j}}{dK}\right|_{\log_{10}}=\frac{|\bar{k}_{j,r_{j}}|_{\log_{10}}}{r_{j}}-\frac{(r_{j}-1)}{r_{j}}|dK|_{\log_{10}},

resulting in a straight line in the logarithmic plane with a slope
t=-\frac{(r_{j}-1)}{r_{j}}
for increasing values of
|dK|_{\log_{10}}
.

\hfill\Box

IV
Algorithm for The Root locus calculation

The poles of the feedback system
G_{0}(s)
in (
3
) are function of the parameter
K
:
p_{j}=p_{j}(K)
.
Let
{\bf p}(K)
denote the vector of all the poles of function
G_{0}(s)
:

{\bf p}(K)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}p_{1}(K)&p_{2}(K)&\ldots&p_{n}(K)\end{array}\right]^{\mbox{\tiny T}}.

The root locus of the feedback system
G_{0}(s)
is the plotting of all the components
p_{j}(K)
of vector
{\bf p}(K)
in the complex plane when parameter
K
ranges from 0 to
\infty
. The typical approach consists in computing the components
p_{j}(K)
as the roots of the characteristic equation
\Delta(s,K)=0
in (
4
). We propose the alternative and more efficient method
described in the following algorithm.

Algorithm 1

(Root locus construction) Let
\bar{K}=(K_{a}:\delta k:K_{b})
be the vector of the desired values of parameter
K
constructed with precision
\delta k
.
Let
k_{i}\in\bar{K}
denote the
i
-th value of vector
\bar{K}
, for
i=\{1,\;2,\;\ldots,\;N\}
. A good estimation
\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})
of the components
p_{j}(k_{i})
of vector
{\bf p}(k_{i})
, for
k_{i}\in\bar{K}
,
j\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n\}
and
i=\{1,\;2,\;\ldots,\;N-1\}
, can be computed by using the following difference equations:

\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i+1})=\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})-\left.\frac{N(s)\,\delta k+\Delta(s,k_{i})}{\prod_{h=1,h\not=j}^{n}(s-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i}))}\right|_{s=\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})}

(16)

where
N(s)
is the numerator of function
G(s)
and
\Delta(s,k_{i})
is the characteristic equation of function
G_{0}(s)
in (
4
) for
K=k_{i}
. The initial conditions
\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{1})
of the difference equations (
16
) are the poles
p_{j}(k_{1})
of the feedback function
G_{0}(s)
when
K=k_{1}
:
\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{1})=p_{j}(k_{1})
. Note:
the proposed difference equations (
16
) is to be used for values
k_{i}
corresponding to simple poles
p_{j}(k_{i})
of the feedback function
G_{0}(s)
, and can be used to construct the branches of the root locus of the feedback system
G_{0}(s)
in the regions outside of the branch points.

Root loci of the feedback system associated with
G(s)
in (

) when
K
ranges from

to
10
computed using Algorithm
1
with and without the stabilizing term
\Delta(s,k_{i})
in (
16
).

Proof
.
With reference to Eq. (
7
), substituting
K
with
k_{i}
,
p_{h}
with
\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})
and substituting the infinitesimal quantities
dK
and
dp_{h}
with the small quantities
\delta k
and
\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})
, yields:

\Delta(s,k_{i})+\delta kN(s)=\prod_{h=1}^{n}(s-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})-\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i}))=0,

(17)

where
\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})
is the small distance between the pole
p_{h}(k_{i+1})
and the estimated pole
\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})
due to the small variation
\delta k
of parameter
K
:

\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})=p_{h}(k_{i+1})-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i}).

(18)

Relation (
17
) holds
\forall s\in\mathbb{C}
.
Substituting
s=\bar{s}=\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})
in (
17
) yields:

\Delta(\bar{s},k_{i})+\delta kN(\bar{s})\!=\!-\delta\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})\!\!\!\prod_{h=1,i\not=j}^{n}\!\!\!(\bar{s}-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})-\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i}))\!=\!0.

From
the latter and from (
18
), when
h=j
, it follows that:

p_{j}(k_{i+1})=\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})-\frac{N(\bar{s})\delta k+\Delta(\bar{s},k_{i})}{\prod_{h=1,h\not=j}^{n}(\bar{s}-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})-\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i}))}.

(19)

A good estimation
\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i+1})
of the pole
p_{j}(k_{i+1})
when
K=k_{i+1}
can be obtained from (
19
) by neglecting, for
h\not=j
, the small quantities
\delta\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})
with respect to the values
\bar{s}-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i})
:

\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i+1})=\tilde{p}_{j}(k_{i})-\frac{N(\bar{s})\delta k+\Delta(\bar{s},k_{i})}{\prod_{h=1,h\not=j}^{n}(\bar{s}-\tilde{p}_{h}(k_{i}))},

which coincides with the difference equation (
16
).

Note: an important stabilizing term of Eq. (

V
Pole sensitivity of a dynamical system to its own parameters

Let
{\cal S}({\bf h})
be a linear dynamical physical system, and let
{\bf h}
denote the vector of all its parameters
h_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,p\}
:

{\bf h}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}h_{1}&h_{1}&\ldots&h_{p}\end{array}\right]^{\mbox{\tiny T}}.

The parameters
h_{i}
of a physical system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
can be distinguished in the following three different types
[
15
,
16
]
:

1) The
dynamic parameters

are those that can only
store
energy (example: capacitor);

2) The
static parameters

are those that can only
dissipate
energy (example: resistance);

3) The
connection parameters

are those that can only
convert
energy (example: the torque constant of a DC motor).

The pole sensitivity of the dynamical system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
to its own parameters can be performed if the characteristic polynomial
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
of the system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
is known, which can be obtained in many different ways. The two easiest ways to obtain the polynomial
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
are the following:

1)
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
is the denominator of any transfer function
G_{ij}(s,{\bf h})
calculated between two generic input-output points
(i,\,j)
of the considered system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
:

G_{ij}(s,{\bf h})=\frac{Y_{j}(s)}{X_{i}(s)}=\frac{N_{ij}(s,{\bf h})}{\Delta(s,{\bf h})},

where
X_{i}(s)
and
Y_{j}(s)
are the Laplace transform of the
i
-th input signal
x_{i}(t)
and of the
j
-th output signal
y_{j}(t)
of the considered system, and the polynomial
N_{ij}(s,{\bf h})
is the numerator of the transfer function
G_{ij}(s,{\bf h})
.

2) If the block scheme of the considered system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
is available, then the characteristic polynomial
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
is the numerator of the Mason determinant
M(s)
of the block scheme:

M(s,{\bf h})=\frac{\Delta(s,{\bf h})}{D_{M}(s,{\bf h})},

where the polynomial
D_{M}(s,{\bf h})
is the denominator of the
transfer function
M(s,{\bf h})
.

Property 2

(Characteristic polynomial) All the physical parameters
h_{i}
of system

{\cal S}({\bf h})
appear within the characteristic polynomial
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
in the following way:
a) all the dynamic and static parameters
h_{i}
of system
{\cal S}
appear linearly within the polynomial
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
;
b) the squared forms
h_{i}^{2}
of all the connection parameters of system
{\cal S}
appear linearly within the polynomial
\Delta(s,{\bf h})
.

Proof.
This Property can be proven by referring to the following basic properties of the main power-oriented modeling techniques known in the literature, namely Power-Oriented Graphs (POG)
[
15
,
16
]
, Bond Graphs (BG)
[
17
]
and Energetic Macroscopic Representation (EMR)
[
18
]
: 1) Any physical system can always be obtained by connecting in series or in parallel the physical elements composing it; 2) When a new physical element is connected to a linear system, a new feedback loop appears within the mathematical model of the system; 3) If the new connected element is of
dynamic
or
static
type, then the corresponding parameter
\bar{h}_{i}
appears linearly both in the new feedback loop and in the characteristic polynomial
\Delta(s)
of the new system; 4) On the contrary, if the new inserted element is of
connection
type,
then the corresponding parameter
\bar{h}_{i}
appears twice in the mathematical model of the considered system, because an energy conversion takes place.
This
induces the square
\bar{h}_{i}^{2}
of the physical parameter
\bar{h}_{i}
to appear linearly within the characteristic polynomial
\Delta(s)
of the considered system.

\hfill\Box

When the
i
-th parameter
h_{i}
of vector
{\bf h}
changes from
h_{i}
to
h_{i}+dh_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,p\}
, the characteristic equation
\Delta(s,h_{i})=0
of system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
can always be written using the following truncated Taylor expansion:

\Delta(s,h_{i}+dh_{i})\simeq\Delta(s,h_{i})+dh_{i}\bar{N}_{i}(s,h_{i})=0,

(21)

where polynomial
\bar{N}_{i}(s,h_{i})
can be computed as follows:

\bar{N}_{i}(s,h_{i})=\frac{\partial\Delta(s,h_{i})}{\partial h_{i}}.

(22)

Theorem 2

(Pole sensitivity to system’s parameters) The velocity
\frac{dp_{j}}{dh_{i}}
in the complex plane of the poles
p_{j}
of the system
{\cal S}({\bf h})
,
when the parameter
h_{i}
changes from
h_{i}
to
h_{i}+dh_{i}
, is the following:

\frac{dp_{j}}{dh_{i}}=-\tilde{k}_{ji},\hskip 17.07164pt\mbox{ for }j\in\{1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n\},

(23)

where
\tilde{k}_{ji}
are the residues computed as in (
2
) of the following transfer functions
G_{i}(s)
:

G_{i}(s,h_{i})=\frac{\bar{N}_{i}(s,h_{i})}{\Delta(s,h_{i})},

(24)

where
\bar{N}_{i}(s,h_{i})
is the polynomial defined in (
22
).

Proof.

The relations (
23
) and (
24
) can be derived by applying Theorem
1
to the characteristic equation (
21
).

\hfill\Box

VI
Conclusion

This letter has provided a new interpretation of residues in the partial fraction decomposition, which has been employed as a basis for different applications. The first one is the study of how the speed of the poles of a feedback system change in the complex plane as a function of the control parameter, as well as the study of how the speed of the poles change when the system is subject to parameters variations. The second application is the proposal of a new algorithm for the construction of the root locus, which is proven to be more efficient in terms of execution time than the dedicated MATLAB function, while providing the same output results.
