Design, Synthesis, and Dft Analysis of Benzaldehyde-Based Ethynyl-Functionalized Donor–Acceptor Dicyanomethyl Derivatives
Authors: Hatice Calis Karatekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herein, 2-(p-tolylethynyl)­benzaldehyde­(3A), 2-((4-(dimethylamino)­phenyl)­ethynyl)­benzaldeh yde (3B), 2-((4-methoxyphenyl)­ethynyl)­benzaldehyde (3C), 2-(2-(p-tolylethynyl)­benzylidene) malononitrile (4A), 2-(2-((4-(dimethylamino)­phenyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile (4B) and 2-(2-((4-methoxyphenyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile (4C) were obtained in yields of 95%, 93%, 90%, 85%, 88% and 85%, respectively. These novel benzaldehyde-based, ethynyl-functionalized donor–acceptor dicyanomethyl derivatives (BEDA-DCMs) (4A, 4B, and 4C) were synthesized via Sonogashira coupling and Knoevenagel condensation reactions. The structural features of the synthesized compounds were characterized using melting point determination, elemental analysis, FT-IR and NMR spectroscopy. Their electronic absorption properties were investigated using UV–vis spectroscopy, while their electrochemical behavior was examined by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The electronic structures and reactivity of the compounds were evaluated using density functional theory (DFT)-based HOMO–LUMO, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP), electron localization function (ELF), localized orbital locator (LOL), and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses. The results show that the electronic properties of the benzaldehyde-based derivatives can be effectively tuned by introducing strong electron-donating substituents with ethynyl functionality into the molecular framework.In this context, compound 4B exhibits the lowest HOMO/LUMO energy gap (ΔE = 5.9200 eV) and the lowest hardness value (η = 2.9600 eV) among the derivatives. Therefore, 4B stands out as the most promising candidate in terms of targeted functional performance, particularly in applications where efficient charge transfer and electronic responsiveness are critical, such as catalytic processes and related applications.

Introduction

Organic materials have gained much attention in the past few years due to their potential applications in the field of photonics, electro-optics, color display devices, optical switching, optoelectronics, second-harmonic generation, etc. , In this regard, benzaldehyde is a precursor of great importance used in the pharmaceutical, dye, perfumery, and resin industries. It is mainly used to manufacture lauryl aldehyde, benzyl alcohol, lauric acid, plasticizers, and low-temperature lubricants. − Most reactions involving benzaldehyde occur in a solvent system. Solvent effects are classified into specific and nonspecific interactions. Solvent effects have a direct impact on the chemical equilibrium, reaction rates, and reactivity of the compound. Due to the direct presence of the carbonyl group conjugated with the benzene ring, the carbonyl group of the benzaldehyde molecule shows a high level of sensitivity to changes in the environment. − This structural feature helps to modify the electronic properties of the benzaldehyde derivatives (BzHDs), which are of great importance in the field of pharmaceutical, , agrochemical and perfumery industries.

The halogenated benzaldehydes represent a significant class of benzaldehyde derivatives. The halogenated benzaldehydes comprise those benzaldehydes where one or more of the hydrogen atoms of the benzene ring are replaced by halogen atoms like fluorine, chlorine, bromine, and iodine. The replacement has a significant effect on the physicochemical characteristics of the resultant compounds. The compounds display varying ranges of reactivity attributable to their composition. − The reactivity has ensured that the compounds are exploited for use in widely employed in the synthesis of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), , pesticide production, , and the dye and pigment industries. The reactivity of the halogenated benzaldehydes has also offered an opportunity for their use in different capacities to ensure the prevention of mild steel from corroding when exposed to hydrochloric acid. The reactivity of the halogenated benzaldehydes has also made them applicable for forming inclusion complexes with cyclodextrins. , However, a major challenge has ensured that we do not fully use these compounds to their capability: a continued lack of critical physicochemical information for these compounds. On the other hand, ortho-substituted benzaldehyde derivatives display significantly different chemical properties, depending on whether or not the substituents are electron donors or acceptors. The substituents play a significant role in biological activity. The substitution of a hydrogen atom on the benzene ring with an electron-donating group increases the electron density on the benzene ring, while the substitution with an electron-withdrawing group decreases its density. This differentiation is based on the overall effect of induction, conjugation, and hyperconjugation. − Apart from biological activity, benzaldehyde and benzaldehyde-based heterocyclic compounds have also found significant applications in electrochromic devices − and energy storage technologies, , such as solar cells, fuel cells, and batteries, − owing to their cost-effectiveness, processability, structural flexibility, broad absorption bands, and superior charge carrier properties. Considering that there is a progressive depletion of metallic materials and that environmental issues are becoming more alarming, benzaldehyde-based heterocyclic organic systems have found significant applications as sustainable and green technologies. , Among these benzaldehyde-based heterocyclic organic systems, have found significant applications, owing to their reusability and their potential to reduce hazardous waste materials. In recent years, metal-free donor–acceptor (D–A)- type benzaldehyde derivatives , have attracted considerable interest due to their potential application in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs), , organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) and organic field-effect transistors (OFETs). The covalent attachment of an electron donor to an electron acceptor in these molecules facilitates a strong intramolecular charge transfer mechanism. This property allows these molecules to be fine-tuned to explore their potential applications. Donor–π–acceptor (D–π–A) fluorophores and systems containing electron-withdrawing groups such as dicyano and benzonitrile have been extensively studied because of their broad tunable emission range, high quantum yields, and good stability. Various D–π–A fluorophores exhibit tunable emission from the UV–vis to the near-infrared (NIR) region, large Stokes shifts, and quantum yields of up to 0.80 (in some pyrimidine/thienyl systems); examples with quantum yields of around 40% even in the solid state have also been reported. , With AIE and dual-state emission designs, the problem of aggregation-caused quenching has been overcome, and some asymmetric AIE/DF compounds have been reported to exhibit solid-state photoluminescence quantum yields of up to 93% together with delayed fluorescence. , Dicyano-containing D–π–A structures have achieved external quantum efficiencies exceeding 29%, rapid reverse intersystem crossing, and low efficiency roll-off in OLEDs, thereby providing long lifetimes and practical device performance. , In addition, numerous biosensors and aptasensors based on AIE and D–A systems have been reported, exhibiting high photostability, high quantum yields, rapid response, and practical application potential. − Accordingly, despite many reports on the synthesis and general properties of benzaldehyde derivatives, few reports are available on the structural, photophysical, electrochemical, and theoretical properties of donor–acceptor type benzaldehyde derivatives functionalized by ethynyl linkers and incorporating a dicyanomethyl acceptor unit. The novelty of this study lies in the synthesis of ethynyl-functionalized benzaldehyde derivatives, preparation of final products incorporating a dicyanomethyl unit, and investigation of structural, photophysical, electrochemical, and theoretical properties such as HOMO–LUMO levels and band gaps of these newly designed donor–acceptor type benzaldehyde derivatives.

In the present study, 2-(2-(p-tolylethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile (4A), 2-(2-((4-(dimethyl amino)­phenyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile (4B), and 2-(2-((4-methoxyphenyl)­ethynyl) benzylidene)­malononitrile (4C) derivatives were investigated as a well-controlled donor–acceptor series. These novel benzaldehyde-based ethynyl-functionalized donor–acceptor dicyanomethyl derivatives (BEDA-DCMs; 4A, 4B, and 4C) were synthesized via Sonogashira coupling and Knoevenagel condensation reactions. The structural features of the synthesized compounds were characterized by melting point determination, elemental analysis, FT-IR and NMR spectroscopy, while their electronic absorption properties were investigated using UV–vis spectroscopy. In addition, the electrochemical behavior of the synthesized compounds was investigated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). To gain deeper insight into their electronic structures and reactivity, the experimental findings were further supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Furthermore, HOMO–LUMO energy levels, global reactivity parameters, NBO charge-transfer interactions, and ELF/LOL analyses were jointly examined to elucidate the effects of ortho-substituted donor groups and the ethynyl bridge on the molecular electronic structure.

Electrochemical Tests

Electrochemical tests were conducted using CV and EIS techniques to evaluate the electrooxidation performance of catalysts (4A–4C) in the presence of hydrazine. The measurements were carried out in 1 M NaOH and 1 M NaOH +0.02 M N2H4 solution mixtures. The choice of 0.02 M concentration for hydrazine was based on literature precedents for hydrazine electrooxidation studies in strongly alkaline media. In fact, 1 M NaOH or 1 M KOH with 0.02 M N2H4 has been widely used for obtaining a clean oxidation response and for comparison among catalysts. , Here, no separate concentration optimization was performed, and the same electrolyte composition was used for all catalysts to ensure a consistent comparison of the relative electrocatalytic activities of 4A–4C. The electrochemical data were obtained using a Gamry Interface 1010E potentiostat in a three-electrode system. In this system, a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) with a surface area of 0.07065 cm2 was used as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode.To prepare the catalyst suspension, 3 mg of (4A–4C), 270 μL of isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and 20 μL of Nafion 117 were mixed. Then, 5 μL of the obtained solution was drop-cast onto the surface of the working electrode (GCE), thereby modifying the electrode.

Computational Studies

Density Functional Theory Calculations

DFT calculations were performed to elucidate the electronic structures and reactivity parameters of the (3A, 3B, 3C) and (4A, 4B, 4C) derivatives. All calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16W software package, and the optimized geometries were visualized with GaussView 6.0.16. Geometry optimizations were conducted at the B3LYP/6-311G (d,p) level, incorporating polarization functions to achieve a more accurate description of the electron density. Frequency analyses confirmed the absence of imaginary frequencies, demonstrating that the optimized structures correspond to true minima on the potential energy surface. Frontier molecular orbital analysis provided the energies of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). From these, the HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE = E LUMO – E HOMO), chemical potential (μ = [E HOMO + E LUMO]/2), chemical hardness (η = ΔE/2), and global electrophilicity index (ω = μ2/2η) were derived.These quantum chemical descriptors were used to evaluate the electronic stability, charge-transfer tendencies, and potential reactivity of the catalysts. In addition, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps were generated to visualize the spatial distribution of electron density and to identify electrophilic and nucleophilic regions within the molecules.

Topological Analysis

After converting these chk files into fch format by using Gaussian 16W, the electron localization function (ELF) and localized orbital locator (LOL) analyses were performed for the BEDA-DCMs (3A, 3B, 3C, 4A, 4B, and 4C) by using Multiwfn version 3.8.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Structural Characterization

The synthesis of novel 2-(2-((aryl/alkyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile derivatives (4A–4C) containing an α,β-unsaturated dicyano moiety was accomplished through a two-step reaction sequence, as illustrated in Scheme , involving Sonogashira cross-coupling followed by Knoevenagel condensation. In the first step, the reaction was initiated by a Sonogashira cross-coupling between 2-iodobenzaldehyde (1) and the corresponding alkyne derivatives (2A, 2B, 2C) affording the desired alkyne-functionalized intermediates in high yields. The Sonogashira coupling is a well-established palladium-catalyzed method for the formation of new carbon–carbon bonds between terminal alkynes and halogenated aromatic compounds. In this study, PdCl2 (PPh3)2 was employed as the palladium catalyst, while CuI served as the cocatalyst. It is well-known that a base is required in Sonogashira reactions to abstract the acidic proton of terminal alkynes; therefore, triethylamine (Et3N) was selected to act both as a base and solvent. Under these conditions, the palladium-catalyzed Sonogashira coupling of 2-iodobenzaldehyde (1) with terminal alkynes 2A, 2B, and 2C afforded the corresponding intermediates 3A, 3B, and 3C in yields of 95%, 93% and 90%, respectively. The synthesis of compounds 3A–3C via the Sonogashira reaction is summarized in Scheme . This step successfully introduced the ethynyl functionality required for the subsequent Knoevenagel condensation, which constitutes the final step in the synthesis of the target compounds. In the second step, 2-(aryl/alkyl) ethynylbenzaldehyde derivatives (3A–3C) were reacted with malononitrile in the presence of a weak base to afford the desired α, β-unsaturated dicyano derivatives through a Knoevenagel condensation reaction. Knoevenagel condensation is a widely utilized method for C–C bond formation, typically involving active methylene compounds bearing electron-withdrawing groups. In this context, compounds such as malonic acid and malononitrile are frequently employed, and these reactions generally yield a single product.

Synthesis of 2-(2-((Aryl/alkyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile Derivatives 4

Accordingly, when 2-(p-tolylethynyl)­benzaldehyde (3A) was reacted with malononitrile in toluene at elevated temperature in the presence of Al2O3, 2-(2-(p-tolylethynyl)­benzylidene) malononitrile (4A) was obtained in 85% yield. Under identical reaction conditions, 2-(2-((4­(dimethylamino)­phenyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile (4B) and 2-(2-((4-methoxyphen yl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile (4C) were synthesized in 88% and 85% yields, respectively (Scheme ). This synthetic route enabled the selective and efficient preparation of the target 2-(2-((aryl/alkyl)­ethynyl)­benzylidene)­malononitrile derivatives. The structural elucidation of the synthesized compounds was carried out using NMR and FT-IR spectroscopy. To clearly demonstrate the spectral changes associated with the transformation from aldehyde precursors to α, β-unsaturated dicyano compounds, compound 4B was selected as a representative example. The FT-IR spectrum of 2-(2-((4-(dimethylamino)­phenyl)­ethynyl) benzylidene)­malononitrile (4B) (Figure S27) provides clear evidence for the successful formation of the target structure. A sharp and intense absorption band observed at 2223.0 cm–1 is attributed to the nitrile (–CN) functional group. In addition, the band appearing around 2177.8 cm–1 supports the presence of the ethynyl (–CC–) moiety.The aromatic CC stretching vibrations are represented by bands at 1608.4, 1589.8, and 1566.2 cm–1. The absorption at 3023.4 cm–1 corresponds to aromatic C–H stretching vibrations, while the band at 2911.9 cm–1 is assigned to aliphatic C–H stretching modes. Bands observed at 1464.9 and 1361.3 cm–1 are consistent with C–H bending vibrations of the N,N-dimethylamino (–N (CH3)2) group. Notably, the complete disappearance of the aldehyde carbonyl (CO) stretching band expected in the ∼1700–1720 cm–1 region, along with the absence of aldehyde C–H stretching bands typically observed between 2820 and 2730 cm–1, unequivocally confirms the full conversion of the aldehyde group during the Knoevenagel condensation. Furthermore, bands in the 1295.7–1141.1 cm–1 region are attributed to C–N and aromatic C–C stretching vibrations, while absorptions between 860.9 and 729.1 cm–1 correspond to out-of-plane C–H bending vibrations of the aromatic rings. Overall, the FT-IR spectrum clearly confirms the presence of nitrile, ethynyl, aromatic, and dimethylamino functional groups, while the disappearance of aldehyde-related bands verifies the successful synthesis of compound 4B with the proposed structure. Tructural confirmation was further supported by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy.

4BThe 1H NMR spectrum (400 MHz, CDCl3) of compound 4B clearly validates the successful formation of the target molecule. The singlet observed at δ 8.47 ppm is assigned to the olefinic CH proton of the α,β-unsaturated dicyano moiety, providing strong evidence for the occurrence of the Knoevenagel condensation. Aromatic proton signals appear as multiplets in the δ 7.67–6.66 ppm range and are consistent with the presence of two distinct aromatic rings, namely the benzylidene phenyl ring and the N,N-dimethylaniline-derived ring. In particular, the doublet signal observed at δ 6.66 ppm is attributed to aromatic protons heavily shielded by the electron-donating N,N-dimethylamino group, indicating its strong electron-pushing effect on the conjugated π-system. The singlet at δ 3.01 ppm (6H) corresponds to the two methyl groups of the N,N-dimethylamino substituent, with an integration value in excellent agreement with the proposed structure.The 13C NMR spectrum (100 MHz, CDCl3) further corroborates the structural assignment. Signals observed at δ 158.32 and 150.77 ppm are attributed to highly deshielded carbons within the conjugated system, including CC and CN-type environments. Resonances in the δ 114.03–111.70 ppm range are consistent with the nitrile carbons while the sharp peak at δ 107.85 ppm confirms the presence of the olefinic carbons of the malononitrile moiety. Additionally, signals at δ 83.94 and 82.98 ppm clearly indicate the presence of sp-hybridized alkyne carbons (–CC–), while the signal at δ 40.09 ppm corresponds to the methyl carbons of the N,N-dimethylamino group. All observed carbon resonances are in full agreement with the proposed molecular structure. Collectively, these spectroscopic findings confirm the successful transformation of the aldehyde precursor into the desired α, β-unsaturated dicyano derivative and clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of the applied synthetic strategy.

UV–Vis Absorption Spectra of Compounds 3A–3C and 4A–4C

3A–3C and 4A–4C derivatives were investigated by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy in ethanol (Figure ). The obtained spectral data show that the electronic structure of the compounds is sensitive to both substituent variation and the terminal acceptor group. In the benzaldehyde derivatives (3A–3C), which constitute the first step of the series, the main absorption bands (λmax) shifted depending on the substituents. While compound 3A (–CH3) exhibited maximum absorption at 330 nm, this value increased to 338 nm with the incorporation of a methoxy group (3C). In compound 3B, which contains theN­(CH3)2 group known for its strong mesomeric effect, an absorption maximum of 378 nm was observed. Replacing the aldehyde group (3A–3C) with the more strongly electron-withdrawing malononitrile unit (4A–4C) gives the molecules a clear donor–acceptor (“push–pull”) character. The signal observed at 376 nm for compound 4A indicates that this structural modification enhances excited-state stabilization compared to 3A. The broad and intense absorption band observed at 448 nm for compound 4B confirms that intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) reaches its maximum. In compound 4C, the dual-band structure observed around 280 nm indicates higher–energy transitions. Overall, the spectra show that the sharp bands in the 250–330 nm range correspond to π → π* transitions of the aromatic ring system, while the broad and intense bands above 350 nm reflect ICT-type transitions delocalized over the entire molecule. In conclusion, replacing the acceptor unit with malononitrile and increasing donor strength successfully shifts the optical response of the molecules from the ultraviolet region to the visible region (blue–green light absorption).

Electrochemical Measurements

The electrooxidation activities and charge transfer mechanisms of the 4A, 4B, and 4C derivatives, which were theoretically predicted by DFT analyses to have the highest electrocatalytic potential, were experimentally investigated for hydrazine oxidation. The cyclic voltammograms of compounds 4A, 4B, and 4C in 1 M NaOH and 1 M NaOH +0.02 M N2H4 are presented in Figure . According to the CV data shown in Figure , the organic catalysts 4A, 4B, and 4C exhibited current densities of 4.54, 2.45, and 1.29 mA/cm2, respectively, in 1 M NaOH. In the presence of hydrazine, the same catalysts showed current densities of 4.42, 7.69, and 5.99 mA/cm2, respectively. In the presence of hydrazine, compound 4B reached a current density of 7.69 mA/cm2 and emerged as the most active member of the series. The dimethylamino (–N­(CH3)2) group in the structure of 4B increases π-conjugation and electron density throughout the molecule due to its strong electron-donating character. This push–pull nature allows hydrazine molecules to approach and interact with the catalyst surface more readily, while also supporting a smoother electron transfer process during oxidation. In comparison, compound 4C delivers a current density of 5.99 mA/cm2, whereas compound 4A reaches only 4.42 mA/cm2, reflecting its comparatively lower activity. The weaker electron-donating capacities of the methyl (–CH3) and methoxy (–OCH3) groups compared to the dimethylamino (–N­(CH3)2) group result in lower current densities for these derivatives. The experimental results are in full agreement with the theoretical data obtained from DFT analyses. These findings confirm that the strong donor–acceptor interaction in compound 4B provides the most favorable electrocatalytic environment for hydrazine oxidation.

To further analyze the charge transfer kinetics at the electrode surface of the organic compounds (4A, 4B, 4C), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis was performed in a 1 M NaOH + 0.02 M N2H4 solution. In agreement with the literature the decrease in the diameter of the semicircles observed in Nyquist plots indicates a reduction in charge transfer resistance and, consequently, an increase in catalytic activity. As seen in Figure , the lowest charge transfer resistance for hydrazine oxidation was observed for the 4B catalyst, while the highest resistance was associated with compound 4A. In conclusion, the electrochemical activity order of the compounds (4A–4C) was determined to be 4B > 4C > 4A. The CV data and EIS analyses obtained experimentally are in full correlation with the DFT studies and clearly confirm that compound 4B is a superior organic electrocatalyst for hydrazine electrooxidation.

DFT Calculations

DFT calculations were performed to investigate the structural, electronic and reactivity properties of compounds (3A–3C) and (4A–4C). All calculations were carried out at the B3LYP/6-311­(d, p) level of theory. Figure presents the optimized geometries of compounds (3A–4C).

The Frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) depicted in Figure , together with the orbital energies reported in Table , enable a comparative evaluation of the electronic properties of the 3A–3C and 4A–4C derivatives. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE) is a fundamental descriptor reflecting a molecules resistance to electronic excitation and its tendency toward charge transfer; in general, a lower ΔE is associated with easier electron promotion, higher polarizability, and consequently a greater potential for chemical reactivity. An examination of the calculated ΔE values reveals that the 3A–3C series exhibits relatively large energy gaps in the range of 6.842–7.470 eV (3A: 7.4698 eV; 3B: 6.8420 eV; 3C: 7.2908 eV). These comparatively high ΔE values suggest that these compounds possess electronically more stable structures, in which electronic transitions between Frontier orbitals are less favorable. In contrast, the 4A–4C series displays notably lower ΔE values (4A: 6.6393 eV; 4B: 5.9200 eV; 4C: 6.4267 eV), indicating a reduced energy requirement for electronic excitation and a more favorable electron-transfer process. When evaluated in conjunction with the orbital distributions shown in Figure , the more pronounced delocalization of both HOMO and LUMO densities across the π-conjugated framework in the 4-series further supports enhanced charge transport and facilitated electronic transitions. Among all investigated compounds, compound 4B was determined to possess the lowest HOMO/LUMO energy gap (ΔE = 5.9200 eV) and the lowest hardness value (η = 2.9600 eV). These properties indicate that compound 4B is more polarizable and exhibits more effective electron distribution within the π-conjugated system. In conclusion, the collective evaluation of ΔE and other global reactivity parameters clearly demonstrates that compound 4B has a superior electronic structure compared to the other derivatives. Therefore, 4B stands out as the most promising candidate in terms of targeted functional performance, particularly in catalytic applications where efficient charge transfer and electronic responsiveness are critical. Although the differences in ΔE and chemical hardness among compounds 4A–4C appear moderate, they lead to significant variations in practical electronic behavior. In particular, the lower ΔE and η values of compound 4B indicate enhanced polarizability and more efficient intramolecular charge transfer. These effects are directly reflected in its superior electrocatalytic performance toward hydrazine oxidation, confirming that even small variations in these parameters can result in substantial differences in real-world functionality.

Molecular Electrostatic Potential Analysis

The molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps shown in Figure demonstrate the spatial distribution of electron density and the potential sites for reactivity in 3A–3C and 4A–4C. In the molecular electrostatic potential maps, the red colors indicate the electron-rich sites and negative electrostatic potential, whereas the blue colors indicate the electron-poor sites and positive electrostatic potential. , In addition to the visual interpretation, quantitative MEP parameters (V min and V max) were calculated to provide a more rigorous description of the electrostatic potential distribution (Table S9). The obtained values are summarized as follows: 3A (V min = −5.091 × 10–2 a.u., V max = 5.091 × 10–2 a.u.), 3B (−5.449 × 10–2, 5.449 × 10–2), 3C (−5.151 × 10–2, 5.151 × 10–2), 4A (−4.807 × 10–2, 4.807 × 10–2), 4B (−5.122 × 10–2, 5.122 × 10–2), and 4C (−5.013 × 10–2, 5.013 × 10–2). In 3A–3C series, the molecular electrostatic potential maps show that the negative electrostatic potential is localized mainly at the oxygen-containing functional groups and some parts of the aromatic π-system. This observation is supported by their relatively high V min values and is consistent with the higher HOMO–LUMO energy gap values (ΔE = 6.842–7.470 eV) and higher chemical hardness (η), as shown in Table . In the 4A–4C series, the electrostatic potential variations are more pronounced. The negative potential is more delocalized and extends over larger regions, particularly around the nitrile nitrogen atoms of the malononitrile unit, corresponding to the most favorable sites for electrophilic attack. The positive potential is more homogeneously distributed over different parts of the structure, especially on hydrogen atoms and electron-deficient regions. This behavior is consistent with the lower ΔE (5.920–6.639 eV) and chemical hardness (η) values obtained for the 4-series compounds, as well as their comparable V min/V max distributions. Among all the studied structures, 4B exhibits a more pronounced electrostatic potential distribution compared to 4A and 4C, as also reflected by its V min (−5.122 × 10–2 a.u.) and V max (5.122 × 10–2 a.u.) values. In the 4B structure, the negative electrostatic potential is highly localized in specific functional groups and the π-conjugated system, while the positive potential is uniformly distributed in other parts of the structure. This is fully consistent with the lowest HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE = 5.9200 eV) and the lowest hardness value (η = 2.9600 eV) reported in Table . In addition, the high global electrophilicity index value for 4B indicates a greater ability for electron acceptance, suggesting that this compound may be more effective in such applications. Thus, the MEP method not only visually identifies reactive centers but also, through quantitative parameters, supports the calculated electronic properties and confirms the enhanced reactivity of compound 4B compared to the other compounds in the study.

Electron Localization Function Analysis

The electron localization function (ELF) mapping results depicted in Figure offer detailed insight into the spatial distribution of electron pairs and bond-related properties in compounds 3A–3C and 4A–4C. ELF analysis is known as one of the most important approaches for understanding covalent bond properties, lone pair locations, and π-electron delocalization. In ELF analysis, regions with high ELF values (red and yellow colors) denote regions of localized electrons, whereas regions with low ELF values (blue and green colors) denote delocalized electrons. To complement the visual ELF analysis, quantitative ELF descriptors were calculated in terms of localization index (LI) values for the π-conjugated regions of compounds (3A–3C) and (4A–4C) using Multiwfn 3.8. The results are summarized in (Table S10). From the ELF maps shown in Figure , it is observed that for compounds 3A–3C, regions of high ELF values are mainly localized in σ-bonding regions and around specific heteroatoms. In contrast, the aromatic and π-conjugated systems exhibit a more fragmented electron density distribution, indicating limited delocalization across the molecular framework. These findings are consistent with the relatively large HOMO–LUMO energy gaps (ΔE) and chemical hardness (η) values for the 3A–3C series, reflecting higher stability and lower reactivity. In contrast to the 3A–3C series, the ELF maps of the 4A–4C series demonstrate more pronounced π-electron delocalization. The ELF density is more widely distributed across the conjugated framework, indicating enhanced electron mobility in these systems. This observation is in agreement with the lower ΔE and η values calculated for the 4-series compounds. The quantitative LI results (Table S10) further support these observations. Compound 4B exhibits significantly lower LI values (LI ≈ 0.42–0.81) compared to 4A (LI ≈ 0.69) and 4C (LI ≈ 0.51–0.72). In particular, the lowest LI value (0.422) observed for 4B clearly indicates the highest degree of π-electron delocalization among the studied compounds. In contrast, compound 4A shows relatively higher LI values, suggesting a more localized electronic structure, while 4C exhibits intermediate behavior. Among all the studied compounds, the ELF maps reveal the most significant features for compound 4B. The ELF density in 4B is more extensively and homogeneously distributed over the π-conjugated system, and the lone pair regions exhibit stronger interaction with the bonding framework. This distribution indicates that electrons are delocalized over the entire conjugated system rather than being confined to specific sites. When these results are considered together with the HOMO–LUMO and MEP analyses, it becomes evident that the 4-series, and particularly compound 4B, possesses a more favorable electronic structure in terms of charge transfer capability, polarizability, and chemical reactivity.

Localized Orbital Locator Analysis

The localized orbital locator (LOL) surfaces and two-dimensional plots depicted in Figure offer a detailed insight into the electron localization/delocalization phenomenon in compounds 3A–3C and 4A–4C. LOL analysis is considered an auxiliary tool, which is particularly useful for assessing the spatial mobility of π-electrons and the efficiency of electron sharing in the course of bond formation. In the LOL maps, regions characterized by high LOL values (red and yellow colors) represent electron localization, whereas regions with low LOL values (green and blue colors) represent enhanced electron delocalization.

LOL representation of the compounds (3A–3C) and (4A–4C).

From the LOL maps for compounds 3A–3C, it is evident that regions characterized by high LOL values are mainly concentrated around σ-bonds and particular atomic centers. In the π-system, it is observed that the distribution of electron density is fragmented and confined, suggesting inefficient delocalization of electrons throughout the molecule. This electronic configuration is in agreement with the higher HOMO–LUMO energy gaps (ΔE) and higher values of chemical hardness (η) for 3A–3C compounds. In contrast, for 4A–4C compounds, a notable difference is seen in the LOL analysis. In these molecules, the density of LOL is more extensively distributed along the aromatic rings and the conjugated bonding systems. The smoother and more diffuse electron distribution in 3D LOL surfaces indicates that π-electrons are able to freely move across the molecular skeleton. This results in an enhanced ability for sharing these electrons. This is directly related to the lower ΔE and η values calculated for 4A–4C compounds and can be associated with their higher polarizability. Out of all the investigated systems, compound 4B shows the most pronounced delocalization effect in the LOL analysis. In this compound, low localization (high mobility) electron density is uniformly distributed across the π-conjugated system. This, in turn, suggests that electrons are not localized or confined to specific bonds or particular areas of an atom but are efficiently and effectively shared throughout the entire molecule. Such an electronic arrangement is, of course, entirely consistent with 4B having the lowest HOMO–LUMO band gap and lowest chemical hardness, reinforcing its comparatively “soft” and highly reactive electronic character. Overall, it is evident that LOL analysis has provided clear evidence for a decrease in electron localization and an increase in π-electron mobility as one progresses from the 3-series to the 4-series. When viewed collectively, it is evident that ELF, MEP, and LOL analysis, have provided compelling evidence that the 4-series, and 4B specifically, have a more favorable electronic arrangement for charge transfer, chemical, and functional activities.

Natural Bond Orbital Analysis

The NBO results (Table ) point to a clear difference between the aldehyde (3A–3C) and malononitrile (4A–4C) compounds. In the aldehyde series, the main interactions are n(O) → σ* and are mostly centered on the formyl group, so the electron density tends to stay in that region rather than spreading across the molecule. In the malononitrile derivatives, the situation changes. Strong π (CC) → π*­(CN) and n(N) → π*­(CN) interactions are observed, indicating that charge transfer toward the dicyanovinyl unit becomes more effective. This effect becomes more noticeable as the donor strength increases (NMe2 > OMe > Me), leading to more effective interaction between the donor and acceptor parts of the molecule. Looking at the E (2) values (Table ), compound 4B stands out from the rest. It has the highest average interaction energy (33.78 kcal mol–1), the strongest single interaction (46.52 kcal mol–1), and the largest total stabilization (202.65 kcal mol–1). Its higher standard deviation (σ = 9.77) also suggests a wider range of interaction strengths, meaning that stabilization comes from several interactions working together. In contrast, 4A and 4C show lower values overall and more uniform interaction patterns, which points to a more limited electronic structure. Overall, after the Knoevenagel step, the system shifts from a more localized electronic structure in the aldehyde derivatives to a more delocalized one in the malononitrile derivatives. The strong acceptor character of the dicyanovinyl group, together with improved donor–acceptor interaction, leads to more effective intramolecular charge transfer (ICT), especially in compound 4B.

Conclusion

In this study, compounds 3A–3C and 4A–4C were synthesized in high yields (85–95%). These benzaldehyde-based, ethynyl-functionalized donor–acceptor dicyanomethyl derivatives (BEDA-DCMs) (4A–4C) were synthesized via Sonogashira cross-coupling and Knoevenagel condensation reactions. The structures of all compounds were characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Following characterization, optical and electrochemical measurements were carried out using Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy, cyclic voltammetry (CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The electronic structures and reactivities of the compounds were investigated by density functional theory (DFT) calculations, including highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), molecular electrostatic potential (MEP), electron localization function (ELF), localized orbital locator (LOL), and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses. In conclusion, HOMO–LUMO, MEP, ELF/LOL, and NBO analyses reveal an energy gap in the range of 5.920–6.639 eV for the 4A–4C series. In particular, the lowest ΔE (5.920 eV) and η (2.960 eV) values observed for 4B indicate enhanced π-delocalization and a directional charge transfer driven by strong donor → acceptor interactions. This is supported by the pronounced ICT band at 448 nm in the UV–vis spectrum. In addition, the current density of 7.69 mA/cm2 obtained from CV/EIS measurements experimentally confirms these findings. In this context, compound 4B stands out as a potential candidate for charge transfer-based optoelectronic and electrochemical applications due to its more favorable electronic properties compared to the other derivatives.
