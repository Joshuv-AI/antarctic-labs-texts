A not-so-strange term coming from somewhere
Giacomo Canevari, Kirill Cherednichenko, Arghir Zarnescu

Abstract
We consider Laplace’s equation in a periodically perforated domain, with Robin boundary conditions on the holes and a Robin coefficient inversely proportional to the total surface area of the holes. We show that, in a certain critical regime, the homogenised equation contains an additional zero-order term, which depends nonlinearly on the Robin coefficient. This additional term is defined in terms of solutions to a Steklov-type spectral problem, where the eigenvalue appears both in the equation and the boundary conditions. The “strange term” for the problem with Robin conditions converges to zero as the Robin parameter tends to zero and, at least under suitable assumptions, it converges to the capacitary “strange term” for the Dirichlet problem as the Robin parameter tends to infinity.

Introduction

Homogenisation of boundary value problems for differential operators on perforated domains is a classical topic. The case of the Laplacian on a domain with ‘stiff’ inclusions (with Dirichlet boundary conditions) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
was first studied by Marchenko and Hruslov
[
21
,
22
]
in the context of scattering by domains with fine-grained boundaries, see also
[
15
, Section 3.6]
, and subsequently by Cioranescu and Murat
[
10
,
11
]
, who coined the expression ‘le terme étrange’ for the effect of the perforations on the homogenised description. Denoting by
\varepsilon
the typical distance between the inclusions, an interesting effect occurs if the radii
r_{\varepsilon}
of the inclusion are scaled as
r_{\varepsilon}\sim c\varepsilon^{d/(d-2)}.
In this case, the perforated domain Laplacian
-\Delta_{\varepsilon}
converges to the operator
-\Delta+\mu
on the domain without perforations, where
\mu
is proportional to the harmonic capacity of a single perforation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.
Perforated domain problems have since been studied intensely in various variants, including those with Neumann, and more generally Robin, boundary conditions on the boundaries of the perforations. They are known (at least in some cases) to yield explicitly characterised limit operators, to which the operators of the perforated problems are shown to converge in the norm-resolvent sense
[
6
]
, sometimes with error control in terms of
\varepsilon

[
19
,
18
]
.

The above developments have led to two research trends. On the one hand, they have posed the question about the role of the condition on the boundaries of the perforations on the asymptotic analysis as
\varepsilon\to 0
and its quantitative effect on the limit problem. On the other hand, in several continuum mechanics settings, the problem of accounting for the effect of the surface energy on the asymptotics of the total energy stored within the medium has been prompted from the modelling perspective. For example, the recent works
[
4
,
5
]
have investigated questions of this type in the setting of liquid crystals. Clearly, the existing results for the Dirichlet problem (’infinitely stiff’ inclusions) and the Robin problem (including the Neumann problem in the case of the vanishing Robin coupling) leave a gap between the two. Is there a family of (possibly
\varepsilon
-parameterised) perforated problems whose effective descriptions bridge the gap? In the present paper, we show that the answer is ‘yes’ and provide the related family of problems on perforated domains with
\varepsilon
-dependent surface energy terms — see Remark
2.7
below. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the scaling coefficient for the Robin parameter in the boundary conditions is provided by the inverse total surface area of the perforation, which balances the bulk and surface energy components for continuous functions with the modulus of pointwise values uniformly bounded above above and below: roughly speaking, this ensures a competition between the two for minimising sequences for the total energy, see (
1
) below.

The proof of our main result, Theorem
2.6
reveals a connection of the convergence of energy minimisers for the case of the above ‘critical’ energy scaling to a ‘trace inequality’, i.e. an inequality between the (suitably scaled) boundary and bulk energy integrals, see (
2.10
), where the optimal constant is provided by the lowest eigenvalue of a special Steklov-type boundary-value problem, see (
EVε,κ
), with the spectral parameter present in both the equation and the boundary condition. The said eigenvalue is shown to be provided by the minimum value in a minimisation problem,
see (
2.9
), involving a key parameter
\kappa
that enters the effective ‘strange-term’ formulation (
P0
) for the original perforated problems in question.

The above connection with Steklov-type eigenvalue problems places the problem we study among the material modelling setups where
eigenvalue problems for Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps emerge in the process of quantifying the effect of inclusions on propagation of long waves — up to this point, mainly for periodic settings. The inclusions can be small relative to the separation distance, or have contrasting material properties in relation to the ambient medium, or have a specific scaling for the Robin coupling parameter between the solution and the boundary flux. The related works either begin by assuming Steklov boundary conditions
[
13
,
14
]
and derive the effective (‘homogenised’) medium or lead to a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map through the asymptotic analysis of the effect ion transmission conditions on inclusion boundaries
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
. It is intriguing that the key role in the asymptotic behaviour of the problem (
Pε
) below, where the Robin coupling is scaled as the total surface area of the small inclusions, is played by the eigenvalues
\lambda(\kappa,\varepsilon)
of (
EVε,κ
), a Steklov-type problem with the spectral parameter entering both the equation and the boundary condition.

We conclude this section by noting that
L^{2}
convergence estimates of the kind we discuss here are a natural step towards the quantitative description of evolution problems for inhomogeneous media in question. A relevant example of an early contribution is
[
9
]
. In light of more recent developments, this looks to be complemented with new results in the near future. Another research avenue that can be pursued is the investigation of the effect of perforations with boundary conditions studied in this article in cases when the perforations are situated along a lower-dimensional manifold, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

\hskip 2.27621pt\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{\beta\,u_{\varepsilon}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}=0&\textrm{on }\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\\

From a variational point of view, it is quite natural
to introduce a normalisation coefficient
\mu_{\varepsilon}

in (

(2)

c_{1}\,\sigma(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})\leq\mu_{\varepsilon}\leq c_{2}\,\sigma(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})

Indeed, this choice guarantees that surface and bulk energies

In our analysis, the set of “holes”

is
supposed to satisfy certain conditions.
However, instead of describing the general setting, we first focus on a model example to which our analysis applies;
we will describe the more general setting in Section

In our model example, the set of ‘holes’ is given by

(3)

\mathscr{P}_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{n}+\varepsilon^{\gamma}\overline{D}=\left\{\varepsilon z+\varepsilon^{\gamma}x\colon z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\ x\in\overline{D}\right\}\!,

where

is a bounded domain
of class

and dimension

containing the origin, and

is defined as

(4)

\gamma:=\frac{n}{n-2}.

In this case, the parameter

in (

)
is given by

(5)

\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{\gamma}\sigma(D).

By explicit computation, we see that such

is precisely the total surface area of

Holes of the form (

correspond exactly to the critical scaling

u^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}),\qquad-\Delta u^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\varepsilon}+\alpha u^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{\varepsilon}=f\quad\textrm{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

Cioranescu and Murat
[

to the problem

u^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad-\Delta u^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{0}+\left(\alpha+\Lambda^{\mathrm{Cap}}_{*}\right)u^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{0}=f\quad\textrm{in }\Omega.

Here

is a “strange term”, defined as a solution
to a capacity problem, i.e.

is the minimal Dirichlet energy
of a map that is equal to zero on

For instance, in case

is the unit ball

.
By contrast, Kaizu
[

\begin{cases}-\Delta u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}+\alpha u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}=f&\textrm{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\
\hskip 5.69054pt\partial_{\nu}u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}+\beta u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

has diameter proportional

\begin{cases}-\Delta u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}+\left(\alpha+\beta\sigma_{n}\right)u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}=f&\textrm{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\
\hskip 5.69054pt\partial_{\nu}u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}+\beta u^{\mathrm{K}}_{\varepsilon}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

Therefore, introducing the normalisation coefficient
\mu_{\varepsilon}

in (

6

than to (

as we will see momentarily, in terms of the expression for

The homogenisation limit (in the model example).

A “not-so-strange” eigenvalue problem.

For any given

Proof.

Again, we focus on the case
\kappa\left|\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus H_{\varepsilon}\right|>1
,
as the other one is completely analogous.
We claim that
\mu=1/\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)

satisfies (

v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus H_{\varepsilon})
.
Let

\mu\geq 0

Now, let

satisfies (

Corollary 3.5

Lemma

Lemma 3.6

If the assumptions (

Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0

such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any

with trace equal to zero in

\int_{Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}u(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{0}\int_{Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla u(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

Let

be the
linear extension operator given by Assumption (

),
and let

\left\|E_{\varepsilon}u-\int_{Y}(E_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq\left\|\nabla(E_{\varepsilon}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq C\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon})}\!.

\begin{split}\left|\fint_{Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{Y}(E_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|&\leq\fint_{Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}\left|u(y)-\int_{Y}(E_{\varepsilon}u)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\mathrm{d}y\\

\begin{split}-\Delta U_{\varepsilon}+\alpha U_{\varepsilon}&=-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+w_{\varepsilon}\Delta u_{0}+2\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla u_{0}+u_{0}\Delta w_{\varepsilon}+\alpha u_{\varepsilon}-\alpha w_{\varepsilon}u_{0}\\
&=(1-w_{\varepsilon})f+(\beta\kappa_{*}(\beta)w_{\varepsilon}+\Delta w_{\varepsilon})u_{0}\\
&\hskip 55.48277pt+2\div\!\left((w_{\varepsilon}-1)\nabla u_{0}\right)-2(w_{\varepsilon}-1)\Delta u_{0}\end{split}

in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. By testing this equation againts
U_{\varepsilon}

and integrating by parts,
we deduce

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left(\left|\nabla U_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\alpha U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{\nu}U_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left((1-w_{\varepsilon})f+(\beta\kappa_{*}(\beta)w_{\varepsilon}+\Delta w_{\varepsilon})u_{0}\right)U_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt-2\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(w_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla U_{\varepsilon}+U_{\varepsilon}\,\Delta u_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}(w_{\varepsilon}-1)U_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{\nu}u_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.\end{split}

Equation (
4.22
) in Lemma

together with our choice of

,
implies that

\partial_{\nu}w_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\beta\,w_{\varepsilon}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}w_{\varepsilon}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}

as (normal) traces on

, where

(as in (

This equality, together with the boundary conditions
for

in (

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left(\left|\nabla U_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\alpha U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\beta}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left((1-w_{\varepsilon})f+(\beta\kappa_{*}(\beta)w_{\varepsilon}+\Delta w_{\varepsilon})u_{0}\right)U_{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt-2\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(w_{\varepsilon}-1)\left(\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla U_{\varepsilon}+U_{\varepsilon}\,\Delta u_{0}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}(3w_{\varepsilon}-2)U_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{\nu}u_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}\,w_{\varepsilon}\,u_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.\end{split}

By making repeated use of the elementary inequality
ab\leq\frac{\theta a^{2}}{2}+\frac{b^{2}}{2\theta}

,

,
\theta

),
we deduce

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left(\left|\nabla U_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\alpha U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\beta}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left((1-w_{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\beta\kappa_{*}(\beta)w_{\varepsilon}+\Delta w_{\varepsilon})^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{C\mu_{\varepsilon}}{\beta}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\left(w_{\varepsilon}^{2}+1\right)\mathrm{d}\sigma+\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}w_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma.\end{split}

We can now bound the right the roght-hand side with

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left(\left|\nabla U_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\alpha U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\beta}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}U_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq C\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\mu_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\!,\end{split}

5
The model example revisited

5.1
The model example satisfies (
C1
)–(
C5
)

The goal of this section is to prove that the family of holes
defined by Equation (
3
),
i.e.
H_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{\gamma-1}\overline{D}
,
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain
of class
C^{1,1}
in dimension
n\geq 3
containing the origin and
\gamma:=n/(n-2)
,
satisfies the assumptions (
C1
)–(
C5
).
Conditions (
C1
) and (
C2
) are immediate
from the definition of
H_{\varepsilon}
.
To see that (
C3
), too, is satisfied,
we take a bounded, open cube
Y_{0}
that contains
\overline{D}
,
and consider a bounded extension operator

E\colon H^{1}(Y_{0}\setminus\overline{D})\to H^{1}(Y_{0})
.
We can always assume that
E
maps constant functions
to constant functions, for otherwise, we replace

E
with
\tilde{E}\colon z\mapsto E(z-\fint_{Y_{0}\setminus\overline{D}}z\,\mathrm{d}x)+\fint_{Y_{0}\setminus\overline{D}}z\,\mathrm{d}x
.
Then, the continuity of
E
and the Poincaré inequality imply

\left\|\nabla(Ez)\right\|_{L^{2}(Y_{0})}\leq C\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}(Y_{0}\setminus\overline{D})}\!,

for any
z\in H^{1}(Y_{0}\setminus\overline{D})

and some constant
C
that does not depend on
z
.
Now, we can construct an extension operator

E_{\varepsilon}\colon H^{1}(Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon})\to H^{1}(Y)

satisfying (
C3
) just by a scaling argument.

It still remains to prove that
H_{\varepsilon}

satisfies (
C4
) and (
C5
).
To this end, we recall some notation.
We define
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})
as
set of functions
z\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})

that satisfy
\nabla z\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D},\,\mathbb{R}^{n})

and
\left|\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}\colon\left|u(x)\right|\geq c\}\right|<+\infty

for any
c>0
. The space
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})

is a real Hilbert space with the inner product

(5.1)

\left\langle z,\,w\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}}\nabla z\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x+\fint_{\partial D}zw\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

(see e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 3.3]
).
We recall some equivalent characterisation of this space.
Let
X_{D}
be the set of functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}\to\mathbb{R}

that admit an extension in
C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

The set
X_{D}
is dense in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})
.

Proof.

This is a classical result and we provide a proof for
the reader’s convenience only.
Let
z\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})
.
By making use of the operator

E\colon H^{1}(U\setminus\overline{D})\to H^{1}(U)
,
we can extend any
z\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})

to an element of
H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
still denoted
z
for simplicity. For any positive integer
j
,
we define

z_{j}(x):=\mathrm{sign}(z(x))\min\!\left(j,\,\left(\left|z(x)\right|-\dfrac{1}{j}\right)_{+}\right)\qquad\textrm{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The function
z_{j}
is bounded and supported on a set of
finite measure, so
z_{j}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

(5.2)

\limsup_{j\to+\infty}\left\|\nabla z-\nabla z_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\limsup_{j\to+\infty}\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}(A_{j})}=0,

where
A_{j}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon 1/j\leq\left|z(x)\right|\leq j+1/j\}
.
Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B^{n}_{2})

be a cut off function, such that
\eta=1
in
B^{n}_{1}
,

0\leq\eta\leq 1
in
B^{n}_{2}

and
\left\|\nabla\eta\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B^{n}_{2})}\leq 2
,
and let
\eta_{R}(x):=\eta(x/R)
for any
R>1
.
The function
z_{j,R}:=z_{j}\eta_{R}
is compactly supported and satisfies

(5.3)

\begin{split}\limsup_{R\to+\infty}\left\|z_{j}-z_{j,R}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\limsup_{R\to+\infty}\left(2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R}^{n}}\left|\nabla z_{j}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+8\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{R}^{n}}\left|z_{j}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)=0\end{split}

for any
j\geq 1
. Together, (
5.2
) and (
5.3
)
imply that
z
can be approximated in the

\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})
-norm by a sequence of functions
with bounded support. The lemma follows by a
regularisation argument (e.g., by convolution).
∎

Let
V:=\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})\oplus\mathbb{R}
.
Any function
z\in V
can be written uniquely as

z=z_{0}+L
with
z\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D})

and
L\in\mathbb{R}
a constant, and we write
z(\infty):=L
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an increasing sequence of open sets
such that
\cup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}U_{j}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}
.
Suppose that the sets
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy a uniform Poincaré-Sobolev inequality, i.e. there exists
a constant
C
such that

(5.4)

\left\|z\right\|_{L^{p}(U_{j})}\leq C\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{p}(U_{j})}

for
p:=2n/(n-2)
, any
j\in\mathbb{N}
and any
z\in L^{2}(U_{j})

such that
\int_{U_{j}}z(x)\mathrm{d}x=0
.
Let
z_{j}\in H^{1}(U_{j})
be a sequence of functions such that

(5.5)

\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|\nabla z_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(U_{j})}<+\infty,\qquad L:=\lim_{j\to+\infty}\fint_{U_{j}}z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}

(in particular, we assume that the limit exists).
Then, there exists a (non-relabelled) subsequence
and a function
z\in V
such that
z_{j}\rightharpoonup z

pointwise almost everywhere and weakly
in
H^{1}(U\setminus\overline{D})
for any open
bounded set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
z(\infty)=L
, and

(5.6)

\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}(U\setminus\overline{D})}\leq\liminf_{j\to+\infty}\left\|\nabla z_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(U_{j})}.

Proof.

The assumption (
5.5
), combined with standard
compactness results and a diagonal argument, enables us to
extract a (non-relabelled) subsequence that converges
pointwise almost everywhere and weakly
in
H^{1}(U\setminus\overline{D})
for any bounded, open
set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The limit function
z
must satisfy

\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}(U\setminus\overline{D})}\leq\liminf_{j\to+\infty}\left\|\nabla z_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(U\setminus\overline{D})}\leq\liminf_{j\to+\infty}\left\|\nabla z_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(U_{j})}

for any bounded
U
. Letting
U
invade
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we deduce that the inequality (
5.6
) holds,
and in particular,
\nabla z\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D},\,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. To conclude the proof, it only remains to show that
z-L

decays at infinity, i.e. for any
c>0
, the set

E_{c}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}\colon\left|z(x)-L\right|\geq c\}
has finite measure.
To this end, we define the sets
E_{c}^{j}:=\{x\in U_{j}\colon\left|z_{j}(x)-L\right|\geq c/2\}
.
Since
z_{j}\to z
pointwise almost everywhere, we have

E\subseteq\liminf_{j\to+\infty}E_{j}
up to negligible sets
(more precisely, the difference
(\liminf_{j\to+\infty}E_{j})\setminus E
has Lebesgue measure zero). Moreover,
the
L^{p}(U_{j})
-norm of
\tilde{z}_{j}:=z_{j}-\fint_{U_{j}}z_{j}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is uniformly bounded, due to the assumptions (
5.4
), (
5.5
).
Therefore, Fatou’s lemma implies

\begin{split}\left|E_{c}\right|\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\left|E_{c}^{j}\right|\leq\frac{2}{c}\liminf_{j\to+\infty}\left\|\tilde{z}_{j}\right\|_{L^{p}(E_{c}^{j})}<+\infty.\end{split}

and the lemma follows.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Let
Y_{0}
be an open cube that contains
\overline{D}
.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for
any

and any

This follows from Remark
3.7
,
combined with a scaling argument.

Having introduces the space

, we can now prove that
the family

satisfies the
assumptions (
C4
) and (
C5
).
Actually, we conider (

) first.
Given

\Lambda_{*}(\kappa):=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}}\left|\nabla z\right|^{2}\colon z\in V,\ \fint_{\partial D}z^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=1+\kappa\,z(\infty)^{2}\right\}\!.

In a similar way, if

, we define

(5.8)

\Lambda_{*}(\kappa):=\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus D}\left|\nabla z\right|^{2}\colon z\in V,\ \fint_{\partial D}z^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=-1+\kappa\,z(\infty)^{2}\right\}\!.

Lemma 5.4
.

The family of sets

satisfies
the condition (
C5

\lambda_{*}(\kappa)=\Lambda_{*}(\kappa)

Proof.

We focus on the case
\kappa>1
; in case
0<\kappa<1
,
the proof is analogous.

First step.

First, we claim that

(5.9)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\Lambda_{*}(\kappa).

By Lemma
5.1
and a density argument,
the infimum at the right-hand side
of (
5.7
) does not change if we restrict our
attention to competitors
z\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{D}\to\mathbb{R}

that admit a smooth extension
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and are constant
in a neighbourhood of infinity. Let
z
be such a competitor,
and let

v_{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{z(\varepsilon^{\gamma-1}x)}{c_{\varepsilon}^{1/2}}\qquad\textrm{for }x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus H_{\varepsilon}\textrm{ and }\varepsilon>0,

where
c_{\varepsilon}>0
is a normalisation constant,
to be chosen in a moment. Since
z
is equal to
L

away from a compact set, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough

\partial Y

In particular,
\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon,\kappa}(v_{\varepsilon})>0

for
\varepsilon
small enough and we can
take

so that
\mathscr{G}_{\varepsilon,\kappa}(v_{\varepsilon})=1
.
Then,

is an admissible competitor for

2.8
) and we have

\begin{split}\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\varepsilon^{(\gamma-1)(n-2)}}{c_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla z\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+\mathrm{o}_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1)}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus H_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla z\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

We divide both sides of this inequality by
\varepsilon^{2}
,
pass to the (superior) limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
and take the infimum over all possible
z
.
The claim (
5.9
) follows.

Second step.

Next, we claim that

(5.10)

\Lambda_{*}(\kappa)\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Combined with (
5.9

\lambda_{*}(\kappa)

For the proof, we consider the positive minimiser

. Moreover,

\left\|\nabla z_{\varepsilon,\kappa}\right\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon^{1-\gamma}Y\setminus\overline{D})}=\varepsilon^{-1}\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)^{1/2}

is bounded uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon
and
\kappa
,
by Lemma
3.8
.
Therefore, the Poincaré-Sobolev inequality
(see e.g.
[
20
, Theorem 8.3]
) implies that the

L^{p}(\varepsilon^{1-\gamma}Y\setminus\overline{D})
-norm of
z_{\varepsilon,\kappa}

is uniformly bounded, where
p:=2n/(n-2)
.
Then, elliptic regularity estimates and a bootstra argument
imply that the functions
z_{\varepsilon,\kappa}

restricted to
\partial D
are Lipschitz-continuous,
with uniform bounds on the Lipschitz constant. After a rescaling,
this shows that the condition (
2.14
) is satisfied.
Therefore, Proposition
2.5
applies
and we have
\lambda_{0}=\Lambda^{\mathrm{Dir}}_{*}
.
∎

When
D
is the unit ball
B^{n}
, the problems (
5.7
),
(
5.8
), (
5.14
) and (
5.15
)
can be solved explicitly.

Proof of Proposition
2.10
.

Given a function
z\in V
,
we define
z_{r}\colon[1,\,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
as

z_{r}(\rho):=\fint_{B^{n}_{\rho}}z(\rho\,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\sigma)\qquad\textrm{for a.e. }\rho\in[1,\,+\infty).

By abuse of notation, we identify
z_{r}
with a radial function
defined in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B^{n}
, i.e.
z_{r}(x)=z_{r}(\left|x\right|)
.
Substituting
z_{r}
for
z
decreases the
H^{1}
-seminorm, because

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sigma(\partial B^{n})}\left\|\nabla z_{r}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}^{2}=\int_{1}^{+\infty}z_{r}^{\prime}(\rho)^{2}\rho^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho&=\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left(\fint_{\partial B^{n}}\partial_{\rho}z(\rho\,\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\omega)\right)^{2}\rho^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\\
&\leq\int_{1}^{+\infty}\left(\fint_{\partial B^{n}}\left|\partial_{\rho}z(\rho\,\omega)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma(\omega)\right)\rho^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\\
&=\frac{1}{\sigma(\partial B^{n})}\left\|\nabla z\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, the infima in Equations (
5.7
),
(
5.8
), (
5.14
) and (
5.15
)
can be restricted to radial functions,
of the form
z(x)=z(\left|x\right|)
. Assuming
for instance
\kappa>1
, Lemma
5.4

implies

(5.16)

\lambda_{*}(\kappa)=\inf\left\{\sigma_{n}\int_{1}^{+\infty}z^{\prime}(\rho)^{2}\rho^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\colon z\colon[1,\,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\ z(1)^{2}=1+\kappa z(+\infty)^{2}\right\}\!.

More precisely, the infimum is taken among all
functions
z\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(1,\,+\infty)

such that the integral at the right-hand side of (
5.16
)
is finite and there exists a constant
z(+\infty)\in\mathbb{R}
for which
the difference
z-z(+\infty)
decays at infinity, i.e.

\{\rho\in[1,\,+\infty)\colon\left|z(\rho)-z(+\infty)\right|>c\}

has finite measure for any
c>0
.
We have used the notation
\sigma_{n}:=\sigma(\partial B^{n})
.
By Remark (
5.5
),
we know that the infimum is actually a minimum.
Any minimiser for (
5.16
) must satisfy
the Euler-Lagrange necessary condition for optimality,
which implies

z^{\prime}(\rho)=\frac{c}{\rho^{n-1}}

for a.e.
\rho\geq 1
and some constant
c
.
By integration, we can now express
z(+\infty)

as a fucntion of
z_{1}:=z(1)
and
c
, so that
the constraint
z(1)^{2}=1+\kappa z(+\infty)^{2}

takes the form

z_{1}^{2}=1+\kappa\left(z_{1}+\frac{c}{n-2}\right)^{2}\!.

This is a quadratic equation for
c
, whose solutions are

(5.17)

c_{\pm}(z_{1}):=(n-2)\left(-z_{1}\pm\sqrt{\frac{z_{1}^{2}-1}{\kappa}}\right)\!.

Note that
c_{-}(z_{1})<c_{+}(z_{1})<0
.
The functional to be minimised in (
5.16
)
is proportional to
c^{2}
, so we can disregard the
solution
c_{-}(z_{1})
. Moreover, a standard computation
shows that the minimum value of
c_{+}(z_{1})^{2}
is achieved when

z_{1}=z_{\kappa}:=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa-1}}.

Now, Equation (
5.16
)
reads

\lambda_{*}(\kappa)=\frac{\sigma_{n}\,c_{+}(z_{\kappa})^{2}}{n-2}

and the lemma follows by simple computations.
The case
0<\kappa<1
is completely analogous.
∎

5.2
Other asymptotic regimes

Finally, in this section we consider other regimes
for our model examples, i.e. we assume the holes have the form

(5.18)

\mathscr{P}_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}^{n}+r_{\varepsilon}D

where
r_{\varepsilon}>0
satisfies either
\varepsilon^{-\gamma}r_{\varepsilon}\to 0

or
\varepsilon^{-\gamma}r_{\varepsilon}\to+\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We will deduce the asymptotic behaviour of solutions to (
Pε
) from abstract results, along
the lines of Theorem (
2.6
).

Proposition 5.6
.

Consider a sequence of sets
(H_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
in
Y

that satisfies (
C1
)–(
C3
).
Moreover, suppose the numbers
\Lambda^{\mathrm{Dir}}(\varepsilon)

defined in (
2.1
) satisfy

(5.19)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\Lambda^{\mathrm{Dir}}(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=0.

Then, the unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
Pε
)
satisfies
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
u_{0}
is the unique solution to

(5.20)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{0}+\alpha u_{0}=f&\textrm{in }\Omega\\
u_{0}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

The arguments are similar to those presented already in the previous sections. We only sketch the proof,
so as to illustrate the differences. By combining the assumption (
5.19
)
with the estimate (
3.17
), whose proof carries
over to this setting unchanged, we deduce that

(5.21)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=0\qquad\textrm{for any }\kappa\in(0,\,1).

The uniform estimate (
4.7
) remains true,
and it is still possible to extract a subsequence
\varepsilon\to 0

such that
u_{\varepsilon}
converges in the sense of (
4.8
).
We define correctors
w_{\varepsilon,\kappa}
exactly as in (
4.17
). Equation (
5.21
),
combined with a scaling argument similar to those in Lemma
4.5
, shows that
\overline{w_{\varepsilon}}\to 1

and
\overline{\nabla w_{\varepsilon}}\to 0
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
By Remark
4.4
, we conclude that the limit map
u_{0}

satisfies (
5.20
). Since the limit equation
has a unique solution, we have convergence not
only along a subsequence, but for the whole family
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Consider a sequence of sets
(H_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

in
Y
that satisfies (
C1
)–(
C3
).
Moreover, suppose that there exists
\kappa>1
such that

(5.22)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=+\infty.

Then, the unique solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
Pε
)
satisfies
\left\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{\varepsilon})}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
u_{0}
is the unique solution to

(5.23)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{0}+\alpha u_{0}+\beta u_{0}=f&\textrm{in }\Omega\\
u_{0}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

The uniform estimate (
4.7
) remains valid and allow us
to extract a (non-relabelled) subsequence such that
u_{\varepsilon}

converges to a limit
u_{0}
, in the sense of (
4.8
).
Now, let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)
be
a test function. We claim that

(5.24)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.

Once this claim is proved, we will be able to pass to the limit
in the weak formulation of (
Pε
) and conclude that
u_{0}

satisfies (
5.23
). Since the limit problem
has a unique solution, this argument also proves convergence
for the whole sequence
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The proof of (
5.24
) relies on
the trace inequality in Lemma
4.2
,
particularly on (
4.3
).
(Note that the proof of (
4.3
) remains valid
independently of the assumptions (
C4
)
and (
C5
), although clearly (
4.4
)
depends on them.) By applying the Hölder inequality
and (
4.3
), we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\left|(u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0})\varphi\right|\mathrm{d}\sigma&\leq\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}(u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\varphi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leq C\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla(u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\kappa\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}(u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\left|\nabla\varphi\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\kappa\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\varphi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\end{split}

for any
\kappa>1
and any
\varepsilon
small enough.
We can assume
\widetilde{E}_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}
converges to
u_{0}

weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega)
and strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
by compact Sobolev embedding.
Then, using the assumption (
5.22
),
we conclude that

(5.25)

\begin{split}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\left|(u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0})\varphi\right|\mathrm{d}\sigma=0.\end{split}

Now, we fix
\delta>0
and we
take
v_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega)

such that
\left\|u_{0}-v_{0}\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\delta
.
By applying the Hölder inequality and (
4.3
),
exactly as above, we deduce

(5.26)

\begin{split}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\left|(u_{0}-v_{0})\varphi\right|\mathrm{d}\sigma&\leq C\kappa\,\left\|u_{0}-v_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\kappa\,\delta\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\end{split}

Finally, we estimate the integral of
v_{0}\varphi
on
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. To this end, we decompose
\Omega_{\varepsilon}

as a union of sets of the form
\varepsilon z+\varepsilon Y
, with
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
and approximate
\varphi v_{0}
with a constant
value
\varphi(\varepsilon z)v_{0}(\varepsilon z)
on each of these sets.
Let
Z_{\varepsilon}
be the set of points
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

such that
(\varepsilon z+\varepsilon Y)\cap\Omega\neq\emptyset
.
Since
v_{0}\varphi
is Lipschitz-continuous and equal
to zero out of
\Omega
, we have

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\right|&\leq\sum_{z\in Z_{\varepsilon}}\left|\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\varepsilon z+\varepsilon\partial H_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\varepsilon z+\varepsilon(Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon})}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{z\in Z_{\varepsilon}}\left|v_{0}(\varepsilon z)\varphi(\varepsilon z)\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{n-1}\sigma(\partial H_{\varepsilon})}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}-\varepsilon^{n}\left|Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}\right|\right)\right|\\
&\hskip 36.98866pt+\varepsilon\left\|\nabla(v_{0}\varphi)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\sum_{z\in Z_{\varepsilon}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{n-1}\sigma(\partial H_{\varepsilon})}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon^{n}\left|Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}\right|\right)\end{split}

Recalling the definition (
2.16
) of
\mu_{\varepsilon}
,
and using that the number of points in
Z_{\varepsilon}
is of
order
\mathrm{O}(\varepsilon^{n})
, we obtain

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\right|&\leq C\left\|v_{0}\varphi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}(1-\left|Y\setminus H_{\varepsilon}\right|)+C\varepsilon\left\|\nabla(v_{0}\varphi)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

In particular, we have

(5.27)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}v_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x

because
\left|H_{\varepsilon}\right|\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0

(see Remark
2.2
).
Combining (
5.25
), (
5.26
),
and (
5.27
), we obtain

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left|\frac{1}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{0}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\kappa\delta\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

and, since
\delta>0
is arbitrary,
the claim (
5.24
) follows.
∎

Now, let
(r_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of positive numbers

Proposition 5.8

Let

Proof of Proposition

We only sketch the proof, because several arguments

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{n-2}\lambda(\varepsilon,\,\kappa)=\Lambda_{*}(\kappa)
