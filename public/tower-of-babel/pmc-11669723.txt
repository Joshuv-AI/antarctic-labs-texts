A Graph-based Goat Pangenome Reveals Structural Variations Involved in Domestication and Adaptation
Authors: Peipei Bian, Jiaxin Li, Shishuo Zhou, Xingquan Wang, Mian Gong, Xi Guo, Yudong Cai, Qimeng Yang, Jiaqi Fu, Rongrong Li, Shuhong Huang, Funong Luo, Ali Mujtaba Shah, Johannes A Lenstra, Joram M Mwacharo, Ran Li, Gang Ren, Xiaolong Wang, Cong Li, Wenxin Zheng, Yu Jiang, Xihong Wang, Maria C Ávila-Arcos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPangenomes can facilitate a deeper understanding of genome complexity. Using de novo phased long-read assemblies of eight representative goat breeds, we constructed a graph-based pangenome of goats (Capra hircus) and discovered 113-Mb autosomal novel sequences. Combining this multi-assembly pangenome with low-coverage PacBio HiFi sequences, we constructed a long-read structural variations (SVs) database containing 59,325 SV deletions, 84,910 SV insertions, and 24,954 other complex SV alleles. This resource allowed reliable graph-based genotyping from short reads of 79 wild and 1,148 worldwide domestic goats. Selection signal analysis of SV captured a novel immune-related domestication locus containing the galectin-9 gene and extra copies of the ruminant-specific galectin-9-like genes (LGALS9L), which have high tissue specificity. A segmental duplication in domestic goats generates three additional LGALS9L copies. Ancient goat genome sequences show a gradual increase in frequency of this duplication from the Neolithic to the present. Two other newly detected SVs also have higher selection signals than adjacent SNPs, a truncated-LINE1 deletion in EDAR2 associated with cashmere production and a VNTR-related insertion in PAPSS2 linked to high-altitude adaptation. In summary, the multi-assembly goat pangenome and long-read SV database facilitates detecting complex variations that are important in evolution and selection.Keywords: graph-based pangenome, structural variations, de novo assembly, domestication, low-coverage sequencing

Introduction

Pangenomes capture the full range of genetic diversity within a species, providing a comprehensive view of genetic variations (Sherman and Salzberg 2020). Among these, structural variations (SVs, ≥ 50 bp) induce larger genomic changes in genes and regulatory regions than SNPs and small indels (1 to 49 bp), critically shaping phenotypic diversity and influencing traits related to growth, adaptation, and disease susceptibility (Sudmant et al. 2015; Chiang et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2023; Xia et al. 2023). PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing, with its high accuracy (>99.9%) and long-read capability (Hon et al. 2020), enables more complete genome assemblies and enhances SV detection, particularly in challenging repetitive regions (Nurk et al. 2020, 2022). Recent advancements in multi-assembly pangenomes generated through long-read sequencing have expanded the discovery of complex SVs (Ebert et al. 2021; Liao et al. 2023; Hickey et al. 2024). However, the cost of high-coverage long-read sequencing of a representative cohort (a group of animals within a species) remains prohibitive. Alternatively, the precision and accuracy of PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing for a smaller cohort of animals may be combined with low-coverage whole-genome resequencing (lcWGS) long-read sequencing and a multi-assembly pangenome for the construction of a high-quality long-read SV database (Liao et al. 2023). Using this database, the short-read WGS data from thousands of individuals can be used for genotyping leading to insights on SV prevalence and potential functional relevance (Chen et al. 2019; Yan et al. 2021; Ebler et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023).

The goat (Capra hircus), together with the sheep (Ovis aries), is one of the earliest domesticated livestock species and has a wide adaptability. Previous studies have revealed multiple functional SVs associated with cashmere production (Cai et al. 2020; Han et al. 2022), polledness (E et al. 2021), coat color (Henkel et al. 2019), or enhanced immune resistance (Zheng et al. 2020) in goats. However, these SVs were typically detected via selective signals of surrounding SNPs, while SVs not linked with the SNPs remain undetected (Zhou et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023). Currently, a direct genome-wide identification of goat SVs relies on short-read sequencing (Cumer et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2024), but SVs such as insertions or those embedded in repetitive genomic regions remain challenging due to the limited read length (Lin et al. 2023). The single reference genome is typically a pseudohaplotype, representing only one breed within a species and failing to encompass the entire genetic diversity of the species. Consequently, this leads to reference mapping bias and failure to detect rare or breed-specific variants. A study conducted by the Human Genome Structural Variation Consortium, revealed more than two-thirds of SVs have been missed using short-read data and a single reference genome (Ebert et al. 2021). Therefore, SVs in goats have so far remained largely unexplored.

In this study, we assembled high-quality partially phased assemblies for eight individuals from representative goat breeds and constructed a graph-based pangenome. In addition, the PacBio HiFi low-coverage sequencing for 20 other goat samples provided additional data for SVs discovery. These SVs were genotyped in 1,227 wild and domestic goats. We found that several SVs are linked to major production and adaptation traits, demonstrating that our worldwide catalog of sequence-resolved SVs for goats is a valuable resource for accurate assessment of genetic diversity and functional variation.

Results

Generating Genome Assemblies of Eight Representative Goat Breeds

We generated PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long reads with 18 to 27× coverage for seven geographically diverse female goats from the following breeds: the Shanbei Cashmere Goat, Shannan White Goat, Hainan Black Goat and Chinese Nubian Goat of Asian origin; the Alpine milk Goat and Guanzhong milk goat (a Saanen derivative) of European origin; and the Boer Goat, a crossbreed of Asian and African origins (supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). We further downloaded PacBio HiFi data of a female Tibetan goat with 69× coverage (Li et al. 2022) (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). For each individual goat, one primary assembly and two partially phased assemblies were generated by hifiasm (v0.15.4) (Cheng et al. 2021). We used the Saanen_v1 assembly (Li et al. 2021) as the backbone for reference-guided scaffolding. This assembly was generated by high-coverage PacBio, Illumina and Hi-C data and with only 169 gaps, is more continuous than the goat reference genome assembly GCF_001704415.1_ARS1, which has 773 gaps and an incomplete coverage of sex chromosomes (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online) (Bickhart et al. 2017; Worley 2017). These primary assemblies serve as a potential reference panel for each breed, while the partially phased assemblies are used for pangenome construction and SV detection.

The size of the eight primary genomes ranged from 2.86 to 3.02 Gb and the contig N50 ranged from 26.74 to 97.58 Mb (supplementary fig. S3, Supplementary Material online), representing the length of the shortest contig needed to cover 50% of the assembly, ordered from longest to shortest. Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) is a tool for assessing genome completeness by analyzing conserved single-copy genes across a specific lineage (Waterhouse et al. 2018). BUSCO assessment showed that 94.5% to 95.7% of the orthologs were identified. The haplotype genomes size ranged from 2.80 to 2.92 Gb with a contig N50 from 3.17 to 94.12 Mb and 93.4%—95.6% BUSCO completeness (Table 1, supplementary table S2 and fig. S4, Supplementary Material online). On average and per individual, the assembly quality value (QV) was 66 as estimated using PacBio HiFi reads by Merqury (Rhie et al. 2020) (supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online).

Construction of Minigraph-Cactus Goat Pangenome

Pangenomes were constructed from the 16 partially phased haploid de novo assemblies using the ARS1 assembly as the reference. This generated a Minigraph-Cactus (MC) pangenome containing variants of different sizes (Hickey et al. 2024). The final goat graph pangenome consists of 2,579,541,660 bp, spanning 79,070,614 nodes connected by 107,681,871 edges (Fig. 1b). On average, a node spanned 33 bp connected by 1.36 edges (supplementary table S8, Supplementary Material online). In the MC graph pangenome, 97.29% of the nucleotides and 64.44% of the nodes originate from the linear reference backbone, and the 28,118,732 non-reference nodes contributed additional 113,350,307 bp to the graph (supplementary table S8, Supplementary Material online). The optimal saturation curve of the pangenome growth in size showed that the second and third haplotypes add ∼41.64 Mb and ∼14.24 Mb of autosomal sequence, while the 17th haplotype adds ∼3.4 Mb (Fig. 1c), but still not saturated (supplementary fig. S6 and table S9, Supplementary Material online).

A Gapless Mosaic Centromere-to-telomere Assembly Saanen_v2 Using the Pangenome

To have a suitable reference backbone to assemble the pangenome, we constructed a mosaic gapless goat assembly Saanen_v2 by filling the gaps in Saanen_v1 with a syntenic sequence from the Hifiasm assemblies (see supplementary Methods, Supplementary Material online), leaving only 24 gaps on the Y chromosome. In addition, chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 15, 21, 27 and 28 were extended (supplementary table S10, Supplementary Material online and Fig. 1d), resulting in centromere-to-telomere coverage of the autosomes and an X chromosome telomere-to-telomere assembly. The length of the autosomes and X chromosome of Saanen_v2 is 2.635 Gb, which is 70 and 153 Mb longer than the Saanen_v1 and ARS1 assemblies, respectively. The Saanen_v2 has at least 113 gene duplications that are absent in Saanen_v1. From the filled gaps, 81% (137/169) are adjacent to transposable elements (TEs) (97 gaps for LINEs) or centromeric satellites, and 30% (52/169) correspond to regions of complex SDs (supplementary table S11, Supplementary Material online). Many filled gaps contain complex gene families with tandem duplications (Fig. 1d), such as major histocompatibility complex (MHC), T cell receptor alpha (TRA) genes, immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) genes, multidrug resistance-associated protein (MRPs) genes, olfactory receptor genes (ORGs), guanylate-binding protein (GBPs) genes, galectin 9 (LGALS9 s) genes, zinc-finger (ZNFs) genes, melanoma-associated antigen 10 (MAGEA10 s) genes and RNA genes cluster. The near complete Saanen_v2 genome assembly provided a reference for the assembly and annotation, and identification of large tandem gene clusters, but is a mosaic sequence of different breeds and individuals.

Mapping PacBio HiFi reads from the eight goats to the Saanen_v2 based MC graph, yielded alignments for 96% to 98% of the reads, whereas only 93% to 97% could be mapped to the ARS1-backed MC graph (supplementary fig. S7, Supplementary Material online). The reference genome directly affects the basic framework of the pangenome graph. The better completeness, continuity and improved graph structure of the Saanen_v2-backed graph enabled confident mapping of more reads with fewer false duplication and multiple alignments. The Saanen_v2-backed Minigraph pangenome (only recording SV) (Li et al. 2020) has fewer non-reference sequences but about the same number and size of bubbles as the ARS1-backed pangenome (supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online). Both the ARS1-based and Saanen_v2-based MC graphs are available on Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/), and can be used directly to genotype with PanGenie (Ebler et al. 2022) for individuals which only have short-read WGS data available.

SVs Discovery based on Multi-assembly Graph Pangenome and PacBio HiFi Reads Mapping

The pangenome contains 19 million SNPs, 3.7 million indels and 90,472 SV sites with nested variation for the ARS1-backed MC graph (Fig. 2a and supplementary table S12, Supplementary Material online). To be consistent with the current goat reference genome, we used the ARS1-backed MC graph, and the Saanen_v2-backed MC graph for detailed analysis of specific regions. Variations in the graph are enriched with LINEs and VNTRs. Of the SV sites, 31.58% are retrotransposon-related, with LINE/LINE1 being dominant (Fig. 2b and supplementary fig. S9, Supplementary Material online). This was also observed for the pangenomes of sheep and cattle (Dai et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023), while the SVs in human pangenome graphs are enriched with Alu elements (Li et al. 2020; Liao et al. 2023), indicating TEs are an important source of SVs (Osmanski et al. 2023). More than half of the SV sites are multiallelic (Fig. 2b and supplementary table S12, Supplementary Material online). After decomposition and merging clusters of highly similar alleles, all SV sites represented 118,722 SV alleles (42,771 SV deletion alleles, 50,997 SV insertion alleles and 24,954 other SV complex alleles) (see Methods).

In addition, we performed detection of long-read-based SVs as a complement to the graph pangenome. We called SVs from the eight high-coverage PacBio HiFi data alignment to the ARS1 assembly and used four SV reads mapping-based callers, pbsv (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbsv), SVIM (Heller and Vingron 2019), Sniffles (Sedlazeck et al. 2018), and CuteSV (Jiang et al. 2020) and retained the SVs (biallelic insertions and deletions) identified by at least two callers for each sample. These SVs overlapped by 58% with the set of graph pangenome-derived SVs (Fig. 2d) and were genotyped using Paragraph (Chen et al. 2019), a graph-based genotyper that can accurately genotype known SVs using the aligned sequence reads in BAM format.

To reduce costs and maximize SV detection across more individuals, we generated additional PacBio HiFi reads (0.82 to 1.55× coverage, 65.78 Gb data) and 12-fold-coverage Illumina data per individual for 10 individuals each of Shanbei cashmere and Guanzhong milk goats (supplementary table S13, Supplementary Material online). To test the efficiency of SV detection using low-coverage sequencing, the high-coverage PacBio HiFi data of the eight assemblies were randomly down-sampled to 1× sequencing depth. This generates SVs with 0.93 precision and 0.43 recall compared with the high-coverage data (Fig. 2c). Utilizing low-coverage PacBio HiFi sequencing data from the 20 goats, we obtained 73,946 SVs, 72% of which overlapped with the high-coverage SVs. 20,838 SVs were only found in the low-coverage PacBio HiFi reads for 20 Shanbei cashmere or Guanzhong milk goats (8,780 deletions and 12,058 insertions), whereas the eight genomes with high-coverage PacBio HiFi reads contributed 9,592 additional SVs (Fig. 2d). These findings suggest that multi-sample lcWGS may capture a greater number of SVs detectable by long-read mapping compared to deep sequencing of individual samples.

The final SVs from the 28 individuals are discovered based on PacBio HiFi reads alignment and the ARS1-backed graph pangenome. In total, we detected 169,189 SV alleles (59,325 SV deletion alleles, 84,910 SV insertion alleles and 24,954 other SV complex alleles) (Fig. 2d). The SV size distribution shows peaks at ∼144 bp, 1.3 and 7.8 kb, corresponding to BOV-A2 (SINE), LTR/ERVK and L1_BT (LINE/L1), respectively. These peaks correspond to the length distributions of the TE-SVs and most LINE elements vary in length across genomes due to extensive rearrangements and truncations (Fig. 3a). 281 SV hotspots were detected spanning ∼248 Mb, 104 of which are located within the 5 Mb end of chromosomes. The other 177 nonterminal SV hotspots significantly enriched for SDs (P-value = 0.001, z-score = 11.387, supplementary fig. S10, Supplementary Material online) that are consistent with homologous recombination. From the 177 nonterminal SV hotspots, 168 have not been previously identified by short-read analyses (Huang et al. 2021) (Fig. 3b and supplementary table S14, Supplementary Material online). Annotation revealed that most SVs cover intergenic and intronic regions, and a minority intersect with UTRs and exons (supplementary fig. S11, Supplementary Material online). Permutation tests indicated significant enrichment of nonterminal SV hotspots (P-value = 0.001, z-score = 5.053) and a depletion of all nonterminal SVs (P-value = 0.01, z-score = −1.849) in protein-coding genes (supplementary fig. S12, Supplementary Material online). A total of 3,208 protein-coding genes overlapped at least one SV hotspot. These genes were associated with olfactory transduction and were high in polymorphisms (supplementary fig. S13, Supplementary Material online).

Graph-based SV Discovery in Large-Scale Short-Reads Data and Population Genetic Analysis

Multi-assembly pangenome and long-read SVs database enabled reliable graph-based genotyping from short reads of 79 wild and 1,148 worldwide domestic goats (supplementary table S15, Supplementary Material online). With PanGenie, we genotyped all the variations in the ARS1-backed graph pangenome. We further used Paragraph to genotype SVs identified by the long-read mapping-based approach and merged the PanGenie-called and Paragraph-called SVs into the final set (Chen et al. 2019; Ebler et al. 2022). To ensure a high-quality set of SVs for population genetic analysis, we excluded SVs that failed to be genotyped in >90% of the samples and those whose minimum frequency was less than 0.01, leaving 85,237 reliably genotyped SVs for downstream analysis. SV genotypes in five trios revealed an average mendelian error rate of 4.3%. In addition, we performed validations on the accuracy of SV genotyping for the SVs identified from low-coverage PacBio HiFi read mapping. For a single individual, the recall of short-read SV genotyping was 0.45, and the precision exceeded 0.9 at 10× using the genotyping results from high-coverage PacBio HiFi reads as the truth set (supplementary table S16, Supplementary Material online).

SV based phylogenetic trees are in concordance with previously reported SNP-based phylogeny (Zheng et al. 2020). Domestic goats formed a separate clade from their wild progenitor bezoar and six other wild Capra species. Within the domestic goat clade, populations were basically grouped according to their sampling origin (Europe, Africa, Southwest Asia, South Asia and East Asia) (supplementary fig. S14, Supplementary Material online).

The SVs and SNPs under domestication-driven selection were detected by pairwise genetic differentiation (FST) of domestic goats and their wild ancestor, the bezoars. The highest selection signal of SV is located at chromosome 19:19010695 (Fig. 4a), which contained unannotated genes with no known orthologs. The second highest SV selection signal occurs within the MUC6 with multiple linked SNPs (Fig. 4a and supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online). This is a locus that has been selected during domestication. It is associated with resistance to gastrointestinal pathogens, introgressed from distantly related wild Capra species. In this study, a 58 bp insertion was detected from the low-coverage PacBio HiFi reads of cashmere goats (supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online). The insertion was found in 98% of the bezoar and 6% in domestic goat (supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online), belonging to the bezoar haplotype. For the other wild goat species, West Caucasian ibex, Iberian ibex and Alpine ibex share the noninsertion haplotype with domestic goats (supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online). In addition, the MUC6-insertion was also genotyped in ancient goat genomes (supplementary table S17, Supplementary Material online). In ancient goat, the domestic haplotype without MUC6-insertion first emerged in the sample 7,200 years ago, with its frequency increasing subsequently, which is also similar with SNP study (Zheng et al. 2020). These finding further supports the domestic MUC6 haplotype was introgressed from wild Capra species, then selected during domestication. SVs introduce new potential causal variants to the haplotype.

Conversely, the top SNP-based selection signal on RRM1 corresponds to a single-base causal missense mutation that increased tameness and sociability and reduced anxiety in mice and therefore may at least partially account for the tameness that accompanies domestication (Dou et al. 2023) was not detected in our SV level analysis.

Novel Domestication Locus With Multiple Galectin-9-like Gene Copies

Syntenic alignments of the 19:19010695 locus with the highest SV selection signal between ARS1 and hifiasm assemblies showed a 71 kb novel sequence as well as a 63 kb segmental duplication (SD) with an identity of 99% to the adjacent region (ARS1 chromosome 19:19014884-19077843) (Fig. 4b and supplementary fig. S16, Supplementary Material online). We found the novel sequence in all hifiasm assemblies and the SD in half of the assemblies. Additionally, both the novel sequence and the SD are present in the Saanen_v2 genome. This region contains several genes named in ARS1 as LOC + GeneID (supplementary fig. S17, Supplementary Material online), which are homologous to galectin-9 (LGALS9), a β-galactoside binding lectin. In the ARS1 assembly, LGALS9 gene contains 10 or 11 exons with two homologous carbohydrate-recognition domains (CRDs). The six galectin-9-like genes (LGALS9L1 to LGALS9L6) upstream of LGALS9 contain only four or five exons with one CRD (Fig. 4d and supplementary fig. S17, Supplementary Material online). The protein identity between the LGALS9L genes range from 58.95% to 86.50% (supplementary table S18, Supplementary Material online).

We downloaded the reads used for the current goat reference genome assembly and verified the absence of the 63 kb duplication and 71 kb novel sequence in ARS1 genome (Fig. 4d and supplementary fig. S18, Supplementary Material online). However, our pangenome revealed that a long LGALS9 SV allele includes four additional LGALS9L genes. This is confirmed by RNA-seq data, which revealed 10 LGALS9L genes on the Guanzhong milk goat haplotype genome. The SD introduced three additional galectin-9-like genes (LGALS9L3b to LGALS9L5b, copies of LGALS9L3a to LGALS9L5a, respectively), whereas another copy of the galectin-9-like gene (LGALS9L7) is in the novel sequence (Fig. 4d and supplementary fig. S18, Supplementary Material online). LGALS9L4b and LGALS9L5b are within the duplicated region but the first exon of LGALS9L3b is located in a 10 kb novel sequence between the SD duplicates (supplementary fig. S19, Supplementary Material online). LGALS9L3a contains a signal peptide (SP) sequence previously thought not to be present in the galectins (Nickel and Rabouille 2009).

The SD of LGALS9 locus can be detected in the ∼1,200 short-read genome sequence via read depth. The average normalized read depth of the 63 kb SD repeat unit is 1.12 in bezoars and 1.77 in domestic goats. In ancient goat genomes, the duplication emerged in domestic goats at least 4,000 years ago during the Bronze Age (Fig. 4c and supplementary table S17, Supplementary Material online). Therefore, the SD is more prevalent in domestic goats than in their wild ancestor, the bezoar, and appears to have been selected in domestication.

We searched for LGALS9L orthologs in other species and found that LGALS9L does not occur in the human, camel and pig, while a single LGALS9L gene copy is found in cattle, two LGALS9Ls in yak, two LGALS9Ls in bison, five LGALS9Ls in sheep and, depending on the SV allele, 6, 7 or 10 LGALS9Ls in goats (supplementary fig. S20, Supplementary Material online). The existence of the LGALS9L genes may be a unique feature of ruminant genomes.

Expression Profiles of Duplicated Galectin-9-like Genes

To elucidate the possible function of multiple galectin-9-like gene copies, we quantified expression levels for one LGALS9 and ten LGALS9L genes using the bulk RNA of ∼2,458 samples (supplementary table S19, Supplementary Material online) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of four tissues (skin, pancreas, testis and rumen) from the public database (see Methods). The LGALS9Ls have a higher tissue and cell expression specificity than LGALS9, which is broadly expressed in various tissues (supplementary fig. S21 and S22 and S23, Supplementary Material online), suggesting functional divergence that occurs after the gene duplication event. LGALS9L3-6 genes are all highly expressed in bones, although this is based on a small sample size (n = 2) (supplementary fig. S21, Supplementary Material online).

LGALS9L5a and its copy LGALS9L5b (LGALS9L5a/b) are significantly highly expressed in blood (n = 191, supplementary fig. S21, Supplementary Material online). Here, we reanalyzed the publicly available transcriptome data (Olech et al. 2021) of goat blood samples after infection with high (HPL) or low (LPL) proviral load of small ruminant lentiviruses (SRLV). Interestingly, the expression of LGALS9L5a and LGALS9L5b in the blood of goats carrying HPL of SRLV is nine times higher than in blood of low proviral load (LPL) goats (Fig. 5a, supplementary fig. S24 and table S20, Supplementary Material online). Genes with expression levels that correlated with the levels of LGALS9L5a/b in blood are enriched in the categories: Viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor, Chemokine signaling pathway, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and Human cytomegalovirus infection (Fig. 5b and supplementary table S21, Supplementary Material online). The highest correlation was observed for chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 18 (CCL18), a small cytokine belonging to the C–C chemokine family. High correlations were also found for other chemokine receptor genes such as C–C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3) (Fig. 5c). In the animal QTL database, we found a quantitative trait locus (QTL) associated with respiratory disease susceptibility in the LGALS9 region of Bovine (Hu et al. 2019).

LGALS9L3b is highly expressed in blood and shows a higher expression level than LGALS9L3a (supplementary fig. S21 and S25, Supplementary Material online). Blood expression levels of hemoglobin genes (HBA1, HBA2 and HBB) and erythroid-specific 5-aminolevulinate synthase 2 (ALAS2, a marker gene in developing red blood cells called erythroblasts) have the strongest correlation with the levels of LGALS9L3b (Fig. 5d, supplementary fig. S26 and S27, Supplementary Material online). Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data of fetal rumen also shows a high expression of LGALS9L3b gene in hemoglobin-rich erythroblasts (Fig. 5e). Cell clusters based on scRNA-seq data for known cell type-specific markers profiles were identified in all the cell populations from specific tissues (supplementary fig. S28 and table 2, Supplementary Material online2).

In addition, we found in erythroblasts of fetal rumen a high expression of LGALS9L3a, LGALS9L4a, LGALS9L4b, LGALS9L6 and ALAS2, but only low expression of LGALS9. LGALS9L2 and LGALS9L7 are mainly expressed in dendritic cells (DCs) of testis tissue (supplementary fig. S29, Supplementary Material online). However, LGALS9L7 is specifically expressed in the abomasum, while LGALS9L2 is abundantly expressed in the abomasum and small intestine (supplementary fig. S21, Supplementary Material online). The ancestral LGALS9 play an immunomodulatory role in various microbial infections (Moar and Tandon 2021) and studies in human have shown that erythroid cells inhibit the function of DCs via the LGALS9/TIM3 axis (Wang et al. 2023). Therefore, we hypothesize that the duplicated galectin-9-like genes could have specialized and refined their functions and thus contribute to the intricate immune regulatory network.

Candidate Causal SVs for Cashmere Production and High-altitude Adaptation in Repetitive Genomic Regions

F ST distances between Northern Chinese cashmere goats and other goats reveal two prominent SVs under selection (Fig. 6a), which were missed in a study that used short-reads data and the linear reference genome (Wu et al. 2024). One SV at the enhancer region of FGF5 was previously reported by a SNP level study following fine mapping (Cai et al. 2020). Another SV downstream of the gene ectodysplasin A2 receptor (EDA2R) (X1:18026323) was a deletion of a 2.9 kb-truncated L1 and with higher signals than FST-based SNP analysis (Fig. 6c). The deletion and EDA2R are within a long LD block (supplementary fig. S30, Supplementary Material online). EDA2R is a potential candidate gene involved in the development of hair follicles (Botchkarev and Fessing 2005; Kwack et al. 2019) with multiple candidate variations in the selected long region (X1:17,915 to 18,539 kb). The EDA2R-L1-deletion is absent in all the wild bezoar and six wild Capra species (supplementary fig. S31, Supplementary Material online). In ancient goats, it was detected in samples from the Bronze Age (4000BP) with a frequency of 8% (Fig. 6e and supplementary table S17, Supplementary Material online). This frequency increased to 57% in the Iron Age and to 85% of modern northern Chinese cashmere goats but is only 11% in modern southern Chinese goats. Because LINEs may introduce regulatory sequences or splice sites (Liang et al. 2021; Galbraith and Hayward 2023), the EDA2R-L1-deletion is a promising functional candidate variant for cashmere production.

The strongest FST signal in the Tibetan goat (chromosome 26:42093212) is a 51 bp insertion at the intronic region of PAPSS2 gene and with higher signals than FST-SNP-based analysis (Fig. 6b and 6d). This gene has been detected in a SNP study investigating adaptation in Tibetan goats (Li et al. 2022), but without identifying causal mutations. The insertion is in a VNTR region composed of a 51 bp motif. In the pangenome graph, this region included four SV-bubbles. The selected PAPSS2-SV allele is present in 77% of the Tibetan goats, and 100% of the markhor (Capra falconeri) native to the Himalayan mountains but rare in other goat breeds (supplementary fig. S31, Supplementary Material online). Previous introgression analyses using SNPs suggested that the PAPSS2 haplotype (chromosome 26:42077488-42179191) conferring the high-altitude adaptability in Tibetan goats originated from the markhor (Li et al. 2022). Using the same markhor samples (SRR6246089, SRR6246090, SRR8437778) for analysis, the PAPSS2-SV (chr26:42093212) is positioned on the introgressed fragment. It is likely that this SV allele was introduced into the Tibetan goat population through introgression from markhor. This region overlaps with a goat homolog of the human H3K4Me1 mark (Fu et al. 2022), which usually is enriched at enhancers (Rada-Iglesias 2018), and with a reported regulatory region (Nguyen et al. 2018) (Fig. 6f). In addition, the PAPSS2 gene is fully within an active A compartment and a single Topologically Associating Domain (TAD), with the intronic insertion near, but not overlapping, ATAC-seq peaks (Foissac et al. 2019) (supplementary fig. S32, Supplementary Material online). This illustrates how adaptive introgression may provide alleles that confer adaptation of a recipient population to a new environment.

Discussion

Here, we constructed the first high-quality goat graph pangenome using PacBio HiFi sequencing which made possible the discovery of 113 Mb autosomal novel sequences to the reference ARS1. We have collected samples from eight representative goat breeds, but saturation analysis suggests substantial sequence diversity is still missing. Thus, to capture the extensive variation present in global goat populations, more samples should be added to the pangenome, especially those from the African continent. We also generated a gapless mosaic goat assembly, Saanen_v2, providing a convenient backbone reference for assembling the pangenome.

We integrated the ARS1-backed graph pangenome with the long-read mapping strategy and generated a comprehensive SV database. The graph pangenome captured more SV than long-read mapping strategy, especially for insertions (50,997 vs. 37,404). We also performed the PacBio HiFi lcWGS for an additional 20 goats and contributed 20,838 SVs to the long-read SV database of goats. These include SVs at MUC6 and LGALS9L locus, which are difficult to detect via the conventional approach because of their low frequency and high complexity. Thus, the increased samples with lcWGS indeed enhanced the detection power for SVs, particularly for those with low-to-moderate frequencies. In total, we identified 169,189 high-confidence nonredundant SV alleles. These SVs are enriched with LINE1 and VNTRs. A total of 281 SV hotspots were identified and enriched in protein-coding genes (Ebert et al. 2021; Dai et al. 2023), with 168 being newly discovered in our study in the nonterminal regions of the chromosomes (Huang et al. 2021).

The graph pangenome and long-read SV database enabled reliable graph-based genotyping from 1,227 short reads comprising 79 wild and 1,148 worldwide domestic goats. Subsequent genome-wide analysis of SVs uncovered several strong selective signals overlapping domestication and adaptation genes. A novel domestication locus contained 10 divergent galectin-9-like (LGALS9L) genes and a 63 kb SD region containing three new gene copies. This complex locus is difficult to detect and characterize without high-quality de novo assemblies and the gapless Saanen_v2 reference.

Galectins are known to modulate immune cell function (Liu and Stowell 2023). The positive selection of three LGALS9L copies during goat domestication is supported by an increase in their frequency over time as observed in ancient goats from different periods. Hemoglobin and erythroid-specific genes demonstrate the strongest correlation with LGALS9L3b expression levels in blood. Additionally, scRNA-seq from fetal rumen reveals a high LGALS9L3b expression in erythroblasts, early erythropoiesis cells characterized by increasing hemoglobin content. The expression of LGALS9L5b significantly increased in virus-infected individuals and strongly correlated with the expression levels of genes in the chemokine signaling pathway, such as CCL18 which regulates immune cell migration and inflammation (Chenivesse and Tsikopoulos 2018). The intriguing potential relationship between LGALS9L5b and the chemokine system warrants further investigation. Gene duplication is a key driver of functional diversity in evolution (Kondrashov 2012; Magadum et al. 2013). Domestication is a significant shift for animals, taking them from natural to human-controlled environments. This transition can heighten physiological stress due to overcrowding, diet changes, and new pathogens, emphasizing the need for strong immune systems in high-density settings (Larson and Fuller 2014; Zheng et al. 2020). The diversity and adaptability that have arisen from the duplication and subsequent divergence of LGALS9L genes may have led to a more refined immune system that contributed to the adaptation of goats to human environments.

In addition, we reported two novel SVs in EDAR2 and PAPSS2 with more pronounced selective signals than surrounding SNPs, which are related to cashmere production and high-altitude adaptation, respectively. These SVs were not found in previous studies (Cai et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2024). This is most likely due to the use of long reads, the reliable graph-based genotyping and an increased sample size that includes northern Chinese goat samples. Although direct experimental evidence is still lacking, we provide plausible candidate variants for subsequent research on target genes.

We conclude that our investigation highlights the utility of analyzing pangenomes and of multi-sample low-coverage PacBio HiFi read sequencing for cataloging SVs in goats. Together with graph-based genotyping using existing short-read sequencing data, this allows to explore functional roles of SVs undetected previously in SNP-based research. This study also demonstrates the characterization of large and complex gene families and functional repetitive elements by analyzing high-quality assemblies.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Fresh blood samples were collected from seven representatives of goat breeds across different regions in China, which were used for high-coverage PacBio HiFi sequencing (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online). We also collected fresh blood samples from 10 Shanbei cashmere goats and 10 Guanzhong milk goats (Shanxi Province, China), which were used for low-coverage PacBio HiFi sequencing and whole-genome resequencing on the Illumina platform (supplementary table S13, Supplementary Material online). We also collected short-read sequencing data (∼14.67× coverage) from 79 wild goats and 1,148 domestic goats from representative breeds worldwide (supplementary table S15, Supplementary Material online), as well as short-read sequencing data from 81 ancient goats from the public archives (supplementary table S17, Supplementary Material online).

DNA Extraction and Library Preparation

High-quality genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from fresh blood samples using a standard cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) extraction protocol. Libraries with an average insert size of approximately 15 kb were prepared using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (PacBio), fractionated into narrow fractions on the SageELF (Sage Science), and sequenced on the Sequel II instrument (PacBio) at Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd. Raw data were processed using the CCS algorithm (version 6.0.0) to generate highly accurate PacBio HiFi reads. For short-read sequencing, paired-end short insert (150 bp) libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform.

De Novo Genome Assembly and Quality Assessment

First, we used the fast haplotype-resolved de novo assembler Hifiasm software (v0.15.4, [Cheng et al. 2021]) with default parameters to assemble the PacBio HiFi reads, which produces one primary contig assembly (representing a mosaic of homologous haplotypes) and two sets of partially phased contig assemblies (representing the two haplotypes in a diploid genome, though with occasional switch errors) for each sample. We then applied the reference-guided software RagTag (v2.0.1, [Alonge et al. 2021]) to scaffold the contigs to chromosome level based on the Saanen_v1 assembly (Li et al. 2021). The completeness of the assemblies was assessed with BUSCO (version 5.4.7 [Waterhouse et al. 2018]), using the Metaeuk backend (6.a5d39d9) and the Cetartiodactyla_odb10 database. The assembly base quality values (QV) were calculated with Merqury (version 1.3) (Rhie et al. 2020), with k-mer databases constructed from PacBio HiFi reads using Meryl (https://github.com/marbl/meryl).

Pangenome Graph Construction

For the construction of a goat multi-assembly graph, we refer to the pipeline described in previous pangenome studies (Liao et al. 2023; Hickey et al. 2024). First, Minigraph (Li et al. 2020) was used to construct a Minigraph pangenome graph of SV (recording only SVs ≥ 50 bases) using ARS1 or Saanen_v2 genome assembly as the initial backbone of the graph structure. The phased assemblies were iteratively added to the graph and then remapped back to the graph using Minigraph. These mappings were used as the input to the Cactus genome aligner (Armstrong et al. 2020), which extends the Minigraph pangenome graph with a base-level alignment of the homology relationships and constructs a new MC pangenome containing variants of all sizes.

Pangenome Size and Growth

We used VG Convert with options -gfW to convert from GFA 1.1 to 1.0 and used the tool “Pangenome growth” (https://github.com/marschall-lab/panacus) (pangenome growth -m -t nodes [edges] <graph.gfa > <sample order>) to generate growth plots for autosomal nodes and edges of the ARS1-backed MC pangenome graphs. The tool Heaps of the Odgi pangenome analysis toolkit (Guarracino et al. 2022) was used to estimate how the euchromatic autosomal pangenome grows with each additional genome assembly added. We sampled 200 permutations of genome orderings with “odgi heaps -i < graphs.gfa > -S -n200” and recorded the median pangenome size across all samples in the MC graph. We used the pangenome additional lengths (start with the second) to create the fit curve (supplementary fig. S6 and table S9, Supplementary Material online). The optimal saturation curve (y = 0.0315 × 0.5158, R² = 0.9923) in the MC graph indicated an open pangenome. By power law regression, the goat has a potentially open pangenome (Tettelin et al. 2008).

Alignment of Long reads to Pangenome Graphs

We merged all chromosome gfa files and renamed the node from the corresponding chromosome. PacBio HiFi reads from eight samples were then aligned to the ARS1-based and Saanen_v2-based graphs using GraphAligner (Rautiainen and Marschall 2020) with the option -x vg and stored in the graphical alignment format (Li et al. 2020). For reads that align to different positions in the graph, only the position with the highest alignment score was kept. Reads with <80% of the read length aligned to the graph were removed to avoid low-quality alignments.

Annotating Variant Sites in the ARS1-backed MC Pangenome Graph

We used Vcfbub (v0.1.0, [https://github.com/pangenome/vcfbub]) with options -l 0 -r 10000000 to remove large (>10 Mb) spurious deletions in the MC graph, and alternative alleles that are not covered by any haplotype. Variant sites were then classified into small variant (<50 bp) and SV (≥50 bp) sites. The SV sites were then classified into various repeat classes. In brief, the longest allele sequence of each SV site was extracted and stored in FASTA format. Repeats were analyzed with a method that combined homology comparison and de novo structure analyses. We applied RepeatMasker (v4.1.1) (Tarailo-Graovac and Chen 2009), using the database of repetitive DNA elements from the Repbase library (release 20181026) to classify interspersed repeats in the longest allele sequence of each variation. We identified tandem repeats composed of a motif occurring twice or more by TRF (Tandem Repeats Finder) (Benson 1999). We termed the SVs containing repetitive elements as follows: Mixed-inter: a site involving ≥2 types of interspersed repeats; VNTR: variable-number tandem repeat, a tandem repeat with the unit motif length ≥7 bp; STR: short tandem repeat, a tandem repeat with the unit motif length ≤6 bp; Mixed-repeat: a site involving two types of tandem repeat repeats. The repeat contents of the above site types are larger than 70%. Partial-repeat: a site that the repeated sequences content is less than 70% but more than 20%. To find hits to the ARS1 reference genome, Minimap2 (v2.24, [Li 2018]) with options -cx asm20 was used to align the longest allele sequences to the reference genome. SDs were identified if the longest allele sequence length in a site was >1000 bp and >20% of the bases of these nodes are annotated as SD in the reference.

Decomposition of Variants in the ARS1-backed MC Pangenome Graph-derived VCFs

Biallelic bubbles can be classified as SNPs, indels or SVs. Larger multiallelic bubbles may contain a high number of nested variant alleles overlapping across haplotypes and represented as a single bubble in the graph. We implemented a decomposition approach following (Liao et al. 2023) to obtain the exact coordinates of individual variant alleles carried by a sample in such a region. The decomposition process generated two VCF files, which both are different representations of the same genomic variation and represent the variant structures before and after decomposition. We applied this decomposition method to the MC graph-based VCF file and classified the variant sites in the MC graph into SNPs, indels (1 to 49 bp), SV deletions, SV insertions and other SV alleles (supplementary table S12, Supplementary Material online), defined as follows: SV deletions include all alleles for which length (reference allele) ≥ 50 bp and length (alternative allele) = 1 bp. SV insertions include all alleles for which length (reference allele) = 1 bp and length (alternative allele) ≥ 50 bp. All other alleles with a length ≥ 50 bp were included in the category “others”. We then used the program Truvari (v4.0.0, [English et al. 2022]) with Truvari collapse -r 500 -p 0.95 -P 0.95 -s 50 -S 100000 to merge highly similar alleles.

Detection of High-confidence SVs From 28 Samples by Long-read Mapping-based Approach

PacBio HiFi reads from 28 samples (20 low-coverage and eight high-coverage samples) were aligned to the goat reference genome GCF_001704415.1_ARS1 by the program Pbmm2 (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbmm2) (version 1.4.0; with parameters: –sort –preset HiFi –rg “@RG\tID:SampleID”). We then used four SV reads mapping-based callers, pbsv (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbsv), SVIM (Heller and Vingron 2019), Sniffles (Sedlazeck et al. 2018), and CuteSV (Jiang et al. 2020) to detect of high-confidence SVs. The SVs (biallelic insertions and deletions) that were identified by at least two callers for each sample were retained as described (Li et al. 2023). We also used the Truvari to merge highly similar alleles.

SVs Merging, Annotation and Hotspot Identification

All SVs were identified from the ARS1-backed MC pangenome graph and long-reads mapping-based approach. We used SURVIVOR (v4.0.0, [Jeffares et al. 2017]) to obtain the nonredundant SV set for all high-quality SVs from all the 28 samples. Annotation of the variations callset was performed using SnpEff (version 5.0e) (Cingolani et al. 2012) and ANNOVAR (Wang et al. 2010). SV hotspots were identified through kernel density estimation using the Hotspotter function from the PrimatR package with the parameters “bw = 200000, pval = 1e-08, num.trial = 2000″. We categorized the identified SV hotspots (n = 281) into three groups: “terminal,” located within the last 5 Mb of chromosome ends (n = 104); “known,” overlapping with regions identified in previous studies (Huang et al. 2021) (n = 21); and “novel,” exclusive to our study (n = 168). To assess the overlap between our SV hotspots and protein-coding genes, we extracted 19,643 unique protein-coding genes from the ARS1 generic feature file obtained from the NCBI Assembly database (accession number GCF_001704415.1). We conducted a permutation test with 1,000 iterations using the regioneR package, specifically the permTest function with circularRandomizeRegions. Additionally, we obtained the repeat and gap files (GCF_001704415.1_ARS1_rm.out.gz and GCF_001704415.1_ARS1_genomic_gaps.txt.gz) from NCBI. We combined “Satellite/centr” entries from the repeat file with the gap regions to create a comprehensive mask file. Finally, for comparison, we performed the same permutation test with all SVs.

SV Genotyping With PanGenie

PanGenie does not require any read mapping, which leverages a haplotype-resolved pangenome reference together with k-mer counts from short-read sequencing data to genotype a wide spectrum of genetic variation (such as SNPs, indels and structural variants). We used the ARS1-backed MC graph-based VCF file as a basis for genotyping using default parameters. The program Vcfbub (see above) was used with parameters -l 0 and -r 100000 to filter the VCF. In addition, we removed all records for which more than 70% carry a missing allele (“.”). We then used PanGenie, to genotype these bubbles across all 1,227 samples that were based on Illumina reads. Each VCF was then merged using Bcftools (Danecek et al. 2021). We converted the VCF into a biallelic representation using bcftools norm -m -any and kept all SV alleles with length ≥50 bp. We then merged clusters of highly similar alleles in the VCF using Truvari as described above. We used the multiallelic output VCF as input for PanGenie to genotype bubbles and used the biallelic VCF and the identifiers to translate PanGenie's genotypes for bubbles to genotypes for all individual nested variant alleles. All downstream analyses of the genotypes were based on this biallelic representation.

SV Genotyping With Paragraph

Paragraph models SVs using sequence graphs and SV annotations to provide an accurate genotyping relying on short-read data alignments especially for biallelic insertions and deletions (Garrison et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2019). All short-reads data were mapped to the goat reference genome (ARS1), using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.17-r1188) (Li and Durbin 2010) with default parameters. Using SVs set from the long-reads mapping-based approach identified from PacBio HiFi sequencing data from 28 goats (20 low-coverage and eight high-coverage samples used for de novo assembly), we used Paragraph (v2.4a; with default parameters) to genotype SVs in each individual.

SV Analyses

We used Bcftools to merge the PanGenie-called and Paragraph-called SVs for the 1,227 samples, giving allele/genotype priority to PanGenie. To ensure a high-quality SVs dataset for population genetics analysis, we excluded SVs that failed genotyping in >90% of the samples and those whose minimum frequency was less than 0.01, using VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011), leaving 85,237 reliably genotyped SVs for downstream analysis. To validate the accuracy of genotyping, Mendelian inconsistency rates were calculated using the “bcftools + mendelian -m c” command on resequenced data from 13 reported goat trios (Li et al. 2018).

Identifying the Multiple Galectin-9-like Genes Locus that Differed Between the Assemblies

The Gal9 locus (KSR1- [gal-9 like genes loci] -LGALS9-NOS2) genomic regions from our hifiasm assemblies and ARS1 reference genome were extracted. The program LASTZ (Harris 2007) and Gmaj.jar (Hsu et al. 2010) were used to prepare, visualize and generate dot plots. This detected two copies of a 63 kb region containing three gal-9-like genes with a sequence identity of 99% and a 71 kb novel sequence insertion. Further transcriptome annotation revealed four additional galectin-9-like genes in Hifiasm assemblies of Guanzhong milk goat, three of which were from the 63 kb SD, and the other was located within the 71 kb non-reference genome sequence. We used InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) to identify domains and classify functional sites within gal9 proteins.

Transcriptome Analysis

We downloaded RNA-seq datasets from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive and merged multiple datasets from single samples, yielding ∼2,458 unique RNA-seq samples (supplementary table S19, Supplementary Material online). Raw RNA-Seq reads were processed to trim adapter and low-quality sequences using fastp (Chen et al. 2018). The final cleaned RNA-Seq reads were mapped to the Saanen_v2 assembly and its annotation using Hisat2 (Kim et al. 2015). After alignments, raw read counts for each gene were calculated and normalized to TPM (Transcripts Per Million) using StringTie (Pertea et al. 2015). Subsequently, the TPM values were subjected to logarithmic transformation using the formula log2(TPM + 1) to normalize the data and mitigate variance issues. Log-transformed TPM values were utilized to calculate tissue-specific indices and to quantify the correlation between gene expression levels using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

We collected all the genes that were highly correlated (Pearson's r ≥ 0.7) with the expression level of LGALS9L5a and its copy LGALS9L5b for KEGG pathways analysis. Goat protein sequences were used to conduct functional enrichment tests on the target genes using KOBAS 3.0 (Bu et al. 2021). The P-value was calculated with a hypergeometric distribution. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed to adjust for multiple testing. Pathways with an FDR-corrected P value of <0.01 were considered statistically significantly enriched (supplementary table S21, Supplementary Material online).

Single-cell RNA-sequence Data Analysis

The single-cell data were obtained from the rumen (PRJNA919098; 45d, 60d, 75d, 90d after conception), pancreas (PRJNA856510; 10-d-old and 6-month-old), skin (PRJNA756314) and testis (PRJNA981192) of goats from other studies (Wang et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2023). The scRNA-seq fastq files were aligned and counted using CellRanger v7.1.0 software, with –expect-cells 8000, –nosecondary, –localcores 20, –localmem 100 settings (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell-ranger). Seurat version 4.4.0 and its dependencies were used for primary data analysis of the processed matrix. Each sample data, imported via the Read10× function, were structured into Seurat objects and underwent quality control. The process involved filtering out cells that exceeded the top 5% feature count threshold, below 500 unique feature counts, and cells with mitochondrial proportions > 15%. Following normalization with LogNormalize and scaling, linear dimensional reduction was performed. Before integration, unique identifiers were allocated to each individual's Seurat object, ensuring traceability back to the original data source. Subsequently, Seurat's merge function was employed to combine these labeled objects into a single Seurat object for unified analysis. Integration of samples was achieved through the RunHarmony function of the Harmony R package. Subsequently, principal component analysis and clustering based on differential gene expression (determined by Seurat's FindAllMarkers function at a resolution of 0.6) were performed. Uniform manifold approximation and projection were used for dimensional reduction. Finally, cell types were identified by referencing marker gene lists (supplementary table S22, Supplementary Material online) from relevant articles and comparing differentially expressed transcripts in each cluster with those known to be associated with specific cell types.
