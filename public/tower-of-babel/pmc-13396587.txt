Tonotopic specialization of MYO7A isoforms in auditory hair cells
Authors: Sihan Li, Jinho Park, Tobey M Phan, Giusy A Caprara, Natchanon Sittipongpittaya, Gloria M Sheynkman, Anthony W Peng, Edward H Egelman, Jonathan E Bird, Jung-Bum Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMutations in Myo7a cause Usher syndrome type 1B and non-syndromic deafness, but the precise function of MYO7A in sensory hair cells remains unclear. Using long-read sequencing, we identify and characterize a novel isoform, MYO7A-N, expressed in auditory hair cells alongside the canonical MYO7A-C. Isoform-specific knock-in mouse models reveal that inner hair cells primarily express MYO7A-C, while outer hair cells express both isoforms in opposing tonotopic gradients. Both isoforms are localized to the upper tip-link insertion site, consistent with a role in the tip link for mechanotransduction. Loss of MYO7A-N leads to outer hair cell degeneration and progressive hearing loss. Cryo-EM structures reveal isoform-specific differences at actomyosin interfaces, correlating with distinct ATPase activities. These findings reveal an unexpected layer of molecular diversity within the mechanotransduction machinery. We propose that MYO7A isoform specialization enables fine-tuning of tip-link tension, thus hearing sensitivity, and contributes to the frequency-resolving power of the cochlea.Subject terms: Hair cell, Myosin, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

Precise detection of sound relies on the exquisite mechanical sensitivity of sensory hair cells located in the inner ear1,2. These specialized sensory receptors convert nanoscale mechanical vibrations into electrical signals through a process known as mechano-electrical transduction (MET)1–3. At the heart of this process lies the hair bundle, a staircase-like array of actin-based stereocilia4, whose deflection modulates tension on filamentous structures called tip links, ultimately gating MET channels1–3. The MET complex is positioned between adjacent rows of stereocilia, with channels located at the tips of shorter stereocilia and tethered to the taller neighbors via tip links5,6 (Fig. 1a). Deflection of the hair bundle increases tip-link tension, opening MET channels and depolarizing the hair cell receptor potential. Many core components of this complex have been identified: TMC1 and TMC2 form the MET channel pore7–11, while auxiliary proteins such as TMIE7,12,13, LHFPL513–15, CIB216,17 and others are essential for proper channel function. At the upper insertion point of the tip link, the upper tip-link density (UTLD), a scaffold complex anchors the tip link to the actin core and helps maintain appropriate tension5,18. Proper tip-link tension is essential not only for fast and precise MET channel gating, but also for tuning the overall sensitivity of hair cells to mechanical stimuli, thereby directly impacting hearing sensitivity5,19,20. Reduced tip-link tension delays channel opening and diminishes the receptor potential amplitude, compromising the fidelity of sound encoding20. Interestingly, tip-link tension is not uniform across hair cell types or cochlear regions. Outer hair cells (OHCs) are thought to have higher tip-link tension than inner hair cells (IHCs), and within OHCs, tension increases along the tonotopic axis from apex to base19. These gradients suggest that hair cells dynamically optimize tip-link tension to match their frequency tuning properties and preserve auditory acuity.

Proteins localized to the UTLD include Harmonin (USH1C), Sans (USH1G), and the motor protein Myosin VIIA (MYO7A)18. Harmonin and Sans serve as scaffolds, while MYO7A is proposed to actively generate or stabilize tip-link tension through interactions with actin filaments18. Notably, MYO7A is a well-established human deafness gene and the causal gene for Usher syndrome type 1B, a disorder characterized by congenital deafness, vestibular areflexia, and progressive vision loss21–23. Our previous work demonstrated that deletion of the canonical MYO7A isoform (MYO7A-C) markedly reduced MYO7A protein in IHCs, leading to decreased MET resting open probability (Po) and slowed MET current activation in IHCs20. These findings are consistent with the proposed role of MYO7A in maintaining tip-link tension20. However, this functional role remains under debate. A recent study proposed that MYO7A may act primarily as a structural anchor, passively linking the tip link to the actin cytoskeleton rather than actively generating tension24. In this model, tip-link tension could instead arise from alternative mechanisms, such as actin remodeling at the transducing row of stereocilia or tension in the membrane25. Thus, the precise role of MYO7A in regulating tip-link tension, and whether it functions as a motor, scaffold, or both, remains to be resolved.

In this study, we employed long-read RNA sequencing to characterize the MYO7A transcriptional landscape and identified a novel isoform of MYO7A (MYO7A-N). MYO7A-N is predominantly expressed in OHCs with a basal-to-apical tonotopic gradient, whereas MYO7A-C is enriched in IHCs and displays an opposing gradient in OHCs. Notably, both isoforms are localized at the UTLD, where tip-link tension may be regulated. Deletion of MYO7A-N in mice leads to progressive hearing loss and OHC degeneration. Cryo-EM analysis reveals structural divergence in their motor domains, corresponding to distinct ATPase activities. These findings uncover the structural/biochemical divergence of MYO7A isoforms and suggest a novel mechanism for fine-tuning MET sensitivity and auditory function across hair cell populations.

Results

Survey of cochlear Myo7a isoforms by long-read sequencing

Two major types of Myo7a isoforms have been identified in multiple genomic databases: the canonical isoform (Myo7a-C) and the short isoform (Myo7a-S). These isoforms differ in their transcriptional and translational start sites (ATG), resulting in distinct N-terminal extensions (NTEs) of the motor domain. Our previous study demonstrated that Myo7a-C is predominantly expressed in all cochlear IHCs and exhibits a tonotopic gradient in OHCs20. However, the existence and function of Myo7a-S, along with the potential presence of other isoforms are unknown.

To comprehensively characterize the isoform transcript landscape in cochlear cells, including Myo7a isoforms featuring alternative NTEs, we employed concatenation-based isoform sequencing (Kinnex kits from PacBio) on cochleae from postnatal day 7 (P7) mice26 (Fig. 1b). This single-cell long-read sequencing technique generates a detailed catalog of isoforms across different cochlear cell types (Fig. 1c). Consistent with previous studies using different long-read sequencing approaches in the cochlea27,28, we identified numerous previously unreported isoforms. After filtering ambiguous transcripts such as intergenic transcripts and incomplete transcripts, 27,920,454 (79%) unique reads fully matched the reference genome exon annotation (Fig. 1d). There are also 1,226,438 reads (3%) that were categorized as “novel in catalog,” indicating new isoforms using known splicing donor/acceptor sites in different combinations (Fig. 1d). An additional 6,005,247 reads (17%) were classified as “novel not in catalog,” representing alternative splicing events with previously unidentified donor/acceptor sites (Fig. 1d). As validation of this approach, we successfully identified both long and short isoforms of otoferlin (OTOF), consistent with a recent report27. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis pipeline identified a total of 3707 cells, which were classified into 8 distinct clusters, including hair cells, Hensen cells, Deiters’ cells and other cell types (Fig. 1e). Subsequent analysis detected distinctively expressed isoforms for each cell type (Fig. 1f).

Focusing on Myo7a in the hair cell cluster, we detected transcripts corresponding to the previously reported Myo7a-C and Myo7a-S isoforms. Interestingly, we also identified a novel isoform, which we named Myo7a-N (Fig. 1g-i). To validate the presence of Myo7a-N, we performed 5’ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (5’ RACE) on cochlear cDNA from P0 mice. Myo7a-C and Myo7a-N transcripts were successfully identified in the 5’ RACE cDNA library (Fig. 1i). However, Myo7a-S transcripts were not detected in the same library, suggesting that, if expressed, Myo7a-S is present at very low levels in cochlear hair cells.

Like Myo7a-C and Myo7a-S, Myo7a-N is generated from an alternative transcriptional start site. Sequencing revealed that the unique first exon of Myo7a-N is 145 bp long, with the first 31 nucleotides comprising an untranslated region (UTR), and the remaining sequence encoding a 38-residue NTE. The next exon is shared among all MYO7A isoforms. In MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N, this exon contributes an additional five residues to the NTE, whereas in MYO7A-S the same sequence is part of the 5′ UTR and is therefore not translated. Our data thus reveals a spectrum of NTEs for MYO7A; the shortest isoform, MYO7A-S, lacks an NTE, whereas MYO7A-C has an 11-residue NTE, and MYO7A-N has a 43-residue NTE (Fig. 1h). Importantly, this 43-residue NTE of MYO7A-N is highly conserved across vertebrates, including mice and humans, indicating a potentially critical evolutionary role in hair cell function (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b).

MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N constitute the major MYO7A isoforms in the cochlea

While Kinnex is a powerful tool in identifying isoform transcripts, the read depth was not sufficient for reliable quantification of transcripts. To quantify the transcript levels of the different Myo7a isoforms, we performed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) from inner ear cDNA libraries. We isolated cochlear mRNA at E17, P0 and P20 to trace expression level changes of Myo7a isoforms at different developmental stages. Consistent with our 5’ RACE result, Myo7a-C and Myo7a-N isoforms were the most abundant isoforms detected throughout embryonic and adult stages. In contrast, the transcript of Myo7a-S was expressed at minimal levels (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1c).

The total level of Myo7a transcripts did not change significantly between P0 and P20, while the ratio of different isoforms changed during the course of hair cell maturation: the transcript level of Myo7a-C increased by approximately 2.5-fold from P0 to P20 (Normalized percentage: P0 = 0.18 ± 0.057, P20 = 0.41 ± 0.018; p-value = 0.011(*)). In contrast, the Myo7a-N trended towards a decrease (Normalized percentage: P0 = 0.14 ± 0.055, P20 = 0.056 ± 0.049; p-value = 0.12). Finally, the transcript level of Myo7a-S was barely detectable and remained low during maturation (Normalized percentage: P0 = 0.0095 ± 3.6e-3, P20 = 0.0017 ± 7.2e-4; p-value = 0.060). This result shows that Myo7a-C and Myo7a-N are the predominant isoforms in the cochlea, and that their expression levels are refined during cochlea maturation (Fig. 2a).

Although Myo7a-S is expressed at low levels, it is still possible that it has a specific and indispensable role in hair cell development or function. We tested this hypothesis using two mouse models. First, we generated a mouse line in which both Myo7a-C and Myo7a-N were deleted simultaneously. This mouse line was created by introducing a frameshift mutation in the common reading frame of the Myo7a-C and Myo7a-N, but upstream of the start codon of Myo7a-S. This allowed us to disrupt the translation of the MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N, without affecting MYO7A-S expression. We named this mouse line Myo7a-ΔCN mouse (Fig. 2b). In addition, we sought to generate a mouse line in which only Myo7a-S is disrupted. Because the start codon of the Myo7a-S isoform is located in the shared reading frame of the Myo7a-C and the Myo7a-N isoforms, it was not possible to interrupt the translated portion of Myo7a-S without affecting other isoforms. As an alternative method, we deleted the entire first exon that has the transcriptional start site of Myo7a-S, which theoretically should prevent the transcription of Myo7a-S. This mouse line was named Myo7a-ΔS (Fig. 2b).

First, we characterized the expression pattern of MYO7A in the Myo7a-ΔCN and Myo7a-ΔS mouse lines using a pan-MYO7A antibody20,29. At P5, MYO7A immunofluorescence was abolished in all hair cells of Myo7a-ΔCN mice. In contrast, we did not observe a noticeable reduction of MYO7A immunoreactivity in Myo7a-ΔS cochlea hair cells. This result was consistent with our qPCR analysis and confirmed that MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N are the major expressed isoforms in cochlear hair cells (Fig. 2c).

Next, we used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine the hair bundle morphology in Myo7a-ΔCN mice at P0 and P20 (Fig. 2d-e). Given the complete loss of MYO7A signal in P0 Myo7a-ΔCN hair cells, we anticipated a severely disorganized hair bundle phenotype30. Surprisingly, while both IHCs and OHCs exhibited morphological abnormalities, the disorganization was less severe than that observed in Myo7a full KO mice at P0 (Fig. 2d). This finding suggests that residual MYO7A isoforms, most likely MYO7A-S, can partially compensate for the absence of the major isoforms during embryonic hair bundle development. In contrast, by postnatal day 20, the hair bundle morphology in Myo7a-ΔCN mice had degenerated to a degree comparable to age-matched Myo7a-full KO mice (Fig. 2e). This morphological deterioration of the hair bundle suggests the level of remaining MYO7A (likely MYO7A-S) was not sufficient to maintain the hair bundle morphology in Myo7a-ΔCN mice. This result is consistent with our qPCR analysis that the expression level of Myo7a-S further decreases during hair cell maturation.

To test if MYO7A-S has a specific role in hair cell maintenance, we examined hair bundle morphology of aged Myo7a-ΔS mice. At 30-week, the hair bundle of Myo7a-ΔS mice appeared comparable to WT (wild-type) mice (Fig. 2f), suggesting that MYO7A-S is not necessary for developing or maintaining the hair bundle morphology in mature hair cells.

Finally, we assessed the role of MYO7A-S in hearing by performing ABR measurements in Myo7a-ΔS and Myo7a-ΔCN mice at 3 and 30 weeks of age, with 3 weeks representing the earliest reliable time point for auditory testing. Myo7a-ΔCN mice suffered profound hearing loss and complete lack of distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE) at 3 weeks of age (Fig. 2g). This result is consistent with the disorganized hair bundle morphology shown by SEM (Fig. 2e). In contrast, Myo7a-ΔS mice did not show any significant difference in ABR threshold compared to age-matched WT controls at 3 weeks (Fig. 2g) and 30 weeks (Fig. 2h). At 30 weeks of age, Myo7a-ΔS mice exhibited a statistically significant difference in DPOAE performance relative to WT, however, the effect size was negligible. We therefore consider this effect is likely attributable to normal variability in hearing sensitivity in aged mice rather than a biologically meaningful enhancement (Fig. 2h). Overall, we conclude that MYO7A-S is expressed at low levels during early development but is dispensable for hair bundle development and hearing. Consequently, we decided to focus on the functional differences between the MYO7A-C and the MYO7A-N in the remainder of our studies.

MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N exhibit opposing tonotopic gradients in the cochlea

To explore the function of MYO7A-N, we created a mouse line in which we specifically knocked out Myo7a-N by deleting its entire first coding exon (Myo7a-ΔN mouse) (Fig. 3a). Together with the Myo7a-ΔC mouse line we characterized in our previous study20, we were able to investigate the expression patterns and functional differences of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N in the mouse cochlea. First, to infer the expression pattern of these two isoforms, we performed immunohistochemistry experiments on Myo7a-ΔC and Myo7a-ΔN P5 cochleae using a pan-MYO7A antibody. As described in the previous study, MYO7A immunoreactivity in IHCs of Myo7a-ΔC mice is substantially decreased compared to WT20 (Fig. 3b, c). Homozygous Myo7a-ΔC mice also exhibited a tonotopic reduction of MYO7A signal in OHCs, with a stronger reduction at the apical region and a more modest reduction at the cochlear base (Fig. 3b, c). In contrast, homozygous Myo7a-ΔN mice showed a reduction in total MYO7A levels mainly in basal OHCs, and this reduction of MYO7A became less pronounced toward the apex of the cochlea. No significant MYO7A signal reduction was observed in Myo7a-ΔN IHCs. The quantification of total MYO7A immunofluorescence in the isoform-specific KO mice suggests an inverse expression pattern of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N: IHCs predominantly express MYO7A-C in all regions of the cochlea. MYO7A-N appears to be predominantly expressed in the OHCs with a tonotopic pattern, with a higher expression level at the basal high-frequency region of the cochlea and decreasing toward the apex low-frequency region (Fig. 3d).

It is well established that MYO7A is essential for hair bundle development, and that the depletion of MYO7A results in hair bundle disorganization30,31. We therefore investigated the morphology of hair cells and their hair bundles in Myo7a-ΔN mice by SEM imaging analysis. As reported in our previous study20, despite an approximately 80% reduction of MYO7A in IHCs, Myo7a-ΔC IHC hair bundles retain WT-like morphology at P7 (Fig. 3e). Similarly, Myo7a-ΔN basal OHCs, which show the most significant MYO7A reduction, did not show a substantial change in hair bundle morphology at P7 (Fig. 3e).

Deletion of MYO7A-C results in a reduced resting open probability (Pₒ) and slowed onset of the MET current in IHCs20. We therefore tested whether the deletion of MYO7A-N exhibited similar alterations in MET properties. Because MYO7A-N is most highly expressed in basal OHCs, we performed electrophysiological recordings to assess MET function in basal OHCs from Myo7a-ΔN mice at P7, using fluid-jet stimulation of hair bundles. We observed a slight, but not statistically significant rightward shift in the current–displacement (I–X) curve of Myo7a-ΔN basal OHCs compared with WT controls (Fig. 3f). Consistent with this, detailed analysis revealed no significant changes in other MET parameters, including Pₒ, x₀, half-activation displacement, 10–90% activation width and adaptation (Fig. 3g-j, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Given that MYO7A protein levels are reduced by ~80–90% in Myo7a-ΔC IHCs but only by ~50–60% in Myo7a-ΔN basal OHCs, it is likely that the reduction of MYO7A-N in basal OHCs does not reach critically low levels to cause changes in MET current properties at P7. MET properties in basal OHCs from Myo7a-ΔN mice may deteriorate with age, but this could not be tested due to the technical challenges of recording MET in adult OHCs.

Hair cell loss and progressive mild hearing loss in adult Myo7a-ΔN mice

Although hair bundle morphology and MET properties are not significantly affected in Myo7a-ΔN mice at early postnatal stages, we next explored the effect of MYO7A-N deletion on hearing condition and hair cell survival. We first evaluated the impact of MYO7A-N deletion on hair cell maintenance at 40 weeks of age in the basal cochlear turn. At 40 weeks, both IHCs and OHCs were well maintained in WT mice. In contrast, most OHCs were lost in the basal turn of Myo7a-ΔN cochleae, shown by loss of MYO7A staining and F-actin scars at sites of OHC loss (Fig. 4a). This effect on hair cell survival was further validated by SEM analysis. At 30 weeks of age, Myo7a-ΔN mice exhibited a longer region of complete OHC loss beginning from the basal region compared with WT mice (Fig. 4b, c; ~1500 μm in Myo7a-ΔN vs ~600 μm in WT), consistent with our immunofluorescence observations. Notably, surviving OHCs located ~1500 μm from the base in Myo7a-ΔN mice displayed degeneration of the third row of stereocilia compared with region-matched WT OHCs (Fig. 4d). Collectively, these findings support our hypothesis that MYO7A-N plays an important role in OHC function and long-term maintenance and survival.

We then evaluated the hearing function of Myo7a-ΔN mice at 3, 10 and 40 weeks. At 3 weeks, Myo7a-ΔN mice showed a trend toward elevated ABR thresholds along with a significant deterioration of DPOAE response. The hearing loss progressively developed with age, with ABR thresholds becoming significantly elevated and DPOAE responses markedly reduced at 10 and 40 weeks (Fig. 4e, f). While the changes were significant, ABRs and DPOAEs were only mildly compromised in Myo7a-ΔN mice. This is consistent with the fact that deletion of MYO7A-N reduces total MYO7A levels in OHCs maximally by ~60% even at basal regions. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that MYO7A-N is important for hearing function, likely related to its role in OHC function and cochlear amplification.

MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N localize to the UTLD in hair cells in a tonotopically graded manner

MYO7A at the UTLD is proposed to establish resting tension on the tip link by forming a complex with Harmonin (USH1C) and Sans (USH1G), bridging the actin cytoskeleton with the cytosolic domains of the tip-link protein CDH2318,32–34. To test whether MYO7A isoforms exhibit distinct subcellular localizations, particularly at UTLD, we created mouse lines in which we inserted an HA-tag after the start codon of Myo7a-N (Fig. 5a). Together with the HA-Myo7a-C mouse line we created for our previous study20, we visualized the expression and localization of the two isoforms separately at both tissue and subcellular levels.

We first examined HA-MYO7A-C and HA-MYO7A-N levels in the P5 cochlea. As previously reported, HA-MYO7A-C was predominantly expressed in IHCs in all regions of the cochlea (Fig. 5b-d). HA-MYO7A-C was also relatively abundantly expressed in apical OHCs compared to the cochlear base. In contrast, HA-MYO7A-N was mainly expressed in OHCs, with higher levels in the basal region and decreasing toward the apex (Fig. 5b-d). This inversely correlated expression pattern of the HA-MYO7A-C and HA-MYO7A-N isoforms is consistent with the pattern we inferred from isoform-specific deletion mouse lines (Fig. 3b-d).

We next investigated the subcellular localization of MYO7A-N, especially at the functionally important site of the UTLD. As previously shown for the HA-MYO7A-C mice20, we also detected HA-MYO7A-N immunoreactivity at the UTLD of OHCs (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Quantification analysis showed that HA-MYO7A-C is the predominant isoform in IHC UTLDs, consistent with its high expression level in the cell body (statistical analysis in Fig. 5f legend). We also observed a tonotopic gradient of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N in UTLDs. OHCs have the highest HA-MYO7A-N/HA-MYO7A-C ratio in the basal region, decreasing toward the cochlear apex (Fig. 5f).

Whether MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N are maintained at UTLDs throughout adulthood is critical to our hypothesis that MYO7A isoforms contribute to tensioning of the MET complex. We therefore extended our analysis to 40 weeks of age. We focused on apical regions of the HA–MYO7A-C/N organ of Corti to avoid EDTA-based decalcification, which in our experience can reduce antibody binding affinity. We first confirmed that the differential expression pattern of HA-MYO7A-C and HA-MYO7A-N in the cell body is maintained at 40 weeks: IHCs predominantly express HA-MYO7A-C, whereas OHCs express higher levels of HA-MYO7A-N along with lower amounts of HA-MYO7A-C (Supplementary Fig. 3c). More importantly, consistent with these cell-body expression patterns, HA-MYO7A-C was detected in UTLDs of both IHCs and OHCs, while HA-MYO7A-N was detected in OHC UTLDs and was weakly detectable in IHC stereocilia (Supplementary Fig. 3d). These findings suggest that the differential expression of MYO7A isoforms is maintained in adult hair cells and likely plays an important role in adult hair cell function.

Given the proposed role of MYO7A in tip-link tensioning18,20, we hypothesize that the tonotopically graded distribution of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N within the UTLD of OHCs may contribute to the previously reported gradient of tip-link tension along the cochlear tonotopic axis19. This may occur through isoform-specific differences in motor structure and activity. We thus investigated the relationship between the molecular structures and enzymatic functions of the motor domains of the two MYO7A isoforms.

Cryo-EM determination of the MYO7A motor domain structure and comparative analysis with other unconventional myosins

We determined high-resolution cryo-EM structures of recombinant, purified MYO7A-C-2IQ and MYO7A-N-2IQ constructs containing the motor domain and two IQ motifs. Structures were solved in the ADP-bound state and in complex with F-actin. The resulting structures were resolved at 3.1 Å (MYO7A-C-2IQ) and 2.9 Å (MYO7A-N-2IQ) (Fig. 6a, b, validation provided in Supplementary Fig. 4a-c). To our knowledge, these are the first high-resolution experimentally determined structures of the mammalian MYO7A motor domain to be reported.

To investigate how MYO7A differs from other unconventional myosins expressed in hair cells, we performed a multi-sequence alignment and structural divergence analysis using previously reported cryo-EM structures of MYO15A35 and MYO636 bound to ADP and F-actin (Fig. 6c-h). We found that the ATP-binding pocket subdomains (e.g., switch-I, switch-II and the P-loop), as measured by Cα-Cα distances and root mean square deviation of atomic positions (RMSD), remained highly conserved between these three myosins. Detailed structural comparison of ATP-binding pockets of MYO6, MYO15A, and MYO7A is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a, b. Structures of representative human MYO7A mutations that cause Usher syndrome type 1B are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5c.

At the actomyosin interface, however, substantial structural divergences were observed among MYO6, MYO15A and MYO7A, indicated by significant RMSD values. To quantify the interaction between myosin motors and actin, we calculated the buried surface area of each actomyosin interface, which reflects the extent of contact between proteins. Key residues mediating actin binding were also identified. Four structurally divergent loops, cardiomyopathy loop (CM loop), loop-2, loop-3, and loop-4 were analyzed for their contributions to actin binding37. The CM loop contributes to the strong binding state of myosin for actin38–40. Despite the CM loop of MYO6 having five additional residues, its buried surface area (431 Å2) is comparable to MYO7A (430 Å2) and MYO15A (445 Å2) (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 5d). Loop-3 mediates interactions with actini+241–43. In MYO6, this loop contains nine additional residues, including three arginine residues, which significantly increase its buried surface area (317 Å2) compared to MYO7A (192 Å2) and MYO15A (219 Å2) (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 5d). Loop-4 is known for its role in actin binding and tuning motor velocity44–46 and exhibited comparable buried surface areas for MYO6 (357 Å2) and MYO7A (338 Å2), while MYO15A displayed the smallest interface (202 Å2) (Fig. 6g). Loop-2 is suggested to modulate actin-activated ATPase activity47–50. Loop-2 of MYO15A contains ten additional residues, however, loop-2 of MYO15A was unresolved35, suggesting it likely retains flexibility during its interaction with actin (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Fig. 5d). Despite MYO6 containing five additional residues compared to MYO7A, its buried surface area was significantly smaller (182 Å2 vs. 408 Å2) (Fig. 6h, Supplementary Fig. 5d). Overall, our structural analysis highlights sequence and structural divergence among unconventional myosins, particularly at the acto-myosin interface, which likely contribute to their kinetic functional specializations.

Allosteric changes in the MYO7A head domain induced by isoform-specific NTE domains

We next focused on potential differences in the molecular structure of the two MYO7A isoforms. Since MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N differ exclusively by their NTEs, we were particularly interested in the structure of these unique domains. However, the NTEs of both isoforms were unresolved in our cryo-EM derived models, indicating that the NTEs of both isoforms are likely disordered in the ADP-bound state. We therefore explored the possibility that the distinct NTEs of the two isoforms exert long-range allosteric effects on the structure of critical subdomains in the motor domain. While RMSD analysis revealed a high degree of overall structural similarity between the MYO7A-N and -C isoforms - with an RMSD of 1 Å (maximum Cα deviation of 7.3 Å) across 670 residues (Fig. 7a, Supplementary Movie 1) - three regions exhibited significant local divergence, with deviations of Cα pairs exceeding 6 Å, including loop-2, the N-terminal SH3-like domain, and the relay domain. Loop-2, a critical subdomain involved in interactions with actin, is hypothesized to play a role in facilitating the initial docking stage of actomyosin and activating the myosin ATPase51–53. Our cryo-EM analysis revealed notable structural divergence in loop-2 between MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N: 6 residues (MYO7A-C: G600-R606; MYO7A-N: G632-R638) showed RMSD of ~6 Å, which is significantly higher than the overall RMSD (Fig. 7b, Supplementary Fig. 5e). The deviation of these residues contributes to the differences of their buried surface areas with actin, with MYO7A-C showing a 408 Å2 buried surface, compared to only 297 Å2 for MYO7A-N (Fig. 7b, Table 1). We hypothesize that the higher buried surface of MYO7A-C potentially influences the actin-activated ATPase activity.

The next notable structural divergence is in the SH3-like domain, located between the NTE and the main motor domain (Fig. 7c). This domain has been implicated in regulating myosin activity through interactions with the essential light chains (ELC)54,55, or via autoinhibition56. We identified five residues that differ significantly between the isoforms, S33-V38 in MYO7A-C and S65-V70 in MYO7A-N, with RMSD values around 6 Å (Fig. 7c). These residues are part of the N-src loop and the third β-strand of the SH3-like domain. Notably, MYO7A-C also showed a disruption of the β2 and β4 strands, which are intact in MYO7A-N. Given the SH3-like domain’s proximity to the NTE, these differences may arise from conformational influences of the NTEs. Such structural divergence could alter interactions with the ELC or regulatory light chain (RLC), potentially affecting MYO7A motor function.

We also identified potential structural variances in five residues on the C-terminus of the relay helix (MYO7A-C: E475-E480; MYO7A-N: E507-E512), with RMSD ~ 6 Å (Fig. 7d). The relay helix transmits confirmational changes from motor domain to the converter and lever arm57. In our model, the α-helical structure at the C-terminus of the MYO7A-C relay helix appears disrupted (Fig. 7d). Although the converter domain was not resolved in our cryo-EM data, studies of other myosins indicate it interacts directly with the C-terminal end of the relay helix. Thus, the divergence observed between MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N in this region may impact the transmission of conformational changes from the motor domain to the converter.

In summary, our cryo-EM analysis indicates that the unique N-terminal extensions (NTEs) of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N are likely disordered. Despite the overall structural similarity between the two isoforms, three regions, loop-2, the N-terminal SH3-like domain, and the relay helix, exhibit pronounced local differences, consistent with possible long-range allosteric effects mediated by the distinct NTEs.

MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N exhibit distinct ATPase enzymatic properties

We next examined whether the distinct NTEs, along with the observed structural differences in loop-2, the SH3-like domain, and the relay helix, affect the enzymatic and mechanical activities of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N. To test this, we measured the steady-state, actin-activated ATPase activity of purified MYO7A-C with two IQ domains (C-2IQ), and separately MYO7A-N-2IQ (N-2IQ) (Fig. 8a) using the NADH-coupled assay at 25 °C under saturating ATP conditions. By titrating increasing amounts of F-actin into the reaction, the catalytic rate (kcat) of N-2IQ (kcat = 0.05 ± 0.003 s−1) was significantly reduced compared with C-2IQ (kcat = 0.38 ± 0.02 s−1) (Fig. 8b, c). The apparent affinity for F-actin (KATPase), representing the concentration of F-actin required to reach half-maximal ATPase activation, was not significantly different between C-2IQ (KATPase = 2.7 ± 0.5 μM) and N-2IQ (1.4 ± 0.6 μM) (Fig. 8b, d). Basal ATPase rates (without F-actin) were similar between N-2IQ (kbasal = 0.013 ± 0.003 s−1) and C-2IQ (kbasal = 0.028 ± 0.002 s−1). We conclude that in comparison to MYO7A-C, the altered NTE of MYO7A-N reduced the maximal catalytic activity of the ATPase without altering its apparent affinity for F-actin (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 4, the ratio of MYO7A-C to MYO7A-N exhibits a tonotopic gradient, being highest in the basal region and progressively decreasing toward the apex. Given the distinct ATPase activities of MYO7A isoforms, this systematic shift in the MYO7A-C/MYO7A-N ratio may contribute to establishing the tonotopic gradient of tip-link tension (Table 2).

Light chain binding to the MYO7A motor domain can regulate its ATPase activity58,59. We therefore investigated whether this phenomenon might be responsible for the measured differences in ATPase activity between isoforms. MYO7A has five IQ domains that are reported to be a binding site for calmodulin (CALM), regulatory light chain (RLC; MYL12B) and calmodulin-like protein 4 (CALML4)59–63. We co-expressed N-2IQ and C-2IQ with CALM, MYL12B, and CALML4, in addition to essential light chain (ELC, MYL6) that has been reported as a light chain for other non-muscle myosins64. Both N-2IQ and C-2IQ co-purified with a single light chain analyzed by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 8a), and for both isoforms this was unambiguously identified as MYL12B using LC-MS/MS. Purified N-2IQ and C-2IQ holoenzymes were further analyzed by mass photometry, a label-free optical technique to weigh single molecules65. Mass photometry yielded an average molecular weight of 132 ± 33 kDa for C-2IQ, consistent with the ATPase (119 kDa) plus a single RLC (20 kDa), and 126 ± 30 kDa for MYO7A-N-2IQ, also consistent with the ATPase (122 kDa) plus a single MYL12B (20 kDa) (Fig. 8e). We extended this light chain analysis to MYO7A-N and MYO7A-C proteins truncated to retain all 5IQs (N-5IQ and C-5IQ). Both proteins co-purified with 4 distinct low molecular weight light chains, and these were identified as CALM, MYL12B, MYL6 and CALML4 using LC-MS/MS (Fig. 8a). Mass photometry measured average molecular weights of 228 ± 34 kDa (N-5IQ) and 219 ± 26 kDa (C-5IQ) (Fig. 8f). These results were in close agreement with predicted molecular weights of 216 kDa (N-5IQ) and 213 kDa (C-5IQ), calculated assuming an equal stoichiometry of 1:1:1:1 of MYL6, MYL12B, CALM, CALML4 binding to each ATPase. We conclude there was no intrinsic difference in light chain binding between truncated MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N molecules, and that this was unlikely the cause of altered ATPase activity.

To estimate the relative mechanical output of MYO7A-C vs MYO7A-N isoforms, we used an in vitro gliding filament assay66. Imaging chambers were functionalized with either purified C-2IQ or N-2IQ protein, and rhodamine-labelled actin filaments were bound to the myosin-functionalized surface. The velocity of actin filaments, propelled across the surface by myosin activity, was recorded using TIRF microscopy in the presence of saturating ATP at 30 °C (N-2IQ, Supplementary Movie 2; C-2IQ, Supplementary Movie 3; Supplementary Fig. 6a-b). The average gliding velocities were 9.3 ± 2.7 nm•s−1 (C-2IQ) and 8.5 ± 2.3 nm•s−1 (N-2IQ) (p < 0.01), indicating a modest reduction in the mechanical output of N-2IQ compared with C-2IQ (Fig. 8g). In conclusion, our results show differing ATPase activities of MYO7A isoforms, with MYO7A-N having a significantly reduced catalytic rate compared to MYO7A-C.

Discussion

The molecular properties of MYO7A have been extensively studied, in large part due to its causal role in Usher syndrome type 1B22,23,66. Its mutation leads to congenital hearing loss, disorganized hair bundles and ultimately hair cell loss. However, despite this prominence, its precise functional role, particularly in the context of mechanotransduction, has remained incompletely understood. At the stereocilia base, MYO7A forms a multiprotein complex with PCDH15 to establish ankle links62,67,68. At the UTLD, MYO7A, together with Sans and Harmonin, tether the tip links to the underlying actin filaments in the stereocilia, which is crucial for MET channel function18,20. These interactions highlight multiple roles of MYO7A at key sites of force transmission.

Historically studied as a single isoform motor protein, our findings now reveal that MYO7A exists as at least two major functionally distinct isoforms, MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N, that are differentially expressed across hair cell types and along the cochlear tonotopic axis. The combinatorial expression of these two isoforms likely establishes molecular modulations of overall UTLD properties, presumably enabling graded tuning of tip-link tension in a hair cell type- and position-dependent manner.

We show that MYO7A-C predominates in IHCs and decreases from apex to base in OHCs, while MYO7A-N exhibits the inverse gradient, peaking in basal OHCs where high-frequency sounds are detected. A third isoform, MYO7A-S, lacking an NTE, is minimally expressed and dispensable for hearing. We conclude that MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N represent the core MYO7A isoforms in the cochlear hair cells.

Isoform-specific knockout mouse models uncovered distinct yet complementary phenotypes at the cellular and whole-animal level. Our previous study demonstrated that deletion of MYO7A-C reduced the resting open probability (Pₒ) and slowed the onset of the MET current in IHCs20. In contrast, basal OHCs from Myo7a-ΔN mice at P7 showed no significant difference in MET properties, except a modest rightward shift of I-X curve. This result was expected, as MYO7A protein levels are reduced by only ~50–60% in basal OHCs of Myo7a-ΔN, compared with the ~80–90% reduction observed in Myo7a-ΔC IHCs, which appears to be necessary to elicit changes in MET function.

Consistent differences were also observed at the level of auditory function. Myo7a-ΔC mice display profound ABR deficits with preserved DPOAEs, consistent with impaired IHC function. In contrast, Myo7a-ΔN mice develop only mild, progressive hearing loss and reduced DPOAEs, supporting a role for MYO7A-N in OHC maintenance and cochlear amplification. Notably, our ABR and DPOAE measurements are limited to 32 kHz, which corresponds to cochlear regions that do not exhibit the highest level of MYO7A-N (Fig. 3d). Our functional assessments likely underestimate the true severity of hearing loss in Myo7a-ΔN mice, particularly at higher frequencies where MYO7A-N is most enriched.

Distinct NTEs significantly altered ATPase kinetics between MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N, revealing a potential mechanism for tuning MYO7A activity at the UTLD. Notably, the catalytic rate (kcat) of MYO7A-N was significantly reduced compared with MYO7A-C. This enzymatic tuning could be functionally important at the UTLD: MYO7A-C may support faster, more dynamic cycling on stereocilia actin filaments, whereas MYO7A-N may sustain higher tension by increasing the dwell time in strong actin-bound states. These properties are consistent with the higher tip-link tension observed in basal OHCs19, where MYO7A-N is the dominant isoform. Although we did not directly measure enzymatic activity for the short isoform, MYO7A-S, prior work has shown that its ATPase activity is significantly lower than MYO7A-C59. Our results thus establish a spectrum of MYO7A isoform activities, in which NTE differences correspond to distinct motor outputs.

To understand how motor activity is tuned among MYO7A isoforms, we determined high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the force-generating ATPase (motor) domains of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N. Structural models of the mammalian MYO7A lever arm69 and tail domains34 have been reported, but previous analyses of the motor domain have relied upon molecular dynamics70 or AlphaFold predictions59. Although our experimentally determined motor domain structures are highly similar overall, we identified three key regions that significantly diverge between MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N: loop-2, the SH3-like domain, and the relay helix. Although the NTEs themselves were unresolved in our structures due to potential disorder or conformational flexibility, we propose that they allosterically modulate the MYO7A motor domain and thereby contribute to the divergence observed in in these regions.

Loop-2 is a critical surface loop that forms part of the actin binding interface and influences the actin-activated ATPase activity of myosin motors71. Consistent with this role, we determined that loop-2 of MYO7A-C exhibits a larger buried surface area with actin compared with MYO7A-N, potentially contributes to the observed differences in ATPase activity. Our structures also revealed isoform-specific differences in the SH3-like domain at the N-terminus of the motor domain. Mutations in the SH3-like domain can modulate the ATPase activity of class II myosins72, potentially through interactions with the most proximal light chain on the lever arm54,73,74. We could not assess whether the SH3-like domain of MYO7A formed a similar interface as our cryo-EM derived models did not resolve the lever arm and light chains. Structural determinations of MYO7A isoforms in different nucleotide states (i.e. apo, ADP-vanadate) may help elucidate if this interaction occurs and further explain how structural divergence between isoforms contribute to the reduced catalytic rate of MYO7A-N.

How might the different motor activities of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N be mechanically integrated at the UTLD? MYO7A can form a processive motor by using its tail domain to dimerize in the presence of cargo binding proteins59,75,76. Because MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N share common tail domains, it is likely that homo- and heterodimeric species will form at the UTLD. The properties of MYO7A heterodimers are unknown but will likely exhibit altered activity, similar to hetero oligomers of class II myosins77. Another emerging mechanism to integrate MYO7A isoform activity at the UTLD is through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). The UTLD in hair cells is proposed to assemble via LLPS, and MYO7A forms biomolecular condensates in vitro with UTLD-associated proteins, including harmonin (USH1C) and sans (USH1G)78. Changing ratios of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N within a UTLD condensate may alter its mechanical properties and/or its interaction with stereocilia actin filaments. Characterizing these effects is an important area of future research and will help explain how tonotopic gradients of MYO7A-C vs MYO7A-N contribute to tuning of the hair cell MET machinery.

Our study underscores a broader principle in the myosin superfamily that isoform-specific NTEs can contribute to unique cellular specializations by modulating motor activity. This phenomenon is not unique to MYO7A. A similar role for NTEs has been described for class I myosins, where they control motor output and force sensitivity79. Likewise, MYO15A isoforms that differ solely in their NTEs, localize to distinct rows within the hair bundle and regulate stereocilia size during different stages of development80,81. Taking together, our findings suggest that MYO7A isoform diversity, driven in part by NTEs, provides a molecular mechanism for tuning tip-link tension to meet the distinct mechanical demands of different hair cell types.

A central question raised by our findings is why distinct MYO7A isoforms, each with different motor kinetics, are differentially expressed across hair cell types and along the tonotopic axis. There are notable physiological differences between IHCs and OHCs, along with tonotopic dependencies that correlate with the expression of these MYO7A isoforms. For example, resting open probability, considered a proxy for tip-link tension, differs significantly between hair cell populations, with IHCs exhibiting ~10-20% Po and OHCs ~50%19. Additionally, tip-link tension increases from apex to base in OHCs19, paralleling the gradient of MYO7A-N expression. These correlations suggest that MYO7A isoforms might contribute to spatial tuning of MET tension, potentially optimizing hair cell function across frequencies.

Two models may explain the mechanistic role of MYO7A isoforms in this context: a “dynamic motor” model and a “static anchor” model. The dynamic motor model, historically proposed to explain tip-link tension generation3,32,75,82, directly aligns with our current and previous findings20. It posits that myosin molecules actively contribute to tip-link tension. In this framework, multiple MYO7A molecules interact with the tip link or scaffold proteins at the UTLD, and their dynamic upward movement generates tip-link tension. This model is supported by evidence that MYO7A functions as a processive motor in various organisms58,60,83,84. Our structural and biochemical data suggest MYO7A-C hydrolyzes ATP more rapidly, enabling quicker detachment-reattachment cycling, while MYO7A-N has markedly lower ATPase activity, suggesting longer lifetimes in the strongly bound, post-power stroke state of the myosin cycle, potentially generating a higher sustained force3,32,75,82. The ratio of MYO7A-C to MYO7A-N at the UTLD could therefore modulate tension on the MET apparatus. This could contribute to frequency tuning MET in OHCs, serving as a molecular frequency filter in addition to the tuning provided by basilar membrane mechanics and other mechanisms.

Alternatively, the static anchor model proposes that MYO7A serves primarily as a structural anchor rather than a force generator. In this model, MYO7A isoforms do not actively pull on tip links but instead just stabilize their connections to actin filaments. The MYO7A-C to MYO7A-N ratio could modulate actin-binding affinity, allowing the UTLD to accommodate varying resting tensions set by other mechanisms, such as MET-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling or changes in membrane tension25. This model aligns with recent findings showing that MYO7A is essential for tip-link stability24.

These models are not mutually exclusive because myosin function can vary with the mechanical load. For example, MYO6 can act as either a cargo transporter or a force-bearing anchor depending on force load83. Similarly, MYO7A may function as a processive motor during development to establish tip-link tension and then transition to an anchor that sustains this tension after the MET apparatus matures. While the motor and anchor models provide plausible mechanisms for how MYO7A isoforms contribute to tip-link tensioning, these hypotheses remain speculative and require further experimental validation.

Our findings also have clear implications for inner ear gene therapy. Current approaches typically target the canonical MYO7A isoform85,86. However, the differential expression and distinct functional roles of MYO7A-C and MYO7A-N in hearing suggest that isoform-specific replacement may be necessary for optimal therapeutic outcomes. Meanwhile, our long-read RNA-seq data provides a broad view of transcriptome landscapes across inner-ear cell types. Beyond MYO7A, hair cells likely express specific isoforms to match functional demands (e.g., PCDH1587, MYO15A80,81, harmonin-B88, and OTOF27). An important future direction will be to leverage this isoform information to optimize gene therapy by selecting the most appropriate isoform for therapeutic expression.

Furthermore, our long-read RNA-seq data highlights its potential for identifying previously unannotated transcription start sites. When combined with complementary approaches (e.g., CAGE-seq and ATAC-seq), this strategy should enable discovery of novel promoters that can be tailored to specific gene-expression needs. For example, the spatial specificity of the Myo7a-N expression pattern could be leveraged to drive transgene expression in OHCs with a tonotopic gradient. Overall, our study provides a conceptual framework for developing more precise, cell type- and region-specific gene therapy tools.

Method

Animal care and handling

The care and use of animals for all experiments described conformed to NIH guidelines. Experimental mice were housed in a 12:12 hour light:dark cycle with free access to chow and water in standard laboratory cages in a temperature and humidity-controlled vivarium. The protocols for the care and use of animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at the University of Virginia, the University of Colorado Denver, and the National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders. All the institutions mentioned above are accredited by the American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. C57BL/6 J (Bl6, from Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA) mice and sibling mice served as control mice for this study. Neonatal mouse pups (P0-P5) were sacrificed by rapid decapitation. Mature mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation.

Cochlea single-cell long-read RNA Sequencing

The sample preparation protocol was adapted from a previous publication by the Kelley lab. The organs of Corti were dissected from approximately ten postnatal day 7 (P7) mice of both sexes in cold Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The collected samples were incubated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 10 min. To quench trypsin activity, an equal volume of fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added. The samples were then gently triturated 40 times on ice, followed by filtration through a 40 μm cell strainer. Single cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) containing 0.04% FBS. Cell concentration and viability were assessed using an automated cell counter (RWD-C100, RWD Life Science, TX, USA). Samples with a cell concentration of 800-1000 cells/μL and a viability of >95% were used for sequencing.

Single-cell cDNA library preparation was performed at the Genome Analysis and Technology Core, University of Virginia. The EpiMultiome ATAC + Gene Expression kit (10x Genomics, CA, USA) was used to generate single-cell cDNA libraries, targeting a recovery of 10,000 cells. For long-read sequencing, 75 ng of the single-cell cDNA library was used as the recommended input for Kinnex library preparation. The Kinnex kit (103-072-200, Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA) was used to generate concatemers for long-read sequencing. Library quality was assessed using a Qubit fluorometer (Q33238, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (G2939BA, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The final Kinnex library was submitted to Maryland Genomics (MD, USA) for sequencing on the PacBio Revio system (102-090-600, Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA).

Raw sequencing files were processed using the PacBio Iso-Seq package (version 4.2.0, https://isoseq.how/). Processed reads were mapped to the mouse genome (GRCm39, M36). Transcript classification was performed using Pigeon (version 1.3.0, https://isoseq.how/classification/pigeon.html). Single-cell analysis was conducted using the Seurat package (version 5.2.0, https://satijalab.org/seurat/). All bioinformatics analyses and computations were performed on the University of Virginia High-Performance Computing systems.

5’ Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends (RACE)

5’RACE is conducted to validate the transcriptional start sites of Myo7a isoforms. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596026, ThermoFisher Scientific) from ~10 P7 animals. Then the RACE is performed using 5’ RACE System for Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends, version 2.0 (18374058, ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the protocol. E. coli cDNA library containing Myo7a transcripts are cloned by TA Cloning™ Kit (K202020, ThermoFisher Scientific). E. coli colonies are selected by Myo7a isoforms specific primers. Positive colonies are then sent for whole plasmid Sanger sequencing (Eurofins genomics DNA Sequencing Lab, KY, USA) to identify the sequence of Myo7a transcripts.

Primers

Immunofluorescence

Inner ear organs were fixed in 3% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA) immediately after dissection for 20 min. Samples were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (GIBCO® PBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 5 min each. After blocking for 2 h with blocking buffer (1% bovine serum albumin, 3% normal donkey serum, and 0.2% saponin in PBS), tissues were incubated in blocking buffer containing primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The following antibodies were used in this study: rabbit polyclonal Myosin-VIIa antibody (catalog#: 25-6790, Proteus Biosciences Inc, Ramona, CA. 1:100), mouse monoclonal Myosin-VI antibody (A-9, Santa Cruz, 1:100), and rabbit anti-HA antibody (C29F4, Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA, 1:200). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Leica Stellaris 5 Confocal Microscope Platform or Zeiss LSM880 with AiryScan.

Hearing tests in mice

ABRs of WT and Myo7a-ΔC mice at P17, 4-week, 6-week, and 9-week were determined. Mice were anesthetized with a single intraperitoneal injection of 100 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Fort Dodge Animal Health) and 10 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride (Lloyd Laboratories). ABR and DPOAE were performed in a sound-attenuating cubicle (Med-Associates, product number: ENV-022MD-WF), and mice were kept on a Deltaphase isothermal heating pad (Braintree Scientific) to maintain body temperature. ABR recording equipment was purchased from Intelligent Hearing Systems (Miami, Fl) and sound output was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s procedure. Recordings were captured by subdermal needle electrodes (FE-7; Grass Technologies). The non-inverting electrode was placed at the vertex of the midline, the inverting electrode over the mastoid of the right ear, and the ground electrode on the upper thigh. The electrodes were connected to a one-Channel Opti-Amp Transmitter (Intelligent Hearing Systems, REF# M013009P), interfaced to the Intelligent Hearing Systems Universal Smart Box (REF# M011110). Stimulus tones (pure tones) were delivered at a rate of 21.1/s through a high-frequency transducer (Intelligent Hearing Systems, REF# M014600). Response acquisition is performed by SmartEP software (ver 5.41.02, Intelligent Hearing Systems). Responses were filtered at 300-3000 Hz, and threshold levels were determined from 1024 stimulus presentations at 8, 11.3, 16, 22.4, and 32 kHz. Stimulus intensity was decreased in 5-10 dB steps until a response waveform could no longer be identified. Stimulus intensity was then increased in 5 dB steps until a waveform could again be identified. If a waveform could not be identified at the maximum output of the transducer, a value of 5 dB was added to the maximum output as the threshold. Sound pressure level (dB SPL) is used for data presentation.

DPOAEs of the same group of WT and Myo7a-ΔC mice were recorded. While under anesthesia for ABR testing, DPOAE was recorded using SmartOAE ver. 5.20 (Intelligent Hearing Systems). A range of pure tones from 8 to 32 kHz (16 sweeps) was used to obtain the DPOAE for the right ear. DPOAE recordings were made for f2 frequencies from 8.8 to 35.3 kHz using a paradigm set as follows: L1 = 65 dB, L2 = 55 dB SPL, and f2/f1 = 1.22.

Scanning electron microscopy

Adult mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation before intracardiac perfusion with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) and 2% PFA. The otic capsule was dissected and incubated in postfixation buffer at 4 °C overnight (2.5% glutaraldehyde, in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, with 3 mM CaCl2). For neonatal mouse pups, the samples were dissected and treated with postfixation buffer immediately. The otic capsules from adult mice were incubated for two weeks in 4.13% EDTA for decalcification and then further dissected to expose the organ of Corti. Samples underwent the OTOTO procedure and were dehydrated using gradient ethanol and critical point drying. After sputter coating with platinum, the samples were imaged on Zeiss Sigma VP HD field emission SEM using the in-lens secondary electron detector.

Whole-cell voltage-clamp electrophysiology recordings

Preparation and recordings

Animals were euthanized by decapitation using methods approved by the University of Colorado IACUC. Organs of Corti were acutely dissected from P6 mice. Tissue was viewed using a 100× (1.0 NA, Olympus) water-immersion objective with a Phantom Miro 320 s camera on a Slicescope (Scientifica) illuminated with a TLED + 525 nm LED (Sutter Instruments). Tissue was dissected and perfused with extracellular solution containing (in mM): 140 NaCl, 2 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 2 creatine monohydrate, 2 Na-pyruvate, 2 ascorbic acid, 6 dextrose, pH = 7.4, 300–310 mOsm. In addition, an apical perfusion, with pipettes tip sizes of 150–300 μm, provided local perfusion to the hair bundles.

Electrophysiological recordings

Whole-cell patch-clamp was achieved on OHCs from basal cochlear turns using an Axon 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices) with thick-walled borosilicate patch pipettes (2-6 MΩ) filled with an intracellular solution containing (in mM): 92 CsCl, 3.5 MgCl2, 5 ATP, 5 creatine phosphate, 10 HEPES, 10 cesium BAPTA, 3 ascorbic acid, pH = 7.2, 280-290 mOsm. Experiments were performed at 18–22 °C. Whole-cell currents were filtered at 10 kHz and sampled at 100 kHz using a USB-6356 (National Instruments) controlled by jClamp (SciSoft Company). All experiments used −80 mV holding potential, uncorrected for liquid junction potential. Only cells with initially <200 pA of leak current were kept for data analysis.

Hair bundle stimulation and motion recording

Hair bundles are stimulated with a custom 3D printed fluid jet driven by a piezoelectric disc bender (27 mm 4.6 kHz; Murata Electronics 7BB-27-4L0). Thin-wall borosilicate pipettes were pulled to tip diameters of 5–20 μm, filled with extracellular solution, and mounted in the fluid-jet stimulator. The piezo disc bender was driven by waveforms generated using jClamp and the signals were filtered with an eight-pole Bessel filter at 1 kHz (L8L 90PF, Frequency Devices Inc.) and variably attenuated (PA5, Tucker Davis) before being sent to a high voltage/high current amplifier (Crawford Amplifier) to drive the piezoelectric disc. During stimulations, videos were taken of the hair bundle motion with high-speed imaging at 10,000 frames per second using the Phantom Miro 320 s. Videos were saved for each stimulation and analyzed offline.

Analysis

All data were analyzed offline using jClamp (SciSoft), Matlab (MathWorks), Excel (Microsoft), and Prism 7 (GraphPad). Figures were generated using Matlab, Prism 7, and Adobe Illustrator.

MYO7A-C / N was purified from frozen Sf9 as follows. Cell pellets were lysed using a Dounce homogenizer chilled on ice in extraction buffer (10 mM MOPS pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 0.2 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors (Halt EDTA-free; Thermo Scientific). Cell lysates were sedimented at 48,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C (Beckman JA-20). The supernatant was removed and incubated for 3 hours on ice with FLAG-M2 affinity gel (Sigma-Aldrich). FLAG-M2 affinity gel was packed into a gravity-flow column and washed with high-salt buffer (10 mM MOPS pH 7.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 0.2 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM DTT, 1 mM ATP, 1 mM NaN3, 1 ug/mL leupeptin), followed by a low-salt buffer (10 mM MOPS pH 7.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 0.2 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM DTT, 1 mM NaN3, 1 ug/mL leupeptin). Actin purification and labeling

Actin was extracted from rabbit skeletal acetone powder (Pel-Freeze) in G-buffer (2 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 0.2 mM NaATP, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM NaN3, and 0.5 mM DTT) and purified by cycles of polymerization and depolymerization, as described93. For preparation of fluorescently labeled actin filaments, F-actin was incubated with equimolar rhodamine-phalloidin (Invitrogen) for 30 mins before use, in a buffer of 20 mM MOPS pH7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EGTA, 100 mM KCl, and 1 mM DTT.

Cryo-EM Sample preparation and data collection

Lacey grids were glow-discharged for 20 s using a GloQube. 3 μL of F-actin at 0.5 μM concentration was applied to a lacey grid, incubated for 5 s, then aspirated. Next, 3 μL of the F-actin was applied to the grid, then 3 μL of myosin at 3 μM was applied to the grid and incubated for 5 s. 3 μL was aspirated and the rest of sample was back blotted for 3 s at 99% humidity using a Leica EM GP. Grids were flash-frozen in liquid ethane.

Data collection was done at 300 keV on a Titan Krios (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan). 40-frame movies were collected at 1.08 Å/px with total fluence of 50e-/A2. On-the-fly data preprocessing and quality control were performed using cryoSPARC Live. Motion correction and CTF estimation were done in cryoSPARC (ver. 4.3.1).

Regions of F-actin with myosin bound were manually selected from several micrographs to generate 2D class averages used for automatic template-based particle picking by Template Picker in cryoSPARC. Particles were extracted with box size 512×512 px and subjected to several rounds of 2D classification. For the F-actin/MYO7A-C2IQ a homogenous refinement job was done using particles containing F-actin with bound myosin and a volume generated during cryoSPARC Live session. Per-particle defocus refinement was done using Local CTF Refinement job, and then homogenous refinement was run again. Discrete heterogenous states of F-actin/MYO7A-C-2IQ were analyzed by 3D Classification in cryoSPARC using particles with orientations and shifts from homogenous refinement job, a mask focused on the myosin motor domain, and four classes as input. The resulting volumes were visually inspected and particles from a class with well-defined continuous density for the myosin motor domain were taken for another round of homogenous refinement. An initial MYO7A-C-2IQ model was generated by AlphaFold, and rebuilt to fit the density obtained from the 3D reconstruction. The model was refined in Coot and Phenix using the density obtained from homogenous refinement before post-processing by EM Ready. For the F-actin/MYO7-N-2IQ a starting volume of the F-actin/MYO7-C-2IQ structure was low-pass filtered and used in homogenous refinement. CTF refinement was then performed on the particles. The final MYO7-N-2IQ structure was generated from a local refinement job with a mask around the myosin actin binding site. The output volume was post-processed by EM Ready. Modeling of the F-actin/MYO7-N-2IQ was performed in same manner as above.

Cryo-EM analysis, including calculation of RMSD, buried surface area, mutation modeling and visual representation was performed using UCSF ChimeraX. Subdomain identifications were based on previous publications and conservation analysis by using the BLAT tool from Uniprot Database (https://www.uniprot.org/).

Mass photometry

The mass of purified MYO7A-C/N holoenzymes was measured using label-free mass photometry (OneMP, Refeyn). High tolerance #1.5 coverslips (Thorlabs, 24 ×50 mm) were cleaned by isopropanol and ddH2O with sonication for 5 min each, then dried under a stream of filtered nitrogen. A silicon gasket was placed on the cleaned coverslips to create sample chambers of ~2 mm diameter. MYO7A holoenzymes were diluted to a final concentration of 50 nM in 10 mM MOPS pH 7.0, 250 mM KCl, 0.15 mM EGTA, and 1 mM NaN3, and added to the sample chamber. Single molecule landing events were collected for 90 seconds using AcquireMP (Refeyn), and data were filtered and processed using DiscoverMP (Refeyn). The mass distribution of single MYO7A molecules was fitted with a Gaussian curve using Prism (GraphPad v10).

Steady-state ATPase and motility assays

Steady-state ATPase activity of the MYO7A holoenzyme (200 nM) was measured using a NADH enzyme-linked assay in a final buffer of 10 mM MOPS pH 7.0, 25 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.15 mM EGTA, 2 mM MgATP, 200 µM NADH, 40 units/mL lactate dehydrogenase, 200 units/mL pyruvate kinase and 1 mM phosphoenolpyruvate. The timecourse of NADH absorbance was measured at 340 nm using a dual-beam spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu) with a multicell cuvette thermostatically held at 25 °C. The rate of ATP consumption was calculated from gradient of 340 nm absorbance. Each measured rate was corrected for basal ATPase activity in the absence of F-actin, and for the intrinsic ATPase activity of F-actin. ATP hydrolysis rates were fit to the Michaelis-Menten equation to estimate kcat and KATPase using Prism (GraphPad v10).

For gliding filament assays, lean cover glass (#1.5, 24 × 50 mm) was coated with 0.1% nitrocellulose in amyl acetate (Ladd Industries) and attached to a glass slide using two strips of double-sided adhesive tape spaced 3 mm apart to form an imaging chamber. The chamber was incubated for 5 mins with a GFP antibody (0.1 mg/mL, GFP-20, G6539, Sigma-Aldrich), blocked with 1 mg/mL BSA (A0281, Sigma-Aldrich), and washed with 20 mM MOPS pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EGTA, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT. Chambers were incubated with purified MYO7A holoenzyme (0.3 mg/mL) for 1 min and washed. To block any non-functional myosin heads, chambers were then incubated with 2 µM phalloidin stabilized F-actin that was shredded through a fine-gauge needle to generate small filaments. After a 1 min incubation, chambers were washed with wash buffer supplemented with 2 mM ATP. Chambers were next incubated with rhodamine-labeled F-actin (100 nM) for 1 min, and then washes into motility buffer (20 mM MOPS pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EGTA, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM ATP, 50 mM DTT, 3 mM glucose, 100 µg/mL glucose oxidase, and 20 g/mL catalase) at 30 °C. Filaments were visualized using total internal reflectance fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy (Nikon H-TIRF) using a 100x oil objective (N.A 1.49, Nikon Apo TIRF) and an inverted microscope stand (Ti2-E). Time-lapse images were captured on an EM-CCD camera (Andor iXon DU-897) controlled by NIS-Elements (AR version 5.42.06, Nikon). Images were processed in FiJi94 and stage drift removed using the Stabilizer plugin. Filament velocities were calculated using TrackMate95.
