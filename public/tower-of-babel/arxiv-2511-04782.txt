Abstract
Depending on the persistence of the underlying Markov chain shock, the standard New Keynesian model predicts starkly different conclusions at the Effective Lower Bound. We clear up this morass by using a truncated Markov chain. We prove that the expectations-driven trap à la Mertens & Ravn, 2014 doesn’t arise as an equilibrium outcome. In addition, the equilibrium under a truncated Markov chain is guaranteed to be unique, the effect of government spending is positive on consumption and does not switch signs but may grow unbounded —a puzzle.

Keywords: Truncated Markov Chain, Effective Lower Bound, Fiscal Policy

Introduction

With the Great Recession and the Covid-19 crises, advanced economies have experienced two long-lasting episodes where the Central Bank’s interest rate was stuck at its Effective Lower Bound —henceforth ELB. Given the secular decline in interest rates that have been observed across the board for the same countries, they are bound to experience it again sometime in the near future. Against this backdrop, do we have a reliable framework to think about the effects of policy at the ELB?

In this paper, we argue that the standard New Keynesian (NK) model with the standard shock structure that is often used to understand the mechanisms of policy transmission at the ELB may not be a reliable framework. To understand why, note that ELB episodes in the standard NK model are often modeled as a result of shocks that follow a standard 2-state absorbing Markov chain. This allows one to replicate occasional, but long-lasting ELB episodes as argued in
Dordal-i Carreras et al., 2016
. Let us denote by
p\in(0,1)
the probability that this shock returns to its absorbing state every period.
Eggertsson, 2011
shows that there exists a threshold probability
\overline{p}
such that if
0<p<\overline{p}
then one can end up at the ELB with a sufficiently large negative demand shock. Assuming a perfectly correlated government spending shock, he proves that such a policy
crowds private consumption in
at the ELB. Using a similar setup but assuming that
\overline{p}<p<1
instead,
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
show that one has two Minimum State Variable (MSV) equilibria, one of which features a binding ELB. They interpret this as a sunspot equilibrium and show that government spending
crowds private consumption out
. In addition,
Ascari & Mavroeidis, 2022
shows that in that situation, if the underlying shock is too large, no MSV equilibrium exists.

In a seminal paper,
Leeper et al., 2017
study the fiscal multiplier in a rich set of medium scale models without an ELB constraint. They show that the effects of fiscal policy vary a lot from model to model and conclude that this constitutes a
morass.
We argue that such a morass is present in the standard New Keynesian model with an occasionally binding ELB constraint. As we have seen, the effects of fiscal policy can flip signs depending on the source of the ELB episode, but the same happens for any other policy in that model. Because of this, there have been many attempts to use structural approaches to tease these two situations apart, see
Aruoba et al., 2018
and more recently
Cai et al., 2025
. Both find that short run output multipliers crowd consumption out in Japan, but the reverse hold for the U.S. For a long expected duration of the ELB however,
Cai et al., 2025
find that consumption is crowded in even in Japan.

In this paper, we take a different approach and show that truncating the Markov chain results in a framework with consistent policy recommendations.
Following
Eggertsson & Woodford, 2003
, we assume that there exists a maximum time period after which the Markov chain has to return to its absorbing state. This shock structure will have a number of interesting properties. First, we show that under such a truncated Markov chain there exists only one MSV equilibrium. As a result, non-existence of an MSV equilibrium never arises in our framework. Therefore, one can consider policy experiments that are well-defined regardless of the degree of persistence or the size of the shock.

Zooming in on fiscal policy, we find that regardless of the persistence level, consumption is
crowded in
. This is in stark contrast with the conclusions reached in
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
for a similar model. To understand why, let us denote by
\ell
the maximum duration after which the Markov chain goes back to its absorbing state. In this framework, one can compute in closed form the impact multiplier effect of government spending as a function of
\ell
. It turns out that this effect obeys a simple second order recursion of the form
\mathscr{M}(\ell)=a\cdot\mathscr{M}(\ell-1)+b\cdot\mathscr{M}(\ell-2)
, where
\mathscr{M}(\ell)
denotes the multiplier effect for a maximum duration of
\ell
and where the coefficients
a,b
are functions of structural parameters. Note that the initial conditions of this recursion are given by the pair
\mathscr{M}(1),\mathscr{M}(2)
: the effects under a shock that can last at most 1 and 2 periods. We show that the solution studied in
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
is indeed a fixed point of this recursion. However, the assumption of
\overline{p}<p<1
coupled with a large enough shock precludes the recursion from converging to its fixed point: it diverges away instead. Thus if one assumes
0<p<\overline{p}
instead, as
\ell\to\infty
the recursion converges to its fixed point, which in turn coincides with the constant equilibrium constructed in
Eggertsson, 2011
.

Mathematically, one can solve for the recursion and express
\mathscr{M}(\ell)
for a given
\ell
as a geometric sum with persistence
p
as its main argument. For a large enough demand shock, the coefficients
a
and
b
will reflect dynamics at the ELB. In that situation, it turns out that the radius of convergence for this geometric series as
\ell\to\infty
is given by
0<p<\overline{p}
. In this context, the equilibrium/multiplier constructed in
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
reflects the
analytical continuation
of the geometric series. Strictly speaking, the geometric series is not defined for
p>\overline{p}
, but its analytical continuation is.

Beyond the mathematical properties, the sign flip for the fiscal multiplier can also be given an intuition using a simple aggregate supply/demand graph. To construct this graph, we need to consider the following experiment. Assume that the Markov chain remains in its low state all the way until the maximum date
\ell
. In this context, the economy will settle to a medium run
constant
equilibrium before eventually returning to its absorbing state immediately at time period
\ell
. As
\ell\to\infty
, this construction is exactly the one considered in
Eggertsson, 2011
and
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
. If the aggregate supply/demand curves only cross once inside/outside the ELB, our method gives the same result as in
Eggertsson, 2011
in the limit. If the demand shock is too large so that these two curves do not cross, then our method guarantees that the ELB is binding for any possible duration
\ell
. If the curves cross twice, then the outcome depends on the size of the demand shock. If the demand shock is small our method converges on an outcome where the ELB never binds. If the demand shock is large enough instead, there exists a value of
\underline{\ell}
such that the ELB binds for
\ell\geq\underline{\ell}
.

This graphical approach also sheds some light on whether the NK model delivers the well known puzzles (see
Michaillat & Saez, 2021
) at the ELB. Indeed, it has been shown (see
Bilbiie, 2022
) that the NK model displays a bifurcation at the ELB around
\overline{p}
where it produces unbounded outcomes. Using our method, we show two things. First,
p>\overline{p}
is a
necessary
but not sufficient condition for the puzzles to arise. We need the underlying demand shock to be large enough in magnitude in addition. If the demand shock is not large enough, then there are no puzzles regardless of the level of persistence. Second, the puzzles arise for a much larger region of the state space. Let us denote by
\overline{d}(p)
the minimum value of the demand shock such that the model ends up at the ELB eventually. While in the cited literature the puzzles arise in the vicinity of
\overline{p}
, in our case these arise in
[\overline{p}\ \ 1]\times[\overline{d}(p)\ \ d^{max}]
, where
d^{max}
is the maximum value of the shock.

Resolving the Morass

In this section we depart from most of the recent literature in that we follow
Eggertsson & Woodford, 2003
as well as
Eggertsson et al., 2021
and consider a truncated Markov chain.

Definition 3.1

(Truncated Markov Chain)
.

We assume a stochastic process for
d_{t}
with an initial value of
d
. Up until
\ell
time periods, the process follows a Markov chain with the transition matrix
\mathbf{P}
introduced earlier. If the process is not back to its absorbing state of 0 after
\ell
periods, then it is forced to.

Corollary 0.1
.

In the limit as
\ell\to\infty
, the stochastic process boils down to the standard 2-state Markov process used in the literature.

The main difference between the truncated Markov chain and the standard Markov chain used in the literature is that the latter lacks a terminal condition. Indeed, even though this happens with a vanishingly low probability, it is possible that a standard Markov chain remains in its low state for an arbitrarily long period of time. Given the forward-looking nature of the standard NK model, papers in the literature have to consider this case when computing conditional expectations. To say it differently, a Markov chain has a memoryless property. This means that, no matter how long the chain has spent in the low state, the probability to transition to the absorbing is always
1-p
.

In contrast, our truncated Markov chain features a well-defined terminal condition. This implies that the model itself has a well-defined terminal condition: the non-stochastic steady state with zero inflation. More precisely, as is well known (see
Benhabib et al., 2001
), the standard New Keynesian model features two non-stochastic steady states. We will follow the literature and focus on the well-behaved steady state where the ELB is not binding in the long run. In that context, our objective is to guarantee that, conditional on a demand shock
d
in the short run, there is a unique MSV path back to the well-behaved steady state. For context, the equilibrium concept studied in
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
has two MSV paths back to steady state.

4
Characterizing Fiscal Policy at the ELB

The framework that we have developed in the previous section gives a unique answer to the question: what happens after the economy is hit with a negative demand shock? In that setup, as we have seen, the economy ends up at the ELB if the shock is both large and persistent enough. We do not need to invoke a sunspot for that to happen. Therefore, this framework helps us understand
how
and under which conditions the economy ends up at the ELB.

Ultimately however, policymakers want to know what to do once they find themselves in that position. In other words, we want to understand the effect of policy in that context. In the existing literature, the standard New Keynesian model only gives mixed recommendations: if the economy is at the ELB because of a mildly persistent demand shock, then increasing government spending will crowd private consumption in. If the economy is at the ELB because there are two MSV equilibria and we pick the one with a binding ELB, then increasing government spending crowds private consumption out. The objective of this section is to show that the framework that we have developed in the previous section does
not
give mixed recommendations at the ELB: the sign of the effect on consumption never flips.

Before we go on to describe the effects of government spending at the ELB, we first establish some results about the government spending multiplier in a pure normal time regime. Given the result in the previous section, this regime applies if the demand shock is such that

5
Conclusion

Using a truncated Markov chain, we have shown that one can study the unique path back to the intended steady state in a standard New Keynesian model with an occasionally binding ELB. That shock structure gave us some insights about the results in
Eggertsson, 2011
and
Mertens & Ravn, 2014
. The first one arises as a limit of our approach when persistence is low, but the second one doesn’t. In that context, we have found that fiscal policy through higher expenditures unambiguously increases private consumption at the ELB.

The simple framework that we have considered allowed us to prove formally a number of existence and uniqueness results. It also lead us to the conclusion that the range of parameters that injects a puzzle in the model is much larger than commonly believe.

The natural question that arises then is: what would the prevalence of puzzles look like in a more general model that features endogenous persistence in the form of capital accumulation, consumption habit or inflation indexation? We are tackling this issue in ongoing work.

\psi\tilde{\pi}_{t+\ell-1}^{L}\leq-\mu

\psi\lambda(\sigma\mu-d)\leq-\mu\quad\Rightarrow\quad d\geq\sigma\mu+\frac{\mu}{\lambda\psi}.

(A.6)

Let us define the threshold
\bar{d}(1)\equiv\sigma\mu+\frac{\mu}{\lambda\psi}=\frac{\mu}{\lambda\psi}(1+\lambda\sigma\psi)
. The conditions for the validity of the solution in each case are therefore
d<\bar{d}(1)
for the Normal Time regime and
d\geq\bar{d}(1)
for the ELB regime. These two conditions are mutually exclusive and exhaustive over the domain of
d
. Thus, for any given value of
d
, a valid solution exists in one and only one of the two cases. This concludes the proof.
∎

A.2
Proof of Proposition
3

Lemma 1

(Reversion to Steady State)
.

Under Assumption
2
, if the shock process ends by period
t+\ell
, the unique non-explosive equilibrium path for all subsequent periods is the zero steady state, i.e.,
\tilde{X}_{t+\ell+k}=\mathbf{0}_{2\times 1}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Once the shock has ended (for periods
j\geq t+\ell
), we have
d_{j}=0
and the system becomes deterministic. We define the state vector as
X_{j}\equiv[\tilde{x}_{j},\tilde{\pi}_{j}]^{\prime}
. The model can then be written in the forward-looking form
\mathbb{E}_{j}X_{j+1}=JX_{j}
, where
J=A_{1}^{-1}A_{0}
. The eigenvalues of this transition matrix
J
determine the system’s dynamics.

As shown in the main text, the matrix
A
in the backward-looking representation
X_{j}=AX_{j+1}
is the inverse of
J
. The eigenvalues of
A
,
\Lambda_{A}
, are the reciprocals of the eigenvalues of
J
,
\Lambda_{J}
. Assumption
2
ensures that the eigenvalues of
A
are within the unit circle (
|\Lambda_{A}|<1
), which is the standard Blanchard-Kahn condition.

Equivalently, this implies that the eigenvalues of the forward-looking matrix
J
are all outside the unit circle (
|\Lambda_{J}|>1
). For a deterministic system, this means that the only non-explosive path is for the state to be at the steady state,
X_{j}=\mathbf{0}_{2\times 1}
. Therefore, once the shock ends, the system immediately and permanently reverts to the zero steady state.
∎

Proof.

We prove the uniqueness of the solution path
\{\tilde{x}_{t+k},\tilde{\pi}_{t+k}\}
for all
k\geq 0
. The proof proceeds by backward induction.

Base Case.

The induction begins with the terminal period of the shock,
k=\ell-1
. From Lemma
1
, we know that the state of the economy will be zero for all subsequent periods, which uniquely determines the expectations at
t+\ell-1
to be
\mathbb{E}_{t+\ell-1}[\tilde{X}_{t+\ell}]=0
. With these expectations, the system governing
(\tilde{x}_{t+\ell-1},\tilde{\pi}_{t+\ell-1})
is precisely that of Proposition
2
, which has a unique solution. Thus, the base case holds.

Inductive Hypothesis.

Assume that for some period
k
such that
0<k\leq\ell-1
, the state vector
\tilde{X}_{t+k}
is uniquely determined.

Inductive Step.

We now show that the state vector at the prior period,
\tilde{X}_{t+k-1}
, must also be unique. We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that both the Normal and ELB regimes satisfy their respective verification conditions at time
t+k-1
. Let us define the terms determined by the unique expectations of the state at
t+k
:

\mathscr{E}_{t+k-1}\equiv\lambda pm_{xx}\tilde{x}_{t+k}+p(\lambda\sigma m_{x\pi}+m_{\pi\pi}\beta)\tilde{\pi}_{t+k}-\lambda d.

The verification conditions for each regime are:

(i)

Normal regime is valid

\Leftrightarrow\mathscr{E}_{t+k-1}>-(1+\lambda\sigma\psi)\frac{\mu}{\psi}
.

(ii)

ELB regime is valid

\Leftrightarrow\mathscr{E}_{t+k-1}\leq-(1+\lambda\sigma\psi)\frac{\mu}{\psi}
.

These two conditions are mutually exclusive. It is a contradiction for both to hold simultaneously. Therefore, at most one regime can be valid. Since a solution is guaranteed to exist, the solution at
t+k-1
must be unique.

Conclusion.

By backward induction, the path
\{\tilde{X}_{t+k}\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
is uniquely determined. Combined with the result from Lemma
1
for
k\geq\ell
, the solution path is unique for all
k\geq 0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

A.3
Proof of Proposition
4

Proof of the First Statement (Pure Normal-Time Regime)

We begin by proving the first statement of the proposition: if
d\in[0,\overline{d}(p)]
, the economy remains in the normal-time regime for all periods, and the solution path converges to a finite limit as
\ell\to\infty
. The proof proceeds by establishing two intermediate results. First, we characterize the dynamics of the hypothetical pure normal-time solution path. Second, we derive the conditions under which this path constitutes a valid equilibrium.

The system of equations for the normal-time regime can be written in matrix form as
\tilde{X}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbb{E}_{t}\tilde{X}_{t+1}+Cd_{t}
, where
\tilde{X}_{t}=[\tilde{x}_{t},\tilde{\pi}_{t}]^{\prime}
and

\mathbf{A}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda\sigma\psi}\begin{pmatrix}m_{xx}&\sigma(m_{x\pi}-\psi\beta m_{\pi\pi})\\
\lambda m_{xx}&\lambda\sigma m_{x\pi}+\beta m_{\pi\pi}\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\frac{-1}{1+\lambda\sigma\psi}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

Given the truncated Markov process for the shock
d_{t}=d
, which lasts for a maximum of
\ell
periods with persistence
p
, the solution at time
t
can be found by backward induction:

\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)=\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p\mathbf{A})^{i}\right]Cd.

Our proof relies on the properties of the matrix
p\mathbf{A}
. Assumptions
1
and
2
ensure two crucial properties: (1) the eigenvalues of
A
are real and positive, and (2) the spectral radius of
p\mathbf{A}
is less than one for all
p\in[0,1]
, ensuring stability.
10
10

10

The proofs that Assumption
1
guarantees real eigenvalues and Assumption
2
ensures a spectral radius less than one are standard but involve detailed algebra. They are deferred to online Appendix, section C for the interested reader.

Lemma 2

(Dynamics of the Hypothetical Normal-Time Path)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the hypothetical pure normal-time solution path satisfies:

(i)

Monotonicity:
The path
\{\tilde{X}_{t+k}(\ell)\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
is monotonic. The minimum values for both output gap and inflation occur on impact at time
t
.

(ii)

Convergence:
As the maximum duration
\ell\to\infty
, the on-impact solution converges to a finite steady state, which is the standard MSV equilibrium:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)=(I-p\mathbf{A})^{-1}Cd\equiv\tilde{X}^{N}.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

(i) Under Assumption
1
, the term
(N-\psi\beta m_{\pi\pi})
is positive. Since all other parameters are positive, the matrices
A
and
C
have element-wise sign patterns of ‘+‘ and ‘-‘, respectively. Therefore, the matrix
(p\mathbf{A})
is element-wise positive, and the vector
Cd
is element-wise negative. The solution
\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)
is a sum of element-wise negative vectors. Increasing the maximum duration from
\ell
to
\ell+1
adds another negative term,
(p\mathbf{A})^{\ell}Cd
, to the sum, making the solution vector
\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell+1)
strictly smaller (more negative) than
\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)
. The solution path’s monotonicity then follows from an index symmetry property, which states that the solution at period
t+k
for a shock of maximal duration
\ell
is equivalent to the on-impact solution for a shock of maximal duration
\ell-k
.
11
11

11

Formally, this index symmetry property states that
\tilde{X}_{t+k}(\ell)=\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell-k)
. This result follows directly from the recursive structure of the backward induction solution. A detailed proof can be found in the online Appendix, section C.
Thus, the path
\{\tilde{\pi}_{t+k}(\ell)\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
is lowest at
k=0
.

(ii) The stability condition from Assumption
2
,
\rho(p\mathbf{A})<1
, ensures that the geometric matrix series converges as
\ell\to\infty
. The limit is the standard solution for an infinite geometric series,
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(p\mathbf{A})^{i}=(I-p\mathbf{A})^{-1}
. This directly yields the stated limit, which corresponds to the time-invariant MSV solution derived in Proposition
1
.
∎

With the dynamics of the hypothetical path established, we now determine when it is a valid equilibrium.

Lemma 3

(Validity of the Normal-Time Solution)
.

The pure normal-time solution is the valid and unique equilibrium if and only if
d\in[0,\overline{d}(p)]
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

For the normal-time solution to be valid, the nominal interest rate must remain above its lower bound for the entire duration of the shock:
\psi\tilde{\pi}_{t+k}(\ell)>-\mu
for all
k\in[0,\ell-1]
and for all possible
\ell\geq 1
.

From Lemma
2
, we know the inflation path is monotonic and its minimum value occurs at
t
(on impact). Therefore, the verification condition for the entire path simplifies to a single condition on the on-impact inflation:

\psi\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)>-\mu\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)>-\frac{\mu}{\psi}.

Furthermore, we know from Lemma
2
that
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)
is a decreasing sequence in
\ell
that converges from above to its limit
\tilde{\pi}^{N}
. The tightest possible constraint is therefore the one that must hold in the limit as
\ell\to\infty
:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)=\tilde{\pi}^{N}\geq-\frac{\mu}{\psi}.

Substituting the expression for the MSV inflation
\tilde{\pi}^{N}
gives the condition:

-\frac{\lambda d}{(1-pm_{xx})(1-p\beta m_{\pi\pi})+\lambda\sigma(\psi-pm_{x\pi})}\geq-\frac{\mu}{\psi}.

Rearranging this inequality for
d
yields precisely the threshold from Proposition
1
:

d\leq\frac{\mu}{\lambda\psi}\left[(1-pm_{xx})(1-p\beta m_{\pi\pi})+\lambda\sigma(\psi-pm_{x\pi})\right]\equiv\overline{d}(p).

If
d\leq\overline{d}(p)
, the limiting inflation value satisfies the constraint. Since the entire sequence
\{\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
lies above this limit, the constraint is satisfied for all possible shock durations
\ell
. By Proposition
3
, the solution path is unique.
∎

Combining the results of Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
completes the proof of the first statement of the proposition.

Proof of the Second and Third Statements (Pure ELB Regime)

We now prove the second and third statements of the proposition, which characterize the equilibrium when the shock is large. The second statement asserts that for
d>\overline{d}(0)
, the economy remains in the ELB regime for all periods. The third statement describes the path’s convergence or divergence depending on the shock’s persistence
p
.

In the ELB regime, the system is described by
\tilde{X}_{t}=\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{t}\tilde{X}_{t+1}+C^{*}d_{t}+E^{*}
, where

\mathbf{A}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{xx}&\sigma m_{x\pi}\\
\lambda m_{xx}&\lambda\sigma m_{x\pi}+\beta m_{\pi\pi}\end{pmatrix},\quad C^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-\lambda\end{pmatrix},\quad E^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\mu\\
\lambda\sigma\mu\end{pmatrix}.

For a shock of maximum duration
\ell
, the on-impact solution is found by solving the system equilibrium conditions,
\tilde{X}_{k}=p\mathbf{A}^{*}\tilde{X}_{k+1}+(C^{*}d+E^{*})
, using backward induction. In this process, the term
(C^{*}d+E^{*})
acts as a single, constant forcing term present in each period of the shock. Starting from the terminal condition where
\mathbb{E}_{t+\ell-1}[\tilde{X}_{t+\ell}]=0
, this recursive substitution naturally yields a simple geometric series:

\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)=\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}\right](C^{*}d+E^{*}).

The dynamics of this solution depend critically on the properties of the matrix
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
. Its eigenvalues are always real, but its stability depends on the persistence
p
.
12
12

12

The proof that the eigenvalues of
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
are always real is algebraic. The stability condition,
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})<1
, holds if and only if
F(p)\equiv(1-pm_{xx})(1-p\beta m_{\pi\pi})-p\lambda\sigma m_{x\pi}>0
. The function
F(p)
is a convex quadratic with
F(0)=1
, which defines the threshold
\overline{p}\in(0,1)
as its smaller root. See the online Appendix, section C for detailed proofs.

Lemma 4

(Dynamics of the Hypothetical ELB Path)
.

The hypothetical pure ELB solution path satisfies:

(i)

Monotonicity:
If
d>\overline{d}(0)
, the path
\{\tilde{X}_{t+k}(\ell)\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
is monotonic. The minimum values for output gap and inflation occur on impact at time
t
.

(ii)

Convergence and Divergence:
As the maximum duration
\ell\to\infty
, the on-impact solution either converges or diverges:

•

If
p<\overline{p}
, the path converges to a finite steady state:
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)=(I-p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{-1}(C^{*}d+E^{*})\equiv\tilde{X}^{L}
.

•

If
p\geq\overline{p}
, the path diverges to negative infinity:
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)\to-\infty
component-wise.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

(i) The matrix
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
is element-wise positive. The constant forcing term is
(C^{*}d+E^{*})=(-d+\sigma\mu)[1,\lambda]^{\prime}
. The condition
d>\overline{d}(0)
(defined below) implies
d>\sigma\mu
, so the forcing term is element-wise negative. The solution
\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)
is a sum of strictly negative vectors, and is therefore strictly decreasing in
\ell
. Using the same index symmetry property as in the normal-time case, the minimum of the path
\{\tilde{X}_{t+k}(\ell)\}
occurs at
k=0
(time
t
).
13
13

13

The index symmetry claim,
\tilde{X}_{t+k}(\ell)=\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell-k)
, holds for the ELB regime as well, following the same logic as the proof in online Appendix, section C.1.3.

(ii) The convergence of the geometric matrix series
\sum(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}
depends on its spectral radius,
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})
. This condition is determined by the shock’s persistence,
p
. As shown in the supplementary materials (online Appendix, section C), the stability condition
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})<1
is equivalent to the inequality
F(p)>0
, where
F(p)
is a quadratic in
p
:

F(p)\equiv(1-pm_{xx})(1-p\beta m_{\pi\pi})-p\lambda\sigma m_{x\pi}.

This function is convex with
F(0)=1
and typically crosses zero twice. We define
\overline{p}\in(0,1)
as the smaller of these two roots. Thus, the system is stable if and only if
p<\overline{p}
. We analyze the solution’s limit under two cases.

Case 1:
p<\overline{p}
(Convergence).
In this case,
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})<1
. By the property of Neumann series for matrices, the infinite sum converges to a finite matrix:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}=(I-p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{-1}.

Consequently, the on-impact solution converges to a well-defined, finite vector corresponding to the MSV-ELB equilibrium:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)=(I-p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{-1}(C^{*}d+E^{*}).

Case 2:
p\geq\overline{p}
(Divergence).
In this case,
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})\geq 1
. The largest eigenvalue of
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
is real and greater than or equal to 1, causing the matrix series to diverge. To determine the direction of divergence, we examine the terms of the sum. The matrix
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
is element-wise positive, and the forcing term
(C^{*}d+E^{*})
is element-wise negative. Therefore, each term in the solution sequence,
(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}(C^{*}d+E^{*})
, is a vector of negative numbers. As
\ell\to\infty
, we are adding an infinite number of negative vectors, and the sum diverges. Each component of the solution vector is thus driven to negative infinity:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{X}_{t}(\ell)\to-\infty\quad\text{component-wise}.

This case-by-case analysis characterizes the long-run behavior of the hypothetical ELB path. This result is the key to proving the third statement of the proposition, which we will finalize after establishing the path’s validity.
∎

Having characterized the dynamics of the hypothetical ELB path, we now establish the precise conditions on the shock size
d
under which this path is the valid and unique equilibrium.

Lemma 5

(Validity of the ELB Solution)
.

If
d>\overline{d}(0)
, the pure ELB solution is the valid and unique equilibrium.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

For the ELB solution to be valid, the interest rate rule must select the lower bound in all periods:
\psi\tilde{\pi}_{t+k}(\ell)\leq-\mu
for all
k\in[0,\ell-1]
and all
\ell\geq 1
.

From Lemma
4
, the path
\{\tilde{\pi}_{t+k}(\ell)\}
is monotonically decreasing as we move backward in time from the shock’s end. This means the highest inflation value (the point most likely to violate the condition) occurs at the last period of the shock,
t+\ell-1
. The verification condition for the entire path thus simplifies to checking this single point:

\psi\tilde{\pi}_{t+\ell-1}(\ell)\leq-\mu.

The solution in the final period is independent of
\ell
and
p
, given by
\tilde{\pi}_{t+\ell-1}(\ell)=\lambda(-d+\sigma\mu)
. Substituting this into the inequality gives:

\psi\lambda(-d+\sigma\mu)\leq-\mu.

Rearranging for
d
yields the condition for the pure ELB path to be valid. This defines the threshold
\overline{d}(0)
:

d\geq\sigma\mu+\frac{\mu}{\lambda\psi}\equiv\overline{d}(0).

If
d>\overline{d}(0)
, the condition is satisfied at the path’s highest point. By monotonicity, it is satisfied everywhere else. By Proposition
3
, the solution is unique. This proves the second statement of Proposition 4.
∎

Combining the results of Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
completes the proof of the second and third statements of the proposition.

A.4
Proof of Proposition
5

Proof.

The proof for the mixed-regime case proceeds in two main stages. First, we establish the fundamental properties that any valid mixed solution must possess, particularly its structure and monotonicity. Second, we use these properties to prove the statements of the proposition.

We begin by characterizing the hypothetical mixed-regime path. The following lemma, which is central to the entire proof, shows that any mixed solution has a well-defined structure: a contiguous block of ELB periods followed by a contiguous block of normal-time periods.

Lemma 6

(Characterization of the Mixed Solution Path)
.

For a given shock
(p,d,\ell)
, a valid mixed solution path
\{\tilde{X}_{t+k}^{M}\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
must satisfy the following properties:

(i)

Single Switching Point:
There exists a unique switching point, meaning the path consists of a block of ELB periods followed by a block of normal-time periods. An N-Z-N or Z-N-Z sequence is not possible.

(ii)

Monotonicity:
The path
\{\tilde{X}_{t+k}^{M}\}_{k=0}^{\ell-1}
is monotonically decreasing as we move backward in time from the shock’s end (i.e., it decreases in the original proof’s backward index
k
).

(iii)

Asymptotic Convergence:
If
p<\overline{p}
, the on-impact solution
\tilde{X}_{t}^{M}(\ell)
converges to the pure ELB steady state as
\ell\to\infty
.

(iv)

Asymptotic Divergence:
If
p\geq\overline{p}
, the on-impact solution
\tilde{X}_{t}^{M}(\ell)
diverges to
-\infty
as
\ell\to\infty
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction and is technical. It first establishes that if the Normal-Time guess fails at any period
k
, it must also fail for all preceding periods. This rules out an N-Z-N path and proves the single-switching-point property. The monotonicity of the path and its asymptotic behavior then follow from this established structure. The complete, detailed proof is provided in online Appendix, section C.2.1.
∎

With the structural properties of any potential mixed path characterized, we are now equipped to prove the first statement of the proposition.

Lemma 7

(Existence of a Mixed Solution and the Duration Threshold)
.

If the shock size
d
satisfies
\overline{d}(p)<d<\overline{d}(0)
, there exists a unique integer
\overline{\ell}\geq 1
such that the equilibrium path is a pure Normal-Time solution if
\ell<\overline{\ell}
and must be a mixed-regime path with the ELB binding on impact if
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
.

Proof.

We prove this by analyzing the viability of pure solutions.

1.

A Pure ELB Solution is Impossible.
For a pure ELB path to be valid, the condition
d\geq\overline{d}(0)
must hold. Our initial condition is
d<\overline{d}(0)
, which is a
contradiction
. The path must contain at least one normal-time period.

2.

Analysis of the Pure Normal-Time (PN) Solution.
The on-impact inflation,
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)
, is a monotonically decreasing function of the shock’s duration
\ell
. The condition
d>\overline{d}(p)
implies the limit of this sequence is below the ELB threshold,
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)<-\mu/\psi
. However, the condition
d<\overline{d}(0)
ensures that for
\ell=1
, inflation is above the threshold.

Since the sequence
\{\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
starts above the threshold and converges to a value below it, there must exist a unique integer,
\overline{\ell}
, which is the smallest integer such that
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\overline{\ell})\leq-\mu/\psi
. This leads to two distinct outcomes:

•

If
\ell<\overline{\ell}
: The on-impact inflation
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)
remains above the threshold. By the path’s monotonicity, the ELB never binds. The equilibrium is a
pure Normal-Time solution
.

•

If
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
: Since
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)
is a monotonically decreasing sequence, we have
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\ell)\leq\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\overline{\ell})\leq-\mu/\psi
for all
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
. This means the on-impact inflation violates the Normal-Time condition, causing the
ELB to bind on impact
.

This analysis directly proves the first statement of the proposition. For a shock in the specified range, the solution is pure-normal-time for
\ell<\overline{\ell}
. For
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
, a pure PN solution is invalid, and since a pure ELB solution is also impossible, the unique equilibrium must be a mixed-regime path.
∎

The combination of these two lemmas, which characterize the mixed path and establish the conditions for its existence, completes the proof of Proposition 5.
∎

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

The proof proceeds by first deriving an analytical expression for the consumption multiplier at the ELB and then analyzing its properties.

The Multiplier at the ELB.

Under the condition
d\geq\overline{d}(0)
, the economy is in the pure ELB regime, so the interest rate is fixed at
i_{t}=-\mu
. The model with government spending
g_{t}
can be written in matrix form as
\tilde{X}_{t}=\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbb{E}_{t}\tilde{X}_{t+1}+C_{g}^{*}g_{t}+E^{*}
, where
\tilde{X}_{t}=[c_{t},\pi_{t}]^{\prime}
and

\mathbf{A}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{xx}&\sigma m_{x\pi}\\

\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})\end{pmatrix},\quad E^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\mu\\
\lambda\sigma\mu\end{pmatrix}.

For a shock of maximum duration
\ell
, the on-impact solution for consumption
c_{t}(\ell)
can be found by solving the system backward. The impact multiplier on consumption is given by the partial derivative of
c_{t}(\ell)
with respect to
g_{t}
. This derivative isolates the terms related to
C_{g}^{*}
:

m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)\equiv\frac{\partial c_{t}(\ell)}{\partial g_{t}}=e_{1}^{\top}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}\right]C_{g}^{*},\quad\text{where }e_{1}^{\top}=[1,0].

Let
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}
. Substituting
C_{g}^{*}
gives:

m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)=e_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})\end{pmatrix}=\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})\cdot\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1),

where
\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)
is the (1,2) element of the matrix sum
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)
. Since
\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})>0
, the properties of the multiplier are determined entirely by the sequence
\{\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
.

Monotonicity and Positivity.

We first prove that the multiplier is positive and strictly increasing in
\ell
.

Lemma 8

(Monotonicity and Sign of the ELB Multiplier)
.

The sequence
\{m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
is strictly increasing in
\ell
. Furthermore,
m^{c,PL}_{g}(1)=0
and
m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)>0
for all
\ell\geq 2
.

Proof.

The multiplier is given by
m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)=\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})\cdot\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)
, where
\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)
is the (1,2) element of the matrix sum
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}
. We prove the required properties by analyzing the sequence
\{\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)\}_{\ell=1}^{\infty}
.

Positivity.

The matrix
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is element-wise strictly positive. Therefore, for any
i\geq 1
, the power
(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}
is also element-wise strictly positive. The sum
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)=I+p\mathbf{A}^{*}+\dots+(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell-1}
is the sum of the non-negative identity matrix and (for
\ell\geq 2
) strictly positive matrices. Thus,
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)
is element-wise strictly positive for all
\ell\geq 2
. This implies its (1,2) element
\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)>0
for
\ell\geq 2
. Since
m^{c,PL}_{g}(1)=0
, the multiplier is positive for all
\ell\geq 2
.

Monotonicity.

To show the sequence is strictly increasing, we examine the difference between consecutive terms. As derived from the definition of the multiplier, this difference is given by:

m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell+1)-m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)=\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})\cdot e_{1}^{\top}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell}e_{2}.

The term
e_{1}^{\top}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell}e_{2}
is the (1,2) element of the matrix power
(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell}
. We need to show this element is strictly positive for all
\ell\geq 1
.

The matrix
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is element-wise strictly positive. For the base case
\ell=1
, the (1,2) element of
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
is
p\sigma m_{x\pi}
, which is strictly positive. For any higher power
\ell>1
, the matrix
(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell}
is the product of element-wise positive matrices, which ensures its (1,2) element is also strictly positive.

Since
\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})>0
and
e_{1}^{\top}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell}e_{2}>0
for all
\ell\geq 1
, the difference
m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell+1)-m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)
is always positive. Therefore, the multiplier sequence is strictly increasing.
∎

Asymptotic Behavior.

The long-run limit of the multiplier depends on the convergence of the matrix series
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)
.

Lemma 9

(Asymptotic Behavior of the ELB Multiplier)
.

The limit of the multiplier sequence depends on the shock persistence
p
relative to the threshold
\overline{p}
:

•

If
p<\overline{p}
,
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)=m^{c,PL}_{g}(\infty)>0
.

•

If
p\geq\overline{p}
,
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)=+\infty
.

Proof.

The limit of the multiplier is determined by the limit of the matrix sum. As established in the proof of Proposition 4, this sum converges if and only if the spectral radius
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})<1
, which is true if and only if
p<\overline{p}
.

Case 1:
p<\overline{p}
(Convergence).
The matrix series converges,
\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)=(I-p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{-1}
. The limiting multiplier is:

m^{c,PL}_{g}(\infty)=\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})\cdot e_{1}^{\top}(I-p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{-1}e_{2}=\frac{p\sigma m_{x\pi}\kappa\eta(1-\bar{c})}{(1-pm_{xx})(1-p\beta m_{\pi\pi})-p\lambda\sigma m_{x\pi}}.

Since the system is stable for
p<\overline{p}
, the denominator is positive. The numerator is also positive. Thus, the multiplier converges to a finite positive value.

Case 2:
p\geq\overline{p}
(Divergence).
The spectral radius
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})\geq 1
, and the matrix series diverges. Since all elements of
\mathbf{A}^{*}
are non-negative, the elements of the sum
\mathbf{H}^{*}(\ell-1)
are non-decreasing in
\ell
. The divergence implies that the elements grow without bound. In particular,
\mathbf{H}^{*}_{12}(\ell-1)\to+\infty
. Consequently, the multiplier also diverges:
m^{c,PL}_{g}(\ell)\to+\infty
.
∎

The two lemmas above collectively prove all statements of the proposition.
∎

A.6
Proof of Proposition
8

Proof.

This proof characterizes the government spending multiplier on consumption,
m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)
, in the mixed-solution regime where
\overline{d}(p)<d<\overline{d}(0)
. We prove the proposition’s three statements in order.

Proof of Statement (i).

The first statement follows directly from the proof of Proposition 5. There, we established the existence of a unique integer threshold
\overline{\ell}
for any shock
d
in this range.

•

If the shock’s maximum duration
\ell<\overline{\ell}
, the equilibrium path is a pure Normal-Time solution. Therefore, the multiplier in this case is identical to the pure Normal-Time multiplier,
m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)=m^{c,PN}_{g}(\ell)
, which we proved in Proposition
6
is negative.

•

If
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
, the equilibrium path is a mixed-solution path, beginning with a phase at the ELB.

This establishes the first statement. The remainder of the proof analyzes the multiplier for the case where
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
.

The Mixed Multiplier Formula.

For any shock with duration
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
, the solution path is mixed. The path is constructed by taking the state vector from the longest possible pure-normal-time path,
\tilde{X}_{init}\equiv\tilde{X}^{PN}_{t}(\overline{\ell}-1)
, and evolving it backward for
\ell-(\overline{\ell}-1)
periods using the ELB dynamics. This gives the on-impact state vector:

\tilde{X}_{t}^{M}(\ell)=(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell-(\overline{\ell}-1)}\tilde{X}_{init}+\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}\right](C_{g}^{*}g_{t}+E^{*}).

The on-impact consumption multiplier is the derivative of the first component of this vector with respect to
g_{t}
. Taking the derivative yields the two components of the multiplier:

m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)=\underbrace{e_{1}^{\top}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell-(\overline{\ell}-1)}\frac{\partial\tilde{X}_{init}}{\partial g_{t}}}_{\text{Inheritance Component}}+\underbrace{e_{1}^{\top}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}\right]C_{g}^{*}}_{\text{ELB Component}}.

The “Inheritance Component” is driven by the (negative) PN multiplier vector from the threshold duration
\overline{\ell}-1
. The “ELB Component” captures the accumulating (positive) effects of spending during the ELB phase. It’s crucial to note that for a given set of model parameters and a specific shock
(p,d)
, the threshold
\overline{\ell}
is a fixed integer. Our analysis examines how the multiplier’s characteristics evolve as the maximum duration of the shock,
\ell
, increases and moves past this fixed threshold.

Proof of Statement (iii) (Asymptotic Behavior).

The long-run behavior of the multiplier as
\ell\to\infty
depends critically on the stability of the ELB dynamics, governed by the threshold
\overline{p}
.

Case 1: The Stable Regime (
p<\overline{p}
).
When the ELB dynamics are stable, we can formally prove the limit by analyzing the two components of the mixed multiplier formula as
\ell\to\infty
. The analysis hinges on the stability of the ELB transition matrix,
p\mathbf{A}^{*}
. The condition for this stable case,
p<\overline{p}
, ensures that the spectral radius of the matrix is less than one,
\rho(p\mathbf{A}^{*})<1
.

This stability has two crucial implications. First, for the
Inheritance Component
, the matrix power
(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{\ell-(\overline{\ell}-1)}
converges to the zero matrix as
\ell\to\infty
. Since this is multiplied by a fixed, finite vector (the PN multiplier vector at duration
\overline{\ell}-1
), the entire inheritance component vanishes in the limit. Second, for the
ELB Component
, the same stability condition guarantees that the geometric matrix series
\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}(p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{i}
converges to the matrix inverse
(I-p\mathbf{A}^{*})^{-1}
. The limit of the ELB component is therefore precisely the pure ELB multiplier for an infinite-horizon shock,
m^{c,PL}_{g}(\infty)
.

Combining the limits of the two components-zero for the first and
m^{c,PL}_{g}(\infty)
for the second we have formally shown that:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)=m^{c,PL}_{g}(\infty)>0.

Case 2: The Unstable Regime (
p\geq\overline{p}
).
This is the more complex case. As
\ell\to\infty
, both the negative “Inheritance Component” and the positive “ELB Component” of the multiplier diverge in magnitude, leading to an indeterminate form. To resolve this, we must analyze the full AR(2) structure of the multiplier sequence. For
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
, the solution to this process can be expressed as:

m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)=\alpha^{*}+B_{1}r_{1}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}+B_{2}r_{2}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}

where
\alpha^{*}
is the (unstable) fixed point, and
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
are the real roots (eigenvalues) of the system. In the unstable regime (
p\geq\overline{p}
), the larger root satisfies
r_{1}\geq 1
. Since
r_{1}>r_{2}
, the long-run behavior of the sequence is dominated by the term with the larger root,
B_{1}r_{1}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}
. The core of the proof, detailed in the supplementary appendix, is establishing that the coefficient on this explosive root is strictly positive,
B_{1}>0
. A positive coefficient ensures that the positive, explosive dynamic ultimately dominates the negative dynamic inherited from the normal-time phase. As
\ell\to\infty
, the term
B_{1}r_{1}^{\ell-\overline{\ell}}
grows without bound, driving the entire multiplier sequence with it. Therefore, we conclude that the multiplier diverges to positive infinity:

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)=+\infty.

This case-by-case analysis proves the third statement of the proposition.

Proof of Statement (ii) (The Sign Switch).

This statement follows from combining the results already established. From the proof of statement (i), we know the multiplier sequence begins with negative values, as
m^{c,M}_{g}(\ell)=m^{c,PN}_{g}(\ell)<0
for short durations (
\ell<\overline{\ell}
). Conversely, our analysis for statement (iii) shows that the sequence’s long-run limit is either a finite positive number (if
p<\overline{p}
) or diverges to
+\infty
.

The crucial link between this negative start and positive end is the path’s shape. As established in the supplementary appendix, the multiplier sequence is strictly monotonically increasing once it enters the mixed-regime phase (for
\ell\geq\overline{\ell}
). A sequence that starts negative and increases monotonically towards a positive or infinite limit must cross zero exactly once. Therefore, there must exist a unique threshold
\ell^{+}\geq\overline{\ell}
such that the multiplier becomes positive for all
\ell\geq\ell^{+}
. This proves the second statement of the proposition.

Having proven all three statements, the proof of the proposition is complete.
∎
