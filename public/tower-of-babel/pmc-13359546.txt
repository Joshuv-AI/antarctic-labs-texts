Perspectives of older adults with pre-frailty and frailty when engaging with an online nutrition educational resource: a qualitative study
Authors: Adeline Lau, Chad Han, Kristiana Ludlow, Adrienne M Young, Elizabeth M Miller, Michelle D Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults with pre-frailty and frailty are vulnerable to malnutrition, yet often lack access to tailored, credible, and accessible nutrition education. Digital health tools offer a scalable solution, but little is known about how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty engage with them. This study explored the experiences of community-dwelling older adults with pre-frailty and frailty when engaging with an online nutrition educational resource to help people prevent and manage frailty.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted with community-dwelling older adults (≥ 65 years) who were screened as pre-frail or frail using the FRAIL scale. Participants were given access to an online nutrition educational resource for 2–4 weeks. Semi-structured interviews exploring participants’ general interest in nutrition, their perspectives regarding the content, layout and usability of the online resource, and their learning preferences were conducted. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using codebook thematic analysis.Results19 participants (mean age: 73.7 years) participated in this study, of which 14 were pre-frail (73.7%) and five were frail (26.3%). Six themes were identified: (1) using the resource, (2) drawing on lived experiences and dietary beliefs, (3) connecting nutrition and frailty, (4) tailoring nutrition and learning, (5) learning in different ways, and (6) empowering older adults. While these themes extend beyond resource usability alone, they collectively reflect the broader experiential and contextual factors that shape how participants engaged with and interpreted the online nutrition resource. Participants valued personalisation, multimodal learning formats, and the integration of lived experience. While some participants reported positive dietary changes after engaging with the resource, others described reflective learning without immediate behaviour change. Participants reported that digital literacy and personal learning goals and preferences influenced their experience of engaging with the online resource.ConclusionsOlder adults with pre-frailty and frailty value online nutrition education but require resources that are personalised, credible, and accessible across diverse learning needs. Findings highlight opportunities to embed consumer voices in resource design and provide future directions for refining the current online nutrition resource. This research contributes to evidence on consumer-informed digital nutrition education to support autonomy, dietary self-management, and healthy ageing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07672-8.Keywords: Nutrition education, Frailty, Pre-frailty, Malnutrition, Aged, Health literacy, User-centred design, Educational technology, Online learning, Digital health

Background

Frailty is a multi-dimensional risk state characterised by reduced physiological reserve, functional decline, and increased vulnerability to stressors [1]. Compared to those who are ‘robust’, older adults with frailty tend to have poorer health outcomes, including falls, poor quality of life, hospitalisation, institutionalisation, and mortality [2]. For instance, pooled data show that frailty is associated with almost double the odds of hospitalisation (OR: 1.90, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.74–2.07, p < 0.00001) in community settings [3]. Additionally, when admitted to hospitals, older adults with frailty had in-hospital mortality risk over eight times that of non-frail peers (RR: 8.20), and long-term mortality risk around 3 to 4 times higher [4].

Malnutrition and inadequate dietary intake are key contributors to frailty development and progression [5, 6]. Evidence suggests that tailored nutrition interventions, particularly those supporting adequate protein and energy intake, can mitigate frailty-related outcomes [7, 8]. A key component of such interventions is nutrition education to provide older adults with practical knowledge about protein and energy-dense foods, meal planning and self-management, as well as fostering confidence and autonomy in dietary decision-making [9]. Although nutrition education interventions have demonstrated short-term improvements in dietary intake and nutrition knowledge among community-dwelling older adults [10, 11], their sustainability and applicability to those with frailty remain uncertain. Many existing programs have been delivered in structured, group-based formats targeting the general older population, rather than those experiencing frailty who may have distinct learning needs, health priorities, and barriers to engagement [10, 12]. Moreover, while existing evidence suggests that older adults’ ability to translate nutrition knowledge into sustained dietary behaviour is influenced by factors such as health literacy, motivation, and self-efficacy [13, 14], it remains unclear how these factors specifically interact with the unique challenges faced by older adults with pre-frailty and frailty, including age-related cognitive changes, multimorbidity, and functional decline. Therefore, this highlights the need to explore how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty personally engage with and interpret nutrition information, particularly within online learning environments, to ensure that digital education resources are both accessible and meaningful for this group.

Despite the importance of good nutrition, many older adults experience barriers to accessing clear, practical, and trustworthy nutrition information [15]. Increasingly, digital health and nutrition education platforms are proposed as scalable strategies to deliver information, promote behaviour change, and support ageing in place [16, 17]. Yet, research shows that older adults’ engagement with online resources is heterogeneous, shaped by digital literacy, usability, motivation, and trust in information sources [18–20]. Few studies have examined how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty, specifically, interact with online nutrition resources, and this lack of qualitative evidence on user experience and engagement patterns among older adults with pre-frailty and frailty is a critical gap in the literature. Without understanding how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty perceive online nutrition resources, navigate digital interfaces, and engage with online nutrition educational content, there is a risk that digital tools will be developed without alignment to the unique preferences of older adults, thereby limiting accessibility, usability, and ultimately uptake. Encouragingly, co-design approaches have become more widely adopted in health research in recent years, reflecting a shift toward more collaborative and patient-centred methodologies [21]. Co-design emphasises embedding relevant stakeholder voices in research, intervention, tool, and resource development to improve usability, acceptability, and impact [22]. Health consumers have a wealth of lived experiences and knowledge that can benefit the design of education resources. Early consumer collaboration, with an emphasis on shared decision-making, supports the creation of nutrition education resources that are designed both with and for the intended end-users, addressing their needs, values, circumstances, and preferences [23].

This study was part of a larger co-design project aimed at developing online consumer-informed nutrition resources for older adults with pre-frailty and frailty. The objectives of this study were: (1) to qualitatively explore the self-directed learning experiences of older adults with pre-frailty and frailty when engaging with an online nutrition educational resource; and (2) to investigate participants’ preferred learning styles, including factors that shape their learning preferences and interactions with the online nutrition educational resource. Insights generated from this study will directly inform subsequent studies to iteratively co-design and refine the finalised nutrition educational resource.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative design was employed using interviews and template analysis [24], a type of codebook thematic analysis [24]. A qualitative approach was selected because it enables in-depth exploration of the nuanced, experiential, and contextual factors that shape how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty engage with online nutrition education -- elements that would not be adequately captured through quantitative methods alone. Semi-structured interviews were chosen as they provide both structure and flexibility, allowing participants to describe their experiences in their own words while enabling the interviewer to probe for meaning, clarify perspectives, and explore unanticipated ideas, consistent with best practice in qualitative health research [25]. Thematic analysis was chosen to explore participants’ lived experiences in depth while acknowledging the influence of the researchers’ own values and beliefs on how the dataset has been interpreted and analysed [26]. More specifically, codebook thematic analysis was employed as it provides a structured yet flexible analytic framework that supports collaborative team-based coding and iterative refinement of theme definitions [24]. This method also accommodates both inductive and interpretive analytical processes, allowing the research team to develop a shared, coherent coding structure while remaining responsive to nuanced experiential interview data, consistent with recommendations by Braun and Clarke for rigorous thematic analysis in applied health research [27, 28]. This study was situated within a constructivist epistemology, which recognises that knowledge and meaning are co-constructed through participants’ experiences and the researcher’s interpretation. This orientation was selected as it aligns with the study’s focus on how older adults make sense of and engage with online nutrition education. To ensure transparency in study conduct and reporting, this study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for interviews and focus groups [29] (Additional file 1).

Participants and recruitment

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit community-dwelling older adults (≥ 65 years) who were pre-frail or frail, as assessed by the FRAIL scale (i.e. score of ≥ 1) [30, 31]. Eligible participants were required to be able to read and converse in English, and have access to, and the ability to use, a computer or any other electronic device with internet. People living in residential aged care or hospitalised patients, as well as those unable to provide informed consent were not eligible to participate.

Participants were also recruited nationwide via an email to the Australian Frailty Network Consumer Reference Group (consisting of people affected by frailty and their caregivers) as well as social media platforms, community organisations, and professional bodies. As part of the recruitment process, participants were provided with a Participant Information and Consent Form (PICF) that included a description of the study and outlined the research aims. Data saturation was achieved when more recent participants expressed similar experiences and ideas to previous participants and limited new information was presented [32].

Consumer involvement

EM was involved as a consumer partner throughout the study, contributing lived-experience perspectives from study conceptualisation through to data analysis and manuscript preparation. During four 30–60 min online meetings with AL, EM provided consumer input into the development of study protocols, recruitment materials, and ethics documentation. She reviewed and provided feedback to all participant-facing materials, including the interview guide and data collection questionnaire, to ensure clarity and accessibility, testing these tools herself to confirm usability. During data analysis, EM reviewed the coding template and contributed to the finalisation of themes, offering insights grounded in her lived experience. She also reviewed and provided feedback on the manuscript draft. To enhance clarity around the role and influence of consumer partnership in this study, EM’s consumer involvement was documented using the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public – Short Form (GRIPP2-SF) checklist [34] (Additional file 2).

Study materials and data collection

Online nutrition educational resource

The online nutrition educational resource used in this study was a website developed for the Frailty Interventions Through Specific Therapies (FITTEST) study, which is registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12624000693527) [35]. Targeted for older adults with mild frailty, the FITTEST study website was developed as part of a research study led by the Australian Frailty Network at The University of Queensland and involves researchers from various universities and hospitals in Australia [35]. The aim of the FITTEST study is to evaluate the clinical effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and implementation of a multicomponent frailty management program for mildly frail community-dwelling individuals [35].

While the website provides information and resources pertaining to exercise, nutrition, medication optimisation, social support, goal-setting and self-management [35], the nutrition component was the focus of this study and is referred to as the “online nutrition educational resource” from this point forward. The following description of the online nutrition educational resource has been guided by recommendations for reporting complex interventions to support transparency and reproducibility [36].

This online nutrition educational resource was developed by an Advanced Accredited Practicing Dietitian (AY) with input from other members of the FITTEST research team. The FITTEST Consumer Advisory Group provided early input on the content of the website and provided feedback on the final website prior to study commencement.

The online nutrition educational resource comprised predominantly text-based nutrition educational content organised into topic-specific sections (Table 1) and is supplemented by limited interactive elements. The primary interactive feature of the online nutrition resource is the “Rate your eating habits” quiz, based on the Risk Evaluation for Eating and Nutrition, Version II (SCREEN II) nutrition risk screening tool [37]. Upon completion of this quiz, users receive generalised feedback indicating potential areas of nutrition risk and were directed to relevant sections of the website for further reading (e.g., reduced appetite; keeping hydrated; See Table 1 for list of nutrition topics covered in the online nutrition resource). The quiz was designed to prompt reflection rather than provide tailored dietary prescriptions. While the resource covered a range of nutrition topics relevant to pre-frailty and frailty (e.g. protein intake, appetite, hydration, meal preparation), it did not incorporate algorithm-driven personalisation, and the content presented was largely standardised across users.

Nutrition topics covered in the online nutrition educational resource

In addition to core text-based nutrition information, the online nutrition educational resource includes links to some external content (e.g., recipe ideas and practical tips). No formal usability or readability testing was conducted prior to this study. However, efforts were made to present information in clear, assessable language suitable for older adult users through early input from the FITTEST Consumer Advisory Group. Screenshots of the online nutrition educational resource can be found in Additional file 3 to support transparency of the online nutrition educational resource.

Following participant consent, enrolled participants were provided access to the online nutrition resource with their own unique log-in details, which comprised of a unique username and password. Written instructions on how to locate the online nutrition resource within the FITTEST study website (after logging in) was also provided. Participants were encouraged to access and engage with the website independently at their own time as often as they liked. Overall, participants had access to the online resource ranging from two to four weeks before their scheduled interview. To describe participants’ use of this online nutrition educational resource, basic, non-identifiable website analytics were automatically generated by the site’s embedded analytics tool (Google Analytics) to provide an overview of participant engagement. These summary metrics included total time spent on the resource, number of log-in sessions, and frequency of visits to specific content pages. This engagement tracking data collected were used solely to contextualise qualitative findings on participants’ reported engagement experiences and were not analysed as a primary data source.

Demographic information

Following informed consent from participants, demographic data including age, sex, cultural background, education level, employment status, and frailty status (per the FRAIL scale [30, 31]) were collected via an online survey (along with questions to assess nutrition knowledge; to be reported in a separate manuscript).

Interviews

Following resource access, participants completed a single semi-structured interview conducted by AL between March and May 2025. AL had no prior personal relationships or professional contact with any enrolled participants. Depending on the participants’ preference, the interview was conducted via telephone or in-person. All interviews were conducted individually via telephone, except for two participants (married couple dyad) who preferred to be interviewed in-person at their home together. Interviews ranged from 18 to 62 min, averaging 48 min.

Participant interviews were conducted using an interview guide (Additional file 4) which explored participants’ engagement with the resource, perceived utility, learning preferences, and suggestions for improvement. The interview guide was developed with insights from the study’s consumer partner EM, to ensure the interviews conducted were consumer-centred. AL drafted the initial interview guide and EM provided input and feedback to refine the interview guide. Prior to commencing formal data collection, two pilot interviews were conducted: one with a PhD Nutrition Candidate (25-years-old, non-frail, robust female), and an older adult (67-years-old, non-frail, robust female). Pilot interviews informed some minor refinements to the wording of some interview questions for improved clarity. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded, and written notes during the interviews were also generated by AL. Data were transcribed verbatim in Microsoft Word with AL verifying the transcription against the audio recordings to ensure accuracy. Participants were offered the opportunity to review, amend and provide additional input to their interview transcripts, to ensure the transcripts captured their feedback and opinions accurately; edits and additional information were provided by six participants and were included in the analysis. Finalised interview transcripts were then de-identified before being imported to NVivo 14 software. Filler words (e.g., “um,” “like”) were removed from participant quotations, and ellipses indicate where portions of quotes were removed to enhance clarity or conciseness without altering participants’ intended meaning. Square brackets are used to provide additional context when necessary.

Data analysis

The data extraction process was supported with the use of NVivo 14 software. Data extracted were then inductively analysed using template analysis [24], a type of codebook thematic analysis that assumes an active, interpretive role of researchers within the meaning-making process. Data familiarisation was carried out AL who became immersed in the data by repeatedly reading the transcripts and listening to the audio recordings, while making preliminary notes on points of interest and potential areas for deeper exploration [33]. To ensure rigor and reflexivity in the coding process, two coders (AL and CH) coded the first five interviews independently. An initial coding template was then developed inductively from this subset of transcripts by AL and CH. During this process, interview data were examined systematically and in-depth, with close attention paid to identifying meanings that were relevant to the study’s research questions [33]. All data were coded and assigned descriptive codes by the researcher [33]. Each code represented an interpretive label capturing why a particular data segment was considered meaningful or relevant to our study focus [33]. Through an iterative and interpretive process, related codes were grouped and regrouped to identify potential patterns of shared meaning (themes). To ensure that the themes and sub-theme accurately reflected meaningful patterns within both the coded data and the broader dataset, each initial theme and sub-theme was revisited and refined throughout the data analysis process. The underlying ‘story’ conveyed by each theme and sub-theme was reviewed to confirm that it was conceptually clear and had appropriately addressed the research questions. Themes and sub-themes were also iteratively refined through discussions between the research team. The coding template was then applied to all transcripts by AL, including the initial five. All codes were iteratively refined through discussion, allowing new codes to be added and existing ones to be revised. Any coding discrepancies were also discussed and resolved collaboratively, and the final themes were agreed upon by the research team through an iterative process of review and refinement.

Therefore, the final coding framework comprised a structured set of codes that were iteratively developed and organised into six overarching themes. Initial codes were generated inductively from the data and subsequently grouped into higher-order categories through ongoing refinement of the coding template. A summary of the coding structure, including example codes and their organisation into themes, is provided in Additional file 5.

Trustworthiness

The extent to which the study findings accurately reflect the experiences and intentions of participants is a crucial aspect of the quality and rigour of our qualitative study. The four criteria of trustworthiness identified by Lincoln and Guba [38] have been considered as follows: Credibility was promoted through the use of reflexivity, triangulation and member checking [39, 40]. Throughout the data collection and data analysis processes, AL acknowledged that she brings her personal experiences and background to both the study and to the coding process [41]. This reflexive awareness along with reflective team discussions throughout the data collection and analysis processes helped to reduce the likelihood of the researchers’ unintentional influence on the study findings, while maintaining a reflexive stance on the researchers’ positionality [39]. In addition, triangulation of participants’ semi-structured interview data, their Google analytics engagement data, and the interviewer’s reflexive journaling notes further promoted the credibility of study findings [40]. Member checking was also conducted through the return of interview transcripts to allow participants the opportunity to provide feedback. Furthermore, ongoing collaboration with consumer partner EM throughout the study design, data analysis, and interpretation processes enhanced credibility by ensuring findings remained grounded in the lived experiences of older adults and were interpreted in ways that were meaningful and relevant to the intended end-user population [39, 40].

Transferability was supported by providing rich, detailed accounts of the research setting, participants, and procedures [39]. Dependability, a key dimension of trustworthiness in qualitative research, reflects the stability and credibility of study findings over time. In this context, trust was actively developed through transparent and collaborative research practices [40]. Team discussions were held regularly throughout the data collection and data analysis process to strengthen dependability of the study findings. In addition, an audit trail comprising a record of decisions made during the study, including changes in methodologies or analyses was maintained to facilitate transparency and traceability, which aids in establishing the dependability of this study, and provides insights into potential biases [39].

Lastly, to strengthen confirmability, several strategies, including peer debriefing, member checking, and reflexive journaling were employed. Peer debriefing involved AL seeking feedback from the research team to test interpretations and identify potential bias [39]. Member checking allowed participants to review and verify the accuracy of the findings, ensuring their perspectives were represented faithfully [39]. Maintaining a reflexive journal further enhanced transparency by documenting the interviewer’s reflections, decisions, and evolving understanding throughout the study [39].

Therefore, overall rigour of the study was supported through several strategies embedded within the data analytic process. These included independent coding of a subset of transcripts by a second researcher (CH), iterative discussions to refine the coding template, and regular team meetings to review emerging interpretations. An audit trail documenting coding decisions and theme development was also maintained throughout.

Reflexivity statement

As an Accredited Practising Dietitian, AL has previous experience working with older adults in both clinical and community settings, which informed her interest in exploring how individuals with pre-frailty and frailty engage with nutrition education. Her professional background may have influenced the framing of questions and interpretation of findings, particularly regarding the perceived value of nutrition education and self-management. To mitigate this, AL maintained a reflexive stance throughout data collection and analysis by keeping a reflexive journal, discussing interpretations with supervisors and a consumer partner (EM), and seeking feedback during theme development. This collaborative and reflexive process aimed to enhance transparency, reduce bias, and ensure that participants’ perspectives were represented authentically.

Ethics

This study was approved by Flinders University’s Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC project number 7631). The recruitment and screening of eligible participants was conducted by AL, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Results

28 community-dwelling older adults provided consent to participate in the study. Of these, 19 participants (mean age: 73.7 years; female: 78.9%) were eligible based on their frailty score [30], and all 19 participants completed the study. Participant characteristics are summarised in Table 2. Based on the resource engagement data which were available for 18 participants, on average, participants accessed the online nutrition educational resource approximately five times (mean (SD) number of log-in sessions: 4.7 (3.2)) over the two-to-four-week period for around 20 min each session (mean (SD) time spent (in minutes) per log-in session: 20.4 (12.1)), with a total average time of 93 min spent browsing the online resource (mean (SD) time (in minutes) spent browsing resource: 93.5 (67.8)). The most frequency accessed section of the online resource was “Rate your eating habits” (n = 55), followed by “Maintaining a healthy weight” and “General healthy eating to reduce frailty” (n = 47 each). Other highly accessed topics included “Healthy eating for your muscles (protein)” (n = 46) and “Don’t feel like eating? (low appetite)” (n = 45).

Summary of themes and sub-themes

Theme 1: using the resource

This first theme reflects participants’ perspectives on the usability of the online nutrition educational resource. Insights on clarity and relevance of the content, ease of navigating the resource, and participants’ experiences interacting with the online resource are presented in this theme. This theme also represents participants’ views on how trustworthy participants considered the online resource to be, their overall user experience, and if they had learnt anything new from the online resource.

1a. Content clarity, navigation and design

Most participants expressed how easy it was to understand and navigate the online nutrition educational resource, with some reporting that it would make a good starting point for older adults interested in learning more about nutrition.

“… was easy to navigate through … Easy to read, well laid out. I had no issues whatsoever.” – P17, male, pre-frail

“Anybody … starting off … down this journey and trying to try to work their way around the various complexities of frailty and putting on weight and all that kind of thing. It was a good. It was a good base to start with.” – P11, male, pre-frail

Some participants commented on the comprehensiveness of the information presented and others shared their appreciation for the inclusion of pictures or diagrams which enhanced their understanding of the nutritional content.

“… I just found that it seemed to cover everything that was important … I think it is very informative. As I said, I can’t think of what else you could have added.” – P3, female, pre-frail

“… it fitted well … There was text to explain everything and, you know … some diagrams or pictures … to sort of supplement that, so it worked well.” – P15, female, pre-frail

A few participants shared how they struggled to use the online resource initially, while other participants shared ways to make the online resource even more user-friendly.

“… initially I sort of was clicking on the wrong things, but once I got the gist of it, I managed it quite well.” – P1, female, pre-frail

“… the important things like calories … could be in a different colour. Like red or green or so.” – P5, male, pre-frail

1b. Interactivity

Almost all participants attempted the “Rate your eating habits” quiz on the online resource, and a few participants mentioned how the quiz provided them with direction on which topics to browse in the online resource.

“It [“Rate your eating habits” quiz] did point me to the protein section and that was good … I’ve printed off a few pages from the … website. And I will go back to them. And the protein was one, the fibre was another.” – P1, female, pre-frail

While most participants explored the external links available on the online resource, a few did not, with some participants reporting limited time as the reason, and others sharing how they plan to check out the external links in the future when revisiting the online resource.

“I had to go through the different topics within … my limited time. But I’m really looking forward to going back and exploring the recipe ideas.” – P8, female, pre-frail

1c. Credibility and trust

Most participants expressed confidence in evaluating the information presented in the online resource, with some sharing that the resource appeared trustworthy and credible.

“It was fine. It was all good. I had no negatives in the back of my mind, whether it [online nutrition educational resource] was accurate or truthful or anything. I was quite … confident with everything that’s on there … I trust the source … So, therefore the information and the facts on there, I trust. So, I’m quite happy.” – P11, male, pre-frail

1d. Overall experience and learning impact

In terms of participants’ overall experience with the online resource, almost every participant said they are likely to recommend the online resource to others.

“I would be very likely to recommend this website because there are a lot of people that don’t have any of this information and I think it would be useful to them.” – P16, female, frail

One participant expressed hesitation about recommending the online resource to others, as she spoke about how the resource was targeted to a specific group of older adults, so the information presented could be misleading for others who do not fall within that category.

“I think the information is really targeted to a group of thin, underweight people … therefore people … without that one in that category could be misled. I think it could be harmful” – P10, female, frail

Participants commented on their interest across a range of different nutrition topics presented in the online resource and spoke about the new nutrition knowledge they have learnt. Protein was mentioned most often, in terms of participants’ nutrient of interest, as well as the nutrient they have learnt something new about. Some participants also expressed how the online nutrition resource reaffirmed their current nutrition knowledge.

“It [online nutrition educational resource] gave me lots of information that I didn’t know. Like the amount of protein that I needed. I really found that extremely helpful because I thought that I was eating enough protein … it was a wakeup call for me just to say, ‘You need to look at this, make sure you’re getting more protein in your meal’.” – P18, female, pre-frail

Many participants reported making dietary changes based on what they had learned after engaging with the online resource. Some participants described a shift in dietary awareness rather than behaviour, reporting planned dietary changes to be made in the future.

“Oh, just realising that you have to have good protein and good hydration to keep your muscles good … I just try to drink a bit more [water] … yesterday, I’ve had four quite tall glasses which is a lot for me.” – P4, female, pre-frail

“… as an Australian brought up with, you know, lamb chops and three veg for tea, legumes never ever entered our diet … I think I need to add to my protein … I need to have some variety in my protein, so maybe that’s where I need to bring in the legumes.” – P1, female, pre-frail

Theme 1 highlights the usability of the online resource related to technical design, as well as its accessibility for different digital literacy levels and interests in nutrition.

Theme 2: drawing on lived experiences and dietary beliefs

Theme 2 describes the areas of nutrition that participants were most conscious about, as they emphasised that their dietary practices and engagement with the online resource were strongly shaped by personal pre-conceived ideas, cultural background, and long-held beliefs and preferences about food and nutrition. This theme also sheds light on participants’ perspective on the social context of food, as well as caregiving experiences with regards to food and nutrition. Theme 2 provides important context on how older adults interacted with the online resource, as their lived experiences motivated their learning and framed how they engaged with the online resource.

2a. Dietary consciousness

While participants reported being dietary conscious of a range of different nutrients, protein and fibre (in relation to gut health) were the most mentioned nutrients of interest amongst participants.

“So, I guess I’m concerned with the fact that I need more protein. When I looked at how much protein do I need, I was quite surprised at how much actually older people do need. So, I am definitely improving on my protein intake.” – P18, female, pre-frail

“Staying regular is really important for older people … Whereas before, people would just say ‘Oh constipation, well, just go and take Movicol or take some other sort of tablet or whatever’. But we’ve gotta stop this because I don’t know how it’s affecting your other medicines or your other organs. So, let’s get you know, your custard apple or your papaya …” – P2, male, pre-frail

Some participants shared their personal struggles with weight, and how they have tried to improve their weight status through diet and nutrition. Some participants also expressed how reassured they felt after learning more about maintaining a healthy weight as an older adult.

“I actually look up information on nutrition and diet often. Because I think I’ve been on a diet all my life (laughs) … So always looking at information about dieting.” – P16, female, frail

“I’m always worried about my weight being overweight rather than underweight. And it [online nutrition educational resource] kind of reassured me a little bit that carrying some extra weight was useful. So, it [online nutrition educational resource] made me less worried about that.” – P15, female, pre-frail

2b. Social context of food

When participants shared about how they interacted with the “Eating with others” section on the online nutrition resource, some participants expressed ambivalence towards the social norms about eating with others.

“… eating with others, it’s interesting, isn’t it? Because … I do eat together. But I can remember as a child, that was the big thing. We all sat down together as a family. And that was our family time … eating together around the table. It’s so important. And I just think … it’s being lost.” – P12, female, frail

“Now eating with others, I don’t really agree … I have made myself a hermit … (laughs) during the pandemic. So, I actually discovered I love eating on my own. So, eating with others was not relevant to me … I get that social connection, I call it my own social connection. That way I have a say on who I eat with, I’m quite particular about that. Because rather than becoming a positive experience, it actually becomes a toxic (emphasises this word) experience.” – P8, female, pre-frail

A few participants reported being caregivers for their spouse or older parents and shared their experiences with food and nutrition.

“… my 91-year-old mother lives next door and up until very recently, I was her primary support, slash, assistant, slash, carer … her nutrition is poor and (she) has been losing weight, and trying to encourage her to eat has been a big focus for all of us” – P13, female, frail

2c. Personal dietary beliefs and preferences

When exploring participants’ interactions with recipes available on the online nutrition resource, almost half of participants spoke about their food preferences, with some sharing food and meal preferences in relation to comfort food, their upbringing, and familiarity.

“I like everything except I don’t like noodles very much, and rice. That is a habit from my parents. My mother never cooked it and so you are not used to it.” – P5, male, pre-frail

Participants also expressed their views and preferences about cooking or preparing meals. Some participants divulged how cooking can be a chore or burden for older adults, while others said they enjoyed cooking and prefer to use their own recipes.

“I didn’t look at the recipes very much because I do my own recipes (laughs) … You know, I hand put [sic] these, these, these, these, and it [42] comes out all right [participant gestures to show how she took a handful of different ingredients and put them together].” – P6, female, pre-frail

Theme 2 highlights the interplay between objective nutritional advice and participants’ personal subjective experience in engaging with the online nutrition resource. Participants’ responses suggest that effective nutrition education must acknowledge and integrate these lived realities rather than presenting nutrition solely as clinical information.

Theme 3: connecting nutrition and frailty

In Theme 3, participants conveyed that engaging with the online resource increased their awareness of frailty as a broader concept and enhanced their understanding of nutrition’s role in frailty. For some, this prompted reflection on their own ageing process, while others expressed a greater appreciation of preventative health strategies. As a good understanding of frailty can help support nutrition-related learning and behaviour change, Theme 3 reflects participants’ interest and views on frailty, providing important context about participants’ engagement with the online nutrition resource.

When exploring participants’ overall experience with the online nutrition resource, they spoke about the need to increase awareness of frailty, with many acknowledging how important nutrition is in preventing or managing frailty.

“Nutrition and … other things with frailty … I’ve found the whole concept is really interesting … I wasn’t aware of frailty as … necessarily … a thing (laughs). So, this has been really, really valuable to learn about.” – P7, female, frail

“I think bringing into people’s consciousness about how nutrition can affect frailty is a big topic and I think it needs to be brought into … people’s faces again … the way we eat, what we eat … can affect our health and especially as we’re older, you don’t want to become frail, because frailty can cause so many problems. To try to prevent frailty is such a big thing and nutrition is such … a big, important part of preventing frailty, and I think we do forget that?” – P12, female, frail

Several participants spoke about their personal views on body weight and its impact on health and frailty.

“I mean weight is an issue, so we know that the lower we get, the more frail we become. So, to add more things in, you know, like mayonnaise on the sandwich, for example … that would just increase some calories, to build him [participant’s husband] up … that was in some of the recipes.” – P1, female, pre-frail

Theme 3 illustrates the potential for digital health education to not only inform, but also to reframe how older adults perceive frailty, thereby opening pathways for more proactive engagement with nutrition and health behaviours.

Theme 4: tailoring nutrition and learning

Theme 4 reflects participants’ desire to personalise the online nutrition resource according to their personal health concerns and nutrition goals. A central determinant of participants’ engagement with the online nutrition educational resource was the extent to which participants perceived the content to reflect their personal health status, dietary needs, and lived experiences. Although participants raised specific informational and content related areas of improvement, more importantly, they reported wanting the online resource to provide a more personalised approach to nutrition care. Participants consistently conveyed the importance for more tailored nutrition information, expressing that while the resource provided helpful general advice, it did not always align with their individual health needs, comorbidities, or food preferences. Moreover, participants suggested additional features that the online resource could include to enhance their learning experience and highlighted several considerations when tailoring the resource for older adult users.

4a. Personalisation of nutrition care

Participants said that they wanted a more individualised nutrition care experience, with options to filter out and/or access specific nutrition information that best relates to their current health condition.

“So, you have that pillar for nutrition, which is like mainly for healthy people … And then maybe you have another link to a page, say for chronic disease … they have a list of chronic diseases, so they just click on the one that’s relevant to their condition.” – P14, female, pre-frail

4b. Expanding content and topics

Most participants expressed an appreciation for the wide range of topics covered in the online nutrition educational resource, although some said they felt that the information was not specific or targeted enough to their current nutritional or health status.

“Some [information] didn’t necessarily apply to me yet, but interesting to observe and look into the future at what might be … It wasn’t helpful for me, but maybe for somebody else.” – P1, female, pre-frail

Other participants conveyed the need for content that accounted for differing body profiles, such as being underweight, overweight, or within a healthy weight range. This reflected a broader desire for resources that adapt to individual health conditions, rather than presenting a “one-size-fits-all” model. Therefore, some participants reported wanting more information about maintaining or achieving a healthy weight and explained that the online resource appeared to be better targeted towards older adults with frailty who needed to gain weight.

“I think the information is really targeted to a group of thin, underweight people … and therefore people [who are not thin or underweight] … could be misled … I really think it needs to be spread possibly into three websites, three sections. One is for the thin frail aged; one would be for the normal weight … frail aged, and then there would be one for the overweight, and possibly even for the obese frail aged. Because I think that the diet needs to be quite different in how it delivers nutrition.” – P10, female, frail

Participants offered a wide range of suggestions on what other nutrition-related topics could be included on the online nutrition educational resource that might be relevant for older adults with frailty. Such topics included nutrition and bone health, optimising iron intake in the diet, as well as nutrition information relating to different types of fats and oils. Some participants also requested for personal food intolerances, preferences or allergies to be considered on the website, especially in the recipe sections.

“Everyone likes different–some like a lot of garlic, some don’t like garlic, it’s very good for you, but he [participant’s husband] doesn’t like garlic.” – P6, female, pre-frail

4c. Additional features to enhance learning

Participants shared their ideas for how to enhance the interactivity and self-directed learning experience when using the online resource. For instance, some participants said that they would like an auditory component or audio narration to accompany the text on the online resource.

“I know people now who … have problems reading now with their eyesight. And so, for them to have … spoken different sections where you can just call out like maintaining a healthy weight as a section, and it’s auditory, that would be very helpful … I can be too tired to sit down and read, but I can listen. So, if you had something where someone could just listen to your thing, I think that might be helpful for some people.” – P10, female, frail

Several participants wanted the option to explore a particular topic in greater detail via additional links if it sparked their interest.

“If you make a website too complicated and too long, people will just turn off. They won’t read it. But if you make it general in the way that you’ve done and then links, if people want to explore further, putting links, which is good.” – P11, male, pre-frail

The inclusion of some form of self-assessment of learning was also suggested by participants to ‘check-in’ on their own understanding and learning from the online nutrition educational resource.

“I do like the little pop quizzes about different things … Maybe at the end … you could do a little quiz to see what people have taken in, with the learning that they’ve done.” – P13, female, frail

Participants also expressed interest in connecting with others using videos and social media platforms, or a discussion board or comment section to allow for peer discussion.

“How do you connect with people who are older, who think they know everything? To give them some information in a non-threatening way, but a fun way that’s informative … I think (for) each of the major topics … some more links would be quite useful and it’s important that we encourage people to continue to think about these things, so it might be linking them into, for example, ones [external links] that … have a Facebook page for older people and their health needs.” – P2, male, pre-frail

“Have a discussion board so that people that have questions regarding their nutrition or their whichever pillar they’re looking at on the website, they could engage in discussion about that, about their questions.” P7, female, frail

Participants also expressed interest in a section for other older adults to share their own lived experiences and personal stories in relation to nutrition and frailty.

“And what I like too, is people who have experienced something like they’re better now… this is what I did … someone who have lived experience of frailty, because of poor nutrition. What I did to get better? Or who, or how, was I helped … to get better? Or someone just talking about frailty in general and saying, ‘Yes, yes, one of the pillars of frailty management is nutrition, so this is what I do in my day’… Yeah, talking about real experiences.” – P12, female, frail

While there were mixed responses about including videos on the online nutrition educational resource, most participants wanted the online resource to include videos to enhance their self-directed learning, with some providing content ideas for these educational videos.

“If you add more illustrations and videos and stuff … videos could actually be very helpful because you can see things in action.” – P17, male, pre-frail

4d. Design considerations for older adult users

Participants explained that the online resource should take into consideration the attention span and digital literacy needs of older adults, as well as their level of interest with regard to learning more about nutrition in frailty.

“I think they [videos] can be really valuable, you know if they’re good, interesting videos. Probably nothing worse than a than a long, boring video (laughs) … Short chunks, I think, especially for this age group … short, interesting chunks of information rather than long, drawn out detailed information.” – P7, female, frail

“Yeah, I thought it was interesting for me [emphasises these words], I think there might be some people … that are not interested in that. You’ll be surprised. There are so many people that I know, really couldn’t care less about what they eat, what they do, what they drink and what’s good for them, what’s not good for them.” P4, female, pre-frail

Theme 4 reflects participants’ recognition of the complexity of frailty, where nutritional needs vary widely. Participants’ perspectives highlight the necessity of customisation in online nutrition educational resources, suggesting that adaptability and targeted content are critical for relevance and sustained engagement.

Theme 5: learning in different ways

Theme 5 provides insights on how participants browsed the online nutrition educational resource and explores how participants source and verify general nutrition information online. This theme also describes the learning styles and preferences of participants, along with their personal learning goals and motivation.

5a. Resource browsing behaviours

About half of the participants said that they browsed every topic listed on the online resource instead of focusing on specific topics. Most of these participants reportedly revisited particular topics of interest after they have browsed all the topics listed. Other participants said that they initially browsed the online resource by focusing on their topics of interest, before browsing the remaining topics available on the online resource.

“I think I did start somewhere down in the middle and then went back and read others. So, I started with the one that interested me and then went back. And went through the whole lot.” – P13, female, frail

5b. Evaluating and verifying information

Most participants spoke about looking for information online, with Google as their main source of nutrition information. Additionally, some participants said they turned to books or health professionals for additional information or reassurance.

“I’m usually going to Google first and then I will follow that up with the information I have in my books … Just to see if they match up.” – P4, female, pre-frail

Many participants conveyed confidence in being able to identify credible information online, with some highlighting university or government websites as more credible.

“I don’t specifically try and back up stuff [information found] online … as long as I recognise and I’m confident about the [web]site, then I generally take that as being correct. For example, university [web]sites, medical [web]sites, I tend to accept the facts on there … If I happen to look for a [web]site that’s a little bit … dubious from a source or a contact point of view, then I’ll try and find something else to back it up.” – P11, male, pre-frail

A few participants explained that they considered print media to be more reliable than digital information, while some conveyed scepticism about online information that came from commercial websites or social media.

“Because I’m a retired registered nurse I have medical dictionaries and things here. A lot of them are out of date, but a lot of the information is still relevant.” – P12, female, frail

“I look at the qualifications of the person that’s telling me something and I look at what they’re trying to sell me. And I don’t tend to take any notice of anybody who’s got ‘I’m selling this’ or … you know, ‘This is my brand’. I think ‘rubbish’ and go on to something else.” P19, female, pre-frail

5c. Learning preferences, motivations and goals

When investigating their preferred learning styles, participants’ diverse approaches to learning were evident. Although most participants conveyed that they were visual learners, some participants expressed a change in their learning styles with age.

“If I read something, it seems to register, so I maybe I’ve got a bit of a photographic mindset.” – P3, female, pre-frail

“In the past, and really up until fairly recently, it’s been purely visual. I’ve been happy to read … But I tend to read out loud now, as I do a lot of things I think out loud, so I think auditory is becoming more useful to me as an add-on to help my comprehension.” – P15, female, pre-frail

Some participants expressed a paper-based preference over digital education, while others reported no strong preference regarding delivery of education resources.

“Prefer on paper, but that’s only what I’ve been used to for many years. But I can do both. If something online … catches my attention, then I’ll tend to print that piece out. So that I’ve got a copy of it and instead of going back online again, I’ll have it printed on paper.” – P11, male, pre-frail

Motivation was identified as an important factor in knowledge acquisition. Some participants described learning not only for their own health but also to support family members. They expressed that their motivation to look up health and nutrition information was shaped by both intrinsic interest and perceived responsibility as caregivers.

“I am interested in nutrition because I … try to be healthy … because I have an underlying bowel condition, I’m always aware of foods that may or may not upset my gut, so I’m trying to get a good balance of knowledge.” – P1, female, pre-frail

“I probably look up information weekly. I have a number of interests about it [nutrition], but firstly personal at the moment, and secondly for my wife who has chronic illness … She just had major heart surgery and a heart attack in the last two weeks. And so, I’ve gotta be really careful about what I feed her or how we eat.” – P2, male, pre-frail

With regard to the learning goals for using the online nutrition educational resource, participants reported wanting to broaden or reaffirm their own nutritional knowledge, with some participants expressing an interest to improve their body weight.

“The topics that you brought up [from the online nutrition educational resource] were all good because they tended to reinforce what I already knew. And basically … the conclusions are that I need to do more (laughs).” – P11, male pre-frail

“Now, my interest is in that I need to lose weight to help me live better, into a much older age. That’s why I’m focusing on nutrition … I thought it [online nutrition educational resource] would be a useful tool.” – P17, male, pre-frail

Theme 5 demonstrates the need to consider the diverse learning needs of older adults with pre-frailty and frailty when designing online nutrition educational resources targeted for this vulnerable group of older adults.

Theme 6: empowering older adults

Theme 6 describes participants’ self-awareness and resilience regarding their own health and nutrition and explores their personal views on the use of technology. Insights into the needs of older adults with pre-frailty and frailty, and the factors impacting on their ability to age in place were also evident in this theme.

6a. Self-awareness and resilience

When exploring participants’ interest in nutrition, some acknowledged that their diet or nutrition knowledge is not perfect, and others expressed confidence in knowing what food or nutrition is best for themselves.

“Well, really, I don’t look up [nutrition information] very much. We eat … very healthy. I believe in that. I don’t believe very much in dietitian because I have my own diet, you know. And I’m healthy otherwise I don’t have any sickness or anything. We cook very good. We eat vegetables and everything in a week, you know. And I think that’s enough, you know.” – P6, female, pre-frail

Some participants spoke about how they have had to keep abreast with digital health information and learn to use technology when sourcing health or nutrition information online.

“It’s not pretty good, it’s just that I’ve to (laughs) find what I need … I think I would have been a bit of a dunce head myself had my son not come home early on when the computers first hit … He brought home a computer from his work cause his work had upgraded. He put it on my desk with a note saying, ‘Welcome to the 20th century’ (laughs)” – P3, female, frail

Several participants reported that some older adults may be less inclined to modify longstanding dietary habits or adopt new nutrition information.

“People, when they’re really old, they don’t go after that [online educational resources]. Old people … they do whatever … our friends, I couldn’t imagine with [name of participant’s friend], she wouldn’t even listen, she wouldn’t … she’s not (interested). She thinks she knows everything. ” – P6, female, pre-frail

6b. Supporting ageing in place

Most participants expressed motivation to stay healthy and age in place, with some sharing additional resources they need to support them as they age.

“I think it’s a really good opportunity to create a better environment so that we’re not reliant on the medical system. The healthier we are into old age, the better off the country is going to be … And I think that all of the things that can help us keep on top of our own health … and not put extra pressure on the medical system are valuable.” – P16, female, frail

Theme 6 illustrates the dual role of nutrition education: it can enhance self-efficacy and resilience, yet engagement is mediated by readiness to change and individual attitudes towards ageing and health.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the self-directed learning experiences and preferred learning styles of older adults with pre-frailty and frailty when engaging with an online nutrition educational resource. Six key themes were identified: using the resource; drawing on lived experiences and dietary beliefs; connecting nutrition and frailty; tailoring nutrition and learning; learning in different ways; and empowering older adults. Rather than representing distinct domains, the identified themes illustrate the interconnected factors influencing engagement with digital nutrition education. Collectively, these findings contribute to a deeper understanding of how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty engage with digital nutrition education, highlighting both opportunities and challenges in designing resources that are acceptable, relevant, and effective for this vulnerable group.

A prominent finding was participants’ desire for resources that better reflected their individual health conditions, nutritional goals, and food preferences. This aligns with broader research emphasising that older adults prefer health information that is specific, practical, and relevant to their daily lives [43, 44]. While the current online nutrition resource provided generalisable nutrition information related to frailty, participants perceived it as insufficiently covering the diversity of pre-frailty and frailty phenotypes, including those who are underweight, overweight, or managing comorbidities. These findings highlight a critical limitation of generic digital nutrition resources, which may fail to engage users when content does not align with their perceived identity, health status, or lived experience [45]. Moreover, personalisation of digital health interventions has been shown to enhance engagement and perceived utility among older adults [46–48], reinforcing the need for adaptive features such as filtering options, tailored meal plans, and condition-specific modules. A recent pilot randomised controlled trial by Su and colleagues evaluated a mobile health intervention called “Olitor”, that was designed to enhance compliance to the Mediterranean diet among older adults with frailty [46]. “Olitor” allowed end-users to track their diet, receive feedback on their food choices and receive personalised healthy recipe recommendations [46]. Results from this trial demonstrated a significant improvement in Mediterranean diet adherence, with participants in the intervention group showing a median increase of two points on the adherence scale compared to no change in the control group [46], illustrating the practical benefits of personalised and adaptive digital nutrition platforms that can respond to diverse needs of older adults.

Alongside personalisation, participants described diverse learning preferences, ranging from visual to experiential approaches, with some noting changes in learning style with ageing. These findings echo earlier evidence that older adults value multimodal learning strategies, including visual aids, interactivity, and opportunities for reflection [44, 49]. Importantly, some participants expressed ongoing reliance on printed materials despite confidence in digital use, consistent with literature indicating that hybrid (online and offline) approaches may maximise accessibility [20]. Participants also reported strong motivation to learn about nutrition, either for self-care or caregiving roles, confirming earlier work that older adults’ learning is often driven by personal health goals and family responsibilities [50, 51].

Confidence in assessing online information varied, with some participants relying on university or government sources and others expressed scepticism toward commercial websites. This reflects wider concerns about digital health literacy in older adults, where the ability to discern trustworthy from misleading information remains inconsistent [52, 53]. Embedding credibility markers and transparent sourcing within nutrition resources may therefore be critical to supporting older adults with informed decision-making. For instance, authorship and reviewer credentials could be clearly displayed, and a reference list along with links to original literature or guidelines could be included in the online nutrition resource to enhance the transparency and credibility of the resource. Additionally, inclusion of a date of publication and a “last updated” timestamp can help users assess the currency of the presented information.

This study’s findings also highlight how online nutrition education can empower older adults by fostering self-awareness, confidence, and resilience in managing their health, which is consistent with research showing that digital interventions can strengthen self-efficacy and support ageing in place [54, 55]. However, engagement in the resource was not universal, with some participants sharing that older adults might be resistant to dietary changes or digital learning, reflecting the heterogeneity of older adults’ readiness to adopt new practices [53, 56]. Addressing this variability requires flexible resource design and integration of peer support or lived experience narratives, which several participants have identified as desirable.

In terms of usability, participants generally reported that the resource was easy to navigate and informative, though some encountered minor initial technical barriers in engaging with the online resource on their first use. Nonetheless, usability remains a critical determinant of digital intervention uptake among older adults [57]. Participants’ suggestions for improving usability of the resource, including incorporating audio options, videos, quizzes, and discussion boards, point to opportunities for increasing interactivity, engagement, and accessibility for end-users with different cognitive, sensory, and learning needs and preference. Moreover, short, engaging video content has been suggested to improve comprehension and retention among older adult learners [44, 58, 59].

Several participants reported gaining new insights about nutrition and frailty, particularly regarding protein requirements, and some indicated dietary behaviour changes as a result of engaging with the online nutrition resource. This suggests that even brief engagement with tailored nutrition education can prompt reflection and potential action, consistent with evidence linking nutrition knowledge to behaviour change in older adults [60]. Importantly, several participants noted that exposure to the concept of frailty, and its link to nutrition, increased their awareness of prevention and management strategies. This underscores the role of education in shaping perceptions of frailty, a condition often under-recognised by both older adults and health professionals [61].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the involvement of older adults across the frailty spectrum (both pre-frail and frail), including older adults with caregiving roles, which provided valuable insights into varied experiences with the online nutrition resource. A recognised limitation of qualitative research lies in its inherently interpretive nature [62]. While the researchers aimed to minimise bias and maintain neutrality through strategies such as reflexivity and triangulation, it is acknowledged that their perspectives and experiences shaped the way data was understood. Template analysis allowed for nuanced exploration of participants’ voices, supported by researcher reflexivity. The use of a coding template enabled a systematic yet flexible approach to interpreting participants’ experiences that maintained analytic consistency across transcripts while still preserving space for reflexive interpretation of the data. The involvement of two researchers in the coding process enhanced the rigour of the data analysis through researcher triangulation. The sample size was appropriate for a qualitative study design and is comparable to other similar studies [43, 63, 64]; however, it should be acknowledged that the sample comprised predominantly females, and individuals who were mostly pre-frail and with higher digital literacy, which may limit generalisability of findings to older adults with greater frailty severity or lower digital literacy. In addition, majority of participants have a qualification at diploma or higher which might have also influenced their engagement with the online nutrition resource. Therefore, future studies should consider more inclusive recruitment strategies to include harder to reach older adults across a broader range of frailty phenotypes and varying levels of digital and health literacy. Moreover, recall and social desirability biases in our study should not be ignored. Some participants may have overstated positive outcomes or under-reported barriers in the context of researcher interaction during the semi-structured interviews. Addressing this limitation, the interviewer (AL) received qualitative interview training to ensure participants felt comfortable being honest and transparent during the interview sessions. This training was reflected in the rapport established with participants, the use of open and non-leading questions, and the interviewer’s ability to respond sensitively to participants’ needs during the interviews. Additionally, several interviews demonstrated a high level of participant openness, with individuals willingly sharing personal experiences, challenges, and reflections, suggesting that the interview environment fostered a sense of comfort and trust.

Implications for future practice and research

This study highlights the need for health professionals, including dietitians, general practitioners, and other allied health professionals to consider how digital nutrition resources can complement in-person care for older adults with pre-frailty and frailty. Participants valued tailored information, multimodal learning formats (e.g., audio, video, interactive quizzes), and opportunities for self-assessment, and connecting with other older adults with pre-frailty and frailty. Embedding these elements into routine practice could improve accessibility, support self-directed learning, and foster greater engagement among older adults with varying levels of digital literacy. Advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) could also be incorporated into digital education to address participants’ desire for more tailored individualised nutrition content [65, 66]. Moreover, dietitians and clinicians may benefit from integrating co-designed digital tools into frailty management programs to enhance nutrition knowledge and empower older adults in maintaining independence [61, 67].

At the policy level, these findings reinforce the potential for co-designed digital health resources that align with ageing-in-place strategies. Frailty is associated with significant healthcare costs, much of which are driven by malnutrition, falls, and hospitalisation [61]. Consumer-informed digital nutrition resources could mitigate these negative outcomes by promoting preventive behaviours and timely self-management. Investment in accessible, credible, and adaptable online platforms, coupled with support for digital literacy training, will be crucial to ensure equitable uptake among older adults [68]. Policies that encourage partnerships between health services, academic institutions, and consumer groups could further strengthen the quality and reach of such interventions [69, 70].

The findings from this study have established a foundation for refining and enhancing current online nutrition educational resources designed for older adults with pre-frailty and frailty. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of such online educational resources at scale, examining their impact on nutrition behaviour change, frailty outcomes, and quality of life. Additional work is also warranted to explore how adaptive personalisation, credibility markers, and peer-to-peer features influence engagement and trust in digital health interventions [71, 72]. Finally, economic evaluations comparing digital education with conventional approaches could provide robust evidence for its integration into health services.

Researcher’s reflections regarding consumer involvement in this study

EM’s early and ongoing involvement strengthened the study’s consumer focus and ensured that the design, analysis, and conclusions remained relevant and accessible to older adults. This partnership fostered mutual learning between the lead researcher and consumer partner and promoted a more person-centred interpretation of findings. The consumer partnership process was greatly facilitated by open communication, flexible scheduling, and mutual respect for different types of expertise. Regular meetings and shared decision-making enhanced trust and collaboration; however, conducting meetings exclusively online occasionally limited opportunities for deeper rapport and informal discussion, which may have been easier to build through in-person engagement. Where possible, future projects may benefit from integrating hybrid modes of collaboration (i.e., online and face-to-face) to optimise accessibility while strengthening relational connections.

Conclusion

This study offers novel insights into how older adults with pre-frailty and frailty engage with and learn from an online nutrition educational resource. Unlike previous research that has predominantly focused on usability or digital literacy barriers, this study highlights the deeper, experiential factors influencing user engagement, including the role of lived experiences, personal values, and self-perceived health needs in shaping how older adults interpret and engage with digital nutrition information. Participants described how these personal contexts influenced both their motivation to learn and the perceived relevance of the online content, underscoring the need for tailored, relatable information that acknowledges individual variability within the frailty spectrum.

The findings also demonstrate that older adults approach online nutrition education as an active, reflective process rather than a passive learning experience. Participants valued opportunities to personalise their learning, integrate knowledge into daily life, and validate their experiences through self-directed exploration. These insights expand current understanding of digital engagement by showing that successful online nutrition education for older adults depends not only on usability but also on fostering empowerment, trust, and personal meaning.

Overall, participants emphasised personalisation, multimodal learning options, integration of lived experience, and usability as central to meaningful engagement with the online resource. Several participants reported knowledge gains and dietary behaviour changes, underscoring the potential of such tools to enhance nutritional intake and support frailty prevention and self-management. More broadly, this study demonstrates the importance of embedding older adults’ voices throughout the design of digital health interventions to ensure they are acceptable, relevant, and impactful. Ultimately, consumer-informed online nutrition education holds promise for supporting autonomy, empowerment, and healthy ageing in the community.
