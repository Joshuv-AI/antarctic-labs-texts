Nonlinear relationship between urinary benzophenone-3 and diabetes risk among American adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Leilei Guo, Jian Li, Lina Cai, Li Hu, Yueshan Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSome epidemiological studies suggest that exposure to bisphenol A is associated with diabetes; however, there is limited evidence of a dose-response relationship between urinary benzophenone-3 (BP-3) and diabetes. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between urinary BP-3 levels and the risk of diabetes in American adults.MethodsA cross-sectional study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to assess participants aged 20 and older in the United States from 2005 to 2016. The exposure variable was urinary BP-3 and the outcome diabetes risk. Multivariate logistic regression and subgroup analyses were performed to assess the independent association between urinary BP-3 and diabetes risk. Smooth curve fits were applied to examine the non-linear relationship. We performed several sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our results.ResultsOur study included 8027 participants, 50.3% of whom were male, and with a mean age of 49.3 ± 17.3 years. After adjustment for potential confounders, the analysis indicated that each unit increase in log2-transformed BP-3 was associated with a 5% reduction in diabetes risk (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97, P < 0.001). Compared to the Q1 group, the risk of developing diabetes was significantly lower in the Q4 group (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.52–0.78, P < 0.001). L-shaped relationship was observed between log2 BP-3 and diabetes in adults in the USA, with an inflection point of 6.801 ng/ml (OR = 0.916, 95% CI: 0.885–0.949, P < 0.001). Our sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of our results.ConclusionsUrinary BP-3 levels exhibited an L-shaped relationship with diabetes risk in the United States, with an inflection point at approximately 6.801 ng/mL.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02133-6.Keywords: Urinary benzophenone-3, Diabetes, Endocrine-disrupting chemicals, NHANES, Bisphenol A

Introduction

Diabetes is a major global health challenge characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion and/or action. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) predicts that the prevalence of diabetes will continue to rise, potentially affecting more than 700 million people by 2045 [1]. This rising prevalence entails significant morbidity and mortality risks for patients and substantial economic burdens on healthcare systems worldwide. The etiology of diabetes, traditionally linked to genetic, metabolic, and lifestyle factors, has expanded to include environmental contaminants—notably endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs)—as potentially important contributors to its pathogenesis [2].

EDCs are defined as exogenous substances or mixtures that interfere with hormonal action. These compounds, which mimic or disrupt normal endocrine function, are ubiquitous in industrial chemicals, pesticides, plastics, personal care products, and pharmaceuticals [3]. Notably, the rising prevalence of diabetes in the United States parallels the increased production of synthetic chemicals. While most research had focused on bisphenol A (BPA)—an EDC associated with type 2 diabetes [4, 5]—accumulating evidence indicated that it promoted disease development through multiple pathways, possibly through endocrine disruption (such as estrogenic effects) [6], insulin resistance [7], and effects on the differentiation and metabolism of fat cells [8].

Benzophenone-3 (BP-3), a widely used ultraviolet filter in sunscreens and cosmetics, is absorbed through the skin and detectable in urine and blood, raising health concerns due to its structural similarity to BPA [9]. The relationship between BP-3 and diabetes remained poorly understood, with existing studies having reported inconsistent findings—ranging from null [10] and adverse [11] to protective effects [12]—which were likely attributable to methodological heterogeneity and confounding [13]. Furthermore, although emerging evidence had suggested a potential inverse association, few studies had quantitatively assessed the dose–response relationship and biological mechanisms between BP-3 and diabetes in a U.S. population.

Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) of U.S. adults (2005–2016), we hypothesize that urinary BP-3 levels exhibit a nonlinear relationship with diabetes risk in the U.S. population. The present study was therefore designed to (1) characterize the nature of this dose-response relationship and (2) determine its generalizability across major population subgroups.

Methods

Data source

NHANES is a nationally representative survey carried out by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) in the United States. It utilizes stratified, multistage probability cluster sampling to evaluate the health and nutrition status of the non-institutionalized population. This study used data from adult participants (≥ 20 years old) in the 2005–2016 NHANES cycles. The NCHS Research Ethics Review Committee approved the NHANES research protocol, and participants provided written informed consent at enrollment [14]. All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the NHANES [15]. The Institutional Review Board of Jiang you people’s hospital concluded that the study was exempt since it utilized publicly available de-identified data and waived the need for informed consent. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Study population

Participants in the NHANES (2005–2016) were included in the study. We included 60,936 participants who underwent testing for urinary BP-3. A total of 52,909 participants were excluded for the following reasons: 26,756 on the basis of age (under 20 years), 653 due to pregnancy, 2,567 due to missing diabetes data, 21,121 due to missing urinary benzophenone-3 data, and 1,812 due to missing covariates data. Finally, 8,027 subjects were enrolled. A flowchart of participant enrollment is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of patient selection

Assessment of urinary benzophenone-3

Urine samples were processed and stored at -20℃ prior to being shipped to the National Center for Environmental Health for analysis. To enhance sensitivity and selectivity, phenols were derivatized into alkyl or acyl derivatives before analysis by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. The analytical methodology employed solid-phase extraction coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry, achieving a lower limit of detection for BP-3 set at 0.4 ng/mL. NHANES provides the limit of detections (LODs) for Urinary BP-3, and for concentrations below the LODs, it uses the LODs divided by the square root of 2 as a substitute [16].

Considering the pronounced skewed distribution of urinary BP-3, previous studies have utilized a log-normal distribution for subsequent analyses [17]. We also employed the conventional creatinine adjustment method, which entails dividing the urinary BP-3 concentration in each spot urine sample by the corresponding urinary creatinine concentration to analyze BP-3 levels [13] (sensitivity analysis).

Assessment of diabetes

Diabetes was defined by a self-reported diagnosis, HbA1c ≥ 6.5% [12], fasting plasma glucose (FBG) ≥ 126 mg/dl, or 2-hour plasma glucose (2-h PG) ≥ 200 mg/dl (sensitivity analysis) [18].

Assessment of covariates

Based on prior research and our cohort’s descriptive statistics [12, 19, 20], the potential covariates included age, sex, race (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, or other), education level (< 9 years, 9–12 years, or > 12 years), marital status (married/living with a partner or living alone), family income (low < 1.3, moderate 1.3–3.5, and high > 3.5), smoking status (never, current, or former smoker), alcohol consumption status (non-drinker or drinker), body mass index (normal weight < 25 kg/m², overweight 25–30 kg/m², or obesity > 30 kg/m²), physical activity, serum creatinine, uric acid, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), hypertension, coronary heart disease. Physical activity was defined as the time spent per week engaged in activities such as walking, cycling, housework, work-related tasks, and recreational pursuits. If no physical activity was performed during the week, the time was recorded as zero [21]. Hypertension and coronary heart disease were identified based on participants’ self-reported history of these conditions as diagnosed by a physician. Serum levels of uric acid, creatinine, total cholesterol, and HDL-C were obtained from the NHANES laboratory data.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variable data, we used the mean ± standard deviation for normal distributions and the median for skewed distributions. For categorical variables, we used frequency or percentile. The student’s t-test was used for normally distributed variables, while the Mann-Whitney U test was used for variables with a skewed distribution. The chi-squared test was applied for nominal parameters, and Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used to assess differences between groups. Urinary BP-3 levels were log-2 transformed to approximate a normal distribution as a continuous variable. The study population was divided into four groups based on urinary log2BP-3 levels as follows Q1: <1.93, Q2: 1.94–3.66, Q3: 3.67–5.88, and Q4: >5.89. Confounding factors were a significant concern in the multivariate analysis, and various statistical models were used to ensure the stability of the results. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to examine the association between BP-3 levels and diabetes, adjusting for various potential confounders. A multivariate-adjusted restricted cubic spline model was constructed with 4 knots to fit the OR curves, in order to explore the potential non-linear dose-response relationship between urinary BP-3 levels and diabetes. We assessed the association between BP-3 levels and diabetes using sequentially adjusted multivariate logistic regression models. Model 1 adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic factors: age, gender, race, education level, marital status, ratio of family income to poverty, and physical activity. Model 2 further adjusted for lifestyle and clinical measures: body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, creatinine, uric acid, total cholesterol, and HDL-C. Model 3 additionally adjusted for prevalent comorbidities: coronary heart disease and hypertension.

Subgroup analysis

The subgroups were analyzed using a stratified binary logistic regression model. Based on biological plausibility, clinical relevance, and existing literature, we selected the following variables—gender, race, family income, body mass index, physical activity, hypertension, and coronary heart disease–as stratification factors for subgroup analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was used to assess heterogeneity between subgroups. Likelihood ratio tests were performed to examine interactions between these subgroups.

Sensitivity analysis

A series of sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our research findings [22]. We addressed missing data through multiple imputation (5 replications, chained equations via R mice) and conducted multivariate logistic regression on the imputed data [23]. The potential for unmeasured confounding was assessed using E-values [24]. To reinforce our inferences, we employed several association models—propensity score adjustment (PSA) [25], propensity score matching (PSM) [26], and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) [27]—and compared their resulting effect sizes and p-values. We also explored the relationship using different diagnostic criteria (FBG ≥ 126 mg/dl or 2-h PG ≥ 200 mg/dl) [18], creatinine-adjusted BP-3 concentrations [28], and in a subgroup excluding patients with chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 ml/min/1.73 m²). Lastly, we examined the associations of other environmental pollutants (Bisphenol A and triclosan) with diabetes to assess the specificity of our primary finding.

The statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.2.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software version 1.9.2. Statistical significance was determined at a two-sided P value of < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presented the baseline characteristics of all participants by BP-3 quartiles. A total of 8027 adults were included in this study, of whom 50.3% were male and 49.7% were female, with a mean age of 49.3 ± 17.3 years. Higher levels of BP-3 were more common among non-Hispanic whites, married or cohabitating individuals, those with higher educational attainment and family income, greater physical activity, non-smokers, and alcohol consumers (P < 0.001). Participants with higher BP-3 levels also exhibited lower creatinine and uric acid levels, lower body mass index, higher HDL-C levels, and a lower incidence of diabetes. Furthermore, BP-3 levels were significantly lower in participants with diabetes compared to those without diabetes (P < 0.001). Association between BP-3 levels and diabetes

Univariate analysis revealed that age, female, race, educational level, family income, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, physical activity, creatinine, uric acid, total cholesterol, HDL-C, coronary heart disease, hypertension, and BP-3 were associated with diabetes (Table S2).

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the relationship between BP-3 levels and diabetes. When BP-3 was analyzed as a continuous variable, a significant inverse association between BP-3 and diabetes was observed in model 3, with an odds ratio of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97). After converting the continuous BP-3 variable to a categorical variable based on quartiles, it was observed that the prevalence of diabetes decreased with increasing BP-3 levels. In model 3, the odds ratios for participants in BP-3 quartiles Q2, Q3, and Q4 were 0.80 (95% CI: 0.67–0.96), 0.74 (95% CI: 0.61–0.90), and 0.63 (95% CI: 0.52–0.78), respectively, compared with those in the Q1 reference group (P for trend < 0.001). These results remained robust in the other models (Table 2).

In the analysis using restricted cubic splines, a non-linear relationship (P = 0.009) was observed between BP-3 levels and diabetes, displaying an L-shaped curve. (Fig. 2). Using threshold analysis, individuals with BP-3 levels below 6.801 ng/dL had an OR of 0.916 (95% CI: 0.885–0.949, P < 0.001) for developing diabetes (Table 3). This indicates that for each log2 unit increase in BP-3 levels, the risk of diabetes decreased by 8.4%. Conversely, when BP-3 levels surpassed 6.801 ng/mL, a significant association between BP-3 levels and diabetes was no longer observed, suggesting that the risk of diabetes did not decrease further with increasing BP-3 levels beyond this threshold.

Sensitivity analyses

To robust of our findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. In our core sensitivity analyses, the significant inverse association between BP-3 and diabetes persisted after multiple imputation for missing data (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.51–0.73, P < 0.001; Table S3). This finding was further supported by PSM, which successfully balanced 1,451 participant pairs (Table S4-1) and consistently showed significantly lower diabetes incidence in the high-exposure group across all analytical methods—including PSA and IPTW (Table S4-2). The robustness of this association was additionally confirmed by an E-value of 2.55, which indicated that substantial unmeasured confounding would have been necessary to nullify the observed effect. We further verified the consistency of our findings through several supplementary analyses. The relationship remained stable when we used creatinine-adjusted urinary BP-3 concentrations (Table S6) and after we excluded individuals with chronic kidney disease (Table S7). Although statistical significance was not achieved when applying stricter diabetes diagnostic criteria–likely due to reduced event numbers (285/3031)--the effect direction remained consistent with our primary analysis (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.59–1.26; Table S5). Finally, specificity analyses revealed no significant associations between other environmental pollutants (Bisphenol A and triclosan) and diabetes in fully adjusted models (Table S8), which supported the distinct nature of the BP-3–diabetes relationship observed in our study.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, in cross-sectional study, we consistently observed that urinary BP-3 levels exhibited an L-shaped relationship with diabetes risk in the United States, with an inflection point at approximately 6.801 ng/mL. A variety of stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of these associations. These findings provide new insights for future research and a scientific basis for public health decisions.

Previous investigations had suggested a potential protective association between BP-3 levels and diabetes risk. Li et al. [29]. reported a lower incidence of diabetes among individuals with higher BP-3 concentrations. This pattern was further supported by the EARTH study [30], in which elevated BP-3 levels during early pregnancy correlated with lower glucose levels. Furthermore, the same investigation observed that women with higher mid-pregnancy BP-3 levels were less likely to exhibit abnormal glucose tolerance [31]. Longitudinal evidence corroborated these findings, demonstrating that reduced risk of new-onset diabetes had been associated with higher urinary BP-3 levels [32]. However, it was noteworthy that these previous studies had been primarily conducted in pregnant populations, which limited their generalizability to other demographic groups.

A recent U.S. study [12] reported an inverse relationship between BP-3 levels and diabetes prevalence, which showed consistent association directions across BMI categories (< 30 and ≥ 30 kg/m²) that aligned with our findings. While that study suggested BMI might mediate the relationship between phenols/parabens and diabetes, our analysis did not confirm this mediating effect. Their study primarily examined the combined effects of multiple phenolic compounds, without specifically assessing the BP-3-diabetes dose-response relationship or exploring relevant biological mechanisms.

Unlike previous studies, we not only found that higher concentrations of urinary BP-3 are associated with a lower risk of diabetes, but also identified an L-shaped relationship between urinary BP-3 levels and diabetes risk, with an inflection point at approximately 6.801 ng/mL. The clinical significance of our study was the establishment of urinary BP-3 levels—characterized by an L-shaped inflection point at 6.801 ng/mL—as a valuable indicator for diabetes risk stratification. These findings suggested that clinicians could incorporate urinary BP-3 measurement into routine metabolic assessments to identify at-risk individuals (BP-3 ≤ 6.801 ng/mL) and implement targeted lifestyle interventions, such as weight reduction and physical activity, to mitigate diabetes progression.

In the family income subgroup, a reduced diabetes risk was observed among high-income families, potentially attributable to higher BP-3 exposure from cosmetics more frequently used by this demographic. Similarly, in the physical activity subgroup, individuals with higher activity levels exhibited a lower diabetes incidence, which may have been related to increased sunscreen use during prolonged outdoor exercise [9, 33]. In the gender subgroup, males with BP-3 exposure also showed a reduced diabetes risk; however, the clinical significance of this association requires cautious interpretation due to multiple testing considerations [34], despite the consistent direction of effects observed across all subgroups.

The precise mechanisms underlying the association between BP-3 and diabetes risk have not yet been fully elucidated. Previous studies have demonstrated that phenolic compounds exhibit both anti-androgenic and estrogen-like properties [9]. Estrogens play a crucial role in maintaining body weight and metabolic health by promoting healthier fat distribution and helping prevent insulin resistance [35]. Additionally, BP-3’s anti-adipogenic activity leads to reduced body fat and disruption of normal adipogenesis processes [36, 37]. Individuals with high income and greater physical activity levels tend to use more sunscreen and personal care products, resulting in accumulation of BP-3 and its metabolites in the body, with partial excretion through urine. The accumulated BP-3 may potentially reduce insulin resistance and diabetes risk through its anti-androgenic and estrogen-like properties combined with its anti-adipogenic activity.

This study possesses several notable strengths, including the use of a large, nationally representative sample of U.S. adults. The analysis identified a robust nonlinear relationship between BP-3 and diabetes, revealing greater complexity in this association than a simple linear model would suggest and indicating a potential biological threshold for more precise risk assessment. The investigation further accounted for a comprehensive set of potential confounders—such as physical activity, blood lipids, and uric acid—while using E-values to quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. Another distinctive feature was its specific focus on isolating the effect of urinary BP-3 on diabetes, as opposed to examining mixed phenol exposures. The robustness of the primary findings was consistently supported through multiple sensitivity analyses.

However, this study has some limitations. The reliance on self-reported diabetes or HbA1c may introduce recall bias and under-ascertainment, while the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Although single BP-3 urine measurements may not perfectly reflect long-term exposure, evidence supports their use for certain EDCs [38]. Further, our correction of urinary levels used traditional approaches without specific gravity or covariate-adjusted methods. Additionally, NHANES data restricted our ability to distinguish diabetes types (though type 2 is presumably predominant [39]) and did not include blood BP-3 measurements, precluding combined analysis and highlighting a need for future investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, an L-shaped relationship was observed between urinary benzophenone-3 levels and diabetes risk among adults in the United States, with an inflection point at approximately 6.801 ng/mL. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the relationship between exposure to BP-3 and diabetes.
