Bicombing the mapping class group and Teichmüller space via stable cubical intervals
Matthew Gentry Durham

Abstract
. In this mostly expository article, we provide a new account of our proof with Minsky and Sisto that mapping class groups and Teichmüller spaces admit bicombings. More generally, we explain how the hierarchical hull of a pair of points in any colorable hierarchically hyperbolic space is quasi-isometric to a finite CAT(0) cube complex of bounded dimension, with the added property that perturbing the pair of points results in a uniformly bounded change to the cubical structure. Our approach is simplified and new in many aspects.

2.
Beginning at the end

The purpose of this section is to prove the main consequence of the technical results we will spend the rest of the paper establishing. That is, we will begin by explaining how the Stable Cubical Intervals Theorem

is precisely
T(p,\mathcal{H})
. Set
p_{1}
to be the diagonally opposite corner of
C_{0}
. Equivalently,

is obtained from

by flipping orientations on the hyperplanes in
T(p,\mathcal{H})
.

•

Define
p_{i}
for

.

Thus, at each step
p_{i}\mapsto p_{i+1}
, the path crosses the set of
\prec
-minimal hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}(p_{i},b)
. The following proposition states the basic properties of the Niblo–Reeves paths:

Proposition 2.11
.

Continuing with the above notation, the following hold:

(1)

There exists some
n
so that
p_{n}=b
.

(2)

For all
0\leq i\leq n
, we have
d^{\infty}_{X}(p_{i},p_{i+1})=1
.

(3)

Any path obtained by concatenating
\ell^{1}
-geodesics between
p_{i},p_{i+1}
for
0\leq i<n
is an
\ell^{1}
-geodesic.

Proof.

For (1), observe that the sequence
p_{i}
only crosses hyperplanes separating
a
from
b
. Each step
p_{i}\mapsto p_{i+1}
of the sequence crosses the collection of
\prec
-minimal hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}(p_{i},b)\subset\mathcal{H}
. Now (1) is equivalent to the claim that
\mathcal{H}=\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{H}(p_{i},b)
, which is to say that this process exhausts the set of hyperplanes separating
a
and
b
, which is clear because
\mathcal{H}
is finite.

Item (2) is by construction, since
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
are opposite corners of a cube.

Finally, item (3) follows from the fact that
\mathcal{H}(p_{i},b)\subset\mathcal{H}(p_{i+1},b)
with
\mathcal{H}(p_{i+1},b)-\mathcal{H}(p_{i},b)=\mathcal{H}(p_{i},p_{i+1})
. Hence if
\mathbf{h}\in T(p_{i},b)
, then
\mathbf{h}
does not separate
p_{i}
from

for

. This says that the sequence
\{p_{i}\}
does not cross any hyperplane twice, and it follows that the concatenation of the
\ell^{1}
-geodesic segments will not either, making it an
\ell^{1}
-geodesic.
∎

The following is a useful observation about this construction:

Proposition 2.12
.

Let

be an isomorphism of cube complexes. If
\gamma
is the Niblo–Reeves path between

is the Niblo–Reeves path between

.

We can now state the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 2.13

(Stable moves)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{H}
is hyperplane, and let
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}-\{\mathbf{h}\})

\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}(a)=a^{\prime}
and

(1)

We have

(2)

For all

In other words, Niblo–Reeves paths are stable under hyperplane deletion maps.

The following notation will be useful for us:

Definition 2.14
.

Let

. We set

of

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}(p_{i},p_{i+1})

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}-\{\mathbf{h}\}

.

If

, then

Proof.

This is essentially
[

, Lemma 5.12]
, though the proof is basically immediate in our setting. Note that the transition index

. Since the

and

measures the maximal length of a chain of hyperplanes separating them (plus

more than

∎

Proof of the Stable Moves Theorem
2.13
.

Suppose
\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{2}
separate

and

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathbf{h}_{1}

2.4.
Bicombings

Our ultimate goal is to prove that mapping class groups and Teichmüller spaces admits bicombings (Theorem
A
). Roughly, a bicombing on a space
X
is a family of uniform quasi-geodesic paths that is transitive (i.e., connects any pair of points of
X
), and for which paths in the family between pairs of endpoints that are close to each other uniformly fellow-travel in a parameterized fashion. The notion of bicombing was first introduced by Thurston in the context of algorithmic problems in groups. It is a straightforward but informative exercise to prove that geodesics in hyperbolic and CAT(0) spaces satisfy this property, and this is precisely what the definition is intended to generalize.

The following definition is essentially from
[
2
]
, as restated in
[
32
, Definition 6.4]
:

Definition 2.16

(Bicombings)
.

A
discrete, bounded, quasi-geodesic bicombing
of a metric space
X
consists of a family of discrete paths
\{\Gamma_{x,y}\}_{x,y\in X}
and a constant
\zeta>0
satisfying the following:

(1)

Quasi-geodesic
: For any
x,y\in X
with
d=d_{X}(x,y)
, there exists
n_{x,y}\leq d\zeta+\zeta
so that the path
\Gamma_{x,y}:\{0,\dots,n_{x,y}\}\rightarrow X
is a
(\zeta,\zeta)
-quasi-isometric embedding with
\Gamma_{x,y}(0)=x
and
\Gamma_{x,y}(n_{x,y})=y
; and

(2)

Fellow-traveling
: If
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in X
and
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),d_{X}(y,y^{\prime})\leq 1
, then for all
t\in\{0,\dots,\max\{n_{x,y},n_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\}\}
, we have

d_{X}(\Gamma_{x,y}(t),\Gamma_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(t))\leq\zeta.

For our purposes, the fellow-traveling condition in item (2) is the key challenge. At this point, there are many constructions of uniform quasigeodesics in mapping class groups.

2.5.
Building the bicombing

by taking

to be the image under the quasi-isometry

3.1.
Basic setup of an HHS

Roughly, a
hierarchically hyperbolic
structure is a pair
(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
where
\mathcal{X}
is a uniformly quasigeodesic metric space and
\mathfrak{S}
is an index set for a family of uniformly hyperbolic spaces
\{\mathcal{C}(U)\}_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}
. The ambient space
\mathcal{X}
admits a family of uniformly coarsely Lipschitz projections
\pi_{U}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{C}(U)
, and those projections, along with certain relations on the family
\mathfrak{S}
, largely control the coarse geometry of
\mathcal{X}
.

The following extracts some of the properties we need from the axiomatic setup
[
11
]
, see
[
69
]
for a discussion.

(1)

For each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
there exist an
E_{\mathfrak{S}}
-hyperbolic space
\mathcal{C}(U)
and a
E_{\mathfrak{S}}
-coarsely surjective
(E_{\mathfrak{S}},E_{\mathfrak{S}})
-coarsely Lipschitz map
\pi_{U}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(U),
with
E_{\mathfrak{S}}
independent of
U
.

(2)

The set
\mathfrak{S}
has the following relations:

•

It is equipped with a partial order
\sqsubset
called
nesting
. If
\mathfrak{S}\neq\emptyset,
the set
\mathfrak{S}
contains a unique
\sqsubset
-maximal element
S.

•

It has a symmetric, anti-reflexive relation
\perp
called
orthogonality
. If
U\sqsubset V
, then
U,V
are not related via
\perp
. Furthermore, domains have
orthogonal containers
: if
U\in\mathfrak{S}
and there is a domain orthogonal to
U
then there is a domain
W
such that
V\sqsubset W
whenever
V\perp U.

•

For any distinct
U,V\in\mathfrak{S}
unrelated via
\perp,\sqsubset
, then we say
U,V
are
transverse
and write
U\pitchfork V.

(3)

There exists an integer called the
complexity
of
\mathcal{X}
and denoted
\xi(\mathfrak{S})
such that whenever
U_{1},U_{2},\cdots U_{n}
is a collection of pairwise non-transverse domains, then
n\leq\xi(\mathfrak{S}).

(4)

If
U\sqsubset V
or
U\pitchfork V,
then there exists a set
\rho^{U}_{V}
of diameter at most
E_{\mathfrak{S}}
in
\mathcal{C}(V)
.

(5)

If
U\sqsubset V
, there is a map
\rho^{V}_{U}:\mathcal{C}(V)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(U)
, called the
relative projection
of
U
to
V
which satisfies the following: For
x\in\mathcal{X}
with
d_{V}(\pi_{V}(x),\rho^{U}_{V})>E_{\mathfrak{S}},
we have
\pi_{U}(x)\underset{E_{\mathfrak{S}}}{\asymp}\rho^{V}_{U}(\pi_{V}(x)).

For two points
x,y\in\mathcal{X},
it is standard to use
d_{U}(x,y)
to denote
d_{\mathcal{C}(U)}(\pi_{U}(x),\pi_{U}(y)),
and similarly for subsets of
\mathcal{X}.
For a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X},
we will also use
\mathrm{diam}_{U}(A)
to denote the diameter of the set
\pi_{U}(A)\subset\mathcal{C}(U)
.

3.2.
Basic HHS facts

We now collect some useful facts about HHSes from various places.

The following definition from
[
5
,
4
,
11
]
, will be important for us. The idea here is that we want to combine all of the projections
\pi_{U}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{C}(U)
for each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
into a global projection
\Pi:\mathcal{X}\to\prod_{U}\mathcal{C}(U)
. The following definition uses the relations on
\mathfrak{S}
to constrain tuples in
\prod_{U}\mathcal{C}(U)
.

Definition 3.1

(Consistent tuple)
.

Let
\kappa\geq 0
and let
(b_{U})\in\prod_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}2^{\mathcal{C}(U)}
be a tuple such that for each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
,
the
U
–coordinate
b_{U}
has diameter
\leq\kappa
. Then
(b_{U})
is
\kappa
–consistent
if for all

V,W\in\mathfrak{S}
, we have

\min\{\mathbf{d}_{V}(b_{V},\rho^{W}_{V}),\mathbf{d}_{W}(b_{W},\rho^{V}_{W})\}\leq\kappa

whenever
V\pitchfork W
and

\min\{\mathbf{d}_{W}(x,\rho^{V}_{W}),\mathrm{diam}_{V}(b_{V}\cup\rho^{W}_{V})\}\leq\kappa

whenever
V\sqsubset W
.

•

We refer to the above inequalities as the
consistency inequalities
.

The following is a key fact for us. It says that the image of
\Pi:\mathcal{X}\to\prod_{U}\mathcal{C}(U)
consists of uniformly consistent tuples:

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists
\theta=\theta(\mathfrak{S})>0
so that if
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then the tuple
(\pi_{U}(x))\in\prod_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}\mathcal{C}(U)
is
\theta
-consistent.

On the other hand, we would like to know whether all consistent tuples in
\prod_{U}\mathcal{C}(U)
essentially come from
\mathcal{X}
. That is the content of the following Realization Theorem. In particular, says that any tuple satisfying Definition
3.1
can be coarsely realized via a point in
\mathcal{X}
. It is
[
4
, Theorem 4.3]
for mapping class groups,
[
37
, Theorem 3.2]
for Teichmüller space with the Teichmüller metric, and
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
for the general HHS case:

Theorem 3.3

(Realization)
.

For each
\kappa\geq 1
there exists
\theta_{e},\theta_{u}\geq 0
such that the following holds. Let
\mathbf{b}=(b_{U})_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}\in\prod_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}2^{\mathcal{C}(U)}
be
\kappa
-consistent.

Then there exists
x\in X
so that
d_{U}(b_{U},\pi_{W}(x))\leq\theta_{e}
for all
U\in\mathfrak{S}
. Moreover,
x
is
coarsely unique
in the sense that

\mathrm{diam}_{\mathcal{X}}\{x\in\mathcal{X}|d_{W}(b_{W},\pi_{W}(x))\leq\theta_{e}\textrm{ for all }U\in\mathfrak{S}\}<\theta_{u}.

Remark 3.4
.

Notably, the proof of Theorem
3.3
in
[
11
]
does not involve the hyperbolicity of the associated spaces
\{\mathcal{C}(U)\}_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}
, and actually follows from a weak form which is axiomatized into the HHS setup. In this sense, realization is a purely hierarchical fact which plays a similar role to the existence of a coarse median in Bowditch’s analysis
[
19
]
.

Perhaps the main technique in HHSes is taking an object in the ambient space
\mathcal{X}
and projecting it to all of the hyperbolic spaces, doing hyperbolic geometry, and then reassembling the pieces via hierarchical tools like the Realization Theorem
3.3
. Toward that end, and recognizing that HHSes are fundamentally coarse spaces, we will usually want to restrict ourselves to the
relevant
collection of hyperbolic spaces to which an object has a sufficiently large projection.

Definition 3.5

(Relevant domains)
.

Given a subset
A\subset\mathcal{X}
and
K>0
, we say that
U\in\mathfrak{S}
is
K
-
relevant
for
A
if
\mathrm{diam}_{U}(A)\geq K
. We denote the set of
K
-relevant domains for
A
by
\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(A)
.

The following Passing-Up lemma is a foundational fact about HHSes; in fact, it can be taken to be an axiom
[
36
, Proposition 4.15]
. Roughly speaking, the passing-up lemma says that if
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
have a large number of lower complexity relevant domains, then there must be some higher complexity relevant domain containing at least one of them, thereby allowing us to “pass up” the
\sqsubset
-lattice.

Lemma 3.6

(Passing-up)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an HHS with constant
E_{\mathfrak{S}}
. For every
D>0
there is an integer
PU=PU(D)>0
such that if
V\in\mathfrak{S}
and
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
satisfy
d_{U_{i}}(x,y)>E_{\mathfrak{S}}
for a collection of domains
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{PU}
with each
U_{i}\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
, then there exists
W\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
and
i
with
U_{i}\sqsubsetneq W
such that
d_{W}(x,y)>D.

As an example of how this lemma is foundational, we observe the following corollary. The original proof is embedded in the proof of Theorem
3.3
in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
, but not explicitly stated. We give a proof for completeness:

Corollary 3.7
.

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, the set
\mathrm{Rel}_{E_{\mathfrak{S}}}(x,y)
is finite.

Proof.

Let
C
be the coarse Lipschitz constant for all of the projections
\pi_{U}
for
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, and set
D>Cd_{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)+C
. If
\#\mathrm{Rel}_{E_{\mathfrak{S}}}(x,y)>P(D)
, then the Passing-Up Lemma
3.6
provides a domain
W\in\mathfrak{S}
with
d_{W}(x,y)>D
, which is impossible.
∎

Another fundamental fact about HHSes is the following distance formula, which originated in work of Masur-Minsky
[
54
]
, and found various iterations elsewhere
[
21
,
60
,
35
,
51
]
before being generalized to all HHSes in
[
11
]
. See
[
11
, Theorem 4.5]
for a proof from the now-standard HHS setup, and also
[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
36
, Corollary C]
from proofs using cubical model techniques, which we also recover here below in Corollary
6.11
.

Theorem 3.8

(Distance formula)
.

There exists
K_{0}=K_{0}(\mathfrak{S})>0
so that if
K>K_{0}
and
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, then

d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,y)\asymp\sum_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K}

where the coarse equality
\asymp
depends only on
\mathfrak{S}
and
K
.

Philosophically (for our purposes), the distance formula says that if we want to understand how to move between two points in an HHS, it should be enough to focus on the set of relevant domains between them.

The next definition is of a special kind of path in an HHS. The proof of their existence is always tied up in the proof of the more difficult bound of the distance formula, as is the case in this paper in Subsection
6.4
. Otherwise, we will not need this notion.

Definition 3.9
.

A path
\gamma:I\to\mathcal{X}
in an HHS
(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
is a
L
-
hierarchy path
if its projection
\pi_{U}(\gamma)\subset\mathcal{C}(U)
is an unparameterized
(L,L)
-quasi-geodesic for each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
.

We end this subsection with a basic observation, which generalizes the observation that if two subsurfaces
U,V
are not transverse, then their boundary curves must be disjoint up to isotopy.

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose that
U,V,W\in\mathrm{Rel}_{K_{0}}(a,b)
for
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
.

(1)

If
U,V\sqsubset W
and
U,V
are not transverse, then
d_{W}(\rho^{U}_{W},\rho^{V}_{W})<E_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

(2)

If
U\pitchfork V
and
W\sqsubset V
with
W,U
not orthogonal, then
d_{U}(\rho^{V}_{W},\rho^{W}_{U})<E_{\mathfrak{S}}
.

Finally, we end with a key HHS axiom, originally proven for mapping class groups in
[
52
, Theorem 3.1]
:

Axiom 3.11

(Bounded geodesic image)
.

If
U\sqsubset V
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(V)
is a geodesic with
d_{V}(\rho^{U}_{V},\gamma)>E_{\mathfrak{S}}
, then diam
(\rho^{V}_{U}(\gamma))\leq E_{\mathfrak{S}}.
Furthermore, for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and any geodesic
\gamma
connecting
\pi_{V}(x),\pi_{V}(y)
, if
d_{V}(\rho^{U}_{V},\gamma)>E_{\mathfrak{S}}
, then
d(\pi_{U}(x),\pi_{U}(y))\leq E_{\mathfrak{S}}.

3.3.
Hierarchical hulls and related basic facts

In our HHS setup, the basic object we will want to consider is the following hierarchical notion of a coarse convex hull. These
hierarchical hulls
are built out of consistent tuples of points whose coordinates are contained in the hyperbolic hulls in each domain. More specifically, given a pair of points
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, for each,
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, we set
H_{U}=\mathrm{hull}_{U}(\pi_{U}(\{x,y\}))
, where
\mathrm{hull}_{U}
denotes the hyperbolic hull in
\mathcal{C}(U)
.

Definition 3.12

(Hierarchical hull)
.

Given a constant
\theta>0
, we define the
\theta
-hull
of
x,y
to be all
z\in\mathcal{X}
so that
d_{U}(z,H_{U})<\theta
for all
U\in\mathfrak{S}
.

•

We denote the
\theta
-hull of
x,y
by
H_{\theta}=\mathrm{hull}_{\theta}(x,y)
.

One of the key facts about hierarchical hulls is that they are
hierarchically quasiconvex

[
11
, Lemma 6.2]
, see
[
63
]
for more on convexity in HHSes. We do not need this definition for our purposes, but we do need the following consequence:

Lemma 3.13
.

For any
\theta>0
, there exists is a
(J,J)
-coarse Lipschitz retract
\mathfrak{g}_{H_{\theta}}:\mathcal{X}\to H_{\theta}
, where
J=J(\theta,\mathfrak{S})>0
.

In the above lemma, the map
\mathfrak{g}_{H_{\theta}}:\mathcal{X}\to H_{\theta}
is a hierarchical analogue of a cubical gate map. When
z\in\mathcal{X}
, the point
\mathfrak{g}_{H_{\theta}}(z)\in H_{\theta}
is defined by coordinate-wise closest-point projection of
\pi_{U}(z)
to
H_{U}
and then applying the Realization Theorem
3.3
; see
[
11
, Proposition 6.3]
and the surrounding text for details.

3.4.
Consequences of the Passing-up Lemma
3.6

The following statements are useful for the sort of distance formula related hierarchical accounting that we need to do the proof of the cubical model theorem. The first says that any given relevant domain for a pair
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
can only nest into boundedly-many other relevant domains for
x,y
. It first appeared in
[
32
, Lemma 2.15]
; see
[
24
, Lemma 11.13]
for a different proof and
[
36
, Lemma 4.8]
for a version that allows
x,y
to be at infinity.

Lemma 3.14

(Covering)
.

For any HHS
(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
, there exists
N=N(\mathfrak{S})>0
so that the following holds. For any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and all
U\in\mathrm{Rel}_{50E_{\mathfrak{S}}}(x,y)
, there are at most
N
domains
V\in\mathrm{Rel}_{50E_{\mathfrak{S}}}(x,y)
so that
U\sqsubset V
.

Another consequence of Lemma
3.6
is the following distance formula type statement, which is a less general version of
[
36
, Proposition 4.14]
. Roughly, it says that the number of medium-sized relevant domains for a pair
x,y
can be accounted for (i.e., in terms of the domain projections) by large-sized domains higher up the hierarchy in a precise fashion.

Proposition 3.15

(Bounding large containers)
.

Let
K_{1}>50E_{\mathfrak{S}}
,
K_{2}\geq K_{1}+47E_{\mathfrak{S}}
, and
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K_{1}}(x,y),\mathcal{U}_{2}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K_{2}}(x,y),
and
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{U}_{1}-\mathcal{U}_{2}
. Then the following hold:

(1)

For all but
PU(K_{2})-1
domains
V\in\mathcal{V}
, there exists
W\in\mathcal{U}_{2}
with
V\sqsubset W
.

(2)

For any
W\in\mathcal{U}_{2}
, if
\mathcal{W}=\{V\in\mathcal{V}|V\sqsubset W\}
then we have
\#\mathcal{W}\prec d_{W}(x,y)
with constants depending only on
\mathfrak{S}
and
K_{2}
.

The above proposition is useful for proving the following slight generalization of the Distance Formula
3.8
. It’s useful for circumstances, such as in Section
5.1
, where we want to make distance formula-type arguments where the summands are only coarsely equal to the standard distance formula terms. Probably the key example of this is when we want to replace
d_{U}(x,y)
with
d_{T_{U}}(x,y)
, where each
T_{U}
is a uniform quasi-geodesic in
\mathcal{C}(U)
between
\pi_{U}(x)
and
\pi_{U}(y)
. This originally appeared in
[
55
, Lemma 9.6]
for mapping class groups, and see
[
10
, Theorem 2.9]
(which depends on the distance formula) and
[
36
, Corollary 4.17]
(which does not) for proofs in the HHS setting.

Corollary 3.16
.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. Suppose that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a function satisfying

\sigma(d_{U}(x,y))\asymp d_{U}(x,y)

for each
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, where the constants in
\asymp
are independent of
U
. Then for any
K_{1}>50E_{\mathfrak{S}}
, we have

\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K_{1}}\asymp\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}[\sigma(d_{U}(x,y))]_{K_{1}}

where the constants in
\asymp
depend only on
\mathfrak{S}
,
K_{1}
, and
\sigma
.

4.
From hierarchical hulls to collapsed interval systems

In the next three sections, we will give an overview of the cubical model construction for the hierarchical hull of a pair of points in an HHS from
[
36
]
. The original construction is due to Behrstock-Hagen-Sisto
[
6
]
, and see also Bowditch
[
19
]
for a version in the more general setting of coarse median spaces satisfying HHS-like axioms.

The main goal of this section is to explain how to take a pair of points
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
in an HHS
(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
and produce a family of intervals which encode the relevant hierarchical data in a fairly efficient manner. Roughly, the result of this process is a collection of intervals
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
equipped with a family of relative projections satisfying certain HHS-like properties, where the index set
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(a,b)
is the set of
K
-relevant domains for
a,b
(Definition
3.5
). For any such collection of intervals, one can define a notion of
0
-consistency (an exact form of Definition
3.1
), which defines a special
0
-consistent subset
\mathcal{Q}
of the product
\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U}
. In Section
5
below we will explain why this set
\mathcal{Q}
is quasi-isometric to the hierarchical hull of
a,b
in
\mathcal{X}
. Finally in Section
6
, we will axiomatize the properties any such family of intervals satisfies, explain how it admits a natural wallspace structure, and show that the associated
0
-consistent set
\mathcal{Q}
is isometric to the cubical dual of this wallspace.

4.1.
Basic setup of the section

For the rest of this section, we fix a constant
K\geq K_{0}
, where
K_{0}=K_{0}(\mathfrak{S})>0
is the constant from the Distance Formula
3.8
. In this section, we will be able to allow
K
to be as large as necessary.

Fix also
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
. Let
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(a,b)=\{U\in\mathfrak{S}|d_{U}(a,b)>K\}
denote their
K
-relevant set. To each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
\phi_{U}:T_{U}\to\mathcal{C}(U)
denote a
(C,C)
-quasi-isometric embedding of an interval between
a_{U}
and
b_{U}
, so that
\phi_{U}(a_{U})=\pi_{U}(a)
and
\phi_{U}(b_{U})=\pi_{U}(b)
. Here we can assume that
C=C(\mathfrak{S})>0
depends only on the ambient HHS. Note that we are only taking a quasi-isometric embedding, instead of a geodesic, because that is the level of generality that we will require later.

We also set some terminology for
a_{U},b_{U}
, since they will play a distinguished role below.

Definition 4.1

(Marked points on intervals)
.

For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
the points
a_{U},b_{U}\in T_{U}
are called
marked points
.

We note that in the basic construction explained in this section, marked points will always been the endpoints of the intervals
T_{U}
. This will also be true of our collapsed stable trees defined in Subsection
9.2
, though not necessarily of the stable intervals themselves.

The discussion in this section closely follows
[
36
, Sections 6 and 7]
. Given the technical nature of this part, we will mostly state definitions and important properties, providing sketches where convenient but leaving the details for the reader to verify.

4.2.
Reduced interval systems

Our first goal is to push the hierarchical data associated to
a,b
onto the family of intervals
\{T_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
. This involves producing a notion of relative projection between the intervals and verifying some HHS-like properties for them.

These
interval projections
are defined simply by closest-point projecting the relative projections of
(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
to the images of
\phi_{U}(T_{U})\subset\mathcal{C}(U)
then pulling back to
T_{U}
via
\phi^{-1}_{U}
. We write
\delta^{V}_{U}
, rather than
\rho^{V}_{U}
, for the relative interval projections among the
T_{U}
. These interval projections are interval versions of the “tree projections” described in
[
36
, Section 6]
.

The following lemma, which summarizes the basic properties that we need, says that the
\delta^{V}_{U}
behave in essentially the same way as the
\rho^{V}_{U}
. Its proof is an exercise in the definitions and the basic properties of an HHS; see
[
36
, Lemma 6.13]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
C>0
there exists
R_{C}=R_{C}(C,\mathfrak{S})>0
so that the following holds:

(1)

If
U\pitchfork V\in\mathcal{U}
, then both
\delta^{V}_{U}\subset T_{U}
and
\delta^{U}_{V}\subset T_{V}
are sets of diameter at most
R_{C}
which are
R_{C}
-close to a marked point of
T_{U},T_{V}
, respectively. Moreover,
d_{U}(\phi_{U}(\delta^{V}_{U}),\rho^{V}_{U})<R_{C}
, and similarly for
\delta^{U}_{V}
.

(2)

If
U\sqsubset V\in\mathcal{U}
, then
\delta^{U}_{V}\subset T_{V}
has diameter bounded by
R_{C}
. Moreover,
d_{V}(\phi_{V}(\delta^{U}_{V}),\rho^{U}_{V})<R_{C}
.

(3)

If
U,V\sqsubset W\in\mathcal{U}
with
U,V
not transverse, then
\mathrm{diam}_{T_{W}}(\delta^{U}_{W}\cup\delta^{V}_{W})<R_{C}
.

(4)

If
U\pitchfork V
and
W\sqsubset V
and
W,U
not orthogonal, then
\mathrm{diam}_{T_{U}}(\delta^{V}_{U}\cup\delta^{W}_{U})<R_{C}
.

(5)

(Bounded geodesic image) If
U\sqsubset V\in\mathcal{U}
, then any component
C\subset T_{V}-\mathcal{N}_{R_{C}}(\delta^{U}_{V})
satisfies
\mathrm{diam}_{T_{U}}(\delta^{V}_{U}(C))<R_{C}
.

It will be useful for us to abstract the properties obtained for the intervals
T_{U}
in the last subsection. We are doing this so that we can later plug in the intervals produced by the Stable Interval Theorem
8.14
below directly into this setup.

Definition 4.3

(Interval system)
.

Given
R>0
, an
R
-
interval system
for
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(a,b)
is the following set of data:

(1)

For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, we have an interval
T_{U}
and a
(R,R)
-quasi-isometric embedding
\phi_{U}:T_{U}\to\mathcal{C}(U)
so that
d^{Haus}_{U}(\phi_{U}(T_{U}),\mathrm{hull}_{U}(a,b))<R
.

(2)

For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a marked point
a_{U}\in T_{U}
with
d_{\mathcal{C}(U)}(\phi_{U}(a_{U}),\pi_{U}(a))<R
, and similarly for
b
.

(3)

There are
\delta^{V}_{U}
sets which satisfy the conclusions of Lemma
4.2
.

Remark 4.4
.

When stabilizing the construction of the cubical models in Section
9
, we will want to use the stable intervals provided by Theorem
8.14
below. For these, we will induce an interval system structure by a slightly more careful construction than just working directly with Gromov trees, e.g. item (2) in Definition
4.3
is immediate in that setting. But the upshot is the same: the rest of the material in this section also holds for that setup.

4.3.
Interval consistency

The goal of this subsection is to define a map
\Psi:\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)\to\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}T_{U}
and show that its image satisfies the appropriate analogue of Definition
3.1
in this setting. Toward that end, for any
\alpha>0
, we set
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}\subset\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}T_{U}
to be the set of
\alpha
-consistent tuples.

We can now define our map
\Psi:H\to\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}T_{U}
for
H=\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
. Let
x\in H
and for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
x_{U}
denote a choice of a point in
\psi_{U}(x)=\phi_{U}^{-1}(p_{U}(\pi_{U}(x)))\subset T_{U}
, where
p_{U}:\mathcal{C}(U)\to\phi_{U}(T_{U})
is closest point projection. For each
x\in H
, this defines a tuple
(\psi_{U}(x))=(x_{U})\in\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}T_{U}
. That is, the coordinate-wise maps
\psi_{U}:H\to T_{U}
combine to a global map
\Psi:H\to\prod_{U}T_{U}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
R>0
, there exists
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{0}(\mathfrak{S},R)>0
so that if
\{T_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
is an
R
-interval system for
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
and
x\in\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
, then
\Psi(x)=(x_{U})
is
\alpha_{0}
-consistent.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
. The corresponding tuple
(\pi_{U}(x_{U}))
is
O(\theta,\mathfrak{S})
-consistent by Lemma
3.2
. Moreover, by definition of
H=\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
(Definition
3.12
), we have
d_{U}(\pi_{U}(x_{U}),H_{U})<\theta
, where
H_{U}=\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{C}(U)}(a,b)
and
\theta=\theta(\mathfrak{S})>0
. Hence
d_{U}(\pi_{U}(x_{U}),\phi_{U}(T_{U}))<\theta^{\prime}
for some
\theta^{\prime}=\theta^{\prime}(\mathfrak{S},\theta)>0
. At this point, a straight-forward argument using the properties in Lemma
4.2
gives the required consistency constant
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{0}(\mathfrak{S},R)
. We leave the details to the reader.
∎

4.4.
Interval thickenings

Our next goal is to define a way of collapsing the relative projection data associated to intervals
T_{U}
, i.e. the
\delta^{V}_{U}
, in an interval system to remove coarseness from the setup. The idea is that Definition
4.3
roughly encodes the projection data up to a controlled error. In slightly more detail, observe that each interval
T_{U}
contains the relative projections
\delta^{V}_{U}
for
V\sqsubset U
. We would like to collect close-by relative projections into single intervals called
cluster components
, so that we can later collapse them. One complication is that the
\delta^{V}_{U}
need not be subintervals of
T_{U}
, so we need a couple of constants—one to determine what it means for relative projections to be “close”, and the other to guarantee that cluster components which are not close satisfy a minimum distance bound.

We will use the following slightly more abstract setup, as it will be useful later during our stabilization process:

Definition 4.6

(Thickenings)
.

Let
T
be an interval with a decomposition
T=A\cup B
into collections of segments. Given
r_{1},r_{2}>0
, the
(r_{1},r_{2})
-
thickening
of
T

along
B
defines a decomposition
T=\mathbb{T}_{e}\cup\mathbb{T}_{c}
into
edge

\mathbb{T}_{e}
and
cluster

\mathbb{T}_{c}
components as follows:

(1)

First, take the
r_{1}
-neighborhoods in
T
of the components of
B
. Call the collection of these neighborhood
\mathcal{B}
, and their complement
T-\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}
.

(2)

Second, connect any two subintervals in
\mathcal{B}
which come within
r_{2}
of each other in
T
by the geodesic between them. Call the resulting collection of subintervals
\mathbb{T}_{c}
and set
\mathbb{T}_{e}=T-\mathbb{T}_{c}
.

In our current context, we can take
B
to be the collection of
\delta^{V}_{U}\subset T_{U}
segments for
V\sqsubset U
along with the marked points
a_{U},b_{U}
on
T_{U}
(Definition
4.1
). Thus a thickening of
T_{U}
along this collection
B
expands the
\delta^{V}_{U}
segments and
a_{U},b_{U}
and combines nearby expansions. For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, we denote the resulting decomposition of the thickened interval by
T_{U}=\mathbb{T}^{e}_{U}\cup\mathbb{T}^{c}_{U}
. When we perform an
(r_{1},r_{2})
-thickening to each interval in an interval system, we refer to the resulting collection of thickened intervals as a
(r_{1},r_{2})
-
thickening
of the interval system.

Remark 4.7
.

In our context, we will be able to control the constants
r_{1},r_{2}>0
in terms of the ambient HHS, and, importantly, we will be able to choose our largeness constant
K
to be independent of them.

4.5.
Collapsed intervals and
0
-consistency

Let
\{T_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
be an
R
-interval system for
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
. Fix
r_{i}=r_{i}(R,\mathfrak{S})>0
for
i=1,2
to be determined soon. For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
T_{U}=\mathbb{T}^{e}_{U}\cup\mathbb{T}^{c}_{U}
denote its
(r_{1},r_{2})
-thickening, as in Definition
4.6
.

Let
q_{U}:T_{U}\to\widehat{T}_{U}
denote the map which collapses each component of
\mathbb{T}^{c}_{U}
to a point, each of which we call a
collapsed point
. The resulting object
\widehat{T}_{U}
is an interval. Note that by construction
a_{U},b_{U}\in\mathbb{T}^{c}_{U}
for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
. Hence each of
a_{U},b_{U}
determines a
marked point

\hat{a}_{U},\hat{b}_{U}\in\widehat{T}_{U}
, and these marked points
\hat{a}_{U},\hat{b}_{U}
will be the endpoints of the (collapsed) interval
\widehat{T}_{U}
. Moreover, the decomposition
T_{U}=\mathbb{T}^{e}_{U}\cup\mathbb{T}^{c}_{U}
descends to a decomposition
\widehat{T}_{U}=\widehat{T}^{e}_{U}\cup\widehat{T}^{c}_{U}
, where the components of
\widehat{T}^{c}_{U}
are the collapsed points corresponding to the components of
\mathbb{T}^{c}_{U}
, and the components of
\widehat{T}^{e}_{U}
are isometric images of the components of
\mathbb{T}^{e}_{U}
.

Combining these
q_{U}
maps with our coordinate-wise projections
\psi_{U}:H\to T_{U}
, we get coordinate-wise maps
\hat{\psi}_{U}:H\to\widehat{T}_{U}
to the collapsed intervals, and hence combining these gives a global map
\widehat{\Psi}:H\to\prod_{U}\widehat{T}_{U}
.

We can define
collapsed relative projections

\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}
in the obvious way, by composing with the quotient maps
q_{U}
. Similarly, consistency in this setting is the appropriate analogue of Definition
3.1
, except now all coarseness has been removed:

Definition 4.8
.

A tuple
(\hat{x}_{U})\in\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U}
is
0
-
consistent
if the following hold:

(1)

For any
U\pitchfork V\in\mathcal{U}
, either
\hat{x}_{U}=\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}
or
\hat{x}_{V}=\hat{\delta}^{U}_{V}
.

(2)

For any
U\sqsubset V\in\mathcal{U}
, either
\hat{x}_{V}=\hat{\delta}^{U}_{V}
or
\hat{x}_{U}\in\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}(\hat{x}_{V})\subset\widehat{T}_{U}
.

•

We let

For any

We need to prove
0

\widehat{\Psi}(x)
as in Definition
4.8

First suppose that

5.
\widehat{\Psi}:H\to\mathcal{Q}
is a quasi-isometry

In this section, we explain why the map
\widehat{\Psi}:H\to\mathcal{Q}
(Proposition
4.9
) between the hierarchical hull
H=\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
of a pair of points
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
and the
0
-consistent set associated to any sufficiently wide
(r_{1},r_{2})
-thickening of an
R
-interval system for
a,b
is a quasi-isometry. There are two main parts to this:

(1)

Proving that
\widehat{\Psi}:H\to\mathcal{Q}
is a quasi-isometric embedding, and

(2)

Proving that
\widehat{\Psi}
is coarsely surjective. This is accomplished by defining a coarse inverse
\widehat{\Omega}:\mathcal{Q}\to H
, so that
\widehat{\Psi}\circ\widehat{\Omega}
is coarsely the identity on
\mathcal{Q}
.

As we shall see, these two results have fairly different flavors—(1) is essentially purely hierarchical, while (2) has distinctly cubical elements. Item (1) essentially says that one can encode the distance formula in
\mathcal{X}
as the
\ell^{1}
-distance in a product of intervals. Its proof involves mostly distance formula-type arguments and we present it in full. On the other hand, the proof of item (2), which is contained in
[
36
, Sections 9–11]
, is rather involved and many of the details of the argument are not crucial to our expository purposes in this article. Thus, in this section, we will mostly explain the basics of the construction, including the points where our simpler setting simplifies things considerably.

The one exception here is the Tree Trimming Theorem
5.14
, which is a distance-formula like tool that we need both here and for our stability results in Section
9
. We will discuss it in detail and present its proof in Appendix
A
.

For the rest of this section, fix
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
and let
H=\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
to be their hierarchical hull. Fix a largeness constant
K
, which we will be able to control relative to all other involved constants, and set
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(a,b)
. Finally, fix
R>0
and an
R
-interval system
\{T_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
for
a,b
. Let
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
denote the family of collapsed intervals corresponding to an
(r_{1},r_{2})
-thickening, where
r_{i}=r_{i}(R,\theta,\mathfrak{S})>0
are provided by Proposition
4.9
and Lemma
4.10
.

5.1.
\widehat{\Psi}:H\to\mathcal{Q}
is a quasi-isometric embedding

The content of this subsection is roughly contained in
[
36
, Section 8]
.

Before we state the result, we set some additional notation: For numbers
A,B
, the notation
[A]_{B}
means
0
when
A<B
and
A
when
A\geq B
. For
x\in H
and
U\in\mathcal{U}
, we will write

•

x=\pi_{U}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(U)
;

•

x_{U}=\psi_{U}(x)\in T_{U}
;

•

\hat{x}_{U}=\hat{\psi}_{U}(x)\in\widehat{T}_{U}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There exists
K_{1}=K_{1}(\mathfrak{S})>0
so that for any
K_{2}\geq K_{1}
and any
x,y\in H
, we have that

d_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)\asymp\sum_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K_{2}}\asymp\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}d_{\widehat{T}_{U}}(\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U})

where the constants in
\asymp
only depend on
\mathfrak{S}
and the choice of
K_{2}
.

We note that the left coarse equality of the above statement is simply the Distance Formula
3.8
, hence the content is the right coarse equality. In fact, one can obtain the (more difficult) lower-bound in the distance formula by combining the above theorem with the construction of hierarchy paths via cubical models, as we do below in Corollary
6.11
.

Before beginning the discussion of the proof properly, we make a couple of simplifying observations, which are all basically distance formula-type manipulations.

First, note that if
x,y\in H
and
V\in\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(x,y)
but
V\notin\mathcal{U}
, then
d_{V}(x,y)<K+\epsilon
for some

. This is because
\pi_{V}(x),\pi_{V}(y)\in\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\mathrm{hull}_{V}(a,b))
. Hence by taking
K_{2}\geq K_{1}>K+\epsilon
, we may increase the threshold to remove any extraneous domains. That is:

\sum_{U\in\mathfrak{S}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K_{2}}=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K_{2}}.

We next observe that for any
x,y\in H
, item (4) of Lemma
4.10
implies that
d_{U}(x,y)\asymp d_{T_{U}}(x_{U},y_{U})
, with constants depending only on
\mathfrak{S}
. Thus we can use Corollary
3.16
to replace the terms
d_{U}(x,y)
with
d_{T_{U}}(x_{U},y_{U})
in the sum, namely:

\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K_{2}}\asymp\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}[d_{T_{U}}(x_{U},y_{U})]_{K_{2}}.

The two bounds in Theorem
5.1
are proven separately. The upper bound only requires the basic details from the construction of the
\widehat{T}_{U}
and some distance formula tools. We have included the full proof of the proposition for clarity but also because it is used in proving the existence of hierarchy paths and the upper bound of the distance formula; moreover, the argument is similar to the proof of Corollary
3.16
, which actually implies the proposition.

Proposition 5.2

(Upper bound)
.

There exists
K_{1}=K_{1}(\mathfrak{S})>0
so that for any
K_{2}>K_{1}
and any
x,y\in H
we have

(8)

\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}[d_{U}(x,y)]_{K_{2}}\succ\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}d_{\widehat{T}_{U}}(\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U})

with the constants in
\succ
depending only on
\mathfrak{S}
and
K_{2}
.

Proof.

As above, let
K_{1}\geq K+\epsilon
and set
K_{2}\geq K_{1}+100E_{\mathfrak{S}}
. Let
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K_{i}}(x,y)
, so that
\mathcal{U}_{2}\subset\mathcal{U}_{1}\subset\mathcal{U}

For this, item (9) of Lemma

Combining these observations leads to the computation

\displaystyle\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}d_{U}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}((1+A)\cdot d_{U}(x,y)+A)+K_{2}\cdot P(K_{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+2A)\cdot\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{2}}d_{U}(x,y)+K_{2}\cdot P(K_{2})

for each

V\in\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}

d_{T_{U}}(\delta^{V}_{U},x_{U})>\alpha+R

.

Lemma
4.5
says that the tuples

are all
\alpha_{0}
-consistent. Thus since

and similarly for

,
\alpha_{0}

, and similarly for

. As such, we get that
d_{T_{V}}(x_{V},a_{V})<\alpha_{0}+R
and
d_{T_{V}}(y_{V},b_{V})<\alpha_{0}+R
, and so that
d_{T_{V}}(x_{V},y_{V})\geq d_{T_{V}}(a_{V},b_{V})-2\alpha_{0}-2R

\hat{a}_{V}=\hat{x}_{V}
and
\hat{b}_{V}=\hat{y}_{V}
by requiring

On the other hand, since each

is
\sqsubset_{\mathcal{U}}
-minimal and

, item (4) of Lemma

says that
d_{\widehat{T}_{V}}(\hat{a}_{V},\hat{b}_{V})\succ K
. Hence we obtain that
d_{\widehat{T}_{V}}(\hat{x}_{V},\hat{y}_{V})=d_{\widehat{T}_{V}}(\hat{a}_{V},\hat{b}_{V})\succ K
.

Thus for each

, we have

\displaystyle d_{T_{U}}(x_{U},y_{U})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{\widehat{T}_{U}}(\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{diam}_{T_{U}}(C_{i}\cap[x_{U},y_{U}]_{T_{U}})

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle d_{\widehat{T}_{U}}(\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\#\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\prec

\displaystyle d_{\widehat{T}_{U}}(\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}}d_{\widehat{T}_{V}}(\hat{x}_{V},\hat{y}_{V})\right).

Finally, the Covering Lemma

says that any domain

can nest into only boundedly-many domains in
\mathcal{U}
, with bounds determined by
\mathfrak{S}
. Hence, each
\sqsubset_{\mathcal{U}}
-minimal domain

must account for at most boundedly-many

in the above way. Thus, using an argument similar to the end of the proof of Proposition

, we can account for the distance lost by collapsing clusters in each domain in

with these large
\sqsubset_{\mathcal{U}}
-minimal domains by increasing the multiplicative constant for the estimate in equation (

) by a bounded amount. This completes the proof.
∎

5.2.
The map
\widehat{\Omega}:\mathcal{Q}\to H

With Theorem

is coarsely surjective. The proof of this fact is quite involved and beyond the scope of an expository paper. As such, we will mostly present key ideas and not a full detailed proof because the proof is not greatly simplified versus the most general version, see

In this subsection, we will construct a map

Remark 5.7

5.4.
Tree trimming

In this subsection, we introduce a useful tool for modifying collapsed interval systems and their associated
0
-consistent sets. These “tree-trimming” techniques will allow us to perform controlled collapses of subintervals across the various collapsed intervals
\widehat{T}_{U}
while coarsely preserving the associated
\mathcal{Q}
-distance. We think of them as a more flexible version of the distance formula, as they allow us to modify distance terms instead of just throwing them out by increasing a threshold. For our purposes, they will be used in two different ways: We will need them in the proof of coarse surjectivity of
\widehat{\Psi}:H\to\mathcal{Q}
, and they will also be useful later in Subsection
6.3
to modify the cubical structure on
\mathcal{Q}
.

The main idea is simple: Given a family of subintervals
\mathcal{B}
across of the family of intervals
\widehat{T}_{U}
, we want to collapse these subintervals
\Delta_{U}:\widehat{T}_{U}\to\widehat{T}_{U,0}
coordinate-wise and then combine them into a global collapsing map
\Delta:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is the
0
-consistent set of
\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U,0}
, defined the same way as in Definition
4.8
. Formally, we use the following definition:

Definition 5.13

(Admissible trimming setup)
.

Given
B>0
, a
B
-
admissible trimming setup
is a collection of
trimming intervals

\mathcal{B}
in the various
\widehat{T}_{U,e}
satisfying the following:

•

For each component
E\in\pi_{0}(\widehat{T}_{U,e})
, there are at most
B
-many components
D\in\mathcal{B}
with
D\subset E
.

•

For each component
D\in\mathcal{B}
satisfies
|D|<B
.

Given a
B
-admissible trimming setup
\mathcal{B}
on
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
, for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
let
\Delta_{U}:\widehat{T}_{U}\to\widehat{T}_{U,0}
denote the map which collapses each trimming interval in
\widehat{T}_{U}
to a point. These allow us to induce projection data on the
\widehat{T}_{U,0}
as follows: For
V\sqsubset U
and
V\pitchfork U
, we set
(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U})_{0}=\Delta_{U}(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U})
, and if
U\sqsubset V
, we set
(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U})_{0}=\Delta_{U}\circ\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}\circ\Delta^{-1}_{V}
. We let
\Delta:\prod_{\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U}\to\prod_{\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U,0}
denote the product map.

Theorem 5.14

(Tree Trimming)
.

There exists
B_{tt}=B_{tt}(\mathfrak{S},K)>0
so that for any
B<B_{tt}
, there exists
L=L(\mathfrak{S},B)>0
so that the following hold:

(1)

We have
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})\subset\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.

(2)

There is a map
\Xi:\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{Q}
so that
\Delta\circ\Xi=id_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
. In particular,
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.

(3)

The map
\Delta:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is an
(L,L)
-quasi-isometry.

While the statement is quite believable in light of the Distance Formula
3.8
, the proof of this theorem is quite involved. While these ideas were first developed in greater generality in
[
36
, Section 10]
—where it also works when the underlying data involves more than two points and also hierarchy rays—the proof these is only slightly more involved than that required in our setting. Moreover, many of the technicalities from dealing with rays are dealt with in separate arguments, so the interested reader is encouraged to see a full proof there.

Instead, we will present a short sketch below, which will hopefully provide enough intuition to make the statement believable. We have included a proof of the stated version of Theorem
5.14
in Appendix
A
.

Sketch of proof.

The fact that
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})\subset\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, namely that
\Delta
preserves
0
-consistency, is immediate from the fact that the
\Delta_{U}
just collapse subintervals, so coordinates which were collapsed projections remain collapsed projections, showing item (1).

Item (2) is the most involved part. For each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, let
C_{U}=\Delta^{-1}_{U}(\hat{y}_{U})
. The goal is to pick, for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, a point
\hat{x}_{U}\in C_{U}
and then prove
0
-consistency of the tuple
\hat{x}=(\hat{x}_{U})
. Note that
\Delta(\hat{x})=\hat{y}
for such a tuple, by construction.

The proof is similar in spirit to the cluster-honing procedure from Subsection
5.2
. The main difference is that the collapsing subintervals are not automatically associated to a collection of domains, and this is where the work lies. The argument is inductive on level in the
\sqsubset
-lattice and requires a careful analysis of the construction and number of hierarchal techniques.

Finally, for item (3), since
\Delta|_{\mathcal{Q}}
is surjective and distance non-increasing, it suffices to prove that
d_{\mathcal{Q}}\prec d_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
, and for this it suffices to bound the distances in each
U\in\mathcal{U}
.

Given
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Q}
with
\hat{x}^{\prime}=\Delta(\hat{x})
and
\hat{y}^{\prime}=\Delta(\hat{y})
, the concern is that somehow one will have arbitrarily shrunk distances during the trimming process. However, each edge component of
\widehat{T}_{U}
through which the geodesic
[\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U}]
passes can only contain boundedly-many subintervals collapsed. However, all but at most two of such edge components have endpoints which are cluster points labeled by
\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}
for
V

\sqsubset
-minimal. Since for such
V
, the map
\Delta_{V}:\widehat{T}_{V}\to\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{V}
collapses subintervals of total diameter at most
B^{2}
, we can use these
\sqsubset
-minimal domains to account for all information lost up the hierarchy.

Making this precise takes care and utilizes Proposition
3.15
. The interested reader can see
[
36
, Section 10]
.
∎

5.5.
Converting to simplicial intervals

One application of the Tree Trimming Theorem

is that we can replace the family of collapsed intervals

with a family of simplicial intervals, where the cluster and marked points lie at vertices. The idea here is that one can trim each edge component

combines these to a map between their

Suppose that

are the collapsed intervals associated to an

-thickening of an

•

In particular, up to a uniform quasi-isometry of

See

From

There exists

In particular, by choosing

Sketch.

This proposition is a special case of

It is basically saying that consistency in the interval setting is roughly consistency in the hierarchical setting, and that flowing from the former to the latter and back only moves coordinates a bounded amount.

On the other hand, the proof of (2)—which says that only boundedly-many coordinates move more than a bounded amount—is more involved, but also fairly standard. It uses a variation on the Passing-Up property from Lemma

5.7.
The map

Fix

Let

and

Let

denote the
\sqsubset_{\mathcal{U}}
-minimal domains in

By item (8) of Lemma

6.
Building a cube complex from a hierarchical family of intervals

The goal of this section is to explain how to take a special collection of intervals satisfying certain HHS-like properties, and to produce from them a CAT(0) cube complex. The details of this construction motivate the stabilization techniques we develop later in this article.

6.1.
Hierarchical families of intervals

In this subsection, we axiomatize the properties we need from a family of intervals in order to build a cube complex. The purpose of this axiomatization is to allow us to modify our construction in various ways while preserving the axioms, as any setup which satisfies the axioms produces a cube complex. The properties themselves are motivated by the HHS axioms and the properties in Lemma
4.10
.

Definition 6.1

(Hierarchical family of intervals)
.

A
hierarchical family of intervals (HFI)
is the following collection of objects and properties:

(1)

A finite index set
\mathcal{U}
of
domains
with
relations

\sqsubset,\pitchfork,\perp
so that

•

\sqsubset
is anti-reflexive and anti-symmetric, and gives a partial order with a bound on the length of chains;

•

\pitchfork
is symmetric and anti-reflexive;

•

There is a unique
\sqsubset
-maximal element
S
;

•

Any pair of domains
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
satisfies exactly one of the relations.

(2)

There is a bound on the size of any pairwise
\perp
-comparable subset of
\mathcal{U}
.

(3)

To each
U\in\mathcal{U}
there is an associated finite simplicial interval
\widehat{T}_{U}
.

(4)

A pair of labels
F=\{x,y\}
which determined the endpoints, called
marked points

\hat{x}_{U},\hat{y}_{U}
, of the interval
\widehat{T}_{U}
for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
.

(5)

A family of
relative projections
determining:

•

For each
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
with
V\sqsubset U
or
V\pitchfork U
, there is a vertex
\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}\in\widehat{T}^{(0)}_{U}
called a
cluster point
.

(6)

There exist projection maps
\hat{\delta}^{U}_{V}:\widehat{T}_{U}\to\widehat{T}_{V}
when
V\sqsubset U
, satisfying the following properties:

(a)

(Consistency) For each
f\in F
, we have
\hat{\delta}^{U}_{V}(\hat{f}_{U})=\hat{f}_{V}
.

(b)

(BGI) If
C\subset\widehat{T}_{U}-\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}
is a component, then
\hat{\delta}^{U}_{V}(C)
coincides with
\hat{f}_{V}
for
f\in F
with
\hat{f}_{U}\in C
.

(7)

If
U,V,W\in\mathcal{U}
with
V,W\sqsubset U
,
V\pitchfork W
, and
\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}\neq\hat{\delta}^{W}_{U}
, then
\hat{\delta}^{U}_{V}(\hat{\delta}^{W}_{U})=\hat{\delta}^{W}_{V}\in\widehat{T}_{V}
.

Remark 6.2
.

In
[
36
, Definition 12.1]
, the definition of a

allows for the trees to have more than
2

3
or more points.

The following is an immediate consequence of our work in Section
4
, especially Lemma

:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

,
r_{1}>R_{1}=R_{1}(R,\mathfrak{S})>0
as in Proposition
4.9
and

as in Lemma

-thickening of an

, the associated family of collapsed intervals and their collapsed projections form a hierarchical family of intervals.

As above in Definition
4.8
, we can define a

-consistent subset of the product

. Our next goal is the following theorem:

Theorem 6.4
.

The
0
-consistent subset of an HFI is a CAT(0) cube complex. Moreover, there is a bijective correspondence between its hyperplanes and the edges of the factors.

6.2.
Median graphs and dual cube complexes

The proof of Theorem
6.4
that we give here will use the notion of median spaces. The connection between median graphs and CAT(0) cube complexes goes back to Roller’s
[
62
]
reframing of Sageev’s construction
[
65
]
, see also
[
25
,
57
]
. Notably, Chepoi
[
26
, Theorem 6.1]
showed that every median graph is the
1
-skeleton of a unique CAT(0) cube complex. Bowditch explains these connections in detail in
[
20
, Section 11]
.

Let
X
be a space and
{\sf m}:X^{3}\to X
a ternary operator. We call
(X,{\sf m})
a
median algebra
if for all
a,b,c,d,e\in X
we have

{\sf m}(a,b,c)={\sf m}(b,a,c)={\sf m}(c,a,b),\hskip 7.22743pt{\sf m}(a,a,b)=a,\hskip 7.22743pt{\sf m}(a,b,{\sf m}(c,d,e))={\sf m}({\sf m}(a,b,c),{\sf m}(a,b,d),e).

A graph
\Gamma
is
median
if all choices of
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in\Gamma^{(0)}
, there exists a unique point lying on some geodesic from
v_{i},v_{j}
for all
i,j
; then
{\sf m}(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
is that unique point.

Given
a,b\in X
, the (median)
interval
between
a,b
is
I(a,b)=\{x\in X|\hskip 0.72229pt{\sf m}(a,x,b)=x\}
. We say a median algebra
(X,{\sf m})
is
discrete
when
I(a,b)
is finite for every
a,b\in X
. A
subalgebra
is a subset
S\subset X
which is closed under
{\sf m}
. In particular, locally finite median graphs are discrete median algebras.

Every median algebra
(X,{\sf m})
determines a graph called its
adjacency graph

\Gamma(X)
as follows. We say that
a,b\in X
are
adjacent
when
I(a,b)=\emptyset
. The vertices of
\Gamma(X)
are the points of
X
, with
a,b\in X
connected by an edge in
\Gamma(X)
exactly when
a,b
are adjacent. This graph is median exactly when
(X,{\sf m})
is a discrete median algebra
[
20
, Lemma 16.1.2]
.

Every CAT(0) cube complex
X
admits a canonical median structure as follows. For any
a,b\in X^{(0)}
, let
J(a,b)
denote the set of
\ell^{1}
-geodesics between
a,b
. Then given
a,b,c\in X^{(0)}
, we have
{\sf m}(a,b,c)=J(a,b)\cap J(a,c)\cap J(b,c)
is a single point, and
(X,{\sf m})
is a median structure with
X^{(1)}
a median graph, with notably
J(a,b)=I(a,b)
for all
a,b\in X^{(0)}
. Moreover, it is immediate that
X^{(1)}=\Gamma(X^{(0)})
, since median adjacent vertices in
X^{(0)}
are exactly those spanning an edge in
X^{(1)}
.

Proof of Theorem
6.4
.

First, observe that
\mathcal{Y}=\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U}
is a product of simplicial trees and hence a CAT(0) cube complex.

Next, observe that
\mathcal{Q}\subset\mathcal{Y}
is a cubical subcomplex as follows. For any
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{Q}
, let
C\subset\mathcal{Y}
be the minimal cube containing
\hat{x}
in its interior. Presuming
C
is not a vertex, minimality says that
\hat{x}_{U}
is in the interior of an edge in each of the defining coordinate intervals of
C
, whose domain labels are pairwise orthogonal by item (9) of Lemma
4.10
. This means that we can freely alter those coordinates of
\hat{x}
to be any choice of points in those edges, meaning
C\subset\mathcal{Q}
. Hence
\mathcal{Q}
is a cubical subcomplex of
\mathcal{Y}
.

It is enough then to show that
\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}\subset\mathcal{Y}
is a discrete median subalgebra. For once that is done, it will follow that
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q})
is a median graph, but evidently
\Gamma(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
, as
\mathcal{Q}
is a cubical subcomplex of
\mathcal{Y}
. Since every median graph is the
1
-skeleton of a unique CAT(0) cube complex
[
26
, Theorem 6.1]
, it follows that
\mathcal{Q}
must be that cube complex, completing the proof.

The claim boils down to three exercises that we leave for the reader:

•

Any (finite) product of median spaces admits the structure of a median space, where the median on the product is the tuple of medians in the coordinates.

•

For any
\hat{a},\hat{b},\hat{c}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
, we have
\left({\sf m}_{U}(\hat{a}_{U},\hat{b}_{U},\hat{c}_{U})\right)_{U}\in\mathcal{Q}
. This can be proven using a basic consistency argument (see
[
36
, Lemma 15.12]
).

•

With this median structure,
a,b\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
are median adjacent exactly when they span an edge in
\mathcal{Q}^{(1)}
.

Finally, observe that the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{Y}
are of the form
\mathbf{h}\times\prod_{V\neq U}\widehat{T}_{V}
for
\mathbf{h}
some hyperplane in
\widehat{T}_{U}
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is a median subalgebra of
\mathcal{Y}
, the hyperplanes of
\mathcal{Q}
are just hyperplanes of
\mathcal{Y}
intersected with
\mathcal{Q}
, so the moreover statement follows. This completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
More tree trimming and hyperplane deletions

In Subsection
5.4
, we introduced our tree trimming techniques, which allowed us to take a family of collapsed intervals and collapse a further family of subintervals while maintaining control over their associated
0
-consistent subsets. In this subsection, we need a further refinement applied to HFIs and their associated cube complexes.

To set up the statement, let
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
be a hierarchical family of intervals, as in Definition
6.1
. Fix a constant
B>0
, which will control the size and number of subintervals we will collapse across all of the intervals. In our applications, we will be able to control the size of

, but notably not with respect to the largeness constant

, unlike in the statement of Theorem
5.14
.

Definition 6.5

(Admissible simplicial trimming setup)
.

Given
B>0
, a
B
-
admissible simplicial trimming setup
is a collection of
trimming intervals

\mathcal{B}
in the various
\widehat{T}_{U,e}
satisfying the following:

(1)

For each
D\in\mathcal{B}
with
D\subset E\in\pi_{0}(\widehat{T}_{U,e})
, we have

(a)

D
is a subinterval of
E
with endpoints at vertices, and

(b)

the length
l(D)
of
D
is a positive integer whose length is bounded above by
B
.

(2)

The number of subintervals in
\mathcal{B}
is bounded by
B
, that is
\#\mathcal{B}<B
.

Remark 6.6
.

We note that the conditions in Definition
6.5
guarantee that each quotient interval
\widehat{T}_{U,0}
is a simplicial interval of the usual type we consider. The requirement that each segment
D\in\mathcal{B}
has endpoints at vertices guarantees that when we collapse such a segment, then it only affects the interval-hyperplanes it contains, preserving those in its complement.

We use the same associated notation as in Subsection
5.4
. Furthermore, we note that if
\widehat{T}^{e}_{U}\subset\mathcal{B}
, then
\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{T}_{U})<B^{2}
and
\widehat{T}_{U,0}
is a point. Let
\mathcal{U}_{0}\subset\mathcal{U}
denote the subset of domains for which
\widehat{T}_{U,0}
is not a point. By ignoring the domains in
\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}_{0}
, we can consider the set of collapsed intervals
\{\widehat{T}_{U,0}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}
, where we remove any labels
\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}
from
\widehat{T}_{U}
from any domain
V\sqsubset U
with
V\in\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}_{0}
.

The following theorem expands on Theorem
5.14
in the HFI setting:

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
B>0
and
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
be an HFI. For any
B
-admissible simplicial trimming setup
\mathcal{B}
, the following hold:

(1)

The collections
\{\widehat{T}_{U,0}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
and
\{\widehat{T}_{U,0}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{0}}
are HFIs. We let
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}_{0}
denote their corresponding
0
-consistent subsets.

(2)

We have
\Delta(\mathcal{Q})=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, where
\Delta:\prod_{U}\widehat{T}_{U}\to\prod_{U}\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}
is the global collapsing map.

(3)

There exists a map
\Xi:\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{Q}
with
\Delta\circ\Xi=id_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
.

(4)

The map
\Delta|_{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is a
(1,B^{2})
-quasi-isometry.

(5)

At the level of cube complexes, the map
\Delta:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is a hyperplane deletion map deleting at most
B^{2}
-many hyperplanes.

Proof.

Item (1) is straightforward, since we are just collapsing simplicial subintervals. Items (2)–(3) follow from Theorem
5.14
, where in the argument we just replace the HFI axioms (Definition
6.1
) with the appropriate properties from Lemma
4.10
. The fact that
\Delta
deletes at most
B^{2}
-many hyperplanes from
\prod_{U}\widehat{T}_{U}
, so item (5) follows from the “moreover” part of Theorem
6.4
. Finally, item (4) follows immediately from item (5). This completes the proof.

∎

6.4.
Hierarchy paths and the distance formula

With Theorem
6.4
in hand, we can make some observations about the cubical structure of
\mathcal{Q}
and how it relates to its hierarchical nature. In particular, we get a proof of the (more difficult) lower bound of the Distance Formula
3.8
and the existence of hierarchy paths.

The following lemma is
[
36
, Lemma 14.7]
, and we have included its proof here for completeness.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
\hat{x},\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Q}
. Let
\gamma
be any combinatorial geodesic in
\mathcal{Q}
from
\hat{x}
to
\hat{y}
. Then for all
U\in\mathcal{U}
, the projection
\widehat{\pi}_{U}(\gamma)\subset\widehat{T}_{U}
is an unparameterized geodesic between
\hat{x}_{U}
and
\hat{y}_{U}
.

Proof.

Since combinatorial geodesics do not cross a hyperplane twice and the restriction quotient
\widehat{\pi}_{U}:\mathcal{Q}\to\widehat{T}_{U}
is
1
-Lipschitz, it follows that
\widehat{\pi}_{U}(\gamma)
does not backtrack either. This completes the proof.

∎

This lemma plays two roles: it’s useful for proving that an isomorphism of HFIs induces an isomorphism of their cubical duals (Proposition
6.12
), and, in the next proposition, we use it directly to show that combinatorial geodesics in a cubical model get sent to hierarchy paths (Definition
3.9
) in the ambient HHS. That is the content of the next proposition, which is simplified version of
[
36
, Proposition 14.8]
:

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
a,b\in\mathcal{X}
,
K\geq K_{0}
where
K_{0}=K_{0}(\mathfrak{S})>0
is from Theorem
3.8
,
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(a,b)
, and let
H=\mathrm{hull}_{\mathcal{X}}(a,b)
. Let
\{T_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
be any
R
-interval system for
a,b
and
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
the HFI associated to any
(r_{1},r_{2})
-thickening for

with

a positive integer. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the corresponding
0

•

If
\hat{a},\hat{b}
are the points

corresponding to

Proof.

The following is a fairly complete sketch of the proof, which is simpler than the more general version from
[

, Proposition 14.8]
.

Since the map
\widehat{\Omega}:\mathcal{Q}\to H
is effectively defined coordinate-wise, its enough to consider the coordinate-wise maps
B_{U}:\widehat{T}_{U}\to T_{U}
as described in Subsection
5.2
, as the map

is an
(R,R)
-quasi-isometric embedding by assumption. For this, we can let
\gamma_{U}
denote the coordinate-wise projection of
\gamma
to each
\widehat{T}_{U}

By construction,

-level in
\mathcal{U}

is only possible if

\sqsubset_{\mathcal{U}}

with

is on the side of

(50r_{2}+50r_{1})

implies that

∎

6.5.
Isomorphisms of HFIs and their associated cube complexes

In this final subsection, we will discuss when two HFIs to determine isomorphic cube complexes. This statement, while unsurprising, motivates what happens in the subsequent sections, hence its placement here.

Let
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
and
\{\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
be two HFIs over a common index set
\mathcal{U}
and common relations, i.e.
U,V\in\mathcal{U}
have the same relation in both HFIs. Any family of maps
i_{U}:\widehat{T}_{U}\to\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}
induces a global map
I:\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}_{U}\to\prod_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}
, where
I((\hat{x}_{U}))=(i_{U}(\hat{x}_{U}))
.

The following proposition, which is an interval version of
[
36
, Proposition 14.12]
, gives sufficient conditions for the induced map to be an isometry
I:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
which induces a cubical isomorphism between their dual cube complexes.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
\{\widehat{T}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
and
\{\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}}
be two HFIs over a common index set
\mathcal{U}
with marked points
F=\{a,b\}
, and let
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
be their corresponding
0
-consistent sets. Suppose that for each
U\in\mathcal{U}
, there is a simplicial isometry
i_{U}:\widehat{T}_{U}\to\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}
which also satisfies:

(1)

We have
i_{U}(\hat{a}_{U})=\hat{a}^{\prime}_{U}\in\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}
and
i_{U}(\hat{b}_{U})=\hat{b}^{\prime}_{U}\in\widehat{T}^{\prime}_{U}
.

(2)

For all
V\in\mathcal{U}
with
V\sqsubset U
or
V\pitchfork U
, we have
i_{U}(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U})=\left(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}\right)^{\prime}
.

(3)

For all
V\in\mathcal{U}
with
U\sqsubset V
and
\hat{x}_{V}\in\widehat{T}_{V}
, we have
\left(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}\right)^{\prime}(i_{V}(\hat{x}_{V}))=i_{U}(\hat{\delta}^{V}_{U}(\hat{x}_{V}))
.

Then the corresponding map induces a cubical isomorphism
I:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
.

Sketch of proof.

The proof of this version below is the same as the original, which is, in turn, an exercise in the construction from this section plus Lemma
6.8
. The main idea is that items in the statement guarantee that the global map
I
induced from the coordinate-wise isometries preserves
0

I:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is an isometric embedding, with surjectivity following from a consistency argument. Since the interval-wise maps respect the simplicial structure, we have that

is also a cubical isomorphism via Lemma
2.5
. We leave the details to the reader.

∎

7.
Controlling domains

In this section, we turn toward the proof of our main technical result, namely the Stable Cubical Interval Theorem
9.9
, which is a stabilized version of the cubical model construction for a pair of points
a,b
in any HHS
\mathcal{X}
. The goal is to show that if we perturb
a,b
a small amount to a pair of points
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
with
d_{\mathcal{X}}(a,a^{\prime})\leq 1
and
d_{\mathcal{X}}(b,b^{\prime})\leq 1
, then we can produce cubical models
\mathcal{Q}
for
a,b
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
for
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
which are isomorphic up to deleting boundedly-many hyperplanes. Doing so requires carefully controlling the main inputs into the cubulation machine—namely the families of intervals we construct from the projections of the points.

The first step in the process involves explaining how to gain control over the set of relevant domains (Definition
3.5
) for a pair of points
F=\{a,b\}\subset\mathcal{X}
and its perturbation
F^{\prime}=\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\}
as above. In the next sections, we explain how to take this projection data to build a family of stable intervals that we can plug into the cubulation machine.

The main bit of work for this first part has already been done for us by Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara
[
15
]
and Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara–Sisto
[
14
]
. The work we did with Minsky and Sisto in
[
32
]
mostly adapts their setup to the HHS setting, and makes some straight-forward but important observations.

In
[
32
, Subsections 2.3 and 2.4]
, we were dealing with the situation where
F,F^{\prime}
were finite sets of points with cardinality possibly larger than
2
. Nonetheless, all of the arguments there reduce to considering pairs of points, and so the proofs for our current setting are essentially the same. As such, we will mainly explain the setup and key statements, only giving minimal sketches of proofs.

7.1.
Colorability

The hierarchical setting in which
[
14
]
works is for the following class, which was first defined in
[
32
, Definition 2.8]
and later used in
[
41
,
59
]
:

Definition 7.1

(Colorable HHS)
.

We say that an HHS

(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
is
colorable
if there exists a decomposition of

\mathfrak{S}
into finitely many families
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
, so that each
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
is
pairwise-
\pitchfork
.

•

We refer to the
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
as
BBF families
.

Remark 7.2
.

The notion of colorability was inspired by work of Bestvina-Bromberg-Fujiwara
[
15
]
, who proved that the curve graph is finitely-colorable. As a consequence of this, the set of subsurfaces of a given finite-type surface
S
can be decomposed into finitely-many families of pairwise-interlocking subsurfaces, which the above definition directly generalizes. See
[
32
, Subsection 2.3]
for a detailed discussion of this connection.

7.2.
Stable projections

Our next goal is to state a theorem which equips any colorable HHS with a set of projections which satisfy certain key stability properties. We need some definitions first.

Given a pair of points
F=\{x,y\}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
K>0
, we let
\mathcal{U}(F)=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(F)
. If
V\in\mathcal{U}(F)
, then we let
\mathcal{U}^{V}(F)=\{W\in\mathcal{U}(F)|W\sqsubsetneq V\}
.

The following definition
[
32
, Definition 2.13]
describes the domains we want to control when perturbing
F
:

Definition 7.3

(Involved domains)
.

Let
F=\{x,y\},F^{\prime}=\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
be so that
d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})\leq 1
and
d_{\mathcal{X}}(y,y^{\prime})\leq 1
. For
K>0
, we say that
V\in\mathcal{U}(F)\cup\mathcal{U}(F^{\prime})
is
involved in the transition
between
F
and
F^{\prime}
if one of the following holds:

(1)

\pi_{V}(F)\neq\pi_{V}(F^{\prime})
.

(2)

\mathcal{U}^{V}(F)\neq\mathcal{U}^{V}(F^{\prime})
.

The following, which combines the statements of
[
32
, Theorem 2.9 and Proposition 2.14]
, states that, for any colorable HHS
\mathcal{X}
, we can replace its given projections with coarsely equivalent projections with which we can control involved domains up to a bounded error:

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{S})
be a colorable HHS with standard projections
\hat{\pi}_{-},\hat{\rho}^{-}_{-}
. Then there exists
\theta>0
and new projections
\pi_{-},\rho^{-}_{-}
with the same domains and ranges, respectively, such that the following hold:

(1)

The new projections
\pi_{-},\rho^{-}_{-}
coarsely coincide with the old ones
\hat{\pi}_{-},\hat{\rho}^{-}_{-}
. More precisely:

(a)

If
X,Y
lie in different
\mathfrak{S}_{j}
and
\hat{\rho}^{X}_{Y}
is defined, then
\rho^{X}_{Y}=\hat{\rho}^{X}_{Y}
.

(b)

If
X,Y\in\mathfrak{S}_{j}
, then
d^{Haus}_{Y}(\rho^{X}_{Y},\hat{\rho}^{X}_{Y})<\theta.

(c)

If
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
Y\in\mathfrak{S}
, then
d^{Haus}_{Y}(\pi_{Y}(x),\hat{\pi}_{Y}(x))<\theta
.

(2)

There exists
K_{0}=K_{0}(\mathcal{X})>0
and
N_{0}(\mathcal{X})>0
so that if
K>K_{0}
, then the following holds. Let
x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with
d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})\leq 1
and
d_{\mathcal{X}}(y,y^{\prime})\leq 1
, and let
\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(\{x,y\})
and
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\})
be the relevant sets for the new projections
\pi_{-}
. Then:

(a)

\#(\mathcal{U}\triangle\mathcal{U}^{\prime})<N_{0}
, and if
V\in\mathcal{U}\triangle\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, then
d_{V}(x,y)\prec K
and
d_{V}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\prec K
.

(b)

There are at most
N_{0}
domains
V\in\mathcal{U}(F)\cup\mathcal{U}(F^{\prime})
involved in the transition between
F
and
F^{\prime}
.

Sketch of proof.

The existence of new projections satisfying the conditions of item (1) is essentially an immediate consequence of the construction in
[
14
, Theorem 4.1]
. Item (2), which regards stability of the relevant sets
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, requires a bit more work, but the key ideas are as follows:

Using
[
11
, Proposition 2.8]
(which generalizes
[
28
,
4
,
15
]
), there is a partial order
\prec
on
\mathrm{Rel}(x,y)
, which becomes a total order on
\mathrm{Rel}^{i}(x,y)=\mathrm{Rel}_{K}(x,y)\cap\mathfrak{S}_{i}
for each
i
. In particular, we can think of each
\mathrm{Rel}^{i}(x,y)
as an interval
X_{1}\prec\cdots\prec X_{n}
. One of the additional properties that the new projections
\pi_{-},\rho^{-}_{-}
satisfy (see
[
32
, Theorem 2.9]
) is that if
X\prec Y
along both
\mathrm{Rel}(x,y)
and
\mathrm{Rel}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, then
\pi_{Y}(x)=\pi_{Y}(x^{\prime})=\rho^{X}_{Y}
and
\pi_{X}(y)=\pi_{X}(y^{\prime})=\rho^{Y}_{X}
. Using this, one can show that
\mathrm{Rel}(x,y)
and
\mathrm{Rel}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
can only differ at their first or last elements in the order
\prec
. Hence
\#\left(\mathrm{Rel}^{i}(x,y)\triangle\mathrm{Rel}^{i}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right)\leq 4
; see
[
32
, Lemma 2.11 and Proposition 2.12]
. Since there are only boundedly-many BBF families

, item (
2a
) follows easily.

A similar argument can now be used to control the number of involved domains of type (1) of Definition
7.3
, see the proof of
[
32
, Proposition 2.14]
. For involved domains of type (2), this is an immediate consequence of item (
2a
) and the Covering Lemma
3.14
, which bounds the number of domains
W\in\mathcal{U}
into which a given
V\in\mathcal{U}
can nest.
∎

8.
Stable Intervals

With the new HHS projections provided by Theorem
7.4
, we now turn toward converting stabilized projection data into stable families of intervals, in partivuvlar stabilizing the input into the cubical model construction from Section
4
.

Our ultimate goal is to produce cubical models for pairs of nearby points that differ by a controlled number of hyperplane deletions (Theorem
9.9
). Since the hyperplanes in the cubical models come from edges of the simplicial intervals in our HFIs (Theorem
6.4
), it will suffice to build HFIs where the intervals only differ by boundedly-many bounded-size collections of edges. That is the content of the Stable Intervals Theorem
8.14
.

We first explain the build-up to this theorem and defer the explicit statement. We will work in the setting of a hyperbolic space with a hierarchical-like configuration. The construction is a somewhat long and delicate exercise in hyperbolic geometry and we will mainly provide a detailed sketch.

8.1.
Basic setup and assumptions

For the rest of this section, let us assume that
\mathcal{Z}
is a
\delta
-hyperbolic geodesic space. The first bit of notation we want to set is a choice of geodesic between any pair of points. That is, for any
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}
, we fix (once and for all) a geodesic
\lambda(x,y)
. More generally, if
C,C^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{Z}
are finite subsets, then we let
\lambda(C,C^{\prime})
denote a choice of geodesic between them whose length satisfies
|\lambda(C,C^{\prime})|=d_{\mathcal{Z}}(C,C^{\prime})
, and we require symmetry, namely
\lambda(C,C^{\prime})=\lambda(C^{\prime},C)
.

It will also be necessary to perform a slight modification on the geodesic function
\lambda
, so that its resulting length is an integer. To fix this, we let
\lambda_{0}(C,C^{\prime})
denote the restriction of
\lambda(C,C^{\prime}):[0,d_{\mathcal{Z}}(C,C^{\prime})]\to\mathcal{Z}
to the interval
[0,\lfloor{d_{\mathcal{Z}}(C,C^{\prime})}\rfloor]
. The effect of this is to trim
\lambda(A,B)
so that it has positive integer length and their lengths satisfy
l(\lambda(C,C^{\prime}))-l(\lambda_{0}(C,C^{\prime}))<1
. While not necessary, one could make this symmetric by trimming both the sides by half of
l(\lambda(C,C^{\prime}))-l(\lambda_{0}(C,C^{\prime}))
. In the course of the arguments, however, one can usually work with
\lambda
instead of
\lambda_{0}
.

Now, for the rest of this section, we fix a pair of points
\{a,b\}\subset\mathcal{Z}
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{Z}
be a finite (but possibly arbitrarily large) set of points of
\mathcal{Z}
and let
\epsilon>0
so that
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\lambda(a,b),y)<\epsilon/2
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. We call such an arrangement
(a,b;\mathcal{Y})
an
\epsilon
-
setup
in
\mathcal{Z}
.

In the hierarchical setting,
\mathcal{Z}
will be one of the hyperbolic spaces
\mathcal{C}(U)
for
U\in\mathcal{U}
, and the points
\mathcal{Y}
will be the points in
\rho^{V}_{U}
for
V\sqsubset U\in\mathcal{U}
. The proximity condition of the points in
\mathcal{Y}
to
\lambda(a,b)
comes from BGIA
3.11
.

Notation 8.1

(
O(\epsilon)
-notation)
.

In this section, we will often want to get controlled bounds over various distance quantities, where the bounds will depend on our choice of
\epsilon
and the hyperbolicity constant
\delta
for
\mathcal{Z}
. When a number
A
is bounded above as a function of
\epsilon,\delta
, we will simply write

A<O(\epsilon),

since
\delta
is given to us, while we need some flexibility to choose
\epsilon
.

The following lemma, which is essentially a consequence of the Morse Lemma for hyperbolic spaces, gives us control over how far
\lambda(C,C^{\prime})
can get from
\lambda(a,b)
when
C,C^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{Y}
, and are thus
\epsilon
-close to
\lambda(a,b)
:

Lemma 8.2

(Fellow-traveling,
\delta
-hyperbolic)
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be
\delta
-hyperbolic and geodesic, and
\gamma
a geodesic between
a,b\in\mathcal{Z}
. For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\epsilon^{\prime}=O(\epsilon)>0
so that the following holds:

•

Suppose
\gamma^{\prime}
is a geodesic connecting points
x,y\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\gamma)
, and that
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
are closest points to
x,y
on
\gamma
, respectively. Then any geodesic
[x,y]
satisfies
d_{\mathcal{Z}}^{Haus}([x,y],[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}])<\epsilon^{\prime}
.

As a consequence, if
C,C^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{Y}
are finite subsets, then
\lambda(C,C^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(\lambda(a,b))
. Finally, it will be convenient for us to assume that
\epsilon^{\prime}>\epsilon
.

8.2.
Clusters and the cluster graph

Our first goal is to group the points of
\mathcal{Y}
into
clusters
of nearby sets. To that end, let
E=E(\epsilon)>\epsilon
be sufficiently large, to be determined below.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{E}
denote the graph whose vertices are points of
\mathcal{Y}\cup\{a,b\}
, with two points
x,y\in\mathcal{Y}\cup\{a,b\}
connected by an edge exactly when
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(x,y)<E
. The connected components of
\mathcal{C}_{E}
are called
E
-
clusters
, or simply
clusters
when
E
is fixed.

For any
E
-cluster
C
, let
s(C)=\mathrm{hull}_{\lambda(a,b)}(p_{\lambda(a,b)}(C))
denote the hull (in
\lambda(a,b)
) of the closest point projection of
C
to
\lambda(a,b)
. The set
s(C)
is called the
shadow
of
C

8.5.
The stable interval theorem

With Definition

We first state this technical result, and then prove a corollary which gives the main upshot for our purposes of building stable cubical models.

For a bit of setup, recall the notion of a thickening of an interval from Subsection

8.6.
Detailed sketch of the proof

In lieu of a full proof (see Appendix
B

[

than the original
[

The goal is to show that the stable intervals (Definition
8.6

-setups admit stable decompositions in the sense of Definition

Roughly, these are decompositions of the intervals into collections of subintervals, which are either identical (as in item (d)), or the same length and uniformly close (as in item (e)), or are one of boundedly-many unstable components we can ignore (as in item (f)). We need to show how to constraints on the input data assumed in item (1) translates into control over the components of the stable intervals.

To begin the sketch, first observe that the two main assumptions of Theorem
8.14
on the setups

and

are that

•

are close to both

and
\lambda(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

•

The endpoints pairs
(a,b)

This suggests the following iterated proof strategy:

(1)

First, show that we can build a stable decomposition when we add a single nearby cluster point, while keeping the endpoints the same.

(2)

Next, show that we can iteratively combine stable decompositions to obtain a stable decomposition.

(3)

Finally, show that we can build a stable decomposition when we only wiggle a single endpoint and keep everything else (including the cluster sets) the same.

Steps (1) and (3) can be thought of as
moves
, where we only change one piece of data at a time, and we will see that type (3) moves are basically type (1) moves. For step (2), we will want to iteratively perform type (1) moves by adding the extra cluster points of
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}-\mathcal{Y}
to the setup
(a,b;\mathcal{Y})
, thereby shifting from
(a,b;\mathcal{Y})
to
(a,b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
. Then we make two type (3) moves to shift
(a,b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})\to(a^{\prime},b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})\to(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
. Finally, we make another sequence of iterated type (1) moves to shift from
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
down to
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
. Since this total sequence of moves is bounded by

, we will be done.

For step (1), there are two main cases—the

far from all

-clusters; and the

-cluster. See Figures

Step (2) of the proof now involves iteratively applying step (1), and thus one must show that one can coherently combine two pairs of stable decompositions across a common setup. Figure

The associated stability constants increase a controlled amount with each iteration, but we are assuming that we only need to perform boundedly-many such iterations in Theorem

Finally, step (3) is an application of steps (1) and (2). To get the idea, we can consider the following restricted setup, which we highlight to focus on what happens when we perturb an endpoint. Let

B.2.1.
Completing the proof

To prove Theorem
8.14
, we have two remaining tasks:

(1)

We need to explain how to build a stable decomposition when perturbing an endpoint but keeping the rest of the setup the same, and

(2)

Show how to stick this into an iterative setup.

Perturbing an endpoint
:
This is an iterated application of Proposition
B.1
.

Consider the following restricted setup, which we highlight to focus on what happens when we perturb an endpoint. Let
a,b,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{Z}
be finite with
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon/2}(\lambda(a,b))\cap\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon/2}(a^{\prime},b)
. Consider the following collection of setups, each of which differs by adding or deleting one cluster point, or by switching the role that
a,a^{\prime}
or
b,b^{\prime}
play between cluster or endpoint:

(1)

(a,b;\mathcal{Y})
,

(2)

(a,b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\{a\})
,

(3)

(a,b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\{a,a^{\prime}\})
,

(4)

(a^{\prime},b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\{a,a^{\prime}\})
,

(5)

(a^{\prime},b;\mathcal{Y}\cup\{a^{\prime}\})
,

(6)

(a^{\prime},b;\mathcal{Y})
.

First observe that
\mathcal{Y}
is contained in each of the above sets of cluster points, so we want to see how to build
\mathcal{Y}
-stable decompositions for each, which implies the existence of
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
-stable decompositions for any
\mathcal{Y}_{0}\subset\mathcal{Y}
. Next, observe that in the construction of the stable interval
T(a,b;\mathcal{Y})
, the roles of points of the endpoints
a,b
and the cluster points
\mathcal{Y}
are equivalent in cluster formation process. The only special role that endpoints play in the Definition
8.6
of
T(a,b;\mathcal{Y})
is in the segments defined
within
clusters, namely the
\mu(C)
components. In particular, the edge components (i.e., the components of
T_{e}
) treat endpoints and cluster points equivalently. As the desired stable decompositions from Theorem
8.14
are subsets of the edge components, we are free to treat the points
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}
as cluster points in applying Proposition
B.1
to obtain stable decompositions for each of the steps
(1)\to(2)\to(3)
and
(4)\to(5)\to(6)
. The only exception is the step
(3)\to(4)
, where we are switching the roles of
a
and
a^{\prime}
. Here Proposition
B.1
does not directly apply, but, by the above discussion, these two setups have the exact same clusters and thus the exact same edge components. Hence we get the desired bijections (with their proximity constraints) by identifying the edge components as stable components—which, again, are all exactly the same—and taking the bijection between the complementary components to be the obvious map.

In other words, provided an explanation for how to iteratively combine a sequence of stable decompositions, we can apply this process to move from setup (1) above to setup (6).

Putting it all together
: In the more general setting of the theorem, the above sequence only appears in the middle of a longer sequence we need to consider. In particular, let
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}-\mathcal{Y}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{m}\}
, where
n,m<N
by assumption. Set
\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\mathcal{Y}\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\cup\{z_{1},\dots,z_{i}\}
, and let
\mathcal{Y}_{0}=\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{all}=\mathcal{Y}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Then, by the assumptions in our setup, we have two sequences of pairwise admissible
\epsilon
-setups

(a,b;\mathcal{Y})\to(a,b;\mathcal{Y}_{1})\to\cdots\to(a,b;\mathcal{Y}_{all})\to(a,b;\mathcal{Y}_{all}\cup\{a^{\prime}\})\to(a,b;\mathcal{Y}_{all}\cup\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\})

and

(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})\to\cdots\to(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}_{n})\to(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}_{all})\to(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}_{all}\cup\{a\})\to(a^{\prime},b^{\prime};\mathcal{Y}_{all}\cup\{a,b\}),

which we note are connected in the middle by the above discussion, where we need to switch the roles of both
a,a^{\prime}
and

By Proposition
