Nitric oxide supplementation during extracorporeal resuscitation drives oxidative–inflammatory signaling and metabolic suppression in the post–cardiac arrest heart
Authors: Abou Bakr M Salama, Daniele Linardi, Romel Mani, Maddalena Tessari, Qinghui Ou, Yasmin Y Salem, Ahmed Abdulsaboor, Ahmed M Shaker, Ahmad Gebreil, Ahmed Elbakri, Riham R E Abouleisa, Bahaael El Sady, Giovanni B Luciani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial dysfunction is a major determinant of mortality after cardiac arrest, yet the molecular events driving post-resuscitation injury remain incompletely understood. Nitric oxide (NO) has been proposed as a cardioprotective adjunct during extracorporeal life support (ECLS), but its mechanistic impact on myocardial recovery is unclear. We investigated whether NO supplementation during ECLS modulates oxidative stress, metabolic pathways, and apoptotic signaling in the post–cardiac arrest heart.MethodsMale Sprague Dawley rats underwent hypothermic cardiac arrest followed by ECLS resuscitation with or without NO supplementation (20 ppm). Myocardial tissue was analyzed using bulk RNA sequencing, quantitative RT-PCR, oxidative stress assays (MDA, 3-nitrotyrosine, total oxidant/antioxidant status), and TUNEL staining to characterize pathway-level alterations.ResultsNO supplementation markedly increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis (46%±3 vs. 27%±2; p < 0.0001). Transcriptomic profiling revealed > 550 differentially expressed genes, highlighting upregulation of inflammatory and apoptotic cascades (MAPK, TNF, NF-κB, proteasome/TLR pathways) and profound suppression of metabolic programs essential for myocardial recovery, including fatty acid oxidation, branched-chain amino acid metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. NO induced a striking oxidative–nitrosative imbalance, with elevated MDA, 3-nitrotyrosine, and total oxidant status and reduced total antioxidant capacity, resulting in a threefold increase in the oxidative stress index.ConclusionsNO administration during ECLS drives a coordinated oxidative–inflammatory–apoptotic response and disrupts metabolic pathways necessary for myocardial recovery, suggesting a mechanistic basis for worsened post-arrest myocardial injury. These findings have direct implications for optimizing resuscitation strategies in human ECLS and may inform future therapeutic modulation of NO signaling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01468-7.Keywords: Extracorporeal life support, Cardiac arrest, Nitric oxide, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis, Myocardial injury

Significance of the study

Post–cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction is a major contributor to early mortality, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying impaired myocardial recovery remain poorly defined. Nitric oxide (NO) is frequently considered as a potential adjunct during extracorporeal life support (ECLS), but its effects in the setting of global ischemia–reperfusion injury are controversial. Our study identifies a link between NO supplementation and coordinated activation of oxidative–nitrosative stress, inflammatory signaling, and apoptotic pathways, alongside suppression of metabolic programs essential for cardiomyocyte survival and functional recovery. These data provide a molecular explanation for the potential harm observed with NO during resuscitation and highlight pathways that could serve as therapeutic targets to improve myocardial outcomes after cardiac arrest. The findings carry direct translational relevance for the clinical use of NO in ECLS protocols and inform future strategies for modulating myocardial injury in critically ill patients.

Introduction

Cardiac arrest, characterized by the sudden cessation of the heart’s mechanical activity, leads to immediate circulatory collapse, rapid oxygen depletion, and severe brain dysfunction (Bonow et al. 2011). It is a life-threatening condition with a grim prognosis, as survival rates remain low despite advances in resuscitation techniques. Resuscitation aims to mitigate brain damage, myocardial dysfunction, and multi-organ failure caused by ischemia and hypoxia during cardiac arrest. Despite significant advancements, such as Extracorporeal Life Support (ECLS) and Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO), which restore tissue perfusion and oxygenation, 30-day mortality rates remain high (Conlon et al. 2008).

Numerous cardioprotective strategies have been proposed to mitigate the cardiac insult, including ischemic preconditioning, postconditioning, remote ischemic conditioning, pharmacologic interventions, and metabolic modulation approaches (Penna et al. 2022, Bøtker et al. 2018). Despite extensive experimental success and identification of multiple signaling pathways involved in cardioprotection, translation into consistent clinical benefit has remained limited, largely due to challenges related to reproducibility, model heterogeneity, and complex pathophysiological interactions in clinical settings. These translational gaps highlight the continued need to investigate novel mechanistic targets and refine therapeutic strategies aimed at improving myocardial recovery after ischemic injury.

One promising yet controversial approach is the use of nitric oxide (NO) supplementation during resuscitation. NO, a potent vasodilator, has shown potential in reducing ischemia-reperfusion injury by inhibiting platelet and leukocyte activation, and modulating inflammatory pathways (Shah et al. 1991). However, its role in cardiac arrest resuscitation remains unclear, with conflicting evidence on its benefits and risks.

NO is a small, lipophilic molecule that plays a crucial role in cardiovascular physiology. It regulates vascular tone, inhibits ROS formation, and modulates inflammatory responses (Pinsky et al. 1997). Inhaled NO (iNO) has been successfully used in clinical settings, such as treating hypoxemic respiratory failure in neonates and improving outcomes in cardiac surgery patients (Bhatraju et al. 2015, Checchia et al. 2013). Administering NO during cardiopulmonary bypass (ECLS) in pediatric patients have been shown to reduce postoperative morbidity and improve cardiac performance (James et al. 2016, Hataishi et al. 2006). These findings have inspired further exploration of NO’s potential in cardiac arrest resuscitation.

However, the molecular mechanisms underlying NO’s effects during resuscitation are poorly understood. While some studies suggest that NO supplementation can limit myocardial damage and improve neurological outcomes, others indicate potential deleterious effects, such as increased oxidative stress and apoptosis (Liu et al. 2007). Some animal studies have shown that NO administration during reperfusion can reduce infarct size and improve cardiac function, but these benefits are not consistently observed in clinical settings (Gianetti et al. 2004, Kida and Ichinose 2014). This discrepancy underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the molecular changes associated with NO supplementation during resuscitation.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the molecular effects of NO supplementation during cardiac arrest resuscitation using an experimental rat model. By analyzing cardiac tissue for apoptotic activity, oxidative stress, and gene expression changes, we sought to clarify the potential benefits and risks of NO in this context. Our findings challenge the current enthusiasm for NO supplementation in resuscitation medicine, revealing increased myocardial oxidant stress, activation of inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, and a higher incidence of apoptotic events. These results highlight the complexity of NO’s role in cardiac arrest resuscitation and call for further research to optimize its therapeutic application.

Materials and methods

Surgical protocols

All experimental animal surgeries were performed at C.I.R.S.A.L. (Interdepartmental Research Centre on Laboratory Animals) of the Biological Institutes, University of Verona according European Directive 2010/63/EU. The housing, handling and sacrifice of the animals have been carried out according to current regulations (Declaration of Helsinki and “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals – Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources – National Institutes of Health”).

Male rats of the Sprague Dawley strain weighing 400 ± 50 g were used, kept in temperature and humidity controlled rooms with typical light-dark cycle, and fed with standard diet and water.

First, the rats were anesthetized with diethyl ether vapors, and were intubated by the endotracheal route with an atraumatic tube consisting of a 14G venous cannula. Then The rats were mechanically ventilated with a mechanical rodent respirator (Harvard Model 687, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston MA) with a mixture of oxygen and sevofloran anesthetic 2% (Florane; Abbott, Baar, Switzerland). Respiratory support was continued for the duration of the operation with a fraction of inhaled oxygen (FiO2) of 90%, a tidal volume of 10 ml/kg and a frequency of 80 breathing acts per minute. Ketoprofen 2 mg/kg was injected subcutaneously to maintain analgesia and repeated every 2 h. During the surgical procedure, the temperature of the room was maintained between 23° and 25 °C and a layer of insulating material was placed between the body of the animal and the operation table. The rats were placed in a supine position, the thoracic and the ventral surfaces of were shaved, and the skin was disinfected with chlorhexidine. Monitoring of the ECG and the body temperature was maintained during the entire course of the experiments. The right femoral artery was isolated and cannulated with a 2-Fr catheter (model SPR 838, Millar Instruments, Houston, TX) for monitoring systemic arterial pressure. Finally, the right femoral vein was cannulated with 24 G cannula for the infusion of 2 mg/kg of pancuronium-bromide to obtain complete muscle relaxation and flushed with Normal Saline to avoid thrombosis of the cannula.

To induce hypothermic cardiac arrest, the rats were placed in a supine position on the operating table and wrapped with a previously frozen pad of gel until a rectal temperature is 15 °C. At this point, the electrocardiographic trace detected a variable rhythm between a rate of ventricular fibrillation, marked bradycardia (20 bpm) or asystole. The mean blood pressure detected in these phases varied between 20mmHg and 10 mmHg. At this point the ventilator was turned off, and the rats were left in a state of hypothermic cardiac arrest for 60 min.

During the cardiac arrest phase, the rats were connected to the ECLS circuit. The right external jugular vein was then isolated and a 24 G cannula with is inserted and advanced along its entire length sufficient to reach the level of the right atrium of the rat. The right common carotid artery was cannulated with a 24 G catheter, advanced to the aortic arch and then connected to the arterial perfusion line of the circuit. At the time of insertion of the venous catheter, rocuronium bromide 1–2 mg/kg for muscle relaxation and 500 IU/kg of heparin for anticoagulation were administered.

The ECLS circuit consists of a roller pump (Stöckert SIII, Sorin, Germany), a hollow fiber oxygenator with standard industrial characteristics (Sorin, Mirandola, MO, Italy), a regulator vacuum with a pressure of -30/-40 cmH2O to facilitate venous drainage. All these parts were connected by plastic pipe of 1.6 mm internal diameter. The total filling volume of the circuit including the oxygenator, was 6 ml and consisted of 50% colloid solution and 50% Lactate Ringer. The gas exchange surface was 450 cm2. The heat exchange surface 15.8 cm2 .

The rats were randomized into two groups: ECLS conducted for 1 h (Control group, n = 10), ECLS supplemented with NO 20 ppm (ECLS-NO) administered through membrane oxygenator for 1 h (n = 10). In subjects treated with NO, the INOblender dispenser was also prepared with its INOMAX detector. To proceed with this operation, the Oxygen supply line was connected with 90% FiO2 and the Nitric Oxide cylinder operated at 20 ppm to the mixer. The NO source was conducted to the oxygenator of the ECLS circuit by mixing it with oxygen using INOblender. For the control group, the oxygen supply line was connected directly to the oxygenator.When rats were connected to the ECLS circuit, a flow rate of 80-100 ml/kg/min was maintained with a mean blood pressure range of 70–90 mmHg. The thermal gradient rate of heating is adjusted to achieve a rectal temperature of 35 °C in 1 h (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Tissue collection

At the end of the experiment all rats were sacrificed and the heart was collected through median sternotomy. The samples taken were immediately placed in liquid nitrogen and then stored at -80 °C.

Molecular studies

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized rat hearts, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and analyzed by real-time PCR to quantify gene expression using the ΔΔCT method with GAPDH normalization. Bulk RNA sequencing was performed on isolated RNA to identify differential gene expression through library preparation, high-throughput sequencing, bioinformatics alignment, normalization, and pathway enrichment analyses. Histological assessment included immunofluorescence staining of cryosectioned heart tissue to detect cardiac markers, and apoptosis was evaluated using the TUNEL assay with image-based quantification of labeled nuclei. Oxidative stress was measured biochemically by assessing lipid peroxidation (MDA), 3-nitrotyrosine levels, total oxidant status, total antioxidant status, and calculating an overall oxidative stress index from tissue extracts. Detailed methods are described in the supplementary methods section.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons was used, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.0). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Bulk RNA sequencing to characterize the general transcriptional changes accompanying NO treatment

Previous studies have showed a neuroprotective effect of the NO during cardiac arrest at both the clinical and molecular levels. In this study we are investigating whether NO supplementation during resuscitation would results in a cardio-protective effect at the molecular and genetic level. In the current study, the rats were randomized into two groups: ECLS conducted normally in normothermia for 1 h (Control group, n = 10), ECLS supplemented with NO 20 ppm (ECLS-NO) administered through oxygenator for 1 h (n = 10). Hearts were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after the experiment and RNA was isolated afterwards. RNA Sequencing reviled more than 550 genes are differentially expressed in NO treated group (NO-ECLS) compared to the control group (ECLS only). GO term enrichment shows NO-ECLS group showed up regulation of pathways related to inflammation, immune response, cell cycle arrest, response to stress, and apoptosis activation (Fig. 1a, b). NO-ECLS group showed downregulation of the metabolic genes (Fig. 2a, b).

Down-regulated genes in ECLS + NO group compared to ECLS only. a heat map of caalculated z-score of change gene expression from bulkRNAseq. b gene ontology analysis shows significant down regulation of most metabolic pathways in NO-ECLS compared to ECLS only

Pathway analysis using pathview software to highlight the main molecular pathways that been affected by the profound transcriptional changes

To Further understand the key transcriptomic changes and depict the major affected pathways, Pathview software analysis was used and KEGG graphs were generated for all the differentially expressed genes. Metabolic, signaling, inflammatory, apoptotic, phagocytic, and autonomic pathways among other molecular pathways were analyzed and fold expression of the related genes presented on the respective KEGG graph. The analysis showed that NO-ECLS is associated with complex metabolic derangement as indicated by down regulation of genes related to fatty acids metabolism (Supp. Figure 2, 3), amino acids metabolism (Supp. Figure 4), and all complexes of the electron transport chain (Supp. Figure 5).

NO-ECLS results in increased expression of the genes involved in pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic signaling pathways compared to ECLS only. NO-ECLS resulted in up regulation of genes related to IL1, MAPK, TNF and NF-KB signaling (Xiao 2001) (Supp. Figure 6,7). NO treatment showed increased expression of the genes involved in proteasome subunits (Supp. Figure 8), and Toll-like receptors (TLR) (Supp. Figure 9).

On the other hand, NO-ECLS treatment downregulated of myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) enzyme expression, which is responsible for phosphorylation of myosin light chains and enhancement of muscle contraction. This effect is supposed to be associated with enhanced smooth muscle relaxation (Supp. Figure 10). Moreover, NO-ECLS resulted in lower expression of sympathetic receptors including α-adrenergic receptors (α-AR) and β1adrenergic receptors (β1-AR) and higher expression of β-2adrenergic receptors (β2-AR). NO treatment has led to lower expression of most of calcium handling genes including di-hydropyridine receptors (DHPR), smooth endoplasmic reticulum calcium uptake (SERCA2a) and ryanodine receptors (RYR2) which is supposed to add to cardiac muscle relaxation (Supp. Figure 11,12).

RT-PCR confirms the effects of NO treatment on metabolic, inflammatory, and apoptotic pathways

To confirm our findings from the bulk-RNA sequencing data, we conducted RT-PCR analysis of the key differentially expressed targets genes for samples from baseline (hearts that did not subjected to cardiopulmonary bypass), NO-ECLS and ECLS-only. Gene targets were selected based on pathway-level analyses of the RNA-sequencing data. We prioritized transcripts that showed robust differential expression, served as central regulatory or rate-limiting nodes, and were mechanistically linked to the principal biological processes identified in the study, namely inflammatory activation, adrenergic signaling, and oxidative stress responses relevant to post-cardiac arrest myocardial injury. RT-PCR confirmed the bulk RNA sequencing findings with NO-ECLS found to have increased fold expression of the inflammatory markers including cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX-2) (1.5-fold change), IL-1 (5-fold change), NFκB (5-fold change), and β2-AR (2.7-fold change), while NO treatment has led to normalization of the expression of both α1-AR and β1-AR compared to ECLS only (Fig. 3a-g).

TUNEL assay shows increased apoptotic markers in tissues treated with NO

Finally, to identify the effect of these transcriptomic changes on tissue viability, we conducted TUNEL assay for assessment of the apoptotic nuclei. Tissues exposed to NO has showed significant increase in the TUNEL positive nuclei compared to ECLS only (46%±3 vs. 27%±2) (Fig. 4a, b). This clearly indicates that the transcriptomic changes associated with NO treatment lead to more tissue injury and apoptosis compared to baseline.

Data is presented as mean ± SD. (****p < 0.0001, n = 8 per group)

Oxidative stress assessment in cardiac tissue

To understand the mechanism behind these molecular changes after NO supplementation, we hypothesized that it might be through oxidant stress as reported in earlier studies (Heusch et al. 2023). Accordingly, we measured key biomarkers in cardiac tissue, including Malondialdehyde (MDA), 3-Nitrotyrosine (3-NT), Total Antioxidant Status (TAS), Total Oxidant Status (TOS), and the Oxidative Stress Index (OSI). MDA levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation, were significantly higher in the NO-supplemented group compared to the baseline group, indicating increased oxidative damage to lipids (Fig. 5a). Similarly, 3-NT levels, a marker of protein nitrosylation, were elevated in the NO-supplemented group, suggesting enhanced protein modification due to reactive nitrogen species (Fig. 5b). TAS, reflecting the cumulative antioxidant capacity, was significantly lower in the NO-supplemented group, indicating a depletion of antioxidant defenses (Fig. 5c). Conversely, TOS, representing the total oxidant burden, was markedly higher in the NO-supplemented group, confirming an increase in oxidative stress (Fig. 5d). The OSI, calculated as the ratio of TOS to TAS, was significantly elevated in the NO-supplemented group, reflecting a pronounced imbalance between oxidant and antioxidant systems (Fig. 5e).

Discussion

Nitric oxide is an effective supplement for many purposes including neuroprotection during cardiac and major vascular surgeries, as well pulmonary hypertension management (Remes et al. 2022, Calabrese et al. 2007, Linardi et al. 2021). Some neuroprotective effects for NO in normal and failing hearts has been reported which leads to the hypothesis that NO supplementation may have some therapeutic and cardio-protective effects during resuscitation (Umar and Laarse 2009). The cardio-protective effects in the setting of accidental or surgical cardiac arrest has not been sudied in debth and a little is known about the molecular effects of NO treatment in that sitting.

The reports about cardiac effects of NO treatments are controversial as nitric oxide exerts complex and context-dependent effects on the myocardium. Experimental and clinical studies suggestted that NO can confer cardioprotection by improving coronary perfusion, attenuating ischemia–reperfusion injury, and modulating inflammatory responses (Heusch 2001, Schulz et al. 2004). In certain settings, NO was reported to improve left ventricular function, particularly through vasodilatory and anti-inflammatory mechanisms (Rassaf et al. 2006). However, accumulating evidence indicates that excessive or sustained NO exposure may exert deleterious effects, primarily through enhanced oxidative–nitrosative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and promotion of apoptotic signaling (Hauerslev et al. 2018). In contrast, Bocchi et al. showed that inhalation of NO leads to development of pulmonary edema in severe heart failure cases (Bocchi et al. 1994). Bocchi et al. explained their findings as to be related to increased right ventricular work with acute decompensation and development of acute heart failure (Bocchi et al. 1994). Moreover, Semigran et al. showed that NO inhalation increases left ventricular load by unknown mechanism (Semigran Marc et al. 1994). These dual effects likely depend on factors such as dose, timing, redox environment, and the severity of underlying ischemic injury. In the current study, we investigated the transcriptomic changes in cardiomyocytes after NO supplementation via extracorporeal life support in experimental rat models of cardiac arrest. Only a single NO dosing strategy was evaluated, and cardiac functional parameters were not assessed, representing an important limitation. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the framework of this well-recognized biological ambivalence of NO signaling in the injured myocardium.

Our data shows that nitric oxide supplementation with ECLS induces complex metabolic derangement as indicated by downregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism including both fatty acids oxidation and elongation pathways, down regulation of branched chain amino acids metabolism, and down regulation of respiratory chain complexes. Nitric oxide has pleotropic effects on cardiac cell biology. Physiologic level of NO production enhances fatty acids metabolism, lower glucose utilization and decrease oxygen consumption (Massion et al. 2005, Chen et al. 2002, Zhao et al. 1999). Cytotoxic concentrations of NO, as in heart failure and experimental models of increased nitric oxide synthase, has been reported to inhibit fatty acids metabolism, increase glucose utilization, lower oxygen consumption and ATP production, and increase reactive oxygen species production (Recchia 2002, Brown and Borutaite 2007, Winlaw et al. 1994). Our data is in line with the cytotoxic effect of NO.

Also, our data shows NO supplementation is associated with the profound transcriptomic activation of pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic signaling pathways including: MAPK signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, TLR signaling pathways, and immune-proteasome formation. NO contributes to myocytes loss by conferring an oxidative stress and triggering of apoptosis at pathological concentrations, (Sawyer and Colucci 1998, Albina et al. 1993, Pulkki 1997, Flesch et al. 1999, Sharma et al. 2000). Excess NO leads to formation of reactive nitrogen species (RNS) which contributes to the cell damage (Calabrese et al. 2007). NO mediates the negative inotropic effects of the cytokines (Finkel et al. 1992, Li and Olshansky 2011). In our study, NO directly and actively induce the transcription of inflammatory and apoptotic signaling genes. This pathological activation of inflammation and apoptosis might be mediated by post-translational modification of cellular proteins through nitrosation of their thiol groups (Rocks et al. 2005, Miersch et al. 2005, Mustafa et al. 2009, Frears et al. 1996, Thomas et al. 2010, Stamler et al. 1992, Jaffrey et al. 2001, Foster et al. 2003).

Finally, our analysis shows that NO supplementation downregulates α1, β1 -adrenergic receptors and contractile genes, while upregulate β2-adrenergic receptors. These findings are in line with previous reports. It has been previously reported that NO is responsible for α-adrenergic receptors hypo-responsiveness (Rao et al. 2002). NO is known to modulate the response to β2-adrenergic receptors stimulation (Massion et al. 2005, Rastaldo et al. 2007). NO has been reported to protect the heart against excessive catecholaminergic stimulation producing negative inotropic and chronotropic effects, and reinforcing the vagal tone in the heart (Massion et al. 2005, Balligand et al. 1993). So, NO is well known to maintain the balance between the adrenergic and cholinergic tone on the myocardium (Belge et al. 2005). Accordingly, increased tissue level of NO has been proposed as a strategy for cardio-protection and/or treatment of different cardiac insults (Roe and Ren 2012, Rochette et al. 2013, Jin et al. 2021, Hidemasa et al. 2007).

Despite the beneficial effects of NO on the adrenergic signaling on the heart, NO supplementation through ECLS has led to excessive oxidative stress with an increase in the percentage of apoptosis. This indicates that the cytotoxic and inflammatory effects of NO supplementation overweigh the protective effects. Although this may challenge the whole idea of the use of NO supplementation during cardiac and vascular surgeries, still some points are worth investigation.

It is worth highliting that NO is known to be rapidly inactivated by hemoglobin through dioxygenation to nitrate and methemoglobin limiting its sustained systemic bioavailability. Indeed, circulating hemoglobin efficiently scavenges NO, limiting its diffusion to distant tissues. While secondary carriers such as nitrosyl-hemoglobin or S-nitrosothiols may facilitate delayed NO signaling, most of the administered NO is expected to be rapidly neutralized within the extracorporeal circuit and bloodstream (Lancaster 1994, Doyle et al. 1981). This raises the possibility that the observed myocardial effects may instead reflect indirect mechanisms. One plausible contributor is increased methemoglobin formation, particularly under conditions of high oxygen fractions within the ECLS membrane, which could impair oxygen delivery and exacerbate myocardial injury. Methemoglobin levels were not measured in the present study and therefore represent an important limitation. Future investigations incorporating direct measurements of NO bioavailability, methemoglobin formation, and myocardial protein S-nitrosylation will be essential to clarify whether the observed molecular alterations result from direct cardiac NO signaling or from systemic secondary effects.

Another key limitation to the current study is the use of a single dose of supplemented NO through ECLS. Our previous work showed that NO 20 ppm supplementation has neuroprotective effects in the setting of selective cerebral perfusion and aortic arch surgery (Linardi et al. 2021). Accordingly, this was the dose tested in the current study. However, it has been reported that different tissue levels of NO influence different signaling pathways (Hall and Garthwaite 2009, Naseem 2005, Carpenter and Schoenfisch 2012). So, it is worth studying the effects of supplementation of lower concentrations of NO.

The present study did not include post-resuscitation functional assessment of cardiac performance, nor protein-level validation of the molecular findings. While these limitations are unlikely to alter the overall conclusions, future studies incorporating functional measurements and protein confirmation will be important to strengthen and further substantiate the mechanistic interpretations. Additionally, more in-depth investigations designed to establish causal relationships are warranted.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that nitric oxide (NO) supplementation during cardiac arrest resuscitation exacerbates myocardial injury by inducing oxidative stress, metabolic derangement, and activation of inflammatory and apoptotic pathways. Despite its potential benefits in other contexts, NO supplementation in this setting led to increased tissue apoptosis and oxidative damage, outweighing any protective effects. These findings challenge the use of NO in resuscitation and highlight the need for further research to optimize therapeutic strategies for cardiac arrest.
