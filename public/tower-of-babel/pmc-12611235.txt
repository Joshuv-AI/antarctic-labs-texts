Intestinal Obstruction due to a Trichobezoar: A Rare Complication of Rapunzel Syndrome
Authors: Katelynn Ho, Guy Jensen, Steven Lee, Michael Pickens, Hannibal Person
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTrichobezoars are rare phenomenon with challenging management related to a lack of clear guidance as to when endoscopic vs surgical approaches are indicated. We present a case of a 20-year-old patient with intellectual disability and a remote history of trichotillomania found to have a large gastric trichobezoar leading to Rapunzel syndrome and a subsequent small bowel obstruction, which was managed through combined endoscopic removal and surgical exploration.

INTRODUCTION

A trichobezoar is an aggregate of hair in the gastrointestinal tract and comprises approximately 1%–6% of pediatric bezoar cases.1 In rare circumstances, Rapunzel syndrome occurs when a gastric trichobezoar extends into the small intestine and can lead to complications such as bowel obstruction. Large gastric trichobezoars are generally more difficult to remove endoscopically and laparoscopy or laparotomy may be necessary. There are no current guidelines to determine when endoscopic vs surgical extraction is indicated. This case showcases the safe endoscopic removal of a large trichobezoar by a skilled gastroenterologist while highlighting the unique variables that need to be considered when determining an appropriate strategy for removal.

CASE REPORT

The patient is a 20-year-old with an intellectual disability, a remote history of trichotillomania, posttraumatic stress disorder, and chronic constipation who presented to the emergency room with a 1-day history of generalized abdominal pain and 3 episodes of nonbilious emesis. The patient was tachycardic with a soft abdomen diffusely tender to palpation without peritonitic signs. Pertinent laboratory results revealed an elevated lipase (5,633 IU/L) and C reactive protein (1.4 mg/dL). The pancreas was not well visualized on an abdominal ultrasound. A radiograph (XRAY) showed an above average stool burden with a prominent rectal stool mass. The patient was admitted for management of acute pancreatitis and chronic constipation. Additional history was notable for post-traumatic stress disorder which resulted in multiple years of trichotillomania and trichophagia in childhood, with no reported episodes in the past 5 years. There was no alopecia on examination. By hospital day 2, the patient had worsening abdominal pain and new abdominal distension. A nasogastric tube was placed for decompression. A computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen showed significant dilation of the stomach and small bowel with prominent air-fluid levels. A large hypodense gastric mass was identified suggestive of a trichobezoar (Figure 1). A similar intraluminal mass was found in the distal small bowel concerning for an obstruction due to the migration of the gastric trichobezoar into the small intestine as a complication of Rapunzel syndrome. General surgery was consulted, and the patient was taken to the operating room for an esophagogastroduodenoscopy, diagnostic laparoscopy, and manual fecal disimpaction. A trichobezoar was identified with a gastroscope and small aliquots of hair were removed with rat-tooth forceps. The entire trichobezoar was extracted after approximately 92 passes over 2.5 hours (Figures 2 and 3). A trichobezoar mixed with stool was found on manual disimpaction and removed. On diagnostic laparoscopy, the small bowel was run without identification of a transition zone, suggesting migration of the trichobezoar into the colon. The patient had no operative complications and was discharged on omeprazole for gastritis found on esophagogastroduodenoscopy and polyethylene glycol and senna for constipation.

DISCUSSION

Common symptoms of a trichobezoar include nausea, early satiety, vomiting, abdominal pain, and constipation, which are nonspecific and can be seen in many gastrointestinal conditions.1 Therefore, the prompt recognition of additional risk factors including intellectual disability, adverse childhood experiences, trichotillomania, trichophagia, and female gender may have increased our concern for a trichobezoar and led our team to pursue earlier advanced imaging.2 Abdominal ultrasound, Xray, CT, upper gastrointestinal series, and direct visualization with endoscopy have been used to identify trichobezoars. However, CT is preferred to evaluate for a concomitant intestinal bezoar in cases of bowel obstruction.2 A faster diagnosis may have identified the gastric trichobezoar before migration and decreased the risk of a bowel obstruction.

There are limited data to inform the optimal treatment of gastric trichobezoars when considering endoscopic vs surgical extraction. Over the past 10 years, the success rate of endoscopic removal alone has increased from 5% to 30%.3 This may be due to improved provider skill and new endoscopic techniques and tools available. Fragmentation techniques with polypectomy snares, electrocautery, argon plasma cautery, endoscopic scissors, and hot biopsy forceps are commonly used to assist removal of the trichobezoar with graspers and nets.3 Recently, the use of a variceal ligator cap and dissolution of a trichobezoar with direct injection with Coca-cola has been described.4,5

Endoscopic intervention alone absolves the need for a gastrostomy or enterotomy. In cases of Rapunzel syndrome that were successfully managed by endoscopy only, the time spent on removal ranged from 1 to 5 hours on average in a single sedated period after approximately 2–25 passes.3,6,7 For comparison, our patient underwent complete endoscopic extraction of the trichobezoar after 2.5 hours and 92 passes without the use of fragmentation techniques. Many reports where endoscopy was unsuccessful before surgical conversion did not state the total anesthesia time or number of passes.8,9 One case report of Rapunzel syndrome described transitioning to a laparotomy 1 hour after an endoscopic attempt.10 The wide range of endoscopic techniques used for extraction, variation in procedural time, and number of passes makes it difficult to determine when endoscopy and conversion to surgery is warranted.

Forgoing endoscopy and proceeding with a surgical extraction would have been of questionable benefit for our patient. Our patient experienced no complications associated with endoscopic removal including mucosal trauma or the adverse effects of prolonged anesthesia. This suggests that with a skilled endoscopist, complete endoscopic removal of a gastric bezoar is a viable option. In retrospect, a balloon enteroscopy could have been considered for removal of the intestinal bezoar after the successful extraction of the gastric bezoar given the patient's stability throughout the procedure. Ultimately, avoiding an enterotomy or gastrostomy negated potential complications associated with significant morbidity including enteral leaks, intraabdominal infections, and fistulae formation.

The risks and benefits of endoscopy vs surgery should be approached in a multidisciplinary fashion and discussed with the patient and the family to select the optimal approach and minimize adverse outcomes. The size of the trichobezoar, its location, the skill level and comfort of the endoscopist, and the endoscopic equipment available are factors that may affect this decision. This case highlights the need for additional studies to guide indications to attempt endoscopic removal, anesthesia duration, the number of passes before converting to surgical extraction, and indications to proceed with surgery only.
