Cerebellar GABA and executive function among adult female carriers of the fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 premutation: a pilot study to examine neural underpinnings of the clinical phenotype
Authors: Roslyn Harold, Roma Kamat, Wei Siong Neo, Keisha Novak, Gianna Nossa, Humberto Monsivais, Phillip Durham, Xiaopeng Zhou, Ulrike Dydak, Elizabeth Berry-Kravis, Dan Foti, Bridgette L Kelleher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe FMR1 premutation is associated with a complex clinical phenotype, with increased risk for outcomes across the domains of psychological disorders, motor functioning, and reproductive health. A key gap is understanding intermediate processing that may help to explain how the FMR1 premutation alters brain functioning to confer increased risk across these domains. The current study begins to address this gap by focusing on two candidate, interrelated processes: cerebellar GABA and executive function. Data were collected from a cohort of 15 adult female FMR1 premutation carriers and a comparison group of 15 age-matched unaffected controls. Cerebellar GABA concentration was measured using edited magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MEGA-sLASER) at 3 T. Executive function was measured across two units of analysis: the error-related negativity, an EEG marker of automatic error detection, as well as performance-based measures from validated neuropsychological tests. Premutation carriers exhibited an atypical scalp topography of the error-related negativity. In controls but not in premutation carriers, error-related brain activity was associated with cerebellar GABA concentration and performance-based executive function. This pattern of preliminary findings suggests that the FMR1 premutation may alter the neural network involved in error monitoring. If these findings are confirmed in larger cohorts, they have potential implications for understanding the emergence of the broader clinical phenotype in adult FMR1 premutation carriers.Keywords: MR spectroscopy, FMR1 premutation, Executive function, Cerebellar GABA, Response monitoring, FXPAC

Introduction

The FMR1 premutation occurs in approximately 1 in 200 women (Hantash et al., 2011) and is defined as a 55–200 CGG repeat expansion on the promotor region of the FMR1 gene (where 6–54 repeats are considered normal and > 200 repeats directly causes Fragile X syndrome; Hagerman and Hagerman, 2021). FMR1 premutation carriers (PMC) were initially thought to be relatively unaffected by their CGG expansions (Hagerman and Hagerman, 2004), and so the scientific literature describing the phenotype and its mechanisms is still evolving. Below we summarize the extant literature on Fragile X Associated Conditions (FXPAC; Johnson et al., 2020) including patterns of psychiatric risk, a tremor-ataxia syndrome, and accelerated reproductive aging— then discuss possible neural, psychophysiological, and cognitive mechanisms which might explain these phenomena.

Regarding the psychiatric phenotype, PMC have been shown to exhibit elevated rates of anxiety and mood disorders relative to the general population (Bourgeois et al., 2011, Bourgeois et al., 2009; Cordeiro et al., 2015; Kenna et al., 2013; Movaghar et al., 2019). This has been repeatedly shown in male and female PMC, and these findings have been replicated in samples that account for factors which could reasonably otherwise explain these effects, such as by controlling for possible effects of caregiving stress by examining rates of mood and anxiety disorders in PMC who do not have children (Roberts et al., 2009; Schneider et al., 2016), by controlling for possible effects of illness progression by examining age-matched PMC with and without Fragile-X Associated Tremor-Ataxia Syndrome (FXTAS; Hessl et al., 2005), and controlling for ascertainment bias by sampling participants in a double-blind population-based manner (Movaghar et al., 2019). Further, PMC appear not to be characterized by globally increased risk for psychiatric problems; instead, elevated incidence is constrained to largely to mood disorders, anxiety disorders, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (Aishworiya et al., 2023; Bailey et al., 2008; Hagerman et al., 2018; Hunter et al., 2012; Roberts et al., 2009). This differential pattern of comorbidity suggests PMC are not characterized by globally increased risk for psychiatric disorders but instead may be characterized by unique pathophysiological processes conferred by the FMR1 gene.

In addition to these psychiatric concerns, PMC also present with a tremor-ataxia syndrome and ovarian problems. FXTAS is a neurodegenerative disorder that occurs in approximately 40% of PMC males and 16% of PMC females (Jacquemont et al., 2004; Rodriguez-Revenga et al., 2009), with prevalence varying by age, premutation length, and activation ratio (Hall and Jacquemont, 2010; Leehey et al., 2008; Seritan et al., 2016; Tassone et al., 2007). FXTAS is characterized by development of an intention tremor and subsequent cerebellar gait ataxia, (for a review, see Cabal-Herrera et al., 2020). In women, fragile X-associated primary ovarian insufficiency (FXPOI; Wittenberger et al., 2007) affects approximately 20% of female PMC. Impairments in reproductive health emerge as early as adolescence, and menopause occurs on average 5 years earlier than those without the premutation (De Caro et al., 2008; Murray et al., 2000).

Altogether, it is well established that the FMR1 premutation is associated with widespread clinical outcomes among carriers. It is widely thought that these outcomes are an indirect result of mRNA toxic effects caused by the CGG repeat expansion (Mila et al., 2018; Rosario et al., 2022). What remains unclear, however, are the processes through which this disrupts development and long-term functioning to ultimately result in clinical consequences. Two interrelated, candidate processes which may partially explain this complex phenotype are disruptions in (a) cerebellar functioning, particularly the role of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and (b) executive function.

Pre-clinical studies in premutation-analogue mice have observed upregulation of the cerebellar GABAergic system (D’Hulst et al., 2009; Wenzel et al., 2010). In humans, behavioral studies have extended upon these rodent models: a repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) study observed deficits in GABA-mediated cerebellar-motor functioning among PMC (Conde et al., 2013). Further, procedural learning and postural sway—behavioral outcomes known to be mediated or directly related to the cerebellum—are impaired in PMC relative to controls (Kraan et al., 2014a; Kraan et al., 2013). PMC show reduced functional connectivity between the cerebellum and extrastriate cortex during motor behavior, and this is correlated with motor problems (McKinney et al., 2020). Structural MRI studies in humans have also implicated the cerebellum (Brown and Stanfield, 2015), including those without FXTAS (Battistella et al., 2013; Famula et al., 2018; Loesch et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2017). Interestingly, cerebellar GABA dysfunction has similarly been implicated in depression and anxiety disorders (Chin and Augustine, 2023; Depping et al., 2018; Fatemi et al., 2013), though it has never been assessed how this may relate to psychological functioning among PMC.

Given the growing literature linking cerebellar changes to the premutation and associated symptomatic dysfunction, the preclinical and indirect human evidence pointing towards cerebellar GABA dysfunction, and broader literature linking disruptions in cerebellar GABA to internalizing symptoms, there is a need to investigate cerebellar GABA directly. One method of doing this is by using magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), which allows in vivo quantification of neurometabolites in the brain. In the current study, our goal was to directly test for the first time the concentration of cerebellar GABA in PMC relative to unaffected controls. Specifically, we measured resting-state GABA concentration within a voxel placed in the cerebellum, and we contrasted GABA levels among a cohort of adult female PMC and a comparison group of age-matched controls.

A second candidate process with significant clinical implications is executive function. PMC exhibit mild impairments in executive function, including impaired response inhibition (Cornish et al., 2015; Kraan et al., 2014b; Shelton et al., 2014) and reduced working memory capacity (Shelton et al., 2016; Shelton et al., 2015). This has been observed in both female and male PMC, and it has been proposed as an early biomarker of progression towards FXTAS (Famula et al., 2022; Grigsby et al., 2008; Hessl et al., 2024; Shelton et al., 2016).

In the current study, we assessed executive function using performance-based and neurophysiological measures. One clinically significant sub-process of executive function is error monitoring, which is involved in the dynamic adjustment of cognitive control to maintain task performance (Kerns et al., 2008). Error monitoring can be measured using the error-related negativity (ERN), an event-related potential that is elicited when individuals make errors on speeded reaction time tasks (Falkenstein et al., 1991). The ERN is automatically generated within the anterior cingulate cortex when errors occur, signaling preconscious error detection (O’Connell et al., 2007). Moreover, individual differences in ERN amplitude have been linked to psychiatric disorders: increased ERN amplitude is linked to anxiety and obsessive-compulsive disorders, whereas decreased ERN amplitude is linked to psychotic and externalizing disorders (Olvet and Hajcak, 2008; Weinberg et al., 2015).

Critically, in both clinical and non-clinical populations, ERN amplitude is directly related to performance-based measurements of executive function (Foti et al., 2021), situating it as potentially useful for linking the FMR1 premutation to manifest cognitive impairment—and potentially psychiatric vulnerability as well. ERN amplitude has yet to be examined, however, in relation to the FMR1 premutation. Here, we examined ERN amplitude for the first time among PMC, using a well-validated laboratory paradigm. Further, we considered differences in ERN amplitude alongside performance-based measures of executive function using well-validated neuropsychological tests.

In addition to this literature linking the ERN to manifest cognitive functioning, there is also evidence that the ERN is functionally connected to cerebellar GABA, with findings consistently showing that enhanced activity at GABAA receptors is associated with reductions in ERN amplitude (Jocham and Ullsperger, 2009). Further, an rTMS study demonstrated that reduced inhibitory output of the cerebellum directly reduced ERN amplitude during a response monitoring task (Berlijn et al., 2024). Evidence from other clinical populations has shown that cerebellar degeneration and cerebellar lesions result in decreased ERN amplitudes (Peterburs et al., 2015, Peterburs et al., 2012). Thus, in addition to considering cerebellar GABA and ERN amplitude as candidate process within the premutation, we also considered the functional association between these processes. Specifically, we examined the correlation between cerebellar GABA and ERN amplitude, calculated separately among PMC and unaffected controls.

Altogether, given the clinical characteristics of the FMR1 premutation, we aimed to assess two interrelated, candidate processes that may partially explain the clinical phenotype: cerebellar GABA and the ERN. As this was a preliminary study, the goal was to identify candidate associations to guide future clinical neuroscience research within the FMR1 premutation.

Materials and methods

Participants

Data were collected from 16 female PMC and 16 female controls with no personal or family history of Fragile X syndrome, autism spectrum disorder, or intellectual disability; these are the same participants as are described elsewhere (Harold et al., 2024).1 All participants self-identified as women. Two PMC participants were sisters, and three others were from the same family (sisters, daughter).

PMC participants were recruited regionally through online advertisements, support groups, and the National Fragile X Foundation. Control participants were recruited by posting flyers and through a university newsletter.

Groups were matched based upon age and education level; all participants were screened for a history of serious head injury, psychosis, neurological illness, and past-year substance use disorder. Participants who did not meet exclusion criteria were provided with informed written consent. Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at Purdue University, and all participants were compensated for their time.

Self-reported symptoms

Participants completed a battery of self-report questionnaires to assess for affective symptoms and functional impairment due to tremor. The Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21; Lovibond and Lovibond, 1995) inquiries about past-week symptoms of emotional distress. Each subscale (i.e., depression, anxiety, and stress) contains seven questions, each rated on a 0 (“did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applied to me very much or most of the time”) Likert-type scale. Items are summed and multiplied by two to calculate each sub-score. Thus, each subscale has a range of 0–42.

Participants additionally completed the Columbia University Assessment of Disability in Essential Tremor (CADET; Louis et al., 2000) scale. The CADET asks participants to report on the presence and degree of disability they experience in completing various activities of daily living (e.g., typing, drinking from a glass, using a spoon to drink soup, unlocking a door with a key, etc.). Each item asks participants to rate whether or not they a) have difficulty or disability, b) if they need to modify the way in which they complete the task, and c) if they have experienced any loss of efficiency in completing this task. Finally, participants were additionally asked to self-report on their lifetime history of medical diagnoses which allowed us to capture incidence of FXTAS diagnosis in our sample.

Genetic protocol

Blood samples were collected from PMC and sent to Rush University Medical Center (EBK lab) where FMR1 genotyping for CGG repeat length, FMR1 methylation PCR (mPCR), activation ratio (Hadd et al., 2016), and fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotien 1 (FMRP) measurement were done. Genotyping was performed with the AmplideX® FMR1 and FMR1 mPCR reagents (Asuragen, Inc.) and FMRP was assayed using the Luminex-based method (Lafauci et al., 2013).

Cognitive assessment

Cognitive functioning was assessed using two complementary measures. First, we used the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence-II (WASI-II; McCrimmon and Smith, 2013) to estimate cognitive abilities. The WASI-II is a brief, reliable, and normed measure of intelligence. We utilized the Vocabulary and Matrix Reasoning subtests, from which a reliable and valid estimate of Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient (IQ) can be calculated (Irby and Floyd, 2013). In the Vocabulary subtest, participants were asked to define words presented both auditorily and visually. In the Matrix Reasoning subtest, participants were presented with a pattern and asked to select from 5 multiple-choice options which option completed the sequence.

We additionally administered several modules of the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery (Hodes et al., 2013); here we discuss only the Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention (Flanker), the Dimensional Change Card Sort (DCCS), and the List Sorting Working Memory (LSWM) tests. On the Flanker task, participants are presented with a row of congruent (e.g., <<<<<) or incongruent (e.g., <<> < <) arrows. Participants are asked to quickly indicate the direction that the middle arrow is pointing. This is a measure of inhibitory control and attention, a key component of executive functioning. The DCCS requires participant to quickly match visually presented items either by shape or by color, wherein the matching rule pseudo-randomly changes. This is a measure of cognitive flexibility and attention, both of which are core components of executive functioning. Finally, the LSWM test asks participants to recall and reorder a list of objects which are presented visually and auditorily. During the first half of the test, they are presented with a list of objects belonging to a single category and asked to recite the objects back in size order from smallest to largest. The list of objects increases in length until the participant’s performance indicates that task difficulty has exceeded the participant’s ability. During the second half of the task, participants complete the same task again but this time objects belong to one of two categories and participants must order objects by category and by size. This test therefore indexes participants’ working memory ability, another key facet of executive function.

Neurophysiological assessment

The continuous electroencephalogram (EEG) was recorded from 32 Ag/AgCl active scalp electrodes with an actiCHAmp amplifier (Brain Products) while participants completed a behavioral task using Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc., Berkeley, CA). An arrows Flanker task containing 300 trials was used to elicit error related brain activity (Eriksen and Eriksen, 1974). On each trial, participants were presented with a row of 5 arrows that were either congruent or incongruent and asked to select the direction the middle arrow was pointing. The arrows were present on the screen for 200 ms, and participants had 1800 ms to respond. Between trials, a fixation cross (‘+”) was displayed in the center of the screen; this inter trial interval varied randomly between 500 and 1000 ms; trials were pseudo-randomized.

Offline data reduction and analysis of EEG data was performed in BrainVision Analyzer (Brain Products). EEG data were referenced to the average mastoid (TP9/TP10), filtered from 0.1 to 0.30 Hz, and segmented relative to stimulus onset (−400 to 800 ms). Data were corrected for blinks and eye movements (Gratton et al., 1983). Individual channels were rejected for artifacts if they had a step of 50 μV between samples, a 200 μV difference within 200-ms intervals, or less than 0.5 μV change within 100-ms intervals; additional artifacts were identified by visual inspection.

Segmented data was averaged separately for erroneous and correct trails; baseline correction was conducted relative to the pre-stimulus interval (−400 to −200 ms). The topography and waveforms of the difference between wins and losses was inspected separately for PMC and controls to determine which electrode sites to score error related brain activity. Error-related brain activity was maximal at the expected fronto-central sites in the control group. Unexpectedly, in the PMC group, error related brain activity exhibited a more parietal distribution. Therefore, we scored error-related brain activity at three midline electrodes—Fz, Cz, and Pz—as the average amplitude in microvolts between 0 and 100 ms.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy

MRS data were acquired on a 3 T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany), using a 64-channel head coil. Anatomical 3D T1-weighted images were acquired with an MP-RAGE sequence (Mugler and Brookeman, 1990) for placement of a 25x25x30mm3 voxel for MRS acquisition in the right lobe of the cerebellum (see Fig. 3).

MRS voxel placement and representative MEGA-sLASER spectrum. Panel (A) displays a consistency map of voxel placements overlaid on the MNI152 template demonstrating high reproducibility for the cerebellum MRS GABA acquisition. Panel (B) displays a representative GABA-edited spectrum.

Cerebellar GABA levels were assessed using an interleaved MEGA-sLASER sequence2 with an echo time (TE) of 68 ms and repetition time (TR) of 2000 ms. 55 Hz wide editing pulses were applied at 1.9 ppm (ON) and 7.48 ppm (OFF) in an interleaved fashion. 128 averages of each subspectra were taken with 2048 datapoints and a bandwidth of 2 kHz each. VAPOR water suppression (Tkáč et al., 1999) was used, and an 80-mm slice-selective saturation pulse was applied to suppress subcutaneous lipid adjacent to the VOI.

ON and OFF spectra were used to create difference spectra (ON-OFF) using the FID-A Toolbox (Simpson et al., 2017). GABA concentrations were calculated by quantifying the difference spectra in LCModel (V6.3-1M; Provencher, 2001). The spectra were analyzed between 0.2 and 4.0 ppm, with the baseline knot spacing control parameter (DKNTMN) set to 0.15 ppm. Eddy current correction was applied. Basis sets for LCModel were generated using a density matrix simulation with GABA coupling constants (Kaiser et al., 2007). Only GABA values with Cramer-Rao lower bounds ≤25% were used.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 29.0 for Mac IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, 2023). Descriptive statistics for all demographics and key variables were calculated using the raw data, except for the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery tasks, which are reported as uncorrected standard scores.

Aim 1 sought to understand if there are differences in error-related brain activity between PMC and controls. We tested this using a repeated measures ANOVA, which allowed for investigation of differences in amplitude and topography of the ERN. Accuracy (error or correct) and electrode (Fz, Cz, and Pz) were entered as within-subjects factors and group status (PMC or control) was entered as a between subjects factor. Aim 2 sought to investigate correlations between cognitive, neurophysiological, and spectroscopic units of analysis using Spearman’s rho. Following these correlational analyses, we followed up with Fischer’s R to Z transformation to assess if the strength of the correlations differed between groups.

Participant groups did not differ on current symptoms of depression, anxiety, or stress as measured by the DASS-21. Overall, in both PMC and controls, symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress largely fell in the “normal” to “mild” range. Similarly, there was no observed group difference in lifetime history of major depressive disorder.

Regarding executive functioning, PMC and controls performed similarly on three performance-based tests of executive functioning from the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery. Regarding cognitive ability, PMC and controls exhibited full-scale IQ scores that were not statistically different from one another. Controls presented with slightly, though significantly, higher verbal ability as measured by the WASI-II. Notably, this was attributable to the presence of two unusually high-performing control participants, not due to a deficit in verbal ability among PMC (i.e., PMC displayed ability comparable to the population mean). For completeness, we recalculated all key analyses without these two high-performing controls, but this did not substantively change the results. Thus, for ease of interpretation, we retain these high-performing controls in the results reported below.

Genotyping

Genetic analysis revealed that the PMC in the current study had between 79 and 108 CGG repeats on FMR1. Mean repeat length was 93.87 repeats (SD = 9.71, median = 96.00). The distribution of repeat lengths was roughly symmetrical (skewness = −0.239, SESkewness = 0.580).

Because there is evidence that phenotypic expression among PMC varies with repeat length (Maltman et al., 2021), we conducted linear correlations between repeat length and key study variables. No significant associations were observed between repeat length and executive function (average of Flanker, DCCS, LSWM: rho = 0.201, p = .473), ΔERN (rho = −0.150, p = .593), or cerebellar GABA (rho = −0.093, p = .735).

Tremor/ataxia symptoms

Participants did not report disability or difficulty on any of the activities of daily living measured on the CADET. One PMC reported that they modify how they administer eyedrops and another indicated that they modify how they thread a needle, but otherwise no modifications or loss of efficiency were reported for activities of daily living among PMC. Further, no participants self-reported a history of diagnosis with FXTAS or any other tremor-ataxia syndrome.

Error processing: ERN

The Flanker task during the EEG session elicited the expected behavioral effects, with similar performance across groups. Participants took significantly longer to respond to incongruent stimuli than to congruent stimuli (F1,28 = 214.41, p < .001, η2 = 0.884), and this effect was not moderated by group status (F1,28 = 1.12, p = .299, η2 = 0.038). Regarding accuracy, participants performed significantly worse on incongruent trials relative to congruent trials (F1,28 = 139.38, p < .001, η2 = 0.883) and this was also not moderated by group status (F1,28 = 2.71, p = .111, η2 = 0.088). This significant three-way interaction indicates that the topography of error-related brain activity differed between the PMC and control groups. In controls, the ERN exhibited the typical frontocentral distribution (Fig. 1, bottom), whereas in PMC the ERN had an atypical, parietal distribution. To further explore this interaction, we calculated the ERN difference score (error minus correct; ΔERN) at each electrode site, then contrasted the ΔERN between electrode Pz and electrode Fz (i. e., ΔERN at Pz minus ΔERN at Fz, or ΔΔERN). Finally, we conducted an independent samples t-test between PMC (mean ΔΔERN = −3.74 μV, SD = 3.45 μV) and controls (mean ΔΔERN = −0.26 μV, SD = 3.26 μV) and found that the ΔΔERN was significantly different between groups such that PMC demonstrated greater differentiation in error-related brain activity between frontal and parietal regions than did controls (t28 = −2.84, p = .008, d = −1.04). Among controls, each of the three tests of executive functioning were moderately to highly correlated with one another, and each of the three electrode sites at which ΔERN was quantified were highly correlated with one another. This was not the case among PMC, who exhibited only moderate (and nonsignificant) correlation between Flanker and DCCS performance, and minimal correlation between performance on these tests and LSWM. In assessing the association between units of analysis, we found that among controls, performance based executive functioning and ΔERN were moderately correlated (mean executive functioning and mean ΔERN: rho = −0.558, p = .031), as were mean ΔERN and cerebellar GABA (rho = 0.699, p = .011). These associations were not apparent in the PMC group, with observed effects that were trivial to small in size and not statistically significant (mean executive functioning and mean ΔERN: rho = −0.258, p = .354; ΔERN and cerebellar GABA: rho = 0.059, p = .840). To further understand the observed differences in strength of correlation between key study variables across groups, we calculated Fisher’s r-to-Z transformation for independent samples (Weiss, 2011). The observed strength of the correlation between performance-based executive functioning and error-related brain activity was not significantly different between PMC and controls, though was medium in its effect size (z = 0.99, p = .370, q = 0.366). The observed correlations between error-related brain activity and cerebellar GABA concentration were statistically different between groups, with a large effect size (z = 2.06, p = .040, q = −0.924).

Discussion

The current study examined two interrelated candidate processes that may partially explain the complex clinical phenotype of the FMR1 premutation: cerebellar GABA concentration and executive function. Extant indirect evidence has linked the FMR1 premutation to abnormal cerebellar GABA function, and so we measured cerebellar GABA concentration directly for the first time using MRS. Executive function was measured across two units of analysis: behavioral performance and error-related brain activity from the anterior cingulate cortex.

Contrary to our hypothesis, we found no difference in the concentration of cerebellar GABA between PMC and controls. Likewise, behavioral performance on executive functioning tasks were similar across groups. Error-related brain activity, however, was abnormal among PMC. Specifically, PMC exhibited a more parietal distribution of error-related brain activity, as compared with the expected frontocentral distribution observed among unaffected controls.

Further, we observed evidence of disruption between functional associations across these units of analysis. In controls, ERN amplitude was significantly correlated with performance-based measures of executive function, such that those with a larger (more negative) ERN performed better on three independent executive functioning tasks, consistent with prior research (Foti et al., 2021). The control group also exhibited a strong correlation between cerebellar GABA concentration and ERN amplitude, such that those with reduced cerebellar GABA exhibited a larger (more negative) ERN. We did not, however, observe either of these relationships among PMC. Instead, ERN amplitude and performance-based executive functioning were only weakly correlated with one another, and cerebellar GABA concentration was effectively unrelated to ERN amplitude.

These findings are notable for two reasons. First, the positive findings in controls demonstrate proof-of-concept for theorized—but rarely tested—associations between the ERN and cerebellar GABA concentration. Though a number of studies have indirectly linked decreased ERN amplitude to increased activity at GABAA receptors (Jocham and Ullsperger, 2009) and implicated the cerebellum as an important component of a brain network involved in error monitoring (Becerril and Barch, 2013; Peterburs et al., 2015), this is the first study to our knowledge that has directly linked EEG-measured error-related brain activity to MRS-measured cerebellar GABA concentration in humans. These findings are of interest to a broad array of scientific literatures which have sought to integrate across units of analysis to understand pathways to disease onset.

Further, the lack of analogous associations between cerebellar GABA concentration, ERN amplitude, and performance-based executive function among PMC are potentially informative. The ERN is a well-validated neural measure of executive function and so the lack of association between ERN and performance-based executive functioning, especially in the absence of observable deficits in executive functioning performance, suggests that PMC may be relying upon alternative brain networks to accomplish the same task demands. This is further supported by difference in the topography of the ERN. Moreover, this pattern of intact functioning but disrupted association stands in contrast with other clinical populations. For example, individuals with psychotic disorders exhibit profound impairment in both performance-based executive function and ERN amplitude—yet the association between these units of analysis remains intact (Foti et al., 2021).

In the current study, the topography of the ERN among PMC was noticeably parietal, in contrast to the well-established frontocentral distribution that is typically observed (Olvet and Hajcak, 2008). Notably, this parietal distribution of the ERN of PMC is visually similar to one prior study of individuals with cerebellar degenerative disease (Peterburs et al., 2015). In that study, patients exhibited a parietal distribution, and a blunted ERN amplitude was correlated with greater cerebellar degeneration. Among PMC, the cerebellum is greatly impacted by FXTAS (Brown and Stanfield, 2015; D’Hulst et al., 2009; Kraan et al., 2014a; Kraan et al., 2013; McKinney et al., 2020), and so it is possible that the observed topography reflects presymptomatic cerebellar dysfunction. However, given only a portion of PMC women go on to develop FXTAS (Chonchaiya et al., 2010; Coffey et al., 2008; Rodriguez-Revenga et al., 2013), this explanation may not fully account for the present results. Instead, this disruption may reflect a compensatory mechanism that is more broadly present among PMC. This mechanism may assist in maintaining relative functionality in the face of differences derived from mRNA toxicity and reduced levels of FMRP common to the PMC population as a whole. If this were the case, it would beg the question of what specific neural differences are occurring which necessitate a compensatory mechanism.

Thus, it will be of interest for future studies to test the degree to which this this parietal distribution of the ERN is present in the broader population of PMC, including at lower and higher repeat lengths and in males. It will also be of interest to understand if this topographical difference is present across the lifespan by sampling PMC children and adolescents. Future researchers may also wish to test this in much older cohorts where FXTAS is present in a portion of the sample; this would assist researchers in understanding if this distribution is most apparent among a subset of FXTAS-affected PMC. Moreover, longitudinal studies are needed to map the developmental trajectories of cerebellar GABA, the ERN, and functional outcomes—as well as associations between these units of analysis— over time.

These findings are also of interest in understanding the psychiatric phenotype of PMC, who present with depression and anxiety disorders at higher rates than the general population. It is possible that the differences in the ERN observed here may be of clinical interest in understanding the psychiatric profile of PMC, especially in combination with other neuroimaging methods which may together help us understand pathways to symptom onset that are specific to PMC. Given the relatively large scientific literature linking abnormal ERN amplitude to a range of psychiatric outcomes (though a limiting factor of the current study is a lack of measure of ADHD; Herrmann et al., 2010; Olvet et al., 2010; Olvet and Hajcak, 2008; Riesel et al., 2019), this ERP component may act as a functionally useful tool to bridge research in PMC with the general population to understand pathways of risk for psychological disorders.

The current study is characterized by several strengths and limitations. A notable strength is the incorporation of MRS and ERP methods, which are infrequently utilized together within the broader clinical neuroscience literature. A second strength is the incorporation of standardized, performance-based measures of executive function along with neurophysiological measures of executive function. This multi-method approach will be valuable for future studies to map pathways that can explain how the FMR1 premutation confers increased risk for physical and mental health outcomes.

On the other hand, the sample size of the current study was small, and not all observations are completely independent, as some of the PMC are related to one another. Thus, the findings reported here should be viewed as preliminary. It will be critical for future research to establish the robustness of these effects within larger samples. This small sample size is of further concern because the PMC participants in the current study were relatively constricted in their distribution of observed CGG repeat lengths. Though we did not intentionally sample PMC based upon repeat length, all participants fell in the mid-range of premutation repeat expansion. Therefore, it remains an open question if the effects reported here generalize to the premutation population at large. Further, this limited our ability to assess for non-linear associations between repeat length and functional outcomes. Previous researchers have reported on non-linear associations between repeat length and phenotypic characteristics (Allen et al., 2007; Klusek et al., 2018; Mínguez et al., 2009; Sullivan et al., 2005), but we were unable to adequately test for this in the current study.

Furthermore, though MRS is the only method which allows for quantification of neurotransmitters in specific brain regions, it is not without limitations. Due to the nature of editing by subtracting two large signals from each other to detect a tiny underlying signal, the MRS volume of interest must be rather large (18.75 ml), and the scan takes 8.5 min, making it vulnerable to motion. Furthermore, the “GABA” signal at 3.0 ppm, often labeled as “GABA+”, consists of GABA, homocarnesine and co-edited macromolecules. These macromolecules can contribute up to 50% of the signal (Gu et al., 2018). While a macromolecular-suppressed version of the MEGA editing sequence exists (Henry et al., 2001), it is even more motion-sensitive and has a 50% smaller signal for quantification; it was thus not chosen for this study to minimize data loss. In return, the use of the sLASER localization sequence increases the accuracy of the localization and minimizes loss of the GABA signal compared to the more commonly used MEGA-PRESS sequence.

Despite these limitations, the current study presents novel, preliminary findings regarding cerebellar GABA and executive function in women with the FMR1 premutation which may have potential implications for the broader clinical phenotype. PMC exhibited an atypical scalp topography of error-related brain activity, indicating that error monitoring may operate through a distinct neural network. Among unaffected controls, error-related brain activity was associated with both cerebellar GABA concentration and performance-based scores of executive function, but these effects were not apparent among PMC, suggesting disruptions across units of analysis. Cerebellar GABA and executive function are two promising, interrelated targets for future research to understand the complex clinical phenotype in individuals with the FMR1 premutation.
