Comparative Outcomes of Single- and Split-Fraction Low-Dose Radiation in Mycosis Fungoides
Authors: Sarah J Lange, Nicole Trepanowski, Ayushya Ajmani, Sarah M Gonzalez, Xiaoqing Xia, Frederick Lansigan, Joi B Carter, Lesley A Jarvis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLocalized radiation is a cornerstone therapy for early-stage mycosis fungoides (MF), with low-dose regimens achieving high response rates. However, the optimal fractionation schedule remains unclear. Single-fraction (8 Gy × 1) and split-fraction (4 Gy × 2) regimens are widely used, but comparative data are limited. Reducing treatment burden may be particularly important in rural settings, where travel distance can limit access.Methods and MaterialsThis study aims to compare lesion-level response, recurrence, and durability between 8 Gy × 1 and 4 Gy × 2 low-dose radiation regimens in MF. We performed a retrospective review of 136 lesions from 27 adults with biopsy-confirmed MF treated with localized electron beam radiation at a rural multidisciplinary cutaneous lymphoma clinic (2014-2025). Lesions were categorized as patch or plaque. The primary outcome was complete response (CR) at 6 months. Secondary outcomes included overall response rate, local recurrence, time to next therapy, and toxicity. Analyses included χ²/Fisher’s exact tests, Kaplan–Meier survival, Cox regression, and inverse-probability weighting.ResultsOf 136 lesions, 119 (87.5%) received 4 Gy × 2 and 17 (12.5%) received 8 Gy × 1. Six-month CR was 81.5% versus 82.4%, respectively (P = 1.0). Overall response rate was 98.3% versus 100%, respectively (P = 1.0). Six-month local recurrence occurred in 3.4% versus 11.8% of lesions (P = .17), and additional local treatment within 6 months was required in 12.6% versus 5.9% of lesions (P = .69). No grade ≥2 toxicities occurred. Median patient travel distance was 86.2 miles versus 71.0 miles (P = .069). Multivariable and inverse-probability weighting analyses showed no association between regimen and CR at 6 months.ConclusionsLimitations of this study include retrospective design and small single-fraction sample. Single-fraction 8 Gy was associated with similar short-term outcomes and excellent tolerability, supporting its use as an effective, logistically advantageous option, particularly in rural practice.

Introduction

Mycosis fungoides (MF) is the most common subtype of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), a T-cell malignancy.1 The clinical spectrum of MF spans from early-stage patches and plaques to more advanced tumors or erythroderma.2 Although early-stage MF often follows an indolent course, lesion burden significantly impairs patient quality of life through pruritus, pain, and psychosocial distress.1 Disease severity remains a central prognostic determinant, with recent data demonstrating that increasing body surface area involvement correlates with incremental declines in overall survival.3 Emerging prognostic tools, including the Cutaneous Lymphoma International Prognostic Index, further highlight the need for personalized, risk-adapted therapeutic approaches.4

Radiation therapy (RT) is an essential component of MF management due to the marked radiosensitivity of malignant T cells and the superficial distribution of lesions.4,5 Localized RT achieves complete response (CR) rates exceeding 90% in most series, and in early-stage MF, RT has been associated with improved overall survival through effective clearance of malignant T-cell clones.6, 7, 8 Historically, curative regimens delivered 30 to 36 Gy in multiple fractions, but modern data demonstrate that substantially lower doses, often 8 to 12 Gy total, can achieve comparable response rates with reduced toxicity.9,10 Low-dose RT may exert additional benefit through modulation of the tumor microenvironment, enhancing antitumor immune responses while minimizing collateral damage.10,11

Consensus guidelines from the International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group endorse a broad RT range for MF, from focal low-dose treatment to total skin electron beam therapy, allowing dose selection to be tailored to disease extent and prior treatment history.7 National trends demonstrate underutilization of low-dose regimens despite their established efficacy and safety, reflecting differences in institutional culture, resource availability, and access to multidisciplinary expertise.9

Two low-dose hypofractionated regimens, 4 Gy delivered in 2 fractions (4 Gy × 2) and 8 Gy delivered in a single fraction (8 Gy × 1), are widely used for localized MF lesions.10 Although both fall within International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group-endorsed ranges and are supported by strong clinical rationale, they differ meaningfully in logistical burden. Single-fraction RT eliminates an additional treatment visit, reducing travel time, time off work, and caregiver demands. These considerations are especially salient for patients living in rural or geographically isolated regions, for whom travel distance is a well-documented barrier to radiation access and adherence.12, 13, 14, 15, 16

Despite the widespread use of both regimens, no head-to-head comparative data exist evaluating whether 8 Gy × 1 achieves clinical outcomes equivalent to the traditional 4 Gy × 2 schedule. Existing literature focuses on low-dose RT broadly but does not differentiate between these 2 clinically important fractionation approaches.10 This evidence gap limits the development of guidelines that prioritize both clinical efficacy and the patient-centered goal of minimizing treatment burden.

To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective analysis comparing lesion-level outcomes of 4 Gy × 2 versus 8 Gy × 1 in patients with localized MF treated at a rural multidisciplinary cutaneous lymphoma clinic. We hypothesized that the single-fraction regimen would demonstrate comparable clinical response and local control to the 2-fraction regimen while offering meaningful logistical advantages for patients requiring long travel distances.

Methods and Materials

This single-center, retrospective cohort study evaluated the clinical efficacy and tolerability of 2 localized RT regimens used for biopsy-proven MF lesions. Data were obtained through structured chart abstraction from the institutional electronic medical record (Epic) and the United States Cutaneous Lymphoma Consortium (USCLC) Answers Ahead registry.

Study population

Adult patients (≥18 years) with histologically confirmed MF who received localized electron beam therapy at a rural multidisciplinary cutaneous lymphoma clinic between January 1, 2014, and November 30, 2025, were eligible. Inclusion criteria included documentation of (1) radiation dose and treatment site and (2) at least 6 months of clinical follow-up following localized RT. Patients treated primarily with total skin electron beam therapy or lacking treatment or follow-up documentation were excluded.

Radiation regimens and lesion characterization

All lesions were treated with localized electron beam therapy using one of 2 predefined hypofractionated schedules: ultra-low-dose hypofractionation 4 Gy × 2 fractions for a total of 8 Gy (n = 119 lesions from 24 patients) or single-fraction 8 Gy × 1 for a total of 8 Gy (n = 17 lesions from 5 patients). Two patients contributed lesions treated with both fractionation schedules at separate anatomic sites. Single-fraction 8 Gy × 1 for localized MF was adopted at our institution beginning March 2024, whereas 4 Gy × 2 had been used consistently since 2014, reflecting a recent institutional shift toward simplified hypofractionation. Treatment planning followed institutional standards for superficial electron beam therapy. Bolus application was determined by the treating radiation oncologist based on lesion thickness and anatomic location to optimize surface dose. The lesions included in this study were localized and did not require treatment of complex target geometries that would typically warrant conformal photon techniques such as volumetric modulated arc therapy.

Lesions were characterized by morphology (patch, plaque, tumor) and anatomic location (head/neck, trunk, upper extremities, lower extremities, groin/genitals) based on documentation by the treating dermatologist or radiation oncologist. Tumor-stage lesions were not included in this cohort to ensure a homogeneous cohort of indolent morphologies where low-dose radiation is the standard of care. Lesion dimensions were not consistently documented in the medical record and therefore could not be reliably included in the analysis.

Baseline patient- and lesion-level variables included age at RT initiation, sex, race and ethnicity, clinical stage at RT initiation (IA vs IB), insurance status, distance from residence to treatment center, lesion morphology, anatomic site, total lesion count per patient, immunosuppression status, and concomitant MF-directed therapies at the time of RT (skin-directed, phototherapy, systemic therapy, or none).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the CR, defined as complete clinical clearance of the lesion as documented in the electronic medical record within 6 months of initial treatment. Secondary outcomes included: (1) overall response rate (CR + partial response [PR]), (2) local recurrence at the treated site within 6 months, (3) time to next MF-directed treatment (additional radiation treatment or otherwise) within 6 months, and (4) documented acute or late toxicity.

Clinical response was classified as CR, PR, no response, or progressive disease based on consensus standardized assessment recommendations from the International Society for Cutaneous Lymphomas, the USCLC, and the Cutaneous Lymphoma Task Force of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer.5 All analyses were performed at the lesion level. Response assessments (CR/PR/no response/progressive disease) were made during routine follow-up by a board-certified dermatologist or radiation oncologist. When documentation was unclear, 2 treating physicians performed a consensus classification. Recurrence was accepted based on clinical assessment without requiring biopsy confirmation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline patient and lesion characteristics. Continuous variables (eg, age at RT initiation, distance to treatment center, and follow-up duration) were summarized using median and ranges, given non-normal distributions and small subgroup sizes. Categorical variables (eg, sex, stage, lesion type, comorbidities) were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The 2 treatment groups (4 Gy × 2 vs 8 Gy × 1) were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables (Table 1).

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and conducted under the existing USCLC National Cutaneous Lymphoma Registry protocol. All data were deidentified prior to analysis.

Results

Cohort and lesion characteristics

A total of 136 MF lesions from 27 patients were included, of which 119 lesions from 24 patients (87.5%) were treated with 4 Gy × 2 and 17 lesions (12.5%) from 5 patients with 8 Gy × 1 (Table 1). Patients receiving single-fraction radiation were significantly older at the time of RT initiation (median 86.0 for 8 Gy × 1 vs 70.0 years for 4 Gy × 2, P = .003). Sex distribution, disease stage, insurance status, lesion type, anatomic site, immunosuppression status, and distance traveled for treatment did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1).

All lesions treated with 8 Gy × 1 occurred in patients receiving no concomitant therapy, whereas a substantial proportion of lesions treated with 4 Gy × 2 were managed concurrently with skin-directed therapy (16.8%), phototherapy (10.1%), or systemic therapy (31.0%). Median follow-up duration differed markedly between regimens (357 days for 8 Gy × 1 vs 1190 days for 4 Gy × 2, P < .0001); therefore, all clinical outcomes were assessed within a standardized 6-month window following RT to mitigate follow-up–related confounding.

Lesion-level response within 6 months

Overall response rates at 6 months were high for both regimens (98.3% for 4 Gy × 2 vs 100% for 8 Gy × 1; P = 1.0) (Table 5). Complete response at 6 months was achieved in 97 of 119 lesions (81.5%) treated with 4 Gy × 2 and 14 of 17 lesions (82.4%) treated with 8 Gy × 1 (P = 1). Local recurrence within 6 months occurred in 4 of 119 lesions (3.4%) in the 4 Gy × 2 group (lower extremities, n = 2; head/neck, n = 2) and 2 of 17 lesions (11.8%) in the 8 Gy × 1 group (lower extremity, n = 1; trunk/back, n = 1) (P = .17). The need for any additional treatment within 6 months was uncommon in both groups (12.6% for 4 Gy × 2 vs 5.9% for 8 Gy × 1; P = .69). Among lesions requiring retreatment, the median time to additional therapy was 3 months following initial RT. Treatment was well tolerated in both groups, with mild (grade 1) adverse effects documented in 8 of 119 lesions (6.7%) treated with 4 Gy × 2 and 1 of 17 lesions (5.9%) treated with 8 Gy × 1. Reported adverse effects were mild and infrequent, consisting primarily of transient discomfort (n = 3), alopecia (n = 3), fatigue (n = 1), and skin dryness (n = 1) for those treated with 4 Gy × 2 and only discomfort (n = 1) for those treated with 8 Gy × 1 (Table 5). No grade ≥2 toxicities were observed.

Logistic regression analyses

In multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, stage, lesion type, anatomic site, immunosuppression, distance to treatment, and concomitant therapies, radiation regimen was not significantly associated with CR at 6 months (OR 0.27, 95% CI, 0.05-1.37; P = .12) (Table E1). Increasing age was independently associated with higher odds of CR, while no other covariates demonstrated statistically significant associations (Table E1).

Exploratory IPW analyses yielded similar estimates after accounting for baseline imbalances (OR 0.75, 95% CI, 0.14-3.92; P = .72), although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size (Table 4).

Time-to-event analyses: time to local recurrence and time to next treatment

Kaplan–Meier analyses comparing time to local recurrence and time to next treatment are shown in Fig. 1. No statistically significant difference in time to local recurrence was observed between regimens (log-rank P = .062). Local recurrence events were rare, particularly in the 8 Gy × 1 cohort, resulting in unstable estimates in adjusted Cox models (Table 2). Time to next treatment was similarly limited by sparse events, yielding extreme hazard ratios in multivariable Cox regression (Table 3). These time-to-event analyses are therefore considered exploratory and were not used to infer comparative efficacy between regimens.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of localized RT for MF, we found that single-fraction 8 Gy × 1 and split-fraction 4 Gy × 2 regimens were associated with similar short-term clinical outcomes when assessed within a standardized 6-month window. CR, overall response, local recurrence, and need for subsequent treatment within 6 months did not differ significantly between regimens. Treatment was well tolerated, with low rates of mild adverse effects (6.7% vs 5.9%) and no grade ≥2 toxicities observed.

Clinical interpretation

Patients treated with single-fraction radiation were significantly older than those receiving split-fraction therapy, consistent with clinical selection of less burdensome regimens for older adults. Although comorbidity and functional status were not systematically captured, advanced age often correlates with reduced mobility, increased transportation challenges, and prioritization of treatment convenience. This pattern aligns with prior radiation oncology literature demonstrating that travel burden influences treatment selection, with longer distances associated with reduced receipt of guideline-concordant RT and increased use of hypofractionated schedules.15, 16, 17 Within the rural catchment area served by our institution, these considerations likely contributed to preferential use of single-fraction RT in older patients.13,14

Importantly, after harmonizing outcome assessment to a fixed 6-month follow-up period and adjusting for demographic, disease-related, and treatment-related covariates, radiation fractionation was not independently associated with CR. Multivariable logistic regression and IPW analyses yielded consistent findings after adjustment for measured confounders. Although this study was not powered for formal noninferiority testing, the observed short-term clinical outcomes were similar between regimens.

The radiosensitivity of MF is well established, with durable responses reported across a wide range of low-dose and very-low-dose regimens.10,18 Prior studies in CTCL and indolent lymphomas have demonstrated effective local control with doses as low as 4 to 8 Gy, supporting the biological rationale for simplified fractionation strategies.18 In this context, the comparable 6-month outcomes observed in our cohort reinforce the feasibility of single-fraction RT as an effective skin-directed therapy for selected patients. Notably, all lesions treated with single-fraction 8 Gy × 1 were managed without concomitant MF-directed therapy, whereas many lesions in the 4 Gy × 2 cohort received concurrent skin-directed phototherapy, or systemic treatments, suggesting that comparable outcomes with 8 Gy × 1 were achieved despite its use as monotherapy and may reflect the intrinsic efficacy of the single-fraction regimen.

Time-to-event analyses comparing recurrence and time to next treatment were exploratory and limited by sparse events in the single-fraction cohort, resulting in unstable Cox regression estimates. Although the observed recurrence rate was numerically higher in the single-fraction cohort, the absolute number of recurrence events was small and CIs were wide. Accordingly, these analyses were interpreted descriptively and were not used to infer comparative durability or local control between regimens. These findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Implications for practice

MF is a chronic, relapsing disease that often necessitates repeated courses of skin-directed therapy over many years. In this context, the ability to achieve comparable short-term control with fewer treatment visits has important implications for patient-centered care. Single-fraction RT reduces clinic visits, travel time, and caregiver burden while minimizing treatment fractions and preserving tissue tolerance for potential future reirradiation, which is a critical consideration in a disease characterized by recurrent localized lesions.7,19 These advantages are particularly relevant for older adults, patients with mobility limitations, and individuals traveling long distances for specialized care.7,20 Given the absence of observed differences in short-term efficacy or toxicity, fractionation decisions should incorporate patient preference, logistical burden, and anticipated need for future radiation, rather than efficacy alone.

Single-fraction RT may be particularly appropriate in several clinical scenarios: (1) older adults or those with medical frailty, (2) patients facing substantial transportation or logistical barriers, (3) individuals requiring rapid palliation of symptomatic lesions, and (4) patients likely to need future reirradiation due to chronic or recurrent disease. By combining effectiveness, convenience, and tissue preservation, single-fraction RT offers a practical, patient-centered approach aligned with contemporary trends in low-dose, repeatable RT.

Rural cancer care and travel burden

RT access remains disproportionately limited for rural populations, who travel substantially farther for cancer care and frequently face cross-state travel for specialized services.12,13 In our cohort, the median travel distance was 86 miles. This trend is mirrored in the literature, where patients have been reported to travel more than 7 hours per visit,13 and many must cross state lines for treatment.14 Increased travel distance has been consistently associated with reduced likelihood of receiving recommended RT.15,16 Hypofractionated regimens, including single-fraction schedules, have therefore emerged as important strategies to mitigate access barriers and improve treatment adherence.16,17,21

Beyond individual patient convenience, single-fraction RT has systems-level relevance for rural oncology delivery. Shorter regimens reduce demand on linear accelerator time and staffing resources, potentially expanding access in regions with limited radiation oncology capacity. As health systems increasingly emphasize value-based care, treatment approaches that maintain clinical effectiveness while reducing resource utilization are particularly attractive.21 Our findings support the role of single-fraction RT as a pragmatic, access-conscious option for localized MF in rural settings.

Cost, quality of life, and patient-centered care

Hypofractionated RT has been associated with lower insurer-paid expenditures, reduced patient out-of-pocket costs, and decreased health-system resource utilization across multiple malignancies.17,21,22 Fewer visits also reduce out-of-pocket costs, lost wages, and ancillary expenses for patients, a consideration particularly relevant in chronic, relapsing diseases like MF, where repeated treatments are common. By minimizing cumulative dose exposure and treatment burden, single-fraction RT offers a cost-conscious strategy that aligns with long-term disease management.23,24

CTCL imposes substantial symptom burden and negatively affects quality of life across physical, emotional, and social domains.25,26 Treatment satisfaction is influenced not only by disease control but also by the logistical and financial demands of care.3 When 2 regimens demonstrate similar short-term efficacy, the option that minimizes disruption to daily life, reduces financial toxicity, and lessens caregiver burden more closely aligns with patient-centered care principles.25,26

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the small number of patients and lesions treated with single-fraction radiation limits statistical power, reduces the precision of effect estimates, and precludes formal noninferiority testing. Accordingly, the absence of statistically significant differences should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent efficacy; rather, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Second, the retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias and residual confounding. Although multivariable adjustment and IPW were performed, unmeasured factors—including comorbidity, functional status, treatment intent, and clinician preference—may have influenced treatment selection.

Third, analyses were conducted at the lesion level, and multiple lesions from the same patient were included. Consequently, observations were not fully independent, and within-patient correlation may have resulted in underestimated variance. Because only 5 patients received single-fraction radiation, patient-level hierarchical modeling was not considered statistically reliable.

Finally, follow-up was shorter in the single-fraction cohort, limiting assessment of long-term durability despite the use of a standardized 6-month outcome window. Time-to-event analyses were further limited by sparse recurrence events and should be interpreted descriptively. Lesion size was not consistently documented, toxicity relied on routine clinical documentation, and this single-center rural cohort may limit generalizability to other populations and practice settings.

Conclusion

In this rural single-center cohort of patients with localized MF, single-fraction 8 Gy × 1 radiation was associated with similar short-term response rates and disease control within 6 months compared with the commonly used 4 Gy × 2 regimen, with minimal toxicity. Although this study was not powered to establish noninferiority, the absence of observed differences in short-term outcomes and the logistical advantages of a single-visit approach suggest that single-fraction RT is a practical and patient-centered option for selected patients, particularly older adults and those facing significant travel burden. Future studies should include larger prospective multicenter cohorts to evaluate long-term disease control, patient-reported outcomes, and cost-effectiveness. Incorporating quality-of-life and travel-related measures will help clarify the patient-centered benefits of single-fraction RT, particularly in rural populations. As oncology care increasingly emphasizes accessibility, value, and patient experience, single-fraction radiation represents a feasible strategy to reduce treatment barriers while maintaining effective skin-directed therapy for CTCL in rural and underserved populations.
