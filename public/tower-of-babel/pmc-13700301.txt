Comparative detection of diabetic retinopathy microvascular lesions using single-capture ultrawide-field swept-source OCT angiography versus widefield colour fundus photography

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo compare the detection and quantification of intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMAs) and neovascularization (NV) between widefield colour fundus photography (WF CFP) and single-capture ultrawide-field swept-source OCT angiography (UWF SS-OCTA) across diabetic retinopathy (DR) severity stages, and to evaluate whether paired multimodal review enhances lesion detection and disease classification.MethodsEyes of different DR stages were imaged using 133° WF CFP and 150° UWF OCTA imaging modalities. Images were independently graded by masked, trained graders for the presence and quantification of NVs and IRMAs. Paired multimodal review was then performed bidirectionally for WF CFP and UWF OCTA to assess whether lesion detection could be improved by cross-referencing the corresponding image from the other modality. Intergrader agreement and inter-modality agreement during independent and paired multimodal review were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).ResultsA total of 120 eyes were analysed (NPDR: 64.6%; PDR: 35.4%). Under independent grading, UWF OCTA detected significantly more lesions than WF CFP across all DR stages (all p < 0.001), with poor inter-modality agreement. Paired multimodal review significantly increased WF CFP lesion counts across all groups (all p < 0.001), while UWF OCTA counts remained unchanged or showed no significant increase. Inter-modality agreement improved after paired review (mild–moderate NPDR IRMA ICC: 0.219; severe NPDR IRMA ICC: 0.334; PDR NV ICC: 0.726), however, UWF OCTA continued to detect significantly more lesions (all p < 0.001). Inter-grader agreement for both NV and IRMA grading was higher for UWF OCTA than WF CFP (NV ICC: 0.974 vs. 0.694; IRMA ICC: 0.635 vs. 0.420). In an exploratory finding, 12 eyes (10%) were reclassified from NPDR to PDR following multimodal review.ConclusionsSingle-capture UWF OCTA detects significantly more IRMAs and NV than WF CFP, with superior reproducibility. Multimodal review enhances lesion recognition on WF CFP but does not eliminate inter-modality discrepancies. These findings highlight the potential of UWF OCTA to refine DR staging and influence clinical decision-making, supporting its complementary role alongside fundus photography in specialised diabetic eye care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12886-026-05158-0.

Background

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of preventable vision loss worldwide, affecting an estimated 103 million individuals in 2020 with projections rising to 160 million by 2045 [1]. DR, a progressive microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), spans a continuum from non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) [2], and accurate lesion identification directly informs clinical management, surveillance intervals and the risk of vision-threatening complications [2].

Neovascularization (NV) defines the transition from severe NPDR to PDR, whereas intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMAs) distinguish earlier from advanced NPDR [2, 3]. Accurate detection of both lesions is central to disease staging and therapeutic decision-making [2, 3]. Untreated NV predisposes to vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment and irreversible vision loss [2]. IRMAs, arising adjacent to areas of capillary non-perfusion, reflect retinal ischaemia and progression risk [4], with up to 52% of eyes with severe NPDR progressing to PDR within one year [5].

Colour Fundus Photography (CFP) remains the clinical reference standard for DR grading, underpinning the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) and Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale (DRSS) classification systems [6]. Widefield (WF) platforms now enable single-capture visualisation of up to 133° of the retina, improving peripheral lesion detection [7]. Notably, predominantly peripheral lesions are associated with increased risk of DR progression [8, 9].

However, WF CFP is a two-dimensional modality reliant on surface morphological features, limiting its ability to reliably distinguish IRMAs from NVs and rendering it susceptible to degradation from media opacity [3]. These limitations introduce both perceptual and intrinsic constraints on lesion detection and grading reproducibility. In contrast, ultrawide-field swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (UWF SS-OCTA) provides depth-resolved, flow-based visualisation of retinal vasculature, enabling objective differentiation between intraretinal and preretinal vascular abnormalities [10, 11], with single-capture coverage now approaching the field of view of WF CFP, facilitating direct modality comparisons.

Despite these advances, several key gaps remain. Prior comparative studies often relied on montaged OCTA acquisitions, limited field matching, or qualitative assessment, with few adopting a quantitative, lesion-level approach across the full spectrum of DR severity. Moreover, the extent to which multimodal integration improves lesion recognition and alters disease classification also remains incompletely understood.

The present study compares the detection and quantification of IRMAs and NVs using single-capture 150° UWF SS-OCTA (29 × 24 mm) and 133° WF CFP across all DR severity stages, and evaluates whether a paired multimodal review approach enhances lesion detection and refines disease classification.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary referral eye care centre in Singapore between July 2024 and December 2025. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (2024–3667). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants and data collection

Patients aged ≥ 18 years with type 1 or type 2 DM were prospectively recruited from the outpatient eye clinic. Inclusion required a diagnosis of DR, confirmed on WF CFP and graded according to the International Clinical DRSS.

All patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination. Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, lens status and best correct visual acuity, were extracted from the electronic medical records.

Image acquisition and data preparation

Widefield colour fundus photography (WF CFP)

WF CFP images were acquired using the Clarus 500 system (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). A single 133° field-of-view image was obtained per eye at a lateral resolution of 7.3 μm.

Ultrawide-field swept source optical coherence tomography angiography (UWF SS-OCTA)

UWF SS-OCTA imaging was performed using a 400 kHz device (VG200; Intalight Imaging, China) at a central wavelength of 1050 nm. Fovea-centred scans were acquired using a 29 × 24 mm protocol (approximately 150° field of view).

Each scan consisted of 1536 A-scans per B-scan with 1240 B-scan positions, yielding an approximate lateral resolution of 19 μm and an axial imaging depth of 9.0 mm. Automated segmentation of retinal layers was performed using the device software, with manual correction where necessary. All imaging was performed by trained photographers, and repeat acquisitions were undertaken for optimal image quality.

Lesion definition and annotation

WF CFP-based definitions

IRMAs were defined as tortuous intraretinal vascular segments of varying calibre without evidence of retinal surface breach. NV was defined as abnormal vascular proliferation extending onto the retinal surface or into the vitreous cavity, per ETDRS photographic grading criteria [5].

UWF OCTA-based definitions

For OCTA analysis, the following slabs were used:

All-retinal layer (ARL): from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).Vitreoretinal interface (VRI): 200 μm above to 30 μm below the ILM.

Vitreoretinal interface (VRI): 200 μm above to 30 μm below the ILM.

IRMAs were defined as dilated, tortuous intraretinal vessels without ILM breach, typically adjacent to areas of non-perfusion, confirmed on co-registered structural B-scans with flow overlay. IRMAs were further classified into five morphological subtypes (Fig. 1) based on previously published OCTA grading frameworks [12, 13].

All images were independently graded by two trained graders (Y.A. and P.P.) following a standardised pre-study calibration session. Graders were masked to clinical data and findings from the alternate imaging modality.

Paired multimodal review

Following independent grading, a paired multimodal review was conducted bidirectionally for each eye. Annotated WF CFP and UWF OCTA images were reviewed side by side after spatial alignment. Lesions on one modality served as localisation references for targeted re-inspection of the paired modality. Additional lesions were annotated only if, upon re-evaluation, a corresponding lesion could be directly visualised on that modality and not transferred solely based on the paired image. Intergrader and inter-modality agreement were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.0), with statistical significance defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. Comparisons of lesion counts (IRMAs and NV) between imaging modalities (WF CFP versus UWF OCTA) were conducted using a generalised linear mixed-effects model (GLMMs) with a Poisson distribution (lme4 package), with eye nested within patient as a random effect and fixed effects for imaging modality and DR severity group; estimated marginal means were used for pairwise comparisons. A separate multivariate GLMM evaluated associations between baseline demographic variables and lesion counts, adjusting for potential confounders including age and lens status (phakic, cataractous or pseudophakic). Count-based modelling was selected over binary outcomes to preserve lesion-level information and enhance sensitivity for inter-modality comparisons.

Intergrader agreement and inter-modality agreement were quantified using ICC with the iccCounts package (two-way random-effects Poisson mixed model), reported with 95% confidence intervals. ICC was benchmarked as per Koo et al.: <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, > 0.90 = excellent [15]. Bland–Altman analysis assessed systematic bias, with mean differences and 95% limits of agreement (LOA). Given the predefined primary comparisons, adjustment for multiple testing was not performed.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 142 eyes with both WF CFP and UWF OCTA images were initially enrolled, of which 22 eyes (15.5%) were excluded due to poor image quality. The final analysis included 120 eyes from 82 patients (51 males, 31 females), with 64.6% classified as NPDR and 35.4% as PDR. The mean baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.256 (SD 0.177) logMAR.

Independent lesion grading

Under independent masked grading, UWF OCTA detected significantly more lesions than WF CFP across all DR severity groups (all p < 0.001), with consistently poor inter-modality agreement.

In mild-to-moderate NPDR, IRMAs were detected on UWF OCTA but not on WF CFP (mean 3.22 vs. 0; p < 0.001; ICC 0.092, 95% CI 0-0.270). In severe NPDR, IRMA counts remained significantly higher on UWF OCTA than WF CFP (7.24 vs. 2.06, p < 0.001; ICC 0.146, 95% CI 0.035–0.255). Similarly, in PDR, UWF OCTA identified significantly more NV lesions compared to WF CFP (5.88 vs. 1.29, p < 0.001; ICC 0.188, 95% CI 0.060–0.309) (Table 2).

Paired multimodal lesion grading

Paired multimodal review increased lesion detection on WF CFP across all DR severity groups (all p < 0.001), while UWF OCTA counts showed minimal or non-significant changes.

For IRMA detection, the mean WF CFP counts increased from 0 to 0.689 in mild-to-moderate NPDR (p < 0.001) and from 2.06 to 3.65 in severe NPDR (both p < 0.001). In contrast, UWF OCTA IRMA counts remained unchanged in mild-to-moderate NPDR and showed only a small, non-significant increase in severe NPDR (7.24 to 7.76; p = 0.421). Inter-modality agreement improved modestly following paired review with ICC 0.219 (95% CI, 0.059–0.368) in mild-to-moderate NPDR and 0.334 (95% CI, 0.123–0.516) in severe NPDR, although UWF OCTA continued to detect significantly more lesions than WF CFP in both groups (both p < 0.001).

For NV detection in PDR, WF CFP counts increased significantly from 1.29 to 4.10 following paired review (p < 0.001), whereas UWF OCTA counts showed no significant change (5.88 to 6.00; p = 0.821). Inter-modality agreement for NV was higher following paired review (ICC, 0.726; 95% CI, 0.466–0.870), yet UWF OCTA remained superior in lesion detection as compared to WF CFP (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Multivariate regression analysis demonstrated that differences in lesion counts between WF CFP and UWF OCTA were not significantly associated with age, lens status or BCVA, under either independent or paired multimodal grading. [Supplementary Tables 1–2]

Intergrader agreement of lesions on UWF OCTA versus WF CFP

Intergrader agreement for lesion quantification was consistently higher with UWF OCTA compared to WF CFP. For NV detection, ICC was 0.974 (95% CI, 0.951–0.986) on UWF OCTA versus 0.694 (95% CI, 0.442–0.835) on WF CFP. For IRMA detection, ICC was 0.635 (95% CI, 0.435–0.766) on UWF OCTA compared to 0.420 (95% CI, 0.200-0.595) on WF CFP. Bland-Altman analyses demonstrated reduced variability with UWF OCTA. Case illustration of the grading process

The following cases illustrate the grading workflow, in which independent masked grading was followed by paired multimodal review, leading to the recognition of additional lesions on WF CFP across different DR severity stages.

Cases 1 and 2 (Fig. 3a–f) illustrate how OCTA-guided review can improve IRMA recognition on WF CFP. In Case 1, a left eye graded as mild NPDR on WF CFP showed no IRMA on initial grading; UWF OCTA, however, revealed an IRMA lesion near the inferior arcade, which - once used as a reference during multimodal review - allowed a subtle IRMA to be identified in the same location on WF CFP. Case 2 demonstrates this effect at greater scale: in a right eye graded as severe NPDR, independent WF CFP grading identified several IRMA lesions, while independent UWF OCTA grading on the en face ARL slab revealed a greater number of IRMA distributed more widely across the retina. Paired multimodal review subsequently enabled recognition of additional subtle IRMAs on WF CFP, yielding higher lesion counts than on initial grading alone.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that single-capture UWF OCTA detects substantially more microvascular lesions than WF CFP across all stages of DR, with superior intergrader reproducibility. Importantly, the use of a paired multimodal review design provides novel insight into the nature of missed lesions, distinguishing those intrinsically undetectable on WF CFP from those perceptually overlooked without OCTA-guided spatial referencing. In addition, by quantifying lesion counts rather than relying on binary presence–absence assessments, this study offers a more granular evaluation of lesion burden and inter-modality differences [16].

Prior comparative studies have frequently relied on montaged OCTA acquisitions to achieve widefield coverage, such as five overlapping 12 × 12 mm scans or composite 15 × 15 mm acquisitions [17, 18]. Though effective, these approaches are time-intensive and artefact-prone, limiting routine clinical applicability [11]. In contrast, the present study reflects real-world workflow by evaluating single-capture 150 degree (29 × 24 mm) UWF OCTA against 133 degree WF CFP, enabling direct and clinically relevant comparison between modalities. Furthermore, while previous studies have focused largely on qualitative detection or limited field matching, our approach provides lesion-level quantification across the full spectrum of DR severity.

Other studies have evaluated single-capture UWF OCTA against UWF Fundus Fluorescein Angiography (FFA) or UWF-CFP using image registration within matched retinal fields [17]. In contrast, our study was designed to match our clinical workflow by focusing on the practical utility of single-capture 29 × 24 mm SS-OCTA as a non-invasive modality complementary to standard fundus photography. Clarus 500 CFP was selected due to its comparable 133° field of view and its role as the standard imaging modality in our setting. FFA was not routinely performed, given its invasive and time consuming nature [19].

Within this framework, an important observation was the differential impact of media opacity on image interpretability [20]. Most excluded cases were attributable to ungradable WF CFP due to media opacity. Notably, in a subset of these eyes, UWF OCTA remained interpretable. In some, UWF OCTA not only detected NV on the VRI slab, with B-scan confirmation of ILM breach, but also allowed direct visualisation of the underlying retinal non-perfusion, findings that altered clinical management in these cases.

The superior structural resolution of UWF OCTA translated into more consistent lesion identification across graders, with substantially higher intergrader agreement for NV and substantially higher agreement for IRMA compared with WF CFP. This aligns with Menean et al., who reported a similar disparity, suggesting inferior CFP reproducibility reflects a modality limitation rather than grading variability [21]. CFP’s reliance on two-dimensional morphological features - vascular calibre, loop formation and abnormal branching patterns - limits structural differentiation between NV and IRMA [22], whereas UWF OCTA enables objective lesion identification through depth-resolved B-scan, facilitating reproducible grading independent of observer experience [16, 22].

In PDR eyes, UWF OCTA detected approximately four times more NV lesions than WF CFP under independent masked grading, with poor inter-modality agreement, and paired multimodal review reclassified 12 eyes (10%) from NPDR to PDR. Tsuboi et al. and You et al. similarly identified subclinical NV on OCTA in eyes graded as NPDR on CFP across a range of severity groups, supporting its utility as a non-invasive screen for subclinical proliferative disease [23, 24]. This is clinically consequential, as the NPDR-to-PDR transition represents the threshold for PRP and anti-VEGF intervention [25, 26]. UWF OCTA may therefore be particularly valuable when management hinges on accurate identification of proliferative disease, such as in eyes with borderline fundus findings.

Accurate IRMA identification is equally critical, as its presence defines severe NPDR [27]. WF CFP alone underestimated IRMA burden across the NPDR spectrum, with poor inter-modality agreement at all stages. Notably, no IRMA was identified on WF CFP in mild-to-moderate NPDR, whereas UWF OCTA revealed IRMA in a substantial proportion, consistent with Ding et al., who reported detection rates of 28.8% and 70.6% in mild and moderate NPDR using SS-OCTA [13]. IRMA burden rose markedly from mild-to-moderate to severe NPDR on UWF OCTA, suggesting progressive vascular remodelling rather than a binary marker of severe disease; non-stable IRMA is significantly associated with DR progression and adverse outcomes [4].

The paired multimodal analysis offers mechanistic insight into these discrepancies. WF CFP lesion counts rose significantly after OCTA-guided review, indicating lesions that were perceptually missed rather than absent; yet UWF OCTA still identified a higher burden, indicating that WF CFP is limited both perceptually (observer-dependent) and intrinsically (modality-dependent). UWF OCTA counts, by contrast, changed minimally after paired review, consistent with more complete detection at independent grading. Inter-modality differences were not significantly associated with age, lens status or BCVA on multivariable regression, although the predominance of WF CFP-related exclusions reflects its greater susceptibility to optical degradation from media opacity.

These findings raise an important conceptual question: do lesions detectable only on OCTA carry the same prognostic significance as those visible on CFP? On fundus photography, IRMA is a recognised marker of retinal ischaemia [28], but becomes apparent only at more advanced stages, whereas UWF OCTA resolves it earlier and more subtly. This distinction matters because the predictive value of IRMA for progression to proliferative disease was established using 7-field ETDRS photography, and no comparable prospective validation yet exists for IRMA identified on UWF OCTA [29]. Lesions detected only on UWF OCTA, or only after inter-modality comparison, may therefore represent an earlier point on the same pathological continuum, below the severity threshold historically associated with ETDRS grading, rather than lesions of equivalent prognostic weight. The same question applies to occult NV seen on UWF OCTA alone, whose prognostic significance is similarly unproven. Our previous work using the same single-capture platform showed that OCTA-detected IRMA counts rise with advancing DR severity, consistent with biological plausibility [30]; by design, however, cross-sectional data cannot resolve prognostic equivalence. Resolving this will require prospective correlation of UWF OCTA-detected lesions with disease progression, which our ongoing longitudinal study will address.

Until such validation exists, UWF OCTA cannot replace WF CFP as the reference modality for DR staging. Current severity grading is organised around discrete, categorically defined lesions on fundus photography, each mapped to established thresholds for surveillance and treatment [5, 25, 26]. Despite superior lesion detection, UWF OCTA lacks validated equivalents to these cut-offs, so greater lesion yield does not yet translate into an actionable change in disease stage or management; standardised, outcome-validated OCTA grading frameworks are therefore needed. Collectively, our findings support a pragmatic, stepwise role for the two modalities: WF CFP for initial screening, with UWF OCTA applied selectively in cases of diagnostic uncertainty, suspected progression, or discordant findings.

This study has several limitations. First, FFA was not included as a reference standard; however, its invasive nature limits routine use, and prior studies show good OCTA-FFA agreement for neovascular detection [17]. Second, the sample size limits subgroup analysis, particularly within PDR, and the observed reclassification rate requires validation in larger cohorts. Third, our cohort had relatively preserved central vision (mean logMAR 0.256). Although poor visual acuity would be expected to degrade image quality in both modalities, several excluded cases showed UWF OCTA remained interpretable where WF CFP was not, suggesting the two may not be equally affected; our cohort cannot confirm this, and the comparative performance of the two modalities in eyes with poor visual acuity warrants a dedicated study in a larger, vision-impaired sample. Finally, the manufacturer-reported fields of view (133° for WF CFP; 150° for UWF OCTA) may not reflect equivalent retinal coverage, as field of view definitions and reporting conventions differ across imaging systems [31].

Conclusions

Single-capture UWF OCTA detects significantly more IRMA and NV than WF CFP across all stages of diabetic retinopathy, with superior intergrader reproducibility. Paired multimodal review improved lesion recognition on WF CFP but did not eliminate inter-modality discrepancies, and an exploratory 10% reclassification from NPDR to PDR underscores the potential clinical impact of OCTA-guided assessment. These findings support UWF OCTA as a complementary modality within specialised diabetic eye care, with potential to refine microvascular lesion detection and DR staging beyond what WF CFP alone provides. Whether the additional lesion burden resolved by UWF OCTA improves prediction of disease progression and treatment response remains to be established, and is the focus of prospective longitudinal work incorporating functional outcomes.
