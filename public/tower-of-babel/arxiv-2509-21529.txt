Zero Forcing, Leaky Zero Forcing, and Leaky Forts on Hopi Rectangle Graphs
Ryan Moruzzi, Jr, Sagar Shah, Aaditeya Tripathi

Abstract
We study the family of Hopi rectangle graphs, a planar generalization of the well-studied Aztec diamond graphs. We provide a new definition of this graph family as induced subgraphs of the Cartesian product of two path graphs, which helps frame our study. We then establish that the maximum nullity and the zero forcing number coincide for all Hopi rectangle graphs. Extending to the fault-tolerant framework of leaky zero forcing, we use the notion of leaky forts to get a complete characterization of the ℓ \ell -leaky forcing number for all ℓ ≥ 1 \ell\geq 1 in Hopi rectangle graphs.



Keywords:
zero forcing, leaky zero forcing, leaky forts, maximum nullity, minimum rank, Hopi rectangle graphs, Aztec diamond graphs, planar graphs

AMS subject classification:
05C50, 05C76.

1
Introduction

Zero forcing is an iterative coloring process on a graph
G
where one begins with a subset of vertices colored blue and repeatedly applies a color change rule with the goal of eventually coloring the entire graph blue. The color change rule is as follows: if a blue vertex has exactly one non–blue neighbor, then that neighbor is
forced
to become blue. This forcing may occur over multiple rounds until no further forces are possible. If an initial blue set of vertices is able to color all other vertices of
G
, it is called a
zero forcing set
, with the minimum cardinality of such a set being called the
zero forcing number
of
G
, denoted
Z(G)
.

Although zero forcing can be studied purely from a graph-theoretic perspective, it is closely connected to linear algebra through its relationship with the maximum nullity of a graph. This connection was first established by the AIM (American Institute of Mathematics) Minimum Rank–Special Graphs Work Group
[
1
]
, who introduced zero forcing in the context of the minimum rank and maximum nullity. They showed that the zero forcing number provides a graph-theoretic upper bound on the maximum nullity, and since then, the topic of zero forcing has flourished into a rich area of research in its own right.

Zero forcing can also be viewed as a model for the spread of information in a network, and results in this area have found applications in contexts such as quantum control
[
11
]
and search algorithms
[
14
]
. In such applications, one may be concerned with the presence of faulty vertices that obstruct propagation. Therefore, the notion of a “leaked” or “faulty” vertex was introduced in
[
7
]
, leading to a variation of zero forcing known as
leaky zero forcing
(or simply
leaky forcing
) which follows the same color change rule as zero forcing with the added notion of vertices losing their ability to force. The main question in this setting is: what is the minimum number of vertices required to force the graph despite the presence of
\ell
leaked vertices? This minimum is called the
\ell
–leaky forcing number
of a graph
G
, denoted
Z_{(\ell)}(G)
. This concept of leaky forcing has attracted much recent attention, where the leaky forcing number has been explored for both structural results and for particular graph families. (
[
7
,
12
,
6
,
18
,
10
]
and related variations studied in
[
17
]
).

In this work, we determine the zero forcing and leaky forcing numbers for the family of planar graphs known as Aztec rectangle graphs. These graphs arise as a natural generalization of the Aztec diamond graph, which were originally studied from a combinatorial perspective through the enumeration of domino tilings of the Aztec diamond, the dual of the Aztec diamond graph
[
5
,
2
]
. More recently, extensions to tilings of double Aztec rectangles have also been studied
[
3
]
. Carrying over to graph theory, the equality of the zero forcing number and maximum nullity of Aztec diamond graphs were established in
[
15
]
using novel techniques. Motivated in part by this result about Aztec diamond graphs, our aim in this work is to extend the result in
[
15
]
from Aztec diamond graphs to the broader class of Aztec rectangle graphs. In doing so, we not only determine that the zero forcing number and maximum nullity are equal for this family, but also give a complete characterization of the
\ell
-leaky forcing number for all
\ell\geq 1
.

A second motivation for our study of Aztec rectangle graphs was the curiosity about the origin of their name
“Aztec Diamonds/Rectangle graphs”
. Despite the name, this specific motif does not show up in traditional Aztec art. The first mention of the term “Aztec Diamond” was by the authors in
[
5
]
for which they determined the number of ways one can tile the Aztec diamond using dominoes. Further, in a blog post
[
9
]
, one of the authors of that same paper reflected on the name choice and explained the following:

“In writing up the work I’d done with Elkies, Kuperberg and Larsen
[
5
]
, I dubbed the shapes we’d studied “Aztec diamonds” because the design can be found in much pre-Columbian art. I tried to figure out if there was a specific group of Native Americans most closely associated with the motif but it seemed to be shared between many nations. I eventually concluded that the Hopi made the most use of it, but “Hopi diamond” didn’t sound as good as “Aztec diamond”, so I chose euphony over ethnographic accuracy.”

From this post, the authors selected the name for this family of graphs based on its euphonic appeal rather than strict ethnographic accuracy. While the name may not perfectly align with its cultural or historical origins, and risks cultural appropriation as it repurposes a name with cultural significance without fully engaging with its historical or societal context, it was chosen for its ease of recognition within mathematical spaces. Equipped with this context, we now make the choice to use the term
Hopi
in place of Aztec throughout the rest of the paper.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we establish common notation, definitions, and results used throughout. In Section
3
we establish a novel definition of Hopi rectangle graphs using the integer lattice and determine the cardinality of a general graph within this graph family. In Section
4
, we establish a lower bound for the maximum nullity of Hopi rectangle graphs, which subsequently enables us to show the equality of the maximum nullity and the zero forcing number of this graph family in Section
5
. Last, in Section
6
, we completely characterize the
\ell
-leaky forcing number of Hopi rectangle graphs by way of results about leaky forts.

The size of a Hopi rectangle graph of order

\displaystyle=|HD(1,j)|+3

The size of a Hopi rectangle graph of order

is

Consider the Hopi Rectangle graph

The maximum nullity of Hopi rectangle graphs

As we move toward establishing the zero forcing number of the family of Hopi rectangle graphs, we make use of the notion of an edge covering. Along with the size of the graph, an edge covering provides the necessary tool for bounding the maximum nullity. Recall that an edge covering of a graph
G
is a collection of edges
e\in E(G)
such that every vertex
v\in V(G)
is incident to at least one edge in the collection. For this graph family, we exploit the repeated motif of the cycle graph
C_{4}
. Specifically, we construct an edge covering of
HD(m,n)
by expressing the graph as a union of subgraphs isomorphic to
C_{4}
. Examples are shown in Figure
3

HD(3,1)
is covered by three copies of
C_{4}
, and
HD(2,3)
is covered by six copies of
C_{4}
. In the latter case, the two interior cycles are not counted, since their edges are already covered.

:
Example of edge covers for
HD(2,3)
and

m,n\in\mathbb{N}

Proof.

Consider the Hopi rectangle graph of order
(m,n)
,
HD(m,n)
. Proceeding with induction on
m

. Now, suppose the edges of the graph

, that is,

. Considering

and adjoin

. This can be done by overlapping the degree two vertices along the upper boundary of

(1)

where

Proof.

Proof.

Let

We now arrive at one of our main results, which establishes the values of both the maximum nullity and the zero forcing number for the family of Hopi rectangle graphs.

Theorem 16
.

For all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, the zero forcing number of the Hopi rectangle graph

m+n
. In particular, the maximum nullity and the zero forcing number are equal for this family of graphs.

Proof.

15
, the set
B

m+n

On the other hand, by Corollary
