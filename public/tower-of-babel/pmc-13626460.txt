Strategic Inversion: How Refugees Reconfigure Everyday Resistance under Benevolent Oppression
Authors: Rekha Krishnan, Aloysius Marcus Kahindi, Rajiv Krishnan Kozhikode
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
What does everyday resistance look like among dominated populations experiencing paternalistic care rather than extraction? Research on everyday resistance emerged from contexts where power extracts surplus from the oppressed. In this scholarship, the backstage is a space where the dominated cultivate hidden transcripts to cope with oppression, while the frontstage, where performances are enacted to those in power, is primary. Inversions of this order, when they occur, are momentary. Using ethnographic fieldwork in two Tanzanian refugee camps, we show that under benevolent oppression—control justified by paternalistic care rather than extraction—dominated populations respond through “strategic inversion”: a reordering of dramaturgical space where the backstage is primary and the frontstage remains tactical. Refugees moved the center of social life backstage, into a thriving underground economy, and kept up frontstage displays of helplessness to shield it. Within this alternative social order, competition and cooperation operated as two routes to heshima (dignity/deference). Competition resisted the uniformity of the refugee label, and cooperation rejected the helplessness it presumed. Refugees sustained this inversion through compliance flooding, rule-following performances so dense that officials could not tell forbidden from permitted activity; embedded enforcement, as guards became accomplices in maintaining the clandestine economy they policed; and collective coordination, where communities maintained alert systems and consistent performances to shield backstage activities. Our analysis extends research on everyday resistance by demonstrating that under benevolent oppression, subordinates actively build and experience empowerment, rather than merely imagining alternative moral orders or experiencing them briefly in bounded rituals.

Introduction

How do dominated populations respond when the very structures that oppress them also provide resources? This question is especially important in refugee camps, where approximately 6.6 million refugees—roughly one-fifth of the global refugee population 1 —exist under what we refer to as “benevolent oppression”—control justified by care. These regimes offer basic necessities but restrict individual autonomy. This paradox is typical of humanitarian governance, where control is justified by care rather than extraction. From welfare systems to immigration management to homelessness policing, scholars have shown that care simultaneously constrains those it serves, requiring them to meet behavioral expectations, display gratitude, and act as deserving subjects rather than assert their rights (Fassin 2012; Fong 2020; Ticktin 2006; Tillman 2023). In refugee camps, aid agencies train refugees for “self-reliance” via vocational programs, yet prevent them from economically gaining from those skills in the present (Jansen 2018; Stokes 2019). This results in a temporal paradox. Refugees are suspended between a promised future that never arrives and a forbidden present (Agier 2011). Understanding refugees’ responses to these constraints involves going beyond traditional resistance frameworks, which originated from examining extractive domination: landlords taking peasant surplus, institutions monitoring inmates, and states extracting labor (Ewick and Silbey 2003; Goffman 1961; Scott 1985). Benevolent oppression creates a fundamental change in how power operates, potentially producing different forms of resistance.

Scholars have long debated how refugees respond to such constraints. Early work demonstrated how aid agencies systematically exclude refugees from decision-making about their own futures by using narratives of “dependency syndrome” and “begging mentality” (Harrell-Bond 1986; Kaiser 2000; Kibreab 1993; Turner 2005). Recent scholarship documents how refugees resist these framings, creating elaborate informal economies despite prohibitions on work and trade (Bochmann 2019; Jansen 2018). Studies show that refugees are transforming aid into capital by starting businesses such as IT services and restaurants, creating trade networks across camp boundaries, and taking on informal roles like traders, fixers, and brokers (Bochmann and Purdeková 2025; Boeyink 2022; Turner 2005). This scholarship redirects attention from narratives of victimhood to the forms of agency exercised by refugees, showing how they actively resist oppression in empowering ways. Young men, for example, “regain control of their lives” through involvement in business and politics (Turner 1999:14). Refugees also establish “pockets of sovereignty” outside the reach of authorities and uphold “public secrets” that undermine official systems (Bochmann 2019; Turner 2005:324). Studies of refugee camp economies have documented entrepreneurial ingenuity and informal leadership, but they have yet to systematically theorize how such agency reconfigures the symbolic structure of camp life itself. Does the backstage remain a marginal space of autonomy generating “pockets of sovereignty” (Turner 2005:324), or does it become the primary organizing force of social existence? In this article, we argue that under benevolent oppression, dominated populations systematically invert the dramaturgical space—making the backstage primary and the frontstage tactical—a process we call “strategic inversion.”

We conducted a multi-sited ethnography of clandestine markets across two large refugee camps in western Tanzania. Refugees in the camps we studied could legally barter goods and services with one another in the weekly barter market. Cash transactions and trading outside of camps were illegal, yet most market transactions defied these institutional constraints as they relied on digital currency and extended beyond the barter market. Within this informal infrastructure, refugees collectively constructed what we refer to as “dignity economies”—underground systems of exchange and status-making organized around heshima (dignity/deference). These economies not only provided material resources but also constituted a basis for moral worth and social standing. In this context, the very institutional arrangements designed to regulate and contain refugees—ration systems, aid relationships, vocational programs—became resources for constructing an alternative social order in which entrepreneurial identity, rather than refugee identity, defined one’s actual standing within camp life.

In theorizing strategic inversion, we build on Goffman’s (1959) dramaturgical framework while departing from it in one foundational respect. The backstage is traditionally considered subordinate. It is a space where the dominated cultivate hidden transcripts that enable them to withstand oppression. The frontstage, where performances are enacted for those in power, remains primary (Goffman 1961; Scott 1990). Our findings reveal that benevolent oppression brings about a fundamental inversion of this relationship. The clandestine economy became the primary arena of refugee life in Tanzanian camps. Within this alternative social order, competition and cooperation operated as two routes to heshima. Competition pushed back against the uniformity of the refugee label, and cooperation rejected the helplessness it presumed. We identified three mechanisms that sustained refugees’ strategic inversion. Through compliance flooding, refugees generated such dense performances of helplessness that authorities could not distinguish permitted from prohibited activities. Embedded enforcement shifted guards’ roles from adversaries to accomplices, aligning their interests with the maintenance of hidden economies. And refugees collectively coordinated through alert systems and consistent performances to ensure the maintenance of “public secrets” (Bochmann 2019). Overall, these mechanisms transformed tactical deception into structural reordering.

Theoretical Background

The Puzzle of Care-Based Control

Oppression is conventionally understood as the antithesis of empowerment. However, domination does not always operate through extraction; it can operate through care, raising a puzzling question. Can the very structures designed to control a population inadvertently furnish resources for that population’s liberation? In contrast to forms of domination based on extraction (Ewick and Silbey 2003; Scott 1985), benevolent oppression is characterized by a paradox in which authorities provide for populations’ basic needs, such as food, shelter, healthcare, and education, while simultaneously restricting their autonomy. Fassin (2012) theorizes this entanglement as “humanitarian reason.” As care is founded on compassion rather than rights, those at the receiving end are expected to “show the humility of the beholden rather than express demands for rights” (Fassin 2012:3–4). Recipients are expected to “tell their stor[ies],” “mend their ways,” and “show their gratitude” as the price of aid. Tillman (2023) identifies a parallel logic in what she terms “coercive benevolence”—care that forces the marginalized to comply with institutional expectations or face discipline. A broad literature documents the same fusion of care and coercion across poverty governance (Soss, Fording, and Schram 2011), child protective services (Fong 2020), homelessness policing (Herring 2019), and the management of migrant domestic workers (Parreñas 2021).

Refugee camps governed by humanitarian regimes exemplify this distinctive form of domination (Agier 2011; Kaiser 2000). Across the Global South, millions of forcibly displaced people live in camps where international organizations and host governments meet their basic needs while severely restricting their economic agency and spatial mobility. Camp authorities do not position themselves as exploiters but as caretakers, and restrictions are framed as necessary for refugees’ safety and well-being (Agier 2011; Kaiser 2000; Malkki 2002). This form of benevolent control produces a temporal paradox. On the one hand, refugees are confined to camps and separated from the host society. At the same time, as Stokes (2019) notes, humanitarian organizations train refugees for future participation in the labor market, even as they restrict refugees from engaging in economic activity in the present. This introduces a temporal script where refugees must wait patiently for eventual resettlement while barred from building meaningful economic lives now (Agier 2011). Thus, refugee camps host populations suspended between a prepared but perpetually delayed future and a forbidden present.

Existing frameworks for understanding resistance under domination—particularly dramaturgical approaches that distinguish between frontstage compliance and backstage resistance—emerged from studying extractive domination (Scott 1985, 1990). Under benevolent oppression, the relationship between those who govern and those who are governed operates differently, potentially producing different forms of resistance and different relationships between public compliance and hidden opposition.

Dramaturgical Frameworks: Extractive Domination and the Subordinate Backstage

Goffman’s (1959:101, 105–106) dramaturgical framework establishes a foundational distinction between frontstage and backstage that has shaped decades of scholarship on resistance under domination. The front region constitutes “the place where the performance is given,” where actors maintain expected roles before audiences. The backstage emerges as a hidden realm “where the suppressed facts make an appearance”—a space defined not by what it is, but by its relationship to frontstage demands. This relational subordination manifests in everyday workplace interactions. When word spreads that supervisors approach, shipyard workers scramble to perform exaggerated busyness—frantically bending pipes and tightening already-secure bolts in what Goffman identifies as ritualized displays of deference. Yet these convincing performances depend entirely on backstage preparation where teams rehearse routines, coach incompetent members, and coordinate their deceptions. Most crucially, the backstage provides respite that makes frontstage subordination tolerable. Workers “buffer themselves from the deterministic demands that surround them” by maintaining spaces where they can drop their masks and recover from performance demands. Without the “safe place to hide the jig,” the factory worker cannot sustain the appearance of full-day effort. Backstage thus exists in fundamental tension: it enables the very performances that subordinate workers while providing the refuge that makes such subordination bearable.

The tension between enabling subordination and offering respite from it has shaped scholarly accounts of the tactics subordinates use backstage. Across a range of settings, including asylums where inmates develop an underlife of secondary adjustments (Goffman 1961), villages where peasants maintain hidden transcripts (Scott 1985), and everyday acts of resistance to legal authority (Ewick and Silbey 1998, 2003), subordinates rely on forms of calculated concealment that preserve frontstage compliance. The various practices Scott describes as sabotage, foot-dragging, pilfering, and feigned ignorance all have one thing in common: they need to cover their tracks so as not to provoke retaliation. Johnston (2005) extends this framework to show how small contention and coded speech acts function similarly in repressive contexts. The varied acts of resistance and the sophistication of their concealment reflect rejection of the dominant social order, rather than false consciousness that accepts it. The peasants in Sedaka understand perfectly the mechanics of their exploitation (Scott 1985), and the various subjects interviewed by Ewick and Silbey (1998) recognize the law’s arbitrary power. Yet this awareness coexists with strategic accommodation—ensuring that even successful acts of resistance “preserve, for the most part, the on-stage theater of power” (Scott 1985:273).

Backstage resistance preserves frontstage order, but it simultaneously enables momentary inversions of that order. Scott (1977:29) shows how subordinates inverted established hierarchies, if only temporarily, by participating in rituals that are officially sanctioned. In medieval European Carnival, “social and moral restraints fell away” as “slaves were on an equal footing with their masters.” In these brief moments, the social order was symbolically reversed. Yet these profanations occurred under “rigidly defined, contained, and even sanctioned” conditions set by elites. Scott argues that such rituals represented “an alternative moral universe,” yet they functioned within temporal boundaries. After the festival concluded, participants returned to their subordinate positions. In her documentation of “dark sousveillance,” Browne (2015:77) reveals parallel dynamics under racialized surveillance where Pinkster celebrations, the Totau, and public market “breakdowns” created opportunities for Black participants to engage in “practices, rituals, and acts of freedom.” These moments of “celebratory resistance” were performed under the surveillant gaze. Yet even these bounded inversions could provoke suppression when their content threatened the established order—Albany’s Common Council banned Pinkster in 1811 because it enabled “unsettling exchanges between blacks and whites” (Browne 2015:81). Whether in agrarian villages or racialized surveillance regimes, inversions of power have been temporally bounded.

Despite its structural subordination, the backstage sustains morale by nurturing visions of an alternative social world. Scott (1977:20) demonstrates how peasants maintain collective memories of more equitable arrangements and imagine “a bountiful world where there are no classes, private property or exploitation.” Ewick and Silbey (1998:173) reveal parallel dynamics in contemporary legal consciousness: subjects such as Bess Sherman construct “imaginary spaces” that allow them to experience agency denied to them in reality, and they maintain dignity through the act of imagination. This preservation of hope within accommodation defines resistance under extractive domination. For Scott (1985:350), the revolutionary potential of such practices lies not in open confrontation but in the “steady, grinding efforts to hold one’s own against overwhelming odds,” a persistence that, in his words, “prevents the worst and promises something better.” The backstage thus serves a dual function of enabling survival within existing structures while preserving imaginative resources for their potential transformation. Yet, the frontstage, where landlords extract surplus and authorities enforce compliance, remains the primary site of consequential action. What happens when domination operates through care rather than extraction, when authorities position themselves as protectors rather than exploiters?

Refugee Agency and Economic Life: Resistance in Humanitarian Spaces

A substantial body of research examines the tensions between humanitarian control and refugee agency in camp settings. Scholars have shown that aid agencies frequently characterize refugees as victims, referring to this as “dependency syndrome” or “relief mentality” (Harrell-Bond 1986; Kibreab 1993; Turner 2005). Simultaneously, agencies systematically exclude refugees from participating in decisions about their futures (Kaiser 2000). This scholarship describes how camp governance flattens diverse identities into the uniform category of “refugee.” Hyndman (2000) shows how this produces subjects of care whose agency is curtailed even as their basic needs are met. In his ethnography of camps, Agier (2011) describes camps as liminal “waiting rooms” where humanitarian control produces populations temporally suspended, perpetually waiting for a future that never arrives.

Recent ethnographies reveal that refugees actively resist the constraints imposed on them, often exercising agency through the very power structures designed to contain them. Jansen’s (2018) concept of “digging aid” captures how refugees actively extract resources from humanitarian regimes, transforming aid from assistance into economic input. Other studies have shown how refugees resist a camp’s flattening of roles and resources by seeking status within its constrained spaces. For instance, Boeyink’s (2022) study of ration resale in Tanzanian camps shows how refugees resist encampment’s leveling tendencies by cultivating informal roles as traders, fixers, and brokers, which offer access, recognition, and income. His research highlights how elite actors, including well-connected refugees and local authorities, extract value from the aid system by controlling access to resale markets and offering protection in exchange for loyalty or bribes. Such patronage hierarchies within the camp economy is characteristic of what Hydén (1980) identifies as the “economy of affection” in African political economies.

Some studies suggest that resistance in refugee camps is more systematic and collective than individualistic and opportunistic (Jansen 2018), requiring refugees to “trust and co-operate with each other before they could engage in any concerted activities of deceit” against organizations with “massive resources at their disposal” (Kibreab 2004:12). Bochmann’s (2019) study of refugee camps on the Thai–Burmese border illustrates this: camp residents maintain “public secrets,” collectively falsifying ration books to secure resources while keeping agencies unaware. Similarly, Turner (2005:332) observes that refugees “refus[e] to play the role of innocent victims,” instead, they create “pockets of sovereignty” outside the reach of the UNHCR. However, existing research has not systematically examined whether these “pockets” persist as marginal spaces of autonomy or develop into the primary organizing force of camp life. If backstage economies do more than sustain refugees and instead empower them as economic actors, then the relationship between frontstage and backstage must be fundamentally reconfigured, especially when domination is exercised through care rather than extraction. Under benevolent oppression, we argue, the dramaturgical architecture does not merely adapt—it inverts.

When Care and Control Entwine: Theorizing Strategic Inversion under Benevolent Oppression

Dramaturgical scholarship on resistance has developed largely within settings of extractive domination, while literature on refugee camps has documented the considerable agency among residents. This work diverges on a fundamental matter: the manner in which domination is exercised. In the classic studies of resistance, authorities dominate by taking. The landlord subsists on the surplus produced by the peasant (Scott 1985), the total institution exacts compliance as a condition of membership (Goffman 1961), and the state extracts labor (Browne 2015; Ewick and Silbey 1998). Domination operates through extraction and is enforced by the threat of coercive power. Under benevolent oppression, however, power is exercised under the guise of care. In refugee camps, humanitarian agencies supply food, shelter, healthcare, and education (Agier 2011; Kaiser 2000; Malkki 2002). Yet, these provisions do not make camps less oppressive. Residents are stripped of their prior identities, held within tight spatial boundaries, and left to wait in a present that never resolves.

Accordingly, under benevolent oppression, resistance is correspondingly inverted. Under extractive domination, resistance means denying resources to appropriating classes (Scott 1985). Peasants drag their feet to reduce output, pilfer to reclaim surplus, and sabotage equipment to disrupt extraction. The goal is to minimize what authorities can take. Under benevolent oppression, resistance operates through maximizing extraction from authorities. Jansen (2018) describes this as refugees “digging for aid,” whereby they draw as much as they can from the humanitarian apparatus and convert these goods into resources for economies that function beyond official view. The tactics of deception, concealment, and performance that denied authorities resources in extractive dominance are used to accumulate resources from them under benevolent oppression.

Recent scholarship provides conceptual tools for understanding how subordinates exploit such openings. Innes and Dawson (2022) and Starr (2020) describe the tactic of “flooding the zone,” where subordinates produce so much compliant information that the noise hides their forbidden activities. Similarly, Fink (2023) shows that what people present on the frontstage shapes what they can do backstage. This suggests that refugees who perform complete helplessness may create more space for entrepreneurship backstage than do those displaying capability. Relatedly, guards have the capacity to expose the clandestine backstage and occupy, in Goffman’s (1959) parlance, “discrepant roles” as “informers” who acquire backstage knowledge. In contexts of benevolent oppression, guards rarely act as informers. Instead, as camp economies show, guards become participants in what Hydén (1980) calls the “economy of affection.” The kitu kidogo, meaning something small that is exchanged, serves not as a bribe but as a means of entry into a network of reciprocal obligations. Within this network, guards’ interests become aligned with maintaining the hidden economy rather than exposing it.

Building on extensive ethnographic fieldwork in Tanzanian refugee camps, we explain how resistance systematically inverts the dominant order under benevolent oppression. The oppressed do not merely envision an alternative social order but proceed to build one that affords genuine empowerment. We refer to this collective process as strategic inversion. Through strategic inversion, a dominated population converts the backstage into the principal site of its social existence while sustaining frontstage performances that function as tactical concealment. In the Tanzanian camps, the dignity economies constructed by refugees embodied this inversion. Over the course of our study, we observed how these intricate clandestine systems of exchange and status attainment constituted the central arena of social life.

Methods

Research Context

We located our research in the context of two major Tanzanian refugee camps managed by the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR): Nyarugusu and Mtabila. Established in 1996 for Burundian refugees, both camps housed refugees fleeing violence in neighboring countries. Each camp expanded to house DRC refugees in 2004. During our study, Nyarugusu was the most populated of the camps, with over 60,000 refugees. 2 Mtabila Camp, which was much smaller, also had a sizeable population of 50,000 refugees during our study; this camp was shut down soon after our data collection, after all the refugees living there were forcefully repatriated. Nyarugusu doubled in size to over 130,000 refugees as it welcomed new arrivals from Burundi following instability in the region in 2015.

Refugees in each camp routinely encountered camp authorities, including UNHCR workers and various nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) (Veney 2007). The refugees relied on UNHCR to meet their basic necessities such as food, shelter, clothing, and healthcare. Camp guards—Tanzanian government employees appointed ostensibly to ensure the safety of refugees—policed their activities and restricted mobility in and out of the camp. NGOs were very active in each camp. In collaboration with the UN, NGOs offered refugees primary, secondary, and tertiary education, as well as providing training programs for skills-building.

Unlike other refugee camps in sub-Saharan Africa, where refugees are permitted to engage in informal economic activities and seek employment in their adopted host countries, refugee camps in Tanzania prohibited camp residents from engaging in any type of economic activity with residents of the host nation (Turner 2010). 3 Each refugee family was provided a tent to live in and a tiny piece of land to cultivate. Refugees were allowed to engage in barter transactions with each other in a marketplace within the camps, but they were forbidden from engaging in cash transactions or transacting with Tanzanian civilians. Tanzania has historically been known for its open refugee policy initiated under President Nyerere in 1972. However, the 1998 Refugee Act marked a shift toward stricter regulations.

Our data collection period, from 2011 to 2012, coincided with restrictive times for refugees in terms of economic activity and movement within Tanzania. Although we collected our data a decade ago, the structural conditions we document are not unique to this period but are salient even today, considering the cyclical nature of policy changes. Specifically, the government relaxed these restrictions in 2015, after our data collection period, but re-introduced them in 2018. After the end of our study period, and following the new influx of refugees from Burundi, UNHCR opened a common market within Nyarugusu Camp in 2015 that allowed camp residents to trade with villagers, and cash-based transfer replaced the existing in-kind barter trade (see WFP 2016). However, these provisions were withdrawn in 2018, returning to stringent restrictions (Msoka, Ruzibiza, and Turner 2025). In our Discussion section, we return to how these temporal variations in policy strengthen our theoretical claims. Part A1 of the online supplement presents the timeline of Tanzania’s refugee policies.

Data Sources

We conducted a multi-sited ethnography between 2011 and 2012 across two refugee camps in western Tanzania. Both camps were run by the Tanzanian Ministry of Home Affairs and UNHCR and hence were structured similarly, received the same kind of resources, and experienced similar restrictions on economic and social activities. This allowed us to explore what kind of social order existed across both camps, given they were exposed to similar structural conditions. One of the authors, who was born and raised in western Tanzania and is fluent in Swahili and other widely spoken Bantu languages (Kirundi, Kinyarwanda, and Lingala), conducted nine-month-long ethnographic fieldwork across these sites between 2011 and 2012. Two local graduate researchers from the University of Dar Es Salaam in Tanzania who were trained in conducting qualitative research accompanied and assisted the author in the field. 4

The journey to the camp was long and winding, and the formalities of getting access to the camp were equally demanding. It took us several weeks and numerous visits to the Tanzania Commission for Science and Technology (COSTECH) in Dar es Salam to get our permits to visit the camp and conduct our study. We began our fieldwork once we obtained special permission from COSTECH to conduct ethnographic work in the refugee camps (we address ethical considerations and the approval process in Part A3 of the online supplement). The first round of fieldwork was conducted between April and September of 2011. Based on the insights gained in this round, we conducted our second round of fieldwork between July and September 2012. We collected data from three principal sources: field observations, interviews, and archival data. In Part A2 of the online supplement, we explain how we triangulated across these three data sources. Field Observations

We started our fieldwork at Mtabila Camp. After one round of fieldwork here we moved to Nyarugusu. We visited each camp again in the same sequence for subsequent rounds of data collection. Every week we spent five to six days in a camp to conduct our fieldwork. As we were not allowed to stay inside the camp, we commuted daily from neighboring towns. Both camps were in remote areas that were accessible only by poorly laid roads via unreliable transportation service. It took us several hours to reach each camp, and we used a variety of transportation, ranging from motorcycles to dala-dalas to all-wheel-drive cars. On a typical day, we arrived at a camp around 7 a.m. and spent 8 to 12 hours interacting with camp residents and observing them. In our first phase of fieldwork, we visited NGOs and UNHRC offices within the camps, where we observed their social project activities, training sessions, and interactions with refugees. Hospitals, schools, and training facilities dotted the landscape, and NGOs and offices of camp authorities were tucked away from the hustle and bustle of the camps.

A typical day of fieldwork started with security clearance at the camp entrance. The camps were heavily guarded premises surrounded by barbed fences and two security check posts. Amid overcrowded tents set in a dusty and noisy environment, camp residents farmed the small plots of land allotted to them, kids enjoyed a game of soccer, busy women ran all sorts of errands, and men often idled away under trees or engaged in traditional games. In weekly markets or soko, camp residents bartered their goods. The soko ran from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. on Fridays, and refugees bartered produce, food, and traditional clothing such as Kanga and Kitenge. We observed 14 soko days in Nyarugusu camp and 13 in Mtabila camp between April and September 2011. However, there is more to the camp scene than meets the eye. As we shadowed camp residents, we realized that nestled in cramped tents were myriad businesses, such as repair services, saloons, IT and electronic repair shops, clothing stores, bicycle repairs, jewelry offerings, cafés, food stalls, bars, and streaming services. We observed how entrepreneurs ran these clandestine businesses within their tents. In phase 1, we sought to understand general economic life in camps; in phase 2, we followed several businesses closely.

Our first phase of fieldwork revealed a thriving clandestine market with two distinct layers: smaller individual traders, and established businesses formed around kinship, clan, or friendship ties. Initially, the camp’s residents were suspicious of the ethnographers, but once they found out we spoke fluent Swahili and other Bantu languages, they accepted us and opened up to us. We used snowball sampling and direct solicitation to identify participants for in-depth observation for phase 2 of our fieldwork. Most participants were open about their clandestine economic activities once they became comfortable with us, but not everyone consented to close observation.

Our approach involved initiating conversations with identified traders or businesses. Invitations to their tents to view their products signaled their willingness to be observed. Hesitant individuals would avoid us or request delays (e.g., “come back tomorrow”). Persistence was key, but repeated postponements indicated a strong reluctance to participate. For individual traders and friendship-based businesses, if they were willing to participate, the invitations to tents typically occurred on the second attempt. Kinship- and clan-based businesses required an average of five approaches. At some point during these encounters, if they addressed us as undugu (brother), it signaled they might be open to participating if we pursued further.

We faced two constraints with accessing traders and businesses for in-depth observation. First, given the clandestine nature of the market, obtaining a comprehensive list of entrepreneurs was not feasible. Therefore, we approached those who self-identified as entrepreneurs or were identified by their peers as such. Second, our final sample depended on entrepreneurs’ willingness to allow observation. In Part A3 of the online supplement, we reflect on the methodological and theoretical implications of this sampling strategy. Through this process, we were able to closely follow the activities of 30 established businesses and 29 individual traders across the two camps (Mtabila: 17 individual traders, 15 established businesses: kinship-based = 4, clan-based = 5, friendship-based = 6. Key Characteristics of Established Businesses.

Each established business held its own weekly internal strategy meeting, and we were invited to attend many of these. Due to the covert nature of the meetings, our attendance depended on invitations from the businesses. We were fortunate to be invited to observe a total of 45 strategy meetings held by established businesses across Mtabila (20 meetings) and Nyarugusu (25 meetings) between July and September 2012. We wrote field notes throughout the day and typed them up by the end of the day. In the event of a late return from far-off camps, field notes were typed up at the earliest possible opportunity, always by the weekend. In total, we conducted over 1,320 hours of observations, excluding interviews.

Informal Interviews

We conducted 90 informal face-to-face interviews with representatives of NGOs, UNHCR, camp residents, and founders of refugee-run businesses (i.e., established businesses, seasonal and weekly traders). We conducted our interviews in two phases. The first round of interviews was held between April and September 2011. We began with purposeful sampling of individuals from key categories—NGOs and UNHCR officials, whom we considered knowledgeable at the time about policies and economic activities in the refugee camps. We began our interviews with representatives of several NGOs and UNHCR. NGO and UNHCR offices were located within the camps. We were directed to these offices by the security guards at camp entrances. A UNHCR official introduced us to the NGO representatives located in the camp. Once we reached theoretical saturation we stopped interviewing. This resulted in 18 interviews with NGO and UNHCR representatives in Nyarugusu and 13 in Mtabila. We conducted our interviews with camp officials in English, and they lasted 30 minutes to an hour. We accompanied NGO representatives to the training centers, and they explained their work within the camps. These centers also served as our first point of contact with camp residents, primarily young people who had grown up there. UNHCR officials provided information about the weekly soko (market) for bartering goods, as well as general areas where refugees congregated. To supplement this information and gain a firsthand perspective, we walked around the camps to get a sense of areas where refugees gathered.

In the first phase, we interviewed groups of refugees as they went about their daily lives. Our initial interviews were with groups of informants—men gathered under trees, women lined up for rations or water, residents farming in tiny plots of land allotted to them, entrepreneurs wrapping up their weekly market days, and youth outside training centers and soccer fields. Some residents also gathered around us voluntarily when they knew we were outsiders. These interviews were more in the nature of informal conversations. Questions were posed to a group of residents as we joined their conversations. These informal interviews were conducted in Swahili and other Bantu languages and lasted anywhere from 30 minutes to two hours. We later translated these interviews into English. In total, we had 22 informal group encounters in Mtabila and 28 in Nyarugusu, excluding observations on soko days. The purpose of interviews in the first round of data collection was to understand refugees’ everyday lived experience, how NGOs and camp officials shaped refugees’ economic and social life, the nature of the economic activities they engaged in, and how their economic life was organized.

During phase 2 of our fieldwork, between July and September 2012, we conducted in-depth interviews with 30 established businesses and 29 individual traders across the two camps. Our observations of how these entrepreneurs ran their businesses coincided with our interviews with them. On a given day, we decided which businesses from our list to follow. During our 12-hour-long interactions with these entrepreneurs, our interviews with individual members lasted anywhere from 30 minutes to several hours. Our questions were based on our past and ongoing observations of covert meetings and were mainly carried out as conversations as they shared stories and meals with us. Questions primarily revolved around how they built relationships and networks, how they related to other businesses within the camp, how they managed restrictions placed on them, and how they responded to threats and opportunities in the market.

In phase 2, to prioritize the safety and anonymity of camp residents, we decided against interviewing NGO representatives and UNHCR camp officials. We attempted to interview camp guards observed during weekly business meetings. However, their refusal to participate suggested that approaching UNHCR officials for further interviews could jeopardize the confidentiality we promised residents and potentially cause them harm (Anteby 2024). Furthermore, the NGOs’ primary function was to educate and train camp residents. Their short tenures, typically around three months due to visa restrictions, limited their ability to observe the camps’ everyday realities. Coupled with their lack of influence on economic policy within the camp, this meant they were unlikely to be aware of the clandestine market’s existence. Our initial interviews with NGOs confirmed this focus on training and their positive perception of residents’ willingness to learn new skills. While there remained a possibility that some NGOs might have been aware of the clandestine market, prioritizing the safety and anonymity of camp residents ultimately led us to forgo approaching them in the second phase.

Archival Data

Our archival data collection had two purposes: (1) to gain an understanding of the history of the refugee camps in Western Tanzania before we commenced data collection, and (2) to understand how economic life portrayed by archival reports differed or matched with what we observed and learned from our fieldwork. We collected archival data from UNHCR, NGOs, the Tanzania Ministry of Home Affairs, and UN agencies’ brochures, technical documents, social media postings, websites, newspapers, and academic articles that provided a history of economic and social life within refugee camps.

Data Analysis

Our analysis of the data involved iterating among data collection, data analysis, and existing literature. We constantly compared existing data with new data on emerging themes, which helped us refine subsequent data collection (Glaser and Strauss 1967). Analysis at every stage consisted of multiple readings of interviews and field notes and writing analytic memos while simultaneously considering existing literature that could aid in theoretical interpretation (see Michel 2007). In the process, we kept track of patterns that repeated across different stages, and we discussed analytic memos with colleagues to find out whether they shared our theoretical interpretation (Glaser and Strauss 1967).

Our data analysis was iterative throughout the research period, but it involved two broad stages. Our first stage of analysis occurred in tandem with our data collection, where one author did a periodic analysis of the emerging data, drafted memos, and looked for disconfirming evidence for emerging insights across camps. Our second stage of analysis occurred after fieldwork was completed. In this stage, all three authors analyzed field notes and memos; we transcribed interviews separately and then together. Part A4 of the online supplement provides a detailed description of our analytic strategy.

Strategic Inversion and Dignity Economies in Refugee Camps

We begin by showing how humanitarian governance operated through benevolent oppression in both camps. Authorities prepared refugees for market participation in an indefinite future and prohibited economic action in the present, generating a temporal contradiction characteristic of refugee contexts (Agier 2011; Stokes 2019). This contradiction set the stage for strategic inversion, which involved refugees systematically building an alternative social order in backstage spaces, which became their primary social world. Refugees maintained frontstage performances as protective shields.

We identify three mechanisms through which refugees sustained this inversion. First, refugees engaged in compliance flooding, which involved hyper-visible performances of helplessness. Next, embedded enforcement turned guards from antagonists to accomplices whose material interests became entwined with sustaining hidden economies. Third, we document how entire communities performed collective coordination to protect clandestine activities from detection.

We examine the alternative social order these mechanisms protected. In the dignity economy refugees built backstage, their entrepreneur identities carried their actual standing in camp life. Their frontstage refugee identities were deployed instrumentally, inverting the dramaturgical relationship Goffman describes. Within the dignity economy, competition and cooperation worked together as complementary expressions of heshima, the shared pursuit of dignity that rendered clandestine markets at once arenas of individual distinction and collective resistance.

Benevolent Oppression: Humanitarian Governance and Temporal Suspension

Humanitarian governance in both camps operated through what we came to understand as benevolent oppression—a regime justifying restrictions on autonomy through paternalistic care (Agier 2011; Kaiser 2000; Malkki 2002). Camp authorities positioned themselves not as exploiters but as caretakers, framing prohibitions as necessary for refugee well-being. This position created distinctive structural conditions: refugees possessed material resources (food rations, shelter, vocational training) but lacked sanctioned arenas to deploy these resources toward autonomous economic lives.

Aid workers consistently emphasized two dimensions of the refugee role they sought to maintain: helplessness and uniformity. An International Rescue Committee (IRC) representative in Mtabila Camp explained their mission during our initial interview: “We work with Burundian refugees crossing the border to Tanzania. We facilitate a smooth move for them together with the UNHRC and provide essential items like medicine, food, clothing and transport. These people have been distressed by the war, displacement and separation . . . they have nothing. Many youths you see here have been born in the camp or during the process of displacement. Some of these youth are now over 30 years old. We offer them basic education and technology skills, but their future is very bleak. As an NGO, we strive to provide whatever we can find to facilitate them with humanitarian assistance.” One REDESO representative noted, “The refugees have practically nothing other than a tent and a few bags with their belongings. Their faces have a grim prospect of hope.”

The uniform application of the “refugee” label to all camp residents deprived them of their individuality. This label and economic restrictions systematically rendered them identically dependent, regardless of their individual capacities. For instance, refugees in both camps were allowed to engage in the weekly soko, bartering goods and services with fellow refugees, but engaging in cash transactions and trading outside the camps was strictly prohibited. Market days were Fridays between 6 a.m. and 6 p.m. and uniformed guards closely monitored market activities.

Interviews with camp officials revealed that the soko was offered as an insufficient remedy for the lack of purpose and economic agency resulting from stringent economic restrictions—a space intended to alleviate the boredom brought about by the enforced uniformity within the camp. One UNHRC representative described this succinctly: “The refugees are not permitted to engage in employment or cash earning activities. Yes, as human beings [refugees] they would like to share and even exchange clothes they have for something else that they do not have. It is good for them to do so as it allows them to have such freedom to exchange, to keep them away from being idle and boredom. We, as custodian of the camp, we have no problem at all.”

The temporal paradox, preparing refugees for eventual market participation through vocational training while prohibiting economic action in the present (Agier 2011; Stokes 2019), frustrated many camp residents. Youth who grew up in the camps expressed this contradiction: “I am passionate about information technology and I make use of every available training opportunity. How can I avail of advanced training?” asked one camp resident. “But what is the use if you cannot earn a living with this?” quipped his friend. “We are grateful to the UN for providing our children with an education.” While this young parent expressed gratitude, he was quick to point out the suspended temporality of camp life, “But we are concerned about their readiness for life outside the camp, if and when it comes.”

Against this backdrop of provision without autonomy, refugees accomplished something remarkable. They systematically inverted the dramaturgical order (Goffman 1959), making clandestine economies the primary arena of social life while reducing official camp structures to tactical performances maintained only to protect what truly mattered. The mechanisms through which they accomplished this inversion, which we document in subsequent sections, reveal how resistance operates when domination exists through care and control rather than extraction.

Mechanisms of Strategic Inversion

Weaponizing Victimhood through Compliance Flooding

Refugees’ frontstage performance went beyond “symbolic compliance” (Scott 1985:26) and became what we refer to as compliance flooding. Through compliance flooding, refugees presented a dense surface of rule-following performances that generated so much bureaucratically legible noise that prohibited activities became indistinguishable from permitted ones (Innes and Dawson 2022; Starr 2020). The theatrical fog kept the clandestine economy out of focus. Refugees weaponized the helplessness narrative that humanitarian agencies sought to inscribe.

Overperforming Helplessness to Gain Maximum Resources

Early in our fieldwork, residents consistently overperformed the image of helplessness that camp authorities expected, presenting themselves as homogenous and uniformly powerless. “Are you from the same NGO that offered IT training?” several asked. “We lack adequate training in IT,” another added. “Can you talk to the UNHCR for us? Our rations rarely last a fortnight?” one woman pleaded. “There are mosquitoes everywhere. And we are running short of malaria pills,” an elderly man complained. These performances of desperate need appeared genuine and unrehearsed.

Our understanding shifted dramatically as camp residents became familiar with us and realized we were native speakers of Swahili and fluent in Bantu languages. We soon saw that this weaponization of helplessness narratives was a systematic collective practice. All residents we interacted with maintained consistent frontstage performances when aid workers were present. They sincerely lined up to collect fortnightly rations and occasional doles; appearing to accept the uniform refugee tag, they complained about shortages as they waited in line, expressed gratitude for assistance, and performed the dependency humanitarian workers expected to see.

The frontstage performances reiterated the helplessness associated with the refugee role imposed on them, including the scarcity of food and medicine and the lack of opportunity. However, this hyper-visible compliance and narrative of scarcity masked the backstage reality: camp rations were often used to fuel elaborate backstage economic activities. Hoarding was an essential feature of economic life across camps. When the UNHCR wondered why bacterial and viral infections persisted despite the careful distribution of medicines, little did they know that aspiring entrepreneurs within the camps secretly hoarded them in unsuspected corners of their tents. These entrepreneurs traded their excess rations with neighboring villagers who were eager consumers of many items distributed to refugees by UNHCR that were a rarity in the village markets. Entrepreneurs hoarded all sorts of items, ranging from milk powder to mosquito nets and malaria pills, and recycled them back into the clandestine exchanges with local villagers.

Market Day Negotiations: Theatrical Cover for Digital Transactions

Early in our fieldwork, the weekly soko appeared exactly as officials described: a barter economy where refugees exchanged goods without cash. In the weekly markets, camp residents appeared to diligently adhere to the rules. On a typical day, camp residents clearly bartered goods and services at stalls outside their tents, offering services in a central area, or walking from tent to tent with a bucket full of produce. Many traded food rations for produce grown on camp plots, clothing for radios, or jewelry for cell phones. Men sat under trees repairing bicycles, fixing radios, and mending shoes. Some repaired computers and cell phones. Early in our field work, the camp economy appeared to revolve around barter, as the residents’ frontstage performance intended. No cash appeared to change hands. When asked how much he charged to repair a cell phone, one repairman replied, “Only exchanges. Depends on what is offered in exchange.” There were no price labels attached to the products displayed on the stalls.

However, what was not obvious to an untrained eye was that refugees were generating so much compliant information that authorities could not distinguish genuine compliance from strategic performance, akin to what recent scholarship on dramaturgical strategies describes as “flooding the zone” (Innes and Dawson 2022). The lengthy negotiations that accompanied barter exchanges served dual purposes. These negotiations demonstrated apparent compliance with camp rules while simultaneously providing cover for electronic money transactions with uniformed guards. As one weekly vendor from Mtabila Camp explained, “M-Pesa is the most secure way of transacting. We have peace of mind. We do not worry about detection from camp guards or about money being stolen.” Many transactions were indeed settled through barter, but a large portion were settled using electronic money (mostly the M-Pesa and Tigo Pesa telephone-based money transfer platforms). Digital transactions officially forbidden were more prevalent than barter exchanges officially permitted. This was not sporadic rule-breaking but systematic inversion, where the prohibited became normal and the permitted became performance. Over time, we observed that virtually all significant transactions, especially youth-led friendship-based businesses in both camps violated official rules through e-money platforms. Bigger business dealings required building relationships with guards for transportation, but face-to-face dealings with villagers who had sneaked into the camp were carried out without guard assistance using e-money.

Hyper-Engagement with NGO Programs

The same pattern of flooding the zone appeared in refugees’ relationships with NGOs. Established businesses in the camp maximized participation—attending training sessions, volunteering as instructors, and displaying exceptional dedication. This hyper-visibility as eager learners not only extracted maximum resources: it also obscured actual business operations. For instance, a Norwegian NGO arrived in Mtabila Camp to provide software development training. Several young entrepreneurs from friendship-based IT services businesses attended every session enthusiastically. After gaining software development training, they exhibited willingness to learn newer skills. A respondent from a Norwegian NGO in the camp noted, “We are impressed by the dedication, discipline and hunger to learn among the youth and some individuals here. This is very impressive to me and our organization given their destitute conditions.” As for most NGOs, a top priority for this Norwegian NGO was imparting skills. Thus, when their time in the camp ended, rather than discarding their used materials, they left their computers pre-loaded with the training software with these eager learners. Yet the NGO’s perception of dedicated students preparing for uncertain futures could not have been further from reality. What the NGO did not know was that their eager learners were already running an IT services business. One of the businesses used the program to enhance their mobile marketing skills, and the other used it to simulate team learning and develop video games.

When an NGO in Mtabila Camp ceased operations unexpectedly, they gave away their solar energy station to an IT services business. This business used the energy station not for personal use but to diversify into energy-intensive offerings: they have since become the only reliable round-the-clock supplier of energy for refugees in the camp, and they identified novel uses for the station, addressing seasonal demands like supplying hot water in winter and providing refrigeration services in summer. Such deployment of resources in groundbreaking ways give entrepreneurs a decisive edge over competitors in the underground economy.

In another instance, some young entrepreneurs who jointly operated a business worked for an NGO as instructors in their computer training program. 5 The NGOs relied on these refugee instructors to impart training to other camp residents who sought IT skills. In their role as educators, they made sure they imparted basic required skills to camp residents, but residents always aspired to acquire skills beyond the basics. Capitalizing on this need, the refugee instructors offered private tutoring for a fee.

Through compliance flooding, refugees generated such dense informational noise that authorities could no longer distinguish permitted activities from prohibited ones. Elaborate barter negotiations, enthusiastic NGO participation, and performances of desperate dependency produced a hyper-visible surface of compliance, behind which forbidden e-money transfers, resource hoarding, and clandestine trade networks remained obscured.

Embedded Enforcement: Guards as Strategic Accomplices

Camp guards—ostensibly the primary enforcement mechanism—became embedded in what Hydén (1980) terms the economy of affection. Guards were not potential antagonists but stakeholders whose interests aligned with sustaining rather than exposing hidden economies. The dramaturgical role of guards requires theoretical elaboration. Goffman (1959) explains that when members lack “team loyalty” they become spies, informants, or traitors who pass information to opposing teams. Camp guards clearly lacked loyalty to their official team (the camp administration), yet they do not fit neatly into Goffman’s typology of traitors. Guards occupied a dual position: embedding with particular refugee businesses whose interests they came to share, while maintaining enforcement against those outside these trusted networks. Through gradual incorporation into trusted networks, guards transformed into hybrid figures who were simultaneously enforcers and accomplices.

Elite businesses cultivated these relationships strategically. Family and clan-based businesses developed ties with select guards who became intermediaries in transactions with villagers. Guards helped them locate customers and transport goods from camps to villages. In return, entrepreneurs offered what camp residents called chai kidogo (a little tea). We could not obtain clear answers to what constituted a chai kidogo. It certainly was not a strict one-time instrumental transaction, but a nuanced, repeated gift-giving that varied by occasion. One elite business owner explained, “I make sure I keep my friendship with the guards warm. I have always given them a ‘little chai’—food, medicine, cloth. My business outside the camps depends on their kindness.” Critically, guards accepted chai kidogo only from entrepreneurs with whom they had developed long-standing relationships, refusing overtures from those outside trusted networks. Younger, more tech-savvy entrepreneurs relied less on guards as intermediaries: “Because of technology, we can now access new customers from Kasulu town and Kigoma city very easily. . . . We Skype, we use Twitter, Facetime, Facebook, to name a few.”

Camps are high-security premises, and guards undertook significant risks by aiding entrepreneurs. Only when entrepreneurs played the waiting game—building trust over months or years—did they succeed in this delicate process. Bribery being a punishable offense with dire consequences, established businesses had clearly mastered the art of cultivating these relationships (cf. Stevens and Kahindi 2021).

Each established business held weekly internal meetings where team members jointly strategized ways to differentiate themselves and outcompete rivals. The agenda covered topics from profit and loss to competitors, customers, product and service offerings, and progress from prior meetings. Affiliated guards, present in these sessions, contributed strategic insights, and their perspectives on client acquisition and transportation strategies were valued. Positioned in a discreet corner of the tent, camp guards observed the exchange of ideas, stepping in as guides when explicitly summoned or when the group encountered challenges while brainstorming. During a weekly meeting, a clan-based business sought to understand a puzzling issue. While the rainy season usually brought a spike in demand for their malaria pills, their regular client in a neighboring village reported consistent sales, not the expected increase. As a member read this from their agenda, he turned toward a guard they had previously spoken with to gain insights on the local situation. The guard, already aware of their concern, explained, “Hoarding seems likely. They have pushed up the prices.” Murmurs rippled across the room. After a brief pause, the guard added, “I’ve spoken with a few other potential clients interested in distributing the pills.” The speaker thanked the guard and moved on to the next item on their agenda, while others nodded thoughtfully, seemingly agreeing this was a good solution.

Collective Coordination: Maintaining Team Performances

Embedded guards enabled hidden economies, but sustaining these economies required more than cooperative guards. It demanded that entire communities maintain consistent performances by flooding the zone (Starr 2020). When a camp resident performed scarcity narratives for outsiders or made connections with new clients amid lengthy soko negotiations, others present maintained “the definition of the situation” (Goffman 1959) by adding their own compliant performances to the collective noise. For instance, when one camp resident presented himself as a helpless refugee to a co-author in the field during our first round of fieldwork, those nearby did not flinch. They either played along with similar narratives or remained sympathetic spectators. The negotiations that accompanied barter exchanges operated similarly. On busy soko days, the theatrical fog thickened through coordinated action. Guards would not stay around for entire negotiations, especially on crowded market days. While some seasonal traders engaged guards with extended discussions, others conducted business with visiting villagers who blended into crowds—transacting via e-money or exchanging phone numbers for future contact. As refugees grew comfortable in our presence, they openly carried out e-money transactions as we watched.

This coordination produced what Bochmann (2019) documents in other refugee contexts as “public secrets,” or information entire communities maintain from authorities. In the camps, sustaining such public secrets demanded consistent performances across refugees. Such coordination transformed tactical deception into structural reordering: backstage did not merely provide cover for forbidden acts; it became the actual theater of meaningful social action that entire communities protected through sophisticated collective practices. Backstage gatekeeping was central to this coordination. For outsiders and camp authorities, casual conversations under trees or by food stalls appeared innocuous. In reality, many of these conversations were frontstage performances designed to distract from ongoing clandestine activities. Alert systems operated continuously: the moment anyone spotted a camp official approaching, they notified others via text message or in person, allowing time to conceal evidence. During our visit to an IT services business in Mtabila Camp, team members were discussing movies they planned to screen while one scrolled through options on his laptop. Suddenly, one shouted, “Hide your laptops now!”—responding to a text message from a friend. A camp official passed by headed for camp offices. After he disappeared from sight, discussions resumed.

These coordinated practices kept clandestine economies invisible to authorities. But concealment was not the end—it was the means. Refugees were protecting an alternative social order where the backstage became primary.

The Alternative Social Order: The Dignity Economy

Entrepreneur Identity and Dignity Reclamation

Even as they outwardly accepted the perceived helplessness and uniformity linked to the refugee role in public encounters with authorities, behind the scenes, camp residents grappled with feelings of humiliation associated with the refugee tag. As an elderly resident of Mtabila Camp explained, “We just happen to be here as refugees. We did not choose to be refugees. We were forced by the war, the ethnic cleansing in Burundi. People stared at us, looked down on us as if we were worms, lazy and hopeless. We are not!”

As camp residents became familiar with us and realized we were native speakers of Swahili and fluent in Bantu languages, they revealed they did not relate to the refugee tag given to them. Backstage, camp residents actively disassociated themselves from the refugee role by performing their desired entrepreneur role in a clandestine market invisible to camp authorities. When we asked a group of camp residents gathered under a tree about the restrictions they faced as refugees, a seasonal trader refused to be referred to as a refugee, saying, “I am an entrepreneur, not a refugee.”

What requires explanation is not simply that refugees maintained a backstage where they performed different identities, but the specific articulation between frontstage and backstage (Fink 2023). The entrepreneur identity performed backstage determined social standing, commanded respect, structured employment relationships, and governed status hierarchies, while the refugee identity performed frontstage became purely instrumental. This inverts the typical relationship Goffman (1959) describes, where frontstage performances, even if artificial, still determine social hierarchies. In the camps, backstage became primary and frontstage derivative, a systematic reordering sustained by compliance flooding, embedded enforcement, and collective coordination.

This inversion produced what we term a dignity economy—a social order organized around heshima rather than humanitarian dependency. At its core, heshima means dignity associated with earning one’s own bread. Camp residents earned heshima by performing the entrepreneur role among peers in the clandestine market. Proverbs frequently made their way into backstage interactions, “Asiye fanya kazi na asile: one who does not want to work should not eat” and “Kazana kulima, vyakupewa havitoshelezi: handouts will help one in the short term, but in order to be fulfilled, one needs to earn one’s own living.” By performing their desired entrepreneur role, camp residents converted the humiliation associated with the refugee tag into the dignity associated with economic agency. At its core, heshima represented the dignity of earning one’s own bread, a prerequisite for garnering respect as a human, but it also acquired the meaning of deference toward camp residents who achieved greater entrepreneurial success, with the less successful aspiring to and acknowledging their status. One member of a family-based firm in Mtabila Camp put it succinctly, “We are all lumped together as helpless refugees. But we are all not identical here. We run a family business. This not only keeps us going but has also earned us heshima among the camp residents. People respect us and come to work for us.”

By serving as the organizing principle within this alternative social order, heshima motivated competition and cooperation. Dignity was collectively validated. All participants accepted that entrepreneurship conferred independence and dignity, and each fought to achieve it through competition. We came to understand this during a conversation with a seasonal trader who had worked hard to raise his heshima by building relationships with established businesses: “We used to be small entrepreneurs back in Burundi. Our aim is to grow bigger here. We have all the opportunities to raise our heshima while here.” The phrase “opportunities to raise heshima” suggested that competition created opportunities for distinction, for demonstrating entrepreneurial skill and earning recognition based on business acumen rather than refugee status. This shared premise explains why intense competition strengthened rather than undermined collective resistance.

Competition for Heshima: Establishing Status Hierarchies

Within the dignity economy, differentiating from peers through competition was essential to gaining the deference component of heshima. Camp residents challenged the uniformity imposed by the refugee tag by actively differentiating themselves in the clandestine market through constant innovation and experimentation. Camp entrepreneurs demonstrated ingenuity ranging from resourceful solutions like crafting soap boxes from recycled paper to more sophisticated adaptations such as utilizing charcoal as a coolant for makeshift refrigerators. We observed this competitive dynamic develop as we tracked several businesses’ strategies for establishing market dominance.

Established Businesses versus Aspiring Traders

Businesses based on clan, kinship, and friendship ties formed the market’s elite core. 6 Seasonal and weekly traders remained outside this circle but aspired to enter it. Elite businesses identified weaknesses in promising competitors and aggressively undercut them to assert dominance. A founder of a mechanical services business explained, “We knew that our new competitors did not have a good customer base. Besides, they did not have as many facilities in mechanical repair services to make lots of products. We knew that if we had to squeeze them out of competition, this is what we needed to capitalize on.” This entrepreneur targeted small traders with service-related innovations—discounts, a returns policy, and add-on services for clients.

Established businesses rarely used product innovations to compete with small entrepreneurs. Only when a weekly trader was highly innovative and successful did established businesses enter their space. In the clothing domain, one weekly trader in Nyarugusu Camp did not let her inability to access basic tools such as sewing machines get in the way of her imagination. Camp residents made a beeline for her exquisite, handcrafted clothing. However, the line did not last long. A clan-based clothing business recognized that the quality of handcrafted clothing was fleeting—items quickly wore out or became soiled in the dusty camp environment. Amid their poverty, residents’ demand for practical solutions superseded their yearning for the intricate. This clan-based business identified paraffin wax coating as a solution to keep clothes new long after they left their store. Clients soon took to their paraffin wax coated clothing that came neatly ironed in soft plastic bags. The business found other uses for this dustproof, waterproof material—they created paraffin-coated bags, wallets, and raincoats. Competing for distinctiveness, whether the weekly trader’s handcrafted innovation or the elite business’s strategic response, challenged the uniformity of the refugee tag, even as resource asymmetries shaped who prevailed.

Competition among Elites

Elite businesses used different strategies to outmaneuver their competitors. Rather than crushing rivals, established businesses proved their deference-worthiness by acknowledging each other’s high status through careful observation and entering competitors’ domains with innovations. An innovation arms race thus drove market sophistication. The level of distinctiveness directly contradicted humanitarian assumptions about refugee uniformity and reinforced the primacy of backstage entrepreneurial identity.

Each week, in their strategy meetings, elite businesses attempted to discover competitors’ innovative offerings and strategies. For instance, an IT services business keenly observed and compared their strategies to that of their rivals. Being in IT service, many had posted their marketing ideas on the internet. Discussions mainly focused on insights gained from observing rivals’ marketing strategies. As they brainstormed, one member identified a strategy that was picked up by others: “There are still untapped markets in the villages. They lack IT skills and electricity services. We should find ways to get our services there without the knowledge of others.” Another member proudly added, “Our IT skill is better than anyone else across these villages, towns like Kasulu and even the city of Kigoma. They rely on us to develop software, to repair computers and for other technical IT skills. We are like an IT hub in this region, and we have to penetrate all households.” Assertions such as claiming regional expertise and planning market penetration reflected the entrepreneur identity that strategic inversion made primary.

Although they keenly observed their rivals’ strategies, entrepreneurs took great pains to keep their strategies secret from others. At a movie rental business founded by a young group of friends in Nyarugusu Camp, members anxiously debated their transition from CD and video cassettes to online video streaming. Having learned how to use streaming from an NGO, their business was the first to transition to online video streaming. In a crowded CD and video cassette-based rental service market, the group viewed streaming movies as a game-changer as it opened up a wide range of movies in Swahili, English, Kirundi, and French. A founding member explained how keen they were to guard this from their rivals: “We create a dynamic market for our clients. The extra training and work we get from the NGOs helps us manage our ups and downs. All of this just keeps our rivals guessing. Many of our competitors are confused about who our customers really are, how we keep them, and how we maintain our business.”

When rivals successfully entered domains with superior innovations, established businesses often pivoted to untapped areas with unprecedented offerings, reaffirming their deference-worthiness. We observed this dynamic when two prominent Nyarugusu bicycle repair businesses engaged in escalating innovation competition. The first business was concerned their rival was ahead in acquiring new village customers. Much discussion in their weekly meeting revolved around developing a new product: lubricant oil mixed from cooking and herb oils for bicycle greasing. As bicycles wore out faster during dry months, they planned to offer greasing services to clients who approached them for mechanical repairs, hoping clients might prefer them over rivals after learning about their added service. After several weeks, they gained more clients from their rival through the added service.

Rather than accepting defeat, the rival business brainstormed possible innovations to regain their lost customers. During one of the weekly meetings we attended, they discussed building bicycles that carried five to six passengers. When we returned a month later, this idea had not materialized, but they had diversified into making pushcarts and trolleys for village farmers using wood and used tires. Demand for their pushcarts and trolleys was high, putting them ahead in competition again. They also used worn-out tires to make sandals and storage bags, creating multiple new product lines. Table 3 presents more such examples of competitive actions and responses of established businesses. This capacity to pivot, diversify, and create new markets embodied the entrepreneurial agency the dignity economy made possible.

This innovation arms race created sophisticated market dynamics. Businesses constantly monitored competitors, developed countering strategies, pivoted when outmaneuvered, and sought new domains for establishing dominance. One clan-based business member captured this succinctly: “Everyone knows you’re only as good as your last season . . . but now, you also have to be fast and adaptable.” Another explained, “We don’t want to be in situations like [failed businesses]. We devise strategies that help us remain unique and stay ahead of competition.” An IT services entrepreneur echoed this pressure: “Some other youth groups began the way we did but have died. They got complacent and slept through changes in consumer tastes.” Success required constant innovation, aggressive strategy, and relentless competitive drive.

Overall, competition served a deeper purpose under benevolent oppression: claiming the forbidden present by recreating the dynamics of a real market in defiance of humanitarian uniformity. Dignity economies operate differently than extractive domination, where backstage solidarity requires that the oppressed suppress the “damaging excesses of competition between the poor for the few opportunities available” (Scott 1985:38). Intense competition for heshima strengthened strategic inversion by reconstructing normal market dynamics under abnormal conditions. By creating a functioning market where individuals could distinguish themselves through effort and innovation, refugees rejected the flattening logic of humanitarian governance that rendered them identical recipients of aid.

Cooperation within the Dignity Economy

Competition did not preclude cooperation (see Krishnan et al. 2021), the two operated in tandem. The pursuit of heshima encouraged competition but also required entrepreneurs to work together. This became clear during Umuganura, the harvest festival similar to American Thanksgiving, traditionally associated with promoting togetherness and belonging through harvest sharing (Bekenova 2016; Sayyad 2021). At the festival, entrepreneurs who competed fiercely throughout the year now publicly supported each other. Entrepreneurs with a good harvest shared some of their goods with seasonal traders who were struggling. Elite businesses, usually distant, became more open and friendly toward those outside their elite circle. The spirit of undugu (brotherhood) was clear in these interactions. This cooperation worked through the economy of affection, a network of support and social expectations that shapes economic relationships in many African societies (Hydén 1980). Undugu manifested as affective solidarity and market-based instrumentality across distinct types of relationships (Berry 1993; Lemarchand 1989). Small traders who had similar vulnerabilities held a real moral obligation to assist those in need. However, the language of brotherhood served a different purpose in the relationship between elite businesses and small traders: it enabled strategic exchanges in which small traders could cross status lines, and elites could build flexible supply networks.

Mutual Support among Small Traders

Among small traders, mutual support flowed through shared vulnerability. Dignity, a key aspect associated with performing the entrepreneur role, was not something small traders could take for granted in a market characterized by competition, seasonality of trade, and limited resources. Although all camp residents we met aspired to engage in some kind of trade, motivated by basic needs like supporting families back home, many could not sustain a steady flow of income through the year. Brotherhood, or undugu, became an important binding force in times of need; it helped tide camp residents over during difficult times and reminded them to stand together. Seasonal and weekly traders who enjoyed a bountiful harvest from their designated plots next to their tents were more active during Umuganura, driven by the spirit of undugu. We observed some sharing some of their garden produce (corn, pineapples, beans, and sweet potatoes) with their neighbors and even strangers experiencing hardship.

Brotherhood was also evident in small prayer group meetings. Many camp residents displayed strong spirituality. Small prayer group meetings served not only to remind participants of their common spiritual values, but also to organize support for those in need. Almost every night, small groups of adults gathered in tents or under trees to pray. The needy used this occasion to seek help. In a market where dignity associated with earning one’s own bread was a valued trait, residents hesitated to ask for help directly and instead used poems and proverbs to do so indirectly. Following prayers, financially secure entrepreneurs initiated conversations to understand others’ needs and offered support within their means.

During one prayer session we attended, a poem shared by a participant seemed to resonate with a seasonal trader. Sensing possible hardship, the trader approached the participant after the prayer. Upon learning he was a struggling small trader who could not afford to send his mother her monthly allowance in Burundi, he gestured to his friend to come over, and they both offered him assistance. Giving was viewed as a means of restoring a fellow resident’s (brother’s) dignity. In larger prayer groups, pastors often concluded their prayers with proverbs and verses from the bible that extolled the virtues of giving: “One person gives freely, yet gains even more; another withholds unduly, but comes to poverty. A generous person will prosper; whoever refreshes others will be refreshed. . . . If one part suffers, every part suffers with it; if one part is honored, every part rejoices with it.” Proverbs held deep meaning for camp residents, motivating them to donate whatever resources they had, be it food, clothing, or medicine. These proverbs and acts of giving reflected a shared understanding that their dignity was tied to the market, transcending individual gains.

These self-organized support systems directly contradicted the humanitarian premise that refugees required external provision to survive. By creating their own social safety net through prayer groups, harvest sharing, and mutual assistance, camp residents demonstrated the economic agency and community capacity that benevolent oppression systematically denied them.

Relationships between Elite Businesses and Small Traders

Undugu carried a more instrumental meaning between elite businesses and small traders. For small traders, Umuganura served a strategic purpose beyond celebration. Many used the festival as an opportunity to initiate undugu with established businesses—connections that could lead to employment or partnerships. Considering the general undugu spirit prevalent during Umuganura, it was a time when established businesses were open to friendly interactions with seasonal and weekly traders who normally remained outside the elite circle. We observed several seasonal traders hanging around after harvest celebrations ended, attempting to catch the attention of elite business members. Some received only passing greetings, but others managed to initiate conversations about their businesses and aspirations. One ambitious seasonal trader explained, “I sell vegetables from my plot but I also sell anything that is valuable. . . . I would like to work full-time for a well-known business and learn business skills. I made some good connections today.”

Invitations to personal events, such as weddings and baptisms hosted by members of established businesses, served as tangible manifestations of undugu. These invitations elevated small traders’ heshima among their peers and ignited hope for similar recognition. During a wedding, our conversation with uninvited small traders who lingered outside the festivities centered around the family’s prestige and the one trader fortunate enough to receive an invitation. A trader explained, “He works for them, that’s why. But our turn will come too.” This exchange revealed a shared understanding that these invitations were earned tokens of respect.

Elite businesses also relied instrumentally on small traders in ways that provided competitive advantages. When established businesses faced shortages of critical supplies, they turned to small traders who could quickly source needed items—relationships that gave elites an edge over rivals. As one small trader proudly asserted after being called upon during a malaria pill shortage, “They ran out of malaria pills, and who did they call? Us! They want to do business with us.” These moments were not just financially lucrative for small traders: they were public declarations of worth that elevated their standing among peers. For elites, maintaining networks of smaller traders provided flexibility and access to supplies when competing with rival businesses. These relationships illuminate ongoing debates about African economies of affection. Among small traders, cooperation flowed through affective bonds rooted in shared vulnerability and moral obligation (Hydén 1980). Between elite businesses and small traders, however, undugu opened the door to a more strategic relationship. Small traders leveraged moments of elite need into public declarations of their worth; elites maintained these networks because they provided supply flexibility and competitive advantage. As Lemarchand (1989) argues, affection and self-interest coexist in any exchange, but their proportions vary. Among equals, shared vulnerability gave rise to moral obligations in exchange. Across status lines, self-interest weighed more heavily and exchange became contingent on the interests of both parties.

Competition and cooperation constituted resistance to benevolent oppression by constructing an alternative social order where dignity could be earned. Competition created genuine status differentiation that challenged the refugee tag’s flattening logic; cooperation built networks of mutual support that contradicted the helplessness narrative. Both were necessary for strategic inversion—the systematic transformation of the backstage into the primary arena of social life.

Discussion

Drawing on ethnographic evidence from Tanzanian refugee camps, our study revealed how dominated populations responded to benevolent oppression through “strategic inversion.” Specifically, refugees in our study collectively transformed backstage spaces into their primary social worlds. Simultaneously, they maintained frontstage performances as protective shields to conceal their clandestine activities. These frontstage performances manifested as “compliance flooding,” which involved dense, excessive performances of helplessness that generated bureaucratic noise and diverted attention from their clandestine economic activities. In strategic inversion, the relationship between frontstage and backstage inverts, reconfiguring the dramaturgical architecture when domination operates through care rather than extraction. Refugees did not merely cope with benevolent oppression or carve out marginal spaces of autonomy; they built elaborate dignity economies that became the organizing force of camp life. They relegated official camp life to instrumental performance, which served to protect what truly mattered. Within these dignity economies, competition and cooperation were central to the pursuit of heshima, reestablishing forms of market interaction that challenged the expectation that refugees would remain passive, dependent recipients of aid.

Theoretical Contributions

Our study extends research on everyday resistance by showing how the relationship between frontstage and backstage shifts when domination operates through provision rather than extraction. In Goffman’s (1959) framework, the frontstage is the primary arena of social life where performances are enacted, status is conferred, and consequential action occurs. The backstage, by contrast, serves as a space for preparation and respite from frontstage demands. Even when performers feign frontstage compliance, the frontstage remains primary because it is where social hierarchies are determined and maintained (Scott 1985, 1990). Our findings show that the way power is exercised directly shapes how the backstage functions in relation to the frontstage. Humanitarian authorities provided basic resources, such as rations and vocational training, but refugees were denied formal opportunities to translate these resources into independent livelihoods. However, refugees repurposed these resources to create alternative economies, and within these spaces, actual social hierarchies emerged.

The alternative social order was organized around heshima, which made dignity associated with earning one’s own bread the prerequisite for recognition as a full human being, and allowed refugees to move beyond humanitarian dependency. In the dignity economies we observed, the entrepreneur identity that refugees performed backstage shaped their actual social standing. The entrepreneurial identity provided dignity, shaped exchange relationships, and governed the local status hierarchy. The refugee identity they performed on the frontstage became instrumental. The usual ordering of dramaturgical space is reversed under benevolent oppression. Goffman (1959) and Scott (1990) describe frontstage performances as the place where social hierarchies are determined, even when those performances are feigned. In the camps, the backstage did this work, and the frontstage was sustained for tactical reasons; a systematic reordering we identify as strategic inversion.

In the dignity economy we studied, competition and cooperation operated together, not as a paradox (Scott 1985) but as complementary expressions in the pursuit of heshima. Competition enabled entrepreneurs to resist the homogenizing effects of humanitarian categorization by differentiating themselves through innovation, guarding strategies from rivals, and fighting for status distinctions that humanitarian categories denied. Refugees resisted the helplessness narrative through cooperation. Prayer groups organized support for struggling traders, and harvest festivals enabled the redistribution of resources. Elite businesses extended opportunities to smaller traders, who reciprocated with flexibility and supplies. By reconstructing market normalcy with genuine competition, status differentiation (Krishnan and Kozhikode 2015), and mutual obligation, refugees rejected their assigned role as uniform, dependent recipients of aid.

Next, resistance under benevolent oppression operates through surfeit, rather than sufficiency, of compliance. Under extractive domination, public behavior is often a calculated performance. Scott (1985:273, 284) explains how “deference and conformity, though rarely cringing, continue to be the public posture of the poor.” Compliance is minimal and “a protective disguise” that conceals backstage defiance. Under benevolent oppression, we found resistance operated through dense, excessive compliance that generated so much “noise” (Innes and Dawson 2022:522; Starr 2020) that authorities could not distinguish permitted from prohibited activities. By engaging in compliance flooding, refugees weaponized the helplessness narrative that humanitarian agencies sought to project. Refugees in our study stretched market negotiations to mask digital exchanges and engaged enthusiastically with NGO programs while extracting resources for underground businesses. Through these practices, refugees performed desperate dependency so thoroughly that it became a shield. Extractive domination demands that resistance cover its tracks, making modest compliance strategic. Benevolent oppression, on the other hand, incentivizes performed dependency, making excessive compliance possible.

Finally, our findings shed new light on long-standing debates about the capacity of everyday resistance to challenge power structures. For Scott (1985:350), everyday resistance “prevents the worst and promises something better,” enabling survival within existing structures, preserving hope for their transformation but not fundamentally altering them. Ewick and Silbey (2003:1330) similarly describe backstage resistance as “providing temporary relief from the burden that power imposes.” Comprehensive reviews of resistance scholarship have argued that power-resistance dynamics perpetuate rather than transform subordination: everyday resistance may “strengthen power or create new forms of power techniques” (Vinthagen and Johansson 2013:29) within fundamentally cyclical dynamics where “domination leads to resistance, which leads to the further exercise of power, provoking further resistance” (Hollander and Einwohner 2004:548). These widely held assessments share a common empirical foundation of extractive domination, where backstage practices necessarily remain subordinate because frontstage is where material appropriation occurs—landlords still control rice fields, institutions still command compliance. Our findings demonstrate that under benevolent oppression, everyday resistance can transform, not merely adjust to, the dominant order.

Under extractive domination, subordinates can only imagine alternative moral orders (Scott 1977), or experience them momentarily in bounded rituals like Carnival (Browne 2015; Scott 1977), before returning to subordinate positions. Under benevolent oppression, refugees lived this alternative order daily. Humanitarian categories of vulnerability yielded to entrepreneurial hierarchies; the weekly sanctioned market became mere theater, and real commerce happened through hidden channels. The dignity economies we documented were not marginal adjustments to humanitarian control, but comprehensive alternative orders with their own status hierarchies and moral frameworks. The scale was systematic: virtually all significant transactions violated official rules through e-money platforms; trade networks extended throughout surrounding villages; and underground businesses accumulated substantial capital.

Implications for Refugee Studies

These findings carry implications for refugee studies. Scholars have documented how humanitarian regimes create a temporal paradox by preparing refugees for market participation while prohibiting economic action in the present (Stokes 2019). Aid agencies characterize camp life as “limbo,” “waiting,” or “suspended animation,” where populations exist in perpetual crisis, passively awaiting solutions that never arrive (Agier 2011; Hyndman 2000). Strategic inversion demonstrates that refugees refuse this temporal script not by escaping the camp but by reclaiming the present within it. Through dignity economies, refugees build businesses, compete for status, and pursue heshima in real time. Time moves forward in the backstage even as the frontstage performs patient waiting.

This perspective reframes the central question for refugee studies. Recent ethnographies compellingly document refugee agency as refugees use humanitarian systems (Jansen 2018), create informal economies (Bochmann 2019), and resist imposed identities (Turner 2005). Yet most scholarship asks, “how much agency do refugees possess within oppressive structures?” Strategic inversion suggests researchers ought to shift analytic focus from documenting agency within humanitarian regimes to theorizing how agency reconfigures the symbolic structure of camp life. If backstage economies actually supplant, rather than merely supplement, official structures as the primary arena of social organization, the question becomes how empowerment unfolds in the backstage arena. If humanitarian governance intends to position refugees as victims, strategic inversion captures how refugees render that positioning theatrical. Refugees maintain performances of suspended, dependent life while constructing their own temporal order, living for the present rather than waiting for futures that never arrive.

Generalizability and Future Research

To what extent are these findings generalizable to settings beyond refugee camps? Our findings suggest that strategic inversion may emerge wherever domination operates through provision rather than extraction, and where the governed share sufficient proximity to coordinate collective responses. The first condition appears across diverse settings of humanitarian governance: for example, French welfare recipients recounting difficult experiences accessing social funds (Fassin 2012); undocumented immigrants being granted residence on the basis of illness but denied the right to work (Ticktin 2006); and mothers encountering child protective services where support brings with it the authority to separate families (Fong 2020). The second condition is more variable. In the absence of physical co-location, welfare recipients, undocumented immigrants, and CPS-surveilled mothers may coordinate through social networks, local ties, or recurring institutional encounters. Both conditions are present in long-term shelters, immigration detention facilities, and residential treatment programs where care-based domination operates alongside sustained co-located interaction. These are the settings where strategic inversion is most likely to emerge. Future research may examine whether similar dynamics also emerge in dispersed populations governed by provision and whether they take a recognizably collective form.

The temporal variation in policy further strengthens our theoretical claims. Following our fieldwork in 2015, the Tanzanian government temporarily relaxed economic restrictions. The government authorized a regulated “common market” that allowed refugees to engage in cash transactions with villagers nearby. However, in 2018, the authorities shut down the common market, giving rise to stringent conditions once again (Msoka et al. 2025). Yet, closing the common market did not stop refugees from engaging in economic exchanges with neighboring villages (Msoka and Kweka 2021). Msoka and Kweka (2021) document how refugees and host communities adapted by creating new informal spaces outside camp boundaries for economic exchange. Refugees’ response to these cycles of policy relaxation and restriction suggests the strategic inversion we observed did not constitute a singular historical episode. Instead, this inversion recurs in response to shifting structural conditions. The re-emergence of these dynamics following the reinstatement of restrictive policies in 2018 strengthens our theoretical claims about strategic inversion under benevolent oppression. Future research could examine how strategic inversion adapts when authorities tighten or loosen control.

Through strategic inversion, refugees refused the temporal logic that benevolent oppression sought to impose on them. By creating dignity economies, refugees reclaimed control over their present lives. Skeptics might argue these are simply informal economies, not resistance. This view, however, mistakes mechanism for meaning. Strategic inversion constituted resistance by systematically reordering what counts as primary social life. Refugees did not carve out “pockets of sovereignty” at the margins of humanitarian control (Turner 2005:324); they made the hidden economy the organizing center of social life, and reduced official camp life to instrumental performance. Our theoretical contribution lies in specifying conditions under which rule-breaking becomes the organizing center of social existence, and not in merely documenting it.
