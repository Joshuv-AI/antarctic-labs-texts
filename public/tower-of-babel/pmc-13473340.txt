Comparison of tooth surface roughness after bleaching with conventional and different light-activated methods
Authors: Nozhan Azimi, Bahareh Sanaee, Hasti Seifi, Ali Azadi, Nasim Chiniforush, Hanieh Nokhbatolfoghahaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose the increasing demand for aesthetic dental treatments has made in-office tooth bleaching a popular and minimally invasive procedure. However, bleaching may alter enamel surface roughness, potentially increasing plaque retention and discoloration. Laser activation has been proposed as a safer alternative. This study compared the effects of conventional bleaching, LED activation, and different laser systems (Diode, KTP, Nd-YAG, and CO₂) on enamel surface roughness. Methods sixty freshly extracted human anterior teeth were selected. Samples were randomly assigned to conventional bleaching (without light activation), LED bleaching, and laser bleaching groups, including KTP, Diode, Nd-YAG, and CO2 lasers (n = 10). Pre-treatment surface roughness evaluation was done for all samples. Samples were treated with Opalescence Xtra Boost (Ultradent, USA) for the control group and JW Power Bleaching Gel (Heydent GmbH, Germany) for other groups. Surface roughness was evaluated after bleaching in all groups. Kruskal-Wallis test and Two-Way ANOVA were done to statistically analyze study data, using GraphPad Prism v9, and P-values lower than 0.05 were considered significant. Results LED caused significantly greater surface roughness change compared to bleaching without light (P = 0.0236). Laser-activated groups did not differ significantly from the conventional group or from each other. In the middle third, LED activation resulted in significantly higher roughness compared with the KTP laser (P = 0.029). Conclusion LED activation resulted in greater roughness changes, particularly in the middle third of enamel, while laser activation generally preserved enamel surface integrity, compared to the conventional group.

Introduction

The aesthetic demand for brighter smiles has made dental bleaching a widely utilized and minimally invasive procedure for enhancing tooth shade and color [1]. High concentrations of oxidizing agents, such as 30% to 40% hydrogen peroxide (HP) or 35% to 37% carbamide peroxide (CP), are commonly employed during in-office bleaching protocols [2, 3]. The fundamental mechanism involves HP permeating the interprismatic spaces of the enamel and dentinal tubules, generating oxygen species (ROS) that modify pigmented molecules [1]. Various methods are employed in order to accelerate the whitening process, including the application of light or laser activation systems, to decrease chair time and minimize the gel-tissue interaction time of these potentially toxic agents [4]. Sources such as light-emitting diodes (LEDs), halogen lamps, or lasers can be employed for light-activated bleaching, also referred to as photo-oxidation [5].

Laser irradiation, typically using monochromatic light, generates heat (photothermal effects) that raises the temperature of the bleaching agent, accelerating the generation of ROS and the dissociation of pigmented molecules [1]. Some commonly studied laser types in dental bleaching procedures include the diode laser (often in the near-infrared range, such as 810 nm, 940 nm, or 980 nm) [3], the Nd-YAG laser (1064 nm) [6], CO2 laser (10600 nm) and the KTP laser (532 nm) [7, 8].

A primary concern regarding bleaching procedures is the potential adverse effects on dental hard tissues [9, 10]. The main alterations observed in enamel include changes in morphology, surface porosity, and surface roughness, as well as a decrease in microhardness [11, 12]. Increased surface roughness is a serious complication following bleaching, as rougher surfaces may predispose the tooth to dental caries, discoloration, and increased plaque adhesion (e.g., Streptococcus mutans) [2]. These adverse effects depend on factors such as the composition of the bleaching gels, the peroxide concentration, the pH value, and the application time [13].

Considering these concerns, this study has focused on evaluating different activation techniques, including LED and various laser types (Diode, KTP, Nd-YAG, CO2), to identify protocols that optimize efficacy while minimizing hard tissue changes. Studies have specifically examined the influence of laser activation on enamel microroughness. While conventional (no light) and broadband light activation methods have been linked to significant increases in surface roughness, laser activation has generally been reported to cause milder increases or no significant alterations to the enamel surface, potentially offering a safer technique [2, 14, 15]. Given the need to establish the least invasive yet effective bleaching technique, the current study aims to compare the effects of conventional (no light), LED, and different types of laser activation on tooth enamel surface roughness during in-office bleaching.

Methods and materials

The study was conducted experimentally and in-vitro. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences.

Sample preparation

A total of 60 extracted human anterior teeth, which were free of caries or fractures and extracted for periodontal or orthodontic reasons, were selected. The sample size was determined based on the information obtained from a study [16] on the effect of different concentrations of bleaching agents on enamel surface roughness, with n = 10 per group. After cleaning and polishing with prophylaxis paste, the teeth were stored in 0.5% chloramine-T for one week and then kept in distilled water at room temperature (25 °C) until the beginning of the experiment. The teeth were then mounted using a combination of transparent self-cure acrylic (Bayer Dental, Germany), with the labial surfaces of each tooth exposed. Each specimen was marked at the upper (incisal), middle, and lower (cervical) thirds, and the evaluations were performed in these regions.

Bleaching of the samples

Extracted teeth were divided into six groups (n = 10), and their bleaching and activation procedures were as follows and summarized in Table 1. Pre-treatment surface roughness was measured before grouping the teeth samples, and then they were randomly assigned to the control and experiment groups. Samples in the control group were bleached using a conventional office bleaching technique with Opalescence Xtra Boost (Ultradent, USA), whereas those in the other groups were subjected to laser bleaching with JW Power Bleaching Gel (Heydent GmbH, Germany).

Study group characteristics

Conventional office bleaching (control)

The two syringes (Base and Activator) were mixed, and the resulting red gel was applied to the tooth surface at a thickness of 2 mm. After 20 min, the gel was removed and rinsed off. After a 1-minute rest period, the procedure was repeated twice more. Finally, the teeth were dried.

Activation of bleaching material by LED lamp

After mixing the two syringes (Base and Activator), the red gel was applied at a 2 mm thickness. The LED light (Radii Plus, SDI) was applied to the gel for 30 s, followed by a 1-minute rest period. This process was repeated three times. After a final 3-minute rest, the gel was removed and rinsed. (The handpiece was positioned 1–2 mm from the tooth surface, covering the entire tooth.)

Laser bleaching

Activation of bleaching material by KTP laser

After mixing the syringes, the 2 mm layer of red gel was applied, and the KTP laser (Starline, Lambda Physik, Germany) (λ = 532 nm; E = 4 mJ; t = 20 s; P = 0.2 W; R = 10 Hz; D = 1–2 mm) was applied for 30 s. The tooth was then rested for 1 min. The process was repeated three times, followed by a 3-minute rest before removing and rinsing the gel.

Activation of bleaching material by Diode laser

After mixing and applying the red gel (2 mm), the Diode laser (Gigaa laser, China) (Biostimulation handpiece, λ = 810 nm; P = 1.5 W; Mode = CW; D = 1–2 mm) was applied for 30 s, followed by a 1-minute rest. This was repeated three times, then a 3-minute rest was given before cleaning and rinsing. (The handpiece was 1–2 mm from the tooth, covering the entire surface.)

Activation of bleaching material by Nd-YAG laser

After applying the 2 mm-thick gel, the Nd-YAG laser (Fotona, Slovenia, EU) (λ = 1064 nm; E = 100 mJ; t = 20 s; R = 25 Hz, P = 2.5 W, D = 1 mm) was applied for 30 s, followed by 1 min of rest. The cycle was repeated three times, then followed by a 3-minute rest before removing and rinsing the gel.

Activation of bleaching material by CO₂ laser

After mixing and applying the 2 mm gel layer, the CO₂ laser (Daeshin, model DS-40U, Daeshin Corp. Seoul, Korea) (λ = 10,600 nm; P = 0.6 W; R = 20 Hz; Level 3; Distance: 1–2 mm) was applied for 30 s, followed by 1 min of rest. This was repeated three times, then followed by a 3-minute rest before the gel was removed and rinsed. (The handpiece was positioned about 1–2 mm from the tooth, covering the entire surface.)

Surface roughness evaluation

Pre- and post-treatment surface roughness of all samples was evaluated with a profilometer (TR200, Qualitest, USA). Roughness tester tip touched the tooth surface and explored 2.5 mm diagonally. For each region of the tooth surface, three separate readings were recorded at different locations, and the average surface roughness was determined for each sample. Ra showed the mean surface roughness of enamel.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data distribution. The pooled Ra values (combining all tooth regions) did not follow a normal distribution; therefore, the Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn’s post-hoc test was applied to compare mean Ra change among experimental groups. Moreover, Ra values analyzed separately by tooth region showed normal distribution. Therefore, Two-Way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc test were used to compare the mean Ra change of the tooth’s various regions in different groups. All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism v9, and p-values lower than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Overall, LED caused significantly greater Ra change (Ra difference pre- and post-treatment) compared to bleaching without light (P = 0.0236) (Fig. 1). Laser groups (KTP, Diode, Nd-YAG, CO₂) did not significantly differ from the conventional group or from each other, suggesting they did not worsen surface roughness under our experimental conditions (P > 0.05).

Between-region comparisons (Fig. 2)

In the incisal and cervical regions, no statistically significant differences were observed among any of the activation methods (all P > 0.05). In the middle third, a significant difference was found between the LED (Ra Change = 0.46 ± 0.12) and KTP (Ra Change = 0.18 ± 0.06) groups (P = 0.029). The LED group exhibited a higher Ra change than the KTP group, suggesting that LED activation may induce more pronounced enamel alterations in this area.

Within-group comparisons (surface roughness among tooth regions) (Fig. 3)

Within-group analyses were conducted to determine whether Ra varied among the incisal, middle, and cervical regions of the same tooth for each bleaching activation type.

For the conventional, LED, KTP, Nd-YAG, and CO₂ groups, no statistically significant differences were observed between the three regions (all P > 0.05). This indicates that bleaching or laser exposure in these groups produced a uniform effect across the enamel surface.

In contrast, the Diode group exhibited a significant difference between the incisal (Ra Change = 0.14 ± 0.05) and cervical (Ra Change = 0.46 ± 0.18) regions (P = 0.015), with the cervical region showing higher Ra values. Additionally, a near-significant difference was observed between the middle and cervical regions (P = 0.051), suggesting a tendency for increased surface alteration toward the cervical area.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to compare the effects of conventional, LED, and laser-assisted tooth bleaching methods on enamel surface roughness. Overall, LED-activated bleaching resulted in greater enamel surface roughness changes than bleaching without light, whereas laser-assisted bleaching did not increase surface roughness compared to the conventional method. From a clinical perspective, the LED group demonstrated a marked increase in Ra, suggesting a more pronounced alteration of the enamel surface, increasing the possibility of tooth staining, biofilm accumulation, and caries. Laser groups exhibited smaller changes in Ra, with values remaining relatively close to those of the conventional bleaching group, and they appear to preserve enamel surface characteristics to a greater extent. According to previous studies, laser wavelength, energy density, and activation mode critically influence enamel surface changes [1, 15]. LED activation caused the highest Ra change, which aligns with multiple recent in-vitro studies reporting that LED-assisted bleaching can increase enamel roughness more than laser-assisted protocols [3, 17]. This may be because LEDs often provide broad-spectrum high-intensity visible light, producing uneven thermal effects [17]. Additionally, many commercial LED units lack wavelength-gel optimization, so localized overheating and dehydration of enamel occur. Möbius et al. found that LED-activated bleaching caused greater surface irregularity than diode lasers [14]. Similarly, Anagnostaki et al. noted higher surface roughness and dehydration risk with LED units compared to specific laser wavelengths [1].

Teeth were stored in 0.5% Chloramine-T after extraction which is a widely recommended for the disinfection of extracted teeth, eliminating microbial contamination and maintaining the structural integrity of dental hard tissues. Chloramine‑T does not significantly alter enamel morphology or microhardness [18]. After disinfection, the specimens were stored in distilled water at room temperature (25 °C) until the beginning of the experiment to prevent dehydration and minimize potential chemical alterations of the enamel surface.

Two different bleaching gels were used because each material is specifically designed for a different activation protocol. JW Power Bleaching gel (Heydent GmbH, Germany) was used as the bleaching gel for groups with laser or light activation, as this gel is formulated for use with laser or high-energy light sources. To be more specific, it directly activates the peroxide through photo-thermal and photo-chemical mechanisms, accelerating free-radical release, enhancing bleaching efficacy, and allowing effective whitening in shorter exposure times with controlled enamel effects [19]. Recent studies confirm that laser‑compatible gels require tailored material characteristics, stabilizers, and pH buffering to withstand rapid heating and to ensure controlled enamel interaction during laser activation [4]. On the other hand, Opalescence Xtra Boost (Ultradent, USA) was used for the control group, as it is chemically and self-activated and is not optimized for laser interaction. Evidence shows these gels behave differently when exposed to laser light, often with no additional benefit or potentially uncontrolled thermal effects [20]. We acknowledge that using gels with different formulations and hydrogen peroxide concentrations introduces a limitation; however, our intention was to evaluate clinically relevant bleaching protocols rather than isolate activation mechanisms in a fully standardized chemical environment.

Regarding gel types used, they are both high-concentration, in-office bleaching agents based on Hydrogen Peroxide (HP). JW Power Bleaching Gel contains approximately 35% HP, a neutral pH, and added minerals, which may help limit enamel demineralization. Opalescence Xtra Boost contains a higher HP (~ 40%), includes fluoride and potassium nitrate and has a neutral pH too. Fluoride may reduce mineral loss and preserve enamel microhardness [13]. Both gel types have a neutral pH; therefore, it is less likely that they promote enamel demineralization and surface irregularity in terms of their pH.

KTP laser caused moderate, homogeneous roughness, smoother than LED and all other laser types. These findings align with a study by Awati et al. and Kiryk et al., who reported that KTP laser (532 nm) shows low intrapulpal temperature rise and minimal surface alteration while effectively activating bleaching gels [15, 21]. The 532 nm wavelength is well absorbed by pigments but less by mineral tissue, so KTP efficiently decomposes H₂O₂ without overheating enamel [21]. Hence, our KTP results reflect what the current literature describes as the safest balance between whitening efficacy and surface integrity.

Diode laser produced the smoothest surface on incisal regions, but increased roughness cervically. This is likely due to variations in enamel thickness and specific in-depth activity of diode lasers [7]. Enamel is thinner in the cervical region, making it more vulnerable to demineralization and increased porosity. While the diode laser’s efficiency allows for less contact time between the gel and tooth; resulting in smoother surfaces in thicker incisal areas, its deep penetration can cause more significant structural alterations and brittleness in thinner cervical structures [7, 22]. However, in comparison to Nd-YAG or CO₂ lasers, Shahabi et al. and Saffarpour et al. reported diode lasers at 445–970 nm cause less enamel alteration than Nd-YAG or CO₂, due to lower absorption in hydroxyapatite and shallower energy penetration [6, 8].

The relatively high Ra change in Nd-YAG in our findings may correspond to a deep infrared wavelength (1064 nm) with greater penetration and thermal diffusion, potentially altering enamel microstructure. Additionally, several recent reports showed Nd-YAG may cause subtle surface changes or microcracks at higher energies [6, 23, 24].

CO₂ laser yielded mid-range Ra change values in our study, again, consistent with Laky et al.’s study showing CO₂ can induce superficial mineral fusion or melting, depending on energy. Moderate roughness may arise from alternating micro-melting and recrystallization rather than true demineralization [25].

Our results reinforce the wavelength-dependent influence of photoactivation on enamel surface properties. LED and Nd-YAG lasers; likely due to their broad or deep thermal profiles, produced significantly higher surface roughness, while KTP and diode lasers maintained smoother enamel comparable to or better than conventional bleaching. The diode laser’s localized cervical effect suggests that enamel thickness and regional thermal conductivity modulate surface outcomes.

A limitation of this study is its in-vitro design, which does not fully replicate clinical conditions, such as saliva simulation; therefore, future research should investigate in vivo conditions with larger sample sizes and standardized bleaching protocols to better evaluate the long-term effects of different light-activation methods on enamel integrity. Furthermore, the use of two different bleaching gels might introduce a limitation since gel formulations and Hydrogen Peroxide concentrations differ. Given the increasing clinical use of diode lasers in dental practice, future studies should focus on assessing diode lasers with different wavelengths and parameter settings.

Conclusion

Within the limits of this in-vitro study, most light-activated bleaching methods did not significantly increase enamel surface roughness under the tested conditions compared to conventional bleaching. LED activation, however, resulted in higher Ra values overall and showed a notably greater effect in the middle third of enamel when compared with the KTP laser. Surface roughness was generally uniform across enamel regions for most activation methods.
