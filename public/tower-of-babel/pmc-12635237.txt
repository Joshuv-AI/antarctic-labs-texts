Genomic and biogeographic patterns of endemic geckos in the Galapagos Islands reveal population structure and species delimitation on inhabited islands
Authors: Gabriela Pozo, Juan José Guadalupe, María José Pozo, Diego F Cisneros-Heredia, José Cerca, Pablo Alarcón-Bolaños, María Victoria Suárez, Mateo Dávila-Játiva, Emilia Peñaherrera-Romero, David Brito-Zapata, Daniel Velasco-Cedeño, Maria de Lourdes Torres
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIslands offer invaluable opportunities for studying evolutionary processes due to their isolation and distinct environmental conditions. The Galapagos Islands, renowned for their rich biodiversity, host several endemic gecko species of the genus Phyllodactylus (Gekkota: Phyllodactylidae). Despite their importance derived from their specialized adaptations and their crucial role in maintaining ecosystem balance, few studies have been conducted on these geckos. This highlights the need for comprehensive genomic research to understand their evolutionary patterns and population dynamics. This study elucidates the genetic diversity and population structure of six endemic Phyllodactylus species found on four human-inhabited islands in the Galapagos using a RAD-Seq approach. The analysis of over 9000 loci from 93 individuals revealed five distinct genetic clusters, corresponding to P. baurii, P. galapagensis, P. darwini, P. leei, and a combined cluster of P. simpsoni and P. andysabini. Our results indicate that P. galapagensis clusters with the combined P. simpsoni–P. andysabini group, while P. baurii shows close genetic relationships with both clusters, in accordance with the obtained phylogeny and the sequential emergence of the Galapagos Islands where each species is found. Substantial genetic differentiation was observed between species, with high FST and DXY values. However, our analyses indicate that gecko populations from across Isabela and Fernandina islands exhibit very low genetic differentiation, leading us to propose the synonymization of P. andysabini with P. simpsoni. Within-species population structure was associated with geographic barriers and gene flow restrictions. Surprisingly, human activity does not appear to be causing significant admixture among these populations; instead, population boundaries remain intact, indicating that geographic or behavioral barriers are stronger than human influences in limiting gene flow. Overall, we found low genetic diversity across species, probably due to their endemic nature and island isolation. This genomic study provides insights into the evolutionary dynamics shaping these unique geckos and highlights the importance of employing high-resolution genomic tools in insular ecosystems for their effective conservation and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24790-2.Keywords: Galapagos Islands, Geckos, Phyllodactylus, Genetic diversity, Population structure, RAD-seqSubject terms: Biogeography, Population genetics, Genetic variation

Introduction

Islands offer invaluable opportunities for understanding evolutionary processes due to their isolation and distinct environmental conditions1,2. Two important factors when studying evolutionary processes on islands are oceanic and topographic barriers. In volcanic islands, eruption events and landslides can produce habitat fragmentation and isolate populations, driving genetic differentiation and speciation within islands3. Nonetheless, patterns of genetic differentiation between populations from different islands and populations within the same island suggest oceanic barriers are more effective than topographic barriers at promoting isolation in islands4,5. The Galapagos Islands are renowned for their ecological significance and unique biodiversity. This volcanic archipelago located approximately 1000 km off the coast of Ecuador serves as a natural laboratory for understanding mechanisms of adaptation and speciation6,7.

The fauna of the Galapagos Islands includes large reptiles, such as giant tortoises, which are one of the best-studied inhabitants of the Galapagos8–11 , while information about the evolutionary patterns, species diversity, biogeography, and ecological dynamics of smaller reptiles like the Galapagos leaf-toed geckos and Galapagos racer snakes remain unexplored12,13. Many gecko clades have dispersed and diversified in island ecosystems due to their ability to traverse wide barriers14. Geckos in island ecosystems have unique phenotypes due to the evolutionary processes resulting from their geographic isolation, which can profoundly impact their genetic structure, also influenced by intraspecific competition15–17.

The Galapagos Islands are home to eleven endemic species of leaf-toed geckos of the genus Phyllodactylus (family Phyllodactylidae)12,18. Additionally, four species of non-native geckos have arrived in the Galapagos over the last two centuries and established populations in human-inhabited islands (family Gekkonidae: Hemidactylus frenatus and Lepidodactylus lugubris; Phyllodactylidae: Phyllodactylus reissii; and Sphaerodactylidae: Gonatodes caudiscutatus)13,19–28.

Galapagos leaf-toed geckos are primarily nocturnal, foraging on the ground, rocky crops, tree trunks, or buildings, and seeking shelter during the day under rocks, tree trunks, wood, lava blocks, and cactus stumps29–32. Six of the eleven endemic species of leaf-toed geckos occur on human-inhabited islands: Phyllodactylus andysabini from Isabela Island, Phyllodactylus baurii from Floreana Island, Phyllodactylus leei and P. darwini from San Cristobal Island, Phyllodactylus galapagensis from Santa Cruz Island, and Phyllodactylus simpsoni from Isabela12,32. P. simpsoni is also found on Fernandina Island, which is an uninhabited island. Phyllodactylus andysabini and P. simpsoni are allopatric in Isabela Island, with P. andysabini restricted to the northern part of the island, while P. simpsoni occurs across central and southern regions. Only two of these species occur in sympatry, P. leei and P. darwini, both inhabiting San Cristobal Island. However, they are easily differentiated by their body size, with P. darwini being the largest endemic gecko in the archipelago32. These geckos face threats such as habitat change and loss, as well as impacts from introduced species that may act as predators, competitors, or carriers of diseases and parasites30,31,33,34, similar to what has happened to endemic gecko populations on other tropical islands35–42. It is suspected that gecko populations are decreasing in these islands30,31,33,34, where ongoing introductions of non-native species coincide with habitat change and loss resulting from urbanization and agriculture. These activities could reduce local population sizes, disrupt mating patterns, and facilitate the translocation of non-native geckos that may act as competitors, predators and disease carriers, thus altering gene flow and reshaping population structure. Nonetheless, there are no studies that have tested whether these anthropogenic drivers of dispersal or fragmentation have led to genetic admixture, population bottlenecks, or newly evolving lineages on these islands.

Endemic species hold significant ecological and evolutionary importance, due to their highly specialized adaptations to unique environments, making them key indicators of ecosystem health and resilience. Their restricted geographic ranges and high vulnerability to environmental changes underscore their critical role in conservation efforts to preserve biodiversity and maintain ecosystem balance43. Despite this, there are few genetic studies on Phyllodactylus species native to Galapagos, and to our knowledge, no genomic studies have been reported to date. Existing genetic research has relied on nuclear and mitochondrial markers to investigate their evolutionary history and diversity, predominantly focusing on understanding the biogeographic patterns of the genus and its historical colonization dynamics within the archipelago12,44,45. Studies from 2014 and 2016 by Torres-Carvajal et al. used nuclear and mitochondrial markers to shed light on the evolutionary history and diversification of the Phyllodactylus genus from Galapagos and test colonization hypothesis12,44. Torres-Carvajal et al. (2014, 2016) inferred that these geckos originated from three separate colonization events: one approximately 13 million years ago that gave rise to most extant species, another about 10 million years later that led to the emergence of P. darwini on San Cristobal Island, and a more recent event, less than one million years ago, resulting in the establishment of P. gilberti on Wolf Island12,44. Moreover, the study by Arteaga et al. (2019) described two new species from Isabela Island and proposed an updated molecular phylogeny hypothesis of the genus Phyllodactyus in the Galapagos29.

The aforementioned studies have provided evidence of broad-scale biogeographic patterns—such as the timing and routes of colonization—and underscored considerable diversity within the archipelago, including previously unrecognized lineages. However, nuclear and mitochondrial markers alone provide limited resolution of intraspecific diversity, especially regarding recent population-level processes. Key questions remain about how ongoing ecological changes, including habitat alteration and species introductions, might shape finer-scale population structure, gene flow, and potentially reveal cryptic lineages. Genomic-level studies targeting Galapagos Phyllodactylus would provide the high-resolution data needed to elucidate population structure and genetic diversity more thoroughly while also providing insights into whether and how human-driven ecological and environmental changes might shape these processes. Such an approach is particularly relevant for broader evolutionary and conservation biology because the Galapagos Islands have long served as a global model for understanding speciation, adaptive radiation, and the interplay between natural and anthropogenic forces6,46–48.

The aim of this study was to understand the genetic diversity and population structure of the six endemic Galapagos gecko species that are found on human-inhabited islands using RAD-Seq (Restriction Site Associated DNA Sequencing), which has proven to be a powerful method for understanding population dynamics and evolutionary processes in various organisms49,50. Our results reveal well-defined species boundaries, with one notable exception, alongside a robust population structure apparently not influenced by anthropogenic factors, and low levels of genetic diversity. These findings offer valuable implications for informing conservation strategies, especially given the significant changes currently taking place in the Galapagos Islands as a result of human activities.

Results

RAD-seq

A total of 95 DNA samples were processed for RAD-seq analysis in the present study. RAD-seq yielded a total of 1,021,167,325 forward reads and an equivalent number of reverse reads (Supplementary Table S1). The number of raw reads per sample can be found in Supplementary Table S2. None of the sequences were flagged as poor quality. The sequence length for both files is uniform at 169 base pairs, which is consistent with the size selection performed for sequencing. The GC content differs slightly between the forward and reverse reads, with percentages of 49% and 48%, respectively. Notably, the mean sequence quality, as indicated by Phred scores, remains consistent across both the forward and reverse reads files, with a quality score of 36.

After processing with Stacks and VCFtools, a final dataset of 93 samples was obtained (two samples were removed, one due to low coverage and another due to high levels of missing data; see Methods). The final dataset had 14.8% missing data and included a total of 30,353 SNPs. After filtering, each sample had an average of 8069 loci, with a mean cluster depth of 68.8. The final filtered assembly contained 9381 loci.

To evaluate genetic structure among Galapagos Phyllodactylus geckos, we conducted an admixture analysis using sNMF on 30,353 SNPs from 93 individuals. Cross-entropy scores decreased steadily with increasing K and reached a clear minimum at K = 5, suggesting five major ancestral populations (Fig. 5A). The ancestry matrix for K = 5 revealed discrete genetic clusters corresponding largely to species and geographic groupings, with minimal admixture between clusters (Fig. 5B). A sixth cluster (K = 6) revealed additional substructure within P. simpsoni, primarily in individuals from Isabela, but did not substantially alter the overall clustering (Fig. 5C).

Species delimitation

The multispecies‐coalescent analysis implemented in BPP (A10 framework; see Methods) recovered five well‐supported lineages corresponding to P. darwini, P. leei, P. baurii, P. galapagensis, and the combined P. simpsoni–P. andysabini clade (Fig. 6). Speciation probabilities at every internode were high under rjMCMC algorithm 1 (posterior ≥ 0.95), confirming the distinctiveness of these five lineages. This result is fully congruent with our PCA (Fig. 1B, C), DAPC (Fig. 1D, E), phylogenetic trees (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figures S1, S2 and S3), and admixture analyses (Fig. 5), all of which likewise group P. simpsoni and P. andysabini together.

Discussion

While previous research on Galapagos Phyllodactylus has relied primarily on protein-coding genes (e.g., BDNF, CMOS, RAG1, RAG2, ACM4, and PDC) and mitochondrial markers, our study employed a comprehensive genomic approach, analyzing over 9000 loci and 30,000 SNPs, and was able to elucidate the population structure and genetic differentiation among Phyllodactylus species inhabiting human-populated islands in the Galapagos. This high-resolution analysis provided valuable insights into the genetic diversity within and between species, revealing distinct genetic clusters, challenging previous species classifications, and highlighting patterns of genetic diversity and differentiation. Identifying previously unrecognized conspecific populations can change our understanding of species boundaries and underscore the need for reevaluating population management plans under a unified taxonomic framework. Our findings illustrate how high-resolution genomic tools can reshape species definitions and improve our capacity to predict how anthropogenic changes might affect reptiles in the Galapagos and other insular ecosystems.

Genetic clusters

According to the results obtained in the PCA and DAPC (Fig. 1), the phylogenetic trees (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figures S1, S2 and S3),the haplotype network (Fig. 2B), and the species delimitation analysis (Fig. 6), five distinct genetic clusters corresponding to P. baurii, P. galapagensis, P. darwini, P. leei, and a combined cluster for P. simpsoni and P. andysabini were revealed. This clustering pattern was also supported by population structure analysis using sNMF (Fig. 5A–C), which identified K = 5 as the most likely number of ancestral populations. These findings confirm that P. baurii, P. galapagensis, P. darwini, and P. leei are clearly defined species, as suggested by the literature12,44. This is expected since most of these species are found on different islands, allowing them to evolve in isolation6,47,51. Aside from P. simpsoni and P. andysabini which will be discussed further on, P. darwini and P. leei are the only species in this study that occur in sympatry on the same island, however they are distinctly separated from each other (Figs. 1, 2 and 6). This difference could be attributed to the more recent colonization of the island by the ancestor of P. darwini, which is estimated to have occurred around 3 million years ago. In contrast, evidence suggests that the ancestor of the other Phyllodactylus species in the Galapagos colonized the islands 13 million years ago, and since then these species have been isolated from their continental relatives44. Additionally, the two species are phenotypically distinct, with P. darwinii having a much larger body size compared to P. leei, as well as a different scalation pattern and coloration52. The substantial size difference between these species, together with their disparate colonization times, likely facilitated the co-existence of these two distinct species on the same island by reducing competition for resources through niche partitioning6,53.

The PCA (Fig. 1) shows the P. galapagensis cluster in close proximity to the P. simpsoni–P. andysabini combined cluster, while the phylogenetic trees, haplotype network and species delimitation analysis (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figures S1 and S3, Fig. 6) separate these clusters into two clearly defined groups. Similarly, sNMF supports this separation, with minimal shared ancestry between these clusters at K = 5 (Fig. 5). These findings are consistent with the historical classification of P. simpsoni and P. andysabini as part of P. galapagensis, and their more recent phylogenetic divergence29,44. Additionally, the P. baurii cluster is closely related to the P. galapagensis and P. simpsoni–P. andysabini clusters, reflecting their phylogenetic relationships as reported by previous genetic studies38,39. These results also reflect the chronological formation of the extant islands in the archipelago which are reported to have emerged in three distinct groups. San Cristóbal, where P. leei occurs, arose around 2.5 million years ago along with Española. Santa Cruz (home to P. galapaguensis), and Floreana (P. bauri) formed between 1.5 and 2 million years ago, along with the rest of the central islands. Finally, Isabela and Fernandina, where the P. simpsoni–P. andysabini group is found, emerged about 0.5 million years ago54,55. The chronophylogeny (Supplementary Figure S3) places absolute dates on these splits, reinforcing a vicariance-driven diversification model. Moreover, our ASTRAL species tree (Supplementary Figure S2) is broadly congruent with the ML and Bayesian topologies, indicating that incomplete lineage sorting has minimal impact on these relationships.

P, simpsoni and P. andysabini as a single species

The results of the DXY (Fig. 3B), PCA (Fig. 1), phylogenetic trees (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figures S1, S2 and S3), haplotype network (Fig. 2B), and species delimitation analysis (Fig. 6) collectively support the notion that P. simpsoni and P. andysabini may not be separate species, contrary to the previous classification by Arteaga et al. (2019) who diagnosed them based on morphological traits and five genetic markers29. The sNMF analysis (Fig. 5) further supports this interpretation: all individuals currently classified as P. simpsoni and P. andysabini consistently cluster together under a single ancestry component at K = 5, without distinct substructure. Our study, utilizing over 9000 loci, provides strong evidence that P. andysabini and P. simpsoni are likely conspecific, which is supported by their shared habitat on Isabela Island and corroborated by previous genetic studies12,44. It is noteworthy that the DXY value, which reflects the minimum nucleotide differences between the two species, is close to 0, which reinforces their genetic similarity and suggesting conspecificity56.

Arteaga et al. (2019) proposed two phenetic differences between P. simpsoni and P. andysabini in their original description: the absence of supranasal contact and the presence of a densely stippled throat in P. andysabini29. However, individuals of P. simpsoni also were reported to exhibit these characteristics (43% individuals of P. simpsoni examined by Arteaga et al. (2019) had an absence of contact between supranasals and 36% had a brown stippled throat)29.

Our morphological survey revealed that throat stippling ranged 45% in populations traditionally assigned to P. simpsoni and reached 50% in the two P. andysabini individuals examined here. Supranasal contact likewise varied widely within putative P. simpsoni and P. andysabini. Given this overlap, we believe that no coloration or scalation character reliably separates P. simpsoni from P. andysabini, and that no morphological character is a reliable discriminator between the populations of Isabela and Fernandina islands. Although Arteaga et al. (2019) reported using multiple gene fragments (12S, 16S, ND4, RAG1, and c-mos) totaling 2958 bp, only the genetic variation in a short (181 bp) portion of the mitochondrial 12S gene was emphasized to bolster the relationship and distances between P. andysabini and P. simpsoni29. Reliance on such a limited mitochondrial fragment for justifying taxonomic conclusions is challenging because mtDNA, although useful for inferring historical lineages, is a single, maternally inherited locus susceptible to biases like introgression, incomplete lineage sorting, and cytonuclear discordance57–59. Indeed, a growing body of literature cautions that mtDNA-based phylogeographies may inflate or overlook putative species when not supplemented by nuclear data and thorough morphological analyses60–63. As highlighted in recent controversies64,65, genomic-scale or multi-locus nuclear approaches are critical to disentangle genuine lineage divergence from artifacts driven by single-locus variability or sampling limitations. As remarked by Wüster et al.64 “Taxonomic decisions based on an overreliance on mtDNA and a lack of critical analysis of additional data are a common and major problem in herpetology”.

Both specimens from Wolf Volcano, the type locality of P. andysabini, fell well within the broader genetic cluster attributed to P. simpsoni. All analyses consistently grouped these Wolf Volcano individuals with P. simpsoni from across central-southern Isabela. Similarly, nucleotide diversity and allele frequencies analyses showed no sign of unique lineages or admixtures distinct from P. simpsoni.

Our study illustrates the pitfalls of describing separate species solely from small sample sizes, a handful of overlapping traits, and few genetic markers—an issue recently emphasized in high-profile controversies64. Our findings echo the cautionary message from other recent cases (e.g.,66–70): integrative taxonomic revisions—relying on multiple datasets, robust sampling, and due attention to good taxonomic practices and nomenclatural rules—are vital to avoid incomplete or unstable conclusions. Describing species prematurely can create confusion if the morphological and genetic lines of evidence are not thoroughly examined under best-practice standards66,71,72. Proposed new species should be justified by consistent evidence, particularly when they are based on limited sample sizes and potentially non-fixed traits. Therefore, we propose the synonymy of Phyllodactylus andysabini Arteaga, Bustamante, Vieira, Tapia, Carrión & Guayasamin, 2019 with Phyllodactylus simpsoni Arteaga, Bustamante, Vieira, Tapia, Carrión & Guayasamin, 2019, making P. simpsoni the valid name for all gecko populations from Isabela and Fernandina islands.

Genetic differentiation between and within species

The FST and DXY values found in this study revealed a significant differentiation between species, with FST values ranging between 0.524 and 0.981 and DXY values between 0.02 and 0.117 (Fig. 3). With the exception of P. andysabini and P. simpsoni, these high values indicate clearly differentiated species56. Interestingly, the genetic differentiation between P. darwini and all other species is higher than the values found between all other species (Fig. 3). As mentioned previously, this could be due to the fact that P. darwini is the result of a separate, more recent colonization event, and thus more genetically distinct from all the other species that derived from a single colonization event44. Additionally, the genetic differentiation values seem to be consistent with the island formation age: species from older islands tend to have higher genetic differentiation values54,55,73.

When analyzing the FST values within P. simpsoni, the highest value was found between the samples from the islands of Fernandina and Isabela. Despite this, the Mantel test did not yield significant results (Fig. 4D). It is very likely that the differentiation observed between individuals from these two islands is due to the body of water that separates them, since this acts as a barrier to gene flow, generating isolation and genetic differentiation between sampling locations from different islands74.

FST values within P. baurii, P. galapagensis, P. leii and P. darwini species were low, although a trend could be observed: individuals from opposite ends of each island were more genetically differentiated (Fig. 4). However, the Mantel test was not significant, indicating no correlation between geographic distance and genetic divergence within these species. This could be due to the volcanic nature of these islands. Eruption events and landslides are phenomena that promote habitat fragmentation and genetic isolation, thereby driving speciation as well as differentiation within species living on the same island3.

Anthropogenic impacts are an important factor to consider when studying population differentiation and divergence in the islands. Human activity on islands like San Cristobal, Isabella and Santa Cruz has been substantial, including land use changes, tourism, and urbanization, which can fragment habitats,alter ecosystems, and facilitate non-native species introductions or the translocation of individuals of endemic species75,76 Nonetheless, we observed that the Phyllodactylus species under study maintain clear genetic boundaries, indicating minimal admixture. One explanation could be that endemic geckos rarely move to new islands under current conditions, or strong reproductive barriers preclude interspecific gene flow if they do. By contrast, non-native geckos are documented to disperse extensively across islands19,21, suggesting that endemic species might respond differently to anthropogenic pathways or that their genetic integrity is safeguarded by reproductive isolation. Further research using more detailed sampling of natural and human-dominated habitats, potentially coupled with studies of reproductive compatibility, could provide insights into how human activities may be shaping gecko diversity and identify populations most vulnerable to ongoing ecological and environmental changes to highlight conservation priorities75.

Genetic diversity

The expected heterozygosity (He) values for Phyllodactylus species endemic to the Galapagos Islands ranged from 0.0175 (P. baurii) to 0.0794 (P. simpsoni); while nucleotide diversity ranged from 0.0023 (P. darwini) to 0.0225 (P. simpsoni) (Table 1). Specific indicators of genetic diversity have not been calculated for other species within the Phyllodactylus genus before this study. However, these low levels of genetic diversity are consistent with patterns observed in other island-endemic reptiles, where factors such as geographic isolation, small population sizes, and limited gene flow contribute to reduced genetic variation. For instance, the critically endangered Aeolian wall lizard (Podarcis raffonei), endemic to the Aeolian Islands of Italy, exhibits the lowest genetic diversity among 26 Podarcis species, including other island endemics. Recent genomic analyses revealed that P. raffonei possesses the lowest heterozygosity among seven species belonging to distinct squamate families, underscoring the impact of insular isolation on genetic variation77. Similarly, the Bojer’s skink (Gongylomorphus bojerii) from Mauritius demonstrates significantly lower levels of genetic variation in its southern island population compared to northern populations. Microsatellite analyses revealed strong differentiation (F_ST = 0.16) and low genetic diversity, highlighting the consequences of geographic isolation and limited dispersal on genetic structure78. In contrast, some island-endemic reptiles maintain relatively higher genetic diversity. The Corsican-Sardinian endemic lizard (Podarcis tiliguerta) exhibits unexpectedly high genetic variation at both mitochondrial and nuclear loci, attributed to historical factors such as the complex topography and climatic stability of the Mediterranean islands, which have facilitated the persistence of diverse lineages79. These comparisons illustrate that while low genetic diversity is a common feature among island-endemic reptiles, exceptions exist, often influenced by historical, ecological, and geographical factors. Our findings in Galapagos Phyllodactylus species align with the general trend of reduced genetic variation in insular reptiles, emphasizing the importance of considering island-specific contexts in conservation strategies.

When focusing on the specific gecko group, research has revealed varying levels of genetic diversity. For instance, in Gekko japonicus, Wei et al. (2015) found a mean expected heterozygosity of 0.395 to 0.797 in one population from Wenzhou, Zhejiang Province, China80, while Li et al. (2007) reported a range of 0.584 to 0.917 in different microsatellite markers in seven populations of Gekko swinhonis from China81. It is important to note that firstly, these species are not island species, therefore the genetic diversity values obtained may not be comparable to our study group. Secondly, these studies were done using microsatellite markers, which takes into account few loci (usually less than 20) compared to the thousands of loci analyzed using RAD-Seq. Microsatellites studies that chose only polymorphic loci can overestimate genetic diversity82, and are not ideal for reflecting genome-wide genetic diversity83. In contrast, several studies have shown that techniques such as RAD-Seq are powerful tools for detecting genetic diversity84,85.

The relatively low genetic diversity found in the species of this study can be attributed to their endemic nature, as they are confined to a single island or, in the case of P. simpsoni, two islands. Endemic island species often exhibit lower genetic diversity due to various factors associated with their unique evolutionary history and the challenges posed by insular environments86–89. These species are characterized by reduced genetic variation, which can negatively impact their overall fitness and increase their susceptibility to extinction87. Recent population bottlenecks, rather than the lasting effects of island colonization alone, may have further contributed to the diminished genetic diversity86. Thus, the possible effect of the factors mentioned previously, have probably played an important role in the genetic makeup of these endemic species, leading to reduced genetic diversity.

Within the studied species, P. baurii and P. darwini showed the lowest nucleotide diversity and expected heterozygosity values (Table 1). In the case of P. baurii, this presumably reflects the constraints imposed by its restricted geographic distribution90,91. Floreana, the island where this gecko species is found, has a relatively small area of 173 km2. On the other hand, the low genetic diversity values observed in P. darwini could be explained by its relatively recent colonization history, occurring only 3 million years ago, which contrasts with the much older colonization events of the other studied endemic Phyllodactylus species in the archipelago, dating back 13 million years44. The lower genetic diversity we observed may reflect a more recent founder effect92–94. In addition to low levels of nucleotide diversity and heterozygosity, P. darwini also exhibits a relatively long terminal branch in our coalescent-based species tree (Supplementary Figure S2). This pattern is consistent with a historical founder event followed by isolation and genetic drift95,96. However, we also acknowledge that recent population bottlenecks could potentially produce similar reductions in genetic diversity, and without explicit demographic modeling, these alternative scenarios cannot be fully distinguished94,97.

In all cases, the low nucleotide diversity and expected heterozygosity values (Table 1) may be further influenced by ongoing evolutionary pressures imposed by human activities98. These pressures include the presence of introduced predators like cats and rats, which exert predatory pressure on gecko species, as well as competition and predation from introduced gecko species in the archipelago that cohabit in some places with native species99,100. Human habitat modifications might have also contributed to exacerbate the low genetic diversity observed in the species analyzed in this study101–103.

Conclusion

Our findings revealed clearly defined genetic clusters for five of our studied species: P. darwini, P. baurii, P. simpsoni, P. galapagensis and P. leei. We also observed population structure within species, likely influenced by land barriers such as volcanoes and an ocean barrier restricting gene flow. Our analyses led us to conclude that P. simpsoni and P. andysabini exhibit very low genetic differentiation, leading us to propose their synonymization.

We recognize that only two individuals assigned to P. andysabini constitute a small sample. This limitation could raise concerns about the robustness of our conclusion that P. andysabini and P. simpsoni are conspecific. However, our genomic dataset of > 9000 loci consistently places both putative P. andysabini individuals collected at the species’ type locality squarely within the P. simpsoni cluster, showing no evidence of admixture or a separate lineage—patterns unlikely to result merely from field misidentification, given the strong genetic resolution of RAD-Seq. Contrary to the notion that these two names might represent allopatric populations29 or that hybridization events near Wolf Volcano could produce ambiguous genotypes, our analyses do not reveal any discordance or partial admixture that would indicate recent gene flow between distinct lineages. Instead, the morphological overlap and unified genomic signal suggest a single evolutionary unit. Although P. andysabini was originally described as having supranasals, being “not in contact,” and having a heavily stippled throat, our data show these traits are neither fixed nor unique. Such limited and variably present traits run counter to recommended practices for describing new reptile species, which advise researchers to ensure that diagnostic features reflect unambiguous and statistically robust morphological differences64,66,104,105. Without consistent, discrete morphological boundaries, additional lines of evidence—such as larger morphological samples or genomic-scale data—become essential for confirming species status. Consequently, the morphological overlap reported for P. simpsoni and P. andysabini suggests that the original description may not have met these criteria, underscoring the value of our higher-resolution genomic data, which indicates that both names likely refer to a single species. Absent additional reproductive, ecological, or behavioral barriers, the best-supported scenario is that P. andysabini is a junior synonym of P. simpsoni.

The genetic relationships among species align with the chronological emergence of the Galapagos islands, suggesting that species diversification followed the island formation sequence. Additionally, while human activities such as land use changes, tourism, and urbanization have impacted islands like San Cristóbal, Isabela, and Santa Cruz, these activities appear to have limited influence on gene flow among gecko populations. The observed population boundaries seem to remain intact, likely due to strong geographic or behavioral barriers. A more detailed geographic analysis of sampling sites could further clarify how these factors contribute to genetic isolation and identify populations vulnerable to future environmental changes.

As expected for endemic insular species mostly restricted to a single island, genetic diversity was found to be low across all studied species. P. darwini displayed the highest genetic differentiation from other species, possibly due to its more recent colonization. This species, along with P. baurii, exhibited the lowest genetic diversity. The reduced diversity in P. baurii is likely attributable to its small geographic distribution, while the low diversity in P. darwini may be a result of a more recent founder effect.

Our research findings highlight the importance of using high-resolution genomic tools to study genetic variation and population structure in islands. The knowledge gained from this research will not only enhance our understanding of the evolutionary processes shaping these species but also inform conservation strategies aimed at preserving their genetic integrity and ensuring their long-term survival in the face of anthropogenic challenges.

Methods

Sampling

Fieldwork was conducted in the Galapagos Archipelago from May to August 2023, on the islands of Santa Cruz, Isabela, Floreana, San Cristóbal, and Fernandina (Fig. 1). We collected samples in 27 different point localities, encompassing urban and natural environments. We surveyed native gecko species of Phyllodactylus during the day and night, searching in trees, dry logs, under rocks, under tree bark, and other natural and human-made potential refuges. At each site, we hand-captured up to 10 adult individuals of each species present.

Ethics statement

All procedures were approved and carried out under a research permit (PC-32-22) issued by the Galapagos National Park Directorate and a framework contract for access to genetic resources (MAATE-DBI-CM-2022-0240) issued by the Ministry of Environment, Water and Ecological Transition of Ecuador. All methods were conducted in accordance with the regulations of those authorities and with the guidelines for the use of reptiles in field research by Beaupre et al. (2004)106. Methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines107.

Morphological species identification

Each individual was provisionally assigned to a species based on morphological diagnostic characters as described by18,32,108,109. These characters encompass features such as the presence, number, shape and distribution of tubercles and granules on head, body and limbs; the number and shape of mental, supranasal, midbody and subcaudal scales; snout shape; fingertip shape; and dorsal and ventral coloration. However, we recognized from the outset that the P. simpsoni–P. andysabini distinction posed special challenges, as the morphological traits proposed in the original description as diagnostic characters overlap completely29. The morphological traits used by Arteaga et al.29 to distinguish both species—specifically whether supranasals are in contact and whether the throat is heavily stippled or immaculate—show considerable overlap, particularly given the small sample sizes examined. Best practices in taxonomy emphasize that reliable diagnostic characters should consistently differentiate all or nearly all individuals in one taxon from those in another. In geckos of the family Phyllodactylidae, including all species from Galapagos, morphological diagnoses commonly involve traits such as scale counts (e.g., midbody scales, subdigital lamellae, supralabials), patterns of tuberculation, or distinctive coloration that remains stable throughout ontogeny32,110,111. However, the proposed “differences” occur in only a fraction of each putative species sample and are not strictly mutually exclusive (e.g., ~ 57% of P. simpsoni have contact between supranasals, but the remainder do not; similarly, ~ 64% have an immaculate throat, while the rest do not). These statistics indicate that the purported diagnostic characters are neither fixed nor fully non-overlapping between the two species. We also consider the reported geographic distribution of P. simpsoni and P. andysabini, which are purportedly allopatric. Phyllodactylus simpsoni has been documented on Fernandina and in central and southern Isabela, up to Darwin Volcano, whereas P. andysabini is restricted to northern Isabela (Wolf and Ecuador volcanoes). Importantly, our sampling included samples from individuals of each species’ type locality—western slopes of Wolf Volcano for P. andysabini and Puerto Villamil for P. simpsoni. By including specimens from each species’ type locality, we aimed to resolve whether the morphological identifications align with population genetic structure.

In total, we collected 184 tail-tip tissue samples from endemic Phyllodactylus individuals for genetic analyses (Supplementary Table S3). Samples were initially transported to and refrigerated at the Galapagos Science Center on San Cristóbal Island, later transferred to the USFQ Laboratory of Terrestrial Zoology and finally stored at − 80 °C and analyzed at the USFQ Plant Biotechnology Laboratory in Quito.

DNA extraction and RAD-sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from animal tails using a commercial DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (Qiagen) following manufacturer’s instructions with modifications: we repeated each wash steps with buffers AW1 and AW2, and DNA was eluted in a final volume of 34 ul.

DNA concentration was measured using Qubit 3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and DNA quality was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). DNA integrity was analyzed on a 1.5% agarose gel. Samples were normalized to meet Floragenex (Eugene, OR, USA) requirements.

The DNA concentration and quality for each of the samples that were sent to Floragenex is shown in Supplementary Table S2. The values of DNA concentration among all samples ranged from 8,92 to 25,8 ng/ul. Few samples showed quality ratios outside of the expected range (1,8–2,0). Supplementary Figure S4 illustrates the integrity of DNA samples through agarose gel electrophoresis, where distinct bands indicate the presence of high-quality, non-degraded DNA molecules, suitable for library preparation and sequencing.

95 samples were selected for sequencing, considering the quantity and quality of DNA and the representativeness of the sample distribution in and between islands (Supplementary Table S2).

RAD-Seq was conducted by Floragenex (Eugene, OR, USA) following standard protocols. Genomic DNA underwent quality assessment and quality control (QA/QC) measures before library preparation. The RAD libraries were constructed using the SbfI restriction enzyme. Following restriction, fragments of 169 base pairs were selected for sequencing.

Sequencing of the RAD-Seq libraries was carried out using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform. The sequencing runs were performed with paired-end reads of 150 base pairs, employing the NovaSeq6000 S Prime 300 cycle kit (Sp300). Both lanes of the Sp300 flow cell were allocated exclusively for sequencing. The sequencing data were provided in fastq format for downstream analysis.

Read processing and creation of loci catalogue using stacks

Forward and reverse sequencing reads were analyzed using FastQC112. We then used Stacks v2.65 to filter reads and create a loci catalogue for our population structure and genetic diversity analyses. Specifically, we began using process_radtags to demultiplex and clean the reads, specifying barcode-dist-1 of 3 to allow three mismatches when rescuing single-end barcodes, -c/-clean to remove reads with uncalled bases, -q/-quality to discard low-quality reads, and -r/-rescue to rescue barcodes and cut sites. This approach retained over 80% of the reads.

After demultiplexing we ran the main Stacks pipeline consisting of ustacks (builds loci), cstacks (creates a catalog of loci), sstacks (matches samples against the catalog), gstacks (assembles paired-end contigs, calls variant sites, and genotypes samples), and populations (filters data, calculates population genetics statistics, and exports various data formats)113. Following the recommendations of Catchen et al. (2013)113, Paris et al. (2017)114 and Rivera-Colón & Catchen (2022)115, and based on the scripts found in Cerca et al. (2021a)116, we ran the aforementioned Stacks pipeline using the wrapper program denovo_map.pl, since no reference genome is available for Phyllodactylus species. The populations were delineated according to our population map (list of our 95 Phyllodactylus samples). We optimized the parameters -M (maximum nucleotide mismatches allowed between stacks to be merged into a locus) and -n (maximum nucleotide mismatches allowed between stacks during the construction of the catalog) within the ustacks and cstacks modules of the Stacks pipeline113,114, by exploring a range of values from 1 to 7 for both parameters. Subsequently, we analyzed the cumulative count of loci resulting from various combinations of -M and -n values to identify the inflection point at which the change in the count of -R80 loci (number of polymorphic loci present in at least 80% of the samples) approached zero. The combination of -M 3, -n 3, and -R 80 was determined as the optimal parameter set for the de novo assembly of loci within the Phyllodactylus sp. dataset.

We followed the methodology outlined by Cerca et al. (2021b) to exclude samples and loci exhibiting a high degree of missing data, as these tend to cluster near the center of the principal component (PC) axes (Supplementary Figure S5)117. Samples displaying a mean depth of coverage less than 35 x (individuals 3961 P. galapagensis and 4140 P. darwini)) were removed utilizing VCFtools v.0.1.5 with the -mean_depth flags118. Additionally, we used VCFtools to further filter dataset, by applying the following filters: a mean coverage depth between 15 and 200 (-min-meanDP 15 -max-meanDP 200)118. Finally, 93 out of 95 samples and 30,353 loci were retained for subsequent genetic diversity and phylogenetic analyses.

Population structure analyses

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to assess the genetic variation between the 93 samples of Phyllodactylus species using the R package adegenet119 following the PCA script of Cerca et al. (2021a)116. Before running the PCA we used the option ‘-write-random-snp’ in the populations program to diminish linkage disequilibrium in the dataset, obtaining 9381 SNPs. We then performed a Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) using the adegenet R package119 to identify and describe genetic clusters. The analysis was conducted using 10 principal components (PCs), 6 clusters, and 4 discriminant functions.

Using the program vcf2phylip.py with the –nexus flag120, we derived a PHYLIP file from the dataset comprising all available sites. Then, we employed IQ-TREE v2.1.4121–123 to infer the maximum likelihood phylogenetic relationships among the 93 individuals. The analysis uses the General Time Reversible (GTR) model (-m GTR), and included 1000 bootstrap replicates to determine bootstrap support (-bb 1000) values122. The resultant phylogenetic tree was generated with the treefile tree format code outputted by IQ-TREE and employing the ape and TreeTools packages in R for visualization124. To root the phylogeny, we used the RootTree function from TreeTools, selecting individuals of Phyllodactylus darwini as the most probable root based on the phylogenetic framework proposed by Torres-Carvajal et al. (2014), which suggests a deeper divergence of P. darwini relative to other Galapagos Phyllodactylus species due to different colonization events44. Finally, using a Nexus file containing both the sequence data and the previously inferred phyologenetic tree, we constructed a haplotype genealogy graph using the Fitch algorithm125 with the parameters -m 0.3 and defined our six Phyllodactylus populations using the -p flag, aiming to elucidate the transitions and transversions between the sampled sequences.

Bayesian phylogenetic tree inference was performed using MrBayes v3.2126, implemented through the Geneious Prime software platform (Biomatters Ltd.). The analysis was conducted under the GTR substitution model with a gamma distribution of rate variation across sites, which was partitioned into four categories. A subset of 2000 loci was selected from the full dataset of 9381 for this analysis. The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) was run for 2 million generations, with sampling every 100 generations and a 10% burn-in to ensure stabilization of likelihood values. The sample 4121 (corresponding to Phyllodactilus darwini) was designated as the outgroup to root the phylogenetic tree.

To explicitly account for incomplete lineage sorting (ILS), we inferred a coalescent-based species tree using ASTRAL-III v5.15.3127 from 533 gene trees generated with IQ-TREE v2.2.2.7123. These gene trees were inferred from quality-filtered per-locus alignments derived from the concatenated SNP dataset. The ASTRAL tree was visualized with FigTree v1.4.4128 and compared with the ML and Bayesian trees to assess topological congruence.

For our divergence-time chronophylogeny, we employed a filtered, biallelic, no‐missing‐data VCF of Phyllodactylus individuals and performed all analyses in R using vcfR to parse the VCF129, followed by downstream analysis in BEAST. Due to the computation demands of BEAST, we randomly selected 3 to 4 individuals per species across the phylogeny to represent the real phylogenetic relationship among these species (except for P. andysabini which only had 2 samples).Then, the xml file was prepared in BEAUTi, using the default population assignments and using the time constraints obtained from the available literature12,44. Then we followed the divergence time analysis setting from Zhou et al. (2018)130. Specifically, we applied a prior on the root crown node using an offset of 0 Ma, a mean of 5.45, and a standard deviation of 0.91, with the mean specified in real space. MCMC chain length was set as 100 million generations, with trees sampled every 1000 generations. Finally, we assessed the quality of the final runs using Tracer v.1.7.2 to make sure ESS values are over 200, and conducted the final maximum credibility tree of median heights using TreeAnnotator after discarding 10% of total trees as burn-in.

We evaluated the extent of genetic differentiation among Phyllodactylus species by computing Weir and Cockerham’s FST131 using VCFtools118. Here, P. simpsoni is grouped with P. andysabini due to their genetic similarity and to ensure a minimum of five samples for FST comparisons (as five individuals per group is the minimum number normally used for pairwise FST calculations). Interspecies differentiation DXY analyses of all six species were performed to offer supplementary evidence of genetic divergence, employing the Fitch algorithm125.

To understand the genetic differentiation between sampling locations within species, we performed FST analysis using VCFtools using Weir and Cockerham’s FST118. For P. galapagensis and P. baurii, we calculated pairwise FST for each sampling location, as there were at least five individuals per location. For the other species (P. leei, P. darwini and P. simpsoni/P. andysabini), PCAs were conducted for each species (Supplementary Figures S6 and S7). Since there were discernible clusters in all three cases (i.e. the individuals formed clear, delimited groups in the PCA), the individuals were grouped according to the clusters for FST calculations (see Supplementary Figure S6 and Supplementary Table S3).

We tested for isolation by distance by correlating geographic distances with pairwise genetic differentiation using Mantel tests. Pairwise FST values were linearized following the transformation FST/(1 − FST)132. Geographic distances were calculated as haversine distances between population coordinates (using coordinates found in Supplementary Table S3). Mantel tests were performed using the mantel.randtest() function from the adegenet package119 with 10,000 permutations to assess significance. All analyses were conducted in R. We added a scale bar within each panel using QGIS Desktop 3.26.3133.

To investigate population structure and estimate individual admixture coefficients in Phyllodactylus species from the Galapagos, we performed an admixture analysis using sparse nonnegative matrix factorization (sNMF) implemented in the LEA R package134. The analysis included all 9381 loci and 30,353 SNPs across 93 individuals. We tested values of K (the number of ancestral populations) ranging from 1 to 10, with 10 replicates per K, and evaluated model performance using the cross-entropy criterion. A clear minimum in cross-entropy was observed at K = 5, indicating the presence of five major genetic clusters within the dataset. The resulting ancestry matrix was visualized using barplots, with individuals reordered by species and geographic groupings, using the lattice R package135. Additionally, K = 6 was included to further investigate patterns relevant to the study’s context and aims.

Calculation of genetic diversity

To understand the genetic diversity among species, we computed the expected heterozygosity (He) by pooling all individuals of each species and treating them as a single population. This approach allowed for direct comparisons across species-level summaries of genetic diversity. This analysis was conducted using the populations software in Stacks 2.65113. Additionally, we calculated nucleotide diversity (π) using the Fitch algorithm following the same pooling strategy125.

Species delimitation analysis

We conducted species-delimitation analyses under the multispecies coalescent model using BPP v4 following the A10 (species delimitation) framework. Input data comprised 500 randomly selected loci from or final RAD-seq assembly (9381 loci) and associated genotype calls (filtered VCF), which we imported into R via vcfR129 and converted to a genlight object with dartR136. We then generated BPP‐formatted input files using gl2bpp, and applied custom Python scripts to verify locus counts, rename locus headers by species, and remove individuals or loci containing excessive “NNN” (ambiguities). A simplified guide tree derived from our IQ-TREE maximum‐likelihood topology (with P. simpsoni and P. andysabini grouped) was used to anchor rjMCMC runs. We executed = rjMCMC algorithm 1 under default theta (θ) and tau (τ) priors, a burn-in period of 1000, parameters sampled every 10 MCMC iterations and 5000 samples retained after burn-in period (nsample) estimating speciation probabilities at each node.
