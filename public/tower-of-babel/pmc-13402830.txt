A robust pH-based second-order spectrophotometric method for the analysis of four anti-HIV drugs in pharmaceutical formulations
Authors: S Noroozi Eshlaqi, M R Khoshayand, N Dalali, S Vali Zade, H Abdollahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA robust pH-based second-order spectrophotometric strategy combined with parallel factor analysis (PARAFAC) was developed for the simultaneous determination of elvitegravir (EVG), cobicistat (COBI), emtricitabine (FTC), and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) in pharmaceutical formulations. The proposed approach exploits pH-induced spectral variations arising from acid–base equilibria to generate structured second-order data suitable for multi-way calibration. Due to severe spectral overlap and equilibrium-related rank deficiency, separate PARAFAC models were developed for each analyte using calibration sets composed of single-component and binary mixtures, while independent quaternary mixtures were employed for external validation. The resulting three-way pH–spectral datasets were analyzed using constrained PARAFAC decomposition to resolve chemically meaningful spectral, pH-profile, and concentration-related loadings. Quantitative prediction was achieved through pseudo-univariate calibration based on the summed sample-mode scores associated with the protonated and deprotonated species of each analyte. The optimized models provided satisfactory analytical performance with root mean square errors of prediction (RMSEP) of 0.27, 0.62, 0.18, and 1.73 µg mL⁻¹ for EVG, COBI, FTC, and TDF, respectively. Good linearity, low detection limits, acceptable precision, and satisfactory recoveries were obtained for all analytes. The proposed strategy successfully preserved the second-order advantage, enabling reliable quantification in the presence of uncalibrated tablet excipients without prior separation. In addition to its satisfactory predictive capability, the method provides a simple, low-cost, and experimentally accessible alternative to chromatographic approaches for multicomponent pharmaceutical analysis.Keywords: Second-order calibration, Elvitegravir, Cobicistat, Emtricitabine, Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, PARAFAC, pH-absorbance spectra data matrixSubject terms: Chemistry, Drug discovery

Introduction

Antiviral drugs against human immunodeficiency viruses are among the most essential antiviral drugs, and given the spread of emerging viral diseases such as AIDS, the use of these drugs as treatment is of particular importance. Currently, about 39.9 million people worldwide are infected with HIV. One of the main causes of death globally is HIV. The drug regimen of these patients, which has been recorded in the guidelines so far, is diverse, and the patient must take a large number of pills per day. Since the old regimens are not very satisfactory and sometimes patients forget to take the medication, the use of new combination drugs in the field of AIDS is classified as a challenging and hot topic issue. Therefore, their measurement is critical in the necessity of multidrug therapies1–3. Among these medications is the Stribild tablet, a comprehensive HIV-1 therapy regimen that includes four active ingredients: elvitegravir, cobicistat, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate.

Stribild® (Gilead Sciences, Inc.) is a new single-tablet, fixed-dose formulation of elvitegravir, cobicistat, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate. It has been approved as an antiretroviral treatment by the European Medicine Agency and the US FDA. Each tablet contains 150 mg of elvitegravir, 150 mg of cobicistat, 200 mg of emtricitabine, and 300 mg of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate. It is the first once-daily treatment option that incorporates cobicistat, a novel pharmacokinetic boosting drug that doesn’t affect HIV, and an integrase inhibitor4.

Elvitegravir (EVG), a quinoline monocarboxylic acid, is 6-[(3-chloro-2-fluorophenyl)methyl].-1-[(2 S)-1-hydroxy-3-methylbutan-2-yl]-7-methoxy-4-oxoquinoline-3-carboxylic acid with the chemical formula C23H23ClFNO5, and a relative molecular mass of 447.9 g/mol. It works by inhibiting the HIV-1 integrase. It is part of a combination treatment for HIV-1 infection5.

Cobicistat (COBI), a monocarboxylic acid amide, is 1,3-thiazol-5-ylmethyl N-[(2R,5R)-5-[[(2 S)-2-[[methyl-[(2-propan-2-yl-1,3-thiazol-4-yl)methyl]carbamoyl]amino]-4-morpholin-4-ylbutanoyl]amino]-1,6-diphenylhexan-2-yl]carbamate with the chemical formula C40H53N7O5S2, and a relative molecular mass of 776.0 g/mol. It acts as an inhibitor of P450. It falls within the groups of 1,3-thiazole, morpholine, urea, carbamate ester, and monocarboxylic acid amide6.

Emtricitabine (FTC), an organofluorine molecule, is 4-amino-5-fluoro-1-[(2R,5 S)-2-(hydroxymethyl)-1,3-oxathiolan-5-yl]pyrimidin-2-one, with the chemical formula C8H10FN3O3S, and a relative molecular mass of 247.24 g/mol. It acts as both an HIV-1 reverse transcriptase inhibitor and an antiviral agent. It is a monothioacetal, a nucleoside analog, an organofluorine compound, and pyrimidone7.

Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF), equimolar amounts of fumaric acid, and tenofovir disoproxil are mixed to form tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, a fumarate salt. Tenofovir has the chemical formula C23H34N5O14P and a 635.52 g/mol relative molecular mass. Its chemical name is [[(2R)-1-(6-aminopurin-9-yl)propan-2-yl]oxymethyl-(propan-2 yloxycarbonyloxymethoxy)phosphoryl]oxymethyl propan-2-yl carbonate; (E)-but-2-enedioic acid, and it serves as an antiviral, prodrug, and HIV-1 reverse transcriptase inhibitor8. According to the European Medicines Agency (EMA) report on Stribild, elvitegravir (EVG) is classified as a weak acid due to its carboxylic acid group, with a pKa value of 6.6. Cobicistat (COBI) has three pKa values: 1.8 for the thiazole group, 2.5 for the alkylthiazole group, and 6.4 for the morpholino group. The pKa of emtricitabine (FTC) is 2.65, while tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) has a pKa of 3.759. EVG, COBI, FTC, and TDF are expected to dissociate in an aqueous solution because they possess deprotonation sites. Thus, their dissociation equilibria are critically influenced by the pH of the solution. The differences between the dissociated and non-dissociated electronic structures can be observed in their absorption spectra, which vary at different pH levels.

From hyphenated instrumental devices to simple spectroscopic procedures, the literature review demonstrates that a variety of instrumentation techniques have been used to determine elvitegravir, cobicistat, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate either as single formulations or in multiple combinations with other anti-HIV antivirals from biological liquids to pharmaceutical preparations. Chromatographic techniques are frequently employed for quantitative procedures when determining antivirals10–20. Electrochemical methods have gained significance due to their rapid analysis capabilities and the lack of pretreatment requirements21,22. Moreover, the literature includes publications on the spectrophotometric analysis of these antivirals23–26, which is less complicated than the chromatographic techniques. In analytical chemistry, spectrophotometry is one of the most widely used techniques. However, due to the spectral interference of analytes with inactive compounds or each other, traditional spectrophotometric approaches cannot resolve complex samples that contain both active and inactive components. For these reasons, analysts or researchers have preferred chromatographic strategies for solving their problems, such as analyzing pharmaceutical preparations, biological fluids27–36, other tissues, and aquatic environments37 containing sample matrices or excipients.

The advent of chemometric techniques in analytical chemistry has diminished the issue of spectral overlap due to the enhanced resolving power of several multivariate calibration methods. Unlike univariate calibration approaches, measuring multiple signals in each sample allows for compensating interference contributions in an unknown sample. Simultaneously, first-order multivariate calibration techniques can mitigate spectral interferences from compounds whose fluctuations are incorporated in the calibration procedure. Several first-order calibration techniques have been documented for the concurrent quantification of elvitegravir, cobicistat, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate in combination formulations or binary combinations with other anti-human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antivirals38–40. First-order multivariate calibration techniques can efficiently address spectrum interferences from substances incorporated in the calibration procedure. Conversely, second-order or higher-order data processing techniques can accommodate the influences of hypothetical interferences absent from the calibration set. This benefit is termed the “second-order advantage”. Second-order approaches necessitate a matrix of response data for each sample. This produces a three-dimensional data array formed by stacking the data matrices from several samples.

Second-order data can be generated by instruments that provide a matrix of responses for a single sample, such as through hyphenated techniques or by modulating spectral signals with external variables like pH41–43. This data structure is particularly valuable in analytical chemistry as it enables the second-order advantage, allowing for the quantification of analytes even in the presence of unexpected interferents without the need for physical separation or extensive sample pretreatment42. In pharmaceutical analysis, pH-spectral data, created by varying pH to observe acid-base equilibrium shifts in UV-Vis spectroscopy, often exhibit trilinear behavior—where the signal is proportional to concentration and independent across modes (e.g., wavelength, pH, and sample)—thereby enhancing selectivity, sensitivity, and robustness in complex matrices like drug formulations43,44.

Among the methods for second-order calibration, Parallel Factor Analysis (PARAFAC) is a powerful multilinear decomposition model ideally suited for trilinear pH-modulated spectral data, providing unique solutions under Kruskal’s conditions and enabling full resolution of pure spectral and pH profiles for constituent species, which facilitates chemical interpretability and accurate quantification in the presence of interferences43,45. While Multivariate Curve Resolution-Alternating Least Squares (MCR-ALS) offers greater flexibility by handling potential multilinearity-breaking effects in the pH mode through augmented matrix construction and suitable constraints (e.g., non-negativity or closure), PARAFAC excels when data closely adhere to trilinearity, as commonly observed in controlled pH-gradient systems43,46. Rank Annihilation Factor Analysis (RAFA) provides a simpler alternative exploiting analyte trilinearity for rapid predictions in suitable cases43.

Although several HPLC methods have been reported for the determination of these analytes, our attempts to reproduce these protocols resulted in inadequate peak separation and poor chromatographic resolution. To address these limitations, our group has developed an improved HPLC procedure, which will be presented in a forthcoming study. Moreover, despite the advantages of spectrophotometry—including simplicity, cost-effectiveness, rapid analysis, low solvent consumption, and its alignment with Green Analytical Chemistry principles—conventional spectrophotometric approaches are unable to resolve complex multi-component samples. First-order multivariate calibration methods can reduce spectral interferences from constituents included in the calibration set, yet they inherently lack the second-order advantage. In contrast, second-order calibration strategies enable accurate quantification even in the presence of uncalibrated or unknown interferents, thus motivating the use of our pH-based second-order spectrophotometric PARAFAC approach.

Despite the considerable number of chromatographic and spectrophotometric methods reported for the analysis of anti-HIV drug combinations, several important analytical challenges remain unresolved. Conventional UV–Vis spectrophotometric methods generally suffer from severe spectral overlap and limited selectivity, particularly in complex multicomponent formulations containing excipients or potential degradation products. Although chromatographic techniques can provide adequate separation, they often require expensive instrumentation, extensive solvent consumption, time-consuming procedures, and complex method optimization. In addition, most previously reported multivariate spectrophotometric approaches are based on first-order calibration methods, which inherently lack the second-order advantage and therefore cannot reliably compensate for uncalibrated or unexpected interferences in real samples.

Moreover, despite the growing interest in second-order calibration strategies, applications of pH-induced second-order spectrophotometric data combined with PARAFAC modeling for complex pharmaceutical systems remain relatively limited. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has systematically exploited pH-dependent equilibrium-driven trilinear data for the simultaneous determination of elvitegravir, cobicistat, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate using PARAFAC-based second-order calibration. The present work addresses this gap by developing a robust pH-modulated second-order spectrophotometric strategy capable of resolving highly overlapping spectral signals while preserving the second-order advantage in the presence of uncalibrated interferences.

The present study adopts a systematic approach grounded in the Theory of Analytical Chemistry (TAC) framework, as outlined by Booksh and Kowalski, to develop robust solutions for the quantitative determination of analytes in complex pharmaceutical formulations41. This theory provides a structured guide for selecting optimal instruments, data structures, and calibration methods based on the analytical challenge at hand. In cases involving uncalibrated interfering species—a common issue in real-world samples—TAC identifies second-order calibration as the preferred strategy, enabling accurate quantification through the exploitation of the second-order advantage41. Accordingly, UV-Vis spectroscopy has been employed to generate second-order pH-spectral data, which are particularly suited for this purpose due to their potential trilinear structure arising from acid-base equilibria. While several studies have investigated second-order data generation via pH modulation, applications in pharmaceutical analysis remain limited43,46. Various second-order calibration models are available to process such data; in this work, the PARAFAC model—a well-established trilinear decomposition algorithm—has been selected to fully leverage the second-order advantage in trilinear pH-spectral datasets.

Experimental

Chemicals

All analytical-grade chemicals were purchased from Merck Chemical Company (Germany), and double-distilled water (Milli-Q, Germany) was used throughout. Elvitegravir, Cobicistat, Emtricitabine, Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate standards, and Stribild tablets were donated from Tadbir Kala Jam Company (Iran).

Stock solutions were prepared in acetonitrile at 500 µg/mL for EVG, COBI, FTC, and TDF. They were then kept at 4 °C. Stock solutions were suitably diluted to prepare standard solutions with lower concentrations until each solution’s final solvent included a 65:35 v/v ratio of water to acetonitrile.

Calibration and validation sets

The samples in the calibration set were prepared as single mixtures of each of the analytes and binary mixtures of the analytes in the following concentration ranges: EVG (5–14 µg/mL), COBI (5–15 µg/mL), FTC (10–20 µg/mL), and TDF (15–25 µg/mL) see Table 1 for detailed composition of these samples. Three sample quaternary mixtures were used for the validation set.

Sample preparation

Five Stribild tablets were weighed and ground into powder (Each tablet contains 150 mg of EVG, 150 mg of COBI, 200 mg of FTC, and 300 mg of TDF. The tablets also include the following inactive ingredients: lactose monohydrate, microcrystalline cellulose, silicon dioxide, croscarmellose sodium, hydroxypropyl cellulose, sodium lauryl sulfate, and magnesium stearate. They are film-coated with a coating material containing polyethylene glycol, polyvinyl alcohol, talc, and titanium dioxide). Then, an amount of the tablet powder, equivalent to the weight of one tablet, was transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask, and 70 mL of acetonitrile was added and stirred for 15 min to dissolve. Then, it was made up to volume with acetonitrile. 20 mL of the resulting solution was transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. 7 mL of the supernatant was removed, transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask, and made up to volume with diluent so that the final solvent contained water/ acetonitrile (65:35 v/v). 1 mL of this solution was transferred to a 10 mL volumetric flask and made up to volume with water/ acetonitrile (65:35 v/v) diluent. The sample concentration was 10.5 µg/mL for EVG, 10.5 µg/mL for COBI, 14 µg/mL for FTC, and 21 µg/mL for TDF. The sample was prepared three times.

Apparatus and software

UV-visible spectra were obtained utilizing a Cintra 101 with a 10 mm quartz cuvette. The pH value was determined using a Metrohm 827 pH meter equipped with a glass electrode. Calculations were conducted in the MATLAB environment (version R2018b, MathWorks, Inc.). The MVC2 toolbox was used to perform PARAFAC calculations47.

Procedure

A small amount of 10 M hydrochloric acid was added to each solution before it was made up to volume to lower the samples’ pH to 1 before the analysis. After that, concentrated sodium hydroxide was used to titrate the samples to reduce the impact of dilution. The samples were titrated using sodium hydroxide solution from pH = 1 to pH = 8 with intervals of 0.5 units.

Spectrophotometric measurements were conducted in the wavelength range of 200–400 nm, with an interval of 2.1 nm, on samples containing the analytes of interest across a pH range of 1–8, using an interval of 0.5. This resulted in a two-way data set represented as DI×J, where I corresponds to the pH number (15 in total), and J to the wavelength number (94 in total). Every measurement was taken at room temperature.

The analytical workflow employed in this study was designed to systematically generate and exploit second-order pH–spectrophotometric data for multicomponent analysis. First, controlled pH modulation was applied to induce protonation/deprotonation equilibria of the analytes and thereby generate characteristic pH-dependent spectral variations. UV–Vis spectra were subsequently recorded over the selected pH range to construct two-way pH–spectral matrices for each sample. To preserve the trilinear structure required for PARAFAC modeling and to improve analytical performance, the data were further subjected to pH alignment and wavelength-region optimization. Separate PARAFAC models were then developed for each analyte to minimize the effects of spectral overlap and equilibrium-related rank deficiency. Finally, quantitative prediction was achieved through pseudo-univariate calibration based on the summed sample-mode scores associated with the protonated and deprotonated species of each analyte. This stepwise strategy enabled robust second-order calibration while preserving the second-order advantage in complex pharmaceutical matrices.

Theoretical background

Generation of second-order pH–spectrophotometric data from acid–base drug systems

According to the framework of the Theory of Analytical Chemistry, the choice of data structure and calibration strategy should be driven by the underlying chemical system rather than by purely instrumental considerations. In complex pharmaceutical formulations, where multiple active compounds and excipients may contribute to the measured signal, first-order calibration methods often fail to provide reliable quantification due to overlapping responses and unmodeled interferences. In contrast, second-order calibration offers a powerful alternative by enabling analyte quantification in the presence of unknown or uncalibrated components through exploitation of structured multi-way data.

One practical and chemically meaningful route to generate second-order data is to couple a first-order spectroscopic technique with a controlled chemical perturbation. For systems containing acid–base active compounds, variation of the solution pH represents a particularly effective perturbation variable, as it directly modulates the chemical speciation of the analytes in a predictable and thermodynamically governed manner. Monitoring spectrophotometric responses as a function of pH thus produces pH–spectral data matrices that naturally exhibit multi-way structure.

To elucidate the fundamental characteristics of such data, and without loss of generality, we consider a simplified system consisting of a mixture of two monoprotic acidic drugs, denoted as HA and HB. This choice is made solely for conceptual clarity; the conclusions drawn here can be readily extended to more complex systems involving multiple acid–base equilibria (Eqs. 1–4).

The equilibrium concentrations of the protonated and deprotonated forms of each drug depend on the solution pH according to their respective acid dissociation constants, Ka, HA and Ka, HB. For a given total concentration of each drug (Ct, HA and Ct, HB), the concentration profiles of the species HA/A⁻ and HB/B⁻ as functions of pH are fully determined by acid–base equilibrium theory.

If all four species (HA, A⁻, HB, and B⁻) are spectrophotometrically active, the absorbance spectrum recorded at a specific pH can be expressed as a linear combination of their pure spectra, weighted by their equilibrium concentrations. Consequently, recording UV–Vis spectra over a range of pH values yields a second-order data matrix for each sample, where one mode corresponds to pH and the other to wavelength.

Importantly, although the equilibrium concentrations of individual species vary nonlinearly with pH, they depend linearly on the total concentrations of the parent drugs. This property leads to a structured three-way data arrangement when multiple samples with different drug concentrations are analyzed. In this three-way structure, the spectral profiles of the chemical species and their normalized concentration profiles as functions of pH remain invariant across samples, while the total drug concentrations act as scalar weights in the sample mode. Such data naturally conform to a trilinear model, which forms the mathematical foundation of multi-way calibration methods such as Parallel Factor Analysis (PARAFAC). In this context, each chemically distinct species contributes a rank-one component to the overall data tensor, and the decomposition achieved by PARAFAC directly reflects the underlying chemical equilibria governing the system.

The use of pH as a system variable therefore serves a dual purpose: it enhances spectral selectivity by inducing controlled chemical transformations, and it embeds chemically interpretable structure into the data. This synergy between chemical equilibrium modeling and multi-way data analysis is central to the successful application of second-order calibration methods for the quantitative analysis of multicomponent pharmaceutical formulations.

Regarding rank, the nominal chemical rank is R = 4 (one per species). However, closure rank deficiency in A loading matrix arises from a single independent linear dependency (the sum of all alpha functions equals 1 across both drug pairs), reducing its effective rank to 3. The rank of B loading matrix is 4 if the four spectra are linearly independent. For C loading matrix, the pairwise identical columns (first two for HA, last two for HB) yield a rank of 2.

PARAFAC fitting employs alternating least squares (ALS) with optional constraints like non-negativity (on all modes) to ensure physical feasibility. Uniqueness is potentially ensured under Kruskal’s condition (kA + kB + kC greater than or equal to 2 F + 2)48, where k denotes the k-rank (maximum number of linearly independent columns) of each loading matrix. In this system, assuming kA = 3, kB = 4, and kC = 2, the sum is 9, which is less than 2 × 4 + 2 = 10; thus, the condition is violated, leading to rotational ambiguity and non-unique solutions in the overall decomposition49.

Nevertheless, in the sample mode (C), the profiles are uniquely recovered due to the inherent rank deficiency and linear dependencies, as the subspaces spanned by the columns are fixed by the mass balance. This uniqueness in the rank-overlapped mode has been rigorously demonstrated in prior studies on equilibrium systems49–51, allowing reliable extraction of total concentration scaling despite ambiguity in other modes.

Although the generated pH–spectrophotometric data are strictly trilinear, owing to the linear dependence of absorbance signals on species concentrations (as per the Beer–Lambert law) and the predictable equilibrium-driven variations in speciation, trilinearity by itself does not guarantee essential uniqueness in the PARAFAC decomposition. In other words, while the data perfectly conform to the trilinear model—meaning they can be expressed as the sum of outer products of loading vectors without systematic deviations—this structural property ensures a good fit but does not preclude the existence of multiple equivalent decompositions. The Kruskal condition (kA + kB + kC ≥ 2 F + 2), where k denotes the k-rank of each loading matrix, serves as a sufficient but not necessary criterion for uniqueness51. When this condition holds, the decomposition is essentially unique up to scaling and permutation ambiguities. However, in many real-world equilibrium systems like acid–base titrations, rank overlap (linear dependencies) arising from mass balance constraints (e.g., [HA] + [A⁻] = Ct, HA) often violates the condition, potentially introducing rotational ambiguity where only the subspaces spanned by the loadings are unique, not the individual profiles themselves49. Nevertheless, even in such cases, uniqueness can still be achieved or approximated through additional factors, such as selectivity in the profiles (e.g., regions where only one species contributes significantly) or imposed constraints like non-negativity and unimodality51. For instance, in the rank-deficient sample mode, profiles are frequently recovered uniquely due to the fixed dependencies, as demonstrated in studies on chemical equilibria49,50.

Second-order calibration using PARAFAC

The primary goal of this approach is quantitative determination of the drugs in complex pharmaceutical formulations, where uncalibrated interferences (e.g., excipients or matrix components) may be present. To achieve this, second-order calibration is performed by augmenting the three-way tensor with both calibration and test samples.

The tensor is constructed by stacking pH–spectral matrices from:

A set of calibration samples with known total concentrations (Ct, HA and Ct, HB in the simplified two-drug model).

A set of calibration samples with known total concentrations (Ct, HA and Ct, HB in the simplified two-drug model).

Test samples (e.g., real formulations) containing unknown concentrations of the drugs, potentially along with unmodeled interferents.

Test samples (e.g., real formulations) containing unknown concentrations of the drugs, potentially along with unmodeled interferents.

PARAFAC decomposition of this augmented tensor provides the loading matrix C (sample mode), whose rows correspond to individual samples and columns to the chemical species. Due to the pairwise linear dependencies, the scores for each drug are distributed across its species pair (e.g., columns 1 and 2 for HA/A⁻ pair, columns 3 and 4 for HB/B⁻ pair).

For calibration, the relative scores for each drug in the calibration samples are obtained by summing the corresponding pair of columns in C (e.g., sum of columns 1 + 2 for HA). These summed values are linearly proportional to the known total concentrations and are used to build pseudo-univariate regression models (one per drug): summed score versus Ct47.

This strategy exploits the second-order advantage, which allows accurate quantification of the target analytes even in the presence of uncalibrated or unexpected interferents, provided that the interferents do not destroy the trilinearity of the data and that the number of components F adequately accounts for all contributing species47.

For prediction in test samples, the PARAFAC model (fixed from the augmented decomposition) yields the corresponding summed scores in the test rows of C. These scores are then interpolated into the previously established pseudo-univariate calibration lines to estimate the unknown total concentrations of each drug.

This calibration procedure extends straightforwardly to the full four-drug system by appropriately increasing the number of components F and pairing the columns in C according to each drug’s protonated/deprotonated species, while maintaining the trilinear structure inherent to the equilibrium-governed data.

Results and discussion

General view

In this study, the rationale for using second-order calibration in drug analysis is based on both:

The purposeful generation of second-order data by introducing the pH variable is a simple alternative to obtaining higher-order data, enabling the development of novel analysis strategies for quantifying pH-dependent compounds. Weak acids or bases that ionize less than 100% in aqueous solution are good examples for obtaining such data sets. The ratio of unionized to ionized species of such molecules depends on the pH value of the environment and the pKa value of the species. Changes in the ratio of unionized to ionized species of weak acids and weak bases affect the spectroscopic properties of the molecules, causing bathochromic or hypsochromic shifts in UV-Vis spectra and thus generating pH-spectral data.

And simultaneously designing a versatile second-order calibration model that can be applied to any sample matrix containing the target analytes, regardless of possible interferences. This dual approach not only increases the robustness of the analysis method and improves its predictive capacity, sensitivity, and selectivity but also expands its applicability to a wide range of pharmaceutical and biological samples.

In the present investigation, these principles are realized through the analysis of a four-component antiviral mixture comprising elvitegravir, cobicistat, emtricitabine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, all of which exhibit pH-dependent acid-base behavior with distinct dissociation constants. By systematically varying pH, the speciation of each drug is modulated in a thermodynamically predictable manner, producing trilinear three-way data arrays characterized by invariant pure spectral profiles and normalized pH-dependent concentration profiles across samples. The resulting structure facilitates PARAFAC decomposition, where the inherent selectivity arising from differing pKa values and spectral features of the drugs supports reliable resolution of the components. This chemically driven data generation, coupled with the second-order advantage inherent to trilinear models, provides a robust framework for handling potential spectral overlaps and unmodeled interferences commonly encountered in pharmaceutical matrices.

PARAFAC modeling and second-order calibration strategy

In this study, a three-way dataset was created by merging two-dimensional matrices obtained from recording the spectra of samples across the wavelength range 200–400 nm (step 2.1 nm) and the pH range 1–8 (step 0.5 pH units). To prepare the calibration set, in addition to six binary samples of the analyte mixture, three single samples of each analyte were also prepared and titrated in the specified pH range.

Figure 2 shows the pH-dependent spectral changes for each analyte. These changes result from protonation/deprotonation of ionizable functional groups and from shifts in the electronic structure of chromophores and the solvation shell, thereby altering the absorption maximum or the molar absorption coefficient. Since the medium contains 35% acetonitrile, the pattern of spectral changes is different from that of an aqueous medium. In water–acetonitrile mixtures, the addition of acetonitrile modifies proton activity and the dielectric properties of the solvent, which leads to an apparent shift of pH to higher values. These systematic pH-induced spectral variations generate the trilinear second-order data structure required for PARAFAC modeling.

To achieve accurate quantification in the presence of significant spectral overlap and potential unmodeled interferences from tablet excipients, separate PARAFAC models were constructed for each analyte. This approach was adopted due to the rank deficiency in the concentration mode arising from acid-base equilibria (up to 10 chemical species: 2 for EVG, 4 for COBI, 2 for FTC, and 2 for TDF) and the substantial spectral overlap among the four drugs.

In principle, a global PARAFAC model including all analytes could be considered. However, such an approach becomes highly complex in the present system due to the pH-dependent behavior of the investigated drugs. Each analyte may exist in several protonation states within the studied pH range, and therefore a four-component mixture can potentially contribute up to about ten chemically distinct spectral components to the measured signal. This situation becomes even more complicated in real samples where additional interferents or degradation products may be present. As a consequence, constructing a global model would require handling a large number of strongly overlapping spectral and pH-dependent profiles, leading to severe rank deficiency and a highly complex PARAFAC structure. Preliminary investigations indicated that such a global model becomes computationally demanding, with slower convergence and increased difficulty in simultaneously optimizing key modeling parameters such as the number of components and the wavelength interval used for modeling. In multivariate calibration practice, simpler models are often preferred because they generally provide greater robustness and more reliable predictive performance. For this reason, separate PARAFAC models were constructed for each analyte, allowing more stable model optimization and improved analytical performance while still benefiting from the second-order advantage of the proposed pH-modulated spectrophotometric approach.

For each analyte, the calibration set consisted of six samples: three standards of the pure analyte at different concentrations and three binary mixtures combining the target analyte with each of the other three analytes (one binary per remaining analyte). This design ensures that the calibration data for each model incorporate contributions from all four drugs, allowing the PARAFAC decomposition to account for inter-analyte interferences while building a robust pseudo-univariate relationship for the target analyte. Independent quaternary mixtures were prepared as a validation set to evaluate model performance without overfitting. UV-Vis spectra were recorded at 15 pH levels for all samples, generating three-way arrays (pH × wavelength × samples).

To optimize each model and minimize interference effects, the wavelength range was systematically varied. Consecutive sub-ranges were tested, omitting noisy regions, and the range yielding the lowest prediction error on the validation set—while maintaining chemically interpretable loadings—was selected. The number of factors (F) was similarly optimized by fitting PARAFAC models with varying F and selecting the value that minimized validation error without signs of overfitting (e.g., unrealistic or negative profiles, or lack of convergence in ALS iterations). Non-negativity constraints were applied to all three modes (pH, spectral, and sample), consistent with the physical nature of the data (non-negative concentrations, absorbances, and fractional profiles). Alternating least squares (ALS) was used for fitting, with multiple random initializations to ensure stable solutions. The capacity of PARAFAC to separate strongly overlapping spectra arises from the interplay between the chemical characteristics of the analytes and the trilinear structure of the collected absorbance data. According to the Beer–Lambert law, each analyte contributes a fixed and chemically specific spectral signature, which combines additively with those of the other components. Although these spectra may appear heavily overlapped at a single pH value, the protonation–deprotonation equilibria of each species generate distinct and independent concentration–pH profiles. When the spectral mode, pH mode, and concentration mode are jointly organized into a three-way array, the resulting dataset becomes trilinear, allowing PARAFAC to decompose the data into chemically interpretable spectral, pH-profile, and concentration factors.

This trilinear structure underlies the well-known uniqueness properties of PARAFAC, which enable the recovery of pure component spectra even in the presence of severe overlap—an outcome that cannot be achieved with first-order methods. It is worth noting, however, that in practical spectrophotometric systems the presence of closure-related rank deficiency may introduce a limited degree of rotational ambiguity. Nevertheless, this ambiguity remains small relative to the dominant trilinear signal and does not affect the chemical interpretability or quantitative performance of the resolved factors. As a whole, these features explain why PARAFAC is capable of reliably resolving the underlying spectral contributions of each analyte without prior separation.

Although the PARAFAC model provides strong resolving power and chemically interpretable factors, certain methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, PARAFAC is intrinsically sensitive to measurement noise because the decomposition attempts to fit a strictly trilinear structure to the data. Excessive spectral noise or baseline fluctuations can impair the stability of the extracted factors. In the present work, this limitation was mitigated by optimizing the spectral acquisition parameters, selecting wavelength regions with favorable signal-to-noise characteristics, and validating the model using diagnostic residual analyses. Second, PARAFAC relies on the approximate multilinearity of the three-way dataset. Departures from multilinearity—for example due to minor instrumental drift, slight deviations from Beer–Lambert additivity, or imperfect pH alignment—may introduce small distortions in the resolved profiles. These effects were minimized through preprocessing steps including pH-profile alignment and region selection, which ensured that the final dataset conformed closely to trilinear behavior. Importantly, the remaining deviations were small compared with the dominant systematic structure and did not compromise the chemical interpretability or quantitative accuracy of the model.

In addition to the methodological considerations discussed above, the practical applicability of the proposed strategy may also depend on the reproducibility of the equilibrium-driven spectral behavior under varying experimental conditions. Factors such as solvent composition, temperature, and ionic strength can potentially influence the protonation equilibria of the analytes and alter the consistency of the generated pH-dependent profiles. Moreover, although the method demonstrated satisfactory performance for pharmaceutical formulations containing typical excipients, application to substantially more complex matrices may require additional calibration design considerations or more flexible constrained multi-way models. Future studies may therefore focus on improving the robustness and transferability of pH-modulated second-order calibration strategies across broader analytical environments.

Analysis of real sample

One of the most essential features of PARAFAC modeling is its ability to preserve the second-order advantage. This capability allows the target analyte’s contribution to be correctly extracted even in the presence of unmodeled or unknown interferents, provided the trilinear structure of the data is preserved. In fact, PARAFAC enables effective separation of overlapping signals by simultaneously resolving pH-dependent spectral profiles.

In this study, a versatile and robust calibration method was developed for the accurate measurement of the analytes of interest in tablet formulations. This capability was enabled by generating second-order data from systematic pH changes and UV spectral recordings. PARAFAC’s ability to separate the analyte signal from unmodeled species enables the model to provide accurate predictions even in complex sample matrices.

For the real sample analysis, three independent tablet samples were prepared and titrated with concentrated sodium hydroxide; their UV spectra were recorded. Then, using the calibration set for each analyte—including three single-component standards and three binary standards—and the wavelength range and number of optimized factors obtained, the PARAFAC model was run with a non-negativity constraint to predict analyte concentrations in the tablet sample and in the presence of excipients. Thus, the combination of chemometrics and spectrophotometry provided a simple, cost-effective, and efficient approach for determining the assay of EVG, COBI, FTC, and TDF in Stribild tablet formulations.

The prediction errors and analytical figures of merit obtained in this study reflect the combined influence of several uncertainty sources, including spectral noise, residual pH misalignment, equilibrium-related signal overlap, and PARAFAC model approximation errors. The relatively low RMSEP and REP values observed for the investigated analytes indicate that the proposed strategy effectively controlled these error contributions despite the severe spectral collinearity and multicomponent complexity of the system. In addition, the satisfactory recovery values and precision parameters confirm the robustness and reproducibility of the developed calibration models under realistic pharmaceutical conditions.

It should also be noted that the sensitivity and selectivity achieved by the proposed method are closely related to the preservation of the second-order advantage through PARAFAC decomposition. By exploiting the structured pH-dependent variations of the analytes, the method was able to resolve overlapping contributions and maintain reliable quantitative prediction even in the presence of uncalibrated matrix constituents. Therefore, the obtained analytical performance not only reflects the predictive capability of the calibration models, but also demonstrates the effectiveness of the pH-induced second-order strategy in reducing uncertainty associated with complex pharmaceutical matrices.

Finally, the results show that the spectrophotometric determination of the four analytes, EVG, COBI, FTC, and TDF, in the tablet matrix is possible using second-order multivariate calibration, even under rank-deficient conditions in concentration mode. The PARAFAC models uniquely extracted the spectral, pH, and concentration profiles, and the recoveries obtained in three real samples were satisfactory and within the method’s confidence limits.

From a practical perspective, the proposed second-order spectrophotometric approach offers several operational advantages compared with conventional chromatographic techniques. Chromatographic procedures typically require costly instrumentation, large volumes of organic solvents, frequent column conditioning, extended analysis times, and trained personnel. In contrast, the UV–Vis spectrophotometric workflow employed here is simple, rapid, and does not rely on any organic solvents or specialized equipment. This substantially lowers the overall cost of analysis, minimizes environmental burden, and eliminates column-related maintenance or consumables. Consequently, the developed method provides a fast, low-cost, and solvent-free alternative that is fully compatible with the principles of Green Analytical Chemistry, while still delivering reliable quantitative performance.

Recent advances in analytical chemistry have increasingly emphasized the development of environmentally favorable, low-cost, and information-rich alternatives to conventional chromatographic techniques for pharmaceutical analysis. In this context, second-order calibration strategies combined with multi-way decomposition methods such as PARAFAC have attracted considerable attention due to their ability to exploit complex structured data while preserving the second-order advantage. Several recent studies have demonstrated the growing applicability of multi-way chemometric approaches for resolving highly overlapping analytical systems and improving selectivity in complex matrices. However, many of these approaches still rely on conventional spectral modulation strategies or require more sophisticated instrumental platforms54–56.

The present work extends these developments by systematically exploiting pH-induced equilibrium-driven spectral variation as a practical and experimentally accessible source of second-order information for the simultaneous determination of complex anti-HIV formulations. In comparison with many currently reported multivariate spectrophotometric approaches, the proposed strategy combines experimental simplicity with satisfactory predictive performance and enhanced tolerance toward uncalibrated interferences. These characteristics highlight the potential of pH-modulated second-order spectrophotometric methods as promising alternatives for routine pharmaceutical quality control and future green analytical applications.

Conclusion

This study underscores the critical role of analytical chemistry in the accurate quantification of pharmaceutical formulations, particularly in the context of complex drug combinations. The functional nature of quantitative analysis in this field necessitates the careful selection of appropriate instruments, the structuring of data, and the application of suitable methods. Chemometric techniques, particularly those leveraging higher-order data, offer distinct advantages when analyzing real-world samples, such as pharmaceutical combinations of anti-HIV drugs.

The present work highlights the importance of generating second-order data, a key concept in modern analytical chemistry. By varying the pH during UV spectroscopy, second-order data matrices are formed, enabling more precise measurements of analyte concentrations even in the presence of interferences. The ability to account for the pH-dependent behavior of the analytes is a direct application of the theory of analytical chemistry, where variations in pH induce shifts in the absorption spectra due to protonation/deprotonation equilibria. These changes in spectral profiles are essential for the separation of components in complex mixtures.

The trilinear structure of these second-order data, which incorporates pH as an additional dimension, is crucial for the accurate interpretation of the data. The PARAFAC model, as a trilinear decomposition tool, fully exploits the second-order advantage by resolving spectral overlap and enhancing the precision of quantification. This approach is necessary for obtaining reliable and accurate results, particularly when analyzing samples with potential interferences, such as the combination of Elvitegravir, Cobicistat, Emtricitabine, and Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate in tablet formulations.

This study presents an optimal methodology for the quantitative determination of four HIV drugs in tablets, effectively applying the principles of analytical chemistry and chemometrics. By integrating the concept of second-order data and leveraging the trilinear decomposition capabilities of PARAFAC, we successfully quantified the target analytes in complex samples. This method’s robustness in handling interferences and providing accurate predictions for real samples exemplifies the power of second-order calibration in modern analytical practice.

Future developments can further extend the applicability and analytical performance of the proposed pH-based second-order spectrophotometric PARAFAC method. One important direction is the automation of spectral acquisition across multiple pH values. Implementing an automated pH-profiling system would minimize manual adjustments, reduce experimental variability, and improve the reproducibility of the trilinear data structure required for PARAFAC decomposition. Moreover, coupling the spectrophotometric system with flow injection analysis (FIA) offers an attractive strategy for achieving rapid, precise, and highly repeatable data collection. FIA-based acquisition can substantially shorten analysis time, enhance signal stability, and reduce reagent consumption. These improvements align with the principles of White Analytical Chemistry and are expected to further increase the overall “whiteness” of the method by simultaneously strengthening its analytical, ecological, and practical dimensions.

Beyond its satisfactory predictive performance, the proposed methodology offers several practical advantages for routine pharmaceutical analysis, including low instrumental cost, minimal sample preparation, reduced solvent consumption, and the ability to perform simultaneous multicomponent determination without prior separation. The combination of pH-induced second-order data generation with PARAFAC modeling also provides a flexible analytical framework that may be extended to other equilibrium-driven multicomponent systems exhibiting severe spectral overlap. These characteristics highlight the potential applicability of the proposed strategy in pharmaceutical quality control laboratories and in the development of environmentally favorable analytical methodologies.
