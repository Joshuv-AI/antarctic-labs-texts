The association between eight dietary factors and sarcopenia: evidence from NHANES data
Authors: Xingfu Fan, Yang Luo, Xiaofang Li, Wenqin Tan, Shiping Liu, Jin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia is a generalized skeletal muscle disease that significantly affects daily life and increases the risk of falls, fractures, and other related complications. Recent studies have shown that dietary adjustments can effectively prevent and delay the onset of sarcopenia.MethodsCross-sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2011 to 2018 were used in this investigation. We employed weighted multivariable logistic regression models to investigate the relationship of eight dietary factors with sarcopenia, and further explored these associations using weighted restricted cubic splines (RCS) and subgroup analyses.ResultsA total of 8,221 participants were included in the study, of whom 715 were diagnosed with sarcopenia. In the weighted multivariable logistic regression model (Model 2), after adjusting for confounding factors, a significant negative association was observed between the intake of macronutrients and the incidence of sarcopenia: energy (OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.11–0.34), protein (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.26–0.55), dietary fiber (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.73), carbohydrates (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.38–0.85), total sugars (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.42–0.89), total fat (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.88), total saturated fatty acids (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.40–0.88), and total cholesterol (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.35–0.77). A substantial linear negative link in the occurrence of sarcopenia and these eight dietary factors was found using RCS analysis. Subgroup analyses further confirmed the robustness of the relationship between macronutrients and sarcopenia.ConclusionsIncreasing the intake of dietary fiber, protein, energy and fat, while appropriately managing carbohydrate and cholesterol intake, may help reduce the risk of sarcopenia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01144-2.Keywords: Nutrients, NHANES, Sarcopenia, Total nutrient intakes

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a generalized skeletal muscle disease characterized by a decline in muscle strength and mass [1]. The global prevalence of sarcopenia in the general population ranges from 5% to 10% [2], with an even higher prevalence of 10% to 27% in community-dwelling older adults [3].It is projected that the number of individuals with sarcopenia will increase from 50 million to over 200 million worldwide over the next 40 years [4]. This condition severely impacts daily life, increasing the risk of falls, fractures, osteoporosis, and diabetes [5–8]. For hospitalized surgical patients, sarcopenia is associated with poorer postoperative outcomes, including higher mortality rates, increased complications, and longer hospital stays [9]. These health risks not only place a substantial burden on patients but also lead to significant healthcare costs for families, society, and nations.

Sarcopenia is influenced by various factors, including aging, malnutrition, physical inactivity, and neuromuscular dysfunction [10–14]. Diet, as a modifiable factor, plays a crucial role in the prevention of sarcopenia. Research has shown that improving dietary patterns can help prevent sarcopenia [15]. A review indicated that in individuals with diabetes, dietary habits are significantly associated with the risk of developing sarcopenia [16]. High-protein diets promote muscle protein synthesis and help maintain skeletal muscle mass [17]. Thus, modifying dietary patterns in the general population to reduce the risk of sarcopenia is of great importance. Furthermore, a cross-sectional study involving 126 older men (mean age 87) highlighted the importance of adequate energy and protein intake, as well as fat quality, in preventing sarcopenia in older adults [14]. But this study was only available to older patients and men, and its sample size was limited. Other studies have shown that oral nutritional supplements, parenteral nutrition, enteral nutrition, and high-protein and fiber diets can reduce the risk of sarcopenia in uremic patients [18], and in heart failure patients, high-calorie and high-protein diets can similarly reduce sarcopenia risk [19], though these studies were limited by small sample sizes and the inclusion of only heart failure patients. To date, large-scale, nationwide, and non-specific population studies exploring the association between common macronutrients and sarcopenia in adults are lacking.

Consequently, NHANES data for 2011 to 2018 are used in this analysis. It methodically evaluates the connection between sarcopenia and the consumption of eight key dietary factors, including as energy, protein, carbohydrate, total sugars, dietary fiber, total fat, total saturated fatty acids, and cholesterol. The objective is to present evidence in support of sarcopenia management and prevention.

Materials and methods

Sources of population and data

Including demographics, dietary consumption, laboratory testing, and physical examinations, NHANES offers comprehensive information on the physical wellness and dietary habits of the American people. Approval from an institutional review board was not necessary because this study is retrospective and the data used is freely available.

The study involved Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) scanning. Prior to DXA examination, NHANES excluded the following groups: (1) pregnant women and those with contrast agent allergies; (2) people who have radiation or contrast agent treatment during the previous seven days; (3) individuals weighing over 136 kg or with a height greater than 192 cm. As a result, a total of 39,156 participants were included in the study. The following were the exclusion criteria we used: (1) participants under 20 years old (N = 16,539); (2) participants missing data on limb skeletal muscle mass (N = 11,756); (3) participants without BMI data (N = 35); (4) participants lacking Total Nutrient Intake data from the first or second day (N = 1,953); (5) participants missing covariate data (N = 652). Ultimately, 8,221 participants were included in the final analysis (see Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the inclusion and exclusion of participants

Diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia

Sarcopenia in this study was diagnosed using the Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI), as recommended by the Sarcopenia Definition and Diagnosis Working Group of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) is divided by the body mass index (BMI) to determine this index [20]. According to the NIH’s evidence-based diagnostic criteria, the SMI cutoff values were defined as < 0.789 for men and < 0.512 for women, and individuals meeting these criteria were classified as having sarcopenia and included in the study cohort [21].

Evaluation of macronutrient intake

Macronutrient intake was assessed through a two-stage process. The first dietary intake was collected through a face-to-face interview at the mobile examination center, followed by a second dietary intake assessment through a phone interview conducted 3 to 10 days later and after the first interview. The final dietary intake was taken as the average of both dietary recalls [22]. Eight dietary factors were selected from the NHANES dietary questionnaire for inclusion in the analysis. These macronutrients included: energy (kcal), protein (g), carbohydrate (g), total sugars (g), dietary fiber (g), total fat (g), total saturated fatty acids (g), and cholesterol (mg).

Adding covariates

The covariates included in this study comprise demographic information, lifestyle factors, and medical history. Demographic variables include age, sex, race, education level, marriage, BMI (thin: <18.5 kg/m², normal:18.5–25 kg/m², obese:25–30 kg/m², overweight:≥30 kg/m²), alcohol consumption (defined as individuals who consumed at least 12 alcoholic drinks in the past year, with others classified as non-drinkers), smoking status (never smoked [lifetime consumption of < 100 cigarettes], former smoker [lifetime consumption of > 100 cigarettes but not currently smoking], current smoker [lifetime consumption of > 100 cigarettes and smoking during the survey period]), and poverty-income ratio (PIR, low income ≤ 1, medium income > 1 and ≤ 3, and high income > 3). In addition, medical history information was collected through self-report or from medical diagnoses and relevant laboratory tests. Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) were diagnosed based on criteria including congestive heart failure, heart disease, angina, coronary artery disease, or stroke. Diabetes was diagnosed using one of the following: glycosylated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, self-reported history of diabetes, fasting blood glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L, anti-diabetic medication or use of insulin. Hypertension was diagnosed if three average readings of systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg were recorded, or through self-reported hypertension or current use of antihypertensive medications.

Statistical methods

Individual sample weights were applied according to the NHANES-recommended Dietary two-day sample weight (WTDR2D), and all analyses considered the clustering and stratification design. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n, %) and analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test. To investigate the association between the intake of 8 macronutrients and sarcopenia, weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used. The unadjusted model served as a crude model; Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, education level, and race; Model 2 further adjusted for age, sex, education level, poverty-income ratio, CVD, diabetes, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and BMI. RCS analysis was employed to explore the non-linear relationship between macronutrients and sarcopenia. In constructing the RCS model, three knots were set at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the distribution of eight dietary factors. This approach allows for the flexible capture of the non-linear associations between dietary intake and sarcopenia across varying levels of intake. The RCS model was built using the rcs function from the rms package, with penalized spline terms incorporated to control the model’s complexity and prevent overfitting. We applied weighted logistic regression (svyglm) to fit the model, and the Predict function from the rms package was used to predict the odds ratio (OR) for sarcopenia. Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to assess whether the relationship between macronutrients and stroke differed across subgroups. Data analysis and statistical evaluations were conducted using R Studio software, with statistical significance set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Demographic and baseline characteristics of the study population

Weighted analysis on the 8,221 participants in the research indicates that they reflect an estimated 120 million adults in the US. Participants were categorized according to whether they had sarcopenia or not (for more information, see Table 1). The mean age of the participants was 39.45 ± 11.89 years, with 49.61% male and 50.39% female, and 61.41% identified as non-Hispanic White. Among the participants, 715 (8.70%) were diagnosed with sarcopenia. Participants with sarcopenia were more likely to be older, men, have a lower PIR, weight more, and have less education than those without the condition. Additionally, sarcopenia participants were more likely to have CVD and diabetes. Regarding dietary intake, those with sarcopenia had lower intakes of energy, protein, carbohydrates, total sugars, dietary fiber, total fat, total saturated fatty acids, and cholesterol. But when it came to smoking and marital status, there were no appreciable differences involving people with sarcopenia and those without (P > 0.05).

Association between eight dietary factors and sarcopenia incidence

The eight macronutrients were split up into four groups in order to investigate the connection concerning sarcopenia incidence and the intake of these nutrients. To make the results easier to understand, the first quartile was utilized as the reference. Three logistic regression models were constructed to analyze the effects of these macronutrients on sarcopenia incidence. As shown in Fig. 2, in comparison to the reference group (lowest quartile) within the unadjusted model, a 1-unit increase in energy intake resulted in a 43% reduction in sarcopenia incidence in the highest quartile (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.40–0.81). In Model 2, after adjusting for confounding factors, the sarcopenia incidence decreased by 53% for every 1-unit increase in energy intake (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.31–0.70). Additionally, in Model 2, comparing the fourth quartile of intake for other macronutrients to the reference group (see Figs. 2 and 3), we observed a potential negative correlation between macronutrient intake and sarcopenia incidence: protein (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.26–0.55), dietary fiber (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.34–0.73), carbohydrates (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.38–0.85), total sugars (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.42–0.89), total fat (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.88), total saturated fatty acids (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.40–0.88), and cholesterol (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.35–0.77). Trend test analysis (P for trend < 0.001) in all models showed a significant trend.

Association between the intake of total sugars, total fats, total saturated fatty acids, and cholesterol with sarcopenia

Furthermore, we employed weighted RCS analysis to visually demonstrate the correlation between the eight dietary factors and sarcopenia, as shown in Fig. 4. To account for various confounding factors, we adjusted for age, sex, education, race, PIR, CVD, diabetes, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and BMI. We found a significant linear negative correlation between the intake of these eight dietary factors and sarcopenia incidence. Specifically, as the intake of energy (Fig. 4A, P-nonlinear = 0.980), protein (Fig. 4B, P-nonlinear = 0.758), dietary fiber (Fig. 4C, P-nonlinear = 0.836), carbohydrates (Fig. 4D, P-nonlinear = 0.174), total sugars (Fig. 4E, P-nonlinear = 0.936), total fat (Fig. 4F, P-nonlinear = 0.219), total saturated fatty acids (Fig. 4G, P-nonlinear = 0.103), and cholesterol (Fig. 4H, P-nonlinear = 0.880) increased, the risk of developing sarcopenia decreased.

Stratified analysis of 8 macronutrients

To assess the robustness of the relationship between the intake of eight dietary factors and the risk of sarcopenia, we performed subgroup analysis and interaction tests. After stratifying by sex, education level, PIR, CVD, diabetes, alcohol consumption, and hypertension, we noticed that protein intake and educational attainment significantly interacted (P for interaction = 0.001)., as shown in Fig. 5. Higher intake of proteins was substantially inversely correlated with the risk of sarcopenia in the high education level subgroup, especially in the Q4 group (OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.11–0.34) as compared to the Q1, Q2, and Q3 groups.

Subgroup analysis of the association between protein intake and the risk of sarcopenia

When assessing the robustness of the relationship between dietary fiber intake and the risk of sarcopenia, we found an interaction between dietary fiber intake and alcohol consumption (P for interaction = 0.020) as well as hypertension (P for interaction = 0.042), as shown in Fig. 6. Specifically, a high dietary fiber intake was strongly negatively associated with sarcopenia risk in individuals with a history of alcohol consumption or hypertension.

Subgroup analysis of the association between dietary fiber intake and the risk of sarcopenia

Additionally, our study revealed a strong interaction from cholesterol intake to PIR (P for interaction = 0.012), as shown in Fig. 7. Notably, when exploring the relationship between energy, carbohydrates, total sugars, total fat, and total saturated fatty acids and the risk of sarcopenia, no significant interactions were found (detailed results are presented in supplementary materials, Figs. Discussion

In this large cross-sectional study, we found that increased intake of energy, protein, dietary fiber, carbohydrates, total sugars, total fat, total saturated fatty acids, and cholesterol was significantly negatively associated with the incidence of sarcopenia, particularly for energy, total fat, dietary fiber, total saturated fatty acids and protein. This negative association remained significant even after adjusting for confounding factors. The relationship between these eight dietary factors and sarcopenia incidence was visually represented through RCS analysis.

Subgroup analysis further revealed that education level significantly modulated the link between protein intake and sarcopenia risk. Higher protein intake was linked to a considerably decreased incidence of sarcopenia, and this impact was especially noticeable among those with higher education levels (Q4 group: OR 0.19, 95% CI 0.11–0.34). Similarly, in the subgroup analysis of dietary fiber intake and sarcopenia risk, significant interactions were found with alcohol consumption and hypertension. Higher dietary fiber intake showed substantially inversely correlated with the likelihood of sarcopenia in people with a history of alcohol use or hypertension. Additionally, a significant interaction between cholesterol intake and PIR was observed, with high cholesterol intake offering a protective effect against sarcopenia in higher-income groups. According to these results, nutritional treatments might need to be customized for particular groups of people. No significant interactions were observed in the remaining subgroup analyses, further confirming the robustness of the negative relationship between these eight dietary factors and sarcopenia.

Our study demonstrated that the intake of energy, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and other macronutrients is negatively associated with the incidence of sarcopenia. With the global aging population continuing to rise, studies have shown that appropriate nutritional interventions significantly protect against the onset of sarcopenia [23]. Regarding energy intake, existing research has indicated that sarcopenia patients tend to have lower energy intake, which is closely linked to the occurrence of sarcopenia [24]. Energy regulates sarcopenia onset through complex mechanisms, including modulation of oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, inflammation, apoptosis, and autophagy processes [25]. A study from Japan indicated that each 100 kcal increase in energy intake was negatively associated with the risk of sarcopenia (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78–0.95) [26]. Moreover, a clinical randomized controlled trial in acute stroke patients demonstrated that high energy intake and sufficient rehabilitation time were associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia [27]. Our study’s logistic regression and RCS analyses similarly highlighted the significant negative correlation between energy intake and the risk of sarcopenia.

In our study, intake of lower protein and impaired protein digestion and absorption can lead to sarcopenia by affecting muscle protein synthesis and breakdown. Research has shown that protein plays a crucial role in maintaining muscle protein stability [28]. A meta-analysis of 17 studies demonstrated that protein supplements significantly improve muscle mass and strength in individuals with sarcopenia [29]. Furthermore, higher protein supplementation significantly raised the SMI in a different study assessing the effect of protein supplements on middle-aged people’s muscle mass and strength [30]. In a randomized double-blind trial, participants consuming 1.5 g/kg of protein showed a significant increase in SMI compared to those consuming 1 g/kg of protein [31].

Dietary fiber is broken down into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in the body [32], which help maintain muscle mass and function by promoting fatty acid oxidation [33]. In adults over 40 years old, higher dietary fiber intake is associated with an increase in SMI [34]. Elderly women in the lowest quartile of dietary fiber consumption had a considerably higher risk of sarcopenia than those in the highest quartile, according to a South Korean study, even after controlling for covariates (OR 1.709, 95% CI 1.130–2.585) [35].Additionally, Carbohydrate intake is negatively associated with the risk of sarcopenia. A study from South Korea found that carbohydrate intake was negatively associated with sarcopenic obesity (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.94) [36]. Moreover, research indicates that obese individuals consume more carbohydrates than those with sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity [37]. Existing literature suggests that moderate carbohydrate intake helps maintain insulin sensitivity, promote protein synthesis, and slow the onset and progression of sarcopenia [38].Equally Moderate sugar intake, particularly after exercise, supports muscle protein synthesis and muscle health by promoting insulin secretion and replenishing glycogen stores. However, long-term high sugar consumption may lead to insulin resistance, inhibiting muscle protein synthesis [39].

In our study, we clearly found that the intake of total fat, total cholesterol, and total saturated fatty acids was associated with the occurrence of sarcopenia. Total fat intake includes saturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids, trans fatty acids, and cholesterol. First, existing studies have shown that sarcopenia participants have significantly lower protein and fat intake compared to non-sarcopenia participants [40]. Similarly, higher fat intake has been observed in participants with normal muscle mass among Arab men [41]. Secondly, with respect to cholesterol and vitamin D intake, vitamin D, as a fat-soluble vitamin, can be absorbed with the help of cholesterol [42]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that vitamin D is a successful treatment for individuals with sarcopenia [43]. Finally, studies from the NHANES database (2011–2014) revealed a positive relationship between the SMI and saturated fatty acid intake (β = 0.029, 95% CI 0.002–0.055) [44]. However, other studies have pointed out that a significant increase in fat intake may lead to increased fat cell generation, raising levels of various inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor and interleukins. This creates an inflammatory state in the body, leading to muscle cell apoptosis and necrosis, and ultimately causing sarcopenia [45, 46]. This finding is somewhat at odds with our research. In our study, higher fat intake was negatively associated with the incidence of sarcopenia. One possible explanation is that higher fat intake often indicates a greater overall intake of other macronutrients, whereas lower fat intake may correlate with lower overall energy intake.

Additionally, the subgroup analysis in our study found that, in examining the relationship between protein intake and the risk of sarcopenia, individuals with higher education levels showed a stronger protective effect of protein intake on the occurrence of sarcopenia. A prospective follow-up study over five years clearly indicated that participants with lower protein intake were more likely to have lower education levels [47]. A study in the UK also showed that the risk of sarcopenia varies with education level, with individuals having lower education levels showing a higher risk of sarcopenia [48]. A study in Thailand found that low education level is one of the risk factors for sarcopenia [49]. Higher education levels are often associated with stronger nutritional knowledge and health awareness, which allows individuals to better understand the importance of protein for muscle health and to be more likely to consume adequate protein. Furthermore, individuals with higher education typically have healthier lifestyles, stronger self-directed health decision-making abilities, and better access to resources, which collectively promote healthier eating practices and further reduce the risk of sarcopenia.

Subgroup analysis of dietary fiber intake and the risk of sarcopenia revealed that alcohol consumption and hypertension had a key modulating effect. A study from NHANES clearly indicated that alcohol consumption is closely related to the incidence of sarcopenia [50]. Chronic alcohol consumption can lead to dysbiosis of the gut microbiota and exacerbate systemic inflammation, which in turn affects muscle protein synthesis and increases the risk of sarcopenia [51]. Dietary fiber, after being absorbed by the body, is broken down into SCFAs, which help regulate the proliferation and differentiation of intestinal cells, as well as intestinal endocrine cells, increasing the absorption and availability of amino acids, energy, and other nutrients, while reducing the absorption of harmful substances, thus delaying the onset of sarcopenia [52]. In terms of inflammation, SCFAs can reduce the release of pro-inflammatory factors and alter gene expression through increased histone acetylation, thereby inhibiting inflammation and delaying the onset of sarcopenia [53]. For individuals with hypertension, studies have indicated that elevated blood pressure leads to arterial stiffness and increased peripheral resistance, resulting in a reduction in skeletal muscle mass, and hypertension has been identified as an independent risk factor for SMI [54]. Randomized controlled trials have shown that dietary fiber intake can improve atherosclerosis, vascular function, and cardiovascular disease [55–57]. These factors contribute to the key role of dietary fiber intake in individuals with alcohol consumption or hypertension in reducing the risk of sarcopenia.

In the subgroup analysis of cholesterol intake and sarcopenia, we found that PIR plays a crucial role. High PIR individuals generally have better nutritional knowledge and resources, allowing them to access more health information and follow healthier dietary patterns, making the relationship between cholesterol intake and muscle health more apparent. In contrast, low-income groups often face more social and economic pressures, making it more difficult for them to access adequate health resources, such as appropriate dietary supplementation and medical interventions.

This study shows a strong correlation among the occurrence of sarcopenia and the consumption of several macronutrients, including calories, protein, carbs, lipids, dietary fiber, sugars, saturated fatty acids, and cholesterol. Optimizing dietary intake can effectively prevent and manage sarcopenia, especially by increasing the intake of dietary fiber, protein, energy, and moderate fat, which significantly reduces the risk of sarcopenia. Dietary interventions in daily life can help maintain muscle mass, improve overall health, and reduce the risk of related complications such as falls and fractures. Personalized dietary recommendations, combined with health education, can enhance patients’ health awareness and dietary management abilities, thereby improving their quality of life and reducing healthcare burdens. Therefore, appropriate dietary recommendations not only help reduce the incidence of sarcopenia but also improve patients’ physical functions, delay muscle degeneration during aging, and provide long-term health benefits.

The strength of this study lies in its use of the NHANES database, which ensures a nationally representative sample and broad generalizability. Additionally, this study evaluated the relationship between the intake of eight dietary factors (energy, protein, carbohydrates, fats, dietary fiber, sugars, saturated fatty acids, and cholesterol) and sarcopenia, offering valuable insights into how dietary adjustments can reduce the risk of sarcopenia. However, there are some limitations. First, the study can only reveal associations between macronutrient intake and sarcopenia, without establishing causal relationships between diet and the onset of sarcopenia. Second, sarcopenia was diagnosed based solely on the SMI standard recommended by the FNIH, without incorporating muscle strength or functional indicators, which may lead to potential misclassification. Future research should integrate muscle strength and functional indicators to enhance the comprehensiveness and accuracy of the diagnosis. Furthermore, this study used absolute intake values (kcal/day or g/day) rather than standardizing by body weight (e.g., kcal/kg/day or g/kg/day). Although this approach is consistent with most NHANES studies, it may not fully reflect the physiological adequacy of individuals. Future studies should consider weight-standardized intake to improve clinical relevance. Additionally, this study relied on self-reported dietary data, which may be subject to recall bias and misreporting. Since only two 24-hour dietary recalls were used, the estimated short-term intake may not accurately reflect long-term dietary patterns. More precise statistical methods, such as the NCI method, could better estimate long-term intake, but this was not applied due to data limitations. Lastly, physical activity levels were not included in the model, primarily due to data missingness and concerns about the reliability of self-reported measures. Future research should employ more objective and reliable methods for measuring physical activity. Despite this, our study provides preliminary evidence of a negative association between the intake of eight common dietary factors and the risk of sarcopenia, but further longitudinal and multicenter clinical studies are needed for validation.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence of the relationship between dietary intake and sarcopenia, highlighting the potential of diet in influencing sarcopenia risk. However, as an observational study, the findings cannot establish causality. This study offers valuable insights for clinical guidance, suggesting that dietary adjustments may be considered as part of strategies for preventing and managing sarcopenia, but further prospective research is needed to confirm its causal relationship.
