Efficacy and safety of interleukin-6 receptor inhibitors in myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD): A systematic review and meta-analysis

Introduction

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) is increasingly recognized as a distinct autoimmune inflammatory demyelinating disorder, separate from multiple sclerosis and aquaporin-4 immunoglobulin G–positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4-IgG+ NMOSD).1,2 MOGAD is characterized by antibodies directed against conformational epitopes of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein and encompasses a broad clinical spectrum, including optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, cortical encephalitis, and brainstem or cerebellar syndromes.1–4

Relapses occur in a clinically important proportion of patients and may result in cumulative visual and neurological disability, impaired quality of life, and prolonged corticosteroid dependence.3,5 Although acute attacks frequently respond to high-dose corticosteroids, the optimal strategy for long-term relapse prevention remains uncertain. Maintenance therapies, including azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, intravenous immunoglobulin, and prolonged oral corticosteroids, have demonstrated variable effectiveness in observational studies and clinical practice.6–8 Rituximab appears less consistently effective in MOGAD than in AQP4-IgG+ NMOSD, with relapses reported despite adequate peripheral B-cell depletion.7,8 Recent reviews and practical treatment recommendations have consequently emphasized the need for treatment strategies supported by MOGAD-specific evidence rather than extrapolation from NMOSD.9–11

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a pleiotropic proinflammatory cytokine involved in B-cell activation, plasmablast survival, autoantibody production, differentiation of T-helper 17 cells, and disruption of blood–brain barrier integrity.12,13 Increased IL-6 concentrations have been demonstrated in inflammatory demyelinating disorders, including NMOSD and MOGAD, providing a biologically plausible rationale for therapeutic IL-6 receptor blockade.9,14 Nevertheless, MOGAD and AQP4-IgG+ NMOSD are immunologically and clinically distinct disorders, and treatment effects observed in one condition cannot be assumed to translate directly to the other.9,15

Tocilizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-6 receptor, has increasingly been used in patients with relapsing or treatment-refractory MOGAD, initially in small case series and subsequently in prospective and retrospective cohorts.16–20 Satralizumab, a recycling monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-6 receptor with subcutaneous administration and an extended half-life, has demonstrated relapse-preventive efficacy in NMOSD and has also been evaluated in patients with MOGAD.21,22 A recent international multicentre cohort of 116 patients with MOGAD provided the largest observational evaluation of IL-6 receptor blockade to date and included comparison with a historical intravenous immunoglobulin cohort. 23 Emerging targeted therapies are therefore expanding the therapeutic landscape of MOGAD, although their comparative effectiveness, optimal treatment positioning, and long-term safety remain incompletely defined.10,11,23

The available evidence remains predominantly observational and is limited by heterogeneous relapse definitions, variable follow-up durations, mixed MOGAD/NMOSD populations, concomitant corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy, and inconsistent safety reporting. We therefore conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of IL-6 receptor inhibitors in patients with MOGAD. The primary objective was to estimate the proportion of patients remaining relapse-free during treatment, while secondary objectives were to evaluate changes in annualized relapse rate, neurological disability, MRI activity, and infectious adverse events.

Methods

Study design and reporting

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. 24 A predefined review plan specifying the eligibility criteria, outcomes, data-extraction variables, quality assessment, and statistical approach was developed before screening and data extraction.

The review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through July 14, 2026. Google Scholar was used as a supplementary search source, with the first 140 results ranked by relevance screened using the prespecified search terms. Reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were also manually searched to identify additional eligible studies.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to MOGAD and IL-6 receptor inhibition, including “MOGAD,” “myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein,” “MOG antibody disease,” “MOG-IgG,” “tocilizumab,” “satralizumab,” “interleukin-6 inhibitor,” “IL-6 receptor inhibitor,” and “anti-IL6R.” Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were applied as appropriate for each database.

Complete database-specific search strings, search dates, numbers of records retrieved, and details of the Google Scholar screening procedure are provided in Supplementary Appendix S1. Duplicate records were identified using titles, authors, publication years, digital object identifiers, and other bibliographic information, followed by manual verification.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible when they:

1. Included patients with clinically and serologically confirmed MOGAD;

1. Included patients with clinically and serologically confirmed MOGAD;

2. Evaluated an IL-6 receptor inhibitor, including tocilizumab or satralizumab;

2. Evaluated an IL-6 receptor inhibitor, including tocilizumab or satralizumab;

3. Included at least five IL-6 receptor inhibitor-treated patients with MOGAD;

3. Included at least five IL-6 receptor inhibitor-treated patients with MOGAD;

4. Used a prospective observational, retrospective cohort, or comparative observational design; and

4. Used a prospective observational, retrospective cohort, or comparative observational design; and

5. Reported extractable efficacy or safety outcomes.

5. Reported extractable efficacy or safety outcomes.

Randomized trials were eligible when full results were available in an eligible publication. Narrative reviews, editorials, preclinical or animal studies, individual case reports, case series involving fewer than five treated patients, and studies without extractable clinical outcomes were excluded.

When multiple publications described overlapping populations, patient characteristics, participating centres, recruitment periods, and author groups were compared. The largest or most comprehensive dataset was retained to avoid double counting.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Reports considered potentially relevant by either reviewer underwent full-text assessment. Eligibility decisions were made independently, and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were recorded. Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a predefined standardized form. Extracted variables included study author, publication year, country, design, participating centres, sample size, number of patients with MOGAD, number receiving an IL-6 receptor inhibitor, treatment type and dose, treatment duration, follow-up duration, previous treatment failures, comparator therapy, and outcome denominators.

Efficacy data included the number of patients remaining relapse-free, the number experiencing at least one relapse, annualized relapse rate before and during treatment, time to first relapse, Expanded Disability Status Scale scores, visual outcomes, and MRI activity.

To account for differences in relapse ascertainment, the relapse definition used by each study, minimum symptom duration, requirement for objective neurological findings, use of MRI confirmation, independent adjudication, exclusion of pseudo-relapses, and follow-up interval were extracted and summarized separately.

Concomitant treatment variables included baseline corticosteroid use, maintenance dose, corticosteroid dependence, tapering schedule, duration of overlap with IL-6 receptor inhibition, concomitant immunosuppressive therapy, and reported corticosteroid-sparing outcomes. When these data were unavailable, they were recorded as not reported rather than inferred.

Management of mixed MOGAD/NMOSD cohorts

For efficacy outcomes, only data specifically attributable to patients with MOGAD were extracted. Mixed-cohort efficacy results were excluded when the MOGAD subgroup could not be separated. Each outcome was classified as originating from a MOGAD-only cohort, a separately extractable MOGAD subgroup, or a mixed population without separable MOGAD data.

For safety outcomes, MOGAD-specific numerator and denominator data were preferentially used. When MOGAD-specific safety data were unavailable, mixed MOGAD/NMOSD safety data were considered only when the adverse event was clearly attributable to IL-6 receptor inhibitor exposure and a valid treated-population denominator was reported. Such analyses were considered exploratory, and the underlying population was identified explicitly. Event categories with potentially overlapping patients were not combined unless the study reported the number of unique patients experiencing at least one event.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of IL-6 receptor inhibitor-treated patients who remained relapse-free during the follow-up period reported by each study.

Secondary efficacy outcomes included:

Change in annualized relapse rate;

Stabilization or improvement in neurological disability;

Visual outcomes;

MRI stabilization or reduction in inflammatory activity; and

Time to first relapse, where available.

The principal safety outcome was the proportion of patients experiencing a reported infectious adverse event. Severe infections, treatment discontinuations, hospitalizations, laboratory abnormalities, and noninfectious adverse events were summarized separately when reported.

Because follow-up duration and definitions of relapse and infection differed across studies, pooled proportional outcomes were interpreted as exploratory summaries rather than standardized time-specific incidence estimates.

Methodological quality assessment

All included studies were observational and were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. The scale evaluates three domains: selection, with a maximum of four points; comparability, with a maximum of two points; and outcome ascertainment and follow-up, with a maximum of three points. Studies scoring 7–9 points were classified as high quality, those scoring 5–6 points as moderate quality, and those scoring below 5 points as low quality. Two reviewers completed the assessments independently, with disagreements resolved through consensus. The quality assessment is presented in Figure 2.

Statistical analysis

Single-arm proportional meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated. Relapse-free and infectious adverse-event proportions were transformed to the logit scale and pooled using inverse-variance weighting with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator for between-study variance. A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied only to studies reporting zero events or complete event occurrence. Pooled estimates and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were subsequently back-transformed to proportions.

Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test, the I2 statistic, and τ2. I2 was interpreted as a measure of statistical rather than clinical heterogeneity because differences in study design, patient selection, follow-up duration, relapse ascertainment, treatment regimens, and concomitant therapy could remain despite a low I2 value.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies, particularly the largest included cohort. Funnel-plot assessment, statistical testing for publication bias, and meta-regression were not performed because fewer than 10 studies were available. Analyses were conducted in R using RStudio and the metafor package. Forest plots were generated for relapse-free and infectious adverse-event outcomes.

Results

Study selection

The search identified 1,842 records through PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, with an additional 10 records identified through citation tracking and supplementary searching. After removal of 521 duplicates, 1,321 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 1,256 were excluded. Full-text eligibility assessment was subsequently performed for potentially relevant reports.

Six unique studies fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included in both the qualitative synthesis and relapse-free quantitative analysis. The study-selection process is presented in Figure 1.

Study characteristics

All six included studies were observational: one prospective open-label single-arm study and five retrospective cohort studies, including two comparative real-world analyses. Studies were conducted in China, France, Switzerland, and international multicentre collaborative settings. Tocilizumab was the predominant IL-6 receptor inhibitor, while satralizumab was included in the Vilaseca cohort.

The relapse-free synthesis included 182 IL-6 receptor inhibitor-treated patients with confirmed MOGAD. Three parent studies also enrolled patients with NMOSD; however, only separately extractable MOGAD-specific efficacy numerators and denominators were included in the efficacy analyses.

Overall, the included cohorts were enriched for patients with relapsing, highly active, or previously treated MOGAD, and several studies specifically enrolled treatment-refractory patients with continued disease activity despite prior immunotherapy. IL-6 receptor inhibition was therefore frequently introduced after failure of, or in addition to, corticosteroids, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, or other maintenance therapies, although treatment context was not uniform and some patients received IL-6 receptor inhibition earlier in the disease course. Clinical phenotypes also varied: Xu et al. exclusively enrolled patients with recurrent treatment-refractory MOG-IgG-associated optic neuritis, whereas Capecchi et al. included patients with optic neuritis, myelitis, and multifocal disease; phenotype-specific outcomes were not consistently reported in the broader MOGAD cohorts.

Where explicitly reported, tocilizumab was most commonly administered intravenously at approximately 8 mg/kg every 4 weeks, but dose, route, dosing interval, and treatment duration were incompletely reported in several studies. Previous and concomitant treatment exposure also differed: all patients in Ringelstein et al. had previously received rituximab, Xu et al. used tocilizumab as add-on therapy in 9 of 10 patients, whereas Capecchi et al. used tocilizumab as monotherapy and included patients treated earlier in the disease course. Corticosteroid dependence and concomitant immunotherapy were variably reported across studies. These differences indicate clinically meaningful heterogeneity despite the low statistical heterogeneity observed in the pooled analysis.

Clinical outcomes and safety of IL-6 receptor inhibitors in MOGAD.

Relapse definitions, follow-up, and concomitant treatment

Relapse definitions and ascertainment were not uniform across studies. Several studies defined relapse as new or objectively worsening neurological symptoms lasting at least 24 hours without an alternative explanation, whereas the level of detail regarding neurological confirmation, MRI support, and independent adjudication varied. Follow-up duration also differed substantially, meaning that the pooled relapse-free outcome represented relapse freedom over each study’s reported observation period rather than at a uniform time point (Supplementary Table S1).

Relapse-free outcomes

All six studies contributed MOGAD-specific relapse-free data. Among 182 treated patients, 157 remained relapse-free during the reported follow-up periods. The pooled relapse-free proportion was 85.0% (95% CI 78.7%–89.6%), with no detected statistical heterogeneity (I2=0%; τ2=0) (Figure 3).

Forest plot demonstrating pooled relapse-free proportion following IL-6 receptor inhibitor therapy in patients with MOGAD using random-effects proportional meta-analysis.

Individual relapse-free estimates ranged from 70.0% to 100%. Complete relapse suppression was reported by Comet et al. in 18/18 patients. Relapse-free proportions were 78.6% in Ringelstein et al., 87.5% in Capecchi et al., 70.0% in Xu et al., 87.5% in Shi et al., and 86.2% in Vilaseca et al. The largest cohort contributed 100 relapse-free patients among 116 treated participants and therefore accounted for the greatest statistical weight. Leave-one-out analysis produced pooled relapse-free estimates ranging from 82.4% (95% CI 69.7%–90.5%) after exclusion of Vilaseca et al. to 86.1% (95% CI 79.9%–90.7%) after exclusion of Xu et al., indicating that omission of any single study did not materially alter the overall estimate.

Annualized relapse rate

ARR decreased consistently following IL-6 receptor inhibitor initiation. Ringelstein et al. reported a reduction in median ARR from 1.75 to 0, while Capecchi et al. observed a reduction from 0.75 to 0. Xu et al. reported a decrease from 1.9 to 0, and Comet et al. demonstrated a significant reduction during anti-IL-6R exposure among patients with paired data.

Shi et al. reported significantly greater ARR reduction with tocilizumab than with rituximab or prednisolone. In the Vilaseca cohort, pretreatment ARR decreased from 0.64 to 0.09 relapses per person-year during IL-6 receptor blockade, corresponding to an incidence rate ratio of 0.08.

Disability and visual outcomes

Neurological disability generally remained stable or improved. Capecchi et al. reported a significant improvement in median EDSS from 2.8 to 2.3. Shi et al. found greater EDSS improvement with tocilizumab than with rituximab or prednisolone. Ringelstein et al. reported overall disability stabilization, while the largest Vilaseca cohort demonstrated stable EDSS scores during treatment.

Xu et al., which specifically enrolled patients with refractory MOG-IgG-associated optic neuritis, reported improvement in visual acuity following treatment. Comet et al. did not demonstrate a significant overall EDSS change.

MRI outcomes

Radiological outcomes were incompletely and inconsistently reported. Ringelstein et al. observed reduced inflammatory brain MRI activity during tocilizumab treatment, while Capecchi et al. reported no radiological progression. Shi et al. demonstrated a 4.3% annual reduction in T2 lesion volume with tocilizumab compared with a 1.2% increase with rituximab. Standardized aggregate MRI outcomes were not available from all cohorts.

Safety outcomes

Three studies provided extractable unique-patient numerators for reported infectious adverse events. These included 4/16 patients in Capecchi et al., 1/10 in Xu et al., and 34/116 in Vilaseca et al., giving 39 patients with reported infectious adverse events among 142 treated patients.

The pooled proportion of reported infectious adverse events was 28.0% (95% CI 21.1%–36.0%), with no detected statistical heterogeneity (I2=0%; τ2=0) (Figure 4. Individual study estimates were 25.0%, 10.0%, and 29.3%, respectively.

Forest plot demonstrating pooled infectious adverse-event proportion following IL-6 receptor inhibitor therapy in patients with MOGAD.

This safety analysis was considered exploratory because infection definitions differed across studies. Capecchi et al. reported infections requiring medical treatment, Xu et al. reported an upper respiratory tract infection, and Vilaseca et al. reported any documented infection. Severe infections occurred in 10/116 patients in the Vilaseca cohort, while three patients discontinued IL-6 receptor blocker therapy because of adverse events. Ringelstein and Comet were not included in the pooled infection estimate because a unique numerator for patients experiencing at least one infection was unavailable.

Methodological quality assessment

All included studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Four studies were classified as high quality: Ringelstein et al. and Capecchi et al. each scored 7/9, Vilaseca et al. scored 8/9, and Shi et al. scored 9/9. Comet et al. and Xu et al. each scored 6/9 and were classified as moderate quality. No study was classified as low quality. The detailed methodological quality assessment is presented in Figure 2.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized six observational studies comprising 182 patients with MOGAD treated with interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) inhibitors. The pooled relapse-free proportion was 85.0% (95% CI 78.7%–89.6%), accompanied by consistent reductions in annualized relapse rate (ARR). Neurological disability generally stabilized or improved, and MRI inflammatory activity decreased or remained stable where assessed. The pooled proportion of reported infectious adverse events was 28.0% (95% CI 21.1%–36.0%). These findings suggest that IL-6R inhibition may provide clinically meaningful relapse control in selected patients with MOGAD; however, the exclusively observational evidence precludes conclusions regarding causal or comparative efficacy.

Relapse prevention is a central therapeutic objective in MOGAD because recurrent inflammatory attacks can result in cumulative visual, motor, sensory, and sphincter disability.3,5 The natural history of MOGAD is heterogeneous, ranging from a monophasic course to recurrent disease. In a large UK incident cohort, cumulative relapse risks were 31.7% at 4 years and 36.3% at 8 years, corresponding to estimated relapse-free proportions of approximately 68.3% and 63.7%, respectively. 25 Relapse risk also varied according to age and presenting phenotype. These natural-history estimates should not be directly compared with the present pooled relapse-free proportion because the IL-6R inhibitor cohorts were enriched for patients with relapsing, highly active, previously treated, or treatment-refractory disease rather than representing an incident or unselected MOGAD population. Moreover, relapse freedom in the present analysis was measured over the treatment period reported by each study rather than at a standardized time point.

The biological rationale for IL-6R blockade in MOGAD is plausible. IL-6 promotes B-cell differentiation, plasmablast survival, autoantibody production, T-helper 17 responses, and disruption of blood–brain barrier integrity.12,13 Increased IL-6 concentrations have been demonstrated in inflammatory demyelinating disorders, providing a mechanistic basis for therapeutic IL-6R inhibition.9,14 Nevertheless, MOGAD and AQP4-IgG-positive NMOSD are immunologically and clinically distinct disorders, and therapeutic effects established in NMOSD cannot be assumed to translate directly to MOGAD. Early reports of tocilizumab in refractory MOGAD provided an initial clinical signal, 16 while the present synthesis evaluates a broader body of MOGAD-specific observational evidence.

The relapse-free findings were broadly consistent across the included studies, with study-level estimates ranging from 70.0% to 100%. Comet et al. reported no relapses among 18 treated patients, whereas Ringelstein et al. observed relapse freedom in 11 of 14 patients with treatment-refractory disease and a reduction in median ARR from 1.75 to 0.17,20 Xu et al. reported a reduction in median ARR from 1.9 to 0 in patients with recurrent treatment-refractory MOG-IgG-associated optic neuritis. 18 Capecchi et al. found that 14 of 16 patients remained relapse-free during longer-term follow-up, while Shi et al. reported greater reductions in ARR and EDSS with tocilizumab than with rituximab or prednisolone.19,26 The largest cohort, reported by Vilaseca et al., contributed 116 patients and demonstrated an ARR reduction from 0.64 to 0.09 relapses per person-year, with 100 patients (86.2%) remaining relapse-free. 23 Leave-one-out analysis indicated that the overall estimate remained broadly stable after exclusion of individual studies.

Importantly, the low statistical heterogeneity should not be interpreted as indicating uniform clinical populations or treatment protocols. Where dosing was clearly reported, tocilizumab was generally administered intravenously at approximately 8 mg/kg every 4 weeks, particularly in the Ringelstein and Xu cohorts.17,18 However, dose, route, treatment interval, and duration were incompletely reported in several studies; some patients transitioned to subcutaneous administration, and the largest cohort also included satralizumab.19,23 Clinical populations likewise differed. Several cohorts were enriched for patients with highly active or treatment-refractory disease and substantial previous treatment exposure, whereas Capecchi et al. also included patients treated earlier in the disease course.17–19 Phenotypic composition varied as well: Xu et al. exclusively enrolled patients with recurrent refractory optic neuritis, whereas Capecchi et al. included patients with optic neuritis, myelitis, and multifocal disease.18,19 Available aggregate data did not provide sufficiently consistent phenotype-specific relapse-free outcomes to determine whether response to IL-6R inhibition differs between optic neuritis, myelitis, cerebral/ADEM, or other MOGAD phenotypes. Thus, I2=0% reflects low detected statistical heterogeneity but does not eliminate clinically important variation in dosing, phenotype, treatment sequencing, follow-up, or concomitant therapy.

Observational evidence from other maintenance therapies provides clinically relevant context for the pooled 85.0% relapse-free proportion. In the international rituximab study by Whittam et al., 53 of 101 patients (52.5%) with relapsing MOGAD who initiated rituximab after two or more attacks remained relapse-free over a median 12.1 months. 8 Rituximab therefore reduced relapse activity, but substantial breakthrough disease remained. Consistent with this observation, Barreras et al. reported that 14 of 23 patients with MOGAD relapsed during a median 2.1 years of rituximab treatment, corresponding to 9 of 23 patients (39.1%) remaining relapse-free. 27 These findings reinforce that relapse prevention with B-cell depletion may be incomplete in treatment-experienced MOGAD.

Maintenance intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) has demonstrated more favorable relapse-prevention signals in relapsing MOGAD. In an international cohort of 59 adults, 58 (98%) had relapsing disease before IVIG initiation and 37 (63%) received IVIG because previous immunotherapy had failed. 28 Twenty patients experienced at least one relapse during treatment; consequently, 39 of 59 patients (66.1%) remained relapse-free over a median treatment duration of 1.7 years. A clinically important dose-frequency relationship was observed: 24 of 29 patients (82.8%) receiving at least 1 g/kg every 4 weeks remained relapse-free compared with 15 of 30 (50.0%) receiving lower or less frequent dosing. 28 Consistent with these findings, the historical IVIG comparison within the Vilaseca cohort suggested better relapse control with IL-6R blockade than with lower-dose IVIG, whereas no statistically significant difference was demonstrated compared with higher-dose IVIG. 23 Against this background, the pooled 85.0% relapse-free proportion observed with IL-6R inhibition is clinically encouraging, particularly because several contributing studies included patients with highly active, previously treated, or treatment-refractory disease. However, these cross-study comparisons are contextual rather than evidence of comparative superiority because patient selection, baseline disease activity, treatment sequencing, concomitant therapies, follow-up duration, dosing, and relapse definitions differed substantially.

Treatment selection must also account for safety, tolerability, and treatment burden. In the long-term rituximab cohort of patients with NMOSD and MOGAD, infections requiring hospitalization occurred in 13% and IgG hypogammaglobulinemia in 17% of the overall treated population. 27 These safety estimates were reported for the combined cohort rather than the MOGAD subgroup and therefore should be interpreted accordingly. IVIG does not produce sustained systemic immunosuppression, although repeated infusions and treatment burden remain important considerations. In the 59-patient adult MOGAD cohort, only two patients discontinued IVIG because of adverse effects. 28 IL-6R blockade, in contrast, requires attention to infectious complications, hematological and hepatic abnormalities, lipid changes, and infusion or injection reactions. These differences emphasize that therapeutic selection should consider relapse control together with age, phenotype, previous treatment response, comorbidity, infection risk, route and frequency of administration, tolerability, availability, and cost.

The observed stabilization or improvement in neurological disability is clinically relevant but requires cautious interpretation. MOGAD-related disability is predominantly attack-associated, and prevention of additional attacks may stabilize EDSS without implying direct neurological recovery. Capecchi et al. observed improvement in median EDSS from 2.8 to 2.3, while Shi et al. reported greater EDSS improvement with tocilizumab than with rituximab or prednisolone.19,26 Xu et al. demonstrated improvement in visual acuity in patients with refractory optic neuritis. 18 However, recovery from preceding attacks, rehabilitation, corticosteroid treatment, and regression to the mean may have contributed to these observations. Disability outcomes were not consistently measured at standardized time points, and EDSS may inadequately capture visual morbidity, which is particularly relevant in MOGAD.

MRI findings were generally supportive but insufficiently standardized for quantitative synthesis. Ringelstein et al. reported reduced inflammatory brain MRI activity during tocilizumab treatment, Capecchi et al. observed no radiological progression, and Shi et al. reported a reduction in annual T2 lesion volume with tocilizumab compared with rituximab.17,19,26 Although these findings provide supportive evidence of reduced inflammatory activity, variation in imaging protocols, timing, anatomical coverage, and outcome definitions prevented meaningful pooling. Future studies should adopt prespecified imaging endpoints, including new or enlarging T2 lesions, gadolinium-enhancing lesions, optic nerve activity, and spinal cord lesion burden.

The pooled proportion of reported infectious adverse events with IL-6R inhibition was 28.0%, based on studies providing extractable unique-patient infection numerators. Capecchi et al. reported infections requiring medical treatment in 4 of 16 patients, Xu et al. reported an upper respiratory tract infection in 1 of 10 patients, and Vilaseca et al. reported infections in 34 of 116 patients, including severe infections in 10 patients.18,19,23 Other reported adverse events included transient liver-enzyme abnormalities, dyslipidemia, diarrhea, and infusion or injection intolerance. This pooled safety estimate should be interpreted cautiously because definitions and ascertainment of infection differed substantially across studies. Follow-up duration, age, prior immunotherapy, corticosteroid exposure, comorbidities, and concomitant immunosuppression may also have influenced infection risk. Accordingly, the pooled proportion is not a standardized incidence rate and should not be used for direct numerical safety comparisons with rituximab or IVIG.

Confounding from corticosteroids and other background therapies remains an important limitation. In Xu et al., 9 of 10 patients received tocilizumab as add-on therapy, and eight continued low-dose corticosteroids. 18 Ringelstein et al. also included patients receiving concomitant therapy, whereas Capecchi et al. evaluated tocilizumab as monotherapy.17,19 Because corticosteroid dose, taper duration, maintenance exposure, and steroid-sparing outcomes were inconsistently reported, the pooled relapse-free estimate cannot be attributed solely to IL-6R inhibition in all cohorts. Confounding by indication is also likely because IL-6R blockade was frequently selected for patients with more active disease or multiple previous treatment failures, while treatment choice may have been influenced by phenotype, comorbidity, prior response, clinician preference, and access.

This review has several strengths. It incorporates the largest available MOGAD-specific IL-6R inhibitor cohort and uses MOGAD-specific efficacy numerators even when parent studies contained mixed MOGAD/NMOSD populations. Relapse-free outcomes, ARR, disability, MRI activity, and safety were considered together, and methodological quality was assessed consistently using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. The primary pooled outcome was supplemented by leave-one-out analysis, and the safety synthesis was restricted to studies providing extractable unique-patient infection numerators.

Several limitations remain. Only six studies were eligible, and all were observational. The largest cohort contributed most of the statistical weight, and smaller studies reporting complete or near-complete relapse suppression raise the possibility of small-study and publication effects. Formal publication-bias testing and meta-regression were not appropriate because fewer than 10 studies were available. Follow-up durations and relapse definitions differed, and relapse-free proportions therefore represented different observation periods rather than a standardized time-specific probability. Dose, route, dosing interval, and treatment duration were incompletely reported in several studies, while clinical phenotype and previous or concomitant treatment exposure also varied. Individual-patient data were unavailable, preventing reliable dose-specific, phenotype-specific, corticosteroid-dependence, adult-versus-paediatric, or tocilizumab-versus-satralizumab analyses. Selection bias, retrospective outcome ascertainment, regression to the mean, confounding by indication, and concomitant therapy may have inflated apparent treatment effects. The protocol was not prospectively registered, and safety outcomes were inconsistently defined and could not be adjusted for treatment exposure.

Future research should prioritize adequately powered randomized controlled trials with standardized relapse definitions and independent adjudication. Comparative studies should evaluate IL-6R inhibition against clinically relevant alternatives, particularly adequately dosed IVIG and rituximab, while stratifying patients by age, phenotype, prior relapse frequency, previous treatment failure, MOG-IgG status, and corticosteroid dependence. Standardized reporting of IL-6R inhibitor dose, route, dosing interval, treatment duration, corticosteroid exposure, concomitant immunotherapy, relapse rate per person-year, relapse-free survival, disability, visual function, MRI activity, quality of life, severe infections, and treatment discontinuation would substantially improve comparability across studies. Longer follow-up is required to determine durability of relapse prevention, optimal treatment duration, steroid-sparing effects, and outcomes after treatment withdrawal.

Conclusion

IL-6 receptor inhibition was associated with substantial relapse control and generally stable clinical and radiological outcomes in selected patients with MOGAD. The pooled relapse-free proportion of 85.0% is clinically encouraging, particularly given the inclusion of several highly active and treatment-refractory cohorts. However, confidence in this estimate remains limited by observational study designs, variable treatment regimens and follow-up, clinical heterogeneity, concomitant therapies, and the influence of one large cohort. IL-6 receptor blockade should therefore be considered a promising maintenance strategy while robust randomized and comparative evidence is awaited.
