Directional Dynamics of the Non-Hermitian Skin Effect
Bin Yi

Abstract
The dynamical consequences of the non-Hermitian skin effect (NHSE) remain largely unexplored despite extensive studies of its static properties. Here we address this gap by applying quantum Liang information flow (QLIF)—an inherently directional measure of causal influence—to the non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model with non-reciprocal hopping. Unlike symmetric correlation functions, QLIF directly captures the directional asymmetry 𝕋 R → L ≠ 𝕋 L → R \mathbb{T}_{R\to L}\neq\mathbb{T}_{L\to R} characteristic of non-reciprocal systems. We demonstrate a “scissors effect” where the asymmetry Δ 𝕋 \Delta_{\mathbb{T}} varies approximately linearly with the non-reciprocity parameter γ \gamma for small | γ | |\gamma| , and exhibits non-monotonic dependence on the skin length ξ \xi , with optimal asymmetry at moderate skin localization. The velocity ordering v eff ​ ( γ < 0 ) > v eff ​ ( 0 ) > v eff ​ ( γ > 0 ) v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma<0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma>0) reveals NHSE-induced blocking of information flow against the skin direction. Three distinct temporal regimes emerge: light-cone-bounded spreading, γ \gamma -dependent stabilization, and coherent oscillations. These results establish the first quantitative connection between static skin localization and directional information dynamics, offering new insights into information propagation in non-reciprocal quantum systems.

We consider a non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) chain with
N

37

H=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\mathcal{T}_{0}\,\mathbf{c}_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\left(\mathbf{c}_{j+1}^{\dagger}\mathcal{T}_{+}\,\mathbf{c}_{j}+\text{H.c.}\right),

(1)

where
\mathbf{c}_{j}=(c_{\alpha,j},c_{\beta,j})^{T}

\mathcal{T}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{1}+\gamma\\
t_{1}-\gamma&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{T}_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
t_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2)

Here
t_{1}
(

\gamma
parametrizes non-reciprocity

For
\gamma\neq 0

;
16

determines localization:
\gamma>0
(
r<1
) gives left-boundary localization;
\gamma<0
(
r>1
) gives right-boundary localization. The skin length
\xi=1/|\ln r|
diverges in the Hermitian limit.

We set

L=42

;

\mathbb{T}_{B\to A}(t)=S_{A}(t)-S_{A\!\not\!B}(t),

where
S_{A\!\not\!B}
is the entropy under frozen-
B
evolution (Fig.

\mathbb{T}_{B\to A}\neq\mathbb{T}_{A\to B}

Scissors effect.

Our first key result concerns the breakdown of left-right symmetry in QLIF when non-reciprocity is introduced. In the Hermitian limit (
\gamma=0

\mathbb{T}_{R\to L}(t)=\mathbb{T}_{L\to R}(t)

\gamma<0

\gamma>0

\alpha\to\beta

\mathbb{T}

Three temporal regimes.

The time evolution exhibits three distinct regimes (Fig.
2

t\lesssim 4
), QLIF remains negligible until information propogates to observation sites, governed by the Lieb-Robinson bound. For
\gamma\neq 0

4\lesssim t\lesssim 10

\gamma

\text{sgn}(I_{\gamma})\approx-\text{sgn}(\gamma)
for
|\gamma|\geq 0.3

I_{\gamma}
is the time-integrated QLIF, reflecting sublattice-level probability accumulation. (III) At late times (
t\gtrsim 10

T_{\text{osc}}\approx 2\pi/\Delta E
determined by intrinsic energy-level spacing, independent of system size (see Supplemental Material
1
). The NHSE protects oscillation amplitude against dilution in large systems because skin-localized eigenstates maintain high local probability density.

Skin length dependence.

While
\gamma
directly enters the Hamiltonian, the physically relevant quantity controlling eigenstate localization is the skin length
\xi=1/|\ln r|
, where
r=\sqrt{|(t_{1}-\gamma)/(t_{1}+\gamma)|}
determines the exponential envelope
|\psi_{n}|\sim r^{n}
of skin-localized eigenstates. Figure
5

|\Delta_{\mathbb{T}}|
exhibits
non-monotonic
dependence on
\xi

\xi_{\text{opt}}\approx 3
–
4
before declining.

The physical mechanisms can be understood through three regimes: In the
strong localization regime
(small
\xi
), eigenstates are excessively confined near boundaries, suppressing signal propagation to bulk observation sites—paradoxically, extreme NHSE confines dynamics to boundaries and reduces the measurable effect. In the
optimal regime
(moderate
\xi
comparable to observation distance), non-reciprocity-induced symmetry breaking is strong while bulk transport remains viable, producing maximal directional asymmetry. In the
weak localization regime
(large
\xi
), the system approches the Hermitian limit and directional signatures diminish. The
\gamma<0
and
\gamma>0
branches show different peak positions, reflecting the interplay between skin direction and measurement geometry. This non-monotonic behavior establishes the first quantitative link between static skin localization length and dynamic information transfer, implying that
moderate
non-Hermiticity produces the strongest QLIF signatures.

NHSE blocking and velocity ordering.

To probe the causal structure of information propagation, we measure the onset time
t^{*}
—the earliest time at which
|\mathbb{T}_{j_{0}\to j_{0}+d}|>\epsilon

\epsilon=10^{-6}

v_{\text{max}}=2\min(t_{1},t_{2})=1.0
and the Lieb-Robinson bound
v_{\text{LR}}=2\max(t_{1},t_{2})=2.0

A clear
\gamma

v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma<0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma>0).

(5)

\gamma>0
localizes eigenstates toward the left boundary, impeding rightward propagation. Modes travelling
against

This NHSE blocking provides a dynamical manifestation of the static eigenstate localization, demostrating that the skin effect not only reshapes the eigenstate distribution but fundamentally alters information transport.

V
Discussion

Our results reveal how each observable connects to the underlying physics. The scissors effect (Fig.
2
) demonstrates that QLIF directly captures the directional bias induced by non-reciprocal hopping. The full-range
\gamma
scan (Fig.
3
) reveals non-monotonic behavior with a striking prediction: the strongest NHSE (largest
|\gamma|
) produces the
weakest
measurable asymmetry. This occurs because as
|\gamma|\to t_{1}
, intracell hopping becomes completely unidirectional (
t_{1}\pm\gamma\to 0
or
2t_{1}
), confining all eigenstates to within 1–2 sites of the boundary. Consequently, excitations initialized in the bulk cannot propagate, and both
\mathbb{T}_{R\to L}
and
\mathbb{T}_{L\to R}
vanish. The optimal asymmetry at moderate
|\gamma|\approx 0.15
–
0.3
reflects a balance: non-reciprocity is strong enough to break symmetry, yet skin localization remains weak enough to permit bulk transport. This non-monotonic behavior, consistently observed in both the
\gamma
-scan (Fig.
3
) and
\xi
-scan (Fig.
5
), provides clear experimental guidance: moderate non-Hermiticity maximizes observable signatures. The sublattice analysis (Fig.
4
) distinguishes genuine non-Hermitian effects from structural artifacts. The velocity ordering (Fig.
6
) provides direct evidence of NHSE blocking.

QLIF overcomes limitations of conventional measures: unlike symmetric correlation functions, it captures the asymmetry
\mathbb{T}_{R\to L}\neq\mathbb{T}_{L\to R}
characteristic of non-reciprocal dynamics. While Lieb-Robinson bounds
23
;
6
rely on quasiparticle propagation
7
—problematic for non-Hermitian systems with biorthogonal eigenstates
16
—QLIF operates directly at the level of entropy production. Our results extend recent Hermitian applications
9
to the non-Hermitian regime.

Several directions remain open: interactions may modify the competition between NHSE localization and many-body delocalization; the interplay with nontrivial topology near
t_{2}=t_{1}

19
merits investigation. Experimental verification appears feasible in photonic lattices
33
;
32
and topolectrical circuits
14
;
12
. Extensions to many-body systems and higher-order skin effects
41
would further illuminate directional dynamics.

VI
Conclusion

We have applied quantum Liang information flow to the non-Hermitian SSH model, establishing the first quantitative connection between skin localization and directional information dynamics. Our principal findings are: (i) a scissors effect with
\Delta_{\mathbb{T}}\propto\gamma
for small
|\gamma|
; (ii) non-monotonic
\xi
-dependence with optimal asymmetry at moderate localization; (iii) velocity ordering
v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma<0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma>0)
demonstrating NHSE blocking; and (iv) three distinct temporal regimes. These results establish QLIF as a powerful probe for non-Hermitian dynamics, opening new avenues for understanding directional information propagation in non-reciprocal quantum systems.

A.2
Derivation of Light-Cone Velocity

Starting from the dispersion relation Eq. (
7
), the quasiparticle group velocity is

v_{g}(k)=\frac{\partial E}{\partial k}=-\frac{t_{1}t_{2}\sin k}{E(k)}.

(8)

To find the maximum of
|v_{g}(k)|
, we analyze

|v_{g}(k)|=\frac{t_{1}t_{2}|\sin k|}{\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}+2t_{1}t_{2}\cos k}}.

(9)

At the critical point
t_{1}=t_{2}\equiv t
, the dispersion simplifies to

E(k)=t\sqrt{2+2\cos k}=2t\left|\cos\frac{k}{2}\right|,

(10)

and the group velocity becomes

|v_{g}(k)|=\frac{t^{2}\cdot 2|\sin(k/2)\cos(k/2)|}{2t|\cos(k/2)|}=t\left|\sin\frac{k}{2}\right|,

(11)

which is maximized at
k=\pi
, yielding
v_{\mathrm{max}}=t
.

For general parameters, the maximum group velocity is given by

v_{\mathrm{max}}=\min(t_{1},t_{2}).

(12)

To prove this, assume
t_{2}\leq t_{1}
and set
v_{g}(k^{*})=t_{2}
. This gives
t_{1}\sin k^{*}=E(k^{*})
, which after squaring and simplifying yields
(t_{1}\cos k^{*}+t_{2})^{2}=0
, hence
\cos k^{*}=-t_{2}/t_{1}
. This solution exists when
t_{2}\leq t_{1}
, and the corresponding velocity is exactly
v_{g}(k^{*})=t_{2}=\min(t_{1},t_{2})
.
This result is analogous to the transverse-field Ising model, where
v_{\mathrm{max}}=\min(J,h)
with
J
the exchange coupling and
h
the transverse field

A.3
Lieb-Robinson Bound

The Lieb-Robinson theorem
23
;
6
provides a rigorous upper bound on information propagation in lattice systems with local interactions. For a one-dimensional chain with nearest-neighbor hopping of strength
J
, the Lieb-Robinson bound takes the form

\|[A_{x}(t),B_{0}]\|\leq C\|A\|\|B\|e^{-a(|x|-v_{\text{LR}}|t|)},

(13)

where
v_{\text{LR}}
is the Lieb-Robinson velocity and
a
is a decay constant. For tight-binding models, a standard estimate gives
v_{\text{LR}}\sim 2J_{\max}
where
J_{\max}
is the largest hopping amplitude
6
. For the Hermitian SSH model (
\gamma=0
) with hopping amplitudes
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, we use

v_{\text{LR}}=2\max(t_{1},t_{2}).

(14)

For our parameters (
t_{1}=1
,
t_{2}=0.5
), this yields
v_{\text{LR}}=2.0
.

The bound guarantees exponential suppression outside the light cone but does not forbid small leakage. This explains why onset times in Fig.
6
can appear slightly “superluminal”—we detect the exponentially small signal beyond the strict light cone.

Note that
v_{\text{LR}}\geq v_{\max}
always holds: the Lieb-Robinson bound is a rigorous upper limit, while the maximum group velocity
v_{\max}=\min(t_{1},t_{2})
derived from the dispersion relation is typically tighter. For
t_{1}=1
,
t_{2}=0.5
:
v_{\max}=0.5
(per unit cell) versus
v_{\text{LR}}=2.0
.

A.4
NHSE-Induced Velocity Ordering

The onset time
t^{*}
(earliest time with
|\mathbb{T}|>10^{-6}
) reveals
\gamma
-dependent velocity ordering:

v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma<0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)>v_{\mathrm{eff}}(\gamma>0).

(15)

This reflects NHSE blocking:
\gamma>0
localizes eigenstates toward the left boundary, impeding rightward propagation. At large distances (
d\gtrsim 10
), this blocking becomes dramatic as information must propagate against the skin direction.

Appendix B
Numerical Methods

B.1
Exact Diagonalization

We numerically diagonalize the non-Hermitian SSH Hamiltonian using standard linear algebra routines. For non-Hermitian matrices, we compute both right and left eigenvectors, though for QLIF calculations only the right eigenvectors are needed.

B.2
Time Evolution and QLIF Implementation

For a single-particle state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j}\psi_{j}c_{j}^{\dagger}|0\rangle
, the site entropy reduces to binary entropy:

S_{A}=-|\psi_{A}|^{2}\ln|\psi_{A}|^{2}-(1-|\psi_{A}|^{2})\ln(1-|\psi_{A}|^{2}).

(16)

The freezing operation sets all matrix elements connected to site
B
to zero:

(H_{\!\not\!B})_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }i=B\text{ or }j=B,\\
H_{ij}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(17)

Time evolution is performed via exact diagonalization:

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{n}e^{-iE_{n}t}|n\rangle\langle n|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(18)

where
\{E_{n},|n\rangle\}
are eigenvalues and right eigenvectors. For non-Hermitian
H
, biorthogonal normalization is employed.

B.3
Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR)

The inverse participation ratio (IPR) is a standard measure of localization
8
and has been widely used to characterize the skin effect in non-Hermitian systems
42
;
25
. It is defined as

\text{IPR}=\sum_{j}|\psi_{j}|^{4}\bigg/\left(\sum_{j}|\psi_{j}|^{2}\right)^{2},

(19)

ranging from
1/L
(extended) to
1
(fully localized).

Here we derive the IPR for eigenstates exhibiting the non-Hermitian skin effect.

For a skin-effect eigenstate localized at the left boundary, the probability amplitude decays exponentially:

|\psi_{n}|^{2}=A\,r^{2(n-1)},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,N,

(20)

where
n
labels the unit cell,
r=|\beta|
is the skin parameter [Eq. (
)], and
A
is the normalization constant.

Normalization.

From
\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\psi_{n}|^{2}=1
:

A\sum_{n=1}^{N}r^{2(n-1)}=A\frac{1-r^{2N}}{1-r^{2}}=1,

(21)

giving

A=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-r^{2N}}.

(22)

IPR calculation.

The IPR is

IPR

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}|\psi_{n}|^{4}=A^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}r^{4(n-1)}

\displaystyle=A^{2}\frac{1-r^{4N}}{1-r^{4}}=\left(\frac{1-r^{2}}{1-r^{2N}}\right)^{2}\frac{1-r^{4N}}{(1-r^{2})(1+r^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-r^{2})(1-r^{4N})}{(1-r^{2N})^{2}(1+r^{2})}.

(23)

Using the identity
1-r^{4N}=(1-r^{2N})(1+r^{2N})
:

\text{IPR}_{\text{th}}=\frac{1-r^{2}}{1+r^{2}}\cdot\frac{1+r^{2N}}{1-r^{2N}}.

(24)

Limiting cases.

•

Strong localization
(
r\ll 1
,
N
large):
\text{IPR}\to(1-r^{2})/(1+r^{2})\to 1
.

•

Extended states
(
r=1
): Using L’Hôpital’s rule,
\text{IPR}\to 1/N
, the expected value for uniform distribution.

•

Finite-size correction
: For
r<1
and large
N
,
\text{IPR}\approx(1-r^{2})/(1+r^{2})
.

The IPR values listed in Table
1
are obtained from direct numerical diagonalization, which includes finite-size corrections and deviations from the idealized exponential decay near boundaries. For the Hermitian case (
r=1
), the table correctly shows
\text{IPR}=1/L\approx 0.024
for
L=42
sites.

B.4
Oscillation Analysis

Late-time QLIF oscillations have period
T_{\text{osc}}\approx 2.2
independent of system size
L
, ruling out finite-size boundary reflections. The period is determined by intrinsic energy-level spacing:
T_{\text{osc}}\sim 2\pi/\Delta E
where
\Delta E\approx 2.9
matches the bulk bandwidth.

The NHSE protects oscillation amplitude: comparing
L=20
to
L=120
, the Hermitian amplitude decays by
\sim 3\times
while the non-Hermitian (
\gamma=0.3
) amplitude decays by only
\sim 1.3\times
, because skin-localized eigenstates maintain high local probability density.

B.5
Sublattice Configuration for QLIF Measurements

The SSH model possesses a bipartite structure with alternating
\alpha
(even sites) and
\beta
(odd sites) sublattices. When measuring the directional asymmetry of QLIF, it is essential that all relevant sites—the initial excitation site
j_{0}
and the left/right observation sites—reside on the
same sublattice
.

If the observation sites are placed on a different sublattice from the initial state, the different hopping structure (intracell vs. intercell) introduces an intrinsic asymmetry even in the Hermitian limit (
\gamma=0
). Specifically, for
\gamma=0
with mixed-sublattice configuration:

\mathbb{T}_{R\to L}(t)\neq\mathbb{T}_{L\to R}(t),

(25)

with differences reaching
|\Delta_{\mathbb{T}}|\sim 0.07
at
t=10
. This spurious asymmetry is entirely a consequence of the sublattice mismatch, not the non-Hermitian physics.

By contrast, when all sites are on the same sublattice (e.g., all on
\alpha
), the Hermitian case exhibits perfect symmetry:

\mathbb{T}_{R\to L}(t)=\mathbb{T}_{L\to R}(t)\quad(\gamma=0,\text{ same sublattice}),

(26)

with numerical deviations at the level of machine precision (
\sim 10^{-15}
).

For the results presented in this work, we use the “same-sublattice” configuration: the initial state is placed on site
j_{0}=20
(
\alpha
sublattice), with observation sites at
j_{0}\pm d
where
d
is an even integer to ensure all sites remain on the
\alpha
sublattice. The standard configuration uses
d=6
, corresponding to a distance of 3 unit cells.
