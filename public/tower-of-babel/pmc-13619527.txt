Sport-based interventions for post-disaster mental health and psychosocial recovery: a multi-level systematic review and meta-analysis within the MHPSS framework
Authors: Tayfun Kara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disasters and humanitarian crises affect both individual mental health and broader psychosocial functioning, but these outcome domains are not interchangeable. This systematic review and meta-analysis examined structured sport-, exercise-, play-, yoga/body-awareness-, physical-education-, and sport-for-protection-based interventions within a mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) framework. Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, Scopus, SPORTDiscus, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar were searched for studies published from January 1, 2000, through November 1, 2025. Eligible populations were affected by disasters, conflict, forced displacement, refugeehood, humanitarian crises, or crisis-related restrictions. Outcome-specific quantitative analyses were conducted for comparable mental health symptoms, whereas heterogeneous psychosocial and community constructs were synthesized narratively and integrated through a joint interpretation matrix. Twenty-eight studies or programme evaluations were included; 20 contributed data to at least one quantitative analysis, with fewer studies contributing to each controlled outcome model. Random-effects models indicated favorable small-to-moderate average effects for PTSD symptoms (Hedges’ g = −0.32, 95% CI [−0.50, −0.14]; I2 = 58%), depression (g = −0.44, 95% CI [−0.60, −0.28]; I2 = 47%), and anxiety (g = −0.41, 95% CI [−0.58, −0.24]; I2 = 52%). Narrative findings identified routine, emotional regulation, belonging, social connectedness, life skills, and community cohesion as possible complementary pathways and outcomes. However, variation in populations, designs, intervention components, comparators, and measures, together with risk-of-bias concerns and small outcome sets, limits certainty and generalizability. Sport-based interventions may function as scalable community or focused non-specialized components of MHPSS when they are trauma-sensitive, culturally adapted, safeguarded, monitored, and linked to referral pathways. They should not be interpreted as substitutes for specialized mental health care, trauma-focused treatment, child protection, or clinical risk management.

Introduction

Disasters are multidimensional crises that profoundly affect individuals’ mental health, social relationships, sense of security, and communities’ capacity for reorganization (1). Extraordinary situations such as earthquakes, floods, epidemics, war, and mass displacement interrupt individuals’ living spaces, social support networks, economic security, and expectations regarding the future, thereby extending the effects of traumatic experience far beyond the moment of the event itself (2). Factors emerging after disasters, such as loss, uncertainty, housing problems, disruption of family integrity, and interruption of educational and working life, may pave the way for psychological outcomes such as post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, and a persistent state of hypervigilance (3–6).

In this review, “sport-based interventions” is an umbrella term for structured exercise, play, sport-for-protection, yoga or body-awareness practices, physical education, and planned physical activity delivered with an explicit mental health, psychosocial, protective, or recovery-oriented purpose. Two outcome families are distinguished throughout. Mental health outcomes refer to symptoms or clinically adjacent indicators assessed at the individual level, including post-traumatic stress, depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation. Psychosocial outcomes refer to individual–social and community processes such as emotional regulation, perceived control, routine, social connectedness, belonging, life skills, social participation, community cohesion, and resilience. These domains may interact, but they are not treated as interchangeable: symptom change was eligible for outcome-specific quantitative pooling when comparable data were available, whereas heterogeneous psychosocial processes were synthesized narratively. This distinction follows the layered logic of mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS), in which basic services and security, community and family supports, focused non-specialized supports, and specialized services are complementary rather than substitutable (7, 8).

Contemporary disaster management approaches acknowledge that psychosocial recovery is as critical as physical recovery (9). However, access to professional mental health services in post-disaster settings is often limited. The inadequacy of services, spatial and economic barriers, concerns about stigmatization, the collective nature of trauma, and individuals’ distant attitudes toward traditional psychological counselling services increase the need for complementary and community-based forms of intervention (10). At this point, physical activity, play, and sport-based interventions are regarded as holistic psychosocial support tools with the potential to strengthen emotional regulation, social reconnection, routine formation, and community solidarity. The importance of sport-based interventions in the context of post-disaster recovery stems from their capacity to affect individual and social levels simultaneously. Physical activity may generate regulatory effects on body perception, sleep patterns, energy levels, and mood, which are disrupted after trauma (11). Structured exercise, yoga, team games, and play-based sport programmes may help individuals re-establish bodily awareness. In addition, sport and play activities carried out in groups provide individuals with a safe space for social contact, strengthen peer support, create a sense of common purpose, and may reconstruct the sense of belonging weakened after traumatic experience (12). Therefore, sport-based programmes may offer a more natural, accessible, and easily participatory form of support for children and young people, particularly when compared with psychosocial interventions based on verbal expression (13–15).

Another important aspect of sport and physical activity in post-disaster conditions is their ability to generate routine and predictability in environments where everyday life has been interrupted. Regular physical activities enable individuals to acquire a structured rhythm during the day, reduce bodily tension, and engage in safe interaction with their social environment (16). Sport-based programmes implemented in temporary accommodation areas, schools, community centres, and youth spaces may also support the reorganization of social life after disasters. Elements such as teamwork, fair play, cooperation, mutual trust, and orientation toward a common goal make visible the community-level solidarity-strengthening dimension of sport (17). Within this framework, sport-based psychosocial support programmes conducted with children and adolescents carry particular importance. It may not always be possible for children to directly express, in verbal form, the feelings of fear, loss, insecurity, and uncertainty they experience after disasters (18). Play and sport may mediate the expression of these emotions in more indirect, safe, and developmentally appropriate ways. Movement, play, and group interaction may support the child in regaining a sense of control, forming bonds with peers, and feeling competent. For this reason, physical education, sport, and play activities may be considered among the complementary components of post-disaster child protection and psychosocial support systems (19–21). Another advantage of sport-based interventions is that they are relatively low-cost, culturally adaptable, and applicable on a community basis. In post-disaster environments where resources are limited, it may not always be possible to deliver individual therapy services requiring high levels of expertise to large population groups (22). In contrast, appropriately structured sport, play, and physical activity programmes can reach broader populations through the participation of teachers, coaches, volunteers, local institutions, and community leaders (23). However, this accessibility does not mean that every sport-based programme will automatically produce a therapeutic effect. Interventions must be designed in a trauma-sensitive manner, participant safety must be ensured, they must be carried out with content appropriate to age and developmental stage, and they must be adapted to the cultural context (24–26). To strengthen the theoretical basis of this review, the present study is framed around a multi-level Sport-Based Psychosocial Recovery Model. This model assumes that sport-based interventions may influence post-disaster recovery through three interrelated pathways (27). The first is a biological pathway, through which structured movement may contribute to physiological regulation, reduced bodily arousal, improved sleep, and stabilization of stress-related somatic responses (28). The second is a psychological pathway, through which sport and play may support self-efficacy, emotional regulation, perceived control, motivation, and the reconstruction of daily routine. The third is a social pathway, through which group-based activities may foster connectedness, belonging, peer support, trust, communication, and community cohesion (29). The 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes illustrate the relevance of this multi-level perspective, as such disasters disrupt not only individual psychological functioning but also the routines, relationships, and community structures that sustain recovery (30).

Although the literature presents promising findings regarding post-disaster sport and physical activity, the evidence is fragmented across crisis contexts, intervention forms, populations, and outcome domains (12, 31, 32). Studies have examined earthquakes, war and displacement, refugee settings, disaster-risk communities, and pandemic restrictions; interventions range from yoga and structured exercise to play-based programmes, physical education, and sport-for-protection. Direct comparison is further complicated by small samples, short follow-up, inconsistent reporting of intervention fidelity, and substantial variation in outcome measures. The primary objective of this review was therefore to estimate outcome-specific effects on PTSD, depression, and anxiety when controlled quantitative data were sufficiently comparable. The secondary objective was to synthesize evidence concerning broader psychosocial recovery, including routine, emotional regulation, social support, belonging, life skills, community cohesion, and resilience. The review question was: among disaster-, displacement-, refugee-, pandemic-, war-, or humanitarian-crisis-affected populations, what effects do structured sport- and physical-activity-based interventions have on (a) individual mental health symptoms and (b) broader psychosocial recovery, compared with usual care, no intervention, waitlist, an alternative intervention, or—only in analyses kept separate from the main controlled models—baseline conditions?

Method

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed to evaluate sport-based interventions across two related but analytically distinct evidence streams: outcome-specific quantitative evidence for mental health symptoms and structured narrative evidence for broader psychosocial recovery. The review question and eligibility criteria were specified using PICOS, and reporting followed PRISMA 2020 and Cochrane methodological guidance (33, 34).

Protocol and reporting framework

A review protocol was developed before full-text eligibility decisions, data extraction, and quantitative synthesis, but it was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO because preliminary scoping and screening had already begun when registration was considered. Before full-text assessment, the review team specified the review question, eligibility criteria, outcome families, extraction fields, risk-of-bias tools, and planned synthesis strategy. Because the protocol was not publicly time-stamped, selective methodological decision-making cannot be excluded completely; this limitation is considered when interpreting the review’s findings.

Information sources and search limits

Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, Scopus, SPORTDiscus, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar were searched for records published from January 1, 2000, through November 1, 2025. The start date was selected to capture contemporary MHPSS practice and the period in which structured sport-, play-, movement-, and physical-activity-based programmes were increasingly reported in humanitarian and post-disaster settings. Search concepts covered three blocks—crisis exposure, sport or physical activity, and mental health or psychosocial outcomes—and were combined with Boolean operators. No language filter was applied during database searching. At full text, reports were included when they were available in English or Turkish or could be interpreted reliably by the review team; records that could not be accessed or interpreted sufficiently for eligibility assessment and extraction were excluded. Peer-reviewed studies and relevant official or programme reports were eligible, whereas popular media, opinion pieces without empirical or implementation data, and promotional materials were excluded (see Table 1).

Eligibility criteria, selection, and data extraction

Eligible populations were children, adolescents, or adults directly affected by a natural disaster, war or conflict, forced displacement, refugeehood, a humanitarian emergency, or crisis-related restrictions. Eligible interventions had to be structured, involve sport, exercise, play, physical activity, physical education, yoga/body awareness, or sport-for-protection, and state a mental health, psychosocial, protective, or recovery-oriented aim. For the systematic review, eligible reports had to provide empirical outcome data, qualitative findings, or implementation evidence relevant to at least one prespecified mental health or psychosocial domain. Protocols and publications based on overlapping samples could inform context or implementation but were not treated as independent outcome studies. Studies of routine physical activity without a crisis-exposed population, unstructured recreation without a stated recovery purpose, elite-sport performance, purely biomedical outcomes without a mental health or psychosocial measure, and reports lacking interpretable data were excluded. For the main meta-analysis, an independent comparison group and extractable post-intervention or compatible change-score data were additionally required; uncontrolled pre–post evidence was reserved for separate sensitivity or descriptive analyses.

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and then full texts. Disagreements were resolved by consensus; inter-rater agreement was strong (Cohen’s κ = 0.82). Extracted fields included publication year, country and crisis context, design, participant characteristics, sample size, intervention content and dose, comparator, measurement tool, follow-up, outcome data, implementation features, and synthesis role. When multiple reports used the same sample, the primary outcome report was used for pooled estimates and secondary reports were retained only for non-duplicative outcomes or contextual interpretation (see Figure 2).

Rationale for separate and integrated synthesis

The quantitative and narrative components answered different but complementary questions. Meta-analysis was restricted to outcomes that were sufficiently comparable in construct and reporting—principally PTSD, depression, and anxiety—and estimated the average direction and magnitude of symptom change. Narrative synthesis addressed psychosocial constructs for which statistical pooling would have obscured substantive differences in level of analysis, measurement, and programme purpose. It also examined mechanisms, feasibility, cultural adaptation, safeguarding, and implementation conditions. The two streams were integrated after separate analysis by mapping each pooled symptom outcome against recurrent psychosocial mechanisms and contextual conditions; convergence, complementarity, and discordance were then examined in an integration matrix (Table 2).

For continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences were calculated as Hedges’ g with small-sample correction. Negative values indicate lower symptom scores in the intervention group when higher scores represented worse symptoms. The main models prioritized post-intervention between-group effects from randomized or comparative studies. Compatible change scores were used only when direction and variance could be harmonized; uncontrolled pre–post estimates were not combined with controlled effects in the primary models. Where a study reported multiple measures of the same construct or multiple intervention arms sharing a comparator, effects were coded to avoid double-counting participants. Binary outcomes were summarized as odds ratios when pooling was appropriate.

Random-effects models were selected a priori because true effects were expected to vary across crisis contexts, populations, intervention components, delivery settings, and measures. Between-study variance was represented by τ2; Cochran’s Q tested whether observed dispersion exceeded sampling error, and I2 described the proportion of variability attributable to heterogeneity rather than chance. I2 was interpreted as a descriptive indicator rather than a fixed threshold. Prespecified exploratory subgroup categories were intervention type, population group, and crisis context. Sensitivity analyses excluded uncontrolled designs, high-risk-of-bias studies, and overlapping samples. Publication bias procedures were used only when the number of studies was adequate; with small outcome sets, funnel plots, Egger’s test, and trim-and-fill were treated as low-power, exploratory diagnostics rather than proof that bias was absent. Meta-analysis was conducted in Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software. Effect-size estimation followed established meta-analytic procedures (62). The structured narrative component was guided by principles for transparent narrative synthesis (72). Results

A total of 28 independent studies or programme evaluations met the systematic-review criteria. Twenty contributed extractable data to at least one quantitative analysis, although the number contributing to each primary controlled outcome model was smaller and varied by outcome. Reports that were qualitative, implementation-focused, protocol-only, insufficiently reported for effect-size calculation, or based on an already represented sample contributed only to narrative or contextual synthesis. Accordingly, “20 studies eligible for quantitative analysis” should not be read as 20 independent studies in every outcome model.

Study characteristics

As a result of the search, 3,528 records were identified; after duplicates were removed, 2,657 studies were evaluated in terms of title and abstract. Eighty-eight full texts were examined, and 60 studies that did not meet the criteria were excluded. A total of 28 studies were included in the systematic review and qualitative synthesis. Of these, 20 studies provided sufficient quantitative data and were included in the meta-analysis, while the remaining studies contributed to narrative synthesis, meta- synthesis, or contextual interpretation because of qualitative design, protocol status, insufficient quantitative outcome data, or risk of double counting.

The included studies were organized into synthesis categories according to crisis context, population group, intervention type, study design, and role in the overall synthesis. Unlike a grouped presentation, each citation is presented separately in Table 3 to make clear how individual studies contributed to the qualitative synthesis, narrative synthesis, conditional quantitative synthesis, subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, or main meta-analysis. Randomized controlled trials and comparative quantitative studies were prioritized for the main quantitative synthesis. Uncontrolled pre–post studies were considered only in sensitivity or separate pre–post analyses. Qualitative studies, field reports, implementation reports, and protocols were not treated as equivalent to controlled outcome studies, but they were retained to clarify programme mechanisms, feasibility, cultural adaptation, implementation barriers, and contextual conditions. Secondary analyses derived from the same trial were reported separately and were not double-counted in pooled outcome models.

Mental health outcomes

Mental health outcomes were limited to individual symptom or risk indicators: PTSD, depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation. PTSD, depression, and anxiety were considered for outcome-specific pooling; suicidal ideation was represented by a secondary analysis from one programme family and was therefore synthesized narratively. Psychosocial outcomes—including routine, emotional regulation, communication, belonging, social connectedness, life skills, and community cohesion—were not relabelled as mental health effects. This distinction prevents improvement in a social or developmental construct from being interpreted as equivalent to clinical symptom reduction.

The effects of sport- and physical activity-based interventions on mental health were evaluated through outcomes related to PTSD symptoms, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, and psychosocial recovery. The findings show that structured exercise, yoga/body-awareness practices, school-based physical education, online or home-based exercise, physical activity counselling, and sport-for-protection programmes generally produced results in favour of the intervention in terms of mental health symptoms, although the magnitude and consistency of effects differed across study designs and crisis contexts (35–43). Yoga and body-awareness-oriented approaches were particularly relevant for PTSD and stress-related symptoms, whereas structured exercise, online exercise, and sport-for-protection programmes contributed mainly to depression and anxiety outcomes (36, 39, 41, 42, 44). However, due to heterogeneity in study design, sample characteristics, crisis context, intervention content, comparator structure, and measurement tools, the results need to be interpreted separately according to outcome domains. While quantitative synthesis was possible for PTSD symptoms, depression, and anxiety, variables such as positive mood, social connectedness, communication, problem-solving, and psychosocial recovery were evaluated mostly within the scope of narrative synthesis and meta-synthesis (45–49) (see Table 4).

Psychosocial recovery and community resilience

Table 5 summarizes non-clinical psychosocial and community-level evidence by citation. These findings were not pooled because the studies differed in construct, unit of analysis, design, and measurement: for example, communication skills, safe social space, life skills, and community cohesion are related but not statistically interchangeable outcomes. The narrative synthesis therefore examined recurring mechanisms and implementation conditions rather than calculating a single “psychosocial recovery” effect. Across earthquake, displacement, refugee-camp, and disaster-risk settings, recurrent themes included restoration of routine, emotional expression, peer connection, belonging, teamwork, coping, and participation. Confidence in these themes is lower than confidence in a replicated pooled effect because much of the evidence was qualitative, uncontrolled, or programme-specific.

Psychosocial recovery and community resilience outcomes.

Table 5 shows that the effect of sport- and physical activity-based interventions on post-disaster psychosocial recovery is not limited solely to the reduction of individual mental health symptoms. The findings reveal that physical activity may have a supportive function in multidimensional domains such as psychological resilience, social connectedness, emotional release, communication, problem-solving, teamwork, fair play, safe group experience, life skills, and community participation (39, 45–50, 78). In particular, evidence from the Bam earthquake programme, the Game Connect sport-for-protection programme, the Merapi disaster-risk school-based physical education studies, and TeamUp-related movement-based interventions suggests that sport may contribute to the re-establishment of relationships within the community after disaster or displacement by reducing experiences of loneliness, insecurity, and social disconnection and by strengthening belonging, trust, peer support, and shared routine (39, 46–49, 51). However, these effects depend not on sport being inherently therapeutic, but rather on the intervention being designed in a safe, culturally adapted, age-appropriate manner and integrated with psychosocial support principles. Therefore, the studies in question indicate that sport-based interventions can be considered complementary psychosocial support tools that may strengthen post-disaster community resilience when embedded within broader MHPSS systems.

Summary of meta-analysis results.

Eligibility for quantitative synthesis was determined at the outcome level. Randomized and comparative quantitative studies with an independent comparator and extractable data were prioritized for the primary models. Uncontrolled pre–post studies, protocols, qualitative studies, field reports, implementation evaluations, and secondary reports from already represented samples were not pooled in the main controlled estimates. This design-based separation was necessary because combining within-person change with between-group effects would change the estimand and could exaggerate apparent intervention benefit.

Risk of bias and study quality

Risk of bias varied materially by design and affects the certainty—not merely the description—of the review conclusions. Randomized trials provided the strongest basis for causal inference, but common concerns included unclear allocation concealment, the impossibility of participant and provider blinding in behavioural programmes, attrition, adherence, and selective or incomplete outcome reporting. Lack of participant blinding was not treated automatically as fatal; its importance depended on whether outcomes were self-reported, whether outcome assessors could be blinded, and whether deviations from the intended intervention were balanced across groups. Non-randomized comparative studies were more vulnerable to baseline imbalance, confounding, selection into participation, and differential co-intervention. Uncontrolled pre–post studies could describe change but could not separate intervention effects from spontaneous recovery, regression to the mean, historical events, or concurrent support. Qualitative and implementation studies were not entered into the quantitative risk-of-bias model and were used only for mechanisms, acceptability, feasibility, and context (see Table 7).

Discussion

Integrated interpretation within the MHPSS framework

The two evidence streams converge on a qualified conclusion. The meta-analysis indicates favorable average changes in PTSD, depression, and anxiety symptoms, while the narrative synthesis identifies routine, emotional regulation, social connection, belonging, life skills, and safe participation as plausible psychosocial functions. The narrative evidence therefore adds explanatory and contextual depth; it does not constitute an independent confirmation of the pooled symptom effects. Nor does convergence establish mediation, because few included studies formally tested whether changes in belonging, routine, or regulation produced symptom improvement. Within the MHPSS framework, the evidence most strongly supports sport-based programmes as community and family supports or focused, non-specialized supports that should connect—through referral pathways—to specialized care when clinical risk or severe impairment is identified (7, 8).

Interpretation of effect sizes and heterogeneity

The pooled effects were small-to-moderate for PTSD (g = −0.32), depression (g = −0.44), and anxiety (g = −0.41). These standardized effects describe average differences across dissimilar measures; they do not translate directly into diagnosis, remission, or clinically important change for an individual participant. Their public-health relevance may nevertheless be meaningful when low-threshold programmes reach populations with limited access to formal services. That potential must be balanced against I2 values of 47–58%, which indicate that the magnitude of benefit varied across settings and was not explained by sampling error alone. Differences in age, trauma exposure, displacement status, programme content and dose, comparator, adherence, and outcome measurement are plausible sources, but the available evidence does not permit confident attribution of heterogeneity to any single factor.

Study-level findings illustrate why the pooled estimates require caution. The uncontrolled refugee-camp study by Knappe et al. (38) reported improvements across several outcomes, but without a concurrent comparator the changes cannot be attributed securely to the intervention. In contrast, Nordbrandt et al. (41) found no clear additional PTSD benefit when body-awareness therapy or mixed physical activity was added to treatment as usual. More recent pragmatic trials in asylum-seeker and displacement settings show that rigorous evaluation is feasible, yet effects remain dependent on attendance, programme fidelity, comparator content, and camp or community conditions (37, 39). The pooled values should therefore be read as a promising average signal, not as evidence that every sport-based programme will produce a similar effect.

Complementarity of symptom and psychosocial findings

For depression and anxiety, the narrative themes of routine, achievement, social contact, and safe group participation are consistent with behavioural and social pathways through which structured activity could help. The Game Connect trial, for example, combined sport with protection and life-skills components and reported reductions in depression and anxiety among displaced and host-community youth (39). Because multicomponent interventions do not isolate the active ingredient, these results cannot be attributed to physical exertion alone. The same caution applies to PTSD: body awareness, predictable structure, and perceived control are plausible mechanisms, but they remain hypotheses unless tested in mediation or component studies.

Psychosocial findings extend the review beyond symptom reduction without being treated as clinical outcomes. Across the Bam, Merapi, Rohingya, Uganda, and Greek camp contexts, reports described emotional expression, peer connection, communication, cooperation, belonging, coping, and safe social space (45–50). These themes help explain how programmes may contribute to recovery at interpersonal and community levels. However, construct heterogeneity, qualitative designs, small or purposive samples, and limited fidelity reporting reduce certainty and transferability. Evidence concerning suicidal ideation is particularly preliminary because it derives from a secondary analysis within one programme family; it cannot support sport as a substitute for suicide-risk assessment, crisis response, or clinical care (52).

Conditions for safe and effective implementation

The findings do not support an assumption that sport is inherently therapeutic. Benefits are most plausible when programmes are voluntary, trauma-sensitive, developmentally appropriate, culturally adapted, accessible across gender and disability, and delivered by trained facilitators within a predictable and psychologically safe environment. Safeguarding procedures should address bullying, harassment, sexual exploitation and abuse, discriminatory team selection, injury, excessive competition, and disclosure of protection concerns. Programmes should include informed participation, confidential reporting routes, facilitator supervision, attendance and adverse-event monitoring, and clear referral pathways for severe symptoms, suicidality, child-protection concerns, or functional deterioration. Community consultation and co-design are needed to adapt activity type, clothing, mixed-gender participation, timing, language, and family involvement without diluting core safety and support functions.

Operationally, sport-based interventions should be positioned within a mapped local MHPSS system rather than delivered as isolated recreation. Schools, temporary accommodation areas, community centres, youth spaces, and rehabilitation services may be suitable platforms when basic safety and access are secured. A minimum implementation package should specify the target population and tier of support; intervention components, frequency, and dose; facilitator competencies and supervision; safeguarding and referral protocols; cultural adaptations; fidelity indicators; and mental health, psychosocial, participation, and adverse-event outcomes. Partnerships among mental health professionals, protection actors, educators, coaches, disability organizations, local authorities, and community leaders can help ensure that programmes extend access while remaining connected to focused and specialized services.

Limitations and research priorities

Broader literature retained in the reference list was used to delimit the scope and interpret the findings rather than to enlarge the pooled evidence base. General evidence on physical activity and mental health, trauma recovery, and child resilience informed the conceptual interpretation (64, 68, 70). Evidence from sport-for-development literature also informed the conceptual framing (66, 73, 74). Post-earthquake documentation and crisis-focused sport research helped situate the review within disaster and community settings (61, 65, 67). Evidence from severe-mental-disorder programmes, refugee trials and secondary analyses, and earthquake-focused therapeutic play was used only for contextual comparison and emerging research directions (63, 69, 71, 76). By contrast, occupational musculoskeletal prevention and family-based child-protection literature fall outside the review’s eligible outcome population and were not treated as intervention-effect evidence (60, 75). This distinction preserves the prespecified crisis-exposure and MHPSS eligibility boundaries.

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. The protocol was not prospectively registered. The included evidence was clinically and methodologically heterogeneous, and the number of robust randomized trials was limited, especially in post-earthquake and community-based humanitarian settings. Some studies had small samples, short follow-up, incomplete reporting of adherence or facilitator training, and unclear intervention fidelity. Controlled and uncontrolled evidence required separation, and outcomes such as suicidal ideation, community resilience, social connectedness, and life skills were represented by few or non-comparable studies. Small outcome sets also limited the power of funnel plots and regression-based publication-bias tests; the absence of a strong signal cannot be interpreted as absence of bias. Finally, the broad crisis scope—including disaster, conflict, displacement, refugee settings, and pandemic restrictions—supports a cross-context MHPSS perspective but limits direct generalization to any single disaster type.

Future studies should preregister protocols, use adequately powered randomized or well-controlled comparative designs, report allocation, attrition, adherence, intervention fidelity, adverse events, and safeguarding, and include longer follow-up. Core outcome sets should distinguish clinical symptoms from psychosocial and community constructs. Component and mediation analyses are needed to determine whether physical exertion, routine, group belonging, facilitator relationships, protection content, or life-skills modules drive change. Equity analyses should examine age, gender, disability, socioeconomic position, displacement status, and cultural context.

Conclusion

Sport-, play-, exercise-, movement-, physical-education-, yoga/body-awareness-, and sport-for-protection-based interventions may contribute to post-disaster and humanitarian mental health and psychosocial recovery. The pooled findings indicate favorable small-to-moderate average effects on PTSD, depression, and anxiety, while the narrative evidence identifies possible contributions to routine, regulation, belonging, social connection, life skills, and community cohesion. Confidence is moderated by heterogeneity, risk of bias, small outcome sets, and variation in programme content and context. Sport-based interventions should therefore be implemented as complementary, safeguarded, culturally adapted components of multi-layered MHPSS systems—not as replacements for trauma-focused treatment, clinical risk management, child protection, or specialized mental health care.
