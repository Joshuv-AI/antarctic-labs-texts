Towards cost-effective cognitive impairment diagnosis systems by emulating doctors’ reasoning with deep reinforcement learning
Authors: Ying Meng, Chenyu Zhang, Junming Jiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment diagnosis in aging adults is of great significance but faces challenges like high costs and complex clinical reasoning. Traditional clinical procedures often require comprehensive neuropsychological assessments involving numerous cognitive tests, interviews, or biomarker analyses. While these approaches maximize diagnostic accuracy, they are inherently time-consuming, expensive, and place a heavy burden on both patients and clinicians.MethodsThis study used data from the CHARLS-HCAP program, which included 1,478 participants classified as dementia. We propose a new framework for cost-effective cognitive impairment diagnosis system using deep reinforcement learning. The diagnostic workflow is formalized as a sequential decision-making process in which the agent dynamically determines which features (i.e. cognitive tests and questionnaires) to acquire at each step. Combined with a supervised classifier serves as a virtual ‘clinician’, the currently available information is used to provide probabilistic feedback on the patient's state. The Rainbow DQN-based agent is trained end-to-end to learn an optimal query strategy that mimics a physician's reasoning: it adaptively selects subsequent cognitive assessments and ultimately renders a categorical diagnosis of dementia severity, aiming to maximize expected diagnostic accuracy while minimizing cumulative costs, representing patient burden and resource utilization.ResultsIn our study, the reinforcement learning–based diagnostic system achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.877 (micro-average) and 0.823 (macro-average) during autonomous inquiry. It attained state-of-the-art diagnostic accuracy while using only an average of 14.76 questionnaire items per patient—reducing, the total estimated assessment time by 48.3% compared to full-feature supervised learning baselines. This reduction corresponds to a meaningful decrease in assessment time cost and cognitive burden for patients. Ablation experiments also validated the effectiveness of our components, highlighting the critical role of reward exploration. Finally, through system inquiry quality analysis and policy distillation visualization, the parameters of the neural network were approximated and converted into diagnostic pathways, thereby enhancing clinicians’ and patients’ trust in the model.ConclusionsWe developed a reinforcement learning–based diagnostic system for cognitive status within a population cohort. This model effectively optimizes the long-standing economic and promotional issues of community dementia screening, demonstrating ​strong accuracy, economic efficiency, and methodological advances. Furthermore, by analyzing the diagnostic logic, we enhanced clinicians’ and patients’ trust in the model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06916-3.

Introduction

Dementia poses a major challenge in the aging population. Strikingly, nearly 75% of persons living with dementia (PLWDs) remain undiagnosed [1], complicating the accurate estimation of community PLWD numbers. In the context of care resources scarce, allocating resources to support optimal person-centered care shall correspond to disease severity [2]. Recently, the Alzheimer's Association Workgroup introduced revised biomarker-based criteria for the clinical staging of Alzheimer's disease (AD) [3]. Although the numeric stages 1–6 align with the global deterioration scale [4], practical difficulties remain in choosing specific cognitive and functional measures to assess severity.

Existing literature attempting to develop models for identifying at-risk dementia or predicting the progression to dementia can be categorized into two types: Type A, the models mainly rely on established risk factors [5–9]; Type B, the models utilize extensive cognitive assessment data or even introduce costly biomarkers [10]—Type A models often neglect disease-specific features or cognitive performance metrics; Type B models primarily forecast the presence of cognitive impairment without addressing severity. In addition, the process of stratifying cognitive impairment is often complex and expensive, involving a wide range of neuropsychological tests, clinical assessments, and biomarkers, with limitations in terms of efficiency and cost-effectiveness.

Recent advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence have opened new avenues for addressing challenges in medical diagnosis. Deep learning models, with their ability to learn complex patterns from large datasets, have shown success in various medical imaging and diagnostic tasks [11–15]. Reinforcement learning (RL), a subset of machine learning, has gained attention for its potential to model sequential decision-making processes and dynamic interactions between patients and clinicians [16–19]. By learning optimal policies through trial and error, RL offers the possibility of developing intelligent systems that can assist in clinical decision-making and improve diagnostic outcomes.

Several studies have validated the effectiveness of RL in different healthcare domains, such as sepsis treatment [20, 21], medical enquiry assistance and intelligent clinical decision support systems (CDSS) [22, 23]. For example, it has been shown that RL can help optimize treatment strategies for patients with sepsis by dynamically adapting interventions based on patient response [20]. This inspired us to explore the application of RL in the stratification of cognitively impaired populations.

Existing diagnostic methods for RL focus primarily on improving diagnostic accuracy, but tend to ignore the actual reasoning process of the physician and cost issues, which are important to ensure the reliability and economy of the diagnostic system. This issue is particularly salient in the context of community-based dementia screening.

Given the limitations of existing methods and the potential of reinforcement learning, we propose a new framework for cost-effective cognitive impairment diagnosis using deep reinforcement learning. The diagnostic workflow is formalized as a continuous decision-making process in which the agent dynamically determines which features (i.e. cognitive tests or questionnaires) to acquire at each step. Combined with a supervised classifier serves as a virtual ‘clinician’, the currently available information is used to provide probabilistic feedback on the patient's state. The Rainbow DQN-based agent is trained end-to-end to learn an optimal query strategy that mimics a physician's reasoning: it adaptively selects subsequent cognitive assessments and ultimately renders a categorical diagnosis of dementia severity, aiming to maximize expected diagnostic accuracy while minimizing cumulative costs, including patient burden and resource utilization.

Our system comprises two primary components: an evidence acquisition branch, responsible for patient interaction, and a classifier branch, which estimates disease status at each decision point. Both the classifier and the policy network depend on an encoder that derives latent representations from the collected patient data. Crucially, we introduce a reward shaping module, utilizing probabilistic feedback from the classifier to define exploration, diagnostic, and cost-related rewards. Method

Study cohorts

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), launched in 2011, is a national cohort study gathering longitudinal health and social care data from over 30 cities in China [24]. Its dataset is extensively utilized to investigate the impact of social determinants on healthy aging among middle-aged and older adults [25–29].

The CHARLS-HACP protocol was validated in 2017 and later used in the 2018, 2020, and 2022 CHARLS survey waves. The original study design and details of the CHARLS survey are accessible online at http://charls.pku.edu.cn. [24]. The present study utilized data from the CHARLS-HCAP protocol validation study published previously [30]. It included 1478 participants of CHARLS routine and clinical samples: Routine samples (aged ≥ 65, N = 722) were from 50 villages in 6 provinces, and clinical samples (N = 756) from psychiatry and neurology departments of 17 hospitals in the same provinces. All participants were interviewed and rated with CDR and classified into mild (CDR = 1, n = 178), moderate (CDR = 2, n = 102), severe (CDR = 3, n = 36) dementia, very mild dementia/MCI (CDR = 0·5, n = 730), and normal cognition (CDR = 0, n = 432).

The protocol comprises two components: neurocognitive tests administered to respondents and an informant survey conducted with individuals familiar with the respondent. The neurocognitive tests tap into general cognitive function and cognitive domains of episodic memory, working memory, executive function, visuospatial function, language and information processing speed (Table S1). The informant surveys include the Jorm Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE), part II of the Blessed dementia scale (BDS), and the informant part of CSI-D.

State space

At any point during the assessment, the system’s understanding of the patient—called the state—includes four practical pieces of information: (i) which cognitive test groups have already been completed (recorded as a checklist); (ii) the current estimated likelihood of each dementia stage (e.g., 60% MCI, 30% mild dementia), provided by the classifier; (iii) the total estimated time burden from the cognitive tests already administered; and (iv) basic background information like age and education.

Deep reinforcement learning formulation

The goal is to optimize a sequential diagnostic policy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi_{\theta } (s)$$\end{document} parameterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} (deep neural network weights), maximizing expected cumulative reward while controlling the cost of feature acquisition.

As illustrated in Fig. 1B, our framework integrates a classifier-assisted Rainbow DQN agent to learn an adaptive diagnostic policy. Figure 1C shows a brief process of dynamic diagnosis work. Starting from the patient's basic information (such as age, gender, educational level, and residence), the system has established a simulated diagnostic environment, which includes rewards, optional questionnaire groups and corresponding costs, as well as a classifier for estimating the changes in the probability score of the patient's dementia severity, to simulate the evolution of the patient's state. Then, the RL agent successively selects cognitive tests (evidence acquisition) to reduce uncertainty. This process continues until the agent determines that it has collected sufficient evidence, at which point it outputs a diagnosis.

Reward shaping

A key challenge is designing a reward function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_{t}$$\end{document}. We formulate the reward for each step according to the logic of clinical diagnostic environment of doctors. The reward function is designed to mimic clinical priorities: encourage gathering useful information, penalize unnecessary testing, and strongly reward correct final diagnoses.

Implementation details

We utilize a Rainbow DQN [31] as the RL agent, featuring a NoisyNet-based dueling architecture, categorical value distribution (C51), prioritized experience replay buffer, and n-step returns (set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 3$$\end{document}). The dataset was split into 70% training and 30% held-out testing sets; all hyperparameter tuning and model selection were performed using fivefold cross-validation on the training set to ensure robustness.

During training, each episode simulates a single patient assessment, terminating when the STOP action is selected or all feature groups have been queried. Reward-shaping and cost-related hyperparameters were selected via grid search within the cross-validation framework to balance diagnostic accuracy and assessment efficiency.

All experiments were conducted on a high-performance Linux server equipped with an NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU (80 GB VRAM), using Python 3.10.14 and PyTorch 2.0.1 + cu117 (CUDA 11.7).

Results

Data and feature construction

Data preprocessing

All data underwent thorough cleaning. Missing values were imputed using the mean or median for numerical variables, and an 'unrecorded' category was assigned for categorical variables according to their distribution. Abnormal values were detected through statistical methods (such as box plots and predefined clinical ranges), with verified errors excluded or corrected. Correlation analysis was performed to evaluate relationships among predictive features, revealing patterns and potential clusters within diagnostic categories (Figure S1A).

Feature engineering

To optimize predictive capacity, the initial dataset (1478 samples, 1193 variables) was reduced to 96 features by eliminating collinearity and non-informative items. Further reduction was achieved by combining Boruta [29] analysis (Figure S1B), resulting in a set of 27 core features (Figure S1C). By incorporating questionnaire response time features, the final baseline feature set comprised 46 dimensions. Feature importance in model decisions was visualized using the SHAP package (Figure S1D).

Feature clustering and grouping

To optimize the action space for reinforcement learning, we applied spectral clustering to the existing questionnaire features and combined it with prior knowledge about the order of assessment—such as Delayed Recall can only be conducted after the Word Recall Test, and Word Recognition Efficiency is similar. Ultimately, the questionnaire features were divided into 10 different categories, corresponding to the ten possible actions available to the RL agent (see Table S2).

Discussion

For medical diagnosis, clinicians usually start with the patient's main problems, then ask about symptoms and basic information, and require necessary tests to collect comprehensive medical information. The clinical diagnosis of cognitive impairment is the same. The aim of this study is to develop an artificial intelligence-driven clinical diagnosis system that simulates the medical investigation logic of clinicians through reinforcement learning and provides query suggestions and diagnoses.

Reformulating dementia severity stratification problem

We reformulate dementia severity stratification as a RL–based strategy optimization problem. In this framework, the patient’s cognitive impairment state could depend on static features (demographic information, eg, age and education) and longitudinal assessments (eg, general cognition, executive function) [32–36]. Actions correspond to the clinician’s evidence acquisition choices, namely, which cognitive tests and questionnaires. The transition probability reflects the prognosis, denoting the likelihood of transitioning between cognitive states. If a newly acquired state clarifies the stratification of dementia severity or leads to a correct diagnosis, the RL agent receives a positive reward; conversely, misclassification or additional time/resource costs incur penalties.

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, clinicians use static patient features to guide evidence acquisition (actions), which transitions the patient to a new state. Subsequent actions are then determined based on these new states, resulting in a sequence of state–action pairs that delineate the diagnostic trajectory. This trajectory is inherently characterized by delayed feedback, as diagnostic decisions are typically evaluated only after several steps. In the RL process, the agent aims to learn an optimal policy (a mapping from states to actions) that maximizes cumulative rewards over time. The agent is not explicitly instructed on which actions to take, but instead learns through iterative trial and error with the environment. This approach is well-suited to dementia diagnosis tasks, given their inherent temporal delays; decisions are made without immediate feedback regarding test utility, but are instead evaluated on their long-term clinical impact.

Utility of the RL diagnostic system

The RL-based diagnostic system demonstrated clear utility in cognitive impairment classification. It dynamically adapted its evidence acquisition strategy based on case complexity, minimizing the time and resource cost for simpler diagnoses while retaining high diagnostic accuracy for more difficult cases.

Conventional machine learning models are typically trained on datasets with a fixed set of features and assume that the same feature set will be available during deployment. However, this assumption often does not hold in real-world applications, or it may be desirable for the model to infer on different feature sets. Compared with these baselines, reinforcement learning methods overcome the inherent limitation of requiring all features for training, and further improve classification performance. By focusing on the most informative questionnaire items, RL approaches substantially reduce the burden on both patients and clinicians. This selective querying underscores the clinical utility of RL, particularly in settings where both efficiency and diagnostic accuracy are essential.

Diagnostic logic alignment with clinical reasoning

To promote transparency and trust in the RL system, we performed diagnostic logic analysis through policy distillation. By distilling the agent’s decision-making process into an interpretable decision tree (Fig. 3D), we provided clinicians with understandable if–else rules that closely mirror real-world diagnostic pathways.

As shown in Fig. 3B, the number of features selected by the agent strongly correlates with diagnostic difficulty: simpler cases required fewer tests (reducing patient burden), while ambiguous cases triggered more comprehensive inquiry. Furthermore, Interpretability analyses using SHAP (Fig. 3C) highlighted the incremental impact of each acquired feature on individual predictions, offering insights into how the RL agent selects evidence at each step of the diagnostic process.

Clinical Interpretation of Dynamic Feature Acquisition: Notably, the agent consistently selects the brief global screens—TICS, BLESSED, Symbol Coding, and Backward Counting—in > 90% of episodes, using them for efficient initial stratification. In contrast, the full episodic memory battery (WR1–3, Delayed Recall, and WRE) is deployed as a coordinated block in only ~ 34% of cases (152/442), reflecting its procedural dependency (Delayed Recall requires prior Word Recall) and higher time cost; thus, it is reserved for diagnostically ambiguous cases. Similarly, CSI-D2—an informant-reported measure of daily living ability—is used in 84% of episodes, consistent with clinical practice where functional impairment is essential for distinguishing dementia from milder cognitive syndromes such as MCI.

Together, these mechanisms ensure that the RL system does not act as a black box, but rather as a transparent assistant that emulates and explains physician-like reasoning. It allows stakeholders to understand why a particular test was chosen and how the diagnosis was reached.

Strengths of the study

Methodologically, the RL-based diagnostic system is fundamentally distinct from conventional supervised learning approaches for dementia classification. It dynamically selects questionnaire items for each patient, optimizing the balance between diagnostic accuracy and resource consumption. In our study, this approach yielded a 48.3% reduction in evidence acquisition time while improving diagnostic performance. The success of this framework is attributable to the effective formulation of patient states and clinician actions within the RL paradigm, as well as the impact of the reward shaping module.

The study also benefits from unique data characteristics. It utilizes CHARLS data, with validation samples containing detailed CDR staging information, providing high-quality support for model development and verification. Unlike previous studies that focused solely on risk factors or biomarkers, our study integrates cognitive performance and functional status across multiple dimensions, offering a comprehensive representation of disease characteristics.

Besides, the model simultaneously balances accuracy, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility. Through RL agent dynamic selection, the model reduces the cost of use and increases the efficiency of resource utilization.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its significant potential, this study has several limitations and areas for improvement. One limitation is the insufficient granularity of diagnostic indicators; the model employs aggregate scale scores rather than item-level responses as the action space within the RL diagnostic system. Potential redundancy among items across cognitive or functional assessments (eg, IQCODE and BLESSED) may also influence the model’s accuracy and interpretability. Future studies should examine more granular item-level analyses to further improve RL system performance.

Therefore, we propose future studies to validate the model's generalizability with additional cohorts and further optimize its architecture to enhance its adaptability across complex scenarios [37].

Clinical and public health implications

The model enables cost-effective and accurate dementia staging, informing community-level disease management and resource allocation. Estimating patient distribution across different stages facilitates optimal healthcare resource planning and cost reduction. This is particularly beneficial for low- and middle-income countries, where access to specialized dementia care is limited, and healthcare infrastructure faces significant constraints [38–40]. With continued advancements in digital health and telemedicine, RL-based systems are poised to play an increasingly important role. Meanwhile, this implementation strategy, which provides RL diagnostic logic analysis (Fig. 3), ensures acceptance and trust of the results by healthcare professionals and patients.

Conclusion

We propose a novel framework for cost-effective cognitive impairment diagnosis that synergistically combines deep reinforcement learning with a supervised classification model. The dementia severity stratification workflow is formalized as a sequential decision-making process in which the agent dynamically determines which features (i.e. cognitive tests or questionnaires) to acquire at each step. This system not only reduces unnecessary assessments and associated costs, but also produces transparent and interpretable decision pathways that closely resemble expert clinical reasoning, facilitating adoption in resource-constrained healthcare settings. We believe that our framework represents a significant step forward in the development of efficient and accurate diagnostic solutions for cognitive impairment, with the potential to deliver substantial benefits for both healthcare providers and patients.
