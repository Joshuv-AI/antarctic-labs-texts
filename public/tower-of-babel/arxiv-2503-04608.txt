Algebra of Invariants for the Vlasov-Maxwell System
Philip J. Morrison

Abstract
The algebra of invariants for both the relativistic and nonrelativistic multispecies Vlasov-Maxwell system is examined, including the case with a fixed ion background. Invariants and their associated fluxes are obtained directly from the Vlasov-Maxwell system. The invariants are shown to Poisson commute with the Hamiltonian and the rest of the Poisson bracket algebra of invariants is identified. Special attention is given to the role played by the monopole condition, ∇ ⋅ 𝐁 \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} . Dedicated to the memory of Robert L. Dewar, treasured friend and colleague.



I
Introduction

In this paper we consider constants of motion for the multispecies Vlasov-Maxwell system of equations with particular emphasis on the role played by the constraints of Gauss’s law and the condition that the magnetic field be solenoidal. The usual method for obtaining the energy-momentum tensor and angular momentum is by means of an action principle and Noether’s theorem, as was done, e.g., for the Vlasov-Maxwell system in
[
1
]
. However, an alternative is to proceed with the gauge-free Hamiltonian structure of the Vlasov system in terms of the noncanonical Poisson bracket
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
, which is done here.

Although early gauge-free field theoretic structures and generalized Poisson brackets appeared in the physics literature
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
, the first occurrence in the plasma literature appeared in
[
12
]
and in
[
3
,
13
,
14
]
, which can be consulted for review.

It was first pointed out in
[
3
]
that the role of the Gauss’s law and the solenoidal magnetic field constraints appear in the Hamiltonian formulation on different footing: the solenoidal magnetic field is necessary for the Jacobi identity, an essential property of the Poisson bracket of a Hamiltonian formulation, while Gauss’s law appears as a true Casimir invariant, an invariant associate with degeneracy of a Poisson bracket. Thus monopole theories are not Hamiltonian field theories. (See
[
15
,
16
]
for further details.) This distinction between the constraints is carefully tracked as we investigate constants of motion and their associated algebra of invariants. Throughout an eye is kept out on the possible ramifications of the use of invariants in the context of numerical simulation.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
the Vlasov-Maxwell system, its noncanonical Hamiltonian structure, and the associated Jacobi identity and collection of Casimir invariants are reviewed. Next, in Sec.
III
the ten conservation laws for the full relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system along with its Casimir invariants are directly obtained, including their associated fluxes. The role played by the constraints in leading to invariance is explicitly delineated. Similarly the invariants of the nonrelativisic theory are explored. In Sec.
IV
the algebra of invariants, the pairwise Poisson brackets of all invariants are calculated. It is shown that the Poisson bracket with invariants produces a realization of the algebra associated with the Poincaré group in the relativistic case and the Euclidean group in the nonrelativistic case. Section
V
contains discussion of the consequences of preceding sections. In particular, in Sec.
V.1
it is shown that the usual system that freezes ion motion does not conserve invariants, notably momentum. Computational consequences are discussed in Sec.
V.2
and a system that describe the nonlinear dynamics of the weibel instability are discussed in Sec.
V.3
. Next, in Sec.
V.4
possible ramifications of the asymmetry of the constraints are mentioned. The paper concludes in Sec.
VI
with a summary.

II
The Vlasov-Maxwell System

II.1
Equations of Motion

The relativistic Vlasov equation for a particle species
s
, charge
q_{s}
, and mass
m_{s}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\cdot\nabla f_{s}

(1)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\bigg(\mathbf{E}+\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\times\mathbf{B}\bigg)\cdot\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}=0.

while the nonrelativistic version is given by

\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla f_{s}+\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}=0\,.

(2)

In (
1
) and (
2
)
f_{s}
denotes the phase space density, which is a function of
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}
and
t

Both the relativistic and nonrelativistic cases are coupled nonlinearly to Maxwell equations,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t}-\nabla\times\mathbf{B}=-\mathbf{J}\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}+\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=0\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=\rho\,,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0\,,

(5)

with the sources in the relativistic case given by

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}q_{s}\int f_{s}\,d^{3}v\quad\mathrm{and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}q_{s}\int\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v\,,

(6)

which together constitute a four-vector, while in the nonrelativistic case they are given by

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}q_{s}\int f_{s}\,d^{3}v\quad\mathrm{and}

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{J}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}q_{s}\int\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v\,.

(8)

\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\{\Psi,H\}\,,

(9)

\Psi=(f_{s},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})

\{\,,\,\}

The Vlasov-Maxwell noncanonical Poisson bracket, which is the same for both the relativistic and nonrelativistic theories, is given by

\displaystyle\{F,G\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\frac{1}{m_{s}}\int\left[\frac{\delta F}{\delta f_{s}}\frac{\delta G}{\delta f_{s}}\right]{f_{s}}\,d^{3}xd^{3}v

[f,g]\coloneqq\nabla f\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\mathbf{v}}-\nabla g\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\,.

(11)

With the relativistic Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{1}{2}\int\big(\left|\mathbf{E}\right|^{2}+\left|\mathbf{B}\right|^{2}\big)\,d^{3}x\,,

II.3
Jacobi identity and Casimir invariants

In order for the formalism described in Sec.
II.2
to actually be a Hamiltonian formalism, it is necessary for the bracket of (
10
) to statisfy the Jacobi identity (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
13
]
),

\{\{F,G\},H\}+\{\{G,H\},F\}+\{\{H,F\},G\}=0\,,

(14)

for all functionals
F,G,H
. The early calculation of
[
3
]
(see
[
15
]
for explicit details) revealed

\displaystyle\{\{F,G\},H\}+\mathrm{cyc}=

(15)

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\!\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}^{3}}\!\int\!\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}\,\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta F}{\delta f_{s}}\!\cdot\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta G}{\delta f_{s}}\!\times\!\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\frac{\delta H}{\delta f_{s}}\right)f_{s}\,d^{3}x\,d^{3}v\,.

Thus the Jacobi identity is not satisfied unless it is restricted to functionals defined on divergence-free magnetic fields. Observe, that of the two constraints of (
5
) the Jacobi identity only relies on
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
and not on
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=\rho
. Thus the gauge invariant Hamiltonian structure with the Poisson bracket of (
10
) only requires
\mathbf{B}
interpreted as a 2-form to be closed, but it need not be exact.

Casimirs are constants of motion that originate from the degeneracy of the Poisson bracket and, consequently, they are invariants for any Hamiltonian. They are functionals that satisfy
\{C,\mathcal{G}\}=0
for any functional
G
.

For the Vlasov-Maxwell bracket several such Casimirs
C(f,\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})
are known
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. First, for a arbitrary function
\mathcal{C}_{s}
of
f_{s}
the following is a Casimir:

C_{s}=\int\mathcal{C}_{s}(f_{s})\,\,d^{3}xd^{3}v\,.

(16)

This family of Casimirs is a manifestation of Liouville’s theorem and corresponds to conservation of phase space volume
[
17
]
.
Next, there are two Casimirs related to constraints of (
5
),

\displaystyle C_{E}

\displaystyle=\int h_{E}(\mathbf{x})\,\big(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho\big)\,d^{3}x\,,

(17)

\displaystyle C_{B}

\displaystyle=\int h_{B}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}\,d^{3}x\,,

(18)

where
h_{E}
and
h_{B}
are arbitrary functions of
\mathbf{x}
. The functional
C_{B}
is not a true Casimir because it must be identically zero for the Jacobi identity to be satisfied. For this reason it is sometimes called a pseudo-Casimir. It acts like a Casimir in that its Poisson bracket with any other functional vanishes, but it must be born in mind that the Jacobi identity is only satisfied when
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
. In contrast, dynamics with an initial condition
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho\neq 0
is still Hamiltonian dynamics. This distinction followed from the direct (and tedious) Jacobi identity calculation of Ref.
3
, written out explicitly in an appendix of
[
15
]
.

III
Invariant densities and fluxes

In this section we calculate directly from the equations of motion the conservation laws for densities and their corresponding fluxes. A conservation law has the form

\frac{\partial R}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=0

(19)

for a density
R
with associated flux
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
. Similarly, if
\mathbf{R}
is a vector density, such as momentum, we have

\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}=0

(20)

where
\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}
is the corresponding tensor flux. Observe, if the flux, vector or tensor, through a boundary vanishes, then
we have the constants of motion,

\frac{d}{dt}\int R\,d^{3}x=0\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\frac{d}{dt}\int\mathbf{R}\,d^{3}x=0\,.

(21)

For the case of a phase space density
\mathcal{C}
the conservation law is given by

\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\cdot\,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{v}=0

(22)

and similar to (
21
) we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int\mathcal{C}\,\,d^{3}xd^{3}v=0\,.

(23)

For the relativistic case we expect 10 constants of motion, corresponding to the 10 elements of the Poincaré group, which is appropriate for relativistically covariant theories. For the nonrelativistic case we lose invariants because it mixes the relativistic invariance of Maxwell’s equations with a Gallilean invariant from of the Vlasov equation. We will see that there are 7 invariants, 6 corresponding to invariants associated with the Euclidean group plus the Hamiltonian.

III.1
Casimir Fluxes

The density associated with the Casimir (
16
) has the conservation law for the relativistic Vlasov system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}_{s}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bigg(\mathcal{C}_{s}\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\bigg)

(24)

\displaystyle\hskip 34.14322pt+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\cdot\,\left(\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\mathcal{C}_{s}\,\bigg(\mathbf{E}+\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\times\mathbf{B}\bigg)\right)=0\,,

while for the nonrelativistic Vlasov system it has the form

\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}\mathbf{v})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\cdot\,\left(\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}\,\big(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\big)\right)=0\,.

(25)

Consider
C_{E}
of (
17
),

\frac{\partial\big(h_{E}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho)\big)}{\partial t}=h_{E}\left(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\right)\equiv 0\,,

(26)

where for the relativistic case
\mathbf{J}
is given by (
6
) and for the nonrelativistic case by
\hat{\mathbf{J}}
of (
8
). This follows from (
3
) and (
1
) or (
2
). Thus there is no flux associated with this Casimir.
Similarly, because
C_{B}
of (
18
) only depends on Maxwell’s equations, its conservation property is the same for both the relativistic and nonrelativistic cases. From (
4
) it follows immediately that

\frac{\partial(h_{B}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})}{\partial t}=h_{B}\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\equiv 0\,.

(27)

Likewise, there is no flux associated with this Casimir.

III.2
Conservation of momentum

The relativistic and nonrelativistic Vlasov-Maxwell systems conserve the same total momentum

\mathbf{P}=\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,\,d^{3}xd^{3}v+\int\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}\,d^{3}x\,,

(28)

which has the associated momentum vector density

\mathbf{R}^{P}=\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}\,,

(29)

i.e.,
\mathbf{P}=\int\mathbf{R}^{P}\,d^{3}x
. To obtain the momentum flux tensor
\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{P}
, we calculate
\partial\mathbf{R}^{P}/\partial t
and insert (
1
), (
4
), and (
3
). Then, upon making use of the vector identities

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla{|\mathbf{B}|^{2}}/{2}-\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{B}\ (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})\,,

we obtain

\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}^{P}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{P}=-\mathbf{B}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})+\mathbf{E}\,(\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\,,

(30)

with

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\frac{\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v

(31)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\mathbf{E}\otimes\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{B}+\underline{\underline{I}}\,\left(|\mathbf{E}|^{2}+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}\right)\!/2\,,

where
\underline{\underline{I}}
is the unit tensor.

From (
30
) it follows that

\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt}=\int\big(\mathbf{E}\,(\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})-\mathbf{B}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}\Big)d^{3}x=0\,,

(32)

where the first equality of (
32
) uses a no flux boundary condition (e.g. periodicity) and the second follows if Gauss’s law is satisfied and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
.

Similarly, for the nonrelativistic theory we obtain the momentum flux

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}}^{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}m_{s}\int{\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v

(33)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\mathbf{E}\otimes\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{B}+\underline{\underline{I}}\,\left(|\mathbf{E}|^{2}+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}\right)/2\,.

III.3
Conservation of energy

From (
12
) it is evident that the relativistic energy density is

R^{H}=\sum_{s}{m_{s}}\int\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\frac{1}{2}\big(\left|\mathbf{E}\right|^{2}+\left|\mathbf{B}\right|^{2}\big)\,,

(34)

while from (
13
) the nonrelativistic energy density is seen to be,

\hat{R}^{H}=\sum_{s}\frac{m_{s}}{2}\int\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\frac{1}{2}\big(\left|\mathbf{E}\right|^{2}+\left|\mathbf{B}\right|^{2}\big)\,.

(35)

As for the momentum flux, we proceed directly to obtain

\frac{\partial R^{H}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{H}=0\,,

(36)

where we identify

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{H}=\mathbf{R}^{P}=\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}\,,

(37)

using (
29
).

For the nonrelativistic theory the calculation yields

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}^{H}=\sum_{s}\frac{m_{s}}{2}\int|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\,\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}\,.

(38)

III.4
Conservation of center of mass

Consider the quantity

\mathbf{M}=\int R^{H}\mathbf{x}\,d^{3}x-\mathbf{P}\,t\,,

(39)

with the associated vector density

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{H}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}^{P}t

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{s}\!{m_{s}}\!\int\!\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\frac{1}{2}\big(\left|\mathbf{E}\right|^{2}+\left|\mathbf{B}\right|^{2}\big)\!\bigg)\,\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\!\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}\bigg)\,t\,.

Clearly, up to boundary terms,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{M}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\partial R^{H}}{\partial t}\mathbf{x}\,d^{3}x-\mathbf{P}-\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt}t

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\mathbf{x}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{R}^{P}\,d^{3}x-\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}=0\,,

assuming
d\mathbf{P}/dt=0
.

Again, to obtain the flux we consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}^{M}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R^{H}}{\partial t}\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}^{P}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}^{P}}{\partial t}\,t

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{H})\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}^{P}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\big(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{P}+\mathbf{B}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{E}\,(\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\big)\,t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{H}\!\otimes\mathbf{x}-\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{P}\,t\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\,\big(\mathbf{B}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{E}\,(\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\big)\,t\,.

Thus,

\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}^{M}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{M}=\big(\mathbf{B}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{E}\,(\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\big)\,t\,,

(43)

with

\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{M}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{H}\!\otimes\mathbf{x}-\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{P}\,t\,.

(44)

In the nonrelativistic case, there does not appear to be an invariant of the type of
\mathbf{M}
. this is likely a manifestation that the nonrelativistic Vlasov equation has neither full Galilean nor Poincaré invariance, while the Maxwell equations have Poincaré invariance. Thus the nonrelativistic theory does not have either full group.

III.5
Conservation of angular momentum

The total angular momentum is given by

\mathbf{L}=\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,\,d^{3}xd^{3}v+\int\mathbf{x}\times(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B})\,d^{3}x\,,

(45)

with the density

\mathbf{R}^{L}=\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{v}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v+\mathbf{x}\times(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B})\,,

(46)

Direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}^{L}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{s}m_{s}\int(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{v})\,\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\cdot\nabla f_{s}\,d^{3}v

\displaystyle+\rho\,\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{x}\times(\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{x}\times(\nabla|\mathbf{E}|^{2}/2+\mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{E})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{x}\times(\nabla|\mathbf{B}|^{2}/2+\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B})-\mathbf{x}\times(\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B})\,.

In order to simply this, the following are useful:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\times\nabla h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla\times(\mathbf{x}h)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}\times(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}))_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}x_{j}\big(\partial_{\ell}(B_{\ell}B_{k})-B_{k}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_{\ell}(x_{j}B_{\ell}B_{k})-\epsilon_{ijk}x_{j}B_{k}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}x_{j}\frac{v_{k}v_{\ell}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\partial_{\ell}f_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\ell}\bigg(\epsilon_{ijk}\frac{x_{j}v_{k}v_{\ell}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\,f_{s}\bigg)\,,

where
\partial_{\ell}=\partial/\partial x_{\ell}

We obtain

\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}^{L}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{L}=-(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{B})(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})+(\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{E})\,(\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\,,

(47)

with the angular momentum flux tensor
\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}^{L}
given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{L}_{\ell i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\epsilon_{ijk}\frac{x_{j}v_{k}v_{\ell}}{\sqrt{1+\left|\mathbf{v}\right|^{2}}}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v

(48)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ij\ell}x_{j}\,\left(E_{\ell}E_{k}+B_{\ell}B_{k}\right)+\epsilon_{ij\ell}x_{j}\,\left(|\mathbf{E}|^{2}+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}\right)/2\,.

The nonrelativistic case has the flux

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}^{L}_{\ell i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s}m_{s}\int\epsilon_{ijk}x_{j}v_{k}v_{\ell}\,f_{s}\,d^{3}v

(49)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ij\ell}x_{j}\,\left(E_{\ell}E_{k}+B_{\ell}B_{k}\right)+\epsilon_{ij\ell}x_{j}\,\left(|\mathbf{E}|^{2}+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}\right)/2\,.

IV
Algebra of Invariants

Poisson brackets, like that of (
10
), together with the set of functionals on which it acts constitute a Lie algebra, in fact a Lie algebra realization on functionals. A closed subset of functionals is a Lie subalgebra. One such closed subset is the algebra of invariants, these are functionals that Poisson commute with the Hamiltonian, but need not commute with each other, i.e., the algebra is not in general abelian. Poisson’s theorem, which follows from the Jacobi identity, states that the Poisson bracket of any two invariants is also an invariant. Therefore a closed algebra can be obtained by calculating the Poisson brackets of all invariants with each other.
In this section we first show that the invariants obtained in Sec.
III
commute with the Hamiltonian
H
, then calculate their mutual Poisson brackets.

IV.1
Relativistic Invariants

For the relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell theory we first calculate
\{H,\Phi\}

with
H
given by (
12
) and
\Phi\in\{H,\mathbf{P},\mathbf{L},\mathbf{M}\}
. Obviously by antisymmetry,
\{H,H\}=0
.
Before calculating the other brackets, for convenience we rewrite the invariants as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbf{R}^{P}\,d^{3}x\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbf{R}^{L}\,d^{3}x=\int\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{R}^{P}\,d^{3}x\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(R^{H}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{R}^{P}t)\,d^{3}x\,,

(52)

with
\mathbf{R}^{P}
given by (
29

IV.4
Lie algebra realization of invariants

Finite-dimensional Lie algebras in terms of matrices are Lie algebra representations, while infinite-dimensional Poisson brackets defined on either functions or functionals are realizations (see e.g.
[
10
]
), e.g., the bracket of (
10
) defined on the set of all functionals of
\Psi=\{f_{s},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}\}
is closed, bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity (provided
\mathbf{B}
is solenoidal).

As noted at the beginning of the present section, a subalgebra of a Poisson bracket realization composed of the functionals that satisfy
\{H,\Phi\}=0
is an algebra of invariants, and this subalgebra is closed because of Poisson’s theorem.

The results of Secs.
IV.1
and
IV.3
show that for the Vlasov-Maxwell realization with bracket (
10
) the algebra of invariants has a complicated structure because of terms containing
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}
and

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho
. Because of the Jacobi identity the
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}
should be posthaste set to zero to insure that we have an algebra. Moreover, from Sec.
III
we see all quantities
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{L}
, and
\mathbf{M}
require in addition
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho=0
in order to in fact be invariants. Thus upon restricting to the Casimir leaf, i.e., constraint subspace with
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho=0
, we obtain the following Lie algebra of invariants, for the relativistic case

\displaystyle\{P_{i},P_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(95)

\displaystyle\{L_{i},P_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}P_{k}

(96)

\displaystyle\{L_{i},L_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}L_{k}

(97)

\displaystyle\{M_{i},P_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}H

(98)

\displaystyle\{M_{i},M_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{ijk}L_{k}

(99)

\displaystyle\{L_{i},M_{j}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}M_{k}

(100)

with
\{H,\Phi\}=0
. A physicists then immediately recognizes Eqs. (
96
)–(
100
) to be a realization of the ten parameter Poincaré Lie algebra possessed by relativistic field theories. (Note, sometimes
\{X_{i},H\}=P_{i}
is used instead of
\{M_{i},H\}=0
.)

The elements of the Poincaré algebra are generators of the various infinitesimal transformations. For example,
\{\Psi,H\}
generates infinitesimal time translations of our field dynamical variables, as is evident from the Hamiltonian formulation of Sec.
II
, while infinitesimal space translation of the distribution functions are generated via

\{f_{s},P_{i}\}=-\partial_{i}f_{s}\,.

(101)

In this way the the entire Poincaré group, composed of a semidirect product of the subgroup of homogeneous Lorentz transformations generated by
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{M}
with space-time translations generated by
\mathbf{P}
and
H
, generates transformations of our field variables. See, e.g.,
[
11
]
where this is worked out for the source-free Maxwell equations and
[
5
]
where they were first worked out for the Vlasov-Maxwell system. Also note, a representation with generators for the Gallilean group in the context of MHD can be found in
[
3
]
.

The above Lie algebra expressions, in fact the entire theory
[
20

and to arbitrary 3-space coordinates by writing in the Hamiltonian
|\mathbf{v}|^{2}=v^{i}v_{i}

|\mathbf{E}|^{2}=E^{i}E_{i}

|\mathbf{B}|^{2}=B^{i}B_{i}

For the nonrelativistic case, restriction to the Casimir leaf with

V
Discussion

V.1
Frozen background ions

Consider a single species plasma, say electrons, with a neutralizing positive background charge
\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, say ions.
Observe
\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})
may, but need not, be constant in space and by neutralizing we mean
\int(\rho_{e}-\rho_{i})d^{3}x=0
,
where
\rho_{e}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the time dependent electron density.
For this case, Gauss’s law is given by

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=\rho_{i}-\rho_{e}\,,

(105)

and the Poisson bracket (
10
) has the local Casimir invariant, equivalent to (
17
), of

\mathcal{C}_{E}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}+\rho_{e}\,,

(106)

as can be shown by direct substitution. Observe that the Casimir does not recognize the existence of the background charge.

Because the background is stationary, the total momentum is given by

\mathbf{P}=\int m_{e}\mathbf{v}\,f_{e}\,\,d^{3}xd^{3}v+\int\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}\,d^{3}x\,,

(107)

and according to (
32
) it satisfies

\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{e}}{dt}=\{\mathbf{P}_{e},H\}=-\int\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x},t)\,\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})\,d^{3}x\,.

(108)

Thus, even when evaluated on the constraints of (
5
) with flux conserving boundary conditions, this well-known system of plasma physics does not conserve momentum, even in the case where
\rho_{i}
is constant in space as well as time.

The quantity
\mathbf{E}\,\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is the force per unit volume on the ions caused by any electric field in the system. Since the ions are forced to be stationary, the total force on the electrons as a back-reaction is
-\int\mathbf{E}\,\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}
. For the usual case of spatially constant
\rho_{i}
, (
108
) is

\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{e}}{dt}=-\rho_{i}\int\!\mathbf{E}\,d^{3}x\,.

(109)

In (
109
) various boundary conditions are possible and
\mathbf{E}
may have longitudinal (electrostatic) as well as transverse contributions.

A similar state of affairs applies on
\nabla\cdot E-\rho=0
for the conservation of angular momentum and the other invariants.

V.2
Computational consequences

The connection between momentum and charge conservation is well known in the computational science of the Vlasov equation. However, we amplify on this theme in this subsection, throughout which it will be assumed that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
is exactly maintained.

Consider the case of Sec.
V.1
with the constant ion background density. Assuming a spatial
2\pi
-cube with periodic boundary conditions, and writing

\mathbf{E}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{k}}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,,

(110)

which is often the case for Vlasov computations, (
109
) becomes

\frac{d\mathbf{P}_{e}}{dt}=-\rho_{i}\,\mathbf{E}_{0}\,,

(111)

where
\mathbf{E}_{0}
is the
\mathbf{k}=0
Fourier component. Thus, if
\mathbf{E}_{0}
is removed from a code, it will conserve momentum – if not there can be secular growth.

More generally, suppose in a computation Gauss’s law is not maintained, either because of an improper initial condition or because of numerical error, i.e., a charge imbalance
\Delta_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\rho-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}
ensues. If this happens, (
32
) implies

\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt}=\{\mathbf{P},H\}=\int\mathbf{E}\,\Delta_{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\,d^{3}x

(112)

and we have violation of momentum conservation. For this reason codes such as GEMPIC
[
21
]
that exactly conserve Gauss’s law (as well as
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
) are desirable. In the Fourier PIC context of
[
22
,
23
]
this has been noted. Alternatively, enforcing momentum conversation in the GEMPIC context, i.e., using finite element exterior calculus, can be achieved by incorporating Dirac constraint theory (see e.g.,
[
24
,
19
]
).

Examination of the results of Sec.
III
shows that the feature above not only occurs for momentum but for other invariants as well. Equation (
47
) shows that conservation of the angular momentum
\mathbf{L}
similarly relies on maintaining the constraints. The Dirac constraint theory is a promising tool for preserving the entire algebra of invariants in the GEMPIC framework. Indeed momentum conservation for Vlasov-Poisson system has been achieved by this method
[
25
]
, but it remains to be done for the full algebra of invariants. Of particular interest might be the invariant
\mathbf{M}
, which to our knowledge has not been monitored in any computation. From (
43
) we see the right-hand side has the constraints with a factor of
t
, thus if these are not satisfied
\mathbf{M}
may experience rapid growth. Therefore, this invariant could be a particularly good diagnostic for assessing the accuracy of numerical algorithms. However, again, for schemes like GEMPIC
[
21
]
it can be exactly conserved using the Dirac method.

V.4
Constraint asymmetry

As has been noted several times in this paper, the constraints
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho=0
do not play the same roles in the Hamiltonian framework of the Vlasov-Maxwell system. While Gauss’s law is a bona fide Casimir invariant,
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
is necessary for satisfying the Jacobi identity and therefore is essential for the existence of the Hamiltonian structure. This property was unknown prior to
[
3
]
and appears to not have been fully appreciated. It is a general property of the full Maxwell equations coupled to dynamical matter fields, and a version of it even exists in Dirac’s theory of monopoles
[
15
,
16
]
.

In the context of magnetohydrodynamics, a noncanonical Poisson bracket independent of
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
was obtained in
[
30
]
, but despite considerable effort it does not appear to be possible to remove it for the Vlasov-Maxwell system. However, using Dirac constraint theory it was shown in
[
19
]
that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
can be reduced to a boundary condition at infinity, but this amounts to usual proof that demonstrates closed and exact differential forms are equivalent on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Considerable effort has been spent on determining the validity of the Bohm-Aharnov effect, both theoretically and experimentally, where the
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
issue is at the heart of the matter. In
[
31
]
it was pointed out that experimental verification of the electric Bohm-Aharonv effect has not achieved the same level of credibility as the magnetic. Perhaps this distinction between
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
and
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}-\rho=0
, which we have pointed out, is the essence of this difference. Further pursing this is beyond the scope of the present paper.

VI
Summary

In this paper we have reviewed the Hamiltonian structure of the Vlasov-Maxwell system, both nonrelativisitic and relativistic. The Hamiltonian for each case and the common Poisson bracket and associated Casimir invariants were given. Conservation laws for both cases were given and their associated fluxes. The algebra of invariants, determined by the invariants and the Poisson bracket, was obtained and shown to be realization of the algebra associated with the Poincaré group in the relativistic case and the Euclidean group in the nonrelativistic case. In demonstrating the above particular care was given to the roles played by the constraints of Gauss’s law and the monopole condition, where the asymmetry in these constraints, as first pointed out in
[
3
]
, was described. The special cases of the frozen ion background and the Weibel system were described. The development was given with an eye toward possible computational ramifications.
