Apical Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy and Elite-Level Sports
Authors: Quirina Thio, Juliette van Hattum, Francisca Nijland, Maarten Moen, Arthur Wilde, Harald Jørstad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (aHCM) is a distinct, rarer phenotype with a potentially more favorable prognosis and lower morbidity than nonapical forms of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, yet its specific interaction with elite-level sports remains poorly defined.Case SummaryThis case describes an asymptomatic athlete found to have aHCM, for whom engaging in elite sports from ages 17 to 31 years did not lead to adverse morphologic changes or progression of apical fibrosis or disease, even after the cessation of his professional sports career.DiscussionThis case underscores the importance of recognizing aHCM as a distinct subtype of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, potentially exhibiting a more benign interaction with sports. Furthermore, it highlights the value of a multidisciplinary expert panel in providing sports guidance to elite athletes with cardiovascular conditions.Take-Home MessagesDistinguish aHCM as a subtype that may carry a lower risk profile in elite athletes. Multidisciplinary shared decision-making is essential for individualized sports eligibility.

History of Presentation

A 24-year-old, otherwise healthy, elite (tier 5; world-class) male athlete participating in a mixed, high-intensity, competitive, acceleration-deceleration sport (field hockey) was referred to our tertiary referral center for evaluation for sports eligibility in the presence of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). Five years prior, the patient was found to have an abnormal electrocardiogram (ECG) during routine sports evaluation, after which a transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging were performed, demonstrating no abnormalities. The patient underwent annual follow-up. At 4-year follow-up, TTE showed mild left ventricular (LV) apical hypertrophy, with normal diastolic function. Subsequently, CMR imaging demonstrated late gadolinium enhancement in the hypertrophic apex (LV wall thickness of 19 mm).Take-Home Messages•Recognize aHCM as a distinct subgroup of hypertrophic cardiomyopathies and perform risk stratification in patients with aHCM wishing to engage in elite sports.•This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary expertise and shared decision-making when providing sports advice to elite athletes with aHCM.

Medical and Family History

Medical and family history was negative for cardiovascular disease; in particular, there was no history of sudden cardiac death and cardiomyopathy, apart from the patient's grandfather, who had coronary artery disease at an older age.

Differential Diagnosis

The abnormal ECG, combined with TTE and CMR imaging findings of apical hypertrophy, raised the suspicion of HCM. However, in an asymptomatic Caucasian athlete with a negative family history, other cardiomyopathies, such as arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy with predominant LV involvement, or, less probably, myocarditis, remained possible differential diagnoses.

Investigations

During the first consultation at our clinic, the patient denied any symptoms. He engaged in sports at an international level for 8-12 hours per week. However, for most of the last year, he had been unable to participate in any sports because of a musculoskeletal injury.

The ECG performed during the first consultation at our clinic showed sinus rhythm at 65 beats/min, an intermediate heart axis, normal conduction intervals, high QRS complex voltages consistent with LV hypertrophy criteria, and T-wave inversions in leads II, III, aVF, and V3-V6 (Figure 1). Ambulatory 24-hour ECG monitoring showed no clinically important arrhythmias other than infrequent premature atrial complexes (<1/min) and premature ventricular complexes (maximum of 3/min). When the patient was able to return to sports after his musculoskeletal rehabilitation period, he underwent exercise stress testing on a cycle ergometer using a 25-W/min ramp protocol. Results demonstrated a maximal load of 285 W, without any complaints or ventricular ectopy. Genetic testing with next-generation sequencing and copy number variants analysis revealed no pathogenic variants in 50 genes associated with cardiomyopathies. A new CMR imaging was performed 12 months after the previous one, which showed normal biventricular systolic function and apical hypertrophy with an end-diastolic apical wall thickness of 20 mm (Figure 2). Management

According to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines at the time, a patient with a definite diagnosis of HCM and a low-risk profile was recommended to participate in low-dynamic, low-static sports only.1 However, our patient expressed a strong wish to continue his elite-level sports activities. The case discussion was followed with numerous international experts in the fields of cardiomyopathies and sports.2 Consensus was reached that despite the presence of fibrosis, evidence of clear detrimental structural effects or prohibitive risks of arrhythmias associated with elite-level sports was lacking. After a shared decision-making process, an intensively monitored strategy was chosen, without restrictive sports advice.

Follow-Up

Initially, the patient was followed up every 3 months with exercise tests and ambulatory ECG monitoring. As no arrhythmias were observed after 1 year, this was changed to annual follow-ups. Annual TTEs continued to show normal systolic and diastolic function. Annual CMR studies did not show any signs of clear disease progression, although LV mass fluctuated (Table 1). Ten years after the initial abnormal ECG and 6 years after the definite diagnosis of apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (aHCM), the patient remained asymptomatic, never demonstrated any arrhythmias, and was able to continue his sports activity and compete at the highest international levels, including the Olympic Games.

One year after the cessation of his professional sports career, his ECG remained unchanged with respect to repolarization, although new-onset left atrial enlargement was noted (Figure 3). Additionally, follow-up CMR imaging demonstrated a decrease in LV volume and mass and no increase in maximal wall thickness or fibrosis (Figure 4). Discussion

HCM has historically been considered the leading cause of sudden cardiac death in athletes,3 and numerous patients with HCM have been given negative sports advice regarding elite-level sports. However, the landscape of HCM is shifting, with a number of studies reporting that symptoms/events may be largely independent of sports continuation.4,5 For example, the LIVE-HCM prospective cohort study of 1,660 participants with HCM found no higher rates of death or life-threatening arrhythmias in those exercising vigorously than in those exercising moderately or being sedentary.5 Consequently, the latest European Society of Cardiology sports guidelines endorse a more liberal, individualized, risk-based approach6 and the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology state in their most recent scientific statement that it is reasonable to consider competitive sports participation in patients with HCM.7 The guidelines do not, however, differentiate between different types of HCM, even though HCM subtype might be considered part of the phenotypic risk profile. aHCM constitutes a distinct, albeit relatively rare form of HCM, accounting for 3%-15% of patients with HCM.8 It has been reported to have a more favorable prognosis than nonapical HCM, with a modest burden of myocardial fibrosis, less severe diastolic dysfunction, and lower cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.9

Conclusions

Our case demonstrates that in an athlete with established aHCM, engaging in long-term elite-level sports is not associated with adverse morphologic changes or arrhythmias, which is in line with previous reports.10 This includes no progression of apical fibrosis related to sports, even after cessation of a professional career. Notably, the observed regression of LV mass and volume post career reinforces the concept that the total magnitude of hypertrophy in HCM can include an important, reversible physiological component. This case illustrates that although detraining documents the regression of “added” exercise-induced hypertrophy, it cannot be used in isolation to exclude underlying cardiomyopathy. Potentially, the apical forms of HCM could be considered entities within the spectrum of HCM with distinct risk profiles, with a more benign interaction with sports, and a different course of disease progression as compared with other subtypes of HCM. Finally, our case demonstrates the added value of multidisciplinary expert advice and shared decision-making when providing guideline-based sports advice to elite athletes with HCM.
