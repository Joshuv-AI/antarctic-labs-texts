Choquet rank-dependent utility
Zachary Van Oosten, Ruodu Wang

Abstract
We propose a new decision model under ambiguity, called the Choquet rank–dependent utility model. The model extends the Choquet expected utility model by allowing for the reduction to the rank-dependent utility model in the absence of ambiguity, rather than to the expected utility model. The model has three major components: a utility function u u and a probability distortion g g , which together capture the risk component of the preferences, and generalized probabilistic beliefs ν \nu , which captures the ambiguity component of the preferences. The representation takes the form X ≿ Y ⇔ ∫ Ω u ⁡ ( X ) ​ d ​ ( g ∘ ν ) ⇔ ∫ Ω u ⁡ ( Y ) ​ d ​ ( g ∘ ν ) . X\succsim Y\iff\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}(g\circ\nu)\iff\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}(g\circ\nu). To obtain the axiomatization, we work in the uncertainty setting of Savage with a non-ambiguous source. Afterwards, we discuss ambiguity attitudes and their representation with respect to the generalized probabilistic beliefs, along with conditions for a robust representation. Keywords : Ambiguity, probabilistic sophistication, ambiguity attitudes.



1
Introduction

The expected utility (EU) model, axiomatized by
von Neumann and Morgenstern 1947
, serves as the theoretical foundation for much of today’s theory of decision-making. The EU model has two well-known limitations. First, the EU model is formulated within the risk setting, where acts are given by lotteries over outcomes. This setting is unrealistic in many situations, as a decision maker (DM) is often not fully aware of the distributions of the acts they are comparing.
Second, even in the risk setting, the EU model fails to account for both conceptual and empirical considerations. For example, the EU model cannot account for the empirically observed Allais paradox
(
Allais 1953
)
.

To address concerns associated with the risk setting, the seminal work of
Savage 1954
redefined acts as functions from states of the world to outcomes, rather than as lotteries over outcomes. This setting, commonly referred to as the Savage setting, does not assume that the likelihoods of events are well-specified a priori, and thus, there is no objective way to view the acts as lotteries. By imposing a set of axioms on preferences over these acts,
Savage 1954
was able to elicit a subjective probability, thereby converting the Savage setting to the risk setting. Furthermore, the Savage axioms yield the EU model in the elicited risk setting. This model is now known as the subjective expected utility (SEU) model. However, the critique by
Ellsberg 1961
of the sure-thing principle, an axiom used in
Savage 1954
to derive the SEU model, scrutinized the idea that it was always behaviourally feasible to obtain a unique probabilistic belief from preferences. This led to the development of ambiguity models. Some prominent ambiguity models include the Choquet expected utility (CEU) model of
Schmeidler 1989
, the maxmin expected utility (MEU) model of
Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989
, the smooth ambiguity model of
Klibanoff et al. 2005
, and the variational preferences model of
Maccheroni et al. 2006
. All of these models share a common theme: in the absence of ambiguity, that is, dealing with pure risks, they return to the SEU model.

To address the empirical violations of the EU model within the risk setting, the work of
Quiggin 1982
introduced the rank-dependent utility (RDU) model. This model was a generalization of the EU model that could account for the Allais paradox. Further advancements came with the prospect theory
(
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
)
and the cumulative prospect theory
(
Tversky and Kahneman 1992
)
, a generalization of the RDU model. This model was more behaviorally realistic than the RDU model, as it accounted for framing effects and asymmetries in the valuation of gains and losses. For a comprehensive treatment of the prospect theory, see
Wakker 2010
.

We are now at the crossroad of two streams of literature: the ambiguity models, which reduces to SEU for pure risks, and the rank-dependent models, which do not account for ambiguity.
To unite these two valid considerations, this paper characterizes an ambiguity model that reduces to the RDU model for pure risks. To obtain this model, we will work in a Savage setting that incorporates the notion of pure risks through probabilistic modeling.

To introduce and motivate our setting, we begin by examining how decision-making is typically handled in practice, particularly within the Savage setting, where acts are viewed as mappings from states of the world to real-valued outcomes.

The setting of
Savage 1954
is, in general, not limited to real-valued acts; see
Abdellaoui and Wakker 2020
. We take the perspective of real-valued acts in this paper.
Typically, a DM begins with the statistical estimation of a reference probability measure. This probability measure is then used to derive the distributions for all the acts under consideration. This allows the DM to work in the risk setting. To facilitate decision-making, the DM uses a function that depends on these distributions, such as the expected utility function, to evaluate different distributions. Preference relations that rely exclusively on the distributions of acts under the reference probability measure, rather than on the acts themselves, are said to be probabilistically sophisticated
(
Machina and Schmeidler 1992
)
.
2
2

2

Probabilistic sophistication in
Machina and Schmeidler 1992
also has a consideration of stochastic dominance, which we omit here.

An underlying assumption for the above procedure is that the reference probability measure is sufficiently informative to support decision-making.

In practice, considerable uncertainty often exists about the statistically estimated reference probability measure. This requires the decisions to reflect ambiguity as discussed earlier. Decades of economic incidents have demonstrated that over-reliance on a single probability measure can lead to poor decision-making, particularly when this measure fails to adequately capture important qualitative phenomena. For instance, during the 2008 financial crisis, the probabilistic models used to estimate mortgage default rates failed to account for tail dependencies. As a result, the potential severity of the loss distributions of mortgage-backed securities was significantly underestimated, leading to poor financial decision-making. This underestimation played a critical role in the widespread insolvency of financial institutions exposed to these securities; see
McNeil et al. 2015
.

To account for uncertainty in the reference probability measure,
Shen et al. 2025
considered a model of risk assessment inspired by
Baillon et al. 2025
, where probabilistic sophistication is satisfied only partially, that is, only with respect to acts depending on events that are assumed to be probabilistically well-specified by the DM. In the main result of this paper, the partial form of probabilistic sophistication leads to a characterization of an ambiguity model that reduces to the RDU for pure risks.

Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions and notation

Throughout the paper, we consider a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
representing the future states of the world. A
capacity
is a function
\nu:\mathcal{F}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
\nu(\varnothing)=0
,
\nu(\Omega)=1
, and
\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
with
A\subseteq B.
In some literature (e.g.,
Marinacci and Montrucchio 2004
), a capacity is not assumed to satisfy
\nu(\Omega)=1
, but this difference is not essential. A capacity
\nu
is said to be:
upward continuous
if for all increasing sequences
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
it holds that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu(A_{n})=\nu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A\right)
,
downward continuous
if for all decreasing sequences
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
it holds that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu(A_{n})=\nu\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\right)
, and
continuous
if it is both upward continuous and downward continuous.
A capacity is called a
probability measure
if it is countably additive.
We denote the set of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
by
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

We will fix a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
, which will be referred to as the
reference probability measure
. We interpret the reference probability measure
\mathbb{P}
as the probability measure estimated by the DM to govern decisions. An
act
is defined as a bounded real-valued measurable function. We will denote the space of acts by
\mathcal{X}
. Constant acts in
\mathcal{X}
are identified with constants in
\mathbb{R}
.
Given
X\in\mathcal{X}
, we write
\|X\|=\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}|X(\omega)|
. A set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is said to be
bounded
if
\sup_{X\in\mathcal{S}}\|X\|<\infty
and
B-closed
if, for any bounded
(X_{n})_{n\in N}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
that pointwise converges to some
X\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that
X\in\mathcal{S}
.

For
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
,
X\geq Y
means
X(\omega)\geq Y(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
, we say that
X\geq_{\mu}^{\mathrm{as}}Y
if
\mu(\{X\geq Y\})=1
, and
X\geq^{\mathrm{sd}}_{\mu}Y
if
\mu(X>x)\geq\mu(Y>x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Clearly,

X\geq Y\penalty\ \Longrightarrow\penalty\ X\geq^{\mathrm{as}}_{\mu}Y\penalty\ \Longrightarrow\penalty\ X\geq^{\mathrm{sd}}_{\mu}Y.

For a continuous capacity
\nu
, the
core of
\nu
, denoted by
\mathfrak{C}(\nu)
, is defined as

\mathfrak{C}(\nu)=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}:\mu(A)\geq\nu(A)\text{ for all }A\in\mathcal{F}\right\}.

The following are common properties of a continuous capacity
\nu
.

Supermodularity: For all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
,
\nu(A)+\nu(B)\leq\nu(A\cup B)+\nu(A\cap B)
.

Submodularity: For all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
,
\nu(A)+\nu(B)\geq\nu(A\cup B)+\nu(A\cap B)
.

Balanced:
\mathfrak{C}(\nu)\neq\varnothing
.

Exactness: For all
A\in\mathcal{F}
,
\nu(A)=\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak{C}(\nu)}\mu(A)
.

For more on the core and the previous properties of capacities, see
Denneberg 1994
and
Marinacci and Montrucchio 2004
.

Given a capacity
\nu
, the
Choquet integral
with respect to
\nu
is given by

\int X\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu(X>x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left(\nu(X>x)-1\right)\mathrm{d}x,\penalty\ \penalty\ X\in\mathcal{X}.

We say that
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
are
comonotonic
if for all
\omega,\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega,

(X(\omega)-X(\tilde{\omega}))(Y(\omega)-Y(\tilde{\omega}))\geq 0.

As shown by
Schmeidler 1986
, a function
V:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
can be represented as
V(X)=\int_{\Omega}X\mathrm{d}\nu
for some capacity
\nu
if and only if
V
is monotone (
V(X)\geq V(Y)
whenever
X\geq Y
), comonotonic additive (
V(X+Y)=V(X)+V(Y)
for comonotonic
X,Y
), and normalized (
V(1)=1
). An increasing (always in the non-strict sense) function
g:[0,1]\to[0,1]
with
g(0)=0
and
g(1)=1
is called a
distortion function
.

2.2
The Savage setting with pure risk

We will work in
a refined Savage setting, as described below.
A
preference relation
\succsim

is a total preorder over
\mathcal{X}
, describing the preferences of the DM.
Fix a sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
and assume that the restricted probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{G}})
is atomless; that is,
\{\mathbb{P}(B):B\in\mathcal{G},\penalty\ B\subseteq A\}=[0,\mathbb{P}(A)]
for every
A\in\mathcal{G}
. We write
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
for the subspace of
\mathcal{X}
consisting of
\mathcal{G}
-measurable acts. The
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
is interpreted as the collection of events that the DM regards, prior to making any decisions, as being statistically well-specified under the reference measure
\mathbb{P}
. We refer to this setting as the
Savage setting with pure risk
.

Under our setting, acts
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
exhibit no distributional ambiguity, and the preference relation
\succsim
restricted to
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
should be determined solely by the distributions of these acts under the reference probability measure
\mathbb{P}
. This brings us to our first axiom of risk conformity.
3
3

3

In
Shen et al. 2025
, risk conformity was called partial law invariance.

(RC)

Risk conformity: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying
X=_{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathrm{sd}}Y
,
X\simeq Y
.

If the preference relation
\succsim
satisfies Axiom (RC), then the restriction of
\succsim
to
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
, called the
risk preferences
of
\succsim
, corresponds to a preference relation over lotteries. To evaluate general acts, i.e., acts not necessarily in
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
, the DM must consider both their risk preferences and their perception and attitudes regarding ambiguity.
In the discussions later, the
risk component
of
\succsim
refers to the influence of the risk preferences on the evaluation of general acts, and the
ambiguity component
of
\succsim
refers to the influence of ambiguity on the evaluation of general acts.

A capacity
\nu

is
risk conforming
if
\nu|_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{G}}
.
Risk conformity means the capacity behaves like the reference probability measure
\mathbb{P}
for the events that have no ambiguity.
When
\nu
describes the DM’s evaluation of events, risk conformity is essential, as the DM believes that
\mathbb{P}
is well-specified on
\mathcal{G}
.

The CEU model (
Schmeidler 1989
) was originally formulated in the setting of
Anscombe and Aumann 1963
. We now explain the formulation of the CEU model in the Savage setting with pure risk. The DM has a non-constant increasing continuous
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, hereby called a von Neumann–Morgenstern (vNM) utility function, which describes their evaluation of outcomes.
To describe the ambiguity component of the preferences, the DM has a continuous and risk-confirming capacity
\nu
that can be interpreted as their generalized probabilistic beliefs. The preference relation
\succsim
of the DM in the CEU model is given by

X\succsim Y\iff\int u(X)\mathrm{d}\nu\geq\int u(Y)\mathrm{d}\nu,\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X},

(1)

It is well known that, given a preference relation
\succsim
satisfying (
1
),
u
is unique up to positive affine transformations and
\nu
is unique.
A key conceptual assumption of the CEU model is that if the DM believes the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
uniquely describes their belief regarding uncertainty, i.e., they perceive no ambiguity, then they take
\nu=\mathbb{P}
in (
1
) and their preference relation
\succsim
is given by the SEU model.

It is straightforward to show that a CEU preference relation
\succsim
satisfies Axiom (RC) and the risk preferences for
\succsim
are given by an EU model. As noted in the introduction, the majority of ambiguity models yield the EU model for risk preferences. The work of
Sarin and Wakker 1992
provides an axiomatization of CEU preference relations, but rather than assuming that

\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{G}}
is given exogenously, they incorporate its derivation into the axiomatization.
In the next section, we will axiomatize a class of preference relations that satisfy Axiom (RC), where the corresponding risk preferences are, in general, given by the RDU model of
Quiggin 1982
rather than specifically the EU model.

3
Axioms and representation

We now state and discuss several additional axioms for preference relations
\succsim
. Afterwards, in Theorem
1
, we will present the main representation.

(M)

Monotonicity: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying
X\geq Y
,
X\succsim Y
.

Axiom (M) is standard for preference relations in the Savage setting. If the act
X
pays more than the act
Y
for every state of the world, then it is natural that the DM would prefer
X
to
Y
.

(SRM)

Strict risk monotonicity: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying
X>_{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathrm{as}}Y
,
X\succ Y
.

Axiom (SRM) reflects the fact that the DM believes that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is well-specified on
\mathcal{G}
. Thus, a stronger form of monotonicity, using distributions, hold for acts in
\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
.

(C)

Continuity: For all
X\in\mathcal{X}
,
\{Y\in\mathcal{X}:X\succsim Y\}
and
\{Y\in\mathcal{X}:Y\succsim X\}
are B-closed.

Axiom (C) is commonly used in the literature on risk measures, where it is referred to as the Lebesgue property; see
Föllmer and Schied 2016
.

The axioms discussed thus far concern only certain notions of regularity and essentially impose no specific functional forms on the preference relations. The axioms governing specific forms of the risk preferences will be discussed later. Before providing these axioms, we will recall the notion of matching probabilities. For notational simplicity, we will write
A\succsim B
for
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
to mean
\mathds{1}_{A}\succsim\mathds{1}_{B}
, where
\mathds{1}_{A}
denotes the binary act which yields
1
if
\omega\in A
and
0
otherwise.

Definition 1
.

Given a preference relation
\succsim
, a function
\nu:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
is a
\succsim
-matching probability
if for all
A\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
R_{A}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\nu(A)=\mathbb{P}(R_{A})
and
A\simeq R_{A}
.

Intuitively, a
\succsim
-matching probability uses the restriction of the preference relation
\succsim
on
\mathcal{F}
to produce a description of ambiguity on the level of events. The definition formalizes the idea that, for each event
A\in\mathcal{F}
, the preference relation
\succsim
is used to identify a non-ambiguous probability
\nu(A)
such that a bet paying $1 if
A
occurs (and $0 otherwise) and a non-ambiguous bet that pays $1 with probability
\nu(A)
(and $0 otherwise) are equally preferred. Our definition of a
\succsim
-matching probability only works in the Savage setting with pure risk, as
\mathcal{G}
needs to contain non-ambiguous events to have the aforementioned interpretation.

Proposition 1
.

For every preference relation
\succsim
satisfying Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C), there exists a unique
\succsim
-matching probability
\nu
. Furthermore,
\nu
is a continuous risk-conforming capacity, and

A\succsim B\iff\nu(A)\geq\nu(B),\penalty\ \penalty\ A,B\in\mathcal{F}.

(2)

Let
\succsim
satisfy Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C). By Proposition
1
, the
\succsim
-matching probability is risk conforming. Therefore, the
\succsim
-matching probability is able to identify the ambiguity not perceived by
\succsim
. The following example provides evidence that the
\succsim
-matching probability can be used as a tool to isolate the ambiguity component of
\succsim
.

Example 1
.

Let
\succsim
be a CEU preference relation. It is clear that
\succsim
satisfies Axioms (RC) and (M). Furthermore, by
Wang and Klir 2010
,
\succsim
satisfies Axiom (C). It is straightforward to verify that
\succsim
satisfies Axiom (SRM) if and only if the vNM utility function
u
is strictly increasing. We claim that if
\succsim
satisfies Axiom (SRM), then the unique
\succsim
-matching probability (existence given by Proposition
1
) is given by the unique
\nu
in (
1
). To see this, let
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
R_{A}\in\mathcal{G}
satisfy
A\simeq R_{A}
. Therefore,

u(1)\nu(A)+u(0)(1-\nu(A))=\int_{\Omega}u(\mathds{1}_{A})\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{\Omega}u(\mathds{1}_{R_{A}})\mathrm{d}\nu=u(1)\mathbb{P}(R_{A})+u(0)(1-\mathbb{P}(R_{A})).

Since
u
is strictly increasing,
u(1)>u(0)
and
\nu(A)=\mathbb{P}(R_{A})
. Therefore
\nu
is the unique
\succsim
-matching probability. This provides evidence that the
\succsim
-matching probability can be used as a tool to isolate the ambiguity component of the preferences
\succsim
, as the capacity
\nu
in (
1
) traditionally has this interpretation; see Section
2.2
.

The remaining axioms make reference to certainty equivalents. To show existence, as well as a continuity property, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Given a preference relation
\succsim
satisfying Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C), for each
X\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a unique
c_{X}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
X\simeq c_{X}
. Moreover, if
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a bounded sequence converging pointwise to
X\in\mathcal{X}
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{X_{n}}=c_{X}
.

The following axiom, or variations of it, has often been used for the characterization of the RDU model. In what follows, we use
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cf}}
to denote the set
\{R\in\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{P}(R)=1/2\}
of “coin flip” events. Given
A\in\mathcal{F}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
xAy
denotes the binary act which yields
x
if
\omega\in A
and
y
otherwise.

(RS)

Risk symmetry: For all
R\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cf}}
and
x,y,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
x\geq y
and
z,z^{\prime}\in[y,x]

c_{xRz}Rc_{z^{\prime}Ry}\simeq c_{xRz^{\prime}}Rc_{zRy}.

It is not hard to show that Axiom (RS) is a weaker version of the weak independence axiom from
Quiggin 1982
. The formulation of Axiom (RS) is similar to Axiom (A6) in

To define the final axiom, we need to define a binary operation on
\mathcal{X}
that can be viewed as a subjective mixture operation for acts. As a preliminary step, following the approach of

Proposition 3
.

Let
\succsim
satisfy Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C). Given
R\in\mathcal{G}

\mathbb{P}(A)\in(0,1)

xRy\simeq c_{xRz}Rc_{zRy}.

If the preference relation
\succsim
satisfies Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C), then,
given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, as
x\geq y
or
y\geq x
, we will define
(1/2)x\oplus(1/2)y

R\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{cf}}

\left[(1/2)X\oplus(1/2)Y\right](\omega)=(1/2)X(\omega)\oplus(1/2)Y(\omega).

We are now ready to present the final axiom, which is the comonotonic independence axiom of
Schmeidler 1989
, but for the subjective mixture of acts. The following axiom assumes that the preference relation
\succsim
already satisfies Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C), since the existence of the subjective mixture operation for acts relies on these axioms.

(SCI)

Subjective comonotonic independence: For all pairwise comonotonic
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{X}
,

X\simeq Y\Longrightarrow(1/2)X\oplus(1/2)Z\simeq(1/2)Y\oplus(1/2)Z.

The following result characterizes preference relations following these axioms.

Theorem 1
.

The preference relation
\succsim
satisfies Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), (C), (RS), and (SCI) if and only if
there exist a strictly increasing vNM utility function
u
, a strictly increasing continuous distortion function
g
, and a continuous risk-conforming capacity
\nu
such that

X\succsim Y\iff\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}(g\circ\nu)\geq\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}(g\circ\nu),\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X}.

(3)

Moreover, in the representation (
3
),
u
is unique up to positive affine transformations,
g
is unique, and
\nu
is unique and coincides with the
\succsim
-matching probability.

A preference relation
\succsim
with the form (
3

This terminology is motivated by the fact that, given a CRDU preference relation

Thus, the risk preferences are given by the RDU model of

such that

Given a CRDU preference relation
\succsim

\succsim
-distortion. Likewise, the strictly increasing vNM utility function

In the Savage setting with pure risk, Axiom (RC) ensures that the
\succsim

\succsim

\succsim
. This, in turn, separates it from the
\succsim

\succsim
. In the general Savage setting, i.e., the setting where
\mathcal{G}

Example 2

Consider a general Savage setting without specifying
\mathcal{G}

.

4
Ambiguity attitudes

In this section, we study ambiguity attitudes of CRDU preferences through the matching probabilities.

4.1
Comparative and absolute ambiguity attitudes

We will first define comparative ambiguity attitudes for CRDU preference relations. This will then allow us to define absolute ambiguity attitudes once a normalization for ambiguity neutrality is provided. Many of the notions presented here will be similar to those in
Epstein 2004

Epstein 2004

Epstein 2004
) definition of comparative ambiguity attitudes between two preference relations
\succsim_{1}

\succsim_{2}

5

implies the equivalence of risk preferences between

Given the CRDU preference relations
\succsim_{1}

\succsim_{2}

\succsim_{1}

R\succsim_{1}A\implies R\succsim_{2}A,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ R\in\mathcal{G}\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ A\in\mathcal{F}.

The interpretation of this definition is quite natural:
As events

This is similar to the classic notion of comparative risk aversion (

Yaari 1969

We have the following proposition characterizing comparative ambiguity attitudes.

Theorem 2

Let
\succsim_{1}

\succsim_{2}

\succsim_{2}

In Theorem

This is consistent with the fact that the ambiguity component in the CRDU model is separated from the risk component.
To get a full comparative implication on all acts, instead of only binary ones, we need to force the risk components of both preference relations to be identical, as shown in the next result.

Proposition 4

Let

Our next goal is to define a normalization for ambiguity neutrality.
In

(AN)

Ambiguity neutrality: There exists
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}

(7)

If
\succsim

Proposition 5

Let
\succsim

\succsim

\succsim
-matching probability

Consistency with respect to first-order stochastic dominance, i.e. (

Consider, for example, when
Q_{\epsilon}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}:H(\mu|\mathbb{P})\leq\epsilon\},
where
\epsilon>0

\succsim
given by

X\succsim Y\iff\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{Q}_{\epsilon}}\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{Q}_{\epsilon}}\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}\mu,\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X}

Ghirardato et al. 2004

Example
2
gives a similar example in the framework of variational preferences.

In our Savage setting with pure risk, Axiom (RC) allows us to pin down the DM’s risk preferences. Furthermore, by Proposition
5
, Property (AN) implies that the DM transforms all acts to lotteries and then uses their risk preferences on these lotteries to make decisions. This is why the example for the MEU model presented above does not capture ambiguity neutrality. In the MEU model, the risk preferences are given by the EU model. Under the situation where the set of priors is not a singleton, but the preferences are consistent with respect to first-order stochastic dominance, the preference relation induced on lotteries is not EU. In the example above, the induced preference relation on lotteries coincides with the so-called coherent entropic risk measure (
Föllmer and Knispel 2011
and
Ahmadi-Javid 2012
) composed with the utility transformation. Therefore, ambiguity neutrality, in general, should be equivalent to consistency with respect to first-order stochastic dominance and the induced preference relation on lotteries coinciding with the risk preferences. This definition is consistent with
Ghirardato and Marinacci 2002
, where the authors ambiguity neutrality corresponds to the SEU model.

Finally, we say that a CRDU preference relation
\succsim
is
ambiguity averse (AA)
if it is more ambiguity averse than an ambiguity-neutral CRDU preference relation. A straightforward consequence of Theorem
2
and Proposition
5
is that given a CRDU preference relation
\succsim
, ambiguity aversion is equivalent to the
\succsim
-matching probability being balanced.

In the Savage setting with pure risk, the DM is confident with the model
\mathbb{P}
for the events in
\mathcal{G}
, so decisions regarding acts
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
rely only on the distributions of these acts. The following discussion, however, describes how the DM may utilize the reference probability measure
\mathbb{P}
for all acts
X\in\mathcal{X}
.

Consider the case where the DM estimated the model
\mathbb{P}
to inform investment decisions for their financial portfolio, which they plan to liquidate within a week. Under the reference model
\mathbb{P}
, assume that the weekly losses of a well-established equity
X
and the weekly losses of a recently launched cryptocurrency
Y
share the same distribution. Because the equity has been observed for an extended period, the DM perceives the events related to
X
as unambiguous, i.e.,
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
. When deciding between an investment in
X
versus an investment in
Y
, the DM is automatically drawn to
X
because it has the same estimated distribution as
Y

X\succsim Y
is reasonable. This discussion motivates the following property.

(RAA)

Reference ambiguity aversion: For all
X\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
and
Y\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying
X=_{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathrm{sd}}Y

X\succsim Y

It is straightforward to verify that Property (RAA) is a stronger condition than Axiom (RC). The terminology “reference ambiguity aversion” comes from the following proposition.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\succsim
be a CRDU preference. Then
\succsim
satisfies Property (RAA) if and only if the
\succsim
-matching probability
\nu
is set-wise dominated by the reference probability measure
\mathbb{P}

\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{C}(\nu)

If the CRDU preference relation
\succsim
satisfies Property (RAA), then, by Proposition

\succsim
-matching probability must be balanced. In general, we have

\mbox{Property (RAA)}\implies\mbox{Property (AA) and Axiom (RC), but the converse implication does not hold.}

To see that the converse implication does not hold, we can take
\nu

\mathfrak{C}(\nu)
does not contain
\mathbb{P}
but contains some
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
that coincides with
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathcal{G}
but differs from it outside
\mathcal{G}

4.2
Propensity for diversification

The idea of propensity for diversification in finance has appeared in the setting of risk since the foundational work of
Markowitz 1952
. For decision models that incorporate ambiguity, such a property has been formalized in the classic papers by
Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989
and
Schmeidler 1989
.
6
6

6

In
Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989
and
Schmeidler 1989
, diversification seeking is called uncertainty aversion.
We present the definition of this property below, which is also sometimes called the convexity of the preferences.

(DS)

Diversification seeking: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying
X\simeq Y
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y\succsim X.

If the preference relation of the DM satisfies Property (DS), then the DM prefers mixtures of equally preferred acts. This aligns with the intuitive idea of diversification. We immediately get the following proposition based on the work of
Wakker and Yang 2019
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\succsim
be a CRDU preference relation in (
3
). Then
\succsim
satisfies Property (DS) if and only if the
u
is concave and the capacity
g\circ\nu
is supermodular.

By Proposition
7

\succsim
, Property (DS) depends on both the risk component of
\succsim
, i.e., the
\succsim
-utility and the
\succsim
-distortion, and the ambiguity component of
\succsim
, i.e., the
\succsim
-matching probability. Furthermore, since the
\succsim
-matching probability
\nu
is risk conforming and
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{G}})

\succsim

is convex, see
Föllmer and Schied 2016

Given

and the acts
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}

X

in second-order stochastic dominance (SSD) under
\mu

X\geq^{\mathrm{ssd}}_{\mu}Y

\int_{\Omega}f(X)\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\int_{\Omega}f(Y)\mathrm{d}\mu.
SSD is closely related to strong risk aversion as studied by
Rothschild and Stiglitz 1970

\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
.

(SRA)

Strong risk aversion:
For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying
X\geq^{\mathrm{ssd}}_{\mathbb{P}}Y
,
X\succsim Y
.

A direct consequence of
Schmidt and Zank 2008

\succsim
satisfies Property (SRA) if and only if the
\succsim
-utility is concave and the
\succsim
-distortion is convex. Therefore, Property (DS) implies Property (SRA).

Property (SRA) is strictly weaker than Property (DS), as Property (DS) is a consequence of both the risk and ambiguity components of the preferences, but Property (SRA) is only a consequence of the risk component of the preferences. Ideally, one would like to identify an ambiguity attitude that, together with Property (SRA), implies Property (DS). The following example shows that this can be done for the specific class of CEU preference relations.

Example 3
.

Let
\succsim
be a CEU preference relation; that is,

X\succsim Y\iff\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}\nu\geq\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}\nu,\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X},

where

is the
\succsim
-utility and
\nu
is the
\succsim
-matching probability. By Proposition
7

\succsim
satisfies Property (DS) if and only if
u
is concave and
\nu
is supermodular. Since the supermodularity of

\nu
is balanced, we have
\succsim
satisfies Property (AA). Therefore, the supermodularity of the
\succsim
-matching probability is a stronger form of ambiguity aversion. Furthermore, this stronger form of ambiguity aversion, combined with Property (SRA) guaranteeing the concavity of

For general CRDU preferences, the forward direction of the equivalence established in Example
3
for CEU preferences still holds, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\succsim
be a CRDU preference relation. If
\succsim
satisfies Property (SRA) and the
\succsim
-matching probability is supermodular, then
\succsim
satisfies Property (DS).

Therefore, a combination of both strong risk aversion and a stronger version of ambiguity aversion will ensure that the DM prefers diversification. However, the next example demonstrates that the reverse direction of the equivalence established in Example

In particular, we show the existence of a CRDU preference relation

Assume that there exists a sub-

4.3
The robust interpretation

A preference relation
\succsim
is called a
maxmin expected utility (MEU)
preference relation (
Gilboa and Schmeidler 1989
) if there exists a collection of risk-conforming probability measures
\mathcal{S}
8
8

8

Here, we suppose each
\mu\in\mathcal{S}
is risk conforming to ensure we remain in the Savage setting with pure risk. That is, since
\mathbb{P}
is well specified on
\mathcal{G}
, it should hold that
\mu|_{\mathcal{G}}=\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{G}}
for all
\mu\in\mathcal{S}
.
and a vNM utility

such that

X\succsim Y\iff\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}\mu,\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X}.

(8)

Intuitively, a DM with an MEU preference relation chooses the set
\mathcal{S}
to represent their ambiguity perception and then aggregates their risk preferences, given by the EU model, over this set. This form of aggregation is known as the robust aggregation of risk preferences, and it corresponds to a very strong form of ambiguity aversion; see
Ghirardato et al. 2004
.

For the CRDU preference relation
\succsim
in (
3
), the above idea of robust aggregation of risk preferences means the following property:

X\succsim Y\iff\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak{C}(\nu)}\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}(g\circ\mu)\geq\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak{C}(\nu)}\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}(g\circ\mu),\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X},

(9)

where the set of probability measures
\mathfrak{C}(\nu)
is understood as reflecting the DM’s ambiguity perception. As the following theorem demonstrates, the supermodularity of the
\succsim
-matching probability enables the robust aggregation of risk preferences in the case of CRDU preference relations.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\succsim
be a CRDU preference relation in (
3
). Then the robust aggregation (
9
) holds if and only if
\nu
is supermodular.

It is well-known that Property (DS) connects CEU preference relations to MEU preference relations (
Schmeidler 1989
). In the case of CRDU preference relations, Property (DS) is not sufficient to guarantee the robust aggregation of risk preferences. To see this, let
\succsim
be a CRDU preference relation in (
3
) satisfying Property (DS). Then, by Proposition
7
and
Marinacci and Montrucchio 2004
, it holds that

X\succsim Y\iff\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak{C}(g\circ\nu)}\int_{\Omega}u(X)\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\min_{\mu\in\mathfrak{C}(g\circ\nu)}\int_{\Omega}u(Y)\mathrm{d}\mu,\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X}.

(10)

The expression in (
10
) represents an aggregation, but it does not correspond to the robust aggregation of the DM’s risk preferences. This follows since (
10
) corresponds to an aggregation of the EU risk preferences. However, as
\succsim
is a CRDU preference relation, the risk preferences should be given by the RDU model with distortion
g
. Furthermore, the set
\mathfrak{C}(g\circ\nu)
in (

g\circ\nu
does not represent the ambiguity component of
\succsim
; rather, the
\succsim
-matching probability
\nu
represents the ambiguity component of
\succsim
.

5
Distortion families and an alternative representation

In this section, we will derive an alternative representation for CRDU preference relations when the following additional property is satisfied.

(NSC)

Null set consistency: For all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying
X=^{\mathrm{as}}_{\mathbb{P}}Y
,
X\simeq Y
.

Property (NSC) encodes the idea that the DM is confident that the reference probability measure
\mathbb{P}
correctly identifies sets of probability zero and is indifferent to acts that only differ on a set of probability zero. Property (NSC) is not implied by Axiom (RC), which does not restrict
\succsim
outside
\mathcal{G}
. For
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
, denote by
A\triangle B
the symmetric set difference, that is,
A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
. For a capacity
\nu
, we say that
\nu
is
\mathbb{P}
-consistent
if for all
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}(A\triangle B)=0
,
\nu(A)=\nu(B)
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\succsim
be a CRDU preference relation. Then
\succsim
satisfies Property (NSC) if and only if the
\succsim
-matching probability is
\mathbb{P}

6
Risk preferences given by the dual utility model

The dual utility model of
Yaari 1987
is the special case of the RDU model when the vNM utility is linear. The goal of this section is to axiomatize the subclass of CRDU preferences that have risk preferences given by the dual utility model. For this purpose, we introduce the following axiom, originally formulated by
Yaari 1987
in the risk setting and by
Schmeidler 1989
in the setting of ambiguity.

(CI)

Comonotonic independence: For all pairwise comonotonic
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{X}
,

X\simeq Y\Rightarrow(1/2)X+(1/2)Z\simeq(1/2)Y+(1/2)Z.

Intuitively, the Axiom (CI) says that an act, mutually comonotonic to two indifferent comonotonic acts, cannot be used as a way to hedge, i.e., as a way to change the indifference. Axiom (CI) is structurally similar to Axiom (SCI) presented in Section
3
. However, Axiom (CI) is much simpler to define as it does not need the subjective mixture operation for acts. It turns out that Axiom (CI), together with the earlier regularity axioms, suffices to characterize all CRDU preference relations whose risk preferences are given by the dual utility model.

Theorem 6
.

The preference relation
\succsim
satisfies Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), (C), and (CI) if and only if
there exists a strictly increasing continuous distortion function
g
and a continuous risk-conforming capacity
\nu
such that

X\succsim Y\iff\int_{\Omega}X\mathrm{d}(g\circ\nu)\geq\int_{\Omega}Y\mathrm{d}(g\circ\nu),\penalty\ \penalty\ X,Y\in\mathcal{X}.

(12)

Moreover, in the representation (
12
),
g
is unique and
\nu
is unique and coincides with the
\succsim
-matching probability.

Any preference relation satisfying representation (
12
) is evidently a CRDU preference relation. This consequence is further clarified by the following observation. If a preference relation
\succsim
satisfies the Axioms (RC), (M), (SRM), and (C), then, given
x,y\in\mathbb{R},
we can define the subjective mixture of these outcomes
(1/2)x\oplus(1/2)y
; see Section
3
. If we further assume that
\succsim
satisfies Axiom (CI), then it holds that
(1/2)x\oplus(1/2)y=(1/2)x+(1/2)y
, that is, the subjective mixture is given by the usual arithmetic average. Therefore, Axiom (CI) implies Axiom (SCI).

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a new model of ambiguity, the Choquet rank-dependent utility model. The representation is structurally similar to the Choquet expected utility model of
Schmeidler 1989
, but when confined to pure risks, it reduces to the rank-dependent utility model of
Quiggin 1982
, rather than to the expected utility model of
von Neumann and Morgenstern 1947
. Moreover, our model is formulated in the Savage setting, instead of the Anscombe–Aumann setting.

The main result is Theorem
1
, which axiomatizes this model in the Savage setting with pure risk. An advantage of our approach is that, apart from some regularity axioms, only two axioms are needed to characterize the model. Furthermore, the representation in Theorem
1
shows that the ambiguity component of the preferences is completely captured by the notion of a matching probability.

To properly discuss ambiguity attitudes, we start by defining comparative ambiguity attitudes. Our definition only makes preference comparisons between binary acts as opposed to preference comparisons between all acts. In Theorem
2
, we show that comparative ambiguity attitudes for CRDU preference relations can be fully characterized by their matching probabilities. Absolute ambiguity attitudes can be characterized by matching probabilities.

Given the importance of the propensity for diversification in many areas, in particular finance, in Theorem
3
, we present sufficient conditions for such a property. These conditions are the classical notion of strong risk aversion and the supermodularity of the matching probability. Unlike the Choquet expected utility model, these conditions are not equivalent to the propensity for diversification. This result is normatively appealing as one can maintain the propensity for diversification if they allow themselves to have flexible risk and ambiguity attitudes. Moreover, Theorem
4
establishes that the supermodularity of the matching probability is equivalent to the preference relation admitting a robust aggregation interpretation of the risk preferences.

To offer a different perspective on the ambiguity component of CRDU preference relations, Theorem
5
presents a representation derived under an additional property. This new representation states that the DM distorts the reference probability measure, but allows this distortion to depend on the act being evaluated. The extent to which the distortion function associated with an act differs from the risk distortion determines the DM’s perception of and attitude toward the ambiguity in that act.

Finally, Theorem
6
provides an axiomatization for the subclass of CRDU preference relations whose risk preferences are given by the dual utility model of
Yaari 1987
. This axiomatization is concise as, apart from the regularity axioms from Theorem
1
, only a single axiom of comonotonic independence is needed.

Appendix E
Modularity results for lattices

A lattice is a partially ordered set
(\mathfrak{X},\leq)
in which every pair
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
admits a least upper bound
x\vee y
and a greatest lower bound
x\wedge y
. A function
\varphi:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}

is called
supermodular
if for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
\varphi(x\vee y)+\varphi(x\wedge y)\geq\varphi(x)+\varphi(y),

submodular
if for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
\varphi(x\vee y)+\varphi(x\wedge y)\leq\varphi(x)+\varphi(y),

increasing
if for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
with
x\geq y
,

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(y),
and
decreasing
if for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
with
x\geq y
,

\varphi(x)\leq\varphi(y).

Proposition 11
.

Let
(\mathfrak{X},\leq)
be a lattice,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
,
\varphi:\mathfrak{X}\to[a,b]
be monotonic and supermodular (submodular), and
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be increasing and convex (concave). It holds that
g\circ\nu
is supermodular (submodular).

Proof.

Assume that
\varphi
is supermodular and
f
is convex. For
u,v\in\mathfrak{X}
, define

z=\varphi(u\wedge v)+\varphi(u\vee v)-\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)\geq 0.

Note that

a\leq\varphi(v)+z=\varphi(u\wedge v)+\varphi(u\vee v)-\varphi(u)\leq\begin{cases}\varphi(u\vee v)&\text{if }\varphi\text{ is increasing}\\
\varphi(u\wedge v)&\text{if }\varphi\text{ is decreasing}\end{cases}\leq b.

As
\varphi
is monotonic, there exists
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that

\varphi(u)=\lambda\varphi(u\wedge v)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(u\vee v).

Therefore,

\displaystyle f(\varphi(u\wedge v))+f(\varphi(u\vee v))

\displaystyle\geq f(\lambda\varphi(u\wedge v)+(1-\lambda)\varphi(u\vee v))+f((1-\lambda)\varphi(u\wedge v)+\lambda\varphi(u\vee v))

\displaystyle=f(\varphi(u))+f(\varphi(v)+z)

\displaystyle\geq f(\varphi(u))+f(\varphi(v)).

A similar proof will show the claim when
\varphi
is submodular and
f
is concave.
∎
