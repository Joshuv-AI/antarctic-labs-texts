Divergent Burden Profiles of HIV/AIDS and Other Sexually Transmitted Infections Among Women of Child-Bearing Age, 1990–2021
Authors: Zihuan Li, Shizu Li, Guanwen Lin, Ya Zou, Chuming You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHIV/AIDS and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) remain major public health concerns for women of child-bearing age (WCBA, aged 15–49 years). Although their overall burden has been described in previous studies, the divergent burden profiles of HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA remain less clearly characterized, particularly the contrast between transmission-related burden and severe health loss.MethodsData on incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) of HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA from 1990 to 2021 were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) in rates were estimated using joinpoint regression analysis. Trends were assessed globally and stratified by age group, WHO region, Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) region, and country.ResultsIn 2021, the GBD parent cause category of HIV/AIDS and other STIs was estimated to account for 287,185,456 incident cases, 550,753,107 prevalent cases, 267,911 deaths, and 17,079,099 DALYs among WCBA worldwide. Other STIs accounted for the vast majority of incident and prevalent cases, with 286,514,651 incident cases and 544,454,239 prevalent cases. In contrast, HIV/AIDS contributed most deaths and DALYs, with 265,696 deaths and 16,378,796 DALYs. From 1990 to 2021, the incidence rate of HIV/AIDS decreased annually by 2.13%, whereas the incidence rate of other STIs increased slightly by 0.19% annually. Age-specific, regional, SDI-specific, and national patterns showed substantial heterogeneity, indicating that HIV/AIDS and other STIs did not change in parallel.ConclusionHIV/AIDS and other STIs showed divergent burden profiles among WCBA. HIV/AIDS remained the dominant contributor to deaths and DALYs, whereas other STIs accounted for most incident and prevalent cases. Progress in HIV/AIDS control should not be interpreted as overall STI control. Integrated, age-specific, region-specific, and country-specific strategies combining HIV prevention and treatment with broader STI screening, diagnosis, and management are needed.Keywords: HIV, sexually transmitted infections, women of childbearing age, global burden of disease

Introduction

Women of child-bearing age (WCBA), commonly defined as women aged 15–49 years,1 represent a key population for sexual and reproductive health.2,3 WCBA are generally in a sexually active period of life, they are particularly vulnerable to HIV/AIDS and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs).4

The epidemiological characteristics of HIV/AIDS and STIs differed substantially across regions. HIV/AIDS is associated with severe morbidity, mortality, and long-term treatment needs, whereas many non-HIV STIs, such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, syphilis, and genital herpes, often contribute to a large number of incident and prevalent cases.5 HIV can be transmitted from infected mothers to their children during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding, and more than 95% of pediatric HIV infections are attributable to mother-to-child transmission.4 Among the top ten causes of death and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) in the 25–49 age group, HIV/AIDS ranked second.6 From 1990 to 2021, the number of causes of death highly concentrated in regions comprising less than half of the global population rose, most notably represented by HIV/AIDS.7

Some non-HIV STIs are frequently asymptomatic, which may delay diagnosis and treatment and sustain transmission within populations.3 These STIs can lead to urethritis, cervicitis, and pelvic inflammatory disease.8 Infection during pregnancy is linked to an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including miscarriage and preterm delivery, as well as low birth weight and perinatal mortality in newborns.9,10

African women bear a disproportionate burden of STIs. With over 34 million prevalent cases of chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis, they have the world’s highest prevalence of gonorrhea and trichomoniasis.11 In South Sudan, young women represented 55.6% of adults living with HIV/AIDS, and new infections among young women were 1.5 times higher than among young men.12

Therefore, analysing HIV/AIDS and other STIs as a single broad category may obscure important differences in disease burden, severity, and prevention priorities. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 provides standardized and comparable estimates of HIV/AIDS and other STIs across countries, regions, age groups, and years.13 These data make it possible to assess long-term changes in incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) among WCBA.14–16 Previous studies have described the overall burden and temporal trends of HIV/AIDS and STIs in different populations.2,4 However, less attention has been paid to the divergent burden profiles of HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA, particularly the contrast between transmission-related burden, reflected by incidence and prevalence, and severe health loss, reflected by deaths and DALYs.

To address this gap, this study used GBD 2021 data to assess the global, regional, SDI-specific, age-specific, and national burden of HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA from 1990 to 2021. Rather than treating HIV/AIDS and other STIs as a homogeneous disease group, we focused on their divergent burden profiles and temporal patterns. This study aimed to clarify whether progress in HIV/AIDS control has been accompanied by comparable improvements in broader STI control among WCBA, and to provide evidence for age-specific, region-specific, and country-specific prevention strategies.

Methods

Overview and Definition

The estimates employed in this study to analyze trends in incidence, DALY, prevalence, and mortality of HIV and other STIs from 1990 to 2021 were sourced from the GBD 2021 study, which can be accessed at http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool. Coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), an independent global health research center at the University of Washington, GBD 2021 aggregates a wide range of data sources—totaling 100,983—including vital registration systems, household surveys, verbal autopsies, and censuses.5,7 GBD 2021 encompasses 371 diseases and injuries, along with 88 risk factors, and compiled data from 204 countries and territories. By leveraging this extensive dataset, the current research aims to provide a nuanced understanding of global trends and impacts related to HIV and other STIs.

The GBD 2021 project employed a standardized methodological framework to generate estimates related to HIV and other STIs. The study employed the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm) to integrate a wide array of data sources—including vital registration, verbal autopsies, registries, surveys, police records, and open-source databases—thereby facilitating comprehensive mortality estimates across diverse populations and geographical areas. This statistical method enhances the reliability and precision of mortality estimates. In GBD 2021, deaths with unspecified causes in vital registration systems were initially redistributed using statistical techniques. Epidemiological data known to have biases were adjusted with correction factors estimated through network meta-regressions, while clinical data were standardized to account for variations in healthcare access and diagnostic practices. Additionally, the DisMod-MR 2.1 tool, a Bayesian meta-regression modeling method, was employed to generate internally consistent estimates across various epidemiological metrics, such as age, sex, location, and year. Detailed methodologies for the GBD project have been published in other sources.4 The data sources for HIV and other STIs can be accessed through the interactive citation tool available on the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) website: https://ghdx.healthdata.org/.

Case Definitions

In accordance with the WHO definition, we defined WCBA as those aged 15 to 49 years, in order to more accurately reflect the biological, social, and cognitive development of this population. In GBD 2021, the causes of diseases are categorized into four levels, with HIV and other STIs classified as third-level causes under the category of communicable diseases. In this study, “other STIs” referred to non-HIV sexually transmitted infections classified in the GBD 2021 cause hierarchy, including syphilis, chlamydial infection, gonococcal infection, trichomoniasis, genital herpes, and other specified sexually transmitted infections. Table S1 provides the definitions and corresponding ninth and tenth revision International Classification of Diseases codes.

Characteristics

GBD 2021 provides data on prevalence, incidence, mortality, DALY, the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs) from 1990 to 2021, organized by cause, sex, age and location. In this study, we obtained data on the prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALY of HIV and STIs for individuals aged 15–49 years from the GBD 2021. DALYs in this study referred to cause-specific DALYs due to HIV/AIDS and other STIs. The age range of 15–49 years is divided into the following five-year groups: 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, and 45–49 years. Regions were categorized according to both WHO classifications and SDI-defined regions. The WHO regions consist of African region, the Eastern Mediterranean, European region, the Americas, South-East Asia region, and the Western Pacific region. The SDI was used to assess the social and economic conditions affecting health outcomes, particularly regarding young women’s fertility, education, and income. It was calculated as the geometric mean of the total fertility rate for those under 25, average years of education for individuals aged 15 and older, and lag-distributed income per capita. This index classifies countries into five regions: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high.

Statistical Analysis

For GBD-derived estimates, including counts and rates of prevalence, incidence, deaths, and DALYs of HIV/AIDS and other STIs, 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were calculated as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 500 posterior draws4. To quantify the temporal trends in prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALY rates among WCBA from 1990 to 2021, we employed the National Cancer Institute’s Joinpoint regression program (version 5.2.0) to calculate the AAPCs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), stratified by age subgroups, geographical location, and SDI. Statistical significance was determined at an α level of P <0.05, and all hypotheses were tested as two-sided. Other statistical analyses and visualizations were carried out using R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Global Divergent Burden Profiles of HIV/AIDS and Other STIs Among WCBA

In 2021, the GBD parent cause category of HIV/AIDS and other STIs was estimated to account for 287,185,456 incident cases, 550,753,107 prevalent cases, 267,911 deaths, and 17,079,099 DALYs among WCBA worldwide. However, the distribution of burden differed substantially between HIV/AIDS and other STIs. HIV/AIDS accounted for 670,804 incident cases and 16,497,874 prevalent cases in 2021. Deaths due to HIV/AIDS increased during the early study period and then declined, particularly after the mid-2000s, as shown by the joinpoint regression results. In 2021, HIV/AIDS contributed 265,696 deaths and 16,378,796 DALYs among WCBA. By contrast, other STIs accounted for the vast majority of incident and prevalent cases, with 286,514,651 incident cases and 544,454,239 prevalent cases, but contributed comparatively fewer deaths and DALYs, with 2,215 deaths and 700,303 DALYs.

From 1990 to 2021, the global incidence trends also differed between HIV/AIDS and other STIs. The incidence rate of HIV/AIDS decreased, with an AAPC of −2.13% (95% CI: −2.23 to −2.04), whereas the incidence rate of other STIs increased slightly, with an AAPC of 0.19% (95% CI: 0.18 to 0.20). In contrast, the DALY rate of HIV/AIDS increased over the full study period, with an AAPC of 1.63% (95% CI: 1.56 to 1.74). Although deaths due to other STIs remained relatively low and showed an overall declining trend, the DALYs of other STIs increased again from 2015 to 2021. These findings indicate a clear divergence in burden profiles: other STIs dominated the incident and prevalent burden, whereas HIV/AIDS remained the major contributor to mortality and severe health loss among WCBA (Tables 1 and 2, S2).

Temporal Trends and Recent Rebound of Other STIs Among WCBA

In 2021, other STIs accounted for a substantial non-fatal burden among WCBA, with 544,454,239 prevalent cases and 286,514,651 incident cases worldwide. Chlamydial infection and trichomoniasis accounted for the highest proportions of other STI cases. From 1990 to 2021, the incidence rate of other STIs increased slightly, with an AAPC of 0.19% (95% CI: 0.18 to 0.20). Among the specific infections included under other STIs, trichomoniasis showed a notable upward trend in incidence, whereas the incidence of other STI categories remained relatively stable.

In contrast to the large incidence and prevalence burden, deaths due to other STIs remained low. In 2021, other STIs caused 2,215 deaths among WCBA and showed an overall declining mortality trend. However, the global DALYs attributable to other STIs reached 700,303 in 2021 and increased again from 2015 to 2021, with an AAPC of 1.02% (95% CI: 0.97 to 1.07). These findings suggest that although fatal outcomes due to other STIs were limited, their non-fatal health loss remained substantial and showed a recent rebound, highlighting the need for continued screening, diagnosis, and treatment among WCBA (Figure 1).

Age-Specific Burden Profiles of HIV/AIDS and Other STIs Among WCBA

Age-specific patterns differed between HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA. In 2021, the highest DALY rate of HIV/AIDS occurred among women aged 30–34 years, whereas the highest DALY rate of other STIs occurred among women aged 40–44 years. From 1990 to 2021, DALY rates for HIV/AIDS increased across all WCBA age groups, with the most pronounced increase observed among women aged 40–44 years (AAPC = 3.20%, 95% CI: 3.04 to 3.41). By contrast, most WCBA age groups showed small decreases in DALY rates for other STIs, with the largest decrease observed among women aged 45–49 years (AAPC = −0.35%, 95% CI: −0.37 to −0.33).

The age pattern for incidence also differed between HIV/AIDS and other STIs. In 2021, the highest incidence rate of HIV/AIDS was observed among women aged 25–29 years, whereas the highest incidence rate of other STIs was observed among women aged 30–34 years. From 1990 to 2021, HIV/AIDS incidence decreased across all WCBA age groups, with the greatest decline among women aged 45–49 years. In contrast, the incidence of other STIs increased slightly in most WCBA age groups, with the largest increase observed among women aged 40–44 years (AAPC = 0.18%, 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.19). These findings suggest that HIV/AIDS and other STIs have distinct age-related burden profiles, supporting age-specific prevention and screening strategies among WCBA (Table 1 and Figure 2).

Regional and SDI-Specific Divergence in HIV/AIDS and Other STI Trends Among WCBA

Regional patterns varied substantially between HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA. From 1990 to 2021, the African region and the Region of the Americas were the only WHO regions showing declining DALY rates for HIV/AIDS. In contrast, the Eastern Mediterranean, European, South-East Asia, and Western Pacific regions experienced increasing HIV/AIDS DALY rates, with the largest increase observed in the Eastern Mediterranean region. For other STIs, DALY rates declined only in the African region and South-East Asia, whereas slight increases were observed in the Eastern Mediterranean, European, Region of the Americas, and Western Pacific regions, with the largest increase occurring in the Region of the Americas.

The incidence trends also differed by region. The African region was the only WHO region with decreasing HIV/AIDS incidence among WCBA from 1990 to 2021. During the same period, HIV/AIDS incidence increased sharply in the Eastern Mediterranean, European, and Western Pacific regions. For other STIs, the African region also showed a decreasing incidence trend, whereas the Region of the Americas and Western Pacific region showed slight increases. Across SDI regions, the combined DALY rate of HIV/AIDS and other STIs increased in the high-middle, middle, and low-middle SDI regions, but decreased in the high and low SDI regions. When HIV/AIDS and other STIs were examined separately, HIV/AIDS showed increasing DALY rates in the high-middle, middle, and low-middle SDI regions, whereas other STIs increased mainly in the high-middle and middle SDI regions, remained stable in the high-SDI region, and decreased in the low-middle and low-SDI regions. For the combined incidence rate of HIV/AIDS and other STIs, low-SDI countries were the only SDI group showing a decreasing trend; separately, HIV/AIDS incidence declined in both low-middle and low SDI regions, whereas other STI incidence declined only in the low-SDI region. These findings indicate that the burden and trends of HIV/AIDS and other STIs differed across WHO regions and SDI levels, suggesting the need for region-specific prevention strategies (Figure 3).

National-Level Trends in HIV/AIDS and Other STIs Among WCBA

At the national level, countries were highlighted according to the largest increases and decreases in AAPC for incidence and DALY rates from 1990 to 2021. For HIV/AIDS incidence among WCBA, Pakistan showed the largest increase, with an AAPC of 30.41% (95% CI: 29.11 to 32.50), whereas Burundi showed the largest decrease, with an AAPC of −14.48% (95% CI: −14.69 to −14.28). For other STIs, Vietnam had the largest increase in incidence (AAPC = 0.40%, 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.42), while Turkey showed the largest decrease (AAPC = −0.79%, 95% CI: −0.88 to −0.69).

Similar national heterogeneity was observed for DALY rates. Pakistan had the greatest increase in HIV/AIDS-related DALY rate among WCBA, with an AAPC of 38.18% (95% CI: 37.53 to 38.78), whereas Burkina Faso showed the largest decrease (AAPC = −7.99%, 95% CI: −8.16 to −7.78). For other STIs, Italy had the largest increase in DALY rate (AAPC = 1.25%, 95% CI: 1.20 to 1.29), while Ethiopia had the largest decrease (AAPC = −2.32%, 95% CI: −2.34 to −2.29). These findings indicate substantial country-level heterogeneity in HIV/AIDS and other STI trends among WCBA, supporting the need for country-specific prevention and control strategies (Table S3 and Table S4 and Figure 4 and Figure S1).

Discussion

This study showed that HIV/AIDS and other STIs had divergent burden profiles among women of child-bearing age (WCBA) from 1990 to 2021. Although the global incidence rate of HIV/AIDS declined during the study period, HIV/AIDS remained the dominant contributor to deaths and DALYs. By contrast, other STIs accounted for the vast majority of incident and prevalent cases, but contributed comparatively fewer deaths and DALYs. Age-specific, regional, SDI-specific, and national patterns further indicated that HIV/AIDS and other STIs did not change in parallel. These findings suggest that progress in HIV/AIDS control does not necessarily reflect comparable progress in the broader control of STIs among WCBA.

Since 1990, new HIV/AIDS cases have declined by 25.1%.17 This declining incidence may reflect the benefits of antiretroviral therapy (ART) following its broader availability,18–20 and the global burden of HIV/AIDS has also decreased accordingly. Increasing treatment coverage and preventive strategies, particularly advances in ART and the implementation of treatment-as-prevention programmes,21 reduced the HIV/AIDS burden globally in 2021. In this regard, substantial progress has been made through transformative programs supported by the Global Fund to Fight AIDS.21 These programs have worked closely with host-country governments to achieve a marked decrease in new HIV infections and an increase in the number of people living with HIV accessing ART and achieving viral suppression. Voluntary counseling and testing (VCT), widely considered a critical gateway to HIV prevention and treatment, has been implemented in many countries. As a result, by 2018, approximately 38 million people living with HIV had access to ART.22

HIV/AIDS and other STIs differ not only in magnitude but also in the type of health burden they generate. Worldwide, about half of those living with HIV and AIDS are women, and the majority of these WCBA,23 among these women, those of reproductive age are at the highest risk of HIV infection. Prevalence of HIV is even higher among young women in antenatal care.23 Given safety concerns for birth defects in WCBA potential, preventive measures are particularly important for reducing HIV mortality, especially among women of child-bearing age, as HIV prevalence remains the primary contributor to high mortality rates in this population. Pooled data from 11 nationally representative surveys show that non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor pretreatment HIV drug resistance in women is nearly twice that in men, exceeding 10% in 8 of 11 countries monitored.24 Better ART coverage among women than men is likely due to underlying factors, such as different health-seeking behaviours and the fact that women have multiple entry points to care, including antenatal clinics and prevention of mother-to-child transmission programmes.25

Between 1990 and 2021, the overall decrease in life expectancy of 4.3 years in southern sub-Saharan Africa included a reduction of 2.4 years attributable to HIV/AIDS.7 In 2021, however, HIV/AIDS remained among the top ten leading causes of DALYs in 39 countries and territories and was the leading cause of DALYs in Congo (Brazzaville), Equatorial Guinea, Djibouti, Malawi, Mozambique, Zambia, Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho, and South Africa.5 Global treatment coverage is not increasing at a sufficient rate to meet the joint The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) targets of diagnosing 95% of people living with HIV, providing treatment to 95% of those diagnosed, and achieving viral suppression in 95% of those on treatment by 2030.5,26 In South Africa, the HIV infection rate places the country among the top ten worldwide in terms of multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis cases, with a mortality rate exceeding 20%.27–29 It has also been reported that HIV disease now accounts for 90% of all deaths among child-bearing urban Rwandan women.30 UNAIDS estimates that substantial increases in global funding are required to remain on course to end HIV/AIDS as a public health threat by 2030.31 Progress to slow down transmission, increase treatment rates, and prevent HIV-related deaths is urgently required.

This study focuses on five major STIs, including syphilis, chlamydial infection, gonococcal infection, trichomoniasis, and genital herpes. At the global level, women in Africa bear the highest prevalence of gonorrhea and trichomoniasis.11 In the present study, chlamydial infection and trichomoniasis accounted for large proportions of other STIs cases, with trichomoniasis showing a notable upward trend in incidence. Although deaths due to other STIs remained low and generally declined, DALYs attributable to other STIs increased again from 2015 to 2021. In contrast to HIV/AIDS, other STIs generated a much larger incidence and prevalence burden, suggesting persistent transmission and a large reservoir of infection. Regarding coinfection with two or more STIs, the prevalence among women was 6% in Madagascar, 19% in Tanzania, and 14% in South Africa, respectively.8,32,33

The high burden of other STIs among WCBA may be partly explained by low education,34 as women with lower educational attainment may face difficulties finding employment or have low-paying jobs, potentially leading them to sell sex for supplementary income. Other contributing factors include a higher number of sexual partners and biological factors such as cervical ectopy.35 Additionally, a substantial proportion of infected WCBA remain undiagnosed and untreated because they do not present with symptoms defined by the WHO syndromic management guidelines, nearly 40% of women with other STIs are asymptomatic according to these criteria.8 Consequently, these asymptomatic cases risk developing complications and contribute to ongoing transmission of other STIs.

Therefore, evaluating HIV/AIDS and other STIs together may obscure their distinct public health implications. HIV/AIDS requires ongoing efforts to prevent mortality and manage disease progression, whereas other STIs call for strengthened screening, diagnosis, treatment, and transmission control. To help reduce the persistent non-fatal burden of other STIs, it is important to strengthen routine screening, improve access to point-of-care testing, ensure timely treatment, and expand partner notification.

The burden of HIV/AIDS and other STIs also differed across age groups. The present study found that HIV/AIDS incidence peaked among women aged 25–29 years, whereas other STIs incidence peaked among women aged 30–34 years. DALY rates also showed different age profiles, with the highest HIV/AIDS DALY rate observed among women aged 30–34 years and the highest other STIs DALY rate observed among women aged 40–44 years. Because other STIs are often asymptomatic, screening remains crucial for early detection. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends annual chlamydia screening for all sexually active women under 25 years of age and for older women with risk factors.36 Screening has been shown to be effective in controlling the transmission of other STIs and preventing reproductive tract diseases in women, underscoring the urgency of developing screening guidelines to improve screening coverage among women.37–39 Furthermore, it has been reported that younger women (aged 15–19 years) were less likely to use modern contraceptives compared with older women (aged 35–39 years).40 This may be because older women tend to have more children and are more motivated to limit or space their pregnancies, whereas younger women may have few or no children.

Regional and SDI-specific patterns further showed that HIV/AIDS and other STIs control progressed unevenly across settings. The African region and low-SDI countries continued to experience a high burden, although some indicators improved over time. In contrast, several regions showed increasing HIV/AIDS or other STIs rates, suggesting that gains in HIV/AIDS control have not been uniformly translated into broader STIs prevention. In most African countries, family planning is integrated into comprehensive healthcare.41 In sub-Saharan Africa, which bears the highest burden of STIs worldwide, reducing STIs incidence remains challenging, partly due to limited diagnostic capacity in many low-income countries.42 Additionally, inconsistent condom use further complicates STIs control efforts. Other significant factors include condom availability, depression, education level, having two or more nonpaying clients, client volume, violence, and police harassment.43

The main strength of this study is that it used GBD 2021 estimates to compare the burden and trends of HIV/AIDS and other STIs among WCBA across global, regional, SDI-specific, age-specific, and national levels. Rather than considering STIs as a single homogeneous group, this study highlighted the divergent burden profiles of HIV/AIDS and other STIs, distinguishing transmission-related burden from mortality and severe health loss. These findings may help inform more targeted prevention strategies for WCBA.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the estimates were derived from GBD 2021 and are therefore affected by the availability and quality of primary data across countries and regions. In settings with limited surveillance, underdiagnosis and underreporting of STIs may have influenced the estimates. Second, diagnostic capacity, screening practices, and reporting systems differ substantially across countries, which may affect comparability across regions and countries. Third, this study focused on WCBA aged 15–49 years and therefore should not be generalized to all females or to males. Fourth, the analysis was descriptive and ecological, and causal interpretations should be avoided. Finally, other STIs were mainly analyzed as an aggregate group in the main results, which may have obscured heterogeneity across individual infections such as syphilis, chlamydial infection, gonococcal infection, trichomoniasis, and genital herpes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, HIV/AIDS and other STIs showed divergent burden profiles among WCBA from 1990 to 2021. HIV/AIDS incidence declined globally but remained the dominant contributor to STI-related deaths and DALYs, whereas other STIs accounted for most incident and prevalent cases and showed persistent or increasing burdens in several settings. These findings indicate that progress in HIV/AIDS control should not be interpreted as overall STI control. Integrated, age-specific, region-specific, and country-specific strategies that combine HIV prevention and treatment with broader STIs screening, diagnosis, and management are needed to reduce the burden among WCBA.
