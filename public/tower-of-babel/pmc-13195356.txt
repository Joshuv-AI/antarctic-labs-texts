Aflatoxin health risk in the feed source: Integrated strategies for feed safety and sustainable One Health
Authors: Seyed Davood Sharifi, Seyed Soheil Ghaemmaghami, Hasan Rouhanipour, Abouzr Najafi, Hossein Manafi Rasi, Reza Baeelashaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAflatoxin contamination in animal feed represents a critical challenge with profound implications for poultry and livestock health, food safety, and public welfare. Primarily produced by Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus, these highly toxic secondary metabolites, especially aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), contaminate agricultural crops during pre-harvest, harvest, and storage phases under warm and humid conditions. Globally, aflatoxin contamination levels in animal feeds vary considerably depending on climatic conditions and storage practices. Regulatory limits generally range from 20 to 300 μg/kg depending on the target animal species, field concentrations in highly susceptible regions often exceed these safety thresholds, posing substantial risks to animal health and food safety. Once ingested, aflatoxins lead to severe hepatic toxicity, immunosuppression, reproductive disorders, and increased susceptibility to infectious diseases in animals. Furthermore, the carry-over of carcinogenic AFB1 into animal-derived products, particularly milk, meat, and eggs, presents a severe public health threat, while causing substantial economic losses through decreased productivity and trade restrictions. Understanding the environmental conditions favoring aflatoxigenic fungi is critical for developing effective mitigation. Preventive measures including timely harvesting, adequate drying, optimized storage conditions, and biological interventions (e.g., non-toxigenic Aspergillus strains) are essential to diminish contamination risk. Coupled with advanced diagnostic technologies, such as immunoassays and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), alongside stringent regulatory surveillance, these interventions minimize exposure. This review adopts a One Health perspective to address the multifactorial complexity of aflatoxin contamination, encompassing fungal ecology, innovations in detection and detoxification methods, and socioeconomic approaches aimed at sustainable feed management.Keywords: Aflatoxins, Aspergillus flavus, Poultry, Mycotoxins

Introduction

Aflatoxins are highly potent polyketide mycotoxins synthesized as secondary metabolites by certain species of Aspergillus, predominantly within the Aspergillus section Flavi [1]. Their occurrence in food, feed, and other agricultural commodities presents significant economic challenges and serious health risks. Notably, aflatoxins possess potent carcinogenic properties can directly alter the molecular architecture of DNA, thereby elevating the risk of liver cancer and other adverse health effects [2], [3]. In addition, exposure to these toxins has been associated with reproductive anomalies, including fetal developmental defects and spontaneous abortions, as well as immunosuppression [4]. As detailed in Table 1, previous studies such as El-Sayed et al. [5] have categorized various mycotoxins including aflatoxins, ochratoxin A, fumonisins, trichothecenes, and zearalenone alongside their specific pathological impacts on human and animal health. For instance, while ochratoxin A is predominantly associated with nephrotoxicity, aflatoxins are definitively linked to severe hepatocarcinogenesis.

The contamination of feed with aflatoxins has emerged as a critical public health concern. As highlighted by Ghaemmaghami [6], mycotoxins in feed are classified as chemical hazards that directly affect livestock performance and indirectly contribute to the transfer of toxic residues into the human food supply. The rapid expansion of the livestock industry has increased the demand for feed ingredients, thereby amplifying production, storage, and transportation volumes and with them, the likelihood of fungal contamination. Particularly, A. flavus and A. parasiticus are well-recognized producers of aflatoxins that can contaminate a wide range of agricultural commodities such as maize, groundnuts, cottonseed, and milk [7], [8]. Contaminated feed can lead to both acute and chronic aflatoxicosis in livestock, manifesting as immune suppression, liver dysfunction, and impaired growth, which not only diminish their productivity but also pose a risk to human health through the food chain [9], [10]. Although other mycotoxins such as fumonisins, ochratoxins, trichothecenes, and zearalenone also threaten feed safety [11], aflatoxins remain among the most critical due to their severe toxicological profile. A study by Rodrigues et al. [12] underscore the importance of systematic sampling at farms and feed mill production sites to accurately assess the prevalence and risks of mycotoxin contamination across diverse geographic regions.

Given the multifaceted impact of aflatoxin contamination on animal health, public safety, and economic stability, there is an urgent need for integrated management strategies. This paper critically examines the challenges associated with aflatoxin management within the feed chain, emphasizing its implications for feed safety and the broader One Health framework. By synthesizing current research, technological advances, and regulatory practices, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview of existing obstacles and propose strategic recommendations to mitigate aflatoxin risks. Ultimately, this integrated approach aims to bridge the gap between scientific research and practical application, thereby enhancing plant safety, feed safety, and health outcomes for both animals and humans.

Despite the extensive body of literature on aflatoxins, a significant research gap remains in synthesizing existing findings through a truly integrated perspective. Previous reviews have largely focused on isolated aspects of the aflatoxin issue, such as analytical detection methods, specific mitigation strategies, regulatory standards, or health hazards in humans or individual livestock species. Only a limited number of studies have attempted to comprehensively link these technical and practical dimensions to their primary source, animal feed. The novelty of the present study lies in its holistic “farm-to-fork” and One Health perspective. Rather than addressing commonly discussed topics in isolation, this review integrates them to illustrate how aflatoxin risks originate at the feed source and subsequently propagate along the food chain. By linking primary feed contamination to animal toxicity and subsequent human health hazards through carry-over effects, and by highlighting the need for coordinated detection, regulatory control, and mitigation beginning at the feed level, this review offers a unified framework that distinguishes it from previous, more fragmented reviews.

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of aflatoxin contamination, its multifaceted impacts, and integrated management strategies through the lens of the One Health framework. The poultry sector is not only a major source of global protein but is also exceptionally vulnerable to the economic and health impacts of aflatoxins, making it an ideal model for examining the complexities of mycotoxin control. The principles, contamination pathways, and control strategies discussed herein are therefore not confined to poultry alone. Rather, we use poultry as a focal point to explore issues that resonate across the entire agri-food system, with direct implications for other key sectors such as dairy (milk), beef, and pork production [11]. By adopting this approach, we aim to demonstrate the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, highlighting the necessity of holistic and collaborative solutions for safeguarding the global food supply.

Aflatoxigenic fungi in animal feed

Although the fungal kingdom is highly diverse, aflatoxin contamination in animal feed is mainly associated with a limited number of species within the genus Aspergillus, particularly A. flavus and A. parasiticus. These saprophytic fungi frequently colonize agricultural commodities such as maize, peanuts, and cottonseed, which are major ingredients of livestock and poultry feed. Their growth and aflatoxin production are strongly influenced by environmental factors, especially high temperature and relative humidity (RH), as well as crop damage and poor storage conditions [13]. Under such circumstances, these fungi produce aflatoxins as secondary metabolites, leading to contamination of feed and potential carry-over into animal-derived food products [14].

Genus Aspergillus

The genus Aspergillus belongs to the phylum Ascomycota and comprises a large number of species widely distributed in soil and organic substrates. Current taxonomic updates recognize multiple subgenera and sections within the genus, reflecting its extensive phylogenetic diversity [15], [16]. While many Aspergillus species play beneficial roles in biotechnology and food fermentation, others are associated with food spoilage and mycotoxin production, making them relevant to feed safety and animal health [17].

Flavus group

Members of the Aspergillus flavus group are of particular importance because several species are capable of producing aflatoxins. These fungi are commonly found in soil and on decaying plant material. Within this group, A. flavus, A. parasiticus, and A. nomius are recognized as the primary aflatoxin producers, whereas closely related species such as A. oryzae and A. sojae are widely used in food fermentation [17].

Aspergillus flavus

Aspergillus flavus is one of the most widespread aflatoxin-producing fungi and is frequently associated with contamination of agricultural commodities both before harvest and during storage. The species is commonly found in soil and plant debris and is considered one of the major contributors to aflatoxin contamination in feed ingredients such as maize and oilseeds. In addition to its relevance to feed safety, A. flavus is also recognized as an opportunistic pathogen in animals and humans [13], [18].

Aspergillus flavus biosynthesis

While the morphological characteristics of A. flavus facilitate its survival and dissemination in agricultural environments, its primary threat to feed safety arises from its metabolic capabilities. Under specific environmental stressors, such as fluctuations in temperature and RH, the fungus undergoes a critical shift from primary vegetative growth to secondary metabolism, initiating the production of toxic compounds. Understanding this transition is essential, as it leads directly to the complex enzymatic pathways responsible for toxin formation. Among the various secondary metabolites produced during this process, aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is considered the most toxic and the most potent naturally occurring hepatocarcinogen. In addition to aflatoxins, Aspergillus flavus produces several other toxic secondary metabolites, including sterigmatocystin, cyclopiazonic acid, aflatrem, gliotoxin, and aspergillic acid [17]. The biosynthesis of aflatoxins is a complex process that involves more than 18 enzymatic reactions, through which acetyl-CoA is ultimately converted into the final aflatoxin products, namely AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2. However, in aflatoxin-producing fungi, the position and location of aflatoxin biosynthesis genes are slightly different. Because the biosynthetic pathway of aflatoxins involves numerous and diverse enzymatic steps, it represents an attractive target for the development of specific inhibitors, particularly natural compounds derived from plants and bacteria [19].

Aspergillosis and aflatoxicosis

Aspergillosis is a respiratory disease, an infection that most usually is found in turkey, chicken, human. Aspergillosis is a term of fungal disease caused by the genus Aspergillus specifically the species Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus fumigatus [20]. Respiratory disease by aspergillosis in commercial poultry manifests in two forms, acute and chronic. It appears acutely for young birds with a very high mortality rate occurring within the first few days, and newly hatched chicks are particularly susceptible to the disease. The infection generally is carried out through inhalation of spore-filled dust from infected dusty range areas. Aspergillosis frequently results from Aspergillus fumigatus [21].

Aflatoxicosis in birds is an intoxication resulting from the ingestion of aflatoxins produced by toxigenic Aspergillus species (e.g., Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus parasiticus), and primarily occurs in tropical and subtropical climates. In broiler chickens, aflatoxicosis predominantly manifests in a chronic form, resulting in stunted growth, body weight loss, increased feed conversion ratio, paralysis, and lameness. In layer hens and breeder hens, aflatoxicosis is consistently associated with symptoms such as reduced production, smaller egg size and weight, and decreased feed intake and aflatoxicosis, in breeder hens, is observed by reduction hatchability of eggs [22].

State of knowledge

The current state of knowledge regarding aflatoxins in animal feed has advanced significantly, shifting from basic toxicological profiling to predictive, proactive, and sustainable management under a One Health framework. Recent literature highlights several critical areas of advancement. First, contemporary research strongly emphasizes the escalating impact of climate change, which has altered the geographical distribution of Aspergillus species, resulting in unprecedented aflatoxin contamination in previously temperate regions and necessitating predictive modeling for food/feed safety [23]. In terms of diagnostics, there has been a notable transition towards rapid, on-site detection technologies. Recent studies focus on the integration of nanomaterial-based biosensors, portable spectroscopy, and Internet of Things (IoT) devices for real-time monitoring of food/feed storage conditions [24]. Furthermore, modern mitigation research has moved beyond conventional chemical binders. The latest advancements focus on biological detoxification such as the use of specific recombinant enzymes, microbial degradation, and targeted probiotics. Recent papers demonstrate that these biological agents can safely neutralize aflatoxins in the gastrointestinal tract of livestock without compromising nutrient absorption, offering a more sustainable approach [25]. Contemporary research heavily underscores the precise carry-over dynamics (e.g., from contaminated feed to milk as aflatoxin M1 (AFM1) or into meat products) utilizing advanced toxicokinetic modeling [26]. Together, these recent breakthroughs highlight a paradigm shift towards integrated, technology-driven feed safety strategies rather than mere end-product testing. Several studies have reported aflatoxin contamination in various animal feed ingredients, such as maize, groundnuts, cottonseed, and soybean meal [27]. It is evident from studies that contamination levels vary depending on prevailing environmental conditions and different storage practices, among other factors. For example, it is estimated that more than 60% of tested samples of maize and groundnuts in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia exceed the safety threshold for aflatoxins, consequently posing a great threat to the health of livestock and humans [7]. Ghaemmaghami et al. [22] found aflatoxins at all stages of poultry feed processing, with their levels within the limits acceptable according to Iranian standards. However, the levels of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1, G2) and total aflatoxins in finished feeds were higher compared to the feed ingredients, with P < 0.05, hence creating poultry health risks. This calls for preventive measures, especially when conditions are not conducive to impede fungal proliferation during feed material production and storage.

Global aflatoxin concerns: A One Health perspective

Mycotoxin contamination of food and feed represents a major global concern, affecting crop safety, animal health, and human well-being. According to estimates by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), approximately 25% of global cropland is affected by aflatoxin contamination, leading to substantial economic losses due to the rejection of contaminated products and restricted market access [28]. In livestock, exposure to aflatoxins is associated with several adverse effects, including growth retardation, immunosuppression, and hepatotoxicity [29]. The public health risks associated with aflatoxins are further amplified by their presence in livestock feed, which enables the transfer of these toxins into animal-derived food products such as milk, meat, and eggs. Notably, when ingested by dairy cattle, AFB1 is metabolized into AFM1 and aflatoxin M2 (AFM2), which are excreted into milk. This metabolic carry-over significantly increases the risk of human exposure, particularly among vulnerable populations such as infants and young children, posing severe toxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic risks [30], [31]. The aflatoxin transmission cycle within the food chain has a profound impact on humans, animals, and the environment [32].

Aflatoxins hazards in human health

Human exposure to aflatoxins constitutes a significant public health threat, primarily facilitated by the entry of contaminated animal feeds into the human food chain [33]. Prolonged consumption of such contaminated foods has been directly linked to severe health outcomes, including childhood stunting, immune dysfunction, and long-term developmental impairments [34]. The burden disproportionately affects developing regions, where chronic dietary exposure is strongly associated with elevated rates of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). AFB1 is classified as a Group 1 human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). A particularly concerning aspect of its carcinogenicity is the synergistic interaction with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, which substantially elevates the risk of liver cancer in endemic populations [35]. Their widespread occurrence, particularly in regions with weak regulatory enforcement, highlights the urgent need for enhanced surveillance systems and comprehensive public health interventions [1]. Furthermore, prioritizing aflatoxin mitigation is well justified given its widespread occurrence, associated ecological risks, and far-reaching implications for international trade [36].

Aflatoxins hazards in feed: impacts on animal health and production

Post-harvest commodities such as maize are especially susceptible to contamination, making the reduction of aflatoxin levels essential to safeguard feed and grain quality. As previously noted, when livestock consume contaminated feed, residues are transferred to animal-derived food products [29], which compromises food safety standards and consumer confidence [37]. This contamination is not limited to terrestrial feed, a high prevalence of aflatoxins has also been detected in fish feed, underscoring its threat to the entire animal production chain [38]. In poultry, AFB1 disrupts metabolic processes and impairs overall productivity, and its occurrence is widely documented in maize and livestock feeds. Crops cultivated in warm climates are especially susceptible to A. flavus, highlighting the need for continuous mycological surveillance and region-specific mitigation strategies [18]. The detrimental effects in broiler and layer poultry include compromised immune function [39] and poor feed conversion efficiency [40]. Prolonged exposure leads to serious hepatic conditions, including liver fibrosis. As previously discussed, a critical concern is the carry-over of these toxins into the human food supply. Several investigations have reported concerning levels of AFM1 in commercially processed dairy products, including pasteurized and ultra-high temperature (UHT) treated milk samples, from multiple urban centers [41]. The growing global demand for feed ingredients has increased the risk of fungal contamination during storage and transportation [42]. Moreover, changing climatic conditions continue to exacerbate the risk of AFB₁ contamination in feed, making it a persistent and pressing concern [43].

A comprehensive assessment of aflatoxicosis in livestock and poultry reveals profound, multi-systemic disruptions that severely compromise production parameters. The fundamental mechanism of this toxicity originates in the liver, where Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes into the highly reactive AFB1–8, 9-epoxide. This electrophilic intermediate covalently binds to cellular macromolecules, particularly DNA and RNA, leading to the severe inhibition of protein synthesis, disrupted lipid metabolism, and the induction of overwhelming oxidative stress, ultimately culminating in hepatocellular carcinoma [12], [22].

Growth performance and feed conversion efficiency

The disruption of hepatic function directly translates into impaired nutrient metabolism and malabsorption. Quantitatively, dietary exposure to even moderate levels of AFB1 (e.g., 100–300 μg/kg) can reduce daily weight gain by 5% to 15% in sensitive species such as poultry and swine [23], [27]. Mechanistically, this growth retardation is compounded by aflatoxin-induced anorexia, reduced pancreatic enzyme secretion, and structural damage to the intestinal microvilli, which collectively result in a drastically poor Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) and significant economic losses.

Immune function

Immunosuppression is a critical, yet often under-quantified, consequence of aflatoxin ingestion. Aflatoxins induce the atrophy of primary lymphoid organs, including the bursa of fabricius in poultry, the thymus, and the spleen. At the cellular level, AFB1 suppresses macrophage phagocytosis, inhibits T-lymphocyte proliferation, and reduces the systemic production of immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG) [44]. Consequently, chronic exposure leads to a higher incidence of secondary opportunistic infections and significantly reduces the efficacy of routine vaccination programs.

Reproductive performance and product quality

The impact of aflatoxins on reproduction involves complex endocrine disruptions. In poultry, AFB1 interferes with hepatic vitellogenesis (yolk precursor synthesis), leading to quantifiable drops in egg production (often exceeding 10% reduction) and poor hatchability [23]. In mammalian livestock, oxidative stress induced by aflatoxins damages ovarian and testicular tissues, altering steroidogenesis. This manifests as decreased conception rates, disrupted estrous cycles, and increased incidences of embryonic mortality or abortion. Beyond animal health, contaminated feed directly impacts human food safety through the carry-over effect. The most strictly regulated pathway is the biotransformation of AFB1 to AFM1 in the mammary glands of dairy cows. Quantitatively, the carry-over rate into milk typically ranges from 1% to 6%, depending on the animal's physiological state and milk yield [14]. Furthermore, continuous exposure results in the bioaccumulation of AFB1 and its metabolites in the liver, muscle tissues (meat), and eggs, negatively altering their biochemical profile, reducing nutritional quality, and posing a direct carcinogenic risk to downstream consumers [30].

Aflatoxins hazards in ecosystem health

Shifts in the geographical distribution and intensity of food and feed contamination, driven by climate variability, pose significant threats to global food security. Key environmental determinants such as temperature, precipitation patterns, and extreme weather events critically influence fungal proliferation and secondary metabolite production [45]. Elucidating the interactions between these environmental stressors and the molecular pathways governing mycotoxin biosynthesis is vital for informing adaptive crop protection strategies [46]. Climate change is anticipated to intensify ecological niches favorable for the growth of Aspergillus species and the subsequent biosynthesis of aflatoxins [47], [48]. In response to these emerging climatic threats, the implementation of adaptive agricultural strategies, including climate-resilient crop varieties and optimized post-harvest handling, is imperative to curtail contamination risks [49].

Integrated One Health management strategies

Effectively addressing the multifaceted challenges posed by aflatoxins requires an integrated approach that bridges human, animal, and environmental domains [28]. A One Health perspective emphasizes that mitigation strategies cannot be implemented in isolation; rather, they must encompass pre-harvest agricultural practices, post-harvest storage innovations, rigorous feed and food safety monitoring, and public health interventions [9], [50]. Farm fungal contamination

Aflatoxin contamination is influenced by a multitude of environmental, agronomic, and storage-related factors occurring both before and after harvest [50].

Pre-harvest factors

Pre-harvest factors include climatic conditions such as high temperature, drought stress, and elevated humidity, and periods of drought or excessive rainfall can create optimal conditions for the growth of aflatoxigenic fungi like A. flavus and A. parasiticus [45]. Additionally, poor agronomic practices, including inadequate irrigation management, lack of crop rotation, insect damage, poor soil fertility, and pest infestation, can increase plant susceptibility to fungal colonization and subsequent toxin production. Insect damage can provide entry points for fungal colonization, increasing the likelihood of aflatoxin synthesis [51]. Climate change further exacerbates these conditions, leading to unpredictable contamination patterns.

Post-harvest factors

Post-harvest factors encompass improper drying and storage, delayed harvesting, and inadequate storage conditions, such as high moisture content and poor ventilation, which create conducive environments for aflatoxin biosynthesis during storage and transport. Inadequate drying methods, high ambient humidity, and poor storage infrastructure can maintain conditions favorable for fungal proliferation and subsequent mycotoxin production [52]. Effective control measures targeting both pre- and post-harvest stages are therefore essential to minimize aflatoxin contamination across the agricultural value chain. The transportation process may also contribute to contamination if storage conditions are not maintained. Fluctuations in temperature and moisture during transit can reactivate fungal growth in previously safe commodities. Addressing both pre-harvest and post-harvest factors requires integrated strategies that encompass improved agronomic practices, pest management, and investment in proper drying and storage technologies.

Aflatoxin detection methods

The accurate and rapid detection of aflatoxins is not only a regulatory necessity but also the fundamental first step that dictates the deployment of subsequent mitigation strategies. Reliable analytical methods are essential to map contamination severity, monitor the supply chain, and, crucially, evaluate the efficacy of any applied pre- or post-harvest interventions. The evolution of detection techniques has led to the development of a range of tools, from high-precision laboratory methods to field-deployable sensors, each serving specific roles in the broader aflatoxin management continuum. Improvements in analytical methodologies have enhanced the detection and quantification of mycotoxins in feed. High-Performance Thin-Layer Chromatography (HPTLC) and ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QqQ-MS/MS) have increased our ability to monitor aflatoxin levels and trace sources of contamination in feed formulation [53]. In addition, research studies by Ochieng et al. [39] and Nishimwe et al. [54] have identified the negative impact of aflatoxin-contaminated feed on livestock productivity and health, thus indicating the urgent need for effective monitoring and efficient control measures. Effective management of aflatoxin contamination begins with robust detection and monitoring strategies [55]. Advancements in aflatoxin detection techniques have significantly improved monitoring capabilities. Currently, several analytical methods are employed to detect aflatoxins in feed, each with distinct advantages and limitations [56].

Chromatographic techniques (HPLC, LC-MS/MS)

Chromatographic methods such as HPLC and liquid chromatography coupled with Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) are considered gold-standard approaches for the detection and quantification of mycotoxins. These techniques offer high sensitivity, specificity, and the ability to simultaneously analyze multiple mycotoxins within complex matrices. LC-MS/MS, in particular, enables structural confirmation of analyses and achieves detection limits in the parts-per-billion or even parts-per-trillion range. Despite their superior analytical performance, these methods require expensive instrumentation, highly trained personnel, labor-intensive sample preparation, and longer processing times. Consequently, their routine application may be limited in low-resource settings or high-throughput diagnostic laboratories.

Biosensors and rapid strip tests

Biosensors and lateral flow (rapid strip) assays represent emerging diagnostic technologies that enable on-site, real-time detection, offering potential for point-of-care applications. Advances in biosensor development, including optical and electrochemical sensors, have shown promise for real-time and in situ monitoring [57]. Although these technologies offer the potential for rapid detection, many remain at the prototype stage and require further validation under field conditions. Their deployment in routine monitoring is also challenged by issues of robustness, calibration requirements, and integration into existing quality assurance frameworks [58].

Immunoassay-based methods and overall challenges

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) and Lateral Flow Immunoassays (LFIA) are widely used techniques that offer rapid, cost-effective preliminary screening. Despite their practicality, these methods generally exhibit lower sensitivity and specificity compared to chromatographic techniques. They are susceptible to cross-reactivity and matrix effects [59], and their limited dynamic range restricts their utility in quantifying extreme concentrations of aflatoxins [60]. While advanced chromatographic analytical methods allow for precise quantification [53], their application is constrained by the need for advanced instrumentation, skilled personnel, and substantial operational costs. Despite notable technological progress, significant challenges persist in achieving affordability, accessibility, and real-time monitoring capabilities, particularly in resource-limited settings [61]. Collectively, the selection of an appropriate detection method involves balancing sensitivity, specificity, speed, and cost, while considering the complex matrix of feed samples and the necessity for routine monitoring [62].

Critical evaluation of aflatoxin detection methodologies

To move beyond a mere descriptive overview of aflatoxin detection methodologies, it is crucial to critically evaluate these techniques based on their Limit of Detection (LOD), sensitivity, economic feasibility, and field applicability. Chromatographic and spectrometric methods, such as HPLC and LC-MS/MS, remain the gold standards [53]. They offer exceptional sensitivity, multi-mycotoxin profiling capabilities, and extremely low LODs (often < 0.1 μg/kg for AFB1). However, their high capital and operational costs, coupled with the need for highly trained personnel and strict laboratory settings, severely restrict their on-site applicability. Conversely, while ELISA provides a cost-effective, high-throughput alternative with acceptable sensitivity for regulatory compliance, it is frequently plagued by matrix interferences in complex feed substrates, leading to potential false positives [59], [60]. For real-time, farm-level screening, Lateral Flow Assays (LFA) and emerging nanomaterial-based biosensors (e.g., aptasensors) represent a significant paradigm shift [57]. While traditionally limited by higher LODs compared to LC-MS/MS, recent advancements have substantially improved their sensitivity, offering a pragmatic and highly field-applicable balance for rapid decision-making in the feed supply chain, as summarized in Table 2 [56], [62].

A critical comparison of current aflatoxin detection methodologies reveals a distinct trade-off between analytical precision and practical field applicability. While chromatographic techniques, notably LC-MS/MS, provide unparalleled sensitivity, accuracy, and the capacity for multi-mycotoxin quantification, their routine application in resource-limited or remote agricultural settings is severely hindered by prohibitive equipment costs, complex sample preparation requirements, and the necessity for highly trained personnel [53]. Conversely, immunological assays such as ELISA and LFA offer rapid, cost-effective, and on-site screening capabilities. However, these methods frequently suffer from matrix interferences and generally exhibit higher limits of detection (LOD), rendering them more susceptible to cross-reactivity and false results [56], [60]. This technological disparity highlights a significant gap in current research, the critical need to develop and commercialize next-generation, nanomaterial-based biosensors integrated with AI that successfully merge the analytical reliability of mass spectrometry with the affordability and portability of LFA [57].

Spectroscopic approaches

In addition to traditional chromatographic and immunological methods, spectroscopic approaches particularly Near-Infrared (NIR), Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR), and Raman spectroscopy have emerged as highly promising tools for food and feed monitoring [40]. These techniques offer rapid, non-destructive, and chemical-free screening of large agricultural batches. Unlike LC-MS/MS or HPLC, which require extensive sample preparation, extraction solvents, and highly skilled personnel, spectroscopic methods are highly adaptable for real-time, on-site, and in-line monitoring in feed mills. However, their primary limitation remains their higher Limits of Detection (LODs). Consequently, they are often paired with advanced chemometric models and machine learning algorithms to enhance their accuracy, making them ideal for rapid mass screening rather than precise ultra-trace quantification [40].

Aflatoxin regulatory standards

Despite advances in detection technologies and mitigation strategies, their control remains a complex challenge. One of the primary impediments to effective aflatoxin management is the inconsistency in regulatory frameworks across different countries and regions. The variability in regulatory standards, coupled with challenges in their enforcement, constitutes a major obstacle in effective aflatoxin management [63]. While some nations maintain stringent permissible limits and robust monitoring systems, others lack the institutional capacity or political will to implement comprehensive control measures. This disparity not only hampers global harmonization of food safety practices but also facilitates the continued circulation of contaminated food products, particularly in international trade. Addressing this issue requires coordinated efforts to develop and enforce science-based, globally harmonized aflatoxin regulations, along with capacity-building initiatives in low- and middle-income countries. A key challenge in aflatoxin regulation is ensuring compliance among smallholder farmers and feed producers, particularly in regions with poor surveillance and limited technical support [64].

Variability in regulatory standards

Different regions and countries impose varying maximum allowable limits for aflatoxins in food and feed, guided by local risk assessments, economic priorities, dietary exposure levels, and public health policies. For example, the European Union enforces some of the strictest aflatoxin limits globally, 2 μg/kg for AFB1 in nuts intended for direct human consumption while several African and Asian countries adopt more lenient standards due to limited testing capacity and economic constraints [65]. This regulatory heterogeneity presents significant challenges for international trade, particularly for exporters in low- and middle-income countries who must navigate a complex landscape of divergent standards. It also places a disproportionate burden on producers operating across multiple jurisdictions, who may incur additional costs for testing, segregation, and certification to comply with varying import requirements [66].

Furthermore, the inconsistency in standards may lead to the redistribution of aflatoxin-contaminated commodities to markets with less stringent regulations, raising ethical concerns about food safety inequities. Considering these challenges, there have been increasing calls for international harmonization of aflatoxin regulations based on scientific risk assessment and global public health goals. Coordinated efforts under the guidance of international bodies such as the Codex Alimentarius Commission are essential to balance trade facilitation with consumer protection worldwide [67].

As discussed, the lack of harmonized international legislation leads to significant variability in regulatory frameworks. Different regions establish maximum permissible limits based on localized risk assessments, consumption patterns, and trade considerations [63]. Enforcement challenges

In many developing regions, the implementation of stringent regulatory standards for aflatoxin control is significantly hindered by infrastructural deficiencies, limited financial and technical resources for routine monitoring, and a shortage of trained personnel [37]. These systemic limitations undermine the capacity of regulatory bodies to enforce compliance, especially in rural or geographically remote areas, where logistical barriers further complicate monitoring and surveillance operations. Furthermore, even in countries where aflatoxin regulatory frameworks are officially in place, disparities in analytical methodologies, inconsistencies in testing protocols, and insufficient laboratory infrastructure often result in unreliable data and inconsistent enforcement [63]. These implementation gaps underscore the critical need for international and national investment in laboratory capacity, training programs, and harmonization of regulatory approaches to ensure equitable and effective aflatoxin risk management across all regions.

Need for harmonization

Harmonizing regulatory standards and establishing internationally accepted guidelines are essential for ensuring consistent and effective aflatoxin control measures across countries and regions. Regulatory divergence not only hampers enforcement but also creates barriers to trade and undermines public health protection. To address these challenges, enhanced collaboration among regulatory authorities, scientific institutions, and industry stakeholders is imperative. Such collaboration should focus on standardizing testing protocols, aligning risk assessment methodologies, and ensuring mutual recognition of analytical results. These harmonization efforts not only contribute to improved food safety and consumer protection but also promote equitable trade practices and foster economic stability in the global agri-food sector [37]. Therefore, effective aflatoxin control requires harmonized international regulatory frameworks, strengthened local enforcement capacities, and improved coordination among global stakeholders. Addressing disparities in standards, testing protocols, and infrastructure particularly in low- and middle-income countries, is essential for safeguarding public health, facilitating fair trade, and ensuring consistent food safety across borders.

Mitigation strategies

Effective aflatoxin management operates as an integrated continuum where the accurate detection methods and established regulatory standards directly inform the application of mitigation strategies. Detection provides the necessary data to trigger interventions, while continuous monitoring validates their success [68]. Once contamination levels are identified and assessed against safety thresholds, targeted mitigation interventions must be deployed to minimize health risks and economic losses. This integrated approach encompasses a broad spectrum of pre-harvest, post-harvest, and dietary strategies designed to prevent fungal colonization, inhibit toxin biosynthesis, or detoxify already contaminated commodities. By closely coupling real-time detection data with scalable mitigation tools, stakeholders can establish a more dynamic, responsive, and effective aflatoxin control framework [69].

Pre-harvest management strategies

Effective aflatoxin mitigation begins at the pre-harvest level through the application of evidence-based agricultural practices aimed at reducing fungal colonization and subsequent mycotoxin biosynthesis. Agronomic interventions serve as a foundational component of integrated aflatoxin control strategies and are particularly critical in regions vulnerable to environmental stressors that favor fungal proliferation [55]. A range of agronomic strategies can be implemented to mitigate mycotoxin contamination, several of which are outlined below [68].

Resistant crop varieties

The development and cultivation of crop varieties with inherent resistance to fungal colonization and aflatoxin accumulation represent a critical component of aflatoxin mitigation strategies [69]. Plant breeding programs primarily target traits such as improved husk tightness, modified kernel composition, and enhanced immune responses, all of which significantly reduce the susceptibility of crops to Aspergillus spp. infection [70].

Optimized irrigation management

Proper irrigation management is essential for minimizing plant stress, thereby reducing the risk of aflatoxin contamination [71]. Controlled water supply not only enhances crop yield but also mitigates drought-induced susceptibility, one of the primary predisposing factors for fungal colonization and mycotoxin production. Maintaining optimal soil moisture levels helps to prevent micro environmental conditions that are conducive to the proliferation of aflatoxigenic fungi.

Timely harvesting and field management practices

Harvesting crops at the optimal stage of physiological maturity is critical for minimizing exposure to aflatoxigenic fungi. Delayed harvesting often results in over-maturation, which heightens the risk of fungal colonization and subsequent aflatoxin accumulation [72]. In parallel, the implementation of crop rotation and integrated pest management (IPM) practices serves to disrupt the life cycle of toxigenic fungi and reduce the overall inoculum load in the field [73]. These strategies collectively contribute to lowering the risk of pre-harvest contamination and enhancing the resilience of cropping systems [55].

Integrated Pest and Weed Management (IPWM)

Insect pests and weed infestations contribute significantly to crop injury, thereby creating physical entry points for aflatoxigenic fungi. Such injuries compromise the structural integrity of plants and facilitate fungal colonization [74]. The implementation of IPWM strategies which encompass biological, mechanical, and chemical control measures can substantially reduce pest-related damage, thereby limiting fungal ingress and subsequent aflatoxin biosynthesis.

Crop rotation and field sanitation

Adopting diversified crop rotation systems, along with rigorous field sanitation practices, plays a pivotal role in disrupting the life cycle of aflatoxin-producing fungi. The removal and proper disposal of infected plant debris reduce the fungal inoculum load in the soil, thus decreasing the risk of contamination in future cropping cycles [75]. These interventions are particularly effective in areas with persistent mycotoxin-related challenges and should be considered essential components of sustainable agronomic management [55].

Post-harvest management

Post-harvest interventions play a critical role in minimizing aflatoxin accumulation by preserving the quality and safety of harvested crops. Since fungal proliferation and mycotoxin biosynthesis can continue or even intensify after harvest under suboptimal conditions, the implementation of effective post-harvest handling and storage practices is essential. To mitigate aflatoxin contamination post-harvest, the following strategies are commonly implemented:

Storage conditions

Maintaining appropriate storage conditions is essential for preventing post-harvest fungal proliferation and subsequent aflatoxin production. This entails the regulation of temperature and RH within storage facilities to inhibit the growth of aflatoxigenic fungi [76]. The implementation of climate-controlled units, ventilated silos, and real-time moisture monitoring systems has been shown to significantly enhance storage safety and reduce contamination risk [76].

Use of additives

The application of approved chemical preservatives can effectively inhibit fungal proliferation in stored agricultural commodities, thereby minimizing the risk of aflatoxin biosynthesis [77]. In parallel, the incorporation of mycotoxin adsorbents or binders into feed is widely employed as a preventive measure to reduce the bioavailability of aflatoxins [78]. These additives function by binding aflatoxins within the gastrointestinal tract, thereby impeding their systemic absorption and reducing the risk of toxin carry-over into animal-derived food products.

Handling practices

Regular cleaning and disinfection of storage facilities, along with proper handling protocols during transportation, are critical components of post-harvest contamination control [76]. These practices help to eliminate residual fungal spores and prevent cross-contamination of newly harvested crops. Additionally, the systematic sorting and removal of visibly moldy, discolored, or damaged grains prior to storage can substantially reduce aflatoxin levels in bulk commodities [79]. Such preventive measures are particularly effective in minimizing the initial fungal load and enhancing the overall safety of stored feed and food products.

The severe health and production impacts documented in the livestock and poultry industries serve as a stark indicator of the broader threat that aflatoxins pose to animal agriculture. The contamination pathways in feed ingredients that affect poultry are fundamentally the same as those responsible for the presence of AFM1 in milk from dairy cattle or for residues in the tissues of swine and beef cattle [31]. Consequently, understanding the challenges within the poultry sector provides valuable insights and a robust foundation for developing cross-cutting control strategies that can strengthen safety across the entire food chain, thereby reinforcing the core tenets of an integrated One Health approach.

Biological control approaches

Biological control represents a vital strategy for reducing aflatoxin contamination in crops and agricultural commodities during both pre- and post-harvest stages. One promising method involves the deployment of competitive atoxigenic strains of A. flavus in the field to suppress toxigenic populations. Emerging detoxification approaches include both physical and biological methods. Technologies such as gamma irradiation, ozone treatment, and microwave heating have been explored for their capacity to degrade aflatoxins without compromising the nutritional quality of feed [80].

Research into biological control methods has demonstrated considerable promise in reducing aflatoxin contamination through environmentally sustainable means. One of the most extensively studied strategies for aflatoxin mitigation is the application of non-toxigenic strains of A. flavus, which competitively exclude toxigenic strains, thereby lowering aflatoxin contamination in crops. Complementary biotechnological approaches, such as the use of detoxifying enzymes or microbial degradation pathways mediated by these atoxigenic strains, also hold significant promises for reducing aflatoxin levels in contaminated commodities. These biological interventions serve as valuable complements to conventional physical and chemical detoxification strategies and offer utility in integrated mycotoxin management frameworks.

Critical comparison of mitigation strategies

While physical, chemical, and biological strategies each offer distinct mechanisms for aflatoxin mitigation, a critical evaluation reveals significant trade-offs regarding efficacy, safety, cost, and nutritional impact. Physical methods, such as sorting and irradiation, are practically applicable for bulk processing but often suffer from high energy consumption, incomplete detoxification, and potential loss of essential nutrients [46]. Chemical interventions (e.g., ammoniation, ozonation) are highly effective and economically viable for large-scale operations; however, they present severe limitations, including the generation of toxic by-products, stringent regulatory restrictions, and adverse alterations to the organoleptic and nutritional profiles of the feed. Conversely, biological methods utilizing microbial degradation or enzymatic cleavage represent the most promising and eco-friendly frontier [52]. These approaches offer high specificity and preserve feed quality without leaving toxic residues. Nevertheless, biological strategies currently face challenges related to the stability of enzymes under varying gastrointestinal or environmental conditions (e.g., pH, temperature), slower detoxification rates compared to chemical methods, and complex regulatory approval pathways for novel biological agents [55]. Therefore, relying on a single mitigation strategy is often insufficient. Current critical consensus suggests that an integrated approach combining rapid physical sorting with targeted biological degradation provides the most optimal balance between maximum detoxification efficacy and minimal nutritional degradation. Although a wide array of physical, chemical, and biological strategies have been developed to mitigate aflatoxin contamination, a critical and systematic evaluation reveals that no single approach is universally applicable or completely devoid of limitations (Table 4). Physical methods (e.g., sorting, irradiation), while non-toxic and conceptually straightforward, are often labor-intensive, capital-dependent, and impractical for large-scale agricultural application in developing regions, occasionally leading to unintended nutritional losses [45], [49]. Chemical interventions (such as ammoniation or ozonation) offer high detoxification efficacy; however, they raise significant biosafety concerns regarding chemical residues, the potential toxicity of degradation by-products, and adverse impacts on the organoleptic properties of animal feed [47], [50]. Conversely, biological control particularly the utilization of microbial degradation or competitive exclusion via non-toxigenic Aspergillus strains is increasingly recognized as a highly sustainable alternative. Nevertheless, its field efficacy remains highly susceptible to environmental fluctuations (e.g., temperature and humidity extremes) and necessitates rigorous ecological assessments to prevent unintended microbial shifts. Consequently, relying on isolated, single-point interventions is systematically insufficient. Achieving sustainable food and feed safety demands a highly integrated, interdisciplinary approach tailored to specific regional contexts. Furthermore, translating these mitigation strategies into practical frameworks requires robust governmental interventions, including subsidized access to biological control agents, continuous educational outreach for local farmers, and stringent, unified enforcement of regulatory limits across the entire One Health continuum [70].

Technological and practical limitations

Current mitigation strategies for aflatoxin contamination face significant technological and operational limitations. While advancements in detection tools and decontamination methods have improved identification and removal of aflatoxins, many technologies remain cost-prohibitive or unsuitable for use in resource-limited settings [55]. Practical challenges, such as the lack of scalable post-harvest interventions, inadequate cold chain infrastructure, and limited adoption of good agricultural practices, further hinder effective control. Additionally, variability in toxin distribution within food commodities complicates representative sampling and accurate quantification. Addressing these limitations requires investment in affordable, field-deployable technologies, alongside integrated strategies that combine pre- and post-harvest interventions tailored to local contexts.

Efficacy and limitations of detoxification methods

A range of detoxification strategies have been explored to mitigate aflatoxin contamination, including physical methods (e.g., sorting, milling, thermal treatment), chemical treatments (e.g., ammonization, ozonation), and biological approaches utilizing microbial enzymes or adsorbents [81]. While these interventions can reduce aflatoxin concentrations to varying extents, their overall efficacy remains inconsistent, particularly under field conditions. Chemical treatments, though effective in toxin degradation, may leave harmful residues or alter the sensory and nutritional qualities of the treated products. Similarly, biological detoxification, although promising in terms of specificity and environmental sustainability, often faces challenges related to scalability, regulatory acceptance, and integration into existing food systems. Furthermore, physical methods are limited by their inability to remove aflatoxins uniformly, especially when the toxin is diffusely distributed within the commodity. Importantly, several detoxification strategies fail to achieve complete toxin removal, thereby posing residual health risks [42]. This is particularly critical in regions where regulatory monitoring is limited and where contaminated products may still enter the food or feed supply chain. Consequently, there is a pressing need for the development of safe, cost-effective, and nutritionally non-destructive detoxification methods that can be readily implemented across diverse agricultural contexts. Future research should focus on optimizing multi-modal detoxification strategies, validating their safety through toxicological assessments, and aligning them with international food safety regulations to ensure practical applicability and consumer protection.

Economic feasibility of advanced technologies

Recent advancements in analytical detection and mitigation technologies such as LC-MS/MS, next-generation biosensors, and molecular biocontrol agents offer high sensitivity and specificity for aflatoxin monitoring and control. However, despite their scientific promise, the widespread implementation of these tools remains limited due to high capital and operational costs, as well as the requirement for specialized infrastructure and trained personnel [82]. These economic and logistical constraints are particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, where budgetary limitations, technical capacity gaps, and competing public health priorities hinder the adoption of such advanced systems. Therefore, the scalability and cost-effectiveness of aflatoxin control technologies must be considered a central component of global mitigation strategies.

Practical limitations in the implementation of methods

While numerous aflatoxin control methods have demonstrated efficacy under controlled laboratory conditions, translating these techniques into practical, field-level applications remains a significant challenge. Key logistical barriers include the need for adequate infrastructure, trained personnel, and technological adaptability to diverse agro-ecological environments [83]. Moreover, rapid screening methods must be sufficiently robust to account for the heterogeneous and often complex nature of feed and food matrices encountered in large-scale agricultural operations. These limitations underscore the importance of sustained investment in research and development, as well as capacity-building initiatives that enable effective deployment of interventions. Equally critical is the design of cost-effective and scalable solutions that are compatible with local practices and resource availability, thereby facilitating integration into existing agricultural and livestock and poultry production systems [84].

Extension and awareness

Stakeholder engagement is a fundamental component of comprehensive aflatoxin control strategies. Given the complex and multispectral nature of aflatoxin contamination, effective mitigation requires the coordinated participation of all actors involved in the production, processing, regulation, and consumption of agricultural products. These include farmers, feed manufacturers, extension agents, veterinarians, public health officials, policymakers, and consumers. Educational initiatives targeting these stakeholders are critical for improving awareness of aflatoxin sources, health impacts, and practical control measures. Smallholder farmers often lack access to reliable information and resources needed to implement best practices.

Stakeholder engagement and capacity building

Many smallholder farmers remain unaware of aflatoxin risks, leading to poor post-harvest handling and storage practices [27]. Raising awareness among key stakeholders, including farmers, feed manufacturers, and supply chain actors is a cornerstone of effective aflatoxin risk management. Educating these groups on the sources, health implications, and prevention strategies associated with aflatoxin contamination is critical to achieving sustainable control [85]. Training programs focusing on crop production, harvest timing, post-harvest handling, proper drying, hygienic storage, and the use of safe feed additives can significantly reduce contamination risks [55], [85]. Such capacity-building initiatives are particularly important in resource-limited settings, where technical knowledge gaps and inadequate infrastructure often exacerbate exposure risks [55]. Capacity-building efforts should also extend to regulatory personnel and laboratory technicians, enabling more accurate surveillance, risk assessment, and enforcement of safety standards. This includes training in sampling protocols, mycotoxin detection techniques (e.g., ELISA, LC-MS/MS), and data interpretation. Strengthening institutional capacity and creating enabling policy environments are equally important to ensure that stakeholder actions are aligned with national and international food safety goals.

Knowledge translation and dissemination

Incorporating aflatoxin-related content into agricultural extension services, vocational training programs, and academic curricula can significantly enhance awareness and technical capacity among stakeholders. Such educational efforts empower individuals involved in the feed and food supply chains, including farmers, processors, and veterinary professionals to adopt evidence-based prevention and control strategies [86]. Successful Knowledge dissemination requires sustained investment, public private partnerships, and the integration of these efforts into national food safety and agricultural development strategies. Tailoring training content to local contexts and cultural practices further improves adoption rates and long-term impact.

Operationalizing One Health: strategies for integrated aflatoxin control

One Health is an integrated and unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimize the health of humans, livestock, poultry and ecosystems. It acknowledges the interdependence of human health, domestic and wild animals, plants, and the broader environment, including ecosystems. While health, food, water, energy, and environmental sectors each have specific concerns, interdisciplinary collaboration is essential to address complex health challenges such as emerging infectious diseases, antimicrobial resistance, food safety, and ecosystem integrity (World Health Organization; WHO Fact Sheet). Although the One Health concept is not new, its importance has intensified recently due to evolving interactions among humans, livestock, plants, and the environment (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; CDC Fact Sheet). Aflatoxin contamination poses a multifaceted challenge at the intersection of human and animal health, agriculture, and the environment. Due to its wide-ranging impacts, effective mitigation requires adoption of the One Health approach an integrated and interdisciplinary strategy that facilitates coordinated actions across sectors.

A critical component of operationalizing the One Health approach is the utilization of environment-animal-human interaction models [28]. These system dynamics models are essential for mapping the complex, unidirectional and bidirectional pathways of aflatoxin exposure across the three domains. For instance, environmental stressors such as fluctuating temperature and humidity directly dictate Aspergillus proliferation and mycotoxin biosynthesis in crops [47], [48]. Contaminated crops then enter the animal domain as feed, leading to compromised livestock health and the biotransformation of AFB1 into the hazardous metabolite AFM1 in dairy and meat products [29], [31]. Finally, the human domain is impacted through the consumption of both directly contaminated agricultural commodities and secondary animal-derived products [10]. Integrating predictive modeling that accounts for these cascading effects enables policymakers and health officials to forecast contamination hotspots under changing climatic conditions and deploy preemptive interventions at the critical environmental or animal interfaces long before human exposure occurs [51]. To better visualize the interconnected nature of these risks, Fig. 2 illustrates the complete aflatoxin contamination pathway, demonstrating the flow of toxicity from farm environments through animal feed, biotransformation within livestock, and ultimate exposure to humans. This visual model underscores the necessity of a One Health approach, as interventions at any single point (e.g., farm level) inherently protect the subsequent stages of the food chain [28]. To fully grasp the One Health implications, it is imperative to explicitly delineate the multidirectional transmission pathways of aflatoxins. The primary environmental pathway begins with toxigenic Aspergillus spores residing in soil and crop debris, which colonize susceptible crops during both pre- and post-harvest stages [79]. From this environmental reservoir, transmission bifurcates into direct and indirect routes. The direct route involves human exposure through the consumption of contaminated agricultural staples, such as maize and peanuts [32]. The indirect, yet equally critical, pathway channels through the animal domain: contaminated crops are processed into livestock and poultry feed [11], [39]. Upon ingestion by animals, AFB1 is metabolized and excreted as AFM1 in milk, or retained as residues in meat and eggs, thereby entering the human diet [30], [41]. Furthermore, an often-overlooked occupational transmission pathway exists precisely at the human-animal-environment interface, agricultural and poultry farm workers face significant exposure risks via the inhalation of aflatoxin-laden dust and fungal bioaerosols within grain silos and animal housing facilities [19]. Mapping these distinct transmission pathways underscores that mitigating human exposure requires interrupting the cycle at its fundamental environmental and agricultural origins.

A conceptual One Health flowchart detailing aflatoxin transmission pathways: from farm and feed to animals and humans.

Aflatoxins represent a complex, interconnected risk that necessitates collaboration among healthcare providers, veterinarians, agronomists, toxicologists, and policymakers to enable comprehensive risk assessment, harmonized surveillance, and targeted intervention strategies. The One Health framework facilitates policy coherence by aligning national food and feed safety regulations with international standards, while promoting equitable allocation of resources. It strengthens the adaptive capacity of food systems in the face of emerging challenges, such as those posed by climate change, which influences fungal ecology and mycotoxin prevalence. To effectively operationalize this approach, there must be deliberate, cross-sectoral collaboration in designing and executing context-specific mitigation strategies. Agriculture, veterinary medicine, food safety, and public health sectors must work in concert to implement practical and science-based interventions [87]. For example, veterinarians and animal and poultry nutritionists should engage with feed manufacturers to formulate aflatoxin-resistant diets, while agricultural scientists advance pre-harvest strategies, including the application of biological control agents and the development of resistant crop varieties. Public health authorities also play a critical role in raising awareness about aflatoxin-related risks and promoting safer food handling and consumption practices.

Moreover, establishing multi-stakeholder platforms comprising researchers, policymakers, industry actors, and farming communities can significantly enhance knowledge exchange and foster the adoption of innovative technologies and best practices [88]. These collaborative networks are essential for building a unified, resilient response to aflatoxin threats and ensuring that interventions are both evidence-based and contextually relevant. By adopting the One Health framework and strategically investing in critical areas, stakeholders can more effectively address the multifaceted challenges posed by aflatoxins. Such integrated initiatives not only improve food safety and public health outcomes but also enhance the resilience and sustainability of agricultural and food systems globally [89]. To advance this interdisciplinary integration, several strategic priorities must be emphasized, as outlined below:

Collaborative surveillance systems

Development of integrated monitoring systems that track aflatoxin levels across the food and feed supply chain from crop production to livestock products enables early detection of contamination and timely response.

Joint risk assessments

Conducting comprehensive assessments that account for the cumulative effects of aflatoxins on human health, animal welfare, and environmental integrity ensures the development of holistic and effective mitigation strategies.

Policy and regulatory alignment

Harmonizing regulatory frameworks across sectors and regions promotes consistency in aflatoxin standards, facilitates international trade, and reinforces public health protection [65].

Capacity building and education

Investing in interdisciplinary training and education programs strengthens the ability of professionals across sectors to collaborate effectively and implement aflatoxin control measures.

Research and innovation

Supporting collaborative research initiatives encourages the development of novel technologies and practices for aflatoxin detection, prevention, and control [90].

Synthesizing disparate findings from agricultural, veterinary, and medical research underscores the necessity of a true One Health approach moving beyond mere multidisciplinary observation to fully integrated intervention strategies [28]. For instance, linking predictive models of climate-driven Aspergillus proliferation in crops [25], [48] with data on the biotransformation of AFB1 to AFM1 in dairy livestock [29], [31] and subsequent human dietary exposure pathways [10], [26] reveals a continuum of interconnected vulnerabilities. A synthesized evaluation indicates that interventions cannot remain siloed; reducing the burden of human hepatocellular carcinoma effectively requires upstream investments, such as subsidizing biocontrol agents for crop farmers and ensuring access to mycotoxin binders for livestock. This holistic integration provides added scientific value by demonstrating that mitigating aflatoxin at its foundational agricultural source is ultimately the most economically viable and effective public health strategy.

Current challenges and research gaps in aflatoxin control

Despite significant advancements in aflatoxin management, current technologies face critical limitations that hinder their universal application. In terms of detection methodologies, highly sensitive and accurate techniques such as LC-MS/MS and HPLC are severely limited by their requirement for expensive infrastructure, extensive sample preparation, and highly trained personnel. These factors render them impractical for continuous, real-time field monitoring, particularly in resource-limited settings [56], [62]. Conversely, while rapid on-site screening tools like LFA provide immediate results, they often suffer from matrix interferences and variable reliability, compromising their utility for strict regulatory compliance [62]. Furthermore, current mitigation technologies present inherent operational trade-offs. Physical and chemical decontamination processes frequently incur high energy costs and can result in the unacceptable loss of essential thermolabile nutrients or the inadvertent generation of toxic chemical by-products [52]. Biological mitigation, although presenting an eco-friendly alternative, is currently constrained by the instability of microbial agents or purified enzymes under varying physiological or environmental conditions (e.g., fluctuating pH and temperature in the gastrointestinal tract), and their commercialization is often delayed by complex regulatory pathways [55].

Beyond these technological hurdles, a critical review of the literature exposes broader knowledge gaps. First, current research predominantly focuses on controlled laboratory efficacy, with a conspicuous lack of longitudinal, large-scale field studies evaluating robustness under fluctuating agro-climatic conditions [47], [84]. Second, while the synergistic toxicity of co-occurring mycotoxins (e.g., aflatoxins combined with fumonisins or ochratoxins) is increasingly acknowledged, current regulatory frameworks and routine detection protocols primarily target isolated aflatoxin fractions [52], [79], representing a major gap in realistic health risk assessment.

Emerging technologies and future directions

Addressing the aforementioned multidimensional gaps necessitates a paradigm shift from reactive mitigation to proactive, technology-driven management. This requires a unified, interdisciplinary One Health approach to link agricultural practices with environmental, animal, and human safety. AI-driven predictive models and climate-adaptive agriculture

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) represents a transformative frontier in aflatoxin prediction. Future research must prioritize AI-driven predictive models capable of analyzing complex, multi-variable datasets including real-time meteorological parameters, soil properties, and agronomic practices to establish highly accurate early-warning systems [46], [91], [92]. Furthermore, with climate change projected to intensify fungal proliferation and aflatoxin biosynthesis through elevated temperatures and drought stress, there is an urgent need to pivot towards climate-adaptive agricultural practices [92]. Key research gaps include the accelerated breeding of drought-tolerant and fungal-resistant crop varieties, the optimization of micro-irrigation systems, and the adaptation of planting timelines to sustainably mitigate the environmental triggers of Aspergillus colonization [45].

Smart sensing systems and next-generation detection

There remains a significant gap in the development of field-deployable, rapid detection technologies capable of real-time monitoring [93]. Future research must focus on engineering multiplexed biosensors and nanomaterial-based platforms capable of simultaneous, real-time quantification of multiple mycotoxins in complex matrices without extensive sample preparation [56], [62]. Integrating these smart sensing systems with the IoT, remote sensing platforms, and molecular biology tools will be crucial for building robust frameworks that provide unprecedented visibility over the entire food and feed supply chain [94].

Advanced mitigation materials and bioengineering

Another critical frontier is the engineering of novel, high-efficiency mitigation agents. While traditional mycotoxin binders are widely utilized, they often inadvertently sequester essential dietary nutrients [29], [52]. Future investigations must focus on synthesizing next-generation nanostructured adsorbents, surface-modified biochars, and carbon-based nanomaterials (e.g., graphene oxide) optimized for specific, highly targeted aflatoxin sequestration [55]. Concurrently, extensive studies are required to genetically engineer robust microbial consortia and recombinant enzymes that exhibit high catalytic detoxification efficiency across diverse physiological conditions, maximizing practical applicability in feed without compromising nutrient bioavailability [95].

Conclusions

The management of aflatoxin contamination in feed has evolved from a focus on basic detection and regulation to a more integrated, multidisciplinary approach. Historical efforts centered on setting regulatory limits and improving post-harvest practices have expanded to include biocontrol strategies, advanced analytical technologies, and educational interventions. Despite progress, aflatoxins remain a persistent threat to food safety and economic stability. By using the livestock and poultry industries as a primary case study, this review highlights systemic vulnerabilities that equally threaten broader food systems, including dairy, beef, and pork production. Addressing this interconnected issue requires sustained international collaboration, investment in farmer education, and continued innovation in emerging predictive and mitigation technologies to ensure a safe and resilient global food production system across all sectors.
