M4-RAG : A Massive-Scale Multilingual Multi-Cultural Multimodal RAG
David Anugraha, Patrick Amadeus Irawan, Anshul Singh, En-Shiun Annie Lee, Genta Indra Winata

Abstract
Vision–language models (VLMs) have achieved strong performance in visual question answering (VQA), yet they remain constrained by static training data. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mitigates this limitation by enabling access to up-to-date, culturally grounded, and multilingual information; however, multilingual multimodal RAG remains largely underexplored. We introduce M4-RAG , a massive-scale benchmark covering 42 languages and 56 regional dialects and registers, comprising over 80,000 culturally diverse image–question pairs for evaluating retrieval-augmented VQA across languages and modalities. To balance realism with reproducibility, we build a controlled retrieval environment containing millions of carefully curated multilingual documents relevant to the query domains, approximating real-world retrieval conditions while ensuring consistent experimentation. Our systematic evaluation reveals that although RAG consistently benefits smaller VLMs, it fails to scale to larger models and often even degrades their performance, exposing a critical mismatch between model size and current retrieval effectiveness. M4-RAG provides a foundation for advancing next-generation RAG systems capable of reasoning seamlessly across languages, modalities, and cultural contexts.



1
Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and vision–language models (VLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in reasoning, summarization, and question answering
5
;
11
. However, their reliance on static training data often leads to outdated or incomplete knowledge, limiting factual accuracy and cross-domain coverage. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a powerful paradigm to overcome this limitation by enriching model outputs with information retrieved from external knowledge sources
21
;
33
. Building on RAG, two major extensions have advanced the field: multilingual RAG
37
;
25
and multimodal RAG
1
;
12
. The former enables cross-lingual information access, allowing queries and documents to appear in different languages, while the latter incorporates visual or structured inputs such as images, tables, or videos into retrieval and generation pipelines. Despite progress in both directions, their intersection, multilingual multimodal RAG, remains largely unexplored. However, real-world information access is inherently both multilingual and multimodal
30
. Current RAG systems and benchmarks rarely assess this combined setting, leaving open critical challenges regarding the alignment between cross-lingual retrieval and multimodal representations, the ability of multilingual models to ground information across modalities, and the adequacy of evaluation metrics in capturing these complex dependencies.

To bridge this gap, we present
M4-RAG
, a comprehensive evaluation of multilingual, multicultural, and multimodal RAG. We construct a benchmark spanning multiple languages and modalities, encompassing both text–text and text–image retrieval scenarios. Our analysis provides the first systematic comparison of model performance under cross-lingual and cross-modal conditions. We find that existing RAG models degrade substantially when queries or retrieved contexts differ in language or modality, underscoring the need for stronger alignment, more robust retrieval, and deeper reasoning integration.

In particular, our research questions are as follows:

RQ1
.

Multimodal retrieval:
How does multimodal retrieval compare to text-only retrieval in supporting downstream generation? Under what conditions does incorporating visual or multilingual context improve—or fail to improve—model accuracy?

RQ2
.

Model scaling:
How does the influence of retrieved evidence change as VLMs scale? Do larger models benefit less from external context due to stronger reliance on parametric knowledge, and how does this interact with retrieval quality?

RQ3
.

Language prompts and context:
How does the choice of query and context language affect RAG performance? Do systems offer equitable support for non-English users, or do mismatches in language introduce systematic degradation?

2
M4-RAG

In this section, we present our framework for multilingual multimodal RAG, detailing the task setup, learning objectives, and the retrieval and generation components. To ensure robust evaluation, we employ an LLM-as-a-judge approach complemented by human validation.

2.1
Tasks and Objectives

We propose
M4-RAG
, an evaluation framework for multilingual multimodal RAG that prioritizes end-to-end task performance while enabling systematic investigation of when and why retrieval systems help or hinder generation quality. Unlike prior work that evaluates retrieval and generation in isolation, we assess their interaction in realistic multilingual settings, where questions, images, and knowledge sources may span diverse languages.

Formally, given a VQA instance consisting of a question
q
in language
\ell_{q}
, an associated image
I
, and a ground-truth response
r^{*}
, along with a multilingual document corpus
\mathcal{C}
containing relevant factual and cultural knowledge, the system must produce a response
r
. The RAG pipeline operates in two stages. First, a retriever
R_{\theta}
selects the top-
k
most relevant passages from the corpus:

R_{\theta}(q,I,\mathcal{C})=D_{k}=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{k}\},

(1)

where each passage
d_{i}
may be in any language
\ell_{d}\in\mathcal{L}
, reflecting real-world retrieval scenarios where relevant information is not restricted to the query language. Then, a VLM
\mathcal{M}
generates a response conditioned on the question, image, and retrieved context:

\hat{a}=\mathcal{M}(q,I,D_{k}),

(2)

which is evaluated against
a^{*}
using task-specific accuracy metrics.

Our primary objective is to understand the conditions under which retrieval augmentation improves or degrades generation performance in multilingual multimodal settings. We investigate three key dimensions:

•

Retrieval impact on generation.
We evaluate each model under both zero-shot and retrieval-augmented settings to quantify the net effect of retrieved context on response accuracy. This analysis reveals whether retrieval provides useful external knowledge or introduces distracting noise, and how this effect varies with model scale.

•

Failure mode diagnosis.
To understand why models succeed or fail, we study the link between retrieval quality and generation outcomes. By analyzing passage relevance scores, we characterize distinct error patterns and measure how model predictions change before and after retrieval. This also enables us to examine how strongly different VLMs rely on their parametric knowledge and how this reliance evolves with model size.

•

Cross-lingual retrieval dynamics.
We assess retrieval and generation performance when both queries and retrieved context appear in non-English languages. This allows us to test whether RAG provides comparable benefits across languages or whether multilingual users face systematic disadvantages, revealing gaps in cross-lingual retrieval effectiveness.

2.2
Evaluation Benchmark Source

We source our VQA pairs from two existing large-scale, culturally-rich datasets,
WorldCuisines

(
32
)
and
CVQA

(
26
)
. These benchmarks form the foundation of our evaluation by providing high-quality, human-annotated examples that are both multilingual and deeply grounded in cultural context.

WorldCuisines
.

WorldCuisines
is a massive-scale benchmark containing 60k VQA pairs that are parallel across 30 languages and dialects, centered on global cuisine. We select
WorldCuisines
due to its extensive multilingual parallelism that enables controlled analysis of cross-lingual retrieval behavior under consistent semantic content. The dataset also includes intentionally challenging scenarios, such as adversarial prompts where the provided context is misleading, which offers a valuable stress test to examine whether RAG can help generation models to re-anchor their responses in factual evidence instead.

CVQA
.

CVQA
is a multilingual dataset with more than 10,000 VQA pairs spanning 10 diverse cultural categories across 30 countries. We include
CVQA
to complement the domain-specific nature of
WorldCuisines
, thereby broadening the evaluation landscape with a wider variety of cultural domains and knowledge sources. This diversity is essential for assessing whether RAG systems can retrieve and reason over a broad spectrum of cultural information, rather than performing well only within a single domain.

2.3
Knowledge Base Creation

To systematically evaluate retrieval quality, we construct a new, large-scale multilingual knowledge corpus for each evaluation benchmark. These corpora are built from Wikipedia snapshots dated April 2025 to ensure broad thematic coverage and temporal alignment with the creation timelines of the associated datasets. This alignment helps preserve contextual fidelity, as cultural information and entity descriptions evolve over time
(
10
)
.

For each VQA instance, we construct a set of multilingual queries that capture complementary types of evidence. These include a question-only query, an answer-only query, and culturally enriched queries that expand an answer with domain-relevant terms (for example, using “Japanese cuisine” for a sushi-related item). For each query, we retrieve the top 25 articles independently in English and in the corresponding target language. After deduplication, this yields 223,468 articles for
WorldCuisines
and 306,794 articles for
CVQA
.

3
Experimental Setup

3.1
Retrieval Settings

To assess the impact of retrieval, we evaluate all VLMs under four main configurations, resulting in six experimental variants per model due to different retrieval strategies. For all RAG-based methods, we retrieve the top-
k
passages with
k=5
.

1.

Baseline (No Retrieval):
The VLM receives only the question
q
and image
I
, without any external context. This serves as a simple baseline to quantify the model’s performance without any retrieval assistance.

2.

Ground-Truth Context:
The VLM is provided with the ground-truth context, representing an upper bound on performance. For
WorldCuisines
, this corresponds to the human-labeled food description from the knowledge base. For
CVQA
, we simulate this using a caption generated by
Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct
. This setup evaluates how much performance improves when the model has access to highly reliable contextual information.

3.

Text-Based RAG:
Retrieval is performed using textual queries derived from the input. This approach explores alternatives to multimodal retrieval by converting visual information into text
(
14
;
38
;
22
)
. Therefore, we consider two settings:

•

Oracle-Query RAG:
The VLM uses the ground-truth context as the query to retrieve passages. This provides a reliable textual query and serves as a strong reference point for text-based retrieval.

•

Caption + Question RAG:
A caption is first generated from image
I
and question
q
using
Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct
. The VLM then combines the question
q
and generated caption to retrieve passages, simulating scenario from multiple past works where the image is converted into text for retrieval
(
14
;
22
)
.

4.

Multimodal RAG:
The question
q
and image
I
are used jointly to retrieve passages, leveraging both textual and visual information holistically. We test two multimodal embedding models:
mmE5
(11B)
(
7
)
and
B3
(7B) from VLM2Vec
(
17
)
.

3.3
Multilingual VQA

We analyze the impact of language on the VQA task in a cross-lingual experimental setting. We measured how a model’s performance changes when the language of its instructional prompts and provided context varies. To do this, we created multilingual versions of our prompts and the oracle contexts for both CVQA and WorldCuisines. We used
Gemini-2.5-Flash
model to produce high-quality translation of two key components. To ensure their fidelity, all translations were subsequently reviewed and validated by annotator.

•

Multilingual Prompts:
The entire instruction template, including system messages and formatting cues, was translated from English into each of the target languages. This creates the
Multilingual Prompts
setting to see whether models achieve better cultural grounding and task performance when instructions are provided in the native language of the query. We measure this by analyzing the performance change from the English prompt baseline.

•

Multilingual Oracle Context:
Similarly, we created the
Oracle Multilingual Context
setting to investigate whether models perform better on a cultural VQA task when the evidence is provided in the culture’s language. For this oracle setup, we translated the ground-truth English context into each target language. This allows us to isolate the model’s ability to perform cultural reasoning when all information is presented in the target language. By comparing this to the English context baseline, we can directly quantify whether models benefit from language that is aligned with the VQA’s cultural context.

4
Results and Analysis

In this section, we present a comprehensive overview of our results, including evaluations across different model families. We conduct an in-depth analysis of how model scaling impacts both retrieval quality and final generation, and we examine the conditions under which RAG succeeds or fails depending on the language, context, or prompts used. This analysis allows us to address the research questions introduced at the beginning of this study and provide detailed insights into the behavior and limitations of multilingual multimodal RAG systems.

4.1
Overall Performance

Figure
2
presents the overall trends across all experiments.
Gemma3 27B
performs the best in both
CVQA
and
WorldCuisines
for aseline, with accuracy of 74.34 and 66.20 respectively. As expected, providing the golden context consistently yields the highest performance across all models and datasets, serving as an upper bound for the quality of contextual information. Here,
Gemma3 27B
still performs the best in
CVQA
, while
Qwen-2.5-VL 72B
performs the best when given the ground truth context in
WorldCuisines
.

Among retrieval strategies, text-based retrieval performs the worst, even worse than the baselines across model sizes and datasets, indicating that naively converting the image to text can introduce noise that harms VLM performance. In contrast, multimodal retrieval consistently outperforms text-based retrieval, although the
B3
embedding model shows comparatively lower gains. We also see reasoning model dominated the section with RAG, meaning that reasoning capability is able to discern the context better compared to non-reasoning model, even when it’s comparably larger in size.

4.2
Model Scaling

Figure
2
also illustrates how model performance scales with size across all model families. While performance generally improves as model size increases, the rate of improvement varies across retrieval strategies. Text-based RAG not only performs the worst overall, but its performance scales poorly with model size, indicating that this retrieval approach is unlikely to provide meaningful benefits even for larger models.

For multimodal retrieval, using
mmE5
or
B3
initially provides gains over the baseline. However, the improvement does not scale consistently, in fact the baseline performance eventually surpasses multimodal RAG for larger models, suggesting that even instruct-tuned models struggle to effectively leverage retrieved context at scale.

When considering reasoning-focused models, we observe a similar trend in
WorldCuisines
and, to a lesser extent,
CVQA
. Although the performance of VQA using
mmE5
has not yet fallen below the baseline, the slope of improvement is modest, indicating that as models continue to scale, the relative benefit of retrieval diminishes. Overall, across all model families, larger models show reduced reliance on external context, with retrieval providing little to no improvement over the baseline at scale.

4.3
When does RAG succeed and fail?

In order to understand the effect of retrieval quality on RAG performance, we analyze the quality of the retrieved context from both multimodal-embeddings on both
CVQA
and
WorldCuisines
using VLM-as-a-judge, to see how relevant is the retrieved context with respect to the image, query, and the actual ground truth answer. Here, we will only show the plots for CVQA since WorldCuisines also provides the same linear trend, in which figures are shown in the supplementary materials instead.

Figure
4
and Figure
5
illustrate the impact of retrieval quality on the efficacy of RAG systems applied to the
CVQA
dataset. Both plots show positive correlation between the average retrieval relevance score with the performance of all tested models. This demonstrates that the relevance of the retrieved context is as aligned with the RAG system’s overall success.

The left plot quantifies the system’s robustness when faced with new context, where it measures the percentage of instances where an answer, already correct without RAG, remains correct after context is provided. The data shows that poor retrieval (scores below 2.0) significantly degrades performance, with retention rates dropping to 40-60%. This indicates that irrelevant context actively misleads the models, causing them to abandon their correct internal predictions. Conversely, as retrieval quality approaches its maximum, retention becomes nearly perfect (95-100%), showing that relevant context reinforces and validates correct answers.

The right plot highlights the system’s corrective power. This metric tracks the percentage of initially incorrect answers that are successfully remediated by the RAG context. This capability is equally, if not more, dependent on retrieval quality. Irrelevant context provides almost no corrective benefit (10-20% rate), whereas high-relevance context enables the models to fix 80-90% of their original errors. However, a high relevance context does not necessarily always correct the system, that is the correction rate does not saturate to nearly perfect (95-100%) unlike shown in the left plot. This indicates that current VLMs still struggle to reliably leverage externally retrieved evidence, even when the retrieved context is entirely correct.

Beyond the main trend, both plots reveal an important scaling effect. Larger models, exemplified by
Qwen2.5-VL 72B
and
Gemma3 27B
, exhibit greater reliance on their parametric knowledge. In the left plot, large models form the upper boundary of correctness retention (i.e., they more often preserve correct baseline answers), while in the right plot they frequently form the lower boundary of correction rate (i.e., they are less likely to change an incorrect baseline when given high-quality retrieved evidence). On the other hand, smaller model would take the counterparts, respectively on both multimodal embedding strategies. Taken together, these patterns indicate that model scale increases inertial priors, that is stronger internal beliefs that are less readily updated by external context. In other words, larger models show reduced context integration (or lower context susceptibility) as they are less prone to be misled by poor retrieval, but at the same time, also less likely to adopt corrective information supplied by good retrieval. This phenomenon suggests a potential point of diminishing returns for RAG, where beyond a certain scale, improvements in model capacity do not straightforwardly translate to better utilization of retrieved evidence.

4.4
Multilingual Performance Gaps

Our cross-lingual experiments reveal that while VLMs can generate multilingual responses, they exhibit a strong bias toward English when handling cultural VQA tasks. We found that deviating from English prompts or context consistently degrades performances, which becomes severe for low-resource languages, challenging the notion that these models are truly language-agnostic.

Our analysis shows that providing instructions in the target language often hinders VQA performance. As seen in Figure
3
, shifting from an English to a multilingual prompt creates a consistent, negative performance change across all resource levels. This suggests that even when a model understands a cultural query, it best interprets the task’s instructions in English, a likely result of its training. Furthermore, this performance gap becomes more significant when analyzing the model’s use of oracle evidence. Our experiments tested whether culturally aligned context in the target language improves performance. Contrary to this expectation, Figure
3
shows that performance deteriorates dramatically, especially for low-resource languages.

This trend is not uniform across all model families. For instance, the
Qwen
family exhibits a sharper collapse in low-resource settings compared to the Gemma models, indicating that simply scaling model size does not solve this underlying bias. Interestingly, we observed that smaller models sometimes show a lesser performance drop. This is likely because they often code-switch to English in their response even when prompted in a target language, whereas larger models attempt to answer entirely in the target language and fail more dramatically.

These findings point to a fundamental limitation in current multilingual VLMs. The models reason more effectively about a cultural VQA task when the query is non-English but the context remains in English, rather than when both are in the target language. This suggests an English-centric reasoning process that poses a major challenge for developing truly multilingual and culturally aware systems.

5
Related Work

Culture has been studied across multiple dimensions, including social norms
13
, country-specific variation
15
;
24
;
26
, general world knowledge
37
, and food-related knowledge
15
;
32
. Understanding these facets is crucial for building AI systems that can reason appropriately across different cultural contexts. Early efforts in multicultural RAG have primarily relied on machine-translated benchmarks
9
;
21
, which often fail to leverage the full potential of high-quality, human-curated data and may introduce translation artifacts that distort cultural nuances.

Existing multilingual text-only retrieval datasets—such as Mintaka
27
, MIRACL
37
, MKQA
23
, and MLQA
20
—cover a wide range of domains, including books, geography, politics, and general knowledge. While these datasets provide extensive language coverage and support research in multilingual natural language understanding, their focus on text alone limits their applicability for tasks that require multimodal reasoning
16
. In particular, tasks such as visual question answering
2
, image-grounded reasoning
28
;
31
, and culturally contextualized visual understanding remain largely unsupported, highlighting the need for benchmarks that integrate both multilingual and multimodal information
8
.

To address these gaps, we introduce a new benchmark that combines multilingual and multimodal inputs, enabling end-to-end evaluation across languages with comprehensive experimental analysis.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we present
M4-RAG
, a large-scale multilingual and multimodal benchmark designed to advance research on multimodal RAG. By spanning 42 languages and 56 regional dialects and registers, and by providing over 80,000 culturally grounded image–question pairs alongside a controlled multilingual retrieval environment,
M4-RAG
offers a rigorous and reproducible testbed for evaluating RAG in realistic settings. Our comprehensive experiments across 11 models under 4 model families demonstrate that while RAG reliably boosts the performance of smaller VLMs, this benefit does not consistently extend to larger models and can even reduce accuracy, in other words, larger VLMs exhibit diminished correction rates and a stronger reliance on their internal parametric knowledge. We also observe that replacing English instructions and context with multilingual ones consistently reduces performance, indicating that current models remain brittle when required to follow non-English prompts and context. These findings highlight the need to rethink retrieval strategies, multilingual grounding, and multimodal integration for next-generation RAG systems. We hope that
M4-RAG
serves as a foundation for future work toward models that not only retrieve more relevant knowledge, but can meaningfully integrate it to reason robustly across languages, modalities, and cultural contexts.

9.3
Language-Wise Performance Analysis

To further investigate the multilingual capabilities of current VLMs, Figures
9
(
WorldCuisines
) and
10
(
CVQA
) break down the performance impact of language choice on instructions. These figures represent the performance change when switching from English instructions to the target language. We observe similar patterns across both benchmarks. While high-resource languages like Chinese, Spanish, and French maintain relatively same performance, low-resource languages such as Amharic, Telugu, and Oromo suffer significant degradation, often dropping by over 5–10%. This confirms an inherent bias in current instruction-tuning approaches: despite being capable of generating multilingual text, these models follow reasoning instructions significantly better when presented in English.

Furthermore, figures reveal a critical limitation regarding contextual grounding. Intuitively, one might expect that answering a culture-specific question would be easier when the supporting evidence (Oracle Context) is provided in that culture’s native language. However, our results indicate the opposite. Across the majority of languages, particularly in the
WorldCuisines
for languages like Yoruba and Marathi, providing ground-truth context in the target language causes a sharper performance decline than simply changing the prompt language. This inverse effect indicates that VLMs treat English as a reasoning pivot: they struggle to integrate non-English evidence, preferring English context even for culture-specific queries where native-language grounding should be advantageous.
