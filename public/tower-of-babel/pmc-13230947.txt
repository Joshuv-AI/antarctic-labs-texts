Exploring micellar behavior of surfactants-assisted electrospinning of polyacrylonitrile nanofibers
Authors: Alia Hindi, Kiran Shahzadi, Muhammad Sarfraz, Muneerah Alomar, Maryam Al Huwayz, Adeolu A. Adediran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current work investigates new and easy method for doping PAN nanofibers with four types of colloidal surfactants to improve their electrical and structural characteristics. Solution conductivity measurements were used to establish each surfactant’s critical micelle concentration to optimally electrospun them into nanofibers. Surfactant addition significantly decreased fiber diameter and enhanced surface homogeneity producing thin fibers (~ 180 nm). When comparing surfactant-modified fibers to pure PAN, impedance spectroscopy showed notable improvement in dielectric relaxation and charge transport behavior. This work is interesting because it establishes previously unreported direct association between electrical impedance response of electrospun PAN nanofibers, micellar behavior and surfactant type. The type of surfactant and its micellar properties have a significant impact on the electrospinning behavior of polyacrylonitrile (PAN) nanofibers. This work opens door for enhanced performance of PAN nanofibers in flexible electronics, energy devices and filtration systems by proving that surfactant-assisted electrospinning is practical, affordable and adjustable method of controlling electrical and morphological characteristics of PAN nanofibers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41133-x.

Introduction

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) is a synthetic organic polymer prepared by polymerization of a mixture comprising acrylonitrile and an unsaturated sulfonic acid or its water-soluble salt. An excellent choice for electrical and electroactive applications, polyacrylonitrile (PAN) was chosen as the basis polymer for this work because to its polar nitrile (-C ≡ N) functional groups, high thermal and chemical stability, and excellent electrospinnability. Strong dipolar nitrile groups enable dipole polarization and effective charge transfer in the presence of an applied electric field, making PAN appropriate for dielectric and impedance research. Additionally, PAN easily dissolves in polar aprotic solvents like N, N′-dimethylformamide (DMF), creating stable, homogenous spinning solutions with adjustable viscosity, which is crucial for electrospinning uniform nanofibers1. It is a thermally stable polymer and needs more than 300 °C for the decomposition process to take place. Usually, the polymer contains 85% of acrylonitrile monomers and 15% of other comonomers that lead to good dyeing of these filaments and an improvement in the properties of others. Polyacrylonitrile is one of the polymers with linear and harsh chains. PAN provides a special blend of surface reactivity, mechanical resilience, and compatibility with surfactant doping when compared to other frequently electrospun polymers like PVDF or PVA. This allows for a methodical examination of the ways in which surfactant chemistry affects fiber morphology and electrical performance. Consequently, PAN offers a useful model system for establishing a correlation between electrical performance, micellar effects, and molecular interactions in surfactant-assisted electrospinning. Characterized by its relatively small size, high polarizability and consisting of hydrogen bonds, Herbert Reyna first made the polymer PAN in 1920. DuPont in 1942 produced acrylic fiber (Orlon yarns) by spinning PAN solution of N, N’-dimethylacetamide2.

Electrospinning of PAN solution can be facilitated by the addition of colloids owing to their ability to decrease the surface tension of spinning solution. Colloid suspensions are also characterized by formation of clusters of particles called micelles when their concentration in solution reaches a certain threshold called critical micelle concentration (CMC)3. Surface-active colloids efficiently impact mechanical and structural properties of polymers since harmonious interaction between polymer molecules is greatly influenced by the presence of these substances4. Surface-active suspensions also affect the shape and dimensions of formed nanofibers5,6. Little data is available on assessing the effect of surfactant colloids on structural properties of dispersed assemblies such as polymer solutions7,8.

Effect of octadecyl amine (ODA) - an active surfactant colloid - on electrospun polyphenolic nanofibers coated with urea was studied by Sun et al.9. Şimşek et al. evaluated effect of surface-active ODA colloid on polyvinyl alcohol and polyvinyl acetate (PVA-co-PVAc) copolymer10. A significant decrease in surface tension of polymers prepared in solution form and consequently a significant decrease in diameters of electrospun polymer nanofibers was found. Using surfactant sodium dodecyl benzenesulfonate with acrylonitrile polymer (PAN/DMF) significantly alleviated surface tension and thus decreased diameter of spun fibers and increased their smoothness and surface area11.

Electrospinning technology is one of currently advanced technologies, and currently constitutes a scientific revolution in production of polymer nanofibers, and although this technique was discovered in 1930 s, it was not applied industrially until last few years due to discovery of its importance in industrial applications, as it produced nanofibers with diameters up to 2 nanometers and even a few micrometers, for several natural, synthetic and mixed polymers, which highlighted its importance by advancing in international research12. The principle of electrospinning is based on electrostatic forces to make and prepare polymer nanofibers with very small diameters. Microfibers prepared by this method have important properties such as surface-to-volume ratio, high permeability, and mechanical properties such as durability, resistance, etc. Electrospinning technology has received special attention from international researchers, due to scientific revolution made in the field of nanoscale applications and their uses, as the number of research centers interested in this technology, universities and scientific institutes exceeded two hundred13.

Recent reviews highlight continuous improvements in process control, functionalization, and scale-up. Electrospinning is still the most popular method for creating high-surface-area polymer nanofibers for filtration, sensors, energy, and biological applications14. Surfactants help electrospinning by lowering surface tension and stabilizing the polymer jet, as numerous investigations have shown. Nanoscale morphology and charge-transport properties interact together to determine device performance, therefore recent research has gone beyond purely morphological optimization to concentrate on the functional features (electrical, electrochemical, and sensing behavior) of nanofiber mats. Among process adjustments, the application of micelle-forming agents or surfactants has become a popular and affordable way to regulate fiber homogeneity, jet stability, and solution surface tension. Microcelle- or reverse-micelle-assisted electrospinning can enhance additive dispersion, decrease bead formation, and yield finer, more consistent fibers, according to a number of recent investigations and method papers15. The quantifiable structural characteristics of electrospun mats are referred to as fiber morphology. These characteristics include the mean fiber diameter and its distribution, the presence or absence of beads, fiber alignment, inter-fiber junction density, surface texture (smooth vs. wrinkled/porous), and the resulting pore-size distribution and overall porosity of the nonwoven network. These features are more than just geometric; they regulate the mat’s mechanical integrity, the accessible surface area, and the tortuosity and continuity of transport channels. For instance, while alignment and junction density can boost tensile strength and offer more consistent anisotropic transport, decreasing diameter and suppressing bead flaws usually increase surface area and enhances filtration or adsorption efficiency. Similarly, in liquid-contact applications, permeability/pressure drop trade-offs and wetting behavior are significantly influenced by pore-size distribution, which is determined by fiber packing and diameter homogeneity16.

Furthermore, in nanofibrous medium, shape directly affects electrical measurements. Porosity and pore connectivity control electrolyte penetration and ionic routes during impedance testing, whereas variations in diameter uniformity and inter-fiber contact density change the effective dielectric environment and contact resistance. Morphology is therefore a crucial link between solution physics and functional electrical behavior because any processing variable (such as surfactant type, micellar state, or solution rheology) that narrows the diameter distribution or modifies bead formation can result in a detectable shift in impedance response17,18. Morphology affects how nanofibrous mats interact with their operating environment in addition to their bulk performance. In sensing and electrochemical applications, smaller, more homogeneous fibers often improve adsorption/filtration and interfacial phenomena by increasing the specific surface area and density of accessible functional sites. On the other hand, flow non-uniformity and stress concentrators that reduce mechanical dependability might result from bead flaws and wide diameter dispersion. While surface texturing or internal porosity can enhance wettability and promote quick mass transfer or additive loading, alignment and junction creation affect load transfer and can provide direction-dependent (anisotropic) transport and electrical routes. Therefore, talking about morphology offers an application-relevant framework for understanding how increased mat performance is a result of surfactant-controlled jet stability and micellar behavior19.

Among process changes, the application of micelle-forming agents or surfactants has become a popular and affordable way to regulate fiber homogeneity, jet stability, and solution surface tension. Micelle- or reverse-micelle-assisted electrospinning can enhance additive dispersion, decrease bead formation, and yield finer, more consistent fibers, according to a number of recent investigations and method papers20.

PAN-based systems are extensively researched for carbonization and electrochemical electrodes, and some studies report conductivity increases following additive or post-treatment. However, there aren’t many systematic studies that (a) measure the behavior of surfactant solutions (such as conductivity and CMC), (b) compare ionic and nonionic surfactants under the same electrospinning conditions, and (c) relate these solution-level differences to the final nanofiber mats’ impedance spectroscopy parameters. Rational surfactant selection for modifying the shape and electrical functionality of PAN nanofibers is hindered by this missing component21.

Specific liquefaction concentration of polymer dope solutions plays an important role in electrospinning process. Proper adjustment of liquefaction concentration of polyacrylonitrile spinning solution is a challenging task. Adding precise quantity of colloidal surfactant into polyacrylonitrile can help effectively control their liquefaction concentration. Different types of surface-active substances (namely octadecyl amine, sodium dodecyl sulfate, Tween 20 and Tween 80) have been added in varying concentrations into polyacrylonitrile solutions in N, N’-dimethylformamide medium to appraise their respective liquefaction concentrations. Concocted surfactant-containing polyacrylonitrile solutions were spun using an electrospinning device to figure out lowest diameter of fabricated nanofibers.

Though a number of studies have been reported that used surfactants like sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), and solutions based on Tween to reduce the surface tension and enhance the electrospinnability of PAN solutions, a majority of the studies focused on morphological aspects and not on the electrical characterization of the obtained nanofibers22. Also, there is no systematic analysis of the association between surfactant chemistry (ionic or non-ionic) and electrical impedance performance of electrospun PAN nanofibers in the literature. The weakness in previous studies was that comparison of surfactants was not very well done and the parameters of specific liquefaction concentration and critical micelle concentration (CMC) were overlooked, which significantly affects the uniformity of fibers and dielectric response23.

To address these gaps, the current study examines how four classes of colloidal surfactants, namely, octadecyl amine (ODA), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), Tween 20, and Tween 80, affect the morphology and electrical characteristics of electrospun electrostatic nanofibers of PAN. This study offers a full picture of how the nature of surfactants affects the electrospinnability and the subsequent electrical functionality of nanofibers made of PANs by linking together conductivity, SEM morphology, and spectroscopic studies of impedance24.

Most of the current research on surfactant-assisted electrospinning is still restricted to morphological optimization and qualitative enhancements in fiber homogeneity, despite a wealth of literature on the subject. Surfactant chemistry, solution-state charge transport, electrohydrodynamic jet behavior, and post-spinning electrical functioning are rarely systematically correlated in a single experimental paradigm. Specifically, the lack of impedance-driven electrical analysis and CMC-based normalization has impeded the creation of logical design guidelines for electroactive PAN nanofibers. In an attempt to bridge this gap, the current work establishes a thorough structure–process–property relationship connecting the ionic nature of surfactants, micellar behavior, electrospinning dynamics, and dielectric performance. This method goes beyond incremental optimization and offers fundamental understanding of how surfactants work in PAN-based nanofiber systems as electrohydrodynamic and electroactive modifiers.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly link surfactant micellization (CMC) and surfactant class with the impedance response of electrospun PAN nanofibrous mats, offering an integrated framework that connects solution colloidal physics to both morphological uniformity and functional electrical behavior. In order to control jet stability, fiber morphology, and the electrical (impedance) response of the resulting electrospun mats, this work aims to clarify how the type of surfactant and its micellar behavior in PAN/DMF spinning dopes govern solution properties (surface tension, conductivity, and flow/rheology). In particular, we compare representative anionic, cationic, and non-ionic surfactants, ascertain their critical micelle concentration (CMC) in the PAN/DMF medium, and correlate trends related to CMC with equivalent-circuit parameters from impedance spectroscopy and diameter distributions derived from SEM.

Experimental

Materials

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN; average molecular weight, Mw ≈ 150,000 g mol− 1; copolymer comprising 85% acrylonitrile monomers and 15% other monomers, as well as 99.5% pure N, N’-dimethylformamide (DMF) solvent were obtained from Merc. Colloidal surfactants such as octadecyl amine (ODA, C18H37NH2) with ≥ 98% purity, anionic sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, C12H25SO4Na) with ≥ 99% purity, nonionic Tween 20 (C58H114O26) with ≥ 99% purity, and nonionic Tween 80 (C64H124O26) with ≥ 99% purity, were all obtained from Alfa Aesar. HCl with 37–38% purity was received from Merc25.

Preparation of colloidal surfactant solutions

Before electrospinning 1 g polyacrylonitrile was dissolved in a weighed solution of DMF solvent so as to prepare 10% w/w spinning solution at laboratory temperature of 25 °C and using a magnetic stirrer until the mixture was completely dissolved. 10% w/w polyacrylonitrile-based spinning solutions were also prepared in same way but with the addition of varying concentrations of surface-active substances so as to assess the effect of nature of different colloidal surfactants on electrospun fibers. Such solutions were prepared by adding varied quantities of various surfactants such as cationic octadecyl amine, anionic sodium dodecyl sulfate, non-ionic Tween 20 and non-ionic Tween 80 separately, so that we maintained the same 10% w/w concentration for each sample26.

Electrospinning of PAN solutions

The electrospinning process was conducted at room temperature (around 25 °C) without any active relative humidity control. All polyacrylonitrile solutions with and without containing surface active colloidal materials were spun to fibers using a French-made WTW electrospinning device displayed in Fig. 127.

Materials characterization

Diameter of resulting fibers was measured with an American-made Tuscan Veca scanning electron microscope (SEM) with following specifications: Flex SEM 1000 II, SE electron image, 20 kV acceleration, 4.0 nm resolution, and 60000X magnification. Prepared samples were analyzed with a programmable Schlumberger phase and gain analyzer (model SI1253) spectrometer in the spectrum range of 0.01–20,000 Hz. It works to make a complex impedance spectrum through which it can deduce and measure a set of parameters such as reckoning equivalent circuit, material characteristics such as ohmic resistance of sample, specific resistance and relaxation time29.

Results and discussion

Determination of solution conductivity and CMC method and interpretation

Conductivity results of all the prepared solutions comprising octadecyl amine, sodium dodecyl sulfate, Tween 20, and Tween 80 at different concentrations as well as their CMC values, are expressed in Fig. 2.

Surfactant concentration was considered as a governing parameter and examined in connection to each surfactant’s critical micelle concentration to develop a methodical concentration–structure–performance relationship. Three concentration regimes are readily distinguished by their measuring their respective conductivity: (i) below the CMC, where individual surfactant molecules predominate; (ii) near the CMC, where micelle formation begins to form; and (iii) above the CMC, where micellar aggregates control the solution behavior. Despite variations in molecular structure and ionic character, this classification allows for the normalization of concentration effects and offers a physiologically valid framework for comparing various surfactants.

Increases in surfactant concentration below the CMC cause a noticeable increase in solution conductivity, which is indicative of increased charge carrier availability and stronger ion-dipole interactions with PAN chains. Conductivity shows a shift in slope close to the CMC, which is indicative of micelle generation and charge redistribution in the solution. Due to the ideal balance between electrostatic forces and viscoelastic resistance, this concentration regime is associated with the most stable electrospinning behavior. Further conductivity enhancement is negligible at concentrations above the CMC, indicating partial charge shielding inside micelles and a decreased contribution to jet stretching efficiency.

The conductivity patterns (Fig. 2) validate the findings that all the surfactants have a significant effect on the ionic environment in the PAN/DMF system. The ionic nature and molecular structure of surfactants have a significant impact on the degree of conductivity improvement. For instance, SDS and ODA generate increased conductivities due to the easy dissociation of their ionic head groups (protonated amine in ODA and sulfate in SDS) in the polar DMF medium, which raises the density of charge carriers. An enhancement of the conductivity before CMC in the SDS and ODA containing solutions indicates that the polar nitrile groups of PAN interact with the surfactant head groups strongly through ion-dipole interactions. Finer fibers are produced during electrospinning as a result of this interaction, which also increases charge mobility and permits jet production. On the other hand, nonionic surfactants (Tween 20 and Tween 80) showed a slow rise in conductivity, suggesting that the ionic component played a minor role. As these observations indicate, the type of surfactant charge does not only dictate the conductivity of the solution, but also the electrohydrodynamic stability of the spinning process. Further increase in surfactant concentration beyond threshold limit slightly increases solution conductivity.

Surfactants significantly change the electrohydrodynamic behavior of the spinning jet by altering solution conductivity, charge density, and micellar structure in addition to reducing surface tension. The equilibrium between polymer solution’s viscoelastic resistance and electrostatic repulsion forces controls jet stability during electrospinning process. Enhanced Coulombic repulsion along the jet axis results from PAN/DMF system’s population of mobile charge carriers being increased by addition of ionic surfactants like ODA and SDS. Longer jet stretching and more stable whipping motion under the applied electric field are the results of suppressing jet breakup and Rayleigh instabilities. Strong ion-dipole interactions between surfactant head groups and the polar nitrile groups of PAN are responsible for the observed increase in conductivity below the critical micelle concentration. These interactions enhance charge transport during jet elongation by facilitating charge delocalization along polymer chains. Despite a rise in surfactant content, the electrospinning process is stabilized above the CMC due to the creation of micellar aggregates, which further contribute to a more uniform distribution of charges within the spinning fluid. As a result, variations in electrospinning kinetics and fiber creation efficiency are closely correlated with the shift in conductivity behavior at the CMC.

On the other hand, non-ionic surfactants (Tween 20 and Tween 80) have little effect on charge transport and mainly affect electrospinning by altering solution viscosity and surface tension. Comparatively thicker fibers and wider diameter distributions result from their limited impact on conductivity, which lessens the electrostatic stretching force acting on the jet. These findings show that surfactant chemistry controls jet stability through electrostatic interactions that regulate charge transfer and electric field coupling during electrospinning, in addition to interfacial effects. Surfactants considerably influence the electric field distribution during electrospinning process by modifying the spinning solution’s electroconductivity and surface tension, which in turn improves the electric field forces and ameliorates the fiber morphology and diameter as described in detail in Sect. “Fibers morphological analysis”.

Additionally, partial ion-dipole association is encouraged by the interaction between the surfactant head group and the polar nitrile (-C ≡ N) groups of PAN. This enhances charge transfer channels in the solution and stabilizes charged species. Therefore, a combination of (i) a larger ionic population, (ii) improved ion–dipole interactions with PAN chains, and (iii) micelle-assisted charge transfer above the CMC threshold results in the overall increase in conductivity with concentration. A clear breaking point is observed in C-vs.-κ plot of Fig. 2 corresponding to stated critical micelle concentration for each solution. Fibers morphological analysis

SEM micrographs of pure and surfactant-doped PAN nanofibers along with their respective histograms of fibers diameter distribution are shown in Fig. 3, which makes it evident how surfactant chemistry affects the diameter distribution, surface texture, and fiber morphology. The pure PAN nanofibers (Fig. 3A) have a broad diameter dispersion between 250 and 340 nm and are comparatively coarse, irregular fibers with noticeable bead formations. Higher surface tension and a lower charge density in the PAN/DMF system lead to instability in the electrospinning jet and partial polymer chain stretching, which results in this wide dispersion.

The fibers become significantly more homogeneous and bead-free with the narrowest diameter dispersion (150s-200 nm) when ODA is added (Fig. 3B). ODA’s amine head partially ionizes, increasing charge repulsion along the jet, while its lengthy alkyl chain interacts hydrophobically with the PAN backbone. Fibers become thinner and smoother as a result of this dual action, which increases the polymer jet’s elongation under the applied electric field. A crisp Gaussian-type distribution, which indicates a reliable spinning process and homogeneous solution properties, is confirmed by the fiber diameter shown in histogram. In comparison to pure PAN, the SDS-doped fibers (Fig. 3C) again show a decrease in diameter (average ~ 205 nm); however, the modest surface roughness suggests a little less control over jet thinning than ODA. This is explained by SDS’s ionic sulfate head group, which boosts solution conductivity but may cause localized charge accumulation that results in mild jet instabilities. However, the better uniformity when compared to pure PAN indicates that the increased electrohydrodynamic stability effectively suppresses bead flaws. The fibers of nonionic surfactants (Tween 20 and Tween 80; Figs. 3D-E) exhibit little strand fusion and are relatively thicker (235 nm and 260 nm, respectively). This may be explained by the increased molecular weight of Tween molecules and weaker ion-dipole interactions, which raise the spinning solution’s viscosity and decrease stretching under the electric field. These samples’ lower observed solution conductivities are supported by their wider diameter dispersion, which suggests less efficient charge transport during electrospinning (Fig. 2).

The CMC-defined regimes clearly dictate the concentration-dependent trend of surfactant concentration’s impact on fiber shape. Maximum jet stability and elongation efficiency are shown by fibers electrospun from solutions close to the CMC, which have the narrowest diameter distributions and the smallest average diameters. The ideal charge density and uniform electric field distribution throughout the rotating jet are the causes of this behavior. However, because increased viscosity and micellar aggregation partially offset electrostatic stretching effects, fibers produced at concentrations well above the CMC only slightly reduce in diameter. These findings show that fiber structure exhibits an optimum close to the CMC rather than being linearly reliant on surfactant concentration. The significance of micellar organization in regulating electrospinning dynamics and fiber homogeneity is emphasized by this non-linear concentration dependence.

Ionic surfactants, especially cationic ODA, are better at reducing surface tension and stabilizing the electrospinning jet, as evidenced by the overall drop in average fiber diameter, which follows the order ODA < SDS < Tween 20 < Tween 80 < Pure PAN. Additionally, a more uniform charge distribution and better solution rheology are suggested by the narrowing of the fiber diameter distribution in samples modified with SDS and ODA. Subsequent impedance tests (Sect. “Equivalent circuit for prepared samples”) indicate that this morphological modification directly affects the nanofibers’ electroactive behavior.

Surfactant-induced changes in jet stability and electric field distribution during electrospinning can be directly connected to the morphological variations seen in the electrospun PAN nanofibers. The enhanced solution conductivity for ionic surfactants, especially ODA, increases the effective electric stress acting on the jet, allowing for more effective elongation and charge injection under a constant applied voltage. Stronger stretching pressures along the jet axis, better polymer chain alignment, and inhibition of bead formation are the main outcomes reflected in terms of producing thinner and more homogeneous nanofibers.

Furthermore, ODA’s amphiphilic shape encourages concurrent hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions with PAN chains, enhancing molecular compatibility and lowering the localized charge accumulation. As a result, the electric field distribution throughout the jet trajectory becomes more uniform, reducing the disturbances that usually result in diameter fluctuations. Therefore, rather than being solely a rheological effect, the narrower fiber diameter distribution seen for ODA-modified fibers indicates improved electrohydrodynamic stability.

Because of the weaker connection between the electric field and the spinning fluid, fibers made with non-ionic surfactants have lower jet stretching efficiency. Surface tension is reduced, but electrostatic repulsion forces are limited by the lack of substantial ionic charge, leading to less noticeable jet elongation and minimally merged fiber morphologies. These results demonstrate that a synergistic balance between electrospinning settings and surfactant-induced charge transport processes, as opposed to relying only on surface tension reduction, is necessary for effective control over nanofiber morphology. ODA produced bead-free fibers with a narrower diameter distribution among the surfactants examined, suggesting improved jet stability and a more consistent charge distribution during electrospinning.

Equivalent circuit for prepared samples

Impedance spectroscopy (IS) method helped determine polarization conductivity behavior of prepared samples by expressing impedance (Z) on a cartesian plot in terms of its imaginary part (-ImZ) and real part (ReZ) as shown in Fig. 4(A-E). This type of electrical impedance corresponds to a circuit containing a capacitor and a resistor in parallel shown in Fig. 4(F).

Sporadic deviation of experimental impedance data from regular semi-circle behavior can be ascribed to layered structure of fibers as well as collegial interaction between created ions and charges. This type of irregular behavior entails its representation by more complex electrical frequency circuits by modeling with an additional RC circuit connected in series with the main circuit33. Equivalent electrical circuit for prepared samples is a capacitor C and a resistance R2 in parallel, connected in series with resistance R1, as shown in Fig. 4(F).

The reduced arc radius of ODA- and SDS-modified fibers shows reduced bulk resistance and increased charge carrier mobility, which confirms the fact that surfactant doping can increase the conductive nature of these materials. The almost same semicircles in high frequency indicate that the crystal orientation in all samples is uniform, and the deviations in low frequency indicate that the interfacial polarization depend on the surfactant. It is also noted that the semicircles are identical at high frequencies, but they are not so at low frequencies35.

In addition to this, the enhanced loss factor at low frequency (Fig. 5) in ODA sample indicates a better dipolar relaxation, which means improved alignment of PAN chains and improved pathways of electronic interactions provided by a surfactant. These results make it evident that the electrospinning with the addition of surfactants can customize the morphology of fibers as well as enhance the dielectric response of the PAN nanofibers. Loss factor increases at low frequencies, then takes a maximum value, and this value differs from one sample to another. Loss factor value decreases slowly in all samples at high frequencies due to the application of a variable electric field to all studied samples. At high frequencies, the polar molecules do not keep pace with the direction of applied electric field, and their frequency becomes different from the frequency of applied field. A potential difference arises between polarization and applied voltage, and the dipoles disappear, and the value of contribution of loss of electron relaxation remains. With increasing frequency, the loss resulting from dipole relaxation increases, in addition to loss resulting from electron relaxation. As the frequency increases to higher values, the intensity of loss resulting from electron relaxation decreases, while the loss resulting from dipole relaxation takes small values36–38.

Further understanding of the conductive mechanisms functioning within the nanofiber mats is provided by dielectric loss behavior. For ODA-modified fibers in particular, the significant increase in tanδ at low frequencies suggests improved space-charge polarization and dipolar relaxation. Higher energy dissipation occurs at low frequencies because charge carriers have enough time to build up at the polymer-surfactant interfaces and align dipolar segments of PAN chains with the applied electric field. Variations in charge mobility and polymer chain dynamics brought about by surfactant incorporation are reflected in a shift in relaxation behavior between different samples. While non-ionic surfactants show longer relaxation because of restricted charge carrier availability, ionic surfactants shorten relaxation times by promoting quicker charge redistribution through ion-assisted hopping mechanisms. All tested samples exhibit decreased dielectric loss at higher frequencies due to dipoles’ incapacity to follow the quickly oscillating field, indicating that dipolar and interfacial polarization predominate over electronic conduction. These findings show enhanced charge transport, decreased bulk resistance, and improved dipolar relaxation work in concert to improve the electrical performance of surfactant-modified PAN nanofibers rather than just morphological refinement.

The current paper is a systematic correlation of the surfactant chemistry with morphological and electrical characteristics of the electrospun PAN nanofibers. With the combination of conductivity, SEM, and impedance analysis, we are able to illustrate first time that octadecyl amine is a preferred dopant to reduce the fiber diameter and increase dielectric relaxation by improving the charge movement. This holistic solution offers new insight into how surfactant polymer interactions can be intelligently tuned to be able to create electroactive nanofibers to be used in sensor, energy, and membrane applications.

The relationship between concentration, structure, and performance is further supported by patterns in electrical performance. Improved charge transport channels linked to thinner fibers and larger interfacial contact area are reflected in the lower bulk resistance and improved dielectric relaxation of nanofiber mats made from solutions close to the CMC. Electrical characteristics approach a plateau at larger surfactant concentrations, suggesting that excessive surfactant content does not result in proportionate performance benefits. Together, these findings show that surfactant concentration controls nanofiber performance via a coupled sequence: electrospinning dynamics is controlled by concentration-dependent solution conductivity, which in turn determines fiber morphology, which in turn determines electrical transport and dielectric behavior. For surfactant-modified PAN nanofibers, this methodical framework establishes a comprehensive concentration–structure–performance relationship.

Significance of the approach and application perspective

Through an analysis of morphological statistics and impedance-determined dielectric parameters by a combination of conductivity, we directly attribute surfactant chemistry to processability (electrospinning) and functional electrical properties of the mat. The observation that a single surfactant (ODA) can concomitantly decrease fiber diameter and enhance dielectric response highlights the potential of the method in the designing of PAN-based nanofibers to sensor applications, dielectric layers and electroactive membranes where nanoscale morphology and electroactivity are both vital.

The experimental data obtained in this work enables the development of a semi-empirical model that connects surfactant chemistry to electrospinning dynamics and electrical performance of PAN nanofibers, going beyond descriptive interpretation. Three experimentally confirmed control parameters, namely (i) solution conductivity (κ), (ii) average fiber diameter (d), and (iii) bulk resistance (R₂) derived from impedance spectroscopy form the foundation of this model.

According to the conductivity CMC analysis, a rise in κ below and close to the CMC indicates improved ion dipole interactions with PAN nitrile groups and increased availability of mobile charge carriers. This increase in κ directly increases the electrostatic stretching force acting on the jet during electrospinning, resulting in decreased fiber diameter and increased jet elongation. Higher conductivity systems in the order ODA > SDS > Tween 20 > Tween 80 result in thinner fibers; experimentally, this connection follows an inverse correlation between κ and d.

Charge transmission through networks of interconnected nanofibers controls post-spinning electrical activity. The impedance results show that the bulk resistance R₂ systematically declines with decreasing fiber diameter and increasing solution conductivity, suggesting that interfacial polarization and short-range hopping – rather than band-type conduction – dominate charge transport. This theory is further supported by dielectric relaxation behavior. Improved dipolar alignment and space-charge accumulation at fiber-fiber interfaces are the causes of the decreased relaxation time and increased low-frequency dielectric loss seen in ionic surfactant-modified fibers. After electrospinning, surfactant-induced charge carriers become partially trapped within the polymer matrix, creating localized polarization centers instead of freely conducting ions, which intensifies these processes.

This framework, which was developed experimentally, shows how surfactants function as both electrohydrodynamic and electroactive modifiers, controlling charge transport pathways in the solid nanofiber mat and jet behavior during electrospinning process. The model offers a novel theoretical foundation for sensible surfactant selection in electroactive nanofiber design and is internally consistent across all observed parameters.

Instead of using surfactants in isolation, the innovative strategy adopted in this study is based on an integrated and mechanism-driven approach. This work establishes an unprecedent quantitative relationship between surfactant-induced micellar behavior and dielectric relaxation in electrospun PAN nanofibers by combining conductivity-based CMC determination, controlled electrospinning, detailed morphological analysis, and impedance spectroscopy. The current findings show that surfactant chemistry directly controls charge transport channels, polarization mechanisms, and relaxation dynamics in the solid nanofiber mats, in contrast to earlier research that mainly treated surfactants as surface tension modifiers. A new conceptual framework for creating electroactive polymer nanofibers with customized electrical properties is offered by the suggested semi-empirical model, which is based on experimental trends rather than assumptions from the literature. This work presents the first CMC-normalized, impedance-based structure–process–property framework linking surfactant chemistry to electrospinning dynamics and dielectric performance of PAN nanofibers.

Conclusion

This work thoroughly examined how surfactant type and micellar behavior affect the electrospinning process and the electrical responsiveness of PAN nanofibrous mats that results. When cationic (ODA), anionic (SDS), and nonionic (Tween 20 and Tween 80) surfactants were compared, distinct variations in solution behavior, jet stability, fiber shape, and impedance characteristics were found. Surfactants added at concentrations around their marginal critical micelle concentration (CMC) have a major impact on the dynamics of electrospinning. Bead-free nanofibers with a narrow diameter distribution were produced using cationic ODA, which was shown to be the most successful surfactant in lowering surface tension and stabilizing the electrospinning jet. A more uniform charge distribution and advantageous solution rheology during fiber production are indicated by this enhanced morphological uniformity. Conversely, larger diameter distributions were generated by anionic and nonionic surfactants, indicating less effective control over jet instability and fiber stretching.

Fiber shape and electrical responsiveness were found to be directly correlated by electrical impedance spectroscopy. Because of increased inter-fiber contact uniformity, a more uniform dielectric environment, and better continuity of charge transport channels within the fibrous network, nanofibrous mats made with ODA-modified solutions showed more consistent impedance behavior. These results show that quantifiable electrical characteristics in the solid nanofibrous mats are closely correlated with changes in micellar structure and surfactant–polymer interactions in the solution stage. To the best of our knowledge, this work reveals a direct relationship between electrospun PAN fiber shape, impedance response, and surfactant-driven micellization behavior. The findings emphasize how crucial surfactant selection is for controlling morphology and adjusting the functional electrical properties of electrospun nanofibers. The knowledge acquired here offers a helpful framework for the logical design of nanofibrous materials for uses in electrochemical interfaces, sensing, and filtering, where regulated morphology and predictable electrical activity are crucial. Future research may apply this method to additional polymer systems and investigate how post-treatment techniques and environmental factors affect the links between structure and property.
