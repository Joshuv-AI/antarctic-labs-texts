Measure equivalence and sofic approximations
Thiebout Delabie, Juhani Koivisto, Romain Tessera

Abstract
We introduce a technique for producing a measure coupling between two sofic groups from a family of maps between their sofic approximations. We exploit this to construct measure couplings between pairs of groups with prescribed integrability conditions. As an application we show that solvable Baumslag-Solitar groups, Lamplighters and the group SOL are all exponentially measure equivalent to one another: in particular they are L p L^{p} measure equivalent for all p < ∞ p<\infty . This is in sharp contrast with the fact that these groups are in general not quasi-isometric to one another: indeed, for instance ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ ≀ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z} is not quasi-isometric to ℤ / 3 ​ ℤ ≀ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z} .

Introduction

Measure equivalence between countable groups has been introduced by Gromov as a measure analogue of the notion of quasi-isometry. Recall that two countable groups
G
and
H
are measure equivalent (ME) if there exists a standard measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
equipped with free, commuting, measure preserving actions of
G
and
H
, such that both actions admit fundamental domains with finite measure.
A well-known consequence of a celebrated theorem of Ornstein-Weiss
[
OW80
]
is that all infinite countable amenable groups are measure equivalent.

As we shall recall in the next paragraph, the data of the two fundamental domains can be used to define two cocycles. These can somehow be viewed as measured analogues of mutual morphisms between the groups, which are in a weak sense inverses of one another. It turns out that some rigidity can be restored by imposing integrability conditions on these cocycles. For instance Bowen proved that
L^{1}
ME preserves the volume growth
[
Aus16
]
. A general theory of “quantitative” ME was developed in
[
DKLMT22
]
(see next paragraph). This theory raises two main problems:

•

finding limitations on the integrability properties of a measure equivalence coupling between two groups;

•

conversely constructing measure equivalence couplings between those groups whose integrability properties match these limitations.

In
[
DKLMT22
]
, it is shown that the volume growth and more importantly the isoperimetric profile can both be used to answer the first question (for amenable groups). Regarding the second problem,
it could be tempting to exploit the proof of Ornstein-Weiss’s theorem. However, their result happens to be too general to produce measure equivalences with good integrability conditions. To remedy for this, the authors of
[
DKLMT22
]
exploit a notion of Følner tiling sequences
1
1

1

Følner tiling sequences appear in
[
Dan16
]
in a different context and under a different name (see also
[
CC19
]
).
to produce explicit ME couplings between amenable groups with prescribed integrability conditions. This method has then been used extensively in
[
Esc24a
]
to prove that the constraint given by the isoperimetric profile is nearly sharp in a wide class of situations (see §
1.2
).
More recently, in
[
DLIT25
]
Llosa and the first and third author use Følner tiling sequences in nilpotent groups to classify them up to

ME for

.

Despite their undeniable usefulness, Følner tiling sequences present several limitations. First, they might not exist in all amenable groups. Besides, even when they do, they might not yield ME couplings with sharp integrability conditions. Finally, Følner tiling sequences are limited to amenable groups.

The goal of this note to present a more general framework to produce various forms of couplings: measure equivalent, orbit equivalent but also a less known form of coupling called “measure subgroup couplings”.
Besides, our method applies to the wider class of sofic groups.

\lim_{n}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(r)}\right)=1,

.

Let us end this section with a problem. The previous theorem implies in particular that amenable Baumslag-Solitar groups
\operatorname{BS}(1,n)
, for
n\geq 2
are
\exp^{\diamond}
-ME.
What can be said of the class of
non-amenable
Baumslag-Solitar groups
\operatorname{BS}(m,n)
, with
1<m<n
?
By contrast with the amenable case, Whyte has famously proved that any two groups from this class are quasi-isometric
[
Why01
]
.
Recently, Gaboriau, Poulin, Tserunyan, Tucker-Drob, and Wrobel have announced a proof of the measurable analogue of this result, namely that any two groups from this class are ME. Can this be generalized to
L^{p}
ME, or even
\exp^{\diamond}
-ME?

Organisation

In §

, we recall some basic facts about ME and OE couplings, and their quantitative versions. Subgroup couplings are not addressed there, as the definitions given in the introduction are sufficient for our purpose. While the use of a (non-principal) utrafilter

is omnipresent in this paper, the only non-elementary statement we need about it is Loeb’s construction of an ultralimit of probability spaces, which is recalled in §

.
In §

we introduce the concept of
\mathcal{U}
-statistical properties of a sequence of maps between finite metric spaces (equipped with the uniform probability measure).
The main part of the paper is §

, where the various couplings are constructed (the plan of this section is explained there).
In §

7

Finally in §

2.1
Variations on (quantitative) measure and orbit equivalence

We begin by recalling the notion of measure equivalence. For the sake of clarity, we first recall some terminology following
[
Mac57
]
. A
standard Borel measure space

(X,\mu)
is a Borel space
(X,\mathcal{B}(X))
, frequently also known as a measurable space, with a measure
\mu
on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
given by the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of some Polish (separable and completely metrizable) topology on
X
. The elements of
\mathcal{B}(X)
are called
Borel
. A Borel subset
X_{0}\subseteq X
is
conull
if
\mu(X\setminus X_{0})=0
and a property that holds for all
x\in X_{0}
is said to hold
almost everywhere
or
a.e.
The space
(X,\mu)
is said to be a standard Borel probability space if
\mu(X)=1
.

By a
measure preserving action
of a finitely generated group
G
on
(X,\mu)
, for short
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
, we mean that the action map
G\times X\rightarrow X
given by
(g,x)\mapsto g\ast x
is Borel and that
\mu(g\ast E)=\mu(E)
for all
g\in G
and all
E\in\mathcal{B}(X)
. A measure preserving action on a standard Borel probability space is said to be probability measure preserving. Finally, by a
measure fundamental domain
for
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
we mean a Borel set
X_{G}\subseteq X
for which
\mu(g\ast X_{G}\cap X_{G})=0
for all
g\in G\setminus\{e_{G}\}
and
\mu(X\setminus G\ast X_{G})=0
.
The definition of measure equivalence can now be stated as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Two infinite finitely generated groups
G
and
H
are said to be
measure equivalent
if there exists a standard Borel measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
of infinite measure with commuting measure preserving actions of
G
and
H
with measure fundamental domains of finite measure, that is, there exist Borel sets
X_{G},X_{H}\subseteq\Omega
of finite measure for which

•

\mu(g\ast X_{G}\cap X_{G})=0
for all
g\in G\setminus\{e_{G}\}
and
\mu(\Omega\setminus G\ast X_{G})=0
;

•

\mu(h\ast X_{H}\cap X_{H})=0
for all
h\in H\setminus\{e_{H}\}
and
\mu(\Omega\setminus H\ast X_{H})=0
.

The quadruple
(\Omega,X_{G},X_{H},\mu)
is called a measure equivalence coupling from
G
to
H
and it is cobounded if the fundamental domains are contained in finitely many translates of each other.

Remark 2.2
.

If
(\Omega,X_{G},X_{H},\mu)
is a measure equivalence coupling and
\mu(X_{G}\triangle X_{H})=0
, then
X_{G}
is a measure fundamental domain for
H
, and we can take
X_{G}
as a common measure fundamental domain for both actions.

To quantify measure equivalence we need some terminology regarding integrability of
cocycles
. Towards this, let
G
be some finitely generated group with a measure preserving action on a standard Borel measure space
(X,\mu)
with finite measure, and let
c\colon G\times X\rightarrow H
be a Borel function into another finitely generated group
H
.
We say that
c\colon G\times X\rightarrow H
is a
Borel cocycle
if for each
g,g^{\prime}\in G
, we have
c(gg^{\prime},x)=c(g,g^{\prime}\!.\hskip 1.0ptx)c(g^{\prime},x)
for a.e.
x\in X
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a non-decreasing maps, let
X
be a finite measure space and let
G
and
H
be two groups with a cocycle
c\colon G\times X\to H
. The cocycle
c
is
\varphi
-integrable if there exists a
\delta>0
such that

\int_{X_{H}}\varphi\left(\delta|c(g,x)|\right)d\mu(x)<+\infty

for every
g\in G
and
c
is strongly
\varphi
-integrable (or
\varphi^{\diamond}
-integrable) if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
\delta>0
and
C>0
such that

\int_{X_{H}}\varphi\left(\delta|c(g,x)|\right)d\mu(x)\leq C\varphi(\varepsilon|g|)

for every
g\in G
.

We will write
\operatorname{L}^{p}
-integrable when
\varphi(x)=x^{p}
and write
\operatorname{L}^{\infty}
when
x\mapsto c(g,x)
is essentially bounded for every
g\in G
.
Note that when
Y
is of finite measure, then
\psi
-integrability implies
\varphi
-integrability, whenever there exists
K>0
such that
\varphi(x)\leq K\psi(Kx)+K
.

A measure equivalence
(\Omega,X_{G},X_{H},\mu)
comes naturally equipped with two Borel cocycles given by its measure fundamental domains. These are given by the Borel maps
\alpha\colon G\times X_{H}\rightarrow H
where for every
g\in G
, we have that

g\ast x\in\alpha(g,x)^{-1}\ast X_{H}

for a.e.
x\in X_{H}
, and
\beta\colon H\times X_{G}\rightarrow G
where for every
h\in H
, we have that

h\ast y\in\beta(h,y)^{-1}\ast X_{G}

for a.e.
y\in X_{G}
. The map
\alpha\colon G\times X_{H}\rightarrow H
is a Borel cocycle with respect to a measure preserving action
G\curvearrowright(X_{H},\mu|_{X_{H}})
where for every
g\in G
, we have that

g\cdot x=\alpha(g,x)\ast g\ast x

for a.e.
x\in X_{H}
, and
\beta\colon H\times X_{G}\rightarrow G
is a Borel cocycle with respect to a measure preserving action
H\curvearrowright(X_{G},\mu|_{X_{G}})
given by

h\cdot y=\beta(h,y)\ast h\ast y

for a.e.
y\in X_{G}
.
The Borel cocycles defined in this way are called the
measure equivalence cocycles
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be non-decreasing maps, let
G
and
H
be two groups with a measure equivalence coupling
(\Omega,X_{G},X_{H},\mu)
from
G
to
H
, let
\alpha\colon G\times X_{H}\to H
be the cocycle such that
g*x\in\alpha(g,x)^{-1}X_{H}
and let
\beta\colon H\times X_{G}\to G
be the cocycle such that
h*x\in\beta(h,x)^{-1}X_{G}
.
The measure equivalence coupling
(\Omega,X_{G},X_{H},\mu)
from
G
to
H
is
(\varphi,\psi)
-integrable if
\alpha
is
\varphi
-integrable and
\beta
is
\psi
-integrable.

We now turn our attention to orbit equivalence of groups, which implies measure equivalence. Towards this, we recall that a probability measure preserving action
G\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
of a finitely generated group
G
on a standard Borel probability space
(X,\nu)
is
essentially free
if there exists a
G
-invariant conull Borel subset of
X
on which the action is free. The definition of orbit equivalence can now be stated as follows.

Definition 2.5
.

Two infinite finitely generated groups
G
and
H
are said to be
orbit equivalent
if there exists a standard Borel probability space
(X,\nu)
with essentially free probability measure preserving actions
G\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
and
H\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
and a conull invariant Borel subset
X_{0}\subseteq X
such that
G\ast x=H\ast x
for all
x\in X_{0}
.
The space
(X,\mu)
is called an orbit equivalence from
G
to
H
.

Like measure equivalence, orbit equivalence defines two Borel cocycles, given by
\alpha^{\prime}\colon G\times X\rightarrow H
and
\beta^{\prime}\colon H\times X\rightarrow G
, where for each
g\in G
, we have
g\ast x=\alpha^{\prime}(g,x)\ast x
for every
x\in X_{0}
, and for each
h\in H
, that
h\ast x=\beta^{\prime}(h,x)\ast x
for every
x\in X_{0}
. These are called the
orbit equivalence cocycles
.

As mentioned before, orbit equivalence implies measure equivalence. In fact, we have an equivalence, when we assume the fundamental domains in the measure equivalence coupling to be the same. In addition, the correspondent cocycles are the same.

Proposition 2.6

(Folklore)
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be non-decreasing maps and let
G
and
H
be two infinite, finitely generated groups. There exists a measure equivalence coupling
(\Omega,X,X,\mu)
from
G
to
H
that is
(\varphi,\psi)
-integrable if and only if there exists an orbit equivalence coupling
(X,\nu)
from
G
to
H
that is
(\varphi,\psi)
-integrable.

Proof.

Let
(\Omega,X,X,\mu)
be a measure equivalence coupling from
G
to
H
. As previously, we write
\alpha\colon G\times X\rightarrow H
and
\beta\colon H\times X\rightarrow G
for the measure equivalence cocycles.

First we need the action of
G\times H
to be essentially free as well. In general this is not the case. Therefore we make the following construction.
Let
\mathcal{B}=\{0,1\}^{G\times H}
and equip it with the shift action
G\times H\curvearrowright\mathcal{B}
. Considering each copy of
\{0,1\}
with the equiprobability measure, we obtain a product measure
\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{B}
with respect to which the shift action is probability measure preserving and essentially free as the product group is infinite. Extending the action of
G
and
H
diagonally to
\Omega\times\mathcal{B}
we now obtain a new measure equivalence
(\Omega\times\mathcal{B},X\times\mathcal{B},X\times\mathcal{B},\mu\times\mu^{\prime})
for
G
and
H
and where the action of
G\times H
is essentially free. Its measure equivalence cocycles are given by
\alpha^{\prime\prime}\colon G\times(X\times\mathcal{B})\rightarrow H
where
\alpha^{\prime\prime}(g,(x,z))=\alpha(g,x)
and
\beta^{\prime\prime}\colon H\times(X\times\mathcal{B})\rightarrow G
where
\beta^{\prime\prime}(h,(x,z))=\beta(h,x)
which are
\varphi
-integrable and
\psi
-integrable respectively.

Now, the induced actions of
G
and
H
on
(X\times\mathcal{B},\mu_{X}\times\mu^{\prime})
are essentially free and probability measure preserving, where
\mu_{X}=\mu(X)^{-1}\mu|_{X}
.
We claim that these induced actions are orbit equivalent, and that their cocyles are given by the measure equivalence cocycles, which proves that
(X\times\mathcal{B},\mu\times\mu^{\prime})
is a
(\varphi,\psi)
-integrable orbit equivalence coupling.
First, we note that for all
g\in G
, we have
g\ast(x,z)\in\alpha(g,x)^{-1}\ast X\times\mathcal{B}
for a.e.
(x,z)\in X\times\mathcal{B}
. Fixing
g\in G
and letting
h=\alpha(g,x)
, we then have for the induced actions that
g\cdot(x,z)=g\ast\alpha(g,x)\ast(x,z)
and
h\cdot(x,z)=\beta(\alpha(g,x),x)\ast\alpha(g,x)\ast(x,z)
for a.e.
(x,z)\in X\times\mathcal{B}
.
Now, without loss of generality it suffices to show that
\beta(\alpha(g,x),x)=g
for a.e.
(x,z)\in X\times\mathcal{B}
, as it proves that
G\cdot x\subseteq H\cdot x
and that its orbit equivalence cocycle
G\times(X\times\mathcal{B})\to H
is the same as
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
.
This is true since
\alpha(g,x)\ast(x,z)\in\beta(\alpha(g,x),x)^{-1}\ast X\times\mathcal{B}
and
g\ast(x,z)\in\alpha(g,x)^{-1}\ast X_{G}\times\mathcal{B}
for a.e.
(x,z)\in X\times\mathcal{B}
and
X\times\mathcal{B}
is a measure fundamental domain. Moreover, for every
g\in G
, we have
g\cdot(x,z)=\alpha^{\prime\prime}(g,(x,z))\cdot(x,z)
for almost every
(x,z)\in X\times\mathcal{B}
. This concludes the first implication.

Towards proving the converse, let
(X,\nu)
be an orbit equivalence coupling from
G
to
H
that is
(\varphi,\psi)
-integrable. Now, take
\Omega=X\times G
with
\mu=\nu\times\bar{\mu}
, where
\bar{\mu}
is the counting measure. Note that
X
can be seen as the subset
X\times\{e_{G}\}
of
\Omega
.
Next, let
G\times H
act on
(\Omega,\mu)
such that
(g,h)\ast(x,g^{\prime})=(h\cdot x,gg^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}(h,x)^{-1})
. Then
(\Omega,X,X,\mu)
is a
(\varphi,\psi)
-integrable measure equivalence coupling.
Indeed,
\Omega
is standard Borel, as it is the product of two standard Borel spaces. The space
X
is a measure fundamental domain of
G
, since
G
acts by left multiplication on the
G
-component in
\Omega
. Towards proving that
X
is also a fundamental domain for
H
, note that
h\ast(x,e_{G})\in\beta^{\prime}(h,x)^{-1}\ast X
for every
h\in H
and
x\in X
, note that
\beta^{\prime}(h,x)=e_{G}
if and only if
h=e_{H}
for almost every
x\in X
because the action of
H
on
X
is essentially free and note that
h\mapsto\beta^{\prime}(h,x)
is surjective for almost every
x\in X
. Hence,
X
is a fundamental domain for the
H
-action and
\beta(h,(x,e_{G}))=\beta^{\prime}(h,x)
. Finally, note that
(g,\alpha^{\prime}(g,x))\ast(x,e_{G})\in X
, since
\beta^{\prime}(\alpha^{\prime}(g,x),g\cdot x)=g
for every
g\in G
and almost every
x\in X
. So,
\alpha(g,(x,e_{G}))=\alpha^{\prime}(g,x)
.
Thus,
G
and
H
have commuting, measure preserving actions on
\Omega
, both actions have
X
as a fundamental domain with finite measure and the cocycles
\alpha
and
\beta
are
\varphi
-integrable and
\psi
-integrable respectively.
This concludes the proof.
∎

2.2
Ultralimit of probability spaces

We briefly recall the Loeb construction of an ultralimit of probability spaces (see
[
CKD13
,
Car15
]
for more details). This material will be used in §
4.3
. In what follows,
\mathcal{U}
denotes a non-principal ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
.

Let
\{(X_{n},\nu_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite probability spaces. The ultraproduct
X_{\mathcal{U}}
is defined as usual: we consider the equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathcal{U}}
on
\prod_{n}X_{n}
defined as
(x_{n})_{n}\sim_{\mathcal{U}}(y_{n})_{n}
if
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\colon x_{n}=y_{n}\}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and we set

X_{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}X_{n}/\sim_{\mathcal{U}}.

We will denote by
x_{\mathcal{U}}
and
A_{\mathcal{U}}
elements and subsets of
X_{\mathcal{U}}
. For a sequence
\{x_{n}\in X_{n}\}_{n}
, we will denote its class in
X_{\mathcal{U}}
by
[x_{n}]_{\mathcal{U}}
and similarly for a sequence of subsets
\{A_{n}\subset X_{n}\}_{n}
we will denote its class by
[A_{n}]_{\mathcal{U}}
. If each
X_{n}
is a probability space, then there is a canonical way to construct a probability measure on
X_{\mathcal{U}}
, the Loeb probability space, such that sets of the form
[A_{n}]_{\mathcal{U}}
are measurable.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\{(X_{n},\mu_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite probability spaces and let
X_{\mathcal{U}}
be the ultraproduct of the sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n}
. Then there exists a complete probability space
(X_{\mathcal{U}},\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{U}},\mu_{\mathcal{U}})
such that

1.

for every sequence
\{A_{n}\subseteq X_{n}\}_{n}
of subsets, the set
[A_{n}]_{\mathcal{U}}
is measurable and
\mu_{\mathcal{U}}([A_{n}]_{\mathcal{U}})=\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mu_{n}(A_{n})
,

2.

for every measurable subset
A_{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq X_{\mathcal{U}}
there exists a sequence of subsets
\{A_{n}\subseteq X_{n}\}_{n}
such that
\mu_{\mathcal{U}}(A_{\mathcal{U}}\Delta[A_{n}]_{\mathcal{U}})=0
.

3
Statistical coarse geometry of finite metric spaces

This section is devoted to
\mathcal{U}
-statistical metric properties of a sequence of maps between finite metric spaces. We believe that the interest of these definitions goes beyond the setting of this paper, and we encourage the interested reader to extend them even beyond the framework of finite metric spaces
8
8

8

A possible generalization of finite metric space adapted to these notions could be that of a random pointed metric spaces: a measurable family of pointed metric spaces indexed by a probability space.
.

3.1
\mathcal{U}
-statistical coarse properties

We say that a sequence
(X_{n})_{n}
of finite metric spaces is
uniformly locally finite
if for every
R>0
there exists a
N_{R}>0
such that the cardinality
\#B_{X_{n}}(x,R)\leq N_{R}
for every
x
in any of the
X_{n}
where
B_{X_{n}}(x,R)=\{y\in X_{n}\mid d_{X_{n}}(x,y)\leq R\}
.
Given a finite set
X
, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{X}
the uniform probability measure on
X
.

In what follows, we let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-principal ultrafilter on
\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n}
and
(Z_{n})_{n}
be two sequences of metric spaces and
\mathfrak{u}_{n}\colon Y_{n}\to Z_{n}
be a sequence of maps.

•

Assume that
Y_{n}
is finite. We say that the sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective
if

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{Y_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in Y_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{Y_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(y)))\geq C\right\}\right)=0.

•

Assume that
Z_{n}
is finite. We say that the sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective
if

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{Z_{n}}\left(\left\{z\in Z_{n}\mid d_{Z_{n}}(z,\mathfrak{u}_{n}(Y_{n}))\geq C\right\}\right)=0.

•

Assume that
Y_{n}
and
Z_{n}
are finite. The sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely bijective
if it is both
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective and
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective.

Using the material of §
2.2
,
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}
can be interpreted as a probability measure on a certain (non-standard) space. This allows to reformulate the above notions as follows:
\mathcal{U}
-statistical coarse injectivity means that the probability that the diameter of the preimages are larger than
C
tends to zero as
C
tends to infinity. Similarly, is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective means that the probability that an element of the target space is at distance
C
from the image tends to zero as
C
tends to infinity.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n}
and
(Z_{n})_{n}
be two sequences of uniformly locally finite metric spaces and let
\mathfrak{u}_{n}\colon Y_{n}\to Z_{n}
be a sequence of maps, then

•

If the sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective, then
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\#Z_{n}/\#Y_{n}
is finite.

•

If the sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective, then
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\#Y_{n}/\#\operatorname{Im}\mathfrak{u}_{n}
(so in particular
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\#Y_{n}/\#Z_{n}
) is finite.

Proof.

When this sequence of maps is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective, then there exists
C>0
such that
\#\{z\in Z_{n}\mid d_{Z_{n}}(z,\mathfrak{u}_{n}(Y_{n}))\leq C\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\#Z_{n}
. As
(Z_{n})_{n}
is uniformly locally finite, for every
C
there exists a constant
N_{C}
such that every ball in
Z_{n}
of radius
C
has cardinality at most
N_{C}
for any
n
. Hence,
\frac{1}{2}\#Z_{n}\leq N_{C}\#\mathfrak{u}_{n}(Y_{n})\leq N_{C}\#Y_{n}
. Therefore
\#Z_{n}/\#Y_{n}\leq 2N_{C}
, which proves the first claim.

When this sequence of maps is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective, then there exists
C>0
such that

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{Y_{n}}\left(\{y\in Y_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{Y_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(y)))\leq C\}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

As
(Y_{n})_{n}
is uniformly locally finite, for every
C
there exists a constant
N^{\prime}_{C}
such that every ball in
Y_{n}
of radius
C
has cardinality at most
N^{\prime}_{C}
for any
n
. Hence,
\frac{1}{2}\#Y_{n}\leq N^{\prime}_{C}\#\mathfrak{u}_{n}(Y_{n})
. Therefore
\#Y_{n}/\#\mathfrak{u}_{n}(Y_{n})\leq 2N^{\prime}_{C}
, which proves the second claim.
∎

We now turn to “statistical versions” of metric properties such as: Lipschitz, expansivity, coarse equivalence…

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n}
and
(Z_{n})_{n}
be two sequences of finite metric spaces that are uniformly locally finite and
\mathfrak{u}_{n}\colon Y_{n}\to Z_{n}
be a sequence of maps.

•

The sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz
if for every
r>0
,

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{Y_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in Y_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{Z_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(B_{Y_{n}}(y,r))\geq C\right\}\right)=0.

•

The sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically expansive
if for every
r>0,

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{Y_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in Y_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{Y_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(B_{Z_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(y),r))\geq C\right\}\right)=0.

•

The sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically a coarse equivalence
if it is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz,
\mathcal{U}
-statistically expansive and
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely bijective.

An example of a
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarse equivalence is a sequence of quasi-isometries with uniform constants.

Remark 3.4
.

Note that
\mathcal{U}
-statistically expansivity implies
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarse injectivity.

3.2
Reformulation for sofic approximations of finitely generated groups

We start by the following useful observation.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G
and
H
be two groups each with finite generating set
S_{G}
and
S_{H}
, respectively, and let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n}
be sequences of sofic approximations of the Cayley graphs
(G,S_{G})
and
(H,S_{H})
respectively.
If
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely bijective, then for all
r\geq 0

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r)}\right\}\right)=1.

Proof.

Let
\eta=\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\#\mathcal{H}_{n}/\#\mathcal{G}_{n}
, which by

Proposition

3.2
is finite.
Since balls of bounded radius in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
have bounded size (because
\mathcal{G}_{n}
has bounded degree),
\mathcal{U}
-statistical coarse injectivity implies that

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\#\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(x)\leq C\right\}\right)=1.

Hence it is enough to prove that for each
C
,

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r)},\;\#\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(x)\leq C\right\}\right)=0

But this quantity is at most

\eta C\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{H}_{n}\mid y\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r)}\right\}\right),

which equals
0
since
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is a sofic approximation.
∎

Note that a map between two graphs is
C
-Lipschitz if and only if adjacent vertices are sent to vertices at distance at most
C
. Something similar happens for
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz maps between sofic approximations, as shown by the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
G
and
H
be two groups each with finite generating set
S_{G}
and
S_{H}
, respectively, and let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n}
be sequences of sofic approximations of the Cayley graphs
(G,S_{G})
and
(H,S_{H})
respectively.
Let
\mathfrak{u}_{n}\colon\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
be a sequence of maps.
The following are equivalent:

1.

for every
s\in S_{G}
we have

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(y),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(ys))\leq C\right\}\right)=1;

2.

for every
g\in G
we have

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(y),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(yg))\leq C\right\}\right)=1;

3.

the sequence
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz

Proof.

Clearly
2
implies
1
. The fact that
3
implies
2
results from the following inequality

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(B_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(y,|g|)))\leq C\right\}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(y),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(yg))\leq C\right\}\right).

Conversely, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(B_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(y,R)))\geq C\right\}\right)\leq\sum_{|g|\leq R}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(B_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(y,|g|)))\leq C\right\}\right),

which gives that
2
implies
3
.

To complete the proof of the proposition, it thus remains to show that
1
implies
2
. This simply follows by writting
g=s_{1}\ldots s_{n}
and arguing by triangle inequality. We leave the details to the reader.
∎

Below is an analogous result for expansivity.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G
and
H
be two groups each with finite generating set
S_{G}
and
S_{H}
, respectively, and let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n}
be sequences of sofic approximations of the Cayley graphs
(G,S_{G})
and
(H,S_{H})
respectively.
Let
\mathfrak{u}_{n}\colon\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
be a sequence of maps.

1.

If the sequence
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective, then it is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically expansive if and only if for every
h\in H
we have

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x))\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)h))\geq C\right\}\right)=0.

2.

If the sequence
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
has
C^{\prime}
-dense images, and has preimages of bounded diameter, then it is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically expansive if and only if for every
h\in B_{H}(e_{H},2C^{\prime}+1)
,

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(y)\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(yh))\geq C\right\}\right)=0

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{U}
-statistical expansivity obviously implies (
1
), the useful implication being the converse one.
Assume (
1
) holds. Then by
Proposition

3.5
, we have

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(B(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),r))\geq C\right\}\right)

is equal to

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(B(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),r))\geq C,\;\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r)}\right\}\right).

Assuming that
\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r)}
implies that the ball of radius
r
around it are vertices of the form
\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)h
for
h\in H
with
|h|\leq r
.
Hence the latter is at most

\sum_{h\in H,|h|\leq r}\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x))\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)h))\geq C\right\}\right)

which equals
0
by (
1
).

Let us prove the second statement.
First by our assumption on the diameter of preimages,
1
is equivalent to having for all
h\in H
,

\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(y)\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(yh))\geq C\right\}\right)=0.

Now assume that the latter holds for
|h|\leq 2C^{\prime}+1
.
We argue by contradiction, letting
h\in H
be the shortest element for which we do not have convergence. First, note that
|h|\geq 2C^{\prime}+2
. Thus, we can take
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
with
h=h_{1}h_{2}
,
|h_{1}|,|h_{2}|\geq C^{\prime}+1
and
|h_{1}|+|h_{2}|=|h|
. Next, for every
y\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
there exists an
h_{y}\in B_{H}(e_{H},C^{\prime})
such that
zh_{1}h_{y}\in\operatorname{Im}(\mathfrak{u}_{n})
. Observe that
|h_{z}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1}h|=|h_{z}^{-1}h_{2}^{-1}|\leq 2C^{\prime}+1
.
Hence

\displaystyle\lim_{C\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(y)\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(yh))\geq C\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{C\to\infty}\sum_{h^{\prime}\in B_{H}(e_{H},C^{\prime})}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(y)\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(yh_{1}h^{\prime}))\geq\frac{C}{2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle+\lim_{C\to\infty}\sum_{h^{\prime}\in B_{H}(e_{H},C^{\prime})}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{y\in\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{n})\mid\operatorname{diam}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(yh_{1}h^{\prime})\cup\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(yh))\geq\frac{C}{2}\right\}\right).

Observe that
|h^{\prime-1}h_{1}^{-1}h|\leq 2C^{\prime}+1
, hence both terms equal
0
, leading to a contradiction. This ends the proof.
∎

4
Construction of a measure coupling

This section contains the core of the paper, namely the proofs of Theorems
1.6
, and
1.7
.
It is organized as follows:
First, §
4.1
provides a “topological coupling”, namely a Polish metric space
\Omega
on which the two groups admits free commuting actions by homeomorphisms. The topological space in question is roughly the set of functions from
G
to
H
, slightly modified to “force” the
G
-action to be free. This coupling does not require any specific assumption on the groups, but is not yet equipped with an invariant measure.

In §
4.3
, we independently construct a measure coupling (subgroup/ME depending on the context), which is obtained as follows. We start taking an ultralimit of probability spaces
(X,\nu)=\lim_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{G}_{n},\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}})
, which comes equipped with a p.m.p. action of
G
, and with a cocycle
\overline{\alpha}:G\times X\to H
, whose existence, relying on the maps
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
, is proved in §
4.2
. This part of the argument has been roughly outlined in the introduction.

We then push forward the measure
\nu
on the space
\Omega
through the map
X\to\Omega
using the cocycle, yielding a measure coupling between the groups (either subgroup or ME depending on the conditions on
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
).

4.1
The topological coupling space
\Omega

We shall make the following assumptions, depending on whether we aim to construct a ME-coupling between
G
and
H
or simply a measure subgroup coupling from
G
to
H
.

•

In the first case we let
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n}
be sofic approximations of
G
and
H
respectively and
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
be a sequence of maps. Various assumptions will be made on this sequence of maps, but ultimately, we will assume it to be
\mathcal{U}
-statistically a coarse equivalence.
As we shall see partial results will be obtained under weaker assumptions, in particular assuming that
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz and
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely bijective.

•

In the second case, we will consider a sequence
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to H
, where
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz and
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective (or simply injective, which should be enough for applications).

We start with a informal discussion to motivate our definition of the coupling space below.
The first idea is to work with the space
H^{G}
, which comes equipped with the action of
G\times H
:

(g,h)\ast f=hf(g^{-1}\cdot)
. Note that
H
acts freely, and a fundamental domain
X_{H}
for
H
is simply the set of functions mapping
e_{G}
to
e_{H}
. Moreover the
G
-action is free in restriction to the subspace of injective maps:
G\to H
.

The second step is to equip the space
H^{G}
with a
G\times H
-invariant Borel measure. This can be done exploiting the sequence of maps
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
(see §
4.4
). Under the additional assumption that the
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
are injective, the measure will be supported on injective maps, and so our
G
-action will be essentially free. In this case,
G
acts essentially freely and it is easy to define a Borel fundamental domain for
G
as well (in restriction to this subspace), which contains
X_{H}
.
But a problem arises if we only assume that
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
are
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective. Under this sole assumption, the measure will only be supported on functions with finite pre-images, which is not sufficient to ensure that the
G
-action is essentially free.

In
[
Das18
]
, Das considered a special case where
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
were (uniform) quasi-isometries. Since quasi-isometries have pre-images of bounded cardinality, the problem was solved by replacing
H
by
H\times F
where
F
is a finite group, and perturbating the maps
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
to make them injective.

Here, in the absence of a uniform bound on the size of preimages, we will have to enlarge the space to
H\times\mathbb{N}
. To force the injectivity of the maps
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
, we define auxilliary maps
\rho_{n}\colon\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathbb{N}
as follows.
For every
x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
, we fix an arbitrary indexing of
\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\{\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\})=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{m-1}\}
. We then define
\rho_{n}(x_{i})=i
. This provides us with injective maps
\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}\times\mathbb{N}\colon x\to(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),\rho_{n}(x))
. Moreover, this leads us to the following definition of coupling space (the relation between the two will become clear in §
4.4
).

Definition 4.1

(Coupling space)
.

Let
\Omega=(H\times\mathbb{N})^{G}=H^{G}\times\mathbb{N}^{G}
, endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence, which is a Polish space. We consider the natural (continuous) action of
G\times H
:
(g,h)*(\mathfrak{a}_{H}(x),\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{N}}(x))=(h\mathfrak{a}_{H}(g^{-1}x),\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{N}}(g^{-1}x))
for
g,x\in G
,
h\in H
and
(\mathfrak{a}_{H},\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{N}})\in(H\times\mathbb{N})^{G}
.

Note that

\Omega_{0}=\left\{(\mathfrak{a}_{H},\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{N}})\in\Omega\mid\mathfrak{a}_{H}(e_{G})=e_{H}\right\}

defines a clopen fundamental domain for the action of
H
.

Definition 4.2

(Basis of the topology)
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq G
be a finite subset,
\mathfrak{a}\in\Omega
. Then, we define

W_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma}=\{\mathfrak{c}\in\Omega\mid\forall g\in\Sigma\colon\mathfrak{a}(g)=\mathfrak{c}(g)\}.

We denote by
\mathfrak{A}_{\tau}
the collection of subsets
W_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma}
and the emptyset.

Clearly,
\mathfrak{A}_{\tau}
forms a basis for the topology on
\Omega
. The
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{A}_{\tau}
contains the entire topology
\tau
of
\Omega
. Indeed since
(\Omega,\tau)
is second countable, every set in
\tau
is a countable union of basis elements in
\mathfrak{A}_{\tau}
. It follows that
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)=\sigma(\mathfrak{A}_{\tau})
and
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\right)
is standard Borel. Note also that since
G
is countable, the sets
W_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma}
are measurable for all (not necessarily finite) subsets
\Sigma\subset G
.

Remark 4.3
.

For all
g\in G
,
h\in H
and
W_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma}\in\mathfrak{A}_{\tau}
we have that

(g,h)*W_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma}=W_{(g,h)*\mathfrak{a},g\Sigma}.

4.2
Almost cocycles

Our main tool is a notion of “almost cocycle” associated to the maps
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
and to the “almost actions” of the groups
G
and
H
on their sofic approximations.

The idea is to associate for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
a map
T_{n}(\cdot,x):G\to H
such that

\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg)=\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)T_{n}(g,x)
. Such map is defined unambiguously if
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
takes values in
H
(in our “context of measured subgroups”).

We want to think of
T_{n}
as a cocycle, i.e. a map satisfying the following cocycle relation

T_{n}(gg^{\prime},x)=T_{n}(g,x)T_{n}(g^{\prime},xg).

(4)

Of course, such a cocycle does not exist as we don’t have actions of
G
and
H
on
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\mathcal{G}_{n}}
. Note that even when such actions exist: e.g. if
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{G}_{n}
are finite quotients of
H
and
G
, the fact that
H
-action is not free forces
T_{n}
to depend on some arbitrary choice that prevents it from satisfying (
4
).
Here is a precise definition of
T_{n}
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
G
and
H
be two groups with sofic approximations
(\mathcal{G}_{n})_{n}
and
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n}
, and let
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
(or
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to H
) be a sequence of maps.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define a map
T_{n}\colon G\times\mathcal{G}_{n}\to H\cup\{\infty\}
as follows.
If the following conditions are satisfied (in case
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to H
, we omit the second condition):

•

x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(|g|)}
,

•

\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r)},
where
r=d(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg))
,

then we let
T_{n}(g,x)=h
, where
h
is the unique element of
H
of length
r
such that
\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg)=\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)h
else we set
T_{n}(g,x)=\infty
.

For all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{n}(g,g^{\prime})
the set of elements
x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(|g|)}
such that (
4
) makes sense and is satisfied.

Remark 4.5
.

We start observing that
\mathcal{C}_{n}(g,g^{\prime})
is contained in the set
x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(|g|)}
such that
\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(r_{n}(x))},
where
r_{n}(x)=\max\{d(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg)),d(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xgg^{\prime})\}
.

The following proposition shows that
T_{n}
plays the role of an “almost cocycle” in the sense that it will be behave “statistically” like a cocycle.

Proposition 4.6
.

Assuming

•

either that
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to H
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz
;

•

or that
\mathfrak{u}_{n}:\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz and
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely bijective
,

then for every
g,g^{\prime}\in G
,

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\mathcal{C}_{n}(g,g^{\prime})\right)=1,

and

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{|T_{n}(g,x)|_{S_{H}}\leq R)\right\}\right)=1.

Proof.

The first case is straightforward: the first statement directly stems from the fact that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is a sofic approximation of
G
, and the second one from the
\mathcal{U}
-statistical Lipschitz condition. Let us focus on the second case, which is more subtle. By Remark
4.5
the first statement follows if we can prove that

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(r)}\mid\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{n}^{r_{n}(x)}\right\}\right)=1,

where
r=\max\{g,gg^{\prime}\}
, and
r(x)=\max\{d(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg)),d(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xgg^{\prime})\}
.

Since
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is statistically Lipschitz, we have

\lim_{R\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(r)}\mid r_{n}(x)\leq R\right\}\right)=1.

Hence, it is enough to show that for every
R
,
\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}^{(r)}\mid\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(R)}\right\}\right)=0
, or simply that

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\notin\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(R)}\right\}\right)=0.

Now, since
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective, we have that
\#\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x))
is bounded up to an arbitrarily small proportion of
x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Therefore we are left to show that

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\frac{\#\left(\mathcal{H}_{n}^{(R)}\right)^{c}}{\#\mathcal{G}_{n}}=0,

Finally, since the sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely surjective, we have, by
Proposition

3.2
, that
\#\mathcal{G}_{n}\leq C\#\mathcal{H}_{n}
for some constant
C>0
, so the result follows from the fact that
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is a sofic approximation. Thus, the first statement follows. The second statement results from the fact that for every
g
, with probability tending to
1
,
|T_{n}(g,x)|_{S_{H}}=d_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg),\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x))
, combined with the fact that
\mathfrak{u}_{n}
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically Lipschitz (we leave the straightforward details to the reader).

∎

We end this paragraph with the following facts that will come in complement of the previous proposition. Recall that
\rho_{n}
is the auxilliary function
\mathcal{G}_{n}\to\mathbb{N}
defined in §
4.1
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Assuming that the sequence
(\mathfrak{u}_{n})
is
\mathcal{U}
-statistically coarsely injective
. Then,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\rho_{n}(x)\leq N\right\}\right)=1.

Moreover, for every
g\in G\setminus\{e_{G}\}
,

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg)=\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)\;{\text{a}nd}\;\rho_{n}(xg)=\rho_{n}(x)\right\}\right)=0,

and

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\exists g\in G,\;\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)=\mathfrak{u}_{n}(xg)\textnormal{ and }\rho_{n}(xg)=0\right\}\right)=1.

In other words,

\lim_{\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{n}\mid\exists g\in G,\;T_{n}(g,x)=e_{H}\textnormal{ and }\rho_{n}(xg)=0\right\}\right)=1.

Proof.

By definition of
\rho_{n}
,
\rho_{n}(x)
is at most the cardinality of
\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x))
. Hence the first statement is an immediate consequence of
\mathcal{U}
-statistical coarse injectivity. Let
r_{n}:=\operatorname{diam}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}^{-1}(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(x)))
.
The second statement follows from the definition of

8
Application to the class
\mathcal{M}

This section is dedicated to the proof of
Theorem

1.11
. Our main contribution is the following more specific theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
has a real eigenvalue
\lambda>1
.
Then there exists a mutually cobounded
(\operatorname{L}^{\infty},\exp^{\diamond})
-measure equivalence coupling from
\mathcal{L}_{k}
to
\operatorname{SOL}_{A}=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Recall that according to our notation,
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\pi:\mathcal{L}_{k}\to\mathbb{Z}
be the projection to
\mathbb{Z}
. Given
q\geq 1
, the pre-image of
q\mathbb{Z}
by
\pi
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z})^{q}\wr\mathbb{Z}
, which itself is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to
\mathcal{L}_{k^{q}}
. In particular,
\mathcal{L}_{k^{q}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{k}
are quasi-isometric. Hence in order to prove the theorem, it is harmless to assume that
k
is arbitrarily large. For reasons that will appear later (which are not essential), we will assume from now on that
k\geq\lambda
.

We will use the fact that
\operatorname{SOL}_{A}
is a uniform lattice in the group
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{R}
where
\mathbb{R}
acts by
t\cdot(x,y)=(k^{t}x,k^{-t}y)
.

The proof of the theorem goes in three steps: first we construct a sequence of maps between Følner sequences of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
(by far the main step of the proof). Actually we will construct a unique map between the groups and consider its restriction to a Følner sequence. Second, we will perturb this map slightly (at bounded distance) to have it land in a copy of
\operatorname{SOL}_{A}
embedded as a lattice in
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Along the way all the metric estimates will be in terms of the word metrics of the groups. Hence a last technical step will be required to translate them in terms of the intrinsic graph distances of the Følner sets.

First step: constructing a map from
\mathcal{L}_{k}
to
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

We start defining generating sets for the groups
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
We let
S^{\mathcal{L}}=S_{1}^{\mathcal{L}}\cup S_{2}^{\mathcal{L}}
, where
S_{1}^{\mathcal{L}}
consists of elements
(x,0)
, where
x
is supported at
0
, and
S_{2}^{\mathcal{L}}=\{(0,\pm 1\}
.
Similarly we let
S^{\operatorname{SOL}}=S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}}\cup S_{2}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
, where
S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
consists of elements
(x,0)
, where
\|x\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and
S_{2}^{\operatorname{SOL}}=\{(0,t\}
, with
|t|\leq 1
. We shall denote by
|g|
the word length of an element of either
\mathcal{L}_{k}
or
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
We shall use repeatedly the following easy estimates, whose verifications are left to the reader.
For
g=(x,j)\in\mathcal{L}_{k}
,

\max\left\{\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{supp}x\cup\{0\}),|j|\right\}\leq|g|\leq 2\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{supp}x\cup\{0\})+|j|.

and for
g=(x,j)\in\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}},

\max\left\{\log_{k}\|x\|_{\infty},|j|\right\}\leq|g|\leq 2\log_{k}(1+\|x\|_{\infty})+2|j|.

We consider the following Følner sequences of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
:

F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}}=\left\{(x,m)\mid\operatorname{supp}x\subset[-n,n],\;0\leq m\leq n\right\},

and

F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}=\left\{(x,m)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\mid\|x\|_{\infty}\leq k^{n+1},\;0\leq m\leq n\right\}.

Let us check that these are indeed right Følner sequences. For both, almost invariance under right multiplication by
S_{2}^{\mathcal{L}}
(resp.
S_{2}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
) is true because it only changes the second coefficient. For
F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}}
, we observe that it is invariant under right multiplication by
S_{1}^{\mathcal{L}}
, so it is duly a Følner sequence. Note that right multiplication by
S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
preserves each coset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Besides, for every

, we have

([0,k^{n+1}]^{2}\times\{j\})S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}}\subset[-k^{j},k^{n+1}+k^{j}]^{2}\times\{j\}.

The Haar measure
\lambda
on
\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
is simply the product of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Therefore, we have

\lambda(([0,k^{n+1}]^{2}\times\{j\})S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}})\leq(k^{n+1}+2k^{j})^{2},

so

\lambda(F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}})\leq\int_{0}^{n}(k^{n+1}+2k^{j})^{2}dj=nk^{2n+2}+4k^{n+1}\int_{0}^{n}k^{j}dj+2\int_{0}^{n}k^{2j}dj\leq(n+6)k^{2n+2},

And since
\lambda(F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}})=nk^{2n+2}
, we deduce that

\frac{\lambda(F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}S_{1}^{\operatorname{SOL}})}{\lambda(F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}})}\leq\frac{n+6}{n},

which tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Hence
F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
is a Følner sequence.

We now define a map
\mathfrak{v}:\mathcal{L}_{k}\to\operatorname{SOL}_{\mathbb{R}}
as follows. For
x=(\varepsilon_{i})\in\oplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
, we set

\mathfrak{v}(x,m)=\left(\sum\varepsilon_{i}k^{i},\sum\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i},m\right).

Before analyzing the properties of this map, let us pause, and work out the following baby case as a warmup. Consider the function
f:\oplus_{n}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined for each
x=(\varepsilon_{i})
by
f(x)=\sum\varepsilon_{i}k^{i}
. Suppose we equip the group
\oplus_{n}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
with the length
|x|=\max(\operatorname{supp}x)
, and
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is equipped with the distance
d(x,y)=\log(1+|x-y|)
. Note that this is similar to the restriction of
\mathfrak{v}
to
\bigoplus_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
. It is easy to see that the map
f
is injective, Lipschitz, and takes values in
\mathbb{N}
. Besides, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

maps bijectively
K_{n}=\{x\mid\operatorname{supp}x\subset[0,n]\}
to
I_{n}=[0,k^{n+1}-1]\cap\mathbb{N}
. By contrast, this map is not coarsely expansive. This results from the fact that
\mathbb{R}
is Archimedean, which manifests itself through carries: assume we add
1
to
x=\sum\varepsilon_{i}k^{i}
, and then write the
k
-adic decomposition of the resulting number, we get
x+1=\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\neq\varepsilon_{i}
for all
i\leq m
, where
m
is the smallest integer such that
\varepsilon_{i}\neq k-1
. Hence the pre-image of

lies at distance
m
from
x
, and
m
can be arbitrarily large. However, large values of

happen “exponentially rarely”. Let us indeed evaluate the proportion of elements of
x\in K_{n}
such that
d(x,f^{-1}(f(x)+1))\geq m
: since it imposes that the first
m-1
coordinates of

are equal to

, this proportion is at most
k^{-m+1}
.

We are now ready to study our map
\mathfrak{v}
, for which a similar phenomenon occurs, although in a more complicated way, as shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

The map
\mathfrak{v}
is
2
-Lipschitz, and maps

to a
(1+\log_{k}(1+2k))
-coarsely dense subset of
F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
.
It satisfies the following (statistic expansivity) property: for all integers

,
the proportion of points
g\in F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}}
such that
\operatorname{diam}\mathfrak{v}^{-1}(B_{\operatorname{SOL}}(\mathfrak{v}_{n}(g),q))\geq 2m+3q

4k^{-m+1}
. Finally, For every
r

(2q+1)k^{2q+1}
.

Proof.

Let us prove Lipschitz: for this it is enough to consider neighboring vertices: for those who differ by their
\mathbb{Z}
-coordinate, the statement is obvious, so consider the case of two elements
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{k}
such that
g^{\prime}=gs
for some
s\in S_{1}^{\mathcal{L}}
. We then have
g=(x,j)
and

where
x=(\varepsilon_{i})
and

only differ in their
j
-coordinate. We have
\mathfrak{v}(g)=(y,j)
, where
y=(\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{i},\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i})
and
\mathfrak{v}(g^{\prime})=(y^{\prime},j)

\mathfrak{v}(g)^{-1}\mathfrak{v}(g^{\prime})=\left(\sum_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})k^{i-j},\sum_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})k^{j-i},0\right)=(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j}-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{j}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{j},0),

which has length at most
2
. Hence the map is
2
-Lipschitz.

Let us prove that
\mathfrak{v}
maps
F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}}
to a coarsely dense subset of
F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
. The fact that
\mathfrak{v}(F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}})\subset F_{n}^{\operatorname{SOL}}
is obvious, so we focus on the coarse density. We shall prove that every element

lies at bounded distance from
\mathfrak{v}(F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}})
. Perturbating

at distance at most one, we can assume that
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
g=(x,j)\in\mathcal{L}_{k}
, with

.
We have
\mathfrak{v}(g)=\left(\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{i},\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i},j\right)

\mathfrak{v}(g)^{-1}h=\left(\left(k^{-j}a-\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{i-j}\right),\left(k^{j}b-\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i+j}\right),0\right).

Since

, we have
\lfloor k^{-j}a\rfloor\leq k^{n-j+1}
and
\lfloor k^{j-1}b\rfloor\leq k^{n+j}

-adic decompositions are respectively supported in
[0,n-j]
and
[0,n+j-1]
. Now, choose
\varepsilon_{i}
be such that

is the
k
-adic decomposition of

\sum_{i<j}\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i+j-1}
is the
k
-adic decomposition of
\lfloor k^{j-1}b\rfloor

\varepsilon_{i}
is supported on

, hence that

.
Besides,

\left|k^{-j}a-\sum_{i\geq j}\varepsilon_{i}k^{i-j}\right|=k^{-j}a-\lfloor k^{-j}a\rfloor\leq 1

\left|k^{j}b-\sum_{i<j}\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i+j}\right|=k\left|k^{j-1}b-\sum_{i<j}\varepsilon_{i}k^{-i+j-1}\right|=k(k^{j-1}b-\lfloor k^{j-1}b\rfloor)\leq k.

We therefore have
\left|k^{-j}a-\sum_{i}\varepsilon_{i}k^{i-j}\right|\leq 2
and

We now turn our attention to the last two properties, which will be proved all at once.

g\in F_{n}^{\mathcal{L}}
.
Let

such that

g^{-1}g^{\prime}=((-j)\cdot(x^{\prime}-x),j^{\prime}-j).

Write

and
x^{\prime}=(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i})
. We have

\mathfrak{v}(g)^{-1}\mathfrak{v}(g^{\prime})=\left(\sum_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})k^{i-j},\sum_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}-\varepsilon_{i})k^{j-i},j^{\prime}-j\right).

, hence
\operatorname{diam}((\operatorname{supp}(x^{\prime}-x)-j)\cup\{0\})\geq m+q

\ell_{+},\ell_{-}
be respectively the maximal, minimal integer such that
\varepsilon_{\ell_{+}}\neq\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\ell_{+}}

\varepsilon_{\ell_{-}}\neq\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\ell_{-}}

Note that either
