Bikini Skar: A Novel Approach to Extended Lipoabdominoplasty for Minimally Visible Scars
Authors: Juan M. Martínez, Marco E. Guerrero, Natalia A. Lacouture
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Abdominoplasty is a widely performed procedure to improve abdominal contour, particularly in patients with significant weight loss or postpartum changes. Despite advances in technique, scar visibility and asymmetry remain common concerns. The Bikini Skar technique was developed to optimize scar placement using vector-based planning, aiming to improve aesthetic outcomes and conceal scars within minimal clothing.Methods:We conducted a retrospective study of 20 female patients who underwent extended lipoabdominoplasty using the Bikini Skar technique between 2023 and 2024. Inclusion criteria included a body mass index of 30 kg/m2 or less and excess infraumbilical skin or diastasis. Patients with uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes mellitus, autoimmune disorders, or venous disorders were excluded. Pre- and postoperative photographs were taken at 72 hours, 1 month, 3 months, and 1 year. Patient satisfaction was measured using the BODY-Q scale. All patients provided informed consent for the anonymized use of data.Results:The average fat removal was 4500 mL. At 1-year follow-up, 95% of patients reported high satisfaction with scar position, body contour, and gluteal appearance. Complications were minor and occurred in 25% of cases, including seromas, hypertrophic scarring, and wound dehiscence, all of which resolved without major intervention.Conclusions:The Bikini Skar technique is a safe, reproducible alternative to traditional abdominoplasty that improves scar concealment and overall patient satisfaction. By applying cross-vector tension strategies, it achieves aesthetically pleasing results while reducing common complications related to scar migration and lateral skin redundancy.

Takeaways

Question: Do scar visibility and asymmetry in traditional abdominoplasty procedures often lead to patient dissatisfaction, particularly when scars extend beyond the bikini line?

Findings: In a retrospective study of 20 patients undergoing extended lipoabdominoplasty with the Bikini Skar technique, 95% reported high satisfaction with scar concealment and overall aesthetic outcomes. Complication rates were low and minor, with no major adverse events reported at 1-year follow-up.

Meaning: The Bikini Skar technique offers a safe, reproducible approach to enhance contouring while positioning the scar discreetly within the bikini line for superior aesthetic results.

INTRODUCTION

Body contouring has become both a health and aesthetic priority, particularly as obesity and postpartum changes increasingly impact the global population. As a result, the demand for abdominal contouring procedures has grown significantly. Abdominoplasty has emerged as a standard procedure performed by plastic surgeons to address excess abdominal skin and skin laxity.1

The earliest documented abdominal contouring procedure was performed by Kelly in 1899 at Johns Hopkins Hospital, where he coined the term “abdominal lipectomy.” Kelly noted that resecting excess skin not only improved aesthetic appearance but also addressed abdominal wall pathologies.2 Over time, this procedure has undergone various refinements. In 1924, Thorek introduced umbilical detachment to facilitate broader tissue removal. In 1957, Sir Harold Gillies advocated for flexed knees postoperatively to reduce scar tension, whereas Gonzáles Ulloa’s belt lipectomy in 1960 advanced aesthetic outcomes.3 More recently, techniques such as liposuction-assisted abdominoplasty (Illouz, 1992) and lipoabdominoplasty (Avelar and Saldanha) have further enhanced results.4

Despite these advances, common aesthetic complications include “dog ears” and poorly positioned scars.5 These are often preventable through precise preoperative planning and scar extension. Importantly, cephalic tension forces can displace scars superiorly.6 Given the aesthetic significance of discreet scarring, particularly in relation to swimwear and intimate apparel, we propose the Bikini Skar technique. Various articles in the literature address the management of abdominoplasty techniques to achieve symmetrical and aesthetic scars, by managing variations in marking, incision design, intraoperative positioning, and even the use of anchoring sutures.7

This approach uses strategic vector planning to optimize scar positioning, prevent cephalic migration, and reduce lateral tissue redundancy. The technique aims to deliver symmetrical, aesthetically pleasing results while enabling patients to wear minimal clothing without visible scarring.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study included 20 female patients who underwent extended lipoabdominoplasty using the Bikini Skar technique between 2023 and 2024.

Inclusion Criteria

The study included adult female patients with infraumbilical skin laxity, diastasis recti, a history of massive weight loss, and a body mass index of 30 kg/m2 or less. All patients sought abdominal contour improvement through lipoabdominoplasty.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients with a body mass index greater than 30 kg/m2, uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes mellitus, autoimmune disorders, venous insufficiency, or bleeding disorders were excluded.

Data Collection

Photographic documentation was obtained preoperatively and at 72 hours, 1 month, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Patient satisfaction was measured using the BODY-Q, a validated patient-reported outcome instrument designed for body contouring procedures following weight loss. The survey assessed satisfaction with body image, abdominal appearance, scar location, and gluteal contour. All patients provided written informed consent for the use of anonymized clinical data and images for research and educational purposes.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Marking

With the patient in the supine position, bimanual cephalic traction was applied to the abdominal flap. A central line was drawn 5–7 cm cephalic to the vulvar notch, depending on the patient’s anatomy. Lateral extensions of 5–6 cm were marked on each side of the previous mark (totaling 10–12 cm).

Guided by the inguinal fold angle, a new angle was projected toward the anterior superior iliac crest (typically 15–20 cm). Although seated, the markings were checked for symmetry.

Lateral extension markings were guided by 2 anatomical landmarks: the superior gluteal border and the posterior axillary line. A line was marked from the inguinal anterior line to the superior gluteal border. A pinch test was used to delineate the lateral resection area. In patients with minimal laxity, the posterior axillary line was used as the maximum extent for fat excision beneath the iliac crest. Liposculpture

Abdominal liposuction was performed using the Saldanha technique. Modified Klein tumescent solution was infiltrated, followed by Vibration Amplification of Sound Energy at Resonance (VASER)–assisted emulsification and power-assisted liposuction. Deep and superficial liposuction targeted central negative spaces (the midline and semilunar lines), with deep liposuction carried out across the remainder of the abdomen, including the portion of the flap that would remain after skin resection. The patient was then repositioned prone for posterior liposuction and gluteal fat grafting using the static injection, migration, and equalization technique.8

Abdominoplasty

In the prone position, the extended lateral and posterior resections were performed as previously marked limits. The superficial fascia layer of subcutaneous tissue was removed in the first third of the extension, preserving deep fascia to avoid gluteal deformity, whereas the remaining two-thirds of the extension tissue was excised down to the external oblique fascia in its posterior portion. Layered closure was completed, and the patient was repositioned supine. A detailed explanation of extended lateral resection is provided in Video 2. A suprapubic incision and dissection were carried out to the rectus sheath, extending superiorly to the umbilical area; umbilical exteriorization was performed, and dissection continued up to the xiphoid process, followed by rectus plication. With the patient positioned in a semiseated position at 25–30 degrees, the flap was advanced and resected using a mirrored approach at the same angle as the inguinal fold (as in the case presented in the video, 145 degrees), to maintain tension vector alignment and to ensure adequate scar positioning (Fig. 1). Diamond umbilicoplasty was performed, and the umbilicus was fixed in place. No Baroudi sutures were used during the procedure. A drain was inserted via a counter incision at the mons pubis.

RESULTS

All 20 procedures were completed successfully. The average fat volume extracted was 4500 mL, and the mean gluteal fat grafting volume was 600 mL. Using the BODY-Q scale, patients reported high satisfaction regarding abdominal appearance, scar position, and overall body image at the 1-year follow-up. The overall satisfaction rate was 95% (Fig. 2).

The scar was effectively concealed under minimal clothing, with no visible lateral extensions. DISCUSSION

Scar placement remains a significant concern for patients undergoing abdominoplasty, often shaping their decision to pursue surgery in the first place. Traditional techniques can result in visible, high-riding scars that surpass the boundaries of underwear or bikini lines, undermining patient satisfaction despite an otherwise successful contour. This is especially distressing for patients who anticipate wearing swimwear or fitted clothing postoperatively and expect the scar to remain discreetly concealed. Superior displacement of the scar is not only aesthetically displeasing but can also lead to functional and anatomical distortions, such as the upward displacement of pubic hair onto the lower abdomen.9 These complications are frequently associated with inadequate preoperative planning, surgical techniques that fail to respect the natural dynamics of tissue movement, and insufficient anchoring or fixation of the abdominal flap during closure.9,10

Recent advancements in surgical technique and medical literature emphasize the importance of incorporating biomechanical principles into surgical procedures. Abdominoplasty incorporates this into planning, particularly the use of traction vectors to optimize both functional and aesthetic outcomes. The concept of traction vectors is fundamental for redistributing skin and fascial tension in a way that respects natural tissue movement and healing patterns. This approach not only improves abdominal contour and waist definition but also significantly reduces the risk of complications such as scar migration, tissue redundancy, or skin necrosis. A carefully planned vector of tension can create a downward and inward pull that favors low scar positioning and smooth skin redraping across the abdominal wall.11,12

Taylor13 describes how a deeper understanding of abdominal zones of adhesion, along with the dynamics of the superficial fascial system, is essential to plan effective closure strategies. He proposes that the ideal vector of skin closure is directed obliquely inward, capitalizing on the region’s natural skin laxity, especially in the anterolateral abdomen and lower thorax. This oblique traction not only helps in preventing superior scar migration but also enhances waistline definition and creates a more tapered, aesthetically pleasing contour.13

Building on these principles, the Bikini Skar technique was developed to intentionally guide scar placement within the natural lines of minimal garments. Rather than concealing the scar as an afterthought, this technique integrates scar positioning as a primary design goal, aligning it with the anatomical and biomechanical characteristics of each patient. Although patients often express concern about the perceived length of the scar, our clinical experience consistently shows that a longer, well-positioned scar within the bikini line is vastly preferable to a shorter, misplaced scar accompanied by residual lateral fullness or dog-ears.

The key to this approach lies in precise vector control and thoughtful extension of the incision into naturally concealed zones. By doing so, the Bikini Skar technique provides not only superior control of soft tissue shaping but also greater predictability in achieving symmetrical and inconspicuous scar placement. The result is a harmonious integration of scar, contour, and clothing coverage, offering patients an outcome that aligns more closely with their aesthetic goals and body image expectations. As surgical techniques continue to evolve, incorporating biomechanical understanding and patient-centered design into procedural planning is essential to meet the growing demands for both form and function in aesthetic body contouring.

In this context, extended abdominoplasty represents an additional refinement of surgical design, particularly in patients with more extensive lateral tissue excess. Extended abdominoplasty is a well-established variation of traditional abdominoplasty, indicated for cases where redundant skin and adipose tissue involve not only the anterior abdomen but also the flanks and lateral abdominal regions. In this technique, the horizontal incision is deliberately lengthened toward the posterior axillary line, enabling resection of excess tissue beyond the iliac crests. This allows for the correction of deformities that standard abdominoplasty cannot adequately address, while still avoiding the circumferential excision characteristic of a belt lipectomy. Extended abdominoplasty is especially valuable in patients who have undergone massive weight loss, such as postbariatric patients, where skin laxity and adiposity are distributed more broadly. When combined with precise vector planning and thoughtful scar placement strategies, this approach further enhances contouring results and helps achieve low, concealed scars that respect the patient’s anatomical and aesthetic boundaries.14,15

This study has certain limitations that should be considered when planning future research. The follow-up period of 1 year is relatively short to assess long-term outcomes related to scar stability, tissue settling, and patient satisfaction over time. Additionally, the number of cases included is limited, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods are necessary to confirm these results and to evaluate the durability of the aesthetic and functional benefits described.

CONCLUSIONS

The Bikini Skar technique is a safe, reproducible alternative to conventional abdominoplasty. It improves abdominal, waist, and gluteal aesthetics while placing the scar within the confines of minimal clothing. The use of cross-vector tension distribution helps reduce complications associated with cephalic scar migration and lateral fullness. Patient satisfaction rates were high, and complications were minor. This technique offers a reliable option for plastic surgeons seeking to optimize both functional and aesthetic outcomes in body contouring surgery.
