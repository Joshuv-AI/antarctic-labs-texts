Paraneoplastic Hypereosinophilia With Multisystem Involvement as an Atypical Presentation of Lung Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Sara Bravo, Alexandra Machado, Paulo Medeiros, Rita Q Costa, Joao S Gonçalves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypereosinophilia (HE), defined as an absolute eosinophil count (AEC) exceeding 1,500/µL, is an uncommon finding with heterogeneous etiologies, including neoplastic causes. When associated with organ damage, it is defined as hypereosinophilic syndrome. Although hematological malignancies more frequently drive hypereosinophilia, it can rarely occur as a paraneoplastic phenomenon in solid tumors. Crucially, peripheral eosinophil counts do not reliably correlate with the severity of tissue injury, and significant organ dysfunction may arise even with mild-to-moderate eosinophilia.We report the case of a 66-year-old man, an active smoker, who presented with retrosternal chest pain, elevated high-sensitivity troponin, and markedly increased D-dimer levels. Initial chest computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed a solid pulmonary nodule. The patient was admitted under a diagnosis of non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). During hospitalization, peripheral AEC peaked at 2,900 cells/μL. A paraneoplastic aetiology was established after ruling out secondary and clonal causes. Clinical course was marked by rapidly progressive multiorgan complications, including cerebral ischemia, splenic and renal infarctions, cutaneous embolic phenomena, and severe eosinophilic endomyocarditis. Although diagnosis of metastatic lung adenocarcinoma was confirmed by cervical lymph node biopsy, the patient suffered rapid deterioration and died before tumour-directed therapy could be initiated.Paraneoplastic hypereosinophilia in lung adenocarcinoma is an exceptionally rare manifestation associated with an aggressive tumor biology and poor prognosis. This case highlights its potential for devastating multiorgan involvement despite non-extreme peripheral eosinophilia, underscoring the vital need for early etiological recognition to prevent irreversible clinical deterioration.

Introduction

Eosinophilia is defined as an absolute eosinophil count (AEC) exceeding 500 cells/µL in peripheral blood, whereas hypereosinophilia (HE) denotes counts above 1,500 cells/µL. When eosinophil-mediated tissue infiltration results in organ dysfunction, the condition is characterized as hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) [1,2]. Although often idiopathic, secondary eosinophilia may arise from parasitic infections, drug hypersensitivity, autoimmune disorders, or solid malignancies, whereas primary eosinophilia is typically driven by clonal hematological disorders [1,3].

HES is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition. While the skin, lungs, and gastrointestinal tract are most frequently affected, cardiac and central nervous system involvement carry a particularly poor prognosis [4,5]. Importantly, the peripheral eosinophil count does not reliably predict the severity of organ damage; persistent HE may remain clinically silent, whereas mild eosinophilia can cause devastating tissue injury [2]. Therefore, prompt etiological recognition is paramount to guide targeted therapy and prevent irreversible clinical deterioration.

Here, we report a rare case of paraneoplastic HE secondary to metastatic lung adenocarcinoma, featuring severe multisystem involvement that predominantly affected the heart, alongside neurological, cutaneous, renal, and splenic complications.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 66-year-old male, active smoker with a 45 pack-year history, who presented to the emergency department with 5 days’ duration of retrosternal chest pain. He denied having dyspnea, palpitations, dizziness, or syncope. The patient was tachycardic on admission (heart rate 112 beats/minute). His blood pressure was 138/74 mmHg, with normal body temperature. Pulmonary auscultation revealed crackles in both base lungs, and cardiac auscultation revealed a regular but tachycardic rhythm. The neurological examination revealed no focal neurological deficits. Complete blood count and chemistry showed mild eosinophilia of 940 cells/µL (reference value: <500 cells/µL), thrombocytopenia of 105,000 cells/µL (reference range: 150,000-450,000 cells/µL), C-reactive protein (CRP) of 36.1 mg/L (reference value: <5 mg/L), and elevated high-sensitivity troponin (hsTn) 2,050 ng/L (reference value: <34ng/L). The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm, with a slight elevation of point J in leads V4-V6. Echocardiography demonstrated preserved global systolic function without regional wall motion abnormalities. Due to markedly elevated D-dimer levels of 30,138 ng/mL (reference value: <500 ng/mL), a chest CT angiography was performed; pulmonary embolism was excluded, but an irregular 15×13 mm solid nodule in the left apicoposterior segment, with a mediastinal-hilar lymph node conglomerate, was revealed (Figure 1). The patient was admitted with Killip class I non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). Coronary angiography showed a 50-60% left main coronary artery ostial stenosis, and conservative management was adopted.

On day 2, the patient developed a cough and hypoxemic respiratory failure (pO2 68 mmHg), accompanied by leukocytosis 13,200 cells/µL (reference range: 4,000-12,000 cells/µL) and elevated CRP (222.2 mg/L), prompting empirical antibiotic therapy with amoxicilin/clavulanate. Two days later, his neurological status deteriorated with fluctuating consciousness. A brain CT revealed a left posterior cerebellar hypodensity consistent with a subacute ischaemic lesion. Concurrently, he developed microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (hemoglobin level 8.2 g/dL, a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level of 1,092 U/L, decreased haptoglobin, and schistocytes 1.8% on the peripheral blood smear) and new-onset thrombocytopenia (39,000 cells/µL), which precluded a planned transthoracic lung biopsy. Over the following days, he developed hyperactive delirium, worsening systemic inflammatory response, and fever, with CRP peaking at 287 mg/L. Antibiotic therapy was escalated to piperacillin/tazobactam. An abdominopelvic CT later revealed multiple new poorly defined hypoenhancing hepatic lesions (the largest measuring 35×26 mm), massive splenic infarction (Figure 2), and left renal infarction.

Multiple expansive hepatic formations and massive splenic infarction

Abdominopelvic computed tomography scan showing a massive splenic infarction (yellow arrow) and multiple expansive hepatic formations with hypo-enhancing and poorly defined borders (the largest 35x26 mm) (white arrows), raising suspicion for hepatic metastases. Their hypoenhancing appearance likely reflects relative hypovascularity compared with the surrounding liver parenchyma, with reduced contrast uptake due to tumour infiltration and necrosis.

Whole-body PET-CT demonstrated metabolic activity in the pulmonary nodule, mediastinal-hilar lymphadenopathy, and hypermetabolic hepatic foci, strongly suggestive of occult metastatic malignancy. Persistent eosinophilia was noted from admission (initially 940 cells/µL, later exceeding 2,900 cells/µL). A systematic investigation for HE excluded primary causes (absence of the FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion gene; reactive peripheral immunophenotyping). Secondary causes were also ruled out: parasitological testing, serum IgE, tryptase, autoimmune markers, and drug hypersensitivity screens were all unremarkable, though empirical albendazole and ivermectin were administered due to recent travel to Cape Verde islands.

On day 9, after improvement in platelet counts, cervical lymph node and bone marrow biopsies were performed. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated after biopsy sampling. As shown in Figure 3, a short course of corticosteroids, consisting of prednisolone followed by methylprednisolone pulses, induced only a transient reduction in the eosinophil count.

On day 12, the patient developed right lower limb monoparesis and new violaceous lesions on the right foot, compatible with systemic embolic phenomena. A repeat transthoracic and subsequent transoesophageal echocardiography (Figures 4A-4B) revealed preserved left ventricular systolic function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 52%, moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation, and vegetations on both mitral valve leaflets, the largest measuring 14 mm in its greatest diameter. Infective endocarditis was excluded based on modified Duke criteria following persistently negative serial blood cultures and negative Bartonella, Coxiella, and Tropheryma whipplei serologic testing. Given the combination of HE, non-infective mitral vegetations, systemic embolisation, and progressively rising hsTn (peaking at 23,321 ng/L), eosinophilic endomyocarditis was suspected, and low-molecular-weight heparin was initiated. Cardiac MRI showed mildly reduced global systolic function, due to mid anterolateral and basal inferolateral akinesia, with basal anterolateral hypokinesia. T2-weighted images demonstrated myocardial hyperintensity in the apical anterior and septal segments, consistent with focal ventricular oedema. Myocardial T2 relaxation time was increased (54 ms), and global remote myocardial extracellular volume was moderately increased (48%). Accordingly, cardiac MRI confirmed non-dilated cardiomyopathy, active myocardial inflammation, and extensive mixed subendocardial/intramyocardial late gadolinium enhancement (Figures 5A-5D).

The coexistence of extensive subendocardial LGE and additional intramyocardial enhancement in a non-coronary distribution favoured eosinophilic endomyocarditis rather than isolated acute myocardial infarction, particularly in the setting of hypereosinophilia, persistent troponin elevation, mitral vegetations, and systemic embolisation.

Histopathological analysis of the bone marrow biopsy demonstrated involvement by metastatic solid malignancy, while cervical lymph node biopsy subsequently confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma of pulmonary origin. A fluoro-CT-guided lung biopsy was performed on day 20. The following day, the patient suffered rapid clinical deterioration with a new embolic cerebrovascular event, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and anuria, leading to his death two days later. Post-mortem histopathology of the lung biopsy confirmed the definitive diagnosis of pulmonary adenocarcinoma.

Discussion

Eosinophils play established roles in phagocytosis, hemostatic activation, and direct cytotoxicity. Mature eosinophils are produced in the bone marrow under cytokine stimulation, particularly interleukin-5 (IL-5), interleukin-3 (IL-3), and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) [6]. Tissue accumulation can cause severe organ damage through cytotoxic degranulation and pro-inflammatory mediator release. Tumor-associated eosinophilia may result from tumor necrosis, metastatic bone marrow involvement, or local tumor-driven stimulation. Although less frequent in solid tumours than in hematological malignancies, it occurs in lung cancer via tumor-derived cytokines and chemotactic mediators like eotaxins. In this case, bone marrow metastases likely sustained eosinophilic hematopoiesis and systemic activation [6].

The patient presented with a solid pulmonary nodule, later confirmed as lung adenocarcinoma, making eosinophilic endomyocarditis the first clinically dominant manifestation of this malignancy. Although this cardiac diagnosis remained presumptive due to the lack of an endomyocardial biopsy (gold standard), the clinical evidence was compelling. Eosinophilic endomyocarditis classically progresses through necrotic, thrombotic, and fibrotic stages over weeks to months [7,8]. Remarkably, all three phases coexisted here: elevated hsTn indicated myocardial necrosis; mitral vegetations with systemic embolisation reflected thrombosis; and extensive late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI demonstrated macroscopic replacement fibrosis. This overlap suggests either advanced cardiac involvement at presentation or an accelerated course driven by tumour aggressiveness. Furthermore, the absence of a culprit coronary lesion and persistent hsTn elevation without dynamic ECG changes supports that the initial NSTEMI-like presentation represented eosinophilic cardiac involvement.

The diagnosis was further supported by the patient's eosinophil kinetics. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated on day 9 in view of suspected eosinophil-mediated organ involvement, particularly cardiac and neurological involvement, where prompt eosinophil-lowering treatment is warranted despite an ongoing aetiological work-up. Corticosteroid pulses induced an abrupt, transient reduction in the AEC, followed by rapid recurrence after withdrawal. This fluctuating pattern strongly points toward a persistent, tumor-driven eosinopoietic stimulus [5].

During the thrombotic phase, eosinophil-mediated endocardial injury and granule protein release render the endocardium highly thrombogenic. Subsequent thrombus fragmentation, potentially favoured by mitral regurgitation turbulence, explains the cerebellar, splenic, renal, and peripheral embolic events. Moreover, the coexistence of consumptive thrombocytopenia and bone marrow infiltration created a complex haemostatic paradox, where marked prothrombotic activity occurred despite a low platelet count, making anticoagulation exceptionally challenging [9]. Nevertheless, although eosinophilic endocardial injury provides a plausible explanation for the embolic events, the exact thrombotic mechanism remains uncertain, with concomitant malignancy-associated thrombosis or non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis remaining plausible alternative or overlapping mechanisms.

Another distinctive feature in this case was the discrepancy between the peak peripheral AEC (2,900 eosinophils/μL) and the severity of organ damage. Organ injury in HE depends primarily on local tissue eosinophil activation and degranulation rather than peripheral counts alone [2]. Released granule proteins, such as major basic protein, eosinophil cationic protein, and eosinophil peroxidase, exert potent cytotoxic, cardiotoxic, and prothrombotic effects, explaining the severe multiorgan injury despite non-extreme peripheral HE [2,5]. In this case, bone marrow metastasis and a persistent tumor-derived eosinopoietic stimulus, potentially mediated by IL-5, may have sustained chronic high-grade eosinophil activation, explaining the marked dissociation between peripheral AEC and the severity of cardiac, neurological, cutaneous, renal, and splenic injury.

Ultimately, treating paraneoplastic HE relies on controlling the underlying malignancy. While corticosteroids can temporarily mitigate organ dysfunction, their effect is transient without definitive tumour-directed therapy. Unfortunately, the patient suffered rapid clinical deterioration and died post-biopsy before oncological treatment could be initiated.

Conclusions

This case illustrates paraneoplastic hypereosinophilia as an atypical initial manifestation of metastatic lung adenocarcinoma, presenting with probable eosinophilic endomyocarditis, bone marrow and lymph node involvement, and suspected hepatic metastases. The extensive cardiac, neurological, cutaneous, renal, and splenic involvement highlights the exceptional rarity, diagnostic complexity, and aggressive tumor biology of this presentation. This case further reinforces that paraneoplastic eosinophilia in lung adenocarcinoma may indicate a high metastatic burden and poor prognosis, and that meticulous investigation of uncommon etiologies may lead to unexpected diagnoses.
