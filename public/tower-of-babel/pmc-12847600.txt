Lipid metabolism in colorectal cancer: dual roles and statin therapy
Authors: Pengge Lu, Zhaohui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a prevalent global malignancy with increasing incidence. This review systematically explores the intricate and often paradoxical dual roles of lipid metabolism components—triglycerides, cholesterol, HDL-C, and LDL-C—in CRC development and progression. A key focus is on the therapeutic potential of statins, cornerstone lipid-lowering agents. We summarize evidence that statins may confer protection through multifaceted mechanisms: inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, modulating inflammation and immune responses, and reprogramming the gut microbiome. However, we critically synthesize the significant controversy surrounding their efficacy, attributing discrepant findings to factors such as statin lipophilicity, treatment duration, and tumor anatomical subsite. Ultimately, this review highlights the complex interplay between lipids and CRC and underscores the need for stratified, personalized approaches in future research and potential therapeutic applications.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common malignant tumor of the digestive tract in our country, which has become a major public health problem that seriously threatens national health and places a heavy disease burden on society. In our country, the incidence of CRC (in men and women) has a significant upward trend from the age of 40 years, peaking at 75–79 years. However, CRC cases under the age of 30 are common. It is noteworthy that males generally have a higher incidence rate than females at all ages, and this gap gradually widens with age [1]. CRC is the 3rd/4th leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with 2.2 million new cases projected by 2030 [2, 3]. The vigorous reduction in CRC incidence in China is a substantial public health challenge that needs to be urgently addressed. There are three possible pathways for CRC to occur: The pathway from nothing to something (de novo), the pathway of inflammation and cancer, and the pathway of adenoma and adenocarcinoma (including the serrated path), with the pathway from adenoma to adenocarcinoma being the most important. CRC formation is a gradual progression, with development from polyp to cancer usually taking 5–10 years, which provides a critical window of time for the early prevention of CRC [1].

Recently, the incidence of dyslipidemia in Chinese adults has remained relatively high [4–6]. Elevated levels of triglycerides (TG), TC, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in the blood and decreased levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) are usually referred to as hyperlipidemia. Our 2018 survey reported mean serum levels of TC (4.8 mmol/L), LDL-C (2.9 mmol/L), and TG (1.7 mmol/L) in adults ≥ 18 years [5]. A comparative analysis of data from the 2015 national survey revealed a significant increase in the mean values of various blood lipid indices. Moreover, the overall prevalence of dyslipidemia among adults aged 18 years and older was 35.6%, which is still on the rise compared to 2015. The increase in the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia (TC ≥ 6.2 mmol/L) was the most prominent among all disorders [5].

Data from several studies have revealed a positive correlation between hyperlipidemia and colorectal carcinogenesis, promoting the development and progression of CRC, while others have revealed a negative correlation; that is, hyperlipidemia is a consequence of colorectal carcinogenesis. Among the many studies evaluating the association between statins and CRC, the best-known and most cited is the study of the molecular epidemiology of CRC conducted by Poynter et al. [7]. The study, which covered a control population in 2015 and a diseased population of 1953 from northern Israel, was a case-control study that investigated the association between statins and CRC. The case-control study revealed that the long-term use of statins for at least 5 years significantly reduced CRC risk by up to 43%[7]. A meta-analysis of observational studies concluded that statins could reduce the incidence of CRC by up to 14% [8], indirectly supporting an association between dyslipidemia and CRC risk. Evidence suggests statins may have therapeutic potential for advanced colorectal tumors; however, the mechanism of action is not yet fully understood. Whether abnormal lipid metabolism affects CRC development, whether CRC causes dyslipidemia, or whether the two are mutually reinforcing is controversial, and the related mechanisms are yet unclear.

TG and CRC

TG provide energy to cells through catabolism and are the main form of energy storage in the body. They are classified as endogenous (synthesized by the liver) and exogenous (absorbed in the intestine) according to their sources. Endogenous and exogenous TG enter target cells through blood circulation using chylomicrons and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) as carriers, respectively. The metabolic process of TG is regulated by insulin and glucagon. Catalyzed by lipoprotein lipase, TG are hydrolyzed to glycerol and free fatty acids (FFAs), of which FFAs enter muscle tissue as energy metabolism substrates through a specific transport mechanism [9]. Notably, adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) is the rate-limiting enzyme for triglyceride hydrolysis. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a core regulator of cellular energy metabolism, senses changes in intracellular AMP/ATP ratio and regulates ATGL expression and activity [10], thereby modulating lipolysis and cancer cell adaptation to hypoxia [11]. This process contributes to reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation from oxidized fatty acids, which sustain cancer cell survival under hypoxic conditions and promote tumor growth [11]. These findings demonstrate the pro-cancer effects of impaired triglyceride metabolism.

Furthermore, TG synthesized by the liver (endogenous) enter target cells through the circulation using VLDL as a carrier. Several studies have revealed that elevated serum triglyceride concentration is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease [10]. Moreover, there are few studies on TG and CRC in China and abroad. Some studies by meta-analysis of lipid metabolism and the risk of intestinal cancer have observed that elevated serum triglyceride levels can increase the risk of developing intestinal cancer [12]. However, Chung et al. identified that triglyceride levels were negatively associated with the risk of progressive adenoma and advanced CRC and that cholesterol and triglyceride levels were significantly associated with a reduced risk of cancer, with odds ratios of 0.2 and 0.3, respectively [13]. This situation may be related to nutritional deficiencies in patients with advanced disease and metabolic changes in the body. Accordingly, the link between TG and CRC production and progression has not yet been fully clarified, and it may be related to the health status of the body and the stage of disease progression.

The apparent paradox of TG’s pro-cancer effects in early stages (via ROS generation [11]) and its negative association in advanced cancer [13] can largely be explained by reverse causality and cancer cachexia. During early carcinogenesis, elevated TG levels provide energy and signaling molecules that fuel tumor growth. However, in advanced stages, the catabolic state of patients, marked by weight loss and metabolic wasting, results in decreased serum TG levels. This highlights the need to interpret lipid levels in the context of disease progression and patient nutritional status, rather than in isolation.

Total cholesterol and CRC

Cholesterol is a cyclopentane polyhydrophenanthrene derivative that belongs to the class of steroids. Cholesterol in the body comes from food intake and can be synthesized endogenously to produce cholesterol in almost all human tissues except brain tissue and mature red blood cells. Among all organs, the liver is the key site for cholesterol production. In the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, hydroxymethylglutaryl monoacyl-coenzyme A reductase (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase [HMG-CoA reductase]), as the key rate-limiting enzyme of this metabolic pathway, catalyzes the conversion of hydroxymethylglutaryl monoacyl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) to mevalonate (mevalonate), an irreversible reaction. Cholesterol is a key component of cell membranes and a major raw material to produce vitamin D, bile acids, and steroid hormones [14].

Evidence indicates that cholesterol is strongly associated with various malignant tumors, such as lung, gastric, colorectal, breast, prostate, and renal cancers [15, 16]. A study by Wulaningsih and his team suggests that higher total cholesterol levels may increase the risk of developing rectal cancer [17]. The specific mechanism by which cholesterol contributes to the development of CRC may be the eventual conversion of cholesterol to bile acids, which have a similar chemical structure to many carcinogenic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Primary bile acids are converted to secondary bile acids stimulate the sustained proliferation and tissue invasion and metastasis of CRC cells, thereby accelerating the progression of CRC through a mechanism that may involve the activation of protein kinase C and cyclooxygenase-2 [18]. Cholesterol can realize its oncogenic potential by exacerbating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses and has an impact on immune system surveillance [19]. However, it has been revealed that cholesterol levels decrease in patients with advanced CRC, suggesting that cholesterol and bowel cancer are negatively correlated. To validate this idea, by continuously monitoring serum cholesterol levels in pre-CRC disease and the course of CRC, further studies have shown that serum cholesterol is indeed a key element in promoting colorectal carcinogenesis. However, serum cholesterol levels may gradually decrease as the disease progresses [16]. This is likely attributable to the poor nutritional status of patients with advanced and progressive CRC. As things stand now, current research remains in the speculative stage regarding the risk relationship between high cholesterol levels and CRC.

Cholesterol’s dual role in CRC exemplifies a time-dependent risk. High cholesterol levels may contribute to CRC initiation and early promotion via bile acid conversion and pro-inflammatory pathways [18, 19]. In contrast, advanced CRC often shows hypocholesterolemia due to cachexia and the tumor’s high metabolic demands, rather than a protective effect. Future research should stratify analyses by cancer stage to better understand this complex relationship.

HDL-C and CRC

HDL-C is also known as “good cholesterol” because of its anti-atherosclerotic effect. In general, serum HDL-C levels are negatively correlated with the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [20–22]. However, the relationship between HDL-C and cancer, particularly CRC, is relatively complex, with both protective effects and the possibility of promoting cancer development and progression in some cases. HDL-C has the biological function of reverse cholesterol transport, which transports cholesterol from the peripheral tissues to the liver for metabolism. Furthermore, HDL-C has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory functions. These functions may indirectly influence cancer development and progression by affecting the tumor microenvironment. Some studies have revealed a negative correlation between HDL-C levels and CRC risk. For example, a large cohort study conducted by van Duijnhoven and his team revealed that there was a correlation between elevated HDL-C and apolipoprotein A concentrations and a reduced risk of CRC [23]. A case-control study by Liu and his team identified that HDL-C may be an independent risk factor for CRC [24], and the exact mechanism of this is unclear. However, an association between HDL-C and CRC has not been observed in many studies. In a meta-analysis of serum lipids and colorectal tumors, Tian et al. did not observe a significant correlation between HDL-C levels and CRC risk [25]. Most of the current evidence comes from observational studies, and to avoid confounding factors that could bias the results, a recent Mendelian randomization study on the relationship between HDL-C and CRC concluded a significant correlation between HDL-C and a reduced risk of CRC [26]. The relationship between HDL-C and CRC is complex and involves multiple mechanisms, including lipid metabolism, inflammation, and immune modulation. Serum HDL-C has been revealed to inhibit the growth and proliferation of tumor cells by suppressing the production of inflammatory factors [27], such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6, and other inflammatory cytokines. Meanwhile, HDL-C can improve the antioxidant capacity of cells [28–30].

Observational studies are confounded by factors such as inflammation and poor health. However, a Mendelian randomization study [26] offers stronger evidence for a modest protective causal role of HDL-C. The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of HDL-C provide a plausible biological mechanism. Conflicting findings from earlier studies likely resulted from inadequate control of these confounding factors.

LDL-C and CRC

Data from several epidemiological surveys, genetic association analyses, and clinical cohort studies have collectively confirmed a clear pathogenic role for LDL-C in ASCVD pathogenesis [31]. However, relatively few studies have been conducted on the relationship between LDL-C and cancer. In 2024, Chan et al. conducted a Mendelian randomization study to investigate the association between lipid levels and drug targets for LDL-C lowering and CRC risk [32]. A weak positive correlation was identified between genetically predicted LDL-C levels and CRC, and this association persisted after correction for HDL-C, TG, and body mass index (BMI). This result implies that LDL-C may influence colorectal carcinogenesis through a mechanism different from the traditional obesity-related pathway. Conversely, in another Mendelian randomization study conducted by Luo et al. in 2021, only total cholesterol was observed to be potentially associated with the risk of CRC, while a non-significant association was identified between LDL-C and colorectal cancer [33]. Similarly, the results of a large prospective study in 2020, which included more than 380,000 people, revealed that after adjusting for BMI and waist circumference, LDL-C was non-significantly associated with the overall risk of CRC. However, TG were associated with an elevated risk of cecal cancer [34]. Some studies have noted that the role of LDL-C may vary depending on the anatomical location of the CRC. For example, LDL-C is more strongly associated with distal colon cancer than with proximal colon cancer, while its association with rectal cancer is relatively weak [33, 34]. Regarding the potential oncogenic mechanism of LDL-C, Deng et al. pointed out that the proposed mechanism via LDL receptor-mediated cholesterol delivery fuels cancer cell growth by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, a classic driver of sustained proliferative signaling and evasion of apoptosis [35]. Additionally, LDL-C induced oxidative stress (ROS) can promote genomic instability and tumor-promoting inflammation via the MAPK pathway [34], directly linking dyslipidemia to core hallmarks of cancer.

The association between LDL-C and CRC risk remains inconclusive but appears weaker than for other lipids. Its effect may be ​anatomically site-specific, with stronger evidence for distal colon cancer [33, 34]. The proposed mechanism via LDL receptor-mediated cholesterol delivery and activation of oncogenic pathways (PI3K/AKT) is compelling but requires further validation in human CRC models. The lack of a strong consistent signal suggests LDL-C may be a modifier rather than a primary driver of CRC risk (Table 1).

Statins and CRC

Clinical evidence on statins and CRC risk

Among many lipid-lowering drugs, statins are commonly used in clinical practice and have significant efficacy due to their unique mechanism of action. Therefore, statins are used as basic drugs for the treatment of dyslipidemia. HMG-CoA inhibitors can reduce the activity of HMG-CoA during cholesterol production, inhibit the endogenous synthesis of cholesterol, and simultaneously induce LDL-R expression on the cell surface, thus accelerating the catabolism of serum LDL [36]. HMG-CoA inhibitors significantly reduce serum TC and LDL-C levels, elevate HDL-C levels, and reduce serum TG levels to some extent.

Numerous studies have indicated that statins may have a positive effect on the prevention of CRC [37]. However, there is a lack of sufficient evidence to support this [38]. Epidemiological studies have reported mixed results regarding the association between statins and CRC risk, with risk reductions ranging from 47% to 7%, with significant variations in the results. Therefore, to provide evidence-based medical support, Chi Jiahao conducted a meta-analysis on the association between statins and CRC in 2021 [39]. The results of this study were no different from the findings of a meta-analysis study that encompassed 42 cohort studies, case-control studies, and randomized controlled trials in 2014 [40], which concluded that statin use is associated with reduced CRC incidence. This conclusion was also confirmed by some scholars in animal models [41]. The effect of fat-soluble statins was also more significant (risk-reward ratio [RR] = 0.86, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.74–0.99, P = 0.04) [40]. Recent findings suggest that the use of statins after diagnosis of CRC is effective in reducing the risk of death overall and for specific tumors (modified RR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.22–0.68) [42]. Similarly, a study by Ling et al. demonstrated a 30% reduction in cancer-specific mortality compared to patients with CRC not using statins [43].

Furthermore, an observational study in Sweden revealed that in a population of patients with inflammatory bowel disease [44], the risk of cancer decreased significantly after two years of continuous use of statins, with the advantage ratio decreasing from 0.59 to 0.46 and then further decreasing to 0.38 after five years, a figure that revealed that statins significantly reduced the incidence rate of CRC in patients with CRC. It also confirms that statins can improve the prognosis of CRC, reduce adverse effects such as recurrence and metastasis rates, and have lower toxicity.

The significant heterogeneity in statin-CRC studies can be attributed to several key factors : (1) Study Design: Case-control studies [7, 39] are prone to recall and selection bias, while prospective cohorts [45] and RCTs (though limited for CRC outcomes) provide stronger evidence. (2) Statin Properties: Lipophilic statins (e.g., simvastatin, atorvastatin) more readily penetrate cell membranes and show a stronger association with reduced risk (RR = 0.86) than hydrophilic ones (e.g., rosuvastatin) [40]. (3) Tumor Anatomy: Statins may confer greater protection against distal colon cancer than proximal colon cancer, possibly due to differences in embryonic origin and bile acid exposure [33, 34, 45]. (4) Treatment Duration: A minimum of 5 years of use is often required to observe a significant effect, suggesting a cumulative biological impact [7].

Molecular mechanisms of statins in CRC

Studies have revealed that statins of different chemical structures present multi-target mechanismsin preventing and treating CRC. By inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase activity [36], statins disrupt the cholesterolbiosynthesis pathway in tumor cells, affecting cell membrane stability and signal transduction. This core mechanism synergizes with anti-inflammatory effects (e.g., simvastatin reducing TNF-α expression [46, 47]) and immune modulation (e.g., atorvastatin enhancing 5-Fu sensitivity via myeloid-derived suppressor cell regulation [48, 49]). Simultaneously, fluvastatin alters CRC cell proliferation by downregulating lncRNA PTPRG-AS1 [50], while pitavastatin induces apoptosis through inhibition of inflammatory gene transcription [51].

Modulation of gut microbiome

Furthermore, gut microbiome reprogramming plays a critical role: atorvastatin enhances tryptophan metabolism by intestinal microorganisms [52], and increased Lactobacillus abundance activates CD4 + CD8αα + intraepithelial lymphocytes that differentiate into immune-regulatory T cells [53], collectively optimizing the intestinal microecological environment to inhibit tumor growth. These interconnected mechanisms - cholesterol pathway disruption, inflammatory factor downregulation, and microbiome-mediated immune activation - synergistically promote autophagy and apoptosis of tumor cells across multiple CRC development stages (Fig. 1).

In the field of chemoprevention and adjuvant therapy of colorectal cancer, the clinical application of statins remains controversial. Some scholars believe that there is a non-significant association between dyslipidemia and CRC and that statin lipid-lowering drugs do not lead to a reduction in the risk of CRC, nor do they have a significant reduction in prolonging patient survival. The results of a prospective study in the United States indicated a non-significant association between serum cholesterol levels and the use of lipid-lowering drugs and the incidence of CRC [45]. It was also observed that long-term statin use may be associated with an increased risk of CRC, particularly proximal CRC. Shadman et al. conducted a study on the association of NSAIDs and statins with CRC based on a female population [54]. A case-control study of the association between NSAIDs and statins and the risk of CRC in a female population demonstrated that neither lipophilic nor hydrophilic statins nor the duration of treatment reduced the risk of CRC.

In summary, there remains much controversy regarding whether statin lipid-lowering drugs can be used for CRC prevention and treatment. Consequently, more large-scale prospective studies, comprehensive clinical studies, and clinical experimental studies are needed to clarify the specific role of statins in CRC prevention and treatment.
