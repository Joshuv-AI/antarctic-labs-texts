On the polynomial equation P ⁡ ( Q ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x m ) ) = Q ⁡ ( P ⁡ ( x 1 ) , … , P ⁡ ( x m ) ) P(Q(x_{1},...,x_{m}))=Q(P(x_{1}),...,P(x_{m}))
Arseny Mingajev

Abstract
. We consider the equation P ⁡ ( Q ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x ν ) ) = Q ⁡ ( P ⁡ ( x 1 ) , … , P ⁡ ( x ν ) ) P(Q(x_{1},...,x_{\nu}))=Q(P(x_{1}),...,P(x_{\nu})) in polynomials Q ∈ ℂ ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x ν ] Q\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\nu}] , P ∈ ℂ ⁡ [ x ] P\in\mathbb{C}[x] , and prove that if deg ⁡ ( P ) > 1 \deg(P)>1 , then it is only solvable in polynomials that are affinely conjugate to monomials.



1.
Introduction

In 1922 and 1923, Gaston Julia
[
3
]
and Joseph Ritt
[
4
]
proved that, if two polynomials in one variable
P(x)
and
Q(x)
commute with each other, then one of three statements is true:

(i)

(Ritt,
[
4
]
)
P
and
Q
are both affinely conjugate to monomials;

(ii)

(Ritt,
[
4
]
)
P
and
Q
are both affinely conjugate to Chebyshev polynomials;

(iii)

(Julia,
[
3
]
)
P^{\circ\nu}=Q^{\circ\mu}
for some positive integers
\nu,\mu.

An elementary proof of this theorem was published by Block and Thielman in a 1951 paper,
[
1
]
. The results of Ritt and Julia can be interpreted as results about commuting rational maps
\mathbb{CP}^{1}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. The commutativity of rational maps
\mathbb{CP}^{N}\to\mathbb{CP}^{N}
is still a problem that is being investigated; see
[
2
]
and the references therein.

In this paper, we consider another multivariable version of this problem. For each natural number
\nu>1
we denote by
\mathscr{S}_{\nu}
the subset of
\mathbb{C}[x]\times\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\nu}]
consisting of polynomials
P\in\mathbb{C}[x]
and
Q\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\nu}]
,
\deg P,\deg Q,\geq 1
, satisfying the functional equation

P\big(\,Q(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\nu})\,\big)=Q\big(\,P(x_{1}),\dotsc,P(x_{\nu})\,\big)

(1.1)

The main goal of this paper is to describe the structure of
\mathscr{S}_{\nu}
for
\nu\geq 2
. A few elementary observations are in order.

Denote by
\Aff
the group of affine bijections

\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto az+b\in\mathbb{C},\;\;a,b\in\mathbb{C},\;\;a\neq 0.

For any natural number
n
, the group
\Aff
acts on
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}]
by conjugation,

\Aff\times\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dotsc,z_{\nu}]\ni(\alpha,R)\mapsto C_{\alpha}(R)\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dotsc,z_{\nu}],

\;C_{\alpha}(R)(z_{1},\dotsc,z_{\nu}]=\alpha\circ R\big(\,\alpha^{-1}(z_{1}),\dotsc\alpha^{-1}(z_{\nu})\,\big).

An elementary computation shows that

\big(\,P,Q\,\big)\in\mathscr{S}_{\nu}\iff\big(\,C_{\alpha}(P),C_{\alpha}(Q)\,\big)\in\mathscr{S}_{\nu},\;\;\forall\alpha\in\Aff.

In other words,
\mathscr{S}_{\nu}
is invariant with respect to the action of
\Aff
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Any polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
,
\deg P>0
is affinely conjugate to a polynomial vanishing at
0
. Moreover, if
\deg P>1
, then
P
is affinely conjugate to a polynomial with leading coefficient
1
and vanishing at
0
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\Aff
,
\lambda(z)=z-r
, where
r
is a root of the equation
p(x)-z=0
. Then
\lambda^{-1}(0)=r
and

C_{\lambda}(P)(0)=P\big(\,\lambda^{-1}(0)\,\big)-r=P(r)-r=0.

This proves the first part. Suppose now that
d=\deg P>1
and
P(0)=0
. If
\alpha(z)=cz
,
c\neq 0
, then
C_{\alpha}(P)(z)=cP(z/c)
. If
p_{d}
is the leading coefficient of
P
, then the leading coefficient of
C_{\alpha}(P)
is
c^{1-d}p_{d}
. Since
d>1
we can find
c
such that
c^{1-d}p_{d}=1
.

\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}\sqcup

For any multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\nu}
we set

|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{\nu},\;\;x^{\alpha}:=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{\nu}^{\alpha_{\nu}}.

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{C}[x]
,
Q\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\nu}]
,
\nu\geq 2
,
\deg P=n>1
. If
(P,Q)\in\mathscr{S}_{\nu}
and

\forall i\in\{1,\dotsc,\nu\},\;\;Q\not\in\mathbb{C}[z_{i}],

(1.2)

then there exists an affine transformation
\lambda\in\Aff
, a constant
c\in\mathbb{C}
, and a nonzero multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\nu}
such that

C_{\lambda}(P)(x)=x^{n},\;\;C_{\lambda}(Q)(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\nu})=cx^{\alpha},\;\;c^{n-1}=1.

Remark 1.3
.

(a) Observe that if
(P,Q)\in\mathscr{S}_{\nu}
are nontrivial monomials
Q=cx^{\alpha}
and
P(x)=x^{n}
, then the condition
c^{n-1}=1
is automatically satisfied.

(b) The condition (
1.2
) is necessary. Indeed , if
Q
depended only on one variable, then according to
[
4
]
the equation (
1.1
) has nonmonomial solutions.

\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}\sqcup

The proof will be by induction.

2.
The two-variable case

Let

P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}x^{k}.

We can view
Q(x,y)
either as a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}[y][x]
or as a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}[x][y]
.
In the first case we can express
Q
as

Q(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}Q_{i}(y)x^{i}.

and in the second case we can express
Q
as

Q(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}R_{i}(x)y^{i}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Under the conditions of the main theorem
1.2
, if
(P,Q)\in\mathscr{S}_{2}
, then the leading coefficients
Q_{m}(y)
and
R_{\ell}(x)
are non-constant.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. In this case
Q(x,y)
can be expressed as

Q(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)+xyq(x,y),

where the highest power of
x
in
q(x,y)
is less than
\deg(f)-1
, and the highest power of
y
in
q(x,y)
is less than
\deg(g)-1
. Moreover
\min(\deg(f),\deg(g))>1
.

The commutation equation (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

P(f(x)+g(y)+xyq(x,y))=f(P(x))+g(P(y))+P(x)P(y)q(P(x),P(y)).

By the statement of the theorem
1.2
, either
g(y)\neq 0
or
q(x,y)\neq 0
. Suppose first that
q\neq 0
. If
\deg(f)=N
, the term with the highest power of
x
in
xyq(x,y)
is of the form
q_{1}(y)x^{N-a},a>0
.

The highest power of
x
with a non-constant coefficient in
P\big(\,f(x)+g(y)+xyq(x,y)\,\big)
is
N(n-1)+N-a=Nn-a
,
n=\deg P
.

However, only
P(x)P(y)q(P(x),P(y))\in\mathbb{C}[y][x]
has coefficients that are nonconstant as functions of
y
. The highest
p
such that the coefficient
C_{p}[y]
of
x^{p}
is non-constant coefficients
n(N-a)=nN-na
. Since
n>1
, this is impossible.

If
q(x,y)=0
, then (
1.1
) reads

P(f(x)+g(y))=f(P(x))+g(P(y)).

This is not solvable because
\deg P>1
. Indeed
P(f(x)+g(y))
contains terms divisible by
xy
, and
f(P(x))+g(P(y))
does not.

An analogous proof works for powers of
y
.

\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}\sqcup

The equality
P(Q(x,y))=Q(P(x),P(y))
can be rewritten as

\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\Big(\,\sum_{i=0}^{m}Q_{i}(y)x^{i}\,\Big)^{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}Q_{i}(P(y))P(x)^{i}.

Equating the terms with the highest power of
x
we deduce

Q_{m}(y)^{n}=a_{n}^{m-1}Q_{m}(P(y)).

(2.1)

Denote by
\mathscr{R}
the set roots of

we denote by

Q_{m}(y)=q_{m}\prod_{r\in\mathscr{R}}(y-r)^{m(r)},\;\;q_{m}\neq 0,

and (

q_{m}^{n}\prod_{r\in\mathscr{R}}(y-r)^{nm(r)}=a_{n}^{m-1}\underbrace{\prod_{r\in\mathscr{R}}\big(\,P(y)-r\,\big)^{m(r)}}_{R(y)}.

(2.2)

Lemma 2.2
.

The polynomial
Q_{m}
has a single root of multiplicity
m
, i.e.,
|\mathscr{R}|=1
. Moreover, if
r_{0}
is this unique root of
Q_{m}
, then
P(y)=(y-r_{0})^{n}+r_{0}
.

Proof.

The equality (
2.2
) shows that there exists a map
\phi:\mathscr{R}\to\mathscr{R}
such that

P(r)=\phi(r),\;\;\forall r\in\mathscr{R}.

(2.3)

On the other hand, for any
r\in\mathscr{R}
, the equality (
2.2
) implies that the nonempty set
P^{-1}(r)
is contained in
\mathscr{R}
. Indeed, if
P(y_{0})=r
, then
R(y_{0})=0
so
Q_{m}(y_{0})=0
and thus
y_{0}\in\mathscr{R}
. Hence
\phi
is surjective. Since
\mathscr{R}
is finite,
\phi
is also bijective.

We claim that for any
r\in\mathscr{R}
we have
P^{-1}(r)=\{\phi^{-1}(r)\}
. Set
r^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}(r)
. The equality (
2.3
) shows that
\phi^{-1}(r)\in P^{-1}(r)
. Conversely, for any
r^{\prime\prime}\in P^{-1}(r)
we have
P(r^{\prime\prime})=r=\phi(r^{\prime\prime})
and since
\phi
is bijective we deduce
r^{\prime\prime}=\phi^{-1}(r)=r^{\prime}
.

We conclude that for any
r\in\mathscr{R}
we have

P(y)=\big(\,y-\phi^{-1}(r)\,\big)^{n}+r.

(2.4)

Let

. Set
s_{j}:=\phi^{-1}(r_{j})
,

. Then

(y-s_{1})^{n}+r_{1}=P(y)=(y-s_{2})^{n}+r_{2},\;\;\forall y\in\mathbb{C}.

The Newton binomial formula implies that

y^{n}-ns_{1}y^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{n}s_{1}^{n}+r_{1}=y^{n}-ns_{2}y^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{n}s_{2}^{n}+r_{2}.

We have
n-1\geq 1
. Equating the coefficients of
y^{n-1}
in the above equality we deduce
s_{1}=s_{2}
. Since
\phi
is bijective we conclude that
r_{1}=r_{2}
so that
\mathscr{R}
consists of a single root
r_{0}
. The equality (
2.4
) shows that
P(y)=(y-r_{0})^{n}+r_{0}
.

\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}\sqcup

The above discussion shows that if

is affinely conjugate to the monomial

. The equation (

Q(x^{n},y^{n})=Q(x,y)^{n}.

(2.5)

To tackle the two-variable case, we must first learn how to solve the much simpler one-variable case

Q(x)^{n}=c^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{p}(x-x_{k})^{n}=Q(x^{n})=c\prod_{k=1}^{p}(x^{n}-x_{k}).

For

, the polynomials

If

The inductive step

c+bP(x_{m})^{k}+P(x_{m})^{k+1}q_{0}(P(x_{m}))=(c+bx_{m}^{k}+x_{m}^{k+1}q_{0}(x_{m}))^{n}.

(3.3)

Since
(\ref{3.3})
is of the type
Q(P)=Q^{n}
in one variable, it is only solvable when
P(x)
is conjugate to a monomial by section 2. Then by Lemma
3.1
, this means that
Q
must also be conjugate to a monomial.

If
c=0
, then

P(x_{m})^{k}q(P(x_{1}),...,P(x_{m}))=x_{m}^{kn}q(x_{1},...,x_{m})^{n},

and since
q(P(x_{1}),...,P(x_{m}))
is not divisible by
x_{m},
then
P(x_{m})^{k}
has to divide
x_{m}^{kn}
. However, since
\deg(P)=n
, this only leaves the possibility that
P=x^{n}
, and once again
Q
has to be a monomial by the previous lemma.

1.2
