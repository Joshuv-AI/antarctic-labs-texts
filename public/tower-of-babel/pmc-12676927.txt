Analyzing the Role of Surgical Management of Initial Fractures of the Distal Radius in Refracture among Elderly Koreans: A Nationwide Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Soo Hwan Kang, Soo Min Cha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe hypothesized that surgical treatment for acute distal radius fractures (DRFs) plays a major role in minimizing malunion; thus, we sought to estimate the overall incidence of refracture in the distal radius in elderly populations using a real-world, nationwide cohort from the National Health Information Database of Korea.MethodsA total of 201,146 participants from 49.4 million Korean people met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: female sex, age older than 65 years at the time of index injury (first episode of DRF), diagnosis between 2010 and 2015, and at least 2 years of follow-up since the index fracture.ResultsAmong the 201,146 enrolled patients, 22,597 (11.3%) experienced a refracture during the follow-up period. Of the 178,549 patients who did not experience a refracture, 119,871 (59.6%) were treated conservatively (scenario 1), while 58,678 (29.2%; scenario 2) underwent surgical management without refracture. The surgical methods used in these patients included Kirschner wires (19,823, 9.8%), plates without removal (12,601, 6.2%), and external fixators (1,253, 0.6%). Among the 58,678 surgically treated patients, 25,001 later underwent implant removal (plate). Refractures were identified in 22,597 patients (11.3%), with 4.1% treated conservatively again at refracture (scenario 3) and 2.9% treated surgically for refractures after initial recovery from the first fracture via a conservative approach (scenario 4). Additionally, corrective surgeries for second fractures were identified in 1,046 patients (0.5%) (scenario 5), where the initial fracture was managed conservatively. Within the current cohort, refracture after surgical treatment for the first event occurred in 7,386 patients (3.7%; scenarios 6, 7, and 8).ConclusionsThe refracture rate within this retrospective DRF cohort was revealed to be as common as the overall DRF rate in older patients. Considering the lack of knowledge regarding refractures to date, surgical treatment at initial fracture appears to minimize the risk of typical malunion, thus providing an easier approach to treating refractures. However, further studies should be conducted to rationalize surgical treatment for DRF with respect to refractures at the radius malunion.Keywords: Refracture, Radius, Malunion, Correction

Fractures of the distal radius are common injuries, accounting for 12% to 17% of all fractures.1) In particular, this injury has been reported as the most common fracture type in upper extremities in older adults.2) Most of these extra-articular fractures occur as a result of low-energy falls during daily living. Despite the differences in health care systems across countries, extra-articular distal radius fractures (DRFs) have been managed conservatively by primary orthopedic clinicians based on prevailing evidence.3) Only a small group of patients experience debilitating residual symptoms after bone healing. Ideally, surgeons would be able to identify this group of at-risk patients, offering early surgical options and optimizing the best risk-benefit ratio for both patients and the health care system.

Some evaluation tools have been used to help identify at-risk patients; however, most of them only predict “malunion vs. proper union,” not dysfunction. Fortunately, malunion does not always result in dysfunction, especially in osteoporotic extra-articular fractures.4) Thus, a recent report proposed a paradigm shift in the management of DRFs in this age group, in which universal conservative treatment is promoted as the first-line treatment for all DRFs in patients with low-level activities in their everyday lives, with corrective surgery being used only as a staged procedure in patients who report residual dysfunction after adequate rehabilitation.5)

Another debate has emerged regarding whether refracture in the ipsilateral radius in older adults could be conservatively managed more than once. In some studes, “refracture of the distal radius” was defined as “acute on chronic distal radius fracture” or “secondary fracture occurring after malunion of a primary distal radius fracture.”6) However, the incidence or epidemiology of this refracture in the distal radius is unknown. Management options for refracture, even in a fragile radius, depend on the previous treatment conducted (conservative vs. operative),7,8,9) and concerns for existing malunion were observed mostly in patients with refractures after conservative treatment (Fig. 1). Additionally, there are no established guidelines for the treatment of refracture in the distal radius after conservative treatment, regardless of malunion. We hypothesized that surgical treatment, especially open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), for acute DRFs plays a major role in minimizing malunion; thus, we sought to estimate the overall incidence of refracture in the distal radius in the elderly population. Additionally, we presumed that trends in the management of refractures could be investigated through a real-world nationwide cohort. We herein report the role of surgical management for DRF in elderly patients using the National Health Information Database of Korea.

METHODS

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungnam National University Hospital (IRB No. CNUH 2023-06-041). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Study Subjects

In this retrospective cohort study, we selected subjects from the Cohort of the Korean National Health Insurance Service - National Health Information Database (NHIS - NHID) collected by the Korean NHIS (NHIS-2024-1-107). The Korean NHIS website (https://nhiss.nhis.or.kr/bd/ay/bdaya001iv.do) was used to access the NHIS and other related cohorts. For this study, 201,146 participants from a pool of 49.4 million Korean people met the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) female sex, (2) age over 65 years at the time of index injury (first episode of DRF), (3) diagnosis between 2010 and 2015, and (4) at least 2 years of follow-up after the index fracture. All NHIS participants were followed up until December 31, 2022, unless death or disqualification from National Health Insurance, such as emigration, occurred. Since the NHIS is the sole provider of the Health Insurance Service Policy universal health care program, all personal details, age, and medical treatment data are collected and stored in the NHIS database for Korean people designated as insured workers, insured self-employed persons or medical aid beneficiaries.10,11) The dataset included comprehensive information on ambulatory and inpatient medical claims, including codes for treatment procedures, and primary and secondary diagnoses.

Cohort of DRF in the Elderly Female Population

The eligibility criteria for DRF in the elderly population were as follows: (1) first visit to the emergency department or clinic regardless of the level of institution with a diagnostic code for DRFs (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] S52) or a subtype fracture code according to combined ulnar fracture (styloid, metaphyseal) (S52520, S52521, S52530, S52531, S52540, S52541, S52580, S52581, S52590, S52591, S5260, S5261);12,13) (2) some heterogeneous codes, such as M8003, M8013, M8023, M8033, 8043, M8053, M8083, M8093, M8403, M8413, M8423, M8433, M9073; (3) at least a 2-year fracture-free period in the ipsilateral distal radius; and (4) treatment with traditional methods, such as cast, closed pinning, ORIF, external fixator, and corrective osteotomy-internal fixation following refracture, according to the code for each treatment method (T6030/T6151/T6152, N0996/N0998/N1601/N1603/N1611, N0971/N0977, N0304G/N0307G/N0051002) (Supplementary Material 1); (5) inclusion was limited to osteoporotic DRF (patients older than 65 years at the time of injury). Patients with a history of ipsilateral DRF before 2010 and those older than 65 years at the time were excluded as the reason for refracture. Additionally, during a minimum follow-up of at least 2 years, patients with index fractures that occurred within 2 years before the conclusion of the observation duration (December 31, 2022) were excluded.

Designation of 8 Scenarios

For all enrollees, we set 8 scenarios to identify all possible treatment practices (Fig. 2). Scenario 1: conservative treatment for the first fracture/without refracture. Scenario 2: primary surgical treatment (closed pinning, ORIF, external fixator) for the first fracture/without refracture. Scenario 3: conservative treatment for the first fracture and conservative treatment for refracture. Scenario 4: conservative treatment for the initial fracture and surgical treatment for refracture (closed pinning, ORIF, and external fixation without correction). Scenario 5: conservative treatment for the initial fracture and surgical correction for a malunited radius at refracture. Scenario 6: retained metal device (plate) after primary surgical treatment of the initial fracture, and conservative treatment of a refracture. Scenario 7: retained metal device (plate) after primary surgical treatment for the first fracture, followed by surgical treatment for refracture (fixation without correction or correction). Scenario 8: no retained metal device, including the plate after primary surgical treatment for the initial fracture, and conservative or surgical treatment for refracture (fixation without correction or correction).

Designations for the 8 scenarios. Tx: treatment, Ex Fx: external fixator.

Definitions/Designations for Each Practice

All designations for practices in the 8 scenarios were defined according to the time since diagnosis of the first fracture. Conservative treatment included all procedures, such as splinting, casting, or bracing, at the time of injury. Additional procedures in which the products were fitted long-arm to short-arm were considered index practices of conservative treatment (scenario 1). Primary surgical treatment included all types of surgical procedures, regardless of whether they were “percutaneous” or “open”, limited to surgical practices conducted within 2 months of injury (scenario 2). All surgical options, including plates, were regarded as “plating” regardless of whether additional K-wire fixation was used or not. The “external fixator” was designated in the same way. Any surgical practice encoded as “corrective osteotomy and fixation” within a 2-month period was regarded as primary surgery (index), meaning “reduction and fixation.” Repeating cast/splint application or surgical procedures within 6 months was considered the same procedure as the initial treatment. The subsequent surgical procedure within 6 months after the conservative treatment due to delayed union/nonunion was regarded as scenario 2. In these cases, the type of surgery, including the use of a bone graft, was not further distinguished or classified. Additionally, subsequent casts/splints within 6 months after primary surgical treatment due to the same fracture were assigned to scenario 2. Refracture was defined as a new injury in the ipsilateral radius, occurring after complete union at least 2 years after the first management. Even though some patients might experience malunion after initial conservative treatment, if surgical treatment was encoded as described in scenario #2, they were assigned to scenario 4 because only “acute fracture on chronic” was managed surgically as “fixation without correction.” The treatment code of “corrective osteotomy” was classified into scenario 5, regardless of the coding “internal fixation,” “osteosynthesis,” “revision osteosynthesis,” “bone graft,” “pinning,” or “external fixator.” Finally, scenarios 6, 7, and 8 were defined similarly, but only under the conditions of plate retention or removal for the index injury.

RESULTS

Among the total enrolled patient cohort, 22,597 patients (11.3%) experienced a refracture during the follow-up period (Fig. 3, red box). Of the 178,549 patients who did not experience a refracture, 119,871 (59.6%) were treated conservatively (scenario 1), while 58,678 (29.2%, scenario 2) underwent surgical management without refracture. The surgical methods used in these patients included K-wires (19,823, 9.8%), plates without removal (12,601, 6.2%), and external fixators (1,253, 0.6%). Among the 58,678 surgically treated patients, 25,001 later underwent implant removal (plate) under anesthesia. Therefore, among the 37,602 patients who received plating without refracture, plate removal was performed in 62.6% (25,001 patients).

Diagram illustrating patient distributions across the 8 scenarios/groups. Detailed definitions of each scenario are provided in Fig. 2.

Among the refracture group (22,597 patients), 8,266 patients were treated conservatively again after refracture (4.1%, scenario 3), whereas 5,899 patients (2.9%) were treated surgically for refracture with a history of conservative treatment after the first fracture (scenario 4). In this subcategory, refractures were managed via typical methods, such as K-wire fixation, ORIF with a plate, or an external fixator. Among these patients, the plate was removed in 1,592 patients and was retained in the remaining 4,307 patients. Additionally, corrective surgeries for refractures were identified in 1,046 patients (0.5%) (scenario 5), in which the initial fracture was managed conservatively. In this group, 703 radii were corrected with a plate, and 275 and 68 radii were corrected with a K-wire and an external fixator, respectively.

Within the current cohort, refracture after the surgical treatment of the first fracture occurred in 7,386 patients (3.7%; scenarios 6, 7, and 8). K-wire fixation, ORIF with a plate, or an external fixator was applied at the initial fracture in 2,727, 4,356, and 303 patients, respectively. Among the 4,356 patients managed with a plate, the plate was retained in 3,527 patients without removal. Of these 3,527 patients, only 967 patients who experienced refractures were treated conservatively (0.5%, scenario 6). Among the 3,527 patients (with plates), 2,560 were managed surgically (1.3%, scenario 7). The initial plate was replaced with a new device in all patients. Among these patients, typical fixation without correction (reduction and fixation) was performed in 2,434 patients; however, corrective surgery was conducted in 126 patients (Figs. 3 and 4).

Diagram of the detailed distributions among refracture scenarios/groups. Detailed definitions of each scenario are provided in Fig. 2.

Among 3,859 patients without a metal device after primary surgery at the first fracture, 2,611, 1,182, and 66 patients were treated conservatively, with fixation without correction (reduction and fixation) performed for refracture, and with corrective surgery, respectively (1.9%, scenario 8) (Figs. 3 and 4).

DISCUSSION

Our hypothesis for this study was that surgical treatment, especially ORIF, for acute DRFs plays a major role in minimizing malunion for several reasons. First, particularly in this age group vulnerable to porotic bony structures, most of the dorsal cortex in the distal radius comminutes to tiny pieces, even though it is seen as a simple or minimally displaced extra-articular pattern on simple radiographs. Since the 2000s, various locking plate/screw systems have been introduced, and excellent efficacies on these devices have been reported worldwide.14,15) After repeated modification/update of devices for easy surgical handling and reducing the bulkiness for unexpected complications, the tendency of surgery for acute injuries has gradually increased.3,16,17) Among the radiographic parameters, compression of the locking plate using a metaphyseal cortical screw on the volar cortex guarantees the restoration of radiologic “volar tilting,” which was previously most frequently deteriorated, due to the angled design ranging from 10° to 15°. Second, most fractures in the elderly female population are classified “extra-articular,” with the expectation of a more favorable prognosis than complex DRF.18) Intra-articular fractures with more than 2 mm of step-off, both column fractures and rare patterns, such as Barton’s fracture, are more commonly diagnosed in younger patients or males. However, these classifications have worse outcomes with an increased risk of symptomatic malunion even after surgery because there is more complex breakage in the articular cartilage than in the metaphyseal lesion-DRF.19,20) Finally, the ulnar positive variance, regardless of previous symptoms before radial fracture, could sometimes be further aggravated due to radial shortening, especially after conservative treatment. However, these positive ulnar variances within a few millimeters could be resolved by locking plates at the time of reduction/distraction for fractures,21,22) similar to severely metaphyseal comminuted fractures (Fig. 5).23,24,25)

(A) Distal radius fracture accompanied by ulnar positive variance (a shortened radius due to metaphyseal comminution; white lines). (B) It was fixed primarily with a locking plate under manual reduction/distraction. (C) Final follow-up.

We focused on the incidence of refracture investigated in our study. During a mean of 11.7 years of follow-up until 2022, an 11.3% refracture rate was identified; this indicates that after initial DRF at 65 years of age, the risk for the same fracture associated with osteoporosis in the ipsilateral wrist remains as significant as the original incidence of DRF of 18% in this age group.2) The overall ratio of osteoporosis treatment covered by national health insurance was similar between the total cohort and the refracture group at approximately 85%. Thus, refracture several years after the first episode may be regarded as another fragile fracture or presumed to be due to frequent falls associated with hip or spine fractures or even activities of daily living with extended life expectancy.

A recent article proposed a paradigm shift in the management of DRFs, focusing on randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews.5) The findings strongly suggested that in elderly patients over 65 years of age, most DRFs can be managed conservatively. The authors recommended exploring the feasibility of advocating for universal conservative treatment for patients with less functional demands while having the option of staged surgery in the form of corrective osteotomy when there is symptomatic malunion amenable to anatomical correction. They emphasized that future research should also aim to identify patients who would benefit from surgical intervention by considering the type of functional recovery needed rather than relying predominantly on the patient’s age as the determining factor in the decision-making process. We strongly agree with this recommendation.

However, refractures in patients with symptomatic malunion have been scarcely investigated as of late.6,26) Two previous reports involved corrective osteotomy using the secondary fracture line of the acute fracture. However, malunion sometimes requires another osteotomy in addition to the secondary fracture line or more complicated steps of surgery (Figs. 6, 7, 8). Occasionally, concurrent surgical manipulation of the ulna, such as secondary ulnar impaction associated with radius malunion, requires a simultaneous shortening procedure during the correction of the radius.27,28) Conclusively, as described above, our authors suggest that refractures resulting from symptomatic malunion as well as acute on chronic DRFs may become more common in some nations with accelerated aging.17,18)

Interestingly, the scenarios of surgical treatment for refracture seem to infer the pattern of refracture based on the type of initial treatment. Rare cases were identified in scenario 6 (0.5%). We presume that, first, the refracture rate would be minimized owing to the protection effect of the retained metal device (plate), and second, the refracture might occur proximal to the proximal end of the plate, necessitating surgical management (as in distal onethird metadiaphyseal fractures). In scenario 7 (1.3%, 2,434 fixations without correction: 126 with correction), “surgery at initial fracture and retained plate” leads to refracture as the periprosthetic fracture pattern of a well-united radius or concurrent plate failure. Importantly, in scenario 8, an extremely small number of corrective surgeries occurred despite the “removed plate,” indicating that initial surgery could result in a properly united radius, and refracture seems to be the first episode of DRF or a periprosthetic fracture pattern of the well-united radius (Fig. 9).

(A) Refracture that occurred after previous distal radius fracture was surgically treated. Only radial lengthening was needed; thus, plating with slight distraction was performed (“corrective surgery” in scenario/group #8). (B) After removal of the plate, refracture sometimes manifests in a more proximal area without requiring corrective surgery.

The strength of this study is the use of Korean nationwide data. Some orthopedic surgeons treated fractures without considering whether they were refractures on radiographs. Without medical history provided by patients, the individual data from the NHIS were not searched by physicians or surgeons in clinics or emergency departments. ORIF for a suspected refracture would reveal a previous fracture due to subtle angulation or deterioration of the volar metaphyseal cortex. Thus, changes to corrective surgery rather than ORIF is warranted because fixation without correction of a malunited cortex is challenging due to the contour mismatch with manufacturing of the volar locking plate. We herein suggest that surgery, especially for the first episode of extra-articular DRF, plays an important role in minimizing malunion, even though we acknowledge that not all malunions cause symptoms or dysfunction. Concurrent development of anesthetic skills, such as brachial plexus blocks or wide-awake local anesthesia no tourniquet (WALANT), could facilitate the utilization of wrist surgery in older individuals.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the nature of current NIH data has no information for radiographic images; thus, many parameters associated with radiography and surgery have not been fully evaluated. However, Korean NHI inspection for “improper payment” and “reducing fees for service” has been strictly maintained. Thus, the diagnostic codes for the classification of fracture type or surgical practices were controlled and followed appropriately. Second, the risk factors for refractures, such as epidemiologic factors, comorbidities, and medications for various types of diseases related to aging, could not be investigated through this survey. A prospective cohort study in the future should establish treatment guidelines identifying risk factors for refracture. Third, we acknowledge that in actual clinical practice, some surgeons may treat malunion or deformity using complex ORIF codes (e.g., N0613, N1611) without assigning the specific “corrective osteotomy” code (e.g., N0304G, N0307G). Such variability in coding practice could result in an underestimation of the true frequency of “corrective surgery” in our dataset. Therefore, the observed rates of corrective surgery across scenarios should be interpreted with caution, as some cases may have been classified under other surgical categories despite involving corrective intent. It is also regrettable that no baseline survey has been conducted, even among Specialists in Hand Surgery certified by the Korean Society for Surgery of the Hand, regarding coding trends for the corrective surgery. If such data were available, the proportional distribution from that survey could have been incorporated into the present nationwide analysis to better account for this variability. Finally, the removal of metal devices differs across nations somewhat depending on the common medical practices within a specific country. In Korea, when the plate is usually removed postoperatively between 1 and 1.5 years after proper union, the scenarios are further classified by this reason. The refracture pattern can be different depending on the radius with which the metal is retained. Additionally, the 2-year timeframe for defining the refracture was somewhat arbitrarily and loosely set. Thus, the factors related to “plate removal” could be applied differently according to the condition of the national standards in other studies.

In conclusion, the refracture rate within the retrospective DRF cohort was as common as the overall DRF rate in elderly individuals. Considering the limited knowledge regarding refractures until recently, surgical treatment at initial fracture seems to minimize the risk of malunion, thus providing an easier approach to reduce refractures. However, further studies aimed at rationalizing surgical treatment for DRF in elderly individuals should be conducted with respect to refracture at the malunited radius.
