Magnitude of poor multidrug-resistant tuberculosis treatment outcome among multidrug-resistant tuberculous patients at tertiary hospital, South West Ethiopia
Authors: Gemechu Gelana Ararame, Hailegabreil Nardos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe persistent spread of multiple drug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) is one of the most pressing and challenging issues facing the global TB control efforts. Standard first-line treatment for MDR TB is nearly ineffective for patients infected with strains of the disease that are resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin. Objective: To assess the magnitude of poor MDR-TB treatment outcomes among MDR-TB patients at the MDR-TB clinic, tertiary Hospital, Southwest, Ethiopia.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the MDR-TB clinic, Mattu Karl Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (MKCSH), from 2018 to 2024. A data collection checklist was used to collect information on sociodemographic, clinical characteristics, medication, and treatment outcomes from the patient’s medical cards. The collected data were cleaned, coded, entered, and analyzed using SPSS version 20 computer software. Descriptive data analysis was done to describe variables by percent and frequency. Data were presented in tables and pie charts.ResultA total of 72 (54.2% male and 47.8% female) MDR-TB patients were included in this study analysis. Among a total, 54.2% were men. The mean age of the patients was 31.42 years with a standard deviation of 7.81 years, and 25% were positive for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The most common adverse drug reaction was gastric disturbance (45.8%). The majority, 79.2% had a successful outcome: 75% were cured and 4.2% have completed treatment. The magnitude of poor MDR-TB treatment outcome was 20.8%: 12.5% were death and 8.3% defaulted from treatment.ConclusionThe magnitude of poor MDR-TB treatment outcomes among MDR-TB patients was high. Strengthening patient support systems and optimizing treatment protocols are essential strategies to reduce the prevalence of poor outcomes.Keywords: MDR-TB, Magnitude, Treatment outcome, MKCSH, Ethiopia

Introduction

MDR-TB is a rapidly-emerging disease that is characterized by challenging treatment and high rates of morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Recently, strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex(MTBC) that are resistant to one or more anti-TB medications have emerged, complicating efforts to manage the global TB epidemic [3, 4]. As World Health Organization (WHO) reported in 2023, about 10.6 million people globally contracted TB in 2022, an increase from 10.3 million in 2021 and 10.0 million in 2020 [5]. Misuse of anti-TB medications by doctors, patients, and manufacturers is the root cause of drug resistance [6–8]. One of the biggest global health and medical concerns is the rise of extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) and MDR-TB [9]. It poses a threat of turning TB into an incurable and deadly illness, especially in low-resource nations where HIV/AIDS is highly prevalent [10].

MDR-TB is MTBC resistant to at least both isoniazid (H) and rifampicin(R) [11–14]. XDR-TB is MDR as well as any fluoroquinolone, and any of the second-line injectable anti-TB drugs (capreomycin, kanamycin, and amikacin) [15]. Treating MDR-TB with second-line medications is more time-consuming, complicated, expensive, and prone to significant side effects [15–17]. According WHO report in 2023, 175,923 people were enrolled on treatment for MDR/RR-TB worldwide [18]. Ethiopia is the 15th of the world’s 27 countries with the highest burden of MDR-TB [19]. Pooled prevalence mortality associated with complications among MDR-TB patients in Ethiopia was 12.25% (9.39–15.11%) in 2018 [20].

Poor MDR-TB treatment outcomes were found to be associated with male sex, positive smear at treatment initiation, HIV co-infection, treatment with < 2 active drugs, prior MDR-TB treatment, using ≤ 5 drugs for 3 months or longer, higher baseline resistance, prior TB, low BMI, and fluoroquinolone resistance [21, 22].

To our knowledge, there is no study done on the magnitude of MDR-TB poor treatment outcomes among MDR-TB patients in this study area. Hence, we aimed to assess the magnitude of MDR-TB poor treatment outcomes at MDR- TB clinic, MKCSH, southwest Oromia, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting and period

The study was conducted at the MDR-TB clinic of Mattu Karl Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (MKCSH) from 2018 to 2024. It is found in Ilu Aba Bor Zone, Mattu city, Southwest Oromia, Ethiopia. It is located 600 km away from the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa. It serves as an MDR TB clinic for three regions namely Oromia, the southern nation nationalities and people of Ethiopia, and Gambela (Fig. 1).

Study site: Mattu town, Southwest Oromia, Ethiopia

Study design

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted.

Source population

All patients treated for MDR-TB at the TB clinic, MKCSH, were considered as source population.

Study population

All MDR TB patients who were treated with anti-TB from 2018 to 2024, and who fulfilled the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria

All MDR TB Patients aged ≥ 18 years, who had completed medical records and whose medical records were found in this MDR TB clinic.

Exclusion criteria

MDR-TB patients whose medical records not available, incomplete and transferred out.

Sample size

All adult MDR-TB patients treated at MDR TB clinic, MKCSH fulfil the inclusion criteria from 2018 to 2024.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Poor treatment outcome.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors: Age, sex, weight, occupation, level of education, area of residence. Disease-related factors: Clinical features, smear test, culture test, comorbid condition (HIV or others), categories of MDR-TB: new, previously treated, adverse drug events, additional drug resistance, initial treatment regimens, and concurrent medication.

Data collection tool and procedure

A data collection tool was developed by reviewing different literatures, previous studies and guidelines. This data collection tool contains sociodemographic factors (Age, sex, weight, occupation, level of education, and area of residence), clinical factors (clinical features, smear test, culture test, comorbid condition and categories of MDR-TB and treatment outcome were collected from the patients’ medical records that found at MDR-TB clinic by using this checklist. The primary outcome of this study was poor MDR-TB treatment outcomes. This outcome was assessed from the patients’ medical cards, which were diagnosed by a physician working at the MDR-TB clinic.

Data quality control and management

Data collection training was given for data collectors (two first degree nurses and supervisors (two first degree pharmacists) on data collection methods and the process of data collection. Pretest was done on 5% of study participants, then data collection was modified. To assure the quality of data, the collected data were checked for completeness on daily bases.

Data processing and analysis

Data were cleaned, coded, entered into epi data version 4.6, and exported and analysed by SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics, percentages, and frequencies were presented in tables and figures.

Operational definitions

Successful treatment outcome

was defined as patients who were cured or who successfully completed treatment.

Treatment failure

was defined as patients who did not recover from MDR-TB or died.

Poor treatment outcome

was defined as treatment failure, default or death from any cause.

Medication related characteristics and treatment outcome

Of all MDR-TB patients, 37.5% were treated with combination of seven TB drugs (Isoniazid (H) + Ethambutol (E) + Pyrazinamide (Z) + Levofloxacine (Lfx) + Proteonamide (Pto) + Bedaquiline (Bdq) + Clofazimine (CfZ)) + Vitamin B6(Vit-B6) for initiation phase, and almost half, 47.62% of patients were treated with a combination of Ethambutol (E) + Pyrazinamide (Z) + Levofloxacine (Lfx) + Clofazimine (CfZ) + Vit-B6 for their continuation phase of MDR-TB treatment. Successful treatment outcome among MDR TB patients was 79.2%: 75% were cured and 4.2% had completed treatment (Table 3). Poor MDR TB treatment outcome was 20.8%: 12.5% were died and 8.3% defaulted from treatment (Fig. 2).

Discussion

A significant proportion (20.8%) of patients had poor outcomes (that is, mortality and default from treatment). This finding is relatively similar to a previous study reported in Ethiopia (23.2%) [23]. This poor treatment outcome was lower than studies reported in Korea (34.1%) [24], Ethiopia (49.9%) [21], China(59.05) [25] and Kenya (23.5%) [26]. In contrast, this magnitude of poor treatment outcome was higher than previous studies reported in Peru (8%) and Latvia (13%) [27]. The possible reason for the disagreement result might be due to differences in the socio-demographic characteristics of the study population, economic status, study design, and sample size differences.

In our study findings, the prevalence of mortality among MDR TB was 12.5%. This finding is in agreement with a study found in Ethiopia (12.7%) [23]. However, this finding is lower than the previous study reported from Kenya (14.5%) [26]. Additionally, our study funding is higher than the previous study reported from Ethiopia (7.9%) [21]. Although the default from treatment was 8.3%. This finding is in agreement with a study reported from Kenya (8.3%) [26]. The overall treatment success rate of 79.2% exceeds the global average of approximately 60–70% reported by WHO [28], indicating substantial progress. However, the 20.8% poor outcome rate, comprising 12.5% mortality and 8.3% default, remains a concern. Mortality among MDR-TB patients is often associated with delayed diagnosis, extensive disease burden, comorbidities such as HIV, and adverse drug reactions [29]. HIV co-infection was present in 25% of patients. HIV status significantly influences TB treatment outcomes, with co-infected patients often experiencing higher mortality and default rates. The integration of TB and HIV services remains critical to improving outcomes. The observed adverse events, notably gastrointestinal disturbances (45.8%) and skin discoloration (8.3%), could impair adherence and contribute to defaulting, emphasizing the need for vigilant monitoring and supportive care.

Good adherence was documented in 70.8% of patients, which correlates with favourable outcomes; however, nearly 30% exhibited poor adherence, a well-known risk factor for treatment failure and resistance amplification [30]. The high incidence of adverse drug reactions may partly explain adherence issues, underscoring the importance of managing side effects proactively.

The predominance of young adults (mean age 31.42 years) aligns with previous studies indicating that MDR-TB disproportionately affects the economically productive age group. The slight male predominance (54.2%) is consistent with global trends, possibly reflecting gender-related differences in exposure and healthcare-seeking behaviour [31]. Notably, over half of the patients resided in rural areas (58.3%), highlighting geographic disparities in access to healthcare and possibly contributing to delayed diagnosis and treatment initiation, factors associated with poorer outcomes [32].

A significant proportion of patients (87.5%) had a history of TB contact, emphasizing the role of ongoing transmission and the importance of contact tracing and preventive strategies [33]. The high prevalence of undernutrition, with 83.3% having a BMI < 18.5, aligns with evidence linking malnutrition to increased susceptibility to TB and poorer treatment outcomes [34]. Undernutrition can impair immune responses, complicate treatment adherence, and increase mortality risk.

Most patients (70.8%) were newly diagnosed, suggesting ongoing transmission of MDR strains rather than solely acquired resistance from previous treatment failures. The high prevalence of resistance to rifampicin alone (91.7%) reflects the typical MDR profile, although the presence of additional resistance patterns warrants ongoing molecular surveillance. The detection of resistance via GeneXpert in 94.4% of cases underscores the utility of rapid diagnostics in this setting, consistent with WHO recommendations for prompt diagnosis [35].

Limitations of the study

This is a retrospective cross-sectional study that relied on routinely collected clinical data. Due to the retrospective nature, some interesting variables were missed. Finally, our small sample size limited our statistical power to detect associations for some predictors and may have precluded our ability to adequately adjust for potential confounders. Additionally, due to the small sample size it reduces the generalizability of the findings to the large population. Therefore, a further multicentre, large sample size, and prospective observational study is recommended to assess the poor treatment outcomes of MDR-TB.

Conclusion

The study findings showed that the magnitude of poor MDR-TB treatment outcomes among MDR-TB patients was high. Strengthening patient support systems and optimizing treatment protocols are essential strategies to reduce the prevalence of poor outcomes. The results call for intensified efforts at the clinical and programmatic levels to improve MDR-TB treatment success rates and mitigate the public health impact of resistant TB strains in the region.
