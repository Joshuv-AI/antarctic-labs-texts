Comparison Between Weight-Based Variable-Voltage and Fixed Volume-Voltage Protocols in Cancer Surveillance Computed Tomography
Authors: Joseph J. Ipsen, Kirsten Richards, James H. Seow, Alexander Diamant, Alvin Tanaya, Arathy Harikumar, Eric Williams, Angela Kilcoyne, Ashik Amlani, Christopher J. Welman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study assessed whether the weight-based, variable tube voltage (Bayer MEDRAD Centargo SMART) protocol compared with a fixed contrast, fixed tube voltage (referred to as the ‘OLD’) protocol for cancer patients undergoing CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis (CAP) scans could reduce contrast dose, without a reduction in contrast enhancement.Methods:A total of 149 oncology patients, each underwent 2 surveillance CT CAP scans using both the OLD and SMART protocols, typically with a 3-month interval between scans. Contrast volume was recorded for each protocol as well as the CT attenuation measurement Hounsfield unit (HU) at the ascending aorta, pulmonary trunk, right atrial appendage, middle hepatic vein, main portal vein, and renal cortex, as well as dose-length product (DLP, mGy/cm) and CT Dose Index volume (CTDIvol, mGy).Results:The SMART protocol used less contrast per patient with a median volume of 80 versus 100 mL representing a 20% absolute reduction. Quantitative contrast enhancement measurements demonstrated non-inferior HU values in the SMART protocol at all sites. This was achieved with a 19% DLP and 9% CTDIvol decrease in radiation exposure.Conclusions:The weight-based contrast, variable tube voltage protocol effectively reduces contrast volume without increasing radiation dose and does not reduce contrast enhancement.

CT plays an essential role in the diagnosis, treatment and surveillance in most cancers.1 Although protocols for cancer CT surveillance vary widely, intravenous (IV) iodinated contrast-enhanced images increase diagnostic accuracy.2 Administration of iodinated IV contrast is associated with risk of contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN). Recent data indicates the risk of CIN is close to 0% in patients with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of >45 mL/min/1.73 m², between 0 and 2% for eGFR of 30 to 44 and 0 to 17% with eGFR <30.3,4 The risk of CIN is also higher in hospitalized oncology patients and those undergoing chemotherapy.5 CIN is costly to the individual, increasing morbidity and mortality, and health services by increasing length of stay.6 It has been well documented that larger contrast volumes result in increased rates of CIN.7,8 Although the risk of CIN is considerably less than previously thought,3 the goal should still be to keep contrast use as low as reasonably achievable.

In addition to patient risks, cost to the community, both financially and through environmental impacts, are important factors to consider. Although primarily used in a catheterization laboratory, the largest public hospital network in Australia recently published an annual expenditure of $465,000 using 15.9 L of iodinated contrast per day.9 Furthermore, emerging evidence highlights that contrast media are being found in sewage, surface, and drinking water in many regions of the world.10 Although contrast agents are intrinsically low in toxicity, decreases in usage may help reduce any potential by-product risk for the aquatic environment and drinking water.10

Given these factors, multiple studies have demonstrated that iodinated CT contrast volume can be reduced while still producing a diagnostic image, although they have been focused on either the thorax or abdomen separately.11–17 To our knowledge, a weight-based contrast volume protocol has not been assessed in oncology patients undergoing surveillance CT chest, abdomen and pelvis (CAP) scans. This study aimed to assess whether contrast volume could be reduced with a weight-based protocol without a reduction in contrast enhancement or increase in radiation exposure.

METHODS

This retrospective self-controlled cohort study compared contrast volume, contrast enhancement, and radiation dose between a variable tube voltage weight-based contrast volume (SMART) protocol of 0.32gI/kg and fixed tube voltage fixed 100mL contrast volume protocol (referred to as the 'OLD' protocol). This involved analysis of patients with CT CAP scans completed from December 2023 onwards under both protocols as part of routine cancer surveillance. To calculate the required sample size, a paired t test-based power calculation was performed based on an alpha of 0.05, power of 0.8, margin of 5% and SD of 16.7 (based on a normal distribution, with values of 0 to 100) resulting in a number required of 69. With opportunity and access to resources we collected 149 samples, raising the expected power to 0.98.

Ethics approval was obtained through the South Metropolitan Health Service (SMHS) GEKO Quality Activity (54579).

Patient Selection

Records of patients scanned under the new SMART protocol were screened for eligibility from December 2023 onwards until 149 records were identified. Patients were included if they were aged older than 18 years, had a recorded estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) >30 mL/min/1.73m2, had a diagnosis of malignancy under surveillance and at least one CT CAP scan on the OLD protocol.

Scanning Protocols

CT CAP scans were performed with patients lying in the supine position with arms over their head. Scans were acquired in the cranio-caudal direction, with breath holding and extended from the thoracic inlet to the pubic symphysis.

The OLD scanning protocol utilized a Philips iCT 128-slice CT scanner (Philips Healthcare, Netherlands) with a fixed tube voltage of 120 kVp and adaptive mAs modulation. Raw data were obtained in 0.9 mm slices of 0.45 mm increments on a 512-matrix, reconstructed to 3 mm thickness at 3mm increments. 100 mL of contrast was injected as a biphasic injection of 75 mL at 2.2 mL/s followed by a delay of 13 seconds and subsequent administration of 25 mL at 2 mL/s. The line was flushed with 10 mL saline to clear any residual contrast within the tubing. The total time for injection was 59 seconds with scan initiated at 70 to 80 seconds post contrast administration to obtain a split bolus chest and abdomen acquisition. The standard tube voltage (kVp) was set at 120 kVp, although this could be modulated at the discretion of the Medical Imaging Technologist (MIT) for very low or high weight patients. The timing of the study acquisition (70 to 80 seconds) relied on MIT experience. If the patient was perceived to have a low ejection fraction, the MIT would select a delayed trigger (80 seconds) compared with 70 seconds for an expected normal ejection fraction.

The SMART protocol utilized a Siemens Force CT (Siemens Healthineers, Germany) with variable tube kVp and mAs modulation (CARE Dose4D). Raw data were obtained in 1mm slices of 0.7 mm increments on a 512-matrix utilizing a Br40 algorithm. Images were reconstructed to 3 mm thickness at 3 mm increments. The patient’s weight was entered into the MEDRAD Centargo CT Injection System (Bayer, Australia) pump together with the CT scanner-selected tube voltage (kVp) obtained from a single planning slice through the chest at the level of the main pulmonary artery. This automatically calculated a biphasic contrast protocol using a proprietary method based on a dose of 0.32gI/kg. Biphasic injection commenced with scanning initiated when a tracking scan with a ROI placed on the main pulmonary artery (PA) had achieved an attenuation of 150 HU. The combination of SMART protocol and PA tracking compensates for the ejection fraction, providing consistent arterial chest/aortic and portal-venous abdominal imaging. As an example, an 80 kg patient with a planned tube voltage of 110 kVp, would be injected with 85 mL of contrast at a rate of 3.4 mL/s followed by a normal saline flush of 10 mL at 3.4 mL/s. After a 17 second delay, the second contrast injection of 20 mL at 1.8 mL/s would be followed by a saline flush of 20 mL at 1.8 mL/s. Both injections produced a constant bolus shape without initial ramp or late downslope.

Omnipaque 350 (GE Healthcare) or Ultravist 370 (Bayer) containing 350 or 370 mgI/mL, respectively, was used in both the OLD and SMART protocols and injected through either a 20G or 22G cannula in the antecubital fossa or wrist. Pressure monitoring was limited to 300psi.

Image Assessment

Using 3 mm thickness reconstructed slices, mean CT attenuation measurement in Hounsfield Units (HU) were obtained with a circular Region of Interest (ROI) with an area of 50 mm2 (±5 mm2) at the ascending aorta (Asc A), pulmonary trunk (PT), right atrial appendage (AtA), middle hepatic vein (MHV), main portal vein (MPV), and renal cortex (RC) as depicted in Figure 1. Cancer may be prothrombotic and hence inclusion of the right atrial appendage, main pulmonary arteries and portal vein was deemed an important criterion, which may allow identification of asymptomatic central thrombus which can confer high mortality rates.18 The right atrial appendage was also chosen to assess whether there was excess attenuation from the injected contrast, which might result in streak artifact. To compare radiation exposure between the SMART and OLD protocols, dose-length product (DLP) and CT Dose Index volume (CTDIvol) were collected from the study radiation dose report.

The 6 key sites for CT attenuation measurement in Hounsfield Units. A black circle was superimposed over the region of interest measurement to improve visual contrast.

Statistical Analysis

The paired sample data set was assessed for completeness, with all 149 paired samples analyzed. There were no outliers on visual graph assessment. The OLD versus SMART protocol was compared for each measure. We performed a confidence-interval-based non-inferiority analysis with a set margin of 5% variance for each measure and a 95% CI. The 5% margin was calculated from the mean of the OLD protocol for each site. Where lower values indicated worse performance (eg, HU values), the 95% CI of the mean of OLD-NEW was required to be below this margin to demonstrate non-inferiority. Where higher values indicated worse performance (eg, DLP), the direction of the comparison was reversed. In this case, the 95% CI of the mean of OLD-NEW was required to be larger than the reversed-sign of this margin.

DISCUSSION

Fixed contrast volumes have historically been utilized to achieve image consistency and provide an MIT-led service.19 This relies on a homogeneous population weight, which is becoming more variable in the Australian obesity epidemic with 65.8% of adults being overweight or obese (body mass index, BMI >25 kg/m²) in 2022.20 This epidemic is expected to increase, with the 2025 population estimated as 35% with obesity (BMI>30 kg/m²) and 13% with severe obesity (BMI>35 kg/m²) compared with 19% and 5%, respectively, in 1995.21 Wide weight ranges impact contrast dose, with previous studies suggesting patients with lower weights receiving excessive iodinated contrast and in comparison, those who were overweight an insufficient amount.19,22 Insufficient contrast enhancement can reduce diagnostic accuracy, with reported ideal HU values of >90 at the renal cortex and the liver.23–25 Diagnostic accuracy can also be reduced by excessive contrast causing streak artefact, although typically only seen at HU values >2000.26 Our study demonstrated that cancer patients fall outside the expected weight trends of the Australian population, with a median weight of 78 kg. This may be in part due to “cancer cachexia” a state of malnutrition with weight-loss.27 This highlights the importance of weight-based protocols to reduce excessive contrast dosage. Although not assessed in our study, total blood volume has been demonstrated as an important variable, particularly in the female population who may benefit from lower contrast dose,28 which warrants further evaluation in future studies.

A recent study highlighted the large variability and lack of standardization of iodinated contrast study protocols.29 Reduction in contrast dose has been evaluated since at least 1995,30 with the aim of balancing between image quality and radiation exposure. Ultimately numerous protocols achieve similar results, varying according to institutional preferences with some previous studies highlighting that “many roads lead to Rome”.31 Previous contrast volume-saving protocols have utilized complex and time-consuming calculations involving height, weight, body mass index, and body surface area.32 Additional studies have developed artificial intelligence (AI) models that incorporate patient attributes such as height, weight, sex, and age to optimize abdominal organ parenchymal enhancement.25 Although early results using AI models are promising, multiple challenges such as integration, assessment of accuracy and reliability remain.33 In comparison, the weight-based protocol of 1 mL/kg described by Yap et al13 represents a simple, easily implemented and highly replicable alternative. This study assessed a similar weight-based protocol, with each patient acting as their own control having undergone a CT CAP scan on the OLD fixed volume-tube voltage and the new SMART variable tube voltage, weight-based protocol. In combination with the MEDRAD Centargo SMART contrast pump, which required only input of the patient’s weight and kVp obtained from the planning image, this protocol was easily adopted at our institution.

The 20% absolute reduction in contrast volume of 80 mL in the SMART protocol compared with 100 mL in the OLD protocol may reduce patient’s risk of CIN, which has been shown to increase with increasing contrast dose.7,8 There are also important implications for health care savings. A recent study by Kisby et al34 highlighted that in Western Australia between 2012 and 2022 the per-quarter number of total CTs increased by 65% compared with a population growth of 17%. A tertiary Australian hospital recently published an annual contrast expenditure of $465,000, with an average of 15.9 L of IV iodinated contrast given per day.9 Although the newer Centargo injector pump usually costs more than older single-dose pump injectors, it enables larger (500 mL) single contrast bottles to be used for multiple patients, reducing contrast and consumable wastage compared with single-dose systems.35,36 A recent study from an American center highlighted that 100 mL single-dose bottles wasted an average of 19.7 mL per CT scan representing over 969 L of contrast wasted per year.37 Furthermore, this institution projected the multi-dose system decreased pharmaceutical and plastic waste by approximately 73% and 93%, respectively, with an overall estimated annual savings of $494,000.37 Our experience matches the literature with the larger single contrast bottle decreasing turnaround time between scans,38 improving efficiency and reducing staffing costs per patient imaged. Although difficult to estimate overall cost savings because of regional pricing differences, the improved efficiency and reduction in contrast volume by 20% as demonstrated in this study clearly represents an important area of cost savings. In addition, decreased contrast volume would help reduce any potential by-products in the aquatic environment and drinking water.

This study had several limitations. A major confounder included the use of two different CT scanners, which may impact image quality and radiation exposure. Specifically, the two CT scanners had different detector efficiencies, beam filtration, and iterative reconstruction algorithms, which could directly impact HU measurement and dose metrics. Furthermore, the variable tube voltage protocol directly impacts HU, which varies by kVp depending on the underlying tissue density.39 For instance, when kVp is reduced, the HU value of bone increases, whereas fat decreases and soft tissues remain similar.39 These variations are typically small in soft-tissue equivalents with the absolute difference typically less than 10 HU.40,41 Although measuring HU as a surrogate image quality marker without quantitative signal and contrast to noise ratios is suboptimal, the study by Ha et al42 demonstrated that a lower kVp protocol resulted in significantly more noise quantitatively, without reduced image quality on qualitative analysis. Furthermore, Yap et al13 demonstrated that a weight-based protocol did not result in a significant alteration in noise level, nor signal and contrast to noise ratios. We opted to only assess median HU given that lower kVp protocols resulted in more quantitative noise that did not reduce image quality on Likert or pair-wise comparison.

Moreover, the contrast type and concentration between scans were not standardized resulting in inability to accurately determine iodine dose. This also impacts contrast enhancement (HU), with higher iodine concentration resulting in increased HU,43 a consideration when attempting to optimize contrast dose. Although the self-controlled study design eliminates potential confounders,44 the retrospective nature did not allow for collection of the patients weight, eGFR, contrast type, or cannulation site at the OLD CT CAP scan. In addition, it is feasible that the patients’ weight, disease burden, and physiology could have changed between scans.

Although image quality was not qualitatively assessed, these potential benefits of contrast volume reduction did not reduce contrast enhancement (HU). All 6 key sites of contrast enhancement (HU) demonstrated non-inferiority in the SMART compared with the OLD protocol. Fleischmann and Setiawan reported that soft-tissue organ portal-venous enhancement >90 HU was ideal, values which were obtained from the renal cortex and hepatic vessels in almost all cases in this study.23,25 In addition, although non-diagnostic for subsegmental pulmonary emboli, the HU values at the right atrial appendage and main pulmonary artery would enable detection of large thrombus. This is important in the prothrombotic state demonstrated in cancer patients, who are often asymptomatic with a high mortality of over 80% reported if not identified.18 Our study, which only measured contrast enhancement (HU) does not infer improved or reduced image quality, which is ideally assessed qualitatively, with either a Likert or pair-wise comparison, which could be assessed in follow-up studies.

In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that bolus tracking compared with a fixed scan initiation time results in improved contrast enhancement within the thorax and abdomen.45,46 Our two protocols differed in scan initiation time, with the SMART protocol utilizing bolus tracking and the OLD protocol initiated at a fixed 70 to 80 second delay. The increased HU values seen in the SMART protocol may be in part due to compensation for individual cardiovascular function and reflect superior timing irrespective of contrast volume and kVp changes.

These results were achieved without an increase in radiation dose, with lower median DLP and CTDIvol in the SMART protocol (Fig. 4). It is important to note that although the median radiation exposure values were lower, multiple confounders likely influenced this including CT scanner variability, inter-operator scan range selection, potential patient weight change, and variable tube kVp.

In conclusion, the weight-based contrast volume, variable tube voltage protocol effectively reduced contrast volume without a decrease in contrast enhancement (HU) and without increased radiation exposure for patients undergoing CT CAP scans in malignancy follow-up. Although this may be in part due to differences in CT scanners and protocol techniques, this has the potential to reduce patient harm and hospital expenditure. In addition, although further investigation is required, this may reduce environmental pollutants. Given the lower weights demonstrated in this study, this targeted protocol may be of increased benefit particularly to cancer patients. This protocol is easily implemented, simple, and reproducible. Although intrinsic limitations are acknowledged, this study highlights the importance of re-evaluating basic principles, adapting scanning technique and protocols to newer technology, with the aim of improving health care outcomes.

Further studies should aim to qualitatively evaluate image quality with Likert comparison and could assess whether further reductions in radiation dose could be obtained while maintaining image quality. In addition, standardized contrast dosing, sex-specific and body-weight-specific subgroup analysis would provide valuable insight into optimal contrast dose.
