Dapagliflozin binds PRMT7 to inhibit p38 MAPK phosphorylation and macrophage foam cell formation in atherosclerosis
Authors: Yan Fang, Sulei Li, Cheng Qin, Yucheng Chou, Naiyuan Sun, Yan Ma, Yang Zhou, Hui Li, Huanhuan Feng, Xiaohang Yuan, Haoyi Ye, Xiaoshuang Wu, Na Kang, Hongyan Chen, Lei Gao, Feng Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDapagliflozin exerts anti-atherosclerotic effects, yet its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. In high-fat diet-fed ApoE-deficient mice and ox-LDL-stimulated RAW264.7 foam macrophages, dapagliflozin alleviated aortic atherosclerotic lesions and intracellular lipid accumulation. Proteomic analysis identifies 73 differentially expressed proteins, with upregulated ATP Binding Cassette Subfamily G Member 1 (ABCG1) and downregulated lipoprotein lipase (LPL) in dapagliflozin-treated macrophages. Molecular docking reveals high-affinity binding between dapagliflozin and protein arginine methyltransferase 7 (PRMT7), whose overexpression in plaque foam macrophages is validated by scRNA-seq and immunofluorescence. Functional assays demonstrate that PRMT7 promotes macrophage foam formation and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) phosphorylation, while dapagliflozin inhibits p38 MAPK activation via targeting PRMT7. Notably, PRMT7 knockdown abolished dapagliflozin’s regulatory effects on p38 MAPK, ABCG1, LPL, and foam cell formation. In conclusion, dapagliflozin alleviates atherosclerosis (AS) by binding PRMT7 to suppress p38 MAPK phosphorylation and regulate ABCG1/LPL expression, thereby attenuating macrophage foam formation. These findings uncover the anti-atherosclerotic mechanism of dapagliflozin and contribute to precision therapeutic strategies for AS.Subject areas: Biochemistry, Biological sciences, Cell biology, Functional aspects of cell biology, Health sciences

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the arterial wall and remains the leading cause of heart failure and cardiovascular disease-related mortality worldwide.1,2,3 Dapagliflozin, a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) originally developed for the treatment of diabetes mellitus, has demonstrated significant cardiovascular benefits.4,5,6,7 The DAPA-HF (dapagliflozin and prevention of adverse outcomes in heart failure) trial clearly showed that dapagliflozin was superior to placebo in preventing cardiovascular death and heart failure events.7,8 The relative and absolute risk reductions in death and hospitalization were consistent across subgroups, including patients with and without diabetes.8 Several studies have suggested that the anti-AS effects of dapagliflozin may underlie its cardiovascular protective actions; however, the precise mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.5,9,10

Macrophages display functional heterogeneity in disease and play a critical role in promoting the initiation and progression of AS.11,12 They trigger the initial inflammatory response of the arterial wall by engulfing oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) to form foam cells.13 Moreover, macrophages modulate plaque stability by secreting proinflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes or by facilitating cholesterol efflux, ultimately leading to plaque rupture and severe cardiovascular events.14,15,16 Recent studies have demonstrated that epigenetic modifications play crucial regulatory roles in lipid metabolism in macrophages.17,18 For instance, acetylation relaxes chromatin structure, enhances the binding of transcription factors, and thereby upregulates ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 1 expression—promoting cholesterol efflux and reducing intracellular lipid accumulation.19 Ubiquitin-specific peptidase 9, X-linked, has been identified as a factor that suppresses lipid uptake in macrophages by removing the K63 polyubiquitin chain at the K27 site of the class A1 scavenger receptor.18 To date, few studies have directly explored the effects of dapagliflozin on epigenetic modifications in AS. The lack of evidence regarding its epigenetic mechanisms leaves a substantial gap in understanding its comprehensive anti-AS potential.

The protein arginine methyltransferase (PRMT) family, which mediates the arginine methylation of proteins—a key epigenetic modification—plays crucial regulatory roles in AS.20,21,22,23 PRMT1 is the most abundant PRMT subtype and a major driver of pro-AS processes.23 Mechanistically, PRMT1 methylates class II transactivator, promoting its degradation and thereby regulating AS progression.23 PRMT5 overexpression induces histone dimethylation, whereas pharmacological inhibition of PRMT5 markedly suppresses neointimal formation in AS mice.21 Despite the well-recognized significance of the PRMT family in AS, research on PRMT7 remains limited, representing an important knowledge gap. Further investigation into this enzyme may clarify its potential role in AS pathogenesis.

Therefore, we aimed to perform proteomic sequencing to identify the downstream targets of dapagliflozin and molecular docking and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses to explore its non-SGLT2 binding proteins. Through gene manipulation experiments, we sought to verify the anti-AS effects mediated by the dapagliflozin-bound proteins. These investigations are expected to provide a deeper understanding of the cardiovascular benefits of dapagliflozin and contribute to the precise and effective treatment of cardiovascular diseases.

Results

Dapagliflozin alleviates AS and macrophage foam formation

In AS model mice, Oil Red O (ORO)-positive regions appeared bright red under light microscopy, indicating lipid-rich plaque accumulation. Following dapagliflozin treatment, the percentage of gross ORO-positive areas was significantly reduced (Figures 1A and 1B; p < 0.05). In addition, hematoxylin-eosin (HE), ORO, and Masson’s trichrome staining of aortic root sections (Figures 1C–1F and S1) further demonstrated that the aorta root lesion areas (p < 0.05), the percentages of necrotic core areas (p < 0.05) and ORO-positive areas (both p < 0.05) decreased, whereas collagen fibers areas increased (p < 0.05) after dapagliflozin treatment. Collectively, these pathological alterations indicated that dapagliflozin contributes to the inhibition and stabilization of AS plaques. To examine the effect of dapagliflozin on ox-LDL-induced foam macrophages, Nile red staining was performed to evaluate intracellular lipid droplets.24,25,26 As shown in Figures 1G and 1H, dapagliflozin markedly reduced lipid droplet accumulation in foam macrophages (p < 0.01). These findings indicate that dapagliflozin exerts a protective effect against AS and alleviates macrophage foam formation.

Proteomics identifies the downstream molecules of dapagliflozin related to macrophage foam formation

The downstream molecules of dapagliflozin associated with macrophage foam formation were investigated through proteomic analysis. As illustrated in the volcano plot (Figure 2A), 73 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified by label-free proteomics, among which 31 were upregulated and 42 were downregulated. The heatmap (Figure 2B) demonstrated clear discrimination between the distributions of the 73 DEPs. Furthermore, focusing on pathways associated with macrophage-derived foam cell differentiation (GOx0010742), four DEPs were identified: ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 1 (ABCG1), glycoprotein nonmetastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1), and lipoprotein lipase (LPL). Notably, compared with that in the Ctrl group, the expression of ABCG1 was upregulated, whereas the expression of LPL was downregulated in the Dapa group (Figure 2C).

To validate the proteomic findings, we examined the expression levels of ABCG1 and LPL at both the cellular and tissue levels. As shown in Figures 2D–2F, compared with foam macrophages, dapagliflozin-treated macrophages exhibited significantly decreased LPL expression (p < 0.01) and increased ABCG1 expression (p < 0.01). Consistent with the in vitro results, Figures 2G–2J show that, relative to AS mice, dapagliflozin-treated AS mice displayed a reduced number of LPL-positive cells (p < 0.01) and an enhanced number of ABCG1-positive cells (p < 0.01) in plaques. Collectively, proteomic and validation experiments at both the cellular and tissue levels demonstrated that dapagliflozin alleviates macrophage foam formation by increasing ABCG1 expression and decreasing LPL expression. However, the binding target through which dapagliflozin exerts this effect on foam macrophages remains unclear.

Molecular docking reveals PRMT7 as the binding target of dapagliflozin

When the therapeutic effect of a compound is known but its underlying mechanism remains unclear, reverse target hunting is typically employed.27 The target category chart of dapagliflozin obtained through reverse target hunting is shown in Figure 3A. The predicted targets included electrochemical transporters (28.0%), enzymes (28.0%), kinases (16.0%), family A G protein-coupled receptors (12.0%), cytosolic proteins (4.0%), oxidoreductases (4.0%), surface antigens (4.0%), and unclassified proteins (4.0%). The top 10 predicted targets are presented in Figure 3B. The first four corresponds to the well-established binding targets of dapagliflozin, including the sodium/glucose cotransporter and the sodium/myo-inositol cotransporter. Therefore, molecular docking was conducted for the remaining predicted targets, revealing that dapagliflozin exhibited a relatively strong binding affinity for PRMT7. The 3D structures of dapagliflozin and PRMT7 are shown in Figure 3C. Binding affinity was used to evaluate the strength of the interaction between dapagliflozin and PRMT7. Molecular docking yielded a binding affinity of −6.263 kcal/mol, indicating a strong interaction between dapagliflozin and PRMT7, as shown in the 3D docking view of Figure 3D. Further analysis revealed that dapagliflozin was predicted to bind to PRMT7 through key interactions with the residues identified in Figure 3E. Specifically, dapagliflozin formed conventional hydrogen bonds with Arg189 and Ser192, with interaction distances of 3.2 and 3.1 Å, respectively, and exhibited a Pi-Sigma interaction with Phe197. These interactions suggest that Arg189, Ser192, and Phe197 may collectively contribute to the stabilization of dapagliflozin at the PRMT7 binding site.

As PRMT7 is the only known arginine monomethyltransferase, macrophages treated with dapagliflozin were examined by western blot and mass spectrometry to determine arginine monomethylation (mme-R) levels and verify the inhibitory effect of dapagliflozin on PRMT7. Western blot results demonstrated that dapagliflozin treatment reduced the expression levels of mme-R compared with the control, as shown in Figure S2. Meanwhile, as the separated components elute from the chromatographic column and continuously enter the mass spectrometer, the instrument performs data acquisition through sequential scanning, with each scan generating one mass spectrum. The plot constructed with ion intensity as the ordinate and time as the abscissa is defined as the total ion chromatogram (TIC). The TIC acquired via mass spectrometry and the Excel file detailing peptide information and variable modification are included in Figure S3 and Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Mass spectrometric analysis revealed that dapagliflozin treatment led to a reduction in global protein, peptide, and arginine site monomethylation levels, which is visually presented in Figure 3F. Collectively, these findings confirm that dapagliflozin binds to and inhibits PRMT7, suggesting that PRMT7 is a potential binding target through which dapagliflozin alleviates macrophage foam formation.

Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals increased PRMT7 in foam macrophages

To elucidate the role of PRMT7 in foam macrophages, scRNA-seq was performed to examine PRMT7 expression in these cells. Dataset GSE190220 comprises scRNA-seq data from the aortas of apolipoprotein ApoE−/− mice fed either a chow or a Western diet. The t-SNE algorithm, a widely used nonlinear method for reducing high-dimensional data, was employed for visualization. As shown in Figure 4A, the t-SNE plot identified 11 distinct cell clusters: B cells, macrophages/monocytes, T cells, neutrophils, immune cells, fibroblasts, dendritic cells, Schwann cells, platelets, mast cells, and plasma cells. To identify foam macrophages, subgroup analysis of macrophage/monocyte clusters was performed, revealing eight distinct subclusters (Figure 4B). The expression of marker genes across these subclusters is displayed as a bubble plot (Figure 4C). Fabp5, Mmp12, and Spp1 were highly expressed in subcluster 2, which was therefore defined as the foam macrophage population. The 10 most highly expressed genes in subcluster 2 are shown in the violin plot in Figure 4D; these genes were primarily associated with foam macrophages, including Mmp12, Cd9, Spp1, Gpnmb, and Fabp5. The expression of Prmt7 in subcluster 2 was visualized for AS and control mice (Figure 4E). Compared with control mice, Prmt7 expression was increased in AS mice. These scRNA-seq findings indicate that Prmt7 is upregulated in foam macrophages and may contribute to the mechanism by which dapagliflozin exerts its inhibitory effects on macrophage foam formation.

Increased expression of PRMT7 in foam macrophages

To validate the scRNA-seq results, PRMT7 expression was examined both in vivo and in vitro. IF staining and quantitative analysis (Figures 5A and 5B) showed that, compared with that in control mice, PRMT7 expression was significantly increased in macrophages within AS plaques (p < 0.01). Similarly, as shown in Figures 5C and 5D, western blotting analysis revealed that PRMT7 expression was elevated in foam macrophages compared with normal macrophages (p < 0.01). Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro findings confirm increased PRMT7 expression in AS and foam macrophages, suggesting that PRMT7 may exert a potential regulatory role in AS.

PRMT7 regulates macrophage foam formation and phosphorylation of p38 MAPK

Nile red staining was used to assess macrophage foam formation. As shown in Figure 6A, numerous lipophilic particles were observed in foam macrophages induced by ox-LDL. Quantitative analysis (Figure 6B) revealed that PRMT7 overexpression exacerbated lipid accumulation in foam macrophages (p < 0.01), whereas PRMT7 knockdown significantly reduced lipid accumulation (p < 0.001). These results demonstrate that PRMT7 regulates macrophage foam formation. P38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) is a key signaling molecule involved in cellular stress responses, inflammation, and metabolism. It promotes macrophage foam formation by enhancing lipid uptake and inhibiting cholesterol efflux. Western blotting results (Figures 6C–6E) showed that compared with the ox-LDL group, PRMT7 overexpression significantly enhanced p38 MAPK phosphorylation (p < 0.05), whereas PRMT7 knockdown reduced it (p < 0.01). Together, these findings suggest that PRMT7 regulates macrophage foam formation and p38 MAPK phosphorylation.

Dapagliflozin reduces phosphorylation of p38 MAPK in foam macrophages

When ox-LDL binds to cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) receptors on macrophage membranes, it promotes the endocytosis of ox-LDL and activates downstream intracellular signaling cascades, such as the MAPK kinase 6 (MKK6)-p38 MAPK pathway, thereby regulating macrophage foam formation. The results of PRMT7 gene intervention experiments confirmed that PRMT7 regulates the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and macrophage foam formation. Similarly, the results shown in Figures 6F and 6G demonstrated that dapagliflozin significantly decreased the phosphorylation level of p38 MAPK in foam macrophages (p < 0.01). In addition, dapagliflozin did not significantly alter the expression of PRMT7, CD36, or phosphorylated MKK6 in foam macrophages, providing further support for the mechanism by which dapagliflozin exerts its actions by regulating p38 phosphorylation, as shown in Figures S4–S7. Therefore, dapagliflozin likely reduces p38 MAPK phosphorylation by inhibiting PRMT7, and regulates LPL and ABCG1 expression, ultimately alleviating macrophage foam formation.

PRMT7 mediates the regulatory effects of dapagliflozin on LPL and ABCG1 expression

After PRMT7 knockdown, the effects of dapagliflozin on p38 phosphorylation, LPL and ABCG1 expression in foam macrophages were determined, and lipid droplets were assessed by Nile red staining. As illustrated in Figures 7A–7E, western blot analysis revealed that, compared with the shNC group, PRMT7 knockdown significantly reduced PRMT7 expression (p < 0.001), p38 phosphorylation (p < 0.001), and LPL expression (p < 0.001), while increasing ABCG1 expression (p < 0.001) in foam macrophages. Consistent with previous findings, dapagliflozin markedly decreased p38 MAPK phosphorylation (p < 0.01) and LPL expression (p < 0.01), and increased ABCG1 expression (p < 0.01). Notably, these effects of dapagliflozin on p38 MAPK phosphorylation, LPL, and ABCG1 expression were abolished by PRMT7 knockdown. Meanwhile, lipid droplet analysis also found that dapagliflozin inhibited macrophage foam formation (p < 0.01), which was eliminated by PRMT7 knockdown (Figures 7F and 7G). Collectively, these results demonstrated that dapagliflozin regulates LPL and ABCG1 to inhibit macrophage foam formation via the PRMT7-p38 MAPK pathway, as illustrated in Figure 7H.

Discussion

Dapagliflozin is a novel hypoglycemic agent that exerts cardiovascular protective effects independent of its glucose-lowering activity.28,29,30 In this study, dapagliflozin was found to stabilize AS plaques by reducing macrophage foam formation, with LPL and ABCG1 identified as downstream molecules. PRMT7 was identified as a binding target of dapagliflozin and was found to be highly expressed in foam macrophages. Furthermore, PRMT7 regulated macrophage foam formation and p38 MAPK phosphorylation, both of which were observed in foam macrophages treated with dapagliflozin. Collectively, dapagliflozin alleviated macrophage foam formation by inhibiting PRMT7-mediated phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and modulating the expression of LPL and ABCG1 in foam macrophages. This study provides new insights into the complex mechanisms underlying the cardiovascular protective effects of dapagliflozin and contributes to precision therapy for cardiovascular diseases.

The cardiovascular protective effects of dapagliflozin have attracted considerable attention in recent years.5,7,8,9 The DAPA-HF trial established the standard therapeutic role of dapagliflozin in patients with cardiovascular disease, regardless of the presence or absence of diabetes.7 In the DECLARE-TIMI 58 and CAMEO-DAPA trials, recent studies have shown that dapagliflozin is associated with a lower risk of hospitalization among participants with AS cardiovascular disease or those at high AS risk and improves arterial stiffness in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.28,29,30 These large-scale clinical trials collectively demonstrate the anti-AS effects of dapagliflozin. In preclinical and imaging research, Chen et al. and Leng et al. provided initial proof of concept for the plaque-stabilizing effects of dapagliflozin in AS mice, while Lee et al. demonstrated that dapagliflozin treatment mitigates inflammatory responses in macrophages in an AS rabbit model.9,31,32 Liu et al. further reported that dapagliflozin treatment is associated with a significant reduction in coronary artery inflammation, as assessed using the novel biomarker peri-coronary fat attenuation index.33 The present study further explored the mechanisms underlying the effects of dapagliflozin on AS plaques and foam macrophages. Consistent with previous research, our findings confirm that dapagliflozin enhances the stability of AS plaques.9 Importantly, this work provides new insight into how dapagliflozin inhibits macrophage foam formation and contributes to plaque stabilization. In this study, PRMT7 was identified as a binding target of dapagliflozin through molecular docking analysis. In vitro experiments further demonstrated that dapagliflozin reduced the level of mme-R catalyzed by PRMT7. As PRMT7 is the only known arginine monomethyltransferase, these findings confirm the inhibitory effect of dapagliflozin on PRMT7.20,34 In addition, Gessner et al. reported that dapagliflozin treatment modulates L-arginine-related biomarkers and cardiovascular risk estimation.35 The observed changes may therefore be associated with the inhibitory effect of dapagliflozin on PRMT7. Notably, the role of PRMT7 in the context of AS has not been previously reported, and this study initially explored the role of PRMT7 in the regulation of macrophage foam formation.

The biological function of PRMT7 in various diseases remains controversial. Studies have demonstrated that PRMT7 is regulated by estrogen and exerts a cardioprotective effect in postmenopausal cardiomyopathy, whereas following myocardial infarction, PRMT7 promotes angiogenesis of vascular endothelial cells and thereby contributes to the recovery of cardiac function.36,37 Other investigations focusing on the immunoinflammatory regulatory role of PRMT7 have revealed that inhibition of PRMT7 ameliorates monocyte recruitment to injured sites in inflammatory diseases.38 Furthermore, PRMT7 has been reported to promote metastasis in multiple types of tumors and regulate skeletal muscle oxidative metabolism.39,40 Therefore, PRMT7 exhibits distinct functions under different disease conditions and pathological mechanisms.41 The present study identified that PRMT7 promotes p38 phosphorylation via monomethylation, accelerates foam cell formation, and destabilizes atherosclerotic plaques. AS is a chronic inflammatory disease marked by lipid accumulation and immune cell infiltration in arterial walls.12 Macrophages contribute by internalizing ox-LDL, forming foam cells, and driving inflammation, with a high proliferative capacity. Studies have confirmed that the foam cell formation process is accompanied by upregulated expression of cancer-related genes, and antitumor agents have been shown to attenuate the progression of AS.42 Accordingly, the inhibitory effects of PRMT7 on inflammation and proliferation may be relatively beneficial in the context of AS. Concurrently, PRMT7 promotes angiogenesis following myocardial infarction, which is favorable for myocardial repair; however, angiogenesis has been verified to accelerate atherosclerotic progression.43 This further reflects the divergent roles of PRMT7 in cardiovascular diseases. In addition, significant functional heterogeneity of macrophages exists in pathological phenotypes and stages of diseases. Macrophage infiltration is detrimental to infarct size control and the preservation of cardiac systolic function in the early phase of myocardial infarction; in contrast, it promotes tissue repair and sustains cardiac function during the late phase.44,45 Therefore, the biological function of PRMT7 in cardiovascular diseases may vary in various disease types, cell types, and pathological stages. Given the limited studies focusing on PRMT7 in cardiovascular diseases, further investigations are warranted to elucidate the differential functions of PRMT7 in diverse disease models and cell types.

Ox-LDL is a key driver of lipid deposition in AS, primarily binding to the scavenger receptor CD36 expressed on macrophages. The CD36-ox-LDL complex initiates the endocytic process of ox-LDL, activates downstream signaling pathways, and ultimately leads to foam cell formation. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD36-ox-LDL binding activates the MAPK cascade, nuclear factor κB pathway, and NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing three inflammasome pathway.46,47 In the present study, dapagliflozin did not significantly affect the expression of CD36 or its downstream MKK6 but primarily inhibited the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and modulated the expression of LPL and ABCG1, thereby reducing macrophage foam formation. The involvement of the p38 MAPK cascade in macrophage foam formation has been reported in several studies.48,49 It has been shown that activation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway promotes foam cell formation, whereas macrophage foam formation can be suppressed by p38 MAPK inhibitors. These findings collectively suggest that macrophage foam formation is largely dependent on p38 MAPK activation. As a central mediator of ox-LDL uptake and its downstream proatherogenic signaling, p38 MAPK plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of AS. Therefore, the key molecule targeted by dapagliflozin that mediates its inhibitory effects on macrophage foam cell formation and p38 MAPK phosphorylation remains to be elucidated. The present study initially identified the role of PRMT7 in regulating macrophage foam cell formation and p38 MAPK activation. To validate this role, PRMT7 overexpression and knockdown experiments were performed in foam macrophages. PRMT7 overexpression markedly increased p38 MAPK phosphorylation levels, whereas PRMT7 knockdown significantly reduced them. Correspondingly, modulation of PRMT7 expression affected macrophage foam cell formation. Previous studies have revealed that PRMT7 methylates p38 MAPK at arginine residue 70, thereby promoting its activation.50 PRMT7 enhances methylarginine-specific modification of p38 MAPK, whereas mutation of arginine residue 70 impairs PRMT7 function. The methyltransferase activity of PRMT7 is essential for p38 MAPK activation, and silencing PRMT7 reduces p38 MAPK phosphorylation.40 Furthermore, PRMT7 depletion reduces p38 MAPK activation, while PRMT7 overexpression enhances it in myoblasts.40 Consistent with these findings, dapagliflozin decreased p38 MAPK phosphorylation and LPL expression, and increased ABCG1 expression, which were abolished by PRMT7 knockdown. These findings indicate that dapagliflozin regulates macrophage foam formation and p38 MAPK phosphorylation by binding to and inhibiting PRMT7.

In conclusion, our study offers new mechanistic insight into the anti-AS activity of dapagliflozin. We demonstrate that dapagliflozin directly interacts with and inhibits PRMT7, which in turn alleviates foam cell formation and regulates p38 MAPK phosphorylation as well as the expression of LPL and ABCG1 in foam macrophages. These findings advance our understanding of the cardiovascular protective mechanisms of dapagliflozin and contribute to precision therapeutic strategies for AS.

Limitations of the study

This study has three main limitations, which should be addressed in future research. First, this study revealed that PRMT7 regulated macrophage foam formation in vitro; therefore, the effects of PRMT7 in vivo need to be further explored. Second, in the present study, dapagliflozin inhibited the expression of both MSR1 and GPNMB in macrophages. However, PRMT7 had no significant effect on MSR1 or GPNMB expression in foam macrophages. Further investigations are warranted to clarify the precise relationship between MSR1/GPNMB and dapagliflozin. Finally, another limitation lies in the use of the RAW264.7 cell line and exclusive employment of male mice, which fails to fully recapitulate macrophage heterogeneity in mice in vivo.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal model and intervention

Based on previous studies, eight-week-old male Apolipoprotein E-deficient (ApoE−/−) mice (20–22 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd and fed a high-fat diet containing 21% fat and 0.15% cholesterol for 16 weeks to establish the AS model.14 The mice were randomly divided into two groups: the AS group and the dapagliflozin-treated AS group (AS + Dapa). Mice in the AS + Dapa group received a daily intragastric administration of dapagliflozin at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight for 16 weeks, whereas mice in the AS group received an equal volume of normal saline through the same route. At the end of the 16-week experimental period, the aortas were collected for gross Oil Red O (ORO) staining and histopathological sectioning.

Statement of ethics

All mouse experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (2021-X17-105). All experimental procedures adhered strictly to the guidelines outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, thereby ensuring the welfare and ethical treatment of the animal subjects.

Cell culture and treatment

RAW 264.7 macrophages were cultured in six-well plates containing complete culture medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics). The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until they reached 70–80% confluence. To induce intracellular lipid accumulation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing ox-LDL (50 μg/mL) for 24 h. Based on previous studies and preliminary experiments, dapagliflozin was added simultaneously with ox-LDL.9,10

Method details

Aorta gross ORO staining

Gross ORO staining of the aorta was performed to evaluate the severity of AS plaques throughout the entire vessel. Lipid-rich plaques appeared as bright-red regions, whereas normal aortic tissues appeared pale-yellow or colorless. The ORO working solution was prepared by mixing the stock solution with distilled water in a 3:2 (v/v) ratio. The aortas were immersed in filtered ORO working solution at room temperature and protected from light. After staining, the aortas were spread on a flat surface and photographed. The percentage of AS lesion area was quantified using ImageJ software.

Aortic root HE, ORO, and Masson trichrome staining

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining was performed to observe structural alterations in AS lesions. The sections were immersed in hematoxylin solution to stain cell nuclei (which appeared blue) and in eosin solution to stain the cytoplasm (which appeared pink). ORO staining was used to specifically label lipid accumulation within AS lesions. The dehydrated sections were immersed in ORO working solution (prepared as previously described), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining to visualize the nuclei. Masson’s trichrome staining was applied to differentiate collagen fibers within AS lesions. The sections were sequentially immersed in Weigert’s iron hematoxylin, Ponceau–fuchsin, and aniline blue solutions, in which collagen fibers appeared blue. The stained sections were imaged using a NanoZoomer S360 digital slide scanner. AS lesions were characterized by intimal thickening, the accumulation of lipid-laden foam cells, and the infiltration of inflammatory cells. For quantitative analysis, the lesion area was determined using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed to visualize the colocalization of specific antigens in aortic root sections. The sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 to disrupt cell membranes, blocked to reduce background fluorescence, and then incubated with a mixture of primary antibodies in a humidified chamber overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Antifade mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to counterstain the nuclei and preserve fluorescence signals. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope. The number of cells in the LPL/ABCG1 and F4/80 double-positive regions was counted in each field of view to evaluate the expression levels of LPL and ABCG1.

Detection of protein monomethylation levels by mass spectrometry

Relative quantitative analysis of protein arginine monomethylation was performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry platform. After dapagliflozin intervention, the macrophage proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and the polyacrylamide gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. The gel strips containing target proteins were subsequently excised into 1–2 mm3 fragments. Following in-gel protein reduction and alkylation, trypsin solution was added to digest the proteins and release peptides. After extraction, purification, and enrichment, the peptides were separated by a chromatographic system. The sample ions were desorbed from the peptide droplets and introduced into the mass analyzer of the mass spectrometer for detection. Raw mass spectrometry data were imported into Proteome Discoverer, and the UniProt database was selected for protein and peptide identification. Arginine monomethylation was set as a variable modification; peptide sequences and fragment ions were matched to identify monomethylated peptides. The level of total protein monomethylation was identified by the number of characteristic peaks of monomethylated peptides in the mass spectrum. Number differences in monomethylated protein, peptides and arginine sites between different samples were statistically analyzed.

Nile red staining

Nile Red, a lipophilic fluorescent dye, was employed to detect foam macrophages. To avoid aggregation and nonspecific binding, the dye was diluted to a working concentration of 1 μg/mL. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100, the Nile Red working solution was added to each well and incubated in the dark. 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used for nuclear staining and cell counting. The images were captured with an inverted laser scanning confocal microscope. Image processing and fluorescence quantification were performed with ImageJ software. Cell counting was performed by investigators who were blinded to the group assignments. The fluorescence intensity of intracellular lipid droplets in each field of view was normalized to the cell number determined by DAPI nuclear staining to assess foam cell formation.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The extracted proteins were mixed with loading buffer and heated to denature and unfold them into linear structures. The denatured proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes to facilitate antibody binding. Following transfer, the membranes were blocked and sequentially incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized using a chemiluminescent substrate and captured with a digital imaging system. The intensities of the protein bands were normalized to β-tubulin using ImageJ software.

Proteomic analysis of macrophages

Proteomic analysis was performed to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) associated with the regulatory effects of dapagliflozin on macrophage foam formation. After dapagliflozin treatment, proteins were extracted, purified, and desalted for sequencing. The qualitative and quantitative analysis of proteins were performed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer coupled with an EASY-nLC 1000 liquid chromatography system. The raw files obtained from the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer were analyzed by Proteome Discoverer 2.4. The UniProt Mus musculus database was used for protein spectrum matching. After normalization, statistical analysis was conducted to identify DEPs between the dapagliflozin-treated group (Dapa) and the control group (Ctrl), with thresholds set at a fold change >1.2 or <0.83 and p value <0.01. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to explore the biological functions of the DEPs. Volcano and heatmap plots were generated to visualize the DEPs related to foam macrophage differentiation.

Reverse target hunting and molecular docking

Reverse target hunting, also referred to as target fishing, involves methods based on two-dimensional and three-dimensional (3D) molecular structural similarity, as well as analyses of biological activity spectra.51 In this study, reverse target hunting was conducted by providing the small-molecule compound, converting it into the corresponding simplified molecular-input line-entry system format, and performing computational analyses using an artificial intelligence model. Based on deep learning algorithms and neural networks, the potential interactions between dapagliflozin and its binding targets were predicted. Molecular docking was then employed to identify the optimal binding sites and conformations of dapagliflozin, thereby predicting its biological activity. The main steps of molecular docking included preparing the three-dimensional structures of the proteins from the Protein DataBank and dapagliflozin from the PubChem compound database, using SiteFinder to predict potential binding pockets, and performing semiflexible docking to determine the most favorable binding poses of dapagliflozin at the protein sites. The docking results were evaluated according to binding scores that reflected parameters such as binding energy and interaction forces. The predicted protein–ligand interactions were subsequently validated by western blotting.

Analysis of GSE190220 single-cell RNA sequencing data

Raw scRNA-seq data from dataset GSE190220 were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database.52 The analysis of GSE190220 followed a standard yet dataset-specific workflow, incorporating quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and functional annotation to characterize cellular heterogeneity. To reduce the dimensionality of the high-dimensional scRNA-seq dataset, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was applied to project the complex gene expression profiles into two-dimensional visualisable plots. Cluster-specific marker genes were cross-referenced with previously published cell type-specific gene lists and databases. Violin and dot plots were generated to illustrate marker gene expression across clusters.

Construction of Prmt7 overexpression or knockdown macrophages

Prmt7 overexpression (oePrmt7) and Prmt7-specific short hairpin RNA (shPrmt7) sequences were cloned and inserted into lentiviral vectors containing a puromycin resistance gene. After lentiviral particles were added to the macrophage culture medium, oePrmt7 or shPrmt7 macrophages were selected using puromycin and confirmed by western blotting.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data obtained from replicate experiments are presented as mean ± SEM (Standard Error of the Mean). Statistical significance between groups was determined using Student’s t test or by one-way ANOVA. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
