Identification of Ctenocephalides felis felis and the detection and characterization of associated Wolbachia endosymbiont in Pakistan
Authors: Nadia Nadia, Muhammad Numan, Ahmad H. Khan, Sudais Rahman, Sidra Batoool, Zhihua Sun, Asma F. Alotaibi, Mashal M. Almutairi, Abid Ali, Alanoud T. Aljasham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fleas are holometabolous, blood-feeding ectoparasites capable of transmitting diverse pathogens of significant veterinary and public health concerns. Their occurrence and abundance within a given habitat depend on environmental factors and the availability of suitable hosts. In Pakistan, research on flea fauna and their associated pathogens has been neglected. To date, no reports have documented the molecular characterization of Ctenocephalides felis felis and their associated bacteria in Pakistan. In the present study, three hundred and eighty morphologically identified C. felis felis specimens-comprising 184 collected from free roaming (stray) dogs (n = 69) and 196 from cats (n = 86), were subjected to DNA extraction followed by amplification of the cytochrome oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1) and citrate synthase gene (gltA) for flea identity confirmation and screening for the presence of associated bacteria, respectively. Amplicons of appropriate base pair sizes were sequenced and submitted to BLASTn and subsequently subjected to phylogenetic analyses. The obtained cox1 sequence from the morphologically identified C. felis felis in this study showed 100% identity and phylogenetically clustered with C. felis felis sequences from India, Australia, Thailand, China, and Laos in GenBank. Similarly, gltA sequences showed 100% identity with the Wolbachia endosymbiont of C. felis reported from the United Kingdom. This study provides the first genetic characterization of C. felis felis infesting dogs and cats, and their associated Wolbachia endosymbiont in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP), Pakistan. These findings provide baseline molecular data and highlight the need for systematic surveillance and management measures to mitigate any potential veterinary and public health threats.

Introduction

Fleas (Insecta: Siphonaptera) are obligatory hematophagous ectoparasites of veterinary and public health significance. They are small (2–10 mm), laterally flattened, wingless, and highly specialized for a blood-feeding lifestyle, exhibiting distinct morphological and physiological adaptations, including laterally flattened bodies, piercing-sucking mouthparts, and enlarged hind legs adapted for jumping and efficient host acquisition [37, 38]. The importance of fleas is largely related to their ability to transmit infectious agents during blood meals [66]. Globally, there are 16 recognized families within the order Siphonaptera, representing over 2,500 species in 240 genera, with the highest diversity in temperate regions [9, 47]. Most flea species of medical and veterinary relevance belong to the Pulicidae and Ceratophyllidae [ 25].

Within the Pulicidae, the cat flea Ctenocephalides felis (Bouché, 1835), and the dog flea Ctenocephalides canis (Curtis, 1826) comprise most flea species infesting companion animals [6, 32, 51]. Among these, C. felis exhibits substantial genetic diversity and cosmopolitan distribution originating from the Ethiopian region [33, 45]. Based on molecular approaches, the genus Ctenocephalides is classified as having three recognized subspecies of C. felis: the cosmopolitan C. felis felis and two Ethiopian region subspecies: C. felis damarensis and C. felis strongylus [33]. Several factors contribute to its global distribution, including its broad resilience to habitat conditions within human dwellings, tolerance to climatic fluctuations, and larvae can exploit organic debris and other flea-associated resources [14, 28].

In addition to cats and dogs, C. felis felis has been reported from a wide range of wild hosts, including opossums and rodents [36], as well as raccoons [49], and is frequently encountered in human dwellings [7, 17, 41]. Accurate identification of flea species is essential, as different species are associated with distinct epidemiological risks [39]. While traditional morphological methods remain fundamental, the use of molecular markers has significantly enhanced species confirmation and phylogenetic resolution [62, 67]. The molecular marker cytochrome oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1) has been widely used for the identification, detection of intraspecies variations, and phylogenetic relationships of various arthropods, particularly ticks and Ctenocephalides spp. [54, 56].

The significance of C. felis felis is attributed to its parasitism and transmission of zoonotic pathogens, including Rickettsia spp., Bartonella spp. [9], and Wolbachia spp. [11, 40, 43, 50]. Wolbachia endosymbionts are estimated to infect approximately 40–66% arthropod species and many filarial nematodes [27, 53, 63]. Although the veterinary and public health significance of fleas has been studied extensively in other climes [30, 34, 61], such studies in Pakistan remain limited. To date, only a few flea species have been morphologically reported from Pakistan, including Pulex irritans, C. felis, C. canis [46, 55] Neopsylla sp., Ischnopsyllus intermedius, Nosopsyllus sp., I. octactenus, Xenopsylla astia, X. cheopis, Synosternus pallidus, and S. cleopatrae [23].

The climate and geography of Pakistan offer suitable conditions for the establishment of many flea species, yet there is no genetic data available to describe these species. Despite their importance, molecular insights into Wolbachia endosymbionts in fleas in Pakistan remain scarce. To our knowledge, no molecular studies have addressed the detection of Wolbachia bacteria in C. felis felis from the Oriental region, including Pakistan, to date. The present study aimed to undertake the morphological identification and molecular characterization of fleas infesting dogs and cats in Pakistan and to investigate the occurrence of associated bacteria.

Material and methods

Study area and flea collections

During September–November 2024 and February-August 2025, fleas were collected from free roaming (stray) dogs and cats across four selected districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province of Pakistan, including Nowshera (34.005152º N, 71.948503º E), Charsadda (34.159023º N, 71.754861º E), Mardan (34.169412º N, 72.082828º E), and Swabi (34.129097º N, 72.465734º E). No samples were found in December 2024 and January 2025 due to winter seasons, as flea activities are significantly reduced during these months. The geographical coordinates of each sampling site were determined using a Global Positioning System (GPS), and the map was created with ArcGIS (v. 10.3.1) (ESRI, Redlands, CA, USA) (Fig. 1). These selected districts were chosen based on their high animal density, accessibility, and representation of different ecological conditions within the province. All samples were collected opportunistically in public areas, and no animals were examined in veterinary clinics or under clinical conditions. Animals were physically examined under field conditions, and fleas were collected by hand and using a flea comb and forceps. Flea collection was carried out only once per animal host. The collected flea samples were preserved in sterile vials containing 70% ethanol and properly labeled with host type, host age, date, season, and location of collection. Morphological identification

The collected flea specimens were examined under a stereomicroscope (Luxeo 6Z, LABOMED, United States) and identified, where possible to the species and subspecies level with the aid of standard taxonomic keys [10, 31, 32, 35, 48].

The collected flea specimens were processed for DNA extraction (five specimens per host from each study site; Table 1). Before DNA extraction, specimens were removed from ethanol and air-dried or incubated at 37 °C for 20–30 min to eliminate the residual ethanol. Subsequently, the fleas were dissected using sterile scalpels and scissors. A conventional PCR was employed for the partial amplification of the cox1 gene using the primers HCO2198: TAA A C TTC AGG GTG ACC AAA AAA TCA and LCO1490: GGT CAA CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G [18]. In addition, PCR amplification of the citrate synthase (gltA) fragment was carried out using primer BhCS781p: GGG GAC CAG CTC ATG GTG G and BhCS1137n: AAT GCA AAA A G AAC AGT AAA CA [44]. Each PCR reaction mixture was performed in a final volume of 20 µl, including 1 µL of each primer (forward and reverse: 10 µM), 3 µL of genomic DNA (100 ng/µL), 3 µL nuclease-free water, and 12 µL of DreamTaq Green Master Mix (2X) (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

The conditions for thermocycling included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, then 39 cycles including denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing (30 s) at 48 °C for cox1 or at 56 °C for gltA, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Each PCR experiment included a negative control (no template DNA) to monitor contamination and a positive control (previously confirmed Rhipicephalus microplus DNA sample) to verify amplification success. Amplicons of expected size were determined by comparison with a DNA ladder. The amplification was analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized using a Gel Documentation System (BioDoc-It™ Imaging Systems, Upland, CA, USA). The amplified DNA was purified using the GeneClean® II kit from Qbiogene (Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France).

Sequencing and phylogenetic analyses

All the PCR-positive amplicons for both the cox1 and gltA fragments were sequenced bi-directionally using the Sanger sequencing approach at a commercial facility (Macrogen, Inc., Seoul, South Korea). The obtained high-quality sequences were trimmed and assembled using SeqMan v. 5 (DNASTAR, Madison, WI, USA) to remove the poor-reading sequence region. Multiple identical sequences for both genes were considered as a single sequence. Subsequently, the sequences were subject to the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTn) in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) [2] to determine their similarities to sequences in the GenBank. The obtained sequences and the GenBank-retrieved sequences, along with an outgroup, were aligned in BioEdit V. 7.0.5 (Raleigh, NC, USA) [24], using the ClustalW multiple alignment tool [60]. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA-11 software. The best-fit nucleotide substitution Kimura 2-parameter model [58] was selected based on model testing. The robustness of the tree topology was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Statistical analyses

The dataset of recorded observations was assembled and structured in Microsoft Excel 2023 spreadsheets, followed by descriptive statistics and graphical analyses.

From this number, 380 were identified as C. felis felis (Table 1; Fig. 2a), and the other 70 specimens remained unidentified due to morphological damage and incomplete diagnostic features, which prevented accurate taxonomic identification. Prevalence and distribution of fleas

Of the 155 infested animals, 73/155 (46.45%) male and 82/155 (53.55%) female hosts were infested with fleas. Infestation rates were 60% (n = 18) in dogs and 50% (n = 6) in cats in Nowshera, 44% (n = 11) in dogs and 89.6% (n = 43) in cats in Mardan, 60.7% (n = 17) and 80.6% (n = 25) in dogs and cats in Swabi and 67.6% (n = 23) in dogs and 80% (n = 12) in cats in the Charsadda district (Table 1).

Overall, the infestation rates of C. felis felis were relatively similar for both male and female hosts. However, male dogs exhibited the highest infestation rate in Charsadda and the lowest in Mardan. Similarly, female cats had the highest infestation as compared to female dogs and male cats. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, studies using molecular methods to characterize C. felis felis and its associated Wolbachia species in fleas infesting stray dogs and cats in Pakistan, along with their veterinary and public health importance, have not received adequate attention. Therefore, this study was designed to address this critical knowledge gap. The prevalence of C. felis felis observed in this study provides insight into its distribution patterns. This finding is consistent with earlier studies from Pakistan, where C. felis felis has been reported as the most common flea species infesting dogs and cats, likely due to suitable environmental conditions [3, 42]. Similar prevalence patterns have also been reported in other tropical and subtropical regions, such as India and Iran, where environmental conditions like high temperature and humidity favor flea survival and reproduction [5, 26, 57]. In contrast, lower prevalence in parts of Europe has been associated with improved animal management and routine use of ectoparasiticides [4, 6, 12, 21]. These differences highlight the influence of environmental and management factors on flea infestation patterns.

Ctenocephalides felis felis has a broad host range, which contributes to its persistence in diverse habitats. In this study, higher infestation rates were observed during the warm season, consistent with previous reports indicating that flea populations increase under favorable environmental conditions such as higher temperature and humidity [15]. The infestation rate in the study area was higher in cats than in dogs, which aligns with earlier findings by [1]. This high infestation rate in cats could be due to the role of felines as the primary host for this species. Additionally, the overall prevalence of infestation was higher in younger dogs and cats than in older animals, a pattern reported in previous studies [16, 65]. Our results are also comparable with those of [13] who stated that highest infestation of ectoparasites occur in young animals due to their poor grooming, compared to older animals.

Molecular characterization based on the cox1 gene confirmed the identity of C. felis felis with sequences clustering in the phylogenetic tree with those reported from multiple geographic regions, including Thailand, India, Laos, Australia, and China. This clustering further supports the genetic conservation of C. felis felis across different regions. Furthermore, phylogenetic analyses showed that C. felis felis forms a sister clade with the subspecies C. felis strongylus and C. felis damarensis, indicating limited evolutionary divergence among C. felis subspecies. In contrast, C. orientis formed a distinct clade, supporting previous findings that it is genetically separate from C. felis and more closely related to C. canis [20, 31].

In this study, Wolbachia endosymbionts were detected in 55% of C. felis felis specimens collected from dogs and cats, indicating a moderately prevalent association of Wolbachia with the C. felis felis species. However, this prevalence is lower than that reported by Tay [59], but higher than that reported by [22] in C. felis and other flea species. Such variations may occur due to the differences in host populations and preferences, geographic location, and detection methods. Although Wolbachia was broadly characterized in arthropods by [29] its presence in fleas was not confirmed at that time. The first conclusive molecular detection of Wolbachia pipientis in fleas, including C. felis felis, was reported by [50]. In the present study, the obtained sequences showed high identity and phylogenetically clustered with flea-derived Wolbachia strains, rather than Wolbachia from other arthropod hosts. These results are consistent with patterns reported in previous studies, where Wolbachia lineages are often structured according to their arthropod hosts [64].

It is important to note that the gltA primers used in this study were originally designed for the detection of Bartonella spp.; however, they successfully amplified Wolbachia sequences, a finding consistent with previous reports of cross-amplification due to conserved regions within the gltA gene among Alphaproteobacteria [8]. While this approach enabled detection, it represents a limitation, as it does not allow precise characterization of Wolbachia at the species or strain level. Thus, the results obtained should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should employ Wolbachia-specific markers such as wsp, ftsZ, and 16S rRNA genes to improve specificity and provide deeper insight into genetic diversity.

These findings have important implications for veterinary and public health, as C. felis felis is a known vector of zoonotic pathogens such as Rickettsia and Bartonella species. Also, the presence of Wolbachia in C. felis felis broadens our understanding of its distribution across different host taxa and highlights its potential relevance for veterinary and public health because this endosymbiont is known in arthropods to influence host biology, including reproduction and interaction with pathogens. Understanding the presence of Wolbachia in fleas could inform the development of alternative vector control strategies that could complement conventional measures, such as regular surveillance, treatment of companion animals, and environmental management.

Conclusion

This study provides the first molecular evidence of C. felis felis from stray dogs and cats, and its associated Wolbachia endosymbiont in Pakistan. Although no zoonotic pathogens were directly investigated in this study, the presence of C. felis felis highlights its potential role as a vector. These findings emphasize the need for further studies on the diversity of fleas infesting domestic and stray animals, as well as the prevalence and role of associated Wolbachia endosymbionts and other pathogens across the country.
