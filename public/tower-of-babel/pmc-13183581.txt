Harnessing normal-shear coupling in metabarriers for deep sub-wavelength underwater noise control
Authors: Vinícius F. Dal Poggetto, Marco Miniaci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anthropogenic underwater noise poses a significant threat to marine ecosystems, disrupting key biological functions. Common mitigation strategies include enclosing noise sources within acoustic barriers. Current designs include locally resonant absorbers, which offer narrow-band performance, and reflective systems with limited effectiveness at low frequencies. In this work, we propose an approach to design thin anisotropic metamaterial-based acoustic barriers for broadband underwater noise attenuation at deep sub-wavelength scales using topology optimization to maximize the coupling between normal stresses and shear strains. Unlike conventional methods, the proposed optimization is formulated in the static regime, relying solely on the homogenized elastic properties of the structured material and not on the characteristics of the surrounding fluid. The resulting metabarriers achieve a high sound transmission loss (STL, 100 dB peak) above 2 kHz, while maintaining a thickness-to-wavelength ratio as low as 1/70 below 1 kHz and STL of approximately 20–30 dB. The influence of hydrostatic pressure on performance is also evaluated, and structural modifications for practical deployment are proposed. The results demonstrate the potential of anisotropy-driven metamaterials as compact and efficient solutions for the control of underwater noise, offering a promising avenue for future acoustic insulation technologies.

Introduction

The rise of marine renewable energy has re-sparked the interest in compact, pressure-resistant devices that can reduce underwater noise. Particularly at low frequencies, human activities such as offshore wind farms, shipping, and military operations increasingly disrupt marine life, interfering with vital behaviors in aquatic species, especially mammals1. Whenever directly addressing the noise source is not possible, an alternative solution is to place barriers between the source and the areas requiring protection. However, reducing noise with ultra-thin, sub-wavelength devices remains a major challenge due to the longer wavelengths (compared to airborne acoustics) and complex fluid-structure interactions taking place in water2,3. Thus, the development of low-frequency, robust noise barriers to protect marine ecosystems is more crucial than ever.

The primary approaches for underwater acoustic insulation materials involve designing the impedance match (or mismatch) of these barriers with respect to the acoustic impedance of water, resulting in (i) absorptive or (ii) reflective behavior4. Common design strategies include fluid or solid-filled cavities, backings, resonators, periodic structures, and metamaterials—composite materials made of periodic arrangements of sub-wavelength structures5–7.

When impedance matching is exploited, the use of barriers made of polymeric materials enhanced with viscoelastic micro- or macro-inclusions and coatings embedded with periodic air pockets is a widely used absorptive approach4,8,9. In this case, insulation performance results from the polymeric matrix material’s molecular chain relaxation, providing frequency-dependent vibration characteristics (e.g., mode shapes) and good performance at high frequencies. Low-frequency absorption, however, remains limited8. Another possible approach is the conversion of acoustic waves into shear waves, exploiting the high shear damping characteristic of certain materials and improving performance10. Whenever inclusions in the matrix are considered, the operating frequency bandwidth is constrained by their resonance frequencies11. To address this limitation, researchers have proposed optimized geometries and material compositions for resonating inclusions, along with tailored properties of the host material to improve attenuation efficiency and broaden the operational frequency range12. An alternative strategy to tuning mechanical local resonators, whose resonance frequency remains fixed over time, is the use of coupled electro-mechanical devices to achieve tunable local resonance mechanisms through shunted piezoelectric patches. In ref. 13, the authors demonstrate that the sound absorption of underwater metamaterials can be adjusted with the use of a negative capacitance in a shunt circuit to achieve quasi-perfect absorption at low frequencies. This concept was later expanded in ref. 14, with an internal acoustic cavity coupled to the shunt circuit, enabling better acoustic absorption performance through optimization of the cavity shape and shunt circuit parameters.

Although relatively well-performing at mid and high frequencies, these approaches perform poorly below 1 kHz. Ensuring impedance matching between resonating elements (such as Helmholtz-like resonators) and water has shown potential as an alternative, at least in the proximity of resonance frequencies. Widely studied in the context of airborne acoustics15, their application in underwater environments has been less explored. Duan et al.16 numerically proved that an underwater quasi-Helmholtz resonator that includes a rubber coating inside its resonant cavity can lead to quasi-perfect absorption (>90%) in the 306–921 Hz frequency range. In another work, Duan et al.17 developed a theoretical model to predict the sound absorption performance of a sub-wavelength quasi-Helmholtz absorber using a fluid-filled square honeycomb core with an interior rubber coating, numerically reporting a semi-absorption band (α > 0.5) for the 279–478 Hz frequency range. Furthermore, Qu et al.5 proposed a structured array of Fabry–Pèrot resonators with an average thickness of 8.9 mm whose impedance matching to water achieves high absorption (α > 0.9) over a broad frequency range (4–20 kHz).

Another explored strategy to increase the absorption spectra of underwater metamaterials is the use of multi-mode coupling. Gu et al.18 presented an underwater sound-absorbing metasurface whose modal coupling between cavity and plate structure enables high underwater sound absorption (α > 0.8) over a broadband frequency range (470 Hz–10 kHz) using a 50-mm thick metasurface. Zhang et al.19 presented a meta-absorber with hexagonal unit cells consisting of steel plate scatterers of various diameters attached to a center support and embedded into an elastomer host material. Results show that the various resonances are combined to yield quasi-perfect absorption in a narrow frequency range (α > 0.94 from 500 to 700 Hz), with a high average sound absorption (α = 0.91) in the 1–10 kHz frequency range using a 70-mm thick sample.

Other absorption-driven approaches include air or air/water-filled Helmholtz resonators, multilayered locally resonant coated plates20, viscoelastic polymer slabs embedded with locally resonant scatterers using hard or air-filled backings21, and hydrosound dampers (nets with air-filled elastic balloons)22. In particular, in offshore installations, such as pile driving for wind farms23,24, a collapsible framework with air-filled Helmholtz resonators was proposed by Wochner et al.25, exhibiting a maximum insertion loss (IL) of 37 dB around 100 Hz (measured using average one-third octave bands). However, drawbacks associated with these approaches include narrow-band performance and the elastic deformation of the resonators induced by hydrostatic pressure or by the oblique incidence of the acoustic wave, which can affect the resonant behavior of the finite system26.

Alternatively, underwater noise barriers can be designed to exhibit a strong impedance mismatch with surrounding water, reflecting acoustic waves to prevent their transmission and presenting a more broadband effectiveness compared to locally resonant-based materials. Typical solutions include (i) arrays of one fluid immersed in another with contrasting properties, such as air bubbles in water, and (ii) mechanical casings that enclose the noise source to confine the energy of the sound waves between the noise source and the barrier. Arrays of bubbles in water can be produced using a perforated hosepipe resting on the seafloor (unconfined air bubble curtain) and encircling the source of noise. Compressed air is then pumped through this channel, and streams of bubbles are created, generating air curtains with a high impedance contrast. For instance, Lucke et al.27 reported a reduction in the order of 10–20 dB, from 500 to 2000 Hz, after the installation of an air bubble curtain to protect porpoises during the installation of a wooden pile. Würsig et al.28 reported ILs of 8–10 dB in the 400–800 Hz bands and 15–20 dB in the 1.6–6.4 kHz bands, indicating significantly poorer performance at lower frequencies. Although typically inexpensive, these systems are not effective in areas with high tidal currents, which can disrupt the air bubbles, causing them to be swept away from the noise source. Some of the other practical problems associated with these systems include the control of bubble size distribution and the generation of a sufficient volume of bubbles with large radii to achieve low-frequency noise attenuation29. For instance, to achieve significant attenuation below 600 Hz, air curtains would required bubbles radii ranging from 8 mm near the surface to 50 mm at a depth of 30 m30. This poses a significant limitation for their use due to the challenges associated with generating and maintaining air bubbles in a controlled manner in an offshore environment, particularly in the low-frequency range. In addition, a significant advantage of purely passive approaches with respect to air bubble curtains is that they do not require energy input, air supply, or active regulation once installed. In this case, passive solutions may offer advantages such as long-term reliability, maintenance, and environmental footprint in applications where continuous operation over extended periods is required.

Architected acoustic panels represent another potential solution to reduce underwater noise, acting as mechanical casings placed around noise sources. However, water belongs to high-impedance media (Zw ≈ 1.5 × 106 N⋅s/m3), implying that barriers aimed at reflections due to a higher impedance with respect to water cannot efficiently isolate sound at low frequencies. To solve this issue, Jansen et al.31 proposed noise mitigation screens made of a double-walled steel cylinder filled with air submerged to 25 m of depth. The method combines (i) water or air/water between the pile and the casing and (ii) water or air inside the casing, producing a frequency-dependent acoustic IL with a slope of approximately 2 dB/octave, exceeding 20 dB above 4 kHz, but limited to 8–11 dB when averaged over a large frequency range (50 Hz to 40 kHz). The attenuation is less pronounced in the low-frequency range, where most of the energy is concentrated (100–250 Hz). Other technological solutions for underwater casing systems have also been reported, although with a lesser degree of maturity in terms of practical deployment32.

Wang et al.33 combined topology optimization and homogenization methods to design lattice materials with low acoustic impedance compared to water. The results revealed that minimal acoustic impedance depends on both the degree of anisotropy and specific structural features that facilitate deformation. Two designs relying on a bi-mode lattice material have been proposed, with a thickness of 17 mm, yielding an average underwater sound transmission loss (STL) of 17 dB from 500–2000 Hz. Chen et al.34 explored the reflection of underwater sound waves on orthotropic solids whose acoustic impedance can be customized through mechanical anisotropy and velocity ratio of the quasi-transverse and quasi-longitudinal waves supported by these materials. Results have shown that a hexagonal lattice with a thickness of 21 mm can yield an average STL of 18.7 dB in the 1.5–3.5 kHz frequency range. Wang et al.35 proposed a metamaterial with a thickness of 54 mm, measuring an average STL of approximately 16 dB in the 400–1200 Hz frequency range. The mutual exclusion of low acoustic impedance and high mechanical properties was overcome by regulating the lattice orientation and incorporating a hierarchical morphology in an anisotropic metamaterial. Wang et al.36 proposed reverse engineering chiral metamaterials with optimized low acoustic impedance and appropriate stiffness using a topology optimization approach, demonstrating an average STL of 13.5 dB within the 1–5 kHz frequency range (for a sample with a thickness of 32 mm), as well as sufficient stiffness to ensure stable acoustic performance under moderate hydrostatic pressure. Despite the described approaches, achieving broadband and efficient underwater noise reduction at low frequencies (below 1 kHz) remains a challenge, especially when using highly sub-wavelength structures that must also withstand hydrostatic pressure. In this work, we address this issue by introducing a periodic metamaterial with a unit cell designed to enable a tailored interaction between normal and shear behavior along predefined directions. A similar idea has been introduced in ref. 34 using a single geometrical realization (honeycomb lattices), investigating the influence of selected dimensionless variables on the acoustic impedance of the resulting structure. Here, we propose an elasticity-based approach to ensure efficient underwater acoustic wave reflection in broad frequency ranges. We start by demonstrating the effect that a coupled normal–shear behavior—as quantified by a single dimensionless parameter—has on fluid-immersed homogeneous anisotropic metabarriers. Then, these results are utilized to formulate a topology optimization problem that maximizes such coupling and whose versatility allows a wide design space to be explored. Unlike previous approaches, we formulate our optimization problem in the static regime (zero frequency), relying exclusively on the homogenized material properties of the structured material, but not on the properties of the surrounding fluid. The obtained results are shown to be valid for broad frequency ranges for various thickness values, also withstanding increasing hydrostatic pressures.

The paper is organized as follows. We begin by presenting the mathematical models and numerical methods used to investigate the STL curves of fluid-immersed anisotropic metabarriers, as well as the formulation of the topology optimization problem used. Then, we present the results of the optimization problem and its corresponding STL curves for an increasing number of unit cells, including suitable modifications to account for the potential exposure of the metabarrier to hydrostatic pressure. Finally, two-dimensional simulations to assess the acoustic attenuation effects for a point source are carried out. Conclusions and an outlook for future work are then discussed.

We include here some remarks concerning the relationship between the reflection coefficient, R = Pr/Pi, and the specific acoustic impedance of the solid structure, denoted by z. These quantities are related at the fluid-structure interface located at x = x0 through the well-known expression4417\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R(x={x}_{0})={\left.\frac{z-{\rho }_{0}{c}_{0}}{z+{\rho }_{0}{c}_{0}}\right|}_{x={x}_{0}},$$\end{document}R(x=x0)=z−ρ0c0z+ρ0c0x=x0,where ρ0c0 denotes the specific acoustic impedance of the surrounding fluid. This expression indicates that ideal cases of perfect reflection (i.e., zero transmission) can theoretically be achieved in two limiting cases: (i) R = + 1 for z → ∞, or (ii) R = − 1 for z → 0. The positive and negative signs correspond, respectively, to phase differences of 0 and π between incident and reflected acoustic waves at the interface. In practice, however, the specific impedance z is finite, which limits the reflection coefficient to ∣R∣ < 1. Achieving extremely large values of z in a water medium is particularly challenging, as it would require unrealistically thick materials due to the relatively high acoustic impedance of water. Consequently, a more practical strategy consists in designing an acoustically “soft” material such that z → 0, thereby creating a strong impedance mismatch through the reduction of the acoustic impedance relative to that of water. This approach is explored in the following section.

As a consequence, an infinite STL value would be achieved close to the zero frequency, for δ → 1−. This correlation indicates that the STL enhancement arises from the large impedance mismatch between the metabarrier and the surrounding fluid, reaching values close to 1% of the acoustic impedance of water for δ = 0.995.

Anisotropic heterogeneous material design: optimization problem of the unit cell

The previous discussion suggests that a suitable optimization objective to obtain the periodic unit cell that forms a metabarrier yielding maximum STL at a minimal operating frequency is the maximization of the mode coupling factor δ. The desired value of δ → 1− would yield an infinite STL at zero frequency. However, this value cannot be achieved, as it represents a structural singularity.

Thus, in order to obtain the periodic metamaterial unit cell, we propose a topology optimization problem which aims to minimize the objective function φ, given by18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{c}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}\limits_{{\bf{q}}\in \{0,1\}^{n_q}} \varphi ({\bf{q}})={\left(|{C}_{16}|/\sqrt{{C}_{11}{C}_{66}}-1\right)}^{2},\\ \,{\mathrm{subjected}}\; {\mathrm{to}} {\omega }_{1} > {\omega }_{\min},\end{array}$$\end{document}minimizeq∈{0,1}nqφ(q)=∣C16∣/C11C66−12,subjectedtoω1>ωmin,where q is a binary vector composed of nq design variables representing the presence of material or voids in the unit cell and ω1 is the first resonant frequency of the system for free boundary conditions excluding rigid body motion, which must be above a certain threshold (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{\min }$$\end{document}ωmin). The proposed objective function aims to minimize the distance between ∣δ∣ and 1, while the first resonance frequency restriction is imposed to ensure connectivity between the pixels of the candidate unit cell, thus discarding solutions that present disconnected (i.e., “floating”) regions of solid material. We note that the objective function is formulated to consider only the effective static properties of the unit cell. On the other hand, polymers typically exhibit viscoelastic behavior, which becomes more pronounced at higher frequencies46. We recall, however, that the complex constitutive relations describing viscoelastic behavior are typically frequency-dependent47. Thus, since the objective of this work is to focus on the investigation of the effect of normal-shear coupling (i.e., a relation typical of the static regime), we do not consider the possible effects of viscoelasticity in our optimization function.

Each square unit cell used in the topology optimization process is described by a distribution of 2q × 2q pixels. The continuity between adjacent unit cells is ensured by the correspondence of pixels at opposing edges. Additionally, a π-rotational symmetry is enforced in the internal region of the unit cell to ensure normal-shear coupling. This scheme is represented in Fig. 3a (top panel), where the continuity between the pixels of contiguous unit cells and the internal π-rotational symmetry are represented, respectively, as “Matching boundaries” (green contour) and “Anti-symmetry” (region inside the dashed red contour). This yields a total of nq = 2q2 − 2q − 1 pixels, which can then be encoded into a corresponding binary vector q (Fig. 3a, middle panel). The represented unit cell can then be discretized using regular square finite elements (FEs) under the assumption of plane strain48, allowing for the computation of effective constitutive matrix of the homogenized unit using the method described in ref. 42. We also note that, as the homogenization procedure only concerns the static properties of the unit cell, evaluating binary vectors in the optimization problem (see Eq. 18) presents a low computational cost, which allows for an efficient use of genetic algorithms49 to determine an optimal solution (Fig. 3a, bottom panel). More specifically, we use MATLAB’s Global Optimization toolbox (GA function), which enables the use of discrete variables. As such, no penalization scheme for continuous variables is considered (e.g., SIMP50). We also do not choose a set of solutions as the initial population for the optimization scheme, relying on the auto-initialization offered by the toolbox. We note that different runs of the genetic algorithm might yield different results, which is inherent to the stochastic behavior of the method in its search for a global optimum.

Results

Result of optimization problem

We apply the optimization procedure considering a structure composed of a plastic material with Young’s modulus E = 3 GPa, Poisson’s ratio ν = 0.3, and specific mass density ρ = 1150 kg/m3. The unit cell has a length of a = 10 mm, which is amenable to fabrication using 3D-printing techniques.

The resulting unit cell obtained using q = 5 (yielding a total of 2q × 2q = 100 pixels, i.e., nq = 39 optimization variables and 239 = 5.5 × 1011 possible designs) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\omega }_{\min }=0.1$$\end{document}ωmin=0.1 rad/s is presented in Fig. 3b (top panel), displaying the discretized geometry of the unit cell and the obtained δ value (δ = 0.9879). Similar solutions were obtained for different runs of the algorithm, so we chose the one with the best computed objective function. The obtained geometry then undergoes a smoothing procedure to yield a geometry amenable to fabrication (Fig. 3b, bottom panel). As a result of the geometrical modifications, a slightly smaller value of δ is obtained (δ = 0.9852).

We then apply two-dimensional periodic Bloch-Floquet conditions to the resulting unit cell51 and compute its dispersion diagram in the ΓX direction, with Γ located at k = 0 and X located at k = π/a. For each wavenumber, we also compute a polarization metric given by p(ux, uy) = ∫S∣uy∣2 dS/∫S∣ux∣2 + ∣uy∣2 dS, where ux and uy are, respectively, the x- and y-direction displacement components of a given wave mode and S is the unit cell two-dimensional domain. As a result, the values p = 0 and p = 1 represent, respectively, purely longitudinal and transverse motion. The band diagram computed using the FE method is shown in Fig. 3c in the [0, 5] kHz frequency range using continuous lines, with polarization values indicated in a color scale ranging from p = 0 (dark blue) to p = 1 (dark green).

The first obtained branch presents p ∈ [0.403, 0.414], demonstrating a predominant longitudinal behavior (p < 0.5), although with a high degree of hybridization between longitudinal and transverse motion. The wave mode corresponding to this branch at the point X is also shown with the colorbar representing displacement values (ux and uy), illustrating this coupled behavior at 2.67 kHz. Above this frequency (indicated with a horizontal red dashed line), the first branch is no longer present in the considered frequency range. Likewise, the second branch presents p ∈ [0.585, 0.589], exhibiting a predominant transverse behavior (p > 0.5) with a significant hybridization between longitudinal and transverse motion. A wave mode corresponding to this branch is shown at 5 kHz, displaying a rigid-body-like vertical motion due to the small wavenumber at this point (k ≈ 0.14π/a). Therefore, it is possible to observe that even though the coupling between normal/shear stresses/strains was optimized in the static regime (i.e., ω = 0), this also implies in coupled longitudinal-transverse motion.

It is also interesting to note that the behavior predicted by the homogenized structure with effective properties (see Section “Computation of STL for a homogeneous anisotropic medium”), represented by the dashed green and purple lines in Fig. 3c, correlates well with the FE-based solution up to nearly 2.3 kHz, when the first branch starts to present significant dispersive behavior. We now proceed to evaluate the STL properties of a metabarrier composed of repetitions of the unit cell obtained in the optimization procedure.

For the sake of completeness, we point out here that several studies have reported a rather good agreement between numerical predictions and experimental STL measurements in comparable configurations, albeit at higher frequency ranges. In refs. 33,35, and36, consistent agreement is observed between computed and measured STLs over the ranges 500–2000 Hz, 400–1200 Hz, and 1–5 kHz, respectively. Moreover, in ref. 34, numerical STL predictions in the 1.5–3.5 kHz range exhibit peaks exceeding 20 dB, with experimentally measured average values of 18.7 dB. Although exact peak values are not always explicitly reported, the agreement in terms of trends and average attenuation levels provides a meaningful benchmark. These results indicate a strong correlation between numerical and experimental STLs in comparable metamaterial-based barriers, thereby supporting the reliability of the numerical framework adopted in the present work and the validity of the predicted low-frequency performance.

At this point, the question may arise whether the STL properties in this type of medium are only due to the high degree of hybridization observed (p ≈ 0.5) in the wave modes shown by the dispersion diagram (Fig. 3c). To verify that this is not the case, we have performed an additional optimization procedure resulting in a structure with a perfect coupling between longitudinal and shear motion (p ≈ 0.5 for all wave vectors in the [0, 5] kHz frequency range). In fact, the STL curves computed for this type of structure (see Fig. S3 in the Supplementary Information) present smaller peak values. This demonstrates that the fundamental ingredient to guarantee large STL values is the mode coupling factor δ, as previously verified in Section “Effect of normal-shear coupling on STL curves”.

Influence of hydrostatic pressure on the design of the metabarrier

After presenting the underlying physical phenomena responsible for generating significant STL levels, we now turn to a practical implementation concerning a fixed number of unit cells. To this end, we proceed with the design of our metabarrier by choosing a fixed number of unit cells with considerable sub-wavelength thickness as well as remarkable STL features. We choose N = 3 (see Fig. 4c, middle left panel), whose thickness-to-wavelength ratio is h/λ ≈ 1/50 in the operating frequency of 1 kHz, also presenting a considerable STL (≈90 dB) at 4 kHz.

To determine a suitable value of hw, we perform quasi-static analyses by imposing a pressure using consistent nodal loads in the x-direction facing inwards the unit cell at the lateral walls of the metabarrier (see Fig. 5a) given by p0 = ρwgd, where g = 9.81 m/s2 is the gravitational acceleration and d is the depth at which the structure is submerged. Additionally, as we consider a plane strain state, the structure is theoretically infinite in the z-direction. Thus, we consider that additional homogeneous covers can be included in the finite structure (z = ± ∞) to impede water penetration inside the metabarrier, only allowing air pockets inside the structure.

A non-linear FE analysis is performed considering large displacements with a Total Lagrangian formulation52, since a priori linearity is not guaranteed and geometrical buckling might occur. We then evaluate the stress distribution in the structure for the maximum depth of d = 50 m53 (i.e., p0 = 0.49 MPa). For the initial case hw = 0 (i.e., no additional thickness), the maximum computed von Mises stress is 108.6 MPa (Fig. 5b, left panel). We then increase the thickness hw until this maximum von Mises stress reaches a value close to 50% of the material’s tensile strength (55 MPa, no yield strength available from the manufacturer’s datasheet). We then end up with a final thickness of hw = 1.0 mm for a maximum von Mises stress of 27.4 MPa (Fig. 5b, right panel). We also verify that the maximum von Mises stress is indeed highly concentrated, while the overall mean von Mises stress levels are considerably lower. It is also worth to notice that the maximum lateral displacements at the left and right edges, computed for the values hw equal to 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 mm, are negligible with respect to the unit cell total length, presenting, respectively, the values 0.49, 0.38, 0.22, and 0.03 mm. The maximum vertical displacements at the bottom and top edges of the metabarrier unit cell are equal to 94.5, 82.8, 49.9, and 6.2 μm, respectively (i.e., negligible with respect to the unit cell dimensions). For the case of increased thickness hw = 1.0 mm, we also plot the computed average von Mises stress level (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{\sigma }_{v}}$$\end{document}σv¯) versus average longitudinal strain (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{\epsilon }_{x}}$$\end{document}ϵx¯) for increasing levels of depth d, as shown in Fig. 5c. The resulting curve shows an excellent correlation with a straight line (i.e., constant slope), thus indicating that, up to the selected depth of d = 50 m, no buckling or other geometrical softening mechanisms occur.

Next, we verify the influence of the additional thickness on the STL curves by re-computing these for increasing values of hw with d = 0 (i.e., only the influence of hw is assessed at these analyses). In this case, we still consider the y-direction unit cell length equal to a. The results, shown in Fig. 5d, show that increasing the lateral thickness hw has a two-fold effect on the STL curves, which present (i) a blue-shift effect in the region below 2.67 kHz and (ii) a red-shift effect in the region above 2.67 kHz. This observation is in agreement with the previous statement that the formation mechanism for the STL peaks and dips is different for the regions divided by the frequency corresponding to the wave mode formed at the X-point (see Fig. 3c), namely, due to (i) the effective medium properties for lower frequencies and (ii) Bragg-scattering-like behavior for higher frequencies. This is also verified by re-computing the effective constitutive matrix for each case, from which we obtain the values of δ equal to 0.9851, 0.9848, and 0.9847, respectively, for hw equal to 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 mm. Such a decrease in the value of δ explains both the blue-shift and the slight decrease in the STL peaks below 2.67 kHz. At the low-frequency region, it is also possible to verify that the behavior of the cases 0.5 and 1.0 mm are similar, which is explained by their approximate δ value. On the other hand, the STL curves obtained at higher frequencies present the opposed behavior, which however cannot be directly correlated to the band diagram presented in Fig. 3c due to the broken periodicity of the system caused by the introduction of the additional thickness. We also note that the STL peaks concerning the case hw = 1.0 mm now present the values 23.5, 21.5, and 84.3 dB, respectively, at 0.73, 1.71, and 3.27 kHz.

We also investigate the influence of the hydrostatic pressure on the STL curves. To this end, we consider the inclusion of an additional stiffness matrix48 caused by the stresses induced by hydrostatic pressure. Figure 5e presents this variation with respect to the depth values d = 0, 10, 20, …, 50 m. It is possible to observe a red-shift effect concerning the whole STL curve, which is explained by the induced compressive stresses causing an overall softening of the metabarrier that decreases its characteristic frequencies. We note that for the case d = 50 m, the highest peak is shifted down to 2.86 kHz (12.5% reduction with respect to 3.27 kHz for d = 0), presenting a mild variation in the peak value (smaller than 2.7%).

Finally, the performance of the metabarrier with respect to varying incidence angles is computed. The results, shown in Fig. 5f, indicate that in the first frequency region (below 2.67 kHz), the STL curves present a mild blue-shift, especially at higher frequencies (close to 2 kHz), however presenting also a significant increase in attenuation for larger incidence angles (close to 40 dB at θ = 80o). Also, for the second frequency region (above 2.67 kHz), the STL peak presents a decreased attenuation and a much more significant blue-shift effect; however, remaining above 60 dB for all computed cases. Once again, the different behaviors displayed by the structure when considering these frequency ranges are due to the different mechanisms that produce attenuation in each case.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have proposed a design strategy to exploit structural anisotropy in metamaterial-based acoustic barriers, yielding efficient sub-wavelength underwater noise reduction. We have calculated the STL curves considering an anisotropic homogeneous medium immersed in a fluid. The first peak of these curves presents (i) an increasing maximum value and (ii) a decreasing frequency as a conveniently defined mode coupling factor between normal stresses and shear strains (and vice versa) approaches unity. We have also shown that the thickness of the anisotropic barrier has a lesser influence on the maximum STL value, suggesting that this can be used to tune the frequency of the STL peaks. This effect is contrary to Bragg-scattering band gaps, whose efficiency in wave attenuation is proportional to the number of unit cells (and therefore thickness) of the structure.

These results were then used to propose a topology optimization problem with the objective of maximizing the absolute value of the mode coupling factor. The dispersion relation obtained using the optimized unit cell indicates a high degree of longitudinal-transverse polarization, which, however, was demonstrated not to be responsible for increasing maximum STL values. A partial band gap was also identified with respect to one of the propagative branches. The STL curves computed for an increasing number of unit cells immersed in water confirm the previous conclusions regarding the effect of increasing thickness values on the STL curves. Interestingly, a deep sub-wavelength behavior is reported for a single unit cell (thickness-to-wavelength ratio circa 1/70). Furthermore, the effect of the partial band gap is confirmed, achieving an almost 90 dB attenuation using three unit cells (30 mm).

The effects of hydrostatic pressure on the configuration of three unit cells were also evaluated, indicating the necessity of including additional layers of homogeneous material on each side of the metabarrier (1 mm each), which is sufficient to reduce stress levels by a fourfold. Including these layers leads to (i) a blue-shift of the STL curves for low frequencies and (ii) a red-shift of the STL curves for higher frequencies, without, however, significantly degrading STL levels. Likewise, the hydrostatic-induced stresses lead to a red-shift in STL curves due to their compressive nature. The STL curves also present (i) increases in peaks for low frequencies and (ii) blue-shifts for higher frequencies for increasing incidence angles. These conclusions demonstrate that the designed metabarrier is robust with respect to variations in external stresses and incidence angles.

Finally, we proposed a convenient transformation for the unit cell, morphing its shape from a rectangular to a circular design. We have also shown the manufacturability of the structure for an external radius of 120 mm and a thickness of 32 mm. The computed time-transient signals indicate a reduction of nearly 98% in energy for an input signal centered at 500 Hz with the use of the metabarrier. Thus, not only does the proposed structure seem suitable for technological exploitation, but its rationally designed anisotropy also indicates a fruitful direction for further investigation in underwater acoustics.
