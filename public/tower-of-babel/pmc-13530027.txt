Global Priorities and Knowledge Gaps in Spinal Trauma, Infection, and Osteoporotic Fractures: Insights From the AO Spine Knowledge Forum Trauma & Infection Needs Assessment Survey
Authors: Alfredo Guiroy, Mitchell K. Ng, Olesja Hazenbiller, Jonathan Dalton, Mohammad El-Sharkawi, Harvinder Singh Chhabra, Sebastian Bigdon, Klaus Schnake, Richard Bransford, Charlotte Dandurand, Gregory D. Schroeder, Andrei Joaquim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignCross-sectional survey study.ObjectivesTo identify clinical knowledge gaps in spinal trauma and infection and assess use of AO Spine tools and recommendations.MethodsA 33-item online survey was distributed to AO Spine members by email and nonmembers through social media. Responses addressed demographics, case volume, knowledge gaps, and preferred educational formats. Descriptive statistics and regional comparisons using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests were performed. Signficance was at the P < 0.05 level.ResultsOf 431 respondents, 412 completed at least one survey section and were included; the overall response rate could not be determined because the number of individuals who received or viewed the survey invitation was unavailable. Most respondents were orthopedic (61.2%) or neurosurgical (35.4%) surgeons. Leading priorities were osteoporotic spinal fractures (49.0%), spinal infection (48.3%), and fragility-associated low-energy sacropelvic fractures (40.3%). Regional variation was significant for sacropelvic fragility fractures (P = 0.038) and osteoporotic fractures (P = 0.028). Key gaps included bone quality optimization and surgical treatment for sacropelvic fragility fractures, comparison of techniques for osteoporotic fractures, and spinal infection algorithms. AO Spine Injury Classifications were routinely used by 89.5% of respondents. Use of the separate AO Spine–DGOU Osteoporotic Fracture Classification and AO Spine Primary Spine Infection Classification was reported by 35.7% and 18.7%, respectively, while PROST and CROST use was reported by 17.4% and 14.5%.ConclusionsSurgeons reported a need for standardized, evidence-based algorithms and accessible educational resources for osteoporotic fractures, spinal infection, and fragility-associated low-energy sacropelvic fractures.

Introduction

Spine trauma and infection continue to place a substantial strain on health systems, especially as populations age and carry more chronic conditions. Vertebral and spinal fractures affect an estimated 7.5 to 8.6 million people each year, with more than five million already living with these injuries.1,2 While age-adjusted rates have held steady or declined slightly, the total number of cases continues to rise, especially in communities facing significant social disadvantage. 2 Many patients with vertebral fractures do not meet traditional osteoporosis thresholds based on bone mineral density (BMD), raising questions about how well BMD alone captures real-world fracture risk.3,4 At the same time, fragility fractures of the sacrum and pelvis have become far more common. Some national reports suggest their incidence has increased sixfold, especially among women over 65. These injuries are not minor, as they are often associated with major loss of function and independence, as well as increased mortality.5-7

Spinal infections, including pyogenic spondylodiscitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, and epidural abscess, are increasing, with reported annual incidence of 12–36 per 100,000.8-10 This rise appears to reflect a combination of factors, including older age, diabetes, immunosuppression, intravenous drug use, and greater reliance on spinal instrumentation. 8-10 These infections are often difficult to manage and can lead to neurological deficits, prolonged treatment courses, and mortality approaching 20% in severe pyogenic cases. 11

Evidence and practice patterns remain uneven. Conservative care is often favored for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, yet decisions on augmentation, bracing, and stabilization vary across centers and reflect local custom rather than shared criteria.12,13 Infection algorithms exist for specific etiologies, but adoption is inconsistent, and many recommendations, including those from the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA), rely on low-quality evidence or expert opinion.13,14 These gaps argue for a unified, globally applicable framework.

To clarify priorities, we conducted the AO Spine Knowledge Forum Trauma & Infection Needs Assessment, an international survey of clinical priorities, decision points, and unmet guidance needs in spine trauma and infection. We aimed to identify areas where surgeons report insufficient evidence, limited confidence, or substantial variation in practice, with an emphasis on spinal infections and osteoporotic or fragility-related fractures. We also characterized the use of classification systems, treatment algorithms, and diagnostic frameworks, and assessed preferences for knowledge translation strategies, including guidelines, decision-support tools, and educational formats. The objective was to characterize the research priorities and knowledge translation needs identified by the global spine community.

Methods

Data Source

We conducted a cross-sectional needs-assessment survey on spinal trauma and infection knowledge gaps and knowledge translation practices using an electronic questionnaire administered in REDCap (v15.0.40). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The study was exempt from Institutional Review Board review. Recruitment targets or quotas were not established for individual geographic regions, and the regional distribution of respondents was not compared with the geographic composition of AO Spine membership; therefore, regional representativeness could not be assessed. No identifying information, such as names, email addresses, or IP addresses, were collected. The survey comprised domains covering general demographics, spine trauma and infection case volumes, general knowledge gaps, specific knowledge gaps (trauma and infection), and an optional knowledge translation section. The survey deadline was September 30, 2025. Overall, 431 responses were received. Of these, 412 respondents completed at least one portion of the survey and were included in the analysis.

Cohorts

The primary cohort comprised all respondents who provided responses to at least one survey section (N = 412). Respondents self-reported demographic and practice characteristics including geographic region, years in practice, specialty, practice setting, practice focus, fellowship training, and professional effort allocation, as well as AO Spine and Knowledge Forum Trauma & Infection membership status. Because the knowledge translation section was optional, denominators for those items varied by question, and item-level analyses used the number of respondents who answered each question as the denominator (reported as “n yes/N answered” in the tables).

Primary Outcomes

Primary outcomes included basic demographics and details about each respondent’s practice. We assessed spine trauma practice characteristics, including annual surgical volume, perceived case complexity, and the range of injuries treated (e.g., upper cervical, subaxial cervical, thoracic, lumbar, osteoporotic, and sacral fractures). For spinal infections, we gathered data on annual surgical caseloads, perceived complexity, infection types managed (e.g., pyogenic, fungal, tuberculosis, and postoperative), and how nonoperative care is organized — whether primarily led by surgeons, medical teams, or a collaborative approach. Beyond clinical activity, the survey explored broader areas of need, including the perceived value of tools such as classification systems, treatment algorithms, clinical guidelines, comparative studies, and data from multicenter research. We specifically asked participants to respond only in the areas in which they usually treat. Respondents also identified priority research areas and unresolved controversies in spine trauma and infection. Trauma-related knowledge gaps were mapped across anatomical regions and fracture types, while infection gaps were categorized by etiology. Finally, we assessed current use of AO Spine tools (e.g., injury classifications, Patient Reported Outcome Spine Trauma (PROST) and Clinician Reported Outcome Spine Trauma (CROST)), and preferences for educational formats and decision-support resources.

Statistical Analysis

Survey responses were summarized as counts and percentages, overall and by world region, as presented in the tables. For questions allowing multiple selections (e.g., “select up to 3 choices”), each response option was treated as a binary indicator (selected vs not selected), with denominators reflecting the number of respondents who answered that question (reported as “n yes/N answered”). Because respondents could select more than one option, responses within a given question were potentially dependent; therefore, options were not compared directly with one another and were analyzed separately for regional differences in selection proportions. Regional differences in response proportions were assessed using chi-squared tests, with Fisher’s exact tests used where appropriate because of small cell counts. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Orthopedic surgeons comprised the majority of respondents (61.2%), followed by neurosurgeons (35.4%). General Knowledge Priorities in Spinal Trauma and Infection

Respondents identified multiple areas in need of further evidence or clarification (Table 4). The highest-priority domains included osteoporotic spinal fractures (49.0%), spinal infection (48.3%), and fragility-associated low-energy sacral or pelvic fractures (40.3%). Specific Knowledge Gaps in Osteoporotic Spinal Fractures

Knowledge gaps in osteoporotic fractures were broad and multifaceted. Respondents most frequently cited the need to compare surgical techniques, including cement augmentation, brace therapy, and vertebral augmentation procedures (44.2%; Table 5). Additional priorities included guidance on choosing between operative and nonoperative treatment (38.1%), standardized treatment recommendations (30.6%), and clarity on long-term outcomes measured using validated spine trauma patient-reported outcome tools (28.6%). Regional differences in postoperative management guidance (brace, activity restriction, rehabilitation) ranged from 7.8% in the Middle East and Northern Africa to 25.3% in Latin America (P = 0.021). Specific Knowledge Gaps in Pyogenic, Bacterial, and Fungal Infections

As shown in Table 6, respondents emphasized the need for evidence-based algorithms for managing pyogenic, bacterial, and fungal spinal infections (54.9%). Other prominent gaps included indications for instrumentation in infected spines (44.7%), antibiotic treatment protocols (empiric vs targeted) (41.5%), and management strategies for elderly or immunocompromised patients (34.2%). Respondents also identified uncertainty regarding the usefulness of minimally invasive techniques in infected patients (34.2%), the role of biopsy versus empiric antibiotics for culture-negative cases (32.0%), and guidelines for spinal deformity after infection (24.6%). Specific Knowledge Gaps in Fragility-Associated Sacral and Pelvic Fractures

Surgeons highlighted several unmet needs in managing low-energy sacral and pelvic fragility fractures (Table 7). Discussion

This global survey sought to understand what spine surgeons feel is missing in the care of trauma and infection cases. Many reported uncertainty about how best to manage fragility fractures or spinal infections, particularly those caused by bacteria or fungi. There was a shared sense that current guidelines often fall short or are not applied consistently. PROST and CROST were also infrequently used, potentially reflecting limited awareness of their clinical and research applications and challenges integrating them into routine workflows. Surgeons expressed a strong desire for clearer guidance, more reliable outcome data, especially for osteoporotic fractures, and practical resources to support informed decision-making. The responses pointed to a broader need for evidence based tools and effective knowledge translation and dissemination strategies in the form of simple, accessible educational resources that can be implemented across diverse practice settings to support consistent, high quality patient care.

The survey’s identification of osteoporotic fractures and low-energy sacral and pelvic fragility fractures as top global priorities aligns with the increasing worldwide burden of these conditions. 1 Rising rates of fragility injuries are driven by aging populations, persistent underdiagnosis of osteoporosis, and inadequate fall-risk assessment, yet clinical guidance has not evolved to meet this growing need. 1 In our study, surgeons frequently noted limited direction for managing these injuries and emphasized the need for clearer recommendations related to surgical treatment, optimization of bone quality before intervention, and structured pathways for nonoperative care. These findings are consistent with recent systematic reviews that describe substantial heterogeneity in the management of sacral and pelvic fragility fractures, with available studies largely retrospective and lacking standardized outcome measures.12,15 Comparative evidence that guides the choice between operative and nonoperative management remains limited, and most published literature reports short-term outcomes without robust long-term patient-reported measures, a trend that parallels the low use of PROST and CROST reported by our respondents. 12 Evidence supporting vertebral augmentation techniques and bracing strategies is similarly inconsistent. Prior studies have demonstrated variable outcomes for vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty, and no clear superiority of rigid bracing compared with soft bracing or no brace, which contributes to practice variability and dependence on multidisciplinary decision-making.16,17 Challenges are further compounded by widespread gaps in osteoporosis screening. Many patients who sustain fragility fractures have not undergone DEXA evaluation or present with T scores above the conventional threshold for osteoporosis, indicating that DEXA alone is insufficient to identify at-risk individuals.3,18 Despite consensus surrounding medical treatment of osteoporosis, global screening and treatment rates remain low, limiting opportunities for early intervention and primary prevention. 19 Collectively, these findings suggest that the development of standardized, evidence-based frameworks and high-quality comparative studies may help address perceived gaps in the management of osteoporotic and fragility-associated spinal and sacropelvic fractures.

The survey’s identification of spinal infection as one of the top global priority reflects the rising incidence and substantial clinical burden of these conditions, as well as the persistent lack of standardized, evidence-based management frameworks. Respondents emphasized the need for clear algorithms to guide treatment of pyogenic, bacterial, and fungal spinal infections and highlighted uncertainty about indications for instrumentation in infected fields. These perspectives are well supported by the existing literature. Spinal infection management is marked by variability across institutions and countries, with notable differences in surgical indications, antimicrobial duration, and follow-up strategies.9,14 Even among patients with similar clinical presentations, treatment decisions often differ by local resources, regional microbiologic patterns, and surgeon preference, leading to inconsistent care pathways. These findings align with recent international survey data. A study by the European Association of Neurosurgical Societies, which surveyed 130 neurosurgeons using standardized clinical vignettes to assess management strategies for pyogenic spondylodiscitis, found wide variation in surgical indications, antimicrobial duration, and follow-up practices, with very low agreement across all questions. 9 Commonly cited surgical indications, including neurologic deficit, spinal instability, and failure of conservative management, remain poorly standardized across studies, and the optimal timing of operative intervention is similarly unclear.

Culture positivity rates for pyogenic spondylodiscitis vary widely from 24-93%, complicating the development of reliable antibiotic protocols and contributing to substantial variation in antimicrobial selection, duration, and monitoring.20,21 This variability is also reflected in national surveys. A cross-sectional analysis of German spine specialists demonstrated significant heterogeneity in diagnostic pathways, operative indications, and follow-up practices for lumbar pyogenic spondylodiscitis, with little consensus on key aspects of care and a clear need for higher-quality evidence to guide management. 21 Guidance for managing fungal spinal infections is even more limited. The low annual surgical volumes reported for both trauma and infection cases highlight the need for practical and accessible knowledge translation tools that can support surgeons who encounter these conditions infrequently. Reported use of specific AO Spine classification systems also varied substantially by region (Table 11). This regional variability supports the need for knowledge translation tools that are practical, widely accessible, and tailored to differences in baseline adoption across settings. In this context, the strong global preference for clinical practice guidelines, validated treatment algorithms, and standardized classification systems reflects a desire for resources that can streamline decision-making and promote consistency of care. The popularity of original publications, peer-reviewed focus articles, and short instructional videos further suggests that surgeons value materials that are both credible and efficiently delivered. These preferences align with broader trends in surgical education, where digital resources and concise procedural guidance increasingly complement traditional learning and help bridge gaps in experience and case exposure. 23 Collectively, these findings can help prioritize multicenter studies addressing the most frequently identified clinical uncertainties and guide the development and dissemination of targeted guidelines, treatment algorithms, and educational resources across regions.

Within this broader agenda, the survey rankings support the initial prioritization of two complementary efforts: evidence-based treatment algorithms for pyogenic, bacterial, and fungal spinal infections, and comparative decision-support guidance for osteoporotic fractures, particularly regarding treatment techniques and operative versus nonoperative management. These areas were identified as the two leading global priorities and contained the most frequently reported domain-specific knowledge gaps. This approach would directly align the Knowledge Forum’s initial resource development with the needs most commonly identified by respondents.

This study has several limitations. Because the data were collected through a voluntary survey, responses may not fully reflect actual clinical behavior. Participation was optional, and item-level completion varied, so we couldn’t calculate a true overall response rate, raising the possibility of nonresponse bias. We also observed uneven geographic representation, with most responses from Europe, Asia-Pacific, and Latin America, and fewer from other regions. This imbalance may limit how well the findings apply to the global spine community. As with any survey-based research, there’s a risk of self-selection bias. Surgeons with a strong interest in trauma or infection may have been more inclined to participate. Responses may also have been influenced by social desirability bias, particularly when reporting use of AO Spine tools, and recall bias may have affected self-reported surgical volumes and practice patterns. In addition, the online survey format may have underrepresented surgeons practicing in regions with limited internet access. Finally, because the questionnaire was developed by experts in spinal trauma and infection and focused on predefined areas identified as clinically important by the AO Spine Knowledge Forum Trauma and Infection, it may have preferentially captured knowledge gaps already recognized within the field. Consequently, other areas of uncertainty, particularly those encountered by less experienced clinicians, may have been underrepresented or omitted. Still, despite these limitations, the study offers valuable, large-scale insight into where knowledge gaps remain and which clinical priorities matter most in spinal trauma and infection care.

Conclusion

Spinal trauma and infection remain areas of substantial clinical uncertainty, with osteoporotic spinal fractures, spinal infection, and fragility-associated sacral and pelvic fractures emerging as leading priorities for further investigation. Initial efforts should prioritize evidence-based treatment algorithms for pyogenic, bacterial, and fungal spinal infections and comparative guidance for osteoporotic fractures, particularly regarding surgical technique selection and operative versus nonoperative management. Future multicenter studies should address these unresolved questions and translate resulting evidence into practical, accessible, and regionally adaptable guidelines and decision-support tools. Aligning research and knowledge-translation initiatives with these priorities may help reduce practice variability and support more consistent, evidence-based care worldwide.
