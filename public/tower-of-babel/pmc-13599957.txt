Impact of time to diagnosis in patients with progressive fibrotic ILD: a systematic review
Authors: Zhongyi Deng, Stefan Karrasch, Peter Alter, Pilar Rivera-Ortega, Juergen Behr, Rudolf Jörres
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionProgressive fibrosing interstitial lung diseases (PF-ILDs), including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), are characterised by irreversible fibrosis and poor prognosis. Despite the importance of timely identification, diagnostic delays remain common. This review aimed to summarise studies reporting time to diagnosis in PF-ILDs to identify reasons for diagnostic delays and the impact on several clinical and economic outcomes.MethodsDatabase (Embase, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, LILACS and grey literature) searches were conducted on 15 July 2024 and updated on 5 June 2026. Observational studies reporting time to diagnosis in PF-ILD published between 2002 and 2026 were identified.Results34 studies representing 36 621 patients from 41 countries met the inclusion criteria. Dyspnoea, cough and fatigue were the most common presenting symptoms. Median time from symptom onset to diagnosis ranged between 6.0 and 25.2 months; mean estimates varied from 9.6 to 39.3 months in IPF and exceeded 30.2 months in non-IPF PF-ILD. Between 30% and 77% of patients experienced delays >12.0 months. An increased diagnostic time was associated with more advanced disease, worse survival, poorer quality of life and increased hospitalisation rate. Across included studies, between 21% and 87% of patients were misdiagnosed (often with cardiovascular or other respiratory diseases) and received treatment for other conditions. Predictors of delayed diagnosis included older age, comorbidities, misdiagnosis, previous therapy and absence of early imaging or multidisciplinary evaluation.ConclusionsPatients with PF-ILD, including IPF, face a long time to diagnosis, associated with unfavourable clinical and healthcare resource outcomes, with limited evidence on cost outcomes. Direct economic burden resulting from delayed diagnosis is currently poorly understood and needs further research.Keywords: Interstitial Fibrosis, Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

Introduction

Progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease (PF-ILD) represents a group of chronic ILDs characterised by inflammation and irreversible progressive lung fibrosis.1 The overall prevalence of PF-ILD is estimated to be 16.7 per 100 000 persons,2 with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the most frequent and severe PF-ILD, serving as prototype.3 It exhibits a prevalence ranging from 3.3 to 45.1 cases per 100 000, with a mean survival of 4 years after diagnosis and absence of antifibrotic therapy,4 5 underscoring the substantial health burden. Other common types of PF-ILD share pathophysiological mechanisms driving self-perpetuating progressive fibrosis,5–7 including those associated with connective tissue disease (CTD-ILD), fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (fHP), idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonia (iNSIP) and unclassifiable fibrotic ILD.8–10 It is estimated that up to 32% of these patients will develop progressive fibrosis,11 characterised by deterioration of lung function, increased risk of acute exacerbations and early mortality.1 9

Achieving timely diagnosis in PF-ILD is challenging. Early symptoms, such as dyspnoea and cough, are often non-specific and may be misattributed to more common conditions.12–14 Limited disease recognition among patients and primary care providers may explain delays in seeking medical care, specialist referral and diagnostic testing.15–19 Even for experienced clinicians, the heterogeneity of PF-ILDs and the complexity of distinguishing them from other ILDs are likely contributors to delays in achieving an accurate diagnosis,15 17 20 despite the use of established guidelines.5 21

Delays between symptom onset and accurate diagnosis burden both patients and healthcare systems. A study from the European MultiPartner IPF Registry showed that patients with IPF who experienced diagnostic delay had more progressive disease development.22 While the past decade has witnessed considerable developments in understanding the pathogenesis of PF-ILD and antifibrotic treatments, which have been shown to reduce lung function decline and prolong survival,23 24 cumulated fibrosis cannot be reversed.25 Consequently, patients diagnosed at later stages may have poorer prognoses and greater medical-resource needs. The diagnostic pathway, often involving numerous consultations and investigations,26 also imposes financial burdens.

However, the available evidence on time to diagnosis varies widely.27 28 Whether delayed diagnosis may potentially impact disease progression and subsequent outcomes remains inconclusive.19 22 29 It is also unclear whether specific patient factors or healthcare providers influence the delay. To date, no systematic review has synthesised the evidence on this topic, leaving a knowledge gap that hinders the development of targeted strategies to improve early diagnosis and optimise patient outcomes.

The aim of this systematic literature review was first to summarise the data on the reported timespan from onset of symptoms to accurate diagnosis in patients with PF-ILDs, and second, to assess the impact of longer diagnostic time on clinical and economic outcomes. The third objective was to investigate potential predictors of longer diagnostic intervals. We intended to evaluate epidemiological information, both peer-reviewed publications and abstracts presented at scientific conferences, quantifying the diagnostic timespan in patients and exploring the extent of heterogeneity. In summary, the present review provides a comprehensive overview of the current evidence, identifies potential areas for future research and underscores the critical need for initiatives aimed at reducing delays in this vulnerable patient population.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The systematic review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines30 and was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) in 2024 (CRD42024582276). The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in online supplemental table S1.

Search strategy and selection criteria

The search strategy was constructed based on the predefined inclusion criteria using keywords (free-text terms) and appropriate subject headings (MeSH/Emtree terms) (online supplemental tables S2,S3). The strategy was peer reviewed by an information specialist using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist (online supplemental table S3).31 The databases were originally searched on 15 July 2024, and an updated search was performed on 5 June 2026. We searched for studies published as full texts or abstracts from 1 January 2002 onwards, based on the introduction of international guidelines for the diagnosis and management of IPF in 2002,32 including MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), LILACS, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and ClinicalTrials.gov, WorldCat and NTIS, without language or regional restriction. Hand searches based on reference lists were executed.

We included observational studies investigating adult patients diagnosed with PF-ILD. Eligible disease subtypes included IPF and other non-IPF PF-ILDs (fHP, CTD-ILD (including rheumatoid arthritis-associated ILD (RA-ILD) and systemic sclerosis-associated ILD (SSc-ILD))), iNSIP and other unclassifiable ILD with a strictly fibrotic, progressive phenotype. Studies were required to report either: (1) a quantitative measure of the time from symptom onset to diagnosis, or (2) the impact of diagnostic delay on subsequent outcomes. Studies focused exclusively on non-progressive or non-fibrotic forms of ILD were excluded.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was time to diagnosis, reported as mean, median, minimum or maximum duration in months. Secondary outcomes included the impact of delayed diagnosis on outcomes in patients with PF-ILDs, determinants of prolonged diagnostic time and pathway intervals. To assess the impact of delayed diagnosis, we defined the following outcomes: disease severity, mortality, lung transplantation rate, quality of life (QoL), healthcare utilisation rate and direct economic outcomes.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The titles and abstracts resulting from the search, after duplicate articles were removed, were evaluated by two reviewers (ZD and AKR) using the CADIMA tool.33 Studies relevant for further eligibility assessment were retrieved in full text. Any discrepancy between the two reviewers was resolved by a third, independent reviewer. The complete reading of the articles was performed by the two reviewers (ZD and AKR), with reasons for study exclusion recorded.

Data were extracted by the ZD and independently validated MK and ZM. Extracted variables included study characteristics (year of study, country, design, data source (questionnaire or electronic records), setting, population size, study duration), patients’ demographic characteristics (age, sex ratio), clinical characteristics (PF-ILDs subtype, disease stage at diagnosis, treatment patterns), patients’ diagnostic journey (time in diagnosis interval and subintervals, diagnosis procedures, attributes to longer diagnosis time) and the impact of delayed diagnosis on outcomes (survival, mortality, QoL, disease stages, healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU)). Any discrepancies between the extractor and validators were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus. We extracted the adjusted HR, with 95% CIs, for each of the events of interest. If these were unavailable, we extracted raw data where possible. For studies identified in the updated search, eligibility assessment, data extraction and methodological quality appraisal were performed by ZD and validated by RJ, with discrepancies resolved by discussion.

Quality rating was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tool checklist.34 The majority of the studies were graded as medium quality (online supplemental table S4).

Data synthesis approach

Though protocol specified that quantitative synthesis would be undertaken where feasible, formal meta-analysis was not performed because of substantial heterogeneity across studies in study populations, PF-ILD subtypes, definitions of diagnostic delay, data sources and reported outcome measures. Findings were synthesised in accordance with the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline.35 The SWiM checklist is displayed in online supplemental table S5.

Studies were grouped by outcome domain. Within these outcomes of diagnostic delay, findings were compared descriptively by disease phenotype, geographical region and subinterval of diagnostic pathway. To ensure comparability across studies, mean time intervals reported in discrete phases were summed to calculate the total time to diagnosis. All duration times were standardised to months. These intervals were further categorised into patient-level and healthcare-level stages to synthesise the primary sources of systemic delay. The interpretability of synthesis findings was considered narratively, informed by study-level methodological quality, consistency of findings, directness to the review question, availability of adjusted estimates and completeness of reporting.

Results

Study selection

The initial search identified 1342 records published from 1 January 2002 to 15 July 2024. After removing 103 duplicates, 1239 records were screened. Of these, 1079 were excluded during title and abstract screening, leaving 160 articles assessed for eligibility. During assessment, a further 138 reports were excluded with given reasons. Citation searching identified five additional eligible studies. During revision, an updated search identified seven newly published studies from 15 July 2024 to 5 June 2026. Finally, 34 studies reporting diagnostic waiting times and/or delayed diagnosis related to outcomes in patients with PF-ILD were included (online supplemental figure S1).

Characteristics of the studies

This systematic review synthesised data from 34 studies encompassing 36 621 patients across 41 countries. A total of 17 studies were from Europe, 9 from North America, 1 from Latin America, 6 from Asia and 1 published from multiple nations across Europe, Asia and North America. In total, 33 studies described time from onset of symptoms to diagnosis, while 8 examined its association with clinical outcomes and 6 investigated its determinants.

All included studies were observational. Most studies (n=19) were based on electronic health records data from hospitals or registries, eight retrieved data from surveys, three used mixed sources, two used quantitative information from qualitative studies, one used claims data and one was based on analysis of referral letters.

Twenty-three studies included only patients with IPF, eight studies included mixed IPF and other ILDs, and two studies specifically targeted patients with non-IPF PF-ILD (table 1). Participants had a mean age ranging from 59.028 to 73.6 years,36 and most cohorts had a male predominance (sex ratio range: 41.337–87.5%38). High proportions of people who smoked previously/people who smoke currently were identified. The common clinical sign of disease was crackles, observed in up to 97.2% of patients during lung auscultation.39–44

Extent of diagnostic delay

Diagnostic time was summarised as means, medians, ranges and proportions exceeding predefined delay cut-offs. The median diagnostic time in patients with IPF ranged from 6 months in a group of eight European countries (Germany, France, Italy, Austria, Spain, Czech Republic, Hungary and the UK) to 25.2 months in Denmark44 45 while mean times ranged from 9.6 to 39.3 months27 28 41 45–49 (figure 1, online supplemental table S6). When comparing across continents, no consistent pattern emerged. In patients with non-IPF PF-ILDs,11 Wijsenbeek et al reported mean time to diagnosis was 37.7, 31.2, 36.1, 30.2, 32.6 and 30.9 months in patients with RA-ILD, SSc-ILD, other CTD-ILDs, iNSIP, hypersensitivity pneumonitis and other unclassifiable ILDs, respectively. Chaudhuri et al50 reported a mean time of 15.5 months in a cohort of patients with non-IPF progressive pulmonary fibrosis, whereas Kaneko et al51 observed mean times of 14.3 months in CTD-ILD and 12.8 months in other ILDs.

Diagnostic pathway

The definitions for both the disease and the diagnostic delay varied across literature. In studies listing the initial symptoms for patients with PF-ILD, we found commonly reported respiratory symptoms were dyspnoea12 18 19 25 27–29 37 41 45 50–57 59 60 and cough,12 18 19 27–29 37 41 45 50–57 59 60 while systemic symptoms included fatigue12 18 19 27 45 52 54 55 59 and weight loss12 18 19 52 55 (online supplemental table S6, figure S3). Symptom onset was most commonly ascertained from patient-reported symptom history or survey responses.

Thirteen studies defined IPF in accordance with the international American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society guidelines,3 21 61 while six studies used International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes.11 46 47 53 56 59 The remaining studies defined with seven self-reported diagnoses,12 18 27 48 50 51 54 six physician-reported diagnoses28 38 52 57 60 62 and one through a registry record.63 One study used physician-reported diagnoses to assess progressive fibrosis phenotypes in non-IPF PF-ILDs after classifying ILD subtypes with ICD codes.11 Only nine studies mentioned that diagnoses were confirmed via multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion.19 29 36 38 45 49 55 58 60

During the process, patients were found to undergo several diagnostic investigations. Imaging studies (eg, X-ray, CT, high-resolution CT (HRCT)) were most frequently reported, while biopsy was used in fewer studies (online supplemental table S8). Notably, as shown in table 3, 21–87.5% of patients underwent misdiagnoses, including other respiratory diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),27 28 44 50 52 54 asthma,18 28 44 50 52 54 bronchitis18 44 54 and pneumonia,18 28 52 as well as cardiovascular disease.41 52 54 These patients were found to receive treatments or management procedures targeting other conditions18 28 29 37 54 58 and experienced longer time to diagnosis,18 29 37 with the median time to correct diagnosis being 11 months, as described by Cosgrove et al.18

Impact of diagnostic waiting time on adverse outcomes

Seven studies explored the association between increased diagnostic time and subsequent prognosis in patients with IPF (table 2). Five studies reported a significant association between longer diagnostic time and worse survival outcomes,19 22 25 36 37 whereas two studies found no significance with either a composite endpoint of death or lung transplantation29 or survival rate.55 Importantly, only a subset of studies adjusted for baseline disease severity. Among these adjusted analyses, significant associations were reported by Shankar et al,36 Hoyer et al19 and Lamas et al,25 whereas Snyder et al55 and Hoffman et al29 found no significant association. In addition, two studies found that longer diagnostic time was associated with greater disease severity at diagnosis, reflected by worse mean forced vital capacity19 and vital capacity.63 Hoyer et al also found the delay was associated with a poorer QoL, with results that were consistent across three different QoL assessment tools.19

No included studies assessed delay-related costs. Evidence was limited to HCRU and treatment-related care pathway indicators. Hoyer et al reported an association between longer delay and increased hospitalisation rates, whereas Pimple et al found no significant association with initiation of antifibrotic treatment.19 53

Discussion

This is the first systematic review synthesising evidence on time to diagnosis and its consequences with PF-ILD. Across 34 studies encompassing over 36 000 patients from 41 countries, diagnostic delays was a relevant global issue. The time from symptom onset to diagnosis typically spans months to years, with median delays of 6–25.2 months and mean delays of 9.6–39.3 months. For non-IPF PF-ILDs, delays exceeded 30 months. Longer diagnostic time appears to be associated with adverse clinical and HCRU consequences. The updated search did not materially change the conclusions but reinforced the problem of prolonged diagnostic time. Taken together, the evidence identifies a complex ‘diagnostic odyssey’ driven by individual-level and healthcare-level factors, demonstrating the need for concerted strategies to expedite recognition, referral and MDT diagnosis, consistent with contemporary guidance for patients with PF-ILD.5 64

Interpretation of diagnostic delays and variation

The reported time to diagnosis varied substantially across studies, likely reflecting differences in healthcare systems, study designs and definitions of diagnostic delay. Geographical patterns remain inconclusive. Some cohorts from well-resourced healthcare infrastructure, such as Denmark and the Netherlands, reported among the longest median times, suggesting that healthcare-system wealth alone does not ensure timely diagnosis.29 44 This may reflect ascertainment bias, wherein these robust longitudinal health records capture earlier, subtle symptoms or strict gatekeeping and resource constraints.27 54 For instance, the current national guidelines in Finland do not classify suspected parenchymal lung disease as grounds for urgent referral, mandating evaluation windows that inherently extend the diagnostic timeline.41 Conversely, variations within single countries suggest that access to advanced diagnostics (eg, HRCT, MDT) remains uneven, particularly affecting rural populations.28 36 54 Such observations call for caution regarding interpretations based on country income status alone.

Subinterval analyses revealed that delays were not solely patient-driven. While the interval from symptom onset to first medical contact was significant, delays also occurred after the initial medical consultation, specifically between the first specialist visit and the final diagnosis (median between 1.2 and 10 months).12 29 44 47 The apparently short referral-to-specialist (0.9–2.4 months) should be interpreted cautiously, as the referral subinterval followed abnormal chest radiography and CT imaging or investigation at other community hospitals,44 47 suggesting it reflects a relatively late stage of work-up. These patterns suggest that parts of the diagnostic pathway may be affected by healthcare-system and specialist-level bottlenecks, including challenges in triage, serial imaging to confirm progression and the logistical complexity of MDT discussions. These requirements may be greater in non-IPF PF-ILD, where clinicians must document progression and resolve a broader differential, plausibly extending the pathway.

Why delays occur: a multilevel view

Our findings indicate that diagnostic delays stem from a multifactorial interplay of patient, clinician and healthcare system barriers. Patient-related factors associated with longer delays included older age, sex, type of symptom and comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease or diabetes, which may obscure the recognition of PF-ILD symptoms or lead clinicians to attribute them to ageing or more common chronic conditions.18 28 This is supported by a large US claims-based study demonstrating higher all-recorded comorbidity prevalence among patients with delayed diagnosis.53 Comorbidities are ubiquitous and continue to pose challenges in IPF,65 with current clinical reviews advocating routine and structured assessments for reflux disease, cardiovascular disease and obstructive sleep apnoea.66 Interestingly, a family history of pulmonary fibrosis was associated with shorter time to diagnosis,29 likely attributable to heightened patient awareness due to known familial risk and clinician suspicion.

Misdiagnosis represents a major, modifiable determinant of delay. Between 21% and 87% of patients were initially misdiagnosed, most frequently with COPD, asthma or heart failure, and one included study showed a median of 11 months from initial incorrect diagnosis to the corrected one.18 This ‘diagnostic masquerade’ may not only prolong the time to antifibrotic therapy but may also expose patients to inappropriate and potentially harmful treatments, such as bronchodilator or systemic corticosteroid therapies.18 67 68 These clinical challenges are further compounded by structural barriers. While complex conditions like ILD require MDT discussion, structural hurdles often impede the necessary care coordination.12 38 69 Consequently, our observations mirror the ‘diagnostic odyssey’ common in other rare diseases, where fragmented care and the diffusion of clinical responsibility across multiple providers hinder definitive action.70 71

Diagnostic delay and outcomes

The synthesis of outcome data underscores the clinical and resource urgency of timely diagnosis. Several studies demonstrated that longer diagnostic delays were associated with reduced survival, with a subset adjusted for baseline disease severity. Delay was also associated with reduced lung function at diagnosis, supporting the biological plausibility that unmanaged disease progresses and leads to irreversible function decline.72 73 At the same time, more advanced disease at diagnosis may explain part, though likely not all, of the observed relationship between diagnostic delay and poorer survival. While two studies did not detect a significant survival difference,25 55 these analyses were limited by either small sample sizes or the use of broad delay thresholds such as >24 months may introduce immortal time bias, where patients must survive a long delay to be classified as delayed, thereby selecting for a relatively healthier cohort.74 Timely diagnosis is clinically meaningful only if followed by timely management.5 75 Treatment therefore represents a potential mediator between diagnostic timing and downstream outcomes. No significant association between diagnostic delay and antifibrotic treatment initiation was found in review, suggesting that post-diagnostic factors, including treatment eligibility, access to specialist ILD care, reimbursement and patient preferences may also influence treatment initiation.64 76

The diagnostic delay also imposes a heavy burden on QoL and HCRU. Patients with delays exhibited significantly worse scores on QoL and higher rates of hospitalisation.19 No included studies directly quantified delay-related cost. However, literature indicates that IPF and fibrotic ILD may impose substantial overall economic burden after diagnosis, and modelling analyses suggest that earlier diagnosis may be cost-effective.77 78 Prolonged diagnostic pathways may also involve redundant consultations, unnecessary tests beyond recommendations and management of advanced disease.3 18 27 28 Patient surveys18 27 also suggested potential consequences for early retirement, disability claims and greater work impairment, indicating the potentially avoidable resources and costs by timely disease identification.

Strength and limitation

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review comprehensively covering evidence on diagnostic delays in PF-ILDs, including IPF. Our methodology was rigorous, following PRISMA guidance with a pre-registered protocol and a comprehensive search strategy incorporating grey literature. However, there are several limitations. Most included studies were retrospective, with substantial heterogeneity regarding definitions, data sources and reporting standards. The lack of standardised definitions for ‘diagnostic delay’ and ‘symptom onset’ impeded direct comparability and precluded meta-analysis; therefore, findings were synthesised descriptively in accordance with SWiM guidance. Diagnostic confirmation was not uniformly based on MDT discussion, the gold standard recommended in major ILD guidelines, and reliance on physician or patient reported diagnosis introduces potential bias. Consistent with the risk-of-bias assessment, most studies were of moderate methodological quality, which supports the descriptive finding that diagnostic delay is common but limits causal interpretation. Evidence during the COVID-19 era remains limited. The pandemic likely created a mixed effect: healthcare system strain may have prolonged delays, while the surge in chest imaging and heightened awareness in the respiratory community may have accelerated time to recognition.79 80 Although some determinants of diagnostic delay were identified, the underlying reasons remain obscure due to the small number of studies addressing this question. Though economic outcomes were prespecified, the eligible literature did not provide direct empirical evidence for delay-attributable costs. Moreover, evidence on non-IPF PF-ILDs remains limited. Nevertheless, given the overlap between IPF and PF-ILDs regarding radiologic, prognostic and clinical attributes, extrapolation from IPF may be reasonable.5–7 81

Implications for practice and research

Reducing delays in the diagnosis of PF-ILD requires a multipronged strategy.82 Education initiatives may encourage patients to seek timely medical attention. Enhancing ILD-specific training for general practitioners is critical to help raise early suspicion. This includes promoting the use of basic, readily available tools in primary care such as lung auscultation and spirometry. Practical cues such as persistent exertional dyspnoea, cough and ‘Velcro-like’ crackles should prompt early chest imaging and spirometry.14 75 Systemically, streamlining referral pathways, strengthening collaboration between primary care providers, pulmonary specialists and MDT ILD teams and expanding access to HRCT imaging are essential to resolving the bottlenecks identified. Moreover, implementation of comprehensive screening strategies for at-risk individuals may further support early detection.75 83

Future research should prioritise establishing a consensus-based definition for ‘timely diagnosis’ to facilitate benchmarking. Efforts are required to focus more on non-IPF PF-ILDs subtypes and access diagnostic pathways in countries across Oceania or Africa. Economic evaluations quantifying the impact of delayed diagnosis on direct utilisation costs, as well as indirect societal costs such as patient work disability and caregiver burden, will be critical to inform resource allocation and policy development. Finally, interventional studies are needed to test whether targeted screening of high-risk populations (eg, those with familial risk, occupational exposures or incidental interstitial lung abnormalities) can shorten diagnostic intervals and improve long-term prognosis.

Conclusion

Diagnostic delay in PF-ILDs including IPF is a prevalent, global phenomenon, contributing to adverse clinical and QoL outcomes and higher healthcare utilisation. This review also highlights that delays are not merely a result of patient hesitation but are compounded by systemic hurdles and misdiagnosis. Concerted efforts to improve early symptom recognition, standardise referral pathways and accelerate access to antifibrotic therapy are imperative to alter the trajectory of these devastating diseases.
