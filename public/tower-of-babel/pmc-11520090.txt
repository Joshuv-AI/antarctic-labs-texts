The relationship between fear of negative evaluation, physical activity, eating behavior and psychological distress among nursing students
Authors: Xiao Zheng, Mengjie Zhang, Lingli Yang, Xinyi Zhang, Shujuan Xiao, Xinru Li, Benli Xue, Yanming Liao, Feng Tian, Chichen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe aim of this research is to explore the interrelationships between different psychological issues and the potential role of eating behavior and physical activity among nursing students.MethodsUndergraduate nursing students (n = 892) from some medical universities in China were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling methods using online platforms. Participants completed measures on demographics, fear of negative evaluation (FNE), social avoidance and distress (SAD), psychological distress (DASS), disordered eating behavior (TFEQ) and physical activity. The relationship models among the aforementioned variables were established using Process 3.5.ResultsA total of 290 males and 602 females were included in this study. The average FNE score of students was (39.44 ± 8.78), SAD was (13.83 ± 7.06), DASS was (22.45 ± 20.47), and TFEQ was (56.09 ± 12.57), respectively. TFEQ and SAD independently and jointly acted as mediators in the relationship between FNE and DASS. Physical activity played a moderating role, with the interaction effect between FNE and groups Q2, Q3, and Q4 determined to be 0.407 (95%CI 0.136 to 0.678), 0.328 (95%CI 0.061 to 0.596) and 0.332 (95%CI 0.073 to 0.591), respectively.ConclusionsThis study supports that disordered eating behaviors have a negative impact on mechanisms of psychological changes, and enhancing physical activity is an effective prevention strategy for psychological distress and disordered eating behaviors among nursing students.KEYWORDS: Psychological distress, Disordered eating behavior, Fear of negative evaluation, Social avoidance and distress, Physical activity

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization(WHO), about 31% of young people experience a mental health problem each year, notably depression and anxiety [1]. This concern is especially pronounced among nursing students post-COVID-19, who are contending with workforce shortages and psychological distress, as reported by the International Council of Nurses [2,3].The psychological distress experienced by nursing students, arising from factors such as complex curricula, challenges in patient care, lack of educational control, and encounters with mortality, poses a significant obstacle to the recovery and rebuilding of the nursing workforce.

Disruptions in eating behavior encompass a range of challenges related to food intake, dietary patterns, and attempts to manage weight, significantly impacting an individual’s physical health and psychosocial well-being [4]. Studies indicate that eating disorders (EDs) are prevalent among college students and often co-occur with other mental health conditions like depression and anxiety disorders [5,6]. EDs and mental health conditions can both contribute to the willingness of nursing students to continue participating in the nursing industry to a certain extent [7,8]. However, there is still a paucity of information of the relationship between different mental health crisis and disordered eating behaviors, and what factors alleviate the mental health crisis of nursing students.

The progression of psychological problems

Negative emotions, particularly fear of negative evaluation (FNE), play a crucial role in an individual’s psychological well-being. The social anxiety theory suggests that an excessive focus on others’ evaluations and the fear of negative appraisal are key triggers for social anxiety and related psychological problems. According to DSM-5 [9], social anxiety disorder (SAD) represents a prevalent psychiatric condition characterized by persistent social fear, specifically emphasizing the fear of negative evaluation [10].

FNE is closely intertwined with social anxiety and a spectrum of psychological distress like depression and difficulties in emotional regulation. For example, research by Alden and Bieling highlighted a significant correlation between FNE and individual social avoidance and emotional distress [11]. Cognitive-behavioral therapy models (CBMs) are recognized as comprehensive frameworks that integrate cognitive and behavioral theories to provide a nuanced understanding of mental activities, emotional responses, and actions. These models are not only designed to understand and predict these aspects but also to influence and shape them in a therapeutic context. The adaptability of CBMs has led to their successful application across a wide range of psychological challenges, such as anxiety, depression, disordered eating, and addiction, making them a cornerstone of modern psychological practice [12,13]. Cognitive behavioral models have also traditionally emphasized FNE from others as a core cognitive mechanism maintaining social anxiety [14,15]. Additionally, studies by Carleton et al. have demonstrated a strong association between FNE, social anxiety, and overall [16]. It’s evident that individuals with high levels of negative appraisal fear might experience elevated social anxiety, affecting future mental health, work, or academic performance [17–19].

Social avoidance, stemming from FNE, has the potential to diminish the advantages offered by social situations, consequently leading to various behavioral and psychological challenges. Despite numerous studies exploring the influence of FNE on social anxiety and psychological distress, the specific mechanisms and factors that moderate this relationship remain largely unknown. A comprehensive exploration of the correlation between FNE and mental health could offer more profound theoretical underpinnings and clinical insights for interventions and treatments targeting psychological well-being.

In this study, we use social avoidance and distress (SAD) as indicators of social anxiety. This approach allows us to construct a model illustrating the intricate interplay between FNE, SAD, and psychological distress. We aim to propose hypothesis 1 and hypothesis 2:

We hypothesize that FNE predicts subsequent SAD and psychological distress.

We hypothesize that SAD mediates the relationship between FNE and psychological distress.

The relationships between psychological problems and behaviors

Eating behavior as medicator

The self-medication hypothesis has been utilized to explain co-occurring of FNE, eating behavior and SAD [20]. The self-medication hypothesis suggests that individuals may utilize a variety of substances or behaviors as a means to mitigate emotional distress and manage psychological stressors. This concept encompasses a range of modalities, including but not limited to food, medications, alcohol, and other addictive substances [21]. Initially conceptualized to explain the etiology of substance use disorders, the hypothesis has since been broadened to encompass the study of eating disorders, especially in cases where these conditions exhibit a significant association with emotional dysregulation [22]. Individuals grappling with FNE often excessively scrutinize and evaluate their behavior and appearance in social contexts, frequently adjusting their behaviors, including dietary habits or exercise, to manage emotional symptoms. Numerous studies have confirmed a positive correlation between FNE and disordered eating behaviors across various demographics, such as individuals identifying as homosexual [23], those managing HIV/AIDS, individuals coping with chronic diseases [24], children [25,26] and even undergraduate students [27,28]. Furthermore, researchers have employed positive restrictive eating attitudes (e.g. a desire to be thin or fear of weight gain) to elucidate the relationship between FNE, eating behavior, and SAD [29]. Gilbert’s study highlighted differing associations between SAD and eating behavior, emphasizing that FNE correlated with restrictive eating attitudes but not with bulimic attitudes among women [30]. Research has found that eating disorders are more prevalent among female college students and are significantly correlated with various psychological health symptoms [31]. Another study indicates that, compared to those without eating disorders, individuals with eating disorders are more likely to express dissatisfaction with their friendships, family life, and social engagements, along with an elevated risk of anxiety, depression [32]. The self-medication hypothesis potentially provides significant insights into the behavioral patterns exhibited by individuals with eating disorders, especially in situations characterized by emotional distress and psychological stress. Nonetheless, there is a clear necessity for additional research to thoroughly investigate the hypothesis’s applicability to the field of eating disorders and to develop more effective therapeutic methodologies.

While research findings differ among diverse populations, they collectively suggest a link between FNE and eating behaviors, somewhat associating them with SAD and other psychological distress. However, there is a notable absence of validation studies among nursing students, particularly nursing students. Nursing students face intense academic pressures and heavy workloads, which can lead to eating disorders as well as psychological distress. Therefore, we propose the following two hypotheses:

We hypothesize that there is a correlation between FNE and eating behavior among nursing students.

We hypothesize that unhealthy eating behavior plays a positive mediator in the relationship between FNE and SAD, as well as psychological distress.

Physical activity as moderator

Given the intricate interplay between FNE, SAD, eating behavior, and psychological distress, exploring the moderating role of physical activity is imperative. The prominence of physical activity stems from its potential as an influential factor impacting mental well-being and its ability to moderate the correlation between psychological aspects and health outcomes. Existing research evidences the potential of regular physical activity in alleviating the effects of anxiety and depression [33]. Moreover, physical activity has shown promise as a protective element against the onset of eating disorders by enhancing body image perception and nurturing a positive body relationship [34].

The relationship between eating behavior and physical activity is multifaceted [35]. Some scholars suggest that physical activity could potentially mitigate the detrimental effects of FNE and social anxiety on mental health by bolstering resilience and bolstering self-esteem [36]. Nonetheless, the available studies exploring the impact of physical activity on the association between FNE, disordered eating behaviors, and psychological distress remain limited. Understanding the potential role of physical activity in the complex relationship among FNE, eating behavior, and psychological distress is crucial. It may assist individuals dealing with FNE in fostering positive behaviors and emotions, thereby reducing psychological risks. Consequently, we propose the following hypothesis:

We hypothesize that physical activity moderates the detrimental influence of FNE on eating behavior disorders.

Cognitive behavioral theory emphasizes the pivotal role of cognitive processes in regulating behavior and emotional responses. Negative emotions can give rise to distorted cognitions. This theoretical framework elucidates how individuals may interpret stress through these cognitive biases, thereby engaging in unhealthy eating behaviors. The self-medication hypothesis complements this understanding by offering a framework that posits individuals might employ eating behaviors to modulate their emotions and mitigate psychological stress. Methods

Study design and study setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design to investigate undergraduate nursing students (n = 932), majoring in nursing at some Medical Universities in China. Participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling methods using online platforms. All students voluntarily participated in this study. Inclusion criteria: (1) current undergraduate nursing students; (2) informed consent and voluntary participation in this study.

Data collection

The distribution of questionnaires was facilitated through Questionnaire Star, a freely accessible online platform for conducting survey-based research in China. Subsequently, to ensure data quality, a thorough review process was conducted involving two evaluators to eliminate responses completed in less than 20 minutes (since the pre-survey indicated an average completion time of 20 minutes) and identically repeated responses (where nearly all options in the questionnaire were duplicated). Consequently, a total of 892 questionnaires were deemed valid, resulting in a valid response rate of 95.7%.

Ethical considerations

It is important that this cross-sectional study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol received approval from the ethics committee of Shunde Hospital of Southern Medical University (KYLS20240403).

Data collection instruments

Fear of negative evaluation (FNE)

Fear of negative evaluation was measured using the Chinese version of the 12-item Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNE), which underwent translation by Chen [37]. The BFNE serves as a self-reported assessment of an individual’s capacity to withstand the prospect of being judged unfavorably or hostilely by other [38]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (extremely characteristic of me), with total score ranging from 12 to 60. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the FNE scale was 0.898, indicating high internal consistency reliability.

Social avoidance and distress (SAD)

The measurement of Social Avoidance and Distress utilized the Scale developed by Watson and Friend [39]. Comprising 28 items, this scale delineates two distinct dimensions: social avoidance and social distress. Responses are recorded on a binary “yes/no” scale, yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 28, with higher scores reflecting elevated levels of both social avoidance and distress. Notably, this scale has been extensively employed within Chinese adolescent populations, exhibiting robust reliability and validity [40]. In this study, Cronbach’s α for the SAD scale was 0.896, indicating strong reliability of measurement.

Psychological distress (DASS)

The psychological distress was measured using a self-report instrument of Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) [41], which comprises 3 subscales (i.e. depression, anxiety and stress), with 7 items per subscale. Each item is graded on a four-point scale to rate the extent to which participants have undergone the emotional state over the past week from 0 (not true) to 3 (very true). The higher the total score, the more severe the psychological distress of student is reported to be. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the DASS-21 was 0.939.

Disordered eating behavior (TFEQ)

The Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire (TFEQ-R21) was utilized to assess eating behavior dispositions, considering cognitive restraint, emotional eating, and uncontrolled eating factors [42]. The original English questionnaire had been translated into Chinese. Participants rated the 16 items of the TFEQ-R21 questionnaire on a four-point scale, reflecting the degree to which they identified with the descriptions provided (1–definitely true; 2–mostly true; 3–mostly false; or 4–definitely false). Higher scores indicated a greater inclination toward dysfunctional eating behaviors [43].Within 16 items, 7 items pertained to uncontrolled eating (UE), 6 items related to emotional eating (EE), and 3 items related to cognitive restraint (CR). The remaining 5 items encompassed aspects such as avoiding stocking tempting foods, eating with less awareness, experiencing hunger, and the extent of self-restraining behavior [40]. The reliability analysis in this study revealed a high level of internal consistency for the TFEQ-R21, with a Cronbach’s α was 0.935.

Physical activity (IPAQ)

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire -long version (IPAQ) was used to measuring physical activity of Participants [44]. The total weighted MET-minutes/week were calculated as duration × frequency per week × MET intensity, which were then summed across activity domains (walking, moderate physical activity, vigorous physical activity) to produce a weighted estimate of total energy expenditure from all reported activities per week. In this study, we analyzed MET-minutes/week using the quartile method to evaluate participants’ physical activity. The participants were divided into four groups: Q1 (<330 MET-minutes/week), Q2 (330–908 MET-minutes/week), Q3 (909–2201 MET-minutes/week), and Q4 (>2201 MET-minutes/week).

Covariates

Demographic characteristics including age, gender (male and female), residence (urban and rural), grade (grade1, grade2 and grade3), and BMI (thin ≤18.4 kg/m2, normal weight 18.5 ~ 23.9 kg/m2, overweight 24.0 ~ 27.9 kg/m2, and obesity ≥28.0 kg/m2).

Statistical analyses

We used SPSS 26.0 to conduct descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analysis on the data to determine the correlation between FNE, TFEQ, SAD, DASS, and IPAQ. Subsequently, relationship models among the aforementioned variables were established using Process 3.5 developed by Hayes [45]. Firstly, we examined the mediation effect of TFQI and SAD in the relationship between FNE and DASS using Model 6 of Process 3.5 (Bootstrap = 5000). Nest, we assessed the moderation effect of TFEQ on the relationships among FNE, TFEQ, SAD and DASS using the model 83 of Process 3.5 (Bootstrap = 5000). Finally, we employed three criteria to further validate the moderation effect of IPAQ in both the mediation model of FNE-TFEQ-DASS and the chain mediation model of FNE-TFEQ-SAD-DASS. All models were adjusted for covariates.

Testing of moderated mediation model.

We applied three criteria to validate the moderating role of physical activity in the mediation models of FNE – TFEQ – DASS and FNE – TFEQ – SAD – DASS. For the FNE – TFEQ – DASS model: Initially, we examined the mediating effects of TFEQ across different physical activity (IPAQ) groups. As shown in Table 6, in group Q2, the mediating effect of TFEQ was 0.103 (95%CI 0.028 to 0.195); in group Q3, it was 0.079 (95%CI 0.020 to 0.151); and in group Q4, it was 0.080 (95%CI 0.020 to 0.151). These results indicated the physical activity of a moderated chain mediation effect. Subsequently, following Hayes’ method, index indicators were assessed, revealing index effects of 0.126 (95%CI 0.019 to 0.252), 0.102 (95%CI 0.007 to 0.213), and 0.103 (95%CI 0.009 to 0.211) for groups Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively. Notably, subsequent comparisons of the differences in chain mediation effects between groups Q2, Q3, and Q4 with group Q1 demonstrated statistical significance, thereby confirming the moderating effect of IPAQ on the chain mediation model.

Discussion

In our study, we found that the average FNE score of nursing students was (39.44 ± 8.78), SAD was (13.83 ± 7.06), DASS was (22.45 ± 20.47), TFEQ was (56.09 ± 12.57). These findings underline the heightened vulnerability of nursing students to encounter mental health challenges compared to their peers. Research indicates that more than one in ten college students encounter stressors leading to psychiatric disorder diagnoses and related complications [46]. Meanwhile, nursing is internationally recognized as one of the most stressful professions [47]. Arnett’s theory of emerging adulthood highlights this phase as a unique life stage characterized by identity exploration, instability, self-focus, the sense of being in-between, and numerous possibilities [48]. Nursing students within this age group may encounter difficulties in social adaptation and role integration, and pressure from the nursing profession itself may lead to potential distress and psychological problems.

This study represents an innovative attempt to delve into the developmental mechanisms of psychological issues among nursing students within an Eastern cultural context and their associated behaviors, drawing upon pertinent theoretical frameworks. Our findings demonstrate that FNE exerts a detrimental effect on the DASS among nursing students, with SAD acting as a mediating factor. The outcomes of this research align with prior investigations that established correlations between depression, anxiety, stress, and social avoidance among college students [2,49]. Social stress is a form of mental stress associated with human relationships that underlies the pathogenesis of mental disorders. Individuals grappling with FNE might exhibit cognitive biases, leading them to interpret social interactions pessimistically and construe reactions from others in a negative manner. Although causal relationships have not been conclusively established, this study reveals the mechanism by which nursing students progress from FNE to SAD, ultimately leading to the development of psychological distress. These findings underscore the importance of considering medical students’ perceptions of interpersonal interactions and their assessments of others. It is essential to implement measured interventions when nursing students experience mild negative emotions, to reduce the risks associated with psychological distress.

The correlation between psychology and behavior is significant. For instance, Trompeter et al. suggest that FNE constitutes a transdiagnostic feature for developing and/or maintaining an eating disorder [26]. Wu et al. found a positive relationship between emotional eating and depression [50]. Furthermore, Nguyen discovered that healthy eating behaviors could potentially prevent medical students from anxiety and depression [51]. Some researchers, through experiments on mice, have found that a high-fat diet may reduce the impact of stress [52]. This study analyzed the role of the TEFQ in the development of psychological distress. The results indicated that TEFQ exacerbates the negative impact of FNE on SAD, and resulting psychological distress among nursing students. We found that nursing students with high FNE adopt eating as a way to cope with their negative emotions or to replace socializing. However, this behavior exacerbates their anxiety. Hence, it is important to monitor the dietary habits of nursing students when they experience mild negative emotions to prevent the onset of various types of eating disorders, thereby averting more severe psychological distress.

Contrarily, this study found that physical activity has a negative impact on disordered eating behaviors among nursing students. That is, individuals at higher risk due to FNE can mitigate the likelihood of developing disordered eating behaviors by engaging in physical activity. Hrabosky et al. discovered that physical activity was unrelated to eating disturbance and psychological functioning indicators among obese patients [53]. However, Mathisen et al. propose that positive effects of physical activity may play a meaningful role in successful ED treatment [54]. In this study, the interaction term between FNE and physical activity showed a positive relationship, indicating that physical activity exhibits a compensatory effect. This means that physical activity may mitigate the negative impact of FNE on the disordered eating behaviors, consequently reducing the risks of SAD and psychological distress among nursing students, at least on the level of disordered eating behaviors. Therefore, physical activity can serve as an intervention tool for mild psychological issues among nursing students, preventing the occurrence of problematic behaviors and the development of more severe psychological distress.

We utilized quartile classification for physical activity levels and determined that the most beneficial range for college students lies between 330–908 MET-minutes per week. This specific range appears to more effectively moderate dietary practices, indicating that students who partake in substantial yet non-excessive physical activity tend to foster a more positive relationship with food. It is observed that certain students, especially those with stringent standards for their physique and physical fitness, may adopt rigorous physical activity alongside restrictive diets to sustain an appealing body shape. A systematic review and meta-analysis study suggests an inverse dose-response relationship between physical activity and depression, where the lower the activity level, the steeper the gradient of the association [55]. Although robust physical activity is instrumental in preserving a healthy weight and enhancing overall well-being, an overly physical activity may precipitate detrimental mental health consequences.

Consequently, nursing students must embrace healthy eating behavior coupled with a sensible regimen of physical activity to uphold a healthy lifestyle. This approach not only nurtures a balanced perspective toward body image and dietary choices but also supports mental health. We propose the introduction of science-driven dietary education programs aimed at cultivating proper eating behaviors among students. This includes teaching the avoidance of extreme dieting practices, binge eating, and other unhealthy eating habits, while emphasizing the importance of balanced nutrition and controlled intake of sugars and fats. By encouraging interdisciplinary collaboration between fields such as nutrition, physical education, psychology, and sociology, we can develop a comprehensive health promotion strategy. This holistic approach ensures that students receive well-rounded health support, promoting regular physical activity and the adoption of sound dietary habits. Moreover, establishing a robust campus mental health support system is crucial, offering easily accessible psychological counseling, stress management workshops, and campaigns to increase mental health awareness. These initiatives are designed to strengthen students’ resilience and contribute to their overall well-being.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. This study was a cross-sectional design, and unable to identify causal relationships. Furthermore, studies should be performed analysis over the long-term, so as to deeply explore the changes of the correlation between eating behaviors, physical activity and psychological distress at different time points.

Conclusions

In this study, we validate a potential relationship between different psychological problems, disordered eating behaviors and physical activity among nursing students. We found a mediation effect of disordered eating behaviors between FNE, SAD and psychological distress, and that physical activity has a compensatory effect on the relationship of disordered eating behaviors and psychological distress. Overall, this study supports the hypothesis that disordered eating behaviors have a negative impact on the development of psychological distress, and enhancing physical activity is an effective prevention strategy for disordered eating behaviors and mental health among nursing students.
