g g -factor theory of Si/SiGe quantum dots: spin-valley and giant renormalization effects
Benjamin D. Woods, Merritt P. Losert, Robert Joynt, Mark Friesen

Abstract
Understanding the g g -factor physics of Si/SiGe quantum dots is crucial for realizing high-quality spin qubits. While previous work has explained some aspects of g g -factor physics in idealized geometries, the results do not extend to general cases and they miss several important features. Here, we construct a theory that gives g g in terms of readily computable matrix elements, and can be applied to all Si/SiGe heterostructures of current interest. As a concrete example, which currently has no g g -factor understanding, we study the so-called Wiggle Well structure, containing Ge concentration oscillations inside the quantum well. Here we find a significant renormalization of the g g -factor compared to conventional Si/SiGe quantum wells. We also uncover a giant g g -factor suppression of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) , which arises due to spin-valley coupling, and occurs at locations of low valley splitting. Our work therefore opens up new avenues for g g -factor engineering in Si/SiGe quantum dots.

Model.
—We consider a quantum dot in a Si/SiGe quantum well with an in-plane magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}
and an arbitrary Ge concentration profile
n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
.
We adopt the gauge
\boldsymbol{A}\!=\!A_{z}(x,y)\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
for the vector potential, where
A_{z}(x,y)\!=\!B_{x}y\!-\!B_{y}x
.
The system is modeled by an effective-mass Hamiltonian, where the two valleys
near the
Z
point in the Si band structure are treated as a pseudospin-
\frac{1}{2}
degree of freedom.
The Hamiltonian can be decomposed into
H=H_{v}+H_{\tau}
, where the intravalley term
H_{v}
is given by

H_{v}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\hat{\pi}_{z}^{2}}{m_{l}}+\frac{\hat{k}_{x}^{2}+\hat{k}_{y}^{2}}{m_{t}}\right)+V(\boldsymbol{r})+\frac{\mu_{B}g_{0}}{2}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(1)

\hat{\pi}_{z}\!=\!\hat{k}_{z}+(e/\hbar)A_{z}(x,y)
,

\hat{k}_{j}\!=\!-i\partial_{j}
,
m_{l}\!=\!0.91m_{e}
and
m_{t}\!=\!0.19m_{e}

Zwanenburg et al. 2013
are the longitudinal and transverse effective masses, respectively, and
\sigma_{j}
are Pauli spin matrices, with
j\in\{x,y,z\}
.
The potential is given by

V(\boldsymbol{r})=V_{0}\cos(\frac{2\pi z}{\lambda})+V_{l}(z)+\frac{m_{t}\omega_{t}^{2}}{2}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)+V_{\text{dis}}(\boldsymbol{r}),

(2)

where the first term, describing a WW, arises from Ge concentration oscillations of wavelength
\lambda
,

V_{l}
is the quantum well confinement potential arising from the Ge concentration profile
n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
, which also includes the external electric fields but no Ge concentration oscillations or random-alloy fluctuations,

\hbar\omega_{t}
is the orbital splitting of an in-plane harmonic confinement potential representing a quantum dot,
and
V_{\text{dis}}
describes the potential fluctuations caused by random-alloy disorder.
The intervalley term
H_{\tau}
is given by

H_{\tau}=\left[V(\boldsymbol{r})e^{-i2k_{0}z}+\beta_{0}e^{i2k_{1}z}D(\hat{k}_{x},\hat{k}_{y})\right]\tau_{-}+h.c.,

(3)

where
\tau_{j}
are Pauli matrices acting in the
\{+z,-z\}
valley space, with
j\in\left\{x,y,z\right\}
,
\tau_{\pm}=(\tau_{x}\pm i\tau_{y})/2
are valley raising/lowering operators, and “
h.c.
” denotes the Hermitian conjugate.
The first term in Eq. (
3
) describes the normal (i.e. spin-conserving) valley coupling, while the second term is the SOC.
Here,
D(\hat{k}_{x},\hat{k}_{y})\!=\!\hat{k}_{x}\sigma_{x}-\hat{k}_{y}\sigma_{y}
is the Dresselhaus SOC operator whose coupling strength
\beta_{0}=8.2~\text{meV}\cdot\text{nm}
is determined from a many-band tight-binding theory
SM
.
The valley and SOC terms both oscillate rapidly, with
2k_{0}\approx 0.83(4\pi/a_{0})
and
2k_{1}=4\pi/a_{0}-2k_{0}
corresponding to wave vectors connecting valleys in the same or neighboring Brillouin zones, respectively, in the Si band structure.
Further details regarding the effective-mass model and its connection to a minimal tight-binding model are given in
SM
.

Effective Hamiltonian.
—Starting from the effective-mass description of Eqs. (
1

3

We begin with two observations.
First, we know from envelope-function theory

Note that

being the wavevector and energy scale of the Ge concentration oscillations, respectively

3

Therefore, the low-energy eigenstates in the absence of these perturbations serve as a good basis set for perturbation theory.
These basis functions are given by

Equation (

) then reduces to

g\approx g_{0}|\cos\theta_{B}^{\prime}|.

(12)

(Here, we have ignored corrections proportional to
g_{\tau}
, since they are relatively very weak.
Note also that
g=g_{+}=g_{-}
in this case.)
The resulting suppression of
g
is of order
g_{0}
, which is so large that it has no precedent in recent Si/SiGe experiments
Kawakami et al. 2014
;
Veldhorst et al. 2015
;
Ferdous et al. 2018a
;
Ruskov et al. 2018
.
In fact, for the special field orientation for which
\boldsymbol{B}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
are orthogonal (
\theta^{\prime}_{B}=\pi/2
),
g
can even vanish.

We can understand the dramatic suppression of
g
as follows.
When the spin-valley coupling dominates in Eq. (
5
),
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
becomes the quantization axis.
To better understand the effect of this, we rotate the spin so that
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
points along the
z
axis.
Again ignoring terms proportional to
g_{\tau}
, we have

\begin{split}H_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}=&~\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}}{2}\tau_{z}\sigma_{z}+|\Delta|\tau_{x}+\frac{E_{z}}{2}\left(\cos\theta_{B}^{\prime}\sigma_{z}+\sin\theta_{B}^{\prime}\sigma_{x}\right),\end{split}

(13)

where
E_{z}=\mu_{B}g_{0}B
is the unrenormalized Zeeman energy.
Note that we have rotated the valley and spin about the
\tau_{z}
and
\sigma_{z}
axes, respectively, to remove terms proportional
\tau_{y}
and
\sigma_{y}
.
In the limit
|\Delta|\ll\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
, and further assuming small magnetic fields for which
E_{z}\ll\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
, the first term in Eq. (
13
) dominates.
The resulting eigenstates are then well-defined spin-valley states, and the spins and valleys are said to be locked, with opposite spin orientations for the two
\pm z
-valley states.
This is similar to spin-valley locking in certain 2D materials with strong SOC, such as 2D transition metal dichalcogenides
Tao and Tsymbal 2019
.
Now,
applying first-order perturbation theory by projecting onto the two lowest-energy states,
we see that only the magnetic field component parallel to
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
contributes to the spin splitting.
Thus, spin-valley locking can be understood as the source of the giant
g
-factor renormalization in Eq. (
12
), because it decouples the quantization axis from the magnetic field axis.

For higher magnetic fields, the first-order perturbation theory breaks down, and we must include the
(E_{z}/2)\sin\theta^{\prime}_{B}\sigma_{x}
term in the analysis. Diagonalizing Eq. (
13
) then yields a non-linear magnetic field dependence, as illustrated in Fig.
3
(a), where we have chosen
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}\!=\!2.5~\mu\text{eV}
(or
B_{\text{sv}}\approx 20~\text{mT}
),
\Delta=0
, and
\theta_{B}^{\prime}\!=\!\pi/3
, yielding
g\!=\!g_{0}/2
as
B\rightarrow 0
.
In this case, the spectrum splits into well-defined lower and upper valley-state manifolds, which are separated by the spin-valley energy
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
at low fields, rather than the conventional valley splitting.
When
B\gtrsim B_{\text{sv}}
, we observe significant deviations from linear spin splitting, indicating that the
g
factor is no longer well defined.

The magnetic field
B
at which the system deviates from linear splitting is clearly controlled by the the strength of the spin-valley field
B_{\text{sv}}
(or equivalently
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
).
Therefore, it is important to understand the distribution of
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
, which shows spatial fluctuations due to random-alloy disorder.
In
SM
, we show that
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
follows a Rayleigh distribution, similar to
E_{v}
.
Importantly, the scale parameter of the Rayleigh distribution is
\sigma_{\text{sv}}\!\propto\!|\beta_{0,0}|\sqrt{\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}}
.
Therefore, the WW, with its enhanced SOC and finite Ge density in the quantum well region, can be expected to typically yield large
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
.
Figure
3
(b) shows a distribution of
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
for a WW with Ge oscillations of wavelength
\lambda=1.57~\text{nm}
and an average Ge concentration of
\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}=0.05
, corresponding to a potential oscillation amplitude of
V_{0}=30~\text{meV}
.
This yields typical
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
values of several
\mu\text{eV}
(corresponding to
B_{\text{sv}}>20
mT), indicating that the linear-regime of the giant
g
-factor suppression should be readily observed in magnetic fields of up to tens of mT.

In addition to low magnetic fields, giant
g
-factor renormalization also requires low
|\Delta|
values.
Indeed, we find the exact
g
-factor of the effective Hamiltonian in Eq. (
5
) to be

g\approx\frac{\sqrt{4|\Delta|^{2}+\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{B}^{\prime}}}{\tilde{E}_{v}}g_{0},

(14)

where
\tilde{E}_{v}=\sqrt{4|\Delta|^{2}+\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}^{2}}
is the generalized valley splitting in the presence of the spin-valley field.
We plot
g
in Fig.
3

|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
for three values of
\theta_{B}^{\prime}
(the relative angle between
\boldsymbol{B}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
).
Here,
g
increases monotonically with increasing
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{sv}
, where

is recovered in the “normal” regime limit of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{sv}\rightarrow\infty
independent of
\theta_{B}^{\prime}
.
We see that the giant
g

|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}=E_{v}/2\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}\lesssim 1
.
Interestingly, we find that the
g

|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}=0
with respect to fluctuations in

Given that
E_{v}
naturally varies in space, as shown in Fig.
1
(b),
the analysis of the previous paragraph suggests an important practical question:
if a dot is tuned to the special working point where
\Delta
and
g
are minimized, do electric-field (“charge”) fluctuations shift the dot position enough to destroy the
g
-factor suppression coming from the spin-valley field?
Fortunately, the answer is no in the case of a WW, due to its enhanced spin-orbit coupling.
To support this claim, we assume the dot is initially tuned to a point where
\Delta=0
, in the absence of charge noise.
We then introduce charge noise as a random in-plane electric field
\boldsymbol{E}
.
As we show in
SM
, this displacement field causes a change in the ratio between the valley coupling and spin-valley energy, given by

\frac{|\Delta|}{\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}|\boldsymbol{E}|}{2m_{t}|\beta_{0,0}|\hbar\omega_{t}}\Xi(\tilde{\Delta}),

(15)

where
\Xi
is a function of the various valley-orbit matrix elements
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
.
Remarkably, we show in
SM
that
\Xi
follows a universal distribution that is independent of the details of the quantum well.
Therefore, the distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
only depends on the strength of the charge noise, the SOC coefficient
\beta_{0,0}
, and the orbital splitting
\hbar\omega_{t}
.
To determine typical values of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
, we assume that each component of
\boldsymbol{E}
is normally distributed, with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of
\sigma_{E}=20~\mu\text{V/nm}
.
For a double dot geometry with dots separated by
d=100~\text{nm}
, this corresponds to detuning fluctuations with a standard deviation of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}=20~\mu\text{eV}
.
(This is a relatively large charge noise
Kranz et al. 2020
, so we are in no danger of making a conservative estimate.)
In Fig.
3
(d), we show the resulting distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
values, for a WW with the same parameters as Figs.
3
(b) and
3
(c), and
\hbar\omega_{t}=1~\text{meV}
.
Importantly, the distribution is strongly concentrated in the range of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}\ll 1
.
This confirms the claim that charge noise does not wash out the desired
g
-factor suppression.

Conclusion.
—We have developed a theory of
g
-factor physics in Si/SiGe quantum dots that expresses
g
in terms of readily computable matrix elements and applies to all heterostructures of current interest.
We use this understanding to reproduce existing results
Veldhorst et al. 2015
;
Ruskov et al. 2018
;
Ferdous et al. 2018a
;
Ferdous et al. 2018b
;
Nestoklon et al. 2008
, while also obtaining solutions that were previously unknown.
In particular, we focus on the case of a long-period WW
McJunkin et al. 2022
, where we observe a
g
-factor renormalization approximately ten times larger than conventional, pure-Si quantum wells.
We also uncover a giant suppression of the
g
factor at spatially varying locations where
E_{v},E_{z}\lesssim\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
, which is of great interest for qubit gate operations, because it allows magnetic field couplings to be suppressed or extinguished.
Importantly, we have shown that charge-noise displacements of a dot away from such interesting working points do not significantly affect their operation.

Our results are also of fundamental interest for all types of Si/SiGe quantum wells, even those without Ge concentration oscillations.
For example, the underlying connection between the
g
-factor physics and valleys is given in Eq. (
9
), which clarifies the relationship between
g
, the valley phase
\phi_{v}
, and the phase
\phi_{g}
of the valley-dependent
g
-factor renormalization
g_{\tau}
.
Although Refs.
Veldhorst et al. 2015
;
Ruskov et al. 2018
include a phase parameter similar to
\phi_{g}
, it was introduced phenomenologically.
In contrast,
\phi_{g}
is derived rigorously in Eq. (
6
), where it arises from the intersubband SOC.
This has important implications for the valley-dependent
g
-factor renormalization
g_{\tau}
, which is found to be robust against alloy-disorder fluctuations, due to the large energy spacing between subbands as compared to the energy scale of the alloy disorder.
In contrast, the valley phase
\phi_{v}
is mainly determined by the locally varying alloy disorder
Losert et al. 2023
, as reflected in local fluctuations of the giant
g
-factor in WWs.
We will make use of these results in forthcoming work
Woo
, by developing an experimental protocol that uses
g
-factor measurements to probe the valley-phase landscape and characterize the valley-coupling parameters that determine the statistics of
E_{v}
.

Derivation of the effective-mass model from a minimal tight-binding model

In the main text, we employ an effective-mass model, where the two valleys of the Si band structure near the
Z
-point are treated as a pseudospin-
\frac{1}{2}
degree of freedom.
In this supplementary section, we show how this effective-mass Hamiltonian is derivable from a minimal tight-binding model.
The usefulness of relating the effective-mass to this minimal tight-binding model is that the form of the spin-orbit coupling in the tight-binding model is easily seen to be directly connected to momentum selection rules derived from atomistic tight-binding models
Woods et al. 2023
.
The form of the inter-valley spin-orbit coupling in Eq. (
3
) of the main text is then a result of expanding the tight-binding model around the valley minima and taking the continuum limit.
For simplicity, we do not incorporate the magnetic field within the tight-binding model, as the main point is to show how the spin-orbit term arises in the effective-mass model.

Our minimal tight-binding model is an augmented version of the tight-binding model of Boykin
et. al

Boykin et al. 2004
, where our new addition incorporates Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling.
Similar to Ref.
Losert et al. 2023
, we consider a tetragonal lattice with longitudinal and transverse lattice constants
a_{l}=a_{0}/4=0.136~\text{nm}
and
a_{t}=a_{0}/\sqrt{2}=0.384~\text{nm}
, where
a_{0}
is the cubic lattice constant of Si.
We index these sites by
(i,j,m)
, where
i
,
j
, and
m
are the indices in the
x
,
y
, and
z
directions, respectively.
Let
\ket{i,j,m,\sigma}
denote the state on site
(i,j,m)
with spin
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
.
The tight-binding model then has matrix elements

\begin{split}\matrixelement{i,j,m,\sigma}{H_{TB}}{i^\prime,j^\prime,m^\prime,\sigma^\prime}=&~\delta_{i,j,\sigma}^{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\left(t_{1}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}\pm 1}+t_{2}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}\pm 2}-2(t_{1}+t_{2})\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\right)\\
&+t_{t}\delta_{m,\sigma}^{m^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\left(\delta_{i,j}^{i^{\prime}\pm 1,j^{\prime}}+\delta_{i,j}^{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\pm 1}-4\delta_{i,j}^{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)\\
&+V_{i,j,m}\delta_{i,j,m,\sigma}^{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},m^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\\
&+it_{\beta}(-1)^{m}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\left[\delta_{j}^{j^{\prime}}\left(\delta_{i}^{i^{\prime}+1}-\delta_{i}^{i^{\prime}-1}\right)(\sigma_{x})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}-\delta_{i}^{i^{\prime}}\left(\delta_{j}^{j^{\prime}+1}-\delta_{j}^{j^{\prime}-1}\right)(\sigma_{y})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\right],\end{split}

(S.1)

where
\delta
is the Kronecker-delta function that vanishes unless the subscripts match the superscripts.
The first line in Eq. (
S.1
) contains the hoppings in the growth direction, where
t_{1}=-0.60~\text{eV}
and
t_{2}=0.62~\text{eV}
are the nearest-neighbor and next-nearest neighbor hopping values.
This next-nearest hopping model along the growth direction was first used in Ref.
Boykin et al. 2004
to match the low-energy valley features of the conduction band structure of Si.
2
2

2

Note that the magnitudes of
t_{1}
and

used here are slightly different than those reported in Ref.
Boykin et al. 2004
, to match the locations of the valley minima

obtained in empirical tight-binding models
Niquet et al. 2009
.
In addition, we note
the different sign used for

, as compared to Ref.
Boykin et al. 2004
, which can be viewed as a gauge transformation in which
the sign of the even-site orbitals is flipped. This gauge choice places the valley minima here at
k_{z}=\pm k_{0}
, whereas
the valley minima sit at
k_{z}=\pm k_{0}\pm 2k_{1}

Boykin et al. 2004

The second line in Eq. (
S.1
) contains the normal (i.e. spin-conserving) hoppings in the transverse direction. Here,

is used to reproduce the transverse effective mass
m_{t}=0.19m_{e}
at the bottom of each valley
Zwanenburg et al. 2013

The third line in Eq. (
S.1
) accounts for the onsite potential
V

Finally, the last line in Eq. (
S.1
) accounts for Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling, where the strength is parameterized by
t_{\beta}
.
Here,
\sigma_{j}
with
j=x,y,z
is a Pauli matrix acting in spin space.
Importantly, the sign of these hoppings flips between each layer along the growth (
z

(-1)^{m}
factor.

To see that the tight-binding model described in real-space in Eq. (
S.1

We define momentum-space basis functions as

\ket{\BS{k},\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}}}\sum_{i,j,m}e^{i(k_{x}x_{i}+k_{y}y_{j}+k_{z}z_{m})}\ket{i,j,m,\sigma},

(S.2)

where
x_{i}=ia_{t}
,
y_{j}=ja_{t}
,

N_{x}
,
N_{y}
, and

\begin{split}\matrixelement{\BS{k},\sigma}{H_{TB}}{\BS{k}^\prime,\sigma^\prime}=&~\delta_{\boldsymbol{k},\sigma}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}\Bigg[4t_{1}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{z}a_{0}}{8}\right)+4t_{2}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{z}a_{0}}{4}\right)+4t_{t}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{x}a_{t}}{2}\right)+4t_{t}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{y}a_{t}}{2}\right)\Bigg]\\

(S.3)

where

(S.4)

\beta_{0}=8.2~\text{meV}\cdot\text{nm}
of our effective-mass model by matching the magnitude of the SOC coefficients of Ref.
Woods et al. 2023
.

II
Effective Hamiltonian derivation details

In the main text, we derived a
4\crossproduct 4
effective Hamiltonian for the lowest-energy orbital of a Si/SiGe quantum dot.
Here, we provide additional details for this calculation.

As stated in the main text, we employ the basis functions
\ket{\nu,n_x,n_y,\tau,\sigma}
, where
\nu
is the subband index,
n_{x}
and
n_{y}
are the in-plane harmonic-oscillator orbital indices,
\tau\in\{+z,-z\}
is the valley index, and
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
is the spin index.
The real-space components of these basis functions take the form
\bra{\BS{r}}\ket{\nu,n_x,n_y}=\varphi_{\nu}(z)\chi_{n_{x},n_{y}}(x,y)
, where
\chi_{n_{x},n_{y}}
is an in-plane harmonic-oscillator orbital and
\varphi_{\nu}(z)=\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(z)U_{0}(z)
is a subband wave function with
U_{0}(z)\!=\!1\!+\!(V_{0}/E_{\lambda})\cos(G_\lambda z)
, and
\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}
is the subband envelope function satisfying

\left[\frac{\hbar^{2}\hat{k}_{z}^{2}}{2m_{t}}+V_{l}(z)\right]\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(z)=\varepsilon_{\nu}\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(z),

(S.11)

where
\varepsilon_{\nu}
is the subband energy.
Note that we can choose
\varphi_{\nu}(z)\in\mathbb{R}
without loss of generality.
The Hamiltonian in this new basis is then given by

\begin{split}&H_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=~\delta_{\mu,\nu}\delta_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\left[\varepsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}+\frac{\mu_{B}g_{0}}{2}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right]+k_{z}^{\mu,\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}+\left(\left[\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}+\beta_{\mu,\nu}D_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\right]\tau_{-}+h.c.\right)+\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu},\end{split}

(S.12)

where
H_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\matrixelement{\mu,\BS{m}}{H}{\nu,\BS{n}}
.
The various matrix elements in Eq. (
S.12
) are given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}=\varepsilon_{\nu}+\hbar\omega_{t}\left(n_{x}+n_{y}\right),

(S.13)

\displaystyle k_{z}^{\mu,\nu}=\matrixelement{\varphi_{\mu}}{\hat{k}_z}{\varphi_{\nu}}\in i\mathbb{R},

(S.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}\pi}{m_{l}\Phi_{0}}\matrixelement{\chi_{\BS{m}}}{A_z}{\chi_{\BS{n}}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}\pi}{m_{l}\Phi_{0}}\left(B_{x}y^{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}-B_{y}x^{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\right)\in\mathbb{R},

(S.15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\matrixelement{\varphi_{\mu} \chi_{\BS{m}}}{V e^{-i 2k_0 z}}{\varphi_{\nu}\chi_{\BS{n}}}\in\mathbb{C},

(S.16)

\displaystyle\beta_{\mu,\nu}=\beta_{0}\matrixelement{\varphi_{\mu}}{e^{i 2 k_1 z}}{\varphi_{\nu}}\in\mathbb{C},

(S.17)

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}=k_{x}^{\boldsymbol{m,\boldsymbol{n}}}\sigma_{x}-k_{y}^{\boldsymbol{m,\boldsymbol{n}}}\sigma_{y},

(S.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\matrixelement{\varphi_{\mu} \chi_{\BS{m}}}{V_{\T{dis}}}{\varphi_{\nu}\chi_{\BS{n}}}\in\mathbb{R},

(S.19)

where

\displaystyle x^{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{m_{y},n_{y}}\frac{\ell_{t}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{m_{x}}\delta_{m_{x},n_{x}+1}+\sqrt{n_{x}}\delta_{m_{x},n_{x}-1}\right)\in\mathbb{R},

(S.20)

\displaystyle y^{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{m_{x},n_{x}}\frac{\ell_{t}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{m_{y}}\delta_{m_{y},n_{y}+1}+\sqrt{n_{y}}\delta_{m_{y},n_{y}-1}\right)\in\mathbb{R},

(S.21)

\displaystyle k_{x}^{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{m_{y},n_{y}}\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}\ell_{t}}\left(\sqrt{m_{x}}\delta_{m_{x},n_{x}+1}-\sqrt{n_{x}}\delta_{m_{x},n_{x}-1}\right)\in i\mathbb{R},

(S.22)

\displaystyle k_{y}^{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{m_{x},n_{x}}\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}\ell_{t}}\left(\sqrt{m_{y}}\delta_{m_{y},n_{y}+1}-\sqrt{n_{y}}\delta_{m_{y},n_{y}-1}\right)\in i\mathbb{R},

(S.23)

with
\ell_{t}=\sqrt{\hbar/(m_{t}\omega_{t})}
being the characteristic quantum dot size in the transverse direction.
We also have the symmetry properties

\displaystyle k_{z}^{\mu,\nu}=-k_{z}^{\nu,\mu},

(S.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}=\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m}},

(S.25)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m}}^{\nu,\mu},

(S.26)

\displaystyle\beta_{\mu,\nu}=\beta_{\nu,\mu},

(S.27)

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}=-D_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m}},

(S.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{m}}^{\nu,\mu},

(S.29)

which are useful when performing the transformation below.

Figure S.2:

). Columns correspond to the four combinations of the valley
\tau

degrees of freedom, while the rows correspond to specified orbital quantum numbers,

.

As explained in the main text, we derive an effective low-energy Hamiltonian for the lowest-energy orbital,
(\nu,n_{x},n_{y})=(0,0,0)
, using a Schrieffer-Wolff transformation
Luttinger and Kohn 1955
;
Winkler 2003
.
To do so, the Hamiltonian is decomposed into
H=H_{0}+\tilde{H}
, where the unperturbed Hamiltonian is given by

(H_{0})_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\delta_{\mu,\nu}\delta_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\varepsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}},

(S.30)

and the perturbing Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
contains the remaining terms in Eq. (
S.12
):

\tilde{H}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=~\delta_{\mu,\nu}\delta_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\frac{\mu_{B}g_{0}}{2}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+k_{z}^{\mu,\nu}\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}+\left(\left[\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}+\beta_{\mu,\nu}D_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\right]\tau_{-}+h.c.\right)+\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}.

(S.31)

We note that the various terms in
\tilde{H}
are much smaller than the bare energy separations
\varepsilon_{\mu,\boldsymbol{m}}-\varepsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}
in
H_{0}
, justifying the perturbation approach. To second-order, the effective Hamiltonian is then given by

H_{\text{eff}}=H^{(1)}+H^{(2)},

(S.32)

where
H^{(1)}
is the perturbing Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
projected onto the low-energy
(\nu,n_{x},n_{y})\!=(0,0,0)
subspace, and
H^{(2)}
accounts for second-order processes involving virtual states in the high-energy subspace, with
(\nu,n_{x},n_{y})\!\neq(0,0,0)
.
The second-order term follows the prescription

H^{(2)}=-{\sum_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}}^{\prime}\frac{\tilde{H}_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{n}}^{0,\nu}\tilde{H}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{0}}^{\nu,0}}{\varepsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}-\varepsilon_{0}},

(S.33)

where the prime mark on the summation indicates that the low-energy contribution,
(\nu,n_{x},n_{y})=(0,0,0)
, is not to be included.
Combining Eqs. (
S.31
) and (
S.33
), and making use of the symmetry properties in Eqs. (
S.24
)-(
S.29
) yields the desired result,

H_{\text{eff}}=~\Delta\tau_{-}+\Delta^{*}\tau_{+}+\frac{\mu_{B}g_{0}}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}\tau_{z}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\frac{\mu_{B}}{2}\left(g_{\tau}\tau_{-}+g_{\tau}^{*}\tau_{+}\right)\left(B_{y}\sigma_{x}+B_{x}\sigma_{y}\right),

(S.34)

corresponding to Eq. (
5
) in the main text, where

\Delta=\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}-2{\sum_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}}^{\prime}\frac{\mathcal{V}^{0,\nu}_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{n}}\tilde{\Delta}^{\nu,0}_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{0}}}{\varepsilon_{\nu,\boldsymbol{n}}-\varepsilon_{0}},

(S.35)

and
g_{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
are given by Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) in the main text.
Note that the second-order term in Eq. (
S.35
) gives only a small correction to the first-order term, allowing us to approximate
\Delta\approx\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}
.

Some examples of second-order processes that contribute to
g_{\tau}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
are shown in Fig.

, where the purple and orange paths contribute to
g_{\tau}B_{y}\tau_{-}\sigma_{x}
and
B_{\text{sv}}^{x}
, respectively.

III
Statistics of alloy disorder matrix elements

As explained in the main text, the valley-orbit coupling responsible for the spin-valley field
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{sv}}
arises from alloy disorder.
It is therefore important to characterize the form and magnitude of the disorder potential
V_{\text{dis}}
and how they affect the statistical properties of the
\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
and
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
matrix elements appearing in Eq. (
S.12
).
In this Supplementary section, we derive these properties.

To understand the physics of alloy disorder, we consider a discretized version of the effective mass model presented in Eqs. (
1
)-(
3
) of the main text. Similar to Ref.
Losert et al. 2023
, we employ a tetragonal lattice with longitudinal and transverse lattice constants
a_{l}=a_{0}/4=0.136~\text{nm}
and
a_{t}=a_{0}/\sqrt{2}=0.384~\text{nm}
, where
a_{0}
is the cubic lattice constant of Si.
Also following Ref.
Losert et al. 2023
, we consider a model where the Si and Ge sites are identical, except that the Ge sites have an extra onsite energy of
E_{\text{Ge}}=0.6~\text{eV}
, which gives the desired band offset between atomic layers with different Ge concentrations. While the actual crystal structure of Si is a diamond lattice, our choice of lattice constants, above, yields a site density that matches the atomic density of the diamond lattice of Si. We therefore expect the essential statistical properties of the alloy fluctuations to be appropriately captured in our model, as confirmed in Ref.
Losert et al. 2023
by comparing the valley splitting statistics of an effective-mass model with a large-scale
\text{sp}^{3}\text{d}^{5}\text{s}^{*}
tight-binding model.
The main, nonfluctuating changes in the Ge concentration are assumed to occur along the growth direction
\hat{z}
, such that
n_{\text{Ge}}=n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
, where
n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
is averaged over a given atomic layer in the
x
-
y
plane. For a layer with Ge concentration
n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
, we then assume that every site is independent of the other sites, with probability
n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
of being Ge and
(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z))
of being Si. The average confinement potential of this layer is given by
E_{\text{Ge}}n_{\text{Ge}}(z)
, which we incorporate into the longitudinal potential term
V_{l}
in Eq. (
2
). Fluctuations about this virtual crystal potential arising from the random assignment of Ge sites are contained in the disorder potential
V_{\text{dis}}
of Eq. (
2
). Thus, a site at position
\boldsymbol{r}
has a disorder potential given by

V_{\text{dis}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\begin{cases}-E_{\text{Ge}}n_{\text{Ge}}(z),&\text{probability}=1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z)\\
E_{\text{Ge}}\left(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z)\right),&\text{probability}=n_{\text{Ge}}(z)\end{cases}

(S.36)

where the first and second lines correspond to Si and Ge sites, respectively.
It is easy to show that the disorder site potential satisfies

\displaystyle\left<V_{\text{dis}}(\boldsymbol{r})\right>=0~,

(S.37)

\displaystyle\left<V_{\text{dis}}(\boldsymbol{r})V_{\text{dis}}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\right>=\delta_{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}E_{\text{Ge}}^{2}n_{\text{Ge}}(z)(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z)).

(S.38)

Next, we consider the disorder matrix elements
\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
and
\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
, where the former is defined in Eq. (
S.19
) and appears in the intravalley Hamiltonian, while the latter is the alloy-disorder component of the normal valley coupling
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
, given by
\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\matrixelement{\varphi_{\mu} \chi_{\BS{m}}}{V_{\T{dis}}e^{-i 2k_0 z}}{\varphi_{\nu}\chi_{\BS{n}}}
.
Since we are considering the discretized version of the effective mass model on a lattice, we must use the discretized versions (
\ket{\bar{\varphi}_{\nu} \bar{\chi}_{\BS{n}}}
) of the continuum basis states, where the latter (
\ket{{\varphi}_{\nu} {\chi}_{\BS{n}}}
) are defined in Eq. (
S.11
).
The disorder matrix elements are then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\sum_{i,j}V_{\text{dis}}(x_{i},y_{i},z_{j})\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(x_{i},y_{i})\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x_{i},y_{i})\bar{\varphi}_{\mu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu}(z_{j}),

(S.39)

\displaystyle\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=\sum_{i,j}V_{\text{dis}}(x_{i},y_{i},z_{j})e^{-i2k_{0}z_{j}}\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{m}}(x_{i},y_{i})\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x_{i},y_{i})\bar{\varphi}_{\mu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu}(z_{j}),

(S.40)

where
i
and
j
are the transverse and longitudinal site indices. From Eq. (
S.37
), it follows that

\Big<\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\Big>=\Big<\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\Big>=0.

(S.41)

Using Eq. (
S.38
), we also obtain the variance

\Big<\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>=E_{\text{Ge}}^{2}\sum_{i}\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x_{i},y_{i})\sum_{j}\bar{\varphi}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}\nu\nu^{\prime}}(z_{j})n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j})(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j})),

(S.42)

where
\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{m}_{1}\boldsymbol{m}_{2}\boldsymbol{m}_{3}\boldsymbol{m}_{4}}(x_{i},y_{i})=\prod_{p=1}^{4}\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{m}_{p}}(x_{i},y_{i})
and
\bar{\varphi}_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}}(z_{j})=\prod_{p=1}^{4}\bar{\varphi}_{\mu_{p}}(z_{j})
.
The variance can be reformulated in terms of the continuum basis functions, using the correspondence

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}_{\nu}(z_{j})\approx\sqrt{a_{l}}\varphi_{\nu}(z_{j}),

(S.43)

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x_{i},y_{i})\approx a_{t}\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x_{i},y_{i}),

(S.44)

which becomes exact as
a_{l},a_{t}\rightarrow 0
. Plugging Eqs. (
S.43
) and (
S.44
) into
Eq. (
S.42
) and approximating the sums as integrals, we find

\Big<\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>=(E_{\text{Ge}}\sqrt{a_{l}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(l)}_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}},

(S.45)

where the longitudinal and transverse covariance tensors are given by

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(l)}_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\int\varphi_{\mu}(z)\varphi_{\nu}(z)\varphi_{\mu^{\prime}}(z)\varphi_{\nu^{\prime}}(z)n_{\text{Ge}}(z)\left(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z)\right)\,dz,

(S.46)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\int\chi_{\boldsymbol{m}}(x,y)\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x,y)\chi_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}(x,y)\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x,y)\,dxdy.

(S.47)

Note that the elements of the covariance tensors reduce in magnitude as the basis states further spread out in space. This reduction is a consequence of averaging over more atoms.
Moreover, Eq. (
S.46
) implies that, when
n_{\text{Ge}}<0.5
, the longitudinal covariance tensor increases in magnitude when the subband wave functions overlap with regions of higher Ge concentration.
Also note that the covariance tensor elements are invariant to the order of the subscripts. One convenient property of the covariance tensors is that they make no mention of the discretized lattice. Therefore, we are free to use the continuum effective mass model or a discretized effective mass model with arbitrarily small lattice constants.
For the geometry considered here, the elements of
\Sigma^{(t)}
may be analytically calculated because
\{\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}}\}
are harmonic-oscillator orbitals. For example, letting
\boldsymbol{m}\!=\!\boldsymbol{n}\!=\!\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\!=\!\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}\!=\!\boldsymbol{0}
, we find

\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{0}}=\frac{m_{t}\omega_{t}}{2\pi\hbar}=\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{t}^{2}}.

(S.48)

where
\ell_{t}=\sqrt{\hbar/(m_{t}\omega_{t})}
is the transverse localization length.

The statistics of
\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
are easiest to understand by decomposing these elements as

\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}=R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}+iI_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu},

(S.49)

where
R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu},I_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\in\mathbb{R}
. We then find that

\displaystyle\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}R_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\sum_{j}\left[1+\cos(4 k_0 z_j)\right]\bar{\varphi}_{\mu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\mu^{\prime}}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu^{\prime}}(z_{j})n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j})\left(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j}\right),

(S.50)

\displaystyle\Big<I_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\sum_{j}\left[1-\cos(4 k_0 z_j)\right]\bar{\varphi}_{\mu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\mu^{\prime}}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu^{\prime}}(z_{j})n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j})\left(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j}\right),

(S.51)

\displaystyle\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\sum_{j}\sin(4 k_0 z_j)\bar{\varphi}_{\mu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\mu^{\prime}}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu}(z_{j})\bar{\varphi}_{\nu^{\prime}}(z_{j})n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j})\left(1-n_{\text{Ge}}(z_{j}\right).

(S.52)

Here we notice that the sine and cosine terms in Eqs. (
S.50
)-(
S.52
) oscillate rapidly in space. Hence, these terms tend towards zero when averaged over the wave function envelopes. For simplicity, we ignore these contributions, leading to the simplified expressions

\displaystyle\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}R_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>

\displaystyle\approx\Big<I_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>\approx\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}\sqrt{a_{l}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(l)}_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}},

(S.53)

\displaystyle\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>

\displaystyle\approx 0.

(S.54)

From Eq. (
S.54
), we see that the real and imaginary components of the matrix elements of
\acute{\Delta}
are uncorrelated. Furthermore, we see from Eqs. (
S.45
) and (
S.53
) that the matrix elements of the intravalley
\mathcal{V}
and intervalley
\acute{\Delta}
are on the same order. Note that fast oscillations also occur when calculating the correlations between
\mathcal{V}
and
\acute{\Delta}
matrix elements, leading to

\Big<\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}R_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>\approx\Big<\mathcal{V}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\Big>\approx 0.

(S.55)

In other words, the matrix elements of
\mathcal{V}
and
\acute{\Delta}
are uncorrelated with one another.

IV
Generating valley splitting landscapes

In Fig.
1
(b) of the main text, we presented an example valley splitting
E_{v}
landscape to illustrate the presence of points of vanishing valley splitting.
Here, we explain how such landscapes are numerically generated.

To generate the valley splitting
E_{v}=2|\Delta|\approx 2|\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}|
as a function of position, we need to take into account the correlations of
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}
between different spatial locations where the quantum dot is centered.
We therefore extend the definition of valley coupling matrix elements
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
to become functions of position,

\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\rightarrow\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}(x,y)=\int\varphi_{\mu}^{*}(z^{\prime})\chi_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{*}(x^{\prime}-x,y^{\prime}-y)V(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})e^{-i2k_{0}z^{\prime}}\varphi_{\nu}(z^{\prime})\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x^{\prime}-x,y^{\prime}-y)\,d\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},

(S.56)

where the centers of the in-plane harmonic-oscillator orbitals have been shifted to
(x,y)
.
The correlation functions in Eqs. (
S.53
) and (
S.54
) are likewise generalized to include the coordinates of the quantum dot center,

\displaystyle\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}(x,y)R_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\Big>

\displaystyle=\Big<I_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}(x,y)I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\Big>=\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}\sqrt{a_{l}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(l)}_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

(S.57)

\displaystyle\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}(x,y)I_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}^{\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\Big>

\displaystyle=0.

(S.58)

where the generalized transverse covariance tensor elements are given by

\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\int\chi_{\boldsymbol{m}}(x^{\prime\prime}-x,y^{\prime\prime}-y)\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}}(x^{\prime\prime}-x,y^{\prime\prime}-y)\chi_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime})\chi_{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime\prime}dy^{\prime\prime},

(S.59)

which clearly has the translation invariance property
\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{m}\boldsymbol{n}\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}(x-x^{\prime},y-y^{\prime},0,0)
.
A direct calculation shows that

\Sigma^{(t)}_{\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{0}}(x,y,0,0)=\frac{1}{2\pi\ell_{t}^{2}}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/(2\ell_{t}^{2})}.

(S.60)

Therefore, Eq. (
S.57
) for
(\mu=\nu=0,\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0})
is given by

\Big<R_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}(x,y)R_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}(0,0)\Big>=\Big<I_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}(x,y)I_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}(0,0)\Big>=\frac{\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}}{2}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/(2\ell_{t}^{2})},

(S.61)

where
\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}
is a prefactor that depends on the details of the Ge concentration profile
n_{\text{Ge}}
and the subband wave function
\varphi_{0}
.

We then consider a two-dimensional lattice of points
\{\boldsymbol{r}_{j}^{\perp}\ =(x_{j},y_{j})\}
for which we will generate
\Delta
consistent with the correlations present in Eq. (
S.61
). Letting
\mathcal{R}_{j}=R_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}(x_{j},y_{j})
, we can define a covariance matrix

\Sigma_{i,j}^{\mathcal{R}}=\left<\mathcal{R}_{i}\mathcal{R}_{j}\right>=\frac{\sigma_{\Delta}^{2}}{2}e^{-|\boldsymbol{r}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}_{j}|^{2}/(2\ell_{t}^{2})}.

(S.62)

Being a real-symmetric matrix,
\Sigma^{\mathcal{R}}
may be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix
O
:
\tilde{\Sigma}^{\mathcal{R}}=O^{T}\Sigma^{\mathcal{R}}O=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N})
, where
N
is the total number of lattice points.
We then define new variables
\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j}\mathcal{R}_{j}O_{j,i}
, which have the property

\left<\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{i}\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\prime}\right>=0,\quad\left<\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{i}\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\prime}\right>=\delta_{i,j}\lambda_{j}.

(S.63)

Hence, each
\mathcal{R}_{j}^{\prime}
variable is described by an independent normal distribution with zero mean and variance
\lambda_{j}
.
The idea is to generate
\{R_{j}^{\prime}\}
by sampling from
N
independent normal distributions, and transforming back to the original variables via
\mathcal{R}_{j}=\sum_{i}\mathcal{R}_{i}^{\prime}O_{i,j}^{T}
.
We can also generate random instances of
I_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}}^{0,0}
using the same procedure.
In this way, we can generate valley splitting landscapes
E_{v}(x,y)=2|\Delta(x,y)|
, like the one shown in Fig.
1
(b), which satisfy the spatial correlations given in Eq. (
S.61
).
The method is efficient because the transformation matrix
O
only needs to be computed once.

In Fig.
1
(b) of the main text, we have adopted the parameters
\sigma_{\Delta}=200~\mu\text{eV}
and
\ell_{t}=10~\text{nm}
, corresponding to an in-plane orbital splitting of
\hbar\omega_{t}=2~\text{meV}
.
This value of
\sigma_{\Delta}
corresponds to a WW with
\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}=0.05
and a harmonic confinement with
\hbar\omega_{z}=10~\text{meV}
as a subband energy splitting.

V
Calculating
g
from the valley splitting landscape

In Fig.
1
(c) of the main text, we plotted
g
near a point with
E_{v}=0
in Fig.
1
(b).
To calculate
g
, we use Eq. (
14
) of the main text.
Therefore, we need the valley-orbit matrix elements
\tilde{\Delta}_{10,00}^{0,0}
and

Therefore, we can extract

VI
Explanation for the node in the intersubband spin-orbit coupling (
\beta_{1,0}
) in a Wiggle Well

In Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) of the main text, we provide analytical results for the intrasubband
\beta_{0,0}
and intersubband
\beta_{1,0}
SOC coefficients of a WW, obtained using the longitudinal harmonic-oscillator wave functions. Here, we explain why
\beta_{1,0}
exhibits a node when
\lambda=\pi/k_{1}
, while
\beta_{0,0}
does not.

Figure S.3:
Subband wave functions
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}
(blue) and
\tilde{\varphi}_{1}
(red) in momentum space for a WW with
\hbar\omega_{z}=20~\text{meV}
,
\lambda=\pi/k_{1}=1.57~\text{nm}
, and
V_{0}=30~\text{meV}
, corresponding to an average Ge concentration of
\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}=0.05
.
The Ge concentration oscillations produce satellite features near
k=\pm 2k_{1}
, where the inset shows a zoom-in of the wave functions near
k=2k_{1}
.
The SOC coefficients
\beta_{0,0}
and
\beta_{1,0}
are both maximized near
\lambda\approx\pi/k_{1}
because this maximizes the overlap integral in Eq. (
S.65
).
However, the intersubband SOC coefficient
\beta_{1,0}
vanishes precisely at this resonance condition, causing a node in Fig.
2
(a), because the satellite feature of
\tilde{\varphi}_{1}
is anti-symmetric with respect to
k=2k_{1}
.

The difference in behavior is best understood by rewriting the SOC coefficients
\beta_{\mu,\nu}
, defined in Eq. (
S.17
), in momentum space:

\beta_{\mu,\nu}=\beta_{0}\int\tilde{\varphi}_{\mu}^{*}(k+2k_{1})\tilde{\varphi}_{\nu}(k)\,dk

(S.65)

where

\tilde{\varphi}_{\nu}(k)\!=\!(1/\sqrt{2\pi})\int\varphi_{\nu}(z)e^{-ikz}\,dz

(S.66)

is the Fourier transform of the subband wave function
\varphi_{\nu}
.
In this way, we see that
\beta_{\mu,\nu}
can be understood as an overlap of wave functions shifted by
2k_{1}
in reciprocal space.
Plugging
\varphi_{\nu}(z)=\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(z)U_{0}(z)
into Eq. (
S.66
), where
U_{0}
is the lowest-energy
\boldsymbol{k}=0
Bloch function defined above Eq. (
S.11
) and
\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}
is the subband wave function envelope defined by Eq. (
S.11
), we find

\tilde{\varphi}_{\nu}(k)=\tilde{\varphi}^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(k)+\frac{V_{0}}{2E_{\lambda}}\left(\tilde{\varphi}^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(k+G_{\lambda})+\tilde{\varphi}^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(k-G_{\lambda})\right),

(S.67)

where

\tilde{\varphi}^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(k)\!=\!(1/\sqrt{2\pi})\int\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}(z)e^{-ikz}\,dz

(S.68)

is the Fourier transform of the subband wave function envelope function
\varphi^{\text{env}}_{\nu}
.
This function should be concentrated near
k=0
, since the electron is delocalized on a length scale exceeding
\lambda
.
From Eq. (
S.67
), we see that the Ge concentration oscillations of a long-wavelength WW induce satellite features near
k=\pm 2k_{1}
, with amplitudes proportional to
V_{0}
.
Such satellite peaks are indeed observed in Fig.
S.3
(dashed box), where
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}
and
\tilde{\varphi}_{1}
are shown for a WW with
\lambda=\pi/k_{1}
.
Since the confinement potential is taken to be symmetric in the current calculations, we obtain subband wave function envelopes that are symmetric and anti-symmetric in
k
for the lowest-energy (
\tilde{\varphi}^{\text{env}}_{0}
) and first-excited subbands (
\tilde{\varphi}^{\text{env}}_{1}
), respectively.
In turn,
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}
and
\tilde{\varphi}_{1}
are nearly symmetric and anti-symmetric about
k=G_{\lambda}=2k_{1}
, respectively, as shown in the inset.
The presence of satellite peaks leads to significant overlaps in Eq. (
S.65
), and strongly enhanced peaks in
\beta_{0,0}
and
\beta_{1,0}
near the resonant wavevector, defined as
G_{\lambda}=2k_{1}
.
However, the antisymmetric nature of both the main peak and satellite peaks of the
\nu=1
(red) wave function causes the overlaps to vanish precisely at the resonance condition, resulting in a node in
\beta_{1,0}
.
Shifting
\lambda
away from
\pi/k_{1}
displaces these small satellite peaks away from
k=2k_{1}
, which reduces the overlaps and and suppresses the peaks in
\beta_{0,0}
and
\beta_{1,0}
.
We emphasize that although the results shown in Fig.
S.3
were obtained a for a symmetric confinement potential
V_{l}
, the same qualitative behavior can also be observed for asymmetric potentials (e.g., when a WW is immersed in an electric field along the growth direction).
This is because the spectral weight of
|\tilde{\varphi}_{\nu}(k)|
is always symmetric about
k=0
.

VII
Distribution of the spin-valley energy
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}

In Fig.
3
(c) of the main text, we presented the distribution of spin-valley energies
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
for a WW.
Here, we describe the derivation of this result.

The
x
-component of the spin-valley field is given in Eq. (
7
) of the main text, and the
y
-component is obtained by making the substitution
\tilde{\Delta}_{10,00}^{\nu,0}\rightarrow-\tilde{\Delta}_{01,00}^{\nu,0}
.
Since the subband energy splitting
\varepsilon_{\nu\neq 0}-\varepsilon_{0}
is much larger than the in-plane orbital splitting
\hbar\omega_{t}
, the spin-valley field is dominated by the
\nu=0
contribution in Eq. (
7
), allowing us to truncate the sum.
We may perform a gauge transformation to choose a convenient phase for
\beta_{0,0}
.
Here we choose
\beta_{0,0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, without loss of generality.
The spin-valley field is then approximately given by

B_{\text{sv}}^{x}=\frac{-4\beta_{0,0}}{\mu_{B}g_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{t}\omega_{t}}{2\hbar}}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{10,00}}{\hbar\omega_{t}},\quad B_{\text{sv}}^{y}=\frac{4\beta_{0,0}}{\mu_{B}g_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{t}\omega_{t}}{2\hbar}}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{01,00}}{\hbar\omega_{t}},\quad B_{\text{sv}}^{z}=0,

(S.69)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}\equiv\imaginary[\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{0,0}]
.
The spin-valley energy is then given by

\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\beta_{0,0}}{\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{t}}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{t}}{\hbar^{2}}}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{10,00}^{2}+\mathcal{I}_{01,00}^{2}}.

(S.70)

Since the long-wavelength WW is expected to be in the alloy-disorder dominated valley splitting regime
Woods et al. 2024
;
Losert et al. 2023
,
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
should have no deterministic contribution, such that
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}\approx\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
.
Moreover, away from any atomic-step disorder at the quantum well interface, any residual deterministic valley coupling
\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}-\acute{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{\mu,\nu}
should be non-zero only for
\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{n}
, because
V-V_{\text{dis}}
only depends on the
z
-coordinate away from a step.
Therefore,
\mathcal{I}_{10,00}=\imaginary[\acute{\Delta}_{10,00}^{0,0}]\equiv I_{10,00}^{0,0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{01,00}=\imaginary[\acute{\Delta}_{01,00}^{0,0}]\equiv I_{01,00}^{0,0}
, where we have used the definition in Eq. (
S.49
). The following statistical properties then follow from Eqs. (
S.41
), (
S.47
), and (
S.53
):

\displaystyle\left<\mathcal{I}_{10,00}\right>=\left<\mathcal{I}_{01,00}\right>=0,

(S.71)

\displaystyle\left<\mathcal{I}_{10,00}^{2}\right>=\left<\mathcal{I}_{01,00}^{2}\right>=\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}\sqrt{a_{l}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(l)}_{0000}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\ell_{t}^{2}}\right),

(S.72)

\displaystyle\left<\mathcal{I}_{10,00}\mathcal{I}_{01,00}\right>=0,

(S.73)

where Eq. (
S.73
) follows from
\Sigma_{10,00,01,00}^{(t)}=0
, because its integrand is odd in
x
and
y
(see Eq. (
S.47
)).
The longitudinal covariance tensor element in Eq. (
S.72
) is dominated by the average Ge concentration inside the WW,
\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}
, such that

\Sigma_{0000}^{(l)}\approx\frac{\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}(1-\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}})}{\sqrt{\pi}\ell_{z}}

(S.74)

where
\ell_{z}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m_{l}\omega_{z})}
is the longitudinal width of the wave function.
Given that
\mathcal{I}_{10,00}
and
\mathcal{I}_{01,00}
are uncorrelated normal variables with zero mean, it follows that
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
follows a Rayleigh distribution, with a probability distribution function given by

f_{\text{sv}}(\varepsilon_{\text{sv}})=\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}}{\sigma_{\text{sv}}^{2}}e^{-\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}^{2}/(2\sigma_{\text{sv}}^{2})},

(S.75)

where
\sigma_{\text{sv}}
is a scale factor given by

\sigma_{\text{sv}}=\frac{\beta_{0,0}E_{\text{Ge}}}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{t}}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{t}}{\hbar^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{l}a_{t}^{2}}{\pi^{3/2}\ell_{z}\ell_{t}^{2}}\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}(1-\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}})}.

(S.76)

Note that Eq. (
S.76
) is actually independent of the in-plane orbital splitting
\hbar\omega_{t}
since the
\ell_{t}^{2}
factor inside the final square root cancels out the
\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{t}}
factor in the denominator of the first factor.
The distribution in Eq. (
S.75
) is plotted in Fig.
3
(b) of the main text for the parameters
\hbar\omega_{z}=20~\text{meV}
,
\lambda=1.57~\text{nm}
, and
\bar{n}_{\text{Ge}}=0.05
, which give
\sigma_{\text{sv}}=4.2~\mu\text{eV}
.

VIII
Distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
for a quantum dot centered on a point of vanishing valley splitting

As stated in the main text, we are interested in the distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
, when charge noise shifts a quantum dot away from the point where
\Delta=0
precisely. Here, we derive Eq. (
15
) of the main text, which describes the distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
.

To study this problem, we consider an in-plane electric field
\boldsymbol{E}=E_{x}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+E_{y}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
, where each field component is normally distributed with zero mean and standard deviation
\sigma_{E}=20~\mu\text{eV}/\text{nm}
.
This noise level corresponds to a standard deviation of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}=20~\mu\text{eV}
for the detuning parameter in a double dot system with a dot separation of
d=100~\text{nm}
(i.e.,
\sigma_{E}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}/d
).
This detuning noise level is on the high end of what has been measured for Si/SiGe quantum dots
Kranz et al. 2020
, so our calculation likely slightly overestimates typical values of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
.

The electric field shifts the dot in space, causing a change in
\Delta
. This can be captured by considering the electric-field-induced coupling between the
s
,
p_{x}
, and
p_{y}
harmonic-oscillator orbitals, which have quantum numbers
(n_{x},n_{y})=(0,0)
,
(n_{x},n_{y})=(1,0)
, and
(n_{x},n_{y})=(0,1)
, respectively.
Ignoring spin, the Hamiltonian in the subspace containing these three low-energy orbitals and the two valley states is given by

H=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta_{s,s}^{*}&-E_{x}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{x},s}^{*}&-E_{y}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{y},s}^{*}\\
\Delta_{s,s}&0&\Delta_{p_{x},s}&-E_{x}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{y},s}&-E_{y}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}\\
-E_{x}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{x},s}^{*}&\hbar\omega_{t}&\Delta_{p_{x},p_{x}}^{*}&0&\Delta_{p_{y},p_{x}}^{*}\\
\Delta_{p_{x},s}&-E_{x}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{x},p_{x}}&\hbar\omega_{t}&\Delta_{p_{y},p_{x}}&0\\
-E_{y}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{y},s}^{*}&0&\Delta_{p_{y},p_{x}}^{*}&\hbar\omega_{t}&\Delta_{p_{y},p_{y}}^{*}\\
\Delta_{p_{y},s}&-E_{y}\ell_{t}/\sqrt{2}&\Delta_{p_{y},p_{s}}&0&\Delta_{p_{y},p_{y}}&\hbar\omega_{t}\end{pmatrix},

(S.77)

where we define
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{n}}^{0,0}
.
We can obtain the effective low-energy Hamiltonian for just the two valley states in the
s
-orbital subspace by applying a second-order Schrieffer-Wolff transformation, giving

H_{\text{eff}}\approx\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta^{*}\\
\Delta&0\end{pmatrix},

(S.78)

where the effective normal valley coupling is given by

\Delta=\Delta_{s,s}+\frac{\sqrt{2}\ell_{t}}{\hbar\omega_{t}}\left(E_{x}\Delta_{p_{x},s}+E_{y}\Delta_{p_{y},s}\right).

(S.79)

If the quantum dot is located at a point of vanishing valley splitting in the absence of charge noise, we have
\Delta_{s,s}=0
in Eq. (
S.79
).
For any given electric field, we can also perform a coordinate transformation such that the electric field points solely in the
x^{\prime}
-direction, giving

\Delta=\frac{\sqrt{2}\ell_{t}}{\hbar\omega_{t}}|\boldsymbol{E}|\Delta_{p_{x^{\prime}},s},

(S.80)

where

\Delta_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}=\cos\theta\Delta_{p_{x},s}+\sin\theta\Delta_{p_{y},s},

(S.81)

and
\theta
is the angle between the
x^{\prime}
and
x
axes.
Importantly, since we consider a symmetric dot,
\Delta_{p_{x},s}
and
\Delta_{p_{y},s}
,
\Delta_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}
should have the same statistical properties as
\Delta_{p_{x},s}
and
\Delta_{p_{y},s}
.
Thus, defining
\Delta_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}=\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}+i\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}
, we have

|\Delta|=\frac{\sqrt{2}\ell_{t}}{\hbar\omega_{t}}|\boldsymbol{E}|\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}+\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}}.

(S.82)

Here, the magnitude of the electric field
|\boldsymbol{E}|
appears rather than its individual components.
Since
E_{x}
and
E_{y}
are uncorrelated Gaussian random variables,
|\boldsymbol{E}|
follows a Rayleigh distribution with a probability distribution function given by

f_{E}(|\boldsymbol{E}|)=\frac{|\boldsymbol{E}|}{\sigma_{E}^{2}}e^{-|\boldsymbol{E}|^{2}/(2\sigma_{E}^{2})}.

(S.83)

Following a similar logic as described for
|\Delta|
, the spin-valley energy
\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
given in Eq. (
S.70
) can be rewritten as

\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\beta_{0,0}}{\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{t}}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{t}}{\hbar^{2}}}\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}+\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s}^{2}}.

(S.84)

Combining Eqs. (
S.82
) and (
S.84
), we obtain the relation

\frac{|\Delta|}{\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}|\boldsymbol{E}|}{2m_{t}|\beta_{0,0}|\hbar\omega_{t}}\Xi(\tilde{\Delta}),

(S.85)

as quoted in Eq. (
15
) of the main text, where

\Xi(\tilde{\Delta})=\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}+\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}+\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s}^{2}}}.

(S.86)

Equation (
S.86
) shows that
\Xi
is a function of three variables:
\Xi=\Xi(\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s},\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s},\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s})
.
Importantly,
\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}
,
\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s}
are independent Gaussian random variables with the same variance:

\left<\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}\right>=\left<\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}^{2}\right>=\left<\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s}^{2}\right>=\frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{Ge}}\sqrt{a_{l}}a_{t})^{2}\Sigma^{(l)}_{0000}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\ell_{t}^{2}}\right).

(S.87)

Given that
\Xi
in Eq. (
S.86
) involves a ratio of these normal variables, the overall scale set by Eq. (
S.87
) is irrelevant.
In other words, the variance given in Eq. (
S.87
) factors out from both the numerator and denominator in Eq. (
S.86
) and cancels.
Therefore, as claimed in the main text,
\Xi
follows a universal distribution that is independent of the details of the quantum well.
The distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
then depends only on the intrasubband SOC magnitude
|\beta_{0,0}|
, in-plane orbital splitting
\hbar\omega_{t}
, and the strength of the charge noise parameterized by
\sigma_{E}
.

Finally, the distribution of
|\Delta|/\varepsilon_{\text{sv}}
values shown in Fig.
3
(d) of the main text is obtained by generating
10^{9}
samples of
\left\{|\boldsymbol{E}|,\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s},\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s},\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s}\right\}
, where
|\boldsymbol{E}|
is sampled from the Rayleigh distribution given in Eq. (
S.83
) and
\mathcal{R}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}
,
\mathcal{I}_{p_{x^{\prime}},s}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{p_{y^{\prime}},s}
are sampled from a normal distribution with zero mean and unit variance, since the variance plays no role as explained in the previous paragraph.
