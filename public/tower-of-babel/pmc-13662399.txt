Structural basis of assembly-dependent activation of the MAD3–MAD4 nuclease in the Pseudomonas aeruginosa MADS defense system
Authors: Jin Zhang, Zengru Li, Le Ye, Yaoyao Hou, Ke Liu, Jinrong Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe methylation-associated defense system (MADS) is a bacterial antiphage immune system that cooperates with CRISPR–Cas systems to restrict phage infection. Within MADS, the MAD3–MAD4 module has been proposed to function as an ATPase–nuclease module related to the OLD nuclease family, but the molecular basis of its assembly and function remains unknown. Here, we combined cryo-electron microscopy, X-ray crystallography, biochemical assays, and mutagenesis to elucidate the architecture and assembly mechanism of the MAD3–MAD4 complex. We found that MAD3 and MAD4 assemble into a stable 2:2 heterotetramer. Structural and biochemical analyses revealed that MAD3 alone lacks detectable ATPase activity, whereas MAD4 is insoluble when expressed alone. Assembly of the MAD3–MAD4 complex induces a conformational rearrangement in which MAD3 adopts an ATPase-competent dimeric conformation, while MAD4 is stabilized through intersubunit interactions. This heterotetrameric assembly exhibits both ATPase and DNA cleavage activities. Overall, our findings reveal the molecular basis of MAD3–MAD4 heterotetramer assembly and provide the first structural and biochemical insights into the function of the MAD3–MAD4 module within the MADS defense system.

Introduction

Bacteria are constantly challenged by bacteriophages and mobile genetic elements that threaten their genomic integrity and cellular fitness. To counteract this relentless evolutionary pressure, bacteria have evolved a sophisticated and diverse arsenal of antiphage defense systems. These include well-established mechanisms like restriction-modification systems and CRISPR–Cas adaptive immune pathways, as well as emerging defense systems such as bacteriophage exclusion systems (BREX) [1], cyclic oligonucleotide-based antiphage signaling systems (CBASS) [2], cGAS-STING-like cyclic nucleotide sensing pathways [3], phosphorothioate-dependent restriction systems [4], the Gabija system [5, 6], and the phage anti-restriction induced system PARIS [7]. These defense systems highlight the remarkable complexity and diversity of prokaryotic antiviral strategies.

The methylation-associated defense system (MADS) employs m6A-dependent DNA methylation to distinguish host genomes from invading phage genomes, enabling selective degradation of foreign DNA [8]. Although phylogenetically widespread, MADS is relatively rare, occurring in ~0.3% of sequenced bacterial genomes [8]. Originally identified in Pseudomonas aeruginosa SMC4386, MADS consists of an eight-gene operon (mad1–mad8) encoding predicted ATPases, nucleases, methyltransferases, kinases, and accessory factors. In its native host, MADS acts synergistically with co-resident CRISPR–Cas systems to provide robust antiphage protection and suppress phage escape through epigenetic diversification [8]. Among the encoded components, the ATPase-associated modules MAD3/MAD4 and MAD7/MAD8 are both required for efficient antiphage defense, as disruption of either module severely compromises MADS function [8]. These observations suggest that the two modules function as non-redundant branches of the MADS defense system.

MAD3 contains a P-loop NTPase domain, whereas MAD4 contains a putative Toprim-like nuclease domain [8] (Fig. 1A). This domain architecture is reminiscent of that of canonical OLD nucleases, in which ATPase and Toprim nuclease modules are functionally coupled to regulate nuclease activity. Similar ATPase-associated nuclease modules are found in several bacterial antiphage defense systems, including Gabija, Eco8, and PARIS [5–7, 9–11]. Despite their distinct molecular organizations, these systems commonly employ assembly-dependent mechanisms to regulate nuclease activation during antiphage defense. However, whether and how MAD3 and MAD4 assemble into a functional complex remains unknown.

Here, we employ cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), X-ray crystallography, biochemical assays, and mutagenesis to elucidate the architecture and assembly mechanism of the MAD3–MAD4 complex. We show that MAD3 and MAD4 assemble into a stable 2:2 heterotetramer that exhibits both ATPase and DNA cleavage activities, providing the first mechanistic characterization of the MAD3–MAD4 module within the MADS defense system. These findings provide a foundation for future studies exploring how this module functions together with other components of the MADS system during antiphage defense.

Materials and methods

Cloning, expression, and purification

For protein expression, the Pseudomonas aeruginosa mad3 and mad4 genes were codon-optimized for expression in Escherichia coli (E. coli). The mad3 and mad4 coding sequences were cloned into a pET-28a vector, in which MAD4 carried an N-terminal 6 × His-tag and an internal ribosome-binding site (RBS) was inserted between mad3 and mad4 to enable bicistronic expression. For the expression of individual proteins, mad3 or mad4 was cloned into pET-28a vectors encoding an N-terminal 6 × His-tag. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells. Cells were grown in LB medium at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.5–0.6 and induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, followed by overnight expression at 16°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, resuspended in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol (v/v), and lysed by high-pressure homogenization. The lysate was then clarified by centrifugation at 11 000 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The supernatant was applied to Ni-NTA affinity resin (GenScript). After washing, proteins were eluted and further purified by SEC on a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl.

MAD3 and MAD4 mutants, including the MAD3E287A/R288D/D291R triple mutant; the MAD3K34A, MAD3E372A, and MAD3R398D single mutants; the Walker A mutant (GANGSGKT replaced with AAAAAAAA); and the MAD4D55A, MAD4D81A, and MAD4K164A mutants, were generated using the same co-expression vector as the wild-type MAD3–MAD4 complex. All mutant complexes were expressed and purified following the same procedures used for the wild-type MAD3–MAD4 complex.

Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)

SEC was performed to assess the oligomeric state of the purified proteins. A total of 500 μl of concentrated wild-type MAD3–MAD4 complex, MAD3–MAD4 complexes containing MAD4 and MAD3 mutants, or MAD3 alone was loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with SEC buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl). The column was calibrated with molecular weight standards (Bio-Rad) under the same buffer conditions.

Size-exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS)

SEC-MALS was performed to determine the absolute molecular masses of MAD3 alone and the MAD3–MAD4 complex. A 100 μl aliquot of purified MAD3 (3.6 mg/ml) or MAD3–MAD4 complex (2.8 mg/ml) was loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with SEC buffer at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min on an HPLC system. The eluate was analyzed using an online UV detector (280 nm), a multi-angle light scattering detector (DAWN HELEOS II, Wyatt), and a differential refractive index detector (Optilab T-rEX, Wyatt). Data were processed using ASTRA software (Wyatt). Molecular masses were calculated using a refractive index increment (dn/dc) of 0.185 ml/g and the peak-averaged Rayleigh ratio.

Toxicity assay

Toxicity assays were performed using an LB agar spot assay. The coding sequences of bicistronic mad3–mad4, mad3, and mad4 were cloned into the pBAD expression vector and individually transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). Single colonies harboring the corresponding constructs or the empty vector were inoculated into LB medium supplemented with kanamycin and grown at 37°C for 4 h. Cultures were then normalized to an OD600 of 0.45 ± 0.05 with fresh LB medium to ensure equivalent starting cell densities. The normalized cultures were serially diluted 10-fold, and 3 μl of each dilution was spotted onto LB agar plates containing kanamycin under either repressive conditions (0.2% glucose) or inducing conditions (0.2% arabinose). Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C, and bacterial growth was documented by imaging. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

DNA cleavage assay

DNA cleavage assays were performed in a 20 μl reaction mixture containing 150 ng of pUC19 plasmid DNA, 2 μM MAD3–MAD4 complex or the corresponding mutants, and either 1 mM of the indicated divalent metal ion or 1 mM EDTA in reaction buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl). Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 30 min and terminated by the addition of EDTA to a final concentration of 10 mM. To assess the relationship between ATP hydrolysis and DNA cleavage activity, DNA cleavage assays were performed with 1 mM MgCl₂ and ATP concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 μM, while other conditions remained unchanged. To further examine the effects of protein concentration and reaction time on DNA cleavage, assays were performed under the same reaction conditions except that the concentration of the MAD3–MAD4 complex was varied (2, 4, 6, 8, or 10 μM) and the reactions were incubated for 30, 60, 90, or 120 min in the presence of 1 mM MgCl₂. Reaction products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 1% (w/v) agarose gels. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

His-tag pull-down assay

To analyze the interaction between MAD3 mutants and MAD4, His-tag pull-down assays were performed using Ni-NTA affinity resin (GenScript). Wild-type and mutant MAD3–MAD4 complexes were incubated with Ni-NTA resin to immobilize the N-terminal 6 × His-tagged MAD4 (MAD3 was untagged). After incubation, the resin was washed sequentially with buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl) containing 20 mM and 50 mM imidazole to remove non-specifically bound proteins. Bound proteins were subsequently eluted with the same buffer supplemented with 300 mM imidazole. Eluted fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE on a 12% gel and visualized using Gel Stain (GenScript).

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay

Binding of the MAD3–MAD4 complex and MAD3 alone to AMP-PNP (ANP) and ATP was examined by ITC. Proteins (30–50 μM) were loaded into the sample cell, and ligands (1 mM) were loaded into the syringe. All proteins and ligands were prepared in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM MgCl₂. ITC measurements were carried out at 25°C using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern). The dissociation constant (Kd) was determined using a one-site binding model, fitted by nonlinear least-squares analysis in Origin 7.0 software (MicroCal ITC200 analysis package, Malvern).

ATPase activity assay

ATP hydrolysis activity was measured using a malachite green-based phosphate detection assay as previously described [12]. The reaction mixtures contained 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.5 mM ATP, and 4 μM of the MAD3–MAD4 complex, MAD3K34A–MAD4 complex, or MAD3 alone. Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 min and terminated at the indicated time point. The released inorganic phosphate (Pi) was quantified by measuring absorbance at 630 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek). A standard curve generated from serial dilutions of phosphate standards was used to calculate the concentration of released Pi. All experiments were independently repeated three times.

Cryo-EM sample preparation, data collection, and 3D reconstruction

The MAD3–MAD4 complex (0.5 mg/ml) was incubated with ANP at a 1:5 molar ratio in the presence of 1 mM Mg²⁺ for 30 min on ice. A second sample containing the MAD3–MAD4 complex (0.5 mg/ml) was prepared in parallel without the addition of ANP. A 3.5 μl aliquot of each sample was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh copper grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences), blotted for 5 s at 8°C using an FEI Vitrobot Mark IV, and imaged on a Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Micrographs were collected using an aberration-free image shift data collection scheme (four images per hole) with EPU data acquisition software (version 3.4) on a Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 keV. The microscope was equipped with a Falcon 4i direct-electron detector and Selectris X imaging filter using a 10 eV slit size. Images were collected with a total dose of 40 e−/Å2 and defocus ranging from −1 µm to −2 µm.

Single-particle cryo-EM image processing

All cryo-EM data were processed using cryoSPARC v4.7.0 (Structura Biotechnology) [13] following standard single-particle analysis procedures. Movie frames were subjected to patch motion correction and patch contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation using the default parameters. Micrographs were manually curated based on motion trajectories, CTF fit values, and other quality metrics. Particles were picked using the reference-free Blob Picker (elliptical blob; minimum particle diameter, 80 Å; maximum particle diameter, 132 Å), followed by the micrograph junk detector. Particles were extracted with a box size of 128 pixels (bin 2; 256-pixel unbinned box size) and subjected to one round of two-dimensional (2D) classification. Three-dimensional (3D) particle curation was performed using multiclass ab initio reconstruction followed by heterogeneous refinement into four classes. A combination of 2D classification, ab initio reconstruction, and heterogeneous refinement was performed several times. The selected particles were re-extracted with a box size of 256 pixels and further refined by reference-based motion correction and non-uniform refinement.

For the MAD3–MAD4–ANP dataset, 12 364 micrographs were collected, of which 11 224 were retained after manual curation. A total of 4 683 737 particles were extracted, and Volume 3 containing 341 976 particles was selected for the final refinement. The final reconstruction reached an overall resolution of 2.64 Å and was sharpened using a B-factor of −120 Å².

For the MAD3–MAD4–ADP dataset, 14 092 micrographs were collected, of which 13 121 were retained after manual curation. A total of 5 174 659 particles were extracted, and Volume 3 containing 500 719 particles was selected for the final refinement. The final reconstruction reached an overall resolution of 2.76 Å and was sharpened using a B-factor of −131 Å².

Model building and refinement

Model building and refinement were based on an AlphaFold3-predicted model [14]. The predicted model was fitted into the density map using UCSF Chimera (version 1.19) [15] and manually adjusted in Coot (version 0.9.8) [16]. Subsequently, the generated model was automatically refined in Phenix.real_space_refine [17] (version 2.0) and manually adjusted in Coot (version 0.9.8) for several iterations. Protein crystallization

MAD3 protein (7 mg/ml) was mixed with reservoir solutions from commercial crystallization screening kits at a 1:1 ratio (0.6 μl and 0.6 μl) and crystallized using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method at 18°C. Crystals of MAD3 were obtained in a reservoir solution consisting of 0.1 M HEPES sodium (pH 7.5), 0.8 M potassium sodium tartrate tetrahydrate, and 30% (w/v) PEG 8000 after 7 days of growth. Before data collection, crystals were cryoprotected by transferring into a reservoir solution supplemented with 20% (v/v) glycerol and flash-cooled in liquid nitrogen.

Crystal data collection and structure determination

Diffraction data for MAD3 were collected at 100 K on beamline BL10U2 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility and processed using XDS [18] and the CCP4 suite [19]. The structure of MAD3 was determined by molecular replacement using PHASER [20], with the MAD3 coordinates derived from the MAD3–MAD4 complex as the search model. The missing residues in MAD3 were manually built into the map using Coot [16]. Structure refinement was performed iteratively using Coot and REFMAC [21, 22]. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

LC-MS was performed to detect nucleotides co-purified with MAD3 and the MAD3–MAD4 complex. Purified proteins were buffer-exchanged into ddH₂O, and bound nucleotides were extracted by methanol precipitation. The extracts were lyophilized, reconstituted, and subjected to LC-MS analysis. An ADP disodium salt standard was analyzed in parallel for peak identification. LC-MS analysis was performed using an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm; Waters). Mobile phase A consisted of 20 mM ammonium acetate in water/acetonitrile (95:5, v/v), and mobile phase B consisted of LC-MS grade acetonitrile. The flow rate was 350 μl/min. Data were acquired in negative ion mode over an m/z range of 200–700 at an Orbitrap resolution of 60 000. Raw LC-MS data were analyzed using Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and extracted ion chromatograms and mass spectra were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

EMSAs were performed to assess the DNA-binding activity of the MAD3–MAD4 complex and MAD3 alone. Proteins (0–4 μM) were incubated with different DNA substrates, including 200 bp double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) with or without cohesive ends, 200 nt single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), and 45 bp dsDNA. The sense strand sequences of the 200 bp and 45 bp dsDNA substrates were 5′-CCTGGGGTGCCTAATGAGTGAGCTAACTCACATTAATTGCGTTGCGCTCACTGCCCGCTTTCCAGTCGGGAAACCTGTCGTGCCAGCTGCATTAATGAATCGGCCAACGCGCGGGGAGAGGCGGTTTGCGTATTGGGCGCTCTTCCGCTTCCTCGCTCACTGACTCGCTGCGCTCGGTCGTTCGGCTGCGGCGAGCGGTA-3′ and 5′-GCGCTCTTCCGCTTCCTCGCTCACTGACTCGCTGCGCTCGGTCGT-3′, respectively. After incubation on ice for 20 min, samples were resolved on a pre-electrophoresed 5% native polyacrylamide gel at 100 V for 2 h at 4°C. Gels were stained with Safe Red nucleic acid dye (Abclonal, RM19009) and imaged using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad) in the ethidium bromide detection channel.

Results

MAD3 and MAD4 form a stable heterotetramer

MAD3 contains a P-loop NTPase domain, whereas MAD4 contains a putative Toprim-like nuclease domain [8] (Fig. 1A). To investigate whether these proteins form a functional assembly, we first examined their oligomeric states. While recombinant MAD3 alone could be expressed and purified as a soluble protein, MAD4 was insoluble when expressed in isolation. To circumvent this, we utilized a co-expression strategy in which MAD4 carried an N-terminal His-tag and MAD3 was expressed without a fusion tag. This strategy yielded soluble MAD3 and MAD4 proteins, suggesting that the presence of MAD3 stabilized MAD4. SEC analysis showed that MAD3 and MAD4 co-eluted as a single peak with an apparent molecular mass of ∼150 kDa, whereas MAD3 alone eluted between 44 and 158 kDa (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1A). Given that the theoretical molecular masses of MAD3 and MAD4 are ~50 kDa and 25 kDa, respectively, these findings suggest that MAD3 and MAD4 likely assemble into a heterotetrameric complex with a 2:2 stoichiometry, whereas MAD3 alone may exist as a homodimer in solution. To determine the stoichiometry of the MAD3–MAD4 complex, we performed SEC-MALS using the purified complex. The experimentally determined molecular weight was ~159 kDa, closely matching the calculated molecular weight of a 2:2 MAD3–MAD4 heterotetramer (154 kDa), thereby supporting a heterotetrameric assembly of the complex in solution (Fig. 1C).

To determine whether the MAD3–MAD4 complex possesses nuclease activity, we incubated the purified complex with supercoiled pUC19 plasmid DNA for 30 min in the presence of Mg²⁺. Under these conditions, the MAD3–MAD4 complex converted supercoiled (S) DNA into nicked (N) and partially linearized (L) forms, demonstrating that the MAD3–MAD4 complex possesses DNA cleavage activity (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1B). Because most nucleases require metal ion cofactors for catalysis, we further characterized the effects of various divalent cations on DNA cleavage. The MAD3–MAD4 complex displayed robust DNA cleavage activity with Mg²⁺ and Mn²⁺, whereas little or no DNA cleavage activity was observed in the presence of Ca²⁺ or Zn²⁺ (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1C). Likewise, no detectable cleavage activity was observed in the presence of EDTA, confirming that the reaction is metal-dependent. We further characterized the nuclease activity by examining DNA cleavage over a range of protein concentrations (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 μM) and incubation times (30, 60, 90, and 120 min), which revealed that DNA cleavage increased progressively with protein concentration and incubation time (Supplementary Fig. S1D–F). Consistent with these in vitro observations, co-expression of MAD3 and MAD4 resulted in cellular toxicity, whereas expression of either protein alone did not show detectable toxicity in vivo (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S1G), indicating that toxicity depends on complex formation.

Cryo-EM structures reveal the assembly of the MAD3–MAD4 heterotetramer

To elucidate the assembly mechanism of the MAD3–MAD4 complex and the structural basis for its DNA cleavage, we first performed extensive crystallization trials. Although crystals were successfully obtained, they exhibited poor diffraction quality, precluding high-resolution structure determination. We therefore used cryo-EM to determine the structure of the MAD3–MAD4 complex. Given that MAD3 contains a P-loop NTPase domain, cryo-EM datasets were collected for the MAD3–MAD4 complex both in the presence of the non-hydrolyzable ATP analogue AMP-PNP (ANP) and in the absence of exogenous nucleotide. We ultimately determined the cryo-EM structures of the MAD3–MAD4–ANP and MAD3–MAD4–ADP complexes at overall resolutions of 2.64 Å and 2.76 Å, respectively (Table 1, Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Figs S2 and S3).

In the MAD3–MAD4–ANP structure, the nucleotide-binding pocket was well resolved, with unambiguous density for the exogenously added ANP molecule (Fig. 2C, top panel). Unexpectedly, datasets collected without added nucleotides also showed well-defined density in this pocket (Fig. 2C, bottom panel), most likely corresponding to endogenous nucleotides co-purified from the E. coli expression host. Retention of endogenous ligands is not unusual in recombinant proteins [23]. To identify the nucleotide co-purified with the MAD3–MAD4 complex, we performed LC-MS analysis, which detected a molecular ion with an m/z value of 426.02, consistent with ADP. This result confirms that ADP co-purified with the MAD3–MAD4 complex and supports assignment of the nucleotide density to ADP (Supplementary Fig. S3D).

Structural comparison of the MAD3–MAD4–ANP and MAD3–MAD4–ADP complexes revealed nearly identical overall structures, indicating that the identity of the bound nucleotides does not substantially alter the overall conformation of the complex (Fig. 2B). In both structures, the cryo-EM density was well defined for most regions of the complex, with only two peripheral regions poorly resolved due to conformational flexibility. Specifically, residues 22–37 and 26–37 in the two MAD4 subunits, together with residues 97–121 and 97–111 in the two MAD3 subunits, could not be reliably modeled. The slightly different unresolved boundaries observed between the two copies of each subunit likely reflect intrinsic conformational flexibility in these regions.

Consistent with the SEC-MALS analysis, both cryo-EM structures reveal a symmetric 2:2 heterotetramer composed of two MAD3 molecules and two MAD4 molecules. The two MAD3 protomers associate in a head-to-head manner to form a dimer, with two nucleotide molecules bound at the dimer interface. The MAD3 dimer forms the central core of the complex, while the two MAD4 molecules symmetrically straddle the apex of the MAD3 dimer. Each MAD4 adopts an extended conformation and interacts extensively with one MAD3 protomer, with additional contacts to the second protomer near the dimer interface, thereby stabilizing the heterotetrameric architecture (Fig. 2A and B). Unless otherwise stated, the structural descriptions below are based on the MAD3–MAD4–ANP complex.

Overall structures of MAD3 and MAD4

MAD3 adopts a conserved P-loop NTPase fold composed of a RecA-like α/β core domain and an α-helical subdomain (Fig. 2D). Structural similarity searches using the DALI server identified several ATPase-related proteins as close structural matches, including the chromosome partition protein SMC from Geobacillus stearothermophilus (Z = 19.3), the DNA repair ATPase Rad50 from Homo sapiens (Z = 18.9), and the Septu system ATPase PtuA from Escherichia coli (Z = 18.4) (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B) [24–27].

MAD4 comprises a Toprim-like domain with a mixed α/β architecture and an adjacent helical domain composed predominantly of α-helices (Fig. 2E). Structural similarity searches identified several Toprim-containing proteins, including nucleases from Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris and Thermus scotoductus (Z-scores of 8.8 and 8.5, respectively). Consistently, structural superposition showed overall structural similarity between MAD4 and these proteins, with RMSDs of 3.9 Å and 3.7 Å over 154 and 125 aligned residues, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4C) [24, 28, 29]. Collectively, these analyses support the assignment of MAD4 as a putative Toprim-like nuclease module.

Biochemical validation of the MAD3–MAD4 heterotetrameric assembly

Within the MAD3 dimer, two ANP molecules are bound at the dimer interface and coordinated by residues from both protomers (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S4B). PISA analysis revealed that dimerization buries ~1764 Å² of surface area and is stabilized by an extensive hydrogen-bonding network involving residues N30, R60, R79, G325, G365, H367, A410, and A441, generating a tightly packed dimeric assembly (Fig. 3B) [30].

Within the heterotetrameric assembly, each MAD4 subunit contacts both MAD3 protomers, forming interfaces with buried surface areas of 1210.9 Å² and 598.6 Å², respectively (Fig. 3C) [30]. The MAD3–MAD4 interface is stabilized by extensive polar interactions and hydrophobic contacts. The major polar contacts consist of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges involving MAD4 residues A11, S13, D50, E51, S72, R136, R175, Q176, R178, and S179 together with MAD3 residues E287, R288, D291, H295, E372, S379, S380, Y382, D383, R398, and R425. In addition, a complementary hydrophobic core, comprising L426, V443, L438, I433, and W429 of MAD3 and G44, L48, L65, P68, and Y69 of MAD4, further reinforces the interface (Fig. 3C).

To validate these intermolecular interactions, we performed site-directed mutagenesis on key interfacial residues of MAD3, including the MAD3E287A/R288D/D291R triple mutant, as well as MAD3E372A and MAD3R398D single mutants. These MAD3 mutants were co-expressed with His-tagged MAD4 and analyzed using His-tag pull-down assays. Compared with wild-type MAD3, the MAD3E287A/R288D/D291R and MAD3R398D mutants showed reduced co-purification with MAD4 (Supplementary Fig. S5). Because MAD4 is poorly soluble when expressed alone and requires MAD3 for stability, disrupting the MAD3–MAD4 interaction would be expected to reduce the recovery of both proteins in the pull-down assay. Together with the structural analysis, these biochemical data indicate that MAD3 serves as a structural scaffold that stabilizes MAD4 within the heterotetramer.

Assembly of the MAD3–MAD4 complex enables ATPase activity and enhances DNA binding

Given that MAD3 contains a P-loop NTPase domain, we next characterized its ATPase activity. Sequence and structural analyses revealed that the catalytic core of MAD3 contains all the conserved P-loop NTPase motifs (Fig. 4A). Structural analysis showed that these motifs are arranged across the MAD3 dimer interface in a trans-acting configuration, in which residues from both protomers contribute to the catalytic site, resembling the composite active site observed in other oligomeric ATPases (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S6). Within the nucleotide-binding pocket, residues E73 and V75 form hydrogen bonds with the adenine base of the bound ANP. In addition, the Walker A motif and H391 from one MAD3 protomer, together with residues D336 and G337 from the other protomer, interact with the nucleotide phosphate groups across the dimer interface through a network of hydrogen bonds (Fig. 4C), supporting the trans-acting arrangement of the catalytic site.

To evaluate the ATPase activity of MAD3 and the MAD3–MAD4 complex, we measured their ATP hydrolysis. The MAD3–MAD4 complex exhibited ATPase activity, whereas MAD3 alone showed no detectable activity under the same conditions (Fig. 4D). ITC measurements further showed that the MAD3–MAD4 complex bound both ATP and the non-hydrolyzable analog ANP with Kd values of 0.52 μM and 2.00 μM, respectively, whereas ATP/ANP binding was not detected for MAD3 alone under the conditions tested (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these results indicate that assembly of the MAD3–MAD4 complex is required for detectable ATPase activity.

To assess the DNA-binding preference of the MAD3–MAD4 complex, we performed EMSAs using a panel of DNA substrates, including 200 bp dsDNA with or without cohesive ends, 45 bp dsDNA, and 200 nt ssDNA. The MAD3–MAD4 complex bound 200 bp dsDNA regardless of end structure, bound 45 bp dsDNA more weakly, and showed no detectable interaction with ssDNA. In contrast, MAD3 alone exhibited only weak DNA-binding activity under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. S7). To explore the structural basis of DNA binding by the MAD3–MAD4 complex, we generated a model of the MAD3–MAD4–DNA complex using AlphaFold (ipTM = 0.81 and pTM = 0.85) (Supplementary Fig. S8). The AlphaFold model, together with the EMSA data, suggests that a composite interface formed by one MAD3 protomer and one MAD4 subunit could contribute to dsDNA binding, whereas MAD3 alone appears insufficient to mediate stable dsDNA binding. Although this model is consistent with the enhanced dsDNA-binding capability of the MAD3–MAD4 complex, the predicted DNA-binding interface remains to be experimentally validated.

Crystal structure of the ADP-bound MAD3 homodimer reveals a more open dimer configuration

To investigate why MAD3 alone lacks detectable ATPase activity, we first examined its oligomeric state using SEC-MALS, as ATPase domains often require dimerization for ATP hydrolysis [31]. SEC-MALS revealed that MAD3 exists as a homodimer with a measured molecular mass of ~100 kDa (Fig. 5A). To assess whether the MAD3 dimer undergoes conformational changes in the absence of MAD4, we crystallized MAD3 alone and determined its structure (Table 2).

The MAD3 crystals belonged to the F222 space group, with one molecule per asymmetric unit. Notably, clear electron density was observed in the nucleotide-binding pocket, even though no ATP, ADP, or non-hydrolyzable analogs had been added during purification and crystallization, and the density was best modeled as an ADP molecule (Fig. 5B). To confirm the identity of the bound nucleotide, we performed LC-MS analysis on the purified MAD3 sample and detected a molecular ion with an m/z value of 426.02, consistent with ADP, supporting the assignment of the bound nucleotide as ADP in the crystal structure (Supplementary Fig. S9). Analysis of the crystal packing showed that two symmetry-related molecules adopted a dimer-like arrangement similar to the MAD3 dimer observed in the cryo-EM structure (Fig. 5C). PISA analysis identified a symmetry-related interface with a buried surface area of ∼1 088 Å² [30]. Together, the crystal structure, PISA analysis, and SEC-MALS results support the conclusion that MAD3 alone forms a stable homodimer in solution.

Structural superposition of the ADP-bound MAD3 homodimer with the ADP-bound MAD3–MAD4 complex showed that the overall architecture of MAD3 is highly conserved between these two structures with an RMSD of 1.78 Å over 747 Cα atoms (Fig. 5C). Although ADP is bound in both structures, the distance between representative residues from the two MAD3 protomers is greater in the crystal structure (12.2 Å) than in the MAD3–MAD4 complex (9.0 Å), indicating that MAD4 binding promotes a more compact MAD3 dimer (Fig. 5C). These observations suggest that MAD4 binding is accompanied by a modest rearrangement of the MAD3 dimer interface, and this rearrangement may contribute to the ATPase activity of the MAD3–MAD4 complex.

Structural and biochemical characterization of the putative catalytic architecture in MAD4

Structural analysis revealed that the Toprim-like domain of MAD4 contains a negatively charged pocket formed by a cluster of acidic residues, including D12, D55, D81, E83, E119, and E121 (Fig. 6A–C). An Mg²⁺ ion is coordinated within this pocket at a position consistent with the canonical metal-binding site of Toprim nucleases (Fig. 6B). In addition, K164 is positioned adjacent to the pocket and may contribute to substrate stabilization through electrostatic interactions (Fig. 6A–C).

To assess the potential role of this putative catalytic site in DNA cleavage, we performed site-directed mutagenesis of candidate catalytic residues. We purified MAD3–MAD4 complexes containing individual MAD4 mutants and analyzed them by SEC. All mutant complexes exhibited elution profiles nearly identical to that of the wild-type complex, suggesting that the mutations did not substantially affect complex assembly or stability of the MAD3–MAD4 complex (Supplementary Fig. S10A and B). We next examined the DNA cleavage activity of the purified mutant complexes. The MAD4D55A, MAD4D81A, and MAD4K164A substitutions resulted in a modest reduction in DNA cleavage activity against pUC19 plasmid DNA (Supplementary Fig. S10C). Together, these structural and biochemical data support the presence of a putative Toprim-like catalytic architecture in MAD4 and suggest that this region may contribute to DNA cleavage by the MAD3–MAD4 complex.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the MAD3–MAD4 complex as an assembly-dependent ATPase–nuclease module, in which ATPase activity, DNA binding, and DNA cleavage activity are all associated with formation of the heterotetramer. Although MAD3 exists as a homodimer in the absence of MAD4, it exhibits negligible ATPase activity. Comparison of the MAD3 homodimer and the MAD3–MAD4 complex reveals that the MAD3 dimer adopts a more compact configuration upon MAD4 binding, and this structural rearrangement accompanies the appearance of ATPase activity (Figs 4 and 5). Similarly, although MAD4 adopts a Toprim-like fold, it is intrinsically unstable when expressed alone. Structural analysis indicates that the putative catalytic site of MAD4 is positioned at the MAD3–MAD4 interface, where extensive contacts with MAD3 are likely to stabilize MAD4 within the complex (Supplementary Fig. S11A and B). The MAD3–MAD4 complex also exhibited stronger dsDNA binding than MAD3 alone in EMSA, and an AlphaFold model of the MAD3–MAD4–DNA complex further suggests that both subunits contribute to a composite DNA-binding surface (Supplementary Figs S7 and S8). Together, these findings highlight the importance of heterotetramer assembly for the function of the MAD3–MAD4 complex.

Although assembly of the MAD3–MAD4 complex is associated with both ATPase and DNA cleavage activities, our current data do not establish a direct mechanistic relationship between ATP hydrolysis and DNA cleavage. Increasing ATP concentrations up to 5 μM did not significantly affect the DNA cleavage activity of the complex (Supplementary Fig. S12A). In addition, the K34A substitution in the Walker A motif reduced ATPase activity to ~50% of that of the wild-type complex, whereas DNA cleavage activity remained comparable to that of the wild-type complex (Supplementary Fig. S12B–D). Together, these observations indicate that the mechanistic relationship between ATP hydrolysis and DNA cleavage remains unresolved.

A central challenge for bacterial immune systems is to balance potent nuclease activity with protection against self-inflicted genomic damage. Across diverse defense systems, this is commonly achieved through assembly-dependent or conformationally controlled activation of nucleolytic effectors. Unlike fused OLD family nucleases in which ATPase and nuclease domains are linked in cis [29], MAD3 and MAD4 exist as separate proteins whose activities are associated with heterotetramer assembly. This architecture introduces an additional regulatory layer by restricting catalytic activity to the assembled complex, which may minimize inadvertent nuclease activation. Because the MADS defense system also encodes another ATPase–nuclease module (MAD7–MAD8), future studies are needed to determine how these modules are coordinated within the intact MADS defense system.

Several ATPase-associated defense systems regulate nuclease activity through assembly-dependent mechanisms, despite major differences in their molecular organization. In the Gabija system, a preassembled GajA tetramer associates with the ATPase GajB to form a 4:4 complex for antiviral immunity [5, 6, 9]. In retron-based systems, such as Eco8, Eco8-OLD forms a symmetric 4:4:4 assembly with a reverse transcriptase and msDNA in an autoinhibited conformation. Upon phage infection, Eco8-OLD is released from inhibition and exhibits non-specific nuclease activity [11]. In the PARIS system, the ATPase AriA and the Toprim nuclease AriB assemble into a propeller-shaped complex, in which AriA functions as an ATP-dependent sensor of phage-associated proteins and promotes activation of AriB nuclease activity, resulting in host tRNA cleavage and inhibition of infection [7, 10]. Our findings indicate that the MAD3–MAD4 complex also employs an assembly-dependent strategy in which heterotetramer formation is associated with ATPase activity, enhanced DNA binding, and DNA cleavage activity.

Collectively, our results show that MAD3 and MAD4 assemble into a heterotetrameric ATPase–nuclease defense module with ATPase, DNA-binding, and DNA cleavage activities. This assembly-dependent organization may contribute to the precise regulation of nuclease activity by restricting catalytic function to the assembled complex. More broadly, these findings expand our understanding of the structural and mechanistic diversity of bacterial antiphage defense systems and add to the growing evidence that regulated oligomerization is a common strategy for controlling nuclease activity in bacterial defense pathways.
