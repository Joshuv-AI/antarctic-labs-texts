Cooling the globe with sustainable biomass and minerals: Hierarchical nanostructuring unlocks next-generation radiative cooling
Authors: Yaoxing Wang, Jian Li, Wentao Gan
Published: 2026

Main text

Driven by the escalating climate crisis and intensifying extreme heatwaves, passive daytime radiative cooling (PDRC) has emerged as a disruptive, zero-energy thermal management strategy.1,2 An ideal PDRC material must simultaneously achieve ultrahigh solar reflectance (>90%) and strong mid-infrared emissivity within the atmospheric transparency window (8–13 μm).3 To date, existing PDRC materials encompassing photonic crystals, inorganic ceramics, and petrochemical-based polymers face three critical bottlenecks: energy-intensive manufacturing, dependence on non-degradable fossil resources, and inadequate mechanical strength or outdoor durability. This challenge raises a fundamental question in sustainable materials engineering: can renewable biomass and natural minerals be rationally structured to achieve efficient radiative cooling, mechanical robustness, and green manufacturing within a single material platform?

Two recent studies demonstrate that high-performance cooling materials can be engineered from wood and minerals through hierarchical nanostructuring, overcoming the limitations of conventional PDRC systems. Wu et al.4 reported a radiative cooling biocomposite from natural wood via a multi-step crystalline restructuring process involving delignification, alkali treatment, particle-solution shock, and air drying (Figure 1A). The resulting material exhibits a solar reflectance of 94.7% and mid-infrared emissivity of 90.1%, delivering a theoretical cooling power of 106 W/m2 and an outdoor sub-ambient temperature drop of 8.8°C (Figure 1C). Concurrently, Chen et al.5 constructed mineral hydroplastics from natural layered minerals (e.g., mica and kaolin) using a nonsolvent-induced phase separation (NIPS) strategy (Figure 1B). By replacing mica with calcite, the hydroplastic attains over 90% solar reflectance and excellent mid-infrared emissivity, yielding an outdoor temperature reduction of 2.6°C (Figure 1D). Furthermore, it exhibits V-0 flame retardancy with a limiting oxygen index of 37.6%, high surface hardness (0.23 GPa), and exceptional moldability. The underlying mechanisms reveal how multiscale structural regulation governs photon and phonon transport. Rather than acting as independent components, polymer/cellulosic matrix and mineral fillers operate as coupled phases: minerals provide strong optical scattering, infrared-active vibrations, hardness, and flame retardancy, whereas polymer or cellulose networks regulate processability, particle dispersion, interfacial adhesion, and stress transfer. In the wood-based cooler, optical and mechanical enhancement arises through complementary pathways. Delignification and alkali treatment induce the transition of cellulose Iβ to II, increasing crystallinity to 77.2%. Subsequent infiltration of SiO2 and h-BN nanoparticles creates dual-phase interfaces with refractive-index mismatches that maximize Mie scattering, while C–O–C and Si–O vibrations contribute to strong mid-infrared emissivity of 90.1%. Mechanically, capillary-driven self-densification and hydrogen-bond reconstruction yield an oriented cellulose framework in which mineral-cellulose interfaces facilitate load transfer and crack deflection. The key innovation, termed “particle-solution shock,” dynamically regulates hydration states to achieve uniform nanoparticle infiltration and robust interfacial bonding. This hierarchical integration of optical scattering centers and mechanical reinforcements effectively reconciles cooling efficiency with structural durability.

In the calcite-based mineral hydroplastic, radiative cooling originates from multiple scattering at the mineral-polymer interfaces, while phonon polariton resonances of Si–O and C–O–C bonds contribute to the mid-infrared emission. The mineral phase contributes reflectance, hardness, and fire resistance, whereas the polyacrylamide (PAM) network provides moldability and cohesion through NIPS-induced chain rearrangement and pressure-assisted densification. Cellulose nanofibers further reinforce the PAM-mineral interface, forming a highly oriented “brick-and-mortar” architecture with superior hardness and wear resistance compared with engineering plastics. Both systems share a common design principle: solvent or nonsolvent stimulation regulates hydration states, molecular rearrangement, and hydrogen-bonded interfaces, thereby integrating radiative cooling, mechanical robustness, and processability within a single material platform.

Despite their multifunctional integration, two material systems exhibit distinct performance trade-offs that define their application scenarios. The wood-based cooler delivers an exceptional cooling power of 106 W/m2 with a tensile strength of 416.7 MPa, making it suitable for load-bearing applications. However, excessive nanoparticle loading (>19 wt %) induces surface cracking, necessitating precise compositional optimization. Moreover, although high crystallinity enhances strength, it restricts flexibility, capping the maximum tensile strain at only 5.6%. Superhydrophobic modification improves moisture resistance but slightly decreases solar reflectance from 94.7% to 93.2%. In contrast, the solvent-responsive optical transparency of mineral hydroplastic offers potential for smart windows, but long-term cycling stability requires further validation. Its outdoor sub-ambient temperature drop (2.6°C) is lower than that of the wood composite (8.8°C), attributed to less efficient optical scattering at the mineral-polymer interfaces relative to the tailored nanoparticle-cellulose topology. In essence, these two paradigms prioritize distinct attributes: the wood-based design prioritizes high strength and cooling efficiency, whereas the mineral-based design centers on flame retardancy and moldability. Future deployment must therefore be carefully tailored to specific scenarios, such as load-bearing exterior walls and adaptive smart windows.

Translating these laboratory advances into practice requires overcoming four challenges (Figure 1E). (1) Manufacturing is costly and energy intensive. Although both routes avoid sintering above 1000°C, particle-solution shock, NIPS, and densification at 100 MPa remain time- and energy-intensive. Wood delignification also consumes NaClO₂ and NaOH and generates wastewater. (2) Long-term durability remains unverified. The wood biocomposite was tested outdoors for only 45 days; the stability of its reflectance and hydrophobic coating under humidity, UV irradiation, temperature cycling, and abrasion is unknown. The mineral hydroplastic retains ∼75% of its strength after repeated recycling, and the durability of reversible transparency remains unclear. (3) Environmental safety and life cycle sustainability require systematic assessment across raw-material sourcing, chemical and energy inputs, service life, recycling, and end-of-life management. The environmental fate and ecotoxicity of h-BN, SiO₂, PVA, and PAM remain unresolved. Although reported as relatively low, its carbon footprint (4.82 kg CO₂ eq. kg⁻¹) still exceeds that of conventional PET (3.81 kg CO₂ eq. kg⁻¹), warranting further optimization. (4) Material discovery remains empirical. Cooling performance reflects coupled composition-microstructure-environment interactions, making trial-and-error development slow and resource intensive and hindering the identification of high-performing, scalable, and sustainable materials.

Looking ahead, PDRC materials must advance from proof of concept to scalable, durable, and integrated building technologies (Figure 1F). Four priorities emerge. (1) Smart, responsive systems should pair low-energy, scalable manufacturing with dynamic thermal regulation. Building on the solvent-responsive transparency of mineral hydroplastics, temperature- and humidity-adaptive materials and electrochromic or photochromic surfaces could adjust cooling power and prevent winter overcooling. (2) Multimaterial integration should combine radiative cooling with load-bearing capacity, sound insulation, self-cleaning, and durability, enabling wood biocomposites to serve as exterior structural components. Coupling PDRC with photovoltaics could improve solar-cell efficiency through passive thermal management. (3) Life cycle green design should unite benign feedstocks, low-energy processing, validated durability, and efficient recovery in a material-building-recycling-remanufacturing loop. Room-temperature, solvent-free methods, such as solar-assisted membrane drying instead of hot pressing, could reduce production costs and environmental impacts. (4) Data-driven design should optimize polymer-mineral-cellulose interfaces by integrating molecular dynamics, finite element analysis, high-throughput screening, and machine learning to predict optimal compositions and nanoparticle morphologies. Hierarchical engineering of earth-abundant wood and minerals could thus enable efficient, adaptive, and sustainable radiative cooling while supporting global carbon neutrality.
