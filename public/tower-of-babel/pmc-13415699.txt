Adherence to evidence-based guidelines for the management of candiduria in a tertiary care center in Riyadh: A retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammed Alessa, Sulaiman Alzunaydi, Hattan Y Alfaqeeh, Khalifa M Binkhamis, Rawan Alzaidi, Thamer Almangour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccording to the Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) 2016 guidelines for the management of candidiasis, the presence of Candida in the urine in asymptomatic patients is usually a colonization rather than an infection, which does not require antifungals treatment. This study aims to assess the management of candiduria at a tertiary hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, in comparison to IDSA guidelines and its impact on patient outcomes. This was a single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional study conducted at a tertiary care center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. All adult hospitalized patients (≥ 18 years old) who had a positive urine culture for a Candida species from Jan 2023 to Dec 2023 were included in the study. All Patients’ data were extracted from electronic health records. IRB approval (E-23–8172) was granted. A total of 217 urine cultures that were positive for a Candida species were included in our study. 166 (76.5%) candiduria episodes were managed in accordance with the IDSA recommendations, while 51 (23.5%) were treated against these recommendations. Urinary symptoms were present significantly more in those who were treated against IDSA recommendations (4.2% vs 74.5%, P < 0.00001). The in-hospital mortality rate (36.7% vs 29.4%, P = 0.34) did not differ significantly between the two groups. This study showed that around 77% of candiduria patients were managed according to the IDSA guidelines. However, while the degree of adherence to the guidelines was high in asymptomatic patients, most of the symptomatic patients were managed inappropriately. Adherence to the guidelines’ recommendations did not significantly affect patients’ outcomes.Keywords: Candiduria, Guidelines, Treatment, Adherence

Introduction

The presence of positive urinary culture for Candida species in patients without symptoms of urinary tract infection (UTI) is clinically challenging (Alfouzan et al. 2017). Some clinicians promptly initiate antifungal agents in such patients, although the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines for the management of candidiasis, which were updated in 2016 and considered the standard of care for the management of many types of candida infections, have recommended against prescribing antifungals for asymptomatic candiduria (Pappas et al. 2016).

Risk factors for the presence of Candida in the urine were investigated in multiple cohort studies, and these studies found that the use of indwelling urinary catheters is the main risk factor, followed by other factors including recent antibiotics use, surgical procedures, long hospital stay, old age, female gender, and being on immunosuppressive drugs. Candida albicans, which is considered a normal flora in many individuals, is the causative pathogen in more than half of the cases (Fisher et al. 2011; Bukhary 2008).

As per IDSA, asymptomatic candiduria is managed by removal of risk factors, mainly indwelling catheter. An exception to this rule includes neutropenic patients, low-weight infants, and patients undergoing urinary procedures (Pappas et al. 2016). For candiduria patients with symptoms suggesting UTI, guideline treatment of choice is oral fluconazole 200-400mg (3–6 mg/kg) daily for 2 weeks. Alternatively, for fluconazole-resistant isolates, amphotericin B deoxycholate or oral flucytosine are the recommended options (Pappas et al. 2016).

In real-world practice, the degree of adherence with these recommendations varies widely, as multiple studies have reported adherence to be as high as 53 to 64% (He Z et al. 2021; Jacobs et al. 2018). Others have found that almost 75% of patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) were prescribed antifungals for candiduria even though most of them did not have any urinary-related symptoms (Radosevich et al. 2016). In a randomized controlled trial that evaluated the effect of antifungal therapy in asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic persistent candiduria, the trial included 316 canadiduria episode among hospitalized patients and found that fluconazole therapy was effective in the rapid eradication of Candida compared to placebo. However, long-term eradication was similar between the two groups (Sobel et al. 2000).

Adherence to the guideline’s recommendation is of utmost importance as it will lead to fewer asymptomatic patients being treated, which is associated with less unnecessary antifungal use and its associated costs and toxicities. In addition, treating symptomatic candida UTIs aims to contain the infection and prevent it from becoming invasive infection.

To our knowledge, no study has been conducted in Saudi Arabia to evaluate the management of candiduria. Thus, the objective of this study was to evaluate if the practice in managing candiduria patients in a tertiary hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, meets the IDSA recommendations, and its impact on patients’ outcomes.

Methodology

Study design and patient population

This is a retrospective, cross-sectional study that was conducted at King Saud University Medical City (KSUMC), a tertiary hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, from Jan 2023 to December 2023. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval (E-23–8172) was granted.

All adult hospitalized patients (≥ 18 years old) who had a positive urine culture for a Candida species during the study period were included. If a patient had multiple urine cultures during the same admission, only the first culture was included.

At KSUMC, Urine cultures were processed according to internal laboratory policies and procedures. Yeast identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Biomerieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France).

Data collection

All relevant patients’ medical records were retrieved from the hospital electronic information system which is known as “EsiHi’ and the following variables were collected: Patient demographics (age and gender), symptoms of UTI (fever, and any documented urinary symptoms in patients’ file), presence of urinary catheter, lab tests (White Blood Cells (WBC) and urinalysis), microbiology cultures, used antifungals (name, dose, route of administration, dosage form, frequency and duration of therapy), repeated urine culture, length of hospitalization, and in-hospital mortality rate. The data was recorded in a predesigned and secured data collection form developed for this study using Microsoft Excel.

Outcomes and definitions

Symptomatic candiduria was defined as the presence of a positive urine culture for Candida species along with fever or any urinary symptoms suggesting UTI in the patient’s records, while asymptomatic candiduria was defined as the presence of a positive urine culture for Candida species with no fever or urinary symptoms. If a patient had another active infection with fever, then an urinary symptom is needed to be included as symptomatic candiduria.

The main outcome was the appropriateness of candiduria management in accordance with the 2016 IDSA guidelines for the management of candidiasis (Pappas et al. 2016), as evaluated by pharmacologic management. Other outcomes include length of hospitalization and in-hospital mortality.

Since we relied on pharmacological management to define adherence to the IDSA recommendations, meeting the guidelines’ recommendations was considered for the following patients: (1) asymptomatic patients who were not prescribed antifungals; (2) patients with neutropenia, those undergoing urinary procedures, or those with concurrent fungal infections who were prescribed antifungals; and (3) symptomatic patients who were prescribed antifungals. The antifungal choice, dose, route, frequency and duration must be appropriate to qualify for the ‘meeting IDSA recommendations’ group.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics using mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range, frequencies, and percentages were performed. Comparisons between groups are made for categorical variables with the use of the Fisher exact test when the variable is dichotomous and the chi-square test when the nominal variable exceeds 2 categories. The student t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare groups for continuous variables. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant. The 95% CI was calculated with the Clopper and Pearson method. Multivariable analysis using logistic regression was used to determine the independent impact of treatment on the in-hospital mortality. Length of stay was analyzed using multivariable linear regression. The statistical analysis was conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Patient’s demographics

Our cross-sectional study involved a total of 217 urine cultures that were positive for a Candida species and met the inclusion criteria. The mean age of the patients was 65.8 years, and females represented 65.4% of the patients. In addition, 18.9% of the patients were in the ICU at the time of urine culture, while the mean Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was 4.9. Candida albicans was the most common species, found in 41% of the cultures, followed by Candida glabrata and Candida tropicalis, which were the cause of 19.9% and 18.9% of the cases, respectively. Management according to IDSA recommendations

Of the 217 candiduria episodes, 166 (76.5%) were managed in accordance with the IDSA recommendations, while 51 (23.5%) were treated against these recommendations. Those who met IDSA recommendations were more likely to be in the ICU (24.1% vs 2%, P = < 0.001), had a lower CCI score (4.6 vs 5.7, P = 0.01), less likely to have UTI symptoms (4.2% vs 74.5%, P = < 0 0.00001), and had more concomitant candida systematic infections (22.3% vs 9.8%, P = 0.048).

Regarding the management, antifungal agents were prescribed for 12.7% of patients who met IDSA recommendations compared to 47.1% of those who did not meet IDSA recommendations (p < 0.001). Patients who met IDSA had a longer median length of stay (30 days vs 14, P = 0.007), and a repeated negative urine culture was available for 49 patients in those who met IDSA and 17 patients in those who did not meet IDSA. However, after adjustment for potential confounders, the length of hospital stay did not significantly differ between the two groups (adjusted β coefficient, 25.6 days; 95% CI, −13–64; p = 0.196).

In-hospital mortality (36.7% vs 29.4%; P = 0.337; OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 0.71–2.75) did not significantly differ between those who met IDSA recommendations versus those who did not. The difference did not significantly differ even after adjusting the difference between the two groups for the ICU admission, CCI, other positive candida culture, and presence of C. glabrata or C. krusei (adjusted OR 1.24; 95% CI, 0.58–2.66). Presence of symptoms

Most of the patients in our sample (172/217) did not have UTI symptoms, accounting for 79% of the population, compared to 21% symptomatic patients. The asymptomatic patients were more likely to be in the ICU (22.7% vs 4.4%, P = 0.005) compared to symptomatic patients but had a lower mean CCI score (4.6 vs 5.9, P = 0.004). Antifungal agents were prescribed for 16.3% of the asymptomatic and 37.8% of the symptomatic patients (P = 0.02), and this resulted in a rate of 92.4% adherence to IDSA recommendations in the asymptomatic group, compared to a rate of 15.6% in symptomatic patients (P < 0.001). Discussion

Candida species can cause a wide range of infections in human beings, ranging from superficial infections to invasive diseases (Tamo 2020). However, in the case of candiduria, it is usually a colonization rather than an infection, especially in asymptomatic patients (Fisher et al. 2011; Kauffman et al. 2000).

Candida albicans was isolated in 41% of our sample. Although albicans was the most common isolated species, this percentage is slightly lower than the mean 51% prevalence reported by other studies (Bukhary 2008), and consistent with a previous study published in Saudi Arabia, which reported C. albicans prevalence of 42% (Alenezy 2013).

The concern that not treating asymptomatic candiduria might lead to disseminated candidiasis was investigated in multiple studies. Although Binelli et al. found a high association between candiduria and candidemia, they suggested that candiduria is unlikely to be the cause of candidemia since in more than 50% of patients, the isolated species differ between the urine and the blood (Binelli et al. 2006). Another study found that among 233 critically ill patients with candiduria, the incidence of concurrent candidemia was reported as 8%, and candiduria preceded candidemia in only 5 patients (Bougnoux et al. 2008).

For these reasons, the IDSA guidelines recommend changing the urinary catheter as the only intervention in asymptomatic candiduria and avoiding antifungal agents in this population (Pappas et al. 2016). In our study, the majority of asymptomatic patients (92.4%) were managed appropriately, as they were not prescribed antifungal agents for the candiduria. Although some of these patients were on antifungal agents, their use was considered appropriate as these were prescribed for invasive candidiasis in 14 patients, while 2 patients were undergoing urinary procedures. This degree of adherence to the guidelines in asymptomatic patients is higher than that reported in other studies, which ranged between 57% (Jacob et al. 2018) and 73% (He et al. 2021). An earlier study, published in 2009, also reported that 111 out of 164 (68%) episodes of asymptomatic bacteriuria or candiduria were managed appropriately without prescribing treatment (Cope et al. 2009). In addition to the guidelines, studies have shown that antifungal agents do not affect clinical outcomes in asymptomatic candiduria (Sobel et al. 2000; Chen et al. 2008).

Although asymptomatic candiduria warrants no treatment, Candida species can cause different infections in the urinary tract, such as cystitis and pyelonephritis. For patients with symptoms of UTI and a urine culture positive for Candida, the IDSA recommends treatment with antifungal agents (Pappas et al. 2016; Behzadi et al. 2015). In our study, 21% of the patients had urinary symptoms, and only 16% of them were managed appropriately as recommended by the guidelines. The main reason for deviation from IDSA recommendations was not prescribing antifungal agents when it is indicated, as most cases of candiduria were not treated, irrespective of the presence of urinary symptoms, followed by treating with inappropriate choice of an agent, dose, route or duration.

The reasons why clinicians in our study did not prescribe antifungal therapy for symptomatic candiduria remain unclear. However, potential contributing factors may include uncertainty regarding the clinical significance of Candida in relation to UTI symptoms, as well as limited awareness of current guideline recommendations. Previous studies reported a slightly better adherence rate with the guidelines in symptomatic patients, which ranged between 34 and 39% of the patients. (He et al. 2021; Jacobs et al. 2018).

The overall in-hospital mortality in our study was 35% and it did not differ between guidelines’ adherent and non-adherent groups (P = 0.337). This rate of mortality is higher than what Kauffman et al. found in which the overall mortality among their candiduria patients was 19.8% (Kauffman et al. 2000), and similar to the reported crude mortality of 31.3% in a study among critically ill patients with candiduria (Bougnoux et al. 2008).

Another clinical outcome of interest was the median length of hospital stay, which was significantly longer in patients who met the guidelines’ recommendations compared to those who did not meet the guidelines’ recommendations (30 vs 14 days, P = 0.007). We have attributed this to the higher disease burden in the guidelines adherent group which was manifested mainly by more patients in the ICU (24.1% vs 2%, P = < 0.001) and more patients with concurrent fungal infections or multifocal colonization (22.3% vs 9.8%, P = 0.048). However, after adjustment for potential confounders, the length of hospital stay did not significantly differ between the two groups. Other study that compared the length of hospital stay in candiduria patients found a slightly longer stay of 34–40 days in those treated against the guidelines’ recommendations compared to 26–27 days in the IDSA adherent group, but the differences did not reach statical significance (P = 0.173) (He et al. 2021).

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, given the retrospective design of this study, unintentional selection and inclusion bias cannot be ruled out. Second, obtaining some patients’ information from the system was challenging due to unreliable documentation, which included missing source control data and missing catheter management information for many patients; thus, we depended on pharmacological management to define adherence with IDSA recommendations, as the catheter management of more than 50% of patients on catheter was not properly documented. Therefore, this may impact the overall outcome, as many patients may clear candida with just source control.

Another limitation is the microbiological cure, as the number of patients with repeated urine culture was not documented at the time of data collection. Assuming all patients had a repeated urine culture, the microbiologic cure would be 29.5% among those who met IDSA recommendations compared to 33.3% among those who did not meet the guidelines’ recommendations (P = 0.604). It is important to note that repeating urine culture is not routinely recommended in the treatment of UTIs; it is generally reserved for certain cases, such as refractory or recurrent UTIs, where repeating the culture would be beneficial (Lulich et al. 2004).

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study showed a high rate (77%) of adherence with IDSA recommendations for candiduria management. However, the high degree of adherence was attributed to the management of asymptomatic patients, as the adherence rate in symptomatic patients was poor. The degree of adherence to IDSA recommendations did not affect patients’ outcomes significantly, except for longer hospital stay in patients who met the guidelines’ recommendations, and this is likely due to a higher disease burden in that group which showed no significant difference after adjustment for potential confounding variables.
