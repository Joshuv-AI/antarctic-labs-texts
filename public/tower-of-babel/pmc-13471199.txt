Protocol for culture of zebrafishdeveloping ovaries as an ex vivo system for investigating early oogenesis and ovarian development
Authors: Amal Shawahny, Yoel Bogoch, Yaniv M Elkouby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA fundamental understanding of early oogenesis is lacking, and zebrafish serve as an excellent model for investigation, based largely on in vivo approaches. We present a protocol for long-term zebrafish ovary culture that supports physiological early oogenesis ex vivo, enabling new experimental approaches. We outline procedures for culture plate and media preparation, ovary dissection, embedding-media-free mounting, and culture handling. In addition, we describe workflows for ovary evaluation and rapid functional assays.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Shawahny et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Developmental biology, High Throughput Screening, Microscopy, Model Organisms, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

This protocol describes the preparation and long-term culture of zebrafish ovaries as an ex vivo system for the study of early oogenesis and ovarian development. The developed culture conditions support physiological oogenesis with normal development of follicles from oogonia. The ex vivo system enables imaging-based analyses and is efficiently used for functional assays using small molecule inhibitors in screening experiments, in cases where fine temporal inhibition which is very difficult to achieved by genetics is required, and in combination with genetics on wild-type and mutant backgrounds. The steps below should be completed before beginning ovary dissection and culture. All animal experiments were supervised by the Hebrew University Authority for Biological Models, according to the Hebrew University School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under ethics requests MD-18-15600-2, and MD-20-16228-4, and accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International. It is necessary to acquire approvals for animal studies from the researcher’s institution.

Innovation

Experimental investigation of early oogenesis has been limited by restricted access to early-stage ovaries and by the demanding nature of in vivo genetic approaches. Existing ovarian culture systems typically support only short-term development for several hours (e.g.,2,3) and do not allow sustained longer developmental progression. This protocol establishes a long-term ex vivo zebrafish ovary culture system that supports physiological oocyte development from oogonia to primordial follicles (zebrafish oocyte stage Ib, prior to yolk accumulation).

The key innovation of this protocol lies in the combination of optimized ovary handling, mounting, and culture conditions that preserve tissue integrity and viability, and supports normal development. Using this approach, ovarian tissue can develop for up to 10 days while retaining normal somatic and germ cell behavior, meiotic progression, and follicle formation. This extended culture window enables experimental access to early oogenesis stages that are difficult to access in vivo. In particular the ex vivo system enables the application of vital dyes and pulse/chase experiments for prolonged tracking of cellular dynamics,1 as well as the use of small molecule inhibitors for functional analyses, combined with prompt examination of phenotypes.1 In addition, the protocol is compatible for long-term live time-lapse imaging, allowing real-time analyses of cellular and sub-cellular processes during oogenesis.4

Overall, this protocol introduces a highly effective, rapid, and straightforward workflow that expands the experimental toolkit for studying early oogenesis. With this protocol, early oogenesis is more accessible for investigation, enabling the resolution of developmental processes and their underlying mechanisms, which are not readily addressed using existing in vivo or short-term culture approaches.

Expected outcomes

This protocol was developed for ex vivo culture of zebrafish ovaries for up to 10 days, and provides conditions to support development of follicles from oogonia. Oogenesis in cultured ovaries progresses from pre-meiotic oogonia stages to primordial follicles at diplotene stages of meiosis I in a similar rate to ovaries in vivo.1 At 7 dpc, ovaries present oocytes throughout these stages. If labeling with EdU at 1 dpc, we expect to detect EdU-labeled cells at 7 dpc, with most cells at leptotene and zygotene stages, some cells at pachytene stages in primordial follicles and few at diplotene stages in follicles.1 Culture conditions further support normal dynamics of somatic cells. EdU labeling detects somatic cell divisions, and they are normally present around developing cysts. Ovaries from mutant fish can be cultured like those of wild-type, and side by side culture enables reliable examination of phenotypes as in vivo.

During and after culture, we visualize ovarian cells by live imaging to monitor development and viability, in confocal, spinning-disc, or fluorescent microscopy, by using vital dyes or transgenic ovaries expressing fluorescent reporters. At the end of culture, we fixed ovaries for immune-fluorescent labeling, fluorescent in-situ hybridization etc.,1,4 and quantitative confocal imaging.

Moreover, we used the ex vivo culture method to discover three previously unknown regulators of oogenesis, as well as the successive experimental investigation that deciphered novel mechanisms controlling oocyte polarity.1 These outputs highlight the effectiveness of the ex vivo system in uncovering new phenomena underlying early oogenesis which would have been very difficult to study in vivo.

Another major outcome is the ability to perform live time-lapse imaging for long periods. Live time-lapse imaging is extremely valuable for understanding cellular and developmental processes in ovaries in real-time.3,12 However, current settings for time-lapse imaging allowed recording for only several hours,2,3 limiting investigation of prolonged real-time processes and the type of experimental questions which can be addressed. Using this protocol we were able to conduct live time-lapse recording of ovaries for ∼60 hours, using spinning-disc confocal microscopy.7 This provides a major advance which enables monitoring more prolonged ovarian processes and expands the breadth of possible investigations.

Limitations

Careful ovary dissection is essential since mechanical stress during tissue isolation or mounting may compromise ovarian viability. Since the dissection and mounting steps are performed outside the hood, there is a risk of contamination which could result in poor ovary viability and/or artefacts. Contaminated samples must be discarded and one should always monitor viability in all experiments as we propose. Therefore, we recommend processing 2–4 ovaries per dish and limiting the number of dishes handled simultaneously to ensure careful manipulation and sterility.

While this protocol enables effective processivity for functional manipulations and imaging based analyses, the use of other methods which require large amounts of starting biological material per sample is more limited. For example, biochemical applications such as immune-precipitation experiments and western blot analyses often require substantial amounts of yielded protein levels which cannot be obtained from only few ovaries (e.g., 1–4 per dish). This issue can be extravagated in cases of mutations that that disrupt ovarian development and result in much smaller underdeveloped ovaries and therefore require more ovaries per sample. However, pooling of samples from multiple experiments is possible.

Finally, as with other ex vivo culture systems, it is possible that culture conditions may not absolutely and adequately mimic all physiological processes. Therefore, findings obtained using this protocol should be validated in vivo.
