Is surgery always warranted for high-grade acromioclavicular dislocations?
Authors: Omid Jamei-Martel, Riccardo Garibaldi, Carmen Castroviejo Fernández, Matthias Biner, Colin Bouduban, Beat Kaspar Moor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal management of high-grade acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocations remains controversial. While surgical treatment improves radiographic alignment, its impact on functional outcomes remains debated. This study prospectively evaluated clinical and radiographic outcomes following clinically guided treatment allocation in patients with Rockwood type III-V injuries.MethodsIn this prospective observational cohort study, 49 consecutive patients with Rockwood type III-V AC dislocations were managed according to predefined clinical criteria and shared decision-making. Surgical treatment was proposed in cases of non-reducible AC joint, persistent scapulothoracic dyskinesis, absence of early subjective symptomatic improvement, or cosmetic concerns. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Shoulder Subjective Value (SSV), Constant Score, and Acromioclavicular Joint Instability score. Standardized bilateral radiographs were analyzed at final follow-up. Regression analyses explored associations between radiographic parameters and clinical outcomes.ResultsTwenty-nine patients were initially managed conservatively and 20 surgically; 3 patients (10%) required delayed surgical reconstruction after failed conservative treatment. Baseline SSV did not differ significantly between groups (40 ± 16 vs. 35 ± 10; P = .26). At a median follow-up of 28 months, no significant difference was observed in SSV between groups (93 ± 7 nonoperative vs. 86 ± 16 operative; P = .30). The Constant Score was slightly higher in the nonoperative group (95 ± 6 vs. 91 ± 6; P < .05), without clinically meaningful difference. Acromioclavicular Joint Instability scores were higher in the surgical group (90 ± 19 vs. 58 ± 9; P < .001), reflecting improved radiographic alignment. Regression analyses demonstrated minimal correlation between radiographic parameters and functional outcomes. The surgical complication rate was 17%, with no revision procedures required.ConclusionIn this prospectively followed, clinically allocated cohort, surgical treatment improved radiographic alignment but did not demonstrate superior functional outcomes compared with conservative management. When guided by predefined clinical criteria, conservative treatment was associated with satisfactory results and a low crossover rate. These findings support individualized, clinically driven decision-making in high-grade AC dislocations.

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocations account for approximately 12% of all shoulder injuries12 and can lead to significant functional impairment, including scapulothoracic dyskinesis, which affects shoulder mechanics and glenohumeral function.3 Given their frequency and potential impact, their optimal management remains a subject of discussion.14 This debate regarding conservative vs. surgical management of AC dislocations has existed for decades,5 and although radiological classification has traditionally played a decisive role, multiple additional factors influence therapeutic choices.

Traditionally, AC joint dislocations have been managed based on the Rockwood classification. Types I and II are generally treated conservatively, while types IV to VI are considered surgical indications. Type III injuries remain in a gray area, with no clear consensus on the best approach, and are often initially treated conservatively, with surgery considered if symptoms persist.2 However, the integrity of the deltotrapezial fascia is essential for AC joint stability, and its reconstruction has been associated with improved functional outcomes.7 Moreover, the reliance on radiologic measurements as a practical tool may not precisely capture the extent of anatomical damage.20,29 In addition, the Rockwood classification, although widely used, does not account for horizontal instability, which can influence clinical outcomes.16,25 Consequently, beyond radiographic evaluation, assessing soft tissue integrity and biomechanical stability is crucial in determining the best treatment strategy, and individualized approaches incorporating functional assessments could optimize patient outcomes.

Other important factors influencing the outcome of conservative treatment include scapulothoracic dyskinesis, which can significantly impact shoulder function and recovery. Persistent dyskinesis may justify surgical intervention, whereas its absence can support a nonoperative approach.3 Evaluating scapular motion is therefore crucial in treatment planning.15 In addition, spontaneous improvement in AC joint positioning over time without progressive coracoclavicular (CC) widening supports the feasibility of conservative management in selected cases.10

Patient-specific factors, such as activity level and occupational demands, also influence treatment decisions. Contact athletes often prioritize a rapid return to play and may favor nonoperative management despite potential cosmetic deformities.6,17 Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that even high-grade dislocations (IV-VI), as classified radiologically, may not necessarily require surgical intervention. Indeed, certain type V injuries can achieve favorable outcomes with conservative treatment.11 Randomized trials comparing operative and nonoperative management for Rockwood type III to V AC dislocations have found no significant long-term differences in physical or mental health outcomes.18,22 Moreover, a review of 6 randomized controlled trials including 357 patients found that surgical intervention did not provide superior quality of life, shoulder function, or return to activities after 1 year, while being associated with a higher risk of complications, such as infections and hardware-related issues.30 Collectively, these findings challenge the traditional assumption that surgery is the preferred approach for high-grade AC dislocations and suggest that conservative management may be a viable alternative in many cases. In the present study, the term “high-grade” refers to injuries traditionally considered as potential surgical candidates (Rockwood type III-V), rather than to a specific vector of instability. Although type IV injuries primarily involve horizontal displacement, they are commonly included within this therapeutic spectrum in clinical practice and comparative studies.1,9

Therefore, the primary research question of this study was whether there is a difference in outcomes between conservative and surgical treatment of high-grade AC joint dislocations in terms of patient-reported outcome measures, clinical scores, and radiological results. If such differences were observed, we further sought to determine whether demographic, radiological, or lesion-specific factors could explain them. We hypothesized that there would be no significant difference in outcomes between surgically and conservatively treated patients, provided that the scapula was dynamically stable and the AC joint was passively reducible (ie, without interposition). If this hypothesis proved incorrect, we expected that radiological, demographic, or lesion-related characteristics would help predict outcome differences and thereby guide therapeutic decision-making.

Research question and working hypothesis

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate clinical and radiographic outcomes in patients with high-grade AC joint dislocations managed according to predefined clinical criteria in a pragmatic setting.

Specifically, we sought to determine whether, among patients without early clinical indicators mandating surgery (ie, persistent scapulothoracic dyskinesis, nonreducible AC joint, or lack of early symptomatic improvement), surgical management provided superior functional outcomes compared to conservative treatment.

A secondary objective was to explore whether demographic, radiological, or lesion-specific factors were associated with outcome differences and could help refine therapeutic decision-making.

We hypothesized that in patients presenting with dynamic scapular stability and a passively reducible AC joint, surgical treatment would not result in clinically meaningful superior functional outcomes compared to conservative management.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This prospective observational cohort study enrolled consecutive patients presenting for the first time to our shoulder consultation with AC joint dislocations classified as Rockwood type III to VI, regardless of the time elapsed since injury. Rockwood classification was established based on clinical examination and standardized radiographic assessment, including bilateral comparative Alexander views, with type IV cases identified by a clinically posteriorly displaced lateral clavicle confirmed on comparative Alexander views.

Rockwood type I and II injuries were not included, as conservative treatment is widely accepted for these types. Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) with no prior shoulder pathology or surgery on the affected side, no concomitant lower limb injury requiring assistive devices, and provided written informed consent.

Between January 2019 and December 2022, defined as the inclusion period, 56 consecutive patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 7 declined to participate and were excluded from the analysis, resulting in a final cohort of 49 enrolled patients. The study was designed to prospectively evaluate outcomes in consecutively treated patients rather than as a randomized comparison, as evidence regarding nonoperative management of high-grade AC dislocations remains heterogeneous and clinical equipoise persists. However, specific clinical features, such as joint reducibility and scapulothoracic dyskinesis, are considered to meaningfully influence individual treatment appropriateness in routine practice. In this context, strict randomization risked disregarding clinically relevant decision-making factors and was therefore considered ethically and pragmatically challenging; consequently, a prospective observational design incorporating predefined clinical criteria and shared decision-making was deliberately adopted.

During follow-up, treatment crossovers, i.e., patients who required conversion from conservative to surgical management, were recorded and included in the analysis.

Conservative treatment

Patients in the conservative group were allowed unrestricted shoulder mobilization and load-bearing according to pain tolerance. All patients were referred to physiotherapy, which focused on strengthening scapular stabilizers and improving coordination of the periscapular muscles. The rehabilitation program was tailored individually but systematically emphasized scapulothoracic control and neuromuscular retraining. Patients were re-evaluated in outpatient consultation at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months after initiating conservative treatment to monitor clinical progress and ensure patient satisfaction.

Surgical technique

Patients in the surgical group underwent arthroscopically assisted AC joint reconstruction using the BiPOD (bidirectional stabilization) technique, which restores both vertical and horizontal stability by reconstructing the CC ligaments and the AC capsule to optimize joint alignment and function.8 Horizontal stability of the AC joint was achieved using the AC limb of the BiPOD construct (Poly-Tape passed through the acromion and clavicle and tied beneath the clavicle). The AC capsule was then repaired and closed in layers together with the deltotrapezial fascia.

In one patient from the primary surgical cohort, concomitant procedures were performed, consisting of a long head of the biceps tenodesis and a supraspinatus tendon repair. All other patients underwent isolated AC joint reconstruction without additional glenohumeral procedures.

In chronic cases (defined as injuries presenting ≥3 weeks after trauma), the reconstruction was augmented using semitendinosus autografts.

All procedures were performed by 2 experienced surgeons who operated independently of each other; both used the same standardized technique. Post-operatively, patients were immobilized in a simple sling for comfort as needed. Active mobilization below the horizontal plane was permitted immediately during the first six weeks, followed by unrestricted range of motion without limitations during the subsequent six weeks. Muscle-strengthening exercises and load-bearing activities were introduced after three months, and the rehabilitation protocol was identical for patients who underwent primary reconstruction and those treated secondarily with tendon graft augmentation.

Data collection and clinical assessments

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from patient records with consent, and missing information was supplemented through direct patient interviews. At the last follow-up visit, which occurred at least 12 months after initiation of treatment (either surgical or conservative), all patients underwent a standardized evaluation including bilateral anteroposterior (AP) and Alexander view radiographs of the AC joints, as well as assessment of the Constant Score, Shoulder Subjective Value (SSV), and Acromioclavicular Joint Instability (ACJI) score, which is a joint-specific outcome measure ranging from 0 to 100 points (higher scores indicating better stability and function) and based on both clinical and radiographic parameters. All evaluations were performed by examiners who were independent from the treating team.

Radiographic analysis

Using the two-circle method,23 CC and AC distances were measured on AP views, with additional AC measurements on Alexander views, and all measurements were compared to the uninjured contralateral side. The presence of CC ligament calcifications was also assessed, and radiographic analyses were performed by 2 independent observers.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables, including age and radiographic measurements, were summarized as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables, such as sex, smoking status, and dominance of the injured shoulder, were reported as frequencies and percentages. For group comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables, and the Wilcoxon test was applied to compare radiographic measurements between injured and contralateral shoulders. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test and likelihood ratio.

Additional statistical analyses included logistic and linear regression models to assess potential predictors of clinical and radiographic outcomes. Logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between activity level and the probability of achieving a high Constant Score (> 85) or a high ACJI score, with results adjusted for robust standard errors. Linear regression models were employed to explore the influence of operative status and radiographic parameters (CC and AC distances) on the ACJI score, as well as the relationship between radiographic findings and the SSV. Statistical significance was set at P < .05, and all analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp Armonk, NY, USA).

A post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary functional outcome (Constant Score) to estimate the minimum detectable between-group difference. With 80% power and a two-sided α of 0.05, the study was able to detect a difference of approximately 5 points between groups. This analysis was exploratory and intended to contextualize the risk of type II error.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with applicable regulations. Patient inclusion started in January 2019 under our institution's general consent framework. During the study, the local ethics committee required a study-specific consent for this project; a dedicated protocol was therefore submitted and approved in January 2022. Participants included prior to this approval were subsequently recontacted and provided study-specific written informed consent a posteriori.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study included 49 patients, of whom 29 were initially managed conservatively and 20 were assigned to surgical treatment (Fig. 1). During follow-up, 3 patients in the conservative group (10%) required conversion to surgery.

At the time of initial presentation, the mean age of the cohort was 49 years (±15), with 47 (±15) years in the nonsurgical group and 51 (±14) years in the surgical group (P = .58). A total of 6% of patients were female and 94% were male, with a similar distribution between groups (P = .48). Regarding occupational demand, 45% of patients performed heavy labor, 43% had light work, and 12% were retired, with no significant difference between groups (P = .98). Twelve percent of patients were smokers, with 8% in the nonsurgical group and 17% in the surgical group (P = .30).

Concerning injury classification, the cohort was predominantly composed of Rockwood type V injuries (82%), with only 8% type III and 10% type IV cases. The distribution of injury types did not differ significantly between treatment groups (P = .56).

No type VI dislocations were observed in this cohort. The injury affected the dominant shoulder in 63% of cases, with 54% in the nonsurgical group and 74% in the surgical group (P = .15). Thirty-one percent of injuries resulted from falls from height, with no significant difference between groups (P = .50). At initial presentation, the mean baseline SSV was 40 (±16) in the nonsurgical group and 35 (±10) in the surgical group (P = .26).

The median time from injury to first consultation or surgical intervention was 13 days (interquartile range [IQR]: 7-19), with similar distributions between the nonsurgical (14 days, IQR: 7-23) and surgical groups (13 days, IQR: 9-18) (P = .99). Indications and timing of surgical reconstruction

Among the 23 patients who ultimately underwent surgical treatment, 20 (87%) received primary reconstructions at the time of initial presentation, while 3 (13%; representing the crossover group) underwent delayed procedures after an initial conservative approach that proved unsuccessful. These 3 delayed cases underwent reconstruction augmented with semitendinosus autografts. No other patients required graft augmentation.

Indications for surgery included isolated nonreducible AC joint in 6 patients (26%), isolated scapulothoracic dyskinesis in 1 (4%), and the combination of both findings in 3 (13%). Cosmetic concerns accounted for 5 patients (22%), and 3 patients (13%) chose surgery despite being eligible for conservative management. In 2 patients (9%), absence of subjective early symptomatic improvement within the first two weeks after injury led to primary surgical intervention at the initial treatment stage.

Regression analyses

Logistic and linear regression models were used to assess factors influencing clinical scores and radiographic outcomes. When the Constant Score was categorized (>85 vs. ≤85), logistic regression analysis showed a nonsignificant effect of activity level (coefficient = −0.86, P = .28; pseudo R2 = 0.03).

For ACJI, logistic regression using a cutoff of 75, based on previous studies,28 revealed a nonsignificant association with workload level (coefficient = 0.18, P = .21; pseudo R2 = 0.03). Linear regression analysis explored the influence of demographic and clinical factors on ACJI, showing no statistically significant effects for smoking status (coefficient = 7.4, P = .49), age (coefficient = −0.14, P = .56), follow-up duration (coefficient = 0.19, P = .36), or Rockwood classification (coefficient = −2.0, P = .73). The model had a low explanatory power (R2 = 0.05).

Similarly, regression analysis found no significant associations between SSV and radiographic variables, with an R2 of 0.02.

Radiographic analyses demonstrated a significant association between CC distance and ACJI: a decrease in CC distance (<6 mm) was correlated with a higher ACJI score (β = 24.3, P < .001). In addition, AC distance showed a strong negative correlation with ACJI when analyzed as a continuous variable. On the Alexander view, each millimeter increase in AC distance was associated with a decrease of 3.2 points in ACJI (P < .001, R2 = 0.27), while on the AP view, each millimeter increase corresponded to a decrease of 2.0 points (P < .001, R2 = 0.26).

To investigate potential temporal allocation bias, a logistic regression analysis was conducted using inclusion date as a predictor of treatment group assignment. This analysis revealed no statistically significant association (coefficient = −0.0002, P = .80, pseudo R2 = 0.001), suggesting that the time of enrollment did not systematically influence the decision between surgical and conservative management.

Complications

Post-operative complications were observed in 17% of the operated patients. A total of 4 complications were recorded, including 1 case of capsulitis (4%) and 3 cases of secondary displacement of the fixation construct (13%). None of these complications required revision surgery.

Discussion

AC joint injuries remain a subject of debate regarding their optimal management. This investigation was conducted as a prospective observational cohort reflecting routine clinical practice rather than as a randomized comparison of treatment modalities. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted in the context of pragmatically allocated care based on predefined clinical considerations and shared decision-making.

We therefore evaluated functional and radiological outcomes between surgically and nonsurgically treated patients while identifying potential factors influencing these results. This prospective cohort of Rockwood type III to V injuries provides insight into real-world treatment allocation and outcomes. As expected, no type VI dislocations were encountered. The prospective design allowed for contemporaneous treatment decisions and follow-up, allowing comparison of outcomes between conservative and surgical approaches following clinically guided treatment allocation.

Our findings suggest that while surgery improves radiographic alignment, it does not necessarily lead to superior functional results. Furthermore, the low crossover rate suggests that initial conservative management may be sufficient in a substantial proportion of patients selected according to these clinical criteria.

Analysis of patient characteristics revealed a statistically significant difference in follow-up duration between surgically and nonsurgically treated patients. The median follow-up was longer in the surgical group, likely reflecting the temporal distribution of cases across the inclusion period, which overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic. During the lockdown and its aftermath, elective surgeries were limited and occurred earlier in the recruitment timeline, whereas conservative cases accumulated later. Graphical analysis of monthly inclusions is consistent with this interpretation (Fig. 3). However, logistic regression using inclusion date as a predictor did not reveal a significant temporal allocation bias, indicating that the timing of enrollment did not systematically drive treatment selection. In terms of patient-reported outcomes, we did not observe a statistically significant difference in SSV between the 2 treatment groups. Interestingly, the absolute SSV values obtained in both our surgically and nonsurgically treated patients were within the same range as those reported in previously published cohorts of surgically treated patients.27,28 Although the Constant Score was statistically higher in the nonsurgical group, the difference was not clinically meaningful, and overall values remained high in both groups. Similarly, the absolute Constant Scores in both our treatment groups were comparable to those reported for surgically treated cohorts in prior studies evaluating post-operative functional recovery.13,19,21,24,26, 27, 28

Radiographically, surgical treatment resulted in better AC and CC alignment, as expected and as previously described in the literature. However, these radiographic improvements did not translate into superior functional outcomes. Our regression analyses demonstrated minimal correlations between radiographic parameters and functional scores, with radiologic measures explaining only a very small proportion of SSV variance. These observations reinforce the growing recognition that radiographic correction alone is not necessarily predictive of patient satisfaction or functional recovery.

The potential role of horizontal instability has been highlighted by several authors,16,19,20,31 who suggest that dynamic horizontal translation may be more clinically relevant than vertical displacement. In our study, horizontal instability was indirectly assessed using the Alexander view, yet no significant association was found between this measure and patient-reported outcomes. This finding supports the hypothesis that radiographic parameters—whether vertical or horizontal—may incompletely capture the factors that ultimately influence functional recovery.

The complication rate in the surgical group was 17%, consisting of adhesive capsulitis and secondary loss of fixation. This frequency is consistent with previously reported complication rates for AC joint reconstruction, which range from 5% to 30% depending on the technique used.4 None of the patients with secondary loss of fixation required revision surgery, and despite the radiographic failure, their functional outcomes—based on Constant Score and SSV—remained acceptable at final follow-up.

The crossover rate from initial conservative management to subsequent surgical treatment was 10% in our cohort, as 3 patients ultimately underwent operative reconstruction.

The first patient was a 40-year-old mechanic and smoker with a Rockwood type III dislocation on his dominant side. Despite appropriate conservative management, he continued to experience function-limiting pain and therefore underwent surgical treatment. Intraoperatively, he was found to have bicipital instability and a partial subscapularis tear, which were treated with tenodesis in addition to AC stabilization. He achieved excellent functional outcomes at 30-month follow-up.

The second patient was a 62-year-old nonsmoker working in the restaurant industry, who presented with a Rockwood type V dislocation. Due to persistent symptoms and difficulty returning to work, he underwent surgery three months later. Intraoperatively, he had a lesion of the bicipital pulley and a partial subscapularis tear. Functional and radiographic outcomes were good at 36-month follow-up.

The third patient was a 72-year-old retired woman who remained painful five months after conservative treatment for a Rockwood type V dislocation. Arthroscopy revealed a subtotal tear of the long head of the biceps tendon and a PASTA lesion (Partial Articular Supraspinatus Tendon Avulsion), treated with tenodesis and débridement. Her post-operative course was complicated by loss of fixation, which was not revised because she remained clinically well, with satisfactory function at last follow-up.

This 10% crossover rate is lower than the 16% reported by Murray et al22 in a randomized controlled trial of Rockwood type III-IV injuries, although differences in study design and inclusion criteria limit direct comparison.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the observational, nonrandomized design introduces the possibility of selection bias, and no a priori matching or sample size calculation was performed to ensure group comparability. Although randomization would have strengthened internal validity, the study was intentionally designed to reflect real-world, clinically guided decision-making in the setting of ongoing clinical equipoise regarding the management of high-grade AC dislocations. As treatment allocation was influenced by clinical presentation, unmeasured confounders may have affected the observed outcomes. However, baseline demographic and injury-related characteristics did not show statistically significant differences between groups. In addition, although the potential influence of concomitant glenohumeral pathology on surgical decision-making must be acknowledged, only 1 patient in the primary surgical cohort underwent additional glenohumeral procedures; this factor was therefore unlikely to have materially influenced treatment allocation or outcomes.

Baseline and immediate post-operative radiographs were obtained as part of routine care but were not subjected to predefined quantitative analysis, as radiographic assessment was prospectively standardized at final follow-up; therefore, early radiographic correction have not been formally compared.

Second, surgically treated patients were assessed at a later time point compared with those managed conservatively. This delay could have led to differences in reported outcomes, as patients further from their initial injury may experience changes in symptom perception, adaptation, or compensatory mechanisms.

Finally, the relatively small sample size limits the ability to detect subtle functional differences, and the possibility of type II error cannot be excluded. A post hoc power analysis indicated that the study was powered to detect a between-group difference of approximately 5 points on the Constant Score; smaller differences may therefore have gone undetected.

Although the cohort included Rockwood type III to V injuries, type V predominated, and the limited number of type III and IV cases might preclude meaningful grade-specific inference.

Conclusion

In this prospectively followed, clinically allocated cohort, surgical treatment resulted in improved radiographic alignment but did not demonstrate superior functional outcomes at final follow-up. Within the context of predefined clinical criteria guiding treatment selection, conservative management was associated with satisfactory outcomes in the majority of patients, with a low rate of secondary surgical intervention. In the crossover cases, associated long head of the biceps lesions were consistently identified, suggesting that concomitant glenohumeral pathology may contribute to persistent symptoms beyond the AC injury itself.
