Comment on “Conductivity of Concentrated Electrolytes”
Olga I. Vinogradova, Elena F. Silkina

Abstract




In their Letter
Avni et al. 2022
Avni
et al.
employed a stochastic density functional theory (SDFT)
Démery and Dean 2016

to derive an approximate formula that describes the conductivity
K
of 1:1 electrolytes of concentration
c_{\infty}
(with ions of a hydrodynamic radius
R
):

1-\frac{K}{K_{0}}\simeq\varrho e^{-a/\lambda_{D}}+\dfrac{1}{6}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+e^{-2a/\lambda_{D}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-\sqrt{2}a/\lambda_{D}}\right)\dfrac{\ell_{B}}{\lambda_{D}},

(1)

where
K_{0}
is the conductivity at an infinite dilution,
\lambda_{D}\simeq 0.305[\rm{nm}]/\sqrt{c_{\infty}[\rm{mol/l}]}
is the Debye length,
\varrho=R/\lambda_{D}
,
a=r_{+}+r_{-}
is the cut-off length defined as the sum of the cation and anion crystallographic radii, and
\ell_{B}
is the Bjerrum length.
These authors conclude that Eq. (
1
) significantly improves the Onsager formula
Onsager 1927
(reproduced when
a=0
),
and provides a reasonable fit to the conductivity data up to 3 mol/l.
Below we argue that Eq. (
1
) does not apply at molar concentrations.

Figure 1:
(a) Electrophoretic term calculated for
R=0.145
nm from Eqs. (
1
) with
a=0
, (
2
), and (
1
) with
a=0.3
nm (from top to bottom); (b) electrophoretic (solid curve) and relaxation (dotted curve) term and they sum (dash-dotted curve) calculated from (
1
).

The first term
in the r.h.s. of (
1
) is associated with an ionic electrophoresis under external field. Since the latter takes its origin in the electrostatic diffuse layer (EDL), on increasing
c_{\infty}
(reducing
\lambda_{D}
) its speed reduces. Consequently, the electrophoretic term should augment. However, in Eq. (
1
)
it exhibits a maximum at
\lambda_{D}=a
and turns to 0, when
\lambda_{D}\to 0
(see Fig.
1
(a)). This implies that the conductivity is the same as at an infinite dilution, which is counterintuitive. Since
a\simeq 0.3
nm
Avni et al. 2022
, the electrophoretic term in (
1
) becomes unphysical when
c_{\infty}\geq 1
mol/l. This term should provide
K/K_{0}\to 0
when
\lambda_{D}\to 0
and reduce to the Onsager electrophoretic term if
\varrho\ll 1
, and is given by
Vinogradova and Silkina 2023

1-\frac{K}{K_{0}}\simeq 1-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mathcal{F}}{1+\varrho},

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}=1-e^{\varrho}\left[5\Ei_{7}(\varrho)-2\Ei_{5}(\varrho)\right]

Henry 1931
.

The second term
in (
1
)
is the so-called relaxation, which is due to an emergence of an additional (retarding) field caused by a distortion of the EDL symmetry. The relaxation contribution (i) should vanish at
c_{\infty}\to 0
and at high salt
Overbeek 1950
: no EDL (of thickness
\lambda_{D}
) - nothing to distort; (ii) (as a second order effect) is always smaller than electrophoretic. However, this term in (
1
) also demonstrates incorrect trend as seen in Fig.
1
(b). Instead of having a maximum and then reducing with salt, its magnitude increases monotonically. When
a=O(\lambda_{D})
this term becomes of the same order as electrophoretic, and in more concentrated solutions even dominates. As a result, when the EDL nearly disappears its relaxation grows. Clearly, it cannot be so. Also included in Fig.
1
(b) are the electrophoretic contribution and the sum of both terms. It becomes evident that the relative success of (
1
) is simply due to a partial compensation of the incorrect behavior of separate contributions when they are summed up.

In summary, Eq. (
1
) by
Avni et al. 2022
derived by including a finite ion size (through
a
) into the SDFT improves the conductivity description compared to Onsager and extends the range up to
ca.
1 mol/l, but at larger concentrations leads to physically wrong predictions. Thus these authors overstated by concluding that their equation applies up to a few mol/l.
