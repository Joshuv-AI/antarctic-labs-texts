Factors that influence joy at work and job satisfaction for allied health professionals in public health services: a theory-informed qualitative study
Authors: Paula A Harding, Doug McCaskie, Emma Schneider, Hank Kim, Martin McCall-White, Cassie McDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA national shortage of allied health professionals, alongside rising demand for services, has heightened concerns about workforce wellbeing and its influence on retention. While factors such as joy in work and job satisfaction are known to affect workforce wellbeing, retention and attrition, they remain underexplored in allied health. This study examined positive and negative influences on joy in work and job satisfaction among allied health professionals in a public hospital.MethodsA theory-informed qualitative study was conducted at a major metropolitan Australian public health service that employs > 1000 allied health professionals across its campuses and services (January–May 2023). Allied health professionals across 10 disciplines and varying levels of seniority were invited to participate in focus groups to explore joy in work and job satisfaction; a purposive sampling approach ensured representation. Focus group questions drew on the Institute of Healthcare Improvement Joy in Work (IHI JiW) Framework and Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory. Transcripts underwent inductive and deductive qualitative content analysis, with themes compared against the two frameworks.ResultsFive focus groups (n = 25) captured perspectives from junior allied health professionals to managers with < 5 to > 10 years’ experience. Six themes shaped joy in work: (1) patient interactions and outcomes, (2) workload balance, (3) teamwork, (4) recognition, (5) time for clinical and administrative tasks, and (6) the physical environment. These aligned with six of the nine IHI JiW components. Analysis also revealed six factors enhancing job satisfaction and nine contributing to dissatisfaction, partially reflecting Herzberg’s Theory. Findings highlighted the interplay between extrinsic (e.g., workload, environment) and intrinsic (e.g., achievement, recognition) influences.ConclusionJoy in work and job satisfaction among allied health professionals in the public hospital setting are shaped by a complex interaction of positive and negative factors that contribute to worker wellbeing. Organisational strategies to enhance workforce wellbeing should adopt a multifaceted approach, addressing systemic and relational determinants to foster retention and reduce attrition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13975-0.Keywords: Allied health, Joy in work, Job satisfaction, Retention

Background

According to the World Health Organisation “there can be no health system without a health workforce…A motivated and competent health workforce in the right numbers and right places and with the right skill mix is central to providing people-centred, integrated care” [1]. Allied health professionals (AHPs) represent the second largest group of healthcare workers in Australia [2]. However, the allied health workforce is facing national shortages that are predicted to worsen, concurrent with a time when demand for allied health services is disproportionately increasing [3].

Allied health is a collective term for a range of health professionals and assistants, other than nurses, doctors and dentists, qualified to support and enable diagnosis of health conditions. Allied health professionals implement evidenced based interventions to maintain and optimise physical, social and mental wellbeing of the community [4]. Allied Health are integral to the delivery of this integrated care across the continuum of life and in a range of healthcare settings such as preventative, acute care, rehabilitation, community health [5, 6]. Despite their critical role, research indicates AHPs are leaving the workforce. Intention to leave rates for some professions such as occupational therapy range from 11 to 74% [7].

The struggle to recruit and retain highly skilled, engaged AHPs post the COVID-19 pandemic has been one of the key drivers behind the rising interest to understand more about the interacting relationships between healthcare worker wellbeing, attrition and retention rates [8, 9]. Professional (workforce) well-being can be defined as job-related and a function of being satisfied with one’s job, finding meaning in one’s work, feeling engaged while at work, having a high-quality working life, and finding professional fulfillment in one’s work [10, 11]. Addressing wellbeing can result in positive outcomes for staff [12] fostering long term productivity, retention of staff and improve treatment outcomes for patients [13, 14].

The limited research exploring retention of AHPs has reported primarily on job satisfaction and on negative factors, such as burnout and inadequate remuneration [10, 15, 16]. A systematic review by Baumgartner et al. (2024) utilised Herzberg’s two factor theory to synthesise results from 16 studies and found negative factors influencing job satisfaction were reported more frequently than positive factors [8]. Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory is a framework commonly used to describe factors impacting workforce retention [16, 17]. Herzberg’s Theory has been recognised since it was published in the 1950’s but recent studies have questioned its currency and applicability across different generations currently in the AHP workforce [18]. The Theory distinguishes between extrinsic hygiene factors (e.g. salary and working conditions) and intrinsic motivators (e.g. recognition, achievement, and the intrinsic value of the work itself). Herzberg suggests that while the absence of hygiene factors can cause dissatisfaction, their presence alone is insufficient for generating true job satisfaction or retention. Instead, motivators, aligned closely with concepts of joy and purpose, are critical to fostering long-term engagement and commitment. Emerging evidence suggests that these positive affective states are integral to employee engagement and professional workforce well-being [19]. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement Joy in Work (IHI JiW) Framework published in 2017, specifically focuses on improving staff well-being. The Framework was developed in response to an alarming number of health professionals experiencing burnout in the United States [20]. The nine core factors that contribute to joy in work are: wellness and resilience, real time management, daily improvement, camaraderie and teamwork, physical and psychological safety, meaning and purpose, choice and autonomy, recognition and awards, and participative management [20]. Implementation of the IHI JiW Framework provides a structured, participative process that healthcare organisations can use to understand the barriers to joy at work and then develop strategies to improve the well-being of healthcare workers.

Joy in work and job satisfaction are two domains of worker well-being, whilst interrelated, are conceptually distinct. The determinants that foster joy and job satisfaction among AHPs in Australian healthcare settings are complex and evidence exploring the relationship between these factors and retention is beginning to emerge [15]. Given that, the aim of this study was to explore factors influencing retention of AHPs at a major metropolitan health service in Australia guided by, and compared to, the IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg Two Factor Theory.

Methods

This study adopted an interpretivist (constructivist) qualitative methodological orientation [22], assuming multiple socially constructed realities and emphasising co-creation and meaning. The qualitative study was undertaken at a major, metropolitan tertiary health service in Melbourne, Australia. The health service employees > 1000 AHPs across its three main hospital campuses, a network of community programs and offers a range of specialist, state-wide services. Five focus groups were conducted using a semi-structured topic guide exploring the two main concepts of: joy in work and satisfaction in the workplace. Due to limitations with time and resources, testing of the interview guide with mock participants was not possible. Questions were developed to identify and explore the experiences of AHPs in the workplace as well as factors that contribute to their sustained employment. Questions were in two sections, developed by drawing from the IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg’s Theory of Job Satisfaction (refer to Additional File 1: Staff focus group topic guide). The IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg’s Theory were selected as both have been used in previous published studies involving health professionals and address the concepts of joy in work and job satisfaction separately [10, 19]. Herzberg’s theory, developed in the 1950’s, focuses on factors relating to job satisfaction which have predominantly been reported in the literature as negative factors [17]. In contrast, the IHI JiW Framework, developed in 2017, is a tool to understand the positive factors that influence joy in work [20]. The use of these frameworks together provide insight into both the negative and positive factors influencing two distinct components (job satisfaction and joy in work) of worker wellbeing and retention. In addition, the applicability for using the frameworks in the current healthcare environment to support strategies to improve retention of AHPs was explored by comparing findings with the constructs of these two frameworks.

Alfred Health Human Research Ethics Committee has granted approval for the research to be done and that the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki regarding human experimentation have been met (Project #443/22). This research was undertaken with appropriate informed consent of participants.

Participants

Allied health professionals from a multisite health service operationally reporting to the Director of Allied Health were invited via email to participate in the focus groups. Emails were sent via multiple distribution lists where staff may have be listed more than once. Approximately 400 AHPs were invited to participate. Participation was voluntary and focus groups were conducted online and in work hours between January 2023 to May 2023. Allied Health included staff from the following professions: Dietetics and Nutrition, Occupational Therapy, Orthotics, Orthoptics, Physiotherapy, Podiatry, Psychology, Social Work, Speech Pathology and Spiritual Care.

The organisation of participants into focus groups was informed by purposive sampling approaches with the aim of including diverse perspectives in each focus group from across the allied health team (e.g., by seniority, years of experience, and professions represented). Pragmatic considerations also informed participant selection (e.g., convenience of focus group location, date and time to reduce burden for participants, avoidance of dependent relationship like manager and direct reports being grouped together). Focus groups held via videoconferencing included 3–5 participants per session. The smaller group numbers in online focus groups were selected to optimise participation and group interaction. All participants who expressed interest in participating were provided with the time and date of a focus group to suit their availability. Two participants who consented to participating were unable to attend on the day due to unplanned leave.

Data collection

Focus groups was the method selected for data extraction to enable a deep exploration and discussion amongst participants of factors influencing job satisfaction and joy in work. This was also to complement results from a previously completed quantitative study that involved an online, anonymous workforce survey of the same group of AHPs [21]. The focus groups were facilitated by CM who is an experienced qualitative researcher employed into a senior allied health research role at the health service. CM did not have any AHPs who were direct reports at the time of facilitating the focus groups nor were they in a dependent relationship with any of the participants.

CM did have collegial relationships with the focus group managers but not the majority of participants. To manage this, CM used a pre-determined script at the start of each focus group to remind all participants of her role and responsibilities as focus group facilitator, the processes of confidentiality that governed the study as per the ethics-approved protocol, and options for accessing support should any participants experience distress after taking part and who felt uncomfortable raising this with her.

Semi-structured interviews were recorded in Microsoft Teams and sent for transcription by an external provider. Information recorded in the chat function was downloaded and included for analysis. Transcripts were deidentified and pseudonyms applied prior to analysis. The duration of the focus groups averaged 78.2 min (ranging from 63 to 85 min). The number of focus groups conducted were based on number of participants who expressed interest and not determined by data saturation as the thematic analysis occurred post completion of the five focus groups.

Analysis

Data were analysed deductively informed by the IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg Theory. The collated data were analysed following the steps of qualitative content analysis by the lead author (PH) and a second author (DM) separately [22]. Qualitative content analysis was used to systematically identify concepts contained within the long transcripts to gain insight into the meaning and context of job satisfaction and joy in work. Steps taken were to become (a) familiar with the data by reviewing the transcripts multiple times, (b) dividing up the text into meaning units and subsequently condensing these meaning units, (c) formulating codes, and (d) developing categories and descriptive themes. A summary of the thematic analysis was circulated to participants for comments via email prior to finalising themes however no feedback was received. A final step involved comparing the findings with the IHI JiW Framework (Table 1) and Herzberg’s Theory (Table 2).

Results

Twenty-five AHPs attended five focus groups. Of the participants, 20 (80%) were female and the most represented professions were Physiotherapy (10, 40%) and Occupational Therapy (5, 20%). There were a mix of positions and seniority represented with managers (28%), senior clinicians (classified as “Grade 4” in the state in which this research was conducted) (28%) and junior clinicians (classified as “Grade 1”) (24%) most represented (Table 3).

The results are presented in two sections according to the emerging themes of joy in work and job satisfaction and contrasted against the IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg Theory respectively. Representative quotes are included to support the results. Participant pseudonyms were removed to reduce the risk of identification.

Section 1: joy in work

Six major themes emerged from the focus group analysis when the concept of joy in work was explored.

Patient interaction and outcomes

Patient interaction and outcomes

A key theme central to joy in work for participants was the ability to make a meaningful difference to patients and the privilege of interacting with patients daily. It captured the purpose of why participants chose to work in public health and included the moments of compassion and kindness experienced during their interactions.

“For me, it’s the mix of having an impact on patients and really trying to improve their life so that they can contribute in a way that’s meaningful for them in the community”. (participant, focus group #3)

2. Workload: demand = supply

Workload: demand = supply

Participants described a good day that brought them joy as one where there were sufficient staffing levels to meet the workload demands. This included having adequate leave cover when staff were absent (planned or unplanned) and a manageable volume of appropriate referrals. Having an assigned workload that was achievable, yet challenging, meaningful, and interesting was integral to joy in work along with having autonomy and control over their workload.

“The work being meaningful really resonates for me” (participant, focus group #1).

These first two themes aligned strongly with JiW Framework components of ‘Meaning and Purpose’, referring to the need for daily work connected to a purpose larger than themselves that services the community and makes a difference. This also aligns with the component of ‘Autonomy and Choice’ which empowers staff to be part of the decision making in the way things are done in their daily work.

3. Time to do… staff and work-related tasks

Time to do… staff and work-related tasks

Time pressures and feeling able to complete the required mix of work tasks as well as look after one’s wellbeing at work was a major theme that influenced joy in work, which builds on theme two (workload: demand = supply) above. Firstly, the time to do the work that needed to be done, including an appropriate balance of clinical, non-clinical and strategic quality work. Secondly, the time to support one’s own needs whether it be finding time to take a break, to learn something new and to support the needs of others, such as supervising junior clinicians and students on clinical placements. Whilst the concept of experiencing time pressure and workload demands were not identified in the JiW framework, having time to put work ‘on hold’ for a break or having the time to support others was important to participants and aligned with the ‘Wellness and Resilience’ component of the JiW Framework that refers to an organisation demonstrating that it values the health and wellness of its employees.

“When you know everyone gets to take their breaks and leave on time, I think it’s a great day too.” (participant, focus group #1)

4. Teamwork

Teamwork

The collaboration and cohesiveness of a supportive multidisciplinary team who communicated effectively with a shared understanding of roles and responsibilities was important for a good day at work. Consistent with the JiW component of ‘Camaraderie and Teamwork’, participants enjoyed problem solving together and working closely with each other to achieve common objectives.

“What makes for a good day at work for me is respectful and collaborative discussions amongst the multidisciplinary team.” (participant, focus group #3)

5. Recognition of a job well done

Recognition of a job well done

Recognition was a major theme and extended from recognising the work done at the end of a busy day to recognising the work done by others and the ability to celebrate small wins and big achievements with their teams. Internally, self-reflecting on one’s own achievements of a job well done would also lead to a good day. Participants’ responses focused on recognition rather than the reward component described in the IHI JiW Framework.

“What makes for a good day is one that’s actually acknowledged, and people are complimented just as much as when things are in chaos.” (participant, focus group #3)

6. The workplace environment matters

The workplace environment matters

Joy in work could be impacted by the environment in which staff worked, both the physical facilities and social environment. A social environment where there was fun, friendships, kindness and the ability to find connections with their colleagues beyond the professional day-to-day work requirements made work enjoyable. Both these factors aligned closely with the IHI Joy at Work component of ‘Physical and Psychological Safety’.

“Lots of natural light, beautiful facilities, lots of equipment that we can use for patients, you just feel so much happier, and the patients feel better as well.” (participant, focus group #5)

The emerging themes that contribute to joy in work strongly aligned with six of the nine components identified in the IHI JiW framework (Table 4). ’Real time measurement’ and ‘Daily improvement’ didn’t feature strongly in our responses to what makes a good day. Real time measurement refers to employing knowledge of improvement science, a critical eye to recognise opportunities to improve. Daily improvement refers to visual measures that are tracked with actions to escalate as required. The other characteristic weakly aligned was ‘Participative Management’ with participants referring to their team around them being integral to bringing joy rather than the role of management.

Discussion

This study examined factors influencing AHPs’ joy at work and job satisfaction using the IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg’s Job Satisfaction Theory. Findings can inform the development of targeted interventions that aim to enhance joy, satisfaction, and ultimately wellbeing leading to workforce retention in public health services. While many themes aligned with the Framework and Theory, not all did. For instance, real-time measurement and daily improvement did not contribute to joy, and the work environment and teamwork acted as motivators rather than hygiene factors when examining job satisfaction. These findings suggest that the IHI JiW Framework and Herzberg’s Theory are useful for examining joy in work and satisfaction in the healthcare workforce, however, some additional factors appear to be important in this setting.

The conceptual map exploring joy in work (Fig. 1) illustrates how factors such as patient interaction, teamwork, recognition, the physical facilities and social environment collectively shaped joy in work for our participants. Some of these factors could be influenced and changed (potentially quite rapidly) by managers and leaders. For example, managers tasked with allocating workloads could prioritise seeking regular feedback on the impact of work volume, case mix and complexity, in combination with the appropriate staffing and skill mix. This needs to be balanced with AHPs being granted agency and autonomy over their workload, and ideally, choice in the clinical setting in which they work. Promisingly, such strategies have reported to reduce levels of burnout in medical professionals [23, 24]. Other factors may be more difficult to change in the short term. For example, the physical facilities available to staff may not be readily changed without capital investment by an organisation, which can be challenging in fiscally constrained public healthcare settings. However, seeking staff input for creative ideas to optimise space (e.g. hot desking, removing outdated equipment and going ‘paperless’) and to modernise the work environment (e.g. welcoming Indigenous artwork or a fresh coat of paint on the walls) may create a more positive, inclusive work experience. This codesign approach utilises the participative management component of the JiW Framework to foster a collaborative and team-based environment.

In our study, participants found both joy and satisfaction from experiences that they perceived had a meaningful impact on patient outcomes. For example, participants highlighted that a moment of feeling like they had made a difference to a patient might be all it would take to feel as though they were having a good day. Similarly, studies involving the nursing workforce have reported significant joy was derived when nurses experienced the positive impact of their work on patient outcomes [25]. Whist interacting and developing connections with patients were considered a positive experience from our findings, dealing with the emotional demands of patient care can also lead to compassion fatigue, emotional exhaustion or burnout when workloads are heavy or resources insufficient [26]. This highlights the interaction of the multiple themes that impact a good day at work such as having adequate staffing that matches workload demands in addition to attending to non-clinical duties or providing supervision to junior AHPs or students on clinical placements. An opportunity for improving AHP workloads in public health services could be through introducing clinical care ratios. Such ratios for allied health have been proposed as a useful tool in workload management and determining appropriate staffing levels [27]). Unlike nursing in Australia which has pre-determined clinical care ratios [28] AHPs do not have widely used/accepted clinical care ratios. Given that workload pressures were identified as a negative factor detracting from joy in work and job satisfaction in our study, implementing clinical care ratios in the future may be an avenue to addressing this issue and reducing staff burnout.

Interestingly, measuring daily improvement to quantify the impact of AHP care on patient outcomes is one of the components of the JiW Framework that did not feature in our findings. Embedding the use of patient reported outcome measures and key metrics to measure and showcase the impact of allied health care can be challenging given the barriers created by multilevel determinants and diversity of the allied health collective workforce [29]. Instead, individualised goal setting with the AHP for performance and professional development, often determined at annual performance reviews and referred to during regular clinical supervision sessions or mentoring, may be a more achievable and effective way for AHPs to measure their direct impact and progress. These reflective sessions also facilitate an opportunity for internal and external recognition when goals are achieved [30]. Herzberg’s Theory considers access to quality supervision as a hygiene factor that when absent leads to job dissatisfaction, which was consistent the experience of our participants. The implementation of resources and toolkits such as the Victorian Allied Health Clinical Supervision Framework into clinical practice could be utilised to improve job satisfaction [31]. Working towards and achieving personal goals has been positively related to work engagement in nursing [32] highlighting the importance of AHPs being supported to have adequate nonclinical time to regularly participate in individualised clinical supervision and performance development. Formal programs supporting new allied health graduates are commonplace in many health services [33] along with organisational led emerging leader programs available to senior staff. Only 4% of participants in our study were Grade 2 AHPs, mid-career AHPs with typically 5years + experience, which coincidently is often the time when some AHPs consider leaving their profession. Greater engagement with Grade 2 AHP’s to understand the factors impacting their job satisfaction and requirements to support their professional growth are required.

Consistent with the JiW Framework, the importance of teamwork and collaboration in fostering joy emerged as a strong theme. Teamwork was also a factor positively influencing job satisfaction, which contrasted with Herzberg’s Theory that describes the hygiene factor of ‘interpersonal relationships’, as the closest factor aligning to teamwork. Our findings are consistent with a systematic review exploring the impact of huddles on a multidisciplinary healthcare team which demonstrated effective teamwork improved staff engagement and job satisfaction [34]. A recent study by Jordan et al. [19] evaluated the impact of a well-being initiative in healthcare workers which included strategies tailored to each team participating in the initiative. Strategies included pre-shift huddles, introduction of Schwartz rounds to discuss patient care issues, and increased opportunities for teams to connect and communicate; all of which appeared to improve at least one outcome for participating teams by improving joy at work or reducing burnout [19]. The implementable and tailorable strategies used in this study show promise and could be trialled with AHPs in public healthcare settings to foster teamwork and collaboration.

Time management was another key theme, with participants emphasizing the importance of having enough time to complete their clinical, administrative, and support tasks, as well as time for breaks and self-care. The challenge of managing time is not unique to AHPs and has also been reported by doctors [35]. Strategies to improve time management for AHPs are often overlooked in undergraduate training and ongoing professional development for staff. Time management has been shown to enhance wellbeing and typically includes the three aspects of structuring, protecting and adapting time to changing conditions [36]. Education and training to develop strategies to improve time management may be a practical tool incorporated into professional development to further enhance joy in work. ‘Bottom-up’ strategies such as education and training for staff could be complemented by ‘top-down’ strategies: manager-led processes that ensure adequate breaks and support staff to leave on time may also be important for reducing burnout and improving joy at work [19].

Consistent with Herzberg’s hygiene factor of ‘salary’, recognition via inadequate remuneration featured as a source of job dissatisfaction for our participants. In Australia, salaries of AHPs vary significantly between states and territories, and in some states public sector wages are higher than the private sector. This study was conducted in a public hospital in the state of Victoria where salaries are comparatively much lower than other states like Queensland. The higher costs of living and accumulated student debts acquired from obtaining professional degrees, including post graduate qualifications, and perceived lack of adequate remuneration are potentially influencing AHPs to pursue other careers. Lack of recognition was cited as the top reason for leaving their profession by participants under 35 years in an Australian workforce survey of 20,449 registered AHPs [37].

The themes we identified from exploring the concepts of joy in work and job satisfaction were interrelated. Whilst our themes aligned to some of the factors from Herzberg’s Theory, factors that our participants perceived as ‘intrinsic’ versus ‘extrinsic’ differed in some instances. For example, Herzberg’s Theory described the working environment and interpersonal relationships to be extrinsic factors whereas our participants described these factors to be intrinsic motivators for them that were driving job satisfaction. Similarly, intrinsic factors such as recognition, achievement and advancement, when absent, led to job dissatisfaction for our participants. Herzberg’s Theory suggests that managers should focus on both hygiene factors and motivators to create a positive and motivating work environment. Simply addressing hygiene factors will not necessarily lead to increased motivation, and focusing solely on motivators without ensuring adequate hygiene factors can lead to dissatisfaction [38]. Our findings did not support factors aligning simply as either motivators or hygiene factors but in some examples, factors could impact both job satisfaction and dissatisfaction. Other studies have also disputed Herzberg’s Theory characterisation of intrinsic and extrinsic factors and their influence on job (dis-)satisfaction. For example, a literature review called for the theory to be revised based on evidence across a range of countries and industries that indicated extrinsic factors impacted job satisfaction [39]. Future studies could consider using more contemporary, alternative models such as the Job Characteristics Model [40] or Self Determination Theory [41] to further understand factors influencing job satisfaction.

Practical implications

This study highlights a range of modifiable factors that managers and healthcare leaders could address to facilitate joy in work and promote job satisfaction for AHPs. Practical measures including using workload management tools (i.e., clinical care ratios, prioritising time for professional supervision to review personal performance development goals and building time management skills) could all be integrated into practice in public healthcare settings. Awareness of creating pleasant and functional work environments to enable AHPs to work and take breaks in comfortable spaces should be prioritised. Structuring wellbeing initiatives for staff based on the JiW Framework may be useful given close alignment of our findings to seven of the nine key components underpinning the framework and a recent study in another Australian health service which showed promise when using this approach [19].

Limitations

This study was conducted at one large, multi-hospital, metropolitan public health service so may not be representative of other health care settings such as regional and rural settings; however, participants were from a range of professions and roles and data were analysed to represent allied health as a collective workforce. Generalisation of the findings may be limited for allied health professions represented in small numbers or not represented at all, males and mid-career AHPs (i.e., in the Victorian public health services classified as Grade 2’s).

Conclusion

The findings of this study contribute to the growing body of research on joy in work and job satisfaction and highlights the complex interplay of positive and negative factors from the perspective of AHPs. These insights underscore the need for healthcare organisations to adopt a multifaceted approach to promoting joy in work and job satisfaction, addressing both structural and relational factors. Future research could further explore the nuances of these factors and their impact on well-being, as well as investigate how individual coping strategies may mitigate the negative effects of workload and time pressures.
