Abstract
. Utilizing the framework of quaternionic contact geometry, we define a sequence of Riemannian metrics { g L } \{g_{L}\} on the quaternionic Heisenberg group ℌ ℍ \mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}} by rescaling the vertical directions. By analyzing the limit of this sequence, we characterize the Carnot-Carathéodory geodesics and provide the explicit description of the Carnot-Carathéodory distance and spheres in ℌ ℍ \mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}} . Furthermore, we derive a general formula for the horizontal mean curvature of hypersurfaces.

Primary 53C17; Secondary 53C42, 53C26.

1.
Introduction

The complete non-compact symmetric spaces of rank one consist of the real, complex, and quaternionic hyperbolic spaces, alongside the Cayley hyperbolic plane. The boundary of each space plays a key role in the geometry and analytic structure. While the boundary of real hyperbolic space carries a conformal structure, the boundary of complex hyperbolic space is endowed with a spherical CR structure. In contrast, the boundary of quaternionic hyperbolic space is characterized by a quaternionic contact (QC) structure.

Specifically, the boundaries of the complex and quaternionic hyperbolic spaces are identified with the one-point compactifications of the Heisenberg group and the quaternionic Heisenberg group, respectively. Both are Carnot groups and fundamental examples of 2-step nilpotent Lie groups, yet they differ fundamentally in their algebraic and geometric dimensions: the center of the standard Heisenberg group is 1-dimensional, while that of the quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
is 3-dimensional. While the geometry and analysis of the Heisenberg group have been extensively documented (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
7
,
11
]
), the quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
remains less explored. This gap is largely due to the algebraic complexity arising from the non-commutativity of the quaternions, which significantly complicates the study of its sub-Riemannian structure and QC structures.

In this paper, we focus on the 7-dimensional quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
, modeled on the manifold
\mathbb{H}\times\text{Im}(\mathbb{H})
. Our work aims to extend foundational results from the standard Heisenberg group to this quaternionic setting.

First, we address the sub-Riemannian geometry of the group. A Carnot-Carathéodory (CC) metric
d_{cc}
can be defined on any Carnot group, see
[
3
,
10
]
. We explicitly describe the structure of horizontal curves joining arbitrary points in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
in Section
3
.

A central theme of this work is the Riemannian approximation of the CC-metric, see Section
4
. We fix a left-invariant metric
g_{cc}
on the horizontal bundle and extend it to the entire tangent bundle. For a triple of positive scaling parameters
L=(L_{1},L_{2},L_{3})
, we define a family of Riemannian metrics
g_{L}
by rescaling the vertical directions. The CC-geodesics in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
are then characterized as the limits of the
g_{L}
-geodesics as the parameters
L_{a}\,(a=1,2,3)
tend to infinity (see Section
5.2
). This follows from
[
5
]
.
In
[
8
]
, Kim and Parker established a comparison between the Korányi distance and the CC-distance by extending the results obtained for the standard Heisenberg group by Basmajian and Miner in
[
2
]
. In the present work, we provide an alternative comparison theorem in Section
5.3
, utilizing a distinct approach based on our Riemannian approximation framework.
We also clarify its structure and compute its horizontal mean curvature (see Section
6
), providing a foundational tool for addressing the isoperimetric problem in the quaternionic Heisenberg group. Furthermore, we identify two explicit examples of horizontal minimal surfaces, providing rare concrete cases in a non-commutative sub-Riemannian environment.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides background on quaternionic contact and CR structures. In Section
3
, we construct explicit horizontal paths. Section
4
develops the Riemannian approximations
g_{L}
, including computations of Ricci and scalar curvatures. Section
5
analyzes
g_{L}
-geodesics and the CC-geodesics. Finally, Section
6
investigates the horizontal mean curvature of hypersurfaces and presents the new horizontal minimal surfaces.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Quaternions

We denote quaternions by

a+b{\bf i}+c{\bf j}+d{\bf k},

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R};\,{\bf i},\,{\bf j},
and
{\bf k}
are the basis vectors satisfying

{\bf i}^{2}={\bf j}^{2}={\bf k}^{2}={\bf i}{\bf j}{\bf k}=-1.

The algebra of quaternions is denoted by
\mathbb{H}
; it is associative and non-commutative. The imaginary quaternions are elements of the set
\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H}=\rm{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{{\bf i},{\bf j},{\bf k}\}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}.
Let
q=x_{1}+x_{2}{\bf i}+x_{3}{\bf j}+x_{4}{\bf k}\in\mathbb{H}
; then its conjugate
\bar{q}
, its imaginary part
\textrm{Im}(q)
and its modulus
|q|
are respectively given by

\displaystyle\bar{q}=x_{1}-x_{2}{\bf i}-x_{3}{\bf j}-x_{4}{\bf k},

\displaystyle\textrm{Im}q=x_{2}{\bf i}+x_{3}{\bf j}+x_{4}{\bf k},

\displaystyle|q|^{2}=h\bar{h}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}.

The exponential of a non-zero imaginary quaternion is give by

{\rm exp}\,q={\rm exp}\,(x_{1}+{\bf v})=e^{x_{1}}\left(\cos|{\bf v}|+\frac{{\bf v}}{|{\bf v}|}\sin{|{\bf v}|}\right).

2.2.
Quaternionic CR and contact structures

Suppose that
M
is a
(4n+3)
-dimensional manifold. Let
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
be an
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued 1-form defined on
M
with codimension
3
distribution
\mathcal{H}={\rm Ker}\,\eta={\rm Ker}\,\eta_{1}\cap{\rm Ker}\,\eta_{2}\cap{\rm Ker}\,\eta_{3}.

Suppose that
J_{a}
,
a=1,2,3,
are the complex structures on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying the unit quaternionic relations:
J_{1}^{2}=J_{2}^{2}=J_{3}^{2}=J_{1}J_{2}J_{3}=-{\rm id}
.

We consider a triple of linearly independent 1-forms
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
; associated to them are six 2-forms
\rho_{\sigma}
,
\sigma=(a,b,c)\in S_{3}
, defined by

(2.1)

\rho_{\sigma}=d\eta_{a}+2\eta_{b}\wedge\eta_{c}.

Here,
S_{3}
is the 3-permutation group. We fix this notation throughout the paper.

Definition 2.1
.

A triple of linearly independent 1-forms
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3})
is called a
quaternionic CR (qCR) structure
if the associated 2-forms given by (
2.1
) satisfy the following:

(1)

they are non-degenerate on the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
and have the same 3-dimensional kernel;

(2)

for
\sigma=(a,b,c)\in S_{3}
, the endomorphisms
J_{a}
of the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
defined by

J_{a}=(\rho_{c}|_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\cdot\rho_{b}|_{\mathcal{H}}
,
a=1,2,3
,
satisfy the unit quaternionic relations.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\sigma=(a,b,c)\in S_{3}
. The three 2-forms
d\eta_{a}
of the associated forms
\rho_{\sigma}
are called
fundamental
if there exists a positive definite symmetric tensor
g
defined on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

d\eta_{a}(X,Y)=g(J_{a}X,Y),\,a=1,2,3,

for any vector fields
X,Y
in
\mathcal{H}
. We then call
\{\eta,\{J_{a}\}_{a=1}^{3},g\}
a
quaternionic contact (QC) structure on
M
with horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
equipped with an
CSp(n)Sp(1)
structure.

Here, note that the 1-form
\eta
is given only up to the action of
SO(3)
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and to a conformal factor, thus one can get a
CSp(n)Sp(1)
-structure on
\mathcal{H}.

2.3.
The quaternionic Heisenberg group and its QC structure

The quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
can be identified with the set
(\mathbb{H},\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H})
with the group law

(2.2)

(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})\ast(q,t)=(q^{\prime}+q,t^{\prime}+t+2\textrm{Im}(\overline{q}\cdot q^{\prime})),

where
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H},

t,\,t^{\prime}\in\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H}
and
\overline{q^{\prime}}
is the quaternionic conjugate. We have the following automorphisms of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:

(1)

Left translations
\tau_{(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})}:

\tau_{(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})}(q,t)=(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})\ast(q,t);

(2)

Rotations
U:

U(q,t)=(qU,t),\quad{\rm for}\quad U\in{\rm Sp(1)},

where
{\rm Sp(1)}
is the set of unit quaternions.

(3)

{\rm Sp(1)}
acts as:

\sigma(q,t)=(\sigma q,\sigma t\sigma^{-1}),\quad{\rm for}\quad\sigma\in{\rm Sp(1)},

where
\sigma
acts on the second factor as isomorphism with
{\rm SO(3)}.

(4)

The inversion
I:

I(q,t)=\left(-(|q|^{2}-t)^{-1}q,\frac{-t}{|q|^{4}+|t|^{2}}\right);

(5)

Real dilations
D_{\delta}:

D_{\delta}(q,t)=(\delta q,\delta^{2}t),\quad{\rm where}\quad\delta>0.

The left translations (type (1)), quaternionic Heisenberg rotations (types (2) and (3)) and real dilations (type (5)) generate the
quaternionic Heisenberg similarity group

{\rm Sp}(1)\times{\rm SO(3)}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\rtimes\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}.

The vector fields

\displaystyle\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}+2x_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}+2x_{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}}+2x_{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{3}}},

\displaystyle\xi_{2}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}-2x_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}-2x_{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}}+2x_{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{3}}},

\displaystyle\xi_{3}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}+2x_{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}-2x_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}}-2x_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{3}}},

\displaystyle\xi_{4}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{4}}-2x_{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}+2x_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{2}}-2x_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{3}}},

as well as the vector fields
T_{i}=\partial/\partial t_{i}
are left-invariant and form a basis for the Lie algebra.
We have the following bracket relations:

\displaystyle[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]=[\xi_{3},\xi_{4}]=-4T_{1},

\displaystyle[\xi_{1},\xi_{3}]=[\xi_{4},\xi_{2}]=-4T_{2},

\displaystyle[\xi_{1},\xi_{4}]=[\xi_{2},\xi_{3}]=-4T_{3}.

We now describe in brief the QC structure of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
: let
q=x_{1}+x_{2}{\bf i}+x_{3}{\bf j}+x_{4}{\bf k}
and
t=t_{1}{\bf i}+t_{2}{\bf j}+t_{3}{\bf k}.
The standard
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued contact form of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
is given by

(2.3)

\omega=dt-q\cdot d\bar{q}+dq\cdot\bar{q}=\theta_{1}\,{\bf i}+\theta_{2}\,{\bf j}+\theta_{3}\,{\bf k},

where

(2.4)

\displaystyle\theta_{1}

\displaystyle=dt_{1}-2x_{2}dx_{1}+2x_{1}dx_{2}+2x_{3}dx_{4}-2x_{4}dx_{3},

\displaystyle\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=dt_{2}-2x_{3}dx_{1}+2x_{1}dx_{3}+2x_{4}dx_{2}-2x_{2}dx_{4},

\displaystyle\theta_{3}

\displaystyle=dt_{3}-2x_{4}dx_{1}+2x_{1}dx_{4}+2x_{2}dx_{3}-2x_{3}dx_{2}.

Note that
\{dx_{1},dx_{2},dx_{3},dx_{4},\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}\}
is the dual basis of
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}\}
.
Now, for
\sigma=(a,b,c)\in S_{3}
,

\ker\theta_{a}={\rm Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4},T_{b},T_{c}\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\ker\,\theta_{1}\cap{\ker}\,\theta_{2}\cap{\ker}\,\theta_{3},
which is a codimension
3
distribution spanned by
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4}.

A metric
g_{cc}
is defined on the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
by

g_{cc}(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})=\delta_{ij}
,
j=1,\dots,4.

This is the
Carnot-Carathéodory metric
defined on
\mathcal{H}
. Explicitly,

g_{cc}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}dx_{i}^{2}.

Remark 2.3
.

Automorphisms (1)–(4) above preserve the metric
g_{cc}
and they are called
quaternionic Heisenberg isometries
. Dilations given by
(5)
scale
g_{cc}
up to the dilation factor.

The three almost complex structures

\{J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}\}
on
\mathcal{H}

are given by

d\theta_{a}(X,Y)=d\theta_{b}(J_{c}X,Y),

for all
\sigma=(a,b,c)\in S_{3}
and for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{H}.

Direct computations yield that

\displaystyle J_{1}\xi_{1}=\xi_{2},J_{1}\xi_{2}=-\xi_{1},J_{1}\xi_{3}=\xi_{4},J_{1}\xi_{4}=-\xi_{3},

\displaystyle J_{2}\xi_{1}=\xi_{3},J_{2}\xi_{3}=-\xi_{1},J_{2}\xi_{4}=\xi_{2},J_{2}\xi_{2}=-\xi_{4},

\displaystyle J_{3}\xi_{1}=\xi_{4},J_{3}\xi_{4}=-\xi_{1},J_{3}\xi_{2}=\xi_{3},J_{3}\xi_{3}=-\xi_{2}.

One verifies directly that

J_{1}^{2}=J_{2}^{2}=J_{3}^{2}=-{\rm Id},\quad J_{a}J_{b}=-J_{b}J_{a}=J_{c}

and that for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{H},
we have:

d\theta_{a}(X,Y)=2\,g_{cc}(J_{a}X,Y)=d\theta_{a}(J_{a}X,J_{a}Y),\quad a=1,2,3.

Let
T_{a}={\partial}/{\partial t_{a}},
then
T_{a}
is the Reeb vector field of
\theta_{a},

a=1,2,3.

Now, we see that
(\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}};\omega,\{J_{a}\}^{3}_{a=1},g_{cc})

is a QC-manifold with distribution
\mathcal{H}.
By Definition
2.1
,
\{\omega,J_{1},\,J_{2},\,J_{3}\}
is a qCR structure on
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
. Further details on the quaternionic Heisenberg group and its fundamental structure can be found in
[
8
,
14
]
.

3.
Horizontal curves in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}

Suppose that a curve
\gamma(\lambda):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
is defined by

\gamma(\lambda)=(\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\lambda)).

The curve
\gamma(\lambda)
is called
horizontal
curve if and only if
\omega(\dot{\gamma}(\lambda))=0.

Lemma 3.1
.

An horizontal curve remains horizontal under left translations as well as under dilations.

One can check that
\omega(\dot{\tau}(\gamma))=0
and
\omega(\dot{D}_{\delta}(\gamma))=0
by a straightforward computation.

The existence of horizontal paths between any two points in Carnot groups (like the quaternionic Heisenberg group) is a consequence of Chow-Rashevskii’s theorem, see
[
6
,
13
]
. However, explicit constructions in the quaternionic Heisenberg group are not commonly available in the literature. We include the construction here for clarity, completeness and potential use in applications. We start with the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

There are horizontal curves joining the origin with the point
(0,t)\in(\mathbb{H},{\emph{\textrm{Im}}}\mathbb{H}).

Proof.

Let
k_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{*}
,
i=1,2,3,4
.
We first consider the curve
\gamma_{1}
given by
\gamma_{1}(\lambda)=(k_{1}\lambda,0)
,
\lambda\in[0,1]
. We have that
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(\lambda)=k_{1}\xi_{1}(\gamma_{1}(\lambda))
and this curve joins the origin with the point
p_{1}=(k_{1},0)
. We now consider the horizontal curve
\gamma_{2}
given by

\gamma_{2}(\lambda)=\left(k_{1}+k_{2}\lambda\,{\bf i},\,-2k_{1}k_{2}\lambda\,{\bf i}\right),\quad\lambda\in[0,1].

We have that
\dot{\gamma}_{2}(\lambda)=k_{2}\xi_{2}(\gamma_{1}(\lambda))
and
\gamma_{2}
joins
p_{1}
and the point
p_{2}=\left(k_{1}+k_{2}{\bf i},\,-2k_{1}k_{2}{\bf i}\right)
.
Similarly, starting from
p_{2}
, we move in the direction of
k_{3}\xi_{3}
along the curve
\gamma_{3}
given by

\gamma_{3}(\lambda)=\left(k_{1}+k_{2}{\bf i}+k_{3}\lambda\,{\bf j},\,-2k_{1}k_{2}{\bf i}-2(k_{1}k_{3}{\bf j}+k_{2}k_{3}{\bf k})\lambda\right),\quad\lambda\in[0,1],

which joins
p_{2}
and
p_{3}=\left(k_{1}+k_{2}{\bf i}+k_{3}{\bf j},\ -2(k_{1}k_{2}{\bf i}+k_{1}k_{3}{\bf j}+k_{2}k_{3}{\bf k})\right)
. Continuing in this fashion, from
p_{3}
, we move in the direction of the vector field
k_{4}\xi_{4}
along the curve
\gamma_{4}
given for each
\lambda\in[0,1]
by

\gamma_{4}(\lambda)=\left(k_{1}+k_{2}{\bf i}+k_{3}{\bf j}+k_{4}\lambda\,{\bf k},\ -2\left((k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}\lambda){\bf i}+(k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}\lambda){\bf j}+(k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{4}\lambda){\bf k}\right)\right);

this curve joins
p_{3}
and the point

p_{4}=\left(k_{1}+k_{2}{\bf i}+k_{3}{\bf j}+k_{4}{\bf k},\ -2\left((k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}){\bf i}+(k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}){\bf j}+(k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{4}){\bf k}\right)\right).

In a similar manner:

•

From
p_{4}
, we travel in the direction of
-k_{1}\xi_{1}
, reaching the point

p_{5}=\left(k_{2}{\bf i}+k_{3}{\bf j}+k_{4}{\bf k},\ -2\left((2k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}){\bf i}+(2k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}){\bf j}+(k_{2}k_{3}+2k_{1}k_{4}){\bf k}\right)\right);

•

from
p_{5}
, we follow
-k_{2}\xi_{2}
, arriving at

p_{6}=\left(k_{3}{\bf j}+k_{4}{\bf k},\ -2\left((2k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}){\bf i}+(2k_{1}k_{3}-2k_{2}k_{4}){\bf j}+(2k_{2}k_{3}+2k_{1}k_{4}){\bf k}\right)\right);

•

from
p_{6}
we proceed along
-k_{3}\xi_{3}
, reaching

p_{7}=\left(k_{4}{\bf k},\ -4\left((k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}){\bf i}+(k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}){\bf j}+(k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{4}){\bf k}\right)\right)

and finally,

•

from
p_{7}
, we move in the direction of
-k_{4}\xi_{4}
, reaching the point

p_{8}=\left(0,\ -4\left((k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}){\bf i}+(k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}){\bf j}+(k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{4}){\bf k}\right)\right).

Thus, the horizontal path comprising the union of all horizontal paths above joins the origin and the point whose vertical component depends linearly on the quantities
k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}
,
k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}
, and
k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{4}
. By choosing suitable values
for the coefficients
k_{i}
, we claim that there always exists a family of horizontal curves connecting the origin to the point
(0,t)
. Actually, if
t=(-4t_{1},-4t_{2},-4t_{3}),
we must solve

\displaystyle k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{1},

\displaystyle k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{2},

\displaystyle k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{3}.

Let
t_{2}+it_{3}=Re^{i\phi}.
Suppose that
z_{1}=k_{1}+k_{2}i,
and
z_{2}=k_{3}+k_{4}i
. Using the second and third equations above, we note that

z_{1}z_{2}=(k_{1}k_{3}-k_{2}k_{4})+i(k_{1}k_{4}+k_{2}k_{3})=t_{2}+it_{3}.

When
R\neq 0,
by setting
z_{1}=r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}
and
z_{2}=r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}
, we derive that
r_{1}r_{2}=R,
and
\phi_{2}=\phi-\phi_{1}.

The equation
k_{1}k_{2}+k_{3}k_{4}=t_{1}
can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{2}r_{1}^{2}\sin(2\phi_{1})+\frac{1}{2}r_{2}^{2}\sin(2\phi_{2})=t_{1}.

Substituting the expressions for
r_{2}
and
\phi_{2}
:

r_{1}^{2}\sin(2\phi_{1})+\frac{R^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}}\sin(2\phi-2\phi_{1})=2t_{1}.

By letting
X=r_{1}^{2}>0,
we get that

(\sin 2\phi_{1})X^{2}-(2t_{1})X+R^{2}\sin(2\phi-2\phi_{1})=0.

A solution exists if there exists a
\phi_{1}\in[0,2\pi)
such that the quadratic has at least one positive real root. By the intermediate value theorem, we can select
\phi_{1}
such that the coefficients
\sin(2\phi)
and
R^{2}\sin(2\phi-2\phi_{1})
have opposite signs. In such a case, the product of the roots is negative, ensuring the existence of one positive real root for
X
.
The case where
R=0
follows trivially by setting
k_{1}=k_{2}=0
, yielding
k_{3}k_{4}=t_{1}
, which is solvable for all
t_{1}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

For any two points in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}},
there always exists a horizontal path joining them.

Proof.

Given two points
P=(q,t)
and
P^{\prime}=(q^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

we define the projection
\pi:\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{H}
by
\pi(q,t)=q.

First, consider an absolutely continuous curve
\alpha(\lambda):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
satisfying
\alpha(0)=\pi(P)
and
\alpha(1)=\pi(P^{\prime}).
Then we lift the curve
\alpha(\lambda)
to
a horizontal curve
\gamma(\lambda):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
such that

\pi\circ\gamma(\lambda)=\alpha(\lambda),\quad\gamma(0)=P.

We claim that such a curve does exist. Indeed, we only need to define
\beta(\lambda):[0,1]\to\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H}
as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(\lambda)=t_{1}+2\int_{0}^{\lambda}(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}^{\prime}-2\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}^{\prime}-2\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}^{\prime}+2\alpha_{4}\alpha_{3}^{\prime})(s)ds

\displaystyle\beta_{2}(\lambda)=t_{2}+2\int_{0}^{\lambda}(\alpha_{3}\alpha_{1}^{\prime}-2\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}^{\prime}-2\alpha_{4}\alpha_{2}^{\prime}+2\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}^{\prime})(s)ds,

\displaystyle\beta_{3}(\lambda)=t_{3}+2\int_{0}^{\lambda}(\alpha_{4}\alpha_{1}^{\prime}-2\alpha_{1}\alpha_{4}^{\prime}-2\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}^{\prime}+2\alpha_{3}\alpha_{2}^{\prime})(s)ds,

where
(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
are the vertical components of the initial point
P.

We have thus constructed a horizontal curve
\gamma(\lambda)
joining the point
P
to a point
R
with coordinates

(\alpha(1),\beta(1))=(q^{\prime},\ \beta(1)).

Assume that

(-q^{\prime},-t^{\prime})\ast(q^{\prime},\ \beta(1))=(0,a)\in(\mathbb{H},\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H}).

By Lemma
3.2
, there exists a horizontal curve joining the point
(0,a)
and the origin.
Then, applying Lemma
3.1
, we conclude that there exists a horizontal curve connecting the point
R
to the point
P^{\prime}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

The
horizontal length
of a curve
\gamma
is defined by

l_{cc}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|(\pi\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(\lambda)\right|\,d\lambda,

where
\pi
denotes the projection onto the horizontal component.

The
Carnot-Carathéodory distance
between two points
P
and
P^{\prime}
in the quaternionic Heisenberg group is then given by

d_{cc}(P,P^{\prime})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\{l(\gamma)\},

where
\Gamma
is the set comprising all horizontal curves joining
P
and
P^{\prime}.

4.
Riemannian approximation

Given
L_{a}>0
,
a=1,2,3,
we define a Riemannian metric
g_{L}
in for any vector fields
X,Y,Z
of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
such that the frame

\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4},(1/L_{1})T_{1},(1/L_{2})T_{2},(1/L_{3})T_{3}\}

is orthonormal. The corresponding co-frame is

\{dx_{1},dx_{2},dx_{3},dx_{4},L_{1}\theta_{1},L_{2}\theta_{2},L_{3}\theta_{3}\}.

Let
T_{a}^{\prime}=({1}/{L_{a}})T_{a}
and
\theta_{a}^{\prime}=L_{a}\theta_{a},

a=1,2,3.

Then
g_{L}
can be written as

g_{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}dx_{i}^{2}+{\sum_{a=1}^{3}\theta_{a}^{\prime 2}}.

Observe that the restriction of
g_{L}
in
\mathcal{H}
is
g_{cc}
.

Recall Koszul’s formula for the Riemannian connection
\nabla
of
g_{L}
:

g_{L}(\nabla_{X}Y,Z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{L}([X,Y],Z)-g_{L}([X,Z],Y)-g_{L}([Y,Z],X)\right),

for any vector fields
X,Y,Z
of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
We calculate straightforwardly:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{1}}\xi_{2}=\nabla_{\xi_{3}}\xi_{4}=-\nabla_{\xi_{2}}\xi_{1}=-\nabla_{\xi_{4}}\xi_{3}=-2L_{1}T_{1}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{1}}\xi_{3}=\nabla_{\xi_{4}}\xi_{2}=-\nabla_{\xi_{3}}\xi_{1}=-\nabla_{\xi_{2}}\xi_{4}=-2L_{2}T_{2}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{1}}\xi_{4}=\nabla_{\xi_{2}}\xi_{3}=-\nabla_{\xi_{4}}\xi_{1}=-\nabla_{\xi_{3}}\xi_{2}=-2L_{3}T_{3}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\nabla_{T_{1}^{\prime}}\xi_{1}=2L_{1}\xi_{2},\quad\nabla_{T_{1}^{\prime}}\xi_{2}=-2L_{1}\xi_{1},\quad\nabla_{T_{1}^{\prime}}\xi_{3}=2L_{1}\xi_{4},\quad\nabla_{T_{1}^{\prime}}\xi_{4}=-2L_{1}\xi_{3},

\displaystyle\nabla_{T_{2}^{\prime}}\xi_{1}=2L_{2}\xi_{3},\quad\nabla_{T_{2}^{\prime}}\xi_{2}=-2L_{2}\xi_{4},\quad\nabla_{T_{2}^{\prime}}\xi_{3}=-2L_{2}\xi_{1},\quad\nabla_{T_{2}^{\prime}}\xi_{4}=2L_{2}\xi_{2},

\displaystyle\nabla_{T_{3}^{\prime}}\xi_{1}=2L_{3}\xi_{4},\quad\nabla_{T_{3}^{\prime}}\xi_{2}=2L_{3}\xi_{3},\quad\nabla_{T_{3}^{\prime}}\xi_{3}=-2L_{3}\xi_{2},\quad\nabla_{T_{3}^{\prime}}\xi_{4}=-2L_{3}\xi_{1},

and
\nabla_{\xi_{i}}\xi_{i}=0,\,\nabla_{T_{a}^{\prime}}T_{a}^{\prime}=0,\,\nabla_{\xi_{i}}T_{a}^{\prime}=\nabla_{T_{a}^{\prime}}\xi_{i},
for any
i=1,2,3,4,\,a=1,2,3.

Recall also that the curvature
R
is defined by

(4.1)

R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z+\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,

for any vector fields
X,Y,Z
of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Again, direct computations yield that

\displaystyle R(\xi_{1},\xi_{j})\xi_{1}=-12L_{j-1}^{2}\xi_{j}\quad(j=2,3,4),

\displaystyle R(\xi_{3},\xi_{4})\xi_{3}=-12L_{1}^{2}\xi_{4},\,\,R(\xi_{2},\xi_{4})\xi_{2}=-12L_{2}^{2}\xi_{4},\,\,R(\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\xi_{2}=-12L_{3}^{2}\xi_{3},

\displaystyle R(\xi_{i},T_{a}^{\prime})\xi_{i}=4L_{a}^{2}T_{a}^{\prime}\quad(i=1,2,3,4,\,{\rm and}\,\,a=1,2,3).

We have the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

The sectional curvatures of the planes spanned by pairs of vector fields in
\{\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3},\xi_{4},T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime},T_{3}^{\prime}\}
, are given respectively by

\displaystyle K(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=K(\xi_{3},\xi_{4})=-12L_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle K(\xi_{1},\xi_{3})=K(\xi_{2},\xi_{4})=-12L_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle K(\xi_{1},\xi_{4})=K(\xi_{2},\xi_{3})=-12L_{3}^{2},

\displaystyle K(\xi_{i},T_{a}^{\prime})=4L_{a}^{2}.

Proof.

Applying the formula
K(U,V)=g_{L}(R(U,V)U,V)
for sectional curvature of planes spanned by unit vectors
U
and
V
, the proof is straightforward.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

The Ricci curvatures in each direction are, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(\xi_{i})\;=\;-\frac{4}{3}(L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2}+L_{3}^{2}),\quad

\displaystyle i=1,2,3,4,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}(T_{a}^{\prime})\;=\;2L_{a}^{2},\quad

\displaystyle a=1,2,3.

Moreover the scalar curvature is

K=-\frac{10}{21}(L_{1}^{2}+L_{2}^{2}+L_{3}^{2}).

5.
Geodesics

5.1.
Geodesics of the metric
g_{L}

There exists an extensive study of geodesics and their properties in the Heisenberg group; for further reference, see
[
2
,
9
,
11
,
12
]
.
In this section we explicitly calculate the
g_{L}
-geodesics. In the next section
5.2
the Carnot-Carathéodory geodesics will appear by letting
L_{a}\to\infty\,(a=1,2,3).

Let
p
and
q
be two arbitrary points and let also
\gamma(\lambda)=(\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\lambda)):[0,1]\to\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
be a curve joining them. Then the
g_{L}
-length of
\gamma
is

S_{\gamma}=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{4}(\dot{\alpha}_{i}(\lambda))^{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{3}L_{a}^{2}|\theta_{a}(\dot{\gamma}(\lambda))|_{\gamma(\lambda)}|^{2}}d\lambda.

We aim to find the minimizing curve among all the curves joining
p
and
q.
We focus on the energy functional

E(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}(\dot{\alpha}_{i}(\lambda))^{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{3}L_{a}^{2}\left|\theta_{a}(\dot{\gamma}(\lambda))|_{\gamma(\lambda)}\right|^{2}

and we shall make use of the Euler–Lagrange equations to find the length minimizing curve joining
p
and
q
.

First, we claim that

(5.1)

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(\theta_{a}(\dot{\gamma}(\lambda))|_{\gamma(\lambda)}\right)=0,\quad a=1,2,3.

In fact, along the curve we compute

\frac{\partial E}{\partial{\dot{\beta}_{a}}}=2L_{a}^{2}\theta_{a},\quad\frac{\partial E}{\partial\beta_{a}}=0.

The equality
\frac{d}{d\lambda}E_{\dot{\beta}_{a}}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial\beta_{a}}
implies that
2L_{a}^{2}\dot{\theta}_{a}=0,
which proves (
5.1
).
Secondly, we also have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\dot{\alpha}_{1}}

\displaystyle=2\dot{\alpha}_{1}-4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\alpha_{2}-4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\alpha_{3}-4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\alpha_{4},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\dot{\alpha}_{2}}

\displaystyle=2\dot{\alpha}_{2}+4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\alpha_{1}+4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\alpha_{4}-4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\alpha_{3},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\dot{\alpha}_{3}}

\displaystyle=2\dot{\alpha}_{3}-4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\alpha_{4}+4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\alpha_{1}+4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\alpha_{2},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\dot{\alpha}_{4}}

\displaystyle=2\dot{\alpha}_{4}+4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\alpha_{3}-4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\alpha_{2}+4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\alpha_{1}.

On the other hand, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial{\alpha_{1}}}

\displaystyle=4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\dot{\alpha}_{2}+4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\dot{\alpha}_{3}+4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\dot{\alpha}_{4},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial{\alpha_{2}}}

\displaystyle=-4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\dot{\alpha}_{1}-4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\dot{\alpha}_{4}+4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\dot{\alpha}_{3},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial{\alpha_{3}}}

\displaystyle=4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\dot{\alpha}_{4}-4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\dot{\alpha}_{1}-4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\dot{\alpha}_{2},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial{\alpha_{4}}}

\displaystyle=-4L_{1}^{2}\theta_{1}\dot{\alpha}_{3}+4L_{2}^{2}\theta_{2}\dot{\alpha}_{2}-4L_{3}^{2}\theta_{3}\dot{\alpha}_{1}.

Due to (
5.1
) we may suppose that

(5.2)

\theta_{a}(\dot{\gamma}(\lambda))|_{\gamma(\lambda)}=\frac{C_{L}^{a}}{4L_{a}^{2}},

where
C_{L}^{a}
are arbitrary real constants. From the Euler-Lagrange equations
\frac{d}{d\lambda}E_{\dot{\alpha}_{i}}=\frac{\partial E}{\partial\alpha_{i}}
as well as from equations (
5.2
) it now follows that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ddot{\alpha}_{1}&=C_{L}^{1}\dot{\alpha}_{2}+C_{L}^{2}\dot{\alpha}_{3}+C_{L}^{3}\dot{\alpha}_{4},\\
\ddot{\alpha}_{2}&=-C_{L}^{1}\dot{\alpha}_{1}-C_{L}^{2}\dot{\alpha}_{4}+C_{L}^{3}\dot{\alpha}_{3},\\
\ddot{\alpha}_{3}&=C_{L}^{1}\dot{\alpha}_{4}-C_{L}^{2}\dot{\alpha}_{1}-C_{L}^{3}\dot{\alpha}_{2},\\
\ddot{\alpha}_{4}&=-C_{L}^{1}\dot{\alpha}_{3}+C_{L}^{2}\dot{\alpha}_{2}-C_{L}^{3}\dot{\alpha}_{1}.\end{split}

Integrating those equations, we obtain:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\alpha}_{1}&=C_{L}^{1}\alpha_{2}+C_{L}^{2}\alpha_{3}+C_{L}^{3}\alpha_{4}+c_{1},\\
\dot{\alpha}_{2}&=-C_{L}^{1}\alpha_{1}-C_{L}^{2}\alpha_{4}+C_{L}^{3}\alpha_{3}+c_{2},\\
\dot{\alpha}_{3}&=C_{L}^{1}\alpha_{4}-C_{L}^{2}\alpha_{1}-C_{L}^{3}\alpha_{2}+c_{3},\\
\dot{\alpha}_{4}&=-C_{L}^{1}\alpha_{3}+C_{L}^{2}\alpha_{2}-C_{L}^{3}\alpha_{1}+c_{4},\end{split}

where
c_{i}
are arbitrary constants,
i=1,2,3,4.
The
g_{L}
-geodesics are the solutions of the system given by
(
5.2
) and (
5.4
).
Let
C_{L}=-C_{L}^{1}{\bf i}-C_{L}^{2}{\bf j}-C_{L}^{3}{\bf k}.

Since the Riemannian approximants are left-invariant metrics, it is enough to give the explicit solutions of
g_{L}
-geodesics
\gamma(\lambda)
such that
\gamma(0)=O
and
\gamma(1)=(q,t).
We distinguish two cases.

(I) If
C_{L}=0,

we have

\begin{cases}\alpha(\lambda)=q\,\lambda,\\
\beta(\lambda)=0,\end{cases}

for some
q\in\mathbb{H}.

(II) If
C_{L}\neq 0,
we may write (
5.3
) as

\ddot{\alpha}(\lambda)=C_{L}\dot{\alpha}(\lambda),

which is a quaternionic-valued ordinary differential equation. The general solution for
\dot{\alpha}(\lambda)
is given by

\dot{\alpha}(\lambda)={\rm exp}(C_{L}\lambda)C,

where
C=c_{1}+c_{2}{\bf i}+c_{3}{\bf j}+c_{4}{\bf k}=\dot{\alpha}(0)
is a constant quaternion (cf.
[
4
]
).
Since
C_{L}
is a pure quaternion, it satisfies
-C_{L}^{2}=|C_{L}|^{2}.
Integrating the expression for
\dot{\alpha}(\lambda)
, we obtain

\alpha(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{C_{L}}(1-{\rm exp}({C_{L}\lambda}))C.

The squared modulus
|\alpha(\lambda)|
satisfies

|\alpha(\lambda)|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_{i}^{2}=\left|\frac{C}{C_{L}}\right|^{2}(2-2\cos(|C_{L}|\lambda)).

Substituting (
5.4
) into (
5.2
), we obtain the following system for the vertical derivatives:

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{L}^{1}}{4L_{1}^{2}}+2C_{L}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2(c_{1}\alpha_{2}-c_{2}\alpha_{1}+c_{3}\alpha_{4}-c_{4}\alpha_{3}),

(5.5)

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{L}^{2}}{4L_{2}^{2}}+2C_{L}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2(c_{1}\alpha_{3}-c_{2}\alpha_{4}-c_{3}\alpha_{1}+c_{4}\alpha_{2}),

\displaystyle\dot{\beta}_{3}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{L}^{3}}{4L_{3}^{2}}+2C_{L}^{3}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\alpha_{i}^{2}+2(c_{1}\alpha_{4}+c_{2}\alpha_{3}-c_{3}\alpha_{2}-c_{4}\alpha_{1}).

Integrating these equations with respect to
\lambda
, we obtain special solutions for the vertical components
\beta(\lambda)=(\beta_{1}(\lambda),\beta_{2}(\lambda),\beta_{3}(\lambda)),
given by

(5.6)

\beta_{a}(\lambda)=\left(\frac{\lambda}{4L_{a}^{2}}+\frac{4|C|^{2}}{|C_{L}|^{3}}f(|C_{L}|\lambda)\right)C_{L}^{a},\quad a=1,2,3,

where
f(\lambda)=\lambda-\sin\lambda.

In the symmetric case where
L_{1}=L_{2}=L_{3}=L
, the Riemannian geodesics admit an explicit representation, as summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

The
g_{L}
-geodesic
\gamma(\lambda)=(\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\lambda))
joining the origin and the point
(q,t)
satisfies the following equations:

(5.7)

\begin{cases}\alpha(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{{C}_{L}}\left(1-e^{C_{L}\lambda}\right)C,\\
\beta(\lambda)=-\left(\frac{\lambda}{4L^{2}}+\frac{4|C|^{2}}{|C_{L}|^{3}}f(|C_{L}|\lambda)\right)C_{L}.\end{cases}

Here,
C\in\mathbb{H}
and
C_{L}
is a pure quaternion.
The behavior of these geodesics depends on the initial conditions as follows:

(i)

Horizontal Case: If
t=0
, the geodesic is a horizontal straight line of the form
\gamma(\lambda)=(q\lambda,0)
. The geodesic length is given by
S_{\gamma}=|q|
.

(ii)

Vertical Case: If
q=0
, the geodesic is a vertical segment of the form
\gamma(\lambda)=(0,t\lambda)
. The geodesic length is
S_{\gamma}=L|t|
.

(iii)

General Case: If
q\neq 0
and
t\neq 0
, the geodesic is given by (
5.7
), where the parameter
C_{L}
is determined by the relation:

\frac{t+\frac{C_{L}}{4L^{2}}}{|q|^{2}}=\frac{-2C_{L}f(|C_{L}|)}{|C_{L}|f^{\prime}(|C_{L}|)}.

Furthermore, the constant
C
is uniquely determined by
C_{L}
. Specifically, by setting
\lambda=1
, we have

|C|=\frac{|q||C_{L}|}{2\sin(|C_{L}|/2)}.

In this case, the length of the geodesic arc is
\frac{1}{4L}\sqrt{16L^{2}|C|^{2}+|C_{L}|^{2}}.

5.2.
Carnot-Carathéodory geodesics

By taking the limit
L\to\infty
in (
5.7
), we obtain the unit speed Carnot-Carathéodory geodesics
\gamma(\lambda)=\alpha(\lambda),\,\beta(\lambda))
as follows:

(5.8)

\begin{cases}\alpha(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{A}(1-{\rm exp}({A\lambda}))B,\\
\beta(\lambda)=-\frac{4}{|A|^{3}}f(|A|\lambda)A,\end{cases}

where
A\in\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H},

B\in\mathbb{H}
with
|B|=1.

Here, the parameter
\lambda
is exactly the Carnot-Carathéodory distance,
d_{cc}(O,\gamma(\lambda)),
from the origin
O
to the point
\gamma(\lambda)=(\alpha(\lambda),\beta(\lambda)).

Furthermore, the CC-sphere
S_{R}
of radius
R
centered at the origin
O
is defined as

S_{R}=\{(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:d_{cc}(O,(q,t))=R\}.

Lemma 5.2
.

The CC-sphere
S_{R}
is the set of points given by

(5.9)

\left(-\frac{1}{A}(1-{\rm exp}(AR))B,-\frac{4}{|A|^{3}}f(|A|R)A\right),

where
A\in\textrm{Im}\,\mathbb{H},

B\in\mathbb{H}
with
|B|=1
and
f
is the real function with formula
f(\lambda)=\lambda-\sin\lambda
.

5.3.
Korányi metric vs. CC-metric

The
Korányi metric

d_{K}
is another fundamental metric on the quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}.
It is defined by the left-invariant Korányi gauge
\|\cdot\|_{K}
:

d_{K}((q,t),(q^{\prime},t^{\prime}))=\|(q,t)^{-1}\ast(q^{\prime},t^{\prime})\|_{K},

where the Korányi gauge
\|(q,t)\|_{K}=||q|^{2}+t|^{1/2}.

A direct calculation shows that the Korányi distance from the origin
O
to the points on the CC-sphere
S_{R}
defined by (
5.9
) is

d_{K}(O,(q,t))=\|(q,t)\|_{K}=\frac{\left(4(1-\cos(|A|R))^{2}+16f^{2}(|A|R)\right)^{1/4}}{|A|}.

The following
5.3
comprises the relationship between CC and Korányi distances. This is comparable to Theorem 2.2 in
[
8
]
, where the authors studied related problems by extending the results of
[
2
]
. Nevertheless, the framework considered here differs from that of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Consider the origin
O
and the point
(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}.

(i)

If
t\neq 0,
then

d_{cc}(O,(q,t))=\left(\frac{x_{0}^{4}}{4(1-\cos x_{0})^{2}+16(x_{0}-\sin x_{0})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}d_{K}(O,(q,t)),

where
x_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
is the unique solution to the equation

\frac{1-\cos x_{0}}{2(x_{0}-\sin x_{0})}=\frac{|q|^{2}}{|t|}.

(ii)

If
t=0
, then

d_{cc}(O,(q,t))=|q|=d_{K}(O,(q,t)).

Since any two points in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
can be mapped, via a quaternionic Heisenberg similarity, to the origin and a point
(q,t),
we obtain the following conclusion.

Corollary 5.4
.

The Carnot-Carathéodory metric and the Korányi metric on the quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
are quasi-isometric.

6.
Horizontal Mean Curvature of a Hypersurface

In this section, we extend the study of the horizontal geometry of hypersurfaces in the Heisenberg group, as presented in
[
5
]
, to the setting of the quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
.
We begin by defining horizontal mean curvature for regular hypersurfaces of the quaternionic Heisenberg group. After proving a general statement about a class of rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces, we compute the horizontal mean curvature for three fundamental classes of spheres in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
, that is, the Euclidean sphere, the Korányi sphere, and the Carnot-Carathéodory sphere.

Let
x=(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}},
where
q=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{H}
and
t=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\textrm{Im}\mathbb{H}.

We consider a
C^{2}
regular hypersurface
S
defined as

S=\{x\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:u(x)=0\}.

The geometry of
S
is governed by its horizontal distribution. The
horizontal gradient of
u
is given by

\nabla_{0}u=\sum_{i=1}^{4}(\xi_{i}u)\,\xi_{i},

and the
non-characteristic locus
of
S
is the set of points where the horizontal gradient does not vanish, that is, it is the complement of the set

\mathcal{C}(S)=\{x\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}\,|\,|\nabla_{0}u(x)|=0\}.

For the remainder of this section, we let the indices
i
and
j
range over
\{1,2,3,4\}
and
\{1,2,3\}
respectively.
Recall the
L
-Riemannian metric
g_{L}
on
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
:

g_{L}=\sum_{i}dx_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j}L_{j}^{2}\theta_{j}^{2}.

Let
a_{i}=\xi_{i}u
and
b_{j}=T_{j}^{\prime}u
denote the derivatives of
u
with respect to the horizontal vector fields
\{\xi_{i}\}
and the vertical vector fields
\{T_{j}^{\prime}\}
.
We define the norms of the horizontal and
L
-Riemannian gradients, respectively, as

A=|\nabla_{0}u|=\sqrt{\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{L}=\sqrt{\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j}b_{j}^{2}}.

The
L
-Riemannian unit normal vector field to the hypersurface
S
is given by

{\bf n}_{L}=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{L}\xi_{i}+\sum_{j}b_{j}^{L}T_{j}^{\prime},

where
a_{i}^{L}=a_{i}/A_{L}
and
b_{j}^{L}=b_{j}/A_{L}
.
When
L_{j}\to\infty,
then
a_{i}^{L}\to a_{i}/A,
while
b_{j}^{L}\to 0.

In this way, the
L
-Riemannian normal
{\bf n}_{L}
converges to the
horizontal normal vector field

{\bf n}_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\xi_{i}u}{|\nabla_{0}u|}\xi_{i},

which is the projection of
{\bf n}_{L}
onto the horizontal subbundle
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 6.1
.

The
horizontal mean curvature

\mathcal{H}^{0}
of
S
is defined on the non-characteristic locus as

\mathcal{H}^{0}=\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\left(\frac{\xi_{i}u}{|\nabla_{0}u|}\right).

Definition 6.2
.

A
C^{2}
regular hypersurface
S
in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}
is called
horizontally minimal
if its horizontal mean curvature
\mathcal{H}_{0}
vanishes (
\mathcal{H}_{0}=0
) along its non-characteristic locus.

Example
1
.

The hyperplane
H_{1}=\{x\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:U(x)=x_{1}=0\}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{6}
as a submanifold. Direct computations show that
H_{1}
is horizontally minimal. Note that the only characteristic point of
H_{1}
is the origin.

A rather non elementary paradigmatic example follows. Let
f
be a smooth function of the radial horizontal variable
r=|q|.
The following theorem provides an explicit formula for the horizontal mean curvature of a kind of rotationally symmetric hypersurface in
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}},
which may be significant interest in the study the isoperimetric problem within this setting. We let
\tau=|t|
.

Theorem 6.3
.

The horizontal mean curvature
\mathcal{H}^{0}
of the hypersurface
u(x)=\tau-f(r)=0

is given by

(6.1)

\mathcal{H}^{0}=\frac{-4r(2f^{\prime}+rf^{\prime\prime})-3\frac{(f^{\prime})^{3}}{r}}{(4r^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2})^{{3}/{2}}}+\frac{8r^{2}}{f\sqrt{4r^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2}}},

where
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
denote the first and second derivatives of
f
with respect to
r,
respectively.

Proof.

We compute first

\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
\xi_{3}\\
\xi_{4}\end{pmatrix}u=G\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\\
x_{4}\end{pmatrix},

where
G
is the anti-diagonal matrix

G=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{f^{\prime}}{r}&2\frac{t_{1}}{\tau}&2\frac{t_{2}}{\tau}&2\frac{t_{3}}{\tau}\\
-2\frac{t_{1}}{\tau}&-\frac{f^{\prime}}{r}&2\frac{t_{3}}{\tau}&-2\frac{t_{2}}{\tau}\\
-2\frac{t_{2}}{\tau}&-2\frac{t_{3}}{\tau}&-\frac{f^{\prime}}{r}&2\frac{t_{1}}{\tau}\\
-2\frac{t_{3}}{\tau}&2\frac{t_{2}}{\tau}&-2\frac{t_{1}}{\tau}&-\frac{f^{\prime}}{r}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, the norm of the horizontal gradient is
|\nabla_{0}u|=\sqrt{4r^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2}}.

We next compute

\sum_{i}\xi_{i}(\xi_{i}u)=-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}r+3f^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{8r^{2}}{\tau},

which yields

\sum_{i}(\xi_{i}u)\,\xi_{i}(|\nabla_{0}u|)=-\frac{4r+f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}}{|\nabla_{0}u|}f^{\prime}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\left(\frac{\xi_{i}u}{|\nabla_{0}u|}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{\xi_{i}(\xi_{i}u)}{|\nabla_{0}u|}-\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{(\xi_{i}u)\xi_{i}(|\nabla_{0}u|)}{|\nabla_{0}u|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\nabla_{0}u|}\left(-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}r+3f^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{8r^{2}}{\tau}\right)+\frac{4r+f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}}{|\nabla_{0}u|^{3}}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{-4r(2f^{\prime}+rf^{\prime\prime})-3\frac{(f^{\prime})^{3}}{r}}{(4r^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2})^{{3}/{2}}}+\frac{8r^{2}}{f\sqrt{4r^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2}}}.

∎

Corollary 6.4
.

A hypersurface
u(x)=\tau-f(r)=0
is horizontally minimal if

-4r^{2}f(2f^{\prime}+rf^{\prime\prime})-3f(f^{\prime})^{3}+8r^{3}(4r^{2}+(f^{\prime})^{2})=0.

Example
2
.

The hypersurface
H_{2}=\left\{(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:\tau=\sqrt{\frac{4}{3}}r^{2}\right\}
is a horizontally minimal surface in the quaternionic Heisenberg group
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}},
as this follows directly by applying formula (
6.1
) (or Corollary
6.4
). Note that its characteristic locus comprises only the origin.

Example
3
.

The Euclidean sphere is the hypersurface
\{(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:r^{2}+\tau^{2}=R^{2}\}.

We set
f(r)=\sqrt{R^{2}-r^{2}}.
Then applying formula (
6.1
) we find that

\mathcal{H}^{0}=\frac{3(4+R^{2})+8r^{2}(4\tau^{2}+3)}{8r(\tau^{2}+1)^{3/2}}.

Example
4
.

The Korányi sphere is the set
\{(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:r^{4}+\tau^{2}=R^{4}\}.
Then

\tau=f(r)=\sqrt{R^{4}-r^{4}}
and it follows that
the horizontal mean curvature is

\mathcal{H}^{0}=\frac{9r}{R^{2}}.

Example
5
.

The CC-sphere
S_{R}=\{(q,t)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\mathbb{H}}:d_{cc}(O,(q,t))=R\}.

It follows from (
5.9
) that we have:

r=\frac{2}{c}\sin\left(\frac{cR}{2}\right),\quad\tau=\frac{4(cR-\sin(cR))}{c^{2}},

where
c\in\mathbb{R}.
Set
r=f_{1}(c),
and
\tau=f_{2}(c).
Then the horizontal mean curvature can be determined using formula (
6.1
) by setting
f(r)=f_{2}\circ f_{1}^{-1}(r).

The resulting algebraic expression is a rational function in
r,\tau
, and their partial derivatives with respect to the horizontal vector fields. Due to the inherent complexity of the quaternionic Heisenberg group structure, the full expression spans is exceedingly lengthy and is omitted here for brevity.
