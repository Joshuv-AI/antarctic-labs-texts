Less is More Tokens: Efficient Math Reasoning via Difficulty-Aware Chain-of-Thought Distillation
Abdul Waheed Chancharik Mitra Laurie Z. Wang Deva Ramanan Bhiksha RajCarnegie Mellon University {abdulw,cmitra,zihaow3,deva,bhiksha}@andrew.cmu.edu

Abstract
Chain-of-thought reasoning, while powerful, can produce unnecessarily verbose output for simpler problems. We present a framework for difficulty-aware reasoning that teaches models to dynamically adjust reasoning depth based on problem complexity. Remarkably, we show that models can be endowed with such dynamic inference pathways without any architectural modifications; we simply post-train on data that is carefully curated to include chain-of-thought traces that are proportional in length to problem difficulty. Our analysis reveals that post-training via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) primarily captures patterns like reasoning length and format, while direct preference optimization (DPO) preserves reasoning accuracy, with their combination reducing length and maintaining or improving performance. Both quantitative metrics and qualitative assessments confirm that models can learn to "think proportionally"—reasoning minimally on simple problems while maintaining depth for complex ones.

Introduction

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
[
52
]
has emerged as a powerful paradigm to improve the reasoning abilities of large language models (LLMs) across domains such as math, coding, and commonsense reasoning
[
12
,
40
,
16
,
39
]
. By generating intermediate steps, CoT enables models to perform complex multi-step inferences more accurately. However, a critical limitation remains: these reasoning traces are often overly verbose, even for problems that require little or no intermediate reasoning. This leads to inefficient "over-thinking" during inference —longer outputs that result in increased latency and computational cost
[
10
]
. Indeed, recent estimates of energy costs suggest that inference during model deployment will dwarf the cost and environmental impact of training
[
45
]
.

In real-world settings, not all problems require the same amount of reasoning. For instance, simple arithmetic questions can be solved in a single step, while harder algebraic or geometric problems demand detailed multi-step explanations. Yet most CoT-enabled models apply a fixed reasoning strategy across all inputs. This is misaligned with how humans allocate cognitive effort: we intuitively adjust how much we think based on task complexity.

Recent efforts, such as L1
[
2
]
, introduce explicit length control through reinforcement learning, while work like “Learning How Hard to Think”
[
14
]
adapts compute allocation dynamically at inference time. However, these methods either enforce a fixed budget per query or modulate external procedures like a router or sampling, without teaching the model to internally vary its reasoning length. In our work, we address this problem by training the models to
dynamically adjust the granularity and length of their reasoning trajectories based on the difficulty of the input problem
.

We propose a general framework for difficulty-aware chain-of-thought reasoning. First, we estimate the difficulty of each math problem using a LLM based grader aligned with AoPS difficulty scales
1
1

1

https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php/AoPS_Wiki:Competition_ratings
. Next, we compress long CoT traces generated by a strong LLM into concise reasoning sequences, where the amount of compression is guided by the problem’s difficulty. This produces supervision that reflects the ideal level of reasoning granularity per input. We then fine-tune student models using both supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and direct preference optimization (DPO)
[
43
]
, training them to imitate difficulty-aligned traces—thinking minimally on easy questions and extensively on hard ones.

We empirically study this setup across unimodal and multimodal models for the math reasoning task. Our work centers around two key research questions:
(RQ1)
Can difficulty-adaptive models match or exceed the accuracy of full CoT models while reducing unnecessary reasoning?
(RQ2)
Do they offer better efficiency–accuracy trade-offs than static or explicitly length-controlled baselines? Our results demonstrate that difficulty-aware reasoning can reduce reasoning token usage by up to 30%, with minimum to no loss—and in some cases, gains—in final answer accuracy. Compared to uniform-length CoT or fixed-budget approaches, our models learn to allocate reasoning effort more effectively across problem types.

In summary, we present a systematic study of difficulty-aware reasoning in math problem solving. We introduce a scalable framework for generating and training on difficulty-aligned CoT traces, and show that LLMs can be taught to reason proportionally to the complexity of the task. Our findings open up promising directions for building more efficient, interpretable, and adaptive reasoning agents—models that not only solve problems, but also know how hard to think.

2
Preliminaries

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in multi-step reasoning through
Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) prompting. In this paradigm, models produce explicit intermediate steps when solving a problem, which helps improve both interpretability and answer accuracy. However, CoT traces—especially those generated by powerful reasoning or what we call “thinking” models—are often excessively verbose
[
10
]
. They frequently contain
trivial
and/or
redundant
reasoning steps, even for simple problems such as arithmetic or elementary algebra. This verbosity inflates the number of generated tokens, thereby increasing inference latency and computational cost—a key limitation for real-time or resource-constrained deployment.

A central insight motivating our work is that the complexity of reasoning required to solve a problem should vary with the intrinsic difficulty of that problem. Yet, existing CoT methods typically produce reasoning traces of uniform verbosity across a wide range of problem difficulties. This motivates our core hypothesis:
the length and granularity of CoT reasoning should scale with the complexity of the problem
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
denote an input math problem, and let
y\in\mathcal{Y}
be the corresponding correct final answer. A CoT-enabled model
f
solves the problem by generating a reasoning trace
r\in\mathcal{R}
and a final answer
y
, such that

f(x)=(r,y),\quad r=(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{T}),

where
T=|r|
is the length (in tokens) of the reasoning trace. In current models, the expected reasoning length
\mathbb{E}[T\mid d(x)]
tends to be invariant to the problem difficulty
d(x)\in[1,10]
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[T\mid d(x)]\approx\text{constant},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

This lack of adaptability leads to inefficient reasoning. Our goal is to design models where the expected reasoning length is positively correlated with the problem difficulty:

\mathbb{E}[T\mid d(x)]\propto d(x).

Such models would produce concise reasoning for easy problems (
d(x)\ll 10
) and more elaborate reasoning for difficult ones (
d(x)\approx 10
), allocating computational effort proportionally to task complexity.

To enable this, we adopt a teacher–student distillation framework. A high-capacity teacher model
f_{T}
generates detailed reasoning traces
r_{T}
, which are then passed through a difficulty-aware summarization function:

s(r_{T},d(x))\rightarrow\tilde{r},

where
\tilde{r}
is a compressed reasoning trace that reflects the difficulty
d(x)
. A student model
f_{S}
is trained to map

x\mapsto(\tilde{r},y),\quad\text{with }|\tilde{r}|\approx\alpha\cdot d(x),\quad\alpha>0,

thus learning to internalize difficulty-aware reasoning behavior.

This formulation serves two objectives: (1)
Efficiency
, by reducing average token generation per sample, and (2)
Adaptivity
, by tailoring the reasoning depth to the task at hand. Our evaluations assess not only answer correctness but also how well the model modulates reasoning length in accordance with
d(x)
, providing a joint measure of performance and computational efficiency.

3
Method

We present a general framework for difficulty-aware chain-of-thought (CoT) distillation that enables models to adapt their reasoning verbosity based on problem difficulty. Our approach consists of two main stages: (1) data generation through difficulty-aligned compression of
overly
verbose reasoning traces for both unimodal and multimodal mathematical problems, and (2) training using a combination of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and direct preference optimization (DPO). The key intuition is that by teaching models to imitate traces that are adapted to the complexity of each input, we can encourage efficient and interpretable reasoning without compromising accuracy.

3.1
Data Generation

We begin with a collection of math problems
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, each associated with a long, detailed reasoning trace
r_{i}^{\text{long}}
generated by a high-capacity teacher model. While these traces are generally accurate, they tend to be overly verbose—offering exhaustive reasoning even for trivial problems. To correct this mismatch, we design a pipeline that compresses each trace into a more concise form based on the difficulty of the underlying problem. To do this, we use
GTP-4o-mini
(temperature = 0.6) with the prompt shown in Figure
5
and
6
.

Difficulty Estimation.

To assign a scalar difficulty score
d(x_{i})\in[1,10]
to each problem, we use
GPT-4o-mini
(temperature=0). The prompt, as shown in Figure
18
is designed to align with the Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) competition difficulty ratings, following the rubric used in SkyThought
[
46
]
. This procedure ensures that estimated difficulty scores are stable, human-aligned, and reflect the true complexity of the reasoning required (see Appendix
A.2.1
).

Difficulty-Aware Compression.

Given the difficulty score
d(x_{i})
, we compress the long CoT trace
r_{i}^{\text{long}}
into a shorter version
\tilde{r}_{i}
using a prompt-based summarization function:

\tilde{r}_{i}=s(r_{i}^{\text{long}},d(x_{i})),

where
s(\cdot,\cdot)
represents a deterministic compression process instantiated via few-shot prompting. This function adapts the verbosity of the reasoning proportionally to the problem’s difficulty—minimal steps for easy problems and more elaborate chains for hard ones—while preserving logical correctness and final answer. We generate the resulting training tuples
(x_{i},\tilde{r}_{i},y_{i})
, where
y_{i}
is the correct final answer.

In Table
1
, we report the compression ratios across different difficulty levels for both unimodal and multimodal settings. In the unimodal setup, we observe that harder problems tend to require longer reasoning chains, leading to higher compression ratios as difficulty increases. In contrast, the multimodal setup exhibits relatively uniform CoT lengths across difficulty levels, suggesting that the initial reasoning traces are already more optimized. As a result, the compression ratios remain constant or even decrease with increasing difficulty.

Once the difficulty-aligned training data is generated, we train compact student models to replicate this behavior using both supervised and preference-based learning paradigms. We experiment with Qwen2.5-Math (1.5B, 7B) for unimodal, and Qwen2.5-VL (3B, 7B-Instruct) for multimodal setting.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).

SFT serves as the first stage of training, where the model learns to map each input problem to its compressed trace:

\min_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{CE}\left(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),\tilde{r}_{i}\right),

where
f_{\theta}
is the student model and CE denotes the cross-entropy loss between its output and the compressed trace. This objective encourages the model to internalize reasoning styles that are aligned with the problem’s complexity.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

To further refine reasoning behavior, we apply DPO
[
43
]
using preference data. For each input, we treat the compressed trace
\tilde{r}_{i}
as the preferred response and the original long trace
r_{i}^{\text{long}}
as the rejected one. The training objective is:

\min_{\theta}\;\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp\left(\beta\cdot\text{KL}(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),\tilde{r}_{i})\right)}{\exp\left(\beta\cdot\text{KL}(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),\tilde{r}_{i})\right)+\exp\left(\beta\cdot\text{KL}(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),r_{i}^{\text{long}})\right)},

where
\beta
is a temperature parameter and KL denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence. This step explicitly teaches the model to prefer compressed, efficient reasoning over redundant verbosity, guided by human-aligned preferences.

Hybrid Training (SFT + DPO).

We also explore a hybrid training setup where SFT is followed by DPO. This curriculum allows the model to first acquire difficulty-conditioned reasoning patterns through imitation (SFT), and then refine them via preference-based contrastive learning (DPO). We find that this combination yields the most consistent gains across our benchmarks (see Section
5
).

Our method systematically generates and distills difficulty-aware CoT traces, encouraging LLMs to “think harder” only when needed. The approach generalizes across modalities and model sizes, and supports efficient reasoning without sacrificing final answer accuracy. In the next section, we describe our experimental setup and metrics for evaluating reasoning efficiency and correctness.

Our goal is to assess both the reasoning performance and token efficiency of models trained with difficulty-aligned CoT traces. We evaluate our setup across a diverse suite of unimodal and multimodal benchmarks, compare against strong reasoning and instruction-tuned baselines, and analyze model outputs across problem difficulty levels.

Evaluation

Our evaluation consists of three key steps: (1) estimating the difficulty of each evaluation problem using the AoPS-based rating system; (2)

For unimodal we directly extract the and match it with the ground truth answer; however, for multimodal models, we observe occasional failures to adhere to prompt formatting, leading to difficulties in automatic answer extraction. To address this, we use a verifier model: given the model-generated solution and the ground-truth answer, we prompt

LLM-as-Judge Evaluation

Prior work
[
6

Further, a key subgoal of our work is to improve the readability of reasoning traces by compressing them, removing redundant or overly verbose content. However, there are no existing automatic metrics for evaluating this aspect. To address this, we perform a human-aligned evaluation of reasoning traces generated by five different models on 100 math problems using the LLM-as-Judge

Multimodal

In our main results (Figure
1

pass@1

We find that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) alone already provides strong performance and efficiency: for example, the 7B SFT model attains 28% on MathVerse, outperforming both LLaVA-CoT-11B (22%) and Qwen2.5-VL-3B (25%), and achieves 51% on MMStar compared to 45% and 43% from the same baselines. Direct preference optimization (DPO) alone boosts performance selectively—for instance, reaching 16% on OlympiadBench, on par with Qwen2.5-VL-7B, though it lags behind SFT on MathVision (9% vs. 14%). The combined SFT+DPO consistently delivers the strongest overall outcomes, such as 18% on MathVision (vs. 14% for Qwen2.5-VL-7B) and 51% on MMStar, highlighting the complementarity of the two approaches.

These results demonstrate that our approach not only achieves competitive or superior accuracy relative to stronger baselines but also maintains compact reasoning traces. In practice, this translates to reductions of up to 30–35% in reasoning length, while allocating tokens adaptively across problem difficulty. Unlike LLaVA-CoT, which shows limited sensitivity to task difficulty, our SFT+DPO models expend more tokens on challenging tasks while compressing easy cases, yielding a balanced trade-off between accuracy and efficiency.

SFT Learns Length and Format.
Our initial experiments with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) revealed that while models effectively learned to mimic the structure and brevity of our difficulty-aware summarized traces, this came at the cost of reduced accuracy. As seen in Table
2
and Figure
1
, SFT models produced significantly shorter outputs aligned with our compression objectives and consistently adopted the clean, numbered reasoning format used by the
gpt4o_DA2
summarizer (see Appendix
A.4
). This indicates that SFT is effective at learning stylistic and structural patterns present in the training data. However, this stylistic conformity did not translate into better task performance. Despite generating compact and well-formatted responses, SFT models often failed to arrive at the correct final answer, underperforming relative to their base counterparts.

DPO Learns Reasoning Accuracy.
In contrast to our SFT results, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) demonstrated strong improvements in reasoning accuracy across both unimodal and multimodal settings. As shown in Table
2
and Figure
1
, DPO-trained models consistently matched or outperformed their base counterparts in accuracy, confirming its effectiveness in aligning model outputs with correctness-oriented preferences. However, DPO offered limited gains in token efficiency. Unlike SFT, which significantly reduced reasoning length, DPO models retained much of the original verbosity, with only modest compression observed. Qualitative analysis (Appendix
A.4
) supports the observation that DPO outputs emphasized completeness and correctness over brevity.

SFT+DPO Balances Length and Correctness Objectives.

Combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with direct preference optimization (DPO) provides an effective strategy for balancing reasoning conciseness with accuracy. As shown in Tables
2
and Figure
1
, models trained with this two-stage approach achieve up to 30% reduction in token usage while maintaining or improving final answer accuracy across unimodal and multimodal benchmarks. SFT encourages the model to mimic surface-level patterns in the compressed traces—such as structure and brevity—by minimizing cross-entropy loss. However, it does not explicitly optimize for correctness, especially when critical reasoning steps are condensed in the summaries.

DPO complements this by learning from pairwise preferences, optimizing for correctness and clarity without relying on exact sequence matching. When preferred responses are both accurate and well-formatted, DPO implicitly promotes efficient reasoning while preserving answer quality. Additionally, the KL-regularization term in DPO stabilizes performance, preventing overfitting or degradation in reasoning. When applied after SFT, DPO refines correctness without sacrificing conciseness, resulting in models that produce structured, accurate, and difficulty-aware reasoning traces—directly addressing our core research question (RQ1).

Conclusion

We present a scalable framework for difficulty-aware chain-of-thought (CoT) distillation that enables models to reason proportionally to problem complexity. By compressing verbose reasoning traces based on difficulty scores and combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with direct preference optimization (DPO), our method trains models to produce concise yet accurate reasoning. Our results demonstrate that this two-stage approach yields up to 30% reduction in reasoning length without sacrificing final answer accuracy across both unimodal and multimodal math reasoning benchmarks. While SFT excels at learning structured and concise reasoning styles, DPO preserves correctness by aligning outputs with human preferences. Their combination captures the best of both—conciseness and correctness. Qualitative evaluations using the LLM-as-Judge framework further confirm that SFT+DPO models produce clearer, more complete, and less redundant reasoning traces. Overall, this work highlights the potential of adaptive reasoning strategies for building more efficient, interpretable, and task-aligned LLMs—models that not only solve problems but also learn how hard to think.

8
Limitations

While our results demonstrate the effectiveness of difficulty-aware chain-of-thought (CoT) distillation, our approach has several limitations. Our training dataset is considerably smaller than those used in prior large-scale distillation efforts such as DeepSeek-Distil and Nemotron, which may limit generalization to out-of-distribution problems. We evaluate models using the standard pass@1 metric rather than pass@k metrics that better account for generation variability. Due to compute constraints, we rely on parameter-efficient LoRA fine-tuning rather than full model fine-tuning, which may underutilize model capacity. Additionally, our difficulty estimation uses GPT-4o-mini as a proxy for true human difficulty assessment, and errors in these estimates could propagate through the distillation pipeline. These limitations highlight opportunities for further refinement and scaling of our framework in future work.
