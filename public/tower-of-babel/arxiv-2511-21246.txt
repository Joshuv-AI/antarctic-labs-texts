Abstract
. In this paper, we study rigidity of polynomials of arbitrary degree in the presence of neutral dynamics. Specifically, we focus on non-renormalizable (in the sense of Douady and Hubbard) complex polynomials of degree d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 that possess a Siegel disk of bounded type rotation number. We refer to such maps as atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type . In this setting, our main results are: (A) Atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type have locally connected Julia sets; (B) These Julia sets are quasiconformally rigid, i.e., they do not support invariant line fields; (C) Any two combinatorially equivalent atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type coincide up to an affine change of coordinates. In particular, item (C) verifies the notorious Combinatorial Rigidity Conjecture for atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type and arbitrary degree. By bringing neutral Siegel dynamics into the picture, we extend the celebrated higher-degree rigidity theorems of Avila–Kahn–Lyubich–Shen and Kozlovski–van Strien, which until now applied only in the Yoccoz setting, i.e., for finitely many times renormalizable polynomials without irrationally indifferent periodic points.



1.
Introduction

A central theme in one-dimensional complex dynamics is the study of
rigidity phenomena
, that is, understanding to what extent the combinatorial or topological data associated with a dynamical system determines its conformal or analytic structure. This perspective has driven substantial progress in the field, tracing back to the seminal work of Douady and Hubbard
[
DoHu
]
, who introduced the notion of polynomial-like mappings and used them to establish deep structural results about the Mandelbrot set and quadratic polynomials. The landmark results of Yoccoz
[
Hu
]
later established rigidity for a broad class of quadratic polynomials, showing that combinatorial data (the landing pattern of external rays and normalization in the Fatou set) suffice to determine the conformal conjugacy class of the map. These ideas were subsequently extended to higher-degree polynomials, most notably through the development of enhanced puzzle techniques and renormalization theory in the works of Lyubich
[
Ly
]
, Lyubich and Kahn
[
KaLy1
,
KaLy2
]
, Avila, Kahn, Lyubich, and Shen
[
AvKaLySh
]
, Kozlovski, Shen, van Strien
[
KSS
]
, Kozlovski and van Strien
[
KovS
]
, and others.

However, rigidity results often rely on the absence of neutral behaviour in the dynamics. In cases where the dynamics has neutral periodic points, the situation becomes more delicate. For instance, in the parabolic setting, powerful tools like parabolic implosion and Lavaurs maps allow for a fine control of the local and global dynamics, and rigidity results can often be recovered. In contrast, in the presence of irrationally indifferent periodic points, the problem becomes substantially more subtle. One of the better-understood scenarios within neutral dynamics arises in the case of Siegel disks with bounded type rotation numbers.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a polynomial with a fixed point at
0
. We say that
0
is
irrationally indifferent
(with
rotation number

\theta\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})/\mathbb{Z}
) if
f^{\prime}(0)=e^{2\pi i\theta}
. If additionally the dynamics of
f
is linearizable locally near
0
, then
0
is called a
Siegel point
. This means that in some neighbourhood of
0
the map
f
is conformally conjugate to the rigid rotation
z\mapsto e^{2\pi i\theta}\cdot z
. In this case, the maximal domain of definition for such a conjugacy is called the
Siegel disk
, denoted
\Delta_{f}\ni 0
, and
f
is referred to as a
Siegel polynomial
. Lastly, the rotation number, the Siegel point, the Siegel disk and the Siegel polynomial are said to be
of bounded type
if all entries in the continued fraction expansion of
\theta
are uniformly bounded.

The first seminal result on Siegel polynomials was due to Douady–Ghys–Herman–Shishikura (see
[
Do
,
He
]
). Using quasi-surgery, they constructed a Blaschke product model for the dynamics of
f
in case
f
is quadratic (
\deg(f)=2
) and the rotation number is of bounded type. This allowed them to conclude that the Siegel boundary
\partial\Delta_{f}
is a quasi-circle containing at least one critical point (which is unique in the quadratic case). This result was generalized to the cubic case
\deg(f)=3
by Zakeri in
[
Za
]
, then to any degree by Shishikura and Zhang
[
Zh
]
(see Theorem
2.1
). (See also
[
WaYaZhZh
]
.)

The rigidity of Siegel polynomials of bounded type was settled in the quadratic case by Petersen in
[
Pe
]
(see also the work of Yampolsky
[
Yam
]
). Petersen proved that the Julia set
J_{f}
of a quadratic Siegel polynomial
f
of bounded type is locally connected, thereby showing that the topology of the dynamics of
f
is entirely determined by its combinatorial data. He then showed that
J_{f}
has zero measure, which implies that it has a trivial conformal conjugacy class.

In Petersen’s work, it is crucial that
\deg(f)=2
, so that the unique critical point is forced to be contained in the Siegel boundary
\partial\Delta_{f}
. In the case
\deg(f)\geq 3
, it is possible that there exists an external, recurrent critical point whose orbit accumulates to
\partial\Delta_{f}
, but does not land exactly on it. In this highly non-linear situation, the geometry of
\partial\Delta_{f}
could, a priori, become highly distorted, causing a failure of rigidity. This problem is addressed in
[
Y
]
, where it is shown that even in the higher degree case, local connectivity still holds at
\partial\Delta_{f}
. This work is followed in
[
YZh
]
, where it is shown that global rigidity holds for cubic Siegel polynomials of bounded type.

In this paper, we address the rigidity of Siegel polynomials of bounded type for arbitrary degrees. We focus on polynomials that are non-renormalizable in the sense of Douady and Hubbard. This means that the dynamics cannot be decomposed into simpler, more basic parts that can be analyzed separately. We say that such maps are
atomic
.

The main result of this paper is the following rigidity theorem for atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type. This is the first time in the literature that the neutral irrational dynamics is incorporated into the rigidity picture for polynomial maps of arbitrary degree.

Main Theorem

(Rigidity of Siegel polynomials of bounded type)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an atomic Siegel polynomial of bounded type and degree
d\geq 2
. Then:

(A)

each point in the Julia set
J_{f}
is dynamically rigid (i.e., the orbits of any two orbits can be distinguished in terms of symbolic dynamics); in particular,
J_{f}
is locally connected.

(B)

J_{f}
is quasiconformally rigid (i.e., it does not admit non-trivial invariant line fields).

(C)

If
\widetilde{f}
is another atomic Siegel polynomial of bounded-type that is combinatorially equivalent to
f
, then
f
and
\widetilde{f}
are affinely conjugate.

We will define all the concepts in the follow-up sections; in particular, combinatorial equivalence is defined in Section
8
(see Definition
8.5
). This result extends a recent result due to the second author and Zhang
[
YZh
]
on rigidity of bounded-type Siegel polynomials of degree
3
(one free critical point). Main Theorem (
C
) confirms the
Combinatorial Rigidity Conjecture
in the above-mentioned class of polynomials. This conjecture asserts that for polynomials under an
appropriate
definition of the combinatorial equivalence, combinatorial rigidity holds. We remark that our definition includes the condition that
f
and
\widetilde{f}
are
Fatou normalized
and the positions of critical points on the boundary of the Siegel disk are combinatorially the same, see Section
7
for details. Since we assume that
f,\widetilde{f}
are non-renormalizable, their rational laminations, i.e., the combinatorial pattern of landing external rays, are empty. In our context, Fatou normalization means that each critical point
c
in a preimage of a Siegel disk
\Delta_{f}
lands at
0
.

Remark 1.1
.

Our Main Theorem can be easily extended to the cases where one allows for finitely many orbits of periodic Siegel disks and finitely many renormalizations. In those cases, in the definition of combinatorial equivalence one needs to include in the combinatorial data also the landing patterns of external and internal rays. Furthermore, one can also include cycles of attracting and parabolic periodic points.

For clarity of theexposition, in this paper, we restrict our attention to atomic Siegel polynomials.

1.1.
Outline of the proof

The proof uses the following strategy, which was successful in a number of cases (see
[
CDKvS
, Section 2]
), including the one in the current paper:

(1)

Markov partition
: In a given family
\mathcal{F}
of mappings and a map
f\in\mathcal{F}
in this family, construct a puzzle partition (i.e., a Yoccoz- or Markov-type partition) around points in the Julia set
J_{f}
of
f
. In the context of the current paper, the partition is given in terms of Siegel bubble rays extended by external rays, see Section
2.1
.

(2)

Rigidity on the Julia boundary of puzzle pieces
: Establish (dynamical) rigidity on the boundary of each puzzle piece, i.e., for a puzzle piece
P
, show that each point in
\partial P\cap J_{f}
can be surrounded by a union of arbitrary small puzzle pieces. In our case, this result was proven in
[
Y
]
(see Section
2.1
).

(3)

Extract a dynamically natural complex box mapping
: Construct a dynamically natural box mapping (in the sense of Section
3
) around the critical points of
f
that do not land on the puzzle boundary. This mapping controls the dynamics of most critical points, except for those whose orbits accumulate only at the puzzle boundary. In our paper, the construction of such a mapping is carried out in Section
4
.

(4)

Local connectivity and quasiconformal rigidity of the Julia set
: Use the rigidity result for box mappings (i.e., puzzle pieces shrink to points, no invariant line fields) to infer the corresponding results for
f\in\mathcal{F}
. In our case, this is done in Section
5
, where we prove the items (
A
) and (
B
) of the Main Theorem.

(5)

Topological model of the Julia set
: Construct topological models of the dynamics for maps
f\in\mathcal{F}
and prove that they are determined uniquely by combinatorial data. This is done in Section
6
and
7
.

(6)

Topological rigidity of combinatorially equivalent maps
: Prove that the filled Julia sets of maps in
\mathcal{F}
are homeomorphic to the topological models constructed in the previous step. It follows that two maps within the same combinatorial class must be topologically conjugate. This is carried out in Section
8
.

(7)

Conformal rigidity of topologically equivalent maps
: Use quasiconformal rigidity of the corresponding complex box mappings for the topologically conjugate
f,\widetilde{f}
to conclude that
f,\widetilde{f}
are quasiconformally conjugate, and hence, because of quasiconformal rigidity of
J_{f},J_{\widetilde{f}}
and Fatou normalization,
f,\widetilde{f}
are conformally (affine) conjugate.

In our paper, this step is done in Section
9
. This concludes the proof of Main Theorem (
C
).

2.
Some preliminaries

Let
f\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a polynomial of degree
d\geq 2
. Suppose that
0
is an irrationally indifferent fixed point that is the dynamical center of a Siegel disk
\Delta_{f}
with the rotation number
\rho=\rho(f)\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})/\mathbb{Z}
of bounded type. We refer to such mappings as
Siegel polynomials
.

Denote the attracting basin of infinity, the filled Julia set and the Julia set of
f
by
\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{f}
,

K_{f}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{f}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptJ_{f}:=\partial K_{f}=\partial\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{f}.

Let
\phi^{\infty}_{f}:\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{f}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}
be the external Böttcher uniformization which conjugates
f|_{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{f}}
with
z\mapsto z^{d}
. The
external ray
with
external angle

t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is given by

\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t}:=\{z\in\mathcal{A}^{\infty}_{f}\;|\;\operatorname{arg}(\phi^{\infty}_{f}(z))=t\}.

The external ray
\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t}
is said to be
rational
if
t\in\mathbb{Q}
, and
irrational
if otherwise. The accumulation set of
\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t}
is denoted
\omega(\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t})\subset\mathcal{J}_{f}
. We say that
\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t}

lands
at
x
if
\omega(\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t})=\{x\}
. It is well-known that if
t
is periodic, then
\mathcal{R}^{\infty}_{t}
lands at either a repelling or a parabolic periodic point.

We denote by
\operatorname{Crit}(f)
the set of critical points of
f
and say that a Siegel polynomial
f
is
polynomial-like renormalizable around a critical point
c\in\operatorname{Crit}(f)
if there exist a pair of topological disks
U\Subset V\subset\mathbb{C}
and an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\in U
and
f^{n}\colon U\to V
is a branched covering such that its non-escaping set is connected and contains
c
. We exclude trivial renormalizations, when the non-escaping set is equal to
K_{f}
. This is a classical definition due to Douady and Hubbard, and the map
f^{n}\colon U\to V
is called a
polynomial-like restriction
(around
c
). The map
f
is
non-polynomial-like renormalizable
, or more simply
atomic
, if such a situation does not happen, i.e., the critical point does not return to any of its neighborhoods at periodic times. Equivalently,
f
is non-polynomial-like renormalizable around
c
if either
c
is non-recurrent, or there exists a sequence of shrinking neighborhoods
(U_{k})
around
c
so that the return times of the orbit of
c
to the domain
U_{k}
grow as
k\to\infty
.

Finally, the polynomial
f
is at
most finitely many times polynomial-like renormalizable
if
f
has at most finitely many polynomial-like restrictions around some critical point in the sense given above (with non-escaping set getting strictly smaller with each further renormalization).

Observe that if
f
is an atomic Siegel polynomial, then the Julia set of
f
is connected. This is because otherwise one might extract a polynomial-like restriction containing
\Delta_{f}
.

2.1.
Siegel Puzzle Partition

In this section, we provide a puzzle partition suitable for Siegel polynomials. The results of this subsection, unless otherwise stated, are proven in
[
Y
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Shishikura, Zhang)
.

The Siegel boundary
\partial\Delta_{f}
is a quasi-circle that contains at least one critical point
c_{0}
of
f
. ∎

Corollary 2.2
.

There exists a quasi-symmetric map
h:(\partial\Delta_{f},c_{0})\to(\partial\mathbb{D},1)
such that for
g:=f|_{\partial\Delta_{f}}
, we have

h\circ g\circ h^{-1}(z)=e^{2\pi\rho i}z\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for}\hskip 14.22636ptz\in\partial\Delta_{f}.

∎

For

, let

\xi_{s}:=h^{-1}(e^{2\pi si}).

For

, denote

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
c_{k}
is not a critical point for

.

A connected component

Let

•

B_{0}=\Delta_{f}
; and

•

\partial B_{i-i}\cap\partial B_{i}=\{x_{i}\}
is a root of

.

The union

. The point

is called the

of
\mathcal{R}^{B}
. The accumulation set
\omega(\mathcal{R}^{B})
of
\mathcal{R}^{B}
is defined as the accumulation set of the sequence

. Note that

.

Observe that the image of a bubble ray is also a bubble ray. A bubble ray
\mathcal{R}^{B}
is

if

for some

, or
rational
if
f^{n}(\mathcal{R}^{B})
is periodic for some
n\geq 0
. Note that all fixed bubble rays are rooted at
c_{0}
. A bubble ray
\mathcal{R}^{B}
is said to be
d
-adic of generation

is a fixed bubble ray, and

Proposition 2.3

(Rational bubble rays land)
.

Every
p
-periodic bubble ray
\mathcal{R}^{B}
lands at a repelling or parabolic
p
-periodic point
x_{\infty}\in J_{f}
. ∎

An
equipotential curve
at
level

l\in(1,\infty)
of
f
is defined as

\mathcal{Q}_{l}:=\{(\phi^{\infty}_{f})^{-1}(le^{2\pi ti})\;|\;t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\}.

Let
\mathbf{R}_{0}
be the union of closures of all fixed bubble rays, all landing points of these bubble rays, and all external rays that also land at these points. Define the
initial puzzle
(or
puzzle graph
) as

\mathcal{Z}_{0}:=\mathbf{R}_{0}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{2}.

(2.1)

The
puzzle of depth

n\geq 0
is given by

\mathcal{Z}_{n}:=f^{-n}(\mathcal{Z}_{0}).

A connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{n}
is called a
puzzle piece of depth
n
. Note that a puzzle piece is an open topological disk. The puzzle pieces are graded by their depth, and there are only finitely many puzzle pieces of any given depth. Furthermore, the puzzle pieces obey
the Markov property
:

•

every two puzzle pieces
P^{n},P^{m}
,
n>m
, of depths
n
and
m
are either nested or disjoint; in the former case,
P^{n}\subset P^{m}
;

•

f(P^{n})=P^{n-1}
is a puzzle piece of depth
n-1
, and the map
f\colon P^{n}\to P^{n-1}
is a proper holomorphic branched covering between two open topological disks.

The following theorem is a fundamental technical result in the rigidity theory of Siegel polynomials.

Theorem 2.4

(Rigidity at Siegel boundary)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P^{n}
be a puzzle piece of depth
n
such that
P^{n+1}\subset P^{n}
and
\partial P^{n}\cap\partial\Delta_{f}\neq\varnothing
. Then

\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{P^{n}}=\{z\}\subset\partial\Delta_{f}.

Consequently, the Julia set
J_{f}
is locally connected at every point in
\partial\Delta_{f}
. ∎

Combining Proposition
2.3
and Theorem

, we obtain:

Theorem 2.5

(Rigidity at Julia boundary)
.

If
P
is a puzzle piece for
f
, then
f
is dynamically rigid at every point
z\in\partial P\cap J_{f}
in the following sense:

Let
Q^{n}
be the union of closures of all puzzle pieces of depth
n\in\mathbb{N}
containing the point
z
. Then

\bigcap\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}Q^{n}=\{z\}.

∎

3.
Background on complex box mappings

In this section, following
[
CDKvS
]
, we provide some background on the generalized renormalization technique via
complex box mappings
.

Definition 3.1

(Complex box mapping)
.

A holomorphic map
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
between two open sets
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{C}
is a
complex box mapping
if the following holds:

(1)

F
has finitely many critical points;

(2)

\mathcal{V}
is the union of finitely many open Jordan disks with disjoint closures;

(3)

every component
V
of
\mathcal{V}
is either a component of
\mathcal{U}
, or
V\cap\mathcal{U}
is a union of Jordan disks with pairwise disjoint closures, each of which is compactly contained in
V
;

(4)

for every component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
the image
F(U)
is a component of
\mathcal{V}
, and the restriction
F\colon U\to F(U)
is a proper map.

(5)

for each component
U
of
\mathcal{U}
there exists
n\geq 0
so that
F^{n}(U)\setminus\mathcal{U}\neq\emptyset
;

Remark 3.2
.

In this paper, we slightly modify the definition of a complex box mapping given in, for instance,
[
CDKvS
]
, by adding requirement (
5
). This condition guarantees that a box mapping has no cycles of components of
\mathcal{U}
and is clearly satisfied if each component of
\mathcal{U}
has escaping points. In
[
CDKvS
]
, this condition was part of the dynamical naturality assumption, and was called the
no-permutation-condition
. We relax our definition of dynamically natural box mappings (see Definition
3.3
) by moving the aforementioned condition into the definition of a box mapping.

The
filled Julia set of
F
is defined as

K_{F}:=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}F^{-n}(\mathcal{V}).

The set
K_{F}
consists of non-escaping points under the iteration of
F
, i.e., of points with orbits under
F
that are defined for all iterates.

For a given
n\geq 0
, a connected component of
F^{-n}(\mathcal{V})
is called a
puzzle piece of depth
n
for
F
. For
x\in\mathcal{V}
, the
puzzle piece of depth
n
containing
x
is the connected component of
F^{-n}(\mathcal{V})
containing
x
. Note that if
x
escapes
\mathcal{U}
in less than
n
steps, this set will be empty.

Given a point
x\in\mathcal{U}
, the
fiber of
x
is the nested intersection of all puzzle pieces of
F
containing
x
. We denote the fiber of
x
as
\fib(x)
. By our convention, if the orbit of
x
escapes, then
\fib(x)=\emptyset
.

We will denote the set of critical points for
F
as
\operatorname{Crit}(F)
. Let us denote by
\PC(F)
the union of the forward orbits of
\operatorname{Crit}(F)
. Note that
\PC(F)
is different from the classical

, which is defined as a closure of the union of the forward orbits of the critical values for

.

Dynamically natural complex box mappings

Towards defining a more natural sub-family of complex box mappings, let us introduce the following two forward invariant subsets of

.

(1)

Let

be a finite set and

of

and each component of

. If

\displaystyle K_{\text{off-crit}}(F):=\{x\in K_{F}\colon

\displaystyle\text{ of}\,\operatorname{Crit}(F)\text{ such that }\orb(x)\cap W=\emptyset\}

otherwise, i.e. when

(2)

Let

(Dynamically natural complex box mapping)
.

A complex box mapping

is called

if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

the Lebesgue measure of the set

(2)

.

We refer the reader to

3.2.
Combinatorial equivalence of complex box mappings

By Definition
3.1
, each component in the sets
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
for a box mapping
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a Jordan disk. Therefore, by the Carathéodory theorem for every branch

of this mapping there exists a well-defined continuous homeomorphic extension
\hat{F}\colon\cl U\to\cl V
, and by continuity this extension is unique. Let us denote by
\hat{F}\colon\cl\mathcal{U}\to\cl\mathcal{V}
the total extended map.

Definition 3.4

(Itinerary of puzzle pieces relative to curve family)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a complex box mapping, and let

be a finite set with one point on each component of
\partial\mathcal{V}
. Let
\Gamma
be a collection of simple curves in
(\cl\mathcal{V})\setminus(\mathcal{U}\cup\PC(F))
, one for each

, that connects
y
to a point in
X
. Then for every

and for each component

F^{-n}(\mathcal{U})
there exists a simple curve connecting
X
to
\partial U
of the form
\gamma_{0}\ldots\gamma_{n}
where
\hat{F}^{k}(\gamma_{k})\in\Gamma
. The word
(\gamma_{0},\hat{F}(\gamma_{1}),\ldots,\hat{F}^{n}(\gamma_{n}))
is called the
\Gamma
-itinerary
of
U
.

If a component
V
of
\mathcal{V}
is also a component
of
\mathcal{U}
, then the corresponding curve will be contained in the Jordan curve
(\cl V)\setminus(\mathcal{U}\cup\PC(F))=\partial V
, because by definition
\PC(F)\subset\mathcal{V}
and so no point in the boundary of

lies
in
\PC(F)
.

Note the
\Gamma
-itinerary of

is not uniquely defined even though there is a unique finite word for every

(see
[
CDKvS
, Section 5.4]
for details).

Definition 3.5

(Combinatorial equivalence of box mappings)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be two complex box mappings. Let
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
be a homeomorphism with the property that
H(\mathcal{U})=\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
,
H(\PC(F)\setminus\mathcal{U})=\PC(\widetilde{F})\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
and such that it has a homeomorphic extension
\hat{H}\colon\cl\mathcal{V}\to\cl\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
to the closures of
\mathcal{V}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
.

The maps
F
and
\widetilde{F}
are called
combinatorially equivalent w.r.t.
H
if:

•

H
is a bijection between
\operatorname{Crit}(F)
and
\operatorname{Crit}(\widetilde{F})
; for
c\in\operatorname{Crit}(F)
,
\widetilde{c}:=H(c)
is the
corresponding
critical point;

•

there exists a curve family
\Gamma
as in Definition
3.4
so that for every

, and for each

such that both

and
\widetilde{F}^{k}(\widetilde{c})
are well-defined, the
\Gamma
-itineraries of

coincide with the
\hat{H}(\Gamma)
-itineraries of

.

3.3.
Renormalization of complex box mappings

A complex box mapping
F
is called
(box) renormalizable
if there exists
s\geq 1
and a puzzle piece
W
of some depth containing a critical point
c\in\operatorname{Crit}(F)
such that
F^{ks}(c)\in W
for all
k\geq 0
. If this is not the case, then
F
is called
non-renormalizable
.

It is easy to see that for a complex box mapping,
F
is box renormalizable if and only if it is renormalizable in the classical Douady–Hubbard sense (see
[
CDKvS
, Lemma 5.1]
). Recall that one says that a box mapping
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is
Douady–Hubbard renormalizable
if there exist
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
c\in\operatorname{Crit}(F)
and open topological disks
c\in U\Subset V\subset\mathcal{U}
so that
F^{s}\colon U\to V
is a polynomial-like map with connected Julia set containing
c
.

3.4.
Rigidity of non-renormalizable dynamically natural box mappings

The following three theorems are the main results on rigidity of non-renormalizable complex box mappings. For the proofs, see
[
KovS
,
CDKvS
]
.

Theorem 3.6

(Dynamical rigidity)
.

If
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a non-renormalizable dynamically natural complex box mapping, then each point
x\in K_{F}
is contained in arbitrarily small puzzle pieces. ∎

Recall that for a forward-invariant subset
E\subset K(F)
, the
line field
supported on
E
is an assignment of straight lines
\mu(z)
through the origin in the tangent space of each point
z\in E
such that the slopes of
\mu(z)
depend measurably on
z
. This line field is
invariant under
F
if
F^{\prime}
sends the line
\mu(z)
to the line
\mu(F(z))
. In particular, if
K(F)
has zero Lebesgue measure, then it does not support invariant line fields by definition.

Theorem 3.7

(Absence of invariant line fields)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a non-renormalizable dynamically natural complex box mapping. Assume that each puzzle piece
P
of
F
contains an open set of escaping points, i.e.
\inter\left(P\,\setminus K_{F}\right)\neq\emptyset
. Then
F
carries no measurable invariant line fields supported on
K_{F}
. ∎

This theorem is a weaker version of
[
CDKvS
, Theorem 6.1(2)]
, where box mappings carrying invariant line fields are characterized. The condition described in that paper are impossible with the additional assumption imposed in Theorem
3.7
. This assumption will be satisfied in our setting.

Theorem 3.8

(Parameter rigidity)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be a non-renormalizable dynamically natural complex box mapping. Suppose
\widetilde{F}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
is another dynamically natural complex box mapping for which there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
H\colon\mathcal{V}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
so that

(1)

\widetilde{F}
is combinatorially equivalent to
F
w.r.t.
H
, and so in particular
H(\mathcal{U})=\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}
,

(2)

\widetilde{F}\circ H=H\circ F
on
\partial\mathcal{U}
, i.e.
H
is a conjugacy on
\partial\mathcal{U}
.

Then
F
and
\widetilde{F}
are quasiconformally conjugate, and this conjugacy agrees with
H
on
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{U}
. ∎

4.
Extracting dynamically natural complex box mapping

Let
f
be an atomic Siegel polynomial of bounded type with Siegel disk
\Delta_{f}
of bounded rotation type, and
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
be the set of critical points of
f
that do not land

1

in
\partial\Delta_{f}

We start with some preliminary results and definitions.

A set

is

if for every point

, one has that
f^{n}(x)\not\in\inter A
for all

.

Lemma 4.1

(When a union of puzzle pieces is nice)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a nice union of puzzle pieces of depths at most
k\geq 0
. If

is a puzzle piece of depth at least
k
that is not contained in any of the components of the first entry domain to

, then
\mathcal{Y}\cup Y
is a nice set.

In particular, the union of disjoint puzzle pieces of the same depths is a nice set.

For the definition of first entry domain, see
[
CDKvS
, Section 1.5.3]
.

(Note that in this lemma,

can be a component of the first entry domain.)

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Pick
x\in\partial(\mathcal{Y}\cup Y)
. To check that the union in question is nice, we distinguish two cases: 1)
x\in\partial\mathcal{Y}
and 2)
x\in\partial Y
.

In case 1), since
\mathcal{Y}
is nice,
f^{n}(x)\not\in\mathcal{Y}
for all
n\geq 1
. Furthermore,
f^{n}(x)\not\in Y
for all
n\geq 1
because otherwise, by the Markov property, a component of
\mathcal{Y}
that has

on its boundary would be of depth at least
k+1
, which is impossible by assumption.

In case 2),
f^{n}(x)\not\in Y
for all

because
Y
is nice, and
f^{n}(x)\not\in\mathcal{Y}
for all
n\geq 0
because
Y
does not lie in any of the landing domains to
\mathcal{Y}
and hence

is never mapped into a component of
\mathcal{Y}
.
∎

For a nice set

the domain of the first return map to

under

, and by
\mathcal{L}(A)
the domain of the first landing map to

. Note that
\mathcal{R}(A)\subset\mathcal{L}(A)
and
\mathcal{R}(A)=\mathcal{L}(A)\setminus A
. The following statements are useful in constructing complex box mappings.

Lemma 4.2

(First return to protected puzzle pieces)
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be two nice unions of finitely many puzzle pieces such that
\mathcal{X}\Subset\mathcal{Y}
. Suppose that each component
X
of
\mathcal{X}
has the following disjointedness property:

•

for every
k\geq 1
such that
\partial f^{k}(X)\cap\mathcal{Y}\neq\emptyset
, the puzzle pieces
f(X),\ldots,f^{k}(X)
are disjoint from
\mathcal{X}
.

If
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a union of components of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})\setminus\mathcal{X}
, then each component of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
is either a component of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, or is compactly contained in
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

First of all, observe that the union
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a nice set. Hence, the domain of the first return map to
\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a finite union of puzzle pieces. Further note that
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X})
, and hence it is enough to show that each component of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X})
is compactly contained in
\mathcal{X}
.

Let
P
be a component of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X})
, and
X
be the component of
\mathcal{X}
that contains
P
. Our goal is to show that
P\Subset X
.

Assume the contrary, i.e.,
\partial P\cap\partial X\neq\emptyset
. If
k\geq 1
is the first return time of
P
to
\mathcal{X}
, then
f^{k}(P)=:X^{\prime}
is a component of
\mathcal{X}
and
X^{\prime}\subset f^{k}(X)
. Since
\partial P\cap\partial X\neq\emptyset
, the same is true for
X^{\prime}
and
f^{k}(X)
, i.e.,
\partial X^{\prime}\cap\partial f^{k}(X)\neq\emptyset
. By assumption,
X^{\prime}\Subset\mathcal{Y}
, and hence
f^{k}(X)\subset\mathcal{Y}
(by the Markov property) and the boundary of
f^{k}(X)
intersects
\mathcal{Y}
. This is a contradiction to the disjointedness property.
∎

Now we need the following lemmas about the weak protection of critical puzzle pieces. Recall that a critical point
c\in\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
is called
combinatorially recurrent
if its orbit intersects every puzzle piece around
c
. Note that, since the rational lamination of
f
is empty,
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
contains no renormalizable critical points, and hence critical points can be recurrent only in a non-periodic way. Let us distinguish the following subsets of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
: the first one, which we call
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}_{r}(f)
, will be a set of all combinatorially recurrent critical points, while
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}_{nr}(f)
will stand for all combinatorially non-recurrent critical points. Obviously,

\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)=\operatorname{Crit}_{r}^{\star}(f)\sqcup\operatorname{Crit}_{nr}^{\star}(f).

The result, stated below as Theorem
4.4
, is similar to
[
DrSh
, Lemma 4.10]
. But since in the Siegel case the boundary of each puzzle piece intersects the Julia set of
f
in an uncountable set, its proof requires a much more delicate analysis than in the lemma cited above.

We say that a puzzle piece
P
is
weakly protected
if there exists a puzzle piece
Q
such that
P\Subset Q
.

Let
P_{n}\supset P_{n+k}
be two nested puzzles of depth
n
and
n+k
respectively. The puzzle piece
P_{n+k}
is referred to as a
child of
P_{n}
if

f^{i}(P_{n+k})\cap P_{n+k}=\varnothing\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for}\hskip 14.22636pt0<i<k\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptf^{k}(P_{n+k})=P_{n}.

Lemma 4.3

(Two Children Lemma)
.

For every
c\in\operatorname{Crit}_{r}^{\star}(f)
and
n\geq 0
, there exist at least two values
0<k_{1}<k_{2}
such that
P_{n+k_{1}}(c)
and
P_{n+k_{2}}(c)
are children of
P_{n}(c)
.

Proof.

The first child
P_{n+k_{1}}(c)
is given by the component of the first return domain to
P_{n}(c)
containing
c
. Since
f
is not renormalizable, there exists a smallest number
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{mk_{1}}(c)\notin P_{n+k_{1}}(c)
. Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest number such that
f^{mk_{1}+l}(c)\in P_{n}(c)
. We claim that the piece
P_{n+k_{2}}(c)
with
k_{2}:=mk_{1}+l
is another child of
P_{n}(c)
.

Suppose towards a contradiction that
f^{i}(P_{n+k_{2}}(c))=P_{n+k_{2}-i}(c)
for some
i<k_{2}
. If
k_{2}-i>mk_{1}
, then mapping by
f^{mk_{1}}
, we obtain

P_{n+l}(f^{mk_{1}}(c))\subsetneq f^{mk_{1}}(P_{n+k_{2}-i}(c))\subsetneq P_{n}(c),

which contradicts the fact that
P_{n+l}(f^{mk_{1}}(c))
is the component of the first return domain of
P_{n}(c)
containing
f^{mk_{1}}(c)
. For
1\leq j\leq m
, we cannot have

P_{n+jk_{1}}(c)\subsetneq P_{n+k_{2}-i}(c)\subsetneq P_{n+(j-1)k_{1}}(c),

since otherwise, we can map by
f^{(j-1)k_{1}}
to obtain,

P_{n+k_{1}}(c)\subsetneq f^{(j-1)k_{1}}(P_{n+k_{2}-i}(c))\subsetneq P_{n}(c),

contradicting the fact that
P_{n+k_{1}}(c)
is the first child of
P_{n}(c)
. Thus,
i=k_{2}-jk_{1}
for some
1\leq j\leq m
. However, this implies that

f^{(m-1)k_{1}+l}(P_{n+k_{2}}(c))=P_{n+k_{1}}(c),

and so

f^{mk_{1}}(c)\in f^{k_{1}-l}(P_{n+k_{1}}(c))\subset P_{n}(c).

This again contradicts the fact that
P_{n+k_{1}}(c)
is the first child of
P_{n}(c)
.
∎

Theorem 4.4

(Recurrent critical points are weakly protected)
.

For every
c\in\operatorname{Crit}_{r}^{\star}(f)
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
n\geq N
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
P_{n+k}(c)\Subset P_{n}(c)
, and
P_{n+k}(c)
is a child of
P_{n}(c)
.

Proof.

We simplify notation by denoting
P_{n}(c)=P_{n}
. By Lemma
4.3
, there exist a nested sequence

P_{0}=:P_{m_{0}}\supsetneq P_{m_{1}}\supsetneq\ldots

of successive second children (i.e.
P_{m_{i+1}}
is the second child of
P_{m_{i}}
). We will show that

m_{i+1}-m_{i}>m_{i}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for}\hskip 14.22636pti\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
P_{n_{i+1}}
be the first child of
P_{m_{i}}
. Then

m_{i+1}-m_{i}>n_{i+1}-m_{i}\geq m_{i}-m_{i-1}.

Proceeding by induction, suppose that

n_{i+1}-m_{i}\geq m_{i}-m_{i-L}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for some}\hskip 14.22636ptL\geq 1

Observe that

f^{n_{i+1}-m_{i}}(P_{m_{i+1}})\subsetneq f^{n_{i+1}-m_{i}}(P_{n_{i+1}})=P_{m_{i}}\subsetneq P_{m_{i-L+1}}\subsetneq P_{n_{i-L+1}}\subsetneq P_{m_{i-L}}.

(4.1)

Denote

j_{i+1}:=m_{i+1}-n_{i+1}>0.

We claim that

j_{i+1}>m_{i-L}-m_{i-L-1}.

Applying
f^{j_{i+1}}
to (
4.1
), we obtain

P_{m_{i}}\subsetneq f^{j_{i+1}}(P_{m_{i-L-1}})\subsetneq f^{j_{i+1}}(P_{n_{i-L-1}})\subsetneq f^{j_{i+1}}(P_{m_{i-L}}).

Since
P_{m_{i-L-1}}
is a child of
P_{m_{i-L}}
, we have
P_{m_{i-L}}\subseteq f^{j_{i+1}}(P_{m_{i-L-1}})
. Thus, for some
a>0
, we have
f^{j_{i+1}-a}(P_{n_{i-L-1}})=P_{m_{i-L}}
. Since
P_{m_{i-L}}
is a child of
P_{m_{i-L-1}}
, this implies that
P_{m_{i-L-1}}\subseteq f^{j_{i+1}}(P_{n_{i-L-1}})
. The claim follows. Therefore, we have

m_{i+1}-m_{i}>m_{i}-m_{0}=m_{i}.

By Theorem
2.5
, there exist
n_{0}\geq 1
such that
P_{n}\Subset P_{0}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Choose
i\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large so that
m_{i}\geq n_{0}
. Then

P_{m_{i+1}}\Subset P_{m_{i+1}-n_{0}}\subset P_{m_{i}}.

This construction can be done at every sufficiently deep level
N
.
∎

We will now use this result to show how to extract a complex box mapping associated to an atomic Siegel polynomial. The main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5

(Extracting Siegel box mapping)
.

Let
f
be a non-renormalizable bounded-type Siegel polynomial, and let
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)\subset\operatorname{Crit}(f)\cap J(f)
be the set of critical points of
f
that do not land in the boundary of Siegel disks. Then there exists a complex box mapping
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
such that:

(1)

the puzzle pieces of
F
are puzzle pieces of
f
;

(2)

\operatorname{Crit}(F)\subset\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)\subset\mathcal{V}
;

(3)

if the orbit of a point
z\in J(f)
intersects
\mathcal{V}
, then this orbit intersects
K(F)
;

(4)

F
satisfies the condition
K_{\text{well-inside}}(F)=K_{F}
from the definition of dynamical naturality (Definition
3.3
).

Proof.

The required box mapping will be constructed as a first return map to a carefully chosen nice puzzle neighborhood of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
. We construct this neighborhood by induction on the cardinality of the set
\operatorname{Crit}_{r}^{\star}(f)
. It will be convenient to start the whole construction at sufficiently large depth
s\geq 0
chosen so that

\text{if }\orb(c)\cap P_{s}(c^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\text{ for some }c,c^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f),\text{ then }\overline{\orb}(c)\cap\fib(c^{\prime})\neq\emptyset.

(4.2)

In words, this means that if the orbit of a critical point
c
enters a critical puzzle piece of depth
s
around another critical point
c^{\prime}
, then the orbit of
c
must accumulate on the fiber of
c
. Such a choice is possible because we have only finitely many critical points. Note that it is possible that
c=c^{\prime}
. In particular, if
c\in\operatorname{Crit}_{nr}^{\star}(f)
, then the orbit of
c
never re-enters
P_{s}(c)
. Furthermore, by increasing
s
if necessary, we can assume that

\text{if }c^{\prime}\in P_{s}(c),\text{then }c^{\prime}\in\fib(c).

(4.3)

In particular, the last assumption implies that
\fib(c)=\fib(c^{\prime})
.

In order to construct the required neighborhood of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
, we inductively define a sequence of sets
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\mathcal{Y}_{0}=\emptyset,\mathcal{X}_{1}\Subset\mathcal{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{m}\Subset\mathcal{Y}_{m}
,
m\leq\#\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}_{r}(f)
such that each pair
\mathcal{X}_{i},\mathcal{Y}_{i}
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
4.2
and the only components of
\mathcal{X}_{i}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
are critical puzzle pieces of depth at least
s
. Suppose
\mathcal{X}_{k}\Subset\mathcal{Y}_{k}
are defined. If all critical points in
\operatorname{Crit}_{r}^{\star}(f)\setminus\mathcal{Y}_{k}
eventually land into
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
, then we end the induction with the sets
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{X}_{k}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{Y}_{k}
. Otherwise, choose a critical point
c_{k+1}\in\operatorname{Crit}_{r}^{\star}(f)
whose orbit never intersects
\mathcal{X}_{k}
. By (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), this implies that the orbit of
c_{k+1}
never intersects
\mathcal{Y}_{k+1}
. Let
Y_{k+1}\Supset X_{k+1}\ni c_{k+1}
be a pair of puzzle pieces given by Theorem
4.4
. By that theorem, we can choose
Y_{k+1}
to be disjoint from
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
and of depth larger than the depths of the components of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
(and hence of
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
). In this way,
\mathcal{Y}_{k}\cup Y_{k+1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{k}\cup X_{k+1}
are nice set. Indeed, the orbit of
c_{k+1}
never enters
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
and
\mathcal{X}_{k}
, and hence
Y_{k+1}
, respectively
X_{k+1}
cannot be component of the first entry domain to
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
, respectively
\mathcal{X}_{k}
; the niceness now follows by Lemma
4.1
.

Finally, define
\mathcal{Y}_{k+1}:=\mathcal{Y}_{k}\cup Y_{k+1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{k+1}:=\mathcal{X}_{k}\cup X_{k+1}
. Let us check that
\mathcal{X}_{k+1}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{k+1}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
. Clearly,
\mathcal{X}_{k+1}\Subset\mathcal{Y}_{k+1}
. Let us check the disjointedness property. By construction, since the orbit of
c_{k+1}
never lands in
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
, the orbit of
X_{k+1}
has a trivial disjointedness property with respect to the components of
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
, namely,
\partial f^{k}(X_{k+1})\cap\mathcal{Y}_{k}=\emptyset
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. On the other hand, the orbit of
X_{k+1}
has the disjointness property with respect to
Y_{k+1}
by Theorem
4.4
. Note that the orbit of the boundary of
\mathcal{X}_{k}
never intersects
Y_{k+1}
by our choice of depths. Therefore,
\mathcal{X}_{k+1}
,
\mathcal{Y}_{k+1}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
. The induction step is justified, and the induction ends with sets
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\Subset\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
.

By construction, each point in
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}_{r}(f)
is either contained in, or is mapped to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Some of the non-recurrent critical points
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}_{nr}(f)
can also map to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Now let
\operatorname{Crit}^{\circ}_{nr}(f)
be the remaining non-recurrent critical points whose orbits do not intersect
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
(and thus, by assumption (
4.2
), also do not intersect
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
). For each such critical point
c
, let us choose a puzzle piece
c\in Q_{c}
of depth
\ell+1
, where
\ell
is the largest depth of puzzle pieces in
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
(note that
\ell>s
). Furthermore, since the points on the Julia boundary of Siegel puzzle pieces have trivial fibers (Theorem

), for every
Q_{c}
we can choose a puzzle piece

such that
c\in P_{c}\Subset Q_{c}
, i.e.,
P_{c}
is weakly protected by

.

Let us now define

\mathcal{X}:=\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{c\in\operatorname{Crit}^{\circ}_{nr}(f)}P_{c},\quad\mathcal{Y}:=\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{c\in\operatorname{Crit}^{\circ}_{nr}(f)}Q_{c}

By construction,
\mathcal{X}\Subset\mathcal{Y}
. By Lemma
4.1
,
\mathcal{Y}
is a nice set. Finally,
\mathcal{X}
satisfies the assumption of Lemma

, and hence if we define

to be

, then by Lemma

, the first return map
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
to
\mathcal{V}
has the desired structure of a complex box mapping. The asserted properties (1)–(3) follow by construction. Assertion (4) follows verbatim as in Step 2 of the proof of
[
DrSh
, Lemma 5.1]
using the fact that each puzzle piece in
\mathcal{X}
is weakly protected by a puzzle piece in
\mathcal{Y}
.
∎

Let us now show that the box mapping constructed in Theorem
4.5
is dynamically natural. For that, we need to check the condition that the Lebesgue measure
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)
is zero from the definition of dynamical naturality (Definition
3.3
).

Proposition 4.6
.

The box mapping constructed in Theorem
4.5
is dynamically natural in the sense of Definition
3.3
.

This proposition is an immediate corollary to the following much stronger statement:

Theorem 4.7

(Zero measure of accumulation on Siegel disk)
.

Let
f
be a Siegel polynomial. Then the set

J_{\Delta}:=\{x\in J_{f}\colon\omega(x)\subset\partial\Delta_{f}\}

has zero Lebesgue measure.

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

By construction of Siegel puzzle pieces,
K_{\text{off-crit}}(F)\subset J_{\Delta}
. The claim follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

The proof is based on the Lebesgue density point theorem and is similar to
[
Ru
, Lemma 3.12]
. We repeat the proof for the sake of completeness. The main ingredient is the following key theorem from
[
McM
,
Ru
]
:

Theorem 4.8
.

For every point
x\in J_{\Delta}
and scale
r>0
, there is a univalent map

f^{i}\colon(U,y)\to(V,c)

between pointed disks of bounded distortion, where
c\in\partial\Delta
is a critical point, the distance between
y
and
x
is of order
r
, and
(U,y)
is a pointed disk of bounded shape with diameter of order
r
. ∎

(Recall that a pointed disk
(U,y)
has bounded shape if there exists a constant
C>0
(that depends only on
f
) such that
\partial U
contains the disk of radius
t
centered at
y
and is contained in the disk of radius
T
, also centered at
y
, with
T/t\leq C
.)

Pick a point
x\in J_{\Delta}
and sufficiently small
r>0
. We will show that the round disk
\mathbb{D}(x,r)
contains a smaller disk
D
of radius comparable to
r
such that
D\subset F_{f}
. The claim follows by the Lebesgue Density Point Theorem.

By Theorem
4.8
, there is a univalent map
f^{i}\colon(U,y)\to(V,c)
and the disk
(U,y)
is contained in
\mathbb{D}(x,r)
. Since
\Delta_{f}
is a quasi-disk, there is a round disk
D^{\prime}
in
V\cap\Delta\subset F_{f}
of diameter comparable to that of
V
. Using the bounded distortion property, we can now lift the disk
D^{\prime}
to
U
. This will produce a disk (maybe smaller) of radius comparable to
r
that lies in
\mathbb{D}(x,r)\cap F_{f}
.
∎

5.
Proof of Main Theorem (
A
), (
B
)

Recall that if
f
is an atomic Siegel polynomial, then the Julia set of
f
is connected. Furthermore,
f
has a unique Siegel disk
\Delta_{f}
centered at
0
(a neutral fixed point) and has no other neutral or attracting periodic points in
\mathbb{C}
.

In this section, we prove Main Theorem (
A
), (
B
) in the following form:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f
be an atomic Siegel polynomial of bounded type. Then

(1)

all fibers of points in the Julia set
J(f)
are trivial, and hence,
J(f)
is locally connected;

(2)

J(f)
carries no invariant line fields.

For
f
as in the theorem above, let
F\colon\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
be the box mapping constructed in Theorem
4.5
. We have
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)\subset\mathcal{V}
. Recall that
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
is the set of critical points of
f
in
J(f)
that do not lie in the puzzle graph of any depth. Let
\mathfrak{s}
be the smallest depth of the puzzle graph that contains all points in
\left(\operatorname{Crit}(f)\cap J(f)\right)\setminus\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
. If the latter set is empty, we set
\mathfrak{s}=0
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

We start by proving the first assertion.

Let
z\in J(f)
be a point. If the orbit of
z
eventually lands in
\partial\Delta_{f}
, then
\fib(z)=\{z\}
by
[
Y
]
. So we can assume that
z
does not lie on the puzzle graph of any depth.

If the orbit of
z
accumulates on a point in
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
, and hence intersects
\mathcal{V}
infinitely many times, then by assertion
(3)
of Theorem
4.5
, this orbit intersects
K(F)
. Each point in
K(F)
has trivial fiber, hence
\fib(z)=\{z\}
.

The only case left to consider is if the orbit of
z
does not accumulate on
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
, and hence there exists a puzzle neighborhood
X
of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
so that
\orb(z)\cap X=\emptyset
. We can assume that each puzzle piece in
X
is of depth at least
\mathfrak{s}
.

There are two cases:

a)

there exists a point
w\in\omega(z)
that does not belong to the puzzle graph of any depth.

b)

\omega(z)
lies in the puzzle graph and intersects the repelling fixed points that are the landing points of the Siegel bubble rays used in the construction of the initial puzzle partition.

c)

\omega(z)
only intersects the grand orbit of
\partial\Delta_{f}
.

In case a), the idea is standard: there is a pair
P_{m}
,
P_{m+\ell}
of puzzle pieces of depths
m>\mathfrak{s}
and
m+\ell
so that
w\in P_{m+\ell}\Subset P_{m}
. Since the orbit of
z
accumulates on
z
, we can find a sequence
(k_{i})
so that
f^{k_{i}}(z)\in P_{m+\ell}
. For each
k_{i}
, we can pull back conformally the annulus
A:=P_{m}\setminus\overline{P}_{m+\ell}
by
f^{k_{i}}
to obtain a sequence of annuli
A_{i}:=P_{m+k_{i}}(z)\setminus\overline{P}_{m+\ell+k_{i}}(z)
around
z
. By conformality,
\operatorname{mod}(A_{i})=\operatorname{mod}(A)
. Since these annuli can be assumed to be nested, the Grötzsch inequality yields
\fib(z)=\{z\}
.

In case b), the claim follows as in the end of
[
DrSh
, Lemma 4.7]
using the fact that the accumulation points are repelling.

In case c), we can use the same the same argument as in a): Since the orbit of
z
accumulates only on
\partial\Delta_{f}
, it also accumulates on
c\in\partial\Delta_{f}
. By Theorem
2.4
, we can find a sequence of nested puzzle
disks

Q_{1}\Supset Q_{2}\Supset\ldots
containing
c
and shrinking to it. We can now repeat the argument from above.

Finally, for assertion (2), we decompose
J(f)
into 3 disjoint sets
A_{1},A_{2}
and
A_{3}
as follows:
z\in A_{1}
if
\omega(z)\cap K(F)\neq\emptyset
;
z\in A_{2}
if
\orb(z)
does not intersect some puzzle neighborhood of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
but
\omega(z)\subset\partial\Delta_{f}\neq\emptyset
; and
A_{3}:=J(f)\setminus(A_{1}\cup A_{2})
.

•

The set
A_{3}
has zero Lebesgue measure by a standard argument (the set of points in
J(f)
that does not accumulate on the recurrent critical points has measure zero). Hence,
A_{3}
supports no invariant line fields.

•

The set
A_{2}
has zero Lebesgue measure by Theorem
4.7
, and hence again supports no invariant line fields.

•

And finally, if
z\in A_{1}
, then
\orb(z)\in K(F)\neq\emptyset
(assertion
(3)
of Theorem
4.5
). By Proposition
4.6
and Theorem
3.7
,
K(F)
does not support any invariant line fields. Hence, so does
A_{1}
.

Altogether, we obtain that
J(f)
supports no invariant line fields.
∎

6.
Oriented Combinatorial Trees

A (combinatorial) tree
T=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},v^{\operatorname{root}})
is given by its vertex set
\mathcal{V}
, its edge set
\mathcal{E}
and its root
v^{\operatorname{root}}\in\mathcal{V}
. For
v\in\mathcal{V}
, denote by
\mathcal{E}_{v}\subset\mathcal{E}
the set of edges that connect to
v
. We say that
T
is
oriented
if every vertex carries an orientation: a fixed cyclic order on
\mathcal{E}_{v}
for all
v\in\mathcal{V}
. For any subset
X\subset T
, define

\mathcal{E}_{X}:=\bigcup_{v\in X\cap\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{E}_{v}.

Let
\Gamma
be a path in
T
(whose endpoints are contained in
\mathcal{V}
). Its length is denoted
|\Gamma|
. We say that
\Gamma
is
rooted
if it starts at
v^{\operatorname{root}}
. For
v\in\mathcal{V}
, let
\Gamma_{T}(v)
be the unique rooted path containing
v
with minimal length. The
height of
v
is defined as

\operatorname{height}(v):=|\Gamma_{T}(v)|-1.

If
v\neq v^{\operatorname{root}}
, the
joint of
v
is the unique edge
\operatorname{jt}(v)\in\mathcal{E}_{v}
contained in
\Gamma_{T}(v)
. In this case, we define
\operatorname{height}(\operatorname{jt}(v)):=\operatorname{height}(v)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a rooted path of length
0\leq K\leq\infty
. Then there are ordered sequences
\{v^{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}
and
\{e^{i}\}_{i=0}^{K-1}
of vertices and edges whose union is equal to
\Gamma
such that
v^{0}=v^{\operatorname{root}}
and
e^{i}=(v^{i},v^{i+1})=\operatorname{jt}(v^{i+1})
for
0\leq i<K
. We denote
\Gamma\sim\{v^{i}\}_{i=0}^{K}.
The set
\{e^{i}\}_{i=0}^{K-1}
is referred to as the
joint set of
\Gamma
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
{\widetilde{T}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}},\widetilde{\mathcal{E}},{\widetilde{v}}^{\operatorname{root}})
and
T=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},v^{\operatorname{root}})
be oriented trees. A
tree homomorphism

g:{\widetilde{T}}\to T
is a map such that

(1)

for
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
, we have
g(v)\in\mathcal{V}
;

(2)

for
e=(v,u)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
, we have
g(e)=(g(v),g(u))\in\mathcal{E}
;

(3)

g
is orientation-preserving on each vertex; and

(4)

g({\widetilde{v}}^{\operatorname{root}})=v^{\operatorname{root}}
.

If
g
is bijective, then it is called a
tree isomorphism
.

Definition 6.2
.

A
tree cover
g:{\widetilde{T}}\to T
is a tree homomorphism such that the following properties are satisfied.

(1)

For
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
, there exists
\deg(v)\geq 1
, referred to as the
degree of
v
, such that every
e\in\mathcal{E}(g(v))
has exactly
\deg(v)
preimages in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{v}
.

(2)

The set
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g)\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
of vertices whose degrees are at least
2
is finite.

(3)

For
e=(v,u)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
with
v\neq u
, we have
h(v)\neq h({\widetilde{v}})
.

A vertex
c\in\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g)
is said to be
critical
. Define
{\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g)
as the minimal subtree of
{\widetilde{T}}
containing
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g)
(by convention, we set
{\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g):=\{v^{\operatorname{root}}\}
if
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g)=\varnothing
).

Remark 6.3
.

A tree cover without a critical vertex is a tree isomorphism.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
g_{1}:{\widetilde{T}}_{1}\to T
and
g_{2}:{\widetilde{T}}_{2}\to T
be tree covers. If there exists a bijection

\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}:{\widetilde{T}}_{1}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g_{1})\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{{\widetilde{T}}_{1}^{\operatorname{crit}}}\to{\widetilde{T}}_{2}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g_{2})\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{{\widetilde{T}}_{2}^{\operatorname{crit}}}

that restricts to a tree isomorphism from
{\widetilde{T}}_{1}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g_{1})
to
{\widetilde{T}}_{2}^{\operatorname{crit}}(g_{2})
such that
g_{1}=g_{2}\circ\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}
, then
\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}
extends uniquely to a tree isomorphism
\phi:{\widetilde{T}}_{1}\to{\widetilde{T}}_{2}
such that
g_{1}=g_{2}\circ\phi.

Proof.

If
{\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}_{1}(g_{1})={\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}_{2}(g_{2})=\varnothing
, then
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are tree isomorphisms, and
\phi:=g_{2}^{-1}\circ g_{1}
. Uniqueness follows from the fact that the only tree isomorphism
\phi:T\to T
that fixes
\mathcal{E}_{v^{\operatorname{root}}}
is the identity (recall that
\phi
must preserve orientation at every vertex).

Proceeding by induction, suppose that the result holds for tree covers with at most
n
critical vertices. Suppose that
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
have
n+1
critical vertices. We modify these covers to reduce the number of critical vertices.

Choose a critical vertex
c_{1}\in{\widetilde{T}}_{1}^{\operatorname{crit}}
with maximal height with
d:=\deg(c_{1})\geq 2
. Denote the joint at
c_{1}
by
\operatorname{jt}(c_{1})
. The corresponding critical vertex for
g_{2}
is given by
c_{2}:=\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}(c_{1})
with joint
\operatorname{jt}(c_{2})
. Denote
\nu:=g_{1}(c_{1})=g_{2}(c_{2})
and
\operatorname{jt}(\nu):=g_{1}(\operatorname{jt}(c_{1}))=g_{2}(\operatorname{jt}(c_{2}))
.

Denote the set of edges at
c_{1}
in
{\widetilde{T}}_{1}
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}
. Let
g_{1}^{-1}(\operatorname{jt}(\nu))=\{\operatorname{jt}(c_{1})^{i}\}_{i=0}^{d-1}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}
, written in the cyclic order given by the orientation at
c_{1}
with
\operatorname{jt}(c_{1})^{0}=\operatorname{jt}(c_{1})
. Partition
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}
into
d
pairwise unlinked sets
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}^{0},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}^{d-1}
, such that for
0\leq i<d
,
g_{1}
maps
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}^{i}
bijectively onto
\mathcal{E}_{\nu}
, and
\operatorname{jt}(c_{1})^{i}
is the minimal edge for the cyclic order on
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}^{i}
. For
0\leq j<d
, define
T_{1}^{j}
as the connected component of

{\widetilde{T}}_{1}\setminus\bigcup_{i\neq j}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{c_{1}}^{i}

containing
\operatorname{jt}(c_{1})^{j}
. Observe that
g_{1}^{j}:=g_{1}|_{T_{1}^{j}}:T_{1}^{j}\to T
is a tree cover such that
g_{1}^{0}
has exactly
n
critical vertices (
c_{1}
is no longer critical), and
g_{1}^{j}
for
j\neq 0
is a tree isomorphism. Let
g_{2}^{j}:=g_{2}|_{T_{2}^{j}}:T_{2}^{j}\to T
be corresponding tree covers coming from
g_{2}
. By the induction hypothesis,
\phi
extends uniquely as a tree isomorphism between
T_{1}^{i}
and
T_{2}^{i}
for
0\leq i<d
.
∎

Definition 6.5
.

A
tree branched cover
h:T\to T
is a tree homomorphism that satisfies the following properties.

(1)

For
v\in\mathcal{V}
, there exists
\deg(v)\geq 1
, referred to as the
degree of
v
, such that every
e\in\mathcal{E}(h(v))
has exactly
\deg(v)
preimages in
\mathcal{E}(v)
.

(2)

The set
\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{crit}}=\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)\subset\mathcal{V}
of vertices whose degrees are at least
2
is finite.

(3)

There exists a finite subset
\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}=\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)\subset\mathcal{E}
such that for
e=(v,{\widetilde{v}})\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
h(v)=h({\widetilde{v}})
if
e\in\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}
, and
h(v)\neq h({\widetilde{v}})
if otherwise.

(4)

If
e=(v,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{v}
for some
v\in\mathcal{V}
, then there exists
e_{c}\in\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}
and
n\geq 1
such that
e=h^{n}(e_{c})
.

(5)

For
e=(v,{\widetilde{v}})\in\mathcal{E}
with
v\neq{\widetilde{v}}
, there exists
n\geq 0
such that
h^{n}(e)\in\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}
.

A vertex
v_{c}\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{crit}}
and an edge
e_{c}\in\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}
are said to be
critical
. Define
T^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)
as the minimal forward invariant subtree of
T
containing
\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)
(by convention, we set
T^{\operatorname{crit}}(h):=\{v^{\operatorname{root}}\}
if
\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)=\varnothing
).

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
h:T\to T
be a tree branched cover. Then
\operatorname{height}(h^{n}(v))
is non-increasing with
n\geq 0
, and is eventually
0
(i.e.
v
eventually maps to
v^{\operatorname{root}}
). Consequently, if
\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}_{v^{\operatorname{root}}}=\varnothing
, then
\mathcal{V}=\{v^{\operatorname{root}}\}
and
\mathcal{E}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{V}
. Observe that
|\Gamma_{T}(h(v))|\leq|h(\Gamma_{T}(v))|\leq|\Gamma_{T}(v)|
. It follows that
\operatorname{height}(h^{n}(v))
is non-increasing.

If
\operatorname{height}(v)=1
, then
\operatorname{jt}(v)\in\mathcal{E}_{v^{\operatorname{root}}}
. Thus,
h^{n}(\operatorname{jt}(v))\in\mathcal{E}_{v^{\operatorname{root}}}
for
n\geq 0
, and there exists
m\geq 0
such that
h^{m}(\operatorname{jt}(v))\in\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}_{v^{\operatorname{root}}}
. Then
h^{m+1}(v)=v^{\operatorname{root}}
.

Proceeding by induction, suppose that for all
u\in\mathcal{V}
with
\operatorname{height}(u)<\operatorname{height}(v)
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\operatorname{height}(h^{n}(u))<\operatorname{height}(u)
. By the induction hypothesis, we see that
|h^{n}(\Gamma_{T}(v))|<|\Gamma_{T}(v)|
for some
n\in N
. Thus,
v
eventually maps to
v^{\operatorname{root}}
.
∎

Definition 6.7
.

Let
h:T\to T
be a tree branched cover. The
generation
\operatorname{gen}(v)\geq 0
of
v\in\mathcal{V}
is the smallest number
n=\operatorname{gen}(v)
such that
h^{n}(v)=v^{\operatorname{root}}
.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
h:T\to T
and
\widetilde{h}:{\widetilde{T}}\to{\widetilde{T}}
be tree branched covers defined on
T=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},V^{\operatorname{root}})
and
{\widetilde{T}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}},\widetilde{\mathcal{E}},\widetilde{V}^{\operatorname{root}})
respectively. If there exists a bijection

\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}:T^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)\cup\mathcal{E}_{T^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)}\to{\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(\widetilde{h})\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{{\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(\widetilde{h})}

that restricts to a tree isomorphism from
T^{\operatorname{crit}}(h)
to
{\widetilde{T}}^{\operatorname{crit}}(\widetilde{h})
such that
\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}\circ h=\widetilde{h}\circ\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}
, then
\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}
extends uniquely to a tree isomorphism
\phi:T\to{\widetilde{T}}
such that
\phi\circ h=\widetilde{h}\circ\phi.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}_{v^{\operatorname{root}}}=\varnothing
, the result is trivial by Lemma
6.6
. Proceeding by induction, suppose that the result is true for tree branched covers whose total number of critical vertices and edges is less than
n\geq 1
. Let
c
be a critical vertex or edge of
h
with the largest height.

Suppose
c\in\mathcal{E}^{\operatorname{crit}}
. Denote

\mathcal{O}:=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}h^{-i}(c).

For
e\in\mathcal{O}
, write
e=(u_{e},v_{e})
so that
e
is the joint of
v_{e}
, and let
T_{e}
be the connected component of
T\setminus\{e\}
containing
v_{e}
. Define
T^{\prime}
as the connected component of

T\setminus\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{O}}T_{e}

containing
v^{\operatorname{root}}
. Then
h
restricted to
T^{\prime}
is a Fatou tree branched cover with one less critical edge.

Let
\widetilde{c}:=\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}(c)
. Proceed with the same construction as above, labelling the corresponding objects for
\widetilde{h}
by tilde. We again conclude that
\widetilde{h}
restricted to
{\widetilde{T}}^{\prime}
is a Fatou tree branched cover with one less critical edge. By the inductive hypothesis,
\phi_{\operatorname{crit}}
extends uniquely to a tree isomorphism
\phi:T^{\prime}\to{\widetilde{T}}^{\prime}
that conjugates
h
and
\widetilde{h}
.

For
e=(u_{e},v_{e})\in\mathcal{O}
, define
T_{e}^{\prime}
as the connected component of

T_{e}\setminus\bigcup_{d\in\mathcal{O}\cap T_{e}}T_{d}

containing
v_{e}
. If
h^{n}(e)=c
, then
h^{n+1}|_{T_{e}^{\prime}}
is a tree isomorphism from
T_{e}^{\prime}
to
T^{\prime}
. If
\phi
is already defined on

, then there exists a unique Fatou tree
{\widetilde{T}}_{\widetilde{e}}^{\prime}
attached via the edge
\widetilde{e}:=\phi(e)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
to
\widetilde{u}_{\widetilde{e}}:=\phi(u_{e})
such that
\widetilde{h}^{n+1}|_{{\widetilde{T}}_{\widetilde{e}}^{\prime}}
is a tree isomorphism from
{\widetilde{T}}_{\widetilde{e}}^{\prime}
to
{\widetilde{T}}^{\prime}
. In this case,
\phi
extends uniquely to a tree isomorphism from
T_{e}^{\prime}
to
{\widetilde{T}}_{\widetilde{e}}^{\prime}
by Proposition
6.4
(uniqueness is guaranteed by the fact that
\phi
must send

to
\widetilde{e}
). Since

we see that
\phi
extends uniquely to a tree isomorphism from

to
{\widetilde{T}}
so that it conjugates

and
\widetilde{h}
.

The argument in the case
c\in\mathcal{V}^{\operatorname{crit}}
is analogous, except the modification to disconnect copies of Fatou trees attached at iterated preimages of

is done as in the proof of Proposition
6.4
.
∎

7.
Fatou Trees

7.1.
Topological structure on oriented trees

By Carathéodory’s theorem, any full compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
with locally connected boundary can be continuously parameterized by
S^{1}
, and hence is orientable. Henceforth, we endow all such objects with counter-clockwise orientation.

Definition 7.1
.

A
Fatou tree

\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},V^{\operatorname{root}})
is a subset of
\mathbb{C}
with an oriented tree structure that satisfies the following properties.

(1)

Every
V\in\mathcal{V}
is a full compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
with locally-connected boundary and non-empty interior.

(2)

If
V,\widetilde{V}\in\mathcal{V}
,
V\neq\widetilde{V}
and
(V,\widetilde{V})\notin\mathcal{E}
, then
V\cap\widetilde{V}=\varnothing
.

(3)

If
e=(V,\widetilde{V})\in\mathcal{E}
, then
e
is the point in
\mathbb{C}
given by

\{e\}=V\cap\widetilde{V}.

Consider an infinite rooted tree path
\Gamma\sim\{V^{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
in a Fatou tree
\mathcal{T}
. Its accumulation set in
\mathbb{C}
is given by

8.
Topological Models of Atomic Siegel Julia Sets

Let
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an atomic Siegel polynomial of bounded type rotation number
\rho=\rho(f)\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})/\mathbb{Z}
. Recall that this means

satisfies the following properties:

(1)

has a fixed Siegel disk

of rotation number
\rho
.

(2)

has a trivial lamination of rational external rays.

Definition 8.5

(Combinatorial equivalence)
.

Two atomic Siegel polynomials
f,\widetilde{f}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
of bounded type are said to be

if

(1)

\deg(f)=\deg(\widetilde{f}\,)
,
\rho(f)=\rho(\widetilde{f}\,)
;

(2)

\operatorname{adr}_{\operatorname{crit}}(f)=\operatorname{adr}_{\operatorname{crit}}(\widetilde{f})
(see Definition
7.7
);

(3)

the orbits of each pair
c\in\operatorname{Crit}(f)\cap\inter(K_{f})
,
\widetilde{c}\in\operatorname{Crit}(\widetilde{f}\,)\cap\inter(K_{\widetilde{f}})
of the corresponding critical points land in

,
\Delta_{\widetilde{f}}
in the same conformal position with respect to the uniformizing coordinates.

The last condition implies that combinatorially equivalent bounded type Siegel polynomials

and

are conformally conjugate restricted to the interior of their Fatou sets. By Theorems
7.8
and

, this conjugacy can be extended to a global topological conjugacy.

Theorem 8.6

(Topological rigidity)
.

Let
f,\widetilde{f}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type. If

and
\widetilde{f}
are combinatorially equivalent, then they are topologically conjugate, and, restricted to the Fatou sets, this conjugacy is conformal.∎

9.
Proof of Main Theorem (
C
)

The proof of Main Theorem (
C
) splits into two steps: 1) to show that combinatorial equivalence implies topological conjugacy (which is conformal in the Fatou set); 2) to show that topological conjugacy implies quasiconformal conjugacy. This, together with Main Theorem (
B
), implies Main Theorem (
C
). This will conclude the proof.

Step 1) is done in Theorem
8.6
. In this section, we carry out step 2) and prove that topological conjugacy implies quasiconformal conjugacy, which must be, in turn, conformal. The main result of this section is the following theorem:

Theorem 9.1

(Conformal rigidity)
.

Let
f
and
\widetilde{f}
be a pair of atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type. If they are topologically conjugate and, restricted to the Fatou sets, this conjugacy is conformal, then
f
and
\widetilde{f}
are affinely conjugate.

Let us denote the topological conjugacy between
f
and
\widetilde{f}
as
\tau
, e.g.,
\widetilde{f}\circ\tau=\tau\circ f
. By assumption,
\tau
is conformal in the basin of infinity, and hence, coincides with the Böttcher coordinate there. Furthermore, again by assumption,
\tau
is conformal on the grand orbit of the Siegel disk
\Delta_{f}
.

Furthermore,
\tau\colon\partial\Delta_{f}\to\partial\Delta_{\widetilde{f}}
is quasi-symmetric because the maps
f
and
\widetilde{f}
are quasiconformally conjugate to the same Blaschke model.

We say that a homeomorphism
h\colon P\to\widetilde{P}
between two puzzle pieces of
f
and
\widetilde{f}

respects the boundary marking
if it extends continuously to the closures of
P
and
\widetilde{P}
, and this extension, restricted to
\partial P
, agrees with
\tau
.

We say that two puzzle pieces
P
of
f
and
\widetilde{P}
of
\widetilde{f}
are
corresponding
if
\tau(P)=\widetilde{P}
. The corresponding puzzle pieces are necessarily of the same depth and have the same combinatorial structure.

Lemma 9.2
.

For every pair of corresponding puzzle pieces
P
and
\widetilde{P}
, there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\phi_{P}\colon P\to\widetilde{P}
that respects the boundary marking.

Proof.

We work in the Blaschke models
\mathbf{f},\widetilde{\mathbf{f}}
for the polynomials
f
and
\widetilde{f}
. In this model, the closures of
P,\widetilde{P}
correspond to a pair of puzzle pieces
\mathbf{P},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
. In this way, it is enough to establish the claim of the lemma for a pair of the corresponding puzzle pieces
\mathbf{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
with the boundary marking
\boldsymbol{\tau}\colon\partial\mathbf{P}\to\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
. The lemma will then follow by the quasiconformal equivalence between the Blaschke model and the original coordinates.

The Siegel part of the boundary
\partial\mathbf{P}
consists of countably many analytic arcs meeting at a definite angle, while the parts of
\partial\mathbf{P}
in the basins of
\infty
and
0
consist of finitely many analytic arcs meeting at a definite angle as well.

Let
(b_{i})\subset\partial\mathbf{P}
be a collection of Siegel arcs landing at a point
p
(i.e.,
\lim_{i\to\infty}b_{i}=p
); denote by
t_{i}
a common point between
b_{i}
and
b_{i+1}
. Let
r
be the arc of an external ray in
\partial\mathbf{P}
landing at
p
. Assume that
p
is a repelling fixed point (we will discuss periodic and pre-periodic cases later). Choose a sequence
(s_{i})\subset r
of points linked by the dynamics, i.e.,
\mathbf{f}(s_{i+1})=s_{i}
; in this way,
\lim_{i\to\infty}s_{i}=p
. For a given
i_{0}
, we connect
s_{i_{0}}
to
t_{i_{0}}
with an analytic arc
a_{i_{0}}\subset\inter\mathbf{P}
that makes a definite angle with the arcs
b_{i_{0}}
and
r_{i_{0}}:=[s_{i_{0}},s_{i_{0}+1}]\subset r
. Since the angles between
b_{i}
and
b_{i-1}
are definite, we can choose
a_{i_{0}}
so that it also makes a definite angle with
b_{i_{0}-1}
. Furthermore, choose
i_{0}
large enough so that for all
i\geq i_{0}
, all the arcs
b_{i}
and
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
for
i\geq i_{0}
, as well as the curve
a_{i_{0}}
, lie in the domain of the linearizing coordinates of
p
. This allows us to construct a sequence
a_{i_{0}},a_{i_{0}+1},\ldots
of arcs by lifting
a_{i_{0}}
under the local dynamics near the repelling periodic point. In this way, we obtain a sequence
(R_{i})_{i\geq i_{0}}\subset\mathbf{P}
of closed shrinking curvilinear rectangles bounded by four quasi-symmetric arcs
a_{i},b_{i},a_{i+1},r_{i}
. In the same way, we construct the sequence
(\widetilde{R}_{i})_{i\geq i_{0}}\subset\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
of the corresponding shrinking rectangles bounded by the corresponding quasi-symmetric arcs
\widetilde{a}_{i},\widetilde{b}_{i},\widetilde{a}_{i+1},\widetilde{r}_{i}
.

By construction, we have a pair quasi-symmetric maps
\boldsymbol{\tau}\colon b_{i}\to\widetilde{b}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}\colon r_{i}\to\widetilde{r}_{i}
that come from the marking
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. Let
h_{0}\colon a_{i}\to\widetilde{a}_{i}
be any quasi-symmetric homeomorphism, and let
h_{1}\colon a_{i+1}\to\widetilde{a}_{i+1}
be its lift under the linearized dynamics in a neighborhood of
p
. These four maps gives a quasi-symmetric identification
H_{i}\colon\partial R_{i}\to\partial\widetilde{R}_{i}
that respects the marking on the two opposite sides:

H_{i}(z):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &h_{0}(z),&\text{if}&\quad z\in a_{i}\\
&\boldsymbol{\tau}(z),&\text{if}&\quad z\in b_{i}\\
&h_{1}(z),&\text{if}&\quad z\in a_{i+1}\\
&\boldsymbol{\tau}(z),&\text{if}&\quad z\in r_{i}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Extend this quasi-symmetric map to a homemorphism
H_{i}\colon R_{i}\to\widetilde{R}_{i}
that is quasiconformal in the interior of the rectangle. For
i\to\infty
, these maps glue nicely on the union
R_{p}:=\bigcup_{i\geq i_{0}}R_{i}
into a homeomorphism

H_{p}\colon R_{p}\to\widetilde{R}_{\widetilde{p}},\quad H_{p}|R_{i}:=H_{i},

where
\widetilde{p}
is the point corresponding to
p
. This a well-defined homeomorphism that is quasiconformal in the interior of
R_{p}
and that respects the boundary marking
\boldsymbol{\tau}
on
\partial R_{p}\cap\partial\mathbf{P}
.

If
p
is periodic or pre-peirodic, then we construct analogous maps by going to a suitable iterate that fixes
p
(and possibly shrinking the domain of the linearizing coordinates depending on the depth of
\mathbf{P}
). In this way, if
(p_{k})
are all the vertices on
\partial\mathbf{P}
where the Siegel bubble rays meet external rays, we get a collection of homeomorphisms

H_{p_{k}}\colon R_{p_{k}}\to\widetilde{R}_{\widetilde{p}_{k}}

quasiconformal in the interior of each
R_{p_{k}}
.

The domain
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{P}\setminus\bigcup_{k}\inter(R_{p_{k}})
is a finitely-sided closed topological polygon bounded by analytic arcs meeting at definite angles. On each of its sides, we have a quasisymmetric map (that respects the boundary marking on its portion within
\partial\mathbf{P}
). We extend this map to a homeomorphism
H_{0}\colon\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}
quasiconformal in the interior of its domain of definition.

Finally, we define the map
\Psi\colon\mathbf{P}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
as
H_{p_{k}}
in
R_{p_{k}}
for every
k
and as
H_{0}
in
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
. By construction, this is a homeomorphism that is quasiconformal in the interior that respects the boundary marking, as required.
∎

Using this lemma, we can prove the following proposition. The proof is similar to the proof of
[
DrSh

, it follows from the Spreading Principle, and thus is omitted.

Proposition 9.3
.

If
f

\widetilde{f}
are two topologically conjugate, atomic Siegel polynomials of bounded type, then in Theorem

one can choose dynamically natural complex box mappings

on

. ∎

Because the box mappings extracted for Siegel polynomials are non-renormalizable, Theorems

and
3.8
imply that

and
\widetilde{F}
are quasiconformally conjugate and support no invariant line fields on their non-escaping sets. Let us call this quasiconformal homeomorphism

Now we want to globalize

using the Spreading Principle
[

]

[
CDKvS

Recall that
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
is the set of critical points of

that do not lie in the puzzle graph of any depth and

is the corresponding set for

, and
\mathfrak{s}

Theorem 9.4

(Spreading Principle)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a nice puzzle neighborhood of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
of depth at least
\mathfrak{s}
, and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
be the corresponding neighborhood of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(\widetilde{f})
for
\widetilde{f}
. Suppose that there exists a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\phi\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
that respects the boundary marking induced by the conjugacy

. Then
\phi
extends to a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\Phi\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that:

(1)

\Phi=\phi
on
\mathcal{Y}
;

(2)

for each

,

\widetilde{f}\circ\Phi(z)=\Phi\circ f(z);

(3)

the dilatation of
\Phi
vanishes on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\DomL(\mathcal{Y})
, where
\DomL(\mathcal{Y})
is the union of
\mathcal{Y}
and all the landing domains to
\mathcal{Y}
;

(4)

\Phi(P)=\widetilde{P}
for every puzzle piece
P
that is not contained in
\DomL(\mathcal{Y})
,
and
\Phi\colon P\rightarrow\widetilde{P}
respects the boundary marking induced by
\tau
.

Proof.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the collection of puzzle pieces of depth
n\geq 0
for
F
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of all puzzle pieces in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
as well as all critical puzzle pieces that are not contained in
\mathcal{Y}
but do intersect
\operatorname{Crit}(f)
. This is a finite set. Using Lemma
9.2
, for each
C\in\mathcal{C}
let us pick a quasiconformal map

that respects the boundary marking induced by
\tau
; suppose

is the maximum over the quasiconformal dilatations of

for
C\in\mathcal{C}
.

Let
Q
be a component of
\DomL(\mathcal{Y})
, and

be the landing time of the orbit of
Q
to
\mathcal{Y}
. Since
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)\subset\mathcal{Y}
and the depth of
\mathcal{Y}
is larger than
\mathfrak{s}
, the map

is a conformal isomorphism. Therefore, we can pull back
\phi
and define a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism

by the formula

respects the boundary marking induced by
\tau

\phi_{Q}

Let us now inductively define a nested sequence of sets
Y_{0}\supset Y_{1}\supset Y_{2}\ldots
so that:

•

is the union of all puzzle pieces in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
;

•

is the subset of

consisting of puzzle pieces of depth
n+1

\DomL(\mathcal{Y})
.

For each puzzle piece

can lie in

(because otherwise

\widetilde{f}^{k}\circ H_{Q}=h_{f^{k}(Q)}\circ f^{k}
, where

was defined above. In this way,

is a

-quasiconformal map that respects the boundary marking induced by
\tau

Now define a sequence of homeomorphisms
(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
\Phi_{n}\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, as follows. Set

\Phi_{0}(z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tau(z)\quad&\text{ if }&z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\,\setminus Y_{0},\\
&h_{Q}(z)\quad&\text{ if }&z\in Q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

&H_{Q}(z)\quad&\text{ if }&z\in Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\text{ and }Q\subset Y_{n+1},\\

By the Gluing Lemma (see, e.g.,
[

-quasiconformal homeomorphism for each

. Note that the sequence

to obtain a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal map
\phi_{Q}\colon Q\to\widetilde{Q}
. This map respects the boundary marking. Using such maps component-wise, we obtain a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
T_{n}\colon\mathcal{Q}_{n}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{n}
that respects the boundary marking. Note that
K^{\prime}
does not depend on the depth of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.

Combining
T_{n}
and
H
, we obtain a
\max\{K,K^{\prime}\}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
G_{n}
between nice, shrinking neighborhoods
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(f)
and the corresponding neighborhoods
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
of
\operatorname{Crit}^{\star}(\widetilde{f})
. These maps respect the boundary marking. By the Spreading Principle (Theorem
9.4
), we can globalize this map to obtain a
\max\{K,K^{\prime}\}
-quasiconformal map
\Phi_{n}\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\widetilde{f}\circ\Phi_{n}(z)=\Phi_{n}\circ f(z),\quad\forall z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\,\setminus\,\mathcal{Y}_{n}.

Let
\Phi
be a sub-sequential limit of the sequence of uniformly quasiconformal maps
\Phi_{n}
. (Any two such sub-sequential limits must be equal as they coincide on an open and dense set of points.) Since the diameter of each component in
\mathcal{Y}_{n}
shrinks to zero as
n\to\infty
, the map
\Phi
provides the required quasiconformal conjugacy between
f
and
\widetilde{f}
. Since the set of points in
J(f)
that are never mapped to
\mathcal{V}_{1}
is zero, the obtained map will be
K
-quasiconformal, with the support of this dilatation contained in
K(F)
.

By Therorem
3.7
, since the box mapping
F
is dynamically natural,
K(F)
does not support any invariant line fields. Hence,
\Phi
must be conformal. And since it is a global conformal map of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, it must be affine. Theorem
9.1
is proven.
