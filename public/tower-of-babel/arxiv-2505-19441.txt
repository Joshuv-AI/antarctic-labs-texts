Fairness Practices in Industry: A Case Study in Machine Learning Teams Building Recommender Systems
Jing Nathan Yan, Junxiong Wang, Jeffrey M. Rzeszotarski, Allison Koenecke

Abstract
The rapid proliferation of recommender systems necessitates robust fairness practices to address inherent biases. Assessing fairness, though, is challenging due to constantly evolving metrics and best practices. This paper analyzes how industry practitioners perceive and incorporate these changing fairness standards in their workflows. Through semi-structured interviews with 11 practitioners from technical teams across a range of large technology companies, we investigate industry implementations of fairness in recommendation system products. We focus on current debiasing practices, applied metrics, collaborative strategies, and integrating academic research into practice. Findings show a preference for multi-dimensional debiasing over traditional demographic methods, and a reliance on intuitive rather than academic metrics. This study also highlights the difficulties in balancing fairness with both the practitioner’s individual (bottom-up) roles and organizational (top-down) workplace constraints, including the interplay with legal and compliance experts. Finally, we offer actionable recommendations for the recommender system community and algorithmic fairness practitioners, underlining the need to refine fairness practices continually.



Introduction

The widespread deployment of recommender systems (RS) in various sectors, bolstered by advanced machine learning (ML) models
(
Stray et al. 2024
)
, raises critical issues regarding inherent biases. These systems, utilized in domains such as social media
(
Lada, Wang, and Yan 2021
;
Belli et al. 2020
)
, e-commerce
(
Gretzel and Fesenmaier 2006
;
Zalmout et al. 2021
)
, and entertainment streaming
(
Zhao et al. 2019
;
Steck et al. 2021
)
, are susceptible to biases that can amplify psychological, ideological, and societal biases among users
(
Li, Ge, and Zhang 2021
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
. To mitigate these biases, researchers from ML, HCI, policy, and ethics have developed methods to assess and reduce their social impact
(
Barocas and Selbst 2016
;
Kay, Matuszek, and Munson 2015
;
Burrell 2016
)
, resulting in the creation of numerous toolkits for both research and industrial application
(
Bellamy et al. 2019
;
Google 2017
;
Vasudevan and Kenthapadi 2020
;
Cabrera et al. 2019
;
Yan et al. 2020a
)
.

However, industry stakeholders still grapple with the challenge of appropriately applying debiasing methods. Discrepancies in metric selection
(
Saxena et al. 2019
)
and conflicting ideals for model evaluations
(
Binns et al. 2018
;
Lee 2018
)
are common, exacerbated by the gap between academic theory and industry application. Despite existing toolkits, the unique demands of RS practitioners are not fully addressed, leading to cognitive dissonance and inadequate debiasing
(
Holstein et al. 2019
;
Madaio et al. 2022
)
. This paper investigates these challenges with a focus on RS pipelines.

RS has unique debiasing requirements.

Recommender systems, viewed as inherently social and collaborative entities
(
Stray et al. 2024
)
, operate within a landscape where fairness is not a static concept but a dynamic one, with meanings that shift across different contexts
(
Darlington 1971
;
Cleary 1968
;
Dwork et al. 2012
)
. The RS practitioner’s expert eye is uniquely crucial in navigating this fluidity. For example, in a video-sharing platform, while one approach to fairness might involve using straightforward viewer metrics for ranking videos, an alternative approach might incorporate randomness in search rankings to counteract the ”rich-get-richer” effect
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
wherein top-ranked videos get disproportionately more future views. This decision-making process—inherent in ML practice—requires a nuanced understanding of fairness in the specific context of RS, and is a challenge that goes beyond algorithmic solutions and delves into the realm of ethical decision-making.

Moreover, the complexity of RS is amplified by its operation in a multi-sided market, involving diverse stakeholders like content providers, items, and end-users, each with heterogeneous make-ups
(
Burke 2017
;
Abdollahpouri and Burke 2019
)
. RS practitioners, therefore, face their own unique challenge of considering multiple facets of debiasing, tailored to varied contexts and stakeholders. Beyond the technical aspects of debiasing, such as adjusting for demographic disparities among content creators, there is also a need to ensure the dissemination of trustworthy information, requiring a balanced approach that combines technical expertise with ethical considerations. The multi-stakeholder environment demands a comprehensive approach, where RS practitioners’ skills in handling diverse data sources and their deep understanding of algorithmic intricacies become indispensable. They are not just engineers of code but architects of fairness in an increasingly interconnected digital ecosystem.

Finally, the ubiquitous nature of RS and the potential for biased impacts on diverse user groups highlights another dimension where the role of RS practitioners is pivotal. The challenge lies not just in identifying biases, but also in predicting and mitigating their long-term effects such as feedback loops that could perpetuate bias
(
Burke 2017
;
Li, Ge, and Zhang 2021
)
. This task demands a level of foresight and adaptability unique to the ML field, where practitioners must be as proactive in their approach to fairness as they are reactive to emerging biases.

The multifaceted and critical role of RS practitioners in ensuring fairness underscores the importance of RS in various applications. Technical teams of RS serve as the central hub where technical innovation, ethical considerations, and practical applications intersect. Here, the core challenges of RS—such as fairness, bias mitigation, and long-term impacts—are actively addressed and navigated.

Hence, building on existing research,
our study places a unique emphasis on the nuances of RS ML perspectives within “big tech.” In particular, our work extends the prior literature by (a) studying technical team interactions with legal and compliance experts, which are not deeply covered in existing studies, and (b) focusing on holistic, bottom-up development practices from individual contributors rather than a top-down or fully cross-functional team’s viewpoint.
We present a case study that specifically explores how technical machine learning RS researchers and engineers interpret and leverage fairness in RS. This focus is critical in
understanding how to bridge these teams and fairness-focused industry teams
, especially given the existing challenges in selecting appropriate bias metrics
(
Saxena et al. 2019
)
and the notable gap between academic theories and industry practices, which often leads to conflicts among stakeholders regarding which metrics are most important when evaluating models
(
Binns et al. 2018
;
Lee 2018
)
. Although current toolkits assist in the debiasing process, they may not entirely align with the specific needs and cognitive models of RS technical practitioners
(
Holstein et al. 2019
;
Madaio et al. 2022
;
Gu, Yan, and Rzeszotarski 2021
)
. Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of human factors in ML systems for effective industry applications
(
Holstein et al. 2019
;
Madaio et al. 2022
;
Vaughan and Wallach 2020
)
, advocating for the integration of interdisciplinary perspectives, especially from psychology and ergonomics, to enhance the usability and efficiency of these systems.

Our research is based on semi-structured interviews with RS technical practitioners who are integral members of machine learning teams at large corporations.
These practitioners typically hold titles such as ’Machine Learning Engineers,’ ’Machine Learning Researchers,’ or ’Scientists,’ and are actively involved in building Recommender Systems. We examine how they integrate debiasing and fairness into their workflows and uncover their unique perspectives on these challenges. Our findings reveal the necessity for multi-type debiasing strategies and highlight organizational and privacy challenges faced by practitioners in large companies. In response to these findings, we call for more targeted research and the development of practical debiasing systems and encourage dialogue between qualitative and quantitative researchers. Our contributions include:

•

Conducting 11 semi-structured interviews with technical RS practitioners who work on commercial RS products deployed in big tech companies;

•

Leveraging thematic analysis to outline practitioners’ workflows in deploying RS and auditing biases;

•

Identifying key challenges in bias definition, detection, multi-stakeholder consideration, and organizational disconnects;

•

Proposing actionable steps for aligning bias definitions, early debiasing integration, multi-stakeholder fairness, and fostering common ground within and beyond the industry.

Related work

In the following sections, we first review the existing fairness ideas in practice, including fairness for recommender systems. Then, we review the implications of fairness for organizations and policy.

Background on Bias and Fairness

Many statistical definitions of fairness have been developed and used to detect biases at scale in machine learning systems
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Bolukbasi et al. 2016
;
Agarwal et al. 2018
;
Corbett-Davies et al. 2017
;
Zafar et al. 2017
)
. However, there is no single universally agreed upon standard for measuring and acting on bias, especially given the heterogeneity of applications and outcomes across ML
(
Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan 2017
)
.
Recently, metrics and techniques
(
Peng et al. 2022
)
have been integrated into usable open-sourced toolkits such as IBM’s AIF-360
(
Bellamy et al. 2019
)
, Google’s what-if toolkit
(
Google 2017
)
, LinkedIn’s LiFT
(
Vasudevan and Kenthapadi 2020
)
, Microsoft’s DoWhy
(
Sharma and Kiciman 2020
)
, Silva
(
Yan et al. 2020a
)
, FairVis
(
Cabrera et al. 2019
)
, SCODED
(
Yan et al. 2020b
)
and DataPrep
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
, aiming to facilitate in the detection and reduction of bias. Though many metrics exist the form of in systems and open-sourced toolkits, it is still up to the practitioner to choose which metrics are the most appropriate. The question of what fairness metric to choose is one that is fraught to start: prior work has theoretically shown the impossibility of satisfying multiple statistical definitions for quantifying bias
(
Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan 2017
)
. Past this, research has also shown that many practitioners found it difficult to choose a metric to use
(
Saxena et al. 2019
)
, and that practitioner demographics also influence preferences for fairness metric choice
(
Koenecke et al. 2023
)
. Benchmarking conducted by Friedler et al.
(
Friedler et al. 2019
)
reported that existing algorithmic intervention solutions correlate with each other and are sensitive to the composition of training data. As such, practitioners may struggle to find an efficient way to mitigate bias in their existing processes. Further issues may include not having disaggregated results, operating in variable application contexts, and using toolkits that fall behind industrial requirements
(
Barocas et al. 2021
;
Holstein et al. 2019
;
Madaio et al. 2022
;
Gu, Yan, and Rzeszotarski 2021
)
.

Fairness and bias are closely related but distinct. Bias refers to systematic errors or deviations in data or models producing skewed outcomes for certain groups
(
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
)
. Fairness, by contrast, concerns the ethical acceptability of those biased outcomes
(
Corbett-Davies et al. 2023
)
. Because formal fairness definitions can be mutually incompatible, organizations must choose which principles—equal treatment, equal outcomes, or other logics—to prioritize
(
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
;
Ekstrand et al. 2022
)
. This choice often intersects with legal obligations (e.g., anti-discrimination laws) and business goals
(
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
;
Ali et al. 2023
)
. Our study builds on this foundation by examining how fairness is practically construed and measured in RS workflows, focusing especially on cross-functional interactions (e.g., with legal teams).

Existing toolkits aim to detect and mitigate bias
(
Bellamy et al. 2019
)
, but actual uptake is limited by mismatched metrics and organizational barriers
(
Deng et al. 2022
)
. Deng et al. show that cross-functional collaborations—spanning engineering, product, and domain experts—improve toolkit integration
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
. Still, terminological gaps and siloed incentives can undermine collaboration
(
Corbett-Davies et al. 2023
;
Ali et al. 2023
)
. In RS specifically, Smith et al. encourage co-designing fairness metrics with providers for real stakeholder alignment
(
Smith et al. 2024
)
. Our study complements these approaches, focusing on bottom-up, grassroots efforts (often led by individual contributors) that enlist legal and other teams to embed fairness into RS.

Researchers have also come to understand that some factors cannot be quantitatively measured
(
Andrus et al. 2021
)
. To address this issue, researchers from the HCI community have developed taxonomies qualitatively categorizing different types of bias
(
Mehrabi et al. 2021
;
Barocas and Selbst 2016
;
Barocas, Hardt, and Narayanan 2017
)
, and identified the impact of unfairness in different sectors of society such as race and gender
(
Hamidi, Scheuerman, and Branham 2018
;
Binns 2018
;
Schlesinger, O’Hara, and Taylor 2018
;
Woodruff et al. 2018
)
. HCI researchers also have conducted systematic investigations of fairness and participatory practices in the real world
(
Holstein et al. 2019
;
Binns et al. 2018
;
Lee 2018
;
Saxena et al. 2019
;
Rakova et al. 2021
;
Delgado et al. 2023
;
Wang, Hertzmann, and Russakovsky 2024
)
. Veale et al.
(
Veale, Van Kleek, and Binns 2018
)
and Holstein et al.
(
Holstein et al. 2019
)
used mixed quantitative and qualitative methods to describe challenges faced in debiasing traditional ML models (e.g., the lack of established metrics for practitioners to use), and Smith et al. developed REAL ML
(
Smith et al. 2022
)
to help practitioners identify and articulate challenges and limitations. In contrast, our work seeks to understand how practitioners define bias in the context of
recommender systems
, and particularly the disconnects with existing fairness definitions used in other domains. We further focus on ad hoc debiasing as a part of organizational best practices which runs counter to some of the assumptions encoded in existing toolkits and academic best practices.

Fairness for Recommender Systems

Recommender systems pose unique fairness challenges due to multi-sided stakeholder needs—users, content creators, advertisers, and platforms
(
Burke, Sonboli, and Ordonez-Gauger 2018
;
Singh and Joachims 2018
;
Sonboli et al. 2021
)
. For instance, a user-centric approach might emphasize equal accuracy across demographics
(
Sonboli et al. 2021
)
, while provider-focused strategies seek equitable exposure for underrepresented creators
(
Abdollahpouri, Burke, and Mobasher 2019
)
. Biases like popularity effects can compound these issues
(
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
;
Sonboli et al. 2021
)
. Recent studies reveal that practitioners lack standardized procedures for auditing fairness in RS and often resort to ad hoc definitions
(
Smith, Beattie, and Cramer 2023
;
Beattie, Taber, and Cramer 2022
;
Holstein et al. 2019
)
. Our work extends these insights by exploring how individuals define and measure fairness and how they integrate input from legal/compliance perspectives.

Multiple stakeholders can have conflicting fairness values
(
Ekstrand et al. 2022
;
Smith et al. 2023
)
. A system might elevate historically marginalized groups but risk reducing overall efficiency, leading to tension among lenders, borrowers, or business stakeholders
(
Smith et al. 2023
)
. Ekstrand et al. call for holistic frameworks to balance such trade-offs
(
Ekstrand et al. 2022
)
. Similarly, Hutchinson and Mitchell argue no single metric can capture every moral dimension, requiring transparent prioritization
(
Hutchinson and Mitchell 2019
)
. Our findings similarly show teams grappling with these tensions—deciding whether to protect certain content providers despite potential negative impacts on user satisfaction or revenue.

We now discuss what makes RS a unique problem within the fairness literature. Mitigation algorithms have been proposed to reduce biases in ML systems via data pre-processing
(
Feldman et al. 2015
;
Calmon et al. 2017
;
Mehrabi et al. 2019
)
, in-processing
(
Kamishima et al. 2012
;
Beutel et al. 2019b
;
Zemel et al. 2013
;
Wu, Zhang, and Wu 2018
;
Zafar et al. 2017
)
, post-processing
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Bolukbasi et al. 2016
;
Pleiss et al. 2017
)
, or at all stages
(
Celis et al. 2019
)
.
However, fairness poses unique concerns in RS
(
Chen et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
compared with general ML systems, making it difficult to directly adapt these mitigation strategies.

First, recommender systems simultaneously serve multiple stakeholders
(
Abdollahpouri and Burke 2019
;
Burke 2017
;
Burke, Sonboli, and Ordonez-Gauger 2018
)
—for example, both end users who are reading content
(
Leonhardt, Anand, and Khosla 2018
;
Li et al. 2021
)
, and content providers
(
Stray et al. 2024
;
Mehrotra et al. 2018
)
. It is challenging to adapt existing bias definitions
(
Stray et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
from single-sided platforms to handle multiple stakeholders directly
(
Li, Ge, and Zhang 2021
;
Mehrotra, Xue, and Lalmas 2020
)
.

Second, despite the fact that recommender systems are a commonly used technology, the unique constraints of RS complicate fairness goals, as many existing metrics cannot be directly applied. Existing research focusing on multi-objective optimization
(
Burke 2017
;
Burke, Sonboli, and Ordonez-Gauger 2018
;
Mehrotra, Xue, and Lalmas 2020
)
and trade-offs in debiasing
(
Abdollahpouri et al. 2020
;
Abdollahpouri and Burke 2019
;
Rodolfa, Lamba, and Ghani 2021
)
require practitioners to balance externalities among stakeholders, potentially sacrificing certain groups in exchange for improved accuracy or reduced bias in others. Yet, recent work from Ekstrand et al.
(
Ekstrand et al. 2022
)
argues that sacrificing users from certain groups to equalize effective disparities might be not necessary, as utility is not often a zero-sum game.

Third, further challenges arise in practice when using existing computational methods due to the large volume of RS content, resulting in sparse user-content interaction data because users may only interact with a tiny subset of the overall content
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Kearns et al. 2019
;
Beutel et al. 2019b
;
Beutel et al. 2019a
;
Beutel et al. 2018
;
Lum, Zhang, and Bower 2022
)
. Moreover, as a recommender system reuses user-content interaction data generated from the recommendation process to further improve its results, it risks a feedback loop that amplifies disparities
(
Keller 2021
)
.

Industry and Organizational Studies

Fairness efforts do not occur in isolation; they must align with organizational and regulatory priorities. Scheuerman and Ali et al. show that responsible AI initiatives often require linking fairness to explicit business or product goals
(
Ali et al. 2023
;
Scheuerman 2024
)
. Employees can “individualize risk,” taking personal responsibility when formal mandates are weak
(
Ali et al. 2023
)
. Legal teams also play a key role, focusing on privacy or liability rather than proactive fairness
(
Veale and Binns 2017
)
. Our paper spotlights how practitioners interact with these legal and compliance experts, revealing the extent to which fairness is framed as “risk mitigation”—or more broadly as ethical responsibility.

The previous literature has investigated the organizational context of machine learning, identifying the roles different stakeholders play
(
Passi and Sengers 2020
;
Muller et al. 2019
;
Passi and Barocas 2019
;
Madaio et al. 2022
;
Madaio et al. 2020
)
and organizational efforts from internal and external sources to close gaps in accountability
(
Raji et al. 2020
)
for trustworthy systems
(
Brundage et al. 2020
)
. For example, Madaio et al.
(
Madaio et al. 2022
)
identified practitioners’ processes, challenges, and needs; they developed checklists with actionable and concrete items to build foundations for sustainable fairness in an organization. AI ethics researchers have additionally investigated the risks imposed by AI
(
Fjeld et al. 2020
;
Systems, Committee et al. 2021
;
Canca 2020
;
Olteanu et al. 2019
)
, ranging from algorithmic biases to the ethics of using personal data as a part of the production pipeline. These studies inform fairness practices for RS
(
Stray et al. 2024
)
as part of a broader organizational ecosystem.

Additionally, policy action has influenced fairness in recommender systems. The Digital Services Act in the European Union
(
Madiega 2020
)
authorized authorities to enforce yearly assessments of online platforms regarding fairness issues, illegal content, and disruption to human rights. In the United States, California’s 2022 state law AB 587
1
1

1

https://openstates.org/ca/bills/20212022/AB587/
aims to provide additional transparency about large platform recommendations to avoid disinformation and hate speech. Our work was conducted after new regulations were enforced, so we additionally aim to understand the implications and impact of these (and related) regulations on RS practitioners.

Methodology

In this paper, we aim to contextually inform how practitioners integrate fairness assessments into their daily practice. To gather data for this analysis, we conducted 11 semi-structured interviews over a three-week period in 2022. We interviewed RS practitioners from a variety of large companies that deploy highly visible RS.

Protocol
.
We invited participants via social media, emails, and snowball sampling. Participation was voluntary and was supervised by a university internal review board. Practitioners were compensated with Amazon Gift cards. All studies were conducted over remote video chats due to pandemic restrictions. We provided options to record audio, and video, or have experimenters take notes. Practitioners were required to agree to at least one of these options. Note-taking was added as some practitioners expressed concerns about accidentally disclosing confidential company information before their appointment. No such disclosures occurred in our interviews, though a disclosure mitigation process was developed and explained to participants. In total, we recruited 11 machine learning practitioners (averaging 5.36 years of experience) across seven large tech companies that serve billions of customers annually. Further details on participants are available in Appendix Table
1
.

Interviews ranged between 45 minutes to an hour. We started our semi-structured interviews with ice-breaking questions regarding the practitioner’s background, e.g., their years of experience, company background, and the recommender systems built by the practitioner. Practitioners were explicitly instructed to be as detailed as possible but to avoid any confidential information such as revenue or upcoming products. In the second stage, our questions zeroed in on their workflows and debiasing processes. We prepared a set of initial opening questions to encourage practitioners to share their general experiences and provide targets for follow-ups. We then proceeded to a set of questions about metrics, time allocation, and collaboration based on their responses. We closed sessions by inviting practitioners to comment on and rate their experiences working on models in terms of emotional support, work intensity, and stress level. Further details of interview questions can be found in Appendix Table
2
.

Data Processing
.
We seek to understand the debiasing practices of practitioners who develop and deploy large-scale recommender systems; to do so, we performed analysis of responses on two levels: workflow-centric and theme-centric.

First, we examined practitioners’ descriptions both in isolation and in aggregate with regard to their workflows by reading notes and transcripts. We focused on identifying commonalities among participants. At the same time, we also flagged outlier cases in our notes for further examination. We identified key stages practitioners shared in building recommender systems, and the core set of collaborators and external teams with whom they interacted (so as to construct a flow model
(
Wixon, Holtzblatt, and Knox 1990
)
of communications). Data were analyzed during collaborative meetings between authors, with interviewers summarizing their sessions and outlining notes/transcripts relevant to each session under inspection. We then collaboratively sketched and refined models describing both the general flow of information and data during model development as well as the specific debiasing processes followed by practitioners. Findings are described in
RS Workflow and Debiasing Process
.

Second, we conducted thematic analysis to identify core challenges faced by practitioners. We first converted scripts and notes into items on a Miro web board for aggregation and analysis. Authors conducted two coding passes through all quotes and reflections, developing a large set of initial theme groups. In the second round, authors aggregated thematic groups, merging similar themes and recategorizing elements through negotiation. Lastly, through another open discussion, authors broke down themes that had a significantly larger number of notes into finer granularity and placed any remaining unlabeled items into categories. When finalizing results, authors summarized the high level themes identified and pulled representative quotes from the grouped items. Findings about RS practitioners are described in
Challenges Individual RS Practitioners Face in Debiasing
,
and findings about practitioner interactions with other teams are described in
Organizational Challenges Faced by Practitioners
.

In this section, we describe our findings on RS practitioner workflows, and how they integrate debiasing and fairness into their labor. These findings emerge out of our workflow-centric analysis described in the previous subsection.

The RS Workflow

We found that our practitioners had several common key stages in their workflow, including data gathering, data preparation, model construction, model validation, real-world testing, and online model serving—depicted in Figure
3
. Debiasing and investigation of fairness issues occurred at several points throughout the process, corresponding to shifts to new development stages (see yellow arrows pointing to gray boxes in Figure
3
). These transition points were convenient locations for debiasing because they often included transitions between different team members or general organizational meetings.

Practitioners described workflows that evolved from an offline phase (gray boxes in Figure
3
) into an online phase (green boxes in Figure
3
) throughout development. In the offline phase, our practitioners coordinated with the data team to receive access to the necessary data and interacted with policymakers from the legal team to review their requests. The data team negotiated technical details such as API designs for collecting data and data cleaning. This stage aligns with that of general ML pipelines.
Practitioners then spent much of their time implementing new models and conducting rounds of offline model validation. Based on chosen performance metrics (for instance, loss functions), they optimized their model. All agreed that they invested more than half of their work time in these activities. For example, P5 mentioned, “This is the core of our daily work. I spent more than 60% time on model work.” Practitioners were aware of potential bias issues at this stage. Many reported creating hand-crafted tests to identify potential sources of bias. We will elaborate on these tests in the following section.

After offline development, practitioners shipped their model and moved to an online validation phase, where real-world user behavior data and newly generated user-content interaction data come in as users engage with the recommendations. A fairness team, which exists in all practitioners’ organizations under various names (such as Responsible AI), audits and provides feedback to practitioners. Simultaneously, practitioners monitor the model’s performance and might revisit data pre-processing to change their training data (e.g., performing feature transformations, or asking for more features) and incorporate the feedback from the fairness team. Most of our practitioners mentioned that they had discretion as to whether to integrate fairness feedback.

Practitioners were aware of their limited resources, attempting to estimate the relative impact of potential fairness issues for the work required to resolve them, all while faced with expanding job requirements. As the
owner
of a recommender system in an organization, practitioners reported numerous continued engagements such as monitoring the model’s performance. For example, P2 mentioned “We rotated to on-call for any emergency production disruption caused by our model.” Practitioners primarily were concerned about factors such as uptime and did not consider fairness to be a similar level of disruptive emergency necessitating urgent correction.

RS Debiasing Process and Time Allocation

As depicted in Figure
3
, practitioners generally followed three major phases in their debiasing efforts while developing and deploying recommender systems: the prototyping phase, the internal phase, and the collaborative phase. When prototyping, practitioners interacted with the legal and data teams to obtain data access for building the model. Normally, practitioners already had a candidate set of biases they thought might exist in the training data. They employed experience working with similar data, and did not generally cite academia as a source for their candidates. Likewise, they tended to use metrics they had developed in the past as unit tests for their hypothesized biases. Later, after the model was built, practitioners moved to an internal debiasing phase where they validated their metric results with deployments and searched for unexpected results through hand-crafted analysis and revisitation. Participants expressed that there are several rounds of iteration at this stage to find as many types of bias as possible within the timeframe.

After the model was shipped to online testing, the debiasing process entered the third (collaborative) phase with the fairness team and users in the loop. The fairness team offers feedback based on their guidelines and real-world validations. Practitioners incorporate their feedback and integrate user feedback and data to further seek out “deep” hidden biases. They ship the model to production and monitor potential bias reports from other teams and end-users. This process is, again, highly iterative and requires much manual analysis. Our practitioners mentioned that, in each project, they eventually compose over five new metrics tailored to the specific system and context.

Practitioners universally described their workflows—both the general workflow and debiasing process—as highly iterative and time-sensitive. As a result, most also expressed that they felt they had a very limited amount of time to focus on the debiasing process. Nine reported that they spend roughly 5% percent of their work time on debiasing, and two said they spent up to 10% of their time.
When prompted to reflect on this allocation, seven indicated that more time would be helpful, but were neutral about investing more time; and, two thought that they should allocate much more time. P7 pointed to a need to balance the potential impacts of their platform with other benefits should their time be spent on other tasks, stating, “The negative effect spurred from the platform I worked for before is not so ‘bad.’ We don’t have the incentive to invest half of our time [that much] to comprehensively debias.” P1 mentioned that “Even though I wish I could have more time on this, I understand why this proportional[ly] makes sense. From the perspective of our org, prioritizing efforts on improving ‘important’ metrics brings more impact.” Bias issues neither rose to the level of a disruptive emergency, nor did they not earn prioritization from management in evaluating project outcomes.

Some practitioners stressed that flexible time spent on reading and keeping up to date with research might help them to dive deeper into existing mathematical frameworks, permitting them to improve on their ad hoc approaches to metrics. P1 described it thusly: “Even though I think existing metrics might diverge from the recommender systems’ needs, looking at their proofs and also inductions potentially might help.” On the other hand, while practitioners saw the potential benefits of keeping up with academic literature, they rarely had the spare time to prioritize it.

Challenges Individual RS Practitioners Face in Debiasing

In this section, we use thematic analysis to explore how practitioners work with metrics and other debiasing techniques from academia, focusing on sources of friction faced by RS practitioners internal to their teams. We discuss inter-team organizational challenges in the following section. Figure
3
breaks down the specific challenges reported by participants.

Practitioners Find Difficulty in Defining Bias in Recommender Systems

In our interviews, we asked participants to detail how they define bias and the metrics that they use in their day-to-day work. Conceptually, they define bias in two general categories. The first category is bias that violates existing established rules, e.g., discriminating against candidates from certain races. Many internal automated tools and platforms have been proposed to help with this category of biases
(
Bellamy et al. 2019
;
Google 2017
;
Vasudevan and Kenthapadi 2020
)
, which are used by our practitioners with relative ease. The second category, content-sensitive debiasing, is perceived as more difficult and nuanced. One common frustration was that practitioners only have a vague goal (for example, protecting content-creator users), but are not armed with specific tools in their organization to reach the goal, or even a specific standardized method with which to approach the problem.

While definitions and taxonomies have been posed in the fairness research literature for evaluating biases
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
, the practitioners we interviewed largely did not rely on them: practitioners found them hard to directly adapt and ill-suited for their intuitive workflow. For example, P9 reported: “Even though we have a list of categories as you mentioned in research papers, we need to come up with a clear formula and implement it ourselves. It is really domain-specific.”

Internally, there was also a limited amount of documentation that (1) clarified the debiasing goal in concrete mathematics, and (2) documented prior accumulated work from the group. We asked practitioners whether they re-used any existing metrics built internally. Half of our practitioners cited difficulties in doing so. One major reason was that there is no tradition of bias documentation akin to the way that code is documented in an organization. P7 mentioned, “For sure we can find their code, and comments, but without some context archived, for example, some documents, we are not sure whether we can use [them] directly,” and “Documentation is a good idea, it reminds me… however, I am not sure how specific of my debiasing should be included in the documentation…” (P3) The lack of established procedures in a fast-moving recommender system pipeline creates challenges for developing organizational knowledge.

Interestingly, interviewees pointed out that there was more of a “what to debias” question in recommender systems compared with traditional ML systems due to the nature of their design. In a traditional pipeline, fairness assessment focuses on one particular target (for example, ensuring demographic debiasing). However, all of our practitioners expressed that they have to perform more careful and complicated debiasing tasks. For example, they needed to consider both demographic fairness for job seekers from one region, while simultaneously ensuring that advertisers are operating reasonably on the platform in other regions due to different regulatory requirements (though they were unable to detail their debiasing scope due to non-disclosure policies). P3 pointed out:
“Debiasing for multiple purposes is crucial in ensuring a fair and unbiased platform for all stakeholders. It’s not just about the debiasing of content creators based on region, gender, and religion […], but also about addressing other factors that can impact the platform […]. For instance, we need to make sure that products […] are supported fairly, excluding any subsidies as an effort to win or dominate the market they may receive from governments.”

Practitioners Must Account for Debiasing Across Large Numbers of Groups

Prior research emphasizes the dual role of recommender systems in simultaneously connecting and serving multiple stakeholders, such as content providers and consumers
(
Burke 2017
;
Li, Ge, and Zhang 2021
)
. This multifaceted interaction introduces complex challenges, especially in the context of debiasing. Practitioners we interviewed commonly adopted a trade-off strategy due to the heterogeneous and interoperative nature of their systems, often prioritizing one user group over others. However, identifying the criteria for these trade-offs remains a challenge, as highlighted by P9’s statement, “Tradeoffs of a different group of users, or accuracy of the recommendations are hard to be decided,” and P1’s observation of a lack of a ”compelling [Standard Operating Procedure] for those decisions.” This practice, while prevalent, contrasts with recent academic findings suggesting that multi-objective optimization might not always adversely affect other user groups
(
Ekstrand et al. 2022
)
, indicating a disconnect between industry practices and academic research.

A significant challenge identified by practitioners involves ensuring fairness across a wide range of groups within recommender system datasets. Current metrics often fail to address the complexities arising from the large number of groups involved (and the ensuing intersectional groups). P2 described this challenge: “It’s hard to manage disparities between the numerous groups. With thousands of groups to consider, it becomes nearly impossible to ensure uniformity across all.” This sentiment was echoed by P9, who noted, “Most metrics are designed with the assumption of a few groups… They don’t cater to intra-group-level debiasing, making our adaptation efforts cumbersome.” Additionally, P7 highlighted the computational difficulties in handling sparse datasets resulting from subgroup division: “Most of the training data is too sparse, and it is hard for us to enforce the disparities between groups. I cannot look through thousands of groups to make sure that holds.” These insights underline the formidable task of ensuring fairness across a diverse array of groups, a challenge that is central to the operation of recommender systems in the industry.

Practitioners Describe Bias Detection As Requiring Ad-hoc Metrics and Tedious Labor

Practitioners engaged in building recommender systems detailed their approaches to bias detection in RS, often creating bespoke metrics in collaboration with their teams. P2 and P3 highlighted the personalized nature of these metrics, with P2 noting, “We proposed our own definitions of bias,” and P3 adding, “Most of the metrics we developed are intuition-based.” Such approaches underscore the reliance on practitioner expertise and intuition in identifying biases, as echoed by P6: “In general, I would like to write some hand-crafted codes to validate my hypothesis. Experience is a key to debiasing sometimes,” despite the availability of internal debiasing tools.

A significant challenge noted was the “catch-22” of bias metrics within RS. Practitioners reported that while biases are embedded in models, standard metrics often fall short in detecting them. This paradox leads them to leverage intuition and experience in initial bias mitigation, followed by iterative refinement through user feedback:

[P1]: It is not an easy job […], so we need to start from somewhere. After doing some debiasing based on your experience, we look forward to getting feedback from real-world users to further debiasing.

[P7]: It sounds a bit contradictory. We have a model first and keep debiasing the model based on our user’s new data. It although risks bringing in some confirmatory bias, it works for now.

The iterative nature of addressing biases, as described by P7, often involves writing numerous SQL queries for abnormal cases, highlighting the tedious aspect of this task.

Participants also shed light on the challenges of bias identification during user-content interactions in RS. Inherent biases from training data can inadvertently influence RS. In turn, systems driven by user feedback loops can amplify existing biases. This situation is particularly acute in RS, given the volume of data and the subtleties of human interactions. P1 and P2 emphasized the difficulty in uncovering these hidden biases, with P1 stating, “As the bias definitions are so different, and there are so many different user groups, those biases are hiding well behind the recommender systems that we built,” and P2 reflecting, “Most of the time, we have to look carefully to find those hidden biases. It is not fun.”

Despite finding academic metrics less directly applicable (especially given the time constraints of their workdays, as discussed earlier),
practitioners acknowledged the value of best practices and guidelines in providing a foundation and confidence in their decision-making processes. P7 and P9 discussed the utility of these resources:

[P7]: Though sometimes [checklists] are not directly related, I can still check those and they help me think through my reasoning. It made me somehow more confident in my decision.

[P9]: [Checklists] offer more than technical perspectives, and some of those guidelines are robust, [but] they are less domain-specific…

Organizational Challenges Faced by Practitioners

From our thematic analysis, we also locate another primary source of friction: specifically, identifying barriers and disconnects in terms of organization and governance.

Data Privacy and Governance

Due to the sensitive nature of end-user data, practitioners regularly interact with their legal team and data team to confirm the scope of data that can be gathered. With increasing surveillance from authorities and global concerns over privacy on RS, one might expect the relationship between practitioners and internal policymakers to be fraught. However, the internal politics of the situation is much more direct. P2 reported, “If they said no, then it is a clear No, and we know the reasons and risks behind their decision,” and P3 stated, “Their guidelines are very clear, particularly when compared with fairness guidelines, which we don’t really have…” Clear and open guidelines from legal made it easier for practitioners to work with them. Most of our practitioners acknowledged the role of the legal team in avoiding risks. For instance, P2 mentioned that the “legal team serves as the gatekeeper to keep us away from legal risks, and help us comply well with our company’s vision and values.” Additional notes on interactions with data teams can be found in the Appendix.

Practitioners mentioned that most of the concerns raised by their legal teams touch on human rights and privacy (e.g., through the EU Articles
(
Madiega 2020
)
and US Digital Services Oversight and Safety Act of 2022
2
2

2

https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/6796/text
). Recent privacy regulations also served as a baseline to guarantee that sensitive data is not used in practice, becoming de facto standards for practitioners interacting with legal teams. Seven practitioners mentioned that sensitive information such as race, gender, and employment are not shared, and personal information is obfuscated, so they cannot de-anonymize data.

Fairness Lingua Franca

Engaging with external fairness teams revealed a nuanced challenge: attaining mutual understanding and converging perspectives. Practitioners valued the fairness teams’ feedback but sometimes struggled with its practical integration due to a few key complexities:

Firstly, inherent challenges in RS deep learning foundation models sometimes made feedback from fairness teams difficult to implement. P9 observed, “sometimes […] challenge is fundamentally unsolvable, rooted in the deep learning models.” P1 shared, “Their feedback could be something I agree with, but I cannot solve[…].”

Secondly, the multidisciplinary nature of fairness teams, with diverse academic backgrounds, occasionally led to differing use of terminology, affecting inter-team communication. P9 commented on the influence of varied backgrounds and perspectives on bias understanding: “sometimes I don’t understand some phrases they used in their feedback, for example, ‘inductive bias from recommender systems,’ and need to ask for more explanations.” P6 added, “Some of my colleagues are from the field of psychology and communication, and some of those terms I didn’t know before.”

Thirdly, practitioners desired clearer guidance from fairness teams, particularly regarding model development. P7 said, ”Once they mentioned a certain aspect of my model might introduce bias, I wished to understand the reasoning.” P1 echoed, “Our collaborations could be streamlined if there were guidelines as clear as those from legal teams.”

Finally, involving the fairness team earlier in the process could foster smoother collaborations. P9 suggested, “Early involvement of the fairness team would have facilitated a closer working relationship.” P2 noted, “Sometimes there’s a lag in syncing up; feedback might refer to previous versions of our recommender system designs.” Our workflow models (Fig. 1-2) highlight the advantages of early engagement, similar to that with legal teams, in harmonizing interactions and fostering better understanding and consensus. Despite these challenges,
RS practitioners held overall positive attitudes towards experts from fairness teams, acknowledging the invaluable guidance from them
. For instance, P9 also praised their early detection abilities, noting, “They’ve pointed out potential pitfalls early […].”

Calls to Action

In this section, we map the pain points uncovered in our interviews to calls for action, targeted towards RS and fairness communities alike.

Aligning on Bias Definitions with Internally Consistent Documentation

In our interviews, we noticed numerous cases where disconnects occurred due to a lack of understanding of context, project scope, and applications between various members of the institution. Teams are composed of individuals from a wide variety of personal backgrounds, numerous project stages are spread over time, and systems are improved “in motion” after deployment when effects may be hard to predict and can potentially self-reinforce in feedback loops. At the same time, engineers and members of other teams such as fairness and legal are often serving multiple roles in organizations. Fairness teams, in particular, may be overburdened with multiple projects, leading to complicated scenarios where they are not fully aware of project context and status as they move from assessment to assessment.

This is worsened by challenges in documentation, as pointed out by numerous practitioners. While there are standard practices for documenting and approving code changes, there is comparably little infrastructural support for fairness definitions and debiasing. Heger et al.
(
Heger et al. 2022
)
recently supported the idea of data documentation following datasheets for datasets
(
Gebru et al. 2021
;
Papakyriakopoulos et al. 2023
)
, and suggested a set of principles for such documentation. An internal template for debiasing documentation for recommender systems can help to improve the process in the immediate term and also accumulate organizational knowledge.

Considering that recommender systems involve massive amounts of content, large user bases, rapidly changing environments, and large teams of developers, it is surprising that practitioners in our interviews did not identify stronger support for maintaining team cohesion and situational awareness during project development. While collaboration was explicitly cited as a stage of project debiasing by practitioners, it did not receive specific technical or procedural support. We believe there is a need for investigations of collaboration and information exchange during recommender system development and debiasing, leading to the development of novel systems. Such systems might help to maintain situational awareness across teams and perhaps even “bug track” issues related to fairness and bias.

Improving Bias Detection by Integrating Debiasing Early and Often

On a corporate level, we found that practitioners encountered competing incentives for integrating debiasing into their work schedules. While the practitioners’ primary job responsibilities were to create and manage recommender systems, every practitioner interviewed expressed both a personal interest and an explicit requirement to investigate bias in their models.
There could be high-efficiency returns on investment in opportunities for practitioners to integrate debiasing more holistically in their process such that they deliver wholesale improvements.

We noticed many cases where practitioners had to backtrack or iterate using data-driven strategies to detect hidden biases. Had they invested additional time during project formation and received support from fairness teams earlier, perhaps such issues could have been detected when changes would have been easier to make. We see two potential avenues for fostering this change. Similar to the previous subsection,
we propose a need for increased research on protocols and procedures for improving fairness. More importantly, we propose additional investigation into the organizational context of recommender systems in order to identify institutional-specific barriers beyond the scope of our investigation.

Enforcing Multi-stakeholder Fairness Optimization via Investment

To tackle the challenges involved in debiasing RS, the corporation must invest intellectual resources into researching—or working to implement academic research on—several key areas. Firstly, it is necessary to develop more comprehensive optimization approaches—considering multiple objectives—to balance the trade-off between different user groups. Secondly, further investigations into group-wise fairness performance comparisons should address the ”numerous groups scenarios” in RS. Thirdly, there should be an increased focus on intra-group-level debiasing to align with the diversity in the data.

Additionally, the development of standard operating procedures (SOPs) for making trade-off decisions between user groups is highly encouraged. These SOPs should take into account the various ethical considerations involved in making such decisions, and ideally, a committee of members from diverse backgrounds can lead the SOP draft.

Overcoming organizational challenges by fostering common ground

Within-Industry Relationships

In our findings, we identified cases where disconnects between fairness team members and practitioners led to failures in ameliorating bias issues. While legal teams offered explicit guidance and directives to practitioners which were followed, the nature of fairness feedback—reliant on nuance and not necessarily immediately applicable to complex models—proved much harder to parse and actualize in systems. Combined with the fast-paced nature of recommender system development, this led to some disconnects between practitioners and fairness teams despite mutual agreement on the importance of fairness and bias-free results.

These disconnects were inevitable. In our interviews, the fairness teams came from a broader academic background and had their own sets of expertise to capture the issues they observed in the system. On the other hand, practitioners lacked the broad interdisciplinary expertise represented on fairness teams, resulting in a disconnect in fully understanding the implications and context of directives. The dynamic environment of a recommender system exacerbated this gap, leading practitioners to optimize by solving easy issues while neglecting other—potentially more serious—issues. In our findings, we noted that the academic literature was often viewed as a reliable, albeit not universally applicable, resource by practitioners and fairness teams. We posit that additional work might help to provide useful vocabulary and common ground for practitioners. Likewise, embedding fairness practices more deeply in technical teams through additional roles, building domain expertise in mutual fields, and pursuing techniques for more consistent documentation and information flow (especially at the beginning of the RS pipeline) might also help to establish common ground.

Academia-Industry Relationships

Through our semi-structured interviews, we found that knowledge from research communities not only shaped many practitioners’ awareness of fairness but also gave them some basic principles to follow. Yet, practitioners also regularly developed their own ad hoc metrics over those in the literature out of necessity due to misalignment in assumptions as well as convenience. This creates a patchwork of practices that at times adheres to academic recommendations and at other times drift far from them. While practitioners do not necessarily seek to disregard academic best practices, the realities of their positions often shaped the options they preferred.

We call for greater collaboration between industry and academia, acknowledging that accomplishing this goal is challenging and limited by many factors beyond the scope of our interviews. There are clear benefits to both sides: Industry practitioners may benefit from investigating novel approaches. At the same time, research communities can potentially gain exposure to new use cases and develop further approaches based on novel empirical evidence.

The Path Forward: RS Practitioners Emphasize Benefits of Fairness Work Despite Challenges

Despite the aforementioned challenges, we have optimism in the path ahead given the overall importance that RS practitioners ascribe to fairness work. We asked practitioners to describe their emotional stress and satisfaction level in their daily work. We found that while most of our practitioners found that balancing daily work and debiasing is a bit stressful, most of them believe that it is an important and necessary part of their work. P9 explained debiasing as “…tiring but really rewarding.” They viewed fairness as both a benefit and cost in their daily work. Practitioners also cited the uniquely large impact that recommender systems play on society. P1 notes: “It is tiring, however, I believe this is the right thing to do. It is important to remove bias as much as possible as our system serves millions of users worldwide. Discriminating behaviors imposed by our system might greatly affect their life.”

Our interviewees stressed that due to the recent advances in the fairness community, not only is there more attention on recommender system bias but there have also been more qualitative studies to shape how they understand fairness and help them to organize their work better. P10 reported, “I love those papers summarizing the pros and cons in debiasing, they did give me some general guidelines in my daily work. I would say they are pretty foundational,” adding to the value of those general guidelines and checklists proposed by existing researchers.

Practitioners expressed a desire to improve the world through their systems and identified strong intrinsic motivations for improving fairness even if it is arduous. Some practitioners cited fairness discussions from academia; for instance, P1 mentioned, “Thorough discussions from existing papers, you know like a compass, about how unfairness affects other people’s life greatly shaped my view on this. As I have the chance to repair this, I will do my best though it is not easy.” They said, “I don’t want my son or daughter to live in a world where they will judge you by your race, gender, your appearance, etc. Luckily I have the opportunity to be part of this journey.”

Limitations & Conclusions

Our study is subject to a number of limitations. First and foremost, our findings and discussions are based on a limited sample size of practitioners from highly visible companies.
Though we intentionally targeted large scale recommender systems with high societal impact to interview practitioners wrestling with high stakes scenarios, this limits the kind of evidence we were able to gather.
Similarly, this paper focuses on machine learning practitioners working on recommender systems who are leading engineering or research efforts; we do not examine legal or fairness stakeholders. Practitioner-level biases (e.g., the effect of practitioner backgrounds on their choices, experience, and practices) may also confound our findings. The desires we uncovered may also not be fully actionable: there is a fundamental tension between the desire for fairness rules that function similarly to rigid and straightforward privacy rules, and the ability actually to implement such rules in the wild
(
Ali et al. 2023
)
.
Finally, our semi-structured interviews might be limited by the remote setting, potential risks of exposing corporate secrets, or participant self-presentation maintenance. While we found participants to generally be quite frank about their experiences, it is possible that they provided both a more critical and rosier picture of the environment due to their perceived expectations. Although the findings were analyzed using multiple methods, it is possible that not all themes and outliers were identified.

Overall, this paper investigated the fairness practice of RS practitioners through a case study of RS technical practitioners who are building recommender systems. Through 11 semi-structured interviews, we constructed a process-level structure of practitioners’ daily workflow and debiasing on recommender systems and identified key challenges that they face. We find that improving documentation, integrating debiasing early and often with sustainability, investing in the development of multi-stakeholder fairness optimization methods, building common ground within agencies, and promoting relationships between academia in the industry ought to lead to improvements in recommender system fairness. While recommender systems pose immense challenges in terms of complexity and interpretability, we believe there is great potential in improving system fairness through academic-industry collaboration.

Ethical Considerations Statement

This study, approved by an Institutional Review Board (IRB), demonstrates our commitment to ethical research practices in examining debiasing practices among recommender system (RS) practitioners. Throughout the semi-structured interviews, we strictly follow ethical standards, reminding participants to withhold company-sensitive information. We took comprehensive approaches to anonymize personal and corporate data, ensuring the confidentiality of all involved. Moreover, we employed encrypted storage methods to securely store the interview results, upholding the highest standards of data privacy and security.

Researcher Positionality Statement

As researchers of this work, our dual background in machine learning and human-computer interaction profoundly informs our approach to this study. Our expertise in both qualitative and quantitative research methods enabled us to approach this research from a balanced perspective. This involves not only “taking down and summarizing notes” from semi-structured interviews with technical teams but also diving into a critical analysis of the underlying reasons for their explanations and statements. Importantly, while we bring a nuanced understanding of the technical and societal complexities of recommender systems (RS), we also embody the unique perspective of being end-users of these systems. This dual vantage point, as both researchers and users, enriches our insight into the real-world debiasing practices of RS practitioners. However, it is important to note that our experience does not extend to working as RS practitioners within technology companies. This distinction is crucial in our role, as it positions us to critically assess and contribute to the ongoing discourse on fairness and bias within the broader machine learning fairness community, bringing a blend of professional expertise and personal user experience to the fore.

Adverse Impact Statement

While this study aims to foster understanding and improve fairness in recommender systems, we acknowledge the potential for adverse impacts once published. The findings could unintentionally influence RS practitioners to adopt debiasing practices that may not be universally applicable, leading to oversimplification of complex issues. Furthermore, there is a risk that the emphasis on certain debiasing strategies might overshadow other equally important aspects of fairness and bias in RS. We recognize the importance of ongoing critical evaluation and discourse in the community to mitigate these potential negative impacts and encourage a diverse range of perspectives and solutions in addressing structural issues of fairness and bias in recommender systems.

Technical Understanding and Model Transparency

Our participants also conveyed a common complaint about internal tools lacking accountability. 7 practitioners justified that the lack of accountability and transparency of deep learning models made them hesitant to change the actual model. They feared introducing new biases while trying to fix others and viewed the models as black boxes. P6 signaled, “I am not so sure that I fully understand those deep learning models. I would only change the number of layers, loss functions, and hyperparameters if I really need to.” Practitioners, hence, also mention that they preferred data-centric approaches as they provide additional help. P3 said “If… the bias is in data, we can contact the data team to check, and their help is always good.” Moving beyond the immediate concerns of practitioners deploying models that they view as black boxes that are not adequately interpretable, this also points to a larger issue that debiasing strategies are chosen as much for their effectiveness as they are chosen out of concern for potential risk exposure. Yet, when prompted, all practitioners also reported that there exist solutions on the model level as well—despite not pursuing them.
