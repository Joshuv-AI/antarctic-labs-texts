Synergistic remediation of oil-contaminated soil using sorghum and oil sludge-derived biochar
Authors: Hashem Mohammadzadeh Shirazi, Ahmad Ali Pourbabaee, Hassan Etesami, Hossein Asadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oil sludge contamination poses a significant threat to agricultural soil health and productivity. While biochar is a promising soil amendment, the synergistic potential of combining oil sludge-derived biochar with plant systems for holistic remediation remains poorly quantified. In a 60-day greenhouse study using a full-factorial design, we investigated the interactive effects of oil sludge (0–0.5% w/w) and biochar produced from the same sludge (0–1% w/w) on soil biochemistry and forage sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) growth, providing the first quantitative evidence of true synergy through combined assessment of soil biochemical recovery, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) degradation kinetics, and plant physiological resilience—surpassing previous studies that examined these factors in isolation. Biochar application at 1% (w/w) effectively counteracted oil sludge toxicity, reducing soil salinity by ~ 30% and enhancing microbial respiration and enzyme activities (e.g., dehydrogenase increased by > 60%). Crucially, the presence of sorghum amplified these benefits, creating a strong plant-microbe-biochar synergy that accelerated the degradation of total PAHs by approximately 54%—a rate substantially greater than the sum of their individual effects. In plants, biochar mitigated oxidative stress, reducing lipid peroxidation (MDA) by 73% while boosting the antioxidant system. Consequently, biochar restored sorghum growth under high contamination, increasing shoot and root biomass by 43% and 72%, respectively, and maintained nutrient uptake. Our findings demonstrate that sorghum cultivation combined with oil sludge-derived biochar creates a powerful synergistic system for soil remediation. This circular economy approach not only decontaminates soil by enhancing microbial activity but also fortifies plant health, presenting a sustainable, plant-based strategy for the management of oil-sludge impacted agricultural lands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-62095-0.

Introduction

The global reliance on petroleum products fuels economic development but generates hazardous wastes, notably oil sludge, which poses a severe threat to ecosystem health. Globally, millions of tons of oil sludge, a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), are generated annually from exploration, refining, and transportation activities, with significant quantities accumulating in oil-producing regions such as the Middle East, where improper disposal and accidental spills frequently contaminate adjacent agricultural lands1,2. As highlighted in a recent comprehensive review, the co-occurrence of petroleum hydrocarbons and heavy metals in such wastes poses a particularly challenging remediation scenario due to their combined toxic effects on soil biota3. Its improper disposal leads to persistent soil contamination, altering soil physicochemical properties, reducing fertility, and introducing carcinogenic and mutagenic risks to the food web4,5. The hydrophobic nature of petroleum hydrocarbons can create a water-repellent soil environment, inhibiting plant growth by disrupting water and nutrient uptake, reducing photosynthetic efficiency, and causing oxidative stress, ultimately leading to biomass reduction and plant death6,7.

To address these challenges, sustainable remediation strategies have gained prominence. Among these, biochar—a carbon-rich material produced via pyrolysis of organic waste—has emerged as a promising soil amendment. When derived from oil sludge itself, biochar represents a circular economy approach, converting a hazardous waste into a valuable resource2,8. A recent review on biochar-driven rhizoremediation emphasizes that biochar is a pivotal facilitator of plant–microbe synergy for the degradation of organic pollutants, aligning directly with the circular bioeconomy framework of our study9. Pyrolysis stabilizes toxic components and endows the resulting biochar with a porous structure and large surface area, enabling it to adsorb pollutants, improve soil water retention, and enhance nutrient availability10,11. Furthermore, biochar can serve as a habitat for hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms, thereby stimulating microbial activity and accelerating the bioremediation process12,13. Liu, et al. 14 recently demonstrated that biochar application significantly enhances the phytoremediation efficiency of petroleum-contaminated soils by improving soil physicochemical properties and promoting plant growth under stress conditions. This ‘waste-to-resource’ strategy aligns with the principles of cleaner production by minimizing waste liability and creating a valuable product for environmental management.

Despite the growing body of literature on biochar-assisted phytoremediation, reported efficiencies vary widely, ranging from approximately 30% to over 80% hydrocarbon removal depending on biochar feedstock, pyrolysis conditions, application rates, and contaminant characteristics11,12,15. Furthermore, contradictory findings exist regarding biochar’s effects on plant growth and microbial activity in contaminated soils, with some studies reporting enhanced growth and microbial stimulation13,14,16, while others observed limited or even negative effects at high application rates or under specific contaminant conditions17,18. These discrepancies highlight critical knowledge gaps in our understanding of plant–microbe–biochar interactions, particularly regarding the quantitative assessment of true synergy—where the combined effect exceeds the sum of individual effects—in oil sludge-contaminated systems. Moreover, most studies have either focused on biochar derived from agricultural or sewage sludge feedstocks, or examined phytoremediation and biochar application separately, without integrating both approaches using contaminant-derived biochar. To date, no study has systematically quantified the synergistic effects of oil sludge-derived biochar combined with plant cultivation using a full-factorial design that simultaneously assesses soil biochemical recovery, PAH degradation kinetics, and plant physiological resilience. This knowledge gap limits our ability to design effective, circular remediation strategies that transform hazardous waste into a resource for environmental restoration. Oil sludge-derived biochar possesses unique properties that distinguish it from biochars produced from agricultural or sewage sludge feedstocks. The high carbon content and aromatic structure of oil sludge, combined with pyrolysis-induced stabilization of heavy metals, result in a biochar with substantial surface area (up to ~ 215 m2 g−1), microporous structure, and oxygen-containing functional groups that enhance its capacity for pollutant adsorption, nutrient retention, and microbial habitat provision10,11. Moreover, the inherent nutrient content (N, P, and K) of oil sludge-derived biochar contributes to soil fertility improvement, offering a dual benefit of waste valorization and soil restoration that is particularly relevant for degraded agricultural lands8,12. While biochar improves soil conditions, its efficacy can be significantly enhanced through phytoremediation—the use of plants to stabilize, extract, or degrade contaminants.

Forage sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L.), a resilient C4 crop, exhibits remarkable tolerance to abiotic stresses, including salinity and heavy metals, making it a candidate for rehabilitating degraded lands15,16. Recent studies have confirmed that sweet sorghum varieties can achieve up to 69.3% removal of total petroleum hydrocarbons from salinized soils, highlighting their strong potential for phytoremediation of oil-impacted lands16. Its extensive root system can stimulate rhizosphere microbial communities, fostering the degradation of petroleum hydrocarbons while maintaining biomass production under stress conditions19. Moreover, its rapid growth and high biomass yield offer the potential for economic return as forage or bioenergy feedstock, enhancing the practical appeal and sustainability of the remediation strategy.

Critically, a synergistic effect is hypothesized when biochar and phytoremediation are combined. Biochar can ameliorate soil conditions to a point where plants like sorghum can thrive, and in turn, the plant’s rhizosphere activity can enhance the microbial degradation of pollutants adsorbed by the biochar13,20. This plant-microbe-biochar partnership represents a powerful, sustainable strategy for soil restoration. However, while the individual effects of biochar or sorghum have been studied, the dynamic interactions within a system employing oil sludge-derived biochar remain inadequately quantified. A critical knowledge gap exists in understanding the true synergy of this circular approach, specifically its combined temporal impact on both soil biochemical resilience (e.g., enzyme activities, microbial respiration, and PAH degradation kinetics) and plant physiological recovery (e.g., antioxidant defense and nutrient uptake).

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the synergistic potential of oil sludge-derived biochar and sorghum cultivation in remediating oil-contaminated soil. We tested three specific hypotheses: (1) biochar application would mitigate the phytotoxicity of oil sludge by improving soil physicochemical conditions (e.g., reducing salinity and enhancing nutrient availability); (2) sorghum cultivation would stimulate rhizosphere microbial activity, thereby accelerating the degradation of PAHs; and (3) the combination of biochar and sorghum would create a synergistic positive feedback loop, resulting in greater soil recovery and plant resilience than either treatment alone. Using a controlled greenhouse experiment, we investigated the temporal changes in (1) key soil biochemical indices (salinity, organic carbon, NPK, microbial respiration, and urease, phosphatase, and dehydrogenase activities), (2) the degradation rate of PAHs, and (3) sorghum growth performance, nutrient acquisition, and oxidative stress responses. Our findings provide a scientific foundation for an integrated, circular bioeconomy strategy for the sustainable management of oil-impacted agricultural lands.

Materials and methods

Collection and characterization of raw materials

Oil sludge

Approximately 50 kg of oil sludge was collected from oil refinery, Tehran, Iran (35°32′24″N, 51°25′33″E). The sludge was homogenized and characterized prior to use. Proximate and elemental analyses were conducted according to standard methods. The physicochemical properties were determined as follows: moisture, ash content, and fixed carbon were analyzed using ASTM methods (D3173, D3175, and D3174); elemental composition (C, H, N, and S) was determined by dry combustion; and oxygen content was calculated by difference. Total heavy metal concentrations (Pb, Zn, Ni, and Cd) were quantified using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES, SPECTRO ARCOS 12FHE) after complete acid digestion with a HCl: H₂SO₄ (4:4 v/v) mixture to determine the total elemental content of the raw oil sludge. Total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH) were measured based on TNRCC methods 1005 and 4886, and hydrocarbon composition was identified using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS). Based on proximate analysis, the moisture content was 28.3%, ash content measured 7.62%, and total solids accounted for 45.2% by weight. Elemental analysis on a dry weight basis revealed that carbon constituted 48.84%, hydrogen 6.38%, nitrogen 0.091%, sulfur 1.388%, and oxygen—calculated by difference—was 9.55%. The sludge also contained notable concentrations of heavy metals, including Pb at 850 mg kg− 1, Zn at 6100 mg kg− 1, Ni at 2700 mg kg− 1, and Cd at 100 mg kg− 1. In terms of other parameters, the total TPH were measured at 26.5 g kg− 1.

Biochar production and characterization

Biochar was produced from the homogenized oil sludge via slow pyrolysis. The sludge was placed in a closed reactor under a continuous nitrogen flow, heated at a rate of 30 °C min−1 to a temperature of 450 °C, and held for 60 min. These pyrolysis conditions were selected based on established literature for sludge-derived biochar production. The temperature of 450 °C falls within the optimal range (400–500 °C) for slow pyrolysis of sludge, balancing biochar yield with desirable physicochemical properties including effective heavy metal immobilization and reduced bioavailability21,22. A residence time of 60 min is widely employed in sludge pyrolysis studies and represents a standard duration for slow pyrolysis processes23. The heating rate of 30 °C min−1 falls within the range of 2–30 °C min−1 commonly employed for slow pyrolysis of biological sludge24 and represents the upper boundary of the slow pyrolysis regime, which favors biochar as the primary product. The resulting biochar was cooled under N₂, ground, and sieved to < 2 mm. The biochar was characterized for pH (1:10 w/v water suspension), and elemental content (C, N, P, and K) via ICP-OES after digestion25. Biochar had a neutral to slightly alkaline pH of 7.9. The carbon content was measured at 435 g kg− 1 and nitrogen was present at 56 g kg− 1. Phosphorus and K were also detected at 49 g kg− 1 and 31 g kg− 1, respectively. The BET surface area was 215 m² g⁻¹, with a total pore volume of 0.038 cm³ g⁻¹ and an average pore diameter of 1.97 nm, as determined by N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms.

Soil collection

Soil was collected from the top layer (0–30 cm) of an agricultural field at the Agricultural Research Campus of Tehran University, Karaj, Iran (35°56′N, 50°58′E; altitude: 1160 m above sea level). The soil was air-dried, sieved through a 2 mm mesh, and homogenized before use. The soil exhibited a loamy texture with a neutral pH of 7.12 and an electrical conductivity (EC) of 1.62 dS m− 1. It contained 0.07% total N and 0.16% organic carbon. Plant-available K and P were measured at 360 mg kg− 1 and 29 mg kg− 1, respectively. The saturation percentage (water content at complete saturation, determined by the standard soil paste method) of the soil was 41.67%.

Experimental design and greenhouse setup

A factorial experiment was conducted under greenhouse conditions using a completely randomized design (split-plot two-level factorial) with three replications. The factors were biochar (0%, 0.5%, and 1% w/w, denoted B0-B2) and oil sludge (0%, 0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.5% w/w, denoted PS0-PS3). Each combination was applied to 5 kg of soil in pots. The selected sludge concentrations (0.1–0.5% w/w) correspond to realistic contamination scenarios for agricultural lands adjacent to oil infrastructure, resulting in approximately 100–500 mg TPH kg−1 soil after mixing—representative of low-to-moderate petroleum pollution. These levels were chosen based on preliminary trials showing that concentrations exceeding 0.5% sludge caused severe phytotoxicity (> 80% reduction in germination), which would have masked the synergistic effects of biochar and plant cultivation. The selected range therefore provides a sub-lethal stress gradient optimal for evaluating biostimulation and phytoremediation synergy without causing complete system failure. The experiment included two parallel studies: (i) Soil incubation study: 72 pots were maintained without plants and destructively sampled at 0, 20, 40, and 60 days after treatment application to analyze soil biochemical properties. (ii) Plant growth study: 36 pots (including 5 kg soil) out of the 72 pots (a subset of the 72 pots) were sown with forage sorghum seeds (Foman variety) (obtained from the Seed and Seedling Research Institute, Karaj, Iran) and grown for 60 days under controlled conditions (12/12 h light/dark cycle, ~ 10,000 lx light intensity, moisture maintained at field capacity). Each pot contained 5 kg of soil and one plant was kept in each pot. No fertilization was applied to the soil during this investigation. At harvest, plant growth and physiological parameters were measured.

Analytical procedures

Soil biochemical analyses

At each sampling time, soil samples were analyzed for several biochemical properties. Soil pH and EC were measured in a 1:5 soil: water extract using a pH and EC meter26. Soil organic carbon was determined by the Walkley-Black method27. Microbial respiration was measured by trapping CO₂ in NaOH and titrating with HCl, with results expressed as mg CO₂ g−1 soil day−128, and microbial biomass carbon was estimated by the chloroform fumigation-extraction method29. For nutrient content, total N was measured by the Kjeldahl method30, extractable P was determined by the Olsen method31, and extractable K was analyzed by flame photometry32. Soil enzyme activities were assessed as follows: Dehydrogenase activity was determined using the TTC (triphenyl tetrazolium chloride) reduction method, where soil samples were incubated with 3% TTC solution in Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.6) at 37 °C for 24 h, and the resulting triphenyl formazan (TPF) was measured spectrophotometrically at 485 nm33. Alkaline phosphatase activity was assayed by incubating soil samples with p-nitrophenyl phosphate (PNPP) substrate in modified universal buffer (MUB) at pH 11.0 at 37 °C for 1 h, and the released p-nitrophenol was measured at 400 nm34. Urease activity was determined by incubating soil samples with 10% urea solution in phosphate buffer (pH 6.7) at 37 °C for 2 h, and the ammonium released was measured spectrophotometrically35,36. Soil samples contaminated with oil sludge were also prepared for PAHs determination after drying at 40 °C and grinding. PAH extraction was performed using a Soxhlet apparatus with a mixture of dichloromethane and acetone (1:1 v/v) to completely release the target organic compounds. The obtained extracts were passed through a silica gel column to remove impurities. Then, the extracts were concentrated to the final volume using a rotary evaporator, and the concentrated samples were analyzed for the identification and quantification of the 16 USEPA priority PAHs by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Identification and determination of PAH concentrations were performed using internal and external standards. This method is based on EPA Methods 8270 and 3540 and allows for the accurate measurement of 16 USEPA priority PAHs in petroleum-contaminated soils, including naphthalene (2-ring), acenaphthylene, acenaphthene, fluorene, phenanthrene, anthracene (3-ring), fluoranthene, pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, chrysene (4-ring), benzo[b]fluoranthene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, benzo[a]pyrene, dibenz[a, h]anthracene (5-ring), and indeno[1,2,3-cd]pyrene and benzo[g, h,i]perylene (6-ring)37. The total PAH concentration was calculated as the sum of the concentrations of all 16 individual PAHs.

Plant growth and physiological analyses

At harvest (60 days after planting), a comprehensive set of plant growth and physiological parameters was measured. Morphological traits, including plant height, root length, and the dry weight of shoots and roots, were recorded. Chlorophyll content was quantified using a SPAD meter and further verified (Total chlorophyll) by the Arnon method38. Oxidative stress was evaluated by measuring proline content using acid-ninhydrin reagent39 and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, via the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method40. The activities of key antioxidant enzymes, peroxidase and catalase, were assayed spectrophotometrically41,42. Finally, plant nutrient content was analyzed: N was determined by the Kjeldahl method, while P and K concentrations in plant tissue were measured after dry ashing and HCl digestion, using a spectrophotometer and flame photometer, respectively43. A synergy index was also calculated for PAH degradation as: Synergy index = [Observed combined effect] / [Expected additive effect (Individual B2 effect + Individual plant effect)].

Statistical analysis

Following the evaluation of homoscedasticity using Levene’s test and normality with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the data from the soil incubation study (factorial split-plot design with time as a split factor) and the plant study (two-factor factorial design) were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4). Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine the significance of main effects and their interactions. Mean comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Multiple Range Test at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Soil chemical properties

The interaction of biochar, oil sludge, and time significantly influenced soil chemical properties (p < 0.01). Oil sludge contamination at 0.5% (PS3) increased soil EC by up to 30% compared to the uncontaminated control. However, 1% (w/w) biochar (B2) application mitigated this effect, reducing salinity by approximately 30% in contaminated soils. This ameliorating effect was significantly amplified by sorghum cultivation, with salinity in the synergistic B2×PS3 treatment being 45% lower than in the unamended, contaminated control (B0×PS3) (Fig. 1A, B). In contrast, soil pH remained stable across all treatments and was not significantly affected. Soil organic carbon (OC) content was significantly enhanced by biochar application. The combination of 1% biochar and 0.5% oil sludge (B2×PS3) led to the highest and most stable increase in OC over time. This effect was more pronounced in soils cultivated with sorghum (Fig. 1C, D).

Soil biological properties and PAH concentration

The most striking effects of the biochar-sorghum partnership were observed on soil biological properties. Microbial respiration and microbial biomass carbon were significantly enhanced by biochar application, peaking at day 40 (Fig. 3C and D). In the highly contaminated soil (PS3), 1% biochar (B2) increased microbial respiration by over 60% and microbial biomass carbon by more than 80% compared to the control without biochar (Fig. 4A and B). This stimulatory effect was consistently amplified in the presence of sorghum. This revitalized microbial community exhibited markedly enhanced metabolic activity. The activities of key soil enzymes were significantly boosted. Dehydrogenase activity, a direct indicator of microbial metabolic activity, increased by > 60% in the B2×PS3 treatment (Fig. 4C and D). Urease activity, critical for nitrogen cycling, and alkaline phosphatase activity, essential for phosphorus mineralization, also showed significant increases in biochar-amended soils, particularly in the presence of sorghum (Fig. 5A, B, C, and D). The ultimate evidence of successful bioremediation was the accelerated degradation of toxic PAHs. The combination of 1% biochar and sorghum cultivation was exceptionally effective, resulting in a remarkable approximately 54% reduction in total PAH concentration by day 60 (from ~ 50 µg/g to < 25 µg/g soil). This degradation rate was substantially faster than in treatments with biochar alone or sorghum alone (Fig. 6A and B). Quantitative analysis of synergy was also performed using factorial ANOVA and synergy indices. The biochar × plant interaction was statistically significant for PAH degradation (F = 12.47, p < 0.001), microbial respiration (F = 8.93, p < 0.01), and dehydrogenase activity (F = 10.21, p < 0.001), confirming that the combined effect was not merely additive. The synergy index value was 1.42, indicating that the combined B2×PS3 treatment achieved 42% greater PAH degradation than the sum of the individual B2 and plant effects alone, providing quantitative evidence of true synergy.

Plant responses

The improved soil environment directly translated to superior plant health and productivity. Oil sludge stress severely inhibited sorghum growth. At the 0.5% contamination level (PS3) without biochar, plant height, root length, shoot dry weight, and root dry weight were reduced by up to 40–50% compared to the uncontaminated control. Application of 1% biochar (B2) effectively restored plant growth, increasing shoot and root biomass by 43% and 72%, respectively, under high contamination (Fig. 7A-D). Physiological analyses confirmed that biochar conferred resilience. Oil sludge stress caused a ~ 5-fold increase in malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation. Biochar at 1% drastically reduced MDA content by 73%. Concurrently, the osmoregulant proline accumulated in leaves under contamination stress, with levels highest in the B2×PS2 treatment. The antioxidant system was also fortified; the activities of catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD) were significantly enhanced in biochar-amended, contaminated soils, with CAT activity peaking in the B1×PS2 treatment and POD activity highest in B2×PS2 (Fig. 8A-D). Despite the contaminant stress, sorghum plants in the B2×PS3 treatment maintained robust function. Total chlorophyll content was preserved, indicating sustained photosynthetic capacity. This was supported by robust nutritional uptake; the concentration of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in plant tissue was comparable to or even exceeded levels in unstressed plants without biochar (Fig. 9A-D), consistent with the observed improvements in soil nutrient availability.

Impact of plant cultivation on soil nutrient dynamics and microbial activity

The most compelling evidence for the synergistic effect was observed when comparing the combined B2×PS3 treatment (1% biochar + 0.5% oil sludge + plant) against the unamended contaminated control (B0×PS3 without plant). As shown in Table S1, the biochar–plant synergy resulted in dramatic improvements across all measured parameters, with increases ranging from 38.9% (K) to over 107% (urease and dehydrogenase) in soil biochemical properties, alongside a 54.2% reduction in PAH concentration. The overall comparative analysis averaged across all treatments (Table S1) similarly confirmed significant positive effects of plant cultivation on most parameters (p < 0.05), with the exception of soil K and pH, which showed no significant differences. Soil salinity was lower in the plant cultivation scenario (1.5132 ± 0.0254 dS m− 1) compared to no plant cultivation (1.634 ± 0.0299 dS m− 1), indicating a potential benefit of plant growth on soil health. Acidity levels remained consistent across both conditions, recorded at 7.1441 ± 0.0261. Microbial characteristics revealed that microbial biomass carbon was significantly higher in the presence of plants (0.5543 ± 0.016 mg g− 1) compared to no plant cultivation (0.474 ± 0.0131 mg g− 1). Microbial respiration also increased from 0.1487 ± 0.0032 mg CO₂ g− 1 day− 1 without plants to 0.1812 ± 0.004 mg CO2 g− 1 day− 1 with plants. Enzyme activities, including urease, phosphatase, and dehydrogenase, were notably higher in the plant cultivation condition, suggesting enhanced microbial activity and nutrient cycling in the presence of vegetation. Statistical analyses indicated significant differences (p < 0.05) in several parameters, highlighting the positive impact of plant cultivation on soil quality and microbial dynamics.

Discussion

The most significant outcome of this study is the quantitative demonstration of synergy between biochar and sorghum cultivation, supported by factorial ANOVA revealing significant biochar × plant interactions for PAH degradation (F = 12.47, p < 0.001), microbial respiration (F = 8.93, p < 0.01), and dehydrogenase activity (F = 10.21, p < 0.001). The synergy index for PAH degradation was 1.42, indicating that the combined effect exceeded the sum of individual effects by 42%, further evidenced by the highest levels of microbial and enzyme activities in the combined B2×PS3 treatment. The observed ~ 54% reduction in total PAH concentration is comparable to the 54.1% removal reported by Kamranifar, et al. 44 and within the 30–80% range reported in other biochar–plant studies11,12, though lower than the 69.3% removal achieved by Ma, et al.16 using sweet sorghum in salinized soils, likely due to differences in contaminant composition, biochar application rate, and experimental duration. We propose a multi-phase mechanism for this synergy. Initially, biochar acts as a critical soil conditioner, ameliorating the primary abiotic stresses that inhibit biological activity13. The significant reduction in soil salinity can be attributed to biochar’s well-documented ability to improve cation exchange and adsorb soluble salts10,44,45. Furthermore, biochar is known to sequester hydrophobic organic contaminants, reducing their bioavailability and direct toxicity to plants and microbes13,44,46,47. This initial “conditioning” phase is crucial, as it creates a less hostile environment, allowing sorghum to establish its root system—a step that often fails in heavily contaminated soils without amendment14,17,18. While our analysis focused on total PAH concentration as the primary indicator of remediation efficacy, the detailed data collected on individual PAH congeners could potentially reveal ring-number-dependent degradation patterns in future analyses. Specifically, it would be valuable to determine whether low-molecular-weight (2–3 ring) PAHs were preferentially degraded compared to high-molecular-weight (4–6 ring) PAHs, as has been reported in the literature for biochar-amended systems14,16. However, such detailed kinetic analysis is beyond the scope of the present study, which was designed to evaluate the overall synergistic effect of biochar and sorghum on soil health and total PAH removal.

A critical aspect of this conditioning phase, and a key consideration for environmental risk assessment, is the potential for heavy metal immobilization. While the initial oil sludge contained notable total heavy metal concentrations (Pb: 850 mg kg⁻¹, Zn: 6100 mg kg⁻¹, Ni: 2700 mg kg⁻¹, and Cd: 100 mg kg⁻¹), the physiological responses of sorghum provide indirect evidence consistent with reduced metal bioavailability. Specifically, the significant reduction in lipid peroxidation (MDA decreased by 73%) and the substantial recovery of root and shoot biomass (72% and 43%, respectively) under the combined B2×PS3 treatment indicate that the plants were not subjected to severe heavy metal-induced phytotoxicity48,49. This is mechanistically consistent with the high specific surface area (215 m² g⁻¹) and microporous structure of the biochar (pore volume: 0.038 cm³ g⁻¹), which are known to stabilize cationic metals (Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ni²⁺, and Cd²⁺) via surface adsorption, complexation with oxygen-containing functional groups, and precipitation with carbonates and phosphates10,50,51. This indicates that the pyrolysis process and subsequent soil application successfully stabilized these potentially toxic elements within the biochar matrix, preventing their entry into the food chain and ensuring the safety of the phytoremediation strategy. Our findings are consistent with a recent study demonstrating that biochar amendments effectively immobilize zinc and other heavy metals in petroleum-co-contaminated soils while simultaneously enhancing soil enzyme activities such as urease and alkaline phosphatase52. This finding is paramount for validating the circular economy approach, as it demonstrates that converting hazardous sludge into biochar effectively neutralizes the dual-threat of hydrocarbon and heavy metal pollution. However, we emphasize that direct quantification of heavy metal concentrations in post-remediation soil and plant tissues was not performed in this study. Therefore, the evidence for metal immobilization remains indirect, and we do not claim that levels were below specific detection or permissible limits. Future field-scale studies should incorporate a comprehensive heavy metal mass balance to confirm long-term immobilization stability.

Once established, the sorghum plant transitions from a passive recipient to an active driver of remediation. The plant’s extensive root system creates a dynamic rhizosphere, presumably releasing root exudates that serve as labile carbon sources to stimulate a specialized hydrocarbon-degrading microbiome13,53,54. Our data on significantly higher microbial biomass and dehydrogenase activity in the rhizosphere of biochar-amended plants are consistent with this mechanism. Biochar likely enhances this process not only by protecting microbes from residual toxins but also by acting as a porous refuge and a conduit for nutrient exchange13,55–57. This aligns with the concept of “biochar-mediated rhizosphere engineering,” where biochar amplifies the natural capacity of plants to shape their microbial environment for enhanced degradation58–60. Recent work on synergistic biochar-microbe interactions has similarly confirmed that biochar serves as a microbial refuge and nutrient hotspot, substantially increasing the abundance and activity of hydrocarbon-degrading taxa in the rhizosphere61. However, we acknowledge that we did not directly analyze root exudate composition or characterize the soil microbial community structure in this study. Recent studies employing metagenomic and metabolomic approaches have demonstrated that biochar-assisted phytoremediation promotes organic pollutant degradation through modulation of the plant root microbiota9,13,62. Such molecular-level analyses represent a critical direction for future research to fully elucidate the specific microbial taxa and biochemical pathways involved in the observed synergy.

The application of biochar led to a substantial increase in soil organic carbon, which is consistent with its inherent recalcitrant carbon content13,63. However, the more pronounced increase in the presence of sorghum suggests a dynamic interaction, where biochar promotes the stabilization of root-derived organic carbon, leading to greater long-term carbon sequestration64,65. The dramatic enhancement of key enzyme activities (urease, phosphatase, and dehydrogenase) is a critical indicator of restored soil metabolic function. The increase in dehydrogenase activity confirms a general stimulation of microbial catabolic processes, essential for oxidizing organic pollutants66,67. The parallel rises in urease and phosphatase activities directly explain the improved bioavailability of N and P, as these enzymes mineralize organic nutrients into plant-available forms68,69. Biochar is known to enhance enzyme activities by improving soil habitat conditions (moisture, aeration, and pH buffering) and providing a stable surface for enzyme immobilization70,71. The observed > 60% increase in dehydrogenase activity in the B2×PS3 treatment is comparable to the 40–80% increases reported in biochar-amended contaminated soils51,52, and consistent with the 107.9% increase in dehydrogenase observed in Table S1 for the combined treatment. The parallel increases in urease and phosphatase activities (107.2% and 89.2%, respectively) are also within the range of 50–120% increases reported in recent studies52,72. This created a virtuous cycle: improved nutrient availability supported stronger plant growth, which, through enhanced rhizodeposition, further fueled the microbial community responsible for both nutrient cycling and contaminant degradation.

Microbial biomass carbon (MBC) exhibited a distinct peak at day 40 across all treatments before declining by day 60. This temporal pattern is consistent with the “boom-and-bust” dynamics commonly observed in bioremediation studies73,74. The initial increase in MBC (day 0–40) reflects the rapid proliferation of hydrocarbon-degrading microorganisms stimulated by the availability of labile carbon from biochar, rhizodeposits from sorghum roots, and the increased bioavailability of nutrients (N, P, and K) released from the biochar matrix. By day 40, the microbial community reaches its maximum carrying capacity, supported by the peak availability of readily degradable organic compounds. The subsequent decline (day 40–60) is attributed to (i) the depletion of the most readily biodegradable hydrocarbon fractions and labile organic matter, leaving predominantly recalcitrant compounds (e.g., high-molecular-weight PAHs); (ii) increased competition for limiting nutrients (particularly N and P), which become progressively immobilized in microbial cells or depleted through plant uptake; and (iii) natural microbial succession, where the system transitions from exponential growth to maintenance metabolism or dormancy75. Notably, despite the decline in MBC, the sustained high levels of dehydrogenase activity at day 60 indicate that the remaining microbial population remained metabolically active, suggesting that the decline reflects reduced biomass rather than reduced functional capacity.

The physiological data from sorghum plants provides a mechanistic understanding of biochar’s protective role. The drastic reduction in MDA levels in biochar-amended soils is a clear biomarker of reduced oxidative damage to cell membranes. This is a direct consequence of the observed upregulation of the antioxidant enzymes catalase and peroxidase. Our findings suggest that biochar application primed the plant’s antioxidant defense system, enabling more efficient scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by hydrocarbon stress76–78. The preservation of chlorophyll content and the maintenance of robust nutrient uptake (N, P, and K) underscore the functional benefits of this reduced oxidative stress. By lowering the physiological burden of contamination, biochar allowed sorghum to re-allocate energy from stress mitigation to growth and photosynthesis, resulting in the significant recovery of biomass79–81. The accumulation of proline likely contributed to osmotic adjustment and acted as a non-enzymatic antioxidant, further supporting plant tolerance82. The observed 43% increase in shoot biomass and 72% increase in root biomass under the combined B2×PS3 treatment are consistent with the 30–80% recovery reported in other phytoremediation studies with biochar17,18. The 73% reduction in MDA is comparable to the 50–75% reductions reported in biochar-plant studies for heavy metal-contaminated soils48,49.

While our results demonstrate a potent synergistic remediation strategy under controlled greenhouse conditions, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the 60-day pot experiment may not fully capture the long-term dynamics and complexities of field applications, including seasonal variations, weathering of contaminants, and the influence of diverse soil microbial communities. Second, and most critically, we did not measure heavy metal concentrations in post-remediation soil or plant tissues, despite the substantial heavy metal burden of the initial oil sludge. The evidence for metal immobilization is therefore indirect (based on physiological responses) and requires direct analytical confirmation. Third, while our data on enhanced microbial respiration, enzyme activities, and PAH degradation are consistent with rhizosphere stimulation, we did not analyze root exudate composition, characterize the soil microbial community structure (e.g., via 16 S rRNA sequencing), or conduct formal correlation analysis. Fourth, we did not perform metabolomic profiling to identify the specific biochemical pathways involved in PAH degradation. These gaps limit our mechanistic understanding of the synergy. We acknowledge that we did not conduct formal correlation analysis in this study. While the temporal co-variation of microbial biomass, enzyme activities, and PAH degradation suggests functional linkages, we cannot provide statistical correlation coefficients. Furthermore, we did not analyze root exudate composition or conduct metabolomic profiling to identify specific biochemical pathways. Future research should prioritize long-term field trials, comprehensive heavy metal mass balance analyses, and molecular techniques (e.g., metagenomics, metabolomics, and 16 S rRNA sequencing) to elucidate the microbial mechanisms and confirm the long-term stability of the remediation effects9,62.

Conclusion

Under controlled greenhouse conditions, this study provides quantitative evidence of synergy between oil sludge-derived biochar and sorghum cultivation for the remediation of hydrocarbon-contaminated soils. Factorial ANOVA revealed significant biochar × plant interactions for PAH degradation (F = 12.47, p < 0.001), microbial respiration (F = 8.93, p < 0.01), and dehydrogenase activity (F = 10.21, p < 0.001), with a synergy index of 1.42 for PAH degradation—indicating that the combined effect exceeded the sum of individual effects by 42%. Key quantitative outcomes included approximately 54% total PAH reduction, 43% increase in shoot biomass, 72% increase in root biomass, and 73% reduction in lipid peroxidation (MDA). These results are consistent with a multi-phase mechanism wherein biochar conditions the soil environment, enabling plant establishment, while the plant’s rhizosphere activity enhances microbial degradation—a process further supported by the biochar habitat. However, we emphasize that the evidence for heavy metal immobilization is indirect, and direct confirmation through metal analysis was not performed. Furthermore, as a greenhouse pot study, these findings require field validation to assess long-term dynamics and scalability. Addressing these limitations through long-term field trials, heavy metal mass balance analyses, and molecular ecology studies (e.g., metagenomics and metabolomics) will be crucial for translating this promising, circular strategy into practical, large-scale application for the sustainable management of oil-impacted agricultural lands.
