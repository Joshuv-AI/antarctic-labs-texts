Evaluating evidence based options for initial PTSD treatment non-response: A randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: Philip Held, Ilana Hutzler, Marley Warren, Océane Streubel, Sarah A. Pridgen, Dale L. Smith, Marylene Cloitre, Barbara O. Rothbaum, Debra L. Kaysen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) affects approximately 23% of veterans and 8% of U.S. adults, and it is associated with negative physical and mental health outcomes, decreased quality of life, substantial impairments in functioning, and increased suicide risk. Research has demonstrated that 14-50% of those receiving evidence-based PTSD treatments retain significant PTSD symptoms and/or continue to meet criteria for PTSD. Yet, there is currently no empirically-based guidance for optimal subsequent treatment options for these individuals. This lack of empirical guidance leaves individuals and their providers guessing about next steps, possibly prolonging treatment, and increasing feelings of defeat as the number of ineffective courses of care mount. This study will address this gap by examining 1) the feasibility of offering a subsequent course of evidence-based psychotherapy for individuals who continue to meet criteria for probable PTSD (PCL-5 severity ≥28) following an initial course of a Cognitive Processing Therapy and 2) the efficacy of different subsequent psychotherapy-based treatment options for initial non-responders. Moreover, the study will help move toward personalizing treatment by 3) examining potential moderators/predictors of subsequent treatment response, and 4) developing an algorithm to identify likely non-responders prior to completion of an initial treatment course, allowing them to enroll in another potentially effective treatment sooner. Findings from this study will help advance personalized medicine approaches for PTSD treatment by evaluating subsequent treatment options for initial non-responders and identifying individual characteristics that may predict a stronger fit for one subsequent treatment option over another.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06733376.

Introduction

Approximately 23% of U.S. veterans [1] and 8% of U.S. adults [2] suffer from posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). PTSD is associated with high rates of comorbidity and high degrees of functional impairment [3] more so than any other mental health disorder [4]. Untreated PTSD often leads to substantial impairments in work, home, and social functioning, increased risk of suicide, and poorer long-term physical health outcomes [4], [5], [6]. These functional impairments may further exacerbate existing PTSD symptoms in a cyclical manner [7], [8].

Evidence-based treatments and their limitations

Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) [9] and Prolonged Exposure (PE) [10] are recommended as first-line interventions for the treatment of PTSD [11]. Such psychotherapies yield substantial and durable reductions in PTSD symptoms, with benefits documented as far out as a decade post-treatment [12], as well as improvements in quality of life, work, home, and social functioning [13].

Despite the effectiveness of PE and CPT, some service members and veterans (SMVs) are reluctant or unwilling to engage in trauma-focused interventions and prefer to address other issues in treatment that do not involve discussions of their traumatic experiences [14]. Skills training in affective and interpersonal regulation (STAIR) [15], an intervention developed to target social and emotion management skills and improve functioning, is frequently considered a non-trauma-focused treatment option. STAIR has amassed evidence across several randomized clinical trials supporting its effectiveness in reducing PTSD symptoms and improving functioning across populations, including among SMVs [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]. As such, STAIR may be a viable option for individuals who do not respond to or are reluctant to receive a trauma-focused treatment.

Massed treatment

Existing evidence-based PTSD treatments can also be delivered in a massed (also referred to as “intensive” or “accelerated”) format, which involves daily sessions (or multiple sessions per day) instead of the traditional weekly schedule [22], [23]. This allows individuals to complete a full course of treatment in a matter of days or weeks rather than months. Although relatively novel in outpatient settings, massed PTSD treatments have repeatedly been shown to be equally effective as their weekly counterparts, producing large symptom reductions and improvements in functioning that are maintained over time [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. Moreover, massed treatments are generally associated with substantially higher treatment completion rates (>90%) compared to weekly formats (30-60%) [22], [30], [31].

Treatment non-response

Despite the strong effectiveness of first-line PTSD treatments, not all individuals who receive these treatments respond favorably. Estimates indicate that between 14% and 50% of individuals completing these gold-standard first-line PTSD interventions retain significant PTSD symptoms and/or continue to meet criteria for likely PTSD (hereafter collectively referred to as “non-responders”) [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]. Additional evidence suggests that as many as two in three treatment completers retain a diagnosable level of PTSD [36] and therefore continue to be at risk for reduced work, home, and social functioning. Furthermore, meta-analytic evidence indicates that SMVs tend to show attenuated treatment response relative to civilian populations [37]. The determination of treatment-resistant PTSD can be determined by instruments such as The Emory Treatment Resistance Interview for PTSD-Short Version (E-TRIP-S) [38].

Very few studies to date have examined treatment options for non-responders. Individuals may be non-responsive to first-line evidence-based PTSD treatments because they (1) received an inadequate dose of the intervention or (2) the intervention is not intervening on the appropriate mechanisms and thus is ineffective. Based on these hypotheses, studies have tested extending treatment by adding sessions to increase its dose (extension), adding another intervention to enhance treatment response (augmentation), or switching to a different treatment (switching).

Extending treatment by adding sessions and thus increasing dosage may be a viable option for improving PTSD treatment outcomes. One clinical trial in civilians examined whether extending CPT for individuals who did not reach an optimal end state after the planned 12 sessions would improve outcomes [39]. The results of this trial indicated that extending CPT improved outcomes for many initial non-responders. However, some individuals still retained PTSD diagnoses, suggesting that increasing the number of treatment sessions is not effective for all individuals [39]. This study did not compare switching treatments vs. extending treatment, so it is unclear whether the observed results were an effect of receiving more treatment sessions generally, or more sessions of CPT specifically. A recent large-scale retrospective study examined the medical records of veterans who received CPT and PE as part of their United States Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) care. This medical records review allowed for comparison of switching versus extending using real-world data. Results suggested that switching from CPT to PE may be beneficial, but switching from PE to CPT may not be [40]. Extending treatment did not show statistically significant benefits for either treatment. In a study of treatment resistance, 55.6% of patients who did not benefit from an evidence-based treatment for PTSD benefitted from another evidence-based treatment for PTSD [38].

Predicting responses

An additional challenge is identifying non-responders as early as possible during the initial treatment course; there is currently not a well-established method for doing so. As a result, patients and providers generally must wait until the completion of an evidence-based treatment protocol to determine whether the course of care was effective. Without having data-informed treatment decision logic, clinicians may continue to administer a treatment that is ineffective for the patient or discontinue effective treatments too early. This delay may decrease patients’ hope for improvement and increase the chances of discontinuing therapy. Moreover, completing a course of ineffective therapy puts undue and unnecessary burdens on healthcare systems, increases waste in healthcare utilization, and increases problems in access to care due to inefficient use of provider time.

To date, most studies that examine evidence-based PTSD treatment moderators have focused on baseline predictors, including demographic and clinical characteristics. Although these variables are important, studies including our own have shown that few baseline characteristics are generally robust enough predictors of treatment response to influence clinical decisions because of their relatively low accuracy [41], [42], [43]. Previous research has demonstrated that the accuracy of non-response predictions drastically improves from baseline predictions as treatment progresses. Specifically, treatment response has been predicted with >80% accuracy by mid-treatment by accounting for treatment progress variables (e.g., changes in daily PTSD symptom severity) [42].

The present study

The present study has four primary objectives. First, we seek to determine the feasibility of offering a subsequent course of evidence-based psychotherapy for non-responders. To evaluate feasibility, we plan to evaluate preferences in subsequent treatments in the equipoise design, as well as completion rates and satisfaction with each treatment. We hypothesize that at least 75% of individuals who receive a subsequent treatment will complete it and agree or strongly agree that they are satisfied with the subsequent treatment, suggesting feasibility. We do not have a priori hypotheses about whether certain subsequent treatments will be preferred, as all tested treatments have previously been demonstrated to be feasible.

Second, we aim to evaluate the efficacy of three subsequent treatment options for improving PTSD and depression symptoms, as well as functioning, among initial non-responders. We hypothesize that non-responders who receive a subsequent course of treatment will experience at least medium effect size improvements (d = .50 and equivalent) in PTSD and depression symptoms and functioning. Based on prior research [40], we hypothesize that individuals who switch to PE following non-response to CPT will experience greater symptom reductions compared to those who continue with CPT.

Third, we intend to determine whether and when treatment non-response can be predicted with >75% accuracy prior to initial treatment completion and develop easy-to-use algorithms that enable clinicians to identify non-responders. We hypothesize that non-response can be predicted with >75% accuracy by mid-treatment (initial CPT session 6) based on our prior research [41], [42].

Finally, we intend to test demographic, clinical (e.g., baseline symptom severity, comorbidities, treatment history), and initial treatment progress (e.g., changes in symptoms, avoidance, and trauma cognitions during initial treatment) variables as predictors of response to the three subsequent treatment options for initial non-responders. This aim is exploratory, and we do not have specific hypotheses about specific variables that may moderate response to subsequent treatment.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants will be SMVs and civilians who meet diagnostic criteria for PTSD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed.; DSM-5) [44], as assessed by the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5-Revised (CAPS-5-R) [45]. Participants must also have a total baseline PCL-5 score >28, consistent with the threshold for probable PTSD [46]. The index traumatic event must have occurred at least 1 month ago, and they must not have completed another evidence-based cognitive behavioral PTSD treatment in the past 3 months. Inclusion criteria also include ability to read and write in English, willingness to complete self-report measures and clinician-rated assessments, and ability to attend 10 to 20 therapy sessions. Exclusion criteria include current symptoms of unmanaged psychosis or mania, serious medical illness for which hospitalization would be likely, substance withdrawal, and suicidal or homicidal ideation with plan and imminent intent. Individuals will also be excluded based on circumstances that would prevent them from fully engaging in study activities, including intellectual disability or significant cognitive impairment, significant visual or auditory impairment, legal actions related to the index traumatic event, and extenuating life circumstances (e.g., unstable housing, no internet access, etc.). If applicable, participants must have been on a stable dose of any psychotropic medications for at least 1 month at the time of the baseline interview. We plan to enroll a total of 400 individuals to reach a final sample size of N = 300 individuals (i.e., 150 SMVs and 150 civilians) who start an initial course of CPT. This sample size was selected to provide at least 80% power to detect moderate effects across feasibility, efficacy, non-response timing, and moderation aims while accounting for attrition.

Study design

Interested individuals will complete a phone screen, sign an informed consent form, and then complete baseline assessments to determine eligibility. The initial treatment phase will use an open trial design where the entire sample (N = 300) receives a full course of massed CPT, which consists of 10 daily individual CPT telehealth sessions over the course of 2 weeks. Participants will complete daily assessments during treatment. Individuals who exhibit treatment response at the 1-week follow-up (i.e., PCL-5 severity score <28; [46]) will complete follow-up assessments 1-week, 1-, 3-, and 6-months after treatment (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study flow.

Individuals who exhibit non-response (i.e., PCL-5 severity score ≥28; [46]), on the other hand, will be offered a second course of treatment (PE, STAIR, or additional sessions of CPT). This second course of treatment will also consist of 10 daily individual telehealth sessions over the course of 2 weeks. Specifically, we will use an equipoise stratified randomization design in which individuals can de-select up to one subsequent treatment option after reviewing descriptions of each treatment; they will then be randomly assigned to one of the remaining options. Treatment descriptions are based on previous research [47], [48], feedback from a Community Advisory Board (CAB), and approval by clinical experts in each treatment. Participants will complete daily assessments during treatment and, following the completion of the second course of treatment, complete follow-up assessments at 1-week, 1-, 3-, and 6-months after treatment completion.

If a problematic imbalance occurs between treatments groups (e.g., more SMVs than civilians in one group), participants may be assigned to one of the underrepresented groups (when not de-selected by the participant) with binomial probability of .70, rather than .50, using the minimum sufficient balance procedure [49].

Participants and clinicians will not be blinded to the subsequent treatment to which they are assigned. Study therapists will be at least master's level providers and will complete official training workshops in CPT, PE, and STAIR. Study assessors will be at least bachelor's level and will receive rigorous training in the administration of clinical assessments. All treatment sessions will be video- and audio-recorded to evaluate fidelity and quality. Session recordings will be randomly selected (10% of initial CPT sessions, 20% of subsequent treatment sessions) and reviewed by an external fidelity rater with expertise in CPT, PE, and STAIR.

Participants will be recruited via provider referrals and waitlists from the Road Home Program at Rush, Warrior Care Network, and Rush University Medical Center. Participants may also be recruited via social media ads, phone-based “on hold” advertising, fliers, word-of-mouth at local VA Medical Centers, and mass a clinical trial recruitment firm.

To facilitate the translation of study findings into clinical practice, we created a CAB consisting of people with lived experiences with PTSD, military active-duty psychologists, and former executives of veteran service organizations. We consulted CAB members to ensure that the study and design were acceptable to the target population, and they will continue to provide feedback throughout the study via recurring meetings.

Treatments

Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)

CPT is an evidence-based psychotherapy for PTSD [9]. CPT relies on the use of cognitive restructuring techniques to identify and modify maladaptive trauma-related cognitions known as stuck points. CPT is typically conducted as a 12-session protocol, although findings from recent research support a variable treatment length approach [9], [28]. Significant gains in outcomes such as PTSD, functioning, and depression have been demonstrated and maintained in long-term follow-up intervals of up to five years [50], [51], [52]. CPT delivered virtually has been shown to produce the same strong clinical outcomes [28], [53], [54], [55], [56]. The massed delivery of CPT using daily sessions is now well-established and produces similarly large (and in some cases even larger) effects as the more traditional weekly delivery format [26], [28], [29], [56].

Prolonged Exposure (PE)

PE is an evidence-based psychotherapy developed to reduce the intensity and frequency of PTSD symptoms [10]. PE is a structured treatment that involves approximately 10 sessions. It consists of imaginal exposure followed by emotional processing conducted in session and in vivo exposure conducted as homework assignments. The overall treatment goal is to address the two major criteria thought to exacerbate PTSD symptoms: avoidance and erroneous beliefs about oneself, others, and the world [10], fostering emotional processing of the trauma. In a meta-analysis examining the effectiveness of PE, patients who received PE reported more significant reductions in symptoms than 86% of patients in control conditions [57]. A second meta-analysis showed that on average, 68% of those who completed PE no longer met diagnostic criteria for PTSD [58]. Similar to CPT, PE can be effectively delivered via telehealth [59], and massed PE using daily sessions produces large effects that are non-inferior to traditional weekly delivery [21].

Skills training for affective and interpersonal regulation (STAIR)

STAIR is an evidence-based cognitive behavioral therapy for individuals with PTSD [15]. STAIR consists of several treatment modules that can be flexibly delivered to address the unique emotional and interpersonal needs of individuals with PTSD. STAIR does not require individuals to discuss their traumatic experience in detail and demonstrates effect sizes among veterans as large as those found in trauma-focused treatments [12], [60]. We plan to deliver STAIR virtually and in massed format over 10 sessions to align with the other treatments. Following initial psychoeducation, individuals will learn emotion regulation skills. The second half of treatment will transition to focus on the experience of emotions in social and interpersonal contexts, which can help improve overall functioning. Newer studies have demonstrated that STAIR exhibits greater effectiveness compared to alternative second-line treatments for PTSD such as present centered therapy [61], suggesting that STAIR is a viable non-trauma-focused treatment option.

Compensation

Participants will be compensated for the completion of study-related assessments, including at baseline ($20), 1-week post-treatment follow-up ($50), subsequent 1-week follow-up if offered a second course of treatment ($50), as well as at the 1- ($50), 3- ($100), and 6-month ($100) follow-ups for a maximum of either $320 or $370.

Measures

Symptom measures

The primary outcome for this study is PTSD symptom severity measured by the PCL-5 [62] to align with assessment practices that are common in routine care and to facilitate the eventual translations of findings into clinical practice. The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report measure which asked respondents to rate how often they have been bothered by each item on a scale from Not At All (0) to Extremely (4) [62]. A total score of ≥ 28 suggests the presence of probable PTSD [46]. The CAPS-5-R [63], [64] is a semi-structured clinician-rated assessment of PTSD symptoms will also be used to characterize the sample and evaluate overall treatment outcomes, but will not be used to determine the initial responder status.

Additional clinical outcomes will be assessed using validated self-report surveys and interviews, including the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [65], Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 [66], Brief Addiction Monitor [BAM] [67], Posttraumatic Avoidance Behavior Questionnaire (PABQ) [68], Posttraumatic Cognitions Inventory (PTCI) [69], and others (see Table 1). Broader functional outcomes will be assessed via validated self-report assessments, including the World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale (WHOQOL-BREF), WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS 2.0), and Brief Inventory of Psychosocial Functioning [70], [71], [72].

We will assess which subsequent treatments are most frequently de-selected to evaluate the feasibility of providing subsequent courses of evidence-based psychotherapies for non-responders. The study will also evaluate whether at least 75% of non-responders complete a second course of treatment and report finding it to be beneficial via a satisfaction questionnaire.

Data analysis plan

Feasibility analysis

Descriptive statistics will be used to detail the proportion of individuals who complete treatment, quantitative satisfaction ratings, overall perceived feasibility, and perceived feasibility of each treatment condition. We plan to examine differences in satisfaction and feasibility between treatment conditions using logistic and ordinal logistic regressions.

Efficacy analysis

We will use linear mixed effects models (LMMs) to examine change in PTSD and functioning by treatment arm for non-responders. The main effects of treatment arms will provide information about overall differences in outcomes across timepoints, while treatment type by time interactions will indicate differences in the trajectory of change over time based on treatment group. Models will be adjusted for relevant demographic and clinical covariates. Within each of the three groups of non-responders, we will assess effect sizes for PTSD and functioning changes using Gibbons' variant of Cohen's d for repeated measures comparisons. Treatment group differences in PTSD severity and functioning outcomes over time will be explored via ordinal logistic or logistic regression. In addition to evaluating these main outcomes assessed up to 6 months following the completion of the subsequent course of treatment, self-reported PTSD severity assessed via the PCL-5 at each treatment day during the subsequent course of treatment will be assessed to evaluate the amount of symptom changes that occur each treatment day.

Non-response timing analysis

LMMs utilizing data from the initial course of CPT will model the amount of change in PTSD severity (or via separate LMMs, depression severity) per day (i.e., slopes) after controlling for time-varying covariates such as depression symptoms, avoidance, and trauma cognitions. This will allow for predictions of endpoint PTSD severity that can be updated during the initial course of treatment with information about amount of change in PTSD and depression severity that has occurred up to relevant treatment days and adjust predictions accordingly. We will also include baseline predictors, such as demographic variables, clinical comorbidities, and baseline psychiatric functioning as predictors of outcomes over time.

Moderator analysis

The potential effects of demographic and other clinical baseline covariates, and their interaction with treatment group, will be explored to assess the potential for differential effects of treatment type among non-responders at posttreatment (i.e., final day of treatment) and follow-up timepoints. Regression analysis for continuous outcomes, logistic regression for binary outcomes, and ordinal logistic regressions for ordinal outcomes will be conducted for follow-up explorations of the interaction between these variables and treatment type at individual timepoints. Inclusion of treatment type by demographic and baseline clinical variable interaction terms will assess whether any differences in outcomes across the treatment groups are moderated by–or differ based on–these variables.

Discussion

The proposed trial was designed to be directly relevant to both SMVs and civilians with PTSD by informing clinical practice guidelines for the treatment of initial non-responders. Results from this study hold particular relevance for service members, whose unaddressed PTSD may continue to interfere with their ability to perform their duties and may contribute to separating from the military. Improved treatment guidelines for non-responders could translate to meaningful improvements in fitness for duty and overall readiness. Over the longer term, improving treatment outcomes for initial non-responders has the potential to reduce the many concerns that commonly co-occur with PTSD, including death by suicide, homelessness, poorer marital functioning, impairments in work performance, and higher rates of unemployment [73], [74], [75], [76]. Moreover, successfully treating PTSD in individuals who would otherwise leave treatment with significant residual symptoms could meaningfully reduce the risk for suicide, which has been closely linked to unaddressed PTSD [77], [78]. Given the elevated rates of suicide among SMVs, this represents a critical long-term goal.

An important strength of this trial is that the subsequent treatment options under investigation are readily available and widely disseminated, making them more practical for real-world implementation than approaches that would require specialized clinicians, costly training programs, or prolonged rollout timelines. This trial may also advance precision medicine approaches to PTSD treatment by improving our understanding of the extent to which individual characteristics predict differential responses to subsequent treatments, which could ultimately help optimize treatment selection for individual patients. Such findings would support clinicians in making data-informed decisions about which subsequent treatment is most likely to be effective for a given patient based on their personal characteristics and initial treatment trajectory.
