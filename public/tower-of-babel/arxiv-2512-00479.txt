Mind the data gap: Missingness Still Shapes Large Language Model Prognoses Equal contribution
Yuta Kobayashi, Vincent Jeanselme ∗, Shalmali Joshi

Abstract
Data collection often reflects human decisions. In healthcare, for instance, a referral for a diagnostic test is influenced by the patient’s health, their preferences, available resources, and the practitioner’s recommendations. Despite the extensive literature on the informativeness of missingness, its implications on the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) have not been studied. Through a series of experiments on data from Columbia University Medical Center, a large urban academic medical center, and MIMIC-IV, we demonstrate that patterns of missingness significantly impact zero-shot predictive performance. Notably, the explicit inclusion of missingness indicators at prompting benefits some while hurting other LLMs’ zero-shot predictive performance and calibration, suggesting an inconsistent impact. The proposed aggregated analysis and theoretical insights suggest that larger models benefit from these interventions, while smaller models can be negatively impacted. The LLM paradigm risks obscuring the impact of missingness, often neglected even in conventional ML, even further. We conclude that there is a need for more transparent accounting and systematic evaluation of the impact of representing (informative) missingness on downstream performance.

Introduction

When data collection is a consequence of human decisions, patterns of observations are informative signals. Such informative patterns are particularly prevalent in healthcare, where they reflect interactions between patients and healthcare systems
(
Jeanselme 2024
)
, providing insights into patients’ conditions, preferences, resources, and providers’ expertise. For instance, a clinician orders an expensive test only if it is deemed helpful for diagnosis and potentially informs subsequent treatment.

Machine learning practitioners have often leveraged these observation patterns, as indicators of patient health, to improve predictive performance
(
Sisk et al. 2021
)
. For instance, several studies demonstrate that including an indicator of which tests were missing in the data enhances the predictive performance of recurrent neural networks
(
Lipton et al. 2016
;
Che et al. 2018
)
. However, the understanding of missingness in health is limited to the traditional paradigm of task-specific supervised models, which are trained with labeled data on a predefined outcome.

LLMs are being increasingly evaluated for their potential as zero-shot prognosticators relying on encoded medical knowledge priors in pre-training data, with text-serialized input patient context
(
Cui et al. 2025
;
Makarov et al. 2025
;
Hegselmann et al. 2023
;
Requeima et al. 2024
;
Choi et al. 2022
)
. A natural question is to quantify the extent to which informative missingness impacts such general-purpose models pretrained on web-scale data. This capability is particularly relevant in healthcare contexts, where variable names are semantically meaningful and can retrieve the associated clinical knowledge embedded in LLMs. However, it is unclear whether LLMs encode prior knowledge of correlational relationships between missing information and outcomes and how they may leverage such knowledge for zero-shot capabilities when prompted with partially observed information. Actionability of such zero-shot capabilities depends on the accuracy and uncertainty calibration of the prognoses.

Our work explores
how representing potentially informative missingness in prompts impacts predictions and (un)certainty scores of LLM outputs
. Our hypothesis is that, by leveraging contextual cues and encoded medical knowledge, LLMs could infer proxies for a patient’s health from explicit missingness indicators in the serialization of their health record data. Our findings reveal more nuanced conclusions.

Our experiments compare
10
LLMs on
3
different canonical EHR-based prediction tasks (long length of stay, mortality, readmission) - demonstrating the inconsistent effect of missingness serialization and instruction on zero-shot performance, and more importantly, uncertainty quantification. We find that all models are sensitive to explicit missingness serialization, but the magnitude and direction of its effect on both generated text and predictions are model- and task-dependent. Through an aggregated analysis, we evidence that larger models tend to benefit from interventions that represent missingness in input encodings to LLMs, while smaller ones can be negatively impacted. We complement these empirical findings with theoretical insights that support and help explain this trend.

For example, on the canonical mortality prediction benchmark in the MIMIC-IV dataset
(
Kolo et al. 2024
)
, the addition of instructions to ‘reason’ about missing data (or absence of certain information) and indicators of missingness in text-serialized input prompts can shift zero-shot predictions by more than 16% for Qwen 3 14B  and 11% for GPT-OSS 20B. These effects cannot be explained by training leakage, as we replicate results and find consistent patterns on an external dataset, CUMC (see App.
D
).

Together, these findings lead to the following conclusions:

•

Zero-shot capability of LLMs is sensitive to representations of missingness. Missingness-aware inference, using instructions to account for missingness and representation of missingness via indicators, can substantially shift prediction performance and calibration.

•

Different LLMs encode or decode prior knowledge differently, resulting in inconsistent impact of missingness serialization on zero-shot capabilities.

•

Larger models are better able to capture the informativeness of missingness, yet they still require steering to use it correctly.

In summary, our results demonstrate that despite the compelling natural language interface offered by LLMs to interact with partially observed information, missingness continues to impact their predictive accuracy and calibration, underscoring the importance and urgency of systematically studying how missing information impacts LLM inference.

Related work: Informative missingness and LLM predictions

Despite extensive research on missingness and its impact on statistical modeling, the intersection of missingness with LLMs remains underexplored. The following outlines the research conducted on this topic.

LLM pretraining is autoregressive, often relying on next token prediction. Multiple works have explored the capacity of these models to impute missing values, such as infilling blank text
(
Donahue et al. 2020
)
, time series completion
(
Gruver et al. 2023
)
, or missing measurements or values
(
Ding et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024
;
Nazir et al. 2023
)
. However, this focus on imputation is disconnected from settings where these models are used for inferences or predictions. Our focus is not on imputation, but on understanding the impact of encoding missingness on zero-shot predictions.

Focusing on missingness at inference,
Wang et al. 2024
proposes an ask-before-answer using chain-of-thought (CoT) to identify which missing information should be acquired before answering.
Fu et al. 2025
demonstrates how LLMs fail to identify missing text by prompting the model to compare two short vignettes. In their experiments, LLMs are unable to recognize the text that has been synthetically removed. The authors demonstrate that including placeholders in the vignette enhances performance. Despite a simple identification and textual settings, this last conclusion is most closely aligned with the goal of our work to study the impact of indicators on LLM predictions.

3
Experiment Design: Evaluating impact of missingness encoding on LLM predictions

We describe the experimental design and associated findings on the impact of encoding missingness at inference time on LLM predictive performance. Following the literature using LLMs for medical predictions
(
Hegselmann et al. 2025
;
Hegselmann et al. 2023
)
, we provide patient data as text-serialized inputs and prompt
10
LLMs to predict an outcome of interest along with an associated confidence score. To measure the impact of missingness, we consider two dimensions: adding missingness indicators to the text-serialized input and incorporating a prompt to ‘reason’ about missingness, yielding a total of four strategies.

3.1
Experiment design

We propose the following empirical setting.

Models considered.

Our experiments rely on 10 LLMs: Qwen3
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
, considering 8, 14, and 32 billion parameters, GPT-OSS
(
OpenAI 2025b
)
with the 20 and 120 billion parameters, Llama3.1
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
with 8 and 70 billion parameters, MedGemma (27B
Sellergren et al. 2025
), Mistral Small 3.1 (24B
AI 2025
), and GPT-5
(
OpenAI 2025a
)
. This selection aims to cover a diverse set of models with varying sizes and pretraining data, as the model capacity and contextual information may influence LLMs’ capacity to handle missingness at inference. Note that we focus our analysis on local models and only consider GPT-5 for MIMIC-IV under a HIPAA-compliant use agreement.

Tasks.

We adopt three common clinical prognoses tasks: predicting long length of stay (
Long LOS
, i.e., over 7 days), in-hospital mortality (
Mortality
), and 30-day readmission (
Readmission
). To predict these outcomes, we use a subset of continuous laboratory measurements (29 features for the MIMIC-IV dataset
(
Johnson et al. 2020
)
, 34 features for CUMC). Features were selected based on their frequency in the dataset and to ensure coverage of a broad range of clinically relevant measurements. While additional patient context, such as conditions and procedures, may improve predictions, we focus on a small set of measurements to assess the influence of missingness. We use the most recent measurements for each test during a 30-day observation window preceding prediction time, which is 48 hours after hospital admission. If no measurement was recorded during this period, the feature is treated as missing.

Prompts.

Our prompt consists of a serialization of the laboratory tests and task-specific instructions. For serialization, we enumerate all laboratory tests in a textual format, as proposed by
Hegselmann et al. 2025
and adopted in various subsequent works
(
Lee et al. 2025
)
(see App.
A
for a detailed example). To measure the impact of missingness, we explore two different serialization strategies: (i) the addition of a missingness indicator (denoted as
Indicator
), where missing feature names are included with a subsequent string “not measured during observation window”, and (ii) missing features are dropped from the serialization completely, as commonly done in practice (
Dropped
). Additionally, we explore the impact of prompting by adding instructions to reason about the potential informativeness of missing data in recorded measurements. We consider the addition of the prompt: “
In addition to the observed values, consider the missingness of recorded measurements as potentially informative. Explicitly reason about why certain measurements might be missing, and how their absence (not being measured) could affect your answer.
” by itself (
Instruction
) and, in addition to the missingness serialization (
Indicator + Inst.
).

Evaluation.

To limit computational cost, we randomly sample 5,000 unique patients for evaluation. For each, we use the previous prompt and instruct the model to verbalize uncertainty as a probability of observing the outcome of interest
(
Kadavath et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Kapoor et al. 2024
)
. We then process the output to extract the probabilities and evaluate the quality of uncertainty quantification in this zero-shot setting. We focus on the subset of patients for which a ‘probability’ score is reported by all models. We reject samples where outputs were incorrectly formatted by a model (see App.
C.2
). To evaluate the quality of uncertainty quantification, we compute the expected calibration error (ECE), which measures how well predicted probabilities align with observed outcomes (see Appendix
B
for a formal definition). Additionally, we evaluate the impact on discriminative performance using AUROC.

3.2
Results

Figure

Logistic regression shows the informativeness of missingness.

As a baseline, we present 3-fold cross-validated performances of logistic regression models on the same set of 5000 patients. These baselines capture the correlational (and potentially spurious) patterns between missingness and outcomes specific to the observed dataset, resulting in strong predictive performance and well-calibrated uncertainty. The informative nature of missingness is reflected in the improved ECE and predictive performance compared to the regression that ignores these patterns.

Impact of representing informative missingness is inconsistent across LLMs.

While the logistic regression baseline demonstrates the predictive value of missingness, LLMs present mixed conclusions. For instance, GPT-OSS 20B  models show enhanced performance under all interventions when predicting Long LOS but a negative impact for Mortality, suggesting that these models can leverage prior knowledge about potential correlations between missingness and Long LOS. Importantly, we find that the largest benefit is achieved when using instruction to account for missingness in uncertainty quantification (Indicator + Inst.). By explicitly guiding the LLM to ‘reason’ about feature missingness, the final prediction conditioned on the generated response exhibits improved calibration. On the contrary, Qwen 3 14B has lower calibration when integrating the missingness-aware instructions across tasks. While the models perform similarly when ignoring missingness, the calibration error increases by more than 50% for Qwen 3 14B. These results suggest that the encoding and/or decoding of the relationship between missingness and outcomes differs in these models.

Theoretical insights

We propose a theoretical intuition of the previous empirical findings by decomposing the prediction error.

Consider
\mathcal{V}
, the vocabulary and
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{V}^{n}
, the space of all possible token sequences. Let
X\in\mathcal{S}
be the serialization of the observed patient’s measurements, and
Y\in\{0,1\}

M\in\mathcal{S}
denote the serialized missingness indicator, and

is exogenous to the underlying data-generating process, as formalized in Assumption
4.1

Assumption 4.1

(Prompt ignorability)
.

The missingness instructions do not carry information about the patient’s outcome that is not present in the measurements or missingness serialization, i.e.,
I\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid X,M
.

Let
f
be the pretrained LLM, and we denote
f^{\pi}(X)
as the LLM that takes

as input and uses a decoding strategy
\pi

I
as an intervention that influences the decoding, where intuitively the choice of instruction steers the LLM to select the appropriate decoding mechanism for handling the missingness
M

f^{\pi}

\displaystyle\approx p(Y\mid X)

Although the decomposition in Theorem

Theorem 1

(Error decomposition)

For any predictor

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{\pi})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{*}+\big(\mathbb{E}_{X,M}-\mathbb{E}_{X}\big)\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p\,\|\,q\big)+\mathrm{CE}\big(f^{\pi}\big)-\mathrm{CE}\big(q\big)\Big].

\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{*})\leq\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{*})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f)-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f)=\epsilon^{*}+\underbrace{\big(\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f)-\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{*})\big)}_{\text{Approximation error with }X,M}

Therefore, adding missingness indicators may lead to an increase in expected loss when the approximation error is larger with

p
is the true distribution of
Y

\displaystyle q=\min_{q^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[\text{KL}(p\mid\mid q^{\prime})\big]

We can rewrite the model approximation error for expected log loss as follows, consisting of two terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{U}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{U}(f^{*})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[\text{KL}(p\mid\mid q)\big]}_{\text{1. prior misalignment}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[CE(f^{\pi})-CE(q)\big]}_{\text{2. prompt/decoding error}}

where

is the cross-entropy loss under

p
and

\epsilon^{*}
) (ii) prior misalignment of the zero-shot LLM, and (iii) additional error introduced by prompt/decoding.

Decoding error: the effect of instructions.

When the pretraining data is fixed, smaller models may suffer from longer-context degradation or weaker CoT reasoning as
M
is introduced, leading to the second term becoming negative. We anticipate the contribution of the second term to diminish as the models become larger and decoding capabilities improve, which is consistent with our empirical observation where, as model size increases, the explicit missingness serialization improves ECE.

Furthermore, despite the same achievable loss across a given frozen LLM, we find empirically that the additional instructions
I
compromise the decoding ability for smaller LLMs, leading to larger decoding error. Finetuning these smaller LLMs using approaches such as preference optimization
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
may help steer their behavior so that
f^{\pi(I)}
better aligns with the test distribution and recovers the achievable risk.

Prior misalignment: comparing encoded knowledge across model families.

The first shift term is LLM family-specific and corresponds to the world knowledge (or lack of necessary domain knowledge) in the LLM’s specific pretraining. Given that it is unlikely for each LLM to have been pretrained on the exact task with data from
P
, we can also interpret this as a zero-shot baseline error of a frozen LLM independent of steering and decoding policy.

For example, the semantics of the missingness or the relationship between the missingness and patient outcome, may have existed differently in pretraining, compared to what is observed in the hospital’s test distribution
P_{\text{test}}
, leading to a generalization gap. For example, when comparing a medically-tuned model Med-Gemma or GPT to Mistral across the same prompting and decoding policy, we find that Mistral has higher overall ECE, suggesting larger prior misalignment corresponding to the first term and possible lack of required medical knowledge. This may indicate that additional new knowledge may need to be introduced to the LLM via finetuning.

5
Discussion

We demonstrate that general-purpose LLMs don’t address the long-standing statistical challenge of missingness despite zero-shot capabilities and apparent agnosticism in representing missingness. To improve their reliability, the impact of missingness representations should be evaluated in tandem with downstream performance. Particularly, our results indicate that larger models benefit from prompting and serialization of missingness, as measured by predictive performance and uncertainty calibration, whereas smaller models can be negatively affected by these interventions.

Echoing
Nijman et al. 2022
;
Jeanselme et al. 2022
’s criticism of the lack of reporting and inappropriate handling of missing data in machine learning papers, this work emphasizes the importance of such practices for deploying LLMs. While the paradigm made possible by these models further disconnects training data quality, such as missingness patterns, from downstream performance, the field needs to consider this data dimension. Critically, more data or model parameters can not address missing-(not)-at-random patterns of observation
(
Rubin 1976
)
that result in sampling biases.

A key assumption of learning from missing data is that observation patterns remain constant at deployment. This assumption is unlikely to hold as practice evolves
(
Groenwold 2020
)
. While we focus on tasks where downstream users are clinicians or healthcare specialists, this work also has implications for patient-facing LLMs. As the general public increasingly relies on LLMs to seek health advice
(
Ayre et al. 2025
;
Shahsavar et al. 2023
;
Kullgren et al. 2025
)
, the inconsistent impact of representing missing information impacts the downstream reliability of predictions.
For instance, a patient may prompt an LLM with only a subset of the information in a blood test that they do not understand. Ensuring that LLMs handle missingness robustly to such shifts induced by user behavior or practice changes is critical to ensure their safety.

Limitations.

Our analysis presents evidence of the impact of missingness on LLMs’ predictions. The observational nature of our analysis aims to reflect the real-world setting in which these models are used and evaluate their potential capacity to leverage contextual information and external knowledge to address not-at-random missingness patterns. However, this approach limits the study of observational patterns, as one cannot enforce realistic missing-at-random or missing-not-at-random data that would be captured in model pretraining, thereby limiting the understanding of which types of missingness these models may be robust to. Another limitation of our analysis is the focus on zero-shot approaches. While further fine-tuning may improve performance and mitigate the impact of missing indicators, the proposed analysis aims to capture the current use of LLMs as experienced by users of publicly available LLMs. Finally, our regression is based on a limited number of LLMs, which may hurt the generalizability of the results. Our future work will validate these results across a larger number of models.

Conclusion.

The proposed analysis offers a crucial insight: the design of LLM applications must pay more attention to the importance of information the user does
not
provide. Our results show that different LLMs are disparately impacted by informative missingness, with explicit indicators and prompting strategies improving performance for larger models but hurting smaller ones.
In our future work, we will explore how prompt tuning and completion, in which users’ prompts are updated before inference, may improve the quality of LLM prognoses.

A.1
Instructions

Each prompt starts with an initial instruction (“You are a helpful assistant that can answer questions about the patient’s electronic health record.”).

Further, we consider the addition of the following instruction:
“In addition to the observed values, consider the missingness of recorded measurements as potentially informative. Explicitly reason about why certain measurements might be missing, and how their absence (not being measured) could affect your answer.”

This instruction is then followed by a task-specific query, as follows:

LOS.
“Will this admitted patient be hospitalized for longer than 7 days?”

•

Mortality.
“Will this admitted patient experience in-hospital mortality during this hospitalization?”

Appendix B
Expected calibration error

Let
Y\in\{0,1\}
be the true label, and
\hat{p}\in[0,1]
be the model prediction of
Y=1
. We define bins
B_{k},k\in{1,...,}K
that partitions
[0,1]
. The ECE is computed as follows:

\text{ECE}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Pr(\hat{p}\in B_{k})\,\Big|\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\hat{p}\in B_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}\mid\hat{p}\in B_{k}]\Big|

(1)

Appendix E
Proofs

We provide the proof for the decomposition in Theorem
1
, repeated below.

Theorem 1

(Error decomposition)
.

For any LLM-based predictor
f^{\pi}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{\pi})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{*}+\big(\mathbb{E}_{X,M}-\mathbb{E}_{X}\big)\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p\,\|\,q\big)+\mathrm{CE}\big(f^{\pi}\big)-\mathrm{CE}\big(q\big)\Big].

Here
\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{\pi})
and
\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{\pi})
denote the population log loss
under
P_{X,M}
and
P_{X}
, respectively, and
\epsilon^{*}
is the difference between the optimal expected loss under
P_{X,M}
and
P_{X}
.
q
denotes the best predictor achievable zero-shot from the LLM’s pretrained world/domain knowledge,
\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)
is the Kullback–Leibler divergence
between
p
and
q
, and
\mathrm{CE}(\cdot)
denotes cross-entropy.

Proof.

We begin by adding and subtracting the expected loss of the Bayes–optimal predictor
f^{*}
, which yields a telescoping decomposition of the risk difference:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{\pi})=\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{*})-(\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{*}))+\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{*})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X,M}(f^{*})-(\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{X}(f^{*}))+\epsilon^{*}

Now we decompose each of the first two approximation error terms. Let
p=p(Y\mid U)
,
f^{\pi}=f^{\pi}(Y\mid U)
and
q=q(Y\mid U)
. Suppose
f^{*}=p
is the Bayes optimal approximator of
p
. Then we have the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{U}(f^{\pi})-\mathcal{L}_{U}(f^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\bigg[-\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid U}\big[\log f^{\pi}-\log p\big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid U}\big[\log p-\log f^{\pi}+\log q-\log q\big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid U}\big[\log\frac{p}{q}-\log f^{\pi}+\log q\big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\bigg[\text{KL}(p\mid\mid q)+\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid U}\big[-\log f^{\pi}\big]+\mathbb{E}_{Y\mid U}\big[\log q\big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\bigg[\text{KL}(p\,\|\,q)+\text{CE}(f^{\pi})-\text{CE}(q)\bigg]

∎
