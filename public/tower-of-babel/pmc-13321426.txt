Minimally invasive thoracoscopic surgical ablation and hybrid ablation in AF patients: results from a single-arm systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fouad Hanna, Ali Dway, Hamza A Abdul-Hafez, Yousef M Husseiny, Ahmed Youssef Hassan, Mazen Negmeldin A Yassin, Basel Saber Hussein Ayoub, Hadeel Jameel Ayesh, Omneya Kandil, Abdelrahman Elgendy, Nada G Hamam, Abdelrahman El-Helbawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most frequent cardiac arrhythmia in adults, with presentations from asymptomatic to severe cardiovascular morbidities. Cardiac ablation treats patients resistant to antiarrhythmic drugs (AAD) using approaches such as bilateral surgical ablation, which permits targeting of superior vena cava lesions but entails longer procedures and higher complication risk, and hybrid ablation that combines epicardial and endocardial techniques.MethodWe performed a meta-analysis per PRISMA guidelines, including observational and interventional single-arm studies of patients undergoing bilateral surgical or hybrid ablation in single- and double-setting subgroups. Studies were selected by predefined criteria, and data were extracted independently by 12 investigators. Searches covered multiple databases up to March 29, 2025.ResultThirty-two studies with 1,871 patients were evaluated. Overall complication rates were 0.11 (95% CI: 0.08–0.14) for surgical ablation and 0.13 (95% CI: 0.095–0.17) for hybrid ablation, with no significant subgroup difference (p = 0.093). Arrhythmia-free rates were 0.81 (95% CI: 0.69–0.89) for surgical and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.70–0.85) for hybrid ablation (p = 0.305). Mean operative times were 160.9 min (95% CI: 135 − 186.9) for surgical and 192.65 min (95% CI: 109.3–275.9) for hybrid ablation (p = 0.069). Bleeding rates were 0.03 (95% CI: 0.02–0.04) and 0.049 (95% CI: 0.03–0.07), respectively (p = 0.06). Mortality, stroke, conversion to sternotomy, and hospital length of stay showed no significant differences.ConclusionSurgical ablation yielded a slightly higher arrhythmia-free rate and fewer total complications, while hybrid ablation had shorter operative time; subgroup (single vs. double setting) differences were not significant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04135-4.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Hybrid ablation, Bilateral thoracoscopic ablation

Introduction

The most common cardiac arrhythmia in adults is atrial fibrillation (AF), which is estimated to have a prevalence of 60 million cases all over the world [1]. The clinical manifestations of AF may be asymptomatic or present with chest pain, palpitations, and dyspnea. Moreover, patients have the risk of developing significant cardiovascular morbidities, including cardiogenic shock, ischemic stroke, and sudden cardiac death [2]. These negative outcomes place a substantial burden on global healthcare systems, and therefore, it is warranted to comprehensively evaluate the management of symptomatic AF [3, 4].

According to ESC/ACC/AHA guidelines, AF management includes rhythm control (via cardioversion or ablation) plus rate control, anticoagulation, and risk factor modification. Antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) remain a cornerstone in rhythm management but are limited by poor long-term efficacy, frequent adverse effects, and life-threatening toxicities [3, 5, 6].

The Cox-Maze IV procedure remains the surgical treatment of choice for paroxysmal or persistent AF, particularly in patients undergoing concomitant cardiac surgery [5, 7]. However, the need for cardiopulmonary bypass and a high complication rate hinder the approach for many patients [8]. Therefore, minimally invasive ablation approaches were needed to avoid the risks associated with traditional surgical ablation. Catheter ablation has emerged as a superior alternative to medical and surgical therapy for paroxysmal or persistent AF patients, particularly those who failed medical treatment and have contraindications to such therapies [5]. However, the long-term maintenance of sinus rhythm for non-paroxysmal (persistent and long-standing persistent) AF is less than optimal, and the need for repeat ablation procedures is common [9–12].

Considering the limitations and challenges, hybrid ablation has been developed with the aim of combining the advantages of thoracoscopic surgical ablation (direct visualization of anatomical structures and possibility to isolate epicardial lesions) and endocardial catheter ablation (possibility to isolate endocardial lesions). Several studies have reported favorable outcomes of hybrid ablation for persistent AF, establishing it as a valuable treatment approach for non-paroxysmal AF [13, 14]. However, the ideal access and timing of epicardial and endocardial ablation remain debatable. Hybrid ablation can be performed by a unilateral or bilateral approach, with significant variations between the two accesses. Bilateral thoracoscopic approach allows targeting of superior vena cava lesions, although it is associated with a longer procedural duration and an increased risk of complications [15]. On the other hand, a unilateral approach minimizes surgical trauma and is associated with a shorter procedural duration [15]. Comparative studies of both thoracoscopic accesses are lacking, with the bulk of findings derived from case series or retrospective cohort studies with a small sample size.

Additionally, the timing of epicardial and endocardial ablation ranges from same-day to staged procedures. It has been suggested that delaying endocardial ablation after epicardial ablation may allow for resolution of tissue inflammation, which impedes conduction across linear epicardial ablation and thus increases the risk of atrial arrhythmia recurrence [16, 17]. Similarly, few studies have compared timing strategies for the thoracoscopic approach, leaving a knowledge gap that remains to be filled.

In the paroxysmal and persistent AF population, the optimal approach remains unclear. Data derived mainly from retrospective cohort studies and case series are conflicting. Therefore, we performed this systematic review and meta-analysis to pool efficacy and safety data from the available literature, with a primary focus on comparing the outcomes of bilateral thoracoscopic surgical ablation versus hybrid ablation techniques.

Methods

Guidelines and ethics

This systematic review and meta-analysis were carried out according to the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: RD420251107935) [18]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement and the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses of Interventions (version 5.1.0) were strictly followed during the development of this study [19].

Literature review

Information Sources and Search Strategy We comprehensively searched PubMed’, Web of Science’, ‘Scopus’, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (from inception to August, 2025). The keywords used for the participants were [‘‘Atrial Fibrillation’’ or ‘‘AF’’]. This was combined with the keywords for the procedures, which included [‘‘Minimally Invasive Cardiac Ablation,’’ ‘‘Thoracoscopic Ablation,’’ ‘‘Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery,’’ ‘‘VATS,’’ ‘‘Mini-Maze Procedure,’’ ‘‘Cox-Maze Procedure,’’ ‘‘Surgical Ablation,’’ ‘‘Hybrid AF Therapy,’’ or ‘‘Convergent Ablation’’].

Selection process and data management

All electronic databases’ records retrieved through the literature search were imported into EndNote (Clarivate Analytics, PA) to remove duplicates. Next, all obtained citations were exported to Rayyan software (Qatar Computing Research Institute, 2016, Doha, Qatar) for initial title and abstract screening [20] and subsequent full-text screening of eligible titles and abstracts. Two authors performed the screening process, and the results were thoroughly revised. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a senior author. Data were extracted to standardized Google Sheets [Alphabet Inc. (Google), USA]. Subsequently, a senior author reviewed the sheets to identify any inconsistencies in the collected data. The extracted data comprised the following items: [1] characteristics of the included studies [2], characteristics of the included studies’ population, and [3] outcome measures, like sinus rhythm conversion, arrhythmia-free survival, AF recurrence, and complications (i.e., stroke, bleeding, phrenic nerve injury, mortality).

Eligibility criteria

A research question was formulated using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) [21] method to determine our study group. This was followed by a set of inclusion and exclusion criteria, with no limitations according to age, gender, race, country, or date of publication. Every two authors screened the studies independently, and conflicts were resolved by discussion.

Studies were included in our systematic review and meta-analysis if they satisfied the following criteria:

(1) Population: adult patients (> 18 years of age) suffering from paroxysmal, persistent, or long-standing Atrial Fibrillation indicated for surgical correction.

(2) Intervention: Studies that included bilateral radiofrequency Thoracotomy Surgical Ablation or Single and multiple setting Hybrid Ablation.

(3) Outcomes: Studies that reported at least one of the following outcomes: sinus rhythm conversion, arrhythmia-free survival, AF recurrence, and complications (i.e., stroke, bleeding, phrenic nerve injury, mortality).

(4) Study design: RCTs, non-RCTs, prospective or retrospective observational studies published in English. We excluded book chapters, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, case reports, and reviews.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was procedural success, which was defined as patient freedom from arrhythmia. Freedom from arrhythmia was assessed from the end of the procedure (for the surgical ablation group) or after the final stage of therapy (for the hybrid ablation group). It was characterized by the absence of any symptomatic or asymptomatic episodes of atrial fibrillation during follow-up monitoring, which could also allow for the discontinuation of antiarrhythmic medications [22]. Additional primary outcomes related to resource utilization were the duration of the operative procedure and the length of hospital stay.

The secondary outcomes were related to postoperative adverse events, which were as follows: Bleeding incidence, stroke, and conversion to sternotomy.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias (ROB) of all included studies was precisely assessed through 2 blinded reviewers using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool, which is explicitly designed for single-arm studies. This tool is composed of nine main domains: Clarity of stating the Objective, Eligibility Criteria of each study, Representative of the Study Population, Consistency of the Intervention, Validity of the Outcomes, Blinding of Outcome Assessors, follow-up Rates and Attrition, Statistical Analysis, and Handling of Missing Data. Authors’ judgements for each domain were grouped as ‘Low risk,’ Some concerns,’ or ‘High risk’ of bias. A third expert author discussed and resolved any differences in data extraction and bias assessment.

Strategy for data synthesis

The Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) software (Version 3, Biostat, Inc.) was used to perform the statistical analysis. The data synthesis should have focused on the synthesis of pooled summary estimates of each outcome separately in both the surgical ablation and hybrid ablation cohorts due to the nature of the included studies, which were all single-arm (they did not have a direct comparator group).

To compute the rate of events (arrhythmia-free survival, complication rates), the event rate was determined as the percentage of patients who experienced the event. Random-effects model was used as the pooled analysis to take into consideration the heterogeneity among the studies, where it is assumed that the true effect sizes differ across the populations that are included. A logit transformation was used to calculate the pooled estimates and 95% confidence intervals. This transformation ensures that the variance is stabilized and that the pooled proportions remain within the 0 to 1 range. In case of continuous outcomes (e.g., operative time, length of stay in a hospital), the pooled mean estimate was calculated using the mean value presented in each of the studies. As with the dichotomous results, a random-effects model was used, and the findings are presented as the pooled mean with a 95% CI.

Heterogeneity was evaluated and taken as statistically significant when the p-value was below 0.10. The Q (chi-square test) of Cochran and the degree of freedom (DF) were also determined to test the heterogeneity.

The hybrid ablation group was pre-specified to undergo subgroup analysis to determine the impact of the timing of the procedures (single-setting and double-setting) on the results. Tests of statistical significance (p-value < 0.05) were made between subgroups. It was assumed that the study-to-study variance ( tau squared) was equal across all subgroups - this was calculated within subgroups and then pooled across subgroups. When one fails to find a statistically significant difference between subgroups, this should not be construed as an indication that the effect is identical in subgroups because statistical power to find subgroup differences is usually low. To investigate potential sources of between-study heterogeneity, univariable random-effects meta-regression analyses were performed using the method-of-moments (MM) estimator with Z-distribution inference, analyses were limited to outcome–intervention combinations with at least 10 contributing studies (k ≥ 10), consistent with recommendations for adequate moderator estimation. Continuous moderators were examined: mean patient age and mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Both were grand-mean centred prior to analysis to reduce collinearity with the intercept and to yield clinically interpretable regression coefficients (β). Moderator significance was assessed using Cochran’s Qmodel test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Residual between-study heterogeneity was quantified by τ² and I² after moderator adjustment. The proportion of between-study variance explained by each model was estimated using the R² analogue. Given the exploratory nature and the modest number of studies per model, findings are considered hypothesis-generating.

The focus was made on the size of the point estimate and the accuracy of the 95% CI instead of the use of p-values alone. All the tests were two-sided, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as significant, except the test of heterogeneity (p < 0.10).

Results

Selection and characteristics (search results)

The literature search identified a total of 4428 studies: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (n = 23), Scopus (n = 1192), Web of Science (n = 1050), and PubMed (n = 2163). After removing duplicates (n = 1377), 3051 studies were obtained for title-and-abstract screening. According to our inclusion/exclusion criteria, we excluded 2427 papers, and 624 studies were obtained for full-text screening. The full-text screening was done, and only 32 papers were eligible for inclusion from the 4 databases. Finally, 32 studies with a total of 1,871 patients were included in this meta-analysis after full-text review [23–43]. (Fig. 1)

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool. The methodological quality assessment of the included studies indicated a generally low risk of bias, with approximately 78% of the cohort achieving an overall “low risk” rating, while only 10% and 12% were categorized as high or unclear risk, respectively. High rigor was consistently observed across several domains, particularly regarding the clarity of research objectives (D1), representative study populations (D3), and the standardized delivery of interventions (D6). However, systemic high risk was identified in the blinding of participants and outcome assessors (D8 and D12), reflecting a universal limitation likely inherent to the nature of the interventions studied. Furthermore, nearly 50% of the studies exhibited high or unclear risk regarding sample size adequacy (D5), suggesting potential underpowering in a portion of the literature. Despite these limitations and the identification of three specific studies (Edgerton 2 2009, Maclean 2020, and Wojtaszczyk 2017) with an overall high risk of bias, the high degree of compliance in most methodological domains suggests that the synthesized evidence provides a reliable basis for the review’s conclusions. Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluated single-arm bilateral surgical ablation and hybrid ablation for paroxysmal and persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), reporting pooled procedural rates and outcomes, rather than offering head-to-head comparisons. Our study’s primary findings show that bilateral surgical ablation is associated with higher rates of freedom from arrhythmia (83% vs. 75%), and an improved safety profile with a lower complication rate overall (10% vs. 13%) and bleeding rates (2% vs. 4%). In addition, hybrid ablation had a shorter mean operative time (151 min vs. 192 min, respectively), particularly for the single-stage procedure.

Of note, the critical outcomes, such as mortality, stroke, conversion to sternotomy, and lengths of hospital stay, were low in both groups, indicating that both were relatively safe in these aspects. In terms of effectiveness, the surgery arm had a better arrhythmia-free survival, highlighting the effectiveness of a pure surgical strategy. This finding presents a clear picture, with the majority of recent literature comparing hybrid ablation to catheter ablation, hybrid techniques showing increased long-term success, especially in long-standing persistent or persistent AF [15, 44]. Our study demonstrates that hybrid ablation is an improvement upon endocardial catheter ablation but has yet to reach the level of effectiveness of a comprehensive surgical procedure. These findings align with a meta-analysis by Varzaly et al., which reported a rate of 79.4% sinus rhythm with hybrid ablation [14]. Our subgroup analysis surprisingly favored single-setting hybrid procedures, which demonstrated a higher arrhythmia-free rate (78%) compared to double-setting procedures (72%). This suggests that a comprehensive one-stage procedure may provide a more robust lesion set, despite requiring a longer operative time. Although hybrid ablation aims to balance invasiveness and procedure duration [14], this approach does not consistently result in shorter operative time, with single-setting procedures averaging a substantial 281.9 min and double-setting procedures a quite moderate 125.5 min. Despite this, the mean length of hospital stay was not substantially different (6.0 days surgical vs. 5.6 days hybrid).

Of safety concern, higher rates of total complications and bleeding in the hybrid group are also reported in other research work [45, 46]. Far more critically, the double-setting hybrid subgroup developed greater rates of complications (17% vs. 9%) and bleeding (7% vs. 3%) than the single-setting subgroup, suggesting that the cumulative risk of two separate procedures may surpass that of a single, longer operation.

Reassuringly, mortality was similarly low in both groups at 1%, as corroborated by other evidence of no difference in short-term mortality [44, 47]. These findings have direct clinical applicability, implicating a trade-off between efficacy and procedural ease. In those where maximal efficacy is the overriding consideration, surgical ablation alone may be the best strategy, consistent with data for excellent efficacy of the Cox-maze procedure’ [47]. Hybrid ablation is nonetheless a valuable option, especially for those with long-standing persistent AF or failed catheter ablations, as stated in the 2024 ESC Guidelines [48, 49]. Our data on single vs double-setting procedures add another dimension to this decision, with the suggestion that a single-setting approach, although demanding, may offer enhanced efficacy with fewer morbidity. Such a decision should be undertaken by a multidisciplinary heart team after considering patient-specific factors and institutional experience as noted in the 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guidelines [10, 50]. However, these findings should be considered in the context of the study limitations.

First, the review lacks formal statistical reporting of heterogeneity, which may influence the validity of conclusions. Besides, clinical heterogeneity because of combined patient populations and approaches, data pooling, and focus on short and medium-term outcomes limits the scope and relevance of the outcomes in the long term. Transcending these limitations guides some key directions of future research. There is an imperative need for large, multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) between modern surgery and hybrid strategies with standardized end-point definitions and long-term follow-up. Next-generation research should also adopt AF burden as a first-line endpoint for a more complete assessment of efficacy, include patient-reported outcomes, compare these approaches to more recent technologies like pulsed field ablation (PFA) [51], and conduct robust cost-effectiveness analysis.

Overall, this meta-analysis suggests that bilateral surgical ablation has more freedom from arrhythmia and a better safety profile than hybrid ablation in paroxysmal and persistent AF patients. Hybrid ablation is, however, also a practical treatment option, with great variability in outcome based on the technique of procedure (single vs. double-setting). These results underscore the current efficacy, safety, and complexity of procedures, balance, and underscore the need to make the decisions of the procedure patient-specific and the necessity to conduct additional high-quality studies to optimize the technical methods of the procedures, to select patients optimally, and to find the long-term role of each of the mentioned techniques in managing atrial fibrillation.

Impact on clinical practice

The results demonstrate a distinct trade-off of procedural efficacy and complexity, which directly affects the clinical decision-making of patients with paroxysmal and persistent atrial fibrillation. In patients for whom the highest effect is of prime importance, a bilateral surgical ablation is potentially a more effective approach, with a higher rate of arrhythmia freedom (83% vs. 75%), and a better safety profile with a lower overall complications rate (10% vs. 13%) and bleeding (2 vs. 4%). On the other hand, hybrid ablation is also an effective therapeutic alternative, especially in complicated cases, such as in patients with persistent AF over a long duration or after the catheter ablations have failed, as suggested by current guidelines [48, 49]. The decision of the hybrid approach is also subtle; a single-setting procedure might be more effective and less prone to morbidity despite an extended length of operation than the double-setting approach. Finally, these findings support the ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guideline recommendation [51] of 2023 that the choice of treatment is to be individualized by a multidisciplinary heart team with careful consideration of patient and institution-specific factors versus institutional knowledge and the unique risk-benefit profile of each method.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis indicates a higher probability of maintenance of sinus rhythm with a superior profile of safety by bilateral surgical ablation compared to hybrid ablation. This conclusion, however, must be balanced against the inherent limitations of the study, such as the absence of formal statistical reporting on heterogeneity and the clinical variability due to pooling of different patients and short to medium term follow-up. The limitations underscore a need to conduct large, multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized endpoints and long-term follow-up to conclusively compare modern surgical and hybrid methods in the future. Future studies should also consider AF burden as the key endpoint to a more specific efficacy evaluation, integrate patient-reported outcomes, make comparisons with new technologies such as pulsed field ablation, and conduct sound cost-effectiveness analyses. This type of evidence of high quality is essential to maximize patient selection, improve the techniques of the procedures, and to establish the long-term role of each approach in the treatment of atrial fibrillation.
