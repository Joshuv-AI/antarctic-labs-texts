Subclinical Optic Nerve Involvement in Radiologically Isolated Syndrome: Multimodal Detection and Diagnostic Impact
Authors: Christine Lebrun‐Frenay, Cassandre Landes‐Château, Lydiane Mondot, Mikael Cohen, Margherita Popolo, Jérôme de Seze, Bertrand Bourre, Françoise Durand‐Dubief, Olivier Casez, Orhun H Kantarci, Aksel Siva, Stephanie Baillif, Darin T Okuda, Letizia Leocani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesWe aimed to determine the frequency of subclinical optic nerve (ON) lesions using MRI, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and visual evoked potentials (VEP) in radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS), and to assess their diagnostic and prognostic significance.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, multicenter study of 179 RIS individuals followed in clinical practice who met the 2023 RISC criteria. The diagnostic performance of the 2024 McDonald criteria, with and without optic nerve assessment, was evaluated and compared with that of the 2017 criteria. Associations with clinical conversion, comorbidities, and MRI disease activity were analyzed using multivariate models.ResultsSilent ON lesions appeared in 107/179 (59.8%) individuals, mainly detected by VEP (100/164; 61.0%) and OCT (69/118; 58.5%), with routine MRI identifying 31/166 (18.7%). During follow‐up, 71 (39.7%) had a first clinical event. The presence, laterality, or number of silent ON lesions did not correlate with clinical conversion, event type, or MRI activity. Younger age and the absence of comorbidities associated with other MRI lesions, rather than with ON lesions, were associated with clinical conversion. Including ON in the 2024 McDonald criteria increased sensitivity but decreased specificity, with 17/179 (9.5%) meeting dissemination‐in‐space criteria solely due to ON lesions.InterpretationSubclinical ON lesions are common in RIS and are mainly found by OCT and VEP, not routine MRI. Including ON increases sensitivity but does not predict clinical conversion and may lower specificity. These findings suggest cautious interpretation of ON and support a multimodal assessment approach in RIS.Keywords: diagnostic criteria, multiple sclerosis, optic nerve

Introduction

The 2024 updates to the McDonald criteria for diagnosing multiple sclerosis (MS) now include the optic nerve (ON) in the definition of dissemination in space (DIS) [1, 2, 3]. Dissemination in time (DIT) can be identified clinically or substituted with either positive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showing two unique oligoclonal bands (OCBs), an increased kappa free light chains index (KFLi), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings such as new T2 lesions, enlarged lesions, gadolinium‐enhanced lesions, or a specific central vein sign (CVS) [4]. In certain cases, individuals presenting with MRI lesions consistent with MS but lacking prior symptoms or having an atypical presentation, classified as radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS) [5, 6], may now be diagnosed with MS [4]. The new DIS definition, which now includes potential lesions on the optic nerve demonstrated via dedicated MRI, visual evoked potentials (VEP), or optical coherence tomography (OCT), must be accompanied by at least one additional feature resembling DIT on MRI, such as CSF or CVS, in the absence of a more likely explanation for the findings or other comorbidities [7, 8, 9, 10].

We hypothesized that silent ON lesions would improve diagnostic sensitivity but might not independently predict clinical conversion.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective study was conducted at the NeuroSciences Clinical Research Unit in Nice, France. The diagnosis of RIS and the presence of an ON silent lesion on MRI were confirmed following a double‐masked adjudication (CLF, LM). VEPs were prospectively included as part of the routine assessment for RIS diagnosis within 1 month after the initial RIS MRI scan at five MS tertiary centers. VEP curves were analyzed in standard care (MP), anonymized, and reanalyzed for adjudication (LL). Low‐contrast visual acuity (MC) and OCT (SB) were included when available within 30 days of the index MRI scan.

Study Population

Inclusion criteria included individuals with RIS who met the 2023 RIS criteria [6] after review by RISC, had their first abnormal MRI after 2009, and had at least one visit to the MS center between 2009 and 2025. The year 2009 was chosen as the cutoff when the RIS criteria were first established [5]. Comorbidities, such as migraine or vascular disease, that could explain MRI abnormalities or mimic demyelinating disease were recorded to apply the 2024 MS criteria1. Exclusion criteria included previous ophthalmological diseases. Clinical events were classified by the main neurological syndrome at onset. “Long tract sensory” and “long tract motor” syndromes indicate involvement of sensory pathways or corticospinal tracts, such as hemisensory deficits, sensory level, limb weakness, or pyramidal signs.

Study Outcomes

As a primary outcome, we aimed to investigate the prevalence of silent ON lesions in a cohort of RIS.

For secondary outcomes, we planned to (i) evaluate the prognostic value of ON lesions and disease activity on MRI or with a first clinical event, (ii) assess the benefit of specific ophthalmological tests (VEP, OCT, MRI) alone or together for documenting ON location to improve diagnosis specificity, PPV, and NPV for predicting disease activity evidence on MRI or clinical events, and (iii) analyze LC‐LA changes related to silent ON lesions detected via VEP, OCT, or MRI.

Optic Nerve Explorations

A comprehensive 8‐point eye exam was conducted by a trained ophthalmologist as part of routine clinical care. It included testing visual acuity, pupillary responses, ocular motility and alignment, intraocular pressure, finger confrontation testing, perimetry, external examination, slit‐lamp exam, and fundus evaluation. Visual acuity was measured using a Snellen chart, and low‐contrast visual acuity was assessed with Sloan low‐contrast letter charts (1.25% contrast; Precision Vision, Woodstock, IL, USA). Testing was performed monocularly at 2 m following standard Sloan procedures. Participants used their habitual optical correction when available. Charts were illuminated under standardized clinical lighting with chart‐mounted illumination to maintain consistent luminance across sessions. Each eye was tested separately, and the number of correctly identified letters was recorded. Scores were reported as the number of letters correctly identified per eye. An abnormal LC‐LA result was defined as performance below the normative range for the 1.25% contrast chart and/or a clinically significant inter‐eye difference exceeding thresholds used in previous multiple sclerosis studies. Since LC‐LA testing was part of routine clinical care, protocols varied between centers, and testing was not available for all participants.

MRI examinations were performed as part of routine clinical care across participating centers using 3 T systems. Brain MRI protocols included a fat‐suppressed three‐dimensional fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FS 3D FLAIR), with millimetric slice thickness, covering both the cerebral parenchyma and the orbits according to the OFSEP protocol [11]. Examinations with severe artifacts, particularly motion‐related or magnetic susceptibility artifacts, that precluded reliable optic nerve assessment were excluded from the analysis. MRI image analysis was performed in a blinded manner, with no access to patients' ophthalmological data, including VEP and OCT results. Images were independently reviewed by two experienced readers (LM, CLF). In the event of discrepant interpretations, a consensus reading session was held to reach a final shared decision.

The pattern reversal VEP was administered by a skilled neurophysiologist (MP) in accordance with international recommendations [12]. VEP examinations were performed in different participating centers. Subsequently, only explorations using the same equipment (Biqciplus Micromed system) and protocol were included. VEPs were elicited by a 2 Hz reversing check pattern. According to ISCEV guidelines, we used pattern‐reversal VEPs elicited by checkerboard stimuli with a check size of 30 and 15′. We performed two averages, each with 100 sweeps. Between 3 and 5 recordings were taken per eye, and the weighted average was used to measure P100 latency for each eye. An ON lesion was characterized by a prolonged delay (> 120 ms) in the P100 wave and/or a significant inter‐eye difference (> 7 ms) in P100 wave latency and/or the absence of the P100 wave. For VEP, bilateral involvement corresponded to abnormal responses in both eyes. To optimize consistency, all recordings were re‐analyzed centrally by a blinded expert neurophysiologist (LL).

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) examinations were performed using spectral‐domain OCT devices (Heidelberg Spectralis, Germany, SB model), without pupil dilation, for both eyes of each patient. Peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) thickness was measured with a 3.4‐mm circular scan centered on the optic disc, using automated real‐time tracking (ART 100). Macular scans used a high‐resolution raster protocol with 61 B‐scans covering a 30° × 25° area centered on the fovea (768 A‐scans per B‐scan, ART 13). All B‐scans were automatically segmented with Spectralis software and manually reviewed for accuracy. Scan quality was assessed with OSCAR‐IB criteria [13]; poor‐quality exams were excluded. Optic nerve abnormalities were identified using two methods: First, unilateral abnormalities based on inter‐eye differences (≥ 6 μm for pRNFL and ≥ 4 μm for GCIPL) following established criteria; second, bilateral involvement by retinal layer thinning in both eyes relative to the normative database, even without asymmetry. This approach detects both unilateral and bilateral optic nerve abnormalities.

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis of the study population in the RIS cohort was conducted with statistical analyses using EasyMedStat (version 3.22). Continuous variables were summarized as mean and SD; categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Normality and heteroscedasticity were assessed with Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests. Continuous baseline variables were compared across groups using ANOVA, Welch's ANOVA, or Kruskal‐Wallis test, while binary variables were compared using Chi‐Squared tests. Multivariate Cox and logistic regression analyses evaluated factors associated with time to and risk of developing clinical MS. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Belsley–Kuh–Welsch technique, and proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. The Kaplan–Meier method estimated survival probabilities from index MRI to clinical event, with confidence intervals. Survival curves were compared using the log‐rank test. Agreement between optic nerve assessment modalities (OCT, VEP, MRI) was evaluated via Cohen's kappa. The diagnostic performance of DIS (2017 or 2024) was analyzed using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and predictive values, based on outcomes such as RISC 2023 criteria, clinical events, or evidence of disease activity.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

All participants gave written consent before joining the RISC cohort (NCT05388331). Ethics and data access approvals were secured in accordance with French law. The study was registered as (CIL 2022‐BS‐002).

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

The study included 179 individuals with RIS, of whom 139 (77.7%) were women (Table 1), from 5 MS centers (Nice: 164‐CLF/MC; Strasbourg: 4‐JdS; Rouen: 4‐BB; Lyon: 4‐FDD; Grenoble: 3‐OC). Of these, 166 (92.7%) had routine MRI sequences suitable for optic nerve evaluation at referral; 13 (7.3%) had MRIs outside centers or non‐reliable OFSEP protocols and were excluded from optic nerve MRI analysis. One hundred and sixty‐four (91.6%) underwent VEP, 92 (51.4%) LC‐LA, and 118 (65.9%) OCT, with quality control (Figure 1), 15 (8.4%) for VEP, 87 (48.6%) for LC‐LA, and 61 (34.1%) for OCT were excluded due to technical issues or incomplete visits. The median interval between RIS diagnosis and optic nerve assessment was 1.68 months ±3.39 days, with a mean follow‐up of 45.47 months (+/−59.26).

None of the RIS individuals reported a history of orbital pain, any specific ophthalmological symptoms other than migrainous aura, or Uhthoff's phenomenon. The clinical examination did not show a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD); fundoscopy was normal in each case, with no signs of optic disc swelling or pallor.

Motives for MRI scans included headache (29.2%), neurological follow‐up (20.8%), ENT exams (16.9%), trauma (10.1%), psychiatry (7.9%), endocrinopathy (6.7%), ophthalmology (5.1%), and clinical studies (3.3%). Headache was not linked to silent ON lesions (odds ratio (OR) = 1.64; 95% confidence interval (CI) [0.81, 3.35]; p = 0.237) or ophthalmological reasons (OR = 1.37; CI [0.33, 5.65]; p = 0.742).

Seventy‐one individuals (39.66%) experienced a clinical event during follow‐up: 35 myelitis (49.3%), 10 optic neuritis (14.1%), 8 long tracts sensory (11.3%), 7 infra‐tentorial (9.9%), 1 long tract motor (1.4%), and 10 progressive (14.1%). A silent ON lesion during the preclinical phase did not affect the occurrence of optic neuritis as the first clinical event (p = 0.167).

Prevalence of Silent Optic Nerve Lesions

Overall, 107/179 (59.8%) individuals exhibited at least one optic nerve abnormality. Among those with available data, abnormalities were detected by VEP in 100/164 (61.0%), by OCT in 69/118 (58.5%), and by MRI in 31/166 (18.7%) (Figure 2A). Of 92/179 (51.4%) tested for LC‐LA, 10 (10.9%) had abnormal low‐contrast vision, with one silent lesion on paraclinical tests. 40/50 (80.0%) RIS with at least one abnormal test showing a silent optic nerve lesion had normal LC‐LA. Eighty‐two (76.6%) RIS had abnormal unilateral silent ON lesions, and in 25 with bilateral anomalies, 5 had silent bilateral ON on MRI (Figure 2B).

(A) Fat‐suppressed (FS) 3D‐FLAIR MRI showing a silent lesion on the right optic nerve in axial, sagittal, and coronal axes. (B) 3D FS FLAIR MRI demonstrating signal abnormalities of both optic nerves.

All patients with an ON lesion on MRI had abnormal OCT (21/21) (Table 2). Nearly half of patients without MRI lesions (46/94) still had abnormal OCT. OCT and VEP findings almost perfectly matched: All with normal VEPs had normal OCT (42/42), and no patients with normal OCT had abnormal VEPs. Discordant cases (2/65) had abnormal OCT with normal VEP, possibly indicating structural damage without current functional impairment or residual delays. Most with MRI‐detected lesions had abnormal VEPs (26/27), but over half without MRI lesions also had abnormal VEPs (65/124). Among 179 RIS individuals, 107 (59.8%) had at least one silent lesion on at least one test, aligning with 2024 DIS criteria. When at least two of three tests were available, 99 (60.0%) showed silent lesions, and with all three available, 58/95 (61.1%) showed at least one ON anomaly. Agreement between OCT and VEP was nearly perfect (κ = 0.96), whereas agreement between routine MRI and OCT (κ = 0.28) or VEP (κ = 0.23) was low.

Performance of a Silent Lesion on the Optic Nerves for Predicting a First Clinical Event

Seventy‐one (39.7%) RIS individuals had a first clinical event during follow‐up, including 10 (14.08%) with optic neuritis. Survival curves did not differ between those without and with ON lesions (p = 0.167). The event type (RR or PP) was unaffected by ON lesions or by the number of ON abnormal explorations (p > 0.999, p = 0.162). The 5‐year risk was 26% (95% CI: 16–41) with an ON lesion detected preclinically, vs. 38% (95% CI: 28–50) without such a lesion (p = 0.208). At 10 years, risks were 67% (95% CI: 52–80) with preclinical ON lesion, and 47% (95% CI: 31–68) without (p = 0.136) (Figure 3).

Prognostic value of dedicated optic nerve exploration positivity on the occurrence of a clinical event. There was no difference in survival between no ON anomaly and ON anomaly (p = 0.167). At 2 years, 1st clinical event date‐free survival was 83% (No) (95% CI: 71–91) and 82% (Yes) (95% CI: 73–89). At 5 years, it was 74% (No) (95% CI: 59–84) and 62% (Yes) (95% CI: 50–72). At 10 years, survival was 53% (No) (95% CI: 32–69) and 33% (Yes) (95% CI: 20–48). No survival difference at this time point (p = 0.136).

The increase in DIS locations with ON silent lesions from 1 to 2, 2 to 3, or 3 to 4 did not significantly raise the risk of a clinical event (p = 0.673), with rates of 39.29%, 40.3%, and 47.62%, nor was it linked to disease activity (p = 0.961).

Most individuals (113/179; 63.1%) showed DIT on MRI (new T2 or gadolinium‐enhanced lesion) at some point. The DIT rates were significantly influenced by the MS 2024 diagnosis, with 76.5% having comorbidities and 35.85% without (OR: 5.99; CI [2.98; 12.03]; p < 0.001). The presence of an active MRI was not affected by the number of DIS locations, including silent ON lesions (p = 0.816), nor by whether ON lesions were present (OR = 1.07; CI [0.39; 2.96]; p > 0.999).

All RIS individuals met the 2023 RISC criteria. Of these, 148 (82.7%) met 2024 MS criteria without comorbidities, and 126 (70.4%) with comorbidities, mainly headache disorders. Seventeen of 179 (9.5%) RIS individuals with one DIS location were reclassified as 2024 MS with an ON silent lesion in a second region.

In multivariate analysis, a silent optic nerve lesion was not independently linked to the first clinical event (OR = 1.48, p = 0.272) or time to clinical conversion (OR = 1.35, p = 0.297) (Table 3). Older age at RIS diagnosis was associated with a lower risk of a clinical event (OR = 0.96 per year, p = 0.005) but did not affect time to event. Sex had no impact. Neither the RISC 2023 classification nor the fulfillment of DIS 2017 criteria independently predicted clinical conversion. Meeting the 2024 McDonald MS criteria was strongly associated with a clinical event (OR = 8.57, p = 0.002), with a trend toward shorter time to conversion; however, after accounting for comorbidities, it was associated with decreased risk (OR = 0.30, p = 0.019), highlighting the need to consider comorbidities in risk assessment.

Performance of Diagnostic Criteria According to the Optic Nerve Anatomical Location

The 2024 DIS criteria, including optic nerve region, showed higher sensitivity than the 2017 DIS criteria across outcomes (Table S1). Sensitivity increased from 92.7% to 97.1% with RIS 2023 criteria when including optic nerve assessment, but specificity dropped from 52.5% to 25.0%. For the 2024 MS criteria, sensitivity increased to 100% with comorbidities, while specificity remained unchanged at 45.2% without comorbidities and decreased to 26.4% with comorbidities. MRI assessments had higher specificity than OCT and VEP, but OCT and VEP had higher sensitivity at the expense of specificity. Overall diagnostic accuracy stayed similar; for RIS 2023, MRI‐based assessment had 45.0% specificity and 30.0% sensitivity. Including optic nerve assessment improved the negative predictive value, reaching 100% when comorbidities were considered, from 58.1% with DIS 2017. Sensitivity for first clinical event prediction rose from 87.3% to 97.2%, but specificity fell from 20.4% to 11.1%. Diagnostic accuracy was similar (around 43%–47%), with optic nerve lesion increasing sensitivity but lowering specificity compared with DIS 2017.

Discussion

This study examines silent ON lesions in RIS and their potential as a risk factor for future events. Most RIS individuals have silent ON involvement detectable on routine MRI, OCT, or VEPs. The 2024 revision of the McDonald criteria added the optic nerve as a fifth DIS location and aimed to standardize ON assessment [1, 4, 10]. This change boosts sensitivity but lowers specificity [14]. In our cohort, 9.5% of RIS individuals with a single DIS location were reclassified as meeting the 2024 criteria due to silent ON lesions. These findings show the diagnostic importance of ON in early disease and its relevance in early demyelination. In our study, silent ON lesions did not increase the risk of subsequent clinical optic neuritis, indicating that subclinical ON lesions may be a parallel pathological process rather than a direct precursor to symptomatic attacks. We found that silent ON involvement aids in diagnosis but does not predict future clinical outcomes in RIS. The lack of prognostic value reflects the disease's early stage, with a possible ceiling effect of sensitive biomarkers and a mismatch between dissemination and activity.

Optic nerve and anterior visual pathway involvement are well established in MS, with optic neuritis as the presenting symptom in about 20% of patients and occurring in up to 70% over the course of the disease [15, 16]. While orbital MRI sequences detect acute optic neuritis in 80%–90% of cases, they are not routinely used everywhere, and MRI shows silent ON lesions in 5%–25% of cases, due to population and protocol differences [17, 18, 19, 20]. Autopsy studies suggest 80% of MS patients have silent ON lesions, indicating common subclinical demyelination [21]. Interestingly, many patients show no clinical ON signs, such as pupillary defects or optic disc pallor, despite abnormalities on OCT and VEP, likely due to early, subclinical disease in RIS. Many abnormalities were unilateral or mild and missed on routine exams. Also, ophthalmologic assessments in routine settings without specialized review or fundus photography may miss subtle signs. ON lesions can be detected even when visual tests like LC‐LA are normal, but abnormal LC‐LA rarely occurs without ON. LC‐LA may remain normal despite early structural or electrophysiological abnormalities, particularly in the preclinical phases of demyelination. These points highlight OCT and VEP's greater sensitivity than basic exams in detecting subclinical optic nerve lesions.

VEPs provide insights into demyelination and remyelination along the afferent visual pathway. VEP latency in healthy patients is unaffected by age, gender, weight, or visual acuity [22]. Previously, VEP's usefulness in detecting silent ON lesions was uncertain [23, 24], but growing evidence has shown strong links between VEP latency delays, retinal thinning on OCT, and long‐term disability [25]. In our RIS cohort, most individuals showed prolonged VEP latencies, highlighting the importance of ON in earlier disease stages. Importantly, latency, rather than amplitude, appears to be the most reliable marker of demyelination and a predictor of long‐term outcomes [26]. We also observed no correlation between abnormal VEPs and low LC‐LA, likely reflecting the complex interaction between demyelination and axonal degeneration: Latency prolongation may recover with remyelination, while axonal loss develops more slowly and might not be immediately evident in functional assessments. We have to consider that our analysis included only subclinical ON involvement, which may limit the significance of this correlation, owing to a floor effect on LC‐LA abnormalities and/or to recovery of VEP latency after subclinical ON episodes with undetermined timing. Experimental and clinical research supports the idea that significant axonal damage can occur during early, even subclinical, inflammatory events, leading to delayed clinical effects. It also indicates that many ganglion cell axons can sustain critical damage during the early stages of subclinical acute inflammation in the visual pathway [27, 28]. Even earlier in the disease course, during the RIS phase, latency changes occur without noticeable clinical visual dysfunction, and multifocal VEPs show reduced interocular and monocular amplitudes and increased interocular latency, which are associated with a higher risk of clinical conversion [29]. In a previous pilot study, we demonstrated that abnormal VEPs alone did not predict the first clinical event but were associated with a high T2 lesion load [30]. Other studies reported that 14.3% of the RIS cohort showed increased VEP latencies, which was lower than in a CIS cohort at the same center (43.8%) [31]. In symptomatic patients, the inclusion of ON explored with MRI or VEP in the 2024 diagnostic criteria resulted in slightly higher sensitivity but lower specificity, raising questions about the positive effect of including ON in the revised criteria. In our study, lower specificity of OCT/VEP may actually reflect greater sensitivity to remote or subclinical injury [32, 33, 34, 35]. Consistent with prior studies, 39% of individuals in this RIS cohort experienced a clinical event, either acute or progressive, during follow‐up [36, 37, 38, 39]. Although VEP abnormalities are common in primary progressive MS, we could not show predictive value for progression to PPMS in RIS, likely due to the early stage of disease and lack of VEP data at PPMS diagnosis. The expected sensitivity of VEPs for predicting progression could not be confirmed [40]. Including the optic nerve as a DIS location may improve risk stratification and emphasize the need for diagnostic caution, especially in patients without spinal cord lesions. Demonstrating ON lesions as a fifth DIS topography using multimodal tools may reduce concerns about misdiagnosis and, in some cases, eliminate the need for DIT surrogates when lumbar puncture or advanced MRI markers are unavailable [41, 42, 43]. Recent studies, including the combination of OCT and MRI, demonstrate the utility of detecting optic nerve abnormalities [44]. The strong concordance between OCT and VEP highlights their complementary nature: VEP reflects early demyelination and conduction delay, while OCT measures neuroaxonal loss that develops over weeks to months after inflammation. This temporal dissociation may explain the relatively high VEP sensitivity in early RIS, enabling detection of functional impairments before structural thinning appears on OCT. OCT may be a more robust marker of chronic damage once neuroaxonal loss occurs. However, in our study, the VEP‐OCT concordance was almost perfect. This may be explained by the inclusion of eyes with subclinical involvement by definition. It is unlikely that VEPs and OCT were performed close in time to any subclinical ON event, allowing the time for OCT detection of neuroaxonal thinning. Taken together, these findings support the use of MRI, OCT, and VEP together to evaluate subclinical optic nerve involvement and assess its diagnostic impact within the 2024 McDonald criteria.

In this context, careful assessment of comorbidities is essential to reduce the risk of misdiagnosis. The apparent reversal of risk observed in our study reflects how adjustment for comorbidities influences diagnostic accuracy. Without accounting for comorbidities, the 2024 McDonald criteria may classify individuals based on MRI or optic nerve abnormalities unrelated to demyelinating disease, thereby inflating associations with clinical events. Incorporating comorbidities yields a more specific and biologically coherent population, even if it results in lower short‐term risk estimates. This pattern is consistent with the correction of misclassification bias rather than a true reduction in risk. A key finding of our study is this reversal of risk after adjustment. Optic nerve abnormalities initially associated with a higher risk were attenuated or reversed after accounting for comorbid conditions, suggesting that non‐demyelinating processes may contribute to these findings. Highly sensitive modalities such as OCT and VEP may detect nonspecific abnormalities, particularly in a heterogeneous RIS population. These observations underscore the importance of careful phenotyping, including a systematic evaluation of comorbidities and alternative causes of optic nerve dysfunction, to avoid overestimating disease burden and risk.

Our study has limitations, including its retrospective design and lack of comparison with the 2024 MS criteria at the time of RIS diagnosis. Subtle findings such as a relative afferent pupillary defect, mild disc pallor, or the use of a 1.25% chart may have been underdiagnosed without centralized neuro‐ophthalmologic evaluation. VEPs were collected from multiple centers with standardization following guidelines, but variability required centralized adjudication. Different equipment and normative databases across centers are limitations, and differences in stimulation, recording, and references may have affected classification. Recent VEP guidelines emphasize the need for standardization, which was not fully achieved. For this reason, in our study, we included only VEPs obtained with the same equipment and protocol. The higher frequency of abnormal VEPs in our cohort compared with prior studies might be due to the systematic use of standardized VEPs and centralized reading, greater sensitivity to demyelination, and the detection of issues not seen on routine MRI, especially without dedicated orbital sequences. These results underscore VEP's sensitivity and the importance of standardization in multicenter studies. OCT's bilateral involvement assessment is not fully standardized [45]; low‐contrast acuity testing was exploratory and possibly underpowered, showing fewer abnormalities than larger studies. Our focus on RIS patients at an early disease stage and the use of routine clinical protocols may explain the low detection rate of functional abnormalities despite OCT and VEP findings, illustrating that these modalities provide complementary insights but do not determine the timing of pathology. OCT, VEP, and MRI complement each other in assessing optic nerve pathology, capturing structural, functional, and radiological aspects. However, MRI protocols in routine practice lack the dedicated orbital sequences recommended by collaborative research organizations, such as the Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Multiple Sclerosis (MAGNIMS), the Consortium of Multiple Sclerosis Centers (CMSC), and the North American Imaging in Multiple Sclerosis (NAIMS), potentially leading to underestimation of silent ON lesions. Advanced MRI techniques such as 3D DIR [46], STIR, or MP2RAGE [47] offer higher sensitivity but are not widely available and were not used in this multicenter cohort. The study used routine 3D FS FLAIR, which is comparable to coronal T2 orbital MRI for ON diagnosis, but variations across MRI systems may affect lesion detection [19, 48]. The low concordance between MRI and other modalities likely reflects MRI's limited sensitivity rather than true disagreement. The heterogeneity of scans reflects real‐world practice, highlighting the need for dedicated ON sequences rather than direct comparison with OCT and VEP. Discrepancies in prevalence may lead to underestimation of concordance using κ statistics.

This study emphasizes the role of the optic nerve in preclinical demyelinating disease. The inclusion of the ON in DIS criteria enhances diagnostic sensitivity and supports modern MS assessment [49]. Incorporating optic nerve involvement into the 2024 DIS criteria increased sensitivity but decreased specificity, keeping overall accuracy similar across models. The 2024 DIS criteria, compared with 2017, showed 100% sensitivity and NPV when including additional comorbidity features, which is clinically valuable. Adding ON as a fifth topography improved DIS's ability to detect disease activity and clinical events. These results support including the optic nerve to boost early case detection, despite some loss of specificity. The ON assessment method with MRI, VEP, OCT, or a combination should be chosen based on local expertise and practicality. This study mirrors real‐world practice, using routine MRI protocols and multimodal optic nerve assessment outside standardized research settings, which may introduce variability but also increases generalizability. OCT and VEP provide complementary structural and functional information, whereas routine MRI may underestimate optic nerve involvement without dedicated orbital sequences. These points are crucial for diagnosing optic nerve issues in RIS and applying the 2024 McDonald criteria. Future optic nerve biomarkers could help track subclinical neurodegeneration and treatment responses, even in stable patients, aiding future diagnostic and treatment plans.
