Plant-microbiome interactions suppress Fusarium wilt by enriching beneficial Aspergillus in the tobacco rhizosphere
Authors: Shuang Han, Yang Yang, Senqi Xiong, Hao Zheng, Min Yan, Yide Yang, Sai Guo, Hongjun Liu, Jiangang Li, Jie Yin, Rong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) is a globally significant economic crop with a production that is severely threatened by soil-borne fungal pathogens, particularly Fusarium spp. Native rhizosphere microbial communities, especially those of root-associated fungi, have been suggested as sustainable solutions for disease suppression. However, the compositional differences in root-associated fungal communities between healthy and diseased tobacco plants remain poorly characterized.ResultsIn this study, we investigated the root-associated fungal communities in healthy versus Fusarium-infected tobacco plants across three geographically distinct fields in Sichuan Province, China, to identify key microbial taxa involved in disease suppression. High-throughput sequencing revealed distinct fungal community compositions among the sites. The healthy rhizospheres were enriched with beneficial taxa, among which Aspergillus OTU2223 was the most dominant and potentially crucial for disease suppression. In contrast, diseased soils were dominated by pathogenic Fusarium taxa, with Fusarium solani being the most prevalent pathogen. Network analysis revealed that, compared with diseased soils, healthy rhizospheres maintained a greater number of nodes (306 vs. 282) and higher modularity (0.46 vs. 0.31) microbial interactions. We isolated and characterized Aspergillus strain A74, which demonstrated strong antagonistic activity against F. solani f. sp. nicotianae both in vitro and in greenhouse trials, thereby reducing the incidence of Fusarium wilt disease in greenhouse trials. The application of strain A74 maintained the growth parameters (including height, stem diameter, and chlorophyll content) of pathogen-inoculated plants at levels comparable to healthy plants.ConclusionIn summary, our study demonstrated that healthy tobacco plants assemble distinct root-associated fungal communities characterized by beneficial Aspergillus taxa that exhibit exceptional potential as biocontrol agents. These findings provide a theoretical foundation and practical support for the development of Aspergillus-based green biocontrol strategies, highlighting their potential as an environmentally friendly and sustainable approach to disease management in tobacco production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00836-w.

Introduction

Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), which is a globally significant economic crop, plays a crucial role in providing raw materials for various sectors, including the pharmaceutical, chemical, and agricultural industries [1]. Despite its well-established economic significance, tobacco cultivation is increasingly experiencing agronomic challenges posed by soil-borne diseases, particularly those caused by Fusarium spp. [2]. These pathogens are responsible for causing Fusarium wilt, which can lead to yield reductions of 30–70% in affected fields and significantly compromise leaf quality, thereby impacting the overall productivity and profitability of tobacco farming [3]. Current management practices for soil-borne diseases in tobacco cultivation heavily rely on chemical interventions, with global tobacco farming consuming approximately 15% of all agricultural fungicides on an annual basis [4]. However, the long-term use of chemical fungicides is experiencing challenges, including environmental pollution and the development of pathogen resistance, which necessitate the exploration of sustainable and environmentally friendly alternatives [2].

Recent advances in microbiome research revealed that the rhizosphere is a critical environment for plant-pathogen interactions [5]. Unlike endophytic communities that reside within root tissues, rhizosphere fungi occupy the soil–plant interface, where they engage in complex chemical dialogues with both the host and the pathogen [6]. Tobacco plants are associated with distinct rhizosphere assemblages shaped by root exudates, resulting in microbiome signatures that markedly differ between healthy and diseased organisms [7]. While these differences suggest potential biomarkers for disease resistance and opportunities for microbiome-mediated disease control, the underlying community assembly mechanisms-whether driven by active plant recruitment of beneficial taxa, pathogen-mediated disruption, or stochastic processes-remain unresolved and require further mechanistic investigation. Despite these insights, there are critical knowledge gaps regarding the rhizosphere fungal ecology of tobacco. Most studies focused on bacterial components [8], whereas the structurally complex fungal microbiome, which typically contributes more than 40% of the rhizosphere microbial biomass [9], remains poorly characterized. Furthermore, the functional mechanisms linking rhizosphere fungal community dynamics to Fusarium suppression in tobacco require systematic investigations. A deeper understanding of these fungal networks could unlock novel biocontrol approaches, thereby reducing reliance on chemical fungicides while enhancing crop resilience in sustainable tobacco production systems.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted an integrated study combining microbiome analysis with functional characterization. Using high-throughput ITS1 sequencing and co-occurrence network analysis, we systematically characterized the rhizosphere fungal microbiota associated with healthy versus diseased tobacco across three major tobacco-producing regions in Sichuan Province, China. We subsequently isolated and characterized antagonistic fungal strains from the rhizosphere of healthy plants. Finally, via comprehensive greenhouse experiments, we evaluated the biocontrol potential of selected fungal isolates against tobacco root rot. We hypothesized that (1) healthy tobacco plants assemble distinct rhizosphere fungal communities compared with diseased plants, with enrichment of specific beneficial taxa being observed, and (2) these rhizosphere fungi play crucial roles in maintaining plant health via direct pathogen suppression and growth promotion. This study aimed to advance our understanding of plant–microbe interactions in agricultural systems and provide a practical framework for developing microbiome-based solutions for the treatment of soil-borne diseases, thereby increasing the sustainability of tobacco cultivation.

Materials and methods

Field site and sample collection

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from healthy versus diseased tobacco plants across three geographically distinct tobacco-growing regions in Sichuan Province, China: (1) Haoba (HB) village (104.53764°E, 27.93282°N), (2) Longpan (LP) village (104.57704°E, 27.90469°N) in Junlian County, and (3) Baiyu (BY) village (104.859573°E, 27.939665°N) in Gong County. These sites represent the major flue-cured tobacco production areas in southern Sichuan. During the peak disease incidence period, we implemented a stratified random sampling approach based on disease prevalence maps provided by local tobacco stations. At each location, six healthy plants (asymptomatic) and six diseased plants (exhibiting characteristic vascular browning and wilting symptoms) were selected from separate cultivation plots.

The soil sample collection method followed our previous studies [2]. Briefly, bulk soil was defined as the soil loosely attached to plant roots, which was collected by manual shaking. For rhizosphere soil collection, roots were immersed in sterile water and shaken at 170 rpm for 30 min at 30 °C. The resulting soil suspension was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to obtain rhizosphere soil. Notably, root tissue was excluded from all soil samples. Bulk soil samples were used for analyzing soil physicochemical properties, while rhizosphere soil was stored at − 80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction. The basic physicochemical properties of the soil samples from the tobacco planting areas are detailed in Table S1.

Extraction of soil DNA and high-throughput sequencing

Total soil genomic DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of each soil sample (36 total samples: 2 treatments × 6 replicates × 3 positions) using the DNeasy® PowerSoil® Kit (QIAGEN GmbH, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Extraction negative controls, consisting of sterile water processed in parallel with experimental samples, showed no detectable DNA, confirming the absence of contamination. DNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with all samples DNA concentrations > 50 ng/μL, indicating successful extraction. The fungal ITS1 region was amplified using the primer pair ITS1F (5'-GGA AGT AAA AGT CGT AAC AAG G-3') and ITS1R (5'-GCT GCG TTC TTC ATC GAT GC-3') [10]. PCR amplification negative controls (sterile water) confirmed the absence of contamination during amplification. The soil DNA extracts were subjected to amplicon sequencing at Magigene Biotech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Fungal sequencing libraries were constructed following established protocols [11, 12]. Cluster generation was performed using the cBOT system, and paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Paired-end reads were merged using USEARCH (V10) with the “fastq_mergepairs” command, followed by the removal of low-quality sequences. The resulting raw tags underwent additional quality filtering and trimming using “Fastq” to generate high-quality clean tags. These quality-filtered sequences were then analysed through the UPARSE pipeline where sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity threshold [13, 14]. For taxonomic classification, representative sequences from each OTU were compared against the UNITE ITS v7.1 reference database [15]. To ensure dataset purity, we implemented an additional filtering step to identify and remove any remaining non-fungal sequences.

Isolation of Aspergillus strains and Fusarium inhibition assays

Fungal strains were isolated from the rhizosphere soil of healthy tobacco plants via Rose Bengal Agar (RBA), which is a selective medium that suppresses bacterial growth [16]. Briefly, 10 g of soil was suspended in 90 mL of sterile distilled water (10−1 dilution) followed by serial dilutions (10−2, 10−3, and 10−4). Aliquots (100 µL) of each dilution were spread onto RBA plates and incubated at 25 ℃ for up to seven days [17]. Emerging fungal colonies were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) and purified at 28 ℃. For molecular identification, the ITS region was amplified via the universal primers ITS1 (5’-TCC GTA GGT GAA CCT GCG G-3’) and ITS4 (5’-TCC TCC GCT TAT TGA TAT GC-3’) [18]. The amplified fragments were sequenced by TSINGKE Biological Technology Company (Beijing, China). The resulting sequences were compared with those in the NCBI GenBank database using BLAST. Phylogenetic analysis was performed based on ITS sequences using MEGA X software [19]. Evolutionary relationships were inferred using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method under the Tamura-Nei model, and branch support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

The antagonistic potential of the isolated fungal strains against F. solani f. sp. nicotianae was evaluated using a PDA plate assay. Briefly, four 5-mm agar plugs of each test fungus were equidistantly placed near the periphery of a PDA plate, with a plug of F. solani being positioned at the centre. For the control group, a single plug of F. solani was inoculated at the center of the PDA plate without test fungi to assess its inherent growth. All plates were incubated at 28 ℃ for seven days. After incubation, the colony diameter was measured in both treated and control plates. Each treatment was performed with three biological replicates to ensure reproducibility. The inhibitory effect was evaluated by calculating the inhibition rate using the following formula:

Inhibition rate (%) = [(colony size of the pathogen in the control group - colony size of the pathogen in the treatment group)/colony size of the pathogen in the control group] × 100%.

Greenhouse pot trials

The spore suspensions of both beneficial and pathogenic fungi were prepared using standardised methods. Fungal isolates were cultured on PDA plates at 28 ℃ for 7 days until abundant sporulation was observed. The spores were harvested by gently flooding the culture surface with 10 mL of sterile distilled water and scraping with a sterile cell scraper [20]. The resulting suspension was filtered through four layers of sterile cheesecloth to remove mycelial fragments. The spore concentration was determined using an improved Neubauer haemocytometer under a light microscope at 400 × magnification [21] and subsequently adjusted to 1 × 107 spores/mL with sterile distilled water.

Nicotiana benthamiana seeds were surface sterilized via sequential immersion in 70% ethanol (30 s) and 1% sodium hypochlorite (2 min) [22], followed by three rinses with sterile distilled water. Seeds were sown in 6-well seedling trays containing autoclaved substrate (70% peat moss, 20% vermiculite, 10% perlite; 2–3 seeds/well) and maintained in a growth chamber at 25 ℃ with 60% RH and a 16/8 h light/dark cycle [23]. At the 4–5-leaf stage (14 days post-sowing), the seedlings were thinned to one plant/well. Each treatment included three biological replicates, with each replicate consisting of six plants, to ensure statistical reliability. Seven days after thinning, the plants in the treatment groups received beneficial fungal suspensions adjusted to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 107 spores/g soil, whereas those in the control groups received sterile water. After 20 days of colonisation, the plants were challenged with an F. solani suspension adjusted to 1 × 105 spores/g soil [24]. The plants were subsequently allowed to further grow, and disease development was monitored. Disease progression was monitored using the following standardised assessment criteria [25]:

Disease incidence (%) = (number of diseased seedlings/total number of seedlings) × 100%.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

The abundance of F. solani in the soil was quantified using the extracted DNA template and a previously established real-time PCR protocol. Quantification was performed via the species-specific primers FF1 (5'-ACATACCACTTGTTGCCTCG-3') and FR1 (5'-CGCCAATCAATTTGAGGAACG-3') targeting the ITS region [26]. Amplification was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. Standard curves were generated using tenfold serial dilutions (101–108 copies/μL) of a pMD19-T Easy vector (Takara) containing the target ITS fragment. Each sample was analysed in triplicate with negative controls. The data were normalised to the dry weight of the soil and expressed as lg (gene copies per gram of soil) values [27].

Statistical analyses

All of the statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) unless otherwise specified. Bray–Curtis distance was performed to test the significant differences among different sites using the ‘vegan’ package [28]. The microbial alpha diversity indices Chao1 and Shannon were calculated using the ‘vegan’ package (version 3.5.0) [29] after rarefying all samples to 21,403 reads to normalize sequencing depth. Group differences in alpha diversity were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction for multiple testing, which was implemented in the ‘rstatix’ package [30]. To evaluate beta diversity, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis distance was performed using the ‘vegan’ package. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was conducted using the adonis2 function to test for significant differences in microbial community composition between the groups [31]. The relative abundances of the top 10 fungal Phylum/Genera were visualized using stacked bar plots generated with the ‘ggplot2’ package [32]. The discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method was performed via the ‘microeco’ package in R, which identifies statistically significant differences in microbial taxa [33]. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted using the cor.test function, and relationships were visualized using grouped scatter plots created with the ‘ggplot2’ package [34]. A genus-level co-occurrence network was constructed using Spearman correlations, and network analysis was performed via the ‘igraph’ package. A Fusarium-centred subnetwork was extracted and visualized, after which the results were exported in GEXF format using the ‘rgexf’ package for further network visualization and analysis by using Gephi software. The following network topological properties were systematically evaluated [35]: (1) global parameters (such as average degree, network diameter, and average path length) to characterize network complexity; (2) modularity analysis via the fast-greedy algorithm; and (3) node-level features (including a z-P plot classification of topological roles). Data from the pot experiments were analysed using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 9.5 to determine significant differences between the treatments. All of the statistical tests in this study were considered to be statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Fungal community diversity and composition

To assess the influence of geographical variation on fungal communities, we analysed samples from three sampling sites (HB, LP, and BY) based on Bray–Curtis distances. The results indicated significant differences in fungal community structure between all pairs of sites (Fig. 1b). The fungal community composition differed between the healthy and diseased treatments across all three sites (Fig. S1). Notably, the relative abundance of Aspergillus was significantly greater in healthy soil compared with diseased soil, whereas the relative abundance of Fusarium was markedly increased under diseased conditions. Shannon index differed among the treatments, with the diseased group exhibiting a significantly lower value compared with the healthy group in BH and LP, while it is opposite in BY (Fig. 1c–e). In contrast, no significant differences in the Chao1 index were detected between healthy and diseased groups across all sites (Fig. S2). PCoA analysis revealed clear separation between healthy and diseased samples across all three sites (Fig. 1f–h). Permutational multivariate analysis (Adonis) further confirmed significant differences in fungal community structure between the two conditions.

Indicative taxa of healthy and diseased treatments

To identify microbial taxa indicative of healthy versus diseased rhizosphere soils, we performed linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to distinguish OTUs with significant differences in abundance between the two conditions. Using LEfSe analysis with an LDA score threshold of > 4, we identified multiple OTUs exhibiting significant differences across all three sites (Fig. 2).

At the HB site, the healthy samples were significantly enriched in three genus-level OTUs: OTU2223 (g_Aspergillus), OTU2890 (g_Gliocephalotrichum), and OTU3918 (g_Aspergillus), two of which belonged to the genus Aspergillus (Fig. 2a). At the LP site, the healthy samples were characterized by the enrichment of OTU668 (g_Leucocoprinus), OTU2223 (g_Aspergillus), and OTU3973 (g_Trichoderma) (Fig. 2b). At the BY site, the healthy samples showed significant enrichment of OTU2223 (g_Aspergillus) and OTU3507 (g_Lepista) (Fig. 2c). In contrast, the diseased samples across all three sites were consistently enriched with the pathogenic OTU1 (g_Fusarium). Specifically, OTU2223 showed high relative abundance in Health treatment from all three locations, accounting for 2.17%, 8.25%, and 21.4% of the fungal communities in HB, LP, and BY, respectively. Conversely, the pathogenic OTU1 was significantly enriched in diseased samples (Fig. 2d and e).

Collectively, these findings indicated that Aspergillus was a common indicator taxon in the rhizosphere of healthy tobacco plants across different sites, while the pathogenic genus Fusarium was consistently enriched in diseased rhizosphere soils. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis further revealed a significant negative correlation between the relative abundance of OTU2223 and that of the pathogenic OTU1 (r = − 0.68) (Fig. 2f). These results suggested that Aspergillus, as a dominant taxon, may possess potential biocontrol functions, warranting further investigation.

Network analysis of fungal communities at the genus level

To elucidate microbial co-occurrence patterns in healthy versus diseased rhizosphere soils, we constructed association networks based on Spearman rank correlations (r ≥ 0.7, p < 0.05). The healthy rhizosphere network comprised 306 nodes and 3018 edges, with a network density of 0.0647 and an average degree of 19.73 being observed (Table S2). Conversely, the diseased rhizosphere network contained fewer nodes (282) but a significantly greater number of edges (6784), along with increased network density (0.17) and an average degree of 48.11 (Fig. 3a and b). This topological shift suggests that pathogen stress induces a densely interconnected microbial community. Both networks exhibited similar clustering coefficients (0.54 for the healthy network versus 0.59 for the diseased network), thereby implying modestly intensified microbial aggregation in diseased soil. However, fundamental structural differences emerged in their modular organization. Specifically, the healthy network demonstrated greater modular organization, with 11 distinct modules and a higher modularity index (0.46) being observed. Conversely, the diseased network exhibited structural simplification, with only 3 modules and reduced modularity (0.31) being observed (Fig. 3d), thus potentially reflecting functional convergence within the stressed microbiome. Furthermore, the healthy network contained more connectors (keystone taxa linking modules), which are critical for maintaining network connectivity and facilitating cross-modular microbial interactions.

We further analysed the co-occurrence patterns of Fusarium and its associated taxa, which revealed 65 significant associations (29 negative and 36 positive associations) with diverse fungal taxa (Fig. 3e). Notably, Fusarium exhibited particularly strong antagonistic relationships with known biocontrol genera (such as Aspergillus, Beauveria, and Talaromyces), thus suggesting active competition with fungicidal microbiota. Concurrently, Fusarium exhibited pronounced synergism with multiple phytopathogens (most notably Gibberella), thereby implying cooperative pathogenicity within the rhizosphere niche. These antagonistic and synergistic interactions collectively illustrate the complex microbial dynamics whereby the presence of Fusarium may simultaneously suppress beneficial microbiota while fostering pathogenic relationships.

Aspergillus isolates and their properties of Fusarium inhibition

A total of nine Aspergillus strains were isolated from healthy rhizosphere soil and identified based on ITS sequences (Fig. 4a). Plate confrontation assays revealed that all of the isolates exhibited antagonistic activity against the pathogen F. solani f. sp. nicotianae. The average inhibition rates were as follows: A20 (56.0%), A25 (57.4%), A28 (63.9%), A31 (63.7%), A44 (61.5%), A74 (69.1%), A90 (59.0%), A171 (64.2%), and A149 (67.2%). Among them, strain A74, A171 and A149 demonstrated stronger inhibitory effects compared to the others (Fig. 4b). Phylogenetic tree analysis demonstrated that strain A74 formed a distinct cluster with Aspergillus OTU2223, which exhibited the highest ITS sequence similarity to this health-indicative taxon that was previously identified via the LEfSe analysis. Based on these results, strains A74 (representing the OTU2223-associated lineage) and A171 (representing a phylogenetically distinct strain for comparison) were chosen for subsequent greenhouse experiments to evaluate their potential utilisation as antagonistic inoculants against Fusarium wilt.

Biocontrol efficacy of Aspergillus strains against Fusarium wilt

Greenhouse trials revealed significant differences in biocontrol performance between the two selected Aspergillus strains (A74 and A171). The experimental treatments induced significant phenotypic variations in the tobacco plants (Fig. 5a and b). Compared with noninoculated control (CK) plants, plants that were solely inoculated with F. solani f. sp. nicotianae exhibited markedly reduced chlorophyll content (P < 0.05). In contrast, plants treated with Aspergillus A74 maintained chlorophyll levels that were statistically equivalent to those of CK plants (Fig. 5a). Furthermore, tobacco plants treated with A74 exhibited significantly greater plant heights and stem diameters compared to pathogen-only controls (P < 0.05), whereas A171-treated plants exhibited no significant improvements in these growth parameters (Fig. 5a and b).

At two months post inoculation, strain A74 exhibited remarkable disease suppression (P < 0.05), decreasing the disease incidence from 41.67% (pathogen-only control) to 8.33%, which represented an 80% reduction in disease incidence (Fig. 5d). In comparison, strain A171 exhibited moderate but statistically nonsignificant (P > 0.05) biocontrol activity, thus resulting in a 40% reduction in disease incidence (final incidence: 25%). The results of the quantitative PCR analysis supported these observations, thereby demonstrating that both biocontrol strains significantly reduced F. solani gene copy numbers in rhizosphere soils relative to pathogen-only controls, with A74 demonstrating superior suppression efficacy (Fig. 5e). These results further highlight the superior antagonistic capacity of strain A74 against the target pathogen.

Discussion

The rhizosphere microbiome serves as a critical biological interface that governs plant health and disease resistance [36]. This study reveals distinct fungal community composition in tobacco rhizospheres under healthy versus Fusarium-infected conditions across different geographical locations, identifying key disease-suppressive microbial taxa and interactions.

Alpha diversity analysis (Shannon index) showed varied responses among sites, fungal diversity decreased significantly in diseased soils at the HB and LP sites, which is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that soil-borne pathogens destabilize microbial communities via competitive exclusion or toxin-mediated suppression [37]. Notably, Fusarium abundance increased under diseased conditions, while beneficial genera such as Aspergillus were depleted across all sites, thereby suggesting competitive exclusion as a primary driver of disease progression. This depletion of beneficial fungi likely weakens the plant’s natural defence mechanisms, thereby increasing susceptibility to pathogen invasion [38]. PCoA confirmed distinct clustering between healthy and diseased fungal communities across all sites, supported by permutational multivariate analysis. The strong negative correlation observed between Aspergillus (OTU2223) and Fusarium (OTU1), further supports the antagonistic role of Aspergillus in disease suppression, which is potentially accomplished via competition, antibiosis, or systemic resistance induction [39]. This antagonistic relationship has been widely reported in various crops, where Aspergillus species have demonstrated significant potential in suppressing Fusarium and other soil-borne pathogens. For example, in tomato plants, Aspergillus strains have been demonstrated to reduce the incidence of Fusarium wilt by producing antimicrobial compounds that inhibit pathogen growth [40]. In maize, Aspergillus strains such as A. fumigatus AM1 shown to induce systemic resistance against Fusarium pathogens, leading to reduced disease severity and mycotoxin accumulation [41]. Additionally, the volatile organic compound 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol (2,4-DTBP), produced by A. cvjetkovicii, exhibits broad-spectrum antifungal activity against F. fujikuroi in rice, further supporting the role of Aspergillus-derived metabolites in suppressing Fusarium growth and pathogenicity [42]. Cooccurrence network analysis revealed that healthy rhizospheres exhibited greater modularity and more keystone taxa, thereby indicating functional redundancy and resilience [43]. In contrast, diseased networks exhibit a destabilized “high connectivity-low modularity” structure, which is a hallmark of ecosystem dysfunction [44]. These results align with the theory that niche partitioning enhances pathogen resistance [45], as modular networks are better equipped to withstand disturbances and maintain ecosystem stability [46]. However, while our network metrics correlate with disease states, they cannot establish causality. The observed community structures may alternatively reflect pathogen-induced microbiome disruption rather than active plant selection of beneficial taxa. To resolve these competing assembly mechanisms, future studies should apply process-based frameworks (e.g., iCAMP [47]) to quantitatively partition the relative contributions of deterministic versus stochastic processes in shaping rhizosphere communities under disease pressure.

Our in vitro and greenhouse experiments demonstrated that the Aspergillus strain A74 significantly reduced wilt incidence caused by F. solani f. sp. nicotianae in N. benthamiana, while concurrently improving plant growth metrics, including improvements in height, stem diameter, and chlorophyll content. The superior performance of A74 compared with that of other isolates underscored the critical influence of strain-specific traits on disease suppression [48–50]. These findings confirmed that A74 has significant potential for controlling Fusarium wilt but the practical applicability of A74 requires further validation through field trials using cultivated tobacco (N. tabacum).

Conclusion

This study reveals distinct differences in rhizosphere fungal communities between healthy and Fusarium-infected tobacco plants. Healthy plant rhizospheres support beneficial taxa such as Aspergillus with strong antagonistic activity against Fusarium, while diseased rhizospheres exhibit reduced diversity and increased pathogen dominance. The identified Aspergillus strain A74 demonstrates promise as a biocontrol agent, and its integration into sustainable farming practices could help in mitigating Fusarium wilt of tobacco.
