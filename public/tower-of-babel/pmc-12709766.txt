Collision tumor: a parathyroid adenoma unveiling subclinical widespread breast carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Sarra Yacoub, Heyfa Bil Hadj Miled, Wafa Mokni, Hamza Elfkih, Mouna Belakhder, Moncef Mokni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCollision tumors are rare but well-documented phenomena, where two distinct tumors of different origins are found together. We present a unique case of a parathyroid adenoma revealing a subclinical widespread breast carcinoma.Case presentationA 67-year-old Tunisian woman with a history of triple-negative breast carcinoma was diagnosed with hypercalcemia and elevated parathyroid hormone levels. Imaging studies revealed an ectopic mediastinal parathyroid adenoma and a jugulo-carotid mass. Microscopic exam showed a well-circumscribed parathyroid adenoma, surrounded by a thin fibrous capsule, containing multifocal deposits of large, atypical cells negative for parathyroid markers (synaptophysin and chromogranin) and positive for cytokeratin 7. The jugulo-carotid mass was an ectopic thyroid adenoma that was also infiltrated, confirming a subclinical spread of breast carcinoma.ConclusionParathyroid metastases from breast carcinoma are rare, although more frequent than primary malignancies of the parathyroid gland. This case highlights the need to consider metastatic involvement in cases of parathyroid adenoma, particularly in patients with a history of malignancy. Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying tumor-to-tumor metastases and to assess its impact on clinical management and prognosis.

Introduction

Collision tumors are rare but well-documented phenomena, where two distinct tumors of different origins are found together. Tumor-to-tumor metastasis is most commonly described in renal cell carcinoma, and cases involving endocrine tumors as recipients are increasingly recognized [1, 2]. Among these, the coexistence of a parathyroid adenoma (PA) and metastatic breast carcinoma (MBC) remains exceptionally rare, with only seven cases reported in a comprehensive literature review by Bauer L. et al. [1]. The true incidence of this association is likely underestimated, considering that hypercalcemia—a well-known manifestation of breast cancer, occurring in 30–40% of cases—is typically attributed to skeletal metastases in routine clinical practice [3, 4]. Furthermore, the few articles documenting PA metastases are associated with primary tumors other than breast cancer, including lung cancer, renal carcinoma, melanoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma [3, 5]. Herein, we report a case of MBC incidentally discovered within a PA, emphasizing the importance of considering metastatic disease in parathyroid lesions, particularly in oncologic patients.

Case report

A 67-year-old Tunisan woman, diagnosed with triple-negative BC of no special type, underwent a radical mastectomy for invasive breast cancer staged as pT3N1, without any identified metastasis at the time of diagnosis. Adjuvant chemotherapy was indicated. Pre-chemotherapy assessment revealed isolated hypercalcemia, with calcium levels of 2.73 mmol/L (corrected to 2.85 mmol/L). Parathyroid (PTH) hormone levels were elevated at 1943 pg/mL.

On cervical ultrasonography, the right thyroid lobe was multinodular and classified as having Eu-TIRADS 4 nodules. A hypoechoic, heterogeneous, vascularized mass measuring 25 mm × 22 mm, located in the jugulo-carotid region, was identified as a potential ectopic PA. A 99mTc-sestamibi PTH scan more accurately identified an ectopic PA of the right lower parathyroid gland, below the right thyroid lobe, in the upper mediastinal area.

The surgical approach used was an exploratory cervicotomy. A right lobo-isthmectomy was performed, followed by right inferior jugulo-carotid and right paratracheal dissection. This dissection revealed a 30-mm mass with a smooth, regular surface in the lower jugular compartment. The mass was completely excised (Fig. 1). Final microscopic exam of the PTH showed a well-circumscribed PA, mainly composed of chief and oxyphil cells, surrounded by a thin fibrous capsule. The tumor contained multifocal deposits of large, atypical cells showing prominent nucleoli and multiple atypical mitoses, contrasting with the rest of the parathyroid cells (Fig. 2). The jugulo-carotid nodule consisted of an ectopic benign thyroid adenoma that was also infiltrated by atypical cells. Immunohistochemical investigations were performed. Unlike PA cells, the atypical cell deposits were negative for chromogranin and synaptophysin (Fig. 3), estrogen receptor, and HER2 oncoprotein but positive for CK7 (Fig. 4). CK20 and GATA3 were negative. Given the patient’s history, the diagnosis of MBC within a PA was established. Discussion

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy associated with hypercalcemia, and it is attributed in 7% of cases to primary hyperparathyroidism. So far, no common genetic predisposition or shared pathogenesis has been identified between PA and breast cancer [3].

The diagnosis of PTH metastasis is usually unexpected and often under-recognized, as it is typically discovered only when patients develop hyperparathyroidism-induced hypercalcemia [3]. The first documented case of PA metastasis was reported in 1948 and involved breast cancer [2]. To date, breast cancer remains the most frequent primary tumor to metastasize to the PTH gland, accounting for 66.8% of cases [3]. PTH metastases may also originate from leukemias or cutaneous melanoma (11.9%) [2], and, more rarely, from prostate cancer, salivary gland cancer, and hepatocellular carcinomas [2, 5]. These metastases are rarely isolated (3.2% of cases) and almost always associated with widespread disease [1]. In our case, our patient undoubtedly had subclinical widespread metastasis.

Tumor-to-tumor metastasis, though well documented, remains a rare phenomenon. It is defined as the collision of two distinct tumors of different origins, where one acts as the donor and the other as the recipient [1, 2]. The recipient tumor may be either benign or malignant, with clear cell renal cell carcinoma being the most frequent malignant recipient. Among benign tumors, endocrine neoplasms such as those of the thyroid, adrenal cortex, and parathyroid gland account for 45% of all reported benign recipients [2].

PTH metastases appear to be more frequent than primary PTH carcinoma (5.3% versus 1%) [2, 6] and should always be considered when evaluating malignancy-related changes in a PA. Biologically, parathyroid carcinomas are typically associated with markedly elevated serum calcium levels [2], whereas parathyroid metastases may frequently induce hypocalcemia due to PTH tissue destruction [6].

Microscopically, distinguishing PTH metastasis from primary PTH malignancies, including atypical PA and PTH carcinoma, can be challenging owing to overlapping histological features such as cytologic atypia, increased mitotic activity, necrosis, and potential vascular invasion [2]. However, in PTH metastases, these criteria are limited to metastatic deposits without altering the native parathyroid cell populations. Furthermore, the diagnosis of parathyroid carcinoma should be favored whenever macroscopic or microscopic infiltrative growth of the parathyroid capsule is observed [2].

Immunohistochemistry is helpful in distinguishing metastatic deposits from primary PA cells. In our case, breast metastasis was negative for estrogen, progesterone receptors, and HER2 oncoprotein (triple-negative phenotype), making it difficult to specifically identify metastasis origin. In such cases, the use of PTH-specific markers such as synaptophysin, chromogranin, and PTH hormone is sufficient to confirm the diagnosis.

Regarding management, as PTH metastases are almost always discovered as a widespread disease, systematic treatment such as chemotherapy is the cornerstone of treatment.

Conclusion

PA metastases are an under-recognized entity, particularly in the context of breast cancer. Given the histopathological overlap with primary parathyroid malignancies, careful evaluation using histological criteria and immunohistochemistry is essential to ensure an accurate diagnosis. Our case highlights the need to consider metastatic disease when assessing atypical features in a PA, especially in patients with a history of malignancy. Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying tumor-to-tumor metastasis and to assess its impact on clinical management and prognosis.
