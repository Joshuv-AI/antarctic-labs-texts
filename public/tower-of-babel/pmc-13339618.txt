PharmVar GeneFocus: CYP1A2—Clinical Impact, Genetic Variation, and Updated Nomenclature
Authors: Katalin Monostory, Gonzalo Villapalos Garcia, Dora Koller, Solomon Adams, Chad A. Bousman, Evangelia Eirini Tsermpini, Michelle Whirl‐Carrillo, Amy J. Turner, Volker M. Lauschke, Mohamed Nagy, Erin C. Boone, Teri E. Klein, Pablo Zubiaur, Andrea Gaedigk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Pharmacogene Variation Consortium (PharmVar) provides nomenclature for the highly polymorphic human CYP1A2 gene. CYP1A2 plays a crucial role in the biotransformation of several commonly used drugs, including antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics, and methylxanthines. The activity of CYP1A2 varies significantly among individuals due to genetic and non‐genetic factors. This GeneFocus paper offers an overview of the functional significance of CYP1A2 in drug metabolism, CYP1A2 genetic variation, and presents the updated PharmVar star allele‐based nomenclature for CYP1A2. Updates include revisions to previously defined star alleles to align with PharmVar standards, as well as the addition of novel star alleles, providing the pharmacogenetic community with a more comprehensive and high‐quality catalog of CYP1A2 genetic variation. We highlight that the common ‐163C>A (rs762551) variant, which previously defined CYP1A2*1F, is now the sole core variant for a newly established group of CYP1A2*30 alleles. Furthermore, the ‐163C>A variant, which has been associated with increased expression levels in smokers, has been found on various haplotypes in combination with one or more amino acid changing variants, each of which was assigned a unique star number (e.g., CYP1A2*13, *21, *24, and *31). While all alleles containing ‐163C>A may have increased expression levels in smokers, it remains unknown whether the presence of one or more amino acids negates the effect of increased expression levels on CYP1A2 activity. Systematic nomenclature that comprehensively describes CYP1A2 variability is essential for future research aimed at assessing the relationship between CYP1A2 variation, drug metabolism, and the clinical utility of CYP1A2 pharmacogenetic testing.

A BRIEF HISTORY OF RESEARCH ON CYP1A2

Early studies in the 1960s indicated that at least two distinctly different cytochrome P450 (CYP) forms are expressed in rodent liver, the phenobarbital inducible P‐450 and the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) induced P‐448 1 , 2 ; however, in the subsequent decades, the progressive discoveries led to the purification, structural elucidation and functional characterization of many more hepatic CYP isoenzymes. The PAH inducible P‐448 was shown to comprise two isoenzymes, P‐450c and P‐450d in rats, and P1‐450 and P3‐450 in humans. These proteins were eventually designated as CYP1A1 and CYP1A2 based on amino acid sequence homology according to the Cytochrome P450 nomenclature established by Nebert and coworkers in 1987. 3 In the mid‐1980s, a protein with phenacetin O‐deethylase activity was first purified from human liver, and the corresponding cDNA was cloned. 4 , 5 A few years later, the sequence and gene structure of human CYP1A2 were first described in 1989 by Ikeya et al. 6 CYP1A2 is one of the most abundant CYP enzymes expressed in human liver, accounting for 13–15% of the hepatic CYP pool. By contrast, it is detected to a much lower extent in extrahepatic cells and tissues, such as the lung, colon, rectum, and leukocytes. 7 Although substantial interindividual variability in hepatic CYP1A2 activity and expression has been described, 8 the clinical utility of pharmacogenetic testing, as further discussed below, has not been demonstrated. Transcriptional regulation of CYP1A expression has been extensively studied since the late 1970s, revealing the mechanism of induction by polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which involves the aromatic hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and the AhR‐nuclear translocator (ARNT) 9 , 10 , 11 (see more details in the Section Factors influencing CYP1A2 activity).

CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

Ethoxyresorufin O‐deethylation, phenacetin O‐deethylation, and caffeine 3‐N‐demethylation are commonly used markers to characterize human CYP1A2 activity. 12 Although ethoxyresorufin O‐deethylation is catalyzed by CYP1A1 and CYP1A2, it is frequently used as an in vitro marker of hepatic CYP1A2 activity due to its higher relative activity compared with CYP1A1. 13 By contrast, both phenacetin O‐deethylation and caffeine 3‐N‐demethylation are known to be specific indicators of CYP1A2 activity. Furthermore, phenacetin and caffeine are considered probe substrates for in vivo CYP1A2 phenotyping, and the formation of acetaminophen from phenacetin or the plasma paraxanthine/caffeine ratio has been used to characterize hepatic CYP1A2 activity. 14 , 15 Since phenacetin was withdrawn as a therapeutic agent in 1983 due to safety concerns, more recent clinical studies have used caffeine rather than phenacetin as a CYP1A2 probe substrate.

Qualitatively, CYP1A2 is involved in the biotransformation of up to 10% of clinically relevant drugs 16 ; however, its quantitative contribution to the overall metabolism of these substrate drugs can vary significantly. For example, theophylline is extensively metabolized by CYP1A2 (90–95%), while its contribution to the metabolism of other drugs like clomipramine, erlotinib, or pimozide is relatively minor (~10%). 15 CYP1A2 is crucial for the elimination of many therapeutic agents used in psychiatric practice, including various antipsychotics, antidepressants, and anxiolytics. CYP1A2 is involved in the metabolism of both first‐generation antipsychotics, such as chlorpromazine (5–10%), and pimozide, as well as second‐generation antipsychotics, like olanzapine (30–40%), and clozapine (40–55%). However, other enzymes, such as CYPs, flavin‐containing monooxygenases, or UDP‐glucuronyltransferases, also contribute to the metabolism of these drugs. CYP1A2 is also involved in the elimination of several antidepressants, including duloxetine (30–40%), fluvoxamine (5–10%), agomelatine (90%), and imipramine (10–20%). 17 Additionally, associations between CYP1A2 function and the metabolism of the anticancer drugs dacarbazine (20–40%) and flutamide (10–25%), as well as the cholinesterase inhibitor tacrine (>90%), have also been reported. 15 , 18

The cDNA sequence of human CYP1A2 is approximately 70% identical to that of CYP1A1; however, there are surprisingly few common substrates for these two enzymes. While CYP1A2 is the dominant enzyme for caffeine metabolism in humans, 19 CYP1A1 appears to play some role in the demethylation of caffeine in vitro. 20 However, there is no direct evidence that CYP1A1 contributes to systemic caffeine clearance under human physiological conditions. By contrast, procarcinogenic and promutagenic environmental pollutants or food‐derived compounds, such as polycyclic hydrocarbons and heterocyclic aromatic amines, are activated by both CYP1A enzymes. 18 CYP1A enzymes are responsible for the bioactivation of benzo[a]pyrene to the mutagenic 7,8‐epoxide intermediate and of 2‐amino‐1‐methyl‐6‐phenylimidazo‐(4,5‐b)pyridine (PhIP) to N 2‐hydroxy‐PhIP. On the contrary, CYP1A2 is also involved in detoxification processes. For example, it converts the hepatocarcinogenic dietary contaminant aflatoxin B1 into the non‐genotoxic aflatoxin M1 metabolite. CYP1A2 also plays a role in the biotransformation of some endogenous compounds, including 17β‐estradiol, estrone, and melatonin. 21

FACTORS INFLUENCING CYP1A2 ACTIVITY

Variability in hepatic CYP1A2 activity may impact the clearance of several clinically important drugs and environmental entities that are processed by this enzyme. Significant interindividual differences have been observed in vitro when measuring phenacetin O‐deethylation in human liver microsomes, with variations ranging from 24‐ to 164‐fold or intact hepatocyte cultures, showing up to a 12‐fold difference. In vivo studies using caffeine as a probe substrate showed differences of 4‐ to 54‐fold. 8 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 These differences in CYP1A2 activity can be attributed to varying levels of hepatic CYP1A2 protein abundance or alterations in enzyme function, which can be influenced by both genetic and non‐genetic factors.

Physiological and pathophysiological conditions

Among the non‐genetic factors, age and sex are assumed to influence CYP1A2 mRNA/protein expression and activity. Hepatic CYP1A2 expression is negligible before birth and increases after the neonatal period. In children, CYP1A2 levels can reach or even exceed those of adults. 27 Additionally, studies have shown sex‐related differences, with higher CYP1A2 activity noted in men compared with women, which has been attributed to differences in CYP1A2 protein abundance. 28 , 29 Estradiol, the primary estrogen hormone in women of reproductive age, was shown to reduce CYP1A2 protein expression levels. 30

Emerging evidence indicates that acute and chronic inflammation that is associated with certain disease states can downregulate CYP1A2 expression. This process is primarily mediated by pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as interferons, interleukins (e.g., IL‐1, IL‐6), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα). 31 , 32

Environmental factors and drug–drug interactions

Several drugs, dietary components, and environmental factors have been extensively studied for their potential to cause alterations in CYP1A2 activity. These factors can cause apparent changes in CYP1A2 expression by transcriptional induction or in CYP1A2 function by inhibiting enzymatic activity.

Variability in CYP1A2 expression can be attributed to transcriptional induction of the CYP1A2 gene by the proton pump inhibitor omeprazole, as well as by dietary components, such as caffeine, cruciferous vegetables, and charbroiled food, and by components of tobacco smoke. In 1979, a cytosolic protein called the aromatic hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) was identified as a xenobiotic‐sensing receptor 9 which mediates the induction of CYP1A genes. 33 Upon direct binding to the ligand, AhR enters the nucleus and forms a heterodimer with the AhR‐nuclear translocator (ARNT). The ligand‐bound AhR/ARNT dimer then recruits coactivators and forms a transcriptional complex that binds to the xenobiotic response element (XRE) in the promoter of the target genes, including CYP1A2.

Direct inhibition of CYP1A2 activity by fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin, furafylline, ipriflavone, oral contraceptives containing ethinyl estradiol or gestodene, and some natural flavonoids (e.g., galangin, quercetin) can reduce the clearance of CYP1A2 substrate drugs. 34 , 35 , 36

Genetic factors

Numerous single nucleotide variants (SNVs) have been identified in the coding exons of CYP1A2, as well as in the surrounding sequences, including the non‐coding 5′ region and intron 1 sequences which may influence CYP1A2 expression and consequently the function of CYP1A2 enzyme (also see section on CYP1A2 allele frequencies and function below). The most extensively studied SNV is ‐163C>A (rs762551) located in intron 1, which has been associated with increased CYP1A2 transcription in smokers 25 ; however, the mechanism remains unclear. Notably, significant interactions between the ‐163C>A variant of CYP1A2 and smoking, heavy coffee consumption, or omeprazole treatment have been observed. 37 , 38 , 39 Specifically, the basal activity of CYP1A2 is similar in individuals who carry the ‐163A variant and those who do not. However, the inducibility of CYP1A2 by smoking, coffee consumption, or omeprazole appears to be increased in ‐163A carriers, causing accelerated metabolism of CYP1A2 substrates compared with non‐carriers. 40 , 41

Twin studies have suggested that genetic factors highly contribute to the variability in CYP1A2 activity; however, environmental effects, such as smoking and oral contraceptives, appear to mask the heritability component. 42 , 43 It must be noted, though, that differences in CYP1A2 activity have not been proven to be clinically relevant to date.

CLINPGX AND CPIC

ClinPGx (formerly PharmGKB) 44 , 45 is a valuable resource for pharmacogenomics that provides crucial clinical information including pharmacogenomic‐based drug dosing guidelines and drug labels that include pharmacogenomic data, while providing information on how genetic variants influence drug efficacy and toxicity. The ClinPGx CYP1A2 gene page 46 provides structured access to gene‐specific information.

As of February 2026, the ClinPGx CYP1A2 page contains 48 drug‐centered pathways. While all focus on pharmacokinetics, seven also include pharmacodynamics. Among others, CYP1A2 is included in the pathway of neurological, psychiatric, cardiovascular, hematological, and anticancer agents. ClinPGx currently also provides over 180 variant annotations related to CYP1A2, each based on a single curated peer‐reviewed publication, and 28 summary annotations which are evidence‐rated genotype level summaries for specific variant–drug combinations, all annotations are at level 3 or 4 indicating low or unsupported evidence. These annotations span associations between drugs with CYP1A2 genetic variation. Two drug label annotations are provided for rucaparib related to CYP1A2, one from the European Medicines Agency and one from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, but neither has actionable pharmacogenetics information. The same stands for the EMA label for olanzapine related to CYP1A2. ClinPGx includes two annotations of the Dutch Pharmacogenomic Working Group (DPWG) guidelines for CYP1A2 with clozapine and olanzapine, which provide no clinical recommendations citing no gene–drug interactions. The Clinical Pharmacogenomics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) has not published any pharmacogenetics‐based dosing guidelines for CYP1A2.

PharmVar sources clinical allele function information from CPIC, which assigns allele function using a consensus‐based framework. 47 However, since CPIC has no guideline for CYP1A2, it has not evaluated or assigned CYP1A2 allele function.

THE CYP1A2 GENE LOCUS

The relatively small, 7762 bp‐long CYP1A2 gene is located on the positive strand of chromosome 15 at position 15q24.1, while the close‐by CYP1A1 gene is encoded on the reverse strand. CYP1A2 consists of seven exons and six introns. 48 Notably, the ATG translation start site is in the second exon, leaving the first exon untranslated (Figure 1 a ).

Previous work on CYP1A2 genetic variability has primarily focused on the region upstream of the ATG translation start site. Variants in this region have traditionally been referred to as “upstream variants” in the literature and were catalogued as such by the original P450 Nomenclature database, regardless of whether they were in intron 1 or indeed upstream of the non‐coding first exon. Throughout this GeneFocus, variants upstream of the ATG start codon are referred to by their positions on the genomic reference sequence (RefSeq) NG_061543.1 (Figure 1 also shows their respective transcription positions as prescribed by the Human Genome Variation Consortium (HGVS) 49 ). For example, rs762551 located in intron 1 is referred to as ‐163C>A in this GeneFocus throughout the text instead of c.‐9‐154C>A, while the respective transcript positions of all SNVs in coding regions are denoted by their respective transcript positions.

THE PHARMVAR CYP1A2 GENE PAGE

The PharmVar CYP1A2 gene page 50 lists all defined star alleles along with important information, including (i) legacy names, (ii) SNVs defining each allele relative to several reference sequences and their rs identification numbers (rsIDs), (iii) haplotype evidence level, and (iv) references supporting allele definitions or “deposited by.” Each haplotype is assigned to a unique numeric identifier, called the PharmVar ID (PVID). Users can track PVIDs and their haplotype definitions in the database using the PVID Lookup function. Additionally, the page features the Read Me and the Change Log documents, which facilitate cross‐referencing between legacy and PharmVar star allele designations. These documents provide additional relevant gene‐specific information and resources, which are periodically updated.

Variant mapping

The PharmVar CYP1A2 gene page displays variants mapped to the genomic (NG_061543.1) and transcript (NM_000761.5) reference sequences, GRCh37 (NC_000015.9), and the current GRCh38 (NC_000015.10) genome build, facilitating easy cross‐referencing. In addition to positions, the display also provides variant rsIDs (if available) and corresponding amino acid changes. Of note, the Variant Window (activated when clicking on a SNV) provides variant annotations according to HGVS 49 and in PharmVar‐style format side‐by‐side for all reference sequences.

Haplotype evidence levels

PharmVar assigns Definitive (Def), Moderate (Mod), and Limited (Lim) Haplotype Evidence Levels for each star allele, indicating the level of evidence supporting an allele definition (not an allele's function). 51 Several CYP1A2 alleles are currently labeled as “Lim” as they have not been fully characterized according to PharmVar standards. Examples include CYP1A2*2.001, *3.001, and *5.001, which all lack information regarding SNVs in their 3′ untranslated region (UTR). Star alleles with an evidence level of “Mod” were fully sequenced, but their variants were not validated by an orthogonal method and were found only in a single individual; this scenario is exemplified by CYP1A2*8.001, *12.001, *25.001, or *41.001. PharmVar solicits submissions for all alleles with “Lim” and “Mod” evidence levels to substantiate their current definitions and encourages encore submissions for alleles with a definitive evidence level and single citations to further corroborate their haplotype definitions.

Core allele definitions

According to PharmVar terminology, a core allele is defined solely by sequence variants that are present in all haplotypes within the same allele group (i.e, are listed under the same star number) and cause an amino acid change, interfere with splicing, or impact expression levels. These variants are known as core variants or core SNVs. Using this rule‐based system, alleles within a group (also referred to by PharmVar as suballeles) can be collapsed into a single core definition that represents all haplotypes categorized under a star (*) number. For example, as shown in Figure 1b,c , all haplotypes listed under CYP1A2*30 have ‐163C>A as their core variant, while all haplotypes under CYP1A2*22 have two core variants, ‐163C>A and c.894C>A (p.S298R, rs17861157).

ClinPGx uses core allele definitions for summary annotations. All alleles catalogued under the same star number are presumed to be functionally equivalent. Thus, even if a test can distinguish suballeles, these can be simply reported using core allele definitions.

The PharmVar Comparative Allele ViewEr (CAVE) and filter options

The CAVE tool enables users to visually compare core alleles. Figure 2 illustrates the utility of this tool by showing the alleles depicted in Figure 1 b,c . This display mode makes it easy to see which core SNVs are unique and which are shared among star alleles. For example, seven of the selected star alleles have ‐163C>A (CYP1A2*13‐*15, *21, *24, *30, and *31). The only other SNV that is also part of several star alleles (CYP1A2*21‐*23 and *36) is c.894C>A (rs17861157, p.S298R). Currently, all other nonsynonymous core variants are unique to a star allele; for example, c.103delG is exclusive to CYP1A2*27, and c.137G>A is unique to CYP1A2*24. Alternatively, haplotypes can also be selectively displayed with the Haplotype Filter option or searched by using SNV position or their rsID; these options are offered in the Variant Window.

CYP1A2 VARIATION AND NOMENCLATURE

Before CYP1A2 nomenclature was introduced into the PharmVar database in December 2024, several star alleles were incompletely characterized. Some alleles were defined solely based on the testing of selected “upstream” variants, while others included variants as far upstream as −3028. Among those are many of the alleles originally listed under the *1 designation, including CYP1A2*1C, D, E, G, H, J, K, V, and W, 25 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 which have not been transferred into the PharmVar database. For other alleles, such as CYP1A2*4, 54 , 56 it remains uncertain whether they contain the variant c.1548 T>C (rs2470890, p.N516=). Investigators may not have submitted this variant because it is synonymous, or they may not have identified it as a variant since reference sequences used in the past had a “C” at position c.1548, whereas this is now defined as the variant nucleotide. Also, there was no information regarding variation for the 3′UTR for any of the star alleles. To provide a high‐quality haplotype‐based nomenclature for CYP1A2, the expert panel carefully reviewed all existing star allele definitions, including the primary literature. The regions used for star allele definitions are now clearly defined and include untranslated 5′ and 3′ regions, as well as the entire first intron. With the updated CYP1A2 nomenclature system, submissions must include all variants between positions 4974 (5′ limit) and 11,494 (3′ limit) of the genomic reference sequence NG_061543.1, except for introns 2–6. Intronic variants are included in star allele definitions only if they are within a splice recognition site, shown to interfere with splicing or otherwise impact function (see the Read Me document available through the CYP1A2 PharmVar gene page 50 for additional details). Notably, ‐3860G>A (rs2069514) and ‐2467delT (rs35694136), or any other more distant variants, are no longer captured in star allele definitions as explained in the following paragraphs.

CYP1A2 upstream variants have been extensively studied in the past. However, the impact of these variants on CYP1A2 activity and clinical significance remains unclear due to inconsistent findings. We conducted a literature review to evaluate the relevance of the most studied CYP1A2 upstream variants, ‐3860G>A (rs2069514) and ‐2467delT (rs35694136). Briefly, a PubMed search yielded 27 publications for ‐3860G>A and 23 publications for ‐2467delT of which 11 and nine were reviewed for ‐3860G>A and ‐2467delT, respectively. There was insufficient evidence supporting a meaningful association of ‐3860G>A or ‐2467delT with CYP1A2 activity. Evaluated literature is provided in the Supplemental Materials.

We also performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) analyses, using Ldlink, 58 a web‐based LD tool, to query pairwise LD between variants in 1000 Genomes Project data. As shown in Figure 3 and Figure S1 , ‐3860G>A and ‐2467delT are almost completely linked with ‐163C>A (D′ > 0.95) in the global and across all five 1000 Genome major populations (African, Admixed American, East Asian, European, South Asian). Importantly, when ‐3860G>A is present, the resulting haplotype almost always includes ‐163C>A. However, haplotypes containing ‐163C>A do not necessarily include ‐3860G>A. This pattern of linkage disequilibrium may explain why associations between the ‐163C>A variant and CYP1A2 activity are more consistently observed in smokers, whereas those involving the ‐3860G>A variant are inconclusive and may depend on the presence of the ‐163C>A variant.

CYP1A2 linkage disequilibrium analyses of ‐3860G>A, ‐2467delT, and ‐163C>A. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was performed on the 1000 Genomes Project data (Genome Build GRCh38 including all populations; Figure S1 provides population‐specific information), using the web‐based LDlink tool. 58 Pairwise analysis indicates that almost all subjects with ‐163C>A (rs762551) also have ‐2467delT (rs35694136) and ‐3860G>C (rs2069514). However, many subjects with the ‐2467delT and ‐3860G>C variants do not have the ‐163C>A variant.

All haplotypes containing ‐163C>A that do not result in amino acid changes are now classified under a new star number, CYP1A2*30. In addition to CYP1A2*30, there are currently 26 other star alleles that contain ‐163C>A along with one or more amino acid changes, namely CYP1A2*9, *10, *13, *14, *15, *17, *21‐*26, *29, *31, *32, *34, *36, *37, *40‐*45, *47, and *49. By contrast, over 20 star alleles have one or more amino acid changing variant(s) but not ‐163C>A. Selected star alleles are shown in Figure 1 b,c . At the time of this writing, it remains unclear which of the star alleles containing ‐163C>A, aside from CYP1A2*30, are responsible for increased enzyme expression. It is possible that the impact of ‐163C>A in smokers could be negated by other amino acid changes. Therefore, it is important to recognize that tests focused solely on the ‐163C>A variant identify more than half of the currently defined CYP1A2 alleles. As a result, such tests should not be considered a valid method for defining CYP1A2*30 alleles or for predicting CYP1A2 expression levels or in vivo phenotype.

Distant regions upstream of CYP1A2 harboring xenobiotic‐response and other regulatory elements were excluded from star allele definitions. To the best of our knowledge, no variant(s) within the distant regulatory CYP1A2 region have been described to date that impact activity. Should such variants be discovered in the future, PharmVar will assess whether their inclusion into star allele nomenclature would be practical or whether such variants should rather be assessed on their own as exemplified by the CYP2C “cluster” variant rs12777823 59 which is not part of CYP2C star allele nomenclature. Lastly, regulatory variants affecting drug metabolism remain poorly understood across CYP and other pharmacogenes 60 making it currently difficult to integrate expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) into star allele nomenclature.

Considering all this information, the experts concluded that there is insufficient justification to include extended upstream regions beyond the first exon in the updated CYP1A2 allele nomenclature. This decision was made to ensure the clinical utility and clarity of pharmacogenetic recommendations.

CYP1A2 ALLELE FREQUENCIES AND FUNCTION

PharmVar provides global variant frequencies utilizing gnomAD 61 as a resource; these frequencies may vary across different populations. More information regarding differences in SNV frequencies across global populations can be obtained from the external links to dbSNP, 62 gnomAD, and ClinPGx. 45 While the intronic ‐163C>A (rs762551) variant has a high frequency across populations (global frequency of 67% per gnomAD v4.1.0), nonsynonymous SNVs overall appear to have frequencies under 1%. It is stressed that variant frequency data do not necessarily reflect haplotype frequencies for SNVs that are part of two or more haplotypes. Star allele (haplotype) frequencies have not been determined by ClinPGx as CYP1A2 is not currently a CPIC guideline gene. The Pharmacogenetic Allele Frequency (PharmFreq) database 63 currently does also not contain any information for CYP1A2.

There is considerable variability in CYP1A2 activity among individuals, which is, at least in part, assumed to be linked to genetic variation in the CYP1A2 gene. Most studies investigating the functional impact of CYP1A2 variants have focused on the upstream SNVs, particularly on ‐163C>A. Only a limited number of studies have explored and demonstrated associations between CYP1A2 activity and nonsynonymous SNVs in the coding region. In vitro studies using recombinant CYP1A2 expression systems highlighted the functional significance of some nonsynonymous SNVs. 64 , 65 , 66 The core variants of CYP1A2*4 (c.1156A>T, rs72547516, p.I386F), CYP1A2*6 (c.1291C>T, rs28399424, p.R431W), CYP1A2*8 (c.1367G>A, rs72547517, p.R456H), CYP1A2*11 (c.558C>A, rs72547513, p.F186L), CYP1A2*15 (c.125C>G, rs72547511, p.P42R), CYP1A2*16 (c.1130G>A, rs72547515, p.R377Q), and CYP1A2*21 (c.1485C>A, rs143193369, p.Y495X) have been reported to be associated with substantially reduced or even no phenacetin O‐deethylation activity. 65 The c.1156A>T substitution resulting in a p.I386F amino acid change is in the orthosteric substrate recognition site of CYP1A2 and is assumed to contribute to substrate affinity and specificity. The c.1367G>A variant causes a p.R456H change, which is in the heme‐binding region, whereas c.125C>G results in a p.P42R substitution in the proline‐rich region that is required for assuming a functional enzyme conformation. The c.1485C>A produces a truncated protein due to the introduction of a premature stop codon that likely impacts function. Furthermore, two core variants, c.1313C>T (rs45486893, p.T438I) and c.1306G>A (rs144148965, p.D436N), defining CYP1A2*14 and CYP1A2*20, respectively, had a significant increase in activity compared with the CYP1A2*1 reference. Lastly, c.103delG (rs758752876, defining CYP1A2*27), causing a frameshift, and c.137G>A (rs568839929, defining CYP1A2*24), resulting in a premature stop codon (p.W46X), are predicted to abolish function. Additional studies, ideally including in vivo phenotyping and sequencing, are essential to clarify the impact of CYP1A2 allelic variation on activity and potential clinical utility. Since there are currently no CPIC guidelines for this gene, PharmVar shows allele clinical function as “not available” (n/a).

CHARACTERIZATION OF CYP1A2 ALLELES

To address the many questions regarding clinical utility of CYP1A2 variation, future studies investigating the impact of CYP1A2 variation on pharmacokinetics and/or treatment outcome should thoroughly characterize the entire gene, rather than genotyping only a few selected SNVs. Since CYP1A2 is a relatively small gene, it can easily be sequenced in its entirety by Sanger sequencing or ideally by single molecule sequencing that allows variant phasing. These approaches may also include more distant SNVs to gain additional insights regarding their association, or lack thereof, on CYP1A2 activity. For an overview and more general information regarding the characterization of allelic variation, we refer to previously published GeneFocus articles. 67 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73

To validate previously reported CYP1A2 star alleles and to provide a more comprehensive catalog of fully characterized allelic variation, we analyzed publicly available high‐quality Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) data from the 1000 Genomes Project (1K‐GP). 74 Briefly, CYP1A2 variant information within the genomic regions defined by PharmVar was extracted from the 1000 Genomes Project dataset. Haplotype phasing was subsequently performed using the following approach: First, we identified individuals who were either homozygous for all variants or heterozygous for a single variant, as these scenarios allowed for unambiguous phasing. Next, we screened all family trios using RStudio (v4.4.1). Briefly, a matrix of all possible phase combinations was generated for each family member (father, mother, and child), and each possible combination of the parental haplotypes was evaluated against the child's haplotype to determine a compatible inheritance model. If unique maternal and paternal haplotype combinations were identified, the child's phase was resolved with certainty. Subsequently, we inferred the complementary maternal and paternal haplotypes from the child's phased haplotypes. All haplotype assignments used for PharmVar submissions were manually checked for accuracy.

Figure 4 displays the diplotypes determined for an exemplary trio (family ID 2367). Both parents are heterozygous for three variants and homozygous for one variant, while the child is heterozygous for two variants and homozygous for three. Only one combination of haplotypes is possible, allowing for an unambiguous inference of CYP1A2*1.002/*23.001 for the father, *1.002/*22.002 for the mother, and *22.002/*23.001 for the child.

CYP1A2 diplotypes inferred by inheritance. The phase of the SNVs present in this Coriell family trio (family ID 2367, NA19700, father; NA19701, mother, and NA19702, child) was resolved by inheritance. CYP1A2*22 (present in both mother and child) and CYP1A2*23 (present in both the father and child) were first identified in this trio and named by PharmVar. Notably, CYP1A2*22 and *23 both have the ‐163C>A variant in combination with other nonsynonymous SNVs.

In addition, targeted diploid assemblies for the CYP1A gene locus were generated for 137 Coriell samples using PacBio HiFi sequence data that were available through the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium Data Release 2 75 (of note, these 137 samples are not the same as those characterized by the GeT‐RM described below). Among these assemblies, five haplotypes were identified that confirmed existing star alleles, while 15 represented new haplotypes, six of which were previously unknown (CYP1A2*22 and *24‐*28). Notably, CYP1A2*24.001 was identified in the same sample (Coriell ID NA20905), whereas *22.001 was found in a different individual using the 1K‐GP WGS dataset.

These data mining efforts yielded a total of 62 sequences, including 23 new star alleles: CYP1A2*22‐*24, *27, *29, *31‐*47, and *49. In addition, several previously defined haplotypes were revised by adding synonymous variants or variants in previously uncovered regions. A detailed record of submitted sequences and revisions made to existing haplotypes can be found in the Change Log document on the PharmVar CYP1A2 page. 50

The revised and expanded catalog of CYP1A2 star alleles will also be invaluable for bioinformatic tools, such as Aldy, 76 PyPGx, 77 StellarPGx, 78 and pb‐Starphase 79 to improve on diplotype call accuracy from next generation sequencing data, thereby facilitating genotype to phenotype association analyses. If an allele is not defined by PharmVar, tools may return “no call” or “indeterminate” genotype result or default to CYP1A2*1 or another allele.

REFERENCE MATERIALS

A 2016 study performed by the Genetic Testing Reference Material Program (GeT‐RM) characterized 137 genomic DNA reference materials for 28 pharmacogenetic genes, including CYP1A2. 80 , 81 This study identified several CYP1A2 haplotypes using the legacy/previous nomenclature. The alleles examined included *1A, *1C, *1D, *1F, *1K, *1L, *2‐*8, *11, *15, and *16. Samples were identified for CYP1A2*1A, *1C, *1F, *1K, and *1L. GeT‐RM has initiated a new project to update this data and will reassess selected genes, including CYP1A2, using the current PharmVar nomenclature.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This GeneFocus article provides a concise review of CYP1A2 function, the factors that influence hepatic expression and enzyme activity, as well as essential information on CYP1A2 genetic variation. We highlight significant changes made during its transition to the PharmVar database, which now offers a curated catalog of high‐quality, haplotype‐based star allele definitions. This advancement will facilitate future research into the relationships between CYP1A2 genetic variation, drug metabolism, and the use of pharmacogenetic testing, a topic that remains controversial. Previous studies have primarily focused on a few specific variants, such as ‐163C>A (rs762551), ‐3860G>A (rs2069514), and ‐2467delT (rs35694136). However, the latter two variants are no longer included in the current star allele definitions. Additionally, these studies have often overlooked haplotype structure and coding variants, which may partially explain the inconsistent findings and the lack of actionable CYP1A2‐based dosing guidance. Despite the absence of guidelines, CYP1A2 testing, typically limited to the ‐163C>A variant, is available through commercial laboratories. However, this limited CYP1A2 testing is insufficient to accurately infer CYP1A2 activity. The updated CYP1A2 PharmVar nomenclature provides a new framework to more comprehensively investigate CYP1A2 genetic variation to enhance phenotype prediction and reevaluate its clinical applicability.
