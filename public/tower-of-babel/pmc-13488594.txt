Metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins oral exposure via potato chips ingestion: A Monte Carlo-simulation risk assessment for Iranian consumers
Authors: Hamid Ahmadpourmir, Seyedeh Faezeh Taghizadeh, Ramin Rezaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study assesses the risk associated with oral exposure to metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins through the consumption of potato chips among Iranian consumers using Monte Carlo simulation. At the 50th, 80th, and 90th centiles, the calculated non-carcinogenic Hazard Index (HI) indicated no evident risk for adults but a moderate risk for children concerning oral exposure to these substances. Under carcinogenic scenario, for adults, the mean Incremental Life Time Cancer Risk (ILCR) for arsenic (As) and cadmium (Cd) at these centiles exceeded 10−4, indicating potential regulatory concerns under conservative assumptions. Conversely, the mean ILCRs for nickel (Ni) ranged between 10−6 and 10−4 and for lead (Pb) were less than 10−6, falling within an acceptable range. For children, the mean ILCRs at these centiles for As, Cd, and Ni, also surpassed 10−4, reflecting potential regulatory concerns. The mean ILCR associated with lead exposure in children was comparable to those identified for adults. For both adults and children, the HI values associated with pesticides exposure at these centiles were indicative of a moderate risk. Importantly, the percentage of Tolerable Daily Intakes (TDIs) for mycotoxins oral exposure for both adults and children reached intolerable levels. Furthermore, with respect to the carcinogenic risks posed by aflatoxins (AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2), a significantly elevated cumulative risk for children was calculated in comparison to adults. Stringent measures need to be implemented to mitigate the occurrence of the identified contaminants in potato chips, to reduce the oral exposure and potential risk associated, particularly given the widespread consumption of this popular snack by children.

Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum) is the fourth most important global crop, with around 364.8 million tons produced annually [1]. Potato chips are among the most popular snacks for the Iranian population. In fact, potato chips production accounts for the largest proportion of potato cultivation in Iran and the market is constantly facing higher demands [2]. Therefore, occurrence of contaminants in potato chips can potentially pose a considerable health risk to consumers [3].

Metals occur in the environment through natural or anthropogenic sources. Pedogenetic processes result in the occurrence of metals at trace levels [4]. Metals are abundant in the earth's crust but most environmental pollution and human exposure stem from activities such as mining, smelting, industrial production, and agricultural use of metal-containing fertilizers [5], [6]. Agricultural chemical use causes contamination, resulting in heavy metal accumulation in soil, crops, and water. Metals like Cd, which have long half-lives (15–30 years), persist in the environment, posing significant toxicity risks to plants, animals, and humans [7]. On the other hand, heavy metals dissolve readily in aquatic environments, allowing living organisms to absorb them easily and posing significant risks to ecosystems and the health of various species within those environments and food chain [8]. Metals are not bio- or thermo-degradable, they have high bioaccumulation tendencies and they can contaminate potatoes at different stages of cultivation, handling, harvesting, and post-harvesting [9]. Fertilizers and pesticides are considered major anthropogenic sources of introduction of cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), arsenic (As), nickel (Ni) and mercury (Hg) to agricultural soil [10], [11]. Among different crops, potatoes and root vegetables contain the highest levels of Cd, along with cereals and leafy greens [12], [13].

Residues of synthetic pesticides have the potential to contaminate groundwater and agricultural lands, thereby leading to their accumulation within food chains [14]. The use of pesticides for pest control and the management of potato diseases poses a significant health risk to consumers as pesticides residues are reportedly found in potato chips [15], [16], [17]. Given the high consumption of potato chips, especially among children who are a particularly vulnerable group, it is essential to regularly monitor pesticide residues in these products [18], [19], [20].

Fungi contamination may occur in raw, during production or because of improper storage and transportation conditions particularly following prolonged storage under high humidity [21], [22]. Fungi are among the most resilient food contaminants and they are capable of secreting harmful mycotoxins under suitable environmental conditions [23]. Aflatoxins (AFs), classified as secondary metabolites, are produced by strains of Aspergillus flavus, A. parasiticus, and A. nomius [24]. The European Union (EU) has identified AFs, specifically aflatoxin B1, B2, G1, G2, and M1, as well as ochratoxin A (OTA), deoxynivalenol (DON), zearalenone (ZON), sterigmatocystin (STG), patulin (PAT), and fumonisins (FBs), as substances of considerable concern due to their potent toxicological properties and associated health risks [25]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) categorizes AFB1 as a Group 1 carcinogen [26]. Moreover, AFs exert potent teratogenic, mutagenic, estrogenic, and immunosuppressive effects [27], [28].

The contamination of raw agricultural commodities by toxic elements, agricultural chemicals, and fungal metabolites is a well-documented global concern. However, evaluating the risks associated with oral exposure to solely these toxic agents in raw crops overlooks the profound impact of food processing. During the manufacture of potato chips, industrial frying and moisture reduction can significantly concentrate non-volatile contaminants such as heavy metals, while heat-stable mycotoxins easily survive processing temperatures [29]. Furthermore, current food safety practices frequently assess these hazards in isolation. In reality, however, consumers are simultaneously exposed to potentially toxic metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins that can potentially produce additive or synergistic toxicological effects and may cause various health condition upon repeated long-term exposure even when individual contaminant levels fall below permissible limits [30]. Such exposure is particularly critical for vulnerable populations. Due to their developing physiological systems and a significantly higher food-intake-to-body-weight ratio, children absorb and retain higher relative concentrations of dietary toxins than adults, highlighting the necessity to age-segregate risk modeling. Current food safety enforcement primarily relies on Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) established strictly for raw agricultural commodities. However, there is a profound scarcity of standardized regulatory thresholds outlined specifically for multi-class contaminants in processed snack foods.

Despite the high consumption rate of potato chips in Iran, there is insufficient comprehensive data regarding the simultaneous presence of multiple chemical residues in these products. To address this gap, this study focuses on a multi-class contaminant screening combined with a dietary risk model tailored to different age groups. Thus, the present study aimed to (1) simultaneously analyze the occurrence of metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins in potato chips samples from market in Iran and (2) conduct a risk assessment for oral exposure to these chemicals for Iranian adult and children consumers. Considering the popularity of this product as a commonly consumed snack, findings of this study are essential to safeguard consumers health.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

A total of forty potato chips samples (commonly known as “salty” or “simple” chips as only salt is mentioned as a flavor in the market in Iran) were collected from Iran markets. These samples represented ten distinct batches from four brands called A, B, C, and D in this study. Notably, at the time of sample collection, all samples were purchased in similar packaging. Furthermore, the samples were stored under conditions close to those of the retail environments until subsequent analyses. For sample preparation, each sample was separately ground by a food grinder to a fine powder and stored at −18 °C until analysis.

Chemicals and standards

All standards of metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins (98.0–99.0% purity) and solvents (HPLC grade) including ethyl acetate (EtOAc), methanol (MeOH), acetonitrile (MeCN), nitric acid (HNO3), formic acid, and n-hexane were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany).

Instrumentation

Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) for metals analysis

Subsequent to the digestion of the samples, Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES, SPECTRO ARCOS apparatus (Germany)) was employed for the simultaneous detection of metals. This analysis was executed with a Flared end EOP Torch measuring 2.5 mm in diameter.

The plasma power was 1.2 kW, with the argon (flow rate 15.0 L/min) and an auxiliary flow of 1.50 L/min. Readings lasted 60 s, nebulizer pressure was 250 kPa, and optimal parameters comprised a radio-frequency generator at 1400 W. The total duration for sample uptake, rinse, and stabilization was 240 s, with 45 s for both rinse and pre-flush. There was no delay or interval between replicate analyses. A solid-state detector was used, and the spray chamber featured a Charge-Coupled Device (CCD) and Modified Lichte cyclonic design. The sample injection pump was set to 30 rpm [31].

Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) for pesticides analysis

An Agilent 7890 A Turbo MSD 5975 C (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA), equipped with a programmable temperature vaporizer (PTV) inlet, 7683B auto injector, and HP−5MS segment (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm), was utilized with helium as the carrier gas flowing at 1.0 mL/min. The temperature program started at 70 °C for 1 min, then increased to 130 °C at 10 °C/min, then to 230 °C at 6 °C/min, and finally, to 250 °C at 8 °C/min and kept for 13 min. For each sample, 1 µL was injected at 250 °C. The main ion for each compound was determined through electron ionization at the level of 70 eV, employing selected ion monitoring (SIM) techniques. This methodology enabled the identification of pesticides based on their distinctive ions and GC retention times [6].

Liquid chromatography coupled with triple quadrupole and ion trap tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS-IT) for mycotoxins analysis

Mycotoxins were identified and quantified using an Agilent 1200 Liquid Chromatograph coupled with 3200 QTRAP® Mass Spectrometer. The analytes were separated using a Gemini NX-C18 column (150 × 2 mm, particle size 3 μm, 110 A), preceded by a C18 column. The analysis time was 30 min with a 20 μL injection volume. The mobile phase had two eluents: eluent A containing H2O, 5 mM ammonium formate, and 0.1% formic acid, and eluent B containing MeOH, 5 mM ammonium formate, and 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was initially increased to 0.400 mL/min, consistent to 100% eluent B. Subsequently, during the interval from 21 to 30 min, the flow rate was reduced to 0.250 mL/min with a composition of 10% eluent B and 90% eluent A. The mass spectrometric characteristics used in this study included multiple reaction monitoring, with the ionization source operating under turbo spray conditions and utilizing positive ionization polarity (ESI+).

Extraction procedures

Extraction procedure for determination of metals

For the analysis of metals, 5 g of each sample was subjected to digestion utilizing 30 mL of HNO3 (65%) and 10 mL of H2O2 (30%) within a microwave digestion apparatus for 25 min and then, the final mixture was diluted to 50 mL using 1% HNO3. Clear liquid samples were subjected to ICP-OES analysis [32].

Extraction procedure for determination of pesticides

For this analysis, 2.5 g of each sample was weighed and placed in a 50-mL falcon tube. Then, the samples were mixed with the internal standard (5 µL/mL), triphenylmethane, dissolved in EtOAc. The solution was incubated for a period of 1 hr in a cold and dark place. Subsequently, deionized distilled water (5 mL) was added, and the solution was subjected to vigorous stirring for 1 min. Then, MeCN (10 mL) and n-hexane (5 mL) were mixed and the solution was thoroughly agitated. A mixture of MgSO4 and NaCl (4 and 1 g, respectively) was shaken and centrifuged at 4000g/−5 °C for 10 min. Following the initial preparation, 5 mL of the supernatant was transferred into a 15-mL falcon tube containing sorbents, specifically 0.4 g of C18, 0.2 g of Primary and Secondary Amine (PSA), and MgSO4 (1 g). The solution was stirred for 2 min and then, centrifuged at 4000g for 10 min at a temperature of −5 °C. Then, 4 mL of the supernatant was carefully transferred to a 5-mL flask. Next, 2 μL of the final solution was injected into the GC-MS system using MeCN and MgSO4 for analyte extraction. Here, PSA and C18 sorbents eliminated non-polar substances like lipids that could interfere with the analysis. To mitigate matrix effects, spiked samples were analyzed, determining pesticide concentrations by interpolating relative peak areas against the internal standard peak area, referencing the spiked calibration curve for accuracy [33], [34], [35].

Extraction procedure for determination of mycotoxins

A solution of EtOAc and formic acid at a ratio of 95:5 (v/v) was added to 5 g of each sample. Then, the samples were homogenized utilizing the IKA Ultra-Turrax T−50 homogenizer for a duration of 5 min, after which, the organic phase was extracted. Furthermore, the samples underwent a sequential extraction process with EtOAc and formic acid at a ratio of 20:5 (v/v), repeated three times to ensure thorough extraction. The supernatant was evaporated using a rotary evaporator (Buchi V−850, Switzerland) at a temperature of 35 °C and under a pressure of 120 mmHg. Subsequently, the extracts were dissolved in 10 mL of a solvent mixture comprising EtOAc and formic acid, and freeze-dried using Operon FDB−5503 (Korea). The residue was dissolved in 1 mL of a MeOH-water solution (50:50 v/v) and agitated for 2 min to ensure thorough mixing. In order to eliminate any impurities, the resulting mixture was subjected to filtration through a nylon membrane filter with a pore size of 0.22 µm. The purified extracts were then preserved for subsequent analysis via LC-MS/MS-IT [36], [37].

Analytical performance

Metals levels determination: analytical performance

A multi-element standard solution was prepared at 1000 mg/L for plotting calibration curves in quantitative metal analysis. The stock solution was diluted with 0.2% HNO3 to reach the required concentration. Recovery rates were determined by preparing spiked samples in triplicate and following established sample preparation protocol. Recoveries were calculated using calibration curves from the spiked samples, ensuring the precision and accuracy of the quantitative analysis [6].

Pesticides levels determination: analytical performance

Triphenylmethane internal standard solution (1 mg/mL) was prepared in EtOAc, and stored in dark vials at −20 °C. For recovery assessments, a pesticide standard mixture solution (10 µg/mL) was also prepared in EtOAc. Each pesticide/pesticide mixture was individually injected into GC-MS. Calibration curves were plotted by calculating the ratio of the peak area of standards to the internal standard peak area, against concentration. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 3, referencing background noise from blank samples; the limit of quantification (LOQ) was calculated based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 10, using matrix-matched standards regardless of matrices [38].

Mycotoxins levels determination: analytical performance

The post-spiked samples were diluted to align with the standard curve. If recoveries vary, an appropriate approach must be applied to reduce drift [39]. According to Reichert et al., the mycotoxins under examination were systematically categorized into two distinct groups. Group I included AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, AFG2, and OTA, which were spiked at concentrations of 0.005, 0.01, and 0.02 mg/kg. Group 2 comprised DON, ZON, PAT, fumonisin B1 (FB1), and fumonisin B2 (FB2), with spiking concentrations of 0.25, 0.50, and 1.00 mg/kg. This categorization facilitates a more precise analysis of mycotoxins [39]. In order to validate the method, over the course of two distinct days, the replicates (n = 4) at the specified spiked levels were analyzed. Moreover, the analytes were evaluated within both non-spiked and blank samples. The LOD was established as one-third of the LOQ. The LOQ for each mycotoxin was recognized at the lowest validated concentration. The mean of the spiked assays was used to calculate the recovery rate [39], [40].

Tolerable Daily Intake (TDI) scenario

The % Tolerable Daily Intake (TDI) was calculated using Eq. 5 to estimate risks posed by oral exposure to mycotoxins in the analyzed chips samples [61]. %TDI< 100 indicates a tolerable exposure level and %TDI> 100 indicates an intolerable level [25], [62].(5)%TDI=EDITDI×100

Based on the IARC and the World Health Organization (WHO), the TDI for AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2 is 0.001 µg/kg bw, for OTA, DON, ZON, and PAT is respectively 0.016, 1.00, 0.25, and 0.40 µg/kg bw, and for FB1 and FB2 is 2.00 µg/kg bw [26], [63].

Cancer risk scenario

Previous studies have demonstrated that AFs possess the capacity to induce mutagenicity, hepatotoxicity, immunotoxicity, and carcinogenicity [64], [65]. Consequently, there exists considerable concern regarding the potential of AFB1 to synergistically enhance the risk of malignancies. Observational studies have indicated the possibility of a synergistic interaction between AFB1 and the Hepatitis B Virus (HBV), which may exacerbate the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [66]. The Hepatitis B Virus, a member of the Hepadnaviridae family, has attained a significant prevalence, infecting nearly 2 billion individuals worldwide. HBV is recognized as a contributing factor to HCC, as evidenced by the observation that approximately 75% of HCC patients in China and Africa are also infected with HBV [67].

The assessment of cancer risk was conducted exclusively for AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2 which have been classified by the IARC as Group 1 carcinogens, indicating their definitive carcinogenicity to humans [68] (Eq. 6).(6)Cancerrisk=EDI×CPS

CPS: The carcinogenic potency slope for ingestion: 0.3 and 0.01 cancers per year per 105 population per ng of AFs (AFB1 + AFB2 + AFG1 + AFG2) per kg bw per day, respectively for people with a positive surface antigen of hepatitis B (HBsAg+) and for people with a negative surface antigen of hepatitis B (HBsAg-), respectively [69].

Monte Carlo simulations

Monte Carlo simulations (MCS) utilize random samples from probability distributions [70]. This study used MCS to analyze non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic scenarios, estimating risk for adolescents and children exposed to metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins from potato chips consumption, generating confidence intervals across 10,000 iterations using JMP 9 [71].

Statistical analysis

To assess the statistical differences among mean values, the JMP 9 software (Campus Drive, Cary, NC 27513) was employed. Determination of significant differences among mean values was conducted using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method at a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Analytical performance

Analytical performance parameters for metals determination

Recoveries were determined at three concentrations (0.01, 0.05, and 0.20 mg/kg). Mean recoveries were in the range of 77.45–99.38%, with an associated relative standard deviation (RSD) of ≤ 7.85%. Coefficients of determination (R2) exhibited significant linear relationships (98.0–99.5%) for all the calibration curves. LODs and LOQs were similar for all metals (0.001 and 0.003 mg/kg, respectively) with the exception of Ni (0.005 and 0.015 mg/kg, respectively) (Table S1).

Analytical performance parameters for pesticides determination

Recoveries were assessed at three distinct concentrations: 0.01, 0.03, and 0.10 mg/kg. The mean recoveries ranged from 77.10% to 99.12%, accompanied by RSD of less than 7.45%. Furthermore, the R² demonstrated robust linear relationships, varying between 98.0% and 99.3% across all calibration curves. The values of LODs and LOQs are detailed in Table S2.

Analytical performance parameters for mycotoxins determination

The recoveries were evaluated at three distinct concentrations for each category of mycotoxins. The average recoveries were determined to range from 77.14% to 99.43%, with RSD not exceeding 7.60%. Moreover, the R² indicated strong linear correlations, varying between 98.3% and 99.4% across all calibration curves. The LODs and LOQs of mycotoxins analysis are shown in Table S3.

Discussion

The present study assessed the risk of oral exposure to metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins occurring in potato chip samples collected from market in Iran, under carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic scenarios for Iranian adults and children. The risk assessment for metal exposure indicated that, in the context of the non-carcinogenic scenario, the HI values for adults remained below one at three distinct centiles, thereby suggesting no significant health risk. In contrast, the HI values for children at these centiles exceeded one, presenting a moderate risk. Under carcinogenic risk assessment for adults, the mean ILCRs associated with As and Cd exposure, at the three centiles, exceeded 10−4, reflecting potential regulatory concern under conservative assumptions. Conversely, the mean ILCRs for Ni fell within the range of 10−6 to 10−4, which is deemed acceptable and for Pb, the mean ILCRs at these centiles remained below 10−6, showing a within-safe-range cancer risk. In the case of children, the mean ILCRs for As, Cd, and Ni, at these centiles, exceeded 10−4, reflecting potential regulatory concern under conservative assumptions and for Pb, the mean ILCRs were below 10−6. For adults and children, HI values for pesticides at the 50th, 80th, and 90th centiles reflected a moderate risk.

The Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS) approach offers significant advantages in healthcare risk assessment by addressing uncertainty and variability in human exposure to different chemicals. Instead of single-point estimates, MCS uses probability distributions for input parameters and considers thousands of iterations. On the other hand, conventional deterministic risk assessment relies on average estimates that ignore variability and uncertainty in exposure levels. Conversely, MCS approach accounts for uncertainty in risk modeling, identifies potential health risk ranges, and enables sensitivity analyses to uncover influential variables [72], [73], [74].

Chung et al. reported that the mean level of As in the chips samples collected from China was 25 µg/kg [75]. Yaghi et al. reported the occurrence of Cd, Cr, and Pb at the respective mean values of 0.257, 0.260, and 1.971 mg/kg and the maximum values of 0.421, 0.716, and 2.900 mg/kg in chips samples collected from Libya [76]. Besides, in a study from Turkey, mean values of Cd and Cr were 0.012 and 0.392 mg/kg [77]. A study published in 2025 using atomic absorption spectrophotometer, showed the absence of Pb, As, Cr, or Cd (i.e. none of these metals were detected) in potato chips samples collected from 20 random brands from Bangladesh local market [78]. Moreover, in potato chips samples collected from Quetta, Pakistan, manganese and nickel were within the FAO/WHO tolerable range as analyzed by atomic absorption spectrometery and researchers concluded that the analyzed samples were safe to ingest [79]. Also, Gopalani et al. employed atomic emission spectrometer with Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) to analyze potato chips sampled from Nagpur City, India for heavy metals; results showed that Cd concentration was below detectable levels in all samples and Co>Cr>Pb showed the least levels among the analyzed metals. Importantly, this study also showed significant variations in recovery results among various wet digestions [80].

A study from Iran on chips samples reported the insecticide diazinon occurrence at a mean level of 0.130 µg/g (130 µg/kg) while chlorpyrifos was below LOD [81]. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the European Union’s maximum residue limit (MRL) for diazinon in potatoes and many other crops is 0.01 mg/kg (10 µg/kg). Given that both diazinon and chlorpyrifos have been widely used in Iranian potato farming for pest control, their presence in finished food is a point of concern [82]. Of note, in our study, organophosphate residues were either absent or barely quantifiable. Chronic dietary exposure to organophosphates, even at low doses, has been associated with neurodevelopmental issues in children and endocrine effects in adults, further emphasizing the importance of evaluation of long-term exposure to low doses of contaminants [83], [84], [85]. A survey on potato chips samples from the supermarkets in Nara, Japan reported that the levels of sprout inhibitors maleic hydrazide and chlorpropham were far below USEPA legislated limits [86]. More importantly, to safeguard consumers, especially the young, we recommend strengthening both monitoring and preventive measures. Regulatory agencies should implement regular surveillance programs for metals, mycotoxins, and pesticides residues in snack foods and staple products, not just in raw agricultural commodities. On the preventive side, proper agricultural practices must be promoted; for instance, selecting potato cultivation areas with low background metal content, using organic or low-metal fertilizers, and avoiding irrigation using water contaminated with industrial effluents can reduce metal uptake by crops. Similarly, integrated pest management strategies can minimize the need for chemical pesticides, and any necessary pesticide use should be in accordance with safety guidelines to prevent occurrence of residues at excessive levels. In manufacturing, adhering to proper hygiene and quality control such as using clean raw materials, maintaining equipment, and controlling storage humidity, will help prevent fungal contamination and toxin production.

In the current study, under carcinogenic scenario, the risk associated with oral exposure to AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2 - considering both the presence of the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg+) and its absence (HBsAg-)- was substantially higher for children than for adults. A previous study was designed to examine the levels of AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, AFG2, as well as the total aflatoxin concentrations in 27 samples of diverse packaged salty snacks (potato chips, corn chips, and snack) which were randomly acquired from various markets in Istanbul, Turkey; the findings indicated elevated levels of aflatoxin in the final products [87]. Another study investigated mycotoxins in 15 potato chips samples from Iraq and showed that the levels of all mycotoxins (aflatoxin, OTA, and ZON) in samples were within safe limits for consumption [88]. Moreover, a study aimed to assess the occurrence of AFs and FBs in sun-dried sweet potato chips from Tanzania. Eighty samples were analyzed for AFB1, AFB2, FB1, and FB2 contamination. Results indicated that contamination levels exceeded acceptable limits set by East African standards and the Codex Alimentarius Commission, a joint FAO/WHO initiative. Improving post-harvest methods, particularly in handling and processing, is crucial to reduce the risk of AFs and FBs contamination in potato chips [89]. Also, Iqbal and Razis investigated AFs levels in 480 commercial and 365 ready-to-eat sweet potato samples from Pakistan, to quantify contamination levels and assess dietary exposure. The dietary intake assessment indicated that female subjects aged 21–30 years had the highest exposure (3.16 µg/kg bw/day) to AFs contamination in sweet potatoes. This highlights the significant contamination issue and the necessity for enhanced storage methods to minimize exposure [90].

Overall, the current risk assessment indicates that oral exposure of Iranian consumers to metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins through ingestion of the analyzed potato chips samples, falls mostly within tolerable levels. In chemicals exposure risk assessments, bio-persistence and bio-accumulation should be emphasized; for instance, Pb and Cd exposure -due to accumulation and irreversible impact on the human body even in trace amounts- is of great toxicological importance. Mycotoxins, while not present at high levels in our samples, occur due to various reasons such as inadequate storage practice. Pesticides which were present at low levels in our sample, serve to highlight the interface between crop production and product quality. We suggest that in future studies, similar risk assessments be conducted across a higher number of brands, and following sampling from a wider geographical distribution of chips-producing industries to cover wider potato cultivation zones. We envision that the findings of this multi-contaminant screening and risk assessment have direct practical applications. For regulatory authorities, the presented data provides a scientific baseline to update food safety standards and establish specific maximum permissible limits for multiple chemical residues in processed snack foods including potato chips. For the food industry, these results can potentially pinpoint hot-spots that can create critical risk in the manufacturing pipeline; specifically, the prevalence of heavy metals underscores the need for stricter raw material sourcing and agricultural soil validation, while mycotoxin presence highlights a need for optimized post-harvest storage parameters to prevent fungal growth. Finally, for public health policymakers, the bold contrast between adult and pediatric risk thresholds serves as an empirical foundation for developing targeted dietary advisories and nutritional interventions aimed at mitigating chemical exposure in vulnerable pediatric cohorts.

Conclusion

This study provided a comprehensive screening of multi-class chemical contaminants (metals, pesticides, and mycotoxins) in commercial potato chips from Iran, coupled with a risk assessment for both pediatric and adult populations. Our assessment of metals exposure showed that under non-carcinogenic scenario, adult HIs were below one (no risk) while children's HIs indicated moderate risk. In adults, mean ILCRs for As and Cd exceeded 10−4, reflecting potential regulatory concern under conservative assumptions, for Ni, fell between 10−6 and 10−4 (i.e. acceptable range) and for Pb were below 10−6 (i.e. within safe cancer risk range). In children, mean ILCRs for As, Cd, and Ni surpassed 10−4, reflecting potential regulatory concern under conservative assumptions, and for Pb were similar to those noted for adults. TDIs for mycotoxins oral exposure at the 50th, 80th, and 90th centiles for adults and children exceeded one. Furthermore, cumulative cancer risk associated with AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2 exposure, taking into consideration the presence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg+) and its absence (HBsAg-), for children surpassed those for adults. Certain challenges still obstruct the optimal accuracy of the risk assessment, primarily due to data gaps in local food consumption rate and the inherent socioeconomic variations within the studied demographic groups. These can be alleviated through more frequent, large-scale dietary surveys. Overall, the findings of this study emphasize the importance of prioritizing the evaluation of dietary risks based on multiple contaminants screening as single contaminant screening drastically underestimates real-world exposure levels. Our findings highlight the need for continuous monitoring and stricter regulatory thresholds for processed snack foods to safeguard public health, particularly for vulnerable populations.
