Monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio and cardiovascular comorbidity in COPD
Authors: Chengying Zhu, Changjiao Luan, Yuhui Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is frequently complicated by cardiovascular disease (CVD), which significantly increases morbidity and mortality. The monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (MHR) has emerged as a novel inflammatory biomarker associated with various cardiovascular conditions. However, its relationship with CVD in COPD patients remains understudied. This study aimed to investigate the association between MHR and coexisting CVD in patients with COPD. This cross-sectional investigation analyzed the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data from 2007 to 2018, focusing on adults aged ≥40 years with physician-diagnosed COPD. The primary outcome encompassed cardiovascular conditions including congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, or myocardial infarction. MHR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and in quartiles. Four sequential logistic regression models were applied: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for sex, age, and race/ethnicity; Model 3 further adjusted for education, marital status, smoking, and poverty-income ratio; Model 4 additionally adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, and body mass index. Dose–response relationships were assessed using restricted cubic splines. A total of 1064 COPD patients were included (mean age, 62.8 ± 10.5 years; 56.6% female), of whom 206 (19.4%) had coexisting CVD. Compared with patients without CVD, those with CVD were older (median, 68 vs 61 years; P < .001), more often male (62.1% vs 38.9%; P < .001), and had a higher prevalence of diabetes (43.2% vs 24.4%; P < .001) and hypertension (82.5% vs 54.6%; P < .001). MHR levels were higher in the CVD group than in the non-CVD group (0.542 vs 0.423; P < .001). In the fully adjusted model, each 1-unit increase in MHR was associated with higher odds of CVD (odds ratio, 3.27; 95% confidence interval, 1.59–6.74; P < .001). A graded association was observed across MHR quartiles (P for trend < .001), with the highest quartile showing greater odds of CVD than the lowest (odds ratio, 2.70; 95% confidence interval, 1.57–4.73; P < .001). Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a nonlinear positive association between MHR and CVD. In COPD patients, higher MHR was independently associated with a greater prevalence of coexisting CVD, with a graded and nonlinear pattern. Given the cross-sectional design, causal inference is not warranted, and prospective studies are needed.

1. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide,[1] characterized by long-term respiratory symptoms and progressive airflow limitation.[2] Although primarily considered a lung disease, COPD is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder with multiple extrapulmonary manifestations. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is one of the most common and serious comorbidities in COPD patients, significantly contributing to mortality in this population.[3]

Chronic systemic inflammation is a central link between COPD and CVD. Persistent airway inflammation in COPD drives systemic monocyte activation and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators,[4] while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) provides cardiovascular protection through reverse cholesterol transport and anti-inflammatory effects.[5] COPD patients commonly show both elevated monocyte counts and reduced HDL-C levels, producing an inflammatory–lipid imbalance that may amplify cardiovascular risk.

The monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR) integrates these 2 opposing processes in a single measure derived from routine complete blood count and lipid testing, requiring no additional laboratory cost.[6] Elevated MHR has been associated with adverse outcomes in acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, and stroke populations,[7] and may offer better risk discrimination than monocyte count or HDL-C alone.[8]

Despite this rationale, evidence on MHR in COPD patients is limited. Most prior MHR studies have been conducted in general populations or in patients with a single CVD subtype, and large-scale data from a high-risk comorbid population such as COPD are lacking. Whether MHR is independently associated with the broad spectrum of cardiovascular comorbidities seen in COPD, and whether the association follows a linear or threshold pattern, has not been systematically evaluated using nationally representative data.

Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007 to 2018, this study examined the association between MHR and coexisting CVD in adults with COPD. We further explored dose–response relationships through quartile and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses and assessed consistency across demographic and clinical subgroups. Findings may inform the use of accessible biomarkers for cardiovascular risk stratification in COPD.

2. Methods

2.1. Research participants

Data were derived from NHANES 2007 to 2018, which provides nationally representative health information for the US civilian noninstitutionalized population through multistage probability sampling.[9] The NHANES protocol received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Study outcomes

From an initial pool of 59,842 participants across the 6 survey cycles, we applied systematic exclusion criteria to establish our analytical cohort. Participants were excluded if they were younger than 40 years of age (as COPD prevalence is significantly lower in younger adults and current clinical guidelines recommend COPD screening primarily for individuals aged ≥40 years with risk factors), lacked a confirmed COPD diagnosis, had missing laboratory data for monocyte counts or HDL-C levels, had incomplete CVD status information, or had missing data on essential covariates. Our final analytical sample comprised 1064 adults aged 40 years and older who met all inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Additional information regarding NHANES methodology is available at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes).

Flowchart of participant selection. Of the 59,842 participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007 to 2018, individuals were sequentially excluded for age younger than 40 years (n = 37,126), absence of a COPD diagnosis (n = 20,458), missing cardiovascular disease data (n = 384), missing monocyte or HDL-C data (n = 289), and missing demographic (n = 312) or clinical (n = 209) covariates, yielding a final analytical sample of 1064 participants (COPD without CVD, n = 858; COPD with CVD, n = 206). COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, CVD = cardiovascular disease, HDL-C = high-density lipoprotein cholesterol.

The primary outcome was the presence of CVD, defined as a self-reported, physician-diagnosed history of any of the following 4 conditions: congestive heart failure (NHANES variable MCQ160B), coronary heart disease (MCQ160C), angina pectoris (MCQ160D), or myocardial infarction (MCQ160E). For each condition, participants were asked: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had [condition]?” Participants who answered “yes” to any of the 4 questions were classified as having CVD; those who answered “no” to all 4 were classified as not having CVD. Responses of “don’t know,” “refused,” or missing for any of the 4 items were treated as missing, and these participants were excluded from the analysis. CVD status was analyzed as a binary outcome (presence vs absence). These 4 conditions were selected because they represent the major atherosclerotic and ischemic cardiovascular events most strongly linked to systemic inflammation and most clinically relevant in COPD populations.

2.3. Research variables

COPD identification was based on participant self-report of physician-diagnosed chronic bronchitis and/or emphysema, assessed through questionnaire items MCQ160G (emphysema) and MCQ160K (chronic bronchitis). Participants were asked: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had emphysema/chronic bronchitis?” Only participants answering “yes” to either question were classified as having COPD. Participants answering “no” to both questions, “don’t know,” “refused,” or with missing responses were excluded as lacking a confirmed physician diagnosis.[10]

We excluded participants reporting any malignancy (MCQ220); recent infectious illness, including influenza, pneumonia, or ear infections, within 30 days (HSQ520); and gastrointestinal illness with vomiting or diarrhea within 30 days (HSQ510). Participants with incomplete information on key study variables were also excluded from the analysis.

MHR served as our primary exposure variable, calculated as the ratio of absolute monocyte count (×103 cells/μL) to HDL-C concentration (mmol/L). Monocyte counts were obtained through automated complete blood count analysis, while HDL-C levels were measured using enzymatic methods following standardized laboratory protocols.

Demographic variables included sex (male/female), age (continuous), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, Other Hispanic, Other race), educational attainment (<9th grade, 9th–11th grade, high school graduate, some college/associate degree, college graduate or higher), marital status (married, living with partner, divorced, separated, widowed, never married), current smoking status (yes/no), and poverty-income ratio (PIR, continuous scale 0–5). These data were collected through standardized questionnaires.

Clinical measurements included body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings. Laboratory parameters encompassed HDL-C (mmol/L) and the calculated MHR value. Hypertension was defined as self-reported physician diagnosis or current use of antihypertensive medications. Diabetes mellitus was identified through self-reported physician diagnosis or current use of antidiabetic medications.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are summarized using appropriate descriptive measures: frequencies and percentages for categorical data and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous data. Group comparisons employed Pearson’s chi-square tests for categorical outcomes and 2-sample t tests for continuous measurements. MHR was analyzed both as a continuous variable and stratified into quartiles based on the distribution in our sample: Q1 (0.054–0.315), Q2 (0.316–0.439), Q3 (0.440–0.602), and Q4 (0.606–1.525), with Q1 serving as the reference category.

Associations between MHR and CVD were evaluated using a hierarchical, sequential multivariable logistic regression strategy with progressively expanded adjustment for potential confounders. Covariates were selected a priori based on biological plausibility and prior literature on MHR and cardiovascular risk, and were grouped into 3 blocks (demographic, socioeconomic/lifestyle, and clinical) added in a stepwise manner. Model 1 (crude) included MHR as the only predictor, with no adjustment. Model 2 additionally adjusted for demographic variables: sex (male/female), age (continuous, years), and race/ethnicity (Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, Other Hispanic, Other race). Model 3 further adjusted for socioeconomic and lifestyle variables: education level (<9th grade, 9th–11th grade, high school graduate, some college/associate degree, college graduate or higher), marital status (married, living with partner, divorced, separated, widowed, never married), current smoking status (yes/no), and PIR (continuous). Model 4 (fully adjusted) further included clinical comorbidities relevant to cardiovascular risk: hypertension (yes/no), diabetes mellitus (yes/no), and BMI (continuous, kg/m2). The same set of models was applied with MHR as a continuous variable and as quartiles (Q1 as reference). Subgroup analyses examined effect modification across demographic and clinical strata, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, smoking status, PIR, and BMI categories, with interaction terms (MHR × stratification variable) entered into fully adjusted models and tested using likelihood ratio tests (P < .05). Potential nonlinear exposure-response relationships were investigated using RCS methodology with knot placement at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the MHR distribution.[11] The presence of nonlinearity was assessed by jointly testing the statistical significance of the second and third spline transformation coefficients. To assess the stability of model estimates, several diagnostic checks were performed. Multicollinearity among covariates in the fully adjusted model was evaluated using variance inflation factors, all of which were below 2, indicating no concerning collinearity. Model fit was compared across the 4 sequential models using the Akaike information criterion, with progressively lower Akaike information criterion values supporting improved fit with adjustment. The robustness of the MHR–CVD association was further supported by the consistency of estimates across 3 complementary modeling approaches – MHR as a continuous variable, in quartiles, and modeled flexibly using RCSs – and by the stable direction and significance of estimates across the 4 sequentially adjusted models. Only participants with complete data on all study variables were included (complete-case analysis). All statistical computations were performed using the R statistical computing environment (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with appropriate analytical packages. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all tests.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study population

The analytical cohort comprised 1064 participants, including 462 (43.4%) males and 602 (56.6%) females. CVD was present in 206 (19.4%) participants, while 858 (80.6%) had COPD without cardiovascular complications. The baseline characteristics of all participants by CVD status are shown in Table 1, which revealed notable differences in age, sex, marital status, diabetes, hypertension, and MHR. Individuals with CVD demonstrated advanced age (median, 68 vs 61 years; P < .001), male predominance (62.1% vs 38.9%; P < .001), and elevated prevalence of diabetes mellitus (43.2% vs 24.4%; P < .001) and hypertension (82.5% vs 54.6%; P < .001). Importantly, MHR levels were markedly higher in the CVD group compared with the non-CVD group (0.542 vs 0.423), representing a 28% relative increase. No notable differences were observed in BMI, race/ethnicity, education level, PIR, or smoking status between the 2 groups.

3.2. Association between MHR and CVD

Logistic regression analysis showed a positive association between MHR and CVD in COPD patients (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), each 1-unit increase in MHR was associated with higher odds of CVD (odds ratio [OR], 3.27; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.59–6.74). The association remained statistically significant across all 4 sequentially adjusted models, with the magnitude attenuated but the direction preserved after progressive adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates (Table 2).

3.3. The nonlinear relationship between MHR and CVD

To further assess whether the association was linear or showed a threshold pattern, RCS modeling was performed. After adjustment for all confounders, the RCS model showed a nonlinear association between MHR and CVD (Fig. 2). The association was modest at lower MHR levels and increased more steeply at higher MHR concentrations, with an inflection appearing around MHR 0.4 to 0.5.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

The positive association between MHR and CVD was observed across most subgroups (Fig. 3). Estimates were similar in males (OR, 3.12; 95% CI, 1.41–6.89) and females (OR, 3.45; 95% CI, 1.28–9.32), and in participants aged 40 to 59 years (OR, 2.84; 95% CI, 1.32–6.13) and ≥60 years (OR, 3.85; 95% CI, 1.64–9.05). Estimates were also positive among normal-weight participants (OR, 4.23; 95% CI, 1.45–12.34), non-Hispanic Whites (OR, 3.76; 95% CI, 1.65–8.58), and non-Hispanic Blacks (OR, 2.89; 95% CI, 1.12–7.45). Several subgroups had wide CIs reflecting smaller sample sizes, and the corresponding point estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Formal interaction testing revealed no evidence of effect modification by sex, age, race/ethnicity, education level, BMI, smoking status, or hypertension (all P for interaction > .05).

4. Discussion

4.1. Principal findings

In this cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2007 to 2018, higher MHR was associated with greater odds of coexisting CVD in adults with COPD, with the association persisting after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates. The association showed a graded pattern across MHR quartiles and a nonlinear shape, with a steeper increase in odds at MHR levels above approximately 0.4 to 0.5.

4.2. Comparison with previous studies

Several earlier studies have examined MHR in cardiovascular populations, but few have focused on patients with COPD. In acute coronary syndrome, MHR has been correlated with disease severity and short-term outcomes,[12] and in heart failure cohorts, higher MHR has been linked to worse functional class and mortality.[13] Similar associations have also been described in ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack populations,[14] and in coronary heart disease cohorts.[15] The most directly relevant prior work is a single-center study by Yakar and Kanbay, who compared 185 COPD patients with 89 controls and reported that MHR was significantly higher in COPD patients with CVD than in those without CVD (16.4 ± 4.2 vs 12.7 ± 4.9, P = .007), supporting MHR as a candidate marker of cardiovascular comorbidity in COPD.[16] Our analysis extends that observation to a large, nationally representative sample and characterizes the dose–response shape using both quartile and spline approaches. Beyond MHR specifically, recent reviews continue to emphasize that systemic inflammation and lipid abnormalities are central to the elevated cardiovascular risk seen in COPD,[17] which is consistent with the direction of our findings. Our point estimate (Model 4 OR, 3.27 per unit) is numerically larger than the per-unit or per-standard deviation estimates reported in general or single-disease cardiac populations. Two factors likely contribute to this difference. First, COPD patients have a higher background burden of systemic inflammation and HDL dysfunction than the populations studied previously, which may amplify the apparent effect of MHR. Second, our outcome was a composite of 4 self-reported cardiovascular conditions rather than a single endpoint, broadening the events captured. The direction of the association is therefore consistent with prior work, while its magnitude in this comorbid population should be confirmed in independent COPD cohorts.

4.3. Mechanistic considerations

MHR captures 2 opposing processes that are both perturbed in COPD: monocyte-driven systemic inflammation on the one hand, and HDL-C–mediated reverse cholesterol transport and antioxidant activity on the other.[18] Higher MHR thus reflects a shift toward a pro-inflammatory, pro-atherogenic state. Several lines of evidence support this interpretation. In COPD, persistent airway and systemic inflammation has been shown to alter HDL composition and impair reverse cholesterol transport via reduced ABCA1- and ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux from monocyte-derived macrophages.[19] More broadly, under sustained inflammatory and oxidative conditions, HDL particles can lose their normal antiatherogenic properties and acquire pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidant features, so that the absolute HDL-C concentration no longer fully reflects HDL functionality.[20] In this context, MHR may capture functional inflammatory–lipid imbalance better than monocyte count or HDL-C alone, particularly in chronic inflammatory diseases such as COPD. The nonlinear pattern observed in the spline model, with a steeper rise in odds above MHR 0.4 to 0.5, is consistent with the idea that modest inflammation may be partially offset by residual HDL function, while at higher MHR levels this compensation is exceeded. We emphasize that this interpretation is mechanistic speculation rather than a clinically actionable threshold.

4.4. Subgroup findings

In subgroup analyses, the direction of the MHR–CVD association was consistent across strata of sex, age, race/ethnicity, smoking status, and hypertension, and no formal interaction was statistically significant. However, the precision of subgroup estimates varied considerably. Among normal-weight participants, for example, the OR was numerically higher (OR, 4.23) but the 95% CI was wide (1.45–12.34), reflecting the small number of CVD events in that subgroup. Similar imprecision was seen in some race/ethnicity strata. Differences in point estimates across subgroups should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of effect modification, and any apparent heterogeneity is more likely to reflect limited statistical power than a true biological difference. Adequately powered analyses in larger COPD cohorts will be needed to determine whether the strength of the MHR–CVD association truly varies by BMI, race/ethnicity, or other factors.

4.5. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow inference about temporal sequence or causality. Because MHR and CVD were measured at the same time, we cannot determine whether elevated MHR contributes to cardiovascular pathology or whether established CVD and its treatments (e.g., chronic inflammation related to atherosclerosis or pharmacotherapy affecting lipid profile and monocyte counts) influence the measured ratio. Reverse causation is therefore a real possibility, and the cross-sectional OR should be read as a measure of association rather than risk.

Second, residual confounding is likely. NHANES does not include several variables that plausibly affect both MHR and CVD risk, including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and other circulating inflammatory markers, statin and corticosteroid use, Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease stage and lung function, and habitual physical activity. Statin use, in particular, could bias estimates in either direction, since statins reduce monocyte activity and modestly raise HDL-C while being preferentially prescribed to patients at higher cardiovascular risk; their broader anti-inflammatory effects on circulating monocytes and HDL function are well documented.[21] Although our fully adjusted model accounts for the major demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders available in NHANES, unmeasured confounders may still explain part of the observed association.

Third, both COPD and CVD relied on self-reported physician diagnoses, raising the possibility of misclassification. Prior work using NHANES has shown reasonable agreement between self-reported chronic conditions and medical records, although agreement is generally better for cardiovascular diagnoses than for obstructive lung disease.[22] Any non-differential misclassification would tend to bias results toward the null, but undiagnosed COPD or CVD cases in the comparison group cannot be excluded. Spirometry was unavailable, so COPD severity could not be characterized; spirometry-confirmed Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease staging is recognized as essential for accurate COPD assessment.[23]

Fourth, MHR was measured at a single time point; we could not assess whether short-term variation, for example, during a COPD exacerbation, affected the observed association.

5. Conclusion

In this cross-sectional analysis of 1064 adults with COPD from NHANES 2007 to 2018, higher MHR was independently associated with a greater prevalence of coexisting CVD, and the association persisted after sequential adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates. The relationship showed a graded pattern across MHR quartiles and a nonlinear shape on RCS modeling, with a steeper rise in odds at MHR levels above approximately 0.4 to 0.5. Subgroup analyses showed a consistent direction of association across strata of sex, age, race/ethnicity, smoking status, and hypertension, although precision was limited in some subgroups. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that the imbalance between monocyte-driven systemic inflammation and HDL-mediated anti-inflammatory and reverse cholesterol transport activity is involved in the high cardiovascular comorbidity burden of COPD. Given the cross-sectional design, our results describe an association rather than a predictive or causal relationship and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Prospective cohort studies in COPD populations, with longitudinal MHR measurements, objective COPD severity assessment, and incident cardiovascular outcomes, are needed before any conclusions can be drawn about the role of MHR in cardiovascular risk evaluation in this population.
