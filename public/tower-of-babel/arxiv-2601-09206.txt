Discretization of the Mikhailov model
Song-lin Zhao, Xiao-gang Mu, Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
In this paper the Mikhailov model is discretized by means of the Cauchy matrix approach. A pair of discrete Miura transformations are constructed. The discrete Mikhailov model is a coupled system, in which one equation comes from the compatibility of the two Miura transformations, the other is transformed from the discrete negative order Ablowitz-Kaup-Newell-Segur system by using the Miura transformations. Explicit solutions, including solitons and multiple-pole solutions, are presented via two Cauchy matrix schemes respectively, namely, the Ablowitz-Kaup-Newell-Segur type and the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili type. By straight continuum limits, semi-discrete and continuous Mikhailov models together with their Cauchy matrix structures and solutions are recovered. Keywords: Mikhailov model, Cauchy matrix, discretization, Miura transformation, solution Mathematics Subject Classification: 37K60

Introduction

The Mikhailov model is a relativistically invariant coupled system
(
[
9
]
, eq.(4.21))

\displaystyle u_{xt}+u-2\mathrm{i}uvu_{x}=0,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle v_{xt}+v+2\mathrm{i}uvv_{x}=0,

(1.1b)

where
\mathrm{i}
is the imaginary unit.
It was found by Mikhailov (cf.
[
9
]
) in the research of the
massive Thirring model (in light-cone coordinates)
[
24
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\chi_{1,x}-\frac{m}{2}\chi_{2}-g|\chi_{2}|^{2}\chi_{1}=0,

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\chi_{2,t}-\frac{m}{2}\chi_{1}-g|\chi_{1}|^{2}\chi_{2}=0,

(1.2b)

where
|\chi_{i}|^{2}=\chi_{i}\chi_{i}^{*}
and
*
stands for complex conjugate.
This model describes interaction of two chiral components of a two-dimensional fermion field

\chi=(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})
, where

m
is the mass parameter of the fermion and
g
is the parameter for the coupling constant
controlling the self-interaction.
In fact, the Mikhailov model (
1.1
) allows a reduction (via
v=u^{*}
)

u_{xt}+u-2\mathrm{i}|u|^{2}u_{x}=0,

(1.3)

which provides a solution to (
1.2
) (for
m=2,g=1
) by defining
[
9
,
13
]

\chi_{2}=u_{x}^{*}e^{-\mathrm{i}\beta},\quad\chi_{1}=-\mathrm{i}u^{*}e^{-\mathrm{i}\beta},\quad\beta=\int^{x}_{-\infty}|u_{x}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(1.4)

It is Mikhailov who first presented a Lax pair for the massive Thirring model
[
24
]
,
from which the Kaup-Newell (KN) spectral problem followed
[
13
,
14
]
.
It turns out that the Mikhailov model is the first equation in the negative order
potential KN hierarchy (see, e.g.
[
2
]
, eq.(2.13)),
and its Lax pair reads (e.g.
[
2
,
8
]
)

\displaystyle\bm{\Phi}_{x}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}\bm{\Phi},\quad\bm{\Phi}_{t}=\bm{\mathcal{V}}\bm{\Phi},

(1.5a)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{U}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\lambda^{2}&\lambda u_{x}\\
\lambda v_{x}&-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\lambda^{2}\end{array}\right),\quad\bm{\mathcal{V}}=\mathrm{i}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{-2}+uv&-\lambda^{-1}u\\
\lambda^{-1}v&-(\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{-2}+uv)\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{\Phi}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}
and
\lambda
is the spectral parameter.
It is also notable that the reduced Mikhailov model (
1.3
)
was rederived later
as a generalization of the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation
using its bi-Hamiltonian structure by Fokas and Lenells
[
7
,
18
]
,
as well as derived from a physics context of modeling propagation of nonlinear pulses in monomode optical fibers
by Lenells
[
16
]
.
Therefore equation (
1.3
) is also known as the Fokas-Lenells (FL) equation,
which is a reduction of the Mikhailov model (
1.3
),
and then can be studied from the later, e.g.
[
23
]
.

Recently, the Mikhailov model (
1.3
) was reformulated
from the Cauchy matrix structure of the self-dual Yang-Mills (SDYM) equations
[
19
]
.
The Cauchy matrix approach was first systematically introduced in
[
25
]

to construct solutions of discrete integrable equations of the
Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) type.
Later it was developed to more general cases in
[

29
]
,

,
21
]
.
It is not only a direct method to construct and study integrable systems,
but also a possible way to discretize integrable equations.
The key step in the discretization is to introduce proper discrete dispersion relations to replace the
continuous ones in the Cauchy matrix scheme of the original continuous integrable equations.
In such a way, for example, a negative order Ablowitz-Kaup-Newell-Segur (AKNS
(-1)
) system,
the modified Korteweg-de Vries-sine Gordon (mKdV-sG) equation
and
(2+1)
-dimensional Toda equation
have been discretized in
[
36
]
,
[
4
]

and
[
28
]
, respectively,

More recently, it was extended to the study of
q
-difference equations
[

]
.

The purpose of this paper is to discretize the Mikhailov model (
1.1
).
However, this will be much more complicated than the work for the AKNS

system in
[

]

In fact, in the Cauchy matrix formulation of the AKNS

For the Mikhailov model (

More than that, there is a Miura transformation (

]

to link the Mikhailov model and the AKNS

which comes from the Miura transformation between the

In the discretization of (

All these are the difficulties that we have to overcome in the discretization of (

In Ref.
[

]
, the Mikhailov model (

) was formulated

Note that solutions of the SDYM equations can be constructed from

These structures are helpful in the formulation of the solutions of the Mikhailov model (

It has been shown in

The paper is organized as follows.
In the next section, the discretization of the Mikhailov model

There is an appendix where we list the unreduced discrete NLS system,
and two unreduced discrete derivative NLS systems of the KN type

2
Discretization of the Mikhailov model

The Cauchy matrix approach is in principle starting from a Sylvester equation together with
properly defined plane wave vectors and a set of master functions.
In the following we will show how this approach works in the discretization of integrable equations
as well as in the study of integrable systems.

2.1
Cauchy matrix schemes: KP-type and AKNS-type

We start from the following Sylvester equation

\bm{K}\bm{M}-\bm{M}\bm{L}=\bm{r}\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}},

(2.1)

where
\bm{K},\bm{L}
are constant matrices in
\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
,

\bm{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}[n,m]
,

\bm{r},\bm{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 2}[n,m]
are matrix functions of
(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

\bm{r}
and
\bm{s}
are composed by plane wave vectors which will be specified later.
Introduce a set of functions

\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}\bm{L}^{j}(\bm{C}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r},~~~i,j\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.2)

where
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a matrix
which is chosen such that
\bm{C}+\bm{M}
is invertible.
Note that each
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
is a
2\times 2
matrix.
We call
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
master functions because they are the main objects that
are used to characterize integrable equations in the Cauchy matrix approach.
They are connected to each other by some algebraic relations when

\bm{K},\bm{L},\bm{C}
satisfy certain relation.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
11
,
21
]

For the master functions
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
defined in (
2.2
) with
\bm{M},\bm{K},\bm{L},\bm{r}
and
\bm{s}

satisfying the Sylvester equation (
2.1
),
the following recurrence relations hold:

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j+1)}=

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i+1,j)}-\bm{S}^{(0,j)}\bm{S}^{(i,0)},

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i,j-1)}=

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(i-1,j)}+\bm{S}^{(-1,j)}\bm{S}^{(i,-1)},

(2.3b)

provided that
\bm{K},\bm{L}
and
\bm{C}
satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{C}-\bm{C}\bm{L}=\bm{0}.

(2.4)

These recurrence relations will be used in our derivation.
The proof for (
2.3a
) can be found in Proposition 2 in
[
21
]

while (
2.3b
) was proved in Proposition 4 in
[
11
]
.

As we pointed out in
[
21
]
, the constraint (
2.4
) gives rise to
two Cauchy matrix schemes, namely, the KP-type and the AKNS-type. Let us elaborate them in the following.

•

KP-type: For given
\bm{K}
and
\bm{L}
, if we suppose they do not share any common eigenvalues,
it follows that (
2.4
) has a unique solution
\bm{C}=\bm{0}
(see
[
27
]
).
In this case,
\bm{M}
is also uniquely determined by the Sylvester equation (
2.1
)
for given
\bm{K},\bm{L},\bm{r}
and
\bm{s}
, and
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
are reformulated as

\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}\bm{L}^{j}\bm{M}^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r}.

(2.5)

This provides a Cauchy matrix scheme (which we call the KP-type) to characterize (matrix) KP system
(cf. Chapter 9.7 in
[
10
]

and
[
6
]
for the scalar case.)

•

AKNS-type: When
\bm{K}=\bm{L}
, it requires
\bm{K}
and
\bm{C}
commute. In this case,
\bm{C}
can be normalized to
be the identity matrix
\bm{I}
(see
[
21
]
)
and the uniqueness of solutions to the Sylvester equation (
2.1
)
can be fulfilled in the following way.
Let
N=2\mathcal{N}
be even and introduce block matrices
\bm{K}
,
\bm{M}
,
\bm{r}
,
\bm{s}
:

\displaystyle\bm{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}_{1}&\\
&\bm{K}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}&\bm{M}_{1}\\
\bm{M}_{2}&\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{r}_{1}&\\
&\bm{r}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{s}=\begin{pmatrix}&\bm{s}_{1}\\
\bm{s}_{2}&\end{pmatrix},

(2.6)

where for
\iota=1,2
,
\bm{K}_{\iota}
and
\bm{M}_{\iota}
are
\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
matrices,

\bm{r}_{\iota}
and
\bm{s}_{\iota}
are
\mathcal{N}
-th order column vectors.
With the above setting the Sylvester equation (
2.1
) with
\bm{L}=\bm{K}

can be decoupled as

\bm{K}_{1}\bm{M}_{1}-\bm{M}_{1}\bm{K}_{2}=\bm{r}_{1}\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}_{2},\quad\bm{K}_{2}\bm{M}_{2}-\bm{M}_{2}\bm{K}_{1}=\bm{r}_{2}\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}_{1}.

(2.7)

Thus,
\bm{M}_{1}
and
\bm{M}_{2}
can be uniquely determined respectively
if we assume
\bm{K}_{1}
and
\bm{K}_{2}
do not share eigenvalues
[
27
]
.
This gives rise to a Cauchy matrix scheme to formulate the AKNS system
[
37
]
.
In this case, the master functions
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
take a form

\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}\bm{K}^{j}(\bm{I}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r}.

(2.8)

We end the discussion of this subsection with the following remarks.

Remark 2.1
.

In the following investigation, we always assume
\bm{K}
and
\bm{L}
do not share any common eigenvalues in the
KP-type scheme and
\bm{K}_{1}
and
\bm{K}_{2}
do not share any common eigenvalues
in the AKNS scheme.
In the discretization implemented in the next subsection we still employ the generic Sylvester equation (
2.1
)
and assume it determines
\bm{M}
uniquely,
while we will back to the two special schemes in Sec.
3
when we present solutions
for the obtained discrete Mikhailov model.

Remark 2.2
.

We only need to consider the canonical forms of
\bm{K}
and
\bm{L}
because any matrices
that are similar to them lead to same
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
.
In fact, let

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{K}}=\bm{T}_{1}\bm{K}\bm{T}_{1}^{-1},\quad\bar{\bm{L}}=\bm{T}_{2}\bm{L}\bm{T}_{2}^{-1},

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{M}}=\bm{T}_{1}\bm{M}\bm{T}_{2}^{-1},\quad\bar{\bm{r}}=\bm{T}_{1}\bm{r},\quad\bar{\bm{s}}=(\bm{T}_{2}^{-1})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}~\bm{s},\quad\bar{\bm{C}}=\bm{T}_{1}\bm{C}\bm{T}_{2}^{-1},

(2.9b)

where
\bm{T}_{1}
and
\bm{T}_{2}
are transform matrices. It is easy to see that

\bar{\bm{K}}\bar{\bm{M}}-\bar{\bm{M}}\bar{\bm{L}}=\bar{\bm{r}}\bar{\bm{s}}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}

(2.10)

and

\bm{S}^{(i,j)}=\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}\bm{L}^{j}(\bm{C}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r}=\bar{\bm{s}}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}\bar{\bm{L}}^{j}(\bar{\bm{C}}+\bar{\bm{M}})^{-1}\bar{\bm{K}}^{i}\bar{\bm{r}},

(2.11)

which indicates that
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
are invariant in terms of similarity transformations of
\bm{K}
and
\bm{L}
.

2.2
Cauchy matrix approach to the discrete Mikhailov model

2.2.1
Dynamics of master functions

In the discrete context, functions are defined on discrete independent variables
n,m,\cdots
,
and derivatives are replaced by shifts with respect to these independent variables.
Before we proceed, let us recall some conventional notations used in discrete equations.
Suppose
f(n,m)
is a function (can be a matrix function) defined on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We denote

f:=f(n,m),~~\widetilde{f}:=f(n+1,m),~~\widehat{f}:=f(n,m+1),~~\widehat{\widetilde{f}}:=f(n+1,m+1).

(2.12)

To obtain a discrete version of the Mikhailov model (
1.1
), apart from the Sylvester equation (
2.1
),
i.e.

\bm{K}\bm{M}-\bm{M}\bm{L}=\bm{r}\bm{s}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}},

(2.13a)

we introduce the following discrete dispersion relations:

\displaystyle p\widetilde{\bm{r}}=p\bm{r}+\bm{K}\bm{r}\bm{a},\quad q\widehat{\bm{r}}=q\bm{r}+\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{r}\bm{a},

(2.13b)

\displaystyle p\widetilde{\bm{s}}+\bm{L}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}~\widetilde{\bm{s}}\bm{a}=p\bm{s},\quad q\widehat{\bm{s}}+(\bm{L}^{-1})^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}~\widehat{\bm{s}}\bm{a}=q\bm{s},

(2.13c)

where
\bm{a}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)
is the Pauli matrix
\sigma_{3}
,

p
and
q
serve as spacing parameter in
n
-direction and
m
-direction, respectively.

For given
\bm{K}
and
\bm{L}
, one can get
\bm{r}
and
\bm{s}
from (
2.13b
) and (
2.13c
).
Note that in light of Remark
2.1
, we assume
the Sylvester equation (
2.13a
) has a unique solution
\bm{M}
.
It then follows that the following evolutions for
\bm{M}
hold: (
[
11
]
, Proposition 6)

\displaystyle p(\widetilde{\bm{M}}-\bm{M})=

\displaystyle\bm{r}\bm{a}\widetilde{\bm{s}}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}},

(2.14a)

\displaystyle q(\bm{M}-\widehat{\bm{M}})=

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{r}\bm{a}\widehat{\bm{s}}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}\bm{L}^{-1}.

(2.14b)

One can refer to
[
11
]
for the proof.

Equations (
2.14
) encode all the information on the dynamics of the matrix
\bm{M}
,
with respect to the discrete
variables
n
and
m
.
Then, in light of equation set (
2.13
) together with the above dynamics (
2.14
) for
\bm{M}
,
one can derive the following shift relations of the master functions
\{\bm{S}^{(i,j)}\}
:

\displaystyle p\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(i,j)}+\bm{a}\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(i,j+1)}=

\displaystyle p\bm{S}^{(i,j)}+\bm{S}^{(i+1,j)}\bm{a}-\bm{S}^{(0,j)}\bm{a}\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(i,0)},

(2.15a)

\displaystyle p\bm{S}^{(i,j)}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j+1)}=

\displaystyle p\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(i,j)}-\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(i+1,j)}\bm{a}+\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{(0,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,0)},

(2.15b)

\displaystyle q\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i,j)}+\bm{a}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i,j-1)}=

\displaystyle q\bm{S}^{(i,j)}+\bm{S}^{(i-1,j)}\bm{a}+\bm{S}^{(-1,j)}\bm{a}\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i,-1)},

(2.15c)

\displaystyle q\bm{S}^{(i,j)}-\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,j-1)}=

\displaystyle q\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i,j)}-\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(i-1,j)}\bm{a}-\widehat{\bm{S}}^{(-1,j)}\bm{a}\bm{S}^{(i,-1)}.

(2.15d)

Again, one can refer to
[
11
]
(Proposition 7) for the proof of the above relations.
Note that in the proof the constraint (
2.4
) was used.

2.2.2
Discrete AKNS
(-1)
system

To have a discrete Mikhailov model, we concentrate on
the functions
\bm{S}^{(0,0)}
,
\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}
and
\bm{S}^{(0,-1)}
,
and for convenience, we introduce the following notations

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{S}^{(0,0)}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}&u_{2}\\
u_{3}&u_{4}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{v}=\bm{I}-\bm{S}^{(-1,0)}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\\
v_{3}&v_{4}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{w}=\bm{I}+\bm{S}^{(0,-1)},

where

Note that in this paper we employ
\bm{I}
to express the identity matrix

Next we will work on the variables

and
\bm{w}
.

We observe that equation (
2.3b
) with specific

(i,j)=(0,0)
,

\displaystyle\bm{v}\bm{w}=\bm{I},

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(1,-1)}=\bm{w}\bm{u},

)

\displaystyle p(\widetilde{\bm{v}}-\bm{v})=(\bm{a}\widetilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{u}\bm{a})\widetilde{\bm{v}}=(\bm{a}\bm{u}-\widetilde{\bm{u}}\bm{a})\bm{v}.

Similarly, taking

in (

) and (

\displaystyle p(\widetilde{\bm{w}}-\bm{w})=\bm{w}(\bm{u}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\widetilde{\bm{u}})=\widetilde{\bm{w}}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}\bm{a}-\bm{a}\bm{u}).

(3.17a)

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\delta z}[f(z)]=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial^{\mathcal{N}-1}_{\delta z}f(z)}{(\mathcal{N}-1)!}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}_{\delta z}f(z)}{2!}&\partial_{\delta z}f(z)&f(z)\\
\frac{\partial^{\mathcal{N}-2}_{\delta z}f(z)}{(\mathcal{N}-2)!}&\cdots&\partial_{\delta z}f(z)&f(z)&0\\
\frac{\partial^{\mathcal{N}-3}_{\delta z}f(z)}{(\mathcal{N}-3)!}&\cdots&f(z)&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots\\
f(z)&\cdots&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}_{\mathcal{\mathcal{N}}\times\mathcal{\mathcal{N}}}.

(3.17b)

-th order multiple-pole solution to the discrete Mikhailov model (

Remark 3.1
.

\bm{F}_{\delta z}[f(z)]
in (
3.17a
) is a lower triangular Toeplitz matrix commuting with a Jordan block of same order.
All such matrices compose a commutative set
\accentset{\circ}{G}^{[\mathcal{N}]}
with respect to matrix multiplication
and the subset

G^{[\mathcal{N}]}=\big\{\mathcal{C}\big|~\big.\mathcal{C}\in\accentset{\circ}{G}^{[\mathcal{N}]},~|\mathcal{C}|\neq 0\big\}

is an Abelian group. Such kind of matrices play useful roles in the expression of multiple-pole solutions.
For more properties of such matrices one can refer to
[
33
,
35
]
.

\bm{H}_{\delta z}[f(z)]
in (
3.17b
) is a skew triangular Hankel matrix, which is symmetric. It is easy to find that

\bm{H}_{\delta z}[f(z)]\bm{F}_{\delta z}[f(z)]
is a symmetric matrix, i.e.

\bm{H}_{\delta z}[f(z)]\bm{F}_{\delta z}[f(z)]=\bm{F}^{\hbox{\tiny\it{T}}}_{\delta z}[f(z)]\bm{H}_{\delta z}[f(z)]
.

4
Continuum limits

We explore in this section continuum limits of the discrete Mikhailov model (
2.57
)
by shrinking the lattice grid to a continuous set of values corresponding to spatial and temporal coordinates.
From the system (
2.57
), through one step straight continuum limit, we obtain
a semi-discrete Mikhailov model (see (
4.4
)),
i.e., the differential-difference Mikhailov model with one discrete and one continuous independent variables.
Subsequently, Cauchy matrix schemes of the AKNS-type and the KP-type will be
presented for the semi-discrete Mikhailov model.
The second step continuum limit leading to the continuous Mikhailov model (
1.1
) will be
performed in this section as well.

4.1
Semi-discrete Mikhailov model

In the first step we let both
q
and
m
tend to infinity but keep
m/q
finite.
Introduce

x=\frac{m}{q},

(4.1)

where
1/q
serves as the grid parameter in the discretization of
x
.
With this setting
we reinterpret the dependent variables
\mu
and
\nu
as

\displaystyle\mu(n,m)=\mu(n,x),\quad\nu(n,m)=\nu(n,x)

(4.2)

without making confusions,
while the shifted variables in
m
-direction give rise to

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}=

\displaystyle\mu(n,x+1/q)=\mu+\mu_{x}/q+\mu_{xx}/(2q^{2})+\cdots,

(4.3a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\mu(n+1,x+1/q)=\widetilde{\mu}+\widetilde{\mu}_{x}/q+\widetilde{\mu}_{xx}/(2q^{2})+\cdots,

(4.3b)

and a similar formula for
\widehat{\widetilde{\nu}}
.
Inserting them into the system (
2.57
) and then
from the leading term (in terms of
1/q
) we obtain a semi-discrete system:

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nu}+\nu)(\widetilde{\mu}+\mu)(\widetilde{\mu}_{x}+\mu_{x})-\mathrm{i}[(\mu_{x}-\widetilde{\mu}_{x})P+4(\widetilde{\mu}+\mu)]=0,

(4.4a)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\nu}^{2}-\nu^{2})(\widetilde{\mu}_{x}-\mu_{x})-2(\widetilde{\mu}+\mu)(\widetilde{\nu}_{x}\widetilde{\nu}+\nu_{x}\nu)-\mathrm{i}[4(\widetilde{\nu}+\nu)-(\widetilde{\nu}_{x}-\nu_{x})P-(\widetilde{\nu}-\nu)P_{x}]=0,

(4.4b)

where

\displaystyle P:=P(n,x)=[4p^{2}-(\mu+\widetilde{\mu})^{2}(\nu+\widetilde{\nu})^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.4c)

This is the semi-discrete Mikhailov model.

In the above limit, the discrete plane wave factors in (
3.1
) give rise to

\displaystyle\rho_{\iota}(z)=\left(1+(-1)^{\iota-1}\frac{z}{p}\right)^{n}e^{(-1)^{\iota-1}z^{-1}x}\rho^{0}_{\iota}(z),

(4.5a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\iota}(z)=\left(1+(-1)^{\iota-1}\frac{z}{p}\right)^{-n}e^{(-1)^{\iota}z^{-1}x}\sigma^{0}_{\iota}(z),

(4.5b)

with
\iota=1,2
,
which now serve for the semi-discrete system (
4.4
).
Moreover, solutions for semi-discrete Mikhailov model can be provided from
Cauchy matrix schemes as well.

Proposition 4.1
.

Formulae (
3.7
) and (
3.2
) provide solutions to
the semi-discrete Mikhailov model (
4.4
), respectively,

(4.6a)

\displaystyle p\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{\iota}=p\bm{r}_{\iota}+(-1)^{\iota-1}\bm{K}_{\iota}\bm{r}_{\iota},\quad\bm{r}_{\iota,x}=(-1)^{\iota-1}\bm{K}^{-1}_{\iota}\bm{r}_{\iota},

(4.6b)

\displaystyle p\widetilde{\bm{r}}_{\iota}=p\bm{r}_{\iota}+(-1)^{\iota-1}\bm{K}\bm{r}_{\iota},\quad\bm{r}_{\iota,x}=(-1)^{\iota-1}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{r}_{\iota},

(4.7b)

(4.7c)

with

To implement the other straight continuum limit, we set

\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty,\quad p\rightarrow\infty,\quad\text{while}\quad t:=n/p\quad\text{finite},

(4.8)

We also set

\mu(n,x)=\mu(t,x),\quad\nu(n,x)=\nu(t,x),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}=

\displaystyle\nu(t+1/p,x)=\nu+\nu_{t}/p+\nu_{tt}/(2p^{2})+\cdots.

After the continuum limit (

5
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have derived the discrete Mikhailov model (
2.57
)
by means of the Cauchy matrix approach.
The key step in the discretization is to find a pair of discrete Miura transformations,
i.e. (
2.39
) and (
2.53
).
Among the two equations in the discrete Mikhailov model (
2.57
),
one equation, (
2.57
), results from equation (
2.27b
) by means of the Miura transformations,
the other, (
2.57
), is from the compatibility of the two Miura transformations.
This inherits the continuous Cauchy matrix structure (cf.
[
19
]
)
except the continuous Miura transformation contains a single equation ((2.13) in
[
19
]
).
Apart from equations, we also constructed explicit solutions, including solitons
and multiple-pole solutions, from two Cauchy matrix schemes,
namely, the AKNS-type and the KP-type.
By straight continuum limits we recovered the semi-discrete and continuous Mikhailov models
together with their Cauchy matrix structures and solutions.
It is notable that the continuum limits show that the discretization is direct,
i.e. the discrete variables
n
and
m
directly correspond to
t
and
x
, respectively.

Let us address several problems related to the approach and results of the present paper.
First, in this paper we not only presented a pair of discrete Miura transformations,
but also we have showed a way how to construct them.
This is useful in discretizing other integrable equations where Miura transformations are involved.
For example, it is known that there is a Miura transformation
to connect the unreduced NLS system and the unreduced
Gerdjikov-Ivanov (GI) derivative NLS system
[
12
]
.
This indicates the GI system might be discretized if proper discrete Miura transformations could be found.
It then possible to formulate the Cauchy matrix structures
for all the discrete derivative NLS type systems.
The second problem is about possible reductions of the discrete Mikhailov model (
2.57
) or
(
2.58
).
In Appendix we collect some fully discrete equations of the unreduced NLS and derivative NLS systems.
Neither of them admits one-field reduction.
So far we do not find any one-field reduction for (
2.57
) either.
However, in
[
26
]
the massive Thirring model has been discretized.
Whether the discrete Mikhailov model (
2.57
) or
(
2.58
) can be reduced to the discrete massive Thirring model will be investigated later.
Besides, what we have not touched upon is the Lax integrability of the discrete Mikhailov system (
2.57
).
In fact, in the Cauchy matrix, there is an object
\bm{u}^{(i)}=(\bm{C}+\bm{M})^{-1}\bm{K}^{i}\bm{r}

can be defined along with
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
.
Note that in this paper
\bm{u}^{(i)}
is a
N\times 2
matrix rather than a vector.
Such
\bm{u}^{(i)}
will satisfy a set of linear relations which can be used to
construct Lax pairs for some equations involving
\bm{S}^{(i,j)}
.
As examples one can refer to
[
4
]
and Chapter 9 of
[
10
]

where
\bm{u}^{(i)}
is a vector.
However, for the discrete Mikhailov system (
2.57
), the involved variables are

u_{3}
and
v_{2}/v_{4}
, and
\bm{u}^{(i)}
is no longer a vector,
one needs to develop special approach to construct Lax pairs by using
\bm{u}^{(i)}
.
In addition, for the discrete equations presented in Appendix,
their Lax pairs can be constructed from deforming Darboux transformations
of the related continuous spectral problems,
which implies the multidimensional consistency (see
[
10
]
) behind them.
For the discrete Mikhailov system,
how to describe its multidimensional consistency (with other system)
and whether its Lax integrability can de described from this way will be also investigated in the future.
