Self-Control Buffers the Association Between Aggression and Problematic Internet Gaming in Adolescents
Authors: Yeon Jung Lee, Jung Ho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective Problematic internet gaming is an increasing mental health concern in adolescence; however, not all individuals exposed to known risk factors develop maladaptive gaming behaviors. Although aggression and self-control have each been linked to gaming-related problems, their interactive effects remain insufficiently understood. Methods This study examined whether self-control moderates the association between aggression and problematic internet gaming among adolescents. Baseline data from 1,914 first-grade middle school students participating in the internet gaming disorder cohort study (Internet User Cohort for Unbiased Recognition of Gaming Disorder in Early Adolescence) were analyzed. Gaming-related problems were assessed using the Internet Gaming Use–Elicited Symptom Screen. Aggression and self-control were measured with validated self-report scales. Depressive symptoms, trait anxiety, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms were included as covariates. Group differences were examined using t-tests and chi-square tests. Results Moderation analyses tested whether self-control altered the association between aggression and gaming-related problems. Self-control significantly moderated the association between aggression and gaming-related problems, such that higher self-control attenuated the positive relationship between aggression and problematic gaming. Gaming-related problems were more prevalent among males, adolescents whose parents had a high school education or lower, and those not residing in two-parent households. Conclusion Self-control influences the strength of the association between aggression and maladaptive gaming behaviors in adolescence, underscoring its role as a protective factor in problematic internet gaming.Keywords: Internet gaming disorder, Adolescents, Aggression, Self-control, Risk factors

INTRODUCTION

As online gaming has become deeply embedded in adolescents’ daily lives, concerns regarding excessive and dysregulated gaming have intensified, positioning problematic gaming as an important public health concern. In response to mounting empirical evidence, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [1] identified internet gaming disorder as a condition warranting further investigation, and the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision [2] subsequently recognized gaming disorder as a dieISSN 1976-3026 OPEN ACCESS agnosable mental health condition characterized by impaired control over gaming and persistent engagement despite significant functional impairment and adverse consequences. These classifications underscore that the core features of problematic gaming include impaired control, prioritization of gaming over other activities, and continuation despite negative consequences.

Recent meta-analytic findings estimate that approximately 7%–10% of adolescents and young adults exhibit problematic levels of gaming [3,4]. In South Korea, national survey data indicate a 1-month prevalence of 8.4% [5], suggesting that a substantial proportion of adolescents may fall within an at-risk range. Importantly, problematic internet gaming has been consistently associated with a broad spectrum of adverse outcomes, including depressive and anxiety symptoms, impulsivity, aggression, academic underachievement, sleep disturbance, and impairments in family and peer functioning [6,7]. Longitudinal [8] and cross-lagged evidence [9,10] suggests bidirectional associations between problematic internet gaming and psychologi-cal problems, emphasizing the critical importance of early detection and intervention to prevent symptom escalation and chronicity.

Early intervention frameworks for problematic internet gaming emphasize the reduction of modifiable risk factors alongside the enhancement of protective capacities [11]. Previous research suggests that problematic internet gaming may be related to a range of external environmental factors, such as family context, as well as adolescents’ emotional regulation and behavioral difficulties at the individual level [12,13]. In particular, aggression—identified as a risk factor for problematic internet gaming—may be further heightened by excessive gaming. Heightened aggression may contribute to increased family and interpersonal conflict and may also aggravate comorbid psychiatric conditions, which in turn could reinforce gaming immersion and gradually foster a self-perpetuating cycle [14,15]. Aggression has been shown to increase as executive functioning— an essential set of cognitive self-regulatory abilities—declines [16]. Impairments in executive functions—including inhibitory control, working memory, and planning—alongside cognitive rigidity and deficits in social cue interpretation, may heighten aggression by undermining behavioral and emotional regulation and promoting hostile attribution biases [17,18].

Self-control, a key subcomponent of executive functioning, has consistently been proposed as a central protective factor against negative behavioral outcomes, including problematic internet gaming. Conversely, deficits in self-control have been repeatedly reported to significantly increase the risk of developing problematic gaming [19,20]. Prior research indicates that problematic gaming is positively associated with aggression and negatively associated with self-control [21], with systematic review evidence confirming a consistent inverse relationship between self-control and problematic internet gaming [22]. Prior studies [21,22] have largely examined aggression and self-control as separate predictors of problematic internet gaming. However, this approach may overlook the possibility that self-control changes the strength of the association between aggression and gaming problems. Based on a protective-reactive framework, self-control may buffer the impact of aggression by preventing aggressive impulses from developing into maladaptive gaming behaviors. This conditional perspective may help explain individual differences in vulnerability among adolescents with high aggression. Meanwhile, difficulties in regulating aggression and self-control may be exacerbated by comorbid psychiatric conditions—such as depression, anxiety, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)—which are themselves risk factors for gaming disorder [5,7]. Accordingly, it is important that these comorbidities are also considered when examining the interplay between aggression, self-control, and problematic internet gaming.

Accordingly, the present study examines whether self-control may moderate the association between aggression and problematic gaming among school-based adolescents, while statistically adjusting for relevant emotional and neurodevelopmental symptom domains, including depressive symptoms, anxiety, and ADHD symptoms. By clarifying the conditional role of self-control in the context of heightened aggression, this study aims to refine theoretical models of problematic internet gaming and inform the development of more targeted and developmentally sensitive intervention strategies.

METHODS

Participants

The Internet User Cohort for Unbiased Recognition of Gaming Disorder in Early Adolescence (iCURE) study is a nationwide, school-based prospective cohort study conducted in South Korea between 2015 and 2016 that recruited students in Grades 3–4 of elementary school and Grade 1 of middle school, along with their parents [23]. For the present study, the analytic sample was restricted to students enrolled in the first year of middle school. Accordingly, baseline data from 1,914 first-year middle school students participating in the iCURE study were analyzed.

Cases with missing data were handled using listwise deletion, resulting in minor variations in the analytic sample size across analyses (range: 1,907–1,914). Baseline assessments were collected using web-based self-report questionnaires completed by the students, while parents provided additional demographic and family-related information via structured interviews or self-administered questionnaires [23].

Problematic internet gaming was assessed using the Internet Gaming Use-Elicited Symptom Screen (IGUESS) [24]. Consistent with prior validation studies, participants with IGUESS scores of 10 or higher were classified into the problematic internet gaming (PIG) group, whereas those scoring below 10 were classified into the non-problematic internet gaming (NIG) group [24]. Chronological age was not directly available due to data anonymization. However, because all participants were enrolled as first-year middle school students, age could be reasonably inferred based on grade level and year of birth.

The original iCURE cohort study received approval from the relevant institutional review board [23]. The present study involved a secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available data and was therefore exempted from additional review by the Institutional Review Board of Soonchunhyang University Seoul Hospital (no. SCHUH 2025-04-019-002).

Assessments

Internet Gaming Use-Elicited Symptom Screen

The IGUESS [24] is a self-report screening tool designed to identify excessive internet gaming in adolescents. It is based on the nine criteria for internet gaming disorder outlined in the DSM-5 and evaluates gaming-related behaviors over the past 12 months. The scale consists of nine items; each rated from 0 to 3. A total score of 10 or higher indicates an elevated risk of developing gaming-related problems. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85 [24]. In the present study, participants were classified into two groups based on their IGUESS total scores. Individuals scoring 10 or higher were categorized as the PIG group, whereas those scoring 9 or below were classified as the NIG group.

Gottfredson Self-Control Scale

The Gottfredson Self-Control Scale (GSCS) [25] is a self-report measure designed to assess essential traits linked to low self-control, such as impulsivity, risk-seeking behavior, a preference for immediate gratification, and impatience. It consists of 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Total scores can range from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater levels of self-control. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.78 [26].

Korean version of the Aggression Questionnaire

The Korean version of the Aggression Questionnaire (AQK) is a self-report questionnaire developed by Buss and Perry [27] to assess aggression. It consists of 29 items across four subscales— physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, and hostility— and is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 29 to 145. Higher scores indicate greater levels of aggression. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.86 [28].

Children’s Depression Inventory

The Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) is a questionnaire for children and adolescents aged 7–17 years that asks them to report their own depressive symptoms [29]. It covers mood-related, cognitive, and behavioral features of depression and has shown good psychometric properties in both clinical and non-clinical samples. The CDI contains 27 items; each scored on a three-point scale that reflects the severity or frequency of symptoms. The Korean version of the CDI, standardized by Cho and Lee [30], showed good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.88.

Trait Anxiety Inventory for Children

The Trait Anxiety Inventory for Children (TAIC) is a selfreport scale developed to assess children’s trait anxiety levels [31]. The TAIC consists of 20 items, with total scores ranging from 20 to 60 points. A total score of 40 or above has been suggested to indicate high trait anxiety. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93 [32].

Korean ADHD Rating Scale

The Korean ADHD Rating Scale (K-ARS) is a parent-report instrument assessing inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms [33]. Using a 4-point frequency scale, caregivers rate how often specific behaviors occur. The scale has been validated for use with Korean children and is commonly applied in clinical and research contexts [34]. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α >0.70).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0; IBM Corp.) and Python (Version 3.4; Python Software Foundation). For descriptive and group comparison analyses, participants were classified into the PIG and the NIG based on an IGUESS cutoff score of 10. Group differences in categorical variables were examined using chi-square (χ²) tests, and differences in continuous variables were assessed using independent-samples t-tests.

To examine whether self-control moderated the association between aggression and problematic internet gaming, moderation analyses were conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS (Model 1) [35]. Aggression was entered as the independent variable, problematic internet gaming scores as the dependent variable, and self-control as the moderator. Depressive symptoms, trait anxiety, and ADHD symptoms were included as covariates in light of previous findings suggesting their relevance to problematic internet gaming and associated psychopathology [4,6].

Simple slope analyses were performed to estimate the conditional effects of aggression on gaming-related problem scores at low (16th percentile), medium (50th percentile), and high (84th percentile) levels of self-control. In addition, Johnson– Neyman analyses were conducted to identify the range of selfcontrol values for which the association between aggression and problematic internet gaming was statistically significant. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-tailed α level of 0.05.

Supplementary Table 1 presents analyses examining associations between adolescents’ self-control level (GSCS ≤68 vs. ≥69) and parental socio-familial characteristics, including parental educational attainment, occupation, and marital status, using chi-square tests and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (p<0.05).

RESULTS

Out of the 1,914 participants, 159 (8.3%) were identified as PIG. Significant between-group differences were identified for gender, parental educational attainment, and parental marital status. The proportion of boys was significantly greater in the PIG group than in the NIG group (χ²=8.321, p=0.004). Moreover, adolescents in the PIG group were more likely to have parents with a high school education or lower (χ²=11.398, p=0.001) and to reside in single-parent or other non–twoparent households (χ²=4.596, p=.032). In contrast, no significant between-group differences were observed for parental occupation or socioeconomic status (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

The present study examined the joint roles of aggression and self-control in problematic internet gaming among schoolbased adolescents and identified several noteworthy patterns. The findings suggest that self-control may be associated with a conditional role in the relationship between aggression and problematic internet gaming, such that higher levels of selfcontrol were linked to a weaker association between aggression and problematic internet gaming. In addition, problematic internet gaming was more frequently observed among adolescents whose parents had attained a high school education or less and among those residing in non–two-parent households. Problematic internet gaming was also more prevalent in males than in females [5,36].

The present findings suggest that self-control may be associated with a buffering or attenuating role in the association between aggression and problematic internet gaming. From a theoretical perspective, social learning theory and cognitive– contextual perspectives suggest that repeated exposure to violent environments and gaming contexts in which aggressive behaviors are rewarded and function as effective problemsolving strategies may contribute to the reinforcement of aggressive tendencies [37,38]. In online gaming environments, where positive emotional reinforcement is often provided with limited or unrealistic consequences or punishment, the negative outcomes of aggressive behavior may be underestimated, which may, in turn, be associated with increased aggressive impulses in everyday life [39]. Adolescence represents a particularly vulnerable developmental period, as ongoing maturation of the prefrontal cortex may be accompanied by relatively immature self-regulatory capacities, potentially increasing vulnerability to problematic internet gaming and the reinforcement of aggressive tendencies.

Importantly, the present findings indicate that aggression does not uniformly confer risk for problematic internet gaming; rather, its association with problematic internet gaming appears to be conditional upon adolescents’ level of self-control. From a self-regulatory perspective, adolescents with high-er self-control may have greater capacity to inhibit impulsive responses, tolerate frustration, and disengage from immediate reward-seeking tendencies during gaming, which may reduce the likelihood that aggressive impulses are linked to problematic internet gaming behaviors.

By identifying self-control as a moderating factor, rather than merely a correlational protective factor, this study adds to the existing literature by suggesting a conditional pathway through which aggression may be related to problematic internet gaming. Taken together, these findings may help refine existing models of problematic internet gaming by highlighting heterogeneity in risk processes and underscoring the potential role of self-regulatory capacity in shaping behavioral outcomes. These findings suggest that enhancing self-control may offer a more practical clinical target than modifying aggressive traits. Notably, Johnson–Neyman analysis indicates that the negative impact of aggression on problematic gaming tends to diminish at self-control (GSCS) scores of 69.2 or above. Therefore, implementing evidence-based self-regulation protocols in school or clinical settings might help attenuate the pathway from aggression to compulsive gaming.

Notably, adolescents with problematic internet gaming were more likely to have parents whose highest educational attainment was high school or less and to reside in non–two-parent households. These findings suggest that, beyond individuallevel psychological factors, family-level contextual resources may be relevant to adolescents’ vulnerability to problematic internet gaming. Prior developmental research has suggested that parental monitoring tends to be associated with adolescents’ self-control, which in turn has been linked to lower levels of problematic technology use [40]. In addition, theoretical models of self-control development propose that broader family contexts—such as parental autonomy support and environmental stability—are relevant correlates of adolescents’ selfregulatory capacities [41]. Taken together, the present findings suggest that limited family-level contextual supports may cooccur with lower self-regulatory capacity, and that this pattern may be linked to greater vulnerability to gaming-related problems. Further longitudinal research is needed to clarify whether, and in what ways, these contextual characteristics may be related to adolescents’ vulnerability to problematic internet gaming across development.

Consistent with previous research [5,36], the prevalence of problematic internet gaming was significantly higher among male adolescents than among female adolescents, a pattern that has been repeatedly observed across epidemiological studies of adolescent gaming behaviors.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the associations among aggression, self-control, and problematic internet gaming, underscoring the need for longitudinal or experimental studies to clarify temporal and developmental processes. Second, the reliance on self-report measures may have introduced reporting biases; future research would benefit from incorporating multi-informant assessments and objective indicators of gaming behavior. Third, although family structure and parental educational attainment were examined, more proximal family processes (e.g., parenting practices or parental monitoring) were not assessed. Finally, missing data across self-report measures resulted in minor variations in sample size across analyses, which should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Future studies should further examine how family contextual factors interact with self-regulatory capacities and whether the moderating role of self-control varies across gender and developmental stages.

Overall, the present study provides evidence that self-control may be relevant to understanding adolescents’ vulnerability to problematic internet gaming. The observed moderating association between aggression and gaming outcomes suggests that self-regulatory capacity may be related to variability in how behavioral risk factors are associated with gaming behaviors during adolescence. In addition, associations observed with family context and gender indicate that problematic internet gaming may be more appropriately examined within a developmental framework that accounts for both individual-level characteristics and broader contextual factors. Although these findings may have implications for the consideration of prevention or intervention approaches that include self-control–related components, such implications remain tentative and should be interpreted cautiously in light of the cross-sectional nature of the data. Further longitudinal research will be necessary to clarify developmental pathways and to more precisely inform future intervention efforts.
