Can positive emotional writing improve the emotional health level of international medical students? Evidence from a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qiaoling Xiao, Yang Cao, Shuo Wu, Yu Zou, Xi Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression and anxiety are common psychological issues among international medical students in universities, which have serious negative impacts on their learning and life. The present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of positive emotional writing in alleviating symptoms of anxiety and depression, as well as enhancing subjective well-being among this population.MethodThis study was conducted at an international college of a comprehensive university in central China. A total of ninety-two participants who met the inclusion criteria were recruited to participate in a single-blind randomized controlled trial, in which participants were not aware that there was an experimental group and a control group. Participants in control received daily psychological care weekly for 8 weeks (n = 46). Correspondingly, participants in experimental group received the positive emotional writing intervention on the basis of daily psychological care. Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and General Well-being Scale (GWB) were used to evaluate the effect of the intervention. Data from 89 students who completed the entire study (experimental group, n = 44; control group, n = 45) were analyzed.ResultsAfter the intervention, the scores of SDS and SAS in the experimental group significantly decreased, while the subjective well-being score significantly increased. Although the SAS score of the control group after intervention was significantly lower than before, the decrease in SDS and SAS scores, as well as the increase in GWB score, were significantly greater in the experimental group than in the control group.ConclusionThe findings suggest that positive emotional writing can effectively reduce the depression and anxiety of international medical students, and significantly enhance their subjective well-being, providing ideas for management to solve the psychological problems of international medical students.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400087815. Registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.Keywords: Positive emotional writing, Depression, Anxiety, Well-being, International medical students

Background

Depression and anxiety have been recognized as the most common mental health issues among university students [1, 2]. Public health events in recent years, such as COVID-19 Pandemic, have further led to the deterioration of college students’ mood problems [3]. A latest survey shows that 23.2% and 10.8% of Chinese university students suffer from moderate or severe depression or anxiety, respectively [4]. The sustained high levels of depression and anxiety among college students not only bring various negative impacts to individuals, but also impose serious burdens on families and society, which is worthy of attention and resolution [5]. What’s more noteworthy is that these two psychological health issues may be particularly prominent among medical international students. Besides the similar pressure experienced by the general college students, medical students might have to face more stress due to the unique characteristics of their profession, such as the extremely long length of schooling, huge pressure of clinical practice, and worries about the future working environment and not achieving societal expectations [2, 6, 7]. Moreover, compared to domestic medical students, those from other countries studying medicine in China may be at greater risk of emotional distress, because they may suffer the issues of trans-cultural adaptation, residential separation from family and language barriers [7]. A recent survey showed that the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms among medical international students in China was as high as 28.5% and 31.6%, respectively [7]. A series of studies have pointed out that depression and anxiety among medical students can lead to many academic problems, such as dropout and burnout [2], as well as habit problems such as substance abuse and internet addiction [8, 9] and even self-harm or suicide behavior [10]. Furthermore, at a higher level, depression and anxiety among international students not only affect their own growth and development, but also have a negative impact on the international image of China’s higher education system and the stability of the entire society [11]. Given the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms among medical international students and their significant negative impact on individuals and society, exploring ways to alleviate negative emotions such as depression and anxiety while increasing positive experiences such as well-being has become an important issue.

There are many ways to alleviate depression and anxiety among college students and improve their mental health, such as providing social support, reading books, and increasing communication with family [4, 12]. Among various ways, written expressive disclosure which was first proposed by Pennebaker et al. in 1986 had been found to be beneficial for health [13–15]. However, further research has found that the effectiveness of written emotional disclosure varies from person to person and may be context-dependent [16]. Participants who gained the most from written expressive disclosure were those who used more positive emotional words [17, 18]. Therefore, in recent years, researchers have integrated positive psychology into written expression, with positive content as the writing theme, and proposed the concept of positive emotional writing which refers to individuals writing about their positive experiences in life [19]. Writing can evoke positive emotions, as the process of integrating thoughts and feelings into text can broaden the scope of attention, generate different perspectives, and promote more flexible ways of thinking [20]. Writing to express positive emotions is more helpful in generating positive emotions because it helps individuals view events in life from a positive perspective [21]. According to the broaden-and-build hypothesis of positive emotion [22], positive emotions may help expand individual’s momentary thought-action resources and enable them to think and solve problems in a more flexible way, and further construct sustainable coping resources and skills, ultimately improving emotional problems and enhancing well-being. A series of studies have tested the role of positive emotional writing in both clinical and non-clinical settings, and found that it could not only reduce negative emotions such as depression and anxiety, but also improve positive experiences such as well-being [19, 23–29]. For example, Smith and his colleagues (2018) recruited 71 participants with an average age of 28.2 years and randomly divided them into the positive emotional writing group and the control group [19]. After positive emotional writing tasks with 20 min per day over three consecutive days, the experimental group had significantly lower levels of trait anxiety and stress compared to the control group. Another study adopted the same positive emotion writing paradigm among 90 undergraduate students to explore its effectiveness, and found that positive emotional writing is beneficial for increasing positive emotions [25]. A study on high school students also found that positive emotional writing with 20 min per day over 30 consecutive days can help reduce anxiety [28]. In addition to its notable effectiveness, positive emotional writing intervention has the advantages of low cost and simple operation, thus having the potential to be widely used in school, communities and clinical settings [28, 30].

Despite its promising prospects, there was limited research on the effectiveness of positive emotional writing on depression, anxiety and well-being among international medical students in China. Considering the important role of emotional issues for medical international students themselves and the entire society, this study aims to explore the impact of writing to express positive emotions on the psychological issues of international medical students in China and provide them with practical help. To our knowledge, this study is the first study to explore the effectiveness of positive emotional writing among medical students and international students. This study will also contribute to provide insights for international student management personnel to address the psychological issues of international medical students.

Methods

Design

This study employed a two-arm (a positive emotional writing condition and a control condition), randomized controlled trial including 2 test periods (pre-test, post-test). The goal was to analyze the effect of positive emotional writing intervention in comparison to the control condition.

This study was approved by the medical ethics committee of Wuhan University of Science and Technology and was registered under 2,024,119. Prior to formal experiment, the content and significance of the study were explained to all participants, and informed consent was obtained.

Participants and recruitment

International medical students from a university in central China were recruited to participate in this study using convenience sampling. The recruitment of participants was handled by the first author, with selection being based on the following criteria: (1) being full-time international medical students with more than one year of schooling; (2) not having previously participated in a positive emotional writing intervention; (3) voluntary participation in the study; and (4) no history of mental illness or psychological treatment within the past 6 months. After the preassessment and screening questionnaires, students who satisfied all the criteria (n = 92) were assigned to the experimental condition (n = 46) or the control condition (n = 46) randomly. The attrition rates of the experimental condition and control condition were 4.34% and 2.17% respectively. The remaining 89 participants who completed the experiment were between the ages of 21 and 28, with an average age of 23.67. Among these 89 participants, the number of boys and girls was 46 and 43 respectively, while 65 and 24 were Christians and Muslims respectively.

Measures

Depression

Self-Rating Depression scale (SDS) which was compiled by Zung was applied to measure the severity of depressive symptoms before and after the intervention [31]. The SDS consists of 20 corresponding question items, each corresponding to a depressive symptom. Participants were asked to evaluate the frequency of corresponding symptoms appearing in the past 7 days on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (none or a little of the time) to 4 (most or all of the time). The higher scores indicate the greater severity of depression. This scale has been widely used in clinical and non-clinical settings, and has been proven to have good reliability and validity by multiple studies [32]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the pre-test and post-test of the scale were 0.876 and 0.845, respectively.

Anxiety

Self-Rating Anxiety scale (SAS) which was compiled by Zung was applied to measure the severity of anxiety symptoms before and after the intervention [33]. The SAS consists of 20 corresponding question items, each corresponding to a anxiety symptom. Participants were asked to evaluate the frequency of corresponding symptoms appearing in the past 7 days on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (none or a little of the time) to 4 (most or all of the time). The standard score is the total score multiplied by 1.25 and the higher scores indicate the greater severity of anxiety. This scale has been widely used in clinical and non-clinical settings, and has been proven to have good reliability and validity by multiple studies [32]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the pre-test and post-test of the scale were 0.823 and 0.836, respectively.

Well-being

The General Well-Being Schedule (GWB) which was compiled by Fazio was applied to measure the happiness levels before and after the intervention [34]. The GWB has a total of 33 items, including concerns about health, energy, life satisfaction and interest, mood depression or happiness, control over emotions and behaviors, and relaxation or tension. The higher scores indicate the more positive well-being. This scale has been widely used in various contexts, and has been proven to have good reliability and validity by multiple studies [35, 36]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the pre-test and post-test of the scale were 0.835 and 0.876, respectively.

Experiences of adopting positive emotional writing

This study employed structured interviews to inquire about the experiences of participants in the experimental group during the intervention process. Participants were asked five open-ended questions developed for this study, as follows: (1) Briefly describe your recent emotional state; (2) Have your feelings of anxiety and depression changed through writing to express positive emotions? If so, what kind of changes have you noticed?; (3) Have you experienced the emergence of positive emotions through writing to express positive emotions?; (4) Do you feel that writing to express positive emotions has helped to increase your sense of happiness?; (5) Are you willing to continue the practice of writing to express positive emotions? Would you consider sharing this habit with others?

Procedure

The entire intervention was conducted in person, with the specific experimental process outlined in Fig. 1. After publishing the recruitment information for the participants, we screened the applicants based on four inclusion criteria, and a total of 92 participants were ultimately selected to participate in the study. This study utilized a simple randomization method to allocate participants into groups, with the specific division into an experimental group and a control group being determined by the random number table method. A non-author conducted the participant allocation independently. He numbered 92 participants and drew 92 random numbers from a pre-existing random number table correspondingly, starting at a random point and following order. After sorting, the participants corresponding to the first 46 random numbers were assigned to the experimental group, and the last 46 to the control group. Before the formal intervention, both groups of participants completed pre-tests on SDS, SAS, and GWB. During the intervention process, the control group received routine psychological care, while the experimental group also received an 8-week positive emotional writing task on this basis. Ultimately, the remaining 89 participants completed post-tests on SDS, SAS, and GWB. The entire experiment spanned a duration of three months. Participants were recruited towards the end of the first month of the spring semester. Following this, a pre-test was administered, and the two-month randomized controlled trial commenced at the outset of the second month. Two months subsequent to the commencement of the trial, a post-test was conducted.

Intervention

The participants in both the experimental group and control group received routine psychological care, which aligned with the actual psychological assistance provided to international students by many universities in mainland China. Specifically, one of the implementers of this study, who concurrently serves as the counselor of these students, would inquire about their recent situations every week, encompassing their living arrangements, academic conditions, and, notably, their psychological states. If students encountered any psychological difficulties, the counselor would promptly provide psychological counseling. Furthermore, the experimental group underwent a positive emotional writing intervention on the foundation of the routine psychological care. Statistical analyses

The SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used to enter and analyze all the data. The continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), and the categorical variables were expressed as frequency or percentage. We tested the requisitions for t-test according to suggestions of Field [37]. Results showed that the data satisfied the two types of prerequisites: homogeneity of variance and normal distribution. Thus, the differences in demographic variables between the experimental group and control group were assessed by independent samples t tests and Chi-square tests. The independent sample t-test was used to test whether there was a significant difference in SDS, SAS and GWB scores between two groups before intervention, and to test whether there was a significant difference in the changes in SDS, SAS, and GWB between the two groups before and after intervention. At the same time, the paired sample t-tests were used to test whether there were significant changes in SDS, SAS, and GWB scores relative to baseline levels after the experiment in the experimental group or control group, respectively. Based on the outcomes of the interviews, a psychology expert who is not the author of this study conducted thematic analysis on the responses provided by the participants. This analysis categorized the attitudes and viewpoints of participants into clear and distinct groups. Furthermore, the quantity of individuals who held various attitudes and viewpoints was presented using descriptive statistics.

Discussion

Firstly, the results of this study showed that positive emotional writing significantly alleviated anxiety and depression among the international medical students in China. After the intervention of 8-week positive emotional writing, the anxiety and depression scores of international medical students decreased significantly. Although the control group also showed decrease in anxiety under the condition of routine psychological care, the overall improvement of the experimental group was significantly better than that of the control group. These results were consistent with some previous findings in other groups [23, 24, 27, 28]. Previous studies have shown that writing to express positive emotions can help individuals view problems in life and learning from a more positive perspective, further promoting the generation of positive emotions [20, 21]. The intervention in this study encouraged students to write the details of past happy events, helping them recall care and support from family, friends, and teachers, thus helping them experience more positive emotions. The broaden-and-build hypothesis of positive emotion suggested that positive emotions could expand individuals’ thinking and activity range, thereby improving their thinking patterns and enabling them to think and solve problems in a more flexible way [22]. Therefore, positive emotions of international medical students stimulated by positive emotional writing intervention could help them think about the setbacks and problems they encounter from multiple perspectives, face and solve them in a more adaptive way, and ultimately alleviate the generation of negative emotions such as depression and anxiety. Overall, guiding international medical students to writing to express positive emotions can improve their dissatisfaction with their current lives and effectively alleviate depression and anxiety. Therefore, considering the implementation context in this study, it is suggested that counselors can integrate positive emotional writing intervention into daily life counseling and psychological education to alleviate students’ anxiety and depression.

Secondly, in addition to non-adaptive emotions, positive emotional writing intervention was proved to improve the subjective well-being of the international medical students in China. After the intervention of 8-week positive emotional writing, the subjective well-being of international medical students improved significantly. Conversely, the subjective well-being of the control group participants even showed a slight decrease. These findings fully demonstrated the advantages of the intervention method of writing and expressing positive emotions in improving the subjective well-being of international medical students. These results were consistent with previous related studies which proved the positive effects of positive emotional writing on continuous positive emotions and emotional intelligence [25, 29]. Positive emotional writing awakened learners’ positive experiences and helped them view life with a more positive perspective, thus promoting the generation of positive emotions [20, 21]. Further, the activated positive emotions would help learners expand their immediate thinking and behavioral resources, enabling them to solve problems in a more adaptive way, and gradually helped them construct sustained coping resources, such as higher emotional intelligence, thereby promoting the generation of sustained positive emotions and improving subjective well-being [22, 38]. Some of the international students who participated in the study said frankly, “When recollecting some warm-hearted things, the affecting emotion has been recalled again, I realize the beauty of life still exists.” “The situation is not that bad, and we should face our life in a positive attitude. I believe that I can do well in my medical studies.” It can be analyzed from this that positive emotional writing helps international medical students turn their attention to the positive side of life, which includes positive experiences and events, and they can draw positive consciousness from it and form a positive attitude, and then adjust their reactions and coping mechanisms to what is happening around them, and ultimately improve their well-being.

Thirdly, the interview findings revealed that the majority of participants in the experimental group reported experiencing significant levels of anxiety and depression recently. Remarkably, they all expressed that positive emotional writing significantly mitigated their feelings of depression and anxiety. As highlighted by some international students, “Expressing positive emotions through writing has aided me in relieving stress and easing anxiety.” “When I express positive emotions in writing, I experience a surge in energy, uplifted spirits, and a noticeable absence of depressive feelings.” These findings once again underscored the beneficial impact of expressing positive emotions through writing in alleviating individual depression, anxiety, and other negative emotions. Conversely, from the perspective of positive psychology, our interview results demonstrated that the majority of participants in the experimental group cultivated more positive emotions by engaging in expressive writing of positive emotions, resulting in enhanced subjective well-being. A participating international student remarked, “Expressing positive emotions through writing has taught me to approach challenges with a positive outlook. Consequently, I find myself experiencing greater happiness daily, and I’ve come to appreciate the fulfillment of studying and living in China.” These interview results provided strong evidence for the positive impact of positive emotional writing on subjective well-being. Lastly, it is heartening that two-thirds of the participants expressed their intent to continue utilizing expressive writing of positive emotions as a strategy to alleviate negative emotions in the future, with nearly half indicating their inclination to recommend this approach to their family and friends.

Limitations and future research

Firstly, the current quantitative findings were based on a relatively small sample of students attending a single university, which may limit their generalizability to other settings. In light of the exploratory nature of our study, findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies would benefit from replicating our experiment with larger sample sizes, which would promote greater generalizability. Secondly, this study explored the effectiveness of positive emotional writing on self-reported depression, anxiety and well-being which might be influenced by social favorability. Future studies could adopt multimodal evaluation methods, including self-evaluation, peer evaluation, subjective evaluation, and objective evaluation. Finally, this study only explored the impact of positive emotional writing on emotional health from a holistic perspective, lacking exploration of the underlying mechanisms. Previous studies have pointed out that the specific content written by learners during the expressive writing process may be a key factor in its effectiveness [17, 26]. Therefore, future research can use content analysis to analyze the specific content written by participants, helping us clarify how positive emotional writing exerts a positive impact on depression, anxiety, and happiness.

Conclusion

Due to the lack of exploration regarding positive emotional writing for international medical students, this study conducted an intervention and explored its effects on mental health. The results indicated that positive emotional writing intervention significantly reduced depression and anxiety symptoms among medical international students, while significantly increasing their subjective well-being. These findings provide valuable insights for addressing the mental health issues of medical international students and improving their overall learning and life experiences.
