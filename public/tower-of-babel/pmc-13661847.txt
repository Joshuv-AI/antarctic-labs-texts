Cytochalasins are a fascinating class of natural compounds: advances in the chemistry, biological activities, mode of action, and structure-activity relationships of novel cytochalasins isolated in the last decade are described
Authors: Antonio Evidente
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytochalasins are a very intriguing group of natural compounds. Since the first isolation of the well-known cytochalasins A and B in 1966, over 300 analogues have been described as metabolites, primarily produced by various fungal genera. Their bioactivities, targets, total syntheses, and biosyntheses had been also extensively investigated. After the review of Scherlach et al., 2010 several other cytochalasins, isolated from different fungal sources, their biological activities and potential applications, were extensively described. In particular, during the last decades (2014–2024), as deduced from SciFinder research 259 articles, excluding reviews, were published essentially on new cytochalasins from new fungal sources and their biological activity and their potential application were reported. The reviews published in the same time (2014–2024) treated essentially the biological activities, the mode of action, the biosynthesis of already known cytochalasins and frequently the description was also extended and compared to other classes of natural compounds. The aims of the present review is to cover this gap reporting about the structure and biological activities of new cytochalasins isolated in the last decade (2014–2024) by fungi, endophytic fungi, essentially cohabitants in plants, and marine organisms. Furthermore, a brief section describing the cytochalasins Produced by lichens is also included. Methods

Search strategy

The search strategy focused on literature directly addressing the primary topic, while studies with less relevant content were excluded. Key electronic databases, search engines as Sci-Finder, Google, Google Scholar and Shi-Hub for articles meeting the inclusion standards. The search involved specific keyword combinations in titles and abstracts, using terms such as: (Cytochalasins, Fungi, Endophytes, Marine organisms, Likens and Biotechnological applications) This strategy yielded a broad selection of pertinent articles.

Inclusion criteria

Publications from the search engines cited in Sect. 1.1 were selected based on their direct relevance to the research topic and their ability to provide accurate, complete and up-to-date data. The inclusion criteria referred to observational and experimental studies examining the origins of cytochalasins, their numerous biological activities, and structure–activity correlation studies, as well as potential practical applications, primarily in agriculture and medicine. Non-original publications (e.g., reviews, letters, or comments), redundant studies, and those lacking full-text availability or addressing unrelated conditions were excluded. Only studies published in English were considered, and copyright was respected through proper citation. This systematic and selective approach ensured that the analysis was grounded in credible scientific evidence and aligned with ethical research standards. The content of the article in each section and sub-section was chronologically reported.

Introduction

Cytochalasins are a large group of natural metabolites, essentially produced by several fungal genera and some other organisms, and are well known for their cytotoxic activity on mammalian cells [1]. The name cytochalasins (cytos = cell, chalasis = relaxation) has been proposed as related at that time to their novel biological effects which were observed as above cited by Dr. Carter [1].

Cytochalasins are a group natural compounds which includes a diverse subgroup with a characteristic tricyclic core structure in which an 11 to 16-membered macrocycle ring, closed either a carbocycle, a lactone or a cyclic carbonate, is fused to a highly substituted perhydroisoindol-1-one [2]. Based on the l-amino acids incorporated into the hydrogenated isoindolone, the canonical cytochalasans are divided into different subgroups as cytochalasins (Phe), pyrichalasins (Tyr), aspochalasins (Leu), alachalasins (Ala) and chaetoglobosins (Trp) [3]. Usually, literature, including SciFinder source used to prepare this review, reported all together the compounds belonging to the first cited four groups as cytochalasins. Thus only chaetoglobusins are not treated in this review. The cytochalasins were also classified on the size of macrocyclic ring [3].

The first two compounds belonging to this group and named cytochalasins A and B (Fig. 1), two 14-cytochalasans, were isolated at almost the same time by Rothweiler and Tam from Phoma exigua var. exigua [4, 5] and from Aldridge et al. [6], who purified the two cytochalasins from the culture filtrates of Helminthosporium dematioideum and Metarrhizium anisopliae. Cytochalasins C and D, two [11]-cytochalasans, were isolated from the same culture filtrates of the last two fungi [6].

Structure of Cytochalasins A and B

Since that time several cytochalasins were essentially isolated by fungi and chemically and biological characterized. These cytochalasins, belonging to the different group of cytochalasans, were about 50 up to 1997 [7] and 300 more up to-day. Their bioactivities, targets, total syntheses, and biosyntheses had been also extensively investigated [3, 8–10]. After the review of Scherlach et al. [3], several other cytochalasins, which had been isolated from different fungal and other sources and their biological activities were extensively described. In particular, during the last decades (2014–2024), as deduced from SciFinder research, 259 articles, exluding reviews, were published. The other reviews, published in the same time (2014–2024), treated essentially the biological activities of already known cytochalasins, focusing on their therapeutic efficacy [11], their mode of action [12], their biosynthesis [10], and their use as chemotaxonomic markers [13]. Frequently, the description was also extended and compared to other classes of natural compounds. Just after this review was completed, Tammam et al. [14] published A review regarding cytochalasins over the period 2000–2023. Although this review cover ca. two decades. It is focused only fungal cytochalasins and in particular on their biosynthetic origins, pharmacokinetics and their promising therapeutic potential in drug discovery and development. In particular, their possible application in cardiovascular, oncology, cancer, inflammatory and autoimmune therapy was extensively treated. However, the approaches used by Tamman et al. is different from that used in the present one. They report in different sections an extensive decription on recent advances in biosynthesis, structural diversity and biological activities of cytochalasins derived from fungi. In particular, the main Sect. 3 is organized on the base of different class and subclass of fungal producer. The main deficiency of the review of Tamman et al. is that they decribed the chemical and biological characterization of on only ca. 10% of the new cytochalasins isolated in the last decade. Thus the aims of the present review is not only to cover this gap discussing the chemical and biological properties of all cytochalasins isolated from fungi (more than 300) but also those produced by marine organisms and other natural sources in the last decade (2014–2024). The very important role of cytochalasins to induced plants disease with economical severe consequence As well as their potential application as bioherbicides and in medicine towards different human diseases is also discussed. Furthermore, the results of several structure activity relationships studies (SAR) as well as some potential biotechnological applications of cytochalasins were also detailed described.

Cytochalasins

Cytochalasins from terrestrial fungi

The terrestrial fungi, including the plant pathogenic ones, are, among other microorganism, a very rich source of bioactive secondary metabolites belonging to several class of naturally occurring compounds and with a variety of potential application in different fields. Frequently, these kind of metabolites are also cytochalasins [15–19].

Cytochalasin from Apergillus spp.

Phytopathogenic fungi negatively affect plant growth and yield. Among them there are microrganisms belonging to the Apergillus genus and other genera which produce toxins that, in turn, accumulate in plants and lead to many problems, starting with the germination inhibition and disturbances in physiological processes [19] (Fig. 1).

Cytochalasins isolated from Aspergillus sp. PSU-RSPG185

The new aspergilluchalasin and aspochalasin J (1 and 2, Fig. 2) were isolated together with phenalenones, aspergillussanones A and B and aspergillulacton, two phenalenones and a γ-butyrolactone, respectively, from the mycelia and liquid culture of the soil fungus Aspergillus sp. PSU-RSPG185. From the same fungus were also isolated aspergillusols A and B, aspergillusic acid, two cyclic carbonate derivatives, a new eutypinic acid derivative, (+)-asperpentyn, 8,9-eutypinic acid, 4-hydroxy-3-prenylbenzoic acid, 2,2 dimethyl-2H-1-chromene-6-carboxylic acid and 2-(1′-methylethenyl)benzofuran-5-carboxylic. All the compounds were tested against KB and Vero cell lines and only aspergillussanones A showed weak activity with IC50 (Half Maximal Inhibitory Concentration) values of 48.4 and 34.2 μM, respectively. Furthermore, any metabolites showed antimalarial or antimycobacterial activity against Plasmodium falciparum, K1 strain or Mycobacterium tuberculosis, respectively [20].

Cytochalasin isolated from Aspergillus sp. PSU-RSPG185 and Aspergillus sclerotioniger

Cytochalasins isolated from Aspergillus sclerotioniger

From another species of Aspergillus, A. sclerotioniger, sclerotionigrin A and B (3 and 4, Fig. 2) were isolated together with the known proxiphomin (5, Fig. 2). The cytotoxicity of compounds 3–5 against CLL (Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia) cells was tested in vitro using a CellTiter-Glo® assay. Compound 5 showed the strongest toxicity with LC50 (Lethal Concentration, 50%) values of ca. 48 μM whereas no effect was found towards healthy B-cells in concentrations < 100 μM. Compounds 3 exhibited minor activity at a concentration of 72 μM, while compound 4 was no toxic. This resulted indicated that the novel stereochemistry in the central ring system of compounds 3 and 4 negatively affected on target interactions [21].

Cytochalasin isolated from Aspergillus flavipes

From Aspergillus flavipes were isolated several new cytochalasins as asperchalasines A-D, (6–9, Fig. 3). Asperchalasine A (6) was the first example of a cytochalasan dimer generated by the fusion of two cytochalasan moieties to epicoccine (10, Fig. 3), resulting an unprecedented reported 13-oxatetracyclo [7.2.1.12, 5.01,6] tridec-8,12-dione core containing 20 chiral centers. Asperchalasines A-D (7–9) were assayed for their cytotoxic activity, and cytochalasin 9 was further studied for its cell cycle regulatory activity. In fact, it caused significant G1-phase cell cycle arrest in four cancer cell lines while have not effect on two normal cell lines [22]. Successively, epicochalasines A and B (11 and 12, Fig. 3), were isolated from the same fungus. These two cytochalasins were the first merocytochalasans bearing caged epicoccine (10, Fig. 3) dimer unit coupled with aspochalasin and this group was named epicochalasine. Compounds 11 and 12 were studied for their effect on the inhibition of proliferation of HL6 and NB4 leukemia cells and both epocochalasins showed significant G2/M phase cell arrest. Furthermore, cytochalasins 11 and 12 induced apoptosis in leukemia cells through the activation of caspase-3 and the degradation of PARP (Poly ADP-ribose polymerase) [23].

Cytochalasins isolated from Aspergyllus flavipes

From the same strain QCS12 of A. flavipes collected in China asperflavipines A and B (13 and 14, Fig. 3), two new structurally complex merocytochalasans, were later isolated Compound 13, which was the first dimeric cytochalasan, showed moderate cytotoxic towards seven cancer cell lines, with IC50 values ranging from 12.7 to 27.6 μM, while compound 14 was inactive up to 40 μM. Cytochalasin 13 induced apoptosis in these cells as was confirmed by successive flow cytometry analysis of cells treated. In addition, compound 13 altered the expression levels PARP and cleaved caspase-3. All these experiments were performed using Jurkat, NB4, and HL60 cells as models. Asperflavipines A (13) caused cell shrinking and severe blebbing of the plasma membrane suggesting that this cytochalasin probably caused apoptosis in these leukemia cells through the activation of caspase-3 and degradation of PARP [24]. From the culture filtrates of the same fungus A. flavipes, aspergilasines A-D (15–18, Fig. 3) four new merocytochalasans, were also isolated. Aspergilasines B-D (16–18) could be key biosynthetic intermediates of epicochalasine B [25].

From the solid culture of another strain of the same fungus, flavichalasines A and B (19 and 20, Fig. 3), flavichalasines C-E (21–23, Fig. 3), and flavichalasines F-M (24–31, Fig. 3), two tetracyclic, three pentacyclic, and eight tricyclic new cytochalasans, respectively, were isolated together with eight known analogues. The latter cytochalasins were identified as aspochalasin D, E, H, M, Q and T, trichalasin H, and aspergillin PZ 32-39, Fig. 3). Flavichalasines were assayed for in vitro cytotoxicity against seven human cancer cell lines (Jurkat, HL60, NB4, 231, HEP-3B, HCT116, and RKO) using taxol as positive control. Flavichalasine F 24 and aspochalasin E (33) showed moderate cytotoxic activitiy, with IC50 values ranging from 9.6 to 26.6 μM. The other compounds had no significant toxicity effects. Furthermore, the same two cytochalasins 24 and 33, induced significant apoptosis of HL60 cells compared to the control. Finally, the latter two compounds also altered the expression levels of caspase-3 suggesting that they caused apoptosis through activation of caspase-3 and degradation of PARP [26].

Working with the same fungus A. flavipes but with another strain QM507, collected in a differ China province, flavipesines A and B (40 and 41 Fig. 3) and asperchalasines E–H (42–45, Fig. 3), two cytochalasans with an unusual ring system 5/6/7/6 ring system with a C-18−O−C-21 bridge. And four merocytochalasans possessing a 5/6/11/5/5/6 ring were isolated together with asperchalasines B-D (7–9). Compounds 42, 7, and 9 show moderate inhibitory activities against isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1). This is the first report on the IDH1 inhibitory activities of cytochalasans. All cytochalasins were tested for their ability to inhibit isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) using the compound AGI-5198,15 as a selective inhibitor of the R132H mutantIDH1, as a positive control (IC50 of 0.2 μM). Compounds 42, 7, and 9 showed moderate inhibitory activity (IC50 values of 17.31, 11.66, and 13.31 μM, respectively). To further clarify the inhibitory mechanism on IDH1R132H, a molecular docking study was conducted, demonstrating that the compounds bind to an allosteric site, locking the enzyme in a catalytically inactive conformation. The ligand-binding pocket of DH1R132H has a croissant-dove shape with many hydrophobic amino acids (Trp124, Ile128, Ile130, His132, Ile251, Met254, Val255, Phe265, Val276, and Cys269) that formed a highly hydrophobic shell. Cytochalasin 42 established clear hydrophobic interactions with many of these amino acids, such as the side chain Ile128, Ile130, Phe265, Val276, and Val281, and hydrogen bonds between the phenolic hydroxy of 42 with Ser278 and Ser280 [27].

Cytochalasins isolated from Aspergillus iizukae

From another Apergillus species, namely A. iizukae, lizukines C-E (46–48, Fig. 4) including two aspochalasins and one brasiliamide derivatives were isolated, together with the already known rosellichalasin and cytochalasin Z17 (49 and 50, Fig. 4) Compound 45 was the first aspochalasin having a 1,2,4-triazole moiety joined to the macrocyclic ring. The three compounds 46–48 were tested for their cytotoxic activity against HL-60 and A549 human cell lines using aspochalasin E (36) as positive control (IC50 1.8 and 3.1 μM respectively). Compounds 46 showed cytotoxic effect with IC50 values of 3.8 and 7.2 μM, respectively, whilst cytochalasins 47 and 48 were no toxic up to a concentration of 20 μM [28].

Cytochalasins isolated from Xylaria species

Fungi of the genus Xylaria are widespread from temperate to tropical zones of the Earth and are commonly found on stumps and fallen branches of deciduous trees. Most species are white-rot-inducing fungi and produce phytotoxic/bioactive metabolites, including cytochalasins [29].

Cytochalasins isolated from Xylaria striata and Xylaria longipes

Xylastriasan A (51, Fig. 5) and ergosterol were isolated from the fruiting bodies of the fungus Xylaria striata. Compound 51 showed moderate cytotoxicity against HEPG2, B16 and A549 cell lines with IC50 values of 93.61, 85.61 and 91.58 μM, respectively. Ergosterol potentiated pentobarbital-induced sleep by not only increasing the number of falling asleep and prolonging sleeping time but also reducing sleep latency at a dosage of 5 mg/kg [30]. Later, from Xylaria longipes cytochalasin C, 6,12-epoxycytochalasin D, 6-epi-cytochalasin P, 7-O-acetylcytochalasin P, 7-oxo-cytochalasin C, 12-hydroxycytochalasin Q (52–57, Fig. 5) were isolated together with zygosporin D, cytochalasin P, 7-O-acetylcytochalasin D, 6,7-dihydro-7-oxo-cytochalasin, 6,7-dihydro-7-oxo-deacetylcytochalasin C and cytochalasin D (58–63 Fig. 5), All cytochalasins were tested for their cytotoxicity against human cancer cell lines HL-60, A549, SMMC-7721, MCF-7, and SW480. Cytochalasins P and D, zigosporin D, 6,7-dihydro-7-oxo-cytochalasin C, and 6,7-dihydro-7-oxo-deacetylcytochalasin C showed cytotoxicity with IC50 value ranging from 4.17 to 37.18 μM. SAR investigation showed that the 7-OH and C-11 substituent are important features for the toxicity against HL-60 cell line [31].

Cytochalasin isolated from Xylaria striata, Xylaria longipes and Xylaria sp. SWUF08-37

Cytochalasin isolated from Xylaria sp. SWUF08-37

Xylochalasin (64 Fig. 5), a new cytochalasin and pentaminolarin, a cyclic pentapeptide, were isolated together with thirteen known compounds, including pyrones, phenols and sterols, from the wood-decaying fungus Xylaria sp. SWUF08-37. The cytotoxic activity of almost all compounds (those isolated lesser than 10 mg were not tested) was assayed toward human carcinoma cell lines (Hela, HT29, HCT116 and MCF-7) and normal Vero cells. The results showed that xylochalasin (64) and pentaminolarin had slight cytotoxicity while the epoxycyclohexenone 4S,5S,6S-4-hydroxy-3-methoxy-5-methyl-5,6-epoxycyclohex-2-en-1-one showed toxicity against all the tested cell lines [32].

Cytochalasins from other fungi

Coryceps taii, is a species of the genus Cordyceps discovered in Guizhou province of China in the 1990s. These fungi, which mainly parasitize the larvae of insects or/and arthropods, was used in folk Chinese medicine or as tonics for hundreds of years and had successively received increasing interest around the world. Indeed, C. taii showed significant pharmacological activities, such as immunomodulatory, anti-aging, antioxidant, and antimicrobial effects [33]. From the ethanol extract of this mushroom, which showed antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo, three new cytochalasins (65–67, Fig. 6) were isolated from together with zygosporin D and deacetylytochalasin C (68, Fig. 6). The cytochalasins 63–65 were tested in vitro against human highly metastatic lung cancer cell 95-D, human lung cancer cell line A-549 and normal hepatocyte HL-7702. Compounds 63 and 64 showed potent antitumor activities against human lung cancer cell 95-D with IC50 value of 3.67 and 4.04 μM, respectively. They had little or no cytotoxicity on normal cells, but showed strong cytotoxic effects on cancer cells 95-D when compared with cisplatin used as positive control [34].

Cytochalasins isolated from other fungal genera

The genus Diaporthe (sexual form of Phomopsis) includes many plant pathogens that cause various diseases in host plants, such as cankers, blights, root rots, fruit rots and leaf spots [35] Previous chemical studies have shown that Diaporthe or Phomopsis species could produce a variety of bioactive metabolites, such as cytotoxic sesquiterpenoids, cytotoxic xanthone dimers and azaphyllones, antibacterial triterpenoids and anti-inflammatory cytochalasins [36]. From the fungal solid culture organic extract of Diaporthe sp. GZU-1021D diaporthichalasins A-C (69–71, Fig. 6) were isolated together with the already known phomopsichalasin G, 21-O-deacetyl-l-696,474 and cytochalasin H (72–74, Fig. 6), biatriosporin N, phomopsichin B, penialidin A and phomoxanthone A from the culture of the fungus. All compounds were tested for their inhibitory activities against LPS-activated NO production in RAW 264.7 cells, using indomethacin as positive control (IC50 29.7 μM). 21-O-Deacetyl-l-696,474, cytochalasin H, biatriosporin N, phomopsichin B, penialidin A and phomoxanthone A inhibited NO production with IC50 values ranging from 1.94 to 16.5 μM. The results showed that diaporthichalasins A-C and phomopsichalasin G, 21-O-deacetyl-l-696,474 and cytochalasin H, which shared the same cytochalasin skeleton, had different inhibitory activity and this is interesting in the SAR point of view. In fact, the monosubstituted benzene ring could play a role in the antiinflammatory activity as resulted by comparison of the structures of diaporthichalasins B (no inhibitory activity) and 21-O-deacetyl-l-696,474 (IC50 7.35 mM). The hydroxyl and the acetyl group at C-18 and C-21, respectively, present in cytochalasin H (IC50, 1.94 μM; CC50 (50% cytotoxic concentration), 3.84 μM) should increase the bioactivity and cytotoxicity as resulted by comparison to those of 21-O-deacetyl-l-696,474 [36].

Perennisubacins A-C (75–77, Fig. 6), together with the already known cytochalasin Z2, cytochalasin B and its 21,22-dihydroderivative (78–80, Fig. 6) were isolated from the fungus Perenniporia subacida, which is one of the most common and widespread polypore in Asia, Europe, and North America [37]. Cytochalasins 75 and 77 showed cytotoxicity against SMMC-7721 and A-549 cell lines with IC50 value of 23.3 and 37.6 μM, respectively. Furthermore, compound 75 inhibited nitric oxide (NO) production with an IC50 value of 18.4 μM [38].

Alternaria alternata belong to a special ecological niches, which appeared interessting for their ability to produce different bioactive secondary metabolites [39] Alternariasin A (81, Fig. 6), a new cytochalasin, was isolated together with altertoxin I, altertoxin II, tenuazonic acid, aloesol and alternariol from the solid cultures of the Midui Glacier-derived fungus. All the compounds were tested for antibacterial activity against S. aureus, B. subtilis, S. pneumoniae and E. coli. Cytochalasin 81 showed toxicity against S. aureus, B. subtilis and E. coli with MIC values of 16.7, 16.7 and 33.3 μΜ, respectively, while ampicillin and gentamicin, used as positive controls, showed MIC values of 0.06, 1.28 and 2.09 μΜ, respectively. The other compounds were not toxic even at 80 μM. All the compounds were also assayed for cytotoxic activity toward five human cancer cell lines, Hela, HepG2, A549, MCF-7, HCT116 and one human glioma cell lines SHSY5Y. Only altertoxin II showed cytotoxicity against all the cell lines tested, showing IC50 values of 6.3, 7.6, 12.7, 17.6, 5.4 and 6.3 µM, respectively, while cisplatin, used as positive control, exhibited IC50 values of 14.1, 15.8, 17.9, 18.2, 9.5 and 12.7 µM [40].

Zopfiellasins A-D (82–85, Fig. 6), two new pairs of epimeric cytochalasins, were isolated from Zopfiella sp. a fungus associated to kiwi and collected during a screening aimed to find anti Pseudomonas syringae pv. actinidiae (Psa). The latter is the most devastating and distructive pathogen of kiwi orchard [41]. Only zopfiellasins A and C (82 and 84) inhibited Psa (MIC (Minimum Inhibitory Concentration) values of 25 and 50 g/mL, respectively). These results suggested that the configuration of C-19 might play an important role in the antibacterial ability. In fact, the opposite S stereochemistry of C-19 in compounds 83 and 85 determined the lacking of any toxicity [42].

The fungus Arthrinium arundinis, obtained from the root of Aconitum brevicalcaratum (Ranunculaceae), was known to produce cytochalasans and alkaloids such as 4-hydroxy-2-pyridone alkaloids [43]. In fact, arundisins A-F (86–91, Fig. 6), four new cytochalasins, were isolated together with to several known cytochalasins from the organic extract of fungal solid culture. Arthriniumnins A-D, as well as five known cytochalasin analogues were also isolated from the same fungus but when it was collected from the sponge Phakellia fusca as reported in the section “2.3 Cytochalasins from marine organism”. Cytochalsins 86–91 were tested for their cytotoxic activity against five cell lines, inhibitory effect on nitric oxide production in LPS-activated RAW264.7 macrophages and antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, B. subtilis, C. albicans and E. coli. Only compounds 86–88 showed cytotoxic activities against five cell lines, and were more cytotoxic to MCF-7 than the positive control (cisplatin, IC50 23.96 ± 1.02 μM). Furthermore, arundisins F (91) had toxicity towards against E. coli (MIC 8.00 μg/mL) while compound 87 showed moderate antifungal activity against C. albicans (MIC of 32.00 μg/mL). All the cytochalasins had no inhibitory effects on nitric oxide production in LPS-activated RAW264.7 macrophages [44].

Boerechalasins A-G (92–98, Fig. 6) were isolated, together with cytochalasin B from the solid culture of the fungus Boeremia exigua, which caused different diseases on various plants such as Luobuma plants and Morus malba in China, Fraxinus excelsior in Poland, Hydrangea paniculate in Italy, Cichorium intybus var. sativum in Chile and Cycas circinalis in India [45]. An unusual sulfoxide moiety, which might be derived from methionine, and an unusual 5-methylcyclohexane-1,2,3-triol substituent at N-2 position, were present in both boerechalasins F and G (97 and 98), respectively. Cytochalasins 92–98 were tested for their cytotoxic activity and compound 97 showed cytotoxicity against MCF-7 (IC50 value of 22.8 μM) while compounds 92 and 96 exhibited anti-inflammatory activity (IC50 values of 21.9, and 5.7 μM, respectively). These results suggested that the OH group at C-4′, present in both cytochalsins could cause their inability to inhibit NO production [46].

Cytochalasins from endophytic fungi

Endophytic fungi, like bacteria, live essentially in all plant species, but not exclusively, without causing harmful effects to their hosts. They promote plant growth by improving nutrient exchange and enzymatic activity. Therefore, they have a significant impact on the physiological processes that take place within their hosts by synthesizing a wide range of secondary metabolites. These metabolites, that include hundreds cytochalasins, often exhibit diverse biological activities and potential applications in diverse fields such as agriculture and medicine [15, 16, 47, 48]

Cytochalasin from Trichoderma spp.

The genus Trichoderma includes fungi capable of colonizing a wide variety of substrates under diverse environmental conditions. They can survive as saprophytes on plant debris and, in some cases, colonize living plant tissue. This ability is due in part to their ability to produce various secondary metabolites, including antimicrobial compounds that can enhance their antagonistic activity as biocontrol agents for crop diseases caused by other fungi [49].

From the endophytic fungus Trichoderma gamsii. collected from Panax notoginseng, a plant used in Chinese folk medicine, trichalasins E, F and H (99–101, Fig. 7), three new cytochalasins, were isolated together with the already known analogues trichalasins C and G, and aspochalasin K (102 and 103 and 104, Fig. 7). Trichalasins E (99) contains a unique hydroperoxyl group and this is the first report among all known analogues. All compounds were tested for their inhibitor activity toward the A549, MDA-MB-231 and PANC-1 cancer cell lines and trichalasin G (103) and aspochalasin K (104) showed moderate activity against MDA-MB-231 cell line (IC50 value of 60.6 μM) [50].

Cyochalasins isolated from Trichoderma species

4'-Hydroxy-deacetyl-18-deoxycytochalasin H, deacetyl-18-deoxycytochalasin H 18-deoxycytochalasin H (105–107, Fig. 7) were isolated from the endophytic fungus Trichoderma harzianum, which was obtained from leaves of Cola nitida. This plant as other Cola species was used for preparation of caffeine. All compounds isolated were tested for their cytotoxicity against L5178Y, A2780 sens, and A2780 CisR cancer cell lines. Cytochalasin 107 showed the strongest activity against all the three cancer cells with IC50 values of 0.19, 0.42, and 6.55 μΜ, respectively. In the SAR point of view the deacetylation of the hydroxy group at C-21, as occurred in compounds 105 and 106, caused a significant decrease of the activity, suggesting that the acetate group plays an important role to impart cytotoxicity. The well known lethal metabolisms could explain this results as the acetate group enhanced the membrane permeation and it could be then hydrolized. Furthermore, the presence of an additional hydroxyl in the aromatic ring of 105 affect the activity of this compound [51].

Cytochalasins isolated from Xylaria spp.

Xylaria species are world spread fungi as above cited as well as also as endophytes of different organisms.

From Xylaria sp., an endophyte fungus collected from Toona sinensis, which is a plant known as Chinese toon and belonging to the family of the Meliaceae used in popular medicine for its anticancer and anti-inflammatory effects, were isolated Cytochalasin Z27 and cytochalasin Z28, (108 and 109, Fig. 8). From the same fungus the already known seco-cytochalasin E, cytochalasin Z18, and cytochalasin E, (110–112, Fig. 8) were isolated too [52]. Cytochalasin E (112) showed strong cytotoxic activity against brine shrimp (LC50 2.79 μM), comparable to that of the positive control toosendanin (LC50 4.03 μM). The same cytochalasin, at concentrations in the range of 10–80 μM, also exhibited higher phytotoxic effects on Lactuca sativa and Raphanus sativus L. seedlings than that of the positive control glyphosate, which is a broad-spectrum systemic herbicide in agriculture. On the contrary, all the other compounds have not shown any effect on both seedlings even at concentration of 100 μM. These results showed again that a carbonate vinyl of the 13 members macrocyclic ring could be important in determining phytotoxic activity. The MIC values of other compounds were larger than 32 μg/mL. These results suggested that the methyl group at C-6 (12-nor-cytochalasin F vs. cytochalasin F) and the hydroxy groups at C-19 and C-20 (20-deoxycytochalasin B vs. cytochalasin B and 19-hydroxycytochalasin B) could be structural features increasing the antibacterial activity. Furthermore, cytochalasins B and F, 19-hydroxycytochalasin B and 20-deoxycytochalasin B, which were the main metabolites, were also tested for their cytotoxicity against three tumor HCT-116, HepG-2, and MCF-7 cell lines. Cytochalasins B and F, and 19-hydroxycytochalasin B showed cytotoxic activity toward all tested cell lines with IC50 values ranging from 5.2 to 12 μM, while 20-deoxycytochalasin B was not toxic suggesting that the hydroxy groups at C-19 and C-20 significantly increased cytotoxicity (19-hydroxycytochalasin B and cytochalasin B vs. 20-deoxycytochalasin B) [121, 122].

Successively, from the same fungus 6β,7β-epoxydeoxaphomin C, 12-hydroxydeoxaphomin C, and 24-nor-cytochalasin B (352–354, Fig. 19), were isolated together with deoxaphomin C (355, Fig. 19) and proxiphomin. All cytochalasins were tested for their antibacterial activities against one human (E. coli) and nine aquatic pathogenic bacteria, namely Aeromonas hydrophilia, E. ictarda, E. tarda, Micrococcus luteus, P. aeruginosa, Vibrio lginolyticus, V. anguillarum, V. harveyi, and V parahaemolyticus), 6β,7β-Epoxydeoxaphomin C showed moderate antibacterial activity against aquatic pathogens E. ictarda and E. tarda (each with a MIC of 32 μg/mL), while deoxaphomin C exhibited strong activities against E. coli and V. harveyi (MIC of 2 and 8 μg/mL, respectively). Proxiphomin showed strong activities against E. coli, V harveyi, and V. parahaemolyticus (MIC values of 8, 4, and 1 μg/mL, respectively). These results suggested that the hydroxylation at C-20 reduced the antibacterial activities (24-nor-cytochalasin B vs. proxiphomin), while hydroxylation at both C-12 and C-20 induced the total loss of activity (12-hydroxydeoxaphomin C vs. 24-nor-cytochalasin B and proxiphomin) [122, 123].

Asporychalasin (356, Fig. 19), a new cytochalasin, was isolated from the marine fungus Aspergillus oryzae, collected from the Red Sea sediments collected off Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Cytochalasin 356 exhibited moderate antiproliferative activity against three human cancer cell lines as lung carcinoma (A549), liver carcinoma (HepG2), and breast carcinoma (MCF7) (IC50 of 8.8 ± 0.4, 7.4 ± 0.2, and 8.3 ± 0.3 values μg/mL, respectively). When was tested asporychalasin (356) on Zebrafish embryos in vivo showed a LC50 (the concentration required to kill 50% of embryos) value, which was higher than 1 mg/mL, suggesting its selective activity on cancer cell lines [123, 124].

From coastal saline soil-derived fungus Aspergillus flavipes RD-13, two new Cytochalasins, named peniotrinins A and B, (357 and 358, Fig. 19), which were macrolactone ring opened derivatives of cytochalasin Z8 and Z7, respectively, were isolated together with cytochalasins Z18 (359, Fig. 19), of which was determined the absolute configuration, cytochalasins E, Z7 and Z8, 6,7-epoxycytochalasin Z8 (360, Fig. 19), cytochalasin Z13, 6,7-epoxycytochalasin Z8, 10-phenyl-[12]-cytochalasin Z9, cytochalasin Z10 and cytochalasin Z12 (360–363, Fig. 19) All cytochalasins were assayed for their cytotoxic activities against NSCLC cells A549, H1299 and H520. Cytochalasins E, Z7, Z8 and 6,7-epoxycytochalasin Z8 were cytotoxic to all these three cancer cells with cytochalasin E being the strongest inhibitor with IC50 values ranging from 0.15 to 0.43 μg/mL. 6,7-Epoxycytochalasin Z8 exhibited significant cytotoxicities toward A549 and H520 (IC50 values of 4.8 and 0.6 μg/mL) while cytochalasins Z7 and Z8 were found to be less active and seco-cytochalasin 358 and 10-phenyl-[12]-cytochalasin Z9 showed weak inhibitions toward A549 and H520 (IC50 values ranging from 21.7 to 40.4 μg/mL). Cytochalasin Z10 only showed cytotoxicity against H520 with the IC50 value of 21.0 μg/mL. However, compounds 357, Cytochalsin Z12, Z13 and Z18, were noncytotoxic against all these three selected cells. These results suggested that the open chain could decrease the cytotoxicities sharply when comparing the cytotoxic data of compounds 357 and cytochalasin Z7, as well as cytochalasin E and Z18 [124, 125].

Vercytochalsins A and B (364 and 365, Fig. 19) were isolated from C. verruculosa above cited as producer of other cytochalasins. Both cytochalasins 364 and 365 were tested for their angiotensin-I-onverting enzyme (ACE) inhibition using captopril as positive control (IC50 15.0 nmol/L), and compound 364 appeared to be a strongest inhibitor (IC50 value 505 nmol/L) compared to the positive control. ACE is regarded as a key enzyme that regulates blood pressure and translates angiotensin I into an-giotensin II in the human circulating or endocrine system. In the antibacterial screening, cytochalasin 365 had potent inhibitory activity against aquatic pathogenic bacteria V. anguillarum, V. harveyi, and V. parahaemolyticus (MIC values 32, 8 and 4 μg/mL, respectively). These results suggested that compound 365 has potential to be a broad-spectrum antibiotic against aquatic pathogenic bacteria in the aquapathogens of the genus Vibrio [125, 126].

From the solid culture of Peniophora sp. SCSIO41203, isolate from a soft coral sample near Weizhou Island (Guangxi, Beihai, China), peniotrinins A and B (366 and 367, Fig. 19), a chetoglobusin (368, Fig. 19), peniotrinins C-E and peniotrinin F, citrinine tetramic acid derivatives, were isolated together with dicitrinin A, phenol A acid, dihydrocitrinone, dihydrocitrinin, (3S, 4S) sclerotinin, tumonoic acids K and L, and alteamide Cytochalasins 366 and 367 and the chetotoglobusin (368) were tested for their anti-prostate cancer activity using 22Rv1 cell lines, which are PCa cells that express the androgen receptor. Peniotrinins A and B (366 and 367), showed weak activity at 10 µM, peniotrinin C inhibited PC-3 cell viability (IC50 of 16.58 ± 0.25 µM). Compound 368 also inhibited cell viability of another PCa cell (DU145), which does not express an androgen receptor (IC50 of 55.92 ± 2.35 µM). Finally, the same chetoglobusin tested on the cell viability of HeLa, a cervical cancer cell, showed a reduced activity (IC50 of 75.51 ± 3.58 µM) [126, 127].

Cytochalasins from other sources

The results reported in the previous three sections confirmed those reported by Skellam [10] on the almost exlusive fungal origin of cytochalasains. In fact, from in deep literature research there are not cytochalasins produced by plant, bacteria, insects or other living organisms at leasts in the last decade. The only exception was the isolation of cytochalasin E from Pleurosticta acetabulum, a lichen widely present in Europe. P. acetabulum is a common lichen growing on nutrient-rich bark from trunks of broad-leaved trees, Ulmus, Fraxinus, Acer pseudoplatanus and Sambucus. P. acetabulum had a significantc toxicity against HT-29 colorectal cancer cells, which is due to the presence of cytochalasin E. (IC50 value of 10 μM). In particular, cytochalasin E specifically inhibited endothelial cell proliferation in vitro and angiogenesis and tumor growth in vivo and when tested on other cancer cell lines (A549, HeLa, BEL-7402, RKO) showed a decreased activity (IC50 ranging between 37 and 64 μM). Thus, HT-29 cells appeared to be particularly sensitive to cytotoxic activity of this cytochalasin [127, 128].

Some biotechnological applications of cytochalasins

Some cytochalasins have shown potential promising activity for applications in agriculture for weed control as biopesticides, and in medicine, as drugs for the treatment of infectious and noninfectious diseases.

Different strains of Phoma exigua var. exigua, collected in Russia and Norway and proposed as mycoherbicides for the biocontrol Sonchus arvensis L. (commonly called perennial sowthistle) produced cytochalasins. S. arvensis L. is a weed that grows vigorously and forms dense patches and easily spreads for the prolific seed production to infest new area. This plant species is considered to be an important weed in Europe and North America as it infests many habitats such as cultivated fields, roadsides, pastures, rangelands, railway embankments, and lawns. When grown in liquid and solid cultures, P. exigua var. exigua produced p-hydroxybenzaldehyde, cytochalasins B, F, Z2, deoxaphomin and cytochalasin Z3 (369, Fig. 19). Cytochalasins Z2 and Z3 were previously isolated together with the well known cytochalasins B, F, deoxaphomin and cytochalasins Z1 and T (370 and 371, Fig. 19), from a wheat solid culture of Pyrenophora semeniperda, a pathogen proposed for the biological control of grass weed Bromus tectorum. B. tectorum (cheatgrass or downy brome) is an exotic winter annual grass weed that causes serious losses in intensive agriculture, particularly in winter cereal crops and is also a major problem on rangelands in the western U.S.A. P. semeniperda, proposed as a potential mycoherbicide for the control of this weed, produced on solid wheat seed cultures all the above cited cytochalasins, except for cytochalasins Z1 and Z2 [128, 129]. Cytochalasins Z1, Z2 and Z3, inhibited the germination of wheat and tomato seedlings in comparison with the other above cited cytochalasins and the 21,22-dihydroderivative of cytochalasin B. Assayed on the leaves of both C. arvense L. and S. arvensis L., deoxaphomin demonstrated the highest level of toxicity on leaves of S. arvensis L. Cytochalasin Z2 appeared to be the least toxic cytochalasan on both plants due to the lack of the secondary hydroxy group on C-7, which in the previous SAR study appeared to be one of the most important structural features to impart phytotoxicity [129, 130].

The potential biotechnological applications of cytochalasins in medicine were extensively described in the Sect. 1–3. Essentially cytochalasins possess a potential anticancer and antibiotic activity.

Cytochalasins acts against microfilaments and are used in basic research to understand cytoskeletal mechanisms. However, as previously reported, they possess strong antitumor activity due to their ability to preferentially damage malignant cells, as demonstrated by their minimal effects on normal epithelial and immune cells. Furthermore, cytochalasins also influence the final stages of mitosis, suggesting their potential use in combination with microtubule-targeting agents to elicit a profound synergistic effect on malignant cells. Therefore, cytochalasins could be used to supplement current chemotherapy therapies to improve their efficacy and reduce the prevalence of drug resistance in the clinical setting [11]. To study these synergistic effects, the cell lines EL4 (H2b T-cell lymphoma), P815 (H2d mastacytoma), Z2B (transformed human Epstein-Barr virus), CH33 (H2k B-cell lymphoma), and LS102.9 (H2s B-cell lymphoma) were exposed to treatments with cytochalasin B alone, vincristine alone, and cytochalasin B/vincristine combined. All cell lines showed the highest rate of DNA fragmentation with the cytochalasin B/vincristine treatment. This rate was in fact greater than the sum of the DNA fragmentation caused by both compounds when applied alone, supporting the hypothesis of their synergistic action. Therefore, this methodology could represent a new chemotherapy approach [11].

An alternative methodology to combat cancer diseases could be the use of cytotoxic cytochalasins associated to sonodynamic therapy (SDT). This latter is an ultrasound-based method in which chemotherapeutic agents known as sonosensitizers are administered to enhance the effectiveness of ultrasound's preferential damage to tumor cells. Cytochalasin B is a cytokinesis inhibitor that preferentially increases and multinucleates malignant cells, making them much more sensitive to ultrasound irradiation. Cytochalasin B preferentially damaged U937 human blood/leukemic cells both before and after sonication and also reduced rapid proliferation because the clonogenicity of U937 cells was significantly reduced after treatment. Therefore, cytochalasin B may have valuable therapeutic applications when combined with SDT [130, 131].

As reported in some cases in Sects. 1–3, cytochalasins have differentiated antibacterial activity against different microbial pathogens among these, Shigella flexneri SDINT was the most sensitive bacterium, and Vibrio cholerae SG24 and V. cholerae PC2 were the most resistant ones. The well-known antibiotic ampicillin showed no toxicity against these three bacteria at concentrations up to 512 μg/mL. Some cytochalasins were selectively effective against these multidrug-resistant bacteria. In particular 18-methoxycytochalasin J, cytochalasins H and J showed inhibitory effects on 4/6 (66.66%), 5/6 (83.33%), and 4/6 (66.66%), respectively, of the studied microorganisms. The observed MBC (Minimal Antibacterial Concentration) values indicated that most of them corresponded to the MIC values. Thus, cytochalasins have potential medicinal application against MDR enteropathogenic bacteria, including clinical isolates of toxigenic V. cholerae, the causative agent of the deadly disease cholera, and Shigella sp., the agent responsible of shigellosis. Furthermore, significant antibacterial activity was also observed against S. aureus [131, 132].

Conclusions

The review reports the advances on chemistry, biological activities, mode of action and structure–activity relationships of new cytochalasins isolated in the last decade (2014–2024). In particular, the content of about hundreds articles published on this topic and found by Sci-Finder Google Scholar, Shi-hub research was reported focusing the attention on the chemitry and biological properties of new cytochalasins isolated from terrestrial, endophytic and likenes marine fungi. The results of several SAR studies were also described as well as the advances potential biotechnological applications of some cytochalasins in agriculture and medicine. Considering the large number of fungi, marine organisms, and other organisms distributed across the underwater world that have not yet been studied, and those known to be able to adapt to their surrounding environment by essentially changing their secondary metabolism, many more cytochalasins could be isolated and studied for their chemical and biological properties. The discovery of cytochalasins with new carbon skeletons is very likely to increase interest in this class of natural compounds for their potential applications as above reported.
