Functional dynamics and interactions within the bacterial community responsible for biodegradable plastic degradation during aerobic composting
Authors: Soo Yeon Lee, Subin Hwang, Ian Cho, Hyoyoung Lee, Jihun Lee, Doyoung Koo, Jae-Woo Kim, Kyung-Suk Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Though biodegradable plastics have been widely developed as sustainable alternatives to petroleum-based plastics, their degradation behavior and microbial interactions in composting environments remain insufficiently understood. In this study, the degradation characteristics of polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), polylactic acid (PLA), polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT), and polybutylene succinate (PBS), and the interactions between bacterial communities and functional genes, were evaluated in a 41-day aerobic composting system using anaerobically digested sewage sludge as substrate. Composting parameters were similarly affected by all biodegradable plastics, and the final compost reached a Solvita compost maturity index of 8.0 with no detectable pathogenic bacteria and a CO2 index of 7.83, indicating stable composting. After 41 days of composting, microcracks and microbial attachment were observed on all biodegradable plastic surfaces, with PHB and PBAT showing the most pronounced structural damage and biofilm formation, whereas microbial attachment to PLA was limited. Although biodegradable plastic addition did not greatly alter the overall bacterial community structure, it selectively promoted specific bacterial genera (Symbiobacterium, Paenibacillus, and Psychrobacillus). PICRUSt2-based functional gene prediction revealed that PHB degradation-related genes exhibited the highest predicted abundance, whereas PLA- and PBS-related genes showed low abundance, indicating differences in functional degradation potential among plastic types. Positive correlations among esterase- and hydrolase-related genes under biodegradable plastic-amended conditions suggest that coordinated microbial functional responses to biodegradable plastic addition. Network analysis further indicated that biodegradable plastic addition influenced interactions between specific bacterial genera and degradation-related functional genes. Overall, this study provides insights into bacterial functional adaptation during biodegradable plastic degradation under aerobic composting conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10532-026-10359-x.

Introduction

Due to its long-term accumulation and adverse impacts on various ecosystems, plastic pollution in terrestrial and aquatic environments is a major environmental concern (Wang et al. 2019). Global plastic production reached approximately 413.8 million metric tons in 2023 and continues to increase annually, further exacerbating plastic pollution worldwide (Plastics Europe 2024). In particular, conventional petroleum-based plastics, such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and high-density polyethylene (HDPE), have extremely low biodegradability, thus microplastic particles can accumulate within the food web and pose a significant health risk to humans (Chamas et al. 2020; Leslie et al. 2022). For this reason, various biodegradable plastics, including polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), polylactic acid (PLA), polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT), and polybutylene succinate (PBS), have been developed and tested (Emadian et al. 2017). Although these materials can be decomposed via microbial metabolism, their actual degradation rate and efficiency under real environmental conditions are generally limited (Emadian et al. 2017; Piao et al. 2024). In particular, the biodegradation efficiency of biodegradable plastics has been reported to depend strongly on the polymer type, chemical structure, crystallinity, and environmental conditions (Piao et al. 2024).

Aerobic composting is a promising treatment strategy for biodegradable plastics because it provides favorable conditions for biodegradation via active microbial metabolism and dynamic changes in temperature (Yu and Flury 2024). During composting, the degradation of biodegradable plastics occurs simultaneously with organic matter decomposition and is driven by rapid shifts in the microbial community structure, together with changes in temperature, moisture content, and pH (Piao et al. 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated that the degradation efficiency of biodegradable plastics under composting conditions varies considerably depending on the polymer type. However, previous studies have reported that certain biodegradable plastics, such as PLA and PBS, exhibit limited degradation even under composting conditions, with microbial attachment and enzymatic activity governing their degradation behavior (Cheung and Not 2024).

Biodegradable plastic degradation during composting is driven by microbial activity and functional metabolism (Cai et al. 2023; Omura et al. 2024). Accordingly, increasing attention has been paid to identifying the microorganisms and functional enzymes involved in biodegradable plastic degradation. Recent studies have shown that Thermobifida-derived cutinases and bacterial genera such as Bacillus and Paenibacillus contribute to polyester degradation through esterase- and lipase-mediated hydrolysis (Yang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024a; Danso et al. 2019). However, the expression of functional genes associated with biodegradable plastic degradation, including those encoding esterases and cutinases, can vary considerably depending on the degradation environment and operating conditions (Wei and Zimmermann 2017).

Previous studies have primarily focused on evaluating individual biodegradable plastics or identifying specific plastic-degrading microorganisms and enzymes under controlled conditions (Shin et al. 2024). However, integrated analyses under practical composting conditions, where multiple biodegradable plastics and complex microbial communities coexist, remain limited (Korean standard soil analysis method 2018; Wang et al. 2024b). Moreover, although changes in microbial communities or biodegradable plastic degradation have been individually investigated, few studies have examined how bacterial community succession is linked to functional gene dynamics during the degradation of multiple biodegradable plastics throughout the aerobic composting process (Korean standard soil analysis method 2018; Cheung and Not 2024). Therefore, in the present study, the degradation behavior of biodegradable plastics during composting, the final compost quality, and changes in the bacterial community structure and predicted abundance of functional genes were evaluated in composting systems with and without biodegradable plastic addition. The ultimate objective of this study was to elucidate the functional characteristics of and interactions within the bacterial community involved in biodegradable plastic degradation under aerobic composting conditions and to provide fundamental insights for the effective treatment of biodegradable plastics and the optimization of composting processes. Unlike previous studies focusing on single polymers, this study aimed to improve our understanding of bacterial community succession and predicted functional responses to mixed biodegradable plastics under dynamic aerobic composting conditions.

Materials and methods

Preparation of biodegradable plastics

Four types of biodegradable plastic (PHB, PLA, PBAT, and PBS) were used in the composting experiment. PHB and PLA were prepared in film form and cut into pieces with a thickness of 0.3 mm and a size of 5 × 6 cm prior to use (Ilkwang Polymer Co., Ltd., Korea). PBAT and PBS were prepared in pellet form, with a pellet size of approximately 3–5 mm, a density of 1.68 g cm⁻3, and a melting point of 250–255 °C. The PBAT and PBS pellets were dried overnight in a hot-air oven at 60 °C before use (IndiaMART, India). Because PHB and PLA were tested as films, whereas PBAT and PBS were tested as pellets, differences in physical form, dimensions, and surface area-to-volume ratio may have influenced the extent of surface alteration observed during composting. Therefore, the SEM observations were intended to qualitatively evaluate representative surface morphological changes for each biodegradable plastic rather than to directly compare biodegradation behavior or degradation rates among different polymer types.

Experimental design

To evaluate the degradation characteristics of the biodegradable plastics and changes in the bacterial community during the aerobic composting of anaerobically digested sludge, a laboratory-scale composting experiment was conducted for 41 days with slight modifications to EN ISO 14855-2:2018 (ISO 14855-2 2018). The composting system consisted of two treatment conditions: a biodegradable plastic-amended treatment (+ BP) and a control without biodegradable plastics (− BP), with 30 kg of the composting mixture prepared for each treatment, which was conducted in triplicate. In the + BP treatment, the prepared PHB and PLA films and PBAT and PBS pellets were added to the composting mixture at 0.5% (w/w) each based on the total composting mixture of 30 kg, corresponding to 150 g each (Cui et al. 2025). Thus, a total of 600 g of biodegradable plastics was incorporated into the + BP treatment. The experimental workflow consisted of two treatments (− BP and + BP), each prepared in triplicate, with compost samples collected at six predefined sampling points (days 0, 5, 8, 16, 33, and 41) for physicochemical characterization, SEM observation, bacterial community analysis, and predicted functional gene analysis. The four biodegradable plastics were introduced as a mixed treatment to simulate a heterogeneous biodegradable plastic waste stream that may occur in practical composting systems. Accordingly, the experimental design was intended to evaluate the overall effect of mixed biodegradable plastics rather than the individual contribution of each polymer.

Composting was carried out in cylindrical containers with a volume of 50 L. To supply air, 15 holes were drilled into each container, with five holes on the top and five holes on each side. The composting mixture consisted of anaerobically digested sludge, the bacterial inoculum, and bulking agents at a ratio of 1.5:1.5:1 (w/w) (Kuroda et al. 2023). Accordingly, for 30 kg of the composting mixture, 11.25 kg of anaerobically digested sludge, 11.25 kg of inoculum, and 7.5 kg of bulking agents were used. The dewatered, anaerobically digested sludge was collected from an anaerobic digester at a sewage treatment plant in Gangwon-do, Republic of Korea, with an initial moisture content of 70.0% (Shinhwa Construction Co., Ltd., Gangneung, Republic of Korea).

Swine manure compost obtained from a farm in Yangseo-myeon, Yangpyeong-gun, Gyeonggi-do, agricultural soil collected from a corn field in Yeongwol-gun, Gangwon-do, where mulching film had been applied for more than five years, and tidal flat sediment collected from Sorae Wetland Ecological Park in Incheon were mixed at a ratio of 1:1:1 (w/w) and used as the microbial inoculum. The inoculum sample was mixed with sterile water at a ratio of 1:1 (w/v), suspended for more than 3 h, and then sieved to remove coarse particles. The resulting inoculum extract was then added to the composting mixture as an inoculating solution instead of water (Xi et al. 2015).

To ensure sufficient porosity, aeration, and moisture balance of the composting mixture, a bulking agent composed of sawdust and rice husk mixed at a ratio of 1:1 (w/w) was added to the anaerobically digested sewage sludge prior to composting. The initial moisture content of the composting mixture was 55.0% (Kuroda et al. 2023). During composting, the moisture content of the compost samples was monitored, and when it decreased below 50%, 2.5 L of triple-distilled water was periodically added to ensure that the moisture content remained above 55%. To maintain the aerobic conditions, the compost piles were manually turned every 10 days.

The composting process was monitored by dividing it into mesophilic, thermophilic, and stabilization/cooling stages based on the compost temperature profile (Fig. 1a). To evaluate the degradation characteristics of the biodegradable plastics and the associated microbial dynamics, compost samples were collected at six time points (days 0, 5, 8, 16, 33, and 41). Characterization of the prepared compost

During the 41-day composting period, the temperature, pH, and moisture content of the compost inside the composting system were periodically monitored. Compost samples collected during the composting process were analyzed according to the Korean Standard Methods for Soil Pollution, with slight modifications (Korean Ministry of Environment 2018). To measure the compost pH, 5 g of a compost sample was mixed with 25 mL of distilled water at room temperature for 1 h, allowed to stand for 30 min, and then measured using a pH meter (420A, Thermo Orion, USA). The moisture content of the compost was determined by drying 2 g of the compost in an oven at 110 °C until a constant weight was reached (VS-4150ND, Vision Scientific Co., Ltd., South Korea), with the value calculated as a percentage based on the dry weight.

In addition to temperature, pH, and moisture content, the physicochemical properties of the prepared compost, including the C/N ratio, ammonia (NH3) and carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, and maturity, were measured following procedures described in a previous study (Wang et al. 2025). Briefly, NH3 and CO2 emission levels were determined using a Solvita® Compost Maturity Test Kit (Woods End Laboratories Inc., USA). A 100 g compost sample was placed in a sealed container, and a Solvita® gel paddle was inserted and allowed to react at room temperature for a specified period. The change in the color of the paddle was then measured using a Solvita® Digital Color Reader and converted into NH3 and CO2 index values to evaluate compost maturity and stabilization. Compost maturity was also assessed based on the Solvita® maturity index. The Solvita® index ranges from 1 to 8, with higher values indicating greater compost stabilization and maturity (Wang et al. 2025).

To evaluate the safety of the compost after 41 days of treatment, the presence of major pathogenic bacterial taxa, including Escherichia coli, total coliforms, and Salmonella sp., was determined using the direct plating method (EPA 1993; Bernal et al. 2022). Briefly, 10 g of the compost was placed in a sterile 200 mL Erlenmeyer flask, mixed with 90 mL of sterile water, and homogenized thoroughly for 2 min. The suspension was then left to stand for 1 min to allow large particles to settle, and the resulting supernatant was used as the stock solution. Serial dilutions (10⁻1–10⁻6) were prepared, and colony enumeration was performed using the dilution that produced countable colonies according to standard microbiological procedures.

Aliquots (0.1 mL) from each dilution were spread onto selective agar media, with all experiments performed in triplicate. MacConkey agar was used to quantify E. coli, with purple or violet colonies counted after incubation at 35 °C for 48 h (Jacob et al. 2020). Total coliforms were quantified using Violet Red Bile (VRB) agar, with red or orange colonies counted after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h (FDA 2020). Salmonella sp. was detected on Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar based on the presence of orange or yellow colonies after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h (ISO 6579-1 2017). Surface observation of biodegradable plastic degradation

After composting, all four types of biodegradable plastic were recovered, focusing on film samples with a size of approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm and pellet samples with a size of approximately 2 mm. The recovered biodegradable plastic samples were washed with triple-distilled water and dried at 45 °C. The sample surfaces were then coated with a 5 nm-thick platinum layer using a metal sputter coater (Kim et al. 2026). Changes in the surface morphology of each biodegradable plastic were subsequently examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; G-20, GSEM, Suwon, Republic of Korea) to qualitatively assess representative surface morphological changes following composting. Because the biodegradable plastics differed in material type and physical form, the SEM observations were not intended for direct comparison of degradation behavior among different polymer types.

Analysis of the compost bacterial community

Bacteria in the compost samples were collected during the composting process and analyzed using Illumina MiSeq sequencing (Lee and Cho 2026). Briefly, 0.3 g of each compost sample was transferred into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, and genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using a NucleoSpin® Soil Kit (Macherey–Nagel, Germany) with a BeadBeater (MP, FastPrep 24 Homogenizer), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The extracted gDNA was eluted with 50 μL of elution buffer and quantified using a SpectraMax QuickDrop Spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, San Jose). The extracted gDNA was stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Bacterial community analysis was performed using Illumina MiSeq sequencing following our previous protocol (Lee and Cho 2026). Genomic DNA was extracted using a NucleoSpin® Soil Kit, and the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by a two-step PCR. Detailed primer sequences, PCR mixtures, thermal cycling conditions, library preparation, and sequencing procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods S1. All sequencing data were deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; accessed on 25 March 2026) under accession number SRP686771.

To predict functional changes in the bacterial community in response to various environmental variables, Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) analysis was employed (Wang et al. 2021; Duan and Li 2023). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was used to annotate the predicted functional profiles. The resulting data were visualized and statistically analyzed using Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles (STAMP), and statistical significance was determined using Kruskal–Wallis tests at p < 0.05 (Duan and Li 2023).

Statistical analysis

Heatmaps showing the classification patterns for the bacterial community were generated using R software version 4.0.1. Principal component-based clustering analysis of the bacterial taxa was performed using the FactoMineR package (Lee and Cho 2026). Network analysis of the major environmental factors in the compost, including the temperature, moisture content, and C/N ratio, biodegradable plastic degradation-related functional genes, and the bacterial community was conducted using extended local similarity analysis (eLSA). Local similarity (LS) values and p-values were determined, and the resulting networks were visualized using Cytoscape version 3.9.1 (Lee and Cho 2026).

Results and discussion

Characteristics of the prepared compost

Changes in the temperature, pH, and moisture content during the composting process showed similar trends across all treatment conditions, regardless of the presence or absence of biodegradable plastics (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1a, the temperature initially ranged from approximately 25–45 °C during the mesophilic stage, then rapidly increased within approximately 7 days and reached the thermophilic stage above 60 °C. Thereafter, the temperature was maintained at approximately 60–65 °C for a certain period before gradually decreasing, exhibiting a typical composting temperature profile associated with the transition to the stabilization and maturation stages.

The pH initially ranged from approximately 7.2–7.5, temporarily increased to around 8.5 as composting progressed, and then decreased to 7.5–7.8 in the final stage (Fig. 1b). This pH variation reflected the typical composting phases associated with ammonia production during organic matter decomposition and subsequent organic acid formation (Kim et al. 2022a). The moisture content was maintained within a range of approximately 55–70% during the composting period, with no notable differences between treatments (Fig. 1b), indicating that moisture conditions suitable for maintaining microbial activity were consistently sustained (Piao et al. 2024). Overall, biodegradable plastic addition did not significantly affect compost physicochemical properties.

After composting, the Solvita® NH3 index, CO2 index, maturity index, and C/N ratio of the final compost were evaluated (Table S1). The NH3 index was 5.0 in both the control (− BP) and biodegradable plastic-amended (+ BP) treatments, while the CO2 index was 7.64 and 7.83, respectively, showing no significant difference between the treatment conditions. In general, a Solvita® maturity index of 7 or higher indicates mature compost in which organic matter decomposition has progressed sufficiently and ammonia toxicity has largely been stabilized, thus reducing the likelihood of adverse effects on plant growth (Kong et al. 2024). In the present study, the Solvita® maturity index was 8.0 for all treatments, indicating that the compost had reached a sufficiently stable and fully mature state (Kong et al. 2024; Lončarić et al. 2024) via organic matter decomposition and nitrogen transformation, with limited accumulation of residual organic acids or nitrogen compounds that could negatively affect plant growth (Lončarić et al. 2024).

The similar NH3 and CO2 indices and maturity values observed between the − BP and + BP treatments indicate that the presence of biodegradable plastic did not markedly affect the stability of the composting process or the achievement of compost maturity in this system. The C/N ratios of the − BP and + BP treatments were 18.0 and 27.4, respectively. Because compost stabilization and maturity are generally achieved when the C/N ratio reaches approximately 15–30, all treatments in this study fell within the acceptable range, suggesting that the compost had reached stability (Ji et al. 2023; Kong et al. 2024). The higher C/N ratio in the + BP treatment was attributable to the persistence of slowly degradable polymeric carbon sources (Ji et al. 2023; Kong et al. 2024). Although biodegradable plastics such as PBAT and PBS can serve as additional carbon sources, they are polymeric materials composed of aliphatic and aromatic ester bonds and their degradation is relatively slow compared with organic substrates (Yang et al. 2023). Consequently, plastic-derived carbon may remain until the later stages of composting, contributing to an increase in the final carbon content (Kong et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the final C/N ratio in the + BP treatment remained within the generally accepted range for mature compost (15–30), indicating that compost stability was not impaired (Ji et al. 2023).

The survival of major pathogenic bacteria, including E. coli, coliforms, and Salmonella sp., before and after composting is shown in Table S1 and Fig. S1. Before composting, E. coli, coliforms, and Salmonella sp. were detected at levels of 4.0 × 103 ± 0.5 × 101, 1.5 × 104 ± 1.2 × 102, and 1.0 × 104 ± 2.3 × 102 CFU g-dry soil⁻1, respectively, on their respective media (Table S1 and Fig. S1a, d, and g). In contrast, after composting, E. coli, coliforms, and Salmonella sp. were not detected in either the biodegradable plastic-amended treatment (+ BP; Table S1 and Fig. S1b, e, and h) or the control treatment (− BP; Table S1 and Fig. S1c, f, and i). These results indicate that pathogenic bacteria were effectively inactivated during the composting process (Werner et al. 2023), with the lack of difference between the + BP and –BP treatments suggesting that pathogen inactivation was mainly driven by the composting process itself. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that pathogenic microorganisms are inactivated during the thermophilic phase of composting (Miguel et al. 2022; Werner et al. 2023). The absence of detectable pathogenic microorganisms in the final compost also indicated that the compost reached a stable state and may have potential value for practical applications, including the promotion of plant growth (Álvarez-Alonso et al. 2023).

Degradation characteristics of biodegradable plastics during composting

The surface structure and degradation patterns of PHB, PLA, PBAT, and PBS before and after composting are presented in Fig. 2. Before composting, all of the biodegradable plastics exhibited a relatively smooth and uniform surface structure, with no apparent cracks or signs of erosion (Fig. 2a–d). The surfaces of PLA and PBAT were particularly homogeneous, indicating minimal physical damage due to external environmental factors (Fig. 2b and c). In contrast, after composting, an increase in surface roughness, microcracks, pores, and clear erosion marks was observed on all of the biodegradable plastics (Fig. 2e–h). PHB and PBS exhibited uneven surfaces with localized surface erosion after composting (Fig. 2e and h). PLA showed a roughened surface with visible erosion features following composting (Fig. 2f). PBAT exhibited surface cracks and structural collapse after composting (Fig. 2g). High-magnification images taken at 5000× confirmed microbial attachment and colony formation on the surface of the degraded biodegradable plastics (Fig. 2i–l). Biofilm formation was observed on the PHB surface, suggesting microbial-mediated surface degradation (Fig. 2i). These results suggest that the degradation of the biodegradable plastics involved a microbially mediated degradation process associated with microbial attachment and subsequent surface deterioration. The observed surface morphological changes are likely associated with the intrinsic physicochemical properties of each biodegradable plastic, including polymer composition and crystallinity. Because the tested biodegradable plastics differed in both material composition and physical form (film versus pellet), the SEM observations should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of surface morphological changes rather than as a direct comparison of degradation among individual polymers. For example, the absence of obvious microbial attachment on PLA may be associated with its relatively high crystallinity and hydrophobicity, which can limit initial microbial adhesion and biofilm formation (Ali et al. 2023; Lors et al. 2024). PLA generally exhibits slower biodegradation because its high crystallinity limits microbial attachment and hydrolysis during composting (Ali et al. 2023; Lors et al. 2024). Therefore, the SEM observations were intended only to qualitatively illustrate representative surface morphological changes and not to directly compare degradation among polymer types. Because all biodegradable plastics were composted together, SEM observations and predicted functional genes were interpreted qualitatively as responses to mixed biodegradable plastics rather than polymer-specific degradation. Future quantitative biodegradation and transcriptomic analyses are needed for validation.

Changes in the bacterial community structure during composting

Analysis of the bacterial community diversity during composting revealed that both the anaerobically digested sewage sludge used as the substrate and the initial samples at day 0 in the − BP and + BP treatments exhibited a high amplicon sequence variants (ASV) value (3566.5 and 4049.5, respectively), Shannon index (9.75 and 9.93), Gini–Simpson index (0.9961 and 0.9966), and phylogenetic diversity (217.19 and 240.76), indicating the presence of a complex bacterial community containing various taxa (Table S2). As the temperature increased, the bacterial community diversity decreased sharply in both treatments. On day 8, the Shannon and Gini–Simpson indices were 7.45 and 0.9791 in the − BP treatment and 7.39 and 0.9683 in the + BP treatment, respectively, representing a lower diversity compared with the initial samples at day 0. However, the ASV value remained higher in the + BP treatment (1487.0) than in the − BP treatment (1310.0). Because both treatments were established from the same homogenized compost substrate, the slight difference in the initial ASV richness is considered to reflect natural biological variation rather than systematic differences between the composting systems. Therefore, the observed temporal changes were interpreted based on the overall trends during composting rather than the initial ASV values alone. During the thermophilic stage on day 16, bacterial diversity decreased to the lowest level. The − BP treatment maintained a higher diversity than the + BP treatment with an ASV value of 847.0 and a Shannon index of 6.77, compared with 722.0 and 6.28, respectively. This suggests that specific bacteria with particular functional traits may have been selectively enriched in the presence of the biodegradable plastic. After the stabilization stage, the diversity levels were similar in the final samples on day 41 for the − BP treatment (ASV, 793.5; Shannon index, 6.59) and + BP treatment (ASV, 838.5; Shannon index, 6.85), indicating that the bacterial community had reached stability. It is possible that the presence of biodegradable plastics induces transient changes in the bacterial community structure but does not greatly affect final community stability.

A comparison of bacterial network stability in the presence or absence of biodegradable plastic is presented in Fig. S2. Network stability is generally evaluated based on changes in connectivity following the removal of nodes, thus indirectly indicating the stability and resilience of a bacterial community against external disturbances (Guo et al. 2022; Ye et al. 2023). A decrease in the connectivity with the removal of a node indicates the disruption of interspecies interactions, and a more gradual decline in connectivity is interpreted to reflect a higher structural stability within the network (Guo et al. 2022). This approach has been widely used to quantify the robustness and vulnerability of microbial co-occurrence networks (Ye et al. 2023). As the number of removed nodes increased, connectivity decreased linearly for both the − BP and + BP treatments, indicating a gradual weakening of the overall network connectivity as nodes were removed from the bacterial community network (Guo et al. 2022). When the two treatments were compared, this decrease was steeper in the + BP treatment (slope = − 9.88) than in the − BP control (slope = − 9.18), though this difference was not significant (p > 0.05). These results suggest that biodegradable plastic treatment did not significantly affect the overall stability of the bacterial community network.

The samples collected on days 5 and 8 showed changes in the community structure as the composting system gradually entered the thermophilic stage. Subsequently, as the composting process progressed toward stabilization and maturation, represented by the day 41 samples, the bacterial community structure also stabilized. Patterns of change in the functional gene composition were also assessed (Fig. 3b). Unlike the bacterial community structure, variation in the functional gene composition did not fully correspond to the different composting stages. In particular, the 5d−BP and 16d+BP samples were positioned close to each other, indicating a similar functional composition, whereas the remaining samples tended to form relatively consistent groups. The 5d−BP sample was taken early in the composting process, during which time the system was entering a thermophilic phase, likely activating functions related to readily degradable organic matter decomposition and various metabolic processes (Wang et al. 2024c). In contrast, the 16d+BP sample originated from the transition toward the stabilization stage, during which the predicted functional gene profile may have reflected both the persistence of residual organic substrates and the emergence of functions associated with more recalcitrant carbon sources (Kim et al. 2023). Therefore, the clustering of the 5d−BP and 16d+BP samples in the ordination plot does not necessarily indicate identical substrate utilization, but rather suggests that the overall predicted functional gene composition shared similar characteristics despite differences in the dominant substrates. The clustering of these samples suggests similar predicted functional profiles despite differences in dominant substrates, consistent with functional redundancy previously reported for compost microbial communities (Louca et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2024). It has been reported that, in the presence of ester-based polymeric substrates such as PBAT and PBS, microorganisms harboring esterase-related enzymes can be selectively activated, causing functional composition at specific time points to more strongly reflect substrate characteristics (Kim et al. 2023; Suresh et al. 2025). Similarly, in the present study, bacteria and functional genes that specialize in the degradation of recalcitrant substrates may have been selectively activated in the 16d+BP sample, where biodegradable plastics were present.

Changes in the bacterial community structure at the genus level were also investigated during the composting process (Fig. 4). The relative abundance data revealed that various bacterial genera were relatively evenly distributed within the anaerobically digested sewage sludge and in the initial day 0 samples, creating a complex community structure without a dominant genus (Fig. 4a). However, during composting, the dominant bacterial genera changed markedly over time. On day 5, Pseudoxanthomonas was dominant in both treatments, accounting for more than 69.5% of the bacterial community. In contrast, on day 8, Thermobispora (19.6%) and Brevibacillus (9.9%) were dominant in the − BP treatment, while Pseudoxanthomonas (21.0%), Symbiobacterium (18.1%), and Paenibacillus (6.46%) were the most common genera in the + BP treatment. During the peak thermophilic stage on day 16, the dominant genera also differed between treatments; Bacillus (16.4%) and Tuberibacillus (14.6%) were the most abundant in the − BP treatment, whereas Brevibacillus (25.7%) and Symbiobacterium (18.3%) were dominant in the + BP treatment. Bacillus has previously been associated with polyester degradation through extracellular hydrolases (Danso et al. 2019; Wei and Zimmermann 2017). Although the enrichment of these genera may have contributed to the surface morphological changes observed in the biodegradable plastics, the present study did not directly evaluate their biodegradation activities. Therefore, this relationship should be regarded as a potential association rather than direct evidence of plastic degradation.

Changes in the abundance of predicted functional genes related to biodegradable plastic degradation during composting

Changes in the abundance and distribution of 25 functional genes associated with biodegradable plastic degradation during composting are presented in Table 1 and Fig. 5. The abundance of these genes tended to increase over time in both treatments (Fig. 5a). The initial abundance was approximately 1.90 × 106 on day 0 and gradually increased as composting progressed. In the − BP treatment, the highest abundance was observed on day 33, reaching 3.65 × 106, followed by a gradual decrease. In contrast, the + BP treatment exhibited a transient increase on day 8, reaching 3.21 × 106, decreased on day 16 to 2.13 × 106, and then increased again on day 33 to 3.47 × 106. These results suggest that the presence of biodegradable plastic may influence the overall temporal pattern of functional gene abundance during composting. The abundance of functional genes was further classified into six categories associated with biodegradable plastic degradation (Fig. 5b). Genes related to organic matter degradation, such as K01126 and K01975, maintained a high abundance over the composting period, with a marked increase observed during the middle and later stages of composting. In particular, K01126 increased sharply after day 16 and reached its maximum abundance on day 33, with values of 8.96 × 105 in the 33d−BP treatment and 7.67 × 105 in the 33d+ BP treatment, after which it remained relatively high. These results suggest that the functional potential for degrading complex organic matter and recalcitrant substrates was enhanced as composting progressed. Previous studies have reported that lignocellulose-degrading bacteria and related carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) become enriched after the thermophilic phase of composting, with carbohydrate metabolism the major functional pathway contributing to the degradation of recalcitrant substrates such as lignin and chitin (Liu et al. 2022a; Li et al. 2023).

Genes associated with PHB degradation, including K05973 and K00074, exhibited the highest functional abundance among the biodegradable plastic degradation-related genes. In particular, K05973 sharply increased to 2.21 × 105 in the 5d+ BP treatment and then gradually decreased, whereas K00074 maintained a relatively high abundance during the composting period. This suggests that microorganisms with the potential to degrade PHB-related substrates were relatively more abundant than those associated with other biodegradable plastics during composting. However, the predicted functional gene abundance should not be interpreted as direct evidence of the extent of polymer degradation. Likewise, the SEM observations qualitatively represent surface morphological changes and are not directly comparable among different polymer types because of differences in their physical form and dimensions. Therefore, the predicted functional gene abundance and SEM observations provide complementary, rather than directly equivalent, information regarding biodegradable plastic degradation. Previous studies consistently report that PHB exhibits higher biodegradability than PLA and PBAT because PHB depolymerases efficiently hydrolyze ester bonds, supporting the relatively high abundance of PHB-related functional genes observed in this study (Fernandes et al. 2024; Payanthoth et al. 2024; Paloyan et al. 2025). Genes associated with PBAT degradation, including K06999, K01045, and K18372, had an intermediate abundance. K06999 exhibited a high abundance during the early stage of composting (5d−BP: 2.68 × 105), but its abundance tended to decrease as composting progressed, suggesting that it was transiently activated during the initial adaptation stage.

Genes associated with PLA degradation, including K00016, K00101, and K01055, generally exhibited a low abundance, with minor increases observed at certain time points. K00016 increased to 1.67 × 105 in the 16d−BP treatment; however, its abundance remained relatively low compared with other functional genes. This suggests that the microbial biodegradation of PLA may be relatively limited compared with that of other biodegradable plastics. Previous studies have reported that PLA can be degraded under industrial composting conditions, but its degradation proceeds slowly, particularly for highly crystalline PLA (Oh et al. 2024). In addition, PLA degradation generally requires hydrolysis and molecular weight reduction before direct microbial utilization, which is consistent with the lower abundance of PLA-related functional genes observed in the present study compared with other biodegradable plastic degradation-related genes (Mistry et al. 2023; Lors et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024b).

The PBS degradation-related gene K03930 had a very low abundance, suggesting that the functional potential for PBS degradation was limited under the composting conditions used in the present study. Hydrolysis-related genes involved in overall biodegradable plastic degradation, including K10804, K03928, K08095, and K01046, also exhibited distinct temporal patterns. K10804 abundance increased during the early and specific composting stages, with high levels found in the 5d−BP (2.36 × 105) and 16d+BP (2.34 × 105) samples, followed by a decrease. In contrast, K03928 continuously increased during the middle and later stages of composting, with a high abundance observed in the 8d+BP (2.13 × 105) and 33d+BP (3.23 × 105) samples. K08095 generally remained at levels of 1.0 × 104–3.0 × 104, although its abundance in the 41d+BP sample (3.05 × 104) was approximately 1.2-fold higher than that in the 41d−BP treatment (2.45 × 104). Similarly, K01046 had an approximately 1.9-fold higher abundance in the 33d+BP treatment (6.60 × 104) than in the 33d−BP treatment (3.52 × 104). Because all four polymers were added simultaneously, the observed changes in bacterial taxa and predicted functional genes cannot be attributed to a single polymer. These results should therefore be interpreted as responses to the mixed biodegradable plastic treatment. In addition, although the plastic degradation-related genes discussed in this study were selected based on previous reports and KEGG annotations, their direct involvement in biodegradable plastic degradation was not experimentally validated. Therefore, future studies should combine genome-resolved analyses, transcriptomics, and enzyme activity assays to verify the specific roles of these genes in biodegradable plastic degradation.

Although these genera are not known PBS degraders, their positive correlations with K03930 suggest utilization of low-molecular-weight intermediates generated during PBS degradation. In addition, K07037, which is associated with organic matter degradation, exhibited positive correlations with Terrimonas and Stenotrophobacter, whereas K01126 was positively correlated with Mycolicibacterium (Fig. 6a). K07037 encodes a cyclic-di-AMP phosphodiesterase involved in environmental adaptation (Hejazian et al. 2024). Its positive correlations with Terrimonas and Stenotrophobacter suggest coordinated activation of stress adaptation and organic matter degradation under the + BP treatment (McKee et al. 2021; Foster et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2022). Therefore, the positive correlations between K07037 and these bacterial genera in the + BP treatment suggest that organic matter degradation and environmental stress adaptation functions were simultaneously activated in the composting environment modified by biodegradable plastic.

K01126 is annotated as glycerophosphoryl diester phosphodiesterase and is involved in phospholipid degradation and organic phosphorus mineralization (Marotta and Weinert 2023; Hejazian et al. 2024). This gene is associated with microbial biomass recycling during composting (Amir et al. 2008; El Fels et al. 2024). Mycolicibacterium has been reported to degrade recalcitrant organic matter (Westermann et al. 2023; Hejazian et al. 2024). Thus, the positive correlation between Mycolicibacterium and K01126 observed in this study suggests that the recycling of bacterial cell residues and complex organic matter was actively promoted under the + BP treatment.

K01046 had a positive correlation with Psychrobacillus (Fig. 6a). K01046, encoding a triacylglycerol lipase involved in ester bond hydrolysis, was positively correlated with Psychrobacillus, suggesting coordinated activation of lipase-related functions under the + BP treatment (Cho and Cho 2026; Ko et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2024). Psychrobacillus is a member of the family Bacillaceae that produces extracellular hydrolases, including esterases and lipases, and has been associated with lipid and complex organic matter degradation in soil and compost (McKee et al. 2021; Kondrotaite et al. 2022; Pham et al. 2015; Li et al. 2024). Bacillaceae members have also been suggested to indirectly contribute to biodegradable plastic degradation through esterase and lipase activities (Kim et al. 2022b; Wu et al. 2023). Therefore, the positive correlation between K01046 and Psychrobacillus observed in this study suggests that, under the + BP treatment, the degradation of biodegradable plastic-derived ester compounds was activated and that Psychrobacillus may have been involved in the lipase/esterase-mediated degradation of organic matter and polyester-derived intermediates.

In contrast, the PBAT degradation-related genes K01045 and K18372 had negative correlations with Mycolicibacterium and Planifilum, respectively (Fig. 6a). K01045 encodes an arylesterase/paraoxonase family enzyme involved in ester bond hydrolysis and polyester degradation (Zuin et al. 2022; Bassu et al. 2022), whereas K18372 encodes methyl acetate hydrolase associated with ester compound degradation (Honeker et al. 2023). Mycolicibacterium is an Actinobacteria genus characterized by a mycolic acid-based cell wall structure and is known to have a strong ability to degrade lipids and recalcitrant organic matter (Sun et al. 2026). However, its negative correlation with K01045 in this study suggests that the time points at which PBAT degradation-related gene activity was high differed from those at which Mycolicibacterium was dominant. Planifilum is a representative thermophilic bacterium isolated from compost and has been reported to be a functional bacterium associated with organic matter degradation during the stabilization and cooling stages of composting (Han et al. 2013; Zhu et al. 2021). Therefore, the Planifilum is likely to be more dominant during more stable organic matter processing stages. In this context, its negative correlation with K18372 may be attributable to differences in substrate preferences and the timing of its dominance. These results suggest that the activity of PBAT degradation-related genes may have been higher under conditions in which compost stabilization had occurred to some extent (Liu et al. 2022b; Chen et al. 2023).

In the + BP treatment, genes such as K10804, K05973, K01045, and K06999 had positive correlations with each other (Fig. 6a). K10804, K05973, K01045, and K06999 are annotated as acyl-CoA thioesterase I, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) depolymerase, arylesterase/paraoxonase, and phospholipase/carboxylesterase family enzymes, respectively, and are known to perform functions associated with ester bond hydrolysis and polyester degradation (Kong et al. 2020). These results suggest that, under the + BP treatment, the biodegradable plastics acted as additional ester-based carbon sources, leading to the simultaneous activation of esterase- and hydrolase-related functional genes (Lai et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024d). In particular, polyester-based biodegradable plastics such as PBAT, PBS, and PHB commonly require ester bond cleavage during the initial stage of degradation. Therefore, different plastic degradation-related genes may have been co-activated and functionally complemented each other during the degradation process (Lai et al. 2023).

Negative correlations were more common in the − BP treatment than in the + BP treatment (Fig. 6b). This may be attributed to stronger functional competition within the bacterial community or differences in substrate utilization between composting stages when biodegradable plastic is not present (Thai et al. 2022). K05973 had positive correlations with K01975 and K10804, but negative correlations with K00074, K01046, and Paenibacillus. K00074 is known to be associated with the metabolism of hydroxybutyrate-related intermediates generated after PHB degradation, whereas K01046 is a lipase-related gene involved in the hydrolysis of ester-containing organic compounds (Cho and Cho 2026). However, because PHB was not present in the − BP treatment, these genes may have been associated with the metabolism of general organic ester compounds and storage carbon materials generated during the composting process (Lu and Daniel 2021). In addition, Paenibacillus is a representative functional bacterium known to produce various extracellular enzymes, including cellulases, proteases, and esterases, and is associated with the rapid degradation of complex organic matter during the early and thermophilic stages of composting (Su et al. 2024). Therefore, the negative correlation between K05973 and Paenibacillus observed in the present study may reflect their activation at different composting stages. Specifically, Paenibacillus likely became dominant during the early stages by preferentially breaking down readily degradable organic matter, whereas other bacterial genera harboring K05973-related functions associated with storage carbon or ester-containing organic matter metabolism may have increased as composting progressed (Cho et al. 2021; Gao et al. 2024).

In contrast, K18372, a gene associated with PBAT degradation, had negative correlations with several bacterial genera in the + BP treatment, whereas it was positively correlated with Comamonas, Litorilinea, and Stenotrophobacter in the − BP treatment (Fig. 6b). However, because PBAT was not present in the − BP treatment, this gene may have been associated with the degradation of various ester-containing organic compounds and intermediate metabolites generated during the composting process (Lu and Daniel 2021; Cai et al. 2022). Comamonas utilizes diverse organic carbon sources, whereas Litorilinea is associated with complex organic matter degradation and low-molecular-weight organic acid utilization (Aksu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). Therefore, the positive correlations between K18372 and these bacterial genera in the − BP treatment suggest that functions related to the degradation of ester-containing intermediate metabolites may have been co-activated during the later-stage stabilization of organic matter in the composting process (Xie et al. 2023).

K01026, a PLA degradation-related gene, exhibited a positive correlation with Litorilinea but a negative correlation with Bacillus in the − BP treatment (Fig. 6b). K01026 is annotated as propionate CoA-transferase and is known to be associated with the conversion of organic acids and lactate-derived intermediate metabolites (Wang et al. 2020; Hejazian et al. 2024). However, because PLA was not present in the − BP treatment, this gene may have been related to the metabolism of various organic acids and LMW carbon sources generated during composting (Pettinato et al. 2025). Litorilinea is an organic matter-degrading bacterium belonging to the phylum Chloroflexi and has been reported to be associated with the utilization of complex organic matter and LMW organic acids under anaerobic or low-oxygen conditions (Lim et al. 2023). In contrast, Bacillus is a bacterial genus associated with rapid organic matter degradation during the early and thermophilic stages of composting (Bhattacharya and Pletschke 2014; Lim et al. 2023). Therefore, the positive correlation of K01026 with Litorilinea and its negative correlation with Bacillus may reflect differences in the dominant bacterial taxa and organic acid metabolism functions between composting stages (Sun et al. 2021; Niu and Li 2022; Finore et al. 2023).

Psychrobacillus had a negative correlation with K01066, which is involved in organic matter degradation, as well as with K01055 and K00101, which are associated with PLA degradation (Fig. 6b). K01066, K01055, and K00101 are annotated as acetyl esterase, 3-oxoadipate enol-lactonase, and L-lactate dehydrogenase, respectively, and are known as functional genes related to the degradation of organic matter and PLA-derived intermediate metabolites (Henrissat and Davies 1997; Raggi et al. 2020; Salam 2024). Because these enzymes participate in broad carbon metabolism and are not exclusive to biodegradable plastic degradation, their presence in both the − BP and + BP treatments should not be interpreted as direct evidence of PLA degradation. Rather, the relatively higher abundance observed in the + BP treatment suggests a potential functional response to biodegradable plastic addition, although these genes may also contribute to the metabolism of other organic substrates present in the compost. In contrast, Psychrobacillus is a member of the family Bacillaceae that is mainly detected during the compost stabilization stage and in general organic matter degradation environments. It has been reported to exhibit traits associated with lipid and simple organic carbon utilization (Shi et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024). Therefore, the negative correlation between Psychrobacillus and these genes may reflect differences between the time points at which complex organic matter degradation was active and the stabilization stage in which Psychrobacillus became dominant (Shi et al. 2021). Although the present network analysis focused exclusively on bacterial genera and predicted bacterial functional genes, fungi are also recognized as important contributors to polyester biodegradation because they produce a wide range of extracellular depolymerizing enzymes and often play key roles during the maturation stage of composting. Therefore, the bacterial associations identified in this study likely represent only part of the microbial interactions involved in biodegradable plastic degradation. Future studies integrating bacterial and fungal community analyses will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the microbial networks governing biodegradable plastic degradation during composting. In addition, these network relationships suggest that biodegradable plastic addition may selectively reorganize microbial functional interactions rather than simply altering the abundance of individual bacterial taxa. The coordinated associations between bacterial genera and predicted degradation-related genes imply that microbial adaptation to biodegradable plastics is likely mediated through cooperative functional responses at the community level. Although these associations do not demonstrate direct functional interactions, they provide ecological insights into how microbial communities may respond to biodegradable plastic addition during composting.

Conclusions

In this study, the degradation characteristics of the biodegradable plastics PHB, PLA, PBAT, and PBS and the associated changes in the bacterial community structure and functional genes were investigated in an aerobic composting system using anaerobically digested sludge as the substrate. Although biodegradable plastic addition did not substantially alter overall bacterial community succession or compromise compost stability and hygienic quality, it selectively reshaped the microbial functional potential by enriching specific degradation-related bacterial taxa and predicted functional genes. These findings suggest that biodegradable plastic addition primarily influences microbial functional adaptation rather than community composition during composting. Integrating bacterial community analysis with predicted functional gene profiling provides a more comprehensive understanding of biodegradable plastic degradation than taxonomic analysis alone. Furthermore, predicted functional gene abundance and surface morphological observations represent complementary but independent indicators of biodegradation. From an engineering perspective, aerobic composting maintained process stability while supporting microbial functional adaptation to biodegradable plastics, providing useful information for optimizing composting strategies and evaluating the environmental compatibility of biodegradable plastic waste treatment. However, because the functional gene analysis in this study relied on PICRUSt2-based predictive profiling, it does not directly reflect actual gene expression or enzyme activity. Therefore, future studies should employ RNA-level analyses, such as metatranscriptomics and RT-qPCR, together with direct enzyme activity assays, to further clarify the mechanisms underlying biodegradable plastic degradation. Future studies integrating bacterial and fungal community analyses with transcriptomics and enzyme activity assays will provide a more comprehensive understanding of microbial adaptation to biodegradable plastics and strengthen mechanistic interpretation of biodegradation under practical composting conditions.
