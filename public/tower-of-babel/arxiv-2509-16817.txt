A Comprehensive Protocol Stack for Quantum Networks with a Global Entanglement Module
Xiaojie Fan, C.R. Ramakrishnan, Himanshu Gupta

Abstract
The development of large-scale quantum networks requires not only advances in physical-layer technologies but also a comprehensive protocol stack that integrates communication, control, and resource management across all layers. We present the first such protocol stack, which introduces a Global Entanglement Module (GEM) that maintains a consistent, network-wide view of entanglement resources through distributed synchronization strategies. By enabling real-time adaptive execution of entanglement distribution plans, GEM bridges the gap between static planning and dynamic operation. The stack naturally supports pre-distributed entanglement, purification, and multipartite state generation, making it applicable to a broad range of quantum networking applications. We design and evaluate multiple adaptive heuristics for real-time execution and show that a lightweight scoring-based strategy consistently achieves the best performance, improving entanglement generation rates by about 20% over a globally optimal but non-adaptive fixed-tree baseline and achieving more than a two-fold improvement relative to recent connectionless approaches. Across all scenarios—including predistribution and fidelity analysis—GEM consistently enables lower latency and robust operation. These results establish a practical pathway toward scalable, adaptive quantum internet systems.

II
Preliminaries

This section reviews essential concepts in quantum networking that inform our protocol stack design. We begin by defining quantum networks and the role of entanglement in enabling quantum communication. We then describe entanglement generation through swapping trees, quantify generation latency, and introduce fidelity and purification mechanisms. Finally, we discuss adaptive entanglement generation strategies that decouple offline planning from real-time execution—a distinction that becomes central in our protocol stack design (see §
III
).

Quantum Network (QN).

A quantum network is a distributed system composed of interconnected quantum nodes, where each node is equipped with quantum processing capabilities and the ability to store and manipulate quantum information. These nodes are linked by quantum channels, which facilitate the generation and transmission of entanglement, and classical channels, which are required for measurement-based operations, coordination, and control messaging. Formally, we define a quantum network as an undirected graph
Q=(V,E)
where
V(Q)
represents the set of quantum nodes and
E(Q)
denotes the set of quantum links connecting them.

QN Communication and Teleportation.

Direct transmission of quantum data is subject to unrecoverable errors, as classical procedures such as signal amplification or retransmission cannot be applied due to the quantum no-cloning theorem
[
9
,
10
]
. An alternative mechanism for quantum communication is
quantum teleportation

B

A
and
B
. EPs thus serve as a fundamental resource that enables reliable quantum communication between distant nodes.

Adaptive Strategies for Entanglement Generation.

Recent adaptive routing strategies
[
12
,
13
]
adopt a two-stage approach that separates offline planning from real-time execution. In the offline stage, an offline plan is computed based on the current network topology and resource estimates. This plan defines both the expected link-level EP generation rates and a default swapping schedule.

During the online/real-time execution stage, the actual availability of entangled pairs may deviate from the offline plan due to stochastic generation outcomes. Adaptive protocols monitor real-time entanglement state and dynamically adjust swapping decisions to prioritize available or higher-quality EPs. This decoupling enables more efficient entanglement distribution, improving both throughput and responsiveness to changing network conditions.

Fidelity and Purification.

Entanglement fidelity measures how close a distributed state is to an ideal Bell pair. In practice, fidelity decays due to decoherence, photon loss, and operational noise. To improve quality,
purification
consumes multiple low-fidelity pairs to probabilistically distill one with higher fidelity
[
14
]
. Success is not guaranteed, and the process reduces the overall entanglement generation rate. In our stack, fidelity is tracked as a resource attribute, and purification is treated as an optional layer service that applications may invoke depending on quality requirements.

Purification-Augmented Swapping Trees (PAST).

Swapping trees specify the order in which elementary entangled links are connected to establish long-distance entanglement. When purification is required, we extend this structure into what we call a
purification-augmented swapping tree (PAST)
. In a PAST, additional unary nodes are inserted to represent purification operations: each node consumes two or more entangled pairs and, if successful, outputs a higher-fidelity pair for subsequent swapping. This unified representation captures both swapping and purification within the same formalism and provides a convenient structure for analyzing entanglement latency, success probability, and resource usage.

Prior Proposed QN Stacks and Our Approach.

Several quantum network architectures have been proposed

,

, including designs inspired by classical layering as well as protocol-level implementations. These efforts have introduced valuable abstractions and mechanisms, but most lack the combined capabilities needed for scalable and adaptive quantum networking. In particular, they do not jointly support real-time adaptive routing, purification, pre-distributed entanglement, and multipartite state generation. Towsley et al.
[
19
]
move beyond fixed-rule execution by enabling local adaptivity, but their hop-by-hop approach lacks global coordination and does not scale well in large networks.

A key limitation of prior stacks is that they do not separate
planning
(e.g., path and swapping tree construction) from
execution
(runtime decisions under stochastic conditions). Without this separation, it is challenging to support real-time adaptation. Recent work on adaptive routing
[
12
,
13
]
highlights the importance of responding to stochastic entanglement outcomes and dynamic network state, but integrating such algorithms requires both flexible execution logic and access to a shared global view. Our design addresses this gap through the
Global Entanglement Module (GEM)

Furthermore, existing proposals rarely incorporate protocol-level support for pre-distributed entangled pairs, which can act as cached resources to reduce latency or extend beyond bipartite entanglement to handle multipartite distribution.

By contrast, our architecture provides clean layer abstractions and well-defined interfaces that accommodate both offline and online entanglement requests, enabling diverse strategies such as real-time adaptive execution, use of pre-distributed entanglement, and multipartite state generation.

III
Proposed Quantum Network Stack

III-A

Motivation and Justification

Quantum network services fundamentally rely on generating entangled pairs (EPs) between distant nodes. Because entanglement degrades rapidly due to decoherence, latency and fidelity are critical performance factors. Meeting these requirements requires not only physical-layer advances but also a protocol architecture that coordinates efficient generation strategies — such as optimized swapping and purification plans — and supports the adaptive use of resources.

Our stack design is motivated by the following four key requirements for scalable and robust EP generation:

1.

Plan Creation:
Before execution, the network must create an EP generation plan based on current resources. This includes (i) selecting a path between source and destination, (ii) allocating generation rates on each link, and (iii) defining a swapping order at intermediate nodes. These decisions determine EP latency/fidelity, formalized as a swapping tree
[
11
]
, or a purification-augmented swapping tree
[
20
]
.

2.

Adaptive Protocols:
Given the probabilistic nature of entanglement generation, runtime adaptation is crucial. Rather than rigidly following a static plan, protocols may alter the swapping order based on real-time conditions, such as EP availability or qubit age
[
12
,
13
]
. For example, prioritizing older or more reliable EPs may improve fidelity and throughput. Such adaptive decisions
necessitate a global view of entanglement resources—motivating the need for a Global Entanglement Module (
GEM
).

3.

Pre-Distributed EPs:
Leveraging previously generated EPs can substantially reduce latency during execution
[
21
]
. These pre-distributed EPs act as cached resources along entanglement paths. Their effective use requires protocol-level awareness of where such EPs exist and their fidelity—again necessitating a global view of entanglement resources (
GEM
).

4.

Multipartite Generalization:
While many communication tasks use bipartite EPs, advanced applications like distributed sensing and measurement-based quantum computing require multipartite states (e.g., GHZ or graph states)
[
22
]
.
Such states can often be constructed by extending or composing EP-based protocols, reinforcing the need for a flexible and extensible control stack.

4.

A cross-layer module that maintains a best-effort, network-wide view of all active entanglements.

Handles the low-level processes for creating link-level EPs over physical channels.

III-C

Global Entanglement Module (
GEM
)

The
Global Entanglement Module (
GEM
)
is a cross-layer coordination mechanism
that maintains a near-real-time, best-effort view of entanglement resources across the network.
It enables decentralized yet globally consistent decision-making for adaptive execution.

Tracked Metadata.

Each node maintains a local
GEM
replica recording the state of all active entanglements, including creation time, fidelity, age, endpoints, and intended usage. This unified view is exposed to upper layers for planning and execution.

State Synchronization.

Local replicas are synchronized via broadcast updates whenever entanglements are created, consumed, purified, or swapped. Updates carry timestamps to ensure that nodes retain the freshest record, achieving eventual consistency under reliable classical communication. For near-term networks of modest scale (e.g.,
SCY-QNet
), broadcast overhead remains tractable.

Role in Adaptive Execution.

By exposing a shared state abstraction,
GEM
enables adaptive swapping, purification, and predistribution. Critically,
GEM
allows decentralized policies to react to real-time network dynamics without requiring strong global consistency. To our knowledge, no prior architecture has introduced such a unifying module, making
GEM
both novel and essential for scalable quantum networking.

III-D

Applications To Be Supported

Our stack abstracts entanglement distribution into a unified service, enabling both foundational and advanced quantum networking applications.

Foundational: Entanglement Generation and Teleportation.

Entangled pair (EP) generation is the core primitive underlying nearly all quantum network tasks. Our stack supports this functionality across multiple execution modes, including static plans, purification, and runtime-adaptive strategies, with the (possibly purification-augmented) swapping tree (see Section
II
) as the fundamental abstraction.
Quantum teleportation, which transfers an arbitrary qubit state using classical communication and a shared EP, is then provided as a basic service layered on top of EP generation.

Advanced: Distributed Quantum Computing and Multipartite State Generation.

Distributed quantum computing (DQC) connects multiple small- or medium-scale processors to execute large-scale quantum algorithms collaboratively
[
2
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. This places stringent demands on entanglement generation rate and fidelity, both of which are addressed by our protocol stack.
Beyond bipartite use cases, advanced applications such as distributed sensing and measurement-based quantum computing require multipartite entangled states (e.g., GHZ or graph states). Our stack supports these by generalizing EP distribution protocols to multipartite settings, ensuring broad applicability across diverse quantum networking workloads.

III-E

Related Work

Prior work on quantum network architectures can be grouped into (i) stack proposals, (ii) protocol-level designs addressing swapping/routing, and (iii) experimental demonstrations. While these efforts provide valuable building blocks, none offer an integrated protocol stack such as ours with adaptive execution,
GEM
, and multipartite support.

Stack Proposals.

Several architectural frameworks for quantum networks have been proposed.
Van Meter and colleagues outlined recursive layering models
[
15
,
16
,
18
,
17
]
that extend classical Internet principles into the quantum domain. In particular,
[
18
,
17
]
adopts a software-defined networking paradigm and introduced flexible rulesets to coordinate entanglement generation and swapping—however, it supports only static entanglement swapping trees.
Dahlberg et al.
[
7
]
introduced a link-layer protocol for heralded entanglement generation.
Pirker and Dür
[
25
]
propose a hierarchical stack tailored for the generation of multipartite graph states; while valuable, it is limited to a single generation scheme and
does not generalize to unicast EP generation or entanglement routing.
Finally,
[
30
]
is a broad vision work which offers
valuable abstractions and directions, but does not address concrete

entanglement generation.
These efforts illustrate how layering concepts can be adapted to quantum settings and provide useful abstractions for entanglement management.
However, they remain high-level or limited in scope: they do not separate planning from execution, lack a unifying data abstraction such as
GEM
, and typically focus on bipartite entanglement without support for pre-distribution or multipartite states, as shown in Table

Relevant Protocol Works.

Although these works do not propose full-stack architectures, they contribute important protocol-layer mechanisms that inform the design of scalable QN stacks. We group them into four main categories:

Fixed Swapping Trees.

Several works compute optimal fixed execution plans or swapping trees for entanglement generation, but do not support runtime adaptation
[

,
32
,
33

In our prior work

Both approaches are limited to static plans and do not integrate into a full stack.

Swap-ASAP.

Other designs adopt a greedy strategy, performing immediate swapping as soon as two entangled pairs become available at intermediate nodes.
Kozlowski et al.
[

implemented such a fixed-path strategy with deferred Pauli corrections, while Pompili et al.

Adaptive Routing Strategies.

More recent work has focused on adaptive routing and execution under stochastic conditions.
In our earlier work
[

, Sundaram and Gupta introduced adaptive algorithms that select routes and swapping operations based on EP availability and decoherence.
Iñesta et al.
[

modeled entanglement distribution as a Markov Decision Process, optimizing swapping and memory cutoff policies, but at prohibitive computational cost.
Bacciottini et al.
[
19

implemented hop-by-hop swapping with dynamic next-hop selection, but their approach incurs high latency due to per-hop waiting and does not generalize beyond bipartite entanglement.

Purification-Aware Plans.

In a recent work from our group
[
20

, Fan et al. proposed algorithms that embed purification into swapping and fusion trees to support fidelity-constrained generation of bipartite and multipartite states, introducing a level-based structure to coordinate purification across overlapping trees.
This line of work addresses fidelity constraints but focuses purely on algorithmic design without proposing a corresponding stack architecture.

Experimental Demonstrations.

Several small-scale testbeds have validated components of the proposed stacks.
For example, Pompili et al.
[

demonstrated entanglement distribution and multi-layer control across a three-node network, and related efforts have implemented link-layer protocols in laboratory settings
[

]

These works provide important proof-of-concept validation but focus on specific mechanisms rather than scalable, integrated architectures.

Summary of Related Work.

In summary, most prior efforts either focus on isolated protocol layers or propose partial stack architectures limited to fixed swapping or unicast EP generation. A unified architecture that integrates offline planning, runtime adaptive execution, pre-distributed EP utilization, and multipartite entanglement generation remains largely unexplored.
In this work, we build upon our prior contributions, which introduced mechanisms such as swapping trees

IV-A

Application Layer: Request Specification

The Application Layer generates
Entanglement Distribution Requests (EDRs)
for quantum applications. Each EDR includes a unique identifier (
Request ID
) and requirements as described below.

Entanglement Requirements.

For bipartite entanglement, i.e., EPs, the request specification
includes the pair of networks over which the remote EP is to be
generated/distributed, along with the performance requirements of the entanglement request. Requirement types can be categorized into:

•

Generating a fixed number of entanglements.

•

Generating entanglements at a specified rate with maximum achievable fidelity.

•

Generating entanglements at the maximum possible rate while maintaining a fidelity threshold.

•

Generating entanglements at a specified rate and fidelity.

•

Entanglements for Distributed Circuits.
Generating a set of entanglements (possibly, with varying specifications as above), with a given consumption order. This request involves the network determining the “batches” in which the entanglements should be generated to enable their most efficient generation, while considering the specified consumption order. This request type is motivated by the application of distributing quantum circuits, and is referred to as
Distributed Circuit Entanglements (DCE)
.

The above is accompanied by the following parameters, as required.

•

Fidelity threshold
: Specifies the minimum acceptable fidelity for the generated entangled pairs.

•

Rate threshold
: Specifies the minimum generation rate required to satisfy the request.

•

Priority (optional)
: Specifies the request’s priority in accessing shared resources.

These
Entanglement Distribution Requests (EDRs)
are then passed to the Transport Layer, which determines how to process them efficiently while coordinating with lower layers of the stack.

IV-B

Transport Layer: Processing and “Congestion” Control

The Transport Layer sits between application requests and lower-level execution, ensuring that entanglement delivery remains reliable and efficient despite the stochastic nature of quantum operations. Its responsibilities fall into three categories: (i) processing complex or meta-requests such as Distributed Circuit Entanglement (DCE) requests, (ii) formulating pre-distribution plans to reduce the latency of future demands, and (iii) monitoring execution to detect performance degradation and trigger corrective actions, akin to congestion control in classical networks.

Processing Complex/Meta Requests.

The Transport Layer is responsible for processing complex/meta requests, e.g., for
Distributed Circuit Entanglement (DCE)
requests described before, the Transport Layer determines how to partition the required entangled pairs into batches that can be efficiently generated while leveraging the specified consumption order and
without exceeding decoherence limits.
One potential approach to determine batches for the DCE requests is to use a dynamic programming-based method
[
2
,
38
]
that computes an optimal batch segmentation when the consumption order is total.
In more general settings where the EP consumption order is partial or unknown, a greedy strategy can be used to select low-latency batches while respecting dependency constraints.

Determining Pre-Distributed Entanglements.

The Transport Layer not only handles application-driven entanglement requests but also formulates plans to proactively generate
pre-distributed
EPs
[
21
]
between appropriate node pairs to help reduce the generation latency of future anticipated EPs. These pre-distributed EPs are proactively generated and stored between the selected node pairs, enabling them to be used immediately in subsequent entanglement generation processes.
The generation of such plans depends on anticipated requests, which may be inferred from historical application activity, and requires knowledge of the network’s overall state. As such, this functionality is best handled by the Transport Layer. The format of a generation plan for pre-distributed EPs typically includes selected node pairs, the target number of EPs to maintain between each of them, and optional thresholds on fidelity and age. Although these thresholds were not considered in earlier work
[
21
]
, we regard them as essential components of a robust planning strategy.

One potential approach to determining generation plans for such pre-distributed EPs is to utilize the strategies in
[
21
]
, which include a greedy selection method that incrementally identifies node-pairs (referred to as
super-links
in
[
21
]
) along disjoint entanglement paths. In each greedy iteration, candidate super-links are evaluated based on their benefit-to-cost ratio, where the benefit reflects the reduction in expected entanglement latency across all request pairs.
Another approach to determining plans for pre-distributed EPs is to use a clustering-based strategy that partitions the set of entanglement requests into
k
groups and selects an optimal super-link for each group.

Execution Monitoring.

The Transport Layer manages the life cycle and reliability enforcement of long-running entanglement requests issued by the Application Layer. While the Application Layer defines performance requirements such as fidelity and generation rate, the Transport Layer is responsible for ensuring that these targets are met reliably over time, based on feedback from lower layers.
To this end, the Transport Layer incorporates an
Execution Monitor
that continuously tracks runtime statistics such as generation rate and estimated fidelity. It evaluates whether the actual system performance during execution aligns with the original request constraints. These responsibilities of the Transport Layer are akin to the role of mitigating/controlling congestion in classical networks.

Corrective Actions.

If a deviation from the desired performance targets is detected — for example, if the fidelity of generated entanglement pairs falls below the specified threshold — the Transport Layer may initiate corrective actions such as the following.

•

Requesting the Distributed Entanglements and/or Swapping Layer to re-plan the entanglement generation strategy, such as selecting an alternative entanglement route, swapping tree, and/or adaptive execution strategy.

•

Adjusting the fidelity or rate requirements of the request and triggering a corresponding update in the entanglement generation plan.

•

Temporarily suspending the request to avoid wasting resources during unfavorable network conditions.

•

Terminating the request if the performance cannot be recovered, and notifying the Application Layer of the failure.

To avoid premature reactions to transient fluctuations, the Execution Monitor only begins correction after a minimum observation window has passed, defined either by the number of samples (
N_{\min}
) or a minimum time duration (
T_{\min}
). Additionally, a cool-down interval
\Delta T
is enforced between successive corrective actions to prevent overly frequent adaptations that could destabilize the system.

If the observed rate or fidelity falls short of the required threshold, the system responds based on a retry counter
c
, which records the number of corrective actions that have already been taken. It first attempts a simple re-planning when
c=0
, then gradually decreases or increases the target performance thresholds if needed (
c=1,2
). If the issue persists, the request is temporarily suspended (
c=3
), followed by one final attempt at re-planning (
c=4
). If none of these steps succeed, the request is ultimately terminated.

The system supports both upward and downward adjustments of thresholds: if the rate or fidelity significantly exceeds its threshold (by more than 30%), the corresponding threshold is reduced by 10% to free up resources. This dual adjustment mechanism balances performance assurance with resource efficiency as shown in Algorithm
1
.
In short, our design incorporates a hierarchical corrective strategy that escalates from re-planning to threshold adjustments, temporary suspension, and eventual termination.

Summary.
Thus, the Transport Layer provides clean and abstracted feedback to the Application Layer, hiding the stochastic nature of entanglement generation at lower layers. This abstraction enables applications to interact with the quantum network through a reliable and predictable service interface.

IV-C

Distributed Entanglements Layer: Plan Creation

The Distributed Entanglements (DE) Layer serves as the planning core of the stack, translating application- or transport-layer requests into executable generation plans. These plans specify how entanglement should be established–through routes, swapping trees, or purification-augmented structures–and provide the blueprint that lower layers execute in real-time. By separating plan creation from execution, the DE-Layer enables the use of globally optimized strategies while still allowing adaptive policies at runtime. Requests may arrive in bulk to be jointly optimized, or individually to be served immediately or accumulated for later batching. The DE-Layer also integrates pre-distribution plans into its framework, treating them as background tasks whose outputs are stored in the
GEM
for future use.

Handling Requests in a Batch.

The QN stack supports the following strategies for generating entanglement distribution plans for batched requests. A key distinction is whether to iterate over optimized choices for each request in the batch, or to compute a globally optimal structure that jointly serves the entire batch.

Iterative Creation of (Purification-Augmented) Swapping Trees.

To serve a batch of requests, the stack iteratively computes the swapping tree for each request. One potential approach is to iteratively use an optimal dynamic programming-based method
[
11
]
, which constructs an optimal swapping tree for a given EP with minimum expected generation latency by defining and computing appropriate subproblems recursively in an efficient manner. Thus, we process a batch of EP requests by constructing the optimal swapping tree for each request iteratively. After creating a swapping tree for a request, its required resources are marked as unavailable for the remaining requests, thereby preventing conflicts when iteratively constructing trees. The dynamic-programming approach has recently been extended to include purification operations
[
20
]
by constructing a purification-augmented swapping tree for each request.

Level-Based (Purification-Augmented) Swapping Structure.

Alternatively, one can directly compute the globally optimal solution for the batch of requests. One approach is to use a linear programming (LP) based approach, which works by first creating a hypergraph that embeds all possible level-based swapping structures. In the hypergraph, (i) each hypervertex is a potential intermediate entanglement state, (ii) each hyperedge (
\{s_{1},s_{2}\},s_{3}
), for vertices
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}
, is a swapping operation that swaps entanglement states
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
to create
s_{3}
,
(iii) a “hyperpath” is a potential swapping tree, (iv) and a hyperflow is a level-based structure; here, a hyperflow is a “combination” of hyperpaths similar to a network flow in a simple graph being a “combination” of simple paths.
Now, the problem of determining the optimal (maximum aggregate generation rate) level-based structure for a given set of entanglement requests can be formulated as a linear program by assigning flow variables to hyperedges (representing generation rates), with linear constraints capturing network resource limitations and ensuring flow conservation. Solving this LP yields the optimal hyperflow and, consequently, an optimal level-based swapping structure
[
11
,
20
]
. The LP approach has also been extended to include purification operations
[
20
]
.

Iterative vs. Level-Based Structure.

When handling entanglement requests in a batch, the stack can choose between the two strategies mentioned above, based on the following trade-offs. The first approach constructs swapping trees for each request individually, allowing for flexibility and per-request granularity, but may be suboptimal on a global scale. The second approach computes a globally optimal level-based structure, which can achieve higher aggregate performance but is computationally more expensive
[
11
]
. We note that the plan creation step by the
DE-Layer
is done offline, and hence, some computation overhead can be tolerated.

Handling a Single Request.

An individual entanglement request can be processed immediately or deferred briefly for potential batching. To process a request immediately, there are two options: (a) Without disrupting (or deteriorating the performance of) ongoing plans, the system utilizes the remaining network resources to compute an optimal swapping tree for the new request, using the iterative approach. (b) Alternatively, all ongoing generation plans are suspended, and the latest request is combined with existing ones to form a batch. The system then recomputes the solution for the entire batch using the aforementioned batched request handling methods. The overall strategy should be to use the first approach most of the time, while employing the second approach sufficiently often to ensure the efficient generation of requests.

Generating Pre-Distributed EPs.

Upon receiving the plans for pre-distributed EPs from the Transport Layer, the Distributed Entanglements Layer translates this specification into swapping trees/structures.
These plans are assigned request identifiers with a reserved value, i.e.,
request_id = pre_distributed
, allowing the stack to recognize them and assign a lower scheduling priority. This ensures that they utilize only idle or surplus resources and do not interfere with other running requests. Once the pre-distributed entanglement pairs are successfully generated, they are stored in the
GEM
and become available to serve other future requests via entanglement swapping or purification.
In the Swapping/Fusion Layer, EPs with
request_id = pre_distributed
are treated as flexible resources whose
request_id
can be interpreted as matching any active request’s ID.

We support two models for generating pre-distributed EPs.
In the
one-time generation model
, a fixed number of EPs is generated before all requests, and no further generation is triggered once the batch is complete.
In contrast, the
continuous replenishment model
monitors the number of pre-distributed EPs for each selected node-pairs and proactively generates additional pairs whenever the available stock falls below a predefined threshold.

Role of
GEM
.

The
GEM
plays a critical role in enabling pre-distribution by treating these pairs as cached resources that can be dynamically re-labeled to satisfy new requests, thereby bridging offline planning with runtime flexibility.

IV-D

Global Entanglement Module: Support for Adaptive Entanglement Generation

The
Global Entanglement Module (
GEM
)
is the enabler of adaptive entanglement generation.
It provides each node with a near-real-time, best-effort view of all currently available entangled pairs,
together with their key metadata such as fidelity, age, creation time, and intended usage.

During execution, the Swapping/Fusion Layer queries the local
GEM
replica to select which entanglement pairs should be swapped, purified, or discarded.
This shared abstraction ensures that local decisions are not made in isolation,
but are instead aligned with the evolving global state of the network.
In particular, adaptive policies such as
youngest
,
oldest
, or the

scoring-based strategy
relies on
GEM
metadata to prioritize high-value pairs
and avoid wasteful operations on degraded ones.

Synchronization across nodes is achieved through lightweight broadcast updates,
which maintain eventual consistency among local
GEM
replicas. This mechanism is
sufficient for near-term networks where classical channels are reliable and the
scale is modest (e.g.,
SCY-QNet
). By tolerating small, temporary inconsistencies,
the design balances update overhead with decision quality, ensuring that adaptive
execution remains both scalable and efficient.

Overall,
GEM
transforms adaptive execution from a local heuristic into a
network-wide coordinated process, providing the foundation on which all
runtime entanglement generation strategies operate.

IV-E

Swapping Layer: Real-Time Plan Execution

The previous layer generates static/offline generation plans, which are then executed in the network by the Swapping Layer, adapting dynamically to real-time variations in entanglement availability resulting from stochastic generation and decoherence.

Motivation for Adaptive Real-Time Execution.

We note that the swapping trees created by the previous layer are based on the expected success of swapping and purification operations, and thus, only guarantee
expected
performance. However, in practice, one "branch" of a swapping tree may complete far earlier than the other, forcing unexpectedly early EPs to wait in memory longer than anticipated. During this wait, EP fidelity may degrade or the EP may even become unusable, reducing overall efficiency and success rate. Adaptive execution seeks to minimize such mismatches. Below, we begin by discussing our adaptive execution approach for a single swapping tree, and then discuss how it can be extended to multiple swapping trees or level-based structures.

Adaptive Swapping over a Single Swapping Tree.

At runtime, the Swapping Layer essentially adapts a swapping tree (created by the
DE-Layer
) by determining the best ”swapping order” (based on the current network state and entanglement availability in
GEM
)---while still keeping the entanglement route unchanged.

More sophisticated strategies that allow real-time adaptation of the

The tree structure may evolve dynamically at runtime, while the default swapping tree provided by the DE-Layer serves both as a fallback configuration—used when no adaptive swapping decision is feasible—and as a baseline for guiding link-EP generation.

To determine the swapping order at runtime, the Swapping Agent at each node consults the
GEM
, which provides a consistent, cross-layer view of entanglement availability. This shared state enables decentralized yet coordinated decisions, ensuring that local swaps align with the global execution context.
Decisions are primarily based on the age, accumulated latency, and expected regeneration latency of the EPs currently available on the entanglement route.
Since these determinations must be made in real time, the decision process is designed to be computationally lightweight. With that in mind, we have explored the following adaptive policies.

1.

Oldest-First:
Reduces the risk of decoherence by prioritizing EPs with greater age.

2.

Youngest-First:
Favors fresher EPs to maximize fidelity.

3.

Longest-Hop:
Prioritizes EPs that have traversed more hops, aiming to accelerate progress on longer entanglement paths.

4.

Shortest-Hop:
Quickly completes shorter-distance EPs to reduce system-wide congestion.

5.

Score-Based:
Determines next swapping operation based on a score (discussed below).

These policies are modular and can be selected or combined based on network conditions or application requirements. The trade-offs between the above policies are as follows. The
Oldest-First
policy proactively acts on the most vulnerable EPs to reduce the risk of being discarded due to decoherence. However, this may delay the use of fresher, higher-fidelity EPs. In contrast, the
Youngest-First
policy prioritizes high-fidelity EPs to maximize output quality. The
Longest-Hop
policy focuses on “consuming” EPs that have already incurred high generation latency before they degrade. Conversely, the
Shortest-Hop
policy prioritizes EPs involving fewer hops, helping release memory more quickly.

Score-Based Swapping.

In the score-based adaptive strategy, each node evaluates its locally stored EPs and computes a score for each eligible swapping to guide swap decisions. The high-level strategy steps are:

•

The scoring considers three key quantities: immediate benefit, opportunity loss, and memory occupancy relief for swapping this EP pair.

•

These quantities are estimated using both offline swapping plans and real-time availability of EPs from
GEM
, as well as local memory usage.

•

The node selects the swapping operation with the highest positive score for swapping. If no swapping operation with a positive score exists, the algorithm waits for a predefined interval or until a new local EP is generated, and then reevaluates all candidate swapping operations.

Extensions to Broader Contexts.

Multiple Trees or Level-based Structures.

The above adaptive execution strategy can also be applied to multiple concurrently executing swapping trees or level-based swapping structures. In the case of level-based swapping structures solution for batched requests, one approach is to first decompose into multiple disjoint swapping trees. Each tree is then executed independently using the adaptive single-path mechanism described above.

Purification-Augmented Plans.

Beyond adaptive swapping strategies, our protocol stack is also designed to accommodate real-time purification decisions.
The key enabler here is the
GEM
, which maintains global metadata on fidelity, age, and usage. Without such a shared state, purification decisions would be purely local and risk wasting resources; with the
GEM
, they become globally informed and adaptive, enabling nodes to determine at runtime whether purification will improve overall generation rate and fidelity, or whether EPs should be used directly for swapping. Designing effective strategies that exploit this capability remains an open direction for future research.

Proactive Strategy for Discarding Decohered EPs.

To mitigate the impact of decoherence, the Swapping Layer proactively discards EPs that are unlikely to contribute to high-fidelity outcomes. Instead of waiting until the final target EP fails, the system evaluates
intermediate
EPs during execution and discards those that are too aged to be useful. This avoids wasting fresh EPs in swaps with degraded ones, improving both efficiency and fidelity.

Intuitively, EPs near the leaves of a swapping tree are cheaper to regenerate and more numerous, so they are subject to stricter age cutoffs. EPs closer to the root are more costly and therefore granted greater leniency. This depth-aware policy ensures that resources are not consumed by low-value swaps while preserving progress toward long-range entanglement. We formalize this using an age cutoff at depth

IV-F

Link Layer: Managing Link-EP Generation

The Link Layer manages entanglement generation across direct links in the quantum network by scheduling link-EP requests through low-level physical operations. Its role is to translate higher-layer requirements into concrete generation attempts, satisfy target rates and fidelities, and resolve contention over shared link resources. In addition, the Link Layer executes purification of link-EPs as instructed by higher layers to improve fidelity before they are passed upward in the stack.

Handling Link-EP Requests.

Entanglement between neighboring nodes can be established using three main architectures:

•

Meet-in-the-Middle:
both nodes send photons to a central Bell-state measurement (BSM) station.

•

Sender–Receiver:
one node emits photons while the other detects them; however, the longer travel distance for each photon reduces fidelity.

•

Memory–Source–Memory:
a central entangled-photon source distributes one photon to each node, which is then stored in local quantum memories.

Though differing in hardware placement, all approaches rely on precise timing to align detection or storage windows. The Link Layer coordinates these timings, arbitrates access to shared resources, and interfaces with the Physical Layer to execute the underlying hardware operations.

For each entanglement generation request, the Distributed Entanglements (DE) Layer issues link-EP requests specifying desired rate and fidelity. The Link Layer aggregates rate requirements across requests and computes how frequently each link should attempt entanglement generation. It then relies on the Physical Layer to carry out synchronized photon emissions or detections with minimal latency. At the node level, the Link Layer multiplexes hardware resources such as excitation attempts across incident links, ensuring global synchronization with neighboring nodes.

Upon a successful heralding of a link-EP, the Link Layer assigns it to one of the active requests in the pool according to a scheduling policy (e.g., weighted round-robin). This assignment enforces fairness and responsiveness across concurrent link-EP requests. Where higher layers have requested purification, the Link Layer invokes local purification steps before returning EPs upward, thereby providing a tunable trade-off between fidelity and throughput.

Overall, the Link Layer does not define new physical mechanisms for entanglement generation, but provides the architectural glue that maps diverse hardware architectures to higher-layer abstractions. By handling synchronization, multiplexing, scheduling, and purification at the link granularity, it exposes a uniform service to the stack while insulating higher layers from hardware-specific complexity.

IV-G

Physical Layer: Hardware-Specific Operations

The Physical Layer encapsulates the quantum hardware responsible for creating and manipulating entanglement
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, while exposing a uniform interface to the Link and Swapping/Fusion layers. Its key responsibility is to translate scheduling instructions from higher layers into low-level operations on photons, detectors, and quantum memories.

To support link-EP generation, the Physical Layer executes tightly synchronized photon emissions, detections, or memory write operations, relying on shared clocks or optical timing signals. It reports heralding outcomes back to the Link Layer along with metadata such as detection time and confidence values. Device-specific enhancements may be included, such as quantum frequency conversion for wavelength adaptation, heralded quantum memories for reliable storage, or entangled photon sources for pair generation.

This layer also defines the scope of quantum repeaters: repeaters implement Physical, Link, and Swapping/Fusion layers while omitting higher-layer functions, thereby providing entanglement generation and forwarding without application-level logic.

By isolating hardware-specific control and exposing consistent interfaces, the Physical Layer enables the stack to operate across heterogeneous devices and deployment scenarios. This modularity ensures that advances in physical-layer devices can be leveraged without requiring changes to higher-layer protocols, preserving clean abstractions across the stack.

Summary.

VII
Conclusion

We have presented a quantum network protocol stack that integrates a Global Entanglement Module (
GEM
) to enable real-time adaptive entanglement generation, routing, and purification. The design brings together offline planning, runtime adaptive execution, pre-distributed EP utilization, and multipartite state generation within a unified architecture. Through extensive NetSquid simulations, we showed that the stack flexibly supports diverse entanglement distribution mechanisms while improving efficiency and robustness compared to existing approaches.

Looking ahead, the architecture is well-positioned for experimental prototyping. It is currently being considered for deployment within
SCY-QNet
, one of the inaugural NSF NQVL projects
[
39
]
that aims to build a quantum network spanning Stony Brook University, Brookhaven National Laboratory, Columbia University, and Yale University. With approximately ten nodes and several quantum processors,
SCY-QNet
provides a realistic near-term platform for validating scaled implementations of the proposed stack and for exploring its integration with modular physical architectures such as Fig.
5
.

Our results establish a foundation for scalable quantum communications, with future work focused on refining adaptive strategies, extending multipartite support, and transitioning from simulation to deployed testbeds. While
GEM
is entirely viable for near-term networks such as
SCY-QNet
, scaling it to larger deployments will require approximate or hierarchical synchronization mechanisms—an important direction for continued research. To our knowledge, this is the first architecture to introduce a dedicated cross-layer coordination module, making
GEM
a novel abstraction that distinguishes our stack from prior designs. Addressing this challenge will be central to realizing robust, flexible, and ultimately global quantum internet architectures.

-A

Application Layer

The Application Layer serves as the entry point for quantum applications to interface with the network. It is designed to abstract the complexity of entanglement generation, exposing a clean, high-level service API that allows applications to submit requests and monitor their fulfillment, without requiring knowledge of the underlying protocol mechanics.

Protocols.

The Application Layer includes the following protocols:

RequestGeneratorProtocol
:
Allows applications to submit new entanglement distribution requests with complete service specifications.

StatusNotificationProtocol
:
Although the Application Layer does not perform active monitoring of runtime metrics, it includes a lightweight protocol module,
StatusNotificationProtocol
, to receive feedback from the Transport Layer. This protocol listens for notifications indicating whether a request has succeeded, failed, been suspended, or completed. The received outcomes are passed to the application logic via an interface, enabling the application to respond appropriately without querying lower-layer state.

APIs and Parameters.

The Application Layer communicates with the Transport Layer through the following APIs:

Request Generation
:
The Application Layer sends its entanglement generation requirements to the Transport Layer through an API with the following parameters:

•

request_id
: Unique identifier for the request.

•

target_nodes
: List of node IDs to be entangled.

•

entanglement_type
: Specifies
bipartite
,
GHZ
, or
graph
.

•

generation_mode
: Specifies one of the supported generation strategies.

•

rate_threshold
(optional): Minimum required generation rate.

•

fidelity_threshold
(optional): Minimum required fidelity.

•

priority
(optional): Request priority tag.

•

entanglement_sequence
(optional): A sequence of entanglements with a given consumption order (used in DQC).

Request Notification
:
The Application Layer receives execution-level updates from the Transport Layer through a notification API. This interface reports only high-level outcomes for each request, using the following parameters:

•

request_id
: The identifier of the corresponding entanglement request.

•

status
: Indicates the current state of the request, such as
completed
,
failed
,
suspended
, or
in_progress
.

•

generated_count
(optional): The total number of entanglement states successfully generated, if applicable.

•

error_message
(optional): A brief reason for failure or suspension, if relevant.

-B

Transport Layer

The Transport Layer handles complex meta-requests, determines predistribution plans to reduce the latency of future requests, and monitors network conditions to ensure reliable and efficient entanglement delivery.

Protocols.

This layer consists of two core protocols:

RequestProcessingProtocol:

Takes the entanglement sequence from the Application Layer and partitions the entanglements into execution batches (used in DQC).

ExecutionMonitoringProtocol
:
This protocol collects entanglement generation reports from lower layers, each containing information such as generation time and fidelity. It aggregates these records to evaluate whether the actual system performance during execution aligns with the original request constraints and initiates corrective actions if necessary.

-C

Distributed Entanglements Layer

The Distributed Entanglements Layer is responsible for executing entanglement distribution algorithms and dispatching the resulting offline instructions to the corresponding layers on other nodes.

Protocols.

This layer consists of two core protocols:

PlanDispatchProtocol
:
At the source node, this protocol receives entanglement generation requirements from higher layers and sends a request to the destination node, where the appropriate entanglement distribution algorithm is invoked. The selected algorithm constructs the required swapping trees (including purification-augmented swapping trees) or level-based swapping structures. It then dispatches offline execution plans to the relevant nodes, specifying how to perform swapping and link-level entanglement generation.

MessagingProtocol
:
This protocol handles incoming messages from the same layer running on other nodes.

APIs and Parameters.

The APIs used for interaction with the Transport Layer have been described above. The corresponding API for communicating with the Swapping/Fusion Layer is defined as follows. Entanglement generation results from the Swapping/Fusion Layer are reported back through the same API previously described as the
Entanglement Report API
.

Plan Dispatch
:
This API notifies lower layers of the specific offline plan for entanglement swapping/fusion, purification, and link-level EP generation. This API includes the following parameters:

•

Swapping/Fusion plans
: Specifies how the node should perform entanglement swapping/fusion. Each plan includes the
Request ID
, the
Left Partner Node ID
, the
Right Partner Node ID
, and the
Expected rate or number

•

Request ID

•

Purification plans
: Each plan includes the
Request ID
, the

-D

Swapping/Fusion Layer

The Swapping/Fusion Layer is responsible for executing entanglement swapping and fusion operations. For clarity, the following discussion uses
swapping
as the representative example, but the corresponding protocols for
fusion
are similar.

Protocols.

In addition to the
MessagingProtocol
, this layer includes the following five core protocols:

SwappingProtocol
:
This protocol carries out entanglement swapping operations based on plans received from higher layers. It performs a Bell State Measurement (BSM) on two local qubits, whose remote entangled partners reside at the Left Partner and Right Partner nodes, as specified by the
Request ID
. Upon a successful BSM, a correction message is sent to the Right Partner node. While in the case of failure, both partner nodes are instructed to discard their associated qubits. This protocol may also adaptively select swapping pairs at runtime using information from the
GEM
, enabling real-time decision-making beyond the static plan.

SwappingCorrectionProtocol
:
When a correction message is received, this protocol applies the corresponding operation to the local qubit. It then notifies the Left Partner node that the correction has been completed and that the target entangled pair has been successfully established.

SwappingAckProtocol
:
Executed at the Left Partner node, this protocol records the successful establishment of a new entangled pair following a completed swapping operation.

SwappingFailProtocol
:
Triggered by failure messages, this protocol ensures proper cleanup by discarding local qubits involved in the unsuccessful swapping attempt.

PurificationProtocol
:
This protocol initiates and coordinates purification operations between two nodes. At the initiating node, the protocol first sends a purification request message to the partner node. Upon receiving an acceptance response, it applies a CNOT gate to the qubits belonging to the two EPs, measures the qubit from the sacrificial EP, and sends the measurement result to the partner node. At the partner node, the protocol locks the corresponding qubit upon receiving the purification request and, once the measurement result arrives, determines whether to retain or discard the qubit based on the purification outcome.

APIs and Parameters.

The APIs used for interaction with the higher layer have been described above. The Swapping/Fusion Layer interacts with the Link Layer via two corresponding APIs. The first is used to deliver link-level instructions, and is identical to the
Link EP generation plans
and
Purification plans
components described in the
Plan Dispatch API
. The second API, used by the Link Layer to report entanglement generation outcomes, is the same as the previously described
Entanglement Report API
.

-E

Link Layer

The Link Layer is responsible for generating link-EPs between nodes directly connected by quantum channels, as well as performing purification operations.

Protocols.

In addition to the previously described
MessagingProtocol
and
PurificationProtocol
, this layer includes the following protocol:

LinkServiceProtocol
:
This protocol manages the generation of entangled pairs between directly connected nodes. To support diverse physical-layer configurations and network conditions, it accommodates multiple entanglement generation schemes, including meet-in-the-middle, sender-receiver, and midpoint-source approaches. The protocol coordinates timing, control signals, and the necessary classical communication required to initiate and confirm successful link-level entanglement.

APIs and Parameters.

The APIs used for interaction with the higher layer have been described above. The APIs connecting to the physical layer are responsible for issuing low-level control commands to trigger specific physical operations, such as initiating photon emission, applying quantum gates (e.g., CNOT, Hadamard), or performing qubit measurements.

-F

Physical Layer

The Physical Layer implements low-level quantum operations, including qubit initialization, gate operations (e.g., CNOT, Hadamard), photon emission and transmission, and measurement. These actions are triggered by control commands issued by higher layers, enabling the physical realization of entanglement generation, swapping, and purification across the network.

-G

Global Entanglement Module (
GEM
)

The
GEM
is a distributed data structure that tracks real-time information about all active entangled pairs across the network. It plays a critical role in enabling adaptive execution at the Swapping/Fusion Layer by providing visibility into entanglement availability and quality beyond the static offline plan. The
GEM
supports both centralized and distributed implementations. In the centralized version, the module is maintained at a single node and queried by others on demand, while in the distributed version, each node maintains a local replica of the module and synchronizes updates via broadcast messaging.

Protocols.

Two core protocols maintain the consistency of the
GEM
:

BroadcastProtocol
:
Whenever a new entangled pair involving a local qubit is created or consumed, this protocol updates the local module and broadcasts the change to all other nodes.

UpdateProtocol
:
Upon receiving a broadcast from a remote node, this protocol updates the local copy of the
GEM
to reflect the current network state.

Data Structure.

Each entry in the
GEM
records metadata such as the identities of the entangled nodes, associated
Request ID
, current fidelity, age (i.e., time since creation), and memory addresses of the involved qubits. This information allows nodes to make informed, policy-driven decisions for adaptive swapping and purification.

APIs and Parameters.

To interact with the Link Layer and Swapping/Fusion Layer, the
GEM
module provides two types of APIs. The first API receives EP update notifications from these layers, where each update corresponds to a single entry in the module. The second API enables the
GEM
to report the complete list of currently available entanglements to these layers, providing a global view to support real-time decision-making.
