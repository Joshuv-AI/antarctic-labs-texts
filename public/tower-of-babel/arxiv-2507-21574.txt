Distributionally robust shape and topology optimization
C. Dapogny 1 , J. Prando 2 and B. Thibert 2

Abstract
. This article aims to introduce the paradigm of distributional robustness from the field of convex optimization to tackle optimal design problems under uncertainty. We consider realistic situations where the physical model, and thereby the cost function of the design to be minimized depend on uncertain parameters. The probability distribution of the latter is itself known imperfectly, through a nominal law, reconstructed from a few observed samples. The distributionally robust optimal design problem is an intricate bilevel program which consists in minimizing the worst value of a statistical quantity of the cost function (typically, its expectation) when the law of the uncertain parameters belongs to a certain “ambiguity set”. We address three classes of such problems: firstly, this ambiguity set is made of the probability laws whose Wasserstein distance to the nominal law is less than a given threshold; secondly, the ambiguity set is based on the first- and second-order moments of the actual and nominal probability laws. Eventually, a statistical quantity of the cost other than its expectation is made robust with respect to the law of the parameters, namely its conditional value at risk. Using techniques from convex duality, we derive tractable, single-level reformulations of these problems, framed over augmented sets of variables. Our methods are essentially agnostic of the optimal design framework; they are described in a unifying abstract framework, before being applied to multiple situations in density-based topology optimization and in geometric shape optimization. Several numerical examples are discussed in two and three space dimensions to appraise the features of the proposed techniques.

1.
Introduction and Related Works

Driven by the need for raw material and energy savings and enabled by the constant progress of high-performance computing, the recent advances in shape and topology optimization now make it possible to optimize devices as diverse as mechanical structures, airfoils and magnetic circuits from the early stages of the design process. However, the systematic deployment of optimal design techniques in industry faces critical challenges, related to their ability to accommodate the requirements of fabrication processes such as milling, casting and additive manufacturing
[
69
,
72
,
79
,
109
,
114
]
and, closer to the purpose of this article, the inevitable uncertainties over the knowledge of real-life conditions.

Uncertainties in optimal design.
Optimal design is about minimizing a cost function depending on the design via its “physical behavior”, under constraints of the same nature.
The mathematical models governing this behavior are inherently built from parameters: for instance, the displacement of a mechanical structure depends on the applied loads and on the properties of the constituent material (its Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio);
likewise, the velocity and pressure within a fluid are influenced by its viscosity and density, the electric and magnetic fields of a wave passing through a photonic device are functions of the operating frequency and the refraction index of the medium, etc.
In practice, these physical parameters are often subject to uncertainty,
either because they are inaccurately measured (which is typical of the loads applied to a mechanical structure), or because they experience uncontrolled fluctuations over time: changes in the ambient medium, wear, etc. Designing systems that are robust to such uncertainties is crucial, as even minor deviations from the assumed values of the physical parameters during the optimization process can severely degrade the performance of the design.
This sharp sensitivity is strikingly illustrated by a situation exhibited in
[
27
]
, where the best elastic microstructure to withstand a specific loading scenario becomes the worst possible configuration when even infinitesimal perturbations of these loads occur.
Understandably enough, the ambition to predict designs with stable performance under uncertain conditions has inspired a thriving literature lately, see for instance
[
77
]
and
[
1
]
for a recent overview.

Existing approaches for optimal design under uncertainties.
Various paradigms can be envisioned to incorporate robustness in an optimal design problem, depending on the assumptions about the uncertain physical parameters of the model.

•

Worst-case approaches are usually adopted when no information is available, aside from a maximum bound on their amplitude: one minimizes the worst (i.e. maximum) value of the cost functional over all the possible perturbations. Such problems generally incur an intense computational effort due to their min-max bilevel structure
[
52
]
.
They are tractable in a few very particular situations; for instance, the quadratic dependence of the compliance of a structure on the applied loads allows to reformulate its minimization under load uncertainty as a semi-definite program
[
12
,
37
,
59
]
.
In general, however, one has to resort to heuristic, approximate linearization techniques based on the “smallness” of the maximum amplitude of the uncertainties
[
3
,
53
]
.
In addition to their high computational cost, worst-case approaches are often overly pessimistic, as they result in designs showing poor nominal performances, for the sake of anticipating an unlikely worst-case scenario.

•

Probabilistic approaches are naturally preferred in the presence of information about the uncertain parameters, e.g. about their law or some of its moments.
Typically, the expected value of the cost functional, its standard deviation, or a weighted sum of both are minimized. Alternatively, so-called reliability-based formulations include constraints on the probability of failure of the system, i.e. on the probability that the cost exceed a certain safety threshold, see for instance
[
56
,
113
]
about the popular First- and Second-Order Reliability Methods (FORM and SORM) in engineering.
Both types of probabilistic frameworks raise the need to calculate integrals over the (high-dimensional) parameter space. This task can be carried out by expansive Monte Carlo sampling methods
[
22
,
26
,
54
]
, accelerated by efficient stochastic collocation techniques and parallelized GPU implementations
[
75
,
76
]
.
A different strategy consists in using the stochastic gradient algorithm to mitigate the number of samples required by the Monte-Carlo strategy, to the expanse of an increase in the number of optimization iterations
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
61
,
90
]
, see also
[
48
,
49
,
106
]
for related strategies. Yet another possibility is to construct a reduced-order model for the solution of the boundary-value problem at play: assuming a finite-dimensional structure for the uncertain parameters (which typically stems from a truncated Karhunen-Loève expansion), this solution is approximated by a polynomial chaos expansion
[
64
]
, or a stochastic Galerkin method is used for its computation
[
105
]
. Eventually, in this setting also, linearization techniques based on the “small” amplitude of the uncertainties, are applicable to build deterministic approximations for the considered statistical quantities of the cost function of interest, see e.g. our previous work
[
4
]
or
[
51
]
.

Distributionally robust optimization.
Classical probabilistic optimal design formulations themselves suffer from a conceptual shortcoming:
in realistic applications, the “true” probability distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{\rm true}}
of the
uncertain parameters is unknown; at best, it can be roughly approximated
based on (often scarce) observations. Distributionally robust optimization has
recently emerged as a sensible paradigm to enforce robustness with
respect to uncertainties in a way overcoming this objection. In
a nutshell, the worst-case of the expectation (or another statistical quantity, such as the standard deviation) of the cost function is minimized over all the probability laws
{\mathbb{Q}}
within a so-called ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
:
{\mathcal{A}}
is made of the laws that are “close” to a given nominal scenario
{\mathbb{P}}
, which is in practice reconstructed from a few samples. Ambiguity sets of various natures can be thought of. The perhaps most intuitive practice
is to define
{\mathcal{A}}
as the set of laws
{\mathbb{Q}}
lying within a certain distance from

{\mathbb{P}}
; this distance can be measured in terms of
the Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
60
]
, a maximum mean discrepancy

[
104
]
, or the Wasserstein distance

[
42
]
. Other ambiguity sets
are made of laws whose first moments are close to those of
{\mathbb{P}}

[
39
,
83
]
.
We refer to

[
71
,
94
,
95
]

for overviews of distributionally robust optimization, to the book
[
25
]

for a focus on machine learning, and to the recent surveys

[
19
,
68
]
.

This recent literature about distributional robustness arises in the
rich and structured framework of convex optimization. Unfortunately,
optimal design problems often lack such convenient features; moreover, they raise
specific difficulties such as the large cost entailed by the numerical evaluations
of the objective function and its derivative, as they depend on the solution to one or several physical boundary-value problems.
Partly for these reasons, despite its natural character, distributionally robust optimization has
been seldom considered in engineering design: beyond our note

[
33
]
, we are only aware of very few contributions
in this direction, dealing with particular situations at that. In

[
63
]
, a model problem of parametric optimal
design is considered, where the uncertain parameter is allowed to take
a finite number of values. The experiments conducted in there clearly demonstrate
the ability of distributionally robust formulations to predict optimal designs
that perform well even in scenarios that are not present in the nominal
law. The article
[
62
]
considers truss structures in a context
where the optimized thickness of the bars is subjected to uncertainty; a semi-definite program is derived to handle
the worst value of the failure probability of the system when the
moments of the law are “close” from reference values.

Summary of our contributions.

This article is the natural sequel of our preliminary note
[
33
]
. Inspired by recent ideas in robust convex optimization and optimal transport theory, we develop tractable distributionally robust versions for a wide range of realistic optimal design problems, that are compatible with the particularities of this field, and notably its lack of mathematical structure and the large computational cost of evaluating the optimization criteria at play.
More precisely, we consider three situations:

•

The ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
is the set of probability laws
{\mathbb{Q}}
whose Wasserstein distance from the nominal law
{\mathbb{P}}
is lower than a certain threshold;

•

The ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
is made of laws whose first- and second-order moments are close to reference values;

•

The statistical quantity which is made robust is no longer the mean value of the cost but its conditional value at risk – a useful notion that allows to handle failure probability constraints.

In the spirit of
[
14
,
110
]
, in all three cases, we rely on suitable entropy-based penalizations, that enable the use of convex duality techniques. Importantly, the proposed methodology is agnostic of the shape and topology optimization framework: it is applicable to both density-based topology optimization and geometric shape optimization based on the method of Hadamard.
To emphasize this genericity, the discussion is framed within a formal, abstract optimal design setting insofar as possible.

Organization of the article.

Using an abstract optimal design setting,
Section

2
introduces the notion of distributional robustness,
as well as the three distributionally robust problems analyzed in the sequel and their tractable reformulations. The next sections are devoted to the application of these concepts in two different optimal design settings:

Section

3
considers the popular engineering framework of density-based topology
optimization. In there, we notably conduct several numerical experiments to appraise the
main features of our formulations. Then,
Section

4
deals with geometric
shape optimization. A conclusion and a few research perspectives are outlined in
Section

5
. The article ends with three appendices, devoted to
formal sketches of the proofs of the mathematical results underlying our developments.

2.
Distributionally Robust Optimal Design Problems

This section presents the three types of distributionally robust optimal design problems considered
in this article and details their tractable reformulation allowed by convex duality; it takes place in a formal and abstract setting, that we first introduce.

2.1.
Presentation of the abstract distributionally robust optimal design framework

Let
{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}
be a subset of a vector space
{\mathcal{H}}
, made of admissible designs
h
.
In
Section

3
,
{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}=L^{\infty}(D,[0,1])
is made of the density functions
h
over a given computational domain
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d=2,3
). In
Section

4
, with a slight abuse of this framework,
{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}
is a set of “true” shapes
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

The mathematical model characterizing the physical behavior of a design
h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}
involves parameters;
these are gathered in a vector
\xi
, lying in a compact subset

\Xi
of a finite-dimensional space
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Assuming a perfect
knowledge of
\xi
, the optimal design problem of interest reads:

(2.1)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi),

where
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)
denotes the cost of a design
h
when the physical
parameters at play equal
\xi
. For simplicity of the exposition,
this formulation omits constraints; as demonstrated by the examples
in
Sections

3
and
4
, their treatment does not entail any additional
difficulty from the conceptual viewpoint.

In practice, the parameters
\xi
are only known imperfectly: they are
modeled as a
k
-dimensional random vector
\xi\equiv\xi(\omega)
, where
the event variable
\omega
belongs to an abstract probability space
({\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{F}},\mu)
. They are described by their law
{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{\rm true}}
, that is an
element in the set
{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
of probability measures on
\Xi
:

\text{For each subset }A\subset\Xi,\>\>{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{\rm true}}(A)\in[0,1]=\text{probability that }\xi\text{ belong to A.}

As we have mentioned, a simple and popular method to make the nominal problem
Eq.

2.1
aware of these uncertainties
is to optimize the mean value of the cost functional
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)
, i.e.:

(2.2)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J_{\text{\rm mean}}(h),\text{ where }J_{\text{\rm mean}}(h)=\int_{\Xi}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{\rm true}}(\xi).

Unfortunately, in realistic applications, the “true” law
{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{\rm true}}
of

\xi
is itself unknown; at best, an estimate
{\mathbb{P}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
is
available, which is for instance reconstructed from a few observed samples

\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{N}\in\Xi
:

{\mathbb{P}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\xi^{i}},

where
\delta_{\xi^{i}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
denotes the Dirac mass at
\xi^{i}\in\Xi
,
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Acknowledging this uncertainty about the law of
\xi
leads to the distributionally robust version of
Eq.

2.1
, minimizing
the worst possible value of the expected cost:

(2.3)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J_{\text{\rm dr}}(h),\text{ where }J_{\text{\rm dr}}(h)=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\Xi}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi).

This formulation features an ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
, made
of laws
{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
which are “close” to
{\mathbb{P}}
. Depending on
the available information about
{\mathbb{P}}
, the set
{\mathcal{A}}
may take different forms. This article proposes three variations around this general concern:

•

In
Section

2.2
, we consider problems where the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}

is made of the probability laws
{\mathbb{Q}}
which are “close” to
{\mathbb{P}}
in
terms of (a suitable version of) the Wasserstein distance.

•

In
Section

2.3
,

{\mathcal{A}}
is defined as the set of probability laws
whose first and second-order moments are “close” to given reference values up to a certain
threshold.

•

Section

2.4
eventually deals with a slightly different situation,
where another measure of risk than the mean value of the cost function
is made robust, namely, its conditional value at risk.

Remark 2.1
.

•

In certain applications, the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}\equiv{\mathcal{A}}(h)
in
Eq.

2.3
depends on the design
h
itself,
see e.g.
[
40
]
. In the (scarce) literature devoted to such problems,
Eq.

2.3
is treated by “freezing” the ambiguity set at the current iteration of the optimization process. Thus, the solution of a version of
Eq.

2.3
featuring a design-dependent ambiguity set boils down to that of a series of such problems where this set does not depend on
h
. For this reason, throughout the sequel, we limit ourselves to considering design-independent ambiguity sets
{\mathcal{A}}
.

•

The assumption that the uncertain parameter
\xi
should lie in a finite-dimensional vector space
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
may seem restrictive at first glance.
In practice, though, infinite-dimensional random vectors are often approximated by a finite-dimensional reduced-order model, obtained e.g. via a truncated Karhunen-Loève expansion, see
Section

3.5
.

2.2.
Ambiguity sets based on the Wasserstein distance to a nominal law

This section considers the situation where the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
in

Eq.

2.3
is defined as the set of probability laws which are “close”
to the nominal law
{\mathbb{P}}
in terms of a suitable distance between probability
measures, namely, the (entropy-regularized) Wasserstein distance from optimal
transport theory. We start with a brief reminder about this notion.

2.2.1.
Preliminaries about the Wasserstein distance

Recalling that
\Xi
is a compact subset of the finite-dimensional
space
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, let
{\mathbb{P}}
,
{\mathbb{Q}}
be two elements in the set
{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)

of probability measures on
\Xi
. The Wasserstein distance
W({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})
between

{\mathbb{P}}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}
is defined as the minimum cost of sending the mass of
{\mathbb{P}}

onto that of
{\mathbb{Q}}
, precisely:

(2.4)

W({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})=\min\left\{\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}c(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi(\xi,\zeta),\>\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi\times\Xi),\>\>\pi_{1}={\mathbb{P}},\>\pi_{2}={\mathbb{Q}}\right\}.

This definition involves couplings
\pi
, that is, probability measures
on the product set
\Xi\times\Xi
; the first and second marginals of
such a coupling are respectively denoted by
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
. These are defined through their action on the set
{\mathcal{C}}(\Xi)
of continuous functions on
\Xi
:

\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}(\Xi),\quad\int_{\Xi}\varphi(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}\pi_{1}(\xi)=\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\varphi(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}\pi(\xi,\zeta),\text{ and }\int_{\Xi}\varphi(\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi_{2}(\zeta)=\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\varphi(\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi(\xi,\zeta).

Intuitively,
\pi(\xi,\zeta)
represents the amount of mass transferred from

\xi
to
\zeta
according to the transport plan
\pi
,
\pi_{1}(\xi)
is the total mass leaving
\xi
and

\pi_{2}(\zeta)
is that arriving at
\zeta
, so that the marginal constraints in
Eq.

2.4
encode that
\pi
transports the “mass distribution”
{\mathbb{P}}
onto
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The “ground cost”
c(\xi,\zeta)
evaluates the cost of transporting one unit of mass from
\xi

to
\zeta
; throughout the article, we use the quadratic cost

(2.5)

\forall\xi,\zeta\in\Xi,\quad c(\xi,\zeta)=\lvert\xi-\zeta\lvert^{2}.

The Wasserstein distance is a principled notion of distance between probability
measures. It has recently aroused much enthusiasm to compare shapes
under extraneous formats in imaging
[
46
]
and as a
loss function between discrete probability distributions in machine
learning
[
81
]
. Notably, it allows to smoothly
discriminate probability distributions with different supports, contrary to the Kullback-Leibler divergence. We refer for
instance to

[
78
,
88
,
98
,
107
]

for mathematical background about optimal transport theory.

Remark 2.2
.

Strictly speaking, it is the square root of the quantity
W({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})
in
Eq.

2.4
(in the present situation where the ground cost
c(\xi,\zeta)
is given by
Eq.

2.5
) that is the distance over
{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
called Wasserstein distance. For simplicity of the terminology, we abusively refer to
W({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})
as the Wasserstein distance.

In our applications, we use a regularized version of
the Wasserstein distance. Let us define the entropy
H(\pi)
of a
coupling
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi\times\Xi)
by:

(2.6)

H(\pi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\log\frac{\text{\rm d}\pi}{\text{\rm d}\pi_{0}}\>\text{\rm d}\pi&\text{if }\pi\text{ is absolutely continuous w.r.t. }\pi_{0},\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Following
[
14
]
, the above definition of
H(\pi)
features a reference coupling
\pi_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi\times\Xi)
, which is given by:

(2.7)

\pi_{0}(\xi,\zeta)={\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\otimes\nu_{\xi}(\zeta),\text{ where }\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)=\alpha_{\xi}e^{-\frac{\lvert\xi-\zeta\lvert^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\mathds{1}_{\Xi}(\zeta),

and the normalization constant
\alpha_{\xi}
is tailored so that

\nu_{\xi}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
. More explicitly, the action of
\pi_{0}

on an arbitrary continuous function
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}(\Xi\times\Xi)

reads:

\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\varphi(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi_{0}(\xi,\zeta)=\int_{\Xi}\left(\int_{\Xi}\varphi(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi).

In
Eq.

2.6
, a coupling
\pi
is called absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{0}
if there exists an integrable function
\alpha\in L^{1}(\Xi\times\Xi;\text{\rm d}\pi_{0})
such that
\pi=\alpha(\xi,\zeta)\pi_{0}
; the latter is often denoted by
\alpha=\frac{\text{\rm d}\pi}{\text{\rm d}\pi_{0}}
.

We eventually define the entropy-regularized Wasserstein distance
W_{\varepsilon}
via a minimization problem similar to that in
Eq.

2.4
, up to an additional penalization by the entropy of couplings:

(2.8)

W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})=\min\left\{\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}c(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi(\xi,\zeta)+\varepsilon H(\pi),\>\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi\times\Xi),\>\>\pi_{1}={\mathbb{P}},\>\pi_{2}={\mathbb{Q}}\right\}.

This addition of a small, strictly convex log-barrier term to the optimal transport problem was proposed in
[
28
]
, with the purpose to ease numerical computations. Since then, this “blurred” optimal transport has found an interest of its own; for instance, it is often preferred to the original optimal transport formulation in supervised machine learning, where it helps to avoid overfitting. We refer to the lecture notes
[
86
]
about entropy-regularized optimal transport.

Remark 2.3
.

The parameter
\sigma
in the definition
Eq.

2.7
of
\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)
reflects the degree of confidence of the user in the nominal law
{\mathbb{P}}
. Loosely speaking, when
\sigma
is “small”,
\pi_{0}
reduces to the trivial coupling
{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\otimes\delta_{\xi}(\zeta)
, sending
{\mathbb{P}}
onto itself by leaving in place each mass unit
\xi\in\Xi
. Then, the entropy penalization
H(\pi)
in the regularized Wasserstein distance
Eq.

2.8
imposes that only couplings
\pi
very close to this “trivial” one should be considered.
When
\sigma
gets larger,
\pi_{0}
acts by “spreading the mass” of
{\mathbb{P}}
at each point
\xi\in\Xi
over a neighborhood with increasing size. The couplings
\pi
with “small” entropy values will then have second marginals corresponding to more and more “blurred” versions of
{\mathbb{P}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Contrary to what is suggested by its misleading name, the quantity
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})
is solely an approximation of (the square of) a distance. For instance,
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{P}})
is often different from
0
. The function
W_{\varepsilon}
can be modified into a proper (squared) distance by setting:

S_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}):=W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})-\frac{1}{2}W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{P}})-\frac{1}{2}W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{Q}},{\mathbb{Q}}).

This so-called Sinkhorn distance is significantly more complicated to handle;
in particular, it does not allow for convenient reformulations of distributionally robust problems similar to those derived below using
W_{\varepsilon}
. We refer to
[
46
,
100
]
for more details about the Sinkhorn distance.

2.2.2.
Distributionally robust optimization using a Wasserstein ambiguity set

In this section, we study the instance of the distributionally robust
optimal design problem
Eq.

2.3
where the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}

is that
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
defined by:

(2.9)

{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}=\Big\{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi),\>\>W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})\leq m\Big\},

i.e.
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
is made of the probability laws
{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
that are within a distance
m>0
from the given nominal law
{\mathbb{P}}
.
The distributionally robust problem
Eq.

2.3
rewrites in this case:

(2.10)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J_{\text{\rm W}}(h),\text{ where }J_{\text{\rm W}}(h)=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\int_{\Xi}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi).

The idea of considering ambiguity sets based on the Wasserstein distance to a fixed nominal law was originally introduced in
[
89
]
, see
[
42
,
67
]
for the subsequent developments of this idea and the review
[
20
]
. The precise formulation involving the entropy-regularized ambiguity set in
Eqs.

2.8
and
2.9
was proposed in
[
14
,
110
]
and then reused in our note
[
33
]
.
We also refer to
[
13
,
47
]
for an account of its generalization guarantees and to
[
108
]
for a recent, open-source implementation of this framework.

The formulation
Eq.

2.10
is a priori intricate, as it is made of nested minimization and maximization problems.
The key point of our methodology is that the worst-case functional

J_{\text{\rm W}}(h)
can be conveniently reformulated as an infimum, thanks to the
following result from convex duality. The latter is proved rigorously in

[
14
]
. For completeness, and since its main argument
will be reused, a short and formal sketch of the proof is provided in

Appendix

A
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Xi
be a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, and let
f:\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function.
Recalling the definition
Eq.

2.9
of the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
,
the following formula holds true:

(2.11)

\sup\limits_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{\rm W}}}\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)=\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(\lambda m+\lambda\varepsilon\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\right).

Remark 2.5
.

As highlighted by the argument in
Appendix

A
, the optimization variable
\lambda
in the right-hand minimization problem of
Eq.

2.11
is the Lagrange multiplier for the inequality constraint
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})\leq m
of the maximization problem at the left-hand side. Hence, the optimal point
\lambda^{*}
accounts for the sensitivity of the optimal value of the quantity
\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)
when the threshold
m
of this constraint is perturbed. In particular, according to the complementarity relations of the first-order necessary conditions of optimality for
Eq.

2.11
,

•

If an optimal law
{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}
in the maximization problem at the left-hand side of
Eq.

2.11
does not saturate the constraint, i.e. if
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}^{*})<m
, then
\lambda^{*}=0
: the optimal value of
\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)
does not change if
m
is slightly modified.

•

On the contrary, if
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}}^{*})=m
, then
\lambda^{*}
may differ from
0
, and it often does in practice: the optimal value of
\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)
may change significantly as soon as
m
is perturbed.

We refer to e.g.
[
85
]
about the classical interpretation of the Lagrange multiplier in optimization theory.

Proposition

2.1
allows to rewrite the distributionally robust problem
Eq.

2.10

in the following way:

(2.12)

\min\limits_{(h,\lambda)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda)\text{ where }{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda)=\lambda m+\lambda\varepsilon\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{{\mathcal{C}}(h,\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi).

This new expression is much more amenable to the numerical treatment than the original one
Eq.

2.10
, as it features a single minimization, over the
product space
{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Problem
Eq.

2.12
can be solved owing to standard numerical methods, such as an alternating descent algorithm, see
Sections

3
and
4
for illustrations.

Remark 2.6
.

•

A result similar to
Proposition

2.1
holds when the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
is constructed from the classical Wasserstein distance, i.e. when

W_{\varepsilon}
is replaced by
W
in
Eq.

2.9
. The main difference lies in the replacement of the “log-sum-exp” operation in
Eq.

2.11
by its formal limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which is a supremum.

•

Different notions of distance between probability measures could be employed in the definition of the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
, such as the Kullback-Leibler divergence, the total variation distance, etc. We refer to
[
68
]
for a more exhaustive list of the possibilities and in-depth discussions about the features of these formulations.

2.3.
Ambiguity sets based on moments

This section assumes that the only available information about the law of the uncertain parameter
\xi
is about its mean
value
\mu_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and covariance matrix

\Sigma_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times k}
, as was initially proposed in
[
39
]
,
see also
[
55
,
73
]
. The ambiguity
set
{\mathcal{A}}_{M}
containing the possible probability laws for
\xi
is defined by:

(2.13)

{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{M}}=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi),\>\>\left\lvert\int_{\Xi}\xi\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\mu_{0}\right\lvert\leq m_{1},\text{ and } \int_{\Xi}(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\leq m_{2}\Sigma_{0}\right\}.

Here,
m_{1}>0
and
m_{2}>0
are given bounds and the second inequality is understood in the sense of positive semi-definite
matrices.

In this situation, taking inspiration from the analysis conducted in
Section

2.2
, we shall derive a tractable alternative form for the distributionally robust problem
Eq.

2.3
by penalizing the mean value
\int_{\Xi}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)
of the cost function by a “small”, strictly convex entropy term. The latter, that we denote by
H({\mathbb{Q}})
(not to be confused with the entropy
H(\pi)
of couplings in
Eq.

2.6
), is defined by:

(2.14)

H({\mathbb{Q}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cl}\int_{\Xi}\log\frac{\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}}\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}&\text{if }{\mathbb{Q}}\text{ is absolutely continuous w.r.t. }{\mathbb{Q}}_{0},\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Here, the reference law
{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
is (the restriction to
\Xi
of) the Gaussian distribution with center
\mu_{0}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}
:

{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}=\alpha e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\xi-\mu_{0})\Sigma_{0}^{-1}(\xi-\mu_{0})}\>\mathds{1}_{\Xi}(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}\xi,\text{ where }\alpha\text{ is a normalization constant.}

These ingredients now allow to introduce the following moment-based distributionally robust optimal design problem:

(2.15)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J_{\text{\rm M}}(h),\text{ where }J_{\text{\rm M}}(h):=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{M}}}\left(\int_{\Xi}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right).

Again, this a priori intricate problem is made tractable thanks to the following proposition, which converts the supremum functional
J_{\text{M}}(h)
into an infimum;
we refer to
[
93
]
for a complete statement and a rigorous proof, a sketch of which is provided in
Appendix

B
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be compact, and let
f:\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function.
The ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\rm M}}
being defined by
Eq.

2.13
with
m_{1}>0,m_{2}>0
, it holds:

(2.16)

\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\rm M}}}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right)=\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0,\>\lvert\tau\lvert\leq 1,\atop{S\in\mathbb{S}^{k}_{+}}}\Bigg\{\lambda m_{1}-\lambda\tau\cdot\mu_{0}+m_{2}S:\Sigma_{0}\\[-10.00002pt]
+\varepsilon\log\left(\int_{\Xi}\left(e^{\frac{f(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}(\xi)\right)\Bigg\},

where
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{k}
is the set of
k\times k
symmetric and positive semi-definite matrices.

The above proposition paves the way to the following alternative formulation for
Eq.

2.15
:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\>\lvert\tau\lvert\leq 1,\atop{\lambda\geq 0,\>{S\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{k}}}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{M}}(h,\lambda,\tau,S),\text{ where }\\[-10.00002pt]
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{M}}(h,\lambda,\tau,S):=\lambda m_{1}-\lambda\tau\cdot\mu_{0}+m_{2}S:\Sigma_{0}+\varepsilon\log\left(\int_{\Xi}\left(e^{\frac{{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}(\xi)\right).

Again, this minimization problem, posed over an augmented set, can be solved by standard numerical algorithms for optimal design.

2.4.
Distributional uncertainties using the conditional value at risk

The previous
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
have considered distributionally robust functionals accounting for the worst-case value of the expectation of the cost
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)
of a design
h
when the law of
\xi
belongs to a certain ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
. Here, we derive robust
versions of probabilistic optimal design problems involving a statistical quantity of the cost which is different from its expectation,
namely, its conditional value at risk. In spite of its popularity, for instance in mathematical finance, the use
of this notion in optimal design seems to be relatively confidential, see
however
[
41
]
and
[
66
]
in the more general context of optimal control of partial differential equations. We exemplify this use in the perspective of reliability-based
optimal design, where the conditional value at risk plays the role of a
surrogate for a probability of failure.

2.4.1.
A few basic facts about value at risk and conditional value at risk

This section collects a few useful facts about the notions of value at risk
and conditional value at risk in the context of interest to us; we
refer to
[
96
,
97
]

for details. For the moment, let us assume that the probability law
{\mathbb{P}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
of the parameters
\xi
of the physical model is known perfectly.
For a fixed design
h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}
, we denote by
t\mapsto\Psi(h,t)
the cumulative
distribution function of the cost
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
when the latter is seen as a function of the
random parameter
\xi\in\Xi
, that is:

\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\Psi(h,t)={\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\leq t\Big\}.

We recall that the function
t\mapsto\Psi(h,t)
is non decreasing, with limits:

\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}\Psi(h,t)=0,\text{ and }\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\Psi(h,t)=1.

This function is continuous from the right, but it fails to be continuous from the left at those points
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
equals
t
on a subset of
\Xi
with positive measure, if any
[
17
]
. For simplicity, we suppose throughout this presentation that
\Psi(h,\cdot)
is continuous; although the definitions and intuitions provided below require technical adjustments, the conclusions of our analyses remain valid in the case where it is not.

Let us now define the main objects at stake.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\beta\in[0,1]
be a given threshold; then,

•

The
\beta
-value at risk (or
\beta
-quantile)
\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))
of the cost
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
is the smallest value
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
greater than
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
with probability
\beta
, i.e.

{\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}=\inf\limits\Big\{t\in{\mathbb{R}},\>\>\Psi(h,t)\geq\beta\Big\}.

•

If
\beta<1
, the
\beta
-conditional value at risk
\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))

of
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
is the average of
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
over those events where it exceeds the threshold
\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))
:

(2.17)

{\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq{\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}\right\}}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi).

Note that, by definition of
\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))
, it holds:

(2.18)

{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq{\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}\Big\}=1-\beta,

and so
Eq.

2.17
is indeed an average. In particular, it holds:

(2.19)

{\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}\leq{\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}.

Intuitively, the cost
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
takes values lower than the
\beta
-value at risk

{\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}
with probability
\beta
, see
Fig.

1
.
This quantity is not fully satisfactory, as it does not supply any information about the tail of the distribution of
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
, i.e. those events
\xi
where
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)
exceeds
\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))
.
On the contrary, the conditional value at risk
{\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}
is precisely the average of
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
over these scenarios.

Figure 1
.
The
\beta
-value at risk
{\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}
of the cost
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
is the lowest level where the cumulative distribution function
\Psi(h,\cdot)
takes values greater than
\beta
; the
\beta
-conditional value at risk
{\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}
is the integral of
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
over those events where it exceeds this value (red area).

The handling of conditional value at risk is eased by the following
representation as a minimum value. The latter is proved in
[
2
,
96
]
; for the sake
of completeness, a formal and intuitive argument is provided in
Appendix

C
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The following representation formula holds true:

{\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}=\inf\limits_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\Xi}\left[{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)-\alpha\right]_{+}\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\right\},\text{ where }[t]_{+}:=\max(t,0).

The minimum in the above program is uniquely attained at
\alpha={\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))}
.

2.4.2.
Reliability-based optimal design using the conditional value at risk

The notion of conditional value at risk paves the way to a convenient reformulation of optimization
problems featuring failure probability constraints, of the form

{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq C_{T}\Big\}\leq 1-\beta,

for a parameter
\beta\in(0,1)
and a given safety threshold
C_{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, see e.g.
[
24
]
.
Indeed, it follows from the definition of
\Psi(h,t)
that:

{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq C_{T}\Big\}\leq 1-\beta\text{ if and only if }\Psi(h,C_{T})\geq\beta.

Since
\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))
is by definition the smallest value
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
the cost is lower than
t
with probability
\beta
, this is in turn equivalent to:

\textrm{{VaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))\leq C_{T}.

Now invoking
Eq.

2.19
, a conservative surrogate for this constraint is:

\textrm{{CVaR}}_{\beta}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot))\leq C_{T}.

Let us apply this observation to a model optimal design problem featuring an objective function
J(h)
and a constraint on the probability that the cost exceed the threshold

C_{T}
:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J(h)\text{ s.t. }{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\xi\in\Xi\text{ s.t. }{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq C_{T}\Big\}\leq 1-\beta.

The foregoing discussion naturally leads to consider the following problem, featuring a more stringent constraint:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\;J(h)\>\text{ s.t. }\texttt{CVaR}_{\beta}(\mathcal{C}(h,\cdot))\leq C_{T}.

Using the representation formula for the Conditional Value at Risk in
Proposition

2.3
,
this problem rewrites as a more conventional optimization
problem over the pair
(h,\alpha)
:

(2.20)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\;J(h)\text{ s.t. }\;\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}\left[\mathcal{C}(h,\xi)-\alpha\right]_{+}\;\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\xi)\leq C_{T}.

Remark 2.7
.

In numerical practice, the non smooth function
\left[\cdot\right]_{+}
featured in
Eq.

2.20
is replaced by the smooth approximation:

\left[t\right]_{+}\approx\dfrac{t}{1+e^{-\gamma t}},\text{ where }\gamma:=20.

2.4.3.
Distributionally robust formulation of problems involving the conditional value at risk

Let us now address the situation where the law of the parameter
\xi
is itself uncertain. To set ideas, we assume that this law belongs to an
ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
of Wasserstein type around a nominal law
{\mathbb{P}}
, see
Eq.

2.9
.
The distributionally robust counterpart of
Eq.

2.20
then reads:

(2.21)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\;J(h)\text{ s.t. }\;\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\min\limits_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\alpha+\dfrac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}\left[\mathcal{C}(h,\xi)-\alpha\right]_{+}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right)\leq C_{T}.

We then use the following proposition, whose proof is similar to that
of
Proposition

2.1
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\Xi
be a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, and let
f:\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a continuous function. Recall that
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\rm W}}
denotes the subset of
{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
defined by
Eq.

2.9
. The following equality holds true:

\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\rm W}}}\min\limits_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\alpha+\dfrac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}f(\xi)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right)=\\
\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0,\atop\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\alpha+\frac{\lambda m}{1-\beta}+\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{1-\beta}\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\right).

As a result, the distributionally robust optimal design problem
Eq.

2.21
rewrites:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\lambda\geq 0,\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\;J(h)\>\text{ s.t. }\;{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{C}}(h,\lambda,\alpha)\leq C_{T},\text{
where }\\
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{C}}(h,\lambda,\alpha)=\alpha+\frac{\lambda m}{1-\beta}+\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{1-\beta}\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{\left[\mathcal{C}(h,\zeta)-\alpha\right]_{+}-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{{\mathbb{P}}}(\xi).

This is a classical constrained optimal design problem, posed over the product space
{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, which can be solved by standard numerical algorithms.

3.
Distributionally Robust Density-Based Topology Optimization

This section slips into the framework of density-based optimal design of mechanical
structures, about which we refer to the classical book
[
16
]
, see also
[
102
]
.
We present various concrete applications of the general methods exposed in
Section

2
in this setting.
The first
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
introduce the physical context and the numerical framework, respectively. Then,
Section

3.3
deals with topology optimization
problems where the uncertain parameters are the loads applied to the structure,
and the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}
is based on the Wasserstein distance to a
nominal law. The next
Section

3.4
illustrates the use of ambiguity sets based on the moments of the uncertain probability law. After that in
Section

3.5
, we look at another type of uncertainty, concerning the material coefficients. Finally, in
Section

3.6
, we handle reliability-based optimization problem using the notion of conditional value at risk.

3.1.
Topology optimization of linearly elastic structures

Let
D
be a fixed hold-all domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d=2,3
); the design variable under scrutiny is a density function

h\in L^{\infty}(D,[0,1])
, representing a relaxed version of the characteristic function of a subset of
D
. Specifically,

•

The regions where
h(x)=1
are made of an isotropic elastic material with Hooke’s law
A
given by:

(3.1)

\text{For any symmetric }d\times d\text{ matrix }e,\quad Ae=2\mu e+\lambda\text{\rm tr}(e)\text{\rm I}.

Here,
\lambda
and
\mu
are the Lamé parameters of the material; these are related to the more physical Young’s modulus
E
and Poisson ratio
\nu
via the following relations:

(3.2)

\mu=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu)},\text{ and }\lambda=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}&\text{if }d=3,\\[5.0pt]
\frac{E}{1-\nu^{2}}&\text{if }d=2\text{ (plane stress).}\\
\end{array}\right.

•

The function
h
equals
0
at points
x\in D
that are completely surrounded by a very soft material with Hooke’s law
\eta A
,
\eta\ll 1
, mimicking void;

•

The intermediate regions where
h(x)\in(0,1)
are composed of a mixture of material and void: their material properties interpolate between those the bulk and void phases.

These features are incorporated into an inhomogeneous Hooke’s tensor
A(h)
associated to the density function
h
.
According to the Solid Isotropic Material Penalization approach (SIMP), the latter is defined by:

(3.3)

\forall x\in D,\quad A(h)(x)=\Big(\eta+(1-\eta)h(x)^{p}\Big)A,

where the penalization exponent
p
is typically set to
3
.

The considered structures are clamped on a region
\Gamma_{D}
of the boundary
\partial D
, and external loads
g:\Gamma_{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are applied on a disjoint region
\Gamma_{N}\subset\partial D
.
In this situation, the displacement of the structure is the unique solution
u\in H^{1}(D)^{d}
to the system of linear elasticity:

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{div}(A(h)e(u))=0&\text{in }D,\\
u=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
A(h)e(u)n=g&\text{on }\Gamma_{N},\\
A(h)e(u)n=0&\text{on }\partial D\setminus\overline{\Gamma_{N}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{D}}.\end{array}\right.

Here, we have denoted by
e(u):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})

the strain tensor associated to a displacement field
u:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
We have also omitted body forces (gravity) for simplicity.

In the sequel, we shall endow the elastic displacement
u
and the associated physical quantities of interest with various indices or arguments to highlight their dependence on the relevant parameters of this system, notably, the design
h
, the loads
g
and the material parameters
E
,
\nu
.

Remark 3.1
.

In practice, the definition of the Hooke’s law
A(h)
in
Eq.

3.3
involves various filters, imparting effects such as smoothness, among others, which are not mentioned for brevity. Note however that the parameters of these filters are updated in the course of the optimization, which partly explains the large jumps that are sometimes visible in the convergence histories of our numerical results.

A general topology optimization problem in the above context reads:

(3.5)

\min_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J(h)\text{ s.t. }G(h)=0,

where the objective and constraint functions
J(h)
,
G(h)
may depend on
h
via the elastic displacement
u_{h}
. Examples of such functionals are:

•

The compliance
C(h)
of the structure, which equivalently accounts for the elastic energy stored in the structure, or the work done by the external loads:

(3.6)

C(h)=\int_{D}A(h)e(u_{h}):e(u_{h})\>\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\Gamma_{N}}g\cdot u_{h}\>\text{\rm d}s;

•

The difference between the volume
\text{\rm Vol}(h)=\int_{D}h\>\text{\rm d}x
of
h
and a target value
V_{T}
.

The differentiation of functions depending on the design via boundary-value problems is a difficult, albeit classical issue in optimal design, see e.g.
[
16
,
23
,
92
]
about the celebrated adjoint method.
For completeness, we recall the following derivative formulas, see e.g.
[
8
,
16
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The compliance and volume functionals are differentiable at an arbitrary density function
h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}
, and their derivatives equal:

C^{\prime}(h)(\widehat{h})=-\int_{D}A^{\prime}(h)(\widehat{h})e(u_{h}):e(u_{h})\>\text{\rm d}x,\text{ and }\text{\rm Vol}^{\prime}(h)(\widehat{h})=\int_{D}\widehat{h}\>\text{\rm d}x.

3.2.
Numerical Strategy

Our numerical framework for solving problems of the form
Eq.

3.5
is based on the open-source implementation
[
31
]
, and more exactly on a version of the latter dedicted to topology optimization
[
29
]
.
In a nutshell, it is a steepest-descent strategy based on the derivatives of the objective and constraint functionals
J(h)
and
G(h)
. The physical boundary-value problems characterizing the elastic displacement
u_{h}
in their expressions are solved by the Finite Element Method, using the open-source library
FreeFem

[
57
]
.
A descent direction is then inferred from these data thanks to the Null-Space algorithm
[
44
,
45
]
, a constrained optimization algorithm in infinite dimension which is suitable for shape and topology optimization applications, see also
[
43
]
for a
python
open-source implementation. These independent operations are orchestrated by a
python
script, and all our computations are performed on a standard
macbook pro
laptop with 16Gb of RAM.

The treatment of distributionally robust problems such as
Eq.

2.12
raises the need to evaluate the probabilistic integrals over the parameter set
\Xi
involved in the expressions of the distributionally robust functionals and their derivatives. To achieve this task, we rely on Monte Carlo sampling: every integral of the form
\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)
is approximated as:

\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}f(\xi^{i}),

where the
\xi^{i}
are
N
independent and identically distributed samples drawn from the probability distribution
{\mathbb{P}}
. In practice, in order to keep the computational effort moderate and since our illustrations do not target industrial complexity, we use a maximum number of
N=10
vectors; these evaluations can be conducted in parallel thanks to the
numpy
vectorization solutions.
On a different note, according to
[
18
]
, we use the so-called “log-sum-exp trick” to overcome the problems posed by the smallness of the integrand, and thus stabilize computations.
Admittedly, this stochastic gradient descent strategy for the resolution of a problem such as
Eq.

2.12
is overly simplistic, and much more advanced techniques could be used to improve its efficiency, starting from improvements of the sampling strategy by variance reduction techniques, about which we refer to the review article
[
80
]
, or more advanced versions of the stochastic gradient method, such as that introduced in
[
48
,
49
]
. Although crucial in real-life applications, we believe that these topics are beyond the scope of the present study.

3.3.
Wasserstein distributionally robust topology optimization under load uncertainty

\xi=g
, and the considered cost function is the compliance
C(h,\xi)
defined in

3.6

(3.8)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\int_{\Xi}C(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\text{ s.t. } \text{\rm Vol}(h)=V_{T}.

Let us recall from the discussion in

(3.9)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\lambda\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\text{Vol}(h)=V_{T},\\

and the probability measure
\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)=\mathds{1}_{\Xi}(\zeta)\alpha_{\xi}e^{-\frac{|\xi-\zeta|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\text{\rm d}\zeta
is that featured in the definition
Eq.

2.7
of the reference coupling
\pi_{0}
. We conduct several numerical investigations in this setting.

with size

9,\!000

is made of four two-dimensional components
\xi_{i}=(\xi_{i,1},\xi_{i,2})
, representing the load applied on the part
\Gamma_{N,i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,4
. More precisely,

•

The loads
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
applied on the arms of the mast represent the weight of the hanging cables.

•

The loads
\xi_{3}
and
\xi_{4}
applied on the lower part of the structure model the effect of wind.

(3.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{div}(A(h)e(u))=0&\text{in }D,\\

As far as the loads are concerned, we observe three scenarios:

•

\xi^{1}=((0,-1),(0,-1),(0,0),(0,0))
, i.e. two vertical loads are applied on the arms
\Gamma_{N,1}
,
\Gamma_{N,2}
of the mast and the regions
\Gamma_{N,3}
and
\Gamma_{N,4}
are free;

•

\xi^{2}=((0,-1),(0,-1),(-1,0),(-1,0))
: the same vertical loads are applied on
\Gamma_{N,1}
,
\Gamma_{N,2}
, and
\Gamma_{N,3}
and
\Gamma_{N,4}
are subjected to a horizontal force directed to the left, mimicking the effect of wind;

•

\xi^{3}=((0,-1),(0,-1),(1,0),(1,0))
: this scenario is similar to the previous one
\xi^{2}
, except that the force applied on
\Gamma_{N,3}
and
\Gamma_{N,4}
is directed to the right.

We first empirically attribute the probabilities
\frac{1}{2}
,
\frac{1}{4}
and
\frac{1}{4}
to these respective scenarios, so that the law
{\mathbb{P}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
of the uncertain parameter

(3.11)

{\mathbb{P}}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\xi^{1}}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\xi^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{\xi^{3}}.

We then optimize the design of the mast with respect to the mean value of its compliance
C(h,\xi)
defined in
Eq.

3.6
, i.e. we solve:

(3.12)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}J_{\text{\rm mean}}(h),\text{ s.t. }\text{\rm Vol}(h)=V_{T},\text{ where }V_{T}=0.3\text{\rm Vol}(D)\text{ and }\\
J_{\text{\rm mean}}(h)=\int_{\Xi}C(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}C(h,\xi^{1})+\frac{1}{4}C(h,\xi^{2})+\frac{1}{4}C(h,\xi^{3}).

We run
400
iterations of the optimization algorithm outlined in
Section

3.2
, corresponding to approximately
30

is depicted on
Fig.

We now come to the situation of interest, where the probability law of the uncertain parameter
\xi
is uncertain. Having little faith in the empirical reconstruction
{\mathbb{P}}
in
Eq.

3.11
, we now minimize the worst value of the expectation of
C(h,\xi)
when the actual law
{\mathbb{Q}}
of
\xi
is “close” to
{\mathbb{P}}
in the sense that it belongs to the Wasserstein ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
in
Eq.

2.9
:

(3.13)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\int_{\Xi}C(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi),\text{ s.t. } \text{\rm Vol}(h)=V_{T}.

Now invoking the material in

2.2.2

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\lambda\geq 0}{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda)\text{ s.t. }\quad\text{Vol}(h)=V_{T},\quad\text{ where }\\
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda)=\lambda m+\lambda\varepsilon\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{C(h,\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\,\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\,\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi),

The design

[

Note that, in some scenarios, the designs optimized at larger Wasserstein radius have better performance; this happens when the applied load corresponds to a perturbation which is “far” from those in the nominal law, and is thus only effectively captured when the radius of the Wasserstein constraint is large enough. On the contrary, when such perturbations are already captured at the initial radius, increasing the radius further may decrease the impact of this capture, ultimately resulting in lower performance.

Moment-based distributionally robust optimization of the compliance of an L-shaped beam

This section discusses a situation where the ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}\equiv{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{M}}
used in the formulation of the distributionally robust problem is defined from the moments of probability distributions, see
Eq.

2.13
in
Section

2.3

As illustrated on

\xi

3.2

2.3

3.14

3.15
:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\>\left(\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\rm M}}}\int_{\Xi}C(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right)\text{ s.t. }\text{\rm Vol}(h)=V_{T},

featuring the moment-based ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{\rm M}}
defined in
Eq.

2.13
; the entropy
H({\mathbb{Q}})
of a law
{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
is defined by
Eq.

2.14
and the reference law
{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}
in this formula is
{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}={\mathbb{P}}
. The parameter
\varepsilon
is set to
\varepsilon=0.01
.
According to
Section

2.3
, this problem equivalently rewrites:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\>\lvert\tau\lvert\leq 1,\atop{\lambda\geq 0,{S\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{m}}}}{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{M}}(h,\lambda,\tau,S),\text{ s.t. }\text{\rm Vol}(h)=V_{T}\text{ where }\\[-10.00002pt]
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{M}}(h,\lambda,\tau,S):=\lambda m_{1}-\lambda\tau\cdot\mu_{0}+m_{2}S:\Sigma_{0}+\varepsilon\log\left(\int_{\Xi}\left(e^{\frac{C(h,\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\right).

3.5.
Distributionally robust topology optimization under material uncertainties

The present section considers uncertainties about the properties of the constituent material of the design.
This situation departs from
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
on at least two aspects: on the one hand,
the uncertain parameter
\xi
does not naturally pertain to a finite-dimensional space; rather, it stems from a truncated Karhunen-Loève expansion of an infinite-dimensional random field. On the other hand,
the considered optimal design problem features a cost function which is not self-adjoint, see
Proposition

3.2
below.

The designs under scrutiny are gripping mechanisms, see
Fig.

12
(a); they are enclosed in a fixed computational domain

D
with size
1\times 1
, equipped with a mesh composed of
9,000
vertices (
\approx 17,000
triangles). A load
g=(0.1,0)
is applied on a region
\Gamma_{N}
of the left-hand side of
\partial D
.

The uncertainties over the physical model concern the (inhomogeneous) Young’s modulus
E
of the constituent elastic material. Following
[
50
,
70
]
, this function is assumed to be of the form:

(3.16)

E(x,\xi)=G(\widetilde{E}(x,\xi)),\text{ where }\widetilde{E}(x,\xi)=E_{0}(x)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}E_{i}(x)\xi_{i},\quad x\in D,\>\>\xi\in\Xi.

Here,

•

The
E_{i}(x)
are given, deterministic elements in
L^{2}(D)
;

•

The uncertain parameter
\xi
lies in a compact subset
\Xi
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. In the nominal situation, its law
{\mathbb{P}}
is the (restriction to
\Xi
of the) normalized
k
-variate Gaussian law.

•

The function
G
equals
G:=F^{-1}\circ\Phi
, where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the
k
-variate Gaussian law and
F
is the cumulative distribution function of each real-valued random variable
E(x,\cdot)
,
x\in D
. In practice,
F
is the cumulative distribution function of the uniform law on
[0.1,1.9]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
; in particular,
E(x,\xi)
is uniformly bounded away from
0
and
\infty
.

Let us briefly explain how the structure
Eq.

3.16
of
\widetilde{E}
shows up in practice, see e.g.
[
26
,
51
,
70
]
. The quantity
\widetilde{E}
is modeled as a random field
\widetilde{E}(x,\omega)
, where the event variable
\omega
belongs to the abstract probability space
({\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{F}},\mu)
: intuitively, a random variable
\widetilde{E}(x,\cdot)
is attached at each spatial position
x\in D
. The available information about
\widetilde{E}(x,\omega)
concerns its mean value
E_{0}(x)
and covariance kernel
\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)
, defined by:

\forall x,y\in D,\quad E_{0}(x)=\int_{{\mathcal{O}}}\widetilde{E}(x,\omega)\>\text{\rm d}\mu(\omega),\text{ and } \text{\rm Cov}(x,y)=\int_{\mathcal{O}}(\widetilde{E}(x,\omega)-E_{0}(x))(\widetilde{E}(y,\omega)-E_{0}(y))\;\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega).

Here, we assume that
E_{0}(x)\equiv 1
and that
\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)
is given by:

(3.17)

\forall x,y\in D,\quad\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)=\alpha e^{-\frac{\lvert x-y\lvert}{l_{\text{\rm cor}}}},\text{ where }l_{\text{\rm cor}}=2e{-2}\text{ and }\alpha=100.

The finite-dimensional structure of
\widetilde{E}(x,\xi)
in
Eq.

3.16
now follows from a Karhunen-Loève decomposition of the covariance kernel
\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)
, truncated after the
k^{\text{th}}
term, see e.g.
[
74
,
99
]
: the pairs
(\lambda_{i},E_{i})
are the eigenelements of the operator

T:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D),\quad T\varphi(x)=\int_{D}\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)\varphi(y)\>\text{\rm d}y.

The first six eigenfunctions of
Eq.

3.17
are displayed on
Fig.

11
, together with a few realizations of
E(x,\xi)
.

Remark 3.2
.

In practice, the covariance kernel
\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)
of the random field
\widetilde{E}(x,\omega)
is itself unknown and it has to be reconstructed from (a few) observed instances of
E(x,\cdot)
(or
\widetilde{E}(x,\cdot)
). Hence, in principle, one should take into account the fact that the basis functions
E_{i}(x)
in the decomposition
Eq.

3.16
are themselves uncertain. This topic of distributionally robust covariance estimators, which goes beyond the scope of this article, is discussed in
[
38
,
84
,
112
]
.

We denote by
u_{h,\xi}
the solution to the linear
elasticity system
Eq.

3.4
when the Young’s modulus featured
in the Hooke’s law
A\equiv A(h,\xi)
in
Eq.

3.1
is that
E(x,\xi)
given in

Eq.

3.16
, see
Eq.

3.2
.
In this situation, we aim to optimize the design
h
of the structure so that it adopts a prescribed displacement
u_{T}
on a region
\Gamma_{T}
located near the jaws of the mechanism. To achieve this, we consider the following problem:

(3.18)

\min\limits_{h}D(h,\xi),\text{ where }D(h,\xi)=\int_{D}\chi(x)\lvert u_{h,\xi}-u_{T}\lvert^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

and
\chi(x)
is a given weight factor, which equals
1
in a thin region around the jaws (depicted in blue in
Fig.

12
(a)) and
0
elsewhere.

The derivative of the least-square functional
D(h,\xi)
is given by the following proposition, whose classical proof is omitted for brevity, see
[
16
]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The function
D(h,\xi)
is differentiable with respect to the design variable
h
, and its derivative reads:

D^{\prime}(h,\xi)(\widehat{h})=\int_{D}A^{\prime}(h)(\widehat{h})e(u_{h,\xi}):e(p_{h,\xi})\>\text{\rm d}x,

where the adjoint state
p_{h,\xi}
is the unique
H^{1}(D)^{2}
solution to the following boundary-value problem:

(3.19)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(A(h,\xi)e(p_{h,\xi}))=-2\chi(x)(u_{h,\xi}-u_{T})&\text{in }D,\\
p_{h,\xi}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
A(h,\xi)e(p_{h,\xi})n=0&\text{on }\partial D\setminus\overline{\Gamma_{D}}.\end{array}\right.

We first consider the optimization of the mechanism in the ideal situation where the Young’s modulus is known perfectly, i.e. we solve
Eq.

3.18
with
\xi=0
, and thus
E(x,\xi)=E_{0}(x)
. The computation requires 2,000 iterations of our optimization algorithm, for a CPU time of about 3h 15 mn. The resulting design and the convergence history are reported on
Fig.

12
(b) and (c).

Remark 3.3
.

In practice, we use small penalizations of the objective function
D(h,\xi)
by the volume
\text{\rm Vol}(h)
and the compliance
C(h,\xi)
of the structure.
The former helps removing unnecessary material islands disconnected from the main structure, while the latter enforces the connectedness of the structure,
in spite of its trend to develop thin hinges, mimicking pointwise junctions, see e.g.
[
115
]
about this common practice in the design of compliant mechanisms.

We now consider a situation where the Young’s modulus
E\equiv E(x,\xi)
is unknown: its expression
Eq.

3.16
stems from the Karhunen-Loève expansion of the covariance kernel
\text{\rm Cov}(x,y)
, after truncation at
k=10
modes. We thus solve a distributionally robust version of the optimization problem
Eq.

3.18
, assuming a Wasserstein ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
based on the nominal law
{\mathbb{P}}=\delta_{0}
, see
Section

2.2
. The problem at hand reads:

(3.20)

\min_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\;\left(\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\;\int_{\Xi}D(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right).

The parameter
\varepsilon
is set to
\varepsilon=0.01
.
Invoking
Proposition

2.1
,
Eq.

3.20
rewrites under the more
convenient form:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\lambda\geq 0}{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda),\text{ where } {\mathcal{D}}_{\text{W}}(h,\lambda)=\lambda m+\lambda\varepsilon\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{D(h,\zeta)-c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi),

where the probability measure
\nu_{\xi}
in
Eq.

2.7
has variance
\sigma^{2}=1e{-1}
.

We solve this problem for several values of the radius
m
of the Wasserstein ball; the results are reported on
Fig.

13
; each computation requires 2,000 iterations of our numerical algorithm, for a CPU time of about 13h.
The resulting designs are quite similar from one another; note however the change in the orientation of the hinges sustaining the jaws between the cases
m=0.5
and
m=1
.
Let us also emphasize that it is particularly difficult to achieve a “true” black-and-white design in this example: in spite of the penalization of the objective function by the volume hinted at in
Remark

3.3
, the designs eliminate intermediate densities at a very slow rate.

This section illustrates the ideas of
Section

2.4.2
, regarding optimal design problems where the conditional value at risk of the cost function is used as a means to account for a failure probability. Before dealing with distributional robustness, we develop a little about this use of conditional value at risk, as it is relatively confidential in optimal design, to the best of our knowledge.

D
with size
1\times 2

8,500

3.6.2.
Enforcement of a safety constraint via the conditional value at risk

We now assume that the load
\xi
applied on
\Gamma_{N}
is uncertain, but that its law
{\mathbb{P}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
is known perfectly: it is (the restriction to a large ball
\Xi\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of) the bivariate Gaussian law centered at
\xi^{0}
, with variance
\sigma^{2}
.
We then wish to solve the following problem:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}}}\text{\rm Vol}(h)\>\text{ s.t. }\>{\mathbb{P}}\Big\{\xi\in\Xi\text{ s.t. }C(h,\xi)\geq C_{T}\Big\}\leq 1-\beta,

where the threshold
C_{T}:=40
is close to the optimal value
C(h^{*}_{\text{det}},\xi^{0})
attained in the unperturbed minimization problem
Eq.

3.21
.
According to the discussion in
Section

2.4
, we replace this problem by the following conservative version:

(3.22)

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\;\text{\rm Vol}(h)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}\left[C(h,\xi)-\alpha\right]_{+}\;\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\leq C_{T}.

We solve this problem for several values of the variance
\sigma^{2}
of the law
{\mathbb{P}}
for
\xi
and the threshold
\beta
:
the resulting designs are presented in
Fig.

15
, see also
Table

3
for the associated values of the volume.
In each situation, about
600
iterations of the optimization strategy described in
Section

3.2
are used, for an approximate CPU cost of 2h 30 mn. Again,
N=10
samples are used for the numerical computation of each probabilistic integral featured in
Eq.

3.22
.
Understandably enough, the closer
\beta
is to
1
, the more stringent the failure constraint, and the larger the resulting volume. Loosely speaking,
the most significant structural modifications occur in the lower vertical part of the domain, which tends to split into two sections joined by a reinforcement crossbar.
We also note that it is more difficult for the design to converge towards clear black-and-white features (with the exact same numerical strategy), as intermediate densities seem to become more favorable.

3.6.3.
Distributionally robust optimal design considering failure probabilities

We now turn to the situation where the law of the load parameter
\xi
is itself uncertain, which raises the need for a distributionally robust formulation of the optimal design problem
Eq.

3.22
. The nominal distribution is made of a single observation, associated to the ideal load
\xi^{0}=(0,-1)
:
{{\mathbb{P}}}=\delta_{\xi^{0}}
.
Assuming an ambiguity set of the Wasserstein type as in
Section

2.2.2
, the distributionally robust version of the problem
Eq.

3.22
reads:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\;\text{\rm Vol}(h)\text{ s.t. }\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\min\limits_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\alpha+\dfrac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}\left[\mathcal{C}(h,\xi)-\alpha\right]_{+}\;\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right)\leq C_{T}.

According to
Section

2.4
, this rewrites:

\min\limits_{h\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\text{\rm ad}},\atop\lambda\geq 0,\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\;\text{\rm Vol}(h)\>\text{ s.t. }\;{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{C}}(h,\lambda,\alpha)\leq C_{T},\text{
where }\\
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{C}}(h,\lambda,\alpha)=\alpha+\frac{\lambda m}{1-\beta}+\frac{\lambda\varepsilon}{1-\beta}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{\left[C(h,\zeta)-\alpha\right]_{+}-\lambda c(\xi^{0},\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi^{0}}(\zeta)\right).

This program is solved for several values of the threshold
\beta
, for two different values of the variance
\sigma^{2}
of the reference coupling
\nu_{\xi^{0}}
, and for the radius
m=0.5
. The results are depicted on
Figs.

16
and
17
. According to intuition, when perturbations become sufficiently large, the optimization algorithm gives privilege to the diagonal bars over the central vertical pillar of the structure. Also, as the variance
\sigma^{2}
increases, the constraint becomes increasingly difficult to satisfy.

4.
Distributionally Robust Geometric Shape Optimization

This section applies the generic distributionally robust formulations introduced in
Section

2
in the context of geometric shape optimization. After presenting the mechanical and mathematical settings in
Section

4.1
, as well as the employed numerical methods for shape optimization in
Section

4.2
, we conduct two numerical experiments in this context in
Sections

4.3
and
4.4
.

4.1.
Shape optimization of linear elastic structures

We first recall a few basic facts about the optimization of the shape of elastic structures.

4.1.1.
Linear elastic structures

The designs considered henceforth are shapes, i.e. bounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d=2
or
3
) representing elastic structures. The boundary
\partial\Omega
of each shape
\Omega
is decomposed into three disjoint regions:

\partial\Omega=\overline{\Gamma_{D}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{N}}\cup\overline{\Gamma},

where:

•

The shape is clamped on the region
\Gamma_{D}
;

•

Surface loads
g:\Gamma_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are applied on
\Gamma_{N}
;

•

The remaining part
\Gamma
is traction-free and it is the only region of
\partial\Omega
which is subject to optimization.

Omitting body forces for simplicity, the elastic displacement of
\Omega
is the unique solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
to the linear elasticity system:

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(Ae(u))=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
Ae(u)n=g&\text{on }\Gamma_{N},\\
Ae(u)n=0&\text{on }\Gamma.\end{array}\right.

Again, we have omitted body forces for simplicity. The isotropic and homogeneous properties of the constituent material of shapes are encoded in the Hooke’s tensor
A
given by
Eq.

3.1
.
In the sequel, we use various indices or arguments for
u
and related quantities of interest, in order to indicate their dependence on the relevant parameters of this model, notably the loads
g
and the shape
\Omega
itself.

An archetypal shape optimization problem in this context reads:

(4.2)

\min_{\Omega}\>C(\Omega)\quad\text{ s.t. }\quad\text{\rm Vol}(\Omega)=V_{T},

where
C(\Omega)
is the compliance of
\Omega
, i.e.:

(4.3)

C(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}Ae(u_{\Omega}):e(u_{\Omega})\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\Gamma_{N}}g\cdot u_{\Omega}\,\text{\rm d}s,

and
\text{\rm Vol}(\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}dx
is the volume, which should match a target value
V_{T}
.

4.1.2.
Shape sensitivity by the method of Hadamard

When it comes to study the sensitivity of a function with respect to the domain, we rely on the boundary variation method of Hadamard, about which we refer to e.g.
[
11
,
58
,
82
,
103
]
.
In brief, this method is based on variations of a reference shape
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of the form:

\Omega_{\theta}:=(\text{\rm Id}+\theta)(\Omega),\quad\text{where }\theta\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\|\theta\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})}<1,

i.e.
\Omega_{\theta}
is a version of
\Omega
deformed by a “small” vector field
\theta
, see
Fig.

18
.

Figure 18
.
Variation
\Omega_{\theta}=(\text{\rm Id}+\theta)(\Omega)
of a shape
\Omega
by the method of Hadamard.

Definition 4.1
.

A functional
F(\Omega)
is called shape differentiable at a particular domain
\Omega
if the mapping

W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni\theta\mapsto F(\Omega_{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}

is Fréchet differentiable at
0
. Its derivative
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)
is called the shape derivative of
F
at
\Omega
, and the following expansion holds true:

(4.4)

F(\Omega_{\theta})=F(\Omega)+F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)+\text{\rm o}(\theta),\text{where }\frac{\text{\rm o}(\theta)}{\|\theta\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})}}\xrightarrow{\theta\to 0}0.

It follows directly from this definition that a vector field
\theta
satisfying
F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)<0
is a descent direction for
F(\Omega)
, i.e. for a small enough pseudo-time step
t>0
,
F(\Omega_{t\theta})<F(\Omega)
. More generally, in the context of a constrained optimization problem, of the form
Eq.

4.3
, the knowledge of the shape derivatives of the objective and constraint functions allows to infer a suitable “descent direction” via a constrained optimization method such as the Null-space algorithm used in this work
[
45
]
.
The shape derivatives of the functions
F(\Omega)
considered in this article often take the convenient form:

F^{\prime}(\Omega)(\theta)=\int_{\partial\Omega}v_{\Omega}\,\theta\cdot n\,\text{\rm d}s.

A descent direction for
F(\Omega)
is immediately revealed from this structure, by taking
\theta=-v_{\Omega}n
on
\partial\Omega
.

4.2.
Numerical resolution of shape optimization problems by a level set based mesh evolution strategy

Our numerical resolution of shape optimization problems of the form
Eq.

4.2
hinges on the level set based mesh evolution method introduced in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, an open-source implementation of which is provided in
[
31
]
.
Briefly, introducing a fixed computational domain
D\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we rely on two complementary representations of each shape
\Omega\subset D
arising during the optimization process:

•

(Level set representation) As proposed in
[
87
]
– see also
[
10
,
111
]
in the shape optimization context –
\Omega
is represented implicitly, as the negative subdomain of a “level set” function
\phi:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\phi(x)<0&\text{if }x\in\Omega,\\
\phi(x)=0&\text{if }x\in\partial\Omega,\\
\phi(x)>0&\text{if }x\in D\setminus\overline{\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

In practice, such a function
\phi
is discretized at the vertices of a simplicial mesh
{\mathcal{T}}
of
D
(i.e. made of triangles in 2d, of tetrahedra in 3d), see
Fig.

19
(a).

•

(Meshed representation) The domain
D
is equipped with a conforming, high-quality simplicial mesh
{\mathcal{T}}
, made of two disjoint submeshes,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathrm{int}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
, which respectively represent
\Omega
and its complement
D\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, see
Fig.

19
(b).

Efficient numerical techniques make it possible to switch from one of these representations to the other:

•

Given a meshed representation of a shape
\Omega\subset D
, one particular level set function for
\Omega
is a calculated as its signed distance function, thanks to the famous Fast Marching Algorithm
[
101
]
; this task relies on the open-source software
mshdist
from our previous work
[
32
]
.

•

Assuming a level set representation of
\Omega\subset D
, from the datum of the values of a level set function
\phi
at the vertices of a mesh
{\mathcal{T}}
of
D
, a new mesh
\widetilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
of
D
in which
\Omega
is explicitly discretized is obtained thanks to a remeshing procedure, implemented in the open-source library
mmg
, see
[
15
,
30
]
.

\begin{overpic}[figures/LevelSet/lsrep_compressed.pdf] \put(2.0,5.0){\fcolorbox{black}{white}{a}}\end{overpic}

\begin{overpic}[figures/LevelSet/meshrep-min.png] \put(2.0,5.0){\fcolorbox{black}{white}{b}}\end{overpic}

Figure 19
.
Two complementary representations of a shape
\Omega\subset D
; (a) Graph of a level set function
\phi:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
; (b) Corresponding meshed representation.

Each operation of the shape optimization workflow is executed by using the most appropriate representation. In particular, “physical” boundary value problems posed on a shape
\Omega\subset D
are solved by applying the Finite Element method on its meshed representation, which allows to use any numerical solver in a black-box manner; in our framework, we use the open-source platform
FreeFem

[
57
]
. Conversely, the level set representation is particularly well-suited to track the evolution of shape
\Omega(t)
over a time interval
(0,T)
, under the action of a prescribed velocity field
V(t,x)
. This evolution is indeed encoded in the following advection-like equation:

\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(t,x)+V(t,x)\cdot\nabla\phi(t,x)=0\text{ for }t\in(0,T),\,x\in D,

which can be solved e.g. by the method of characteristics
[
91
]
; we rely to this end on the open-source code
advection
from
[
21
]
.

Summarizing, this strategy features a body-fitted mesh of the shape
\Omega
at each stage of the workflow, allowing for precise mechanical analyses based on any finite element solver, while leaving the room for arbitrarily large deformations between successive optimization iterations.

4.3.
Distributionally robust shape optimization of the stress within a 2d T-shaped beam under load uncertainties

Let the shape
\Omega
account for a 2d T-shaped beam, enclosed in a box
D
with size
2\times 1
, see
Fig.

20
(a).
The beam is clamped on the upper region
\Gamma_{D}
of
\partial D
;
under ideal conditions, it is subjected to two identical vertical loads
\xi^{0}=(0,-1)
, applied on two small regions at the left and right parts of
\partial D
, whose reunion is denoted by
\Gamma_{N}
. In the following, we denote by
u_{\Omega,\xi}
the displacement of the beam, solution to
Eq.

4.1
when the load
\xi
is applied.

We aim to minimize the total stress within
\Omega
under a constraint on its volume, i.e. we consider the following shape optimization problem:

(4.5)

\min\limits_{\Omega\subset D}S(\Omega,\xi^{0})\>\text{ s.t. } \text{\rm Vol}(\Omega)=V_{T}.

Here, the stress function is defined by

S(\Omega,\xi)=\int_{\Omega}\chi(x)||\sigma(u_{\Omega,\xi})||^{2}\;\text{\rm d}x,

where the stress
\sigma(u)
associated to a displacement field
u
is
\sigma(u)=Ae(u)
, and
\lvert\lvert\cdot\lvert\lvert
denotes the usual Frobenius norm over matrices. The weight
\chi(x)
equals
1
everywhere on
D
except on two small non-optimizable neighborhoods of the region
\Gamma_{N}
where it is set to
0
(these zones are depicted in blue in
Fig.

20
(a)) Eventually, in
Eq.

4.5
, the target volume
V_{T}
is set to
V_{T}=0.3
.

The shape derivative of the stress-based functional
S(\Omega,\xi)
is given by the following proposition, which is taken from e.g.
[
9
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The shape derivative of the functional
S(\Omega,\xi)
equals:

S^{\prime}(\Omega,\xi)(\theta)=\int_{\Omega}Ae(u_{\Omega,\xi}):e(p_{\Omega,\xi})\>\theta\cdot n\>\text{\rm d}s,

where the adjoint state
p_{\Omega,\xi}
is the unique solution in
H^{1}(\Omega)^{2}
to the following boundary-value problem:

(4.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\text{\rm div}(Ae(p_{\Omega,\xi}))=-2\text{\rm div}(\chi(x)A\sigma(u_{\Omega,\xi}))&\text{in }\Omega,\\
p_{\Omega,\xi}=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\\
Ae(p_{\Omega,\xi})n=2A\sigma(u_{\Omega,\xi})n&\text{on }\partial\Omega\setminus\overline{\Gamma_{D}}.\end{array}\right.

We first solve this ideal problem with the algorithm presented in
Section

4.2
.
About
300
iterations of our optimization algorithm are applied, corresponding to a computation of about 16 mn; the resulting shape
\Omega^{*}_{\text{det}}
is depicted on
Fig.

20
(c). The corresponding value of the stress functional is
S(\Omega^{*}_{\text{det}},\xi^{0})=1.99433
. The meshes of
D
produced at each iteration of the optimization process, made of submeshes for the structure
\Omega
and its complement, contain about
6,500
vertices (
\approx 13,000
triangles).

We now assume that the applied load
\xi

\Xi\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and its probability law is also uncertain – the only available information about the latter being that it is “close” to the nominal distribution
{\mathbb{P}}=\delta_{\xi^{0}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)
reconstructed from the single ideal load
\xi^{0}
. We thus turn to a distributionally robust version of
Eq.

4.5
:

(4.7)

\min\limits_{\Omega}\left(\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\int_{\Xi}S(\Omega,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right),\text{ s.t. } \text{\rm Vol}(\Omega)=V_{T},

where
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
is the Wasserstein ambiguity set defined in
Eq.

2.9
.
Using the material in
Section

2.2
, this problem rewrites:

5.
Conclusion and Perspectives

This article was concerned with exploring the idea of distributional robustness in the context of shape and topology optimization: acknowledging that the physical parameters characterizing the behavior of real-life devices are inherently known with uncertainty, and that so is their probability law, we have proposed tractable robust formulations for a wide range of optimal design problems, that incorporate a degree of awareness about these sources of uncertainty. These formulations are not limited to a specific optimal design context and they can be used in both frameworks of density-based topology optimization and geometric shape optimization, in two and three space dimensions.
As demonstrated by the numerical results, one key feature of distributionally robust optimal design formulations is their ability to anticipate scenarios “close”, but different from the observations used for the empirical reconstruction of the probability law of the uncertain parameters.

This work paves the way to research avenues of various kinds. From the theoretical viewpoint, we would like to justify rigorously that our tractable distributionally robust formulations, based on “small” penalizations by suitable entropy functionals, are consistent with their exact counterparts, as the penalty parameter tends to
0
.
From the numerical viewpoint, an obvious perspective concerns the choice of the optimization algorithm. The stochastic gradient indeed produces highly oscillatory trajectories, which can hinder convergence and degrade the quality of the final solution, particularly in high-variance settings. A different optimization algorithm, such as ADAM
[
65
]
, could help to reduce the variance of descent direction estimates.
Furthermore, the naive sampling strategy used in this work for evaluating probabilistic integrals could be improved, for instance by using variance reduction techniques such as importance sampling.
From the modeling viewpoint, manifold physical situations suffer from uncertainty over physical parameters, and we wish to apply our techniques in e.g. thermal conduction, fluid mechanics or electromagnetism, to name a few.
On a different note, the particular situation where the geometry of shapes itself is uncertain has been ignored in this work, in spite of its ubiquity and importance in applications. We believe that, with the help of the linearization techniques similar to those introduced in our former works
[
3
,
4
]
, it is possible to give a computationally affordable formulation of such problems. A foretaste of this important avenue for future research can be consulted in the Ph.D. manuscript
[
93
]
.

Appendix A
Hint of Proof of
Proposition

2.1

Let
A:=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}}\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)
be the maximum value at the left-hand side of the relation to be established
Eq.

2.11
. We classically start by rewriting
A
with the help of a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{W}}
; it holds:

A=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)}\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)+\lambda\left(m-W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})\right)\right).

In fact, the inner infimum in this formula equals
0
when
{\mathbb{Q}}
is such that
W_{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathbb{Q}})\leq m
and
-\infty
otherwise.

Now, formally interchanging the infimum and supremum in the above formula, and invoking the definition
Eq.

2.8
of the regularized Wasserstein distance
W_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot)
, we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}A&=&\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(\lambda m+\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)}\sup\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi\times\Xi)\atop\pi_{1}={\mathbb{P}},\>\pi_{2}={\mathbb{Q}}}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)-\lambda\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}c(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon H(\pi)\right)\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0}\left(\lambda m+\sup\limits_{\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi\times\Xi)\atop\pi_{1}={\mathbb{P}}}\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\left(f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}\pi(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon H(\pi)\right).\end{array}

We now proceed to reformulate the inner supremum, that we denote by
B
. The latter ranges over all probability couplings
\pi
whose first marginal equals
{\mathbb{P}}
. Recalling that the entropy of a coupling
\pi
blows up when
\pi
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{0}
, this supremum can be recast over couplings of the form

(A.1)

\pi=\alpha(\xi,\zeta)\>\pi_{0},\text{ where }\alpha\in L^{1}(\Xi\times\Xi;\text{\rm d}\pi_{0})\text{ satisfies }\int_{\Xi}\alpha(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)=1\text{ for }{\mathbb{P}}\text{-a.e. }\xi\in\Xi.

Hence,

(A.2)

B=\sup\limits_{\alpha\in L^{1}(\Xi\times\Xi;\text{\rm d}\pi_{0})\atop\int_{\Xi}\alpha(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)=1}\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\Big(f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon\log\alpha(\xi,\zeta)\Big)\alpha(\xi,\zeta)\>\text{\rm d}\pi_{0}(\xi,\zeta).

It turns out that this maximization problem, involving a strictly concave objective function, can be solved explicitly. Indeed, according to the first-order optimality conditions, there exists a function
\mu(\xi)
such that its unique solution
\alpha(\xi,\zeta)
satisfies:

f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon\log\alpha(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon+\mu(\xi)=0,

and so:

\alpha(\xi,\zeta)=e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon+\mu(\xi)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}.

Recalling the normalization
Eq.

A.1
, the function
\mu(\xi)
is characterized by:

e^{\frac{\mu(\xi)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}=\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)-\lambda\varepsilon}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)^{-1},

whence:

\alpha(\xi,\zeta)=\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)^{-1}e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}.

Eventually, injecting this expression in
Eq.

A.2
, a simple calculation yields:

B=\lambda\varepsilon\int_{\Xi}\log\left(\int_{\Xi}e^{\frac{f(\zeta)-\lambda c(\xi,\zeta)}{\lambda\varepsilon}}\>\text{\rm d}\nu_{\xi}(\zeta)\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi),

and the desired formula
Eq.

2.11
follows immediately.

\square

Appendix B
Hint of Proof of
Proposition

2.2

We rely on an argument similar to that employed in
Appendix

A
. Let
A:=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{M}}}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right)
be the worst-case of the (penalized) mean value of the function
f(\xi)
when
{\mathbb{Q}}
runs through the moment-based ambiguity set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\text{M}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}(\xi)
. By expressing this constraint with Lagrange multipliers, we obtain:

A=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)}\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0,\atop S\in{\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{+}}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)+\lambda\left(m_{1}-\left\lvert\mu_{0}-\int_{\Xi}\xi\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right\lvert\right)\right.\\
\left.+S:\left(m_{2}\Sigma_{0}-\int_{\Xi}(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right).

Thanks to the simple formula

\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k},\quad\lvert\xi\lvert=\sup\limits_{\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k},\atop\lvert\tau\lvert\leq 1}\tau\cdot\xi,

this rewrites:

A=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)}\inf\limits_{\lambda\geq 0,\atop{\lvert\tau\lvert\leq 1,\atop S\in{\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{+}}}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)+\lambda m_{1}-\lambda\tau\cdot\left(\mu_{0}-\int_{\Xi}\xi\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right)\right.\\
\left.+\>S:\left(m_{2}\Sigma_{0}-\int_{\Xi}(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)\right)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right).

A formal exchange of the infimum and supremum in the above formula then yields:

A=\inf\limits_{\lambda\geqslant 0,\atop{|\tau|\leqslant 1,\atop S\in{\mathbb{S}}^{k}_{+}}}\left(\lambda m_{1}-\lambda\tau\cdot\mu_{0}+m_{2}S:\Sigma_{0}+B\right),\text{ where }\\
B:=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\Xi)}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\int_{\Xi}\xi\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-S:\int_{\Xi}(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right).\\
=\sup\limits_{{\mathbb{Q}}\text{ a.c. w.r.t. }\mathbb{Q}_{0}}\left(\int_{\Xi}f(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\int_{\Xi}\xi\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-S:\int_{\Xi}(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}(\xi)-\varepsilon H({\mathbb{Q}})\right),

where we have sued the fact that
H({\mathbb{Q}})
takes infinite values when
{\mathbb{Q}}
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}
.
We now proceed to calculate the quantity
B
:

(B.1)

B:=\sup\limits_{\alpha\in L^{1}(\Xi,\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}),\atop\int_{\Xi}\alpha\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}=1}\int_{\Xi}\Big(f(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})-\varepsilon\log\alpha(\xi)\Big)\alpha(\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}(\xi).

The necessary optimality condition for this strictly concave maximization problem yields the existence of a real value
\mu
such that the maximizer
\alpha
satisfies:

f(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})-\varepsilon\log\alpha(\xi)-\varepsilon+\mu=0.

By rearranging this identity and using the normalization condition for
\alpha
, we then obtain:

\alpha(\xi)=\frac{1}{\int_{\Xi}\left(e^{\frac{f(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}(\xi)}e^{\frac{f(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})}{\varepsilon}}.

Now inserting this expression for
\alpha
in
Eq.

B.1
, we eventually end up with:

B=\varepsilon\log\left(\int_{\Xi}\left(e^{\frac{f(\xi)+\lambda\tau\cdot\xi-S:(\xi-\mu_{0})\otimes(\xi-\mu_{0})}{\varepsilon}}\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}(\xi)\right),

which leads to the desired result
Eq.

2.16
.

\square

Appendix C
Hint of Proof of
Proposition

2.3

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to providing a sketch of the proof of this result in the case where the cumulative distribution function
\Psi(h,\cdot)
of the law
{\mathbb{P}}
is continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e. there is no subset of
\Xi
with positive measure where the cost function
{\mathcal{C}}(h,\cdot)
takes a given value
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Note however that the result holds true in the general case, see

.
Let us set

F(h,\alpha)=\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\Xi}\left[{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)-\alpha\right]_{+}\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi).

A simple calculation shows that:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}F(h,\alpha)&=&\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha\right\}}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)-\alpha)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\\
&=&\frac{1}{1-\beta}\left(\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}\alpha\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)+\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha\right\}}({\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)-\alpha)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\right),\end{array}

where we have used
Eq.

2.18
to pass from the first line to the second one. Let us now make out two cases:

•

If
\alpha_{\beta}(h)<\alpha
, a simple calculation yields:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}F(h,\alpha)&=&\frac{1}{1-\beta}\left(\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>\alpha_{\beta}(h)\leq{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\leq\alpha\right\}}\alpha\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)+\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha\right\}}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}\max\left({\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi),\alpha\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\\[10.00002pt]
&\geq&\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\\
&=&\phi_{\beta}(h).\end{array}

•

If
\alpha<\alpha_{\beta}(h)
, it holds similarly:

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}F(h,\alpha)&=&\frac{1}{1-\beta}\Big(-\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>\alpha\leq{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\leq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}\alpha\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)+\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\geq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\\[10.00002pt]
&&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>\alpha\leq{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\leq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\Big)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\phi_{\beta}(h)+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\left\{\xi\in\Xi,\>\>\alpha\leq{\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)\leq\alpha_{\beta}(h)\right\}}\left({\mathcal{C}}(h,\xi)-\alpha\right)\>\text{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)\\[10.00002pt]
&\geq&\phi_{\beta}(h).\end{array}

This proves that
F(h,\alpha)\leq\phi_{\beta}(h)
for all
\alpha\neq\alpha_{\beta}(h)
, while the definition of
\alpha_{\beta}(h)
immediately implies that
F(h,\alpha_{\beta}(h))=\phi_{\beta}(h)
; this terminates the proof of the proposition.

\square
