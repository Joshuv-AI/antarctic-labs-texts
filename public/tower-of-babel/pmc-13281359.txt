Culturable microbiota of reared mosquito fauna in Osogbo, Nigeria and their potential biological roles: a molecular insight
Authors: Omotayo O. Oyedara, Sunday O. Olaniyan, Sandra O. Onakhinor, Folasade M. Adeyemi, Abideen A. Wahab, Waidi F. Sule, Babafemi S. Ajisebiola, Temidayo O. Elufisan, Sunday B. Akinde, Monsuru A. Adeleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMosquitoes are vectors of deadly, life-threatening diseases worldwide. There is limited information on the interactions between microbes and local mosquito fauna in the Nigerian ecotype and Osun State. This study employed molecular techniques to characterize microorganisms isolated from the internal tissues of adult mosquitoes reared from larvae and pupae collected from various breeding sites in the Osogbo metropolis, Nigeria. Bacteria and yeasts were isolated from immobilized, surface-sterilized, and homogenized mosquitoes. Molecular identification of microbes was based on the Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) technique and sequencing of the 16S rDNA gene and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region for bacteria and yeasts, respectively. The genus Wolbachia was screened with PCR using Wolbachia-specific primers.ResultsThe adult mosquitoes harboured bacteria, namely Enterobacter bugandensis, Staphylococcus haemolyticus, Staphylococcus capitis, Enterobacter hormaechei, Pantoea dispersa, Sphingobium yanoikuyae, and Aerococcus urinaeequi. Yeasts identified were Meyerozyma caribbica, Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, and Candida orthopsilosis. The genus Wolbachia was not detected in the mosquitoes. The bacteria and yeast isolates from this study can play important roles in the biology of mosquitoes. Notably, S. yanoikuyae possesses bio-degradative potential, as reported in previous studies. Hence, this underscores the need for further investigation of the role of Sphingobium species in mosquito resistance to insecticides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07832-4.

Introduction

Mosquitoes are arthropod vectors responsible for the transmission of disease agents of public health importance, such as malaria, dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever, and lymphatic filariasis [1]. Mosquitoes are associated with microorganisms, which they acquire from breeding sites, during feeding on plant nectar or blood, and during transstadial development [2, 3, 4]. These microorganisms influence the transmission of disease agents, vectorial competence and capacity, reproduction, insecticide resistance, and survival in mosquitoes [5]. Some of these microbes, including Wolbachia (an unculturable intracellular mosquito endosymbiont), have been implicated in para-transgenesis, an approach that reduces pathogen populations in mosquitoes [4, 6, 7].

Most previous studies on mosquito microbiota focused more on bacterial composition, with limited information on the yeast population and its biological functions [8, 9]. Additionally, there is a paucity of information on the microbiota associated with local mosquito fauna in Nigeria. It is expected that studies aimed at understanding the microbial interactions of mosquitoes reared from immature stages with little interaction with the wild environment could offer insights into the microbial species that have the potential to form stable relationships with mosquitoes and be acquired transstadially.

Nonculture-dependent approaches such as metagenomics have provided information on the microbial populations associated with mosquitoes [10, 11]. Nevertheless, the application of culture-dependent approaches is still relevant for studying culturable microbes and understanding their roles in the biology of mosquitoes [1]. The use of molecular methods to identify microorganisms offers advantages over conventional phenotypic methods, which are time-consuming and may sometimes lead to misidentification of microbial species. Therefore, this study employed both cultural and molecular techniques to investigate the culturable microbiota associated with adult mosquito species reared from immature stages collected from breeding sites in Osogbo, Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Sampling sites and sample collection

This study was conducted in Osogbo, a cosmopolitan town situated in the southwestern geographical zone of Nigeria. Osogbo has an area of 47 km² and an elevation of 320 m. It has rainforest and derived savannah vegetation, characterized by a tropical climate, with an average annual rainfall of 1361 mm and a temperature of 25.5°C. Water samples in abandoned containers and tyres, containing mosquito larvae and pupae, were collected from three locations in Osogbo, namely Sabo (7o 46’ N 4o 34’ E), Ajegunle (7o 46’ N 4o 33’ E), and Bishop’s Court (7o 45’ N 4o 32’ E). Additionally, stagnant rainwater was collected from Ilesa garage (7o 45’ N 4o 34’ E), Osogbo (Supplementary Fig. S1). Samples were collected into clean containers covered with mosquito net and transported to the laboratory, where the larvae and pupae were reared to adult stages under standard conditions.

Isolation of bacteria and yeasts from mosquitoes

Emerged adult mosquitoes were euthanized by freezing at -20 °C and then identified with a dissecting microscope and morphological keys described by Gillet (1972) [12]. For bacterial and yeast isolation, individual mosquitoes were rinsed three times with sterile water and later surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol for 10 min, followed by rinsing five times with sterile distilled water and once in 500 µL of phosphate buffer solution (PBS). The final rinse PBS was cultured on nutrient agar (NA) and potato dextrose agar (PDA) to confirm the removal of surface bacteria and fungi, respectively. Surface-sterilized mosquitoes of the same genus and sex and from the same breeding sites were processed as a pool of 3 (minimum) or 10 (maximum) mosquitoes, depending on the number of mosquitoes obtained for each genus. Mosquitoes were crushed and homogenized in 1 mL of PBS using a mortar and pestle. Using the spread plate technique, 10 µL of the homogenate was cultured on NA and PDA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, distinct colonies were subcultured on appropriate culture media to obtain pure bacterial and yeast colonies.

Molecular identification of microbial isolates and detection of Wolbachia in mosquitoes

DNA was extracted from 1 mL of an overnight liquid culture of isolated bacteria (nutrient broth) or yeast (YPD: 1% yeast extract and 2% peptone) using the Bust n’ Grab protocol [13]. For Wolbachia detection, DNA extraction was performed on 426 pooled and homogenized mosquitoes using the NIMR Biotech DNA Purification Kit (NIMR, Lagos) according to the manufacturer’s specifications. PCR amplification was performed using 16S rDNA gene and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region universal primers, respectively, for bacteria and yeast identification [14, 15] and 16S rRNA Wolbachia-specific primers for Wolbachia detection [16] (Supplementary Table S1). The PCR conditions for bacterial 16S rDNA amplification and Wolbachia detection were initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, annealing at 60 °C for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min [16]. For the yeast ITS region, initial denaturation was performed at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 60 s, annealing at 56 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min. A final extension was then performed at 72 °C for 7 min. The amplified product was analyzed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis run at 80 V for 1 h. Successful amplicons were sequenced by Inqaba Biotec West Africa (South Africa). The sequences obtained were edited using FinchTV software and further subjected to a BLAST search against sequences deposited in the GenBank (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST) for the identification of isolated bacteria and yeasts. Phylogenetic analysis and tree construction were done using MEGA 11 [17].

Results and discussion

Identification of emerged adult mosquitoes

A total of 586 mosquitoes were obtained from the four mosquito breeding sites (Supplementary Fig. S1). The highest number of mosquitoes (362; 61.77%) was obtained from Ajegunle (Fig. 1), followed by Ilesa garage (108; 18.43%), Bishop’s court (60; 10.24%), and Sabo area (56; 9.56%). The emerged adult mosquitoes belong to four genera: Aedes (406; 69.28%), Anopheles (69; 11.77%), Culex (67; 11.43%), and Toxorhynchites (44; 7.50%) (Supplementary Fig. S2). These four genera of mosquitoes have been previously reported in Osogbo, where the city has ambient conditions that support the continuous breeding and survival of mosquito vectors [18, 19]. The genera Aedes and Culex were recovered from all the breeding sites. The genus Toxorhynchites emerged only from larvae and pupae collected from Ajegunle and Sabo, whereas Anopheles emerged only from samples collected from Ilesa garage (Fig. 1). The majority of the Culex spp. (50) were recovered from samples collected from Bishop’s court (Fig. 1). The difference observed in the distribution of the mosquitoes in the sampling locations may be attributed to the variation in the breeding habitat requirements of the different genera of mosquitoes. For example, Aedes spp. are known for their preference for artificial containers, unlike Anopheles mosquitoes, which prefer naturally established habitats with less pollution [18, 20]. Culex, Aedes, and Anopheles are involved in the transmission of disease agents for dengue, malaria, Zika, and yellow fever. Hence, their occurrence in Osogbo poses a public health threat to residents around the sample collection sites.

Isolation and molecular identification of bacterial and yeast isolates

For the isolation and molecular identification of bacterial and yeast isolates, 160 mosquitoes (Aedes = 115, Anopheles = 20, Culex = 15, and Toxorhynchites = 10) were processed. Twenty-four bacteria were isolated from the pool of mosquitoes. Meanwhile, 16S rDNA gene PCR amplification was successful for 15 bacterial isolates. However, during the BLAST analysis, only nine sequences returned an identity with sequences in GenBank. According to the BLASTn search analysis, the nucleotide sequences of the gram-negative bacterial isolates shared percentage identity with sequences of Enterobacter bugandensis (97.62–98.07%), Pantoea dispersa (97.91%), Sphingobium yanoikuyae (98.50%), Enterobacter hormaechei (97.28%), and Enterobacteriaceae bacterium (72.37%) (Table 1). The nucleotide sequences of the gram-positive bacterial isolates shared identity with those of Staphylococcus haemolyticus (99.89%), Staphylococcus capitis (99.77%), and Aerococcus urinaeequi (96.07%) (Table 1).

The genera Enterobacter and Pantoea are closely related members of the family Enterobacteriaceae, as observed in the molecular phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2A). The genera Enterobacter, Pantoea, and Staphylococcus have been isolated from different parts and faeces of most mosquito species [21, 22], suggesting their close association with mosquitoes. They have also been suggested as candidates for para-transgenesis in mosquitoes [23]. Moreover, bacterial strains of the genera Enterobacter and Staphylococcus are of public health significance because they are potential human pathogens [24]. Aerococcus urinaeequi is mostly associated with urinary tract infections in horses [25], and its presence has been reported in medically relevant mosquitoes, such as Culex quinquefasciatus and Culiseta longiareolata [26, 27], vectors of the West Nile virus, which causes disease in horses and humans.

Asimakis et al. (2025) [28] identified Sphingobium sp. as part of the bacteria in Aedes albopictus. The biological roles of the genus Sphingobium in mosquitoes have not been experimentally established. Studies have shown that Sphingobium spp. are abundant in different developmental stages of some insects, where they aid in the degradation of complex organic compounds and insecticides [29, 30]. Moreover, Sphingobium sp., which is capable of degrading insecticides such as pyrethroids, fenitrothion, and diazinon, has been isolated from different environmental sources [31, 32]. These findings suggest that Sphingobium may play an important role in the degradation of compounds such as insecticides, hence contributing to insecticide resistance in mosquitoes.

Fourteen yeast isolates were obtained from the analysed mosquitoes based on morphological features. However, only four genes were successfully sequenced (Table 1; Fig. 2B), and their sequences were similar to those of Candida orthopsilosis (100%), Meyerozyma caribbica (99.83%), and Rhodotorula mucilaginosa (99.66%). Studies on the mycobiota of mosquitoes have recently received much attention because of the possibility of mosquitoes serving as vectors for the dissemination of potential yeast pathogens [8, 9]. Candida orthopsilosis and Rhodotorula mucilaginosa are opportunistic pathogens that have been reported in the larvae and adult stages of Culex pipens [33]. Furthermore, Bozic et al. (2017) [8] isolated these yeasts from rearing water used in breeding mosquitoes and established that C. orthopsilosis maintains a stable relationship with laboratory-reared and wild Anopheles gambiae, Culex quinquefasciatus, Aedes albopictus, and Aedes aegypti. Many insects are believed to be in close relationship with the genus Meyerozyma, and their dominance has been reported in the midgut of mosquitoes [34]. Recently, Hu et al. (2025) [35] reported the key role of fungi, including R. mucilaginosa and Candida melibiosica, in the increasing resistance of Aedes albopictus to a pyrethroid insecticide, deltamethrin.

Molecular detection of Wolbachia in mosquitoes

Wolbachia was not detected in the emerged adult mosquitoes despite reports of vertical and maternal transmission of Wolbachia through eggs [5] in insects and some species of mosquitoes. Data on the occurrence of Wolbachia in mosquitoes in the southwestern part of Nigeria are rare, but only a single report among wild-caught Culex mosquitoes in Ede, Osun State, Nigeria, was available [36] as at the time of this study. Recently, two studies reported the detection of Wolbachia in spray-caught Culex sp. collected in Okinni, Osogbo, Nigeria [37], and Aedes albopictus collected in Ado/Ota Local Government Area of Ogun State [38]. In Botswana, Hungwe et al. (2025) [39] reported rare detection of Wolbachia in Aedes aegypti and Anopheles gambiae. The variation and limited information on the occurrence of Wolbachia underscores the need to understand the occurrence of Wolbachia among mosquito species in Nigeria. Additionally, strain variability and confinement of mosquitoes in the laboratory could be responsible for the inability to detect Wolbachia.

Conclusion

The results of the present study revealed that the mosquitoes harboured several microbes that could influence the biological activities of the insects, including metabolic activities, reproduction, survival, and insecticide resistance.

Future perspective

Further studies on the functional characterization of the identified microbiota are required to understand their specific roles in mosquito physiology and vector competence.

Limitations

Wolbachia is not culturable, and the inability to obtain a positive control for the PCR detection is a limitation recorded in this study. Nevertheless, the absence of Wolbachia in mosquitoes in this study was validated with in silico PCR, which confirmed the specificity of the primers used for the PCR detection (Supplementary Fig. S3).
