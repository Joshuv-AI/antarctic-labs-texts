ReaLM : Reflection-Enhanced Autonomous Reasoning with Small Language Models
Yuanfeng Xu, Zehui Dai, Jian Liang, Jiapeng Guan, Guangrun Wang, Liang Lin, Xiaohui Lv

Abstract
Small Language Models (SLMs) are a cost-effective alternative to Large Language Models (LLMs), but often struggle with complex reasoning due to their limited capacity and a tendency to produce mistakes or inconsistent answers during multi-step reasoning. Existing efforts have improved SLM performance, but typically at the cost of one or more of three key aspects: (1) reasoning capability , due to biased supervision that filters out negative 1 1 1 In this work, we refer to reasoning paths that lead to the correct answer as positives, and those that do not as negatives. reasoning paths and limits learning from errors; (2) autonomy , due to over-reliance on externally generated reasoning signals; and (3) generalization , which suffers when models overfit to teacher-specific patterns. In this paper, we introduce ReaLM , a reinforcement learning framework for robust and self-sufficient reasoning in vertical domains. To enhance reasoning capability, we propose Multi-Route Process Verification ( MRPV ), which contrasts both positive and negative reasoning paths to extract decisive patterns. To reduce reliance on external guidance and improve autonomy, we introduce Enabling Autonomy via Asymptotic Induction ( EAAI ), a training strategy that gradually fades external signals. To improve generalization, we apply guided chain-of-thought distillation to encode domain-specific rules and expert knowledge into SLM parameters, making them part of what the model has learned. Extensive experiments on both vertical and general reasoning tasks demonstrate that ReaLM significantly improves SLM performance across aspects (1)–(3) above.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Jaech et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
have demonstrated strong performance in complex reasoning tasks across a wide range of domains
(
Suzgun et al. 2022
;
Dave et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
)
. However, their substantial training and inference costs limit their practicality in real-world deployments. This has motivated growing interest in empowering Small Language Models (SLMs, typically with
\leq
7B parameters)
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
;
Liao et al. 2024
)
with comparable reasoning capabilities. SLMs are significantly more efficient and easier to deploy, especially in vertical-domain applications
2
2

2

Vertical domains are specialized areas within an industry or sector, defined by domain-specific knowledge and requirements.

. However, due to their limited capacity, SLMs often produce errors or inconsistencies in multi-step reasoning.

While recent approaches have materially boosted SLM reasoning, the improvements rarely come for free. That is, they are typically secured by compromising at least one of three pillars vital for real-world deployment:

•

Reasoning capability:
Many methods rely on biased supervision that filters out negative reasoning paths, as seen in RFT
(
Yuan et al. 2023
)
, which uses reject sampling to retain only high-confidence CoTs. This limits the model’s ability to learn from errors, resulting in brittle reasoning behavior and reduced robustness.

•

Autonomy:
Some approaches depend on externally generated reasoning steps (CoTs
3
3

3

External CoTs are intermediate reasoning steps (without final answers) from external LLM, used as input features to guide the SLM-distinguish from CoTs generated internally by the SLM itself.
)
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
to guide the SLM during inference. These CoTs, generated by large teacher models, are used as input features, introducing strong auxiliary dependence and preventing the SLM from reasoning independently — especially in standalone or real-time applications.

•

Generalization:
Methods like LLMs-as-Instructor
(
Ying et al. 2024
)
, which generate hard samples from SLM errors, often restrict learning to the reasoning patterns of teacher models. This teacher-specific bias causes poor generalization to domains where the teacher underperforms or where domain-specific rules differ from those seen during pretraining.

These key limitations — lack of robust reasoning capability, limited autonomy, and poor generalization — are summarized in Fig.
1
. Addressing all three is essential for building practical, self-sufficient reasoning systems using SLMs.

To address these challenges, we propose
ReaLM
, a reinforcement learning framework for robust and self-sufficient reasoning in domain-specific settings. ReaLM tackles all three issues through three complementary components:

(1) Multi-Route Process Verification (MRPV)
improves reasoning capability by encouraging models to reflect on both positive and negative reasoning paths. Unlike prior work that aggregates CoTs indiscriminately
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, MRPV introduces structured supervision that contrasts multiple reasoning trajectories, helping the model identify key decision factors and avoid systematic failure modes.

(2) Enabling Autonomy via Asymptotic Induction (EAAI)
promotes autonomy by gradually reducing reliance on external guidance during training. Inspired by curriculum learning
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Liao et al. 2024
)
, EAAI enables the model to learn to reason independently in an end-to-end manner, without the need for external CoTs at inference time.

(3) Guided CoT Distillation with Benchmarking
enhances generalization. We introduce an industrial benchmark,
Search Ad Relevance Prediction
, which requires fine-grained reasoning under strict domain constraints. Guided CoT distillation enables the model to encode domain-specific rules and expert knowledge into its parameters, making this knowledge part of what the model has learned.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

•

We propose
ReaLM
, a reinforcement learning framework that improves SLM reasoning while addressing the three core limitations: capability, autonomy, and generalization.

•

We introduce
MRPV
, a supervision mechanism that contrasts both positive and negative reasoning paths to extract decisive patterns, enabling robust and reflective reasoning.

•

We design
EAAI
, a progressive training strategy that reduces reliance on external signals, allowing the model to reason independently.

•

We construct a new industrial benchmark and apply guided CoT distillation to help SLMs acquire domain-specific knowledge without requiring explicit rule inputs.

2.1
Multi-Route Process Verification

To enhance reasoning capability, we introduce MRPV, which explicitly contrasts positive and negative reasoning paths and rewards effective reflection. It comprises multi-CoT input construction and a two-stage reward mechanism.

Multi-CoT Input Construction.

As illustrated in
Fig.
2
(a).
1
, for a given question, an LLM is queried
N
times using temperature sampling to produce a set of reasoning paths
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. These CoTs are not filtered, thus incorporating both correct and incorrect reasoning trajectories. Despite leading to wrong answers, incorrect CoTs reflect alternative perspectives that enrich the inference.

After that, a subset of
k\leq N
CoTs is sampled in Fig.
2
(a).
2
and then included as input to the SLM in Fig.
2
(a).
3
. The SLM then produces its own chain-of-thought
C_{\text{out}}
and final answer
pred
, while also assigning a utility score to each
C_{i}
. The outputs (
pred
and utility scores) are used to guide model optimization through the two-stage reward function described below.

Two-Stage Reward Design.

To reinforce both answer correctness and reasoning quality, we introduce a two-stage reward mechanism that encourages explicit reflection:

•

Stage 1 (Answer Supervision):
Rewards the model based on correctness and format of the final answer.

•

Stage 2 (Process Supervision):
Assesses the utility of each external CoT by comparing its conclusion to ground truth, guiding ReaLM to reflect on valuable paths.

Formally, the reward is defined as:

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r_{\text{base}}+r_{\text{gain}},

(1)

\displaystyle r_{\text{base}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }pred\text{ and format are both correct}\\
0.1,&\text{if format is correct but answer is wrong}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle r_{\text{gain}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}scale_{\text{gain}}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i},&\text{if }r_{\text{base}}=1\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(3)

Here,
s_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denotes whether the SLM’s assessment of
C_{i}
matches the conclusion drawn within
C_{i}
. Stage 2 activates only if Stage 1 succeeds, ensuring training stability.

Unlike simply concatenating multiple CoTs — which tends to encourage the model to summarize shared reasoning patterns — we explicitly contrast the differences among CoTs through a two-stage reward mechanism, in order to highlight critical decision factors.

Comparison with GRPO.

While both MRPV and GRPO leverage multiple reasoning trajectories, they differ fundamentally in design. GRPO treats each query-CoT pair as a separate data point and aggregates rewards across a batch of such examples. MRPV, in contrast, integrates all CoTs directly into a single input context and trains the model to reason jointly over these references. This enables token-level interactions and contrastive analysis between reasoning paths, facilitating more reflective and robust inference.

Theoretical Motivation.

We formally analyze the benefit of leveraging diverse CoT references to enhance reasoning performance. Specifically, we demonstrate that incorporating external CoTs can lead to a higher expected reward than relying solely on reasoning generated without such references. The result is stated as follows:

Proposition 2.1.1
(Reward Bound)

Let
C_{\text{SLM}}
denote the reasoning path generated without access to external CoTs. Under mild assumptions (see Appendix C), the expected reward of the output
C_{\text{out}}
, generated with reference to a set of CoTs
{C_{i}}
, satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[R(C_{\text{out}})]\geq\mathbb{E}[R(C_{\text{SLM}})].

(4)

A proof sketch of Prop.
2.1.1
is provided in Appendix C.

Beyond bounding the performance of
C_{\text{out}}
, we also characterize its reward by the underlying contributions of the referenced CoTs. In particular, the following result establishes that the reward of
C_{\text{out}}
can be represented as a learnable weighted combination of the individual CoT rewards.

Proposition 2.1.2
(Reward Decomposition)

Under the same assumptions as Proposition
2.1.1
, there exists a set of learnable weights
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, where

\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}=1
, such that the expected reward of the output
C_{\text{out}}
can be expressed as:

\mathbb{E}[R(C_{\text{out}})]=\mathbb{E}\left[R(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\cdot C_{i})\right]+\Delta_{\text{gen}},

(5)

where
\Delta_{\text{gen}}\geq 0
denotes additional gains from novel or refined generation by the SLM beyond direct imitation of the references.

This decomposition highlights the importance of contrasting all reference CoTs, including both high-quality (positive) and low-quality (negative) paths. Learning appropriate weights over
\{C_{i}\}
enables the model to selectively amplify informative reasoning while suppressing misleading cues. This justifies the necessity of our MRPV. A proof sketch of Prop.
2.1.2
is also included in Appendix C.

2.3
Improving Generalization via Guided CoT Distillation and Benchmarking

To help the model generalize beyond teacher-specific reasoning and adapt to real-world domains, we distill domain-specific rules into the SLM using a proprietary dataset.

Industrial Benchmark: Ad Search Relevance Prediction.

We introduce a high-quality vertical dataset from a commercial search system. Each data point is a tuple
\langle query,ad\_info,label,comment\rangle
, where the
comment
encodes expert rationale and proprietary rules not in public corpora. The
label
is a ternary relevance score (irrelevant, partially relevant, relevant). Following complex proprietary rules, it represents distributions beyond standard LLM pretraining, serving as a challenging real-world benchmark. See Appendix D for available examples.

Rule-Aware CoT Distillation.

To help the model encode domain-specific rules and knowledge in its parameters, in Fig.
2
(a).
1
, we generate rule-aware CoTs using a general teacher LLM by injecting expert comments into prompts with a certain probability. These rule-enhanced CoTs are then sampled for the following training.

Baselines.

To rigorously evaluate ReaLM on the above reasoning tasks, we benchmark it against a diverse suite of SOTA baselines spanning multiple domains, including methods that operate both with/without external CoTs, and trained under SFT as well as RL paradigms, as detailed below:

•

Supervised-Finetuning (SFT) on the final ground truth.

•

Multitask-CoT-Distillation (MT-CoT)
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, using multi-task learning to distill reasoning ability.

•

SKIntern
(
Liao et al. 2024
)
, empowering SLMs to internalize symbolic knowledge gradually.

•

Universal Self-Consistency(USC)
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023b
)
, sampling a diverse set of reasoning paths, and then selects the most consistent answer.

•

CoT-Synthesizer
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, leveraging CoT reasoning to synthesize answers by analyzing complementary information from multiple candidate responses.

•

RL with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
to equip reasoning capability.

Implementation Details.

We queried GPT-4-turbo with a temperature of 1.5 to generate diverse CoTs and predictions. Examples are in Appendix D. For fair comparison, student SLMs are initialized with two pretrained models, DeepSeek-R1-7B
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
and Qwen2.5-7B
(
Hui et al. 2024
)
, to reduce bias from single initialization. All methods share identical experimental settings, including external CoTs and initialization weights when applicable.

For supervised-finetuning (SFT) approaches, we use full-parameter tuning with DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 2
(
Rajbhandari et al. 2020
)
. For RL approaches, we employ GRPO with rule-based reward for CoT judgements and predictions. All experiments were conducted on 8 A100 GPUs with 80GB. More detailed information about training, inference, and hyperparameters are in Appendix B.

3.2
Main Results

EAAI enables ReaLM to perform inference without relying on external CoTs. Without EAAI, ReaLM still requires external CoT inputs. Tab.
1
reports the performance of advanced models and ReaLM, grouped by whether external CoTs are provided during inference.

Under the setting where external CoTs are used (white rows), ReaLM without EAAI outperforms CoT-Synthesizer and other CoT-input-based methods, achieving accuracy gains of 4.2% and 4.1% on Deepseek-R1-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, respectively. These results set a new state-of-the-art among CoT-input-based approaches, demonstrating the superior synthesis capability of MRPV.

Under the setting where no external CoTs are provided (gray rows), the full ReaLM still achieves strong performance, with average accuracy improvements of 2.6% and 2.5% on Deepseek-R1-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, respectively, further validating the effectiveness of our proposed method.

3.3
Analysis

Ablation Studies.

To investigate the impact of process verification, that is,
the additional reward
r_{gain}
for external CoT judgment in Eq.
3
, we perform
ablation studies by removing process verification.

Tab.
2
presents four variants in the ablation study, each applying different subsets of our proposed components during training. The full ReaLM, includes both MRPV and EAAI. Removing process verification from MRPV while retaining multi-CoTs during training yields the MR variant. Notably, variants equipped with the EAAI module during training are capable of performing inference without external CoT.

In Tab.
3
, the ablation results are grouped based on whether the EAAI module is used, that is, whether external CoTs are required during inference. Within each group, we examine the performance impact of removing process verification. It shows that process verification consistently improves performance by approximately 1% on both ID and OOD datasets, confirming its effectiveness and that enabling ReaLM to assess external CoTs’ utility boosts reasoning accuracy.

In addition, the variant of MR+EAAI in Tab.
3
significantly outperforms the RL method, indicating that, beyond process verification, exposing the model to multiple reasoning paths during training further enhances SLM’s performance. This supports Proposition
2.1.1
and further confirms that external CoTs contribute to performance gains.

Moreover, in Tab.
1
, ReaLM (gray row) shows a performance drop compared to ReaLM without EAAI (white row). This suggests that while EAAI enables adaptation to inference without external CoTs and retains part of the performance gain, there is still room for improvement.

Learning from Failure Rational.

We further investigate the root cause of this phenomenon by comparing the prediction errors made by the SLM with those of the LLM. As shown in Tab.

A high SAME Err reflects that error reasoning patterns are shared between the SLM and LLM. While this is not a perfect indicator of reasoning similarity, we adopt it as a practical proxy under the assumption that semantically or structurally similar reasoning paths are more likely to lead to identical mistakes.

As shown in Tab.
4
, MRPV-Reject variants exhibit a higher SAME Err ratio, likely due to the rejection sampling mechanism discarding incorrect training examples, thereby limiting opportunities to learn from failure cases. In contrast, MRPV achieves a significantly lower SAME Err rate than MR, indicating its ability to learn from incorrect CoTs to avoid repeating similar mistakes.

Moreover, DIFF Err remains nearly unchanged across models with and without EAAI, which aligns with our analysis: the performance gains of MRPV primarily come from reducing SAME Err through effective learning from LLM failures.

Two-stage Reward Mechanism

To assess the effectiveness of the two-stage reward mechanism, we remove the gating mechanism in Eq.
3
, such that the auxiliary reward
r_{gain}
is applied regardless of whether the final prediction is correct. This modification leads to a performance drop of ReaLM from 94.4 to 93.8. We hypothesize that in cases where the model makes incorrect predictions, it may optimize the overall reward by focusing on
r_{gain}
— the reward for the auxiliary task — instead of improving main task performance. In contrast, with the two-stage reward mechanism, reasoning is only enhanced when the main task prediction is correct, thus avoiding misleading updates from erroneous samples.

To assess generalization to unseen domains with proprietary rules, we benchmark ReaLM variants and advanced methods on our industrial dataset. As shown in Tab.

We further examine the impact of incorporating proprietary rules. Introducing expert comments boosts performance notably, indicating their utility in facilitating rule acquisition.

Finally, our iterative training pipeline produces ReaLM-R1, which surpasses ReaLM-Zero and achieves the highest overall accuracy, highlighting the benefits of cold-start distillation followed by reinforcement learning.

4
Justifications

This paper investigates the potential of comparative reasoning with SLMs. While prior studies have explored how to enhance the reasoning capabilities of small language models, they often involve trade-offs among reasoning capacity, autonomy, and generalization. We propose a unified framework that systematically improves all three aspects.

Reasoning Capacity.
We show that prompting SLMs to contrast correct and incorrect reasoning paths leads to more accurate and robust inferences. While earlier work (e.g., CoT-Synthesizer) observed that flawed CoTs can occasionally yield correct answers, we go further by offering both theoretical and empirical insights. Sec.
2.1
demonstrates that exposing models to diverse CoTs and training them to assess utility encourages deeper reasoning. Tab.
4
confirms that performance gains come from correcting flawed reasoning patterns, rather than memorizing the answer.

Autonomy.
To reduce reliance on external supervision, we propose EAAI — a module that performs a CoT-level decay. Inspired by curriculum learning used in knowledge distillation, EAAI gradually reduces reliance on external signals during training, promoting independent reasoning.

Generalization.
We inject expert knowledge during the LLM querying stage in Fig.
2
(a).
1
to incorporate domain-specific knowledge, helping SLMs generalize to unseen tasks.

While ReaLM demonstrates strong performance, there remains room for further refinement. To encourage diverse reasoning trajectories, we adopt random sampling for external CoTs. Although this approach may introduce redundancy, it avoids the domain-specific biases raised from manually crafted selection heuristics — especially in vertical domains where such biases may hinder reasoning ability. Finally, we choose the random strategy to preserve reasoning flexibility, accepting a degree of redundancy as a trade-off.

5
Related Works

Reasoning with SLMs.

SLMs offer a cost-effective and flexible alternative to LLMs, prompting efforts to transfer reasoning abilities from LLMs to SLMs. For example,
(
Ying et al. 2024
)
generates challenging examples that expose SLM weaknesses, enabling targeted training. Other methods
(
Zhou et al. 2021
)
further align SLMs with LLMs by supervising them with LLM-generated labels at both output and representation levels. However, these methods often depend heavily on teacher models’ performance on specific tasks, limiting their generalization
(
Ham et al. 2024
)
.

To overcome this, approaches
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
treat CoTs from LLM teachers as features, providing them as inputs to SLMs. Fine-tuning on these CoTs allows SLMs to extend the reasoning process themselves, offering greater flexibility and improved generalization to unseen tasks without explicit teacher intervention.

CoT Distillation.

CoT significantly enhances LLM reasoning by breaking down complex problems into intermediate steps
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023
)
. Recent studies
(
Chenglin et al. 2024
)
show that CoTs can be used to distill teacher models’ reasoning abilities to smaller models, typically by using LLM-generated CoTs to fine-tune student models
(
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. Compared to traditional distillation methods that use soft labels, CoT distillation offers better performance and greater interpretability
(
Han et al. 2023
)
.

However, SLMs’ limited capacity often leads to suboptimal performance
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
when directly applying this method to SLMs. To address this, some studies refine LLM-generated CoTs to improve SLMs’ performance on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks. For example,
(
Yuan et al. 2023
)
applies rejection sampling to select high-confidence CoTs, aligning SLM and LLM outputs. Other methods, such as
(
Lightman et al. 2023
)
, emphasize the correctness of intermediate steps to improve final performance. However, these methods primarily focus on learning from successful reasoning paths and often overlook failure cases.

In contrast, our method leverages both successful and failed reasoning trajectories, enabling the model to compare and learn from both. This broader exploration improves SLMs’ reasoning robustness and generalization.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose ReaLM, an RL framework that enables SLMs with autonomous, robust reasoning capabilities. By learning from both successful and failed reasoning paths, fading external signal, and distilling domain knowledge, ReaLM improves reasoning ability, autonomy, and generalization – addressing the trade-offs that constrain prior approaches.
The effectiveness of its two core modules, MRPV and EAAI, is substantiated through theoretical analysis and targeted ablation studies.
Across a wide spectrum of vertical and general reasoning tasks, ReaLM delivers consistent gains, improving performance by 4.2% with external CoTs and 2.6% without them.

Appendix C
Proof Sketch

Objective.
Given a set of diverse CoTs
\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{k}\}
sampled stochastically, we aim to show that a well-trained SLM with access to these references generates an output
C_{out}
with higher expected reward than the CoT
C_{SLM}
produced without such references.

Formally, let
R(C)
denote the discrete reward of a CoT
C
. We seek to prove:

R(C_{\text{out}})\geq\mathbb{E}[R(C_{SLM})].

(7)

For simplicity, we omit the expectation over
R(C_{out})
, while it still denotes the average reasoning quality.

Assumptions.
To facilitate the derivation, we adopt the following assumptions:

1)

Let
e=\phi(C)
be the semantic embedding of CoT
C
. Then,
C_{out},C_{SLM},C_{i}
correspond to
e_{out},e_{SLM},e_{i}
. There also exists a continuous reward function:

\widetilde{R}(e)=\mathbb{E}_{C\sim p(C|e)}[R(C)],

(8)

such that optimizing
\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}[R(C)]
via policy gradient is equivalent to
\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{R}(\phi(C))]
.

2)

The reasoning ability of LLM we selected is not weaker than that of SLM when no external CoTs are provided..

3)

A well-trained SLM can captures extra gains in performance beyond direct combination of inputs.

4)

The sampled embeddings
e_{i}
are scattered around the optimal
e^{*}
in the latent space.

Derivation.

According to Eq. (
8
), we restate the objective in Eq. (
7
) with
\widetilde{R}(\cdot)
:

\widetilde{R}(e_{out})\geq\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{R}(e_{SLM})].

(9)

By Assumption. 2, the CoT
e_{i}
sampled from the teacher LLM yields at least as high a reward as
e_{SLM}
from the SLM without external references, that is:

\widetilde{R}(e_{i})\geq\widetilde{R}(e_{SLM}).

(10)

By substituting Eq. (
10
) into Eq. (
9
), the problem reduces to proving a stricter inequality by following Eq. (
11
), which, if satisfied, directly implies the original objective:

\widetilde{R}(e_{out})\geq\mathbb{E}_{C\sim p(C|e)}[\widetilde{R}(e_{i})]=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\widetilde{R}(e_{i})}.

(11)

Following Assumption. 3, the SLM may either directly adopt or reject inputs from
{e_{i}}
, and can further improve through refinement or novel reasoning. Formally,

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(e_{out})=\widetilde{R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}e_{i}\right)+\triangle_{gen}\geq\widetilde{R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}e_{i}\right),

(12)

where
\triangle_{gen}\geq 0
represents additional gain from novel or refined generation, and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}=1
denotes the SLM’s weighting over the
\{C_{i}\}
,reflecting its selective adoption or rejection of each
C_{i}
.

Substituting Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
11
), the remaining proof reduces to:

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}e_{i}\right)\geq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\widetilde{R}(e_{i})}.

(13)

Following
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, we prove Eq. (
13
) in the following steps, which in turn completes the argument.

Specifically, we perform a second-order Taylor expansion of the reward function
\widetilde{R}(\cdot)
at the optimal embedding
e^{*}
, where
\nabla\widetilde{R}(e^{*})=0
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(e)

\displaystyle\approx\widetilde{R}(e^{*})+{(e-e^{*})}^{T}\nabla\widetilde{R}(e^{*})+\frac{1}{2}{(e-e^{*})}^{T}H(e-e^{*})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{R}(e^{*})+\frac{1}{2}{(e-e^{*})}^{T}H(e-e^{*}),

(14)

where
H
denotes the Hessian matrix of the reward surface at
e^{*}
, capturing its local curvature.

Let
\delta_{i}=e_{i}-e^{*}
, then applying the second-order expansion in Eq. (
14
) to Eq. (
13
), the inequality becomes:

\displaystyle{(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\delta_{i})^{T}}H(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}\delta_{i})\geq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{{\delta_{i}}^{T}H\delta_{i}}.

(15)

Expanding the left-hand side by diagonal and off-diagonal terms:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}^{2}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}w_{i}w_{j}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{j}.

(16)

Assuming uniform weights
w_{i}=\frac{1}{k}

w_{i}\neq\frac{1}{k}
, becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}(\sum_{i+1}^{k}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{j})\geq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{{\delta_{i}}^{T}H\delta_{i}}.

(17)

Rearranging, we aim to prove:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j\neq i}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{j}\geq(k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{i}.

(18)

Unlike loss functions minimized at local minima, the reward function achieves a maximum at
e^{*}
. Therefore, its Hessian
H
is negative definite, implying
\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{i}\leq 0
.

Given our prior assumption that the CoTs
\{C_{i}\}
are complementary, the deviation vectors
\delta_{i}
and
\delta_{j}
exhibit mild negative correlation—i.e., their directions point oppositely in the reward landscape. As a result, for a negative definite
H
, their cross terms
\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{j}\geq 0
. Hence:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{i\neq j}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{j}\geq 0\geq(k-1)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{i}^{T}H\delta_{i}.

(19)

This confirms that Eq. (
7
) holds under uniform weighting, with equality achieved only when
\widetilde{R}(e_{1})=...=\widetilde{R}(e_{k})=\widetilde{R}(e^{*})
.

For the more general case with non-uniform weights, a well-trained model is expected to assign higher weights to CoTs with smaller deviations
\delta_{i}
(i.e., closer to optimal), and lower—even negative—weights to larger deviations. This selective weighting further increases the left-hand side of Eq. (
19
), making the inequality easier to satisfy. Therefore, Eq. (
7
) is proven, that is, Proposition
2.1.1
is proven.

In addition, Proposition
2.1.2
folows directly from Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
8
).
