Cesarean section and socioeconomic status are associated with Bifidobacterium abundance and intestinal physiology: observations from a cross-sectional study in formula-fed infants
Authors: Regina Flores-Lopez, Carlijn M Maasakkers, Tim T Lambers, Arjen Nauta, Sofía Morán-Ramos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDevelopment of the gut microbiota in the first months of life is influenced by multiple environmental factors such as type of birth and diet which may have prolonged impact on growth and health. However, in developing countries such as Mexico, with high rates of cesarean deliveries, knowledge about gut microbiota development in early life stages are still limited.ResultsThrough characterization, by 16S sequencing, of 307 formula-fed infants aged 1–4 months, we identified that abundance of key taxa was highly associated with birth mode and that socioeconomic status (evaluated as monthly family income) showed a modifying effect. Particularly, infants born by C-section and from families with a higher monthly income showed 57% lower Bifidobacterium abundance compared to those born vaginally and from families with a lower income. Additionally, these children exhibited higher fecal pH and lower fecal acetate. Interestingly, infants with a low weight-for-length z-score showed lower abundance of Bifidobacterium compared to those with an adequate z-score and a trend towards a higher fecal pH compared to those with an adequate z-score.ConclusionsThis study supports that in the first months of life, C-section shape key taxa abundance, such as Bifidobacterium and that socioeconomic status show an important modifying effect. These changes are related to markers of intestinal physiology that shape gut maturation in early life and might be a determinant of current and future health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04528-w.Keywords: Infants, Gut microbiota, Intestinal markers, Growth status markers, Bifidobacterium, C-section, Fecal pH, Calprotectin, Acetate

Introduction

Accumulating evidence has shown that the gut microbiota plays an important role in health by impacting multiple processes, such as energy harvest from the diet, growth-hormone signaling, gut integrity, colonization resistance, and immune tolerance [1].

Early life is a critical period as the gut microbiota establishes during this time and may impact future health [2]. In the first months, the gut microbiota undergoes different developmental phases towards a stable and more adult-like community [3]. These first stages, have typically been characterized as highly dynamic due to an initial low stability, limited bacterial richness, and a low diversity ecosystem that is still establishing [4]. In parallel, the gut is relatively more oxygenated in the first months of life, allowing the growth of aerotolerant and facultative anaerobes, such as lactic acid bacteria and members of the Enterobacteriaceae family [5]. Thus, along with oxygen tension and other physicochemical factors, the microbiota composition changes rapidly until reaching a more stable structure and thereby becoming less susceptible to modulation by external factors [6].

Among the various factors that shape gut microbiota development in early life, mode of delivery has been extensively studied and is known to influence the initial microbial colonization process [7, 8]. Particularly caesarean born infants often exhibit a delayed colonization of relevant vertically transferred taxa from multiple maternal body sites, such as Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides. These maternally derived strains exhibit greater ecological fitness and are more likely to successfully colonize and persist over time compared to microbes acquired from other sources. This underscores the multifactorial nature of early-life gut microbiota development, where perinatal factors such as delivery mode, as well as postnatal environmental exposures such as diet, antibiotic use, exposure to furred pets, presence of siblings, socioeconomic status, geographical region, and even industrialization, can influence microbial assembly and maturation and impact infants´ immunological, and metabolic pathways [1–3, 9]. Altogether this generates variations in the gut microbiome; where perturbations in early life may influence infants’ growth and show adversely long-lasting consequences for health [2, 10].

In fact, despite the scarcity of longitudinal studies that incorporate the characterization of the gut microbiota in infants born by C-section, it is hypothesized that a compromised development of the gut microbiota could be a risk factor for the onset of several diseases later in life, including allergies and obesity [11–14].

Numerous studies have investigated the composition and dynamics of the intestinal microbiota in infants and how they change with age [15–17]. However, for Latin American countries such as Mexico, where the C-section rate represents around 45% of deliveries, and is among the highest in the world [18], the study of early life gut microbiota and stool parameters, that can provide non-invasive insights into intestinal physiology and the host–microbiota interplay during early infancy is still limited [19]. Therefore, as a secondary analysis of a recently completed cross-sectional study in formula-fed Mexican infants [20], the aim of this study was to characterize the gut microbiota of these infants, to evaluate its association with environmental, host intrinsic factors, and fecal parameters, as well as to assess its relationship with growth status markers. Focusing exclusively on formula-fed infants enabled a more controlled assessment of these factors, without the added variability introduced by the complexity of human milk composition.

Materials and methods

Study design and cohort characteristics

This is a secondary analysis of a multicenter, cross-sectional, observational study in Mexican infants who were already consuming different commercial infant formulas. Thus, eligibility was based on their existing feeding practices. Specifically, exclusively formula-fed infants were selected if they had been consuming the same formula for at least three consecutive weeks prior to enrollment [20]. The study was registered in the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform of the WHO as NL7805, (https://trialsearch.who.int/). Infants were enrolled between November 2019 and January 2022 by four different clinics in Mexico: Clínica Pediátrica Pigui S.C. and Hospital Infantil Privado Hip Star Médica in Mexico City, Christus Muguerza Hospital Sur in Monterrey and VITALI Clínica de Rehabilitación Física y Pediátrica in Guadalajara. Inclusion criteria were aged 1–4 months old (as it is the most sensitive period for the presence of gastrointestinal symptoms [21], gestational age of 37–42 weeks, birth weight of 2.5–4 kg and infants whose parents had chosen not to breastfeed or were not able to breastfeed for at least 3 weeks prior to the study. Exclusion criteria included birth weight-for-length percentile > 85 or < 5, participating in another survey or trial, congenital or chronic illness such as gastrointestinal tract or metabolic diseases, and known or increased risk of cow’s milk allergy and/or lactose intolerance (i.e. one of the biological parents/caregivers and or siblings diagnosed with cow’s milk allergy, asthma, hay fever, etc.). Additionally, use of probiotic and/or prebiotic supplements (other than those found in the formula itself), antibiotics and/or other medication at the time of screening or two weeks prior to the start of the study, as well as introduction of solid foods or breast milk feeding were considered elimination criteria as described elsewhere [20].

The study duration per subject was eight days, where a first screening visit took place followed by 7 days where parents or caregivers recorded infant´s information (Supplementary Figure S1). During the screening visit, anthropometric measurements were performed, and questionnaires were conducted. Participants completed the questionnaires where socio-demographic factors, socioeconomic status indicators, and medical history were assessed as previously described in detail [20]. Apgar scores were parent-reported; in accordance with Mexican national guidelines (NOM-007-SSA2-2016), these reflect the 5-minute evaluation typically recorded in clinical practice. A last visit (Day 8) was scheduled for fecal sample collection.

For the current analysis, only children having at least one fecal sample available were considered, thus the study included a total of 307 infants (Supplementary Figure S2).

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were determined by the physician using standardized procedures during the screening visit. Furthermore, weight-for-age, length-for-age, weight-for-length, and head circumference-for-age z-scores were calculated using the anthro R package [22] (Version 1.0.1 WHO, 2023), based on World Health Organization Child Growth Standards. Wasting was defined as weight-for-length <−2 SD and overweight as weight-for-length > + 2 SD of the WHO Child Growth Standards median [23].

Fecal sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing of 16S rRNA

Parents or caregivers were instructed to collect fecal samples at home directly after defecation on three separate days within the study period. Sterile spatulas and cups, as well as written instructions were provided, and parents were advised to closely monitor the time of defecation to immediately fill the cups with a minimum quantity of feces while avoiding contact with urine and other liquids. Samples were then stored at their domestic freezer until transport to the clinic. Fecal samples were received by the physician at the last visit (Day 8) and stored at −20 °C for maximally six weeks. The samples were then transported to Instituto Nacional de Medicina Genómica where they were stored at −80 °C until processing. For infants with more than one available sample, the specimen with sufficient volume to allow all planned analyses to be conducted from the same aliquot was selected. DNA was extracted from 70 to 100 mg of feces using the Power Fecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Inc.), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Concentrations of extracted DNA from each sample were determined by spectrophotometry (Nanodrop 2000c) measurement, and an estimate of sample purity was determined by measuring the A260/A280 absorbance ratio.

The V4 hypervariable region was amplified using 515 F and 806R primers as previously described [24]. PCR was performed on 100 ng DNA, and products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP beads according to the manufacturer instructions (Beckman Coulter). Equimolar ratios of amplicons from the individual samples were pooled and sequenced using an Illumina MiSeq 2 × 250 platform. Concentration of each sequencing pool was determined with Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen).

Fecal parameters

Fecal water content, pH, short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) and calprotectin were included as non-invasive markers of intestinal physiology during early infancy. Fecal calprotectin, a neutrophil-derived protein, used as marker of intestinal immune activation and in infants also as marker of gut maturation [25], was determined using the commercial calprotectin ELISA kit (EK- CAL; Bühlmann Laboratories AG), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Final calprotectin concentrations are expressed as micrograms of calprotectin per gram of stool.

Fecal pH, was used as marker of intestinal pH, that has further shown to have a bidirectional relationship with gut microbial composition. For its determination two different strips (5.0–10 MQuant®, Merck-Millipore and 3.8–5.5 Whatman, GE Healthcare) with 0.5 pH unit gradation were used on (1:10) homogenized fecal samples with sterile water. For fecal water content an aliquot of 100–200 mg was lyophilized for 24 h in a Labconco FreeZone freeze dryer (USA) and subsequently weighed. Fecal water % was calculated as (wet − dry)/wet fecal weight * 100 and used as an objective marker of stool consistency, with values > 85% commonly associated with diarrheal stools [26]. SCFAs were quantified in a subsample of infants (n = 150) by Metabolon, Inc. with a targeted (LC-MS/MS) protocol (Metabolon Method TAM135). Briefly fecal samples were spiked with stable labelled internal standards, homogenized and subjected to protein precipitation with an organic solvent. After centrifugation, an aliquot of the supernatant was derivatized. Subsequently the reaction mixture was diluted and an aliquot injected onto an Agilent 1290/AB Sciex QTrap 5500 LC MS/MS system equipped with a C18 reversed phase UHPLC. The mass spectrometer was operated in negative model using Electrospray ionization (ESI). Sample analysis included calibration standards and quality control samples at multiple concentrations. QC performance was evaluated on each 96-well plate, and acceptance criteria were met for all analytes, ensuring accuracy and reproducibility. Metabolite concentrations are reported in micrograms of metabolite per gram of wet stool. The subset of infants for SCFAs determinations was selected based on sample volume availability, while maintaining the demographic distribution of the population: representation across the combined delivery mode and socioeconomic status groups.

Data processing and gut microbiota characterization

Raw sequence data were processed with QIIME 2 (version 2020.6). Data was denoised using DADA2, with default parameters in the QIIME 2 package. Particularly, the paired-end FASTQ files were processed by demultiplexing, correcting noisy reads, chimera removal, singleton removal, removing low quality regions of the sequences, and construction of a feature table consisting of amplicon sequence variants (ASV) [27]. A total of 21,494,697 sequences were obtained, with a median sequencing depth of 70,340 reads. Alpha diversity was evaluated with Observed ASVs as well as Shannon and Chao1 indexes on rarefied data with a subsampling depth of 21,000 reads. For beta diversity analysis, phylogenetic distance matrices such as weighted and unweighted UniFrac were calculated, as well as non-phylogenetic distances Bray Curtis, and Jaccard. Finally, sequences were aligned against the Silva 138 SSURef NR99 database using a pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier, which applies the nomenclature available at the time of its release. Taxonomic characterization was obtained from phyla to genus levels, where ASV present in less than 5% of the individuals were excluded. Core-microbiota was defined as ASVs observed in 90% of all individuals. Clusters were determined based on lowest Laplace approximation after performing the Dirichlet multinomial mixture models (DMMs) analysis [28]. This approach was applied to explore natural clustering patterns in early microbial profiles and to identify taxon enrichment within clusters, as previously described in infant microbiome studies [3].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.0) and SPSS (version 27). Data normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since all variables showed a non-parametric distribution, for continuous variables median and interquartile ranges were reported while for categorical variables percentages were described. For comparison among groups, a chi-squared test was used for categorical data and U Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables. A Dunn’s post hoc comparison was performed, and P-values were corrected by Bonferroni to ascertain significant differences between groups.

For associations between bacterial alpha diversity estimates and continuous variables, partial Spearman correlations were conducted in R through the “ppcor” package [29] and adjusted by relevant covariates. Statistical significance was considered when P ˂ 0.05.

The association of environmental and intrinsic factors with overall infant gut microbiota composition was tested using the “envfit” function with the package vegan in RStudio [30]. Ordination was based on Bray Curtis and Jaccard dissimilarities (beta diversity estimates) and the significance value was determined based on 2000 permutations. In total, 19 factors with known associations to gut microbiome development in infants were included in the envfit analysis. Specifically, environmental factors such as birth mode, number of children living in the household, family monthly income (as marker of socioeconomic status), infant daycare attendance, pet ownership, mother’s intake of probiotic during pregnancy and geographical region were tested. Fecal parameters, such as fecal water content, calprotectin, and fecal pH were also included. Intrinsic variables were sex, age, APGAR, gestational age, as well as anthropometric measurements (weight and length at birth, and infants´ z-scores upon study inclusion).

To further determine taxa associated with each covariate, the package Microbiome Multivariable Associations with Linear Models (MaAsLin2 v.1.5.1) was used. The approach of this multivariate linear modelling system for microbial data is to select potentially high-dimensional covariates to associate with microbial taxa abundances [31]. ASVs present in less than 5% of samples were excluded to minimize the influence of rare, potentially spurious features [32]. All remaining taxa—regardless of whether they were part of the core microbiota (≥ 90% prevalence) or of intermediate prevalence—were included in association analyses. In the association analyses, all covariates tested in the “envfit” package as well as technical variables, such as plate and extraction responsible, were included in the adjustment, regardless of significance by “envfit”. The applied default parameters for MaAsLin2 were maximum percentage of samples NA, minimum percentage relative abundance 0.05%, P < 0.05, PFDR < 0.25, “LOG” transformation, “LM” analysis method, “none” normalization, and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method for P-value correction [31]. For exploratory analyses involving multiple taxa, statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value (PFDR) < 0.25, following the standard threshold recommended by MaAsLin2 for microbiome studies with high-dimensional data. This threshold aims to balance sensitivity and specificity in detecting associations, particularly in exploratory analyses [31]. However, for targeted analyses involving for example Bifidobacterium, a genus of a priori biological interest, non-parametric tests were used to explore relative abundance differences across selected strata. In these hypothesis-driven comparisons unadjusted p-values (P < 0.05) were considered significant.

Monthly family income (as marker of socioeconomic status) was uniformly collected across all study sites and therefore selected as a standardized indicator. A cutoff of 11,600 MXN was applied to categorize participants into “low” and “high income” groups based on national benchmarks established by CONEVAL (National Council for the Evaluation of Social Development Policy), the federal institution in Mexico responsible for evaluating poverty and welfare criteria.

Dynamics of gut microbiota diversity and the fecal parameters across the first months of life

Gut microbial composition in the first months of life has typically been characterized by low species diversity and high instability [3]. Therefore, alpha diversity indexes were first analyzed across age (1–4 months). Age showed a positive correlation with Observed ASVs (rho = 0.23, P < 0.001) and Chao1 estimate (rho = 0.24, P < 0.001). Consistently, bacterial richness was significantly higher in older age groups (3-<4 months, 4-<5 months) (Supplementary Table S1). As indicated in Table 2, alpha diversity indexes showed a positive correlation with fecal parameters, as markers of intestinal physiology, such as calprotectin levels and pH, and fecal pH showed a positive correlation with age (rho = 0.12, P < 0.05). In contrast, fecal water content was negatively associated with alpha diversity indexes.

Gut microbiota composition

Through 16S rRNA gene amplicon-based sequencing of the V4 region, 6 phyla, 41 families, and 79 genera, present in at least 5% of the infants, were identified in the whole dataset. Particularly, Actinobacteriota (median abundance 58.89%, IQR: 18.58–75.40) and Firmicutes (median abundance 30.63%, IQR:16.58–57.72) were the most abundant phyla; while at family level, Bifidobacteriaceae (median abundance 54.16%, IQR: 18.44–71.71), Lachnospiraceae (median abundance 13.76%, IQR:31.18), and Enterobacteriaceae (median abundance 4.27%, IQR:1.75–10.33) were the dominant taxa. Despite a great variability among infants (Supplementary Figure S3), Bifidobacterium was the most abundant genus (median abundance 54.16%, IQR: 18.44–71.71); and along with Streptococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Ruminococcus gnavus group, Blautia, Enterococcus, Erysipelatoclostridium, and an unassigned member of the Enterobacteriaceae family formed the core microbiota (defined as ASVs observed in 90% of all individuals; Fig. 1).

Dynamics of gut microbiota composition across the first months of life

Changes in microbiota compositional patterns in the first months of life have been described as gut developmental stages [33]. Therefore, to explore such changes in this cohort gut microbial profiles were determined in an unsupervised manner. Based on the genus relative abundance, the dataset formed four distinct Clusters (Supplementary Figure S4). Classification of the 307 samples showed that 41.69% of the infants belonged to Cluster 1, which showed the lowest diversity and was enriched mainly in Bifidobacterium (Supplementary Figure S5a). Cluster 2 comprised 29.64% of the infants, that although with lower abundance, was also enriched in Bifidobacterium, as well as Blautia. Additionally, 22.80% of the participants were classified in Cluster 3, whose driver taxa were Escherichia-Shigella, Streptococcus, and Bifidobacterium, although the latter with very low abundance. Finally, 5.86% of the infants were classified in Cluster 4, that also showed low abundance of Bifidobacterium and an enrichment in Blautia, [Ruminococcus] gnavus group, and Enterococcus (Supplementary Figure S4 and S5a).

When stratifying by age group the proportion of subjects from Cluster 2 increased with age while the proportion of those from Cluster 3 decreased, although this was not significant (Supplementary Figure S5b). When stratified by Clusters, infants from Cluster 1 were mostly from families within the low-income category (P < 0.01), showed lower fecal pH levels (P < 0.001), lower richness and diversity (ASVs and Shannon index P < 0.01) and had the highest fecal water content (P < 0.001, Supplementary Table S2).

To further identify characteristic patterns at each taxonomic level across age, individual compositional features of the gut microbiota were assessed. Proteobacteria phylum showed a negative correlation with age (rho = −0.26, P < 0.001, PFDR < 0.001) and at family level Enterobacteriaceae, Staphylococcaceae, and Clostridiaceae were also negatively correlated with age (Supplementary Table S3). At genus level, the relative abundance of core genera such as an unassigned member of the Enterobacteriaceae family (rho = −0.22, P < 0.001, PFDR = 0.01), Staphylococcus (rho = −0.04, P < 0.001, PFDR < 0.001), Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (rho = −0.14, P < 0.001, PFDR = 0.01), and Streptococcus (rho = −0.18, P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.10) showed a negative correlation with age; while Blautia (rho = 0.20, P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.18) showed a positive correlation (Fig. 2). Relative abundance of Bifidobacterium was also positively correlated with age but at a nominal level (rho = 0.16, P = 0.02, PFDR = 0.43).

Factors associated with gut microbiota composition

Subsequently, it was investigated whether intrinsic and environmental factors, as well as fecal parameters, were associated with interindividual distance of microbial composition. Of the 20 covariables assessed and enlisted in Table 1, fecal pH explained the largest part of the variation in both phylogenetic and non-phylogenetic distances; 3.87% for Weighted UniFrac, 6.47% for Bray Curtis (Fig. 3) and 5.74% for Jaccard (Supplementary Figure S6). Age (2.11%), Birth mode (1.29%), number of children in the household (1.99%) and geographical region (2.42%) revealed a significant effect in Weighted UniFrac; while socioeconomic status (evaluated as monthly family income) explained a significant effect of the variance in gut microbial composition according to Bray Curtis (2.66%). However, factors previously associated with the overall gut microbiota composition in infants, such as sex and pets in the household [34], showed no significant effect in the current study.

Since fecal pH explained a relevant part of the variation in the gut microbiota composition, the association between this fecal parameter and bacterial composition was analyzed. Abundance of the core taxa Ruminococcus gnavus group (P < 0.001, PFDR<0.001), Enterococcus (P < 0.001, PFDR<0.001), Blautia (P < 0.001, PFDR< 0.05) and Erysipelatoclostridium (P < 0.001, PFDR < 0.05), showed positive correlations; while a negative correlation with Bifidobacterium abundance (P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.15) was observed (Supplementary Table S4).

Additionally, to gain insight into the influence of other covariates in microbiota composition, taxa differences among covariate groups were analyzed. Of the 20 analyzed factors, C-section and monthly family income, showed significant association with core taxa members, while daycare attendance, mothers’ probiotic intake, pet ownership and geographic region showed significant associations with non-core taxa (Supplementary Figure S7 and S8). Interestingly, infants within the low-income category showed significantly higher abundance of the microbiota core genus Bifidobacterium (P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.22, Fig. 4a) and in a paired analysis these infants also showed lower pH values (P = 0.01, Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table S5).

Birth mode showed significant associations with core genera; the gut microbiota of infants born by C-section showed higher relative abundance of Escherichia-Shigella (P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.18), Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.15), and an unassigned member of the Enterobacteriaceae family (P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.18); while a lower abundance of non-core genera like Dorea (P < 0.01, PFDR = 0.15) and Parabacteroides (P < 0.01, PFDR=0.16, Supplementary Figure S9). Noteworthy, the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium was lower in infants born by C-section, although only at a nominal level (P < 0.05, PFDR=0.42). When stratifying by age group, this effect seemed to decrease in infants within the higher age groups (Fig. 4d).

Remarkably, the effect of C-section was more evident when considering the monthly income category; infants born by C-section and within the higher income category had 57% lower abundance of Bifidobacterium when compared to those within the low-income category and born vaginally (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, in a paired comparison, these infants also showed higher fecal pH (P < 0.05) and in a subgroup with fecal SCFA measurements (Supplementary Table S6), also lower acetate concentrations (P < 0.01).

Gut microbiota composition and its association with growth status markers

Emerging evidence indicates that the infant gut microbiota may influence growth trajectories in the first year of life [35]. Therefore, the relationship of the gut microbiota with anthropometric scores (as growth status markers), such as weight-for-age, length-for-age, and weight-for-length z-scores was evaluated. Despite no significant correlations were observed between z-scores and alpha diversity indexes (Supplementary Table S7), weight-for-length and length-for-age z-scores showed a significant association with overall gut microbial composition (Fig. 3). At the genus level, no significant associations between relative abundance and growth status markers were detected, through linear modeling with MaAsLin2. However, exploratory visualization of Bifidobacterium abundance, using a loess curve suggested potential differences at the extreme endpoints of the weight-for-length z-score axis (Fig. 5a).

Association between relative abundance of Bifidobacterium and weight for length z-score (a) Exploratory visualization of Bifidobacterium abundance across the full sample (b) Difference in Bifidobacterium abundance between growth status/anthropometric categories evaluated by MaAsLin2 coefficient. The loess smoothing curve in panel a) was included for exploratory purposes to visualize potential non-linear patterns not captured by the linear modeling

This observation prompted the stratification of infants by anthropometric categories [23] and the gut microbiota of normal-weight infants was compared with those with overweight or wasting. Although this study only included full-term infants and with an adequate weight at birth, based on their anthropometrics 4.89% of the infants were classified as wasted (n = 15), while 9.12% were classified as overweight (n = 28). In this small subset, no significant differences were observed in C-section rates or monthly income among groups, nor in alpha diversity indexes (Supplementary Table S8). However, as shown in Fig. 5b, after controlling for potential confounders with the MaAsLin2 algorithm, Bifidobacterium abundance was significantly lower in infants with wasting (P < 0.01), while this difference was not significant for overweight infants (P = 0.84). Furthermore, and in consistency with the negative association between pH and Bifidobacterium abundance, infants classified as wasted tended to have higher fecal pH levels (P = 0.091). To explore whether the presence of potential pathobionts could be linked to growth outcomes, we screened for common potential pathobionts in the infant gut [36]. Among these, only Escherichia-Shigella, Listeria, and Campylobacter, that are usually favored by higher pH, met our detection criteria however they were not significantly increased in infants classified as wasted compared with normal-weight infants [37] (Supplementary Table S9).

Discussion

Microbial colonization and development of the gastrointestinal tract after birth is a relevant process, affected by multiple environmental and intrinsic factors, that can ultimately influence infant health with lifelong consequences [38]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the gut microbiota of formula-fed Mexican infants, to evaluate its association with environmental, host intrinsic factors, and fecal parameters, as well as to assess its relationship with growth status markers. This observational study, which in our knowledge, is the largest gut microbial-based study in infants from Latin America, showed that gut microbiota composition and fecal parameters (as markers of intestinal physiology) differ across the first months of life. Further, it confirms that C-section has a large impact on gut microbial composition, mainly concerning Bifidobacterium abundance. Interestingly, in combination with a higher family income (used as a marker of socioeconomic status), the effect of C-section on lower Bifidobacterium abundance was exacerbated, with accompanying alterations in intestinal physiology markers, namely higher fecal pH and lower fecal acetate concentrations. Finally, infants with a low weight-for-length z-score also showed lower Bifidobacterium abundance and a trend towards higher fecal pH.

It has been described that in the gut of a newborn infant, the seeding of microorganisms as well as intestinal environmental conditions have a major influence on microbiota composition and development [39]. Here we observed a higher gut bacterial richness with increasing age that is consistent with other studies and reinforces that throughout the first months of life the gut bacterial community develops by continuously acquiring and accommodating new microbial taxa [3]. This microbiota development is also closely linked to changes in intestinal physiology such as oxygen levels and pH [6]. Consistent with our results, intestinal pH is reported to increase with age, thus favoring distinct conditions in which specific bacteria or bacterial metabolic pathways thrive. Particularly, it has been shown that bacterial richness increases with higher pH conditions, possibly due to the more permissive pH setting in the colonic environment [37]. Reciprocally, specific bacterial consortia may influence pH and other abiotic conditions over time [6], illustrating the bilateral relationship between diversity and gut physiology [40].

Along with gut maturation, bacterial colonization is taking place, driven by characteristic taxa that reveal a strong temporal organization [15]. These changes have been described as gut developmental stages characterized by key taxa [3]. In our study, abundance of Streptococcus and of an unassigned member of the Enterobacteriaceae family showed a negative correlation with age, while Bifidobacterium abundance correlated positively, and this was also observed in the clusters analysis. This is consistent with multiple studies where these taxa or their corresponding bacterial Cluster, are enriched at birth due to a predominantly aerobic environment, that gradually becomes anaerobic, allowing the colonization of strict anaerobes such as Bifidobacterium [5, 33].

Notably, the composition of the gut microbiota during this period is shaped by environmental and intrinsic factors. Our study confirmed C-section as one of the major drivers of key taxa, as it was associated with higher abundance of members from the Enterobacteriaceae family, as well as with lower abundance of Bifidobacterium. This is consistent with multiple studies showing that infants born by C-section have a gut microbiome with increased gut redox potential, resulting from a delayed establishment of strict anaerobes, as colonization of Bifidobacterium and consequently the acidification of the gut environment is withheld [7, 41, 42]. Cesarean delivery has been associated with adverse effects on immune development, allergies, and inflammatory disorders, likely linked to gut microbial composition in early life [13, 43]. Thus, it will be of value to investigate in future studies whether, these differences in gut microbial composition are indeed related to a higher risk of chronic diseases [44].

Interestingly, infants within the higher monthly family income category had lower Bifidobacterium abundance, as well as higher fecal pH. In fact, the largest difference in Bifidobacterium abundance was observed when comparing infants born by C-section and from families with a higher monthly income to those born vaginally and from families with a lower income. Remarkably, this was accompanied by differences intestinal physiology markers such as fecal acetate and pH.

This and other studies have shown that socioeconomic status can be a relevant driver of gut microbiota composition in children and adults [24, 45–48]. Particularly it has been proposed that it captures lifestyle practices, hygiene, sanitation, and environmental exposures, among others. Despite its role in infants has been poorly described [49], our observations align with previous reports suggesting a historical increase in fecal pH over the past century, which has been correlated with a decline in Bifidobacterium abundance, particularly in high-income countries [50].

Bifidobacterium has been described as a key driver in intestinal maturation through the production of various metabolites [51]. Particularly, SCFAs produced by this taxon provide energy for epithelial cells, enhance defense functions in intestinal cells, and lower intestinal pH, thus restricting growth of some pathogens [52]. More specifically, it has been proposed that acetate produced by Bifidobacterium and a few other bacteria contributes to the prevention of gastrointestinal infections and may lower future atopy risk, by enhancing intestinal defense and promoting oral tolerance to antigens [52–55]. Thus, it will be interesting to further evaluate in longitudinal studies whether the observed differences, not only in Bifidobacterium abundance, but also in fecal acetate concentrations and pH, may have important consequences for future infants’ health.

Noteworthy, other studies have also shown a decreased Bifidobacterium abundance in infants with malnutrition [53]. In support of this, Bifidobacterium supplementation promotes weight gain and in parallel lowers fecal pH in infants with low birth weight [56–58]. This is consistent with the lower Bifidobacterium abundance found here, and a trend towards higher fecal pH observed in infants classified as wasted. Here, no differences in C-section rates or family income were identified between the small subset of children classified as wasted compared to those with adequate weight-for-length z-scores. However, it will be interesting to investigate in a larger sample, whether a lower Bifidobacterium abundance related to C-section delivery in combination with a higher socioeconomic status predisposes to a lower concentration of acetate and thus higher fecal pH as a first step for dysbiosis during malnutrition.

Finally, given the high rates of C-section in Mexico, and in the context of formula-fed infants, our results underscore the need to further investigate the possible mechanisms behind the interaction between delivery mode and socioeconomic status. For example, considering that maternal transmission during vaginal delivery might be essential in acquiring Bifidobacterium strains, it will be interesting to assess whether mothers within the high-income category provide a lower Bifidobacterium seeding that could be exacerbated by C-section.

This study has some strengths and limitations. One limitation is the absence of longitudinal data, which would have allowed us to establish whether the observed differences in gut microbial composition and fecal parameters between age groups are indeed dynamic changes throughout time. Second, data on maternal intrapartum antibiotic exposure, mother´s health or gut microbial composition, that can influence infants’ gut microbial composition were not available. For example, women with obesity are at higher risk of cesarean delivery [59]. Thus, future studies are needed to elucidate whether the effect captured by birth mode or socioeconomic status (evaluated as monthly family income) are related to these factors. We recognize that the effect of cesarean delivery on Bifidobacterium abundance has already been documented in other populations, and that species-level differences were not captured due to the limitations of 16S rRNA sequencing, which may limit the novelty of our findings. Thus, for future studies it could be of value for the identification of specific Bifidobacterium, or pathogenic sub-species or strains associated with socioeconomic status or wasting. Additionally, while we evaluated fecal pH and short-chain fatty acids, we did not quantify other relevant microbial metabolites such as indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), which is produced by Bifidobacterium and may contribute to the modulation of intestinal pH and host immune function [60]. Future studies incorporating these metabolites would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the functional implications of microbial shifts. Finally, the study included only formula-fed infants, thus the result may not be applicable to breastfed infants or from other geographic origins. This however allowed us to narrow the effect of diet to identify other environmental factors influencing gut microbial composition. Notwithstanding these limitations, a major strength of this study is the large sample size in a real-life setting, where objective measurements were evaluated. Furthermore, our study included infants from a developing country with one of the highest rates of C-section worldwide [18] and where gut microbial composition has been poorly evaluated.

In conclusion, our results underscore that in the first months of life, C-section shapes key taxa abundance, such as Bifidobacterium and that and socioeconomic status (evaluated as monthly family income) shows a modifying effect. These factors are also related to markers of intestinal physiology that drive gut maturation at early stages and might be determinant of current and future health.
