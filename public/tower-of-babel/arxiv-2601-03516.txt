Linear-Time ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -Approximation Algorithms for Two-Line-Center Problems
Chaeyoon Chung, Anil Maheshwari, Michiel Smid

Abstract
Given a set S S of n n points in the plane, we study the two-line-center problem: finding two lines that minimize the maximum distance from each point in S S to its closest line. We present a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm for the two-line-center problem that runs in O ⁡ ( ( n / ε ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) O((n/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)) time, which improves the previously best O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n + ( n / ε 2 ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + ( 1 / ε 3 ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) O(n\log n+({n}/{\varepsilon^{2}})\log({1}/{\varepsilon})+(1/\varepsilon^{3})\log({1}/{\varepsilon})) -time algorithm. We also consider three variants of this problem, in which the orientations of the two lines are restricted: (1) the orientation of one of the two lines is fixed, (2) the orientations of both lines are fixed, and (3) the two lines are required to be parallel. For each of these three variants, we give the first ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm that runs in linear time. In particular, for the variant where the orientation of one of the two lines is fixed, we also give an improved exact algorithm that runs in O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) time and show that it is optimal.

\sigma_{\omega}
moves downward is
O(n)
. Now we conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 25
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane, sorted by their
y
-coordinates, and a real value
\omega>0
, we can decide in
O(n)
time whether there exists a pair of slabs of width
\omega
whose union covers
S
and one of which is horizontal.

4.3
Optimization algorithm

We first sort the points of
S
so that
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
are ordered in decreasing order of their
y
-coordinates.
Let
W
denote the set of all pairwise differences between the
y
-coordinates of points in
S
.
Since
W
can be represented as a sorted matrix
[
16
]
, we can identify two consecutive values
w_{0},w_{1}\in W
such that
w_{0}<w^{*}\leq w_{1}
in
O(n\log n)
time, by combining our decision algorithm described in Section
4.2
with an efficient selection algorithm for sorted matrices
[
17
]
.

Notation
We use the same notation
S_{a}
,
S_{b}
, and
\{\ell_{i},\theta_{i},a_{i},b_{i}\mid i=1,2\}
as in Section
4.2
.
For any
w>0
, we denote by
\sigma_{w}
a horizontal slab of width
w
.

Assumption

Throughout this section, we assume that there exists a position of
\sigma_{w^{*}}
such that
width(S\setminus\sigma_{w^{*}})\leq w^{*}
(i.e.,
width(S_{a}\cup S_{b})\leq w^{*}
) and
S_{a}
dominates
S_{b}
in that position.
The other case can be handled symmetrically by reflecting
S
across a horizontal line.

We first introduce two lemmas from
[
6
]
.
Observe that the sequence of changes in the sets
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
that occur as
\sigma_{w}
moves downward is the same for any
w_{0}<w<w_{1}
.
Let
X
denote the set of all pairs
(A,B)
such that
A\subseteq S
and
B\subseteq S
appear as
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
, respectively, during the sweep of the plane by
\sigma_{w}
for
w_{0}<w<w_{1}
.

Lemma 26

(Lemma 9 of
[
6
]
)
.

w^{*}=\min\{w_{1},\min_{(S_{a},S_{b})\in X}\{width(S_{a}\cup S_{b})\}\}
.

Lemma
26
implies that by tracking
width(S_{a}\cup S_{b})
as
\sigma_{w}
moves downward (for any
w_{0}<w<w_{1}
), we can determine
w^{*}
by comparing its minimum value with
w_{1}
.
Lemma
27
and Corollary
28
further refine this observation, implying that
w^{*}
can be obtained more directly from the procedure of the decision algorithm.

Lemma 27

(Lemma 11 of
[
6
]
)
.

For
(S_{a},S_{b})\in X
where
S_{a}
dominates
S_{b}
, it holds that
width(S_{a}\cup S_{b})<w_{1}

if and only if
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})<w_{1}
.
Moreover, if
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})<w_{1}
,
then
width(S_{a}\cup S_{b})=width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})
.

By combining Lemma
26
and
27
, we can obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 28
.

w^{*}=\min\{w_{1},\min\{width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})\mid(S_{a},S_{b})\in X,\;S_{a}\textrm{ dominates }S_{b}\}\}
.

By Corollary
28
,
once we find a position of
\sigma_{w}
where
S_{a}
dominates
S_{b}
and
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})
is minimized,
we can compute
w^{*}
by comparing this value with
w_{1}
.
We now show that we can find the position during the procedures in the decision algorithm presented in Section
4.2
.

We first fix
w_{0}<w<w_{1}
.
Recall that in the decision algorithm, we test whether
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})<w
while
\sigma_{w}
moves downward, maintaining the following data structures
(see
Data structures and Main loop
in Section
4.2
).

•

the dynamic data structures for
conv(S_{a})
and
conv(S_{b})
(from Lemma
20
)

•

the tangent lines
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
, with their corresponding points
a_{1}
,
b_{1}
,
a_{2}
, and
b_{2}

•

the two antipodal pairs of
S_{a}
with respect to the orientations
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, respectively

As
\sigma_{w}
moves downward, we will maintain an additional value
w_{\min}
, which represents the minimum value of
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})
observed so far.
Initially, we set
w_{\min}=\infty
.
Observe that
S_{b}
dominates
S_{a}
from the beginning until a certain moment, after which
S_{a}
dominates
S_{b}
until the end.
Until
S_{a}
begins to dominate
S_{b}
(which can be easily detected using Observation
19
), we just follow the decision algorithm without updating
w_{\min}
.
At the moment when
S_{a}
starts to dominate
S_{b}
, we set
w_{\min}=width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})
.
This can be done in
O(n)
time by following the antipodal pairs in the data structure that we maintain for
conv(S_{a})
.

In the following, we show how
w_{\min}
can be updated at each event during the execution of the decision algorithm.
At the end of the procedure, the minimum between
w_{1}
and the final value of
w_{\min}
is equal to
w^{*}
by Corollary
28
.

We note that the authors of
[
6
]
also used the observation from Corollary
28
to perform a sweep of the slab
\sigma_{w}
.
However, their approach requires an additional data structure to compute
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})
at each event, resulting in
O(\log^{3}n)
time per event.

Insertion into
S_{a}

Let
p
be the point inserted into
S_{a}
, and let
S_{a}^{\prime}=S_{a}\cup\{p\}
.
We define
\{\ell_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{i}^{\prime},a_{i}^{\prime},b_{i}^{\prime}\mid i=1,2\}
analogously for
S_{a}^{\prime}
and
S_{b}
.
That is
\ell_{1}^{\prime}
and
\ell_{2}^{\prime}
are the right and the left outer common tangents of
S_{a}^{\prime}
and
S_{b}
, respectively.
Let
w_{\min}^{\prime}
denote the updated value after inserting
p
.
Therefore,
w_{\min}^{\prime}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2}^{\prime}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})\}
.

Suppose that
p
lies on or between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
.
Then both
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
remain unchanged, and consequently so do
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
.
Since every slab
\sigma_{\theta}(S_{a})
for
\theta\in[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
contains
p
, it follows that
width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})=width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})
.
As
w_{\min}\leq width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})
,
w_{\min}^{\prime}=w_{\min}
.

Now assume that
p
lies to the right of
\ell_{1}
(the case where
p
lies to the left of
\ell_{2}
can be handled symmetrically).
In this case,
\ell_{2}^{\prime}=\ell_{2}
, while
\ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{1}
.
Observe that
\theta_{2}^{\prime}=\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{1}^{\prime}<\theta_{1}
.
Now we show the following.

\vartriangleright
Claim 29.

w_{\min}^{\prime}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})\}

Proof.

Let
a=width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})
,

b=width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})
, and

c=width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})
.
By definition,
c=\min\{a,b\}
.
As
w^{\prime}_{\min}=\min\{w_{\min},c\}
,
we have
w^{\prime}_{\min}=\min\{w_{\min},a,b\}
.
Observe that
w_{\min}\leq width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})\leq b
by the definition of
w_{\min}
and
S_{a}\subset S_{a}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, we have
w^{\prime}_{\min}=\min\{w_{\min},a\}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})\}
.
∎

The above claim allows us to consider only the range
[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]
.
Recall that, in
Testing the width condition
step of the decision algorithm for insertions, the antipodal pairs of
S_{a}^{\prime}
corresponding to the orientation range
[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]
are visited in order.
Therefore,
width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a}^{\prime})
can be obtained directly during this procedure.

Deletion from
S_{b}

Let
p
be the point deleted from

, and let
S_{b}^{\prime}=S_{b}\setminus\{p\}
.
We define
\{\ell_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{i}^{\prime},a_{i}^{\prime},b_{i}^{\prime}\mid i=1,2\}
analogously for
S_{a}
and
S_{b}^{\prime}
.
That is
\ell_{1}^{\prime}
and
\ell_{2}^{\prime}
are the right and the left outer common tangents of
S_{a}
and
S_{b}^{\prime}
, respectively.
Let
w_{\min}^{\prime}
denote the updated value after deleting
p
.
Therefore,
w_{\min}^{\prime}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2}^{\prime}]}(S_{a})\}
.

Suppose that
p
lies strictly between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
.
Then both
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
remain unchanged, and consequently so do
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
.
Therefore,
w_{\min}^{\prime}=w_{\min}
by definition.

Now assume that
p
lies on or to the right of
\ell_{1}
(the case where
p
lies on or to the left of
\ell_{2}
can be handled symmetrically).
In this case,
\ell_{2}^{\prime}=\ell_{2}
, while
\ell_{1}^{\prime}\neq\ell_{1}
.
Observe that
\theta_{2}^{\prime}=\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{1}^{\prime}<\theta_{1}

Therefore,
w_{\min}^{\prime}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})\}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a}),width_{[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]}(S_{a})\}

w_{\min}^{\prime}=\min\{w_{\min},width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a})\}

The above observation allows us to consider only the range

.
Recall that, in
Testing the width condition
step of the decision algorithm for deletions, the antipodal pairs of

corresponding to the orientation range

Therefore,
width_{[\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{1}]}(S_{a})
can be obtained directly during this procedure.

Now we can conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 30
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane and an orientation
\theta
, we can find a pair of slabs such that one of them has orientation
\theta
and their union covers
S
while minimizing the maximum of their widths in
O(n\log n)
time.

In the next theorem, we prove that the algorithm in Theorem
30
is optimal.

Theorem 31
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane and an orientation
\theta
, finding a pair of slabs such that one of them has orientation
\theta
and their union covers
S
while minimizing the maximum of their widths requires
\Omega(n\log n)
time in the algebraic computation tree model.

Proof.

Given a set
P
of
n
points in the plane, it is known that computing
width(P)
requires
\Omega(n\log n)
time
[
22
]
.
We give a reduction from the width problem to our problem considering
\theta=0
.
We show that we can construct a set
Q
of
n+2
points in
O(n)
time such that the following holds:
Let
(\sigma_{h}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})
be a pair of slabs whose union covers
Q
, where
\sigma_{h}^{*}
is horizontal, and the maximum of their widths is minimized.
Let
w^{*}(Q)=\max\{w(\sigma_{h}^{*}),w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\}
.
Then
w^{*}(Q)=width(P)
.

We compute the largest difference between the
x
-coordinates of the points in
P
, and denote it by
L_{1}
. Similarly, we compute the largest difference between the
y
-coordinates of the points in
P
, and denote it by
L_{2}
.
Let
L=\max\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
.

Consider a square of side length
L
that covers
P
, and denote it by
R
.
Let
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be two points on a horizontal line such that
d(q_{1},q_{2})=7L
,
d(R,\ell_{q_{1}q_{2}})=L
, and
R
and
\overline{q_{1}q_{2}}
are vertically center-aligned (see Figure
19
).
Let
Q=P\cup\{q_{1},q_{2}\}
.

It is clear that
w^{*}(Q)\leq width(P)
, as we can construct a pair of slabs of width
width(P)
where one covers
P
and the other covers
\{q_{1},q_{2}\}
.
Thus, it remains to show that
w^{*}(Q)\geq width(P)
.
Assume to the contrary that
w^{*}(Q)<width(P)
.
Since
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
lie on a horizontal line, we have two cases:
(1)
q_{1},q_{2}\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
, or
(2)
q_{1},q_{2}\notin\sigma_{h}^{*}
.

Consider the case where
q_{1},q_{2}\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
By the assumption and the definitions, we have
w(\sigma_{h}^{*})\leq w^{*}(Q)<width(P)\leq L
and

L\leq d(p,\ell_{q_{1}q_{2}})
for every
p\in P
.
Since
q_{1},q_{2}\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
and
w(\sigma_{h}^{*})<d(p,\ell_{q_{1}q_{2}})
for every
p\in P
,
it follows that
P\cap\sigma_{h}^{*}=\emptyset
.
Therefore,
\sigma_{t}^{*}
should cover
P
which implies that
w^{*}(Q)\geq w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\geq width(P)
. This contradicts our assumption that
w^{*}(Q)<width(P)
.

Now, consider the case where
q_{1},q_{2}\notin\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
By the assumption and the definitions,
w(\sigma_{h}^{*})\leq w^{*}(Q)<width(P)\leq L_{2}
.
Therefore, there exists a point
r\in P
not contained in
\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
This implies that
q_{1},q_{2},r\in\sigma_{t}^{*}
.
It follows that
w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\geq width(\{q_{1},q_{2},r\})
.
Since
\overline{q_{1}q_{2}}
is the longest side in the triangle
\triangle q_{1}q_{2}r
, we have
width(\{q_{1},q_{2},r\})=d(r,\ell_{q_{1}q_{2}})
.
As
d(r,\ell_{q_{1}q_{2}})\geq L
, it follows that
w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\geq L
.
Consequently,
w^{*}(Q)\geq w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\geq L\geq width(P)
, which contradicts our assumption.
∎

4.4
Approximation algorithm

Recall that in Section
3.3
, we showed that an
\varepsilon
-certificate
Q\subseteq S
of size
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
can be computed in
O(n)
time.
After obtaining
Q
, we find a pair of slabs
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
such that their union covers
Q
,
\sigma_{1}
has orientation
\theta
, and the maximum of their widths is minimized.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
have the same width (otherwise, we expand the smaller one).
Since
Q\subseteq S
, it follows that
w(\sigma_{1})=w(\sigma_{2})\leq w^{*}
.
Such a pair can be found in
O(|Q|\log|Q|)
time by Theorem
30
.

Let
(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\sigma_{2}^{\prime})
be the pair of slabs obtained by taking the
\varepsilon
-expansions of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, respectively.
By the definition of an
\varepsilon
-expansion,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}
has orientation
\theta
and
w(\sigma_{1}^{\prime})=w(\sigma_{2}^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.
Furthermore, by the definition of an
\varepsilon
-certificate,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}\cup\sigma_{2}^{\prime}
covers
S
.
Therefore,
(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\sigma_{2}^{\prime})
forms a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution.

It takes
O(n+|Q|\log|Q|)
time to obtain
(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\sigma_{2}^{\prime})
.
Let
\mu=\min\{n,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
, then we have
|Q|=O(\mu)
.
Therefore, the total running time is
O(n+\mu\log\mu)
, which can be expressed as both
O(n\log(1/\varepsilon))
and
O(n+(1/\varepsilon^{2})\log(1/\varepsilon))
.
We now conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 32
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane and an orientation
\theta
, we can find a pair of slabs such that their union covers
S
, one of them has orientation
\theta
, and the maximum of their widths is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
in
O(n+\mu\log\mu)
time, where
\mu=\min\{n,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
.

5
General two-line-center

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane, we study the problem of finding a pair of slabs whose union covers
S
while minimizing the maximum of their widths.
In this section, we present a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm that runs in
O(n+(\mu/\varepsilon)\log\mu)
time, where
\mu=\min\{n,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
.

Throughout this section, we denote by
\Sigma^{*}=(\sigma_{1}^{*},\sigma_{2}^{*})
an optimal pair of slabs for the general two-line-center problem. Let
w^{*}=\max\{w(\sigma_{1}^{*}),w(\sigma_{2}^{*})\}
.

We begin by proving Lemma
33
, which will be used to compute a candidate set of orientations for one of the slabs in a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution.

Lemma 33
.

Let
p
and
q
be two points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
\widetilde{w}>0
be a real number, let
c\geq 1
be a constant, and let
\varepsilon>0
be a real number.
In
O(1/\varepsilon)
time, we can compute a set
\Gamma
of
O(1/\varepsilon)
orientations, such that the following holds:

For every finite set
P
of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and for every slab
\sigma
, if

(1)

P\subset\sigma
,

(2)

p,q\in\sigma
,

(3)

d(p,q)\geq diam(P)/4
, and

(4)

w(\sigma)\leq\widetilde{w}\leq c\cdot w(\sigma)
,

then there exists an orientation
\gamma\in\Gamma
and a slab
\sigma^{\prime}
, such that

(1)

P\subset\sigma^{\prime}
,

(2)

w(\sigma^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot w(\sigma)
,

(3)

\sigma^{\prime}
has the orientation
\gamma
.

Proof.

We remark that this proof is based on the approach in
[
1
]
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
p
and
q
lie on a horizontal line.
Let
D=d(p,q)
.
Let
\theta
be the orientation with
0<\theta\leq\pi/2
such that
\sin\theta=\min\{1,\widetilde{w}/{D}\}
.
Let
\delta=c_{\delta}\varepsilon
for some constant
0<c_{\delta}<1
.
Let
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{i}=(i-\lceil\frac{1}{\delta}\rceil)\cdot\delta\theta\mid 0\leq i\leq 2\lceil\frac{1}{\delta}\rceil\}
.
Observe that
\Gamma
contains uniformly placed orientations in the range
[-\theta,\theta]
, and
|\Gamma|=O(1/\varepsilon)
. (Here we slightly abuse the notation by redefining orientations to lie in
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
instead of
[0,\pi]
.)
In the following, we prove that
\Gamma
satisfies the conditions in the statement.

Let
P
be a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
\sigma
be a slab, where
P
and
\sigma
satisfy the four conditions in the statement.
Let
\alpha
be the orientation of
\sigma
, and let
w=w(\sigma)
.
For simplicity, we assume that
0\leq\alpha<\pi/2
. The other case can be shown in a symmetric way.
Since
p,q\in\sigma
, we have
\sin\alpha\leq w/D\leq\widetilde{w}/D
(see the right triangle having
\overline{pq}
as one of its sides in Figure
20
).
Observe that if
\widetilde{w}/D<1
, then
\sin\theta=\widetilde{w}/D
. If
\widetilde{w}/D\geq 1
, then
\sin\theta=1
. In both cases, it holds that
\sin\alpha\leq\sin\theta
. Therefore, we have
\alpha\leq\theta
.

Let
\gamma
be the largest orientation in
\Gamma
such that
\gamma\leq\alpha
.
Since
\alpha\leq\theta
, we have
\alpha-\gamma<\delta\theta
.
Let
R
be the minimum bounding box of
P
with orientation
\alpha
. See Figure
20
.
Denote by
o
and
t
the lowest and highest vertices of
R
, respectively.
Now consider a slab
\sigma^{\prime}
of orientation
\gamma
whose two bounding lines pass through
o
and
t
.
Clearly,
P\subset\sigma^{\prime}
.
We next show that
w(\sigma^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)w
, which completes the proof.

Let
b
be a vertex of
R
other than
o
and
t
.
Consider the line
\ell
orthogonal to
\sigma^{\prime}
that passes through
b
.
The line
\ell
intersects the two bounding lines of
\sigma^{\prime}
.
Observe that one intersection point lies inside
\sigma
, while the other lies outside.
Let
a
and
c
denote these two intersection points, with
a
being the one inside
\sigma
.
Then
w(\sigma^{\prime})=\overline{ab}+\overline{bc}\leq w+\overline{bc}
.

We have
\overline{bc}=\overline{oc}\cdot\tan(\alpha-\gamma)\leq\overline{oc}\cdot\tan(\delta\theta)
.
We consider the following two cases.

Case 1.
\widetilde{w}/D\leq 1/2
.
That is,
\theta\leq\pi/6
.
In this case, since
\delta<1
and
\theta\leq\pi/6
, we have

\tan(\delta\theta)\leq\delta\tan\theta
.
Note that
\delta\tan\theta=\delta\sin\theta/\cos\theta

\leq(2/\sqrt{3})\delta\sin\theta\leq(2/\sqrt{3})\delta\widetilde{w}/D\leq(2/\sqrt{3})c\delta w/D
.
Since
\overline{oc}\leq{diam}(P)\leq 4D
, it follows that
\overline{bc}\leq(8/\sqrt{3})c\delta w
.

Case 2.
\widetilde{w}/D>1/2
.

We have

\tan(\delta\theta)=\sin(\delta\theta)/\cos(\delta\theta)\leq 2\cdot\sin(\delta\theta)\leq 2\delta\theta\leq\pi\delta

since
\delta\theta\leq\pi/3
assuming
\delta\leq 2/3
.
Also,
\overline{oc}\leq{diam}(P)\leq 4D\leq 8\widetilde{w}\leq 8cw
.
Combining these bounds gives
\overline{bc}\leq 8\pi\delta cw
.

By choosing
\delta=\min\{2/3,\sqrt{3}\varepsilon/(8c),\varepsilon/(8\pi c)\}
, it holds that
\overline{bc}\leq\varepsilon w
in both cases.
∎

with orientation

.

By Lemma
3
, we can compute a set
F
of at most
11
pairs such that one of them is an anchor pair of
\Sigma^{*}
in
O(n)
time.
For the moment, assume that we have an anchor pair
(p,q)
of
\Sigma^{*}
. (We will repeat the following process for every pair in
F
.)
Without loss of generality, assume that
p,q\in\sigma_{1}^{*}
and
d(p,q)\geq{diam}((\sigma_{1}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{2}^{*})\cap S)/4
.

By Theorem
11
, we can compute
\widetilde{w}
such that
w^{*}\leq\widetilde{w}\leq 10w^{*}
in
O(n)
time.

Consider a slab
{\sigma}_{0}^{*}
that has the same orientation as
\sigma_{1}^{*}
, has width
w^{*}
, and contains
\sigma_{1}^{*}
.
In particular, if
w(\sigma_{1}^{*})=w^{*}
, then
{\sigma}_{0}^{*}
is
\sigma_{1}^{*}
.

Observe that
P=S\cap(\sigma_{1}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{2}^{*})
and
\sigma_{0}^{*}
satisfy the four conditions in the statement of Lemma
33
for
(p,q)
and the value
\widetilde{w}
computed above, with
c=10
.
Therefore, in
O(1/\varepsilon)
time, we can compute a set
\Gamma
of
O(1/\varepsilon)
orientations such that there exists a slab
\sigma^{\prime}
with orientation
\gamma\in\Gamma
that covers
S\cap(\sigma_{1}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{2}^{*})
and satisfies
w(\sigma^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.
This implies that there exists a pair of slabs whose union covers
S
, one of which has an orientation from
\Gamma
, and whose maximum width is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.

For every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we find a pair of slabs whose union covers
S
, one of which has orientation
\gamma
, and whose maximum width is minimized.
By Theorem
30
, this can be done in
O((n/\varepsilon)\log n)
time in total.
We repeat this procedure for every pair in
F
, which adds only a constant factor to the running time.
Observe that each of the pairs of slabs we obtain has the property that its union covers
S
.
Among these pairs, we output the one with the smallest maximum width.
Thus, we conclude the following lemma.

Lemma 34
.

We can find a pair of slabs such that their union covers
S
and the maximum of their widths is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
in
O((n/\varepsilon)\log n)
time.

To further refine the logarithmic term, we compute an
\varepsilon
-certificate
Q
of
S
(see Section
3.3
).
By Theorem
14
, this can be done in
O(n)
time and
|Q|=O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
.
We then compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate pair
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
for the general two-line-center problem on
Q
, which can be done in
O((|Q|/\varepsilon)\log|Q|)
time by Lemma
34
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
have the same width (otherwise, we expand the smaller one).
Let
w=w(\sigma_{1})=w(\sigma_{2})
.
Since
Q\subseteq S
, it is clear that
w\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.

Let
(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\sigma_{2}^{\prime})
be the pair of slabs obtained by taking the
\varepsilon
-expansions of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, respectively.
By definition,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}\cup\sigma_{2}^{\prime}
covers
S
, and

w(\sigma_{1}^{\prime})=w(\sigma_{2}^{\prime})\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{2}w^{*}=(1+O(\varepsilon))w^{*}
.

The total running time is

O(n+(\mu/\varepsilon)\log\mu)
, where
\mu=\min\{n,\,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
,
which can also be expressed as both
O((n/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon))
and
O(n+(1/\varepsilon^{3})\log(1/\varepsilon))
.

Theorem 35
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane, we can find a pair of slabs such that their union covers
S
and the maximum of their widths is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
in
O(n+(\mu/\varepsilon)\log\mu)
time, where
\mu=\min\{n,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
.

6
Two fixed orientations

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane and two orientations,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, we study the problem of finding two slabs, one has orientation
\theta_{1}
and the other has orientation
\theta_{2}
, whose union covers
S
while minimizing the maximum of their widths. In this section, we present a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm that runs in
O(n+1/\varepsilon)
time.

By rotating the coordinate system, we assume without loss of generality that one of the given orientations is horizontal. Thus, the two orientations can be taken as
0
and
\theta
, where
0\leq\theta<\pi
.
Let
(\sigma_{h}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})
be an optimal pair of slabs where
\sigma_{h}^{*}
is horizontal and
\sigma_{t}^{*}
has orientation
\theta
.
Let
w^{*}=\max\{w(\sigma_{h}^{*}),w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\}
.
Recall that
S
denotes the input set of
n
points in the plane.

6.1
A
2
-approximation algorithm

Let
p_{t}
and
p_{b}
denote the highest and lowest points in
S
, respectively.
Let
p_{\ell}
and
p_{r}
be the two extreme points in
S
in the direction orthogonal to
\theta
. See Figure
21
.
Since we assume
w^{*}>0
,
the size of
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}
is at least two.
Then the following holds.

Lemma 36
.

There exists an optimal pair
(\sigma_{h}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})
of slabs such that two distinct points
p
and
q
from
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}
satisfy
p\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
and
q\in\sigma_{t}^{*}
.
(Note that
p
or
q
may belong to
\sigma_{h}^{*}\cap\sigma_{t}^{*}
.)

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary.
Then for every optimal pair
(\sigma_{h}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})
of slabs, either
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{t}^{*}
or
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}\subset\sigma_{t}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
By definition, the points
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}
determine the minimum bounding parallelogram of
S
; two of its sides are horizontal and the other two sides have orientation
\theta
(see Figure
21
).
Let
R
denote the parallelogram.

If
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{t}^{*}
, then
R\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}
, and hence
S\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
In this case, choose any point
p\in\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}
and consider the line
\ell_{p}
of orientation
\theta
passing through
p
.
Then
(\sigma_{h}^{*},\ell_{p})
forms an optimal pair of slabs, with one slab degenerating to a line.
This contradicts our assumption, since any point in
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}\setminus\{p\}
lies in
\sigma_{h}^{*}
while
p\in\ell_{p}
.

Analogously, if
\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}\subset\sigma_{t}^{*}\setminus\sigma_{h}^{*}
, consider the horizontal line
\ell_{p}
passing through
p\in\{p_{b},p_{t},p_{\ell},p_{r}\}
.
Then
(\ell_{p},\sigma_{t}^{*})
forms an optimal pair of slabs, which contradicts our assumption in the same way.
∎

Using Lemma
36
, we first give a
2

Lemma 37
.

We can compute a
2
-approximation of an optimal pair of slabs in
O(n)
time.

Proof.

For the moment,
assume that we are given a pair
(p_{1},p_{2})
of points in
S
such that

p_{1}\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
and
p_{2}\in\sigma_{t}^{*}
.
Let
\ell_{h}
be the horizontal line through
p_{1}
, and let
\ell_{t}
be the line of orientation
\theta
through
p_{2}
.
Then we have
\ell_{h}\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}
and
\ell_{t}\subset\sigma_{t}^{*}
.

Now we determine by which slab each input point should be covered in a greedy way.
We initialize two sets
H
and
T
as empty sets.
For each
p\in S
, we insert
p
into
H
if
d(p,\ell_{h})<d(p,\ell_{t})
.
Otherwise, we insert it into
T
.
After applying this process to every input point, we compute the minimum horizontal slab enclosing
H
, denoted by
\sigma_{h}
, and the minimum slab of orientation
\theta
enclosing
T
, denoted by
\sigma_{t}
(see Figure
22
).
Now we show that
(\sigma_{h},\sigma_{t})
is a
2
-approximate solution.

We first show that
w(\sigma_{h})\leq 2w^{*}
.
Let
q
and
q^{\prime}
be the lowest and highest points in
H
(see Figure
22
).
Then
w(\sigma_{h})\leq d(q,\ell_{h})+d(q^{\prime},\ell_{h})
. (Equality holds if
q
and
q^{\prime}
are on different sides of
\ell_{h}
.)
If
q\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
, then
d(q,\ell_{h})\leq w^{*}
as
\ell_{h}\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
Otherwise,
d(q,\ell_{t})\leq w^{*}
as
q
should be covered by
\sigma_{t}^{*}
.
We also have
d(q,\ell_{h})<d(q,\ell_{t})
as
q\in H
. Therefore it holds that
d(q,\ell_{h})\leq w^{*}
in any case.
Similarly, we can also show that
d(q^{\prime},\ell_{h})\leq w^{*}
.
Therefore,
w(\sigma_{h})\leq d(q,\ell_{h})+d(q^{\prime},\ell_{h})\leq 2w^{*}
. We can similarly show that
w(\sigma_{t})\leq 2w^{*}
by choosing the two extreme points of
T
in the direction orthogonal to
\theta
.
Note that once we have
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, we can find
\sigma_{h}
and
\sigma_{t}
in
O(n)
time by finding the extreme points in
H
and
T
, respectively.

By Lemma
36
,
we have a constant number of candidates for
(p_{1},p_{2})
.
We apply the above procedure to each candidate pair and select the one that minimizes
\max\{w(\sigma_{h}),w(\sigma_{t})\}
.
The resulting pair
(\sigma_{h},\sigma_{t})
is a
2
-approximate solution.
∎

A
(1+\varepsilon)

By Lemma
37
, we can compute
\widetilde{w}
such that
w^{*}\leq\widetilde{w}\leq 2w^{*}
in
O(n)
time.
By Lemma
36
,
we can compute a set
G
of at most six pairs of points such that there exists a pair
(p_{1},p_{2})\in G
where

p_{1}\in\sigma_{h}^{*}
and
p_{2}\in\sigma_{t}^{*}
in
O(n)
time.
For the moment, assume that we have such a pair
(p_{1},p_{2})
of
G
. (We will repeat the following process for every pair in
G
.)

Let
\ell_{h}
be the horizontal line through
p_{1}
, and let
\ell_{t}
be the line of orientation
\theta
through
p_{2}
.
Then we have
\ell_{h}\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}
and
\ell_{t}\subset\sigma_{t}^{*}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
\ell_{h}
is
y=0
.
We also assume that
w(\sigma_{h}^{*})\geq w(\sigma_{t}^{*})
. We can handle the other case by running the same algorithm in the coordinate system rotated by
\theta
.

Let
L=\{\ell_{i}:y=({\varepsilon\widetilde{w}}/{4})\cdot(i-1-\lceil{4}/{\varepsilon}\rceil)\mid 1\leq i\leq 2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1\}
be the set of horizontal lines.
Note that
\ell_{1}
is
y=-({\varepsilon\widetilde{w}}/{4})\cdot\lceil{4}/{\varepsilon}\rceil\leq-\widetilde{w}

and
\ell_{2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1}
is
y=({\varepsilon\widetilde{w}}/{4})\cdot\lceil{4}/{\varepsilon}\rceil\geq\widetilde{w}
.
The distance between two consecutive lines is
\varepsilon\widetilde{w}/4
and
|L|=2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
.

For two indices
1\leq i\leq j\leq 2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
,
let
\sigma(i,j)
be the horizontal slab bounded by
\ell_{i}
and
\ell_{j}
(see Figure
23
).
If
i=j
, then
\sigma(i,j)
is
\ell_{i}
, which covers the points lying on it.

L
(dashed),

, and

\sigma(3,6)
.
Points
a_{6}
and
a^{\prime}_{6}
(resp.,

\ell_{6}
(resp., strictly below
\ell_{3}
).
Note that the minimum slab of orientation
\theta
enclosing the points outside
\sigma(3,6)
is determined by
a_{6}^{\prime}
and
b_{3}
.

Lemma 38
.

There is a horizontal slab
\sigma
such that
both bounding lines are in
L
,

\sigma_{h}^{*}\subseteq\sigma
, and
w(\sigma)\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}
be the indices such that
\sigma(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is the minimal slab that encloses
\sigma_{h}^{*}
among all slabs
\sigma(i,j)
with
1\leq i\leq j\leq 2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
.
Such indices exist because
d(\ell_{1},\ell_{h})\geq\widetilde{w}\geq w^{*}
and
d(\ell_{2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1},\ell_{h})\geq\widetilde{w}\geq w^{*}
, and
\ell_{h}\subset\sigma_{h}^{*}
.
Then
\sigma_{h}^{*}\subseteq\sigma(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
w(\sigma(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}))\leq w(\sigma_{h}^{*})+\varepsilon\widetilde{w}/2
.
Since
w(\sigma_{h}^{*})\leq w^{*}
and
\widetilde{w}\leq 2w^{*}
, it follows that
w(\sigma(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}))\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.
∎

Observe that once we find a slab
\sigma
satisfying
\sigma_{h}^{*}\subseteq\sigma
and

w(\sigma)\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
, the other slab is determined immediately: it is the minimum-width slab

\sigma^{\prime}
of orientation
\theta
that encloses all points not covered by
\sigma
.
Note that
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
forms a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution.

By Lemma
38
, there are at most
|L|^{2}=O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
candidates for such a slab
\sigma
.
However, we preprocess the input points so that we avoid testing all
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
candidates.

Preprocessing

The lines in
L
partition the plane into
O(1/\varepsilon)
regions.
For each point
p\in S
, we determine the region containing
p
, which can be done in constant time using the floor function.
Within each region, we find the two extreme points in the direction orthogonal to
\theta
.
This takes
O(n)
time in total.

For each line
\ell_{i}\in L
, we store the two extreme points in the direction orthogonal to
\theta
for the points that lie strictly above
\ell_{i}
, and denote them by
a_{i}
and
a^{\prime}_{i}
(see Figure
23
).
Similarly, for each line
\ell_{i}\in L
, we store the two extreme points for those lying strictly below
\ell_{i}
, and denote them by
b_{i}
and
b^{\prime}_{i}
.
This can be done in
O(n)
total time by visiting the partitioned regions in order.

Algorithm

For fixed
i
and
j
,
1\leq i\leq j\leq 2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
, note that the smallest slab of orientation
\theta
that encloses the input points strictly outside of
\sigma(i,j)
is the smallest slab of orientation
\theta
that encloses
\{a_{i},a^{\prime}_{i},b_{j},b^{\prime}_{j}\}
(see Figure
23
).
That is,
width_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(i,j))=width_{\theta}(\{a_{i},a^{\prime}_{i},b_{j},b^{\prime}_{j}\})
.
(Recall the definition of
width_{\theta}
in Section
2
.)
We can compute
width_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(i,j))
in constant time once we know
\{a_{i},a^{\prime}_{i},b_{j},b^{\prime}_{j}\}
.

For
1\leq i,j\leq 2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
,
we define
M[i,j]=d(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})
if
i\leq j
and

d(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})\geq w_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(i,j))
.
Let
M[i,j]=-\infty
if
i>j
or
d(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})<w_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(i,j))
.
We first show that
M
is a sorted matrix in the following lemma.
A smallest non-negative element in a sorted matrix can be found in time that is linear in the number of rows plus the number of columns if each element can be computed in constant time.
Observe that each element of
M
can be computed in constant time by the preprocessing step.

Lemma 39
.

M
is a sorted matrix. In particular, each row is a non-decreasing sequence and each column is a non-increasing sequence.

Proof.

Let
t=2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
, which is the number of rows (resp., the number of columns) of
M
.

Fix an index
i
with
1\leq i\leq t
.
For every
j
with
1\leq j<i
, we have
M[i,j]=-\infty
.
Let
k
be the smallest index with
i\leq k\leq t
such that
d(\ell_{i},\ell_{k})\geq w_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(i,k))
.
If no such
k
exists, then
M[i,j]=-\infty
for all
j\geq i
.
If such a
k
exists, then
M[i,j]=-\infty
for all
j
with
i\leq j<k
, and
M[i,j]=d(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})
for all
j
with
k\leq j\leq t
.
In both cases, the
i
-th row of
M
is a non-decreasing sequence.

Fix an index
j
with
1\leq j\leq t
.
For every
i
with
j<i
, we have
M[i,j]=-\infty
.
Let
k^{\prime}
be the largest index with
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq j
such that
d(\ell_{k^{\prime}},\ell_{j})\geq w_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(k^{\prime},j))
.
If no such
k^{\prime}
exists, then
M[i,j]=-\infty
for all
i\leq j
.
If such a
k^{\prime}
exists, then
M[i,j]=-\infty
for all
i
with
k^{\prime}<i\leq j
, and
M[i,j]=d(\ell_{i},\ell_{j})
for all
i
with
1\leq i\leq k^{\prime}
.
In both cases, the
j
-th column of
M
is a non-increasing sequence.
∎

Let
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
be the pair of indices such that
\sigma(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
is the minimal slab that encloses
\sigma_{h}^{*}
among all slabs
\sigma(i,j)
with

1\leq i\leq j\leq 2\lceil 4/\varepsilon\rceil+1
.
By our assumption that
w(\sigma_{h}^{*})\geq w(\sigma_{t}^{*})
, we have

d(\ell_{i^{\prime}},\ell_{j^{\prime}})\geq w(\sigma_{h}^{*})\geq w(\sigma_{t}^{*})\geq w_{\theta}(S\setminus\sigma(i^{\prime},j^{\prime}))
.
By Lemma
38
,

d(\ell_{i^{\prime}},\ell_{j^{\prime}})\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.
Therefore,

w^{*}\leq M[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]=d(\ell_{i^{\prime}},\ell_{j^{\prime}})\leq(1+\varepsilon)w^{*},

which implies that a smallest non-negative element in
M
exists and is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
.

We compute a pair
(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
of indices such that
M[i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime}]
is a smallest non-negative element in
M
. This can be done in
O(1/\varepsilon)
time.
This directly yields a pair of slabs:
\sigma(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
and the smallest slab of orientation
\theta
that encloses
S\setminus\sigma(i^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
.
Observe that these two slabs together form a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate pair of slabs.

Now, we conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 40
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane and two orientations
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
,
we can find a pair of slabs, one with orientation
\theta_{1}
and the other with orientation
\theta_{2}
, such that their union covers
S
and their maximum width is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
in
O(n+1/\varepsilon)
time.

of

while minimizing the maximum of their widths.

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm that runs in

Throughout this section, let
\Sigma^{*}=(\sigma_{1}^{*},\sigma_{2}^{*})

Notation

\sigma_{1}
and a boundary of
\sigma_{2}
.

and a boundary of

.

(Gap-ratio)
.

Given a pair of interior-disjoint parallel slabs
\Sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})

of

is defined as
g(\Sigma)/W(\Sigma)
.
If
W(\Sigma)=0
, the gap-ratio is not defined.

, we prove the corresponding theorems.

Theorem 42

(Small gap-ratio)
.

,

Theorem 43

(Large gap-ratio)
.

Assuming that there exists an optimal pair
\Sigma^{*}=(\sigma_{1}^{*},\sigma_{2}^{*})
for the parallel two-line-center problem whose gap ratio is at least

,
we can compute a pair of parallel slabs such that their union covers

and the maximum of their widths is at most

in
O(n+\mu\log\mu)
time, where

.
If the assumption on the gap-ratio is not satisfied, the algorithm may return NO.

We first prove Theorem

Theorem 44
.

Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane, we can find a pair of parallel slabs such that their union covers
S
and the maximum of their widths is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
in
O(n+\mu/\varepsilon)
time, where
\mu=\min\{n,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
.

Proof.

We first compute an
\varepsilon
-certificate
Q\subseteq S
of
S
.
By Theorem
14
, this takes
O(n)
time, and
|Q|=O(\mu)
where
\mu=\min\{n,1/\varepsilon^{2}\}
.
Let
w^{*}(Q)
denote the maximum width of an optimal solution for the parallel two-line-center problem on
Q
. It is clear that
w^{*}(Q)\leq w^{*}
since
Q\subseteq S
.

We run the algorithms of Theorem
42
and Theorem
43
on
Q
.
Observe that at least one of them returns a pair of parallel slabs whose union covers
Q
and whose maximum width is at most
(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}(Q)
.
Among the (at most) two pairs, let
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
be the one whose maximum width is smaller.
Without loss of generality, assume that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
have the same width (otherwise, we expand the smaller one). Therefore,

Let

be the pair of slabs obtained by taking the
\varepsilon
-expansions of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}

By the definition of an

The total running time is

7.1
Small gap-ratio (proof of Theorem
42
)

In this section, we assume that there exists an optimal pair
\Sigma^{*}=(\sigma_{1}^{*},\sigma_{2}^{*})
for the parallel two-line-center problem whose gap ratio is at most
\rho
.
Recall that
w^{*}=\max\{w(\sigma_{1}^{*}),w(\sigma_{2}^{*})\}
.
We present an
O(n/\varepsilon)
-time algorithm that outputs a pair of parallel slabs such that their union covers
S

(1+\varepsilon)w^{*}
under the assumption.

Observation 45
.

For a pair
\Sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
of parallel slabs whose gap-ratio is at most
\rho
,
it holds that
g(\Sigma)\leq\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}\cdot w_{\max}
where
w_{\max}=\max\{w(\sigma_{1}),w(\sigma_{2})\}
.

Proof.

Let
g=g(\Sigma)
and
W=W(\Sigma)
.
By definition, we have
W=w(\sigma_{1})+w(\sigma_{2})+g\leq 2w_{\max}+g
.
Since the gap-ratio of
\Sigma
is at most
\rho
, it holds that
\rho\geq g/W\geq g/(2w_{\max}+g)
.
Therefore,
2\rho\cdot w_{\max}\geq(1-\rho)g
, which implies
g\leq\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}\cdot w_{\max}
.
∎

Lemma 46
.

We can compute
\widetilde{w}
such that
w^{*}\leq\widetilde{w}\leq(2+\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho})w^{*}
in
O(n)
time.

Proof.

We first compute

such that
width(S)\leq w^{\prime}\leq 2\cdot width(S)
using a
2
-approximation algorithm for the width,
which can be done in
O(n)
time
[
12
]
.
Let
\widetilde{w}=w^{\prime}/2
.
We show that
\widetilde{w}
satisfies the condition in the statement. Observe that
w^{*}\leq width(S)/2
as a slab of
width(S)
enclosing
S
directly gives us a pair of parallel slabs of width
width(S)/2
whose union covers
S
.
Therefore, we have
w^{*}\leq width(S)/2\leq\widetilde{w}
. It holds that
width(S)\leq W(\Sigma^{*})=g(\Sigma^{*})+w(\sigma_{1}^{*})+w(\sigma_{2}^{*})\leq g(\Sigma^{*})+2w^{*}

\widetilde{w}\leq width(S)
and by Observation

\widetilde{w}\leq(2+\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho})w^{*}

∎

. If no such point exists, then all points of
S
lie on a horizontal line, which implies
w^{*}=0
.
If such
q
exists, then for
w^{*}=0
it must be the case that
p
or
q
(or possibly both) lies on
\sigma_{t}^{*}
.
Let
\ell_{p}
be the line of orientation
\theta
passing through
p
, and define
\ell_{q}
analogously for
q
.
If
w^{*}=0
, then
\sigma_{t}^{*}
must be either
\ell_{p}
or
\ell_{q}
.
We test both cases by checking whether all points of
S
not lying on
\ell_{p}
(resp.,
\ell_{q}
) lie on a common horizontal line.
The procedure above takes linear time in total.

Parallel

Choose two arbitrary points
p
and
q
in
S
, and find a point
r\in S
that does not lie on
\ell_{pq}
.
If no such point exists, then all points of
S
lie on
\ell_{pq}
, which implies
w^{*}=0
.
Assume such a point
r
exists.
If
w^{*}=0
, then at least one slab (of width
0
) in an optimal solution must be
\ell_{pq}
,
\ell_{pr}
, or
\ell_{qr}
.
We test all three cases.
For example, to test whether there exists an optimal pair of slabs where one of them is
\ell_{pq}
, we check whether all points of
S
not lying on
\ell_{pq}
lie on the line parallel to
\ell_{pq}
that passes through
r
.
This entire procedure takes linear time.

9
Future work

We have presented approximation algorithms for several variants of the two-line-center problem. An open question is whether the dependence on
\varepsilon
can be further refined for each variant. For the exact algorithms of the problems, no nontrivial lower bounds are currently known, except for the one-fixed-orientation case, for which we provide an optimal algorithm.

Each of the four problems admits natural generalizations with respect to the dimension
d
and the number of lines
k
(our problems correspond to
d=2
and
k=2
). For higher dimensions (
d\geq 3
) or a larger number of lines (
k\geq 3
), very few exact or approximation algorithms are known.
For the general
k
-line-center problem, the authors in
[
2
]
gave a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm whose expected running time is
O(n\log n)
where the constant depends on
d
,
k
, and
\varepsilon
.
We leave open the question of whether efficient exact and approximation algorithms can be obtained for these and other variants of the two-line-center problem in higher dimensions or with more than two lines.

Another natural variant of the two-line-center problem is the following. Given a set
S
of
n
points in the plane and a fixed orientation difference
\theta
, the goal is to find a pair of slabs whose orientations differ by
\theta
, whose union covers
S
, and that minimizes the maximum of their widths.
An exact algorithm with running time
O(n^{2}\alpha(n)\log n)
is known for this problem
[
6
]
, where
\alpha(n)
denotes the inverse Ackermann function.
To the best of our knowledge, no approximation algorithm is known for this variant.
It is unclear if our approach based on an anchor pair of an optimal pair of slabs and a constant factor solution (as in Lemma
33
and Lemma
47
) can be extended to this variant.
This is because rotating one slab requires rotating the other as well. We addressed this issue in the parallel two-line-center problem by considering two different cases, depending on the gap-ratio of an optimal pair of slabs (see Sections
7.1
and
7.2
).
