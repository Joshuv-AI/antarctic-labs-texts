Surface electrocardiogram (ECG) has long served as a fundamental diagnostic modality for clinicians. Despite advances in advanced cardiac imaging and high-density electroanatomic mapping, this easy and cost-effective tool offers important insights even in complex arrhythmogenic substrates. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Takahashi et al1 present a rare and underreported phenomenon that highlights how surface ECG can predict complex ventricular tachycardia (VT) substrate and serves as a surrogate to successful ablation endpoint.

Multilayer Arrhythmogenic Substrate in Cardiac Sarcoidosis

Cardiac sarcoidosis is an infiltrative cardiomyopathy caused by noncaseating granulomatous inflammation of the myocardium that commonly presents a spectrum of high-grade conduction disease, ventricular arrhythmias, or left ventricular dysfunction. Clinically manifest disease occurs in approximately 5% of patients with systemic sarcoidosis, although autopsy studies reveal cardiac involvement in 20% to 58% of cases. Advanced imaging detects subclinical disease 4 to 5 times more frequently than clinical detection.2, 3, 4, 5 As described in this case, it often results in patchy myocardial inflammation and eventual fibrosis that may involve the mid and epicardial layers. Its predilection for septal involvement also can lead to aggressive and sudden conduction block, which requires careful consideration of the need for primary prevention defibrillation at the time of pacemaker implantation. The adoption of cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography has improved the diagnostic accuracy of cardiac sarcoidosis; however, these imaging modalities may fall short in predicting the functional conduction pathways responsible for arrhythmia maintenance. As a result, cardiac sarcoidosis remains one of the most challenging cardiomyopathies for electrophysiologists.

Double QRS as a Marker of Delayed Epicardial Activation

This report of double QRS is a rare and intriguing observation that is not often appreciated on surface ECG. This phenomenon, in essence, captures the delayed activation of a large mass of myocardium, large enough to create a second delayed QRS complex on surface ECG. Although we often appreciate late activation or functional lines of block while mapping local electrograms, it is rare to observe a similar phenomenon on surface ECG. This is mainly because late potentials account for only a small fraction of net QRS activation, regardless of the extent of scarring. Epsilon waves seen in right ventricular arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy is an example of late activation of a relatively large area of myocardium that can be seen on surface ECG. This case showcases not only a significant zone of slow conduction, but the consequent activation of a large area of excitable myocardium that is able to demonstrate a double QRS. These findings may represent an extreme of a continuum of the previously described “fragmented” QRS by Das and Zipes.6 This pattern of QRS conduction delay has been shown to be an independent predictor of arrhythmic events and mortality in patients with both ischemic and nonischemic cardiomyopathy. Furthermore, this case nicely illustrates real-time ECG changes in response to steroid therapy and ablation. As a point of emphasis—procedural planning for VT ablation relies heavily on the ECG morphology of the clinical VT. Although the index VT was not included by the authors, the recurrent VT morphology described in Figure 6C demonstrates a characteristic broad QRS, high maximum deflection index, and slurred intrinsicoid deflection (pseudo-delta wave), which have been described as predictors of epicardial substrate.7, 8, 9 The evolution of the sinus rhythm ECG, first with delay of the double QRS and eventual resolution, demonstrates how ablation can modify but not entirely terminate critical circuits.

Clinical Implications and Ablation Approach

As is the case with any ablation, appropriate planning and anticipation of unexpected challenges are critical to a successful and safe procedure. At our center, preprocedure imaging and understanding of the clinical cardiomyopathy play an important role in identifying VT substrate. For example, we often encounter nonischemic cardiomyopathies with deeper midmyocardial and epicardial substrate, which appear to have normal or near normal endocardial bipolar voltage mapping. Genetic cardiomyopathies such as laminopathies or desmoplakin/filamin C mutations often can cause fibrosis of the deeper myocardial layers in the septum and the lateral left ventricle. For this reason, we always obtain preprocedural imaging with either cardiac computed tomography or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to identify deep arrhythmogenic substrate and we use unipolar mapping for suspected midmyocardial and epicardial ventricular substrate to identify potential arrhythmogenic regions beyond the endocardium.10 In addition to the mapping challenges, this case underscores the importance of how conventional unipolar radiofrequency ablation may fail to create lesions deep enough to ablate midmyocardial/epicardial abnormal substrate. Alternative strategies such as bipolar radiofrequency ablation, combined endocardial–epicardial approaches, half normal saline irrigation, and alcohol ablation may therefore be necessary for deeper tissue injury.11 In addition, focal pulsed field ablation, bipolar pulsed field ablation, and alternative energy sources such as radiation therapy may have to be an effective approach for such cases.12, 13, 14

Revisiting the Role of Surface ECG

Clinical electrophysiology continues to evolve at an alarming pace, often with technological advancements outpacing our understanding of its clinical implications. This case elegantly merges sophisticated approaches that are necessary for treatment of complex arrhythmia while demonstrating mechanistic evidence with a simple point of care test—the surface 12-lead ECG. The surface markers on ECG likely reflect a full multilayer activation wavefront rather than an isolated epicardial exit. Confirmation of such a mechanism would require intramural recordings in the future. This case is a reminder of the enduring diagnostic value of the surface ECG for electrophysiology practice.
