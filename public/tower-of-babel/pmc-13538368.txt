Population-scale disease-associated tandem repeat analysis reveals locus and ancestry-specific insights
Authors: Indhu-Shree Rajan-Babu, Readman Chiu, Ben Weisburd, Iris Caglayan, Inanc Birol, Jan M Friedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTandem repeat (TR) expansions, including short TRs (motifs ≤6 bp) and variable number TRs (motifs >6 bp), underlie many monogenic disorders, with variable length and sequence influencing pathogenicity, penetrance, severity, and onset. Accurate genotype-phenotype correlation and disease prevalence estimation require characterization beyond repeat length. Here we present a population-scale analysis of 66 disease-associated TR loci using long-read assemblies from 2530 diverse haplotypes from 1265 unaffected donors. Integrating repeat length, motif composition, local ancestry, linkage disequilibrium, and phylogenetic analyses, we reveal extensive locus-, population-, and allele-specific variation shaping disease risk. Up to 8.5% of individuals carry expansions above established pathogenic thresholds, many containing interrupting motifs or sequence structures that attenuate pathogenicity. After excluding alleles from loci with uncertain disease association, non-pathogenic interrupted expansions, and carrier states inconsistent with inheritance patterns, ~4% carried expansions predicted to confer disease risk, largely at adult-onset loci with reduced penetrance. Ancestry-resolved analyses uncover population-specific TR architectures contributing to epidemiological disparities in repeat expansion disorders. Phylogenetic analyses identify conserved ancestral alleles and loci with recent instability. We describe variable linkage disequilibrium patterns and recombination signatures around specific disease-associated TR loci. Our findings emphasize integrating sequence, ancestry, and evolutionary context to understand the complex landscape of disease-associated TRs.Subject terms: Sequence annotation, Structural variation, Genome assembly algorithms

Introduction

Disorders caused by tandem repeat (TR) expansions that exceed locus-specific thresholds at ~70 loci1 affect roughly 1 in 3000 individuals worldwide2. TRs are repetitive DNA sequences composed of motifs or units repeated in tandem, and include both short TRs (motifs ≤6 bp) and variable number TRs (motifs >6 bp). While most repeat expansion (RE) disorders occur across diverse ancestries, some are population-specific or notably prevalent in particular groups3; for example, the TCF4 RE that causes Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy 3 affects ~4–5% of individuals of European ancestry over 40 years of age4.

Disease-associated REs (termed full mutations) exhibit substantial heterogeneity in length and sequence composition, including interruptions and non-canonical motifs that vary by locus and population background and can critically influence disease risk, onset, penetrance, and inheritance5. At certain loci, pathogenicity is also associated with RE-triggered methylation-mediated loss of gene function6.

Variants within or very near TR loci can influence repeat instability, with broader haplotype associations also reported, although some of these may reflect underlying allele-length effects; these local sequence features and haplotype associations can also affect the feasibility of TR imputation or targeted genetic screening7,8. Importantly, these genomic contexts vary considerably across populations due to recombination, selection, genetic drift, and admixture9. Such processes can result in local ancestry at a TR locus that differs from an individual’s genome-wide ancestry, further complicating disease allele distribution and definition.

Recent analysis of a subset of TR loci in short-read sequencing data reported a ~10× higher RE carrier frequency and a 2–3× higher disorder prevalence than previously recognized, and identified numerous premutation carriers at risk of having affected children3. However, short-read sequencing cannot reliably size REs that are longer than sequencing read lengths (~150–350 bp) or detect changes in methylation and sequence composition—the latter known to modify disease at approximately a third of loci5. Accurate estimation of pathogenic RE prevalence and disease risk requires comprehensive assessment of TRs and their genomic context.

Long-read sequencing (LRS) produces reads tens of kilobases in length, enabling direct, base-level characterization of TR length, sequence composition, interruptions, and associated methylation changes in a single assay—offering unparalleled capabilities for accurate RE assessment10,11. Previous LRS-based studies have cataloged population-level TR variation10,12–17; however, some are restricted to a specific population, limiting insights into ancestrally diverse cohorts, and/or often lack comprehensive characterization of repeat heterogeneity—including population-specific alleles, motif structures, genomic context, or local ancestry—at clinically relevant loci. Thus, a detailed, population- and locus-specific analysis incorporating genomic context is needed to enhance disease risk modeling and mechanistic understanding of TR stability.

To address these gaps, we present a comprehensive, ancestry-informed analysis of 66 known disease-associated TR loci using 2530 phased haplotypes from LRS datasets generated by the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (HPRC)18, the 1000 Genomes Project ONT (1kGP-ONT) Sequencing Consortium13, the Human Genome Structural Variation Consortium (Phase 2; HGSVC2)19, and the 1kGP individuals sequenced by Noyvert et al.20. Integrating TR length, sequence motif composition, linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns, recombination, and local ancestry, we delineate locus- and population-specific TR architectures, assess the clinical significance of expanded alleles, and compare human TR sequences to primate orthologs. Our analysis reveals ancestral and recently unstable loci, identifies population-specific alleles underlying epidemiological disparities, and highlights the evolutionary dynamics shaping TR variation. This work establishes a foundational framework for interpreting RE pathogenicity, and evolution by mapping the complex locus-, population-, and allele-specific trajectories of disease-associated TRs in an integrated genomic context, advancing genetic screening, clinical interpretation, and understanding of epidemiological disparities in the distribution of RE disorders, and enhancing our understanding of genome biology.

Results

Locus- and Population-Specific TR Diversity

We genotyped 66 known disease-associated TR loci (Fig. 1a) in 1265 individuals presumed to be unaffected by an inherited disorder from two cohorts, stratified by superpopulation (Africans AFR; Admixed Americans AMR; East Asians EAS; Europeans EUR; South Asians SAS) to assess population-specific variation in TR lengths (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1). TR alleles were classified using locus-specific repeat-size thresholds curated from the literature (Supplementary Data 10) into one of six categories: non-disease-associated, intermediate (alleles above the non-disease-associated range but below the next allelic category for that locus), premutation (alleles below the full-mutation threshold but which may themselves confer disease risk and/or risk of further expansion in subsequent generations), reduced-penetrance (alleles within or near the pathogenic size range but reported in the literature to show lower or variable likelihood of clinical manifestation), full mutation (alleles meeting or exceeding the locus-specific pathogenic size threshold), or unknown if outside these defined size-based allelic categories.

Of the 66 loci analyzed, 48 (73%) exhibited robust multimodality across both cohorts, 16 (24%) were unimodal in both cohorts, with the exception of 2 loci (DIP2B and RILPL1) where modality could not be ascertained with confidence (Supplementary Data 1). Inter-population differences in repeat-length distributions were significant at 36% of pairwise genotype comparisons in Cohort 2 and 13% in Cohort 1, with 57–66% of this variability attributable to AFR individuals (Supplementary Data 2,3). Comparisons between cohorts revealed significant differences at four loci (Supplementary Data 4).

Population-specific TR allele frequencies were evident at several loci. For example, the stable DIP2B 7-repeat allele was nearly fixed in EAS (99%), compared with SAS (81%), AFR and AMR (77% each), and was least frequent in EUR (59%), the only population where the RE-associated intellectual developmental disorder has been reported21. The overall repeat copy distribution in EAS also differed significantly from those in other populations (Fig. 2a). At FGF14, the repeat copy distribution differed significantly between all populations (Fig. 2b). In EAS, the non-disease-associated allele distribution was tightly clustered (~5–50 repeats), remaining below the 200–249 range associated with increased instability and intergenerational expansion to pathogenic sizes (>250)22, consistent with the lower prevalence of FGF14-associated ataxia in EAS22,23.

Sequence composition analysis identified population-, locus-, and allele-specific TR structures. At TCF4, GTG interruption was highly enriched among alleles with 17 repeats (Fig. 2c,d), while at BEAN1, most alleles with 52–57 repeats in AFR individuals displayed a distinctive sequence of motifs: TAACA, two (A)2-4 monomers, TAAA, TA, five TAAAs, and TAA, alternating with canonical (TTTTA)n (Fig. 2e,f), revealing population- and length-specific TR configurations.

At ATXN2, two CAA interruptions were found in 65% of non-disease-associated (≤22 CAG) alleles and 47% of longer alleles. In comparison, a single CAA interruption with longer uninterrupted 5’ (CAG)n tracts was observed in >22 CAG alleles (41%) (Fig. 3a), patterns that may influence repeat stability24. Notably, the AFR population had a higher frequency (26%) of the 23-repeat allele with two CAA interruptions compared to ≤3% in other populations. At RUNX2, the most common structure was (GCG)4-GCT-(GCG)5-GCT-(GCG)3-GCA-GCT-GCA, whereas an alternate structure with (GCG)2-GCA-(GCG)2 in place of (GCG)5 was more prevalent in SAS (17.3%) compared to AFR (1.1%), EAS (3.4%), AMR (7.6%), and EUR (10.1%) (Fig. 3b), reflecting population-specific variation in sequence configuration. At CBL, longer alleles (>11 repeats) more often carried a TGG interruption in comparison to shorter alleles (AFR: 41% vs. 20%; AMR: 30% vs. 17%; EAS: 75% vs. 42%; EUR: 33% vs. 29%; SAS: 82% vs. 40%) (Fig. 3c), suggesting a consistent length-dependent sequence structure at this locus. At FMR1, alleles with >30 repeats were most frequent in AFR (37%) and least frequent in AMR (13%). In EAS, 56% of these alleles carried three AGG interruptions (notably higher than SAS (23%), AMR and AFR (7% each), and EUR (5%)) predominantly in (CGG)9-AGG-(CGG)9-AGG-(CGG)6-AGG-(CGG)9 configuration, which was largely restricted to EAS and SAS (Fig. 3d), consistent with known regional patterns of FMR1 AGG interruptions25. Across loci, AFR individuals exhibited significantly greater structural diversity (i.e., distinct arrangements (sequence or order) and counts of repeat motifs) than EUR in Cohort 1, and than AMR, EAS, and EUR in Cohort 2 (Fig. 4a,b). Cohort 2 also showed more unique repeat structures, which may reflect both its larger sample size and more noise from lower sequencing depth, with the RFC1 locus displaying the highest structural heterogeneity (Fig. 4c,d). Substitution-based non-canonical motifs preserving reading frames were more frequent in coding TRs, whereas indel-based non-canonical motifs were observed in non-coding TRs (Fig. 4e,f). The most frequent non-canonical motifs were consistent across cohorts and populations, often recurring at the same position within the repeat tract and shared across ≥3 populations, providing increased confidence in the validity of these flagged non-canonical motifs (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Collectively, these findings reveal extensive locus- and population-specific TR diversity, characterized by alleles and length-dependent interruption patterns uniquely enriched in specific populations, underscoring their role in modulating repeat stability and epidemiological variation in RE disorders.

Population frequencies of disease-associated TR alleles

Mean frequencies of non-disease-associated and full-mutation alleles were ~95% and ≤0.37%, respectively, across all loci in these two cohorts. No significant differences in mean frequencies were observed between superpopulations across loci (Supplementary Fig. 3a–f).

In addition to the 215 full-mutation alleles that were found across 18 loci, we identified 49 reduced-penetrance alleles across five loci, 14 premutation alleles across five loci, and 744 intermediate alleles across four loci. Although several of these allelic classes were distributed across ≥3 populations (Fig. 5a), many REs exhibited ancestry-specific distributions, including REs of ATXN10 in AMR, ZIC3 in EUR (notably Finnish), and RUNX2 and NOTCH2NLC in EAS, consistent with prior observations26–29. In contrast, FGF14 REs were detected in all populations except AFR.

Technical validation of TR genotyping and sequence inference in Cohort 2

Given the relatively lower sequencing coverage in Cohort 2, we evaluated its potential impact on TR genotyping and RE detection. Mean coverage across disease-associated TR loci was >10× for all loci, including GC-rich regions (C9orf72, AFF2, FMR1), with the exception of FGF14 and TAF1, which had modestly reduced read depth (6–9×) (Supplementary Data 5).

In addition, sequencing coverage in Cohort 2 individuals with premutation, reduced penetrance, or full mutation genotypes was comparable to that observed in individuals with non-expanded alleles. Across the 20 loci harboring these alleles, mean read depths ranged from 4× to 54× (Supplementary Fig. 4). The expanded alleles were supported by 2–34 reads (mean 9.7×) based on Straglr30 estimates (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Furthermore, assembly- and alignment-based genotypes showed strong concordance in repeat-length estimates, supporting reliable RE detection and genotyping from de novo genome assemblies in Cohort 2 (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

Next, we compared repeat-length estimates and non-canonical base content for 24 samples sequenced in both cohorts. Within each individual and locus, TR alleles were ordered by repeat size to define allele 1 (shorter) and allele 2 (longer). Both repeat length and non-canonical base content were highly concordant between cohorts for both allele 1 and allele 2 (Spearman correlation results in Supplementary Fig. 5c–d).

TR genotypes were filtered using minimum per-allele read-support thresholds (Methods), which eliminated most low-confidence calls in Cohort 2. However, these thresholds (2–3 reads) were deliberately chosen to maximize sensitivity for detecting REs at low coverage, and thus some low-support genotypes were retained, which in rare cases affected zygosity inference. For example, in three individuals (HG02895 [TCF4], HG01961 [ATXN10], and NA10835 [ATXN8OS]), assembly-based analysis incorrectly suggested bi-allelic REs. Straglr genotyping and manual inspection confirmed that each individual instead carried a single expanded allele. Such discrepancies likely arise from low read support for the non-expanded allele, incorrect haplotype assignment of noisy reads, or mosaicism. These incorrectly inferred bi-allelic calls had lower per-allele support and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Overall, coverage in Cohort 2 enables reliable RE detection, although repeat-size estimates may be less precise than in high-coverage data and very low coverage can complicate zygosity determination in some cases.

Clinical relevance of disease-associated TR alleles

Among the analysed TR loci, DMD expansions were most prevalent (100/215), consistent with prior reports1, occurring predominantly (92%) in AFR and less frequently in other populations (Fig. 5a). Most DMD expanded alleles exhibited non-canonical motifs, typically at the 3’ end (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Given that only uninterrupted alleles have been associated with Duchenne muscular dystrophy31, and DMD expanded alleles are common in the general population, the disease relevance of interrupted alleles, if any, remains uncertain.

TCF4 full mutations, the second most frequent (30/215), showed no strong population bias, with counts of 5 (AFR), 6 (AMR), 4 (EAS), 8 (EUR), and 7 (SAS). ~60% of TCF4 full mutations were comprised of pure CTGs, while in the remaining full mutations non-canonical bases constituted a variable fraction of the repeat tract: 48% in AFR, 1.5–40% in EUR, 5.6–7.6% in AMR, 0.3% in EAS, and 1.4–2.6% in SAS (Fig. 5b). Most sequences in both cohorts exhibited relatively low non-canonical base content and showed no strong evidence of sequencing error or noise. In contrast, the AFR sample HG02895 from Cohort 2, which had low coverage and an incorrect bi-allelic RE inference, displayed extreme sequence heterogeneity in the assembled tract (Supplementary Fig. 6b), complicating motif interpretation. CCG/GCG non-canonical motifs were observed in two Cohort 2 samples with TCF4 REs; these observations require independent validation, and further work is needed to determine whether variation in interruption types and patterns contributes to ancestry- and individual-specific differences in Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy risk.

Although numerous additional REs were detected, only a subset likely confer disease risk or higher penetrance based on sequence composition. Large alleles at pentanucleotide TR loci mostly involved expansions of canonical or benign motifs. At RFC1, 24 heterozygous carriers of REs (≥75% of the TR tract composed of the pathogenic AAGGG motif) were identified across all populations. One AMR individual was homozygous for the biallelic expansion associated with CANVAS, and six additional individuals had TR tracts composed of 25–75% AAGGG (Fig. 5c). Consistent pathogenic motif annotation was observed for three Cohort 2 ONT assemblies of RFC1 REs with matched HiFi data (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Both ONT and HiFi data demonstrate that the expanded RFC1 alleles were composed largely of the pathogenic AAGGG motif, and short-read-based analysis using ExpansionHunter Denovo32 confirmed the pathogenic AAGGG motif in nine additional individuals with RFC1 REs in our cohort.

At FGF14, most reduced-penetrance and full-mutation alleles exhibited 19–71% non-canonical motif content, with uniquely shared repeat structures enriched for GGA/GCA motifs in AMR and EAS (Fig. 5d), consistent with the non-pathogenicity of interrupted FGF14 REs in SCA27A23. Two ONT assemblies of Cohort 1 samples (e.g., HG00110, HG01501; 18–19× mean coverage) and several large alleles (>180 repeats) from Cohort 2 (e.g., HG00338, HG03595; 11–14× mean coverage) contained nearly pure canonical motifs without spurious non-canonical bases that could reflect sequencing errors. Furthermore, five HiFi assemblies (HG00438, HG00558, HG01943, HG01975, HG00423; 24–62× mean coverage) that displayed GCA-enriched reduced-penetrance alleles in AMR/EAS individuals had closely matching repeat structures across several Cohort 2 samples (e.g., HG01950, HG01942, HG02105; 15–21× mean coverage), both consistent with previously reported FGF14 REs33. This concordance across sequencing platforms, cohorts, and prior literature demonstrates that the recurrent sequence composition of expanded FGF14 alleles is reliably resolved, rather than a platform-specific artifact in Cohort 2.

At ATXN8OS, 12 full mutations were identified; five had pure CTG repeats, while others showed up to 17% non-canonical content in AMR and up to 58% in EUR, including one EUR individual (HG01442) with four CGG interruptions associated with increased SCA8 risk and earlier onset34 (Fig. 5e). ATXN10 REs in AMR (including two compound heterozygotes) harbored previously unreported non-canonical motifs (e.g., GTTCT, ACTCT) of unknown significance with up to 67% non-canonical content (Fig. 5f). Full mutation alleles in NA10835 (ATXN8OS) and HG01961 (ATXN10) had low read support (3×) and were incorrectly inferred as bi-allelic, highlighting that sequence interpretation are less reliable for these specific low-read samples (Supplementary Fig. 6c–d).

In total, 186 of 1265 individuals (14.7%) carried at least one full mutation allele. This initial set included loci with limited or uncertain disease validity, interrupted, potentially non-pathogenic alleles, and carrier states. Excluding only DMD reduced the count to 107 individuals (8.5%). Further excluding interrupted, non-pathogenic FGF14 full mutations, heterozygous females at X-linked loci, and heterozygous carriers at autosomal recessive loci yielded 67 individuals (5.3%), representing genotypes with putative disease risk consistent with expected inheritance patterns and motif composition.

Because several loci have questionable or incompletely established relationships with disease (ZIC3, PABPN1, RUNX2, DIP2B, NIPA1, PRDM12), these were examined separately. Considering only these loci, 20 individuals (1.6%) carried at least one full mutation allele. Finally, excluding these questionable loci in addition to the other filters resulted in 48 individuals (3.8%) with full mutation genotypes at loci (ATXN10 [3], ATXN8OS [12], HTT [2], NOTCH2NLC [1], RFC1 [1], TCF4 [28], ZNF713 [1]) with stronger evidence for disease association, though majority of these (43 individuals) involved adult-onset loci (ATXN8OS, ATXN10, TCF4) with evidence for reduced or incomplete penetrance. Sequence composition analysis with LRS and a focus on loci with higher-confidence disease associations was essential for identifying and excluding alleles that did not meet current criteria for potential disease causality.

LRS further revealed methylation, mosaicism, and X-inactivation patterns relevant for pathogenicity assessment. At DIP2B, a single full mutation in an EUR individual exhibited marked mosaicism (200–1200 CCG repeats) without the hypermethylation associated with gene silencing (Supplementary Fig. 7a). At ZIC3, full mutation allele in a female exhibited skewed X-inactivation of the expanded allele (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These findings highlight the importance of LRS-enabled comprehensive TR assessment to accurately evaluate true disease-associated full-mutation alleles.

Local ancestry patterns of disease-associated TRs

At most disease-associated TR loci, the inferred local ancestry (based on a 500 kb window centered on the TR) matched the reported superpopulation in ~70–80% of AFR, EAS, and EUR individuals. In contrast, only 30–46% of AMR and SAS individuals carried matching ancestry (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Several loci deviated markedly from this pattern, revealing pronounced locus-specific ancestry enrichment. NOTCH2NLC exhibited nearly universal EUR ancestry (~90%) even among AFR individuals. Conversely, PHOX2B and RAPGEF2 carried EAS ancestry in ~30% of EUR individuals, while ZIC3 carried EUR ancestry in ~30–50% of EAS and SAS individuals.

To better understand the genomic context of these loci, we analyzed ±2.5 Mb flanking regions, which revealed contiguous or fragmented ancestry tracts around TRs. At NOTCH2NLC, a broad EUR-enriched segment spanned the TR in all superpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 8b). However, this extended EUR-enriched segment overlaps a broad duplicated genomic region that is likely to have affected sequence reconstruction, variant calling, and local ancestry inference. Besides, the signal could reflect an ancestrally shared haplotype that is present across populations but occurs at higher frequency in EUR, which may bias frequency-based methods such as RFMix35 toward EUR assignment. In contrast, PHOX2B, RAPGEF2, and ZIC3 showed discrete ancestry switches at the TR (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), consistent with recombination events or locus-specific ancestry shifts due to admixture. Notably, PHOX2B and RAPGEF2 also displayed EAS-enriched flanks in AMR, EUR, and SAS individuals.

Inferred local ancestries of premutation, reduced-penetrance, and full-mutation alleles revealed additional patterns (Fig. 6a–e and Supplementary Data 6). In AFR and EUR individuals, local ancestry generally matched the superpopulation. In AMR individuals, ATXN10 reduced-penetrance and full-mutation alleles aligned with AMR ancestry, consistent with the known ancestral origin of ATXN10 REs36,37. By contrast, reduced-penetrance TBP alleles were distributed across multiple ancestries (AFR, EAS, EUR), and FGF14 reduced-penetrance and full-mutation alleles were enriched for AMR or EUR ancestry in AMR, EUR, and SAS individuals but spread across ancestries (except AFR) in EAS, indicating locus- and population-specific ancestry contributions.

TR sequence composition was also associated with ancestry. Nearly pure reduced-penetrance FGF14 alleles (<1% non-canonical content) in EUR and EAS individuals were observed exclusively on EUR ancestry backgrounds, while highly interrupted full-mutation alleles (>49% non-canonical content) mapped to EUR or AMR ancestry in EAS, SAS, and EUR individuals. Full mutations with intermediate non-canonical content (19–47%) in EAS and AMR individuals were more broadly distributed, highlighting the influence of ancestral background on sequence purity and interruption patterns. Similarly, the TGG-interrupted TCF4 17-repeat alleles, most frequent in AMR and observed in other populations, consistently mapped to EUR/AMR local ancestry across AMR, EUR, and SAS individuals (Supplementary Fig. 10a), consistent with a haplotype likely shared among these populations, rather than being specific to AMR.

Together, these results illustrate that disease-associated TRs often reside on population-specific haplotypes shaped by admixture, exhibiting locus- and motif-specific ancestry patterns that diverge from individuals’ overall superpopulation. These localized ancestry switches may reflect the diverse historical and demographic dynamics driving TR variation and disease risk, though some may result from local ancestry misassignment.

Phylogenetic relationships of disease-associated TRs

Phylogenetic analysis of TR sequences in the high-coverage Cohort 1 data revealed locus- and population-specific divergence, reflecting a complex interplay of conservation and diversification. Principal component analysis of tree summary statistics (summarized in Additional Data Table 1) separated deeply branched, unbalanced loci (e.g., RFC1, FGF14), indicative of rapid diversification, from compact, conserved loci in coding, untranslated, or promoter regions (e.g., ATXN7, DMPK), consistent with stronger purifying selection (Fig. 7a).

At 31 loci, the ancestral sequence (derived from multiple alignments of seven primate genomes) was retained in at least one haplotype, and at 19 of these it was present in ≥100 (of 544) haplotypes across all superpopulations (Fig. 7b). This widespread retention likely reflects functional constraints and selective pressures that regulate TR stability and mutation dynamics. High retention was observed at COMP, HOXA13, PABPN1, and ATXN7. At ATXN7, the ancestral (CAG)10 appeared in 409 haplotypes, with other common derived variants carrying 1–3 additional repeats (Fig. 7c). At CBL, the ancestral (CGG)11 persisted in 246 haplotypes, alongside a common derived CGG-TGG-(CGG)9 variant (99 haplotypes), with longer CGG-TGG-(CGG)11-34 alleles enriched in EAS and SAS and shorter (CGG)8 alleles enriched in AFR (Fig. 7d). In contrast, at 12 loci the ancestral sequence was rare (<50 haplotypes) or absent, consistent with elevated mutation rates and/or a selective advantage of derived configurations. At ATXN2, for example, the ancestral (CAG)13-CAA-(CAG)9 appeared in only four haplotypes, whereas the most frequent allele (302 haplotypes) had two CAA interruptions, (CAG)8-CAA-(CAG)4-CAA-(CAG)8, with a slightly modified variant, (CAG)8-CAA-(CAG)5-CAA-(CAG)8, enriched in AFR (Fig. 7e). Similarly, at ATN1, the ancestral CAG-CAA-(CAG)17 was completely absent, with all sequences carrying two CAA interruptions in a CAG-CAA-CAG-CAA-(CAG)8-21 configuration (Fig. 7f), potentially conferring greater stability and reducing disease risk. See Additional Data Fig. 3 for phylogenetic trees of all loci.

At most loci, the most frequent TR allele was shared across superpopulations (Supplementary Data 7), with structurally similar sequences clustering together. However, several loci exhibited population-specific divergence in sequence composition, which may contribute to ancestry-related differences in TR stability. At ATXN1, variants with a single CAT interruption formed a distinct clade from the ancestral and other sequences with two CAT interruptions, with one such variant, (CAG)11-CAT-(CAG)16, enriched in EAS (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Similarly, at ATXN3, sequences with CAA-AAG interruptions formed a separate clade from those with an additional CAA interruption (CAA-AAG-CAG-CAA), with (CAG)2-CAA-AAG-(CAG)15 predominant in EAS and SAS (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

These population-specific variants in ATXN1 and ATXN3 exhibited increased sequence purity (a feature associated with higher instability and disease risk), while other loci showed population-specific non-canonical motifs or elevated mutation rates. This includes the large impure tracts in ATXN10 enriched in AMR (Supplementary Fig. 10d) and BEAN1 alleles with 52–57 repeats harboring distinctive non-canonical motifs in AFR (Supplementary Fig. 11). In contrast, GIPC1 sequences with GGC-GGA-GGG-AGC interruptions formed a distinct clade at the tips of the tree, yet consistently observed across all superpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicative of a recent configuration subject to ongoing diversification.

Collectively, these findings reveal the influence of functional constraints, demographic history, and locus-specific mutation dynamics on sequence purity and structural divergence at disease-associated TR loci.

Population-wide haplotype patterns surrounding disease-associated TRs

Recombination-driven haplotype structures are critical in determining the imputation accuracy of REs, TR stability, and disease risk. We profiled regional haplotype patterns around disease-associated TRs across populations, benchmarking them against local recombination signatures. Consistent with genome-wide patterns16, principal component analysis of phased single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) within 1 Mb of each TR showed that the top three components explained ~80% of the variance, clearly separating superpopulations (Fig. 8a) and highlighting the impact of population history on haplotypic backgrounds and the need for population-specific TR analyses.

LD analysis showed that AFR individuals had more, smaller LD blocks, each with fewer SNVs, consistent with higher recombination and diversity (Fig. 8b–e). Across populations, we observed a significant inverse correlation between average LD block size and the number of recombination hotspots defined as regions with recombination rates exceeding 1 cM/Mb (Supplementary Fig. 13a), reinforcing the critical role of recombination in shaping the haplotype architecture around disease-associated TRs. Of the 66 loci studied, 27 lay within 10 kb of hotspots on both flanks, while 35 were more isolated (Supplementary Fig. 13b,c).

See Additional Data Fig.4 for circos plots of LD and recombination signatures across TR loci, stratified by chromosome. Haplotype structures varied widely: large, dense LD blocks at loci such as ATXN2, THAP11, and AR coincided with recombination deserts (regions lacking a hotspot within 100 kb of the TR) (Fig. 8f–i). A schematic guide to interpreting the circos plot layers is provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. Conversely, loci including CACNA1A and GIPC1 exhibited fragmented LD patterns indicative of recombination-active regions, while ATXN10 and MARCHF6 exhibited localized recombination suppression within broader recombination-active contexts (Supplementary Fig. 15a,b).

LD landscapes surrounding disease-associated TRs are provided in Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17 and summarized in Supplementary Data 8 and 9. In populations except AFR, several TRs (e.g., DMPK, TBP) were spanned by contiguous LD blocks, reflecting relatively conserved haplotypes outside of AFR. Conversely, more TRs (e.g., CACNA1A, SOX3) resided near LD block boundaries or at junctions between adjacent blocks across populations. Notably, in some cases, no LD blocks span the TR in specific populations (e.g., PRNP in EAS, SOX3 across nearly all populations, and FOXL2 in both EAS and EUR). These patterns reflect regional differences in LD structure and imply that imputation accuracy for TR alleles may vary substantially across populations and loci.

Local ancestry switches observed at PHOX2B, RAPGEF2, and ZIC3 (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c) correspond closely with LD block patterns; TRs at these loci were typically positioned near edges of LD blocks, consistent with recombination activity and ancestry transitions influencing haplotype structure.

Evidence of shared haplotypes between populations was apparent at several loci (Supplementary Data 9). For example, ATXN10 exhibited nearly identical regional LD block structures in AMR and EUR (Supplementary Fig. 18). Further, SNVs (minor allele frequency ≤ 0.05) co-segregating with REs highlighted tagging potential: the SNV rs41524547-G, previously associated with reduced-penetrance and full mutation alleles37, occurred on all AMR carriers of expanded ATXN10 alleles in our assemblies. Among 16,328 gnomAD individuals with TR genotypes, 16 (14 AMR and 2 AFR) carried ATXN10 alleles >50 repeats; rs41524547-G was present in all but one AMR carrier. Local ancestry analysis of these 16 individuals confirmed predominantly AMR ancestry, including the two AFR individuals who were assigned AMR local ancestry at the locus. Notably, both bi-allelic RE carriers were homozygous for this SNV (Fig. 9), reinforcing rs41524547-G’s utility as a tagging SNV for imputing ATXN10 REs, particularly within populations maintaining strong LD between the SNV and the TR.

These findings demonstrate heterogeneity in haplotype structures at disease-associated TR loci, reflecting locus-specific genomic features and population-specific evolutionary history, with important implications for RE imputation, interpretation of disease associations, and the design of haplotype-based analyses in diverse populations.

Discussion

We present a comprehensive LRS-based analysis of disease-associated TRs, revealing allele-, locus-, and population-specific variations underlying epidemiological disparities in RE disorders. Such variation is exemplified at the DIP2B locus, where stable alleles were less prevalent in EUR, consistent with the population specificity of the associated disorder21 (Fig. 2a). At FGF14, EAS showed a narrow distribution (5–50 repeats) but a higher frequency of very large (>180) and intermediate (180–250) alleles, which could predispose to disease. However, compared to other populations, most large EAS alleles had significantly higher non-canonical base content due to interruptions, which mitigate pathogenicity and align with the lower prevalence of SCA27B in this population23 (Fig. 2b; Fig. 10a–c). In contrast, EUR and SAS individuals carried more large, uninterrupted alleles (50–200), which are prone to instability and expansion, consistent with higher disorder prevalence in these groups38. Across all populations, alleles with ≥50 repeat copies showed a higher overall proportion of non-canonical bases compared with alleles <50 repeat copies (Fig. 10b). Despite this enrichment, the proportion of non-canonical bases does not increase linearly with repeat length. Instead, within each superpopulation, we observed a significant inverse monotonic relationship between repeat copy number and non-canonical base content across the full allelic spectrum (Fig. 10c). LRS-based profiling classified 87% of long FGF14 alleles as interrupted and likely benign, while delineating population-specific distributions of unstable alleles that mirror RE disorder prevalence. These findings underscore the importance of understanding repeat copy distributions for identifying at-risk populations and demonstrate the utility of LRS-based TR analysis in carrier screening and population risk assessment.

TR size- and ancestry-stratified allele frequency data from large, diverse populations are essential for interpreting RE–disease associations3. Without such context, alleles rare in one population may be misclassified as pathogenic if they are common in underrepresented groups. For example, the DMD RE, previously implicated in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, is rare in most populations but too frequent in AFR to support pathogenicity. Multimodality analysis revealed largely consistent repeat-length distributions across both cohorts, with only four loci showing significant differences between cohorts. However, the larger Cohort 2 enabled detection of inter-population differences at a higher proportion (36% vs 13% of pairwise comparisons), underscoring the need for large, ancestrally diverse datasets to fully capture RE variation and improve pathogenicity inference.

Comprehensive analysis integrating repeat size, sequence composition, zygosity, and inheritance patterns revealed that 3.8% of individuals in the general population carry fully expanded alleles at loci with established disease associations. These expansions often exhibit ancestry-specific patterns and are largely linked to adult-onset disorders with reduced or incomplete penetrance. While these individuals were presumed asymptomatic, some REs may manifest later in life. Notably, six individuals carried dominant alleles for pediatric-onset RE disorders without symptoms, raising questions about the reported pathogenicity and penetrance of these expanded alleles. This includes the X-linked ZIC3 expansion26—previously implicated in a reportedly male-lethal disorder—identified in three apparently unaffected individuals (two females and one male). These findings suggest more REs presumed to be fully penetrant may, in fact, show incomplete penetrance or variable expressivity. This pattern is, in part, expected. Because this study focused on a general, presumably unaffected population, REs associated with highly penetrant, early-onset disorders are expected to be rare in such a cohort due to strong purifying selection, whereas those linked to adult-onset or reduced-penetrance phenotypes may occur at higher frequency. Furthermore, as the datasets analysed here lack detailed clinical phenotype and family history information, we cannot determine whether these individuals are truly unaffected, or whether disease manifestation at adult-onset loci had simply not yet occurred at the time of enrolment.

Mechanistic explanations for the reduced or incomplete penetrance observed at several known disease-associated TR loci provide further support for evaluating REs beyond length alone. The sequence composition of REs is one such modifier (as reviewed in Rajan-Babu IS et al.5.): at FGF14, interruptions reduce repeat tract instability and the likelihood of further expansion to pathogenic size, where uninterrupted GAA full mutations have been proposed to drive haploinsufficiency38, whereas at ATXN8OS and FAME-associated loci, non-canonical motifs (CCG interruptions in SCA8 and TTTCA insertions in FAME) instead increase penetrance by promoting RNA-mediated toxicity through RNA foci formation and repeat-associated non-AUG translation34,39. Haplotype background represents a further modifier of repeat instability: at CNBP locus associated with myotonic dystrophy type 2, loss of CCTG interruptions on a shared founder haplotype produced a premutation allele prone to further expansion40, and a common 5’-flanking variant has similarly been associated with enhanced FGF14 locus stability41. Tissue-specific somatic instability represents a further modifier of penetrance and onset, as shown at HTT, where the CAG repeat expands somatically well beyond the inherited length specifically in striatal projection neurons, with neurodegeneration occurring when a critical size threshold is crossed42. Methylation status can also determine phenotypic outcome at a given repeat size: methylation-mediated silencing underlies fragile X syndrome at full-mutation FMR1 alleles, whereas unmethylated full mutations are instead associated with a distinct, FXTAS-like phenotype43. At DIP2B, methylation status of the expanded allele similarly determines whether gene silencing occurs, which has been proposed to underlie the neurocognitive phenotype associated with FRA12A21. These molecular attributes collectively provide a mechanistic basis for the reduced or incomplete penetrance described above and argue that genetic screening and diagnostic criteria for REs should incorporate motif composition, flanking haplotype context, somatic mosaicism, and epigenetic status, rather than RE length alone.

While demonstrating the power of LRS to resolve TR variation, these findings also highlight the ethical challenges of incidental findings in population-scale studies, where disclosure may be impractical, undesired, or beyond the scope of consent. Population-wide screening must balance these concerns with the need for genetic counseling and the implications of predictive testing in asymptomatic individuals, particularly when identifying carriers of severe RE disorders like Huntington disease.

Our observation of distinctive motif patterns within TR alleles of defined length ranges, such as at BEAN1, supports that specific sequence compositions are associated with particular genotypes, with TR diversity differing between populations, highest in AFR individuals, and often featuring non-canonical motifs or interruptions shared across individuals44. More broadly, the locus- and population-specific evolutionary trajectories uncovered in this study show how TR characteristics, functional constraints, and demographic history contribute to allelic diversity and the emergence of unstable alleles. Differences in sequence purity, motif structure, and conservation across populations may reflect distinct selective pressures and mutational dynamics that drive variation in disease risk and prevalence.

Our results indicate that RE analysis is feasible in datasets with low-to-modest read depths. Although insufficient coverage and mosaicism can complicate zygosity determination and introduce apparent heterogeneity in reconstructed sequences, particularly for REs with limited read support (≤3 reads), REs can be reliably detected and motif inference remains possible for many loci. However, rare or non-recurrent sequence variants, especially frameshifting variants that appear stochastic and lack shared positional patterns across samples are most likely sequencing or assembly artefacts and require orthogonal validation. In addition, because the samples analysed in this study were largely generated from genomic DNA extracted from transformed cell lines maintained in culture for extended periods, some rare sequence variants or subtle differences in allele length, particularly for large REs, may reflect somatic mosaicism or clonal expansion rather than germline variation. Accordingly, our analyses focused on recurrent, shared non-canonical motifs rather than rare or sample-specific motifs, particularly in low-coverage datasets. Our findings support the feasibility of large-scale LRS TR studies using low-to-moderate sequencing coverage, although precise allele sizing and interpretation of rare sequence variants would benefit from higher depth.

Previous local ancestry investigation at disease-associated TR loci relied on broad genomic windows (5 Mb) and short-read sequencing, limiting resolution to smaller REs3. In contrast, our analysis of LRS data—which enables more accurate haplotype phasing—TR genotyping, and sequence composition analysis, focused on a narrower 500-kb region and revealed locus- and allele-specific local ancestry patterns for TRs, including larger REs such as those at the FGF14 and ATXN10 loci. These findings refine and extend prior work by providing finer-scale insights into local haplotype structure across diverse populations.

We further show that disease-associated TRs are embedded within complex, locus- and population-specific genetic backgrounds influenced by selection, recombination, admixture, and local ancestry—often differing from genome-wide ancestry. These patterns highlight the need to interpret REs within their full genomic and ancestral context. The frequent placement of TRs at LD block boundaries or recombination-active regions suggests their susceptibility to haplotype fragmentation, with important implications for imputation, genetic screening, and the transferability of association signals across populations. These insights emphasize that accurate risk prediction and carrier identification require population-aware approaches that account for local ancestry patterns and haplotype diversity, especially in understudied and genetically diverse populations. Because our LD-block analysis captures population-level rather than RE-specific haplotypes, it cannot determine whether the observed ancestry patterns in RE carriers reflect recurrent mutational events or a shared ancestral RE redistributed by recombination and admixture.

By integrating sequence-resolved TR data from LRS with ancestry-aware genomic analysis, our work provides a foundational resource for interpreting TR variation in its full evolutionary, demographic, and genomic context. This integrated approach enables more accurate delineation of both global and population-specific disease risks, facilitates the identification of ancestry-restricted alleles and motif configurations, informs the refinement of pathogenicity thresholds, and supports improved clinical interpretation of REs. As LRS data of diverse populations are generated, integrating detailed sequence, motif, genetic, and local ancestry data will be essential for accurately defining risk alleles, haplotypes, and their prevalence. Our findings also underscore the need to move beyond single-population reference databases and simple allele size cutoffs toward a multidimensional, sequence-, and context-aware interpretation of RE disorders.

Methods

Study cohorts and haplotype-resolved genome assemblies

We analysed 2530 haplotypes from 1265 individuals presumed to be unaffected by an inherited disorder, assembled from PacBio or Nanopore LRS data across two cohorts. The first cohort (n = 273) comprised ~35–40× genomes from three publicly available datasets13,18,19, previously characterized by Chiu et al.16. Per-locus sequencing coverage for the 1kGP-ONT dataset was obtained by intersecting genome coverage BED files with TR coordinates using BEDTools45. For each locus, the mean, median and interquartile range (IQR) of coverage across the 100 samples were computed and summarized in Supplementary Data 5. The sequencing platform and genome assemblers used for each individual sample in Cohort 1 are described in Supplementary Data 1 of Chiu et al.16. The second cohort (n = 1019) consisted of ~15× Nanopore data from 1kGP individuals sequenced by Noyvert et al.20. Per-locus sequencing coverage for this cohort was calculated using mosdepth46 with a -Q 20 mapping quality threshold. Mean, median and IQR coverage values computed for each locus across all samples are also reported in Supplementary Data 5. For Cohort 2, we generated haplotype-resolved assemblies for each individual. After excluding three samples due to read extraction failure and 24 overlapping with the first cohort, 992 genomes from the second cohort were retained for analysis. Individual global ancestry was defined based on population of origin, genetic clustering, and grouping into continental superpopulations (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, SAS).

TR genotype analysis

Disease-associated TR loci were identified in the assemblies following Chiu et al.16, recording the assembly scaffold, coordinates, and strand in BED format for each locus. We used Tabix to query each locus, selecting the closest overlapping TR by minimal start-end difference. TR sequences were extracted with Pysam and genotyped using Tandem Repeats Finder47 with the following parameters: 2 5 5 80 10 10 500 -d -h, consistent with Straglr30. In parallel, disease-associated TR loci were genotyped in alignments using Straglr in targeted mode with --min_support 2. Assembly-derived TR genotypes that showed zygosity discrepancies relative to Straglr alignment-based genotypes, likely due to insufficient read coverage, were excluded. Heterozygous calls were retained when each allele was supported by ≥2 reads, while homozygous calls required ≥3 supporting reads. In Cohort 2, genotypes were filtered out when supported by <4 reads, or by <3 reads for X-linked loci in males. The remaining genotypes were classified by repeat copy number and/or sequence composition using thresholds and repeat motifs curated from Rajan-Babu IS et al.5 and other sources1,48–50. Alleles that did not clearly fall within the established categories (normal, intermediate, premutation, and full mutation) were labeled as unknown, whereas those associated with a lower or variable likelihood of clinical manifestation were labeled as reduced penetrance. The locus-specific thresholds are summarized in Supplementary Data 10.

Assessment of repeat-copy modality

We assessed repeat-copy modality using Hartigan’s dip test applied to haplotype-level repeat-copy counts across all 66 known disease-associated TR loci. Because the study cohorts differed substantially in sample size, we implemented a bidirectional analysis in which the larger Cohort 2 was repeatedly downsampled (1000 iterations, without replacement) to match the haplotype count of the smaller Cohort 1 at each locus. For each locus, unimodality was first tested in the smaller cohort, and the proportion of size-matched resamples of the larger cohort rejecting unimodality (p < 0.05) was calculated across 1000 iterations. This analysis was also applied in reverse to evaluate whether multimodality detected in the full Cohort 2 persisted upon downsampling. Loci were classified as robustly multimodal (p < 0.05 in both cohorts and ≥80% of iterations rejecting unimodality), unimodal (p ≥ 0.05 in both cohorts), or low confidence (p < 0.05 in Cohort 2 but <80% of iterations rejecting unimodality).

TR sequence composition analysis

Disease-associated TR sequences were further analysed with TRMotifAnnotator, a Python tool that identifies both canonical motifs and non-canonical variant motifs resulting from substitutions or indels within the canonical motif. TRMotifAnnotator parses assembled sequences of TR tracts, splitting them at canonical motif occurrences to identify intervening non-canonical motifs. Its output includes (1) a TSV file with repeat genotypes, sequence structures, and motif counts, (2) a color-coded plot where each track represents a sequence and colors denote different repeat motifs, and (3) a html report with embedded visualization and scrollable TSV data table. Motif analysis was limited to haplotypes of individuals for whom sequence spanning the TR was observed in the assembled contigs.

Validation of genotype and sequence consistency

For the 24 individuals present in both Cohort 1 and Cohort 2, we compared repeat copy number (genotype) and non-canonical base content (%) across known disease-associated TR loci. The analysis focused on 60 loci where at least 15 individuals had shared genotypes calls in both cohorts. Genotypes were extracted per individual and locus, and alleles were sorted by repeat copy number such that allele 1 corresponded to the shorter allele and allele 2 to the longer allele, yielding 1,369 paired genotypes. Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ) and corresponding p values were computed separately for repeat genotype and non-canonical base content for allele 1 and allele 2.

Local ancestry, phylogenetic, and linkage disequilibrium analyses

Local ancestries at disease-associated TR loci were inferred from SNVs called on each of the 2530 assembled haplotypes aligned to GRCh38. Phased VCFs containing bi-allelic SNVs from 500-kb regions flanking each TR were extracted; ~90% of these windows contained ≥300 SNVs, providing sufficient marker density. We applied RFMix35 to these VCFs (Supplementary Methods; Ibañez et al.3).

Rooted phylogenetic trees for each disease-associated TR locus were constructed by aligning TR sequences representing unique repeat structures with predicted ancestral TR sequences. The ancestral TR sequences were derived from multiple alignments of seven primate genomes51 (Ensembl) using Clustal Omega52–54. Trees were visualized with iTOL55, and summary statistics were computed with treestats56.

For principal component and LD analyses, we used phased SNV calls from 2504 unrelated individuals in 1kGP phase 3. Principal components were computed from SNVs within 1-Mb window flanking each TR locus using PLINK57. For LD analysis, phased SNVs from 5-Mb windows were processed to generate population-specific DET files, which were then used to analyse LD patterns at disease-associated TR loci.

Statistical analysis

Repeat copy modality was assessed using Hartigan’s dip test for each disease-associated TR locus across both cohorts. To account for cohort size differences, the test was applied to haplotype-count–matched downsampling of Cohort 2 (1000 iterations); unimodality was defined as p ≥ 0.05 and multimodality as p < 0.05, with loci classified based on the proportion of downsampled iterations rejecting unimodality. Statistical comparisons of TR copy distributions across superpopulations (AFR, AMR, EUR, EAS, SAS) and study cohorts were conducted using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to assess differences in repeat copy number distributions at each of the 66 known disease-associated TR loci. To control for multiple testing, Bonferroni correction was applied across all pairwise comparisons: for within-locus comparisons across the five superpopulations, ten pairwise tests per locus (66 loci × 10 = 660 tests) were adjusted; for comparisons of the same superpopulation between cohorts, five tests per locus (66 loci × 5 = 330 tests) were adjusted. Loci with adjusted p values below the significance threshold (0.05) were considered significant. Comparisons of unique repeat structure counts between superpopulations in the study cohorts were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s pairwise tests across all ten comparisons, with Bonferroni adjustment applied. Each superpopulation’s unique repeat structure counts were based on data from 66 TR loci. Allelic frequency comparisons across superpopulations were performed using Welch’s one-way ANOVA, with Games–Howell post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons between the five superpopulations. Bonferroni correction was applied within each allele class to adjust p values for the ten pairwise comparisons. Each superpopulation’s allele frequency estimates were based on data from known disease-associated TR loci. LD block statistics around TR loci were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, with post-hoc unpaired pairwise t-tests for all ten pairwise comparisons across superpopulations for each LD-block measure. Bonferroni correction was applied within each measure to adjust p values for these multiple comparisons. Principal component analysis summarized patterns in phylogenetic tree statistics and bi-allelic SNVs within 1 Mb regions encompassing TR loci. Pairwise comparisons of non-canonical base content between superpopulations at the FGF14 locus were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for all ten pairwise comparisons between the five superpopulations, with Bonferroni correction applied. Significant differences were reported based on adjusted p values. Correlations between FGF14 repeat copy number and non-canonical base content (%) stratified by superpopulation were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation to evaluate monotonic relationships; Pearson correlation coefficients were additionally calculated for comparison. For each test, correlation coefficients (Spearman’s ρ and Pearson’s r) and Bonferroni-adjusted p values (five tests, one per superpopulation) were reported.

All supporting data are included within the article and its Supplementary Information. Additional data files generated in this study are available at Figshare58. Haplotype-level TR genotypes, sequence composition data, summary statistics, and inferred local ancestries are available via REDatlas, an interactive web application that also visualizes the geographical distribution of RE disorders, based on curated data from GeneReviews, OMIM, and recent literature. REDatlas is accessible at (https://redatlas.wf-trs.app/). The publicly available datasets used in this study are accessible at the Human Pangenome Reference Consortium (https://humanpangenome.org/data/), the 1000 Genomes Project LRS, the Human Genome Structural Variation Consortium Phase 2, and the 1000 Genomes Project ONT Vienna dataset (https://ftp.1000genomes.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/ftp/data_collections/1KG_ONT_VIENNA/hg38/).

Detailed command-line workflows and parameters used for genome assembly, variant calling, local ancestry inference, and phylogenetic and LD analyses are provided in the Supplementary Methods. All display items in the manuscript can be reproduced from these resources. The TRMotifAnnotator tool is available at (https://github.com/wf-TRs/TRMotifAnnotator) and is archived at Figshare59. The REDatlas source code is available at (https://github.com/wf-TRs/REDatlas) and is archived at Figshare60.
