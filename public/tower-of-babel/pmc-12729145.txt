Characterising resting motor threshold in depressed individuals across life span: a large retrospective study
Authors: Duanwei Wang, Zhimin Huang, Tao Kong, Fengya Zhen, Cuixia An, Xianwei Che
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a safe and non-invasive treatment strategy for depression. Resting motor threshold (RMT) directly determines rTMS stimulation intensity and measures general neuronal membrane excitability in the corticomotor pathway. However, RMT has yet to be characterised across age cohorts in depressed individuals, by taking into account of critical demographic information such as gender and interhemispheric asymmetry. In a large sample of depressed individuals (n = 258), this retrospective study sort to address this issue. Our results showed that older adults (≥ 50 years, n = 61) had higher RMT than depressed prime-age adults (26–49 years, n = 83). Depressed adolescents (< 18 years, n = 63) also demonstrated higher RMT than prime-age adults, but with a most prominent effect in females. In addition, interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT was observed in young female depressed adults (18–25 years, n = 51), with a higher RMT in the left versus right hemisphere. Meanwhile, no clear gender effect was observed on RMT. Overall, this study provided the first portrait of RMT development in depressed individuals across age cohorts in the context of gender and interhemispheric asymmetry. Our results may have implications for designing rTMS treatment strategies given the role of age and interhemispheric asymmetry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07599-1.Keywords: Depression, RMT, Age, Gender, Interhemispheric asymmetry

Introduction

Depressive disorder is a leading cause of disability worldwide [1], which largely increases suicide rates, family dysfunction, and intergenerational poverty [2, 3]. In terms of prevalence, depressive disorder peaks in mid-life (~ 45–55 years old), but demonstrates increased prevalence in adolescents in recent years [2, 4]. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a safe and non-invasive form of brain stimulation technology that was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for depression treatment [5–7]. Moreover, FDA has recently granted clearance for rTMS to treat depression in adolescents aged 15 and older.

One of the of most important parameters in depression treatment is resting motor threshold (RMT), which is defined as the minimum intensity to induce motor evoked potentials (MEP) > 50µV in a muscle at rest in 5 out of 10 trials [8]. RMT measures general neuronal membrane excitability in the corticomotor pathway, and it determines the stimulation intensity in rTMS treatments, such as 120% RMT for depression [5, 7]. As RMT is such a critical measure for depression treatments, it is important to characterise RMT in the context of certain demographic information.

Among them, age is believed to impact RMT, as some studies found higher RMT in older than young adults in healthy populations [9–11]. However, it is unknown how age influences RMT in depressive disorder, which could inform rTMS treatments for depression. In one way, a high treatment intensity would be adopted if depressed older adults have a higher RMT. This is expected to increase rTMS risk of side effects, which thus requires a slower increase in intensity during RMT determination, as well as more careful observation of tolerability. In another way, older adults tend to have comorbid conditions that require medications, such as hypertension and cerebrovascular disease [12]. A higher RMT and treatment intensity could interact with medication and increase the risk of side effects. Therefore, more careful monitoring of potential side effects is critical for rTMS treatment in older adults. In addition, gender had an interaction with age in RMT, that depressed female adolescents tended to have higher RMT than their male peers [13]. These results highlight the importance of understanding gender differences in RMT in the context of age and other factors.

Although the left DLPFC is mostly recommended for depression treatment, recent studies have confirmed the efficacy of rTMS over the right DLPFC in depression [14, 15]. Two studies indicated that depressed patients showed lower cortical excitability in the left compared to right hemisphere (i.e. a net positive in left minus right RMT) [16, 17]. However, these studies mainly included mid-life depression, and it remains unknown how interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT changes across age cohorts. Identifying interhemispheric asymmetry in depressed individuals could provide insights for the development of rTMS paradigms. For instance, bilateral rTMS treatments over the left and right DLPFC could be optimally designed to reshape interhemispheric asymmetry.

This study was designed to systematically characterise RMT in the context of age, gender, and brain hemispheres in a large sample of depressed individuals (n = 258). We retrospectively reviewed all eligible cases in our centre in a whole year (January 2022 to December 2022), which yielded RMT patterns across age cohorts (12–82 years old). Given the clear antidepressant effects of rTMS, identifying RMT patterns across certain demographic information would inform rTMS treatments for depression.

Method

Participants

This study enrolled participants from the Shandong Mental Health Center between January 2022 and December 2022. The inclusion criteria were: (a) a primary diagnosis of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), confirmed by a trained psychiatrist using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5; (b) willingness to provide written informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: (a) any current or lifetime comorbid psychiatric disorder, including (but not limited to) Bipolar I or II Disorder, any psychotic disorder, or substance use disorder (within the past 6 months); (b) any known neurological disorder (e.g., history of seizure, epilepsy, traumatic brain injury, stroke); (c) any contraindications to rTMS (e.g., metal implants in the head, cardiac pacemaker, cochlear implants); (d) history of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) within the past 6 months; (e) current pregnancy or breastfeeding.

All participants in this study enrolled voluntarily and signed an informed consent form prior to the official initiation of the study. This study (IRB Approval No.: 2023-20) has obtained approval from the Ethics Committee of Shandong Mental Health Center and was conducted in strict compliance with the requirements of the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki).

Participants were classified into four age cohorts according to established neurophysiological classification standards: adolescent (< 18 years), young adult (18–25 years), prime-age adult (26–49 years), and older adult (≥ 50 years) [18].

Experimental procedure

This study investigated the impact of age, gender, and hemisphere on resting motor threshold (RMT). The physicians who performed RMT measurements was blinded to study purpose and did not participate in data analysis. Z.H. was responsible for data analysis who did not participate in data collection.

Prior to RMT determination, participants were instructed to perform repeated cycles of fist clenching followed by immediate relaxation. This active relaxation maneuver helped participants achieve a stable resting state, defined by an EMG activity threshold of less than 10 µV. RMT was measured using a MagNeuro X180 TMS system (Nanjing Vishee Medical Technology Co., Ltd, China) with a figure-of-eight coil. The coil was positioned tangentially to the scalp, with the handle pointing posteriorly and laterally at 45 degrees to the midline to induce current flow perpendicular to the central sulcus. RMT was defined with the traditional relative frequency method, which was the minimal stimulation intensity required to elicit motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) exceeding 50µV in the first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle in at least 5 out of 10 consecutive trials, applied to both the left and right primary motor cortex. A neuronavigation system was also used in RMT (MagNeuro ONE system).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v.25.0 Chicago, Illinois, United States). We implemented a 2 (hemisphere: left, right) * 4 (age groups: <18, 18–25, 26–49, ≥ 50) * 2 (gender: male, female) mixed-design ANOVA to systematically evaluate the main and interaction effects. To control for errors from multiple comparisons, the LSD correction was applied, and a significance level of α < 0.05 was considered.

To investigate potential non-linear associations between age and RMT, we employed Generalized Additive Models (GAMs). Separate models were constructed for each hemisphere, with the natural logarithm of RMT (log(RMT)) serving as the dependent variable to better meet model assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. The effect of age was modelled using a smoothing spline (s(age)). All analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.1) using the mgcvpackage.​.

Supplementary analysis

To assess the influence of symptom severity on the observed RMT effects, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed in a subsample of 73 participants (n = 18, 19, 25, 11 for age < 18, 18–25, 26–49, ≥ 50 years) with available Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) scores. The model included hemisphere (left, right) as a within-subjects factor, and age group and gender as between-subjects factors, with SAS and SDS scores as covariates.

As medications could modulate cortical excitability [19], we further examined the influence of medications on our main findings. A series of 8 medication types were summarised (Table S3) [19]. The presence of significant interactions between several medications and the independent variables violates the assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes required for ANCOVA. Therefore, the analytical strategy was modified. We instead conducted a series of 2 (hemisphere) × 4 (age group) × 2 (gender) × 2 (medication) four-way ANOVAs, treating the medication status (present vs. absent) for each of the eight medication types.

Results

Demographic information

A total of 511 participants were initially screened for eligibility, among whom 269 were diagnosed with depression. After screening, 11 individuals were excluded due to metal implants (n = 2), additional mental health disorders (n = 5), or missing data on RMT (n = 4). In the end, 258 participants (age 12–82 years, 167 females) were included in the analysis.

Effects on RMT

A three-way mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant age × gender × hemisphere interaction effect on RMT (F3, 250 = 3.58, p = 0.02). Post-hoc analysis indicated that prime-age males (M ± SD = 41.13 ± 13.62) had lower RMT than the older adult males (M ± SD = 50.09 ± 10.05, p = 0.036, Cohen’s d = 0.75) in the left hemisphere (see Fig. 1).

(a) Prime-age males had lower left hemisphere RMT than older adult males; (b) Prime-age females had significantly lower right hemisphere RMT than adolescent and older adult females; (c) Among females, left hemisphere RMT was significantly higher than right hemisphere RMT

In females over the right hemisphere, RMT of prime-age adults (M ± SD = 43.35 ± 13.47) was significantly lower than that of those of adolescents (M ± SD = 50.28 ± 13.21, p = 0.019, Cohen’s d = 0.52) and those of old adults (M ± SD = 49.93 ± 17.96, p = 0.031, Cohen’s d = 0.41) (see Fig. 1).

Among young female adults, RMT in the left hemisphere (M ± SD = 49.89 ± 17.54) was significantly higher than that over the right hemisphere (M ± SD = 44.62 ± 11.06, p = 0.033, Cohen’s d = 0.36).

Effects on interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT

As there appeared to have an interhemispheric asymmetry, supplementary analyses were carried out on this possibility. We calculated interhemispheric asymmetry by subtracting the right RMT from the left [20, 21]. Two-way ANOVA (gender, age) was further performed on RMT delta scores. Results showed a significant interaction effect between age and gender (F(3, 250) = 3.58, p = 0.015). Further analysis revealed additional findings (see Fig. 2): among young adults, males (M ± SD=-3.30 ± 11.01) exhibited significantly smaller RMT differences compared to females (M ± SD = 5.27 ± 15.02, p = 0.017, Cohen’s d = 0.65). Within the male group, both young adults (M ± SD=-3.30 ± 11.01, p = 0.029, Cohen’s d = 0.71) and prime-age adults (M ± SD=-2.44 ± 13.65, p = 0.036, Cohen’s d = 0.59) showed significantly smaller RMT differences than adolescents (M ± SD = 6.00 ± 14.90). In contrast, among females, both adolescents (M ± SD=-1.49 ± 8.79, p = 0.028, Cohen’s d = 0.55) and older adults (M ± SD=-1.33 ± 14.63, p = 0.035, Cohen’s d = 0.45) demonstrated significantly smaller RMT differences compared to young adults (M ± SD = 5.27 ± 15.02).

Non-linear relationship between age and RMT

The GAM analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship between age and left hemisphere RMT (Adjusted R² =0.04, edf = 3.54, F = 2.77, p = 0.025). The fitted curve (Fig. 3, orange line) indicated a U-shaped trajectory, where the left RMT decreased from adolescence, reached to a negative peak around 30–40 years of age, and subsequently rebounded in older adulthood.

Bivariate GAM analysis showing the non-linear relationships between Age and log (RMT) for the left (orange line) and right (green line) hemispheres

Similarly, a significant non-linear effect of age was observed for the right hemisphere RMT (Adjusted R² =0.07, edf = 6.76, F = 2.75, p = 0.006). Like the left hemisphere, the developmental trajectory in the right hemisphere also demonstrated a U-shaped with a bit more fluctuations. The RMT values to a negative peak between 40 and 45 years of age before bouncing back in later life (Fig. 3, green line).

In the analyses of symptoms severity, anxiety but not depression, was a covariate in the interaction effect of hemisphere × age × gender on RMT (Table S1). Discussion

Our data demonstrated that, in a large group of depressed individuals, older adults had higher RMT than prime-age adults. The literature has evidenced higher RMT in older adults in healthy populations [9–11, 22]. Our findings extend this evidence to depressed individuals. Moreover, our data further characterised this age effect on RMT, identifying a more prominent effect among male depressed individuals over the left hemisphere with a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.75), as well as in female depressed patients over the right hemisphere. These novel findings help delineate an age effect on corticomotor excitability in depression (potential mechanisms discussed later).

More importantly, our findings demonstrated higher RMT in depressed adolescents compared to prime-age depressed adults. Compared to older adults, the evidence is less consistent in terms of adolescent RMT. One study found that age was associated with higher RMT in depressed individuals. However, most of the participants were depressed adults with only a small number of adolescents [23]. In adolescents, one study found that higher RMT was associated with less pubertal development, indicating a negative relationship between pubertal stage and RMT [24]. However, another study did not demonstrate a linear relationship between age and RMT in depressed adolescents [13]. In a large cohort of depressed individuals, our data confirmed a higher RMT in depressed adolescents (n = 63) compared to prime-age depressed adults (n = 83). Moreover, our findings considered the role of gender and hemisphere, demonstrating a significant effect among female depressed adolescents over the right hemisphere. However, this effect is only small-to-medium in size (Cohen’s d = 0.52). These novel findings help clarify adolescent RMT in depression.

Although age grouping was done based on established neurophysiological classification standards [25], this comes with risks of arbitrary classification and discontinuous modelling. We thus supplemented our findings with other modellings to build up continuous relationships between age and RMT. In both hemispheres, age demonstrated a U-shape relationship with RMT, that RMT decreased from adolescence, reached to a negative peak around prime age (30–40 in left and 40–45 in right hemisphere), and subsequently rebounded in older adulthood. These findings revealed patterns obscured by categorical age-grouping. However, it is acknowledged that there is a lack of healthy controls in this sample. This indicates that our conclusions are not depression-specific, it rather restricts our conclusions to descriptive evidence from a large, depressed cohort.

The age effect on RMT reflect corticomotor excitability across life span. Increased RMT reflects less corticomotor excitability in older adults. This effect is believed to result from reduced synchronization of I-waves or a reduced possibility to recruit later I-waves in the descending volley. I-waves are generated by high frequency and repetitive discharge of corticospinal fibres [26]. From the perspective of brain ageing, ageing-related grey matter reductions and cortical thinning have been reported in several brain regions, including the motor cortex [27–30]. The age effect on RMT may also be caused by the functional change of the motor system, in which the white matter function could be incorporated [31–33]. As RMT is also affected by peripheral neural transmission, alterations in the peripheral nervous system (PNS) may also play a role in increased RMT in older adults [22]. For instance, older adults had lower force of elbow flexion compared to young adults, and lower force of elbow flexion was associated with higher RMT [34]. Meanwhile, in the adolescents, higher RMT largely reflects the development of cortical structures. A lager number of studies have characterised cortical development across life span [35–38]. Later to mature were areas involved in executive function, attention, and motor coordination [39].

Our data also characterised interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT across age cohorts in depressed individuals. In depressed males, interhemispheric asymmetry favouring higher left-side excitability (i.e. a net negative RMT in left minus right) appeared in the young and prime-age adults relative to the adolescent stage. Meanwhile, depressed females demonstrated a distinct interhemispheric asymmetry, with lower left-side excitability in young adults compared to adolescents and older adults. It is worth noting that all these findings had a medium effect size. Moreover, young females had lower left-side excitability compared to their male peers. A few studies demonstrated interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT in depressed individuals, characterised by lower cortical excitability in the left relative to the right hemisphere [16, 17]. Our data further specified this effect, revealing such an abnormality in young female adults. This finding is expected to be associated with biological sex, as young male adults showed a reverse effect in our data. Overall, it is unclear the underlying mechanisms that are associated with this phenomenon.

It is unclear the gender effect on RMT. One study found that females had higher RMT than males in adolescents. This effect was observed in both healthy controls and depressed adolescents [13]. Grey matter volume data also revealed gender difference in cortical development particularly in the adolescent period [38]. However, our data did not support a main effect of gender on RMT in a large sample of depressed individuals. The impact of gender is manifested primarily through its interaction with age and hemisphere.

Our findings may have implications for rTMS studies and clinical treatment. We provided the first portrait of RMT development in depressed individuals across age cohorts. Our data revealed lower RMT in adolescents and older adults compared to prime-age depressed adults. In one way, these findings help understand the process of cortical development. In another way, they support to use higher intensity in rTMS studies and treatments for depressed adolescents and older adults. However, a higher stimulation intensity in rTMS would increase the risks of side effect for depressed adolescents and older adults. This requires a slower increase in intensity during RMT determination and careful observations of tolerability. Moreover, careful monitoring of potential side effects is critical as high intensity rTMS could interact with medications to increase the risks of side effect. Moreover, our data revealed an interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT mainly in young female adults with depression. This finding advocates the development of rTMS paradigms to shape interhemispheric balance in cortical excitability. For instance, bilateral rTMS treatments over the left and right DLPFC could be specifically designed for this population [40–42]. We also explored the impact of potential covariates. Results indicated a confounding role of anxiety (but not depression score) and 5-HT 1A_AG drugs in the interaction effect of hemisphere × age × gender on RMT. These two covariates warrant attention in future investigations.

There were some limitations in this study. This was a retrospective study. Although we included consecutive cases with complete information on gender, age, and bilateral RMT, there is chance that some patients were not included into the analysis due to loss of information. The retrospective study also limits in-depth analyses of the role of medications and scalp-to-cortex distance, which may have an impact on our findings. We specifically focused on RMT in this study, which did not provide information on other cortical measures, such as cortical silent period [43–45], intracortical inhibition [46–48], or intracortical facilitation [49, 50]. This study evaluated the impact of certain critical demographics on RMT. However, our data did not allow to examine the influence of other factors, such as disease duration, severity, scalp-to-cortex distance [51, 52]. We acknowledge that treating depression as a single entity is a limitation and that potential confounds were not fully characterized or controlled, such as clinical subtypes, recurrence status, and co-occurring anxiety. In addition, there was a lack of control for pubertal stage within the adolescent cohort. This was a single-centre study. Our findings may be subject to institutional biases and multi-centre replication is required to validate these findings.

In conclusion, this study provided the first portrait of RMT development in depressed individuals across cohorts. Older depressed adults had higher RMT than depressed prime-age adults. Depressed adolescents also demonstrated higher RMT than prime-age depressed adults, but with a most prominent effect in depressed females. Interhemispheric asymmetry in RMT was observed in young female depressed adults, with a higher RMT in the left versus right hemisphere. Due to is a lack of healthy controls, our conclusions are not depression-specific, but rather descriptive evidence from a large, depressed cohort.
