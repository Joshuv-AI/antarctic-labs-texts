Green function rigidity and the mass of hypersurfaces under inversion
Xuezhang Chen, Jiaxue Gan and Yalong Shi

Abstract
This is a sequel to [ 6 ] . We prove the Green function rigidity conjecture in [ 6 ] for conformal Laplacian in dimension n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 . For the Paneitz operator, we prove the Green function rigidity conjecture when n ≠ 4 ​ k + 2 , k ≥ 2 n\neq 4k+2,k\geq 2 . Important ingredients in our proof are the positive mass theorem and the positive energy theorem for Paneitz operator. As a byproduct, we also obtain a new formula for the ADM mass of an asymptotically flat hypersurface that allows for a non-entire graph. MSC2020: 35J08, 53C18, 53C40, 53C21.

Introduction

A complete Riemannian manifold
(N^{n},g)
is called asymptotically flat of asymptotic order
\tau>0
and regularity order
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, if there exists a compact set
K\subset N
such that
N\setminus K
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash B_{R}(0)
for some
R>0
, and under this coordinate chart,
g-\delta\in O_{\ell}(|y|^{-\tau})
as
|y|\to\infty
. The coordinates
y
are called asymptotic coordinates. Here,
g-\delta\in O_{\ell}(|y|^{-\tau})
means that

|\partial^{k}(g_{\alpha\beta}-\delta_{\alpha\beta})|=O(|y|^{-\tau-k}),\quad\forall~0\leq k\leq\ell.

In the literature, the regularity order
\ell
is usually chosen to be
2
. Accordingly, when we go to study the fourth order energy for the Paneitz operator, the regularity order requires to be
4
.

The ADM mass of an asymptotically flat (AF) manifold
(N^{n},g)
was defined by Arnowitt-Deser-Misner
[
1
]
in the 1960s in the context of general relativity:

m(g):=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{t}^{n-1}}g^{\beta\gamma}(\partial_{\gamma}g_{\alpha\beta}-\partial_{\alpha}g_{\beta\gamma})\nu^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}\sigma

(1.1)

if the limit exists. Here,
\nu
is the outward unit normal vector field on the sphere
\mathbb{S}_{t}^{n-1}:=\{|y|=t\}
with respect to the Euclidean metric. In
[
3
]
, Bartnik proved that when the asymptotic order
\tau>\frac{n-2}{2}
, regularity order
\ell=2
and the scalar curvature is integrable, the mass for AF manifold is independent of the choice of asymptotic coordinates and is thus a geometric invariant for
(N,g)
. See Lee-Parker
[
18
]
for a good introduction to the basics of ADM mass.

In
[
27
,
26
]
, R. Schoen and S.T. Yau proved the Positive Mass Theorem (PMT):
Assume that
(N^{n},g),3\leq n\leq 7
is an asymptotically flat manifold of asymptotic order
\tau>\frac{n-2}{2}
(regularity order at least 2), and
0\leq R_{g}\in L^{1}(N,g)
. Then the mass
m(g)\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
(N,g)
is isometric to the flat
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

When
N
is a spin manifold, an alternative proof is given by E. Witten
[
29
]
in dimension 3 (see also
[
23
]
), which works for all dimension under the spin assumption, see
[
3
]
.

The Positive Mass Theorem plays an important role in the study of first and third named authors
[
6
]
of the Green function for the conformal Laplacian on closed Euclidean hypersurfaces. Based on the belief that the explicit representation formula of the Green function for certain conformally invariant linear (pseudo-)differential operator should determine the geometry and topology of the manifold, the authors
[
6
]
raised the following
Green Function Rigidity Conjecture (
GFRC
)
.

Conjecture
.

Let
M^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a closed embedded hypersurface with induced metric
g
. Assume that for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the Green function
G
for the GJMS operator
P_{2k}^{g}
exists. Suppose one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

2k=n
and
for some

Q\in M
,
G(\cdot,Q)
is of the form
-c_{n}\log\|\cdot-Q\|+c
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
c_{n}=\frac{2}{(n-1)!|\mathbb{S}^{n}|}
;

(2)

2k<n
or
2k>n
when
n
is odd, and
for some

Q\in M
,
G(\cdot,Q)
is of the form
c_{n,k}\|\cdot-Q\|^{2k-n}
, where
c_{n,k}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-k)}{2^{2k}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma(k)}
.

Then
(M,g)
is a round sphere. Here and throughout, let
\|\cdot\|
denote the Euclidean norm.

Recall that the GJMS operator
P_{2k}^{g}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
on a Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
is a conformally invariant linear differential operator whose principal part agrees with
(-\Delta_{g})^{k}
, named after Graham, Jenne, Mason and Sparling
[
8
]
. When
k=1
,
P_{2}^{g}
is precisely the conformal Laplacian:

P_{2}^{g}=-\Delta_{g}+\frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}R_{g},\quad n\geq 2.

When
n=2
, it reduces to the Laplacian.

The operator
P_{4}^{g}
was discovered earlier by Paneitz
[
21
]
in
1983
on a smooth Riemmanian manifold
(M^{n},g),n\geq 3
, and hence also referred to as ‘Paneitz operator’:

P_{4}^{g}=\Delta^{2}_{g}+\frac{4}{n-2}{\rm div}\big(\sum_{\alpha}\mathrm{Ric}(\nabla^{g}\cdot,e_{\alpha})e_{\alpha}\big)-\frac{n^{2}-4n+8}{2(n-1)(n-2)}{\rm div}(R_{g}\nabla^{g}\cdot)+\frac{n-4}{2}Q_{g},

where
Q_{g}
is Branson’s
Q
-curvature, defined by

Q_{g}=-\Delta_{g}\sigma_{1}(A_{g})+4\sigma_{2}(A_{g})+\frac{n-4}{2}\sigma_{1}(A_{g})^{2}.

(1.2)

Here

A_{g}=\frac{1}{n-2}\Big(\mathrm{Ric}_{g}-\frac{R_{g}}{2(n-1)}g\Big)

is the Schouten tensor and
\sigma_{k}(A_{g})
is the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of
g^{-1}A_{g}
.

For more about the GJMS operators and their applications, readers are referred to
[
4
]
for a recent survey.

For the conformal Laplacian and Paneitz operator, the authors
[
6
]
carried out the first step towards the above conjecture. For example, they proved that the pole
Q
of Green function must be an umbilical point, and succeeded in verifying
GFRC
for the conformal Laplacian in dimensions
n=3,4,5
.

The proof of
GFRC
for the conformal Laplacian in
[
6
]
goes as follows. First, the familiar inverted coordinates happen to provide asymptotic coordinates of order
2
for
(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
, where
\hat{g}=G(\cdot,Q)^{\frac{4}{n-2}}g
, such that when
n=3,4,5
, the mass for
(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
is well defined. Then a direct computation yields that the mass of
(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
vanishes. Since the scalar curvature of
\hat{g}
is zero, the Positive Mass Theorem guarantees that
(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
is isometric to the flat Euclidean space. Observe that
\hat{g}=(c_{n,2})^{\frac{4}{n-2}}\|\cdot-Q\|^{-4}g
is essentially the induced metric of
I_{Q}(M\backslash\{Q\})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, where

I_{Q}(z)=Q+\frac{z-Q}{\|z-Q\|^{2}},\quad\forall~z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\backslash\{Q\}

(1.3)

is the inversion of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with respect to
Q
. Thus
I_{Q}(M\backslash\{Q\})
is a complete flat hypersurface. Hartman-Nirenberg’s theorem in
[
13
]
states that this hypersurface must be a generalized cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. This gives an almost explicit parametrization of
I_{Q}(M\backslash\{Q\})
, hence also of
M\backslash\{Q\}
. Combining this with the fact that
Q
is umbilical forces such a generalized cylinder to be a hyperplane. Hence,
(M,g)
is a round sphere.

In this paper, we extend the above result to all dimensions
n\geq 3
.

Theorem 1
.

GFRC
is true for the conformal Laplacian in all dimensions
n\geq 3
.

Very recently, Lai and Zhang
[
16
]
proved the
GFRC
for the Laplacian on surfaces using a different approach. Hence, the
GFRC
is now completely resolved for
P_{2}^{g}
in all dimensions
n\geq 2
.

The general strategy for proving Theorem
1
is similar to that of
[
6
]
. However, there are serious difficulties in higher dimensions.

•

We need to construct asymptotic coordinate system for all dimensions of order
\tau>\frac{n-2}{2}
. The inversion of the usual geodesic normal coordinate is far from enough.

•

Accordingly, to prove the vanishing of mass, we need much more computations even though following the same strategy. Some exceptional phenomenon occurs in dimensions
n=4k,k\geq 2
.

The key idea for solving these problems is to study the contact order of
M
with its osculating sphere at
Q
, with which we construct the
adapted
asymptotic coordinate system for all dimensions. Here for an umbilical point
Q
of
M
with principal curvature
\lambda
, the osculating sphere of
M
at
Q
is the sphere of radius
\frac{1}{|\lambda|}
that tangent to
M
at
Q
with the same outer normal vector. To this end, we are able to prove a general theorem for hypersurfaces, which should be of independent interest.

Theorem 2
.

Let
M^{n},n\geq 3
be a closed embedded hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with induced metric
g
. Assume that
Q\in M
is umbilical. Write
\rho:=\|\cdot-Q\|^{2}
, and set
\hat{g}:=\rho^{-2}g
. Then the following are true.

(1)

If
n=3,4,5
, then
R_{\hat{g}}\in L^{1}(M,\hat{g})
;

(2)

if
n\geq 6
and
R_{\hat{g}}\in L^{1}(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
, or
n=3,4,5
, then there exists an asymptotic coordinate system such that when
4\nmid n
,

(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is asymptotically flat of order
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1
; when
4\mid n
,
(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is asymptotically flat of order
\frac{n}{2}
.

Furthermore, the ADM mass
m(\hat{g})
of
(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is non-negative, and
m(\hat{g})=0
except for
n=4k,k\geq 2
.

Remark 1
.

The integrability of
R_{\hat{g}}
ensures that the contact order of
M
with its osculating sphere at
Q
is at least
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1
(resp.
\frac{n}{2}
) when
4\nmid n
(resp.
4\mid n
). As we shall show, similar phenomena also occur for the
Q
-curvature.

Remark 2
.

When
n=4k,k\geq 2
, there are indeed examples whose mass is strictly positive under the same assumptions of Theorem
2
; see Example
1
.

Thanks to Theorem
2
, the same argument of
[
6
]
applies to establish Theorem
1
when
4\nmid n
. However, to cope with the
4\mid n
case, a direct consequence of Example
1
is that the local analysis in the proof of Theorem
2
fails. At this point, we instead need a new mass formula for asymptotically flat hypersurfaces, which was once discovered by Lam in his thesis
[
17
]
for entire graphs.

Theorem 3
.

Let
N
be a complete non-compact hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with induced metric
g
. Assume that there is a compact subset
K\subset N
such that
N\setminus K
is the graph of a smooth function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}(0)\to\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

|\partial_{\alpha}\psi|+|y||\partial^{2}_{\alpha\beta}\psi|+|y|^{2}|\partial^{3}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\psi|=O(|y|^{-\frac{\tau}{2}}),

with
\tau>\frac{n-2}{2}
. Then there is a global tangent vector field
X
on
N
such that
{\rm div}_{g}X=\langle e_{n+1},\mathbb{n}\rangle R_{g}
where
\mathbb{n}
is the unit normal vector field of
N
such that
\langle e_{n+1},\mathbb{n}\rangle>0
on the end
N\setminus K
for
|y|\gg 1
, and that

m(g)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\int_{N}{\rm div}_{g}X\mathrm{d}V_{g}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\int_{N}\langle e_{n+1},\mathbb{n}\rangle R_{g}\mathrm{d}V_{g}.

In particular, if
R_{g}\equiv 0
, then
m(g)=0
.

Since the metric
\hat{g}
in the proof of Theorem
1
has zero scalar curvature, the above theorem in fact implies that the mass automatically vanishes. Hence, this concludes the proof of Theorem
1
using the same strategy as in
[
6
]
.

We proceed to study the
GFRC
for the Paneitz operator
P_{4}^{g}
. In parallel to Theorem
1
, the
Q
-curvature version of the positive energy theorem in
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
2
]
plays a key role.

In the past decade, some great advances have been made in the constant
Q
-curvature problem on a closed manifold
(M^{n},g)
. For
n\geq 5
M. Gursky and A. Malchiodi
[
9
]
proposed a sufficient condition of
\mathrm{ker}P_{4}^{g}=\{0\}
that
R_{g}\geq 0
and
Q_{g}\gneqq 0
, which leads to the existence of conformal metrics with positive constant
Q
-curvature. Subsequently, Hang and Yang
[
12
, Proposition 1.1]
improved the sufficient condition to the hypothesis that
Y(M,[g])>0
and
Q_{g}\gneqq 0
. The most general existence result for
P_{4}^{g}
and
n\geq 6
was proved by Gursky, Hang and Lin
[
7
]
. For
n=3
, Hang and Yang
[
11
]
proved a PMT theorem associated to the Paneitz operator.

In a recent paper
[
2
]
, on an asymptotically flat manifold
(N^{n},g),n\geq 5
of order
\tau>\frac{n-4}{2}
and regularity order
\ell=4
, R. Avalos, P. Laurain and J. Lira introduced a fourth-order mass (called “energy” by the authors) by

\displaystyle\mathsf{m}_{4}(g)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{t}^{n-1}}(\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}g_{\alpha\alpha}-\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\gamma}\partial_{\alpha}g_{\alpha\gamma})\nu^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}_{t}^{n-1}},

which had been defined earlier by B. Michel
[
20
]
for higher-order GJMS operators.
Furthermore, the authors
[
2
]
established a positive energy theorem; see Section
5
for precise statement.

Next we establish a
Q
-curvature analogue to Theorem
2
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
M^{n},n\geq 5
be a closed embedded hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with induced metric
g
. Assume that
Q\in M
is umbilical. Write
\rho:=\|\cdot-Q\|^{2}
and set
\hat{g}:=\rho^{-2}g
. Then the following are true.

(1)

If
n=5,6,7
, then
Q_{\hat{g}}\in L^{1}(M,\hat{g})
, and
(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is asymptotically flat of order
3
;

(2)

if
n\geq 8
and
Q_{\hat{g}}\in L^{1}(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
, there exists an asymptotic coordinate system such that when when
n\neq 4j+2,j\geq 2
,

(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is asymptotically flat of order
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
; when
n=4j+2,j\geq 2
,
(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is asymptotically flat of order
\frac{n}{2}-1
.

Furthermore, the fourth-order energy
\mathsf{m}_{4}(\hat{g})
of
(M\setminus\{Q\},\hat{g})
is nonnegative, and
\mathsf{m}_{4}(\hat{g})=0
except for
n=4j+2,j\geq 2
.

Using the same strategy as the conformal Laplacian case, we make elaborate efforts to establish the following Green function rigidity for the Paneitz operator.

Theorem 5
.

For
n\geq 5
and
n\neq 4j+2,j\geq 2
, let
(M^{n},g)
be a closed embedded hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that the Yamabe constant

. Then

is true for the Paneitz operator

. When

, under the same assumption,
(M^{3},g)
is globally conformal to
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

At present, we have to exclude the case

in the statement of Theorem

, since the vanishing of

in such dimensions remains open. As in Theorem

, a global analysis together with the fact

2
Integrability of scalar curvature

Let
M
be an embedded hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with induced metric
g
, and
Q\in M
be an umbilical point. Without loss of generality, we may assume
Q=0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Define
\rho:=\|P-Q\|^{2}=\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}\rangle
, and
\eta:=\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{n}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{r}
is the position vector of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
\mathbf{n}
is the inward unit normal vector field. Given any point
P\in M
, we choose an orthonormal frame of
M
,
\{e_{\alpha};1\leq\alpha\leq n\}
. Then we have
\nabla_{g}\rho=2\sum_{\alpha}\langle\mathbf{r},e_{\alpha}\rangle e_{\alpha}=2(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\perp})=2\mathbf{r}-2\eta\mathbf{n}
and consequently

|\nabla_{g}\rho|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha}4\langle\mathbf{r},e_{\alpha}\rangle^{2}=4\|\mathbf{r}\|^{2}-4\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{n}\rangle^{2}=4\rho-4\eta^{2}.

Also, at
P
we have

\nabla_{g}^{2}\rho(e_{\alpha},e_{\beta})=\langle\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}^{g}(\nabla_{g}\rho),e_{\beta}\rangle=2\delta_{\alpha\beta}+2\eta II(e_{\alpha},e_{\beta}),

where
II(e_{\alpha},e_{\beta})=-\langle\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}\mathbf{n},e_{\beta}\rangle
is the second fundamental form. As a consequence,

\Delta_{g}\rho=2n+2\eta H.

To sum up, in any coordinate system we have

|\nabla_{g}\rho|^{2}=4\rho-4\eta^{2},\quad\rho_{,\alpha\beta}=2g_{\alpha\beta}+2\eta h_{\alpha\beta},\quad\Delta_{g}\rho=2n+2\eta H.

(2.1)

Locally
M
is given as the graph of a smooth function
f:B_{\delta}(0)\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(0)=0
and
\nabla f(0)=0
. Here,
\nabla
means the gradient with respect to the flat metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then under the coordinate chart
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
, the induced metric on
M
is
g_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+f_{\alpha}f_{\beta}
, where
f_{\alpha}
means
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{\alpha}}
. It is straightforward that
\rho=|x|^{2}+f^{2}(x)

and

\eta=\frac{f-x\cdot\nabla f}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla f|^{2}}}.

Also, it is direct to check that the second fundamental form is

II=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}x_{\beta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla f|^{2}}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}x_{\beta}

and the mean curvature is

H=\mathrm{tr}_{g}(II)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla f|^{2}}}g^{\alpha\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}.

At
Q
, we have

g_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}=0,\quad h_{\alpha\beta}=f_{\alpha\beta}(0),\quad\mathrm{~~and~~}\ H=\Delta f(0),

where
\Delta
denotes the Euclidean Laplacian. In the following,
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
will always be the above graph coordinate system.

It is well known that if the order
\tau
of an asymptotically flat
n
-manifold is
>\frac{n-2}{2}
, and the scalar curvature is
L^{1}
, then the ADM mass is well defined and independent of the asymptotic coordinates (cf.
[
3
]
). Now we are ready to study the integrability of the scalar curvature. Since the integral of the scalar curvature is geometric and then coordinate free, we prefer to work under the coordinate system
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
.

Under the conformal change of metric
\hat{g}:=\rho^{-2}g
, the scalar curvature transforms by

\displaystyle R_{\hat{g}}=

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\Big(R_{g}+2(n-1)\frac{\Delta_{g}\rho}{\rho}-n(n-1)\frac{|\nabla^{g}\rho|^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\Big(R_{g}+4(n-1)H\frac{\eta}{\rho}+4n(n-1)\frac{\eta^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\Big),

(2.2)

where we used (
2.1
) in the last equality. Fix an umbilical point
Q\in M
. Then in a punctured neighborhood of
Q
, we have

R_{\hat{g}}\mathrm{d}V_{\hat{g}}=\rho^{2-n}\Big(R_{g}+4(n-1)H\frac{\eta}{\rho}+4n(n-1)\frac{\eta^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\Big)\mathrm{d}V_{g}.

(2.3)

Notice that
R_{g}=H^{2}-|II|^{2}
by Gauss equation. Clearing a common nonzero factor
(1+|\nabla f|^{2})^{-1}
in (
2.2
), we only need to expand

\mathcal{R}:=4n(n-1)\Big(\frac{f-x\cdot\nabla f}{\rho}\Big)^{2}+4(n-1)g^{\alpha\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}\frac{f-x\cdot\nabla f}{\rho}+(g^{\alpha\beta}f_{\alpha\beta})^{2}-g^{\alpha\mu}g^{\beta\nu}f_{\alpha\beta}f_{\mu\nu}

around
Q
. For brevity, let
B
denote a matrix
(B^{\alpha}_{\nu})
with the element
B^{\alpha}_{\nu}:=g^{\alpha\beta}f_{\beta\nu}
, then the above equation becomes

\mathcal{R}=4n(n-1)\Big(\frac{f-x\cdot\nabla f}{\rho}+\frac{1}{2n}\text{tr}B\Big)^{2}-\text{tr}\Big(\mathring{B}^{2}\Big),

(2.4)

where
\mathring{B}^{\alpha}_{\nu}=B^{\alpha}_{\nu}-\frac{\text{tr}B}{n}\delta^{\alpha}_{\nu}
is the trace-free part of
B
.

Proposition 1
.

There hold
\mathcal{R}=O(|x|^{2})
and
R_{\hat{g}}=O(|x|^{6})
near
Q
.

Proof.

From the assumption that
Q
is umbilical, near
Q
we write

f(x)=\frac{H(Q)}{2n}|x|^{2}+A_{3}(x)+O(|x|^{4}),

where
A_{3}(x)
denotes a homogeneous polynomial of degree
3
. Set
r:=|x|,A_{3}(x)=r^{3}A_{3}(\theta)
and
\Delta_{\theta}:=\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
, where
\theta=x|x|^{-1}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Short
H
for
H(Q)
, and
A_{3}
for
A_{3}(x)
. Then

x\cdot\nabla f=\frac{H}{n}r^{2}+3A_{3}(\theta)r^{3}+O(r^{4})

and thereby

f-x\cdot\nabla f=-\frac{H}{2n}r^{2}-2A_{3}(\theta)r^{3}+O(r^{4}).

On the other hand, we have

\rho=|x|^{2}+f^{2}(x)=r^{2}\Big(1+\frac{H^{2}}{4n^{2}}r^{2}+O(r^{3})\Big).

Putting the above two equations together yields

\frac{f-x\cdot\nabla f}{\rho}=-\frac{H}{2n}-2A_{3}(\theta)r+O(r^{2}).

Observe that

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}^{\nu}=

\displaystyle g^{\alpha\mu}f_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\delta^{\alpha\mu}-\frac{f_{\alpha}f_{\mu}}{1+|\nabla f|^{2}}\Big)f_{\mu\nu}=\Big(\delta^{\alpha\mu}-f_{\alpha}f_{\mu}\Big)f_{\mu\nu}+O(r^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\delta^{\alpha\mu}-\frac{H^{2}}{n^{2}}x_{\alpha}x_{\mu}+O(r^{3})\Big)\cdot\Big(\frac{H}{n}\delta_{\mu\nu}+\partial^{2}_{\mu\nu}A_{3}+O(r^{2})\Big)+O(r^{2})

\displaystyle\frac{H}{n}\delta^{\alpha}_{\nu}+\partial^{2}_{\alpha\nu}A_{3}+O(r^{2}),

\displaystyle\text{tr}B=g^{\alpha\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}=

\displaystyle H+\Delta A_{3}+O(r^{2})

\displaystyle H+\Big(3(n+1)A_{3}(\theta)+\Delta_{\theta}A_{3}(\theta)\Big)r+O(r^{2}).

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}A_{3}|^{2}-\frac{(\Delta A_{3})^{2}}{n},

\displaystyle\Big(-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2n}\Big)\Delta_{\theta}A_{3}(\theta))r+O(r^{2}).

Hence, we obtain
\mathcal{R}=O(r^{2})
and
R_{\hat{g}}=O(r^{6})
by (

) and (

).

Lemma 1

Let

Proposition 2

\displaystyle\text{tr}B=g^{\alpha\beta}f_{\alpha\beta}={}

\displaystyle\frac{n\lambda}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}r^{2}}}+\Delta A+O(r^{k-1})

3
Adapted asymptotic coordinates and the ADM mass

Throughout this section, we let
\lambda=\frac{H(Q)}{n}
and fix
m=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
as before.

The following elementary lemma demonstrates that for all
n\geq 3
, there exists an
adapted
asymptotic coordinate system for
(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
such that the mass
m(\hat{g})
is well defined thanks to Proposition
2
.

Lemma 3
.

If
f(x)=\phi(x)+O(|x|^{k})
for some integer
k\geq 3
, then there is an adapted asymptotic coordinate system

y=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{x}{\phi(x)}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}|x|^{2}}}{2}\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}

(3.1)

such that
(M\backslash\{Q\},\hat{g})
is asymptotically flat of order
k
.

Proof.

Let
r=|x|
and
t=|y|
.
A direct computation together with (
2.5
) yields

\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}+|y|^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\frac{|x|^{2}}{\phi^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{1}{\phi(x)}.

This gives

\phi(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{1}{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}+|y|^{2}}

and the inverse of the above asymptotic coordinate map is

x=\frac{y}{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}+|y|^{2}}.

(3.2)

In terms of the variable
y
, we can express

f(y)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{1}{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}+|y|^{2}}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}(y)+O(|y|^{2-2k}),

where
\widetilde{A}_{i}(y)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
-i
.

Remark 3
.

Since
f
is smooth by assumption and the coordinate change is explicit, it is evident that the
O(|y|^{2-2k})
term behaves nice when taking derivatives, and is in fact
O_{l}(|y|^{2-2k})
for any
l
by a direct mathematical induction argument. This fact will be frequently used later.

Notice that

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=r^{2}+f^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\frac{1}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{-2k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}\big(1+\lambda\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{2-2k})\big).

So,

\rho^{-2}=(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}\big(1-2\lambda\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{2-2k})\big).

In terms of the new variable
y
we obtain

\displaystyle g=

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}+\mathrm{d}f\otimes\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{y_{\alpha}}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}\right)\otimes\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{y_{\alpha}}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{1}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}(y)+O(t^{2-2k})\right)\otimes\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{1}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}+\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}(y)+O(t^{2-2k})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}-\frac{2y_{\alpha}t\mathrm{d}t}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}}\right)\otimes\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}}{t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}}-\frac{2y_{\alpha}t\mathrm{d}t}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\frac{4t^{2}\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}t}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{4}}-\frac{\lambda t}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda t}{(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{2}}\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)\otimes\mathrm{d}t+O(t^{-2k-2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(t^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4})^{-2}\bigg(\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}-\lambda t\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)-\lambda t\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)\otimes\mathrm{d}t+O(t^{2-2k})\bigg).

Then we have

\displaystyle\hat{g}=

\displaystyle\rho^{-2}(\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}x_{\alpha}+\mathrm{d}f\otimes\mathrm{d}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(1-2\lambda\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{2-2k})\big)

\displaystyle\cdot\bigg(\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}-\lambda t\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)-\lambda t\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)\otimes\mathrm{d}t+O(t^{2-2k})\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(1-2\lambda\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\bigg)\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}y_{\alpha}-\lambda t\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)

\displaystyle-\lambda t\mathrm{d}\big(\sum_{i=k}^{2k-3}\widetilde{A}_{i}\big)\otimes\mathrm{d}t+O(t^{2-2k}).

This directly yields

\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\hat{h}_{\alpha\beta}

with
\hat{h}_{\alpha\beta}=O(t^{-k})
,
\partial\hat{h}_{\alpha\beta}=O(t^{-k-1})
and
\partial^{2}\hat{h}_{\alpha\beta}=O(t^{-k-2})
. Hence
y
is an asymptotically flat coordinate system of order
k
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Proposition
2
, we have
f(x)=\phi(x)+O(r^{m+1})
when
4\nmid n
. By Lemma
3
, we know that
y
is an asymptotic coordinate system of order
m+1
.

Since
m+1=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1>\frac{n-2}{2}
, the ADM mass is well defined. Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\hat{g}_{\alpha\alpha}=

\displaystyle n\bigg(1-2\lambda\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}\widetilde{A}_{i}\bigg)+2\lambda\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}i\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{-2m}),

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{tt}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}y_{\alpha}y_{\beta}t^{-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(1-2\lambda\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}\widetilde{A}_{i}\bigg)+2\lambda\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}i\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{-2m}).

Subsequently, in view of Remark

,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\hat{g}_{tt}-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{g}_{\alpha\alpha})

\displaystyle=-(n-1)\partial_{t}\bigg(1-2\lambda\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}\widetilde{A}_{i}\bigg)+O(t^{-2m-1})

\displaystyle=-2(n-1)\lambda t^{-1}\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}i\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{-2m-1}),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}(n\hat{g}_{tt}-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{g}_{\alpha\alpha})

\displaystyle=2(n-1)\lambda t^{-1}\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m-1}i\widetilde{A}_{i}+O(t^{-2m-1}).

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle m(\hat{g})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{|y|=t}\partial_{t}(\hat{g}_{tt}-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{g}_{\alpha\alpha})+\frac{1}{t}(n\hat{g}_{tt}-\sum_{\alpha}\hat{g}_{\alpha\alpha})\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{|y|=t}O(t^{-2m-1})\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}O(t^{n-2m-2})\mathrm{d}\sigma=0.

For the

∎

Lemma 4
.

When

. Moreover, the mass of

Proposition 3

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\nabla^{g}_{e_{\alpha}}v

\displaystyle=\Big(-e_{\alpha}\langle\mathbb{e},\mathbb{n}\rangle\mathbb{n}-\langle\mathbb{e},\mathbb{n}\rangle\nabla_{e_{\alpha}}\mathbb{n}\Big)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbb{e},\mathbb{n}\rangle h_{\alpha\beta}e_{\beta}.

This follows that

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{g}X

Lemma 5

be an embedded closed hypersurface, and

Proof.

Regarding the isometry as the identity map and
Q
as the origin, we obtain a smooth embedding
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that

g=F^{*}g_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}=|F|^{4}g_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\qquad\mathrm{~~in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{n}

with the normalization that
\lim_{|y|\to\infty}F(y)=0
. Consider an inverted hypersurface

\displaystyle w:=U(y)=I_{Q}\circ F(y),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A nice observation

U^{\ast}g_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}=F^{\ast}I_{Q}^{\ast}g_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}=F^{\ast}(|w|^{-4}|\mathrm{d}w|^{2})=|F(y)|^{-4}g=|\mathrm{d}y|^{2}

implies that

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Using Hartman-Nirenberg’s characterization in
[
13
]
of complete flat hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we know that
(I_{Q}(M\setminus\{Q\}),\hat{g})
is a generalized cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and can be parametrized as

U(t,z)=(x(t),y(t),z),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},~~z\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1},

where
t\mapsto(x(t),y(t))
is a regular plane curve parametrized by arc length

. Consequently,

can be parametrized by

F(t,z)=\frac{(x(t),y(t),z)}{x(t)^{2}+y(t)^{2}+|z|^{2}}.

Using this special parametrization of

\lambda=-2(xy^{\prime}-x^{\prime}y)

, and the other one is

.

Since

must be a hyperplane. As a consequence,

is asymptotically flat of order
\tau=n-4
. So, in both cases
\mathsf{m}_{4}(\hat{g})
is well defined.

By definition of the fourth-order energy we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{m}_{4}(\hat{g})=-\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{t}^{n-1}}\partial_{t}R_{\hat{g}}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathbb{S}_{t}^{n-1}}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}r^{3-n}\partial_{r}R_{\hat{g}}\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(B.1)

Write
\hat{g}=G^{\frac{4}{n-4}}g=(G^{\frac{n-2}{n-4}})^{\frac{4}{n-2}}g
. Then the scalar curvature equation gives

R_{\hat{g}}=G^{-\frac{n+2}{n-4}}\left(-\frac{4(n-1)}{n-2}\Delta_{g}G^{\frac{n-2}{n-4}}+R_{g}G^{\frac{n-2}{n-4}}\right).

For any radial function
u=u(r)
we have

\Delta_{g}u=\Delta u+O(r^{N-1})u^{\prime},

where
\Delta=\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{n-1}{r}\partial_{r}
. Observe that

\Delta_{g}G^{\frac{n-2}{n-4}}=\frac{n-2}{n-4}r^{1-n}\partial_{r}(r^{n-1}G^{\frac{2}{n-4}}G^{\prime})+O(r^{N-1})G^{\frac{2}{n-4}}|G^{\prime}|=-2(n-2)\mathsf{A}r^{-4}+O(r^{-3}).

This follows that

-\frac{4(n-1)}{n-2}G^{-\frac{n+2}{n-4}}\Delta_{g}G^{\frac{n-2}{n-4}}=8(n-1)\mathsf{A}r^{n-2}+O(r^{n-1}).

Also we obtain

R_{g}G^{-\frac{4}{n-4}}=O(r^{6}).

Hence, inserting these estimates into (
B.1
) yields
\mathsf{m}_{4}(\hat{g})=8(n-1)(n-2)\mathsf{A}
.
∎
