Prenatal diagnosis of Apert syndrome caused by a de novo FGFR2 mutation in the second trimester: a case report
Authors: Xiaoying Chen, Pengzhen Jin, Min Chen, Juan Zuo, Jie Liu, Jin Zhu, Miaochun Lin, Zhaohui Li, Minyue Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApert syndrome is a syndromic craniosynostosis primarily caused by pathogenic mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) gene. Current prenatal diagnosis of Apert syndrome predominantly relies on ultrasound imaging in the third trimester (28 weeks of gestation to delivery), which is not conducive to early clinical decision-making.Case presentationIn this case, prenatal ultrasound at the 20 weeks of gestation revealed craniosynostosis, oligohydramnios, and syndactyly of hands and feet in the fetus. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) followed by Sanger sequencing was performed on the affected fetus and its parents. A de novo pathogenic mutation in the FGFR2 gene was identified. Based on the fetal ultrasound findings and genetic test results, Apert syndrome was diagnosed as early as the 20 weeks of gestation (second trimester).ConclusionsThe combination of prenatal ultrasound and WES enables the diagnosis of Apert syndrome in the second trimester, providing valuable support for early prenatal counseling and pregnancy management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09282-z.

Background

Craniosynostosis is defined as the premature fusion of the cranial sutures, with an incidence of 1 per 2000 births [1, 2]. The abnormal fusion of cranial sutures greatly restricts the growth of brain, leading to compensatory overgrowth adjacent to other sutures and therefore, resulting in midfacial dysplasia [3]. Typically, it is sporadic without specific pathogenic causes. To date, approximately 20% of craniosynostosis is identified with genetic causes. Craniosynostosis is one of the most common craniofacial malformations, which is generally classified into syndromic craniosynostosis and non-syndromic (isolated) craniosynostosis. Non-syndromic craniosynostosis refers to isolated premature fusion of cranial sutures without other associated abnormalities. In contrast, syndromic craniosynostosis is defined as premature closure of cranial sutures accompanied by concurrent malformations of the face and extremities [1, 2, 4].

Apert syndrome is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder and a common type of syndromic craniosynostosis. With an incidence of 1 per 6500 births, it is characterized by clinical manifestations of malformations involving the skull, face, hands, and feet. Due to premature fusion of the coronal sutures, the cranial deformity presents as brachycephaly, which is often accompanied by syndactyly of the fingers and toes [5–7]. Suffering from different degrees of intellectual disability, Apert syndrome requires lifelong multidisciplinary management, placing a heavy burden on the family [5, 8]. However, early diagnosis of this disease is of vital significance for preventing the birth of fetuses with congenital anomalies. Due to the difficulty in fully visualizing craniosynostosis on early or mid-trimester ultrasound and the high risk of missed diagnosis of toe syndactyly (attributed to the fetus’s short, closely spaced toes with limited mobility in utero), prenatal diagnosis of Apert syndrome currently relies heavily on third-trimester ultrasound imaging [9–11], which is unfavorable for early diagnosis.

Pathogenic mutations of fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) gene were identified in approximately 98% of Apert syndrome [12], enabling prenatal molecular diagnosis. While several studies have reported the identification of FGFR2 mutations using targeted gene panels following the observation of fetal abnormalities [13, 14], the application of prenatal whole-exome sequencing (WES) for this purpose has been rarely described. Herein, we report a case where craniosynostosis and syndactyly observed at the 20th week of gestation. It was diagnosed with Apert syndrome due to the de novo mutation in FGFR2 identified by the WES, which provided new insights into the prenatal diagnosis of Apert syndrome at the second trimester.

Herein, we report a case where craniosynostosis and syndactyly were detected by ultrasound at the 20th week of gestation. Apert syndrome was subsequently confirmed through the identification of a de novo FGFR2 mutation by WES.

Case presentation

The proband was the first conception of a non-consanguineous Chinese couple. First-trimester combined screening indicated a high risk for trisomy 21, although the fetal nuchal translucency (NT) thickness was 1.0 mm at the 12 weeks’ gestation. Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) subsequently showed low risks for trisomies 21, 18, and 13. Routine prenatal ultrasound scans (US) at 20 weeks revealed an abnormal cranial shape with deep bilateral indentations in the frontoparietal regions. The typical hypoechoic gaps of the bilateral coronal sutures were not visualized, suggesting possible craniosynostosis. Additionally, syndactyly of the hands and feet and oligohydramnios were observed (Fig. 1). Following extensive genetic counseling at 21 weeks, the couple decided to terminate the pregnancy and receive post-procedure genetic testing.

Amniotic fluid was collected during the induction procedure. Standard cytogenetic analysis was performed on cultured amniocytes. Twenty metaphase spreads were analyzed, and five were karyotyped according to the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN 2020). No chromosomal abnormalities were detected (Fig. S1).

To investigate the genetic etiology of fetal malformations, we performed trio-WES. Genomic DNA was extracted from the fetal thigh tissue and peripheral blood from the couple. DNA was isolated with QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. DNA were fragmented by the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon Kit V5 (Agilent, United States) and sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For copy number analysis, the human genome was tiled into non-overlapping 20 kb bins. Sequencing coverage was then calculated as the count of uniquely aligned reads per bin. Generally, when copy number (CN) > 2.8, duplication was defined. Deletion was defined when CN < 1.2 and disomy (1.8 < CN < 2.2) [15].

As previously described [16], the detailed experimental process is briefly summarized as follows: the mean sequence coverage of the exons was at least 90-fold and for over 95% region of interest, the coverage reached no less than 20-fold. Variants were filtered using population databases, which included the genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD: http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC: http://exac.broadinstitute.org/). Subsequently, the variants were cross-referenced against multiple databases, including the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD: http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php), the Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD: http://www.dmd.nl/), and the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and only those with allele frequency ≤ 1% retained for further analysis. To predict the pathogenicity of the mutations, several bioinformatics tools were employed, primarily including SIFT (http://sift.jcvi.org/) and Mutation Taster (http://www.MutationTaster.org/). Whole-exome sequencing initially identified 153,507 variants. Following the application of standard filtering criteria, 696 variants remained for further analysis (Supplementary Table). The interpretation of the variants was in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG). Sanger sequencing (BigDye™ Direct Cycle Sequencing Kit, Applied Biosystems, 4458689) was carried out to validate the variant. The forward primer (5’-AGGCTTTTCTGGCATGAGGTC-3’) and reverse primer (5’-AACACTCTCTGCTGGCTAGTC-3’) were designed using Primer 5.0. The PCR products were purified by gel extraction. Subsequently, they were sequenced on an ABI 3730 DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems™) and analyzed with DNASTAR 5.0 software.

A heterozygous variant of c.758 C > G p.(P253R) was identified in the FGFR2 (NM_000141) gene which was confirmed to be a de novo mutation (PS2) (Fig. 2, Fig. S2). This variant was annotated as pathogenic in the HGMD database (PM) and was not reported in the population frequency gnomAD-exome database (PM2), consistent with its very low allele frequency. The phenotype was consistent with the genotype (PP4). With a single nucleotide change from cytosine to guanine, the missense mutation was predicted to be pathogenic by Mutation Taster (PP3) and was confirmed to cause craniosynostosis in mouse (PS3). In accordance with the ACMG guidelines [17], the variant c.758 C > G p.(P253R) was classified pathogenic.

Discussion

In the current study, craniosynostosis was detected via fetal ultrasound examination at the 20th week and Apert syndrome was diagnosed after the identification of pathogenic mutation in the FGFR2 gene. This case underscores the value of integrating prenatal imaging with molecular genetic testing for the early and definitive diagnosis of Apert syndrome.

The prenatal sonographic diagnosis of craniosynostosis remains challenging. Faro et al. reported that the gap between the frontal bones decreases from 2.2 mm at the 16th week to 0.9 mm at the 32th week [5]. The premature fusion of cranial sutures greatly decreases the space which is essential to ensure the growth of brain [1, 2, 5]. Compensatory overgrowth of the brain adjacent to other sutures results in different degrees of cranial and facial deformity, even contributing to the intellectual disability. Approximately 80% of craniosynostosis presents as an isolated symptom, with no identified causes [2, 18]. If accompanied with other specific symptoms, the prognosis of syndromic craniosynostosis is poor.

Apert syndrome is among the most common syndromic craniosynostoses. To date, nearly 180 syndromes manifest as craniosynostosis were reported, including Saethre–Chotzen, Jackson–Weiss, Crouzon’s, Antley–Bixtler, Apert, Pfeiffer syndrome and so on [18]. Characterized by the triad of craniosynostosis, syndactyly, and midfacial dysplasia often accompanied by intellectual disability. Apert syndrome is one of the most common syndromic craniosynostosis with different degrees of intellectual disability. With the characteristic of syndactyly, midfacial dysplasia and intellectual disability, Apert syndrome requires lifelong multidisciplinary management, placing heavy burden on the family. Early diagnosis of Apert syndrome provides parents with essential information about the fetal condition, supporting informed decisions on pregnancy management and thereby contributing to the reduction of births of fetuses with congenital anomalies.

The demand for early diagnosis of Apert syndrome promotes the advancement of prenatal diagnosis techniques. The first case of prenatal diagnosis of Apert syndrome can date back to 1982. Leonard et al. reported a termination of pregnancy at the 17th week of gestation diagnosed by fetoscopy [19]. However, the invasion of fetoscopy restricts its application and it is not proper for wide use. By 1986, Kim et al. reported a case of Apert syndrome at 28 weeks gestational age diagnosed by two-dimensional (2D) sonographic detection [11, 19]. Nevertheless, ultrasound imaging fails to fully visualize craniosynostosis in many cases, especially at the first or second trimester [10]; therefore the diagnosis of Apert syndrome should be prudently made [9], which restricts the wide application of 2D sonographic detection. Esser et al. identified craniosynostosis and syndactyly in an Apert syndrome case at the 22th week under the guidance of the three-dimensional maximum mode [20]. The introduction of 3D ultrasound enhances the detection ability to some extent [9, 18], but the early identification of cranial and facial deformity remains a challenge.

Prenatal molecular diagnosis is an advanced technique based on chorionic villus sampling, amniocentesis or umbilical cord puncture. The identification of pathogenic mutations can help to diagnose Apert syndrome. The current publications revealed that FGFR2 mutations accounted for more than 98% of Apert syndrome [21, 22], with 2/3 caused by S252W mutation and 1/3 by P253R mutation [12]. FGFR2 is located on chromosome 10q26, composed of 18 exons [8]. It encodes FGFR2, one of the FGFRs family [7], functioning as a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor [12]. Expressed in proliferating cells [23], particularly restricted to sutural mesenchyme [24], FGRF2 plays a crucial role in maintaining the balance between osteogenesis and bone resorption. Down-regulation of FGFR2 promotes differentiation and proliferation [7], ultimately leading to the craniosynostosis. S252W and P253R are common mutations leading to syndromic craniosynostosis. In mouse studies, Kim et al. backcrossed Fgfr2 S252W/+ mice into the FVB background [25]. The premature fusion of facial bones was observed at the Fgfr2 S252W/+ knock-in mice, providing an animal model for the absence of the FGFR2 gene. Furthermore, with Apert syndrome mouse models, Martínez-Abadías et al. identified cleft palate in Fgfr2+/S252W and Fgfr2+/P253R mice, the former presenting a more severe phenotype than the latter [23].

The first prenatal molecular diagnosis of Apert syndrome can be traced to 1999 [13, 14]. Ferreira et al. advocated to perform amniocentesis when severe midfacial dysplasia was observed. FGFR2 and other related genes were packed in gene panels to identify its genetic causes. Likewise, Mahieu-Caputo et al. reported a prenatal identification of pathogenic mutation in FGFR2 by performing a related gene panel in 2001 [14]. The introduction of gene panel provides a repaid method to identify pathogenic mutations and therefore clarifies the diagnosis. However, with a sensitivity of 43% [26], the detection ability is not so satisfactory comparing with WES. In addition, the discrepancy of gene panels between different laboratories for an identical symptom may be significant which might miss some atypical presentations. What’s worse, limited to recorded mutations only, the de novo mutations can never be found by gene panel detecting, which may omit some important de novo genes.

WES addresses the inherent limitations of gene panels, enabling improved diagnostic accuracy [27]. Currently, an increasing number of publications reported the application of prenatal WES when abnormalities were identified by fetal ultrasound [16, 28–30]. However, few studies have reported the use of WES for the prenatal diagnosis of Apert syndrome. Classic ultrasound findings, such as midfacial dysplasia and syndactyly, the latter of which is unique to Apert syndrome [9, 18], are often not detectable until the third trimester. Therefore, the application of WES can help to shorten the time for diagnosis.

We summarized 14 cases of Apert syndrome diagnosed prenatally over the past decade (Table 1). In contrast, the case we present here represents one of the few reported instances of a fetus with the c.758 C > G p.(P253R) mutation identified prenatally. Analysis of these 14 cases revealed that the majority of mutations were c.755 C > G p.(Ser252Trp) (7/14), with only two cases definitively confirmed as c.758 C > G p.(P253R) [31–41]. Similar to other reported cases, our prenatal ultrasound detected the key phenotypic hallmarks of Apert syndrome, including craniosynostosis and syndactyly of the hands and feet. However, a notable distinction is that these features were detected relatively early in our case (at 20 weeks of gestation) and were subsequently confirmed by karyotype analysis, trio whole-exome sequencing, and Sanger sequencing, which identified the c.758 C > G p.(P253R) mutation. While Tonni et al. reported a case where craniosynostosis was detected via ultrasound at 19 weeks, only unilateral syndactyly of the left hand was observed, missing the key feature of symmetric syndactyly in the extremities. Furthermore, that case only analyzed several genes (FGFR1 [exon 8], FGFR2 [exons 8, 10], FGFR3 [exon 7], and Twist [exon 1]) via a genetic panel, failing to rule out the possibility of other genetic abnormalities [33]. Therefore, our study supplements the phenotypic characteristics of Apert syndrome at 20 weeks of gestation. It also demonstrates a precise diagnostic approach—integrating detailed ultrasound with comprehensive molecular analysis—offering valuable clinical guidance for prenatal diagnosis at this gestational stage.

Despite the identification of the c.758 C > G p.(Pro253Arg) variant as a de novo mutation in the proband (fetal) via trio-WES and Sanger sequencing validation of parental peripheral blood cells, the possibility of germinal mosaicism in the parents cannot be formally excluded.

Since our parental genetic testing relied solely on peripheral blood samples, which do not reflect the genetic status of germ cells, we cannot rule out the scenario that one parent carries the variant in germline tissues. This limitation is inherent to most genetic studies utilizing somatic cell samples for parental validation, as direct assessment of germ cells is ethically and practically unfeasible in clinical settings. While germinal mosaicism is uncommon in Apert syndrome [3], its theoretical possibility highlights the need for long-term follow-up and genetic counseling for the family. This limitation also underscores the value of integrating multiple testing modalities in future studies to enhance the accuracy of recurrence risk assessment.

In conclusion, Apert syndrome is typically diagnosed in late pregnancy. In this case, craniosynostosis, oligohydramnios, and syndactyly of hands and feet were observed at the 20th week of gestation. This integrated diagnostic approach provides a model for the early and accurate diagnosis of complex syndromic craniosynostosis. Looking forward, emerging technologies like the analysis of cell-free fetal DNA for FGFR2 mutations, potentially 9 weeks of gestation [42], promise to further revolutionize prenatal diagnosis, making it less invasive and more timely. Our study thus provides a contemporary clinical reference and underscores the importance of a combined imaging and genomic approach for the prenatal management of Apert syndrome.
