Characteristics and immune dynamics of peripheral blood immune cells and cytokines in individuals with chronic hepatitis B virus infection
Authors: Lixin Xiao, Zhuanli Dong, Yao He, Jingchun Fan, Huichun Duan, Xiaomei Jiang, Changhao Wei, Tingting Ma, Yi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related immune injury-mediated local liver inflammation and hepatocyte death are critical for the progression of chronic hepatitis B (CHB). This cross-sectional study aimed to characterize the systematic variations of peripheral blood immune cells and the corresponding changes of cytokines/chemokines during CHB progression, without implying causal relationships. Peripheral blood immune cell subsets were detected by multiparametric flow cytometry, and the levels of cytokines/chemokines were measured using the Luminex discovery assay. The study included CHB patients at different stages and healthy controls (HC), with data compared between groups, A multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to adjust for potential confounding factors including age and gender. Compared with HC, initial CHB patients had a significantly higher frequency of total B cells (p < 0.001) but a lower proportion of CD4 + T cells (p < 0.01). The level of C-X-C chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) was markedly elevated in CHB patients (p < 0.01) and positively correlated with B cell frequency. Notably, CHB patients with serum Hepatitis B Surface Antigen (HBsAg) < 1500 ng/mL had higher CXCL10 levels than those with HBsAg ≥ 1500 ng/mL (p < 0.05). In late-stage HBV infection (HBV-LC and HBV-HCC), the frequency of NK cells was significantly lower than that in CHB patients and healthy controls, while the frequency of T cells showed a relative increase. Additionally, compared with CHB patients and HC, late-stage patients showed significantly higher levels of key cytokines/chemokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), C-C chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), and tumor necrosis factor receptor I (TNFRI). This cross-sectional study reveals distinct alterations in peripheral blood immune cells and cytokines/chemokines across CHB progression, with CXCL10, NK cells, and multiple proinflammatory factors closely associated with disease stage and HBsAg levels. These findings may provide potential biomarkers for monitoring CHB progression and theoretical support for immune-based therapeutic strategies, but causal relationships require verification by subsequent longitudinal studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48947-9.

Introduction

According to WHO, about 257 million people suffer chronic HBV infection worldwide1,2. Lifestyle and/or irregular antiviral treatment results in the progression of HBV infection to chronic hepatitis B (CHB), HBV-related liver cirrhosis (HBV-LC), and even HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HBV-HCC)3,4. HBV is considered to cause liver failure by three sequential major factors: immunologic injury, ischemic-hypoxic injury, and endotoxemia5. Immune cell activation and subsequent cytokine release are associated with hepatocyte injury in the context of HBV infection, which may be involved in the initiation of liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC. Therefore, the immune response is the most critical factor in HBV infection, making the dramatic changes in frequencies of immune cells and levels of cytokines a persistent research focus.

HBV-caused immune damage is mainly attributed to cellular immunity and humoral immunity5,6. On the one hand, multiple types of immune cells are involved in hepatitis B pathogenesis, including dendritic cells (DC), Kupffer cells, T cells, B cells, NK cells, and NKT cells. The host immune system initially recognizes HBV and then activates intrahepatic HBV-specific CD8+ T cells and peripheral cytotoxic T cells to release abundant cytokines7–9. However, recent studies have shown that HBV-specific T-cell responses are timely and efficiently induced in acute self-limited infections but deeply exhausted in CHB10. Moreover, activation of NK/NKT cells can also cause the death of infected hepatocytes7. On the other hand, HBV-induced immune suppression contributes to persistent viral infection and depletion of immune cells such as monocytes/macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressive cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs) and regulatory NK cells (NK-regs)11. Significant changes in cytokine/chemokine levels during immune activation/suppression also play important roles in hepatitis B progression. At present, cumulative studies have revealed the mechanism of immune exhaustion after repeated viral infections, although there is still a lack of systematic characterization of the immune status after chronic HBV infection until HBV-HCC.

To determine the immune status in chronic HBV infection, we detected frequencies of multiple types of peripheral blood immune cells such as CD4+/CD8 + T cells, B cells, NK/NKT cells, MDSCs, and Tregs, as well as levels of pertinent serum cytokines/chemokines, such as B-cell activating factor (BAFF), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 11 (CXCL11), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin 8 (IL-8), interleukin 23 (IL-23), matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1), matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2), matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3), tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFRI), C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CXCL13), interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 21 (IL-21), interleukin 33 (IL-33), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) at different infection stages, using standard multiparametric flow cytometry (FCM) and multiplex cytokine analysis.

Methods

Patients

This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Lanzhou Petrochemical General Hospital (Approval Number: LZSH-IEC-2024-098-1). A total of 105 individuals were recruited for this study.

Each participant (or their legal guardians) signed a written informed consent document before the study. Hepatitis B diagnosis was made following the clinical practice guidelines on the management of HBV infection12. Patients with septic shock, pregnancy, hepatitis C virus (HCV) or human immunodeficiency virus (Hsu et al.,) infection, significant steatosis, organ transplants within five years, and other immunodeficiency disorders were excluded from the study. The 13 healthy volunteers (HC) were hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-negative adult volunteers with no HBV family history. Ninety-two patients were subjected to the following studies. In one study, 12 HBV patients who did not receive any antiviral treatment were further divided into two subgroups based on the guide4 4 in the immune tolerant (IT) subgroup and 8 in the immune reactive HBeAg-positive (IA) subgroup. In another study, 80 post-CHB patients were divided into the HBV-LC subgroup and the HBV-HCC subgroup. HBV-LC was diagnosed following the diagnostic criteria of the guide clinical practice guidelines: management of liver cirrhosis13. HBV-HCC was defined by positive serum HBsAg of more than 6 months and confirmed HCC diagnosis. The clinical characteristics of the subjects are summarized in Table 1. The subjects’ peripheral blood was collected in EDTA blood collection tubes by the staff of the Department of Infectious Disease at Lanzhou Petrochemical General Hospital (Lanzhou, China). CHB.

Collection of sera and PBMCs

Five milliliters of peripheral blood were collected from each person. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min, the plasma was taken and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The whole blood cells were 1:1 diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by density gradient isolation of PBMCs with Ficoll-Paque (GE Healthcare). Isolated PBMCs were resuspended in fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries) containing 10% DMSO and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Multiparametric flow cytometric analysis

Six types of immune cells were examined by a CytoFLEX SRT Cell Sorter (BECKMAN), including immunoreactive T cells, B cells, NK cells, NKT cells, and immunosuppressive MDSCs and Treg cells. The following anti-human antibodies were used to label cell surface markers: CD45-FITC/CD4-PE/CD8-ECD/CD3-PC5(TETRACHROME, BECKMAN), CD45-FITC/CD56-PE/CD19-ECD/CD3-PC5, CD19-PE (TETRACHROME, BECKMAN), CD4-APC (Biolegend), CD25-PE (Biolegend), CD127 (IL-7Ra)-Alexa Fluor 488 (Biolegend), HLA-DR-PE (Biolegend), CD33-FITC (Biolegend), and CD11b-APC (Biolegend) antibodies. The data were analyzed using the CytExpert SRT software.

Multiplex analysis of serum cytokines

Seventeen cytokines/chemokines in serum samples, including IL-6, IL-8, IL-21, IL-23, IL-33, IFN-γ, TNF-α, TNFR1, BAFF, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL13, CCL2, MMP)1, MMP-2, and MMP-314, were tested using the Luminex® Discovery Assay Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (Catalog Number: LXSAHM-17, Lot Number: L141528, R&D systems) on a FLEXMAP 3D® analyzer (Luminex) and analyzed using the xPONENT® software, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Before the tests, the serum samples were thawed and centrifuged at 16,000 g for 4 min. Protein standards were prepared by serial dilution following the vendors’ instructions. The protein standards and samples were examined on the analyzer within 90 min. The Multiplex analyst (5.1) was used to prepare a standard curve for each analyte and to generate a five-parameter logistic (5-PL) curve fit.

Serological and viral markers for hepatitis B

The levels of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBiL), direct bilirubin (DBiL), albumin (Farhat et al.), and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) were measured on an automated analyzer (HITACHI 7600, HITACHI, Tokyo). The levels of serum HBsAg, hepatitis B e-antigen (HBeAg), anti-HBe antibodies, and anti-hepatitis B core antigen (HBc) antibodies were measured with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reagents (KHB, Shanghai, China). Serum HBV DNA levels were assessed using real-time fluorescence detection PCR with a low detection limit of 30 IU/ml (Shengxiang Biotechnology, Changsha, China).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the SPSS 22.0 statistical software and GraphPad Prism (version 8.3, GraphPad Software Inc.). Fisher’s exact test was used for classification. Student’s t-test or nonparametric test was used for continuous data after examining the normality and homogeneity of variances.A multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to adjust for the confounding effects of age and gender on the study results. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Elevation of frequency in peripheral B cells rather than T cells in primary CHB

To evaluate the frequencies of immune cells such as T cells, B cells, NK cells, MDSCs, and Treg cells in HC and CHB patients, we performed flow cytometry analysis. We found that the CD4+ T cell proportion in lymphocytes of CHB patients was lower than that of healthy controls (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1a). However, the B cell proportion in lymphocytes of CHB patients was significantly higher than that of HC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1a). Meanwhile, the MDSC proportion in monocytes of CHB patients was remarkably higher than that of HC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1a), but the Treg cell proportion in lymphocytes in CHB patients was lower than that of HC (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, we divided the CHB group into two subgroups (IT and IA) according to the history of chronic HBV infection in EASL (European Association for the Study of the Liver. Electronic address and European Association for the Study of the 2017). We found elevated proportions in B cells in both subgroups relative to HC, especially the IA subgroup (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1b). The proportion of MDSCs showed the same changes in the IT and IA subgroups relative to HC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1c). However, there were no significant differences in the proportions of CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and NKT cells between CHB patients and HC.

Dynamic change in the level of CXCL10 following an increase in the frequency of B cells in primary CHB patients

To evaluate whether the expression levels of B cell-derived cytokines and chemokines were changed along with increased B cell proportion, the levels of 17 cytokines and chemokines related to immune activation and/or suppression were measured. As expected, several cytokines and chemokines showed differences between HC and CHB patients, especially CXCL10 and IFN-γ. The level of CXCL10 was significantly increased in CHB patients (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, after subgrouping, we found a higher CXCL10 level in the IA subgroup relative to HC and the IT subgroup (Fig. 2a). Meanwhile, we observed a strong positive correlation between CXCL10 and B cell frequency (r2 = 0.4697, p = 0.0139) (Fig. 2b). The significance of B cells in primary CHB was further confirmed by the association between CXCL10 and B cell frequency. Interestingly, CHB patients with serum HBsAg of < 1500 ng/mL had higher CXCL10 expression than those with serum HBsAg of ≥ 1500 ng/mL (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2c). This result was consistent with our previous study showing that the levels of CXCL10 and serum HBsAg appeared “U-shaped” in CHB patients15.

NK cell frequency was significantly lower throughout the late-hepatitis B stages

We also characterized the changes in blood immune cells at late-hepatitis B stages. To our surprise, B cells were significantly decreased at the HBV-LC stage and HBV-HCC stage, especially the HBV-HCC stage (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a). The frequencies of T cells was remarkably increased at the HBV-LC stage and HBV-HCC stage, while the frequency of NK cells was significantly lower than that in CHB patients and healthy controls, showing a more profound decrease (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a). The increase in the frequency of NK cells suggested aggravation of inflammation in chronic HBV infection. Moreover, HBV-LC patients with serum HBsAg of < 1500 ng/mL had a higher NKT cell frequency than those with serum HBsAg of ≥ 1500 ng/mL (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). However, this change was not observed at the HBV-HCC stage. In addition, the frequency of MDSCs was decreased at late-hepatitis B stages but still higher than the HC group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a). Treg cell frequency was higher in HBV-LC patients compared with CHB patients (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a).

Chronic inflammation signifies immune dysregulation in late-hepatitis B patients

To further study the potential immune dysregulation in late-hepatitis B patients, we tested the expression of several cytokines and chemokines. HBV-LC patients and HBV-HCC patients exhibited IFN-γ up-regulation compared with HC and CHB patients (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, and TNFRI showed similar up-regulation in HBV-LC patients and especially HBV-HCC patients (p < 0.001). What’s more, BAFF was up-regulated in HBV-LC patients but rapidly down-regulated in HBV-HCC patients. However, HBV-LC patients and HBV-HCC patients always had higher BAFF levels than healthy volunteers and CHB patients (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

In HBV-LC, LC patients were divided into two subgroups based on their liver compensation status for discussion, namely the compensated and the decompensated cirrhosis group. These changes were closely related to liver decompensation.

In HCC, B cells were negatively correlated with TB (rTBiL=−0.431, p < 0.01); NK cells exhibited a negative correlation with AST and HBsAg (rAST=−0.42, p < 0.01; rHBsAg=−0.509, p < 0.01), while Treg cells demonstrated a significant negative association with CCL2 (rCCL2=−0.484, p < 0.01).

Discussion

Without the effects of immune factors, even active and massive HBV replication in hepatocytes cannot lead to an extensive and serious liver flare. Immunity-related damage plays a vital role in HBV-associated liver failure5. Severe liver injury can exacerbate hepatitis B progression, and various immune cells and cytokines can further result in the chronicity of hepatitis B. The major hypothesis of this study is that dysregulated frequencies of immune cells and levels of cytokines contribute to the immune disorder in HBV infection. Therefore, it is important to understand the roles of immune cells which significantly impact HBV infection. It was observed that during the early stage of chronic hepatitis B virus infection (CHB), B cells and MDSCs showed more prominent changes compared to T cells and NK cells, which may be related to the initial immune response. As the disease progresses to the LC stage, T cells and functional cytokines exhibit significant alterations, while during the HCC stage, most immune cells and cytokines show a trend of dysregulation.

In our study, 13 healthy individuals, 12 patients with initial CHB, 36 HBV-LC patients, and 44 HBV-HCC patients were recruited to represent different stages of infection. Initial CHB is the early stage of chronic HBV infection, while HBV-LC patients and HBV-HCC patients are at the late stages of chronic infection. It was evidented that during the early stage of chronic hepatitis B virus infection, specifically in CHB, B cells and MDSCs played a more prominent immunological role compared to T cells and NK cells. However, as the disease progresses to LC stage, T cells and functional cytokines emerged as the primary immunological drivers of the body’s defenses. Conversely, during the HCC, the majority of immune cells and cytokines exhibited a trend of waning immunological activity.

Of note, the frequency of blood B cells rather than T cells was significantly increased in patients with initial CHB, including those at IT and IA stages. The IA subgroup simultaneously had a higher proportion of B cells and significantly increased MDSCs. Furthermore, significant up-regulation of CXCL10 was seen in CHB patients, especially in the IA subgroup. CXCL10 has been well-documented as a chemokine that attracts CD4+T and CD8+ T cells and regulates their infiltration16. Engaging with CXCR3, which is a chemokine receptor expressed on CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and NK cells, CXCL10 induces directional migration of these effector immune cells to autoimmune lesions (liver), thus making the CXCL10-CXCR3 axis crucial for regulating autoimmunity17. This also explains the results of our study. Due to the up-regulation of CXCL10, effector T cells were recruited to the liver to clear HBV and peripheral T cells were therefore reduced. Besides, we found that CHB patients with serum HBsAg of < 1500 ng/mL had a higher level of CXCL10 than those with serum HBsAg of ≥ 1500 ng/mL. When combining our present and previous research, the baseline CXCL10 level correlated with HBsAg level, although the correlation appeared “U-shaped”. When CXCL10 was < 500 pg/mL, it negatively correlated with serum HBsAg15. Considering the increase of peripheral B cell frequency, we found a positive correlation between frequencies of B cells and levels of CXCL10. This suggests that CXCL10 may simultaneously affect the B cell frequency and influence HBV clearance.

The frequency of CD4+ T cells was increased at late-hepatitis B stages, indicating that the blood T cell amount was relatively restored. It is noteworthy that although the frequency of blood B cells were significantly decreased, the level of BAFF was not remarkably changed in CHB patients but increased at late-hepatitis B stages, indicating that BAFF was not responsible for the increase in the frequency of blood B cells in early CHB. Elevation in B cell frequency might be related to the activation and recruitment of B cells induced by CXCL10-activated T cells. Meanwhile, the significant decrease in the frequency of NK cells was observed along with IFN-γ up-regulation. Some studies reported that NK cells secret IFN-γ to mediate liver inflammation and promote HCC development through the epithelial cell adhesion molecule-epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition axis in HBs-Tg mice18. This further confirms the role of NK cells in liver inflammation during HBV infection and HCC progression. In addition, we observed elevated levels of several pro-inflammatory factors at late-hepatitis B stages, including IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, and TNFRI. These factors are closely related to tumor progression. Previous studies have shown that IL-6 and TNF-α downregulate miR-122 (an anti-tumor factor that is down-regulated in human HCC) via c-Myc-medicated C/EBPα inhibition19,20. Meanwhile, chronic inflammation such as chronic HBV infection amplifies the IL-6/TNF-α/miR-122 feedback circuit to induce inflammation-driven HCC19,21. Besides, CCL2 up-regulates miR-21 through the CCR2/JNK/AP-1 signaling pathway and promotes HCC migration, invasion, and metastasis22. These studies suggest that increased pro-inflammatory factors not only exacerbate liver inflammation and fibrosis at late-hepatitis B stages but also further promote HCC progression.

On the other hand, our study has some limitations. First, this is an observational and cross-sectional study, lacking long-term follow-up data. The findings only reflect the correlation between immune indicators and disease stages, and cannot confirm causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed to track the dynamic changes of immune cells and cytokines in individual patients during disease progression. Second, the comprehensive effects of CXCL10 and B cells, and the underlying mechanism by which CXCL10 influences HBsAg loss remains elusive. Third, his study only evaluated immune cells and cytokines in peripheral blood, without obtaining liver tissue samples for in situ detection. Peripheral blood immune status may not fully represent the local immune microenvironment of the liver, and future studies should combine liver tissue biopsy to verify the correlation between peripheral blood and intrahepatic immune indicators. Fourth, there are differences in age and gender distribution among the study groups. Although multivariate ANOVA was used to adjust for these confounding factors, residual confounding may still exist, which should be considered when interpreting the results.In conclusion, our study characterized the changes in frequencies of peripheral immune cells and levels of cytokines/chemokines during the progression of chronic HBV infection. Significant increases in frequencies of B cells and MDSCs were identified in patients with initial CHB who had mild or no liver inflammation. Meanwhile, blood CXCL10 level was increased but BAFF level remained unchanged, implying that CXCL10-activated T cells rather than BAFF activate and recruit B cells at the early stage of CHB. Besides, a significant decrease in B cell frequency, a remarkable increase in T cell frequency, a significant decrease in NK cell frequency, and IFN-γ up-regulation co-existed at late-hepatitis B stages. Simultaneously, the levels of HCC progression-associated pro-inflammatory factors were also increased in HBV-LC and HBV-HCC patients.
