Needle and Nodule: A Case of Intra‐Lesional Success in Oral Mucocele Treatment
Authors: Soubhagya Ranjan Kar, Sangamesh N C, Noureen Nahar, Jitendra Sharan, Smriti Kc Basnyat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 26‐year‐old male with a lower lip mucocele was successfully treated using weekly intralesional injection of Dexamethasone (2 mL) for 3 weeks. This non‐surgical approach leads to the complete resolution of the lesion within a four‐week duration with no recurrence during follow‐up. Such a treatment modality highlights the effectiveness as a minimally invasive alternative for the management of mucocele of the oral cavity.Keywords: case report, dexamethasone injection, mucocele, non‐invasive therapy, non‐surgical therapy

Introduction

Mucocele represents a frequently encountered benign mucosal pathology arising from minor salivary gland dysfunction secondary to mucin accumulation. It manifests as a localized, fluid‐filled swelling caused by the accumulation of mucin within the tissue [1]. The nomenclature “mucocele” originates from Latin etymology, combining “mucus” denoting mucilaginous secretions, with “coele” signifying cavity formation or hollow compartments [2]. Oral Mucoceles (OM) are classified into two primary types: extravasation and retention. An extravasation mucocele arises from the rupture of a salivary gland duct, leading to the seepage of mucus into adjacent soft tissues. Conversely, a retention mucocele forms due to obstructed salivary gland ducts, resulting in diminished or completely halted glandular secretion [3]. Extravasation mucoceles demonstrate predominant localization within the inferior labial region of the oral cavity, while ductal obstruction variants may manifest throughout any intraoral anatomical site. These pathological entities exhibit no demographic restrictions regarding age distribution or gender preference. Oral mucoceles typically present without associated symptoms [4].

When these cystic lesions develop on the floor of the mouth, they are termed ranulas, characterized by a frog‐like swelling due to the accumulation of mucus, resembling the inflated cheeks of a frog [5]. Mucocele manifestation demonstrates the highest predilection for labial tissues, particularly the inferior lip region, due to enhanced susceptibility to traumatic injury, with secondary involvement of the oral floor, ventral lingual surfaces, and buccal mucosa [6]. Certain mucoceles may resolve on their own within a brief period; however, persistent or chronic lesions often necessitate intervention, such as surgical excision or marsupialization. The literature describes several treatment approaches, including conventional scalpel excision, carbon dioxide laser ablation, laser‐based surgery, micro‐marsupialization, intralesional injection of corticosteroid, and cryotherapy [7].

Surgery is the most frequently used tool, but it has the potential disadvantage of disfiguring the lip and damaging the adjacent ducts, which might lead to the further development of satellite lesions [8]. Dexamethasone, a fluorinated glucocorticoid medication that possesses anti‐inflammatory and anti‐swelling properties, offers a viable non‐invasive treatment for mucoceles by reducing inflammation and mucus accumulation [9]. This approach of treatment has a better patient‐friendly profile; it eliminates the need for sutures or post‐treatment wound management, thus minimizing complications. Thus, it is indicated in patients who seem to be anxious about the surgical procedure, and in the case of recurrence after surgery. Very limited comprehensive information is available in the literature regarding the use of intralesional injection of corticosteroid for the management of mucocele [10, 11]. Through this case report, we would like to highlight a very conservative, safe, and simple technique for the management of oral mucocele.

Case Presentation

History and Examination

A 26‐year‐old male reported a swelling of the lingual aspect of the lower lip for the past 4 weeks. Extraoral clinical examination revealed an apparently symmetrical facial profile with the absence of facial edema and no palpable cervical lymphadenopathy. Oral examination revealed a solitary, clearly circumscribed, hemispherical protrusion situated on the inferior labial mucosa, measuring approximately 6 mm across its greatest dimension (Figure 1). The lesion exhibited a bluish coloration and demonstrated fluctuation upon palpation, with no tenderness elicited on palpation. The overlying mucosal surface appeared normal without evidence of ulceration or secondary bacterial infection. The patient denied any antecedent history of local trauma or significant medical conditions. Mild discomfort was reported exclusively during verbal communication, with no associated pain.

A mucous retention cyst is a non‐vascular lesion formed by a blocked duct of the salivary gland, while a haemangioma is a rapidly growing vascular tumor of endothelial cells that typically involutes, and a vascular malformation is a slow‐growing congenital vascular anomaly. Thus, on the basis of case history and a detailed clinical examination, the lesion was identified as a mucous retention cyst/mucocele of the minor salivary gland of the lower lip.

Various treatment options have been discussed with the patient, including invasive and non‐invasive. Pros and cons of each of the procedures were informed and discussed with the patient before making any final decision. The patient was reluctant to undergo an invasive procedure in the form of surgical excision and opted for the intralesional injection of corticosteroid. After obtaining informed consent and providing comprehensive procedural information, the initial therapeutic intervention was commenced. Four follow‐up sessions were subsequently scheduled with seven‐day intervals between treatments. The intervention site underwent aseptic preparation, followed by the administration of local anesthesia via perilesional infiltration using 2% lidocaine with epinephrine (1:80,000). A 30‐gauge insulin needle enabled accurate intralesional delivery of 2 mL dexamethasone sodium phosphate (4 mg/mL). Pre‐injection aspiration was performed to ensure extravascular needle placement. The corticosteroid was subsequently administered into the lesional base with careful attention to volume control, preventing potential complications such as tissue necrosis or mucosal ulceration (Figure 2). Following the injection, sterile gauze compression was applied to minimize post‐procedural edema and drug extravasation.

Intralesional injection of dexamethasone after achieving local anesthesia by infiltration.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The identical protocol was implemented during the two successive appointments, maintaining weekly intervals. Each session commenced with clinical assessment of lesional dimensions, consistency, and symptomatology, followed by intralesional dexamethasone administration utilizing the previously described technique (Figures 3, 4, 5). At the fourth follow‐up examination, clinical evaluation demonstrated complete mucocele resolution (Figure 6). The mucosal surface demonstrated normal anatomical appearance and consistency, without residual swelling or subjective patient symptoms. The patient confirmed symptomatic resolution and was advised to maintain periodic surveillance appointments for recurrence monitoring. During the six‐month post‐treatment evaluation, no evidence of mucocele reformation was observed.

Discussion

Prevalence of mucocele ranges from 0.4% to 0.8% within the general population, demonstrating negligible gender‐based distribution differences [12]. Lower lip constitutes the predominant anatomical location, representing 40% to 80% of documented instances, with subsequent involvement of buccal mucosa and floor of the mouth [5, 12]. Sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS) serves as a safe, cost‐effective sclerosant for treating oral mucoceles, inducing necrosis and lesion regression with minimal recurrence. Unlike surgical methods, STS is less invasive, reducing risks such as nerve damage or scarring, though its efficacy may vary based on lesion size and location (Table 1) [13].

An interesting, unusual case report by Luiz et al., with multiple oral mucoceles with recurrence, was treated successfully with 0.05% clobetasol propionate in Orabase. There is a decrease in the size of the lesion followed by complete remission, but it is short‐lasting. The lesion reappeared, which was treated symptomatically with periodic corticosteroid application. They have suggested that recurrent inflammation and minor gland duct impairment were the potential contributing factors [14]. In one of the first comprehensive case series on the management of intraoral mucocele using intralesional injection of dexamethasone (1 mL of 8 mg/mL, administered weekly for 3 sessions), Baharvand et al. have reported full resolution of the lesion in 78% of the patients, while 22% show partial shrinkage of the lesion. He also reported no recurrence of the lesion over a 6–20 months duration [10]. Sinha et al. conducted a clinical study on 20 patients aged 10–30 years with oral mucoceles. Patients were administered intralesional betamethasone (1 mL of 4 mg/mL) once weekly for up to 4 weeks. Eighteen lesions completely resolved, and two showed partial shrinkage, with no major adverse effects except minor discomfort in a few cases [11]. Shivhare et al. in a retrospective cohort of 38 oral lesions treated with 3% sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS) sclerotherapy, including 8 mucoceles, reported complete remission in two cases, one each with good, moderate, and mild response, and for three cases there was no response to the STS treatment [15]. Sclerotherapy with STS does offer an alternative to the conventional surgical approach for management of the intraoral mucocele, but its efficacy is less than that of the corticosteroid. Albagieh et al. have reported an interesting case of mucocele of the tongue. Surgical intervention was attempted five times for the removal of a mucocele, followed by its recurrence. A single 0.5 mL intralesional injection of dexamethasone (2 g) resulted in complete resolution, which was sustained over 18 months of follow‐up. The study highlighted the shortcomings of surgical approaches and underscored the effectiveness of nonsurgical corticosteroid therapy for persistent mucoceles. These results support steroid injections as a practical, minimally invasive treatment alternative (Table 2) [16].

Intra‐lesion injection of the corticosteroid can be considered in pediatric patients, who are usually apprehensive about the invasive procedure. Yermalkar et al. documented a case of a recurring lower lip mucocele in a pediatric patient, effectively managed with intralesional triamcinolone acetonide. Two injections, administered 1 week apart, resulted in complete healing without recurrence over 6 months of follow‐up [17].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates effective non‐surgical treatment of an oral mucocele through intralesional dexamethasone injection, offering a conservative alternative to traditional sclerosing agents or surgery. The procedure was simple, well‐tolerated, and led to complete resolution of the lesion without complications or recurrence. Due to its minimally invasive nature, favorable safety, and aesthetic outcomes, this method may be particularly suitable for managing small, superficial mucoceles in younger or anxious individuals.
