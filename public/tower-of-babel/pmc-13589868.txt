Agricultural practices drive multifactorial pyrethroid resistance in Anopheles coluzzii populations from southern Benin

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid spread of insecticide resistance threatens the effectiveness of malaria vector control across sub-Saharan Africa. In southern Benin, the respective contributions of agricultural pesticide use and urban vector control interventions to the selection of resistant Anopheles populations remain poorly documented.MethodsPyrethroid resistance was investigated in Anopheles coluzzii populations collected in southern Benin from two zones: two agricultural areas (Sèmè-Kpodji and Abomey-Calavi) and one non-agricultural urban area (Yenawa). WHO tube bioassays were performed using deltamethrin (0.05%) and alpha-cypermethrin (0.05%). Molecular species was conducted using PCR-SINE200, whereas the kdr-West (L1014F) mutation was genotyped by PCR. The activities of cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, esterases, and glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) were quantified, while the expression levels of CYP6Z1, CYP9K1, and GSTE2 were measured by RT–qPCR.ResultsAll populations exhibited confirmed resistance to both pyrethroids, with mortality rates ranging from 78% to 82% for deltamethrin and from 36% to 89% for alpha-cypermethrin. Resistance to alpha-cypermethrin was markedly greater in agricultural areas, particularly in Sèmè-Kpodji (36%) and Abomey-Calavi (52%), than in Yenawa (89%). Molecular analyses identified Anopheles coluzzii as the sole species detected (100%). The resistant kdr-west allele occurred at high frequencies, ranging from 73.3% to 93.3%, and no susceptible homozygotes were observed. Biochemical assays revealed significantly elevated CYP450, GST, and esterase activities, especially in Sèmè-Kpodji and Yenawa, indicating the contribution of metabolic detoxification mechanisms. In contrast, RT‒qPCR revealed no overexpression of CYP6Z1, CYP9K1, or GSTE2 relative to the susceptible N'Gousso strain (fold-change values of 0.26–0.58, 0.06–0.21, and 0.03–0.06, respectively), despite significant inter-population differences in transcript levels.ConclusionsPyrethroid resistance in Anopheles coluzzii from southern Benin is widespread and multifactorial, involving both the near-fixation of the kdr-west mutation and increased detoxification enzyme activities. The stronger phenotypic resistance observed in agricultural areas highlights the role of agricultural pesticide exposure, whereas elevated kdr resistance markers at urban sites indicate additional selection pressure from public health and domestic insecticide use. These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated resistance management strategies that combine agricultural pesticide regulation, continuous resistance surveillance, and deployment of next-generation vector control tools.

Background

Malaria remains one of the deadliest infectious diseases in human history and continues to represent a major public health issue, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 249 million malaria cases and approximately 608,000 malaria-related deaths were recorded globally in 2022, with the African region bearing more than 94% of this burden [1]. Children under 5 years of age and pregnant women are disproportionately affected, making malaria a leading cause of preventable child mortality on the continent. In West Africa, particularly in Benin, the disease is hyperendemic and remains the leading cause of medical consultation and hospitalization [2]. Data from the National Malaria Control Program reported that approximately 17% of the general population and 39% of children under 5 years of age are affected by malaria, with the disease contributing substantially to school absenteeism, loss of economic productivity, and household impoverishment [3]. Malaria in Benin is predominantly caused by Plasmodium falciparum, which is transmitted almost exclusively by mosquitoes of the Anopheles gambiae sensu lato complex, a group of sibling species uniquely adapted to the ecological and climatic conditions of West Africa [4, 5].

The control of malaria transmission has relied heavily on vector control interventions, primarily insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS), both of which have been credited with driving substantial reductions in malaria burden across Africa over the past two decades [6, 7]. These strategies depend almost entirely on synthetic pyrethroids, a class of insecticides valued for their low mammalian toxicity, rapid knockdown effect, and residual activity. Larval source management, including the use of biological larvicides, such as Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) and environmental modifications, provides a complementary approach for reducing the number of mosquito breeding sites [8]. However, the sustained effectiveness of these interventions is now seriously threatened by the emergence and accelerating spread of insecticide resistance in Anopheles populations throughout sub-Saharan Africa. Resistance to pyrethroids (the cornerstone of both ITN and IRS programs) has been documented in virtually every malaria-endemic country on the continent, including Benin, where mortality rates following standard pyrethroid exposure have repeatedly fallen well below the WHO susceptibility threshold of 98% [9–11]. The operational consequences are alarming: the compromised efficacy of treated nets translates directly into increased malaria transmission, eroding the gains achieved at considerable cost over recent decades.

A critical but insufficiently studied dimension of insecticide resistance in West Africa is the contribution of agricultural pesticide use to the selection and amplification of resistant mosquito populations. The agricultural economy of Benin relies heavily on cotton, maize, rice, and vegetable production, all of which are associated with the intensive and often poorly regulated application of synthetic pyrethroids, organophosphates, and other chemical classes [11]. Farmers frequently apply the same active ingredients used in public health programs (deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, and permethrin) for crop protection, creating dual selection pressure on Anopheles larvae that breed in agricultural water bodies such as irrigation canals, flooded fields, and stagnant pools formed by rainfall and irrigation [11, 12]. This cross-contamination of selection pressures between the agricultural and public health sectors represents one of the most urgent challenges in integrated vector management. Comparative studies conducted in West Africa have consistently revealed higher levels of phenotypic resistance, greater frequencies of knockdown resistance (kdr) mutation, and elevated metabolic enzyme activities in mosquito populations from agricultural areas than in those from non-agricultural control sites [11, 13]. However, in Benin, the systematic investigation of this relationship, which combines phenotypic, molecular, enzymatic, and transcriptomic approaches across agricultural and non-agricultural settings, has remained largely insufficient.

Insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l. is mediated by two principal classes of mechanisms. Target-site resistance, most notably the knockdown resistance (kdr) mutation at codon L1014 of the voltage-gated sodium channel gene (VGSC), reduces the binding affinity of pyrethroids and DDT to their molecular target, conferring strong phenotypic resistance [14]. In West Africa, the L1014F substitution (kdr-West) has reached very high frequencies in Anopheles coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s. populations, and its near-fixation at many sites has been associated with confirmed resistance to multiple pyrethroids [9, 15]. Metabolic resistance constitutes the second major mechanism, involving the enzymatic detoxification of insecticides before they reach their target site. Three enzyme families are principally implicated: cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYP450s), glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and esterases. The overexpression of genes encoding these enzymes, particularly CYP6P3, CYP6M2, CYP9K1, CYP6Z1, and GSTE2, has been documented in pyrethroid-resistant populations across West Africa, and their transcriptional regulation is known to be modulated by environmental insecticide exposure [16–18]. The co-occurrence of both target-site and metabolic mechanisms in the same populations, so-called “multifactorial resistance”, is of particular concern, because it may render current insecticide classes ineffective across a broad spectrum of exposure concentrations and may accelerate the failure of next-generation intervention tools.

Despite growing awareness of the resistance problem in Benin, important knowledge gaps persist. Few studies have directly and simultaneously compared insecticide resistance levels between agricultural and non-agricultural settings using a comprehensive, multi-biomarker approach that integrates phenotypic bioassays, molecular species identification, kdr genotyping, detoxification enzyme activity assays, and cytochrome P450 gene expression profiling by RT–qPCR. Furthermore, the contribution of agricultural pesticide use to the selection of specific resistance alleles and metabolic phenotypes (relative to public health insecticide pressure from ITNs and IRS) remains poorly characterized in the Beninese context. This knowledge gap has direct consequences for the formulation of rational resistance management policies, which require a precise understanding of the ecological and anthropogenic drivers of resistance at the local level [13]. Without this evidence base, decisions on insecticide rotation, the choice of intervention tools, and intersectoral coordination between agriculture and public health are made in an information vacuum, jeopardizing the long-term effectiveness of malaria control programs.

The present study is, therefore, of scientific and public health significance for several reasons. First, it addresses a documented evidence gap by providing, for the first time in southern Benin, a simultaneous and multi-mechanistic comparison of pyrethroid resistance between two high-intensity agricultural areas (Sèmè-Kpodji and Abomey-Calavi) and one urban non-agricultural control site (Yenawa, Cotonou). This comparative design allows the direct attribution of resistance differentials to agricultural versus non-agricultural selection pressures, a distinction that is operationally critical for guiding vector control recommendations. Second, the study employs a comprehensive panel of resistance biomarkers, spanning from the phenotypic (WHO tube bioassays) to the molecular (PCR-based species identification and kdr genotyping), biochemical (CYP450, esterase, and GST enzyme assays), and transcriptomic (RT–qPCR of CYP9K1, CYP6Z1, and GSTE2) levels, enabling a holistic characterization of the resistance phenotype that goes well beyond what most surveillance studies achieve. Third, the study was conducted with Anopheles coluzzii, the dominant malaria vector in southern Benin, whose resistance profile directly determines the operational effectiveness of malaria interventions in this high-burden setting. Fourth, the findings contribute to the baseline evidence needed for the implementation of the WHO Global Plan for Insecticide Resistance Management (GPIRM) and national resistance monitoring frameworks, at a time when next-generation insecticidal tools (including dual-active-ingredient LLINs incorporating chlorfenapyr and pyriproxyfen) are being introduced in Benin [18]. Fifth, the study responds to the call of Ahouandjinou et al. [19] for context-specific, multi-site resistance characterization studies that integrate agricultural land use as an explicit variable in the epidemiological analysis of insecticide resistance. Together, these elements make the present study a timely, methodologically robust, and operationally relevant contribution to the fight against malaria in one of the most affected regions of sub-Saharan Africa.

In this context, the present study, entitled “Agricultural Practices and Multifactorial Insecticide Resistance in an Anopheles coluzzii Population from Southern Benin,” aims to (i) assess the level of pyrethroid susceptibility in An. gambiae s.l. populations from agricultural and non-agricultural sites using WHO standard bioassays with deltamethrin and alpha-cypermethrin; (ii) determine the species composition within the An. gambiae s.l. complex and the frequency of the kdr-West mutation (L1014F) at each site; (iii) quantify the activities of major detoxification enzyme classes (cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, alpha- and beta-esterases, and glutathione S-transferases) in field populations; and (iv) evaluate the relative expression of the resistance-associated genes CYP9K1, CYP6Z1, and GSTE2 by RT‒qPCR to elucidate the contribution of transcriptional regulation to the metabolic resistance phenotype. By combining these four complementary approaches, this study aims to provide an integrated picture of the drivers of pyrethroid resistance in southern Benin that can inform evidence-based vector control strategies at both the national and regional levels.

Materials and methods

Study areas

Field collections were conducted at three sites representative of different levels of agricultural activity in southern Benin (Fig. 1): (i) Sèmé-Kpodji (6°22′ N, 2°37′ E), a peri-urban locality in the Ouémé Department characterized by intensive maize and vegetable production with frequent pesticide applications; (ii) Abomey-Calavi (6° 26′ 55″ N, 2° 21′ 20″ E), located in the Atlantic Department, where mixed farming systems, including vegetable, rice, and cotton cultivation, are associated with regular insecticide use; and (iii) Yenawa, Cotonou (6°22′ N, 2°26′ E), a densely populated urban district with no agricultural activity, was selected as a non-agricultural control site. The three localities experience a humid tropical climate with annual temperatures ranging from 26 to 30 °C and rainfall concentrated during the major rainy season. Numerous aquatic habitats, including temporary puddles, drainage channels, and irrigation canals, provide suitable breeding sites for Anopheles coluzzii populations. Mosquito collection

Anopheles larvae and pupae were collected from natural breeding sites in the three study areas and reared to adults under standard insectary conditions (27 ± 2 °C, 75 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 12 h:12 h light–dark photoperiod). The ability to record immature stage mosquitoes in the field under controlled laboratory conditions ensured the availability of enough adult mosquitoes of known age and physiological status for insecticide susceptibility bioassays. Only 3–5-day-old, non-blood-fed female mosquitoes were used for all experiments.

WHO tube bioassays

Insecticide susceptibility was assessed following WHO standard tube bioassay protocols [20]. The diagnostic concentrations used were deltamethrin 0.05% and alpha-cypermethrin 0.05% (pyrethroids). Batches of 20–25 females were exposed for 1 h to insecticide-impregnated papers, transferred to holding tubes and provided with 10% glucose solution. For pyrethroids (permethrin, deltamethrin, and alpha-cypermethrin), mortality was recorded 24 h postexposure in accordance with WHO susceptibility test guidelines. A minimum of 80 mosquitoes were tested per insecticide per site, and the control groups were exposed to untreated papers. Mortality rates were corrected via Abbott’s formula when control mortality ranged between 5% and 20%. The results were interpreted according to the WHO criteria: susceptibility ≥ 98%, possible resistance 90–97% and confirmed resistance < 90%.

Molecular identification and genotyping of the kdr-L995F mutation

Following bioassays, a subset of 90 mosquitoes (30 per site) was randomly selected from both dead and surviving individuals to avoid bias in the estimation of species composition and resistance allele frequencies. The selected samples were individually preserved in 1.5 mL of ethanol (80%) at −20 °C until further processing. Genomic DNA was extracted via DNeasy Blood and Tissue kits (Qiagen, France) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Molecular species identification was performed on all the selected An. gambiae s.l. using the protocol described by [21]. Genotyping of the kdr-L995F mutation was carried out according to the protocol of Martinez-Torres et al. [14], followed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Biochemical analysis of detoxification enzyme activity

Enzyme activity assays were conducted on 3–5-day-old female Anopheles mosquitoes reared without insecticide exposure to evaluate the activity of major detoxification enzymes. Analyses focused on the main enzymes, including cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and esterases, following standardized protocols described by Brogdon & McAllister [22]. Each mosquito, stored at −80 °C, was homogenized in 200 µL of distilled water on ice and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 2 min to obtain clarified extracts. For cytochrome P450 oxydase assays, 20 µL of extract was mixed with 80 µL of phosphate buffer and 200 µL of TMBZ (3,3',5,5'-tétraméthylbenzidine), which, in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, is oxidized by the heme group of cytochrome P450 enzymes, producing a blue compound. This was followed by the addition of 25 µL of hydrogen peroxide. After a 30-min incubation, the absorbance was measured at 630 nm. The oxidase activity was expressed as nmol of cytochrome P450 per mg of protein. For non-specific esterases, 10 µL of extract was added to 90 µL of 1% TBS (Tris-buffered saline) and incubated for 10 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of substrate (α- or β-naphthyl acetate) and 100 µL of Fast Garnett Salt developer were added. The final absorbance was read at 550 nm, and the enzyme activity was expressed as µmol of naphthol formed per minute per mg of protein. The total protein content was determined by the mixing of two replicates of 10 µL of homogenate with 290 µL of Coomassie Plus protein assay reagent. The mixture was subsequently incubated for 5 min at room temperature, after which the total protein content was read at 590 nm. GST activity was measured by adding 10 µL of extract to 200 µL of a reaction mixture containing 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), reduced glutathione (GSH), and CDNB (1-chloro-2–4-dinitrobenzene). The reaction kinetics were monitored at 340 nm for 5 min, and the activity was expressed as nmol of GSH conjugate formed per minute per mg of protein.

Transcriptomic analysis using RT–qPCR

Before the samples were pooled for RNA extraction, the legs of each mosquito were collected, and genomic DNA was extracted with 2% cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB). Molecular identification of the species belonging to An. gambiae s.l. complex was performed by PCR.

Mosquitoes that were previously identified were individually selected and then pooled for RNA collection. A Qiagen Tissue Lyser II homogenizer (Qiagen; Hilden, Germany) was used to homogenize groups of five An. coluzzii mosquitoes (the predominant vector species tested) sharing the same phenotypes, using 5 mm stainless steel beads. Total RNA was subsequently extracted using the Qiagen RNeasy 96 Kit (Qiagen; Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Approximately 2 μg of each RNA sample was processed using RNase-free DNase RQ1 (Promega; Southampton, UK). Following DNase treatment, RNA (1 μg) from each mosquito group was used for cDNA synthesis with a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems; Loughborough, UK) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The expression of three metabolic genes (CYP6Z1, CYP9K1 and GSTE2), which are often overexpressed in vectors from Benin and neighboring nations in West Africa, was measured using real-time quantitative RT‒qPCR [18]. These three genes were selected a priori on the basis of their consistent association with pyrethroid resistance phenotypes in An. gambiae s.l. populations from Benin and neighboring West African countries [11, 23] and to complement the enzymatic assays (CYP450, esterases, GST) performed in this study rather than providing an exhaustive screen of all known detoxification genes. The DNA sequences and primer efficiency are presented in Table 1. The quantitative PCR (qPCR) efficiency for each targeted gene was assessed by generating a standard curve using a 1:5 serial dilution of cDNA samples. Each 10 μL reaction contained 2 μL of cDNA, 10 μM primer for each gene, and 5 μL of Roche FastStart Essential DNA Green 2× master mix (Roche; Welwyn Garden City, UK). All reactions were performed in triplicate using a StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems; Loughborough, UK). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 10 min at 95 °C; 35 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, 22 s at 60 °C, and 10 s at 72 °C; and a subsequent melting curve analysis. Relative expression factor of each targeted gene from field samples associated with a susceptible laboratory strain (An. coluzzii “N'Gousso”), were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method [24], considering the PCR efficiency. The housekeeping gene RpS7 served as a reference for normalization.

Statistical analysis

All raw data for bioassay testing, molecular genotyping, enzymatic assay readings, and RT‒qPCR were first compiled and organized in Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team), except for the analysis of the enzymatic assay data, which was carried out using GraphPad Prism version 5.

Mortality rates from insecticide susceptibility bioassays were compared among mosquito populations using the Kruskal–Wallis test, given the proportional and potentially no normal distribution of the data. The genotypic (RR, RS, and SS) and allelic (R and S) frequencies of the kdr mutation were calculated from observed counts, and differences in genotype distribution among populations were assessed using the chi-square (χ2) test. Enzyme activities are expressed as the mean ± standard error. Data normality was first verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences among the three populations were then assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons when a significant overall effect was detected.

Relative gene expression (CYP9K1, CYP6Z1, and GSTE2) was calculated from RT‒qPCR data using the 2−ΔΔCt method. After verifying normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance, differences among populations were evaluated using one-way ANOVA. For all analyses, differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Susceptibility to pyrethroids

Susceptibility bioassays confirmed resistance to both alpha-cypermethrin and deltamethrin across the three study sites (Fig. 2). The mortality rate following exposure to deltamethrin was 78.0% (95% CI 70.2–85.8) in Yenawa, 82.0% (95% CI 74.2–89.8) in Sèmè-Kpodji, and 81.0% (95% CI 73.2–88.8) in Calavi. For alpha-cypermethrin, the mortality rates were 89.0% (95% CI 81.2–96.8) in Yenawa, 36.0% (95% CI 32.1–39.9) in Sèmè-Kpodji, and 52.0% (95% CI 46.1–57.9) in Abomey-Calavi.Fig. 2Mortality rates (%) of Anopheles coluzzii exposed to deltamethrin and alpha-cypermethrin in Yenawa, Sèmè-Kpodji and Calavi. Error bars represent standard errors. The dashed green line indicates the WHO resistance threshold (90%), and the dashed red line indicates the WHO susceptibility threshold (100%)

All mortality rates were below the WHO resistance threshold of 90%, indicating confirmed resistance to both pyrethroids in all populations tested. Resistance to alpha-cypermethrin was particularly pronounced in Sèmè-Kpodji and Calavi, where mortality did not exceed 52%, whereas mortality following deltamethrin exposure remained relatively similar across sites (between 78% and 82%).

Molecular identification of species within the Anopheles coluzzii complex and kdr-west genotypes

All 90 mosquitoes analyzed (30 per site) were identified as Anopheles coluzzii. No other species of the Anopheles coluzzii complex were detected in Yenawa, Sèmè-Kpodji, or Calavi.

The West-kdr mutation was detected at a high frequency in all Anopheles coluzzii populations analyzed (Fig. 3). The resistant homozygous genotype (RR) predominated in Yenawa (86.7%, 95% CI 74.5–98.8) and Sèmè-Kpodji (63.3%, 95% CI 46.1–80.6), whereas the heterozygous genotype (RS) was more common in Calavi (53.3%, 95% CI 35.5–71.2). Consistent with the genotypic distribution, the resistant allele (R) occurred at high frequencies, reaching 93.3% (95% CI 87.0–99.6) in Yenawa, 81.7% (95% CI 71.9–91.4) in Sèmè-Kpodji, and 73.3% (95% CI 62.1–84.5) in Calavi. The distribution of kdr genotypes differed significantly among the three study sites (χ2 = 10.73, df = 2, p = 0.0047), with Yenawa showing the highest proportion of RR homozygotes and Calavi the highest proportion of RS heterozygotes. Similarly, R allele frequencies differed significantly across sites (χ2 = 8.50, df = 2, p = 0.014), confirming that the intensity of kdr-mediated resistance varies significantly by locality.

Metabolic enzyme activities in Anopheles coluzzii populations

Significant differences in detoxification enzyme activities were observed among Anopheles coluzzii populations from the three study sites (Fig. 4). Cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (CYP450) activity was significantly greater in Sèmè-Kpodji and Yenawa than in Calavi (p = 0.0011 and p = 0.042, respectively), whereas no difference was detected between Sèmè-Kpodji and Yenawa (p = 0.5816). Overall, elevated CYP450, GST, and α-esterase activities, particularly in Sèmè-Kpodji, suggest the involvement of metabolic detoxification mechanisms in insecticide resistance in southern Benin An. coluzzii populations.

Discussion

In sub-Saharan Africa, malaria remains one of the most pressing public health challenges, with Anopheles gambiae s.l. serving as the primary vector responsible for the vast majority of Plasmodium falciparum transmission. The present study investigated the interplay between agricultural insecticide exposure and pyrethroid resistance mechanisms in malaria vectors from three ecologically contrasted sites in southern Benin. Our findings provide multidimensional evidence (integrating bioassays, molecular identification, kdr genotyping, enzymatic assays, and RT‒qPCR) that contributes substantially to the understanding of resistance dynamics in this region.

Molecular species identification of mosquito populations from the three study sites revealed the presence of Anopheles coluzzii only, with 100% of the 90 samples analyzed displaying the characteristic molecular profile of this species across Yenawa, Sèmè-Kpodji, and Calavi. These findings are consistent with numerous studies conducted in Benin and the wider West African region, which have repeatedly identified An. coluzzii as the dominant or exclusive member of the An. gambiae s.l. complex in urban, peri-urban, and agricultural settings [9, 25]. In the rice-growing and vegetable-farming areas of Benin, the near-exclusive occurrence of An. coluzzii has been attributed to the ecological preference of this species for permanent or semi-permanent water bodies associated with irrigation systems [26]. Similarly, Ahouandjinou et al. [19] reported the exclusive presence of An. coluzzii across study sites in southern Benin during a longitudinal insecticide resistance monitoring effort, confirming the species’ ecological dominance in this subregion. This homogeneity implies that the observed differences in resistance levels are not attributable to species-level variation but rather to differential ecological and anthropogenic selection pressures, including the intensity and nature of insecticide use in each locality.

Susceptibility bioassays revealed widespread resistance to both deltamethrin and alpha-cypermethrin across all three study sites, with mortality rates consistently below the WHO confirmation threshold of 90%. For deltamethrin, mortality ranged from 78% in Yenawa to 82% in Sèmè-Kpodji, remaining uniformly low regardless of the agricultural context. This finding is not unexpected: although Yenawa lacks agricultural insecticide inputs, it is subject to intense non-agricultural selection pressure from urban public health interventions, including widespread deployment of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS) campaigns, and household use of pyrethroid-based aerosols and coils, which together apply cumulative pyrethroid pressure comparable to, or exceeding, that of agricultural pesticide use [15, 27]. This urban selection dynamic, further supported by the elevated kdr-West allele frequency and CYP450 activity observed in Yenawa (discussed below), indicates that non-agricultural settings in densely populated areas can independently drive high levels of pyrethroid resistance and should not be assumed to serve as low-pressure control sites in future comparative studies. This spatial homogeneity is consistent with the near-fixation of the kdr-West mutation documented across the study area and aligns with numerous reports of widespread deltamethrin resistance in Benin published over the past decade [15, 28]. Yahouédo et al. [16] reported deltamethrin mortality rates less than 90% in all monitored villages in southern Benin between 2012 and 2014, a trend reinforced by more recent assessments following large-scale LLIN deployment. In contrast, alpha-cypermethrin susceptibility showed pronounced spatial differentiation: mortality reached 89% at the non-agricultural control site (Yenawa), approaching the WHO susceptibility threshold, whereas it decreased drastically to 36% at Sèmè-Kpodji and 52% at Calavi. This ecological gradient is consistent with the hypothesis that intensive agricultural pesticide use may amplify pyrethroid selection pressure in farming areas, although a causal relationship cannot be firmly established from this cross-sectional design, in agreement with previous studies and national resistance trends [9, 11, 15]. These findings are further corroborated by Ahouandjinou et al. [19], who reported significantly lower pyrethroid susceptibility in An. coluzzii populations from areas with high agricultural pesticide inputs compared with those from urban control sites in Benin and identified alpha-cypermethrin as particularly discriminating for detecting agricultural resistance. The differential responses to the two pyrethroids tested may also reflect variations in the biochemical substrate specificity of resistance mechanisms, notably P450-mediated detoxification, and the distinct chemical structures of deltamethrin and alpha-cypermethrin, which may lead to different metabolic degradation efficiencies [29, 30].

Genotyping of the kdr-West (L1014F) mutation revealed very high frequencies of the resistant allele (R) across all populations: 0.93 in Yenawa, 0.82 in Sèmè-Kpodji, and 0.73 in Calavi. The homozygous resistant genotype (RR) predominated in Yenawa (86.7%) and Sèmè-Kpodji (63.3%), whereas the heterozygous genotype (RS) was more common in Calavi (53.3%). The complete absence of the susceptible homozygous genotype (SS) indicates that the kdr-West allele is approaching fixation throughout these regions. These results are broadly consistent with the resistance landscape documented across Benin. Tokponnon et al. [9] reported kdr allele frequencies between 0.75 and 0.95 across multiple sentinel sites in Benin, confirming that the mutation is ubiquitous regardless of land use context. Ahouandjinou et al. [19] further demonstrated that kdr-West frequencies above 0.80 are now common in both agricultural and urban An. coluzzii populations in southern Benin, suggesting near-saturation under cumulative selection pressure from combined vector control interventions and agricultural chemicals. The counterintuitive finding of a higher R allele frequency in the non-agricultural site (Yenawa) than in the agricultural site can be explained by several non-exclusive mechanisms. First, the intensive use of pyrethroid-based ITNs and IRSs in densely populated urban settings constitutes a powerful selection force that may rival or exceed agricultural pressure [9, 18]. Second, gene flow through active mosquito dispersal between sites could homogenize allele frequencies across the landscape [29]. Third, founder effects and genetic drift in smaller or more isolated agricultural populations may partially offset selection at the R allele. The progressive decrease in R allele frequency from Yenawa (0.93) to Sèmè-Kpodji (0.82) to Calavi (0.73) may reflect these population genetic dynamics alongside differences in the nature and duration of local insecticide application regimes. Notably, this pattern reveals an apparent genotype–phenotype mismatch: Yenawa presented both the highest kdr-West-resistant allele frequency and the highest phenotypic susceptibility to alpha-cypermethrin (89% mortality), whereas Sèmè-Kpodji and Abomey-Calavi, despite lower kdr-West frequencies, presented markedly stronger phenotypic resistance (36% and 52% mortality, respectively). This finding indicates that the kdr-West genotype alone is a poor predictor of phenotypic resistance intensity in this context and strongly suggests that metabolic detoxification mechanisms (rather than target-site resistance) are the primary drivers of the elevated alpha-cypermethrin resistance observed at agricultural sites. This interpretation is further supported by the elevated α-esterase activity specifically detected in Sèmè-Kpodji, suggesting a mechanism-specific rather than allele-frequency-driven explanation for the observed resistance gradient.

In addition to target-site mutations, metabolic resistance mechanisms are characterized by significant variation in detoxification enzyme activities among populations. Compared with that in Calavi, cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (CYP450) activity was significantly elevated in both Sèmè-Kpodji and Yenawa, suggesting enhanced oxidative detoxification capacity in both urban and agricultural contexts. The elevated CYP450 activity in Yenawa (a non-agricultural site) indicates that factors independent of agricultural insecticide use, such as the widespread deployment of household pyrethroid aerosols, ITNs, and IRS campaigns, substantially upregulate detoxification enzymes in urban An. coluzzii. This conclusion is consistent with that of Ahouandjinou et al. [19], who reported significantly elevated P450 activities in urban An. coluzzii populations from Cotonou despite the absence of agricultural activity and attributed this pattern to pervasive domestic insecticide use and pyrethroid-treated nets. These observations align with the broader literature documenting P450-mediated resistance in West African Anopheles populations following sustained public health interventions [31, 32]. Alpha-esterase activity was significantly elevated in Sèmè-Kpodji relative to Calavi, which is consistent with localized induction associated with the intensive application of organophosphate and pyrethroid pesticides in vegetable farming. The absence of significant differences between Yenawa and Sèmè-Kpodji suggests that esterase induction is context-dependent and may reflect the specific pesticide classes used locally [32]. Beta-esterase activity was elevated specifically in Yenawa compared with Calavi, a pattern that diverges from simple agricultural induction models and highlights the role of non-agricultural insecticide sources in urban environments. Glutathione S-transferase (GST) activity was significantly greater in both Sèmè-Kpodji and Yenawa than in Calavi, confirming the multifunctional role of GSTs in insecticide detoxification and the oxidative stress response [32, 33]. The absence of significant differences in total protein content among populations validates the enzymatic comparisons, ensuring that the observed variation in enzyme activity reflects genuine metabolic regulation rather than sampling artifacts.

Gene expression analysis using RT–qPCR revealed a notable paradox: despite the elevated enzymatic activities described above, the relative expression levels of CYP9K1, CYP6Z1, and GSTe2 were consistently below the transcriptional level of the susceptible N’Gousso reference strain (i.e., a fold change of less than 1) across all three populations. This disconnect between enzyme activity and gene expression suggests that increased detoxification capacity may be driven by post-transcriptional or post-translational regulatory mechanisms, including protein stabilization, increased substrate availability, or allelic variants conferring increased specific enzyme activity, rather than by simple transcriptional upregulation. For CYP6Z1, the gene showing the highest relative expression across populations (fold change: 0.58 in Yenawa, 0.52 in Sèmè-Kpodji, 0.26 in Calavi), expression was numerically greater in the non-agricultural site, mirroring the enzymatic data. The expression of CYP9K1 was also greater in Yenawa (0.21) than in agricultural sites (0.07 and 0.06), whereas the expression of GSTE2 remained uniformly low (0.03–0.06) in all populations. Despite these low fold-change values, ANOVA of the delta-Ct values revealed statistically significant inter-population differences for all three genes (CYP9K1: F = 12.87, p = 0.00198; CYP6Z1: F = 5.12, p = 0.0288; GSTe2: F = 23.36, p = 0.00026), indicating real but quantitatively modest transcriptional differences among populations. These results contrast with studies from Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, and Burkina Faso, where CYP9K1 and CYP6Z1 were strongly overexpressed (fold changes > 2) in agricultural An. gambiae populations subjected to intensive pyrethroid regimes [34–36]. The absence of major gene overexpression in this study may reflect (i) the moderate or heterogeneous nature of agricultural insecticide pressure in the study areas relative to the cotton belt of West Africa; (ii) the predominance of kdr-mediated target-site resistance over metabolic resistance as the primary mechanism; (iii) the involvement of other detoxification genes not included in the present panel, such as CYP6P3, CYP6M2, or CYP6P4, which are identified as major metabolic resistance contributors in southern Benin [16, 18]; or (iv) population-specific regulatory differences in resistance gene expression. Similarly, Ahouandjinou et al. [19] reported a disconnect between the enzymatic activity and transcript-level overexpression of classical resistance genes in Beninese An. coluzzii and proposed that post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms and enzyme isoform variation constitute underappreciated contributors to metabolic resistance in this species.

The comparison between agricultural and non-agricultural sites provides important insights into the relative contributions of different selection forces to resistance evolution. In contrast to the hypothesis that agricultural sites would consistently present relatively high resistance levels across all measured parameters, several indicators (including kdr allele frequency, CYP450 activity, beta-esterase activity, and gene expression levels) were actually relatively high or comparable at the non-agricultural urban site (Yenawa). This suggests that the urban environment exerts substantial independent selection pressure through domestic insecticide use, ITN deployment, and IRS campaigns. This observation is consistent with the growing body of evidence that urban malaria control interventions can be as potent a driver of insecticide resistance as agricultural chemicals, particularly in densely populated cities, such as Cotonou [9, 18]. Nevertheless, the alpha-cypermethrin bioassay results and alpha-esterase activities were particularly poor in agricultural areas, suggesting that agricultural chemical exposure may selectively contribute to resistance in certain enzyme systems and for specific insecticide molecules. This differential pattern underscores the complexity of resistance management in areas, where both agricultural and public health insecticide use co-occur and highlights that strategies addressing only one sector are unlikely to be sufficient for sustained resistance mitigation.

From a public health perspective, the multifactorial resistance documented in this study has direct implications for malaria vector control in Benin. The near-fixation of the kdr-West allele across all sites, combined with elevated detoxification enzyme activities, indicates that standard pyrethroid-based interventions (including conventional LLINs and many IRS formulations) face significant efficacy challenges throughout southern Benin, irrespective of local land use. The deployment of dual-acting-ingredient nets incorporating compounds with novel modes of action, such as chlorfenapyr and pyriproxyfen, represents a promising strategy to overcome pyrethroid resistance. Sovi et al. [18] demonstrated in a 3-year cluster-randomized trial in Benin that these nets could reduce pyrethroid resistance selection pressure, although their resistance-mitigating effect was diminished after 24 months, emphasizing the need for regular monitoring and planned rotation of intervention tools. However, the long-term efficacy of pyriproxyfen-based dual-acting-ingredient nets should not be assumed to be permanent, as emerging resistance to pyriproxyfen has already been documented in other mosquito vector species, cited here only as a cross-species precedent rather than a direct comparator for Anopheles, with resistance ratios varying across field populations of Aedes aegypti in Brazil [37], underscoring the need for continuous monitoring of resistance to this insect growth regulator alongside pyrethroids. Ahouandjinou et al. [19] proposed an integrated resistance monitoring framework for Benin that incorporates land use classification, seasonal sampling, and multi-biomarker assessment (phenotypic, genotypic, enzymatic, and transcriptomic), which could be applied in future surveillance cycles in Sèmè-Kpodji, Calavi, and Yenawa to track resistance trajectories and guide evidence-based intervention adjustments.

A critical gap highlighted by the present findings is the lack of intersectoral coordination between the agricultural and public health sectors in the management of insecticide use and resistance. In Benin, farmers routinely apply synthetic pyrethroids and organophosphates to food crops, often without training on resistance management or awareness of consequences for malaria vector control [11, 12]. The selection of resistance by agricultural chemicals not only reduces the effectiveness of vector control interventions but also may accelerate the spread of resistance alleles to neighboring populations through mosquito dispersal [29]. Ahouandjinou et al. [19] called for the establishment of a national insecticide resistance management committee in Benin to coordinate recommendations across the agriculture and health sectors, standardize resistance surveillance protocols, and enforce insecticide rotation policies. At the operational level, the promotion of integrated pest management (IPM) in agricultural settings that combine biological control agents such as Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis, mechanical barriers, crop diversification, and judicious chemical use could substantially reduce the selection pressure exerted on mosquito populations and prolong the useful life of public health insecticides.

Several methodological considerations should be noted when interpreting the results of this study. First, the sample size used for RT‒qPCR (pools of five mosquitoes per group, limited biological replicates) may constrain the statistical power of expression analyses; future studies should aim for individual-level profiling with larger sample sizes. Second, the gene panel assessed (CYP9K1, CYP6Z1, and GSTE2) represents only a subset of detoxification genes implicated in pyrethroid resistance in An. gambiae s.l.; genes, such as CYP6P3, CYP6M2, CYP6P4, and GSTD3, identified as major resistance contributors in southern Benin [16, 18], were not assessed and may account for additional resistance phenotype variance. Third, the study was conducted at a single timepoint, which does not capture seasonal variation or resistance trends over time. Longitudinal sampling across multiple seasons and years, as implemented in the multi-year surveillance framework of [19], would be necessary to evaluate the resistance trajectory and assess the impact of vector control campaigns. Fourth, the absence of insecticide residue measurements in agricultural fields and water bodies limits the ability to quantify the actual pesticide exposure experienced by larval and adult mosquito populations at each site, which would strengthen causal inference about agricultural selection pressure.

Overall, the combined evidence from kdr mutation frequencies, detoxification enzyme activities, and gene expression profiles indicates that pyrethroid resistance in Anopheles coluzzii from southern Benin is likely a multifactorial phenomenon driven by the convergence of target-site mutations and metabolic detoxification mechanisms, shaped by both agricultural insecticide exposure and non-agricultural selection pressures, including vector control interventions. The near-fixation of the kdr-West allele across all study sites, the elevated activities of multiple detoxification enzyme classes, and the context-specific modulation of enzymatic responses together paint a picture of deeply entrenched, multi-mechanistic resistance. The absence of strong transcriptional overexpression of classical metabolic resistance genes despite elevated enzyme activities points to the importance of post-transcriptional regulation and the potential contribution of additional resistance genes not yet characterized in this population. These findings call for an integrated, evidence-based approach to resistance management that simultaneously addresses agricultural chemical use, optimizes vector control tool deployment, and establishes robust longitudinal multi-site surveillance systems. The data generated by this study represent an important contribution to the resistance baseline for southern Benin and should inform the planning of future insecticide resistance management strategies in collaboration with national malaria control programs, agricultural authorities, and international research partners.

Conclusion

This study assessed the influence of agricultural practices on insecticide resistance in Anopheles coluzzii populations across three sites in Benin, including two agricultural areas (Sèmè-Kpodji and Calavi) and one non-agricultural area (Yenawa). The data reveal widespread resistance to pyrethroids, as demonstrated by low mortality rates in susceptibility tests, the high prevalence of kdr mutations, and the modulation of key metabolic genes. Enzymatic analyses revealed that oxidases, esterases, and glutathione S-transferases play key roles in insecticide detoxification. Although stronger modulation was observed in agricultural areas, significant activity was also detected at the non-agricultural site, indicating that resistance is influenced by multiple environmental factors. Overall, our findings suggest that resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l. in this study area is a multifactorial phenomenon likely involving agricultural chemical pressure, local environmental conditions, and genetic adaptation, although further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these dynamics. These findings emphasize the need for integrated vector control strategies that incorporate agricultural pesticide management, resistance monitoring, and alternative control tools to limit the spread of resistant mosquito populations and improve malaria control effectiveness in Benin. Further studies are recommended to explore the interactions between agricultural practices, environmental factors, and insecticide resistance, particularly the role of domestic insecticide use and gene flow between mosquito populations. In addition, alternative vector control strategies such as insecticide rotation, biological control methods, and integrated vector management programs should be strengthened to slow resistance development and improve malaria control outcomes.
