Spectral decomposition of power-bounded operators: The finite spectrum case
Shiho Oi, Jyamira Oppekepenguin

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate power-bounded operators, including surjective isometries, on Banach spaces. Koehler and Rosenthal asserted that an isolated point in the spectrum of a surjective isometry on a Banach space lies in the point spectrum, with the corresponding eigenspace having an invariant complement. However, they did not provide a detailed proof of this claim, at least as understood by the authors of this manuscript. Here, by applications of a theorem of Gelfand and the Riesz projections, we demonstrate that the theorem of Koehler and Rosenthal holds for any power-bounded operator on a Banach space. This not only furnishes a detailed proof of the theorem but also slightly generalizes its scope. As a result, we establish that if T : X → X T:X\to X is a power-bounded operator on a Banach space X X whose spectrum consists of finitely many points λ 1 , λ 2 , … , λ m {\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m}} , then for every 1 ≤ i , j ≤ m 1\leq i,j\leq m , there exist projections P j P_{j} on X X such that P i ​ P j = δ i ​ j ​ P i P_{i}P_{j}=\delta_{ij}P_{i} , ∑ j = 1 m P j = I \sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{j}=I , and T = Σ j = 1 m ​ λ j ​ P j T=\Sigma_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}P_{j} . It follows that such an operator T T is an algebraic operator.

1.
Introduction

Sz.-Nagy
[
9
]
characterized a power-bounded operator on a Hilbert space: an invertible bounded complex linear operator on a Hilbert space is power-bounded if and only if it is similar to a unitary operator. Consequently, a power-bounded operator on a Hilbert space admits a spectral decomposition by applying a spectral measure. What about the case of a Banach space operator?
The origin of the problem can be traced back to a theorem of Gelfand
[
3
]
, which characterizes the identity operator as a power-bounded operator with the spectrum
\{1\}
. Zemánek
[
11
]

provides a very informative and well-written account of developments related to this theorem.
Koehler and Rosenthal
[
8
]
investigated isometries with an isolated spectrum.
Botelho and Ilišević
[
2
]
studied isometries with finite spectrum. See also
[
6
,
7
]
.
Berkson, Gillespie, and Muhly explored
spectral decompositions of Banach space operators in a systematic manner in
[
1
]
.

This paper studies a power-bounded operator on an arbitrary complex Banach space with an isolated spectrum.
In this paper, a power-bounded operator
T
on a Banach space is
an invertible bounded operator which satisfies that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T^{n}\|<\infty,

(1)

where
\mathbb{Z}
is the set of all integers.
A surjective isometry is a power-bounded operator.
A theorem of Koehler and Rosenthal
[
8
]
states that if a surjective isometry on a Banach space has an isolated point in the spectrum, then the isolated point is a point spectrum (eigenvalue) of which eigenspace has a complemented subspace. The proof in
[
8
]
asserts that ”This follows from Stampfli’s work”,
which refers to the reference
[
10
]
.
However, while in
[
10
]
Stampfli primarily studied adjoint-abelian operators, which are generalizations of self-adjoint operators, there appears to be no exact statement or proof, except for Lemma 6 and its proof, that directly addresses iso-abelian operators.
It is worth noting that Koehler and Rosenthal demonstrated that an iso-abelian operator is equivalent to a surjective isometry
[
8
, Corollary 1]
. Nevertheless, the authors of this paper could not find a corresponding proof of the aforementioned theorem of Koehler and Rosenthal in the existing literature.
One of the primary objectives of this paper is to provide an exact proof of a slight generalization of this theorem,
stated as Theorem
3.2
, which we derive by applying a theorem of Gelfand
[
3
]
and the Riesz projections.
As a corollary, we exhibit the spectrum decomposition of a power-bounded operator with a finite spectrum in Corollary
3.3
.
Then, by applying a theorem of Ilišević
[
5
]
(Proposition
3.4
), we see that such an operator is algebraic.

Throughout the paper,
X
is a complex Banach space unless otherwise noted. The algebra of all bounded complex linear operators on
X
is denoted by
B(X)
. The identity operator on
X
is
I
. The spectrum of
T\in B(X)
is
\sigma(T)
. A point spectrum (eigenvalue) is an element
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
such that there exists
0\neq x\in X
with
T(x)=\lambda x
. If
\sigma(T)
consists only of
0
, we say that
T
is a quasi nilpotent.

A theorem of Gelfand

For the readers’ convenience and for the completeness of the paper, we exhibit a proof of a theorem of Gelfand (Theorem
2.3

[
3

).
Although Satz 1 in
[
3

seems to be stated for commutative Banach algebras, but it holds for non-commutative Banach algebras

To prove Theorem

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\chi\colon\mathbb{C}\to X
be an entire map.
Let
P(\theta)=\sum_{l=k}^{m}c_{l}e^{il\theta}
be a triangle polynomial with
c_{k}\neq 0
. Suppose that there exist real numbers
M>0
,
r_{0}>0
and a non-negative integer
\alpha
such that

|P(\theta)|\|\chi(re^{i\theta})\|\leq Mr^{\alpha},\quad\forall r\geq r_{0},\,\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

Then
\chi(w)
is a polynomial of
w
with a degree at most
\alpha
. In particular, if
\alpha=0
, then
\chi(w)
is a constant map.

Proof.

We have a Taylar expansion

\chi(w)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}a_{s}w^{s}.

For
r>0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
, put

J(r,p)=r^{-p}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P(\theta)\chi(re^{i\theta})e^{-(k+p)i\theta}d\theta.

Suppose that
p\in\mathbb{N}
is an arbitrary positive integer with
p>\alpha
. Letting
r\to\infty
we have

\|J(r,p)\|\leq Mr^{-(p-\alpha)}\to 0

(3)

by the inequality (
2
). We have

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(t+s-(k+p))\theta}=\begin{cases}1,&t+s=k+p\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

hence we get

J(r,p)=r^{-p}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\sum_{t=k}^{m}c_{t}e^{it\theta}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}a_{s}r^{s}e^{is\theta}e^{-(k+p)i\theta}d\theta\\
=\sum_{t=k}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}c_{t}a_{s}r^{s-p}\times\begin{cases}1,&t+s=k+p\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\
=\sum_{t=k}^{m}c_{t}a_{p+k-t}r^{k-t},

(4)

where
a_{p+k-t}=0
for
p+k-t<0
. Since
k\leq t\leq m
we have

\sum_{t=k}^{m}c_{t}a_{p+k-t}r^{k-t}\to c_{k}a_{p}

as
r\to\infty
. Since
c_{k}\neq 0
we have
a_{p}=0
by (
3
). This holds for every
p
with
p>\alpha
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
a
be an invertible element in a Banach algebra
B
with unit
e
.
Suppose that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|a^{n}\|<\infty.

Then, every element in the spectrum of
a
is unimodular.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
with
0<|\mu|<1
. Then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mu a)^{n}
converges and

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mu a)^{n}=(e-\mu a)^{-1}

(5)

by the condition (
2.2
). Hence
\frac{1}{\mu}\not\in\sigma(a)
.

Suppose that
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
with
1<|\mu|
. Again by (
2.2
), we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mu a)^{-n}=-(e-\mu a)^{-1}.

(6)

Thus
\frac{1}{\mu}\not\in\sigma(a)
.

As
a
is invertible,
0\not\in\sigma(a)
.

It follows from the above we see that
\sigma(a)\subset\{z\mid|z|=1\}
.
∎

3.
A proof of a theorem of Koehler and Rosenthal

We say that
P\in B(X)
is a projection if
P^{2}=P
. We do not assume a further property, such as the self-adjointness, even if
X
is a Hilbert space. We recall the definition of the Riesz projection.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T\in B(X)
. Suppose that
\sigma(T)
is the disjoint union of two closed subsets
\sigma
and
\tau
.
Suppose that
\Gamma
is a Cauchy contour in the resolvent set of
T
around
\sigma
separating
\sigma
from
\tau
. The operator

P_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}(T-wI)^{-1}dw

is called the Riesz projection of
T
corresponding to
\sigma
.

Note that the Riesz projection does not depend on the choice of the Cauchy contour around
\sigma
separating
\sigma
from
\tau
.
The Riesz projection is, in fact, a projection
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
.
Please refer to
[
4
]
for further properties of the Riesz projection.

The following is a generalization of Theorem 5 in
[
8
]
, which is stated for isometries.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be a complex Banach space and
T\in B(X)
an invertible operator. Suppose that
T
satisfies that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T^{n}\|<\infty.

(11)

Suppose that
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
is an isolated point in
\sigma(T)
.
Then
\lambda
is a point spectrum of
T
, and the eigenspace of
T
corresponding to
\lambda
is
P_{\lambda}(X)
and it has an invariant complement
\operatorname{ker}P_{\lambda}
, where
P_{\lambda}
is the Riesz projection of
T
corresponding to
\lambda
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
we have
|\lambda|=1
.
Let
P_{\lambda}
be the Riesz projection

P_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}(T-wI)^{-1}dw,

where
\Gamma
is a Cauchy
contour (in the resolvent set of
T
) around
\lambda
separating
\lambda
from
\sigma(T)\setminus\{\lambda\}
(see
[
4
]
).
Applying Theorem 2.2 in
[
4
]
, we have

M_{\lambda}\oplus L_{\lambda}=X
, where
M_{\lambda}=P_{\lambda}(X)
and
L_{\lambda}=\operatorname{ker}P_{\lambda}
,
M_{\lambda}
and
L_{\lambda}
are
T
invariant subspaces, and
\sigma(T|M_{\lambda})=\{\lambda\}
and
\sigma(T|L_{\lambda})=\sigma(T)\setminus\{\lambda\}
. Note that
M_{\lambda}\neq\{0\}
. (Suppose that
M_{\lambda}=\{0\}
. Then
L_{\lambda}=X
, hence
\sigma(T|L_{\lambda})=\sigma(T)
, which is a contradiction since
\sigma(T|L_{\lambda})=\sigma(T)\setminus\{\lambda\}
.) We have
T|M_{\lambda}\in B(M_{\lambda})
such that
\sigma(T|M_{\lambda})=\{\lambda\}
. As
\|(T|M_{\lambda})^{n}\|\leq\|T^{n}\|
for every integer
n
, we have
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|(T|M_{\lambda})^{n}\|<\infty
. Applying Theorem
2.3
for
T|M_{\lambda}
we have
T|M_{\lambda}=\lambda I_{M_{\lambda}}
, so that
T(x)=\lambda x
for every
x\in M_{\lambda}
.
Note that
M_{\lambda}
is the eigenspace of
T
for
\lambda
.
The reason is as follows.
As
T|M_{\lambda}=\lambda I_{M_{\lambda}}
,
M_{\lambda}
is a subspace of the eigenspace for
\lambda
. We prove the converse.
Suppose that
0\neq y\in X
satisfies
T(y)=\lambda y
:
y
is an eigenvector for
\lambda
. As
X=M_{\lambda}\oplus L_{\lambda}
,
y=y_{M}+y_{L}
with
y_{M}\in M_{\lambda}
and
y_{L}\in L_{\lambda}
. Then

\lambda y_{M}+\lambda y_{L}=\lambda y=T(y)=T(y_{M})+T(y_{L})=\lambda y_{M}+T(y_{L}),

so

\lambda y_{L}=T(y_{L})=T|L_{\lambda}(y_{L}).

Suppose that
y_{L}\neq 0
. Then
\lambda\in\sigma(T|L_{\lambda})
, which is a contradiction since
\sigma(T|L_{\lambda})=\sigma(T)\setminus\{\lambda\}
. It follows that
y_{L}=0
, hence
y=y_{M}\in M_{\lambda}
.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose that
T\in B(X)
is an invertible operator such that the spectrum
\sigma(T)
is a finite set
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m}\}
.
Then
T
satisfies the condition

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T^{n}\|<\infty

(12)

if and only if
|\lambda_{j}|=1
and there exists a projection
P_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq m
such that

T=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}P_{j}
, which satisfies
the following
(i)
and
(ii)
.

(i)

The image
P_{j}(X)
is the eigenspace of
T
corresponding to
\lambda_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq m
.

(ii)

P_{i}P_{j}=0
for every pair
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
and
I=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{j}
.

In this case,
the projection
P_{j}
is unique in the sense that it is the Riesz projection of
T
corresponding to the spectrum
\lambda_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq m
.
Hence, every eigenspace
P_{j}(X)
and
\operatorname{ker}P_{j}
are
T
-invariant subspaces such that
P_{j}(X)\oplus\operatorname{ker}P_{j}=X
.

Proof.

Suppose that
|\lambda_{j}|=1
and
T=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}P_{j}
for a projection
P_{j}
with the conditions (i) and (ii).
As
P_{j}
is a projection and
P_{j}P_{i}=0
for every pair
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
, we have
T^{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\lambda_{j}}^{n}P_{j}
for every positive integer

. We also have
T^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\lambda_{j}}^{-1}P_{j}
, so
T^{-n}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\lambda_{j}}^{-n}P_{j}
for every positive integer

. As
|\lambda_{j}|=1
for every

, we have

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T^{n}\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|P_{j}\|<\infty
, which assures (

).

Suppose conversely that (

) holds. By Theorem
3.2
we have
|\lambda_{j}|=1
for every
1\leq j\leq m
.
Let
Q_{j}
denote the Riesz projection of
T
corresponding to
\lambda_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq m
. Due to Theorem
3.2

Q_{j}(X)

T
corresponding to
\lambda_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq m
. Thus
Q_{j}
’s satisfy the condition (i).
We prove that
T=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}Q_{j}
and

Q_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq m
satisfies (ii).
We have

\sum_{j=1}^{m}Q_{j}=I.

(13)

In fact, letting
\Gamma_{j}
be a Cauchy contour (in the resolvent set of
T
) around
\lambda_{j}
separating
\lambda_{j}
from
\sigma(T)\setminus\{\lambda_{j}\}
, and
\Gamma
a Cauchy contour around
\sigma(T)

It follows that
+_{j=1}^{m}Q_{j}(X)=X
.
As
Q_{j}(X)
is the eigenspace for
\lambda_{j}
, we have
Q_{i}(X)\cap Q_{j}(X)=\{0\}
for

. This implies that
\oplus_{j=1}^{m}Q_{j}(X)=X
.
For every pair

and
j

, we have
Q_{i}Q_{j}=0
. (The reason is as follows. Due to the definition of the Riesz projection, we have

, where

is the Riesz projection of

corresponding to
\{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\}
. Thus

. ) Thus

(

Suppose that
P_{j}

.

with
i\neq j
.

(ii)

\prod_{j=1}^{m}(T-\lambda_{j}I)=0
and

P_{j}=\frac{\prod_{i\neq j}(T-\lambda_{i}I)}{\prod_{i\neq j}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})},\qquad j=1,\dots,m.

By Corollary
3.3
and Proposition
3.4
, we have the following.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
T\in B(X)
be a power-bounded operator. Suppose that
the spectrum
\sigma(T)
is a finite set
\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m}\}
.
Then
T
is an algebraic operator such that

\prod_{j=1}^{m}(T-\lambda_{j}I)=0
.

Example 3.6
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be any norm on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Suppose that
A=\begin{bmatrix}5&-2\\
12&-5\\
\end{bmatrix}
.
We have

\begin{bmatrix}5&-2\\
12&-5\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
2&3\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
2&3\\
\end{bmatrix}^{-1}.

Hence, the matrix
A
is power-bounded as an operator on
(\mathbb{C}^{2},\|\cdot\|)
. We have the following.
The point spectrum (eigenvalues) of
A
are

\pm 1
. The eigenspace
X_{1}
of
A
corresponding to
1
is
\left\{r\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\end{pmatrix}\mid r\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
, and the eigenspace
X_{-1}
corresponding to
-1
is

\left\{r\begin{pmatrix}1\\
3\end{pmatrix}\mid r\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
.
Then
P=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\
6&-2\\
\end{bmatrix}

is a projection such that
P\mathbb{C}^{2}=X_{1}
, and

Q=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1\\
-6&3\\
\end{bmatrix}
is a projection such that
Q\mathbb{C}^{2}=X_{-1}
, and
PQ=QP=0
.
We have

A=1P+(-1)Q.

Example 3.7
.

Suppose that
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}
. Then
A
is not power-bounded as an operator on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
since
A^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}1&n\\
0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}
and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|A^{n}\|=\infty
.
The point spectrum (eigenvalue) is
1
and the corresponding eigenspace for
1
is
\left\{r\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mid r\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
. Furthermore,
there is no projection
P
such that
A=1P
.
