Integrated coagulation-flocculation with nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membrane for treating sugar cane industry effluent
Authors: Boukary Sawadogo, Abdoul Wahab Nouhou Moussa, Yacouba Konaté, Chayma Tiendrebeogo, Seyram Sossou, Sayon Dit Sadio Sidibé, Harouna Karambiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSugarcane industries, like other agro-food industries, generate significant volumes of wastewater containing high concentrations of organic and inorganic pollutants. Among the proposed treatment solutions, the anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) has proven highly effective in degrading organic pollutants but has limitations in removing color and inorganic pollutants. To address this gap, integrating other technologies with AnMBR is necessary. In this study, we propose coupling AnMBR with either Coagulation-flocculation followed by nanofiltration or reverse osmosis for treating wastewater from sugar industries after initial AnMBR treatment. The wastewater treated by an anaerobic AnMBR pilot plant, fed with wastewater from a sugar cane production unit, was used for the tests. Various formulations of Moringa oleifera were tested, including its defatting and dehulling products with hexane and ethanol. For nanofiltration and reverse osmosis, pressures of 4 and 8 bars respectively were applied. For the coagulation-flocculation phase, granular alum and powdered moringa oleifera seeds were compared as coagulants. Thus, the coupling of the coagulation-flocculation system with nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes effectively reduced both organic and inorganic matter. The use of aluminum sulphate proved more effective at high pollution concentrations, while Moringa oleifera extracted with hexane achieved removal rates exceeding 70 % for heavy metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, Zn). Purification efficiencies of over 90 % were obtained after filtration with the reverse osmosis membrane, while the nanofiltration membrane showed retention rates of 90 % for color, but less than 50 % for monovalent ions. The treated effluent at the outlet of the nanofiltration and reverse osmosis systems complies with Burkina Faso's discharge standards, with superior quality allowing for direct reuse in the industry.Keywords: Anaerobic membrane bioreactor, Cane industry wastewater, Coagulation flocculation, Nanofiltration, Reverse osmosis

Introduction

Sugar is one of the most globally traded commodities, and its industry ranks among the most widespread within agro-industries [1]. It constitutes a vital component of human dietary consumption [2]. Traditionally, the sugar industry was solely dedicated to sugar production, but in recent years, it has diversified to include the production of energy and ethanol [3].

The wastewater from the sugar industry encompasses all water used in the process of sugar and its derivatives production. This industry is notable not only for its intensive water consumption but also for the substantial volume of effluents generated. Each ton of crushed sugarcane requires approximately 1500 to 2000 dm3 of water, resulting in about 1000 dm3 of wastewater discharged [4,5]. These effluents constitute a significant source of environmental pollution, containing high levels of Chemical oxygen Demand (COD), Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD), Total Dissolved Solid (TDS), and Total Suspended Solid (TSS) [2].

The membrane bioreactor (MBR) is recognized as one of the most effective treatment methods for both domestic and industrial wastewater [6]. It operates under aerobic, anoxic, and anaerobic conditions, effectively treating organic-polluted effluents such as industrial wastewater [[6], [7], [8]]. However, despite producing high-quality effluents, MBRs have limitations in retaining ions and removing coloration, thereby restricting effluent reuse without additional treatment [6]. Hence, nanofiltration (NF) and reverse osmosis (RO) are recommended. Their effectiveness in ion retention and color removal is well-established, making them ideal for refining industrial wastewater treatment. When applied after MBR treatment of brewery wastewater, these techniques have led to nearly complete ion removal [6,7]. However, effluents from MBRs do not meet the required quality for direct passage to NF or RO without risking rapid membrane fouling. Therefore, an intermediate treatment is necessary. Coagulation-flocculation, a classic, effective, and easy-to-apply process, is proposed for this stage.

Coagulation-flocculation has proven effective in wastewater treatment, as evidenced by several studies. Aguilar et al. (2002) [9] achieved significant removal of total phosphate and orthophosphates in slaughterhouse wastewater using this method, while Sakhi et al. (2019) [10] demonstrated substantial reductions in heavy metals in textile industry effluents. Parameters such as pH, agitation rates and times, temperature, and the type of coagulant used influence coagulation-flocculation. Commonly used coagulants include aluminum sulphate, iron sulphate (III), aluminum chloride, and ferric chloride [[11], [12], [13]]. Natural coagulants and flocculants, like moringa oleifera, have also shown promise, sometimes proving more effective than mineral counterparts [14,15]. Coagulants and flocculants can be either organic or mineral, with commonly used mineral salts including aluminum sulphate, iron sulphate, aluminum chloride, and ferric chloride [[11], [12], [13]]. However, the use of mineral salts raises concerns regarding health and economic implications [16,17]. There is increasing research focus on natural coagulants and flocculants, which are often as effective, if not more effective, than their mineral counterparts. For instance, studies such as Garika et al. (2022) [14] support that natural alternatives like chitosan, cactus, tannins, Aloe Vera, moringa oleifera, and chestnuts offer promising and reliable results for reducing turbidity, potentially toxic elements, and pathogens in water [15].

Moringa oleifera is widely recognized as the best natural coagulant currently known, capable of replacing aluminum sulphate, the most commonly used coagulant in global water treatment [18]. Belonging to the Moringaceae family, this plant is found in Africa and Asia. Moringa typically grows to about 10 m high and has a light gray to pale brown bark, smooth or occasionally slightly rough. Moringa is used for purifying river water, muddy water, wastewater, as well as in the treatment of honey and sugarcane juice. According to Foidl et al. (2001) [18], moringa seeds act as a primary coagulant by forming permanent natural bridges between colloidal particles, unlike industrial coagulants that may be toxic, thus requiring technical expertise for their correct use. Moringa seeds contain about 42 % oil, composed of approximately 13 % saturated fatty acids and 82 % unsaturated fatty acids [19]. This is why some studies advocate for oil extraction before using moringa cake. Prasad and Shi's (2016) [20] research achieved over 50 % decolorization of distillery wastewater using the same moringa coagulation-flocculation process.

Material and methods

The treated water used in this study comes from an anaerobic membrane bioreactor that processes wastewater from a sugarcane industry. This water was subjected to treatment with aluminum sulphate and Moringa oleifera. Filtration tests were conducted using a combining nanofiltration and reverse osmosis pilot system.

The anaerobic membrane bioreactor

An anaerobic bioreactor, consisting of a 20-liter altuglas tank and a ceramic ultrafiltration membrane, was used in this study to treat wastewater from a sugarcane industry. A peristaltic pump feeds the system in batch mode at a constant flow rate with wastewater collected from an agro-industrial complex located in the Cascades region of Burkina Faso.

The membrane is housed in a cartridge outside the reactor and consists of a P10 ceramic membrane module provided by Pall Exekia, with a filtration surface area of 0.45 m2. It is 1178 cm long and has 20 channels, each with a diameter of 6 mm. The filtration system operates in tangential flow mode (in-out). The retentate is recirculated into the reactor using a volumetric pump, allowing for the adjustment of the tangential velocity along the membrane. The cut-off threshold is between 10 and 20 nm at 15 kD.

A computer-controlled interface, through software, allows for complete supervision of operations, with control over parameters such as pressure, flow rate, pH, and temperature. The experiments were conducted under Sahelian climatic conditions, maintaining the temperature inside the reactor at 35 °C using a cooling system. Additionally, the transmembrane pressure was regulated using a back-pressure valve positioned downstream of the membrane. Fig. 1 presents a schematic representation of the membrane bioreactor.

The nanofiltration/reverse osmosis pilot

Filtration tests were conducted using a pilot plant model MP72SP/n°19 (Deltalab/Cosimi), equipped with a combination of nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes. This system is powered by a high-pressure multistage centrifugal pump (16 bars at 800 L/h) to ensure circulation of the feed fluid. The reverse osmosis membrane used is of TW30 type, while nanofiltration is performed by an NF270 membrane. The pilot plant also includes a prefiltration device consisting of two cartridge filters: a 25 μm filter and an activated carbon filter. For overpressure protection, it is equipped with two safety valves set respectively at 5 and 14 bars. Sensors and flow meters are installed to monitor transmembrane pressure (TMP), flow rates, electrical conductivity, and temperature (Fig. 2). The spiral-wound nanofiltration membrane module (NF270-2540 from Dow Filmtec) is composed of a three-layer composite polymer: polyester (120 μm), polysulfone (40 μm), and a polyamide surface layer (0.2 μm). The main characteristics of the membrane are summarized in Table 1, and the functional diagram is depicted in Fig. 2. These two membranes are among the most efficient in their category. For instance, the NF270 membrane stands out with a high retention rate (90 %) and a high permeate flux (12 L/m³·h) compared to many other membranes. Additionally, compared to other NF membranes, the NF270 exhibits high water permeability (11.3 ± 1.0 L/h m2 bar). As for the TW30 membrane, it is considered the industry standard for reliable operation and the production of the highest quality water.

For this study, the experimental conditions were selected based on the work of Sawadogo et al., 2022, who used the same pilot equipment to treat groundwater aiming to reduce sulphate ions. They found that the optimal transmembrane pressure was 4 bars for nanofiltration and 8 bars for reverse osmosis. Consequently, the current experiments were also conducted at pressures of 4 bars for NF and 8 bars for RO. Additionally, the temperature was maintained at approximately 35 °C to prevent membrane overheating.

The coagulants

The experiments for selecting the coagulant were conducted with two types of coagulants: a mineral coagulant, aluminum sulphate, and a natural coagulant, Moringa oleifera seeds. These seeds were used in two forms: with and without shells, to determine the effect of the shells on the treatment. Additionally, a portion of the shelled seeds underwent oil extraction to evaluate the effect of the oil on the sedimentation phase, as oils can cause flotation phenomena. The solutions used for the experiments with aluminum sulphate and moringa oleifera, in all their forms, are prepared following the methodology described by Prassad in 2008. Each solution is prepared at a concentration of 40 g/L by weighing out 40 g of the corresponding coagulant powder using a precision balance. This powder is then placed into a 1 L volumetric flask, where it is topped up with distilled water up to the mark, and the mixture is homogenized. For moringa, the resulting suspensions are filtered through a Whatman No.42 filter paper after homogenization.

Oil extraction from Moringa oleifera seeds

To evaluate the impact of the presence of oil on the effectiveness of coagulation-flocculation with Moringa oleifera, an extraction of the oil from the seeds was conducted. Prior to extraction, the seeds were shelled and then ground using a mixer. The oil extraction was carried out using the Soxhlet method with hexane and ethanol as extraction solvents, and their products were compared for coagulation-flocculation. The choice of solvent generally depends on its polarity and its ability to dissolve specific compounds. These operations were performed based on the extraction methodology described in the study of Adelodun et al. (2020) [21]. The various coagulants used during coagulation-flocculation operations are illustrated in Fig. 3, Fig. 4.

Coagulation and flocculation

The coagulation-flocculation tests were conducted using a jar test equipped with a six-position flocculator with centralized speed control, utilizing six 1-L beakers. Each beaker was filled with treated effluent from the MBR, to which increasing volumes of alum sulphate (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 mL) or moringa (40, 60, 80, 100, 120, and 140 mL) were added. These volumes correspond to respective concentrations of and 2.96 mg/L, 4.28 mg/L, 5.52 mg/L, 6.67 mg/L, 7.74 mg/L, 8.75 mg/L for moringa oleifera and 0.16 mg/L, 0.31 mg/L, 0.47 mg/L, 0.63 mg/L, 0,78 mg/L, 0.93 mg/L for aluminium sulphate. For coagulation, the flocculator was set at 120 rpm for 3 min (coagulation step) and subsequently, the speed was reduced to 40 rpm for 15 min (flocculation step). This step allowed colloidal particles to agglomerate into larger flocs. Finally, the beakers were left to settle for 1 h and 30 min for all coagulants to allow for the settling of the flocs formed in the preceding step.

Filtration on NF and RO membranes

The clarified waters with each of the five coagulants were then subjected to filtration operations on the pilot scale nanofiltration/reverse osmosis system. The system's performance was continuously monitored. Filtration operations were conducted once a week following each clarification process. The chosen filtration mode was partial recycling with tangential filtration.

For each filtration operation, a 45 L sample from the water resulting from coagulation-flocculation was taken. This sample was introduced into the feed tank and then conveyed to the filtration circuit (nanofiltration or reverse osmosis) using the system's multicellular pump. After passing through the membrane, the sample was collected in the permeate tank. Cleaning of the circuit with distilled water was performed after each filtration operation. Parameters such as conductivity, pressure, feed flow rate, and permeate flow rate were directly displayed on the pilot's control panel. Performance of the treated water clarification tests

Effects of coagulants on treated water physico-chemical parameters

Coagulation-flocculation was used as a pretreatment for the water exiting the membrane bioreactor to protect the nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes used for removing mineral pollution from the wastewater of the sugar cane industry. Aluminum sulphate, chosen as a chemical coagulant, and moringa oleifera, used as a natural coagulant, were compared for their effectiveness in reducing mainly the color and turbidity of the water treated by the AnMBR. Different forms of moringa oleifera, including the use of the shell and oil removal with hexane and ethanol, were tested to evaluate their impact on the efficiency of effluent clarification.

Clarification with whole Moringa oleifera seed powder with husks (MOH)

The best performance in terms of reducing coloration and turbidity was observed with a dose of 100 mL of moringa coagulant solution with husks, corresponding to a concentration of 6.67 g/L. After coagulation treatment, effluent coloration decreased by approximately 34 %, with an average of 1062 mg/L Pt, compared to 1609 mg/L Pt for the initial effluent from the membrane bioreactor. Additionally, about 38 % of turbidity was removed, with the average supernatant turbidity being 42 NTU after treatment, compared to 68 NTU before coagulation-flocculation treatment. Temperature variations were minimal, ranging between 27.9 °C and 28.1 °C, and the use of moringa seeds with husks had no significant impact on sample temperatures. The pH of the AnMBR effluent was 6.9 and trend toward a decrease in pH with increasing coagulant dose was observed with Moringa oleifera solutions. Although the use of high doses of moringa powder as a coagulant for treating wastewater from the sugar industry may increase turbidity, previous studies have shown higher treatment efficiency, achieving up to 85 % reduction in turbidity with whole, husked moringa seed powder. The reduction rates achieved using whole, husked Moringa seed powder are presented in Fig. 5.

Clarification with whole Moringa oleifera seed powder without husks (MO)

Using 140 mL of a coagulant solution based on de-husked moringa powder, at a concentration of 8.75 g/L, resulted in a significant improvement in turbidity and color removal compared to other tested doses. The color concentration decreased from 1609 to 910 mg/L Pt, representing a reduction of 43 %. A similar reduction of 49 % was observed for turbidity, reaching approximately 34 NTU in the supernatant. Previous studies [25,26] have also confirmed the efficacy of de-husked moringa as a coagulant, with effectiveness rates exceeding 80 %. The results obtained with de-husked moringa seed powder are illustrated in Fig. 6. Regarding temperature, it remained stable between 26.0 and 26.3 °C during the coagulation-flocculation operations, indicating that the coagulant had a negligible impact on this parameter. These findings are consistent with previous observations by Kalibbala et al. (2009) [27] and Hendrawati et al. (2016) [28], who also noted minimal temperature variations when using coagulants.

Clarification with de-husked moringa seed powder extracted with ethanol (MOE)

The ethanol-extracted defatted Moringa seed powder was used to test water clarification at the outlet of the BRM at various doses. Results showed variations across analyzed parameters such as pH, turbidity, temperature, and color. A progressive decrease in turbidity and color was observed with increasing coagulant dose. Particularly for turbidity and color, ethanol extraction led to greater reductions compared to untreated Moringa seeds. At a dose of 120 mL (equivalent to 7.74 g/L of coagulant), efficiencies of around 55 % were achieved. Shan et al. (2017) [29] also achieved promising results, with color and turbidity reductions exceeding 90 % during wastewater treatment via coagulation-flocculation using ethanol-defatted Moringa, surpassing the results of this study. Trends observed with the use of ethanol-extracted defatted Moringa seed powder are illustrated in Fig. 7. However, ethanol extraction had no significant effect on pH, maintaining values between 6.94 and 6.40. Regarding temperature, it remained relatively constant, ranging between 27.4 and 27.9 °C.

Evolution of clarified water parameters during coagulation-flocculation with de-husked moringa seed powder extracted with ethanol.

Clarification with de-husked moringa seed powder extracted with hexane (MOHe)

Compared to oil extraction using ethanol, extraction with hexane showed higher reduction rates, reaching 71 % for color and 77 % for turbidity. This difference could be explained by the fact that ethanol, in addition to extracting oils, also carries away some of the proteins responsible for the coagulating power of moringa, thereby reducing its effectiveness. In the supernatant, the observed values were 15 NTU for turbidity and 466 mg/L Pt for color. This significant reduction in color and turbidity was more pronounced with a coagulant dose of 80 mL at a concentration of 5.52 g/L. Rakotoniriana et al. (2020) [30] also obtained significant reduction rates, with notable removal of color (from brown to colorless) and a turbidity reduction of about 98 %, when treating wastewater using hexane-defatted moringa seed powder. A slight acidification of the water was observed following the addition of the coagulant, with the pH dropping from 6.94 to 6.54. Kalibala et al. (2009) [27] noted, in their research, a minor increase in pH during coagulation-flocculation processes using hexane-extracted moringa seeds. Regarding temperature, there were no significant variations during the coagulation-flocculation operations with hexane-extracted moringa seeds. The values for this parameter remained stable, between 28.4 and 28.9 °C. These observations indicate that hexane-defatted moringa seeds do not have a notable impact on pH and temperature variation during coagulation-flocculation operations. The use of hexane-defatted moringa seed powder produced the trends presented in Fig. 8.

Clarification with aluminum sulphate (AS)

An elimination efficiency of up to 88 % is observed for aluminum sulphate used as a coagulant for color reduction. These results were achieved with a dose of 6 mL, corresponding to a concentration of 0.47 g/L, and surpass those of Bailon-Salas et al. (2021) [31], who achieved a maximum efficiency of approximately 56.82 % for the treatment of vinasse by coagulation with aluminum sulphate. A similar trend is observed for turbidity reduction, with a decrease rate of about 90 % for the same coagulant dose (6 mL, or 0.47 g/L). These results confirm those of Ndabigengesere and Subba (1998) [32], who obtained a reduction of more than 90 % when treating turbid water by coagulation with aluminum sulphate. The trends observed with the use of aluminum sulphate are illustrated in Fig. 9. Coagulation-flocculation operations with aluminum sulphate did not have a significant impact on water temperature during treatment, with values remaining around 29 °C. However, the pH decreased from 6.94 to 3.98. These results are consistent with those obtained by Tchamango et al. (2023) [33], who attribute this acidification to the release of protons caused by the hydrolysis of aluminiu ions (Al3+) during chemical coagulation.

Effects of coagulants on treated water ion retention

Moringa seed powder has contributed to partially reducing the ions present in the samples. Reduction rates of 10–30 % were observed for cations (K+, Mg2+, NH4+, Ca2+) and about 40–65 % for anions (Cl−, PO43−, F−, HCO3−). Moringa acts as a coagulant in water treatment due to its content of cationic polypeptides, such as antimicrobial peptides and cationic proteins. These positively charged polypeptides bind to negatively charged particles in wastewater, leading to higher retention of anions compared to cations. When moringa is added to water, the cationic polypeptides form aggregates called flocs, made of heavier and larger particles. This floc formation facilitates their sedimentation and subsequent removal. Additionally, the cationic polypeptides neutralize the negative charge of suspended particles, promoting their agglomeration and precipitation. According to Chee et al. (2020) [34], Moringa oleifera seeds are an excellent coagulant for removing negatively charged ions due to their high porosity, surface texture, and high content of coagulating proteins, around 31.4 %. Hexane-extracted moringa seeds showed the best reduction rates among the different moringa solutions used as a coagulant, with over 65 % elimination of fluoride and bicarbonate ions. Moringa hulls did not show a significant coagulating effect, as results did not indicate notable reduction. Ethanol-extracted oils provided better ion elimination rates compared to untreated moringa, but these rates were still lower than those obtained with hexane-extracted seeds. The oils present in moringa seeds hinder sedimentation during coagulation-flocculation operations due to their oleaginous nature. The use of aluminum sulphate as a coagulant yielded better elimination rates compared to different moringa solutions (moringa seeds with hulls, moringa seeds without hulls, hexane-extracted moringa seeds, ethanol-extracted moringa seeds). With aluminum sulphate, over 50–70 % of anions (Cl−, PO43−, F−, HCO3−) were removed from effluents, and about 20–30 % of cations (K+, Mg2+, NH4+, Ca2+) were retained. It is worth noting that at concentrations below 50 mg/L, hexane-extracted moringa seed powder is more effective than aluminum sulphate, however, its efficacy decreases with high ionic concentrations. For effluents from sugarcane industries, which are heavily loaded with inorganic pollutants, the use of aluminum sulphate as a coagulant provided better results than using moringa seeds. This is because aluminum sulphate primarily reacts with charges, whereas moringa interacts more with proteins. The elimination of conductivity with moringa seed solutions or aluminum sulphate remained around 20 %, due to the low removal rates of salts during various tests. These results corroborate those obtained by Hendrawati et al. (2016) [28] who observed a conductivity reduction of about 10.8 % in wastewater treated by moringa coagulation-flocculation. Tchamango et al. (2023) [33] in treating effluents from an artisanal tannery, attribute the low conductivity removal rates during coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate to the hydrolysis of aluminium ions (Al3+), leading to the release of protons and the generation of sulphate ions, which do not participate in coagulation. Effects of coagulants on treated water heavy metals retention

Heavy metals were better retained during jar test operations with moringa seed solutions compared to aluminum sulphate. The heavy metals monitored in the AnBRM effluents include copper (Cu), total chromium (Cr), zinc (Zn), and cadmium (Cd), with concentrations below 20 mg/L. Extraction of oils from moringa seeds using hexane and ethanol yielded the best results, with removal rates up to 60 %, followed by untreated moringa seeds, which retained over 40 % of heavy metals. Removal rates below 30 % were observed for aluminum sulphate. The various heavy metal removal rates are presented in Fig. 11. The results confirm the hypothesis that the use of moringa seeds as a coagulant is more effective at low pollutant concentrations. These results are generally lower than those obtained by Bouaouine et al. (2017) [35] during the treatment of wastewater from the textile industry using aluminum sulphate coagulation-flocculation, with removal rates of 83 % for chromium, 63 % for zinc, and 77 % for copper. Conversely, for moringa use, Jagaba et al. (2021) [36] achieved removal rates of 94 %, 76 %, and 82 % for copper, lead, and zinc treatment in a palm oil industry effluent. These discrepancies can be explained by differences in effluent composition and the concentrations of coagulant solutions used.

Comparison of coagulants

The treatment efficiency is higher with decorticated moringa seed powder compared to non-decorticated seeds. This finding aligns with that of Ndabigengesere and Subba (1998) [32], who worked with both types of coagulants for tap water treatment. When treating slaughterhouse wastewater, Al-Wasify et al. (2019) [37] achieved 70 % reduction in suspended solids using moringa seeds with hulls as a coagulant, followed by an aeration system. Regarding the solvents used for extraction, samples treated with hexane-defatted moringa seed powder showed better results compared to those treated with ethanol. Among the four types of moringa powder used, hexane-extracted powder proved most effective, confirming studies by Lester-Card et al. (2023) [38] and Elsergany et al. (2023) [39], which suggest optimal effluent treatment occurs when oil is extracted from the seeds. Results obtained using aluminum sulphate surpassed those obtained with the four types of moringa powder. Additionally, aluminum sulphate requires smaller quantities and is more cost-effective than moringa. Furthermore, its use does not require preprocessing such as hulling and/or oil extraction from the seeds, which is necessary for moringa. Jar test operations with both moringa and aluminum sulphate yielded significant reductions in coloration and turbidity. However, for ions and heavy metals, removal rates remained below 50 %. Despite the proven effectiveness of these two coagulants, effluent from clarification tests did not meet wastewater discharge standards or agricultural reuse standards, as indicated in Table 4. Therefore, further treatment is necessary, possibly by coupling with nanofiltration or reverse osmosis membranes.

Performance of nanofiltration and reverse osmosis after treated water clarification tests

The jar test is an essential step to assess the effectiveness of coagulation-flocculation treatments before proceeding to tertiary treatment. After undergoing these preliminary treatment processes, the wastewater generated supernatants containing dissolved pollutants that could not be completely eliminated. Following the jar test experiments, the resulting waters underwent filtration through nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes. The supernatants chosen for further filtration are those using aluminum sulphate (CFAS) and those using Moringa oleifera associated with hexane (CFMOHe), as it is the most effective coagulant among those using moringa seeds.

Performance of the nanofiltration membrane

The clarified effluent from the AnBRM treated with aluminum sulphate (AS) and hexane-extracted moringa oleifera (MOHe) was used for nanofiltration tests. Filtration operations were conducted at ambient temperatures between 26 and 30 °C with an inlet pressure of 4 bars. Additionally, ion retention on the NF membrane was more effective for the removal of divalent ions, with reductions around 90 % for both types of coagulants, compared to monovalent ions, which showed reductions below 50 %. This corroborates the findings of Frenckel et al. (2015) [40] and Sawadogo et al. (2022a, 2022b) [7,41] who found that nanofiltration membranes have high removal efficiency for divalent ions but only partial removal of monovalent ions depending on the membrane's cutoff size.

Using nanofiltration in the treatment of supernatants resulting from coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate and hexane-extracted moringa seed extract showed significant retention of heavy metals, achieving rates of around 80 %. Specifically, cadmium (Cd) was remarkably removed, exceeding 87 % for the supernatants of effluents subjected to coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate and hexane-extracted moringa seed extract. Regarding chromium (Cr) and copper (Cu), removal rates were 93 % and 95 %, respectively, for coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate. Using hexane-extracted moringa seeds, the removal rates were remarkable, reaching 91 % for chromium and 90 % for copper (Cu). Simultaneously, zinc (Zn) retention in the effluent after coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate as a coagulant was around 89 %, slightly higher than the 86 % achieved with hexane-extracted moringa seed extract. These findings highlight the effectiveness of nanofiltration in addressing heavy metal concerns, especially when used alongside traditional coagulation-flocculation methods with aluminum sulphate and natural coagulants like hexane-extracted moringa seeds. The results demonstrate the potential for reducing harmful heavy metals in industrial wastewater, thereby mitigating their adverse environmental impact. According to Ahmad et al. (2020) [42], nanofiltration can remove more than 92 % of copper from wastewater. Jagaba et al. (2021) [36] successfully eliminated 92.7 % of copper and 93.3 % of zinc (Zn) in wastewater through nanofiltration. Ye et al. (2023) [43] found retention rates of 95 % and 98 % for cadmium (Cd) and chromium (Cr) in wastewater treated by nanofiltration.

Furthermore, for COD and color, respective reductions of 96.9 % and 97.9 % were achieved with the effluents clarified with aluminum sulphate. Similar results were found by Diaz-Reinoso et al. (2020) [44] and Silva et al. (2023) [45] for the treatment of whey and concentrated white wine vinasse effluents by nanofiltration. The nanofiltration membrane effectively removed more than 94.7 % of COD and 97 % of color present in wastewater from the coagulation-flocculation process using hexane-extracted moringa. However, it is important to note that in the case of effluents from coagulation-flocculation with Moringa Oleifera, oils can have a detrimental effect on nanofiltration membrane efficiency. This phenomenon manifests through several harmful mechanisms. Firstly, oils tend to accumulate on the membrane surface, causing gradual fouling. Additionally, they reduce membrane permeability, hindering effective filtration. Concurrently, oils can create emulsions with water, generating fine particles that are difficult to remove during nanofiltration. Furthermore, these oils can alter the membrane surface, negatively influencing its selectivity and retention capacity for certain solutes. These phenomena contributed to slightly lower ion removal rates by the nanofiltration membrane for supernatants after coagulation-flocculation with hexane-extracted moringa seeds compared to those obtained with aluminum sulphate coagulation-flocculation. The values obtained for nanofiltration treatment of effluents are recorded in Table 5. After treatment with the nanofiltration membrane, all analyzed parameters, except for monovalent ions, were eliminated by more than 90 %, ensuring compliance with wastewater discharge standards.

More than 97 % of the coloration was retained by the reverse osmosis membrane during filtration tests with aluminum sulphate or hexane-defatted Moringa oleifera. The reverse osmosis membrane proved more effective than the nanofiltration membrane, achieving retention rates above 96 % for all heavy metals analyzed with both supernatants after jar test operations (with aluminum sulphate and hexane-defatted moringa seeds). About 97 % of cadmium was removed in effluents after coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate and hexane-defatted moringa seeds. Chromium and copper also showed impressive retention rates around 98 % in effluents after jar test operations with both coagulants. For zinc, removal rates over 98 % were recorded with the supernatants from coagulation-flocculation using aluminum sulphate as a coagulant, compared to 96 % for hexane-defatted moringa seeds after reverse osmosis membrane filtration.

The aluminum hydroxide flocs formed during coagulation-flocculation are relatively large compared to dissolved metal ions. This increased size facilitates the separation of heavy metals from the rest of the water during reverse osmosis, as reverse osmosis membranes are designed to retain suspended particles and small solutes. Heavy metals can also precipitate in the presence of hydroxide ions generated during coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate. These heavy metal precipitates can be retained by the reverse osmosis membranes, contributing to their removal.

Heavy metal retention on reverse osmosis membranes following coagulation with aluminum sulphate is the result of several mechanisms, including floc formation, electrostatic adsorption, complex formation, particle size, precipitation, and other chemical interactions. These combined mechanisms effectively reduce the concentration of heavy metals in treated water, improving water quality. Moringa seeds contain organic compounds, such as tannins and flavonoids, which can have adsorption and complexation properties with heavy metals. Heavy metals can bind to the active sites of these compounds, forming complexes that are easier to remove during reverse osmosis membrane filtration. In some conditions, using moringa seeds as a coagulant lead to heavy metal precipitation as insoluble compounds, which can also be retained by reverse osmosis membranes.

Thaçi and Gashi (2018) [48] achieved cadmium removal rates over 95 % using reverse osmosis. Turki Rashid et al. (2020) [49] obtained retention rates of 81 %, 78 %, and 80 % for cadmium, copper, and zinc, respectively, when treating an aqueous solution containing phosphogypsum with reverse osmosis. The cumulative effect of oils on the reverse osmosis membrane, driven by moringa seeds, can increase maintenance costs, including energy costs to maintain adequate operating pressure and costs related to membrane cleaning or replacement. These oils, especially at high concentrations, tend to accumulate on the reverse osmosis membrane surface, causing membrane fouling and reducing its water passage capacity. Consequently, operating pressure increases, and membrane productivity decreases. This could explain the slight differences in retention rates during reverse osmosis membrane filtration after coagulation-flocculation with moringa seeds compared to aluminum sulphate.

COD was removed by over 98 % with the reverse osmosis membrane after using hexane-defatted moringa seeds as the coagulant for jar tests. The reverse osmosis membrane provided significant ion retention for effluents from coagulation-flocculation with aluminum sulphate or hexane-defatted Moringa oleifera. As with nanofiltration, all analyzed parameters in effluents treated with reverse osmosis showed removal rates above 90 %, with higher efficacy in retaining monovalent ions compared to the nanofiltration membrane.

Comparison of the performances of nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes

Nanofiltration and reverse osmosis membranes were used to refine cane sugar industry wastewater treated with a membrane bioreactor, followed by an additional coagulation-flocculation step using aluminum sulphate or defatted Moringa oleifera seed powder with hexane. Fig. 12, Fig. 13 illustrate the different rejection rates of the two membranes (nanofiltration and reverse osmosis) after CF-SA and CF-MOH. The results show removal rates ranging from 21 % to 99 % for nanofiltration and from 92 % to 99 % for reverse osmosis. While the removal performances for color, COD, and divalent ions are comparable, Fig. 12 shows that monovalent ions are poorly retained by the nanofiltration membrane compared to reverse osmosis. Similar results were obtained by Sawadogo et al. (2018) [50] in their study on brewery wastewater treatment by coupling MBR with nanofiltration and reverse osmosis. It appears that the reverse osmosis membrane performed better than nanofiltration for all tested waters. Nonetheless, the results demonstrate that nanofiltration achieves high retention rates, ensuring the production of high-quality permeate that meets current environmental standards. This ability to retain contaminants, including heavy metals, significantly contributes to effluent purification. A major advantage of nanofiltration lies in the reduced pressure required for operation. Unlike reverse osmosis, which requires high pressures, nanofiltration can be performed at lower pressures. This characteristic has important economic implications, as it leads to lower operational costs associated with the energy consumption needed to maintain operating pressures. Additionally, nanofiltration offers increased flexibility in solute selectivity. It allows for the specific targeting of components to be retained or removed based on their size and charge characteristics. This selectivity can be adjusted to meet the specific needs of effluent treatment, making it suitable for various industrial and environmental applications.

For a viable technical and economic study, it would be relevant to compare the use of aluminum sulphate and moringa as coagulants for the installation of a wastewater treatment plant in the sugar industry. Although moringa oleifera has shown promising results, the cost of aluminum sulphate, at around two euros per kilogram, is significantly lower than that of moringa, which is thirty euros per kilogram. Additionally, moringa requires extra processing steps, such as decortication and oil extraction, which add further costs. The proposed wastewater treatment plant for the sugar industry should include an anaerobic membrane bioreactor, designed to efficiently treat organic pollution while allowing for the recovery of biogas, which could be used for energy valorization. This energy recovery would help reduce the operating costs of the installation, offering a treatment model that is both sustainable and economically advantageous. The membrane bioreactor would ideally be coupled with a coagulation-flocculation system using aluminum sulphate as a coagulant. The coagulant would be directly introduced into the permeate recovery tank to improve the quality of the treated effluent. Following this, a nanofiltration membrane stage would be implemented to ensure the retention of ions and colorants, thereby optimizing water purification. This integrated treatment scheme offers several advantages. On one hand, the combination of biological treatment with energy recovery through biogas helps partially offset operating costs. On the other hand, the coupling with physico-chemical techniques, such as coagulation-flocculation and nanofiltration, ensures high-quality water, meeting regulatory requirements for environmental discharge or potential industrial reuse.

Conclusion

The effectiveness of three wastewater treatment techniques from the sugarcane industry, originating from an external loop anaerobic MBR, was evaluated: coagulation-flocculation, nanofiltration, and reverse osmosis. This evaluation was based on laboratory experiments divided into two phases: i) treatment of MBR effluents by coagulation-flocculation, and ii) treatment of the resulting effluents from coagulation-flocculation by nanofiltration and reverse osmosis. The results show that coagulation-flocculation is highly effective in decolorizing pretreated sugarcane industry wastewater from a AnMBR, with removal rates reaching 88 % for aluminum sulphate and 70 % for moringa oleifera. Furthermore, moringa seed powder, especially after hexane extraction, can effectively replace aluminum sulphate for heavy metal removal, achieving reduction rates exceeding 60 %. Additionally, treatments using nanofiltration (NF) and reverse osmosis (RO) produce high-purity effluents, with nearly complete removal of color (approximately 96 % for NF and 98 % for RO) and significant reduction in inorganic pollution (between 28 % and 91 % for NF and between 90 % and 99 % for RO).
