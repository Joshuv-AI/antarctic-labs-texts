Postnatal water immersion after perinatal death: a clinical study of appearance preservation and autopsy validity
Authors: Teresa Starrach, Jens H. L. Neumann, Lucia Ehmann, Laura Hahn, Esther Schouten, Sven Kehl, Sven Mahner, Uwe Hasbargen, Sarah Soliman, Christoph Hübener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePerinatal loss is an emotionally devastating event for affected families. In some clinical settings, postnatal immersion of perinatally deceased infants in water is used to preserve the infant’s external appearance and facilitate parental farewell. The objective of this study was to evaluate possible effects of this practice on autopsy findings and diagnostic validity.MethodsIn this observational study, 28 infants after perinatal death with available autopsy data were analyzed. Fourteen infants were immersed in cold water after birth (water group) and fourteen infants were stored without water immersion (control group). Macroscopic appearance, degree of maceration, and microscopic findings in liver and lung tissue were assessed during autopsy. Descriptive analyses explored associations between maceration degree and the intervals between fetal death, birth, and autopsy.ResultsInfants immersed in water showed improved preservation of external appearance, including skin color, texture, and body posture. No macroscopic differences attributable to water immersion were observed during autopsy. No microscopic differences in the two investigated internal organs were observed between the water and control groups. Maceration grade assessed at autopsy showed no consistent increase with longer birth–autopsy intervals, whereas higher grades were more frequently observed with longer estimated intervals between fetal death and birth in both groups.ConclusionIn this observational cohort, postnatal immersion in water after perinatal death was not associated with impaired autopsy findings. Maceration was primarily related to the intrauterine interval between fetal death and delivery rather than postnatal storage conditions. These findings suggest that the water method may offer a simple approach to support parental farewell while maintaining the diagnostic reliability of perinatal autopsy.Trial registrationClinical Trials Register (DRKS) (trial registration number DRKS00031373, URL https://www.drks.de), Registration Date: 27 February 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-026-08472-6.

What does this study add to clinical practice?

Postnatal water immersion of perinatally deceased infants may improve preservation of external appearance and support parental farewell without compromising the diagnostic validity of perinatal autopsy. Maceration was primarily associated with the intrauterine interval between fetal death and delivery rather than postnatal storage conditions. The water method represents a simple and clinically applicable approach in bereavement care.

Introduction

The pre- or peripartum loss of an infant represents a deeply traumatic experience for parents. The joyful anticipation of birth is abruptly replaced by grief over a loss. Parents who lose a child during pregnancy or childbirth constitute a highly vulnerable group and are at increased risk of developing a prolonged grief disorder beyond normal grief reactions [1]. Perinatal loss has been shown to have profound psychological consequences, including post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances [2]. Affected parents also have a higher risk of hospitalization, particularly for affective disorders [3], and an increased long-term mortality compared with parents who have not experienced child loss [4].

Several factors influence the course of grief after perinatal loss, including social support, partnership quality, coping strategies, and the opportunity for parents to say goodbye to their child. Seeing and holding the deceased infant, spending time together, and creating memories such as photographs can positively influence parental grief processing [5, 6]. As parents of a pre- or peripartum deceased infant have no memories beyond pregnancy and birth, the infant’s external appearance during this period plays an important role in facilitating farewell and memory-making [7].

The so-called water method was first described in the Netherlands in 2015 [8]. In this approach, a pre- or peripartum deceased infant is placed in cold water during the parental farewell period, which may last from several hours to several days. Cold water immersion may reduce skin discoloration, limit tissue damage, and minimize postmortem deformation of the head and body, allowing the infant to assume a more natural fetal posture [8, 9].

Determining the cause of pre- or peripartum death remains a central medical objective. Establishing the cause of death may support emotional closure for parents and is essential for counseling regarding recurrence risk in subsequent pregnancies. Diagnostic evaluation may include genetic, serological, and infectious investigations as well as perinatal autopsy, making the diagnostic reliability of autopsy findings crucial.

To date, no systematic study has investigated whether postnatal water immersion affects macroscopic or microscopic tissue characteristics or compromises the diagnostic validity of autopsy findings. In particular, it remains unclear whether water immersion influences postmortem changes such as maceration or interferes with pathological assessment. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the effects of the water immersion method on macroscopic appearance and microscopic findings in the liver and the lungs in infants after perinatal death.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

This monocentric descriptive study was conducted between October 2021 and May 2023 at the Perinatal Center and the Department of Pathology of LMU University Hospital. Pre- or peripartum deceased infants from the 12th week of gestation onward who were delivered intact were eligible for inclusion provided that parental consent for autopsy and study participation was obtained. The cause of death was not a selection criterion; infants who died intrauterine as well as those who died peripartum due to late termination of pregnancy or obstetric complications were included. Infants for whom no autopsy was performed were excluded. Infants who were placed in water postpartum constituted the intervention group.

The control group was assembled retrospectively and included stillborn infants who were not immersed in water. These comprised two subsets: first, infants born before the introduction of the water method at our institution (prior to 2021), and second, infants born between 2021 and 2023 whose parents declined water immersion. From the pre-implementation period, the most recent consecutive eligible cases prior to the introduction of the water method were included. No matching for gestational age or other variables was performed. As a consequence, the control group consisted predominantly of preterm cases, reflecting the chronological case selection prior to implementation of the water method. All included cases were identified from institutional pathology records, and no additional selection criteria were applied beyond parental consent for autopsy. Cases without available autopsy data were excluded from the final analysis.

The water method

Immediately after birth, deceased infants in the intervention group were placed in cold water with an average temperature of 12–15 °C. Ordinary tap water was used and changed daily. Infants were stored in water in a refrigerator at a constant temperature of 5 °C. Infants could be removed from the water at any time for parental farewell rituals or photographic documentation and were otherwise kept immersed. Depending on parental needs, the duration of water immersion ranged from several hours to several days. Infants were removed from the water before the transfer to the Department of Pathology.

Control group storage

Infants in the retrospectively assembled control group were wrapped in a dry towel and stored in a refrigerator at 5 °C according to standard institutional practice. They were intermittently removed from refrigeration for farewell rituals and/or photographic documentation before transfer to pathology.

Photo documentation

In the intervention group, standardized photographic documentation was performed before initial water immersion and subsequently once daily until transfer to pathology. Photographs included frontal, dorsal, and lateral views of the body as well as frontal and profile views of the face. For the control group, photographic documentation was available from the immediate postpartum period as well as photographs taken by pathologists prior to autopsy.

Autopsy

All infants were transferred to the Department of Pathology together with written parental consent for autopsy. Following verification of consent, a standardized perinatal autopsy was performed. Gross examination included measurement of crown–heel length, crown–rump length, head circumference, and body weight. External features were documented, and visible abnormalities or malformations were recorded. Body orifices (mouth, nose, ears, and anal canal) were inspected to exclude atresia. All infants were carefully exenterated, and the heart, lungs, thymus, gastrointestinal tract, liver, spleen, kidneys, adrenal glands, gonads, and umbilical cord were removed and examined for gross abnormalities. When the degree of maceration allowed, the brain was also examined. Tissue samples from major organs were fixed in formalin, processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Microscopic evaluation assessed organ development in relation to gestational age and documented all histopathological findings, including tissue architecture and cellular integrity.

Degree of maceration

The degree of maceration was primarily assessed by gross examination of the skin, focusing on the extent of epidermal detachment and desquamation. The degree of epidermal exfoliation was considered proportional to the severity of maceration. In addition, internal organs were evaluated histologically for signs of autolysis, such as tissue liquefaction and cellular disintegration. The liver was used as a key reference organ due to its susceptibility to autolytic changes.

The classification of maceration grades was based on the system originally described by Langley [10]. Based on these macroscopic and microscopic findings, an overall maceration grade was assigned for each case. Grading was performed by a single experienced pathologist during autopsy using the same assessment procedure in both study groups, according to the predefined macroscopic and histological criteria described above. Blinding to water immersion status was not performed. Photographic documentation was used for descriptive illustration only and was not used for formal maceration grading.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of LMU Munich (approval number 21–0176) on May 27, 2021, and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised 2008). Written informed consent was obtained from parents, and data protection regulations were strictly adhered to. All photographs were obtained with explicit parental consent. The study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00031373) on 27 February 2023.

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size, analyses were primarily descriptive and performed in collaboration with the Institute of Medical Information Processing, Biometry, and Epidemiology (IBE), and were intended to provide an exploratory assessment rather than formal hypothesis testing.

As the exact timing of intrauterine fetal death could not be determined, the interval between fetal death and delivery was classified into predefined time categories rather than recorded as an exact duration. Classification was based on maternal reports of the last perceived fetal movements and on prior clinical or ultrasound examinations documenting fetal viability. Accordingly, cases were categorized as postnatal death, early intrauterine fetal death (0–24 h before delivery), intermediate intrauterine fetal death (25–48 h before delivery), or advanced intrauterine fetal death (> 48 h before delivery).

In contrast, the interval between delivery and autopsy, as well as the duration of water exposure in the water group, was precisely recorded in hours.

Results

Description of intervention and control group

Between October 2021 and May 2023, 15 infants were initially included in the intervention group (water group) and were placed in cold water postpartum. The control group (standard storage) comprised 15 stillborn infants born between August 2020 and January 2023 who were not immersed in water. A single case from the water group (W9) and a single case from the control group (C2) were excluded from further analysis due to the absence of an autopsy, resulting in 14 infants per group available for analysis.

Gestational age at birth ranged from 15 + 4 to 38 + 5 weeks in the water group and from 15 + 3 to 26 + 6 weeks in the control group. Birth weights ranged from 40 to 2695 g in the water group and from 60 to 890 g in the control group. Infants in the water group had a higher gestational age compared with those in the control group, with the inclusion of term and near-term infants in the water group, whereas the control group consisted exclusively of preterm infants.

In the water group, infants were immersed in water for periods ranging from 11 to 49 h. The time interval between birth and autopsy as well as the estimated interval between fetal death and birth covered a broad range in both groups.

Causes of death in the water group included intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD, n = 8), intra-amniotic infection (IAI, n = 3), medically indicated termination of pregnancy (TOP) (n = 2), and preterm premature rupture of membranes before viability (PPROM, n = 2). In the control group, causes of death were IUFD (n = 6), IAI (n = 5), medically indicated TOP (n = 2), and PPROM (n = 2).

Two cases (W9 and C2) were excluded from further analysis due to the absence of an autopsy. Detailed case characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Macroscopic effects of the water method

Degrees of maceration were defined and assessed in both groups during autopsy.

In infants who were submerged in water after birth, several macroscopic effects were observed. Infants immersed in water consistently adopted a more natural flexed fetal posture during immersion, and postmortem body deformations were less pronounced (Fig. 1). Pre-existing skin detachments associated with advanced maceration did not appear to progress further during water storage but remained largely unchanged. Microscopic effects of the water method

The liver and lungs were selected as representative organs for comparative histological analysis, as these tissues are particularly susceptible to autolytic changes and are routinely examined in perinatal autopsies.

Histological examination of the liver showed comparable findings in both groups. Tissue architecture was preserved, with no differences in hepatocellular integrity, sinusoidal structures, or portal tracts. Discussion

The present study represents the first systematic investigation assessing whether postnatal immersion in water after perinatal death affects autopsy outcomes. The findings suggest that water immersion was not associated with impaired diagnostic validity of postmortem examinations. Infants placed in water after birth demonstrated improved preservation of external appearance—particularly with regard to skin color, texture, and body posture—compared with infants who were not immersed. These differences were visual and did not interfere with autopsy evaluation.

Benefits and clinical relevance of the water method

The primary benefit of the water method lies in the improved preservation of the infants’ external appearance. In emotionally highly distressing situations such as perinatal loss, this allows parents to spend more time with their child in a dignified condition. Numerous studies have demonstrated that memory-making practices, including seeing, holding, and photographing deceased infants, have a beneficial impact on grief processing and psychological well-being [2–5].

Perinatal loss is associated with substantial psychological burden, including increased rates of depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and complicated grief [6, 7]. Long-term effects on parental morbidity and mortality have also been reported [8, 9].

Against this background, the water method may be regarded as a low-threshold intervention that supports parental farewell and may positively influence the grieving process. Clinical experience from the Netherlands and Germany further indicates that this method is well accepted in clinical practice and effectively preserves the infants’ external appearance over time [10, 11].

Impact on pathology and potential morphological changes

A central objective of this study was to determine whether postnatal immersion in water induces macroscopic or microscopic changes in the skin or internal organs that could compromise autopsy findings [3]. Forensic literature describes characteristic changes in submerged bodies, including so-called “washerwoman’s skin,” epidermal detachment, and pallor. At the same time, it is well established that water may slow autolysis and reduce bacterial activity due to its cooling effect [3, 12].

In the present study, however, no temperature differences existed between the water and control groups, as all infants were stored in the same refrigerator at a constant temperature of 5 °C. During the parental farewell period, infants in both groups were kept at room temperature and were not immersed in water. Consequently, within the context of the water method, reduced bacterial activity during water storage—rather than temperature differences—may have been the only relevant factor.

The analyses revealed neither increased skin alterations in infants stored in water nor macroscopic or microscopic differences in internal organs (e.g., liver and lungs) between the groups. Furthermore, in cases involving congenital anomalies (W10 omphalocele, W13 cerebral abnormality, C8 hypoplastic left heart syndrome), diagnostic assessment during autopsy was not impaired.

Maceration and time-dependent factors

The findings indicate that the degree of maceration was not influenced by the water method but was primarily determined by the time in utero after intrauterine death and the intervals between death, birth, and autopsy. Longer intervals between intrauterine death and delivery were associated with more advanced maceration. This observation is consistent with the results of a systematic review [13].

Classical pathological descriptions indicate that early changes such as epidermal detachment may occur within hours after intrauterine death, whereas organ alterations typically develop only after several days [10]. Within the limitations imposed by the small sample size, no differences in maceration attributable to water immersion were observed.

Limitations and challenges

This study has several limitations. The small sample size represents the principal limitation and reflects the challenges of recruitment in emotionally highly distressing situations. Obtaining informed consent and willingness to participate in research following perinatal loss is inherently difficult, and many families decline autopsy, further reducing the number of eligible cases. Due to the limited sample size, the study was not powered to detect subtle associations between duration of water immersion and maceration or appearance preservation. Larger studies are therefore required to validate these findings and to enable more differentiated evaluation.

Another limitation is the inherent subjectivity in assessing external appearance. Although maceration grading followed established criteria, evaluation of external appearance remains partly observer-dependent. Perceptions of esthetic quality and grading of maceration are subject to interindividual variability, and substantial interobserver variability in the pathology of perinatal deaths has been well documented [13, 14]. Maceration grading was performed by a single pathologist without blinding to water immersion status, which may have introduced observer bias and precluded assessment of interobserver reliability. Furthermore, intra-amniotic infection was more frequent in the control group and may have influenced microscopic pulmonary findings independently of postnatal storage conditions, representing an additional potential confounder.

The retrospective assembly of the control group without matching for gestational age resulted in an imbalance between groups. The inclusion of term infants in the water group and the predominance of preterm infants in the control group may have influenced maceration patterns and limited comparability between groups. Minor differences in documentation between prospectively and retrospectively collected data cannot be excluded.

Conclusion and perspectives

In summary, the findings of this study suggest that postnatal immersion of infants after perinatal death in water is not associated with impaired autopsy outcomes. At the same time, the method appears to support preservation of the infants’ external appearance and may facilitate parental farewell. The psychological significance of such farewell rituals is well established [1, 5].

Overall, the water method represents a simple, practical, and emotionally supportive technique that may offer meaningful benefit in clinical practice by supporting the parental grieving process.
