AI, digital twins, and healthcare utilization behaviour: evidence from a Swiss population survey
Authors: Claudia M Witt, Jiahui An, Markus Christen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increasing development of artificial intelligence (AI) and digital twin technologies in healthcare raises important questions about their future acceptance. Investigating how healthcare utilization behaviour influences openness to such future digital innovations can support the design of health systems that align with utilization behaviour and expectations.MethodsA nationally representative online survey of 1,486 adults in Switzerland was conducted in 2023. Participants were categorized into five groups based on their healthcare utilization behaviour: conventional medicine users, complementary medicine users, integrative medicine users, self-treatment users, and pharmacy users. The survey assessed digital literacy, current use of digital tools, and attitudes toward AI and digital twins. Digital twin understanding was ensured through an explanatory video and comprehension checks. Weighted analyses and regression models were used to evaluate differences between groups. (no health care intervention on human participants; no requirement for trial registration or ethics approval).ResultsAcross all healthcare utilization behaviour groups, the majority of participants reported a willingness to use digital twin services, with significantly higher agreement among self-treatment users. This group also demonstrated the highest digital literacy, most frequent use of health-related apps, and strongest familiarity with AI tools such as ChatGPT. They rated the potential benefits of digital twins more highly and expressed greater trust in both public and private institutions offering these services. Complementary and integrative medicine users reported lower digital literacy and were less likely to consider digital twins beneficial across domains such as cost reduction, therapy validation, and disease prediction. Conventional medicine users expressed specific concerns about digital twins replacing physicians, rated predictive features less favourably, and showed the lowest support for independent personal use of digital twins. Pharmacy users showed moderate willingness, with relatively high trust in institutions and greater appreciation for the coordination and predictive functions of digital twins.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that attitudes toward AI-based health technologies are closely tied to existing patterns of healthcare usage. While digital twins are generally well received, differences in acceptance highlight the need to tailor implementation strategies to specific user profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13786-3.Keywords: Digital health, Digital twin, Healthcare services, Representative population survey, Artificial intelligence, Complementary and integrative medicine

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming healthcare – from diagnostics to treatment planning and patient management. AI applications span a wide range of domains; in a field like oncology, they include medical imaging, pathology, patient triage, radiotherapy, genomics-guided therapy, and surgical interventions. These advancements are increasingly integrated with existing technologies such as clinical informatics systems, natural language processing and digital twin models [1].

A digital twin in healthcare is defined as “a viewable digital replica of a patient, organ, or biological system that contains multidimensional, patient-specific information and informs decisions” [2]. For a whole person, it would be a virtual representation that enables dynamic simulation of potential treatment strategies, monitoring and prediction of health trajectories, and early intervention and prevention—based on multi-scale modelling of multi-modal data, including clinical, genetic, molecular, environmental, and social factors [3]. A key feature would be the ability to continuously update in real time using data from diverse sources such as electronic health records, wearable devices, environmental data, and monitoring systems, allowing for adaptive and personalized healthcare interventions.

Digital twin technology has seen widespread adoption across diverse industries, and its application in healthcare is rapidly gaining momentum. The emerging concept of digital twins for health offers significant potential to transform healthcare systems by enhancing service delivery, disease prevention and treatment, and overall health maintenance—ultimately aiming to improve patient outcomes and quality of life [3].

While fully integrated digital twins representing the entire human body remain a vision for the future, research on organ-specific digital twins—such as those for the heart, lungs, and brain—has already advanced significantly, with applications in device design, surgical planning, and treatment optimization [3]. However, the majority of the digital twin projects (98%) are still in preclinical phases [2]. Nevertheless, given the rapid pace of technological advancement, it is essential to begin preparing now for the integration of digital twins in healthcare to ensure their future deployment is ethical, effective, and aligned with clinical and societal needs.

As AI tools and digital twins transition from research to clinical application, it becomes crucial to understand the factors driving their adoption and integration into healthcare systems. Currently, research and development in the field remain predominantly technology-driven, often overlooking the lived experiences and needs of users. Findings from a recent scoping review highlight the urgent need for a shift toward a more human-centred research and design agenda when it comes to AI tools —one that places individuals and evolving teams at the core to ensure that technological innovations are meaningful, usable, and contextually relevant [4]. Human factors encompass a variety of elements. This also includes the utilization of different formats of healthcare delivery available today—such as medical doctors, licensed therapists, and pharmacies or even self-treatment—and the types of therapies used, ranging from conventional to complementary and integrative therapies.

According to the World Health Organisation [5] complementary therapies refer to a broad set of health care practices that are not part of that country’s conventional medicine and are not fully integrated into the dominant health care system [5]. Complementary therapies, for example, acupuncture, herbal medicine or hypnosis, can be provided by doctors or licensed therapists. In Switzerland, due to health insurance coverage, it is common for primary care doctors to offer an integrative medicine approach, combining selected complementary therapies with conventional healthcare.

The healthcare utilization behaviour of individuals, including the individuals’ preferred types and formats of care, reflects a complex interplay of values and beliefs. While the reasons for those behaviours are multifaceted, they are not so difficult to observe or document. Consequently, comparing different healthcare utilization behaviour groups could provide valuable insights into the likelihood of adopting emerging digital health technologies, such as AI-driven tools and digital twins. When planning the survey we had two main hypotheses on healthcare user-group behaviour:

Persons who prioritize self-treatment methods are more receptive to digital health in general, and to digital twins in particular.

Persons who prioritize self-treatment methods are more receptive to digital health in general, and to digital twins in particular.

Persons who prioritize complementary and integrative medicine tend to be less receptive to digital health overall, including digital twins.

Methods

The study was designed as a representative population survey in order to: (1) categorize healthcare utilization behaviour groups, (2) identify attitudes and expectations in the population towards AI and digital twins, and (3) determine differences between healthcare utilization behaviour groups for those attitudes. In the following, we outline the survey methodology, the data analysis and the group categorization.

Survey

Questionnaire structure

The questionnaire was structured as follows: Preliminary questions were used to ensure the representativeness of the survey. Participants were able to choose the language of the questionnaire—available in the three official Swiss languages: German, French, and Italian—and provided informed consent to participate in the study.

The questionnaire was then divided into four parts: In the first part, socio-demographic information was collected, including age, gender, language, residence region, education level, political orientation to serve as control variables. Digital literacy was assessed using Hargittai’s digital skills scale, a standard scale widely applied internationally [6, 7]. Participants rated their understanding of six Internet-related concepts (advanced search, PDF, spyware, wiki, cache, phishing) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = no understanding, 5 = full understanding). In addition, AI familiarity was measured separately by asking participants about their awareness and use of ChatGPT, ranging from ‘never heard of’ to ‘regular use for personal/professional purposes.’ These two measures capture strongly related but distinct constructs: general digital literacy and specific AI familiarity. In addition, participants documented whether their health insurance included complementary medicine coverage. Education levels followed the standard classification adapted to the Swiss education system [8] and were later grouped into three broader categories: “Academic education” for those with university degree, “Intermediate education” for secondary to non-university tertiary qualifications, and “Basic education” for all other educational backgrounds. Political orientation was measured on a scale from 0 (far left) to 10 (far right), with 5 representing a central political stance. Health insurance status was assessed in relation to complementary medicine coverage, with response options including: “Yes”, “Yes, but I hardly ever need it”, “No”, “No, but desired”, and “I don’t know”. ChatGPT knowledge was assessed using the following options: “I have never heard of a program called ChatGPT”, “I have heard of ChatGPT, but have not looked into it further”, “I have read about ChatGPT but have never tried it myself”, “I have tried ChatGPT myself, but don’t use it otherwise” and “I use ChatGPT regularly for private and/or professional purposes”.

The second part consisted of general questions on (generative) AI; the results of this part are reported elsewhere, with the exception of two questions regarding the acceptance of AI use in a medical context [9].

In the third part, participants indicated their utilization for various medical services, including current digital healthcare and different forms of medical care in the event of illness (e.g., general practitioners, integrative care, self-treatment, pharmacies). They also reported how important they considered access to these various options for their personal healthcare.

In the fourth part, questions about the use of digital twins in healthcare were presented. This section began with a video explaining the concept of a “digital twin” and outlining possible use scenarios. It was followed by two control questions to assess whether participants had sufficiently understood the concept; persons who answered incorrectly were excluded from the analysis. Participants were then asked whether they would personally use a digital twin as depicted in the video, which reasons for using a digital twin they found important, and what expectations they had regarding the quality and safety of the technology. Additionally, they were asked to evaluate the plausibility of various arguments both supporting and opposing the use of digital twins. Finally, participants assessed their level of trust in institutions that may use digital twins—such as hospitals and private companies—as well as their agreement with general statements concerning the introduction of this technology into society (e.g., data sharing).

At the end of the survey, participants could make anonymous general comments on the survey. The questionnaire is available as supplementary online material (original languages and English translation).

Survey execution

The representative population survey was conceptualized by the research team. Several pretests were conducted to ensure comprehensibility and usability. The survey was distributed as a nationally representative study by a professional survey company (gfs Bern; https://www.gfsbern.ch/en/) between July 26 to August 22, 2023.

In total, the survey was accessed 2,056 times. 526 persons (25.6%) dropped out of the analysis due to incomplete data (e.g., because the quota was reached or because persons were allowed to drop part 4 of the survey); 44 persons (1.8%) were excluded because they failed answering the control questions for the digital twin video. In total, data of Ntotal = 1,486 persons were used for the analysis. The data were weighted to ensure that the sample was representative of the general Swiss population in terms of age, education, gender, language region, and political orientation. The weighting factors were provided by the survey company. Weighting corrects for the underrepresentation of individuals with lower education levels, which is common in surveys with a technical focus. As a result, the total sample size (Ntotal) for some variables in the weighted data may slightly differ from the original Ntotal = 1,486.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, and standard deviation (SD), were calculated for baseline demographic data and responses to digital twin-related questions within each group. Findings are presented as weighted means and standard deviations, as well as weighted counts and percentage within each group for the respective variables. The data is presented in tables; the findings are also visualized as figures that are available as supplementary material (the numbering of the figures equals the numbering of the tables).

We also assessed significant differences between individual groups and the overall sample. To do so, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to evaluate variations in responses across groups, followed by post-hoc analyses to determine whether specific group differences were significant. To reduce the risk of Type I errors resulting from multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. The analysis was conducted using RStudio (version 2023.09.1) and included both numerical and categorical variables derived from the questionnaire responses.

Attitude analysis

Binary logistic regression explored the factors influencing respondents’ attitudes towards specific digital twin aspects. We excluded the “I do not know” option from analysis. The remaining response options were coded into two binary categories to highlight the distinction between negative and positive sentiments, or levels of certainty. A value of “1” represented more negative or certain sentiments (aggregated from original categories 3 and 4), while “0” represented more positive or uncertain sentiments (categories 1 and 2). Binary logistic regression, known for its robust handling of binary outcomes, helped clarify the impact of various predictors on the dependent variable.

Numerical variables analysis

Numerical responses were analysed using weighted linear regression to evaluate the effect of group membership. This was implemented using the lm function from R’s base package. For each numerical question, we iterated over groups to fit a model with the response as the dependent variable and group membership as an independent variable, applying sample weights through the weights argument of the lm function.

Categorical variables analysis

For categorical responses, we converted variables to a binary format and performed weighted logistic regression using the glm“ function (also from R’s base package), specifying the “family = binomial” parameter, again applying sample weights via the weights argument.

Group categorization

Group definition

Participants were categorized based on their healthcare utilization behaviour, as derived from their questionnaire responses, especially the rankings for healthcare providers (Table 1). The groups were defined based on respondents’ priority (1 = high priority, 2 = moderate priority, 3 = low priority) for the type of healthcare and/or the provider they would choose in the event of illness or inability to work and perceived need for treatment. This categorization allowed for an in-depth analysis of digital twin technology acceptance across diverse group profiles.

Results

This section presents the outcomes of our in-depth analysis concerning responses to our questions across the five health-related groups. We first outlined the demographic profiles and digital literacy levels in order to identify whether the groups differ with respect to those descriptors. Then, we described the trends regarding the use and views of digital twin technology in healthcare; both overall and specified for the groups. This included examining variables such as likelihood of use, perceived benefits, trust in institutions, and expectations from digital twin-derived therapies.

It is important to consider that varying sample sizes within subgroups can affect statistical power, effect size (Cohen’s d), and within-group variability, potentially explaining some disparities in significance. As a result, even when the mean responses of two subgroups appear to be comparable, individual variables may yield differing significance levels when compared to the overall mean.

Demographic characteristics of the groups

A total of 1,486 individuals participated in the survey (see Table 2), and more than half (58%) reported having additional health insurance to cover treatments by licensed complementary medicine therapists. When comparing the healthcare utilization behaviour groups with respect to gender, we found that conventional medicine users were more likely to be male, while complementary medicine users and pharmacy users were more likely to be female. With respect to age, we found that integrative medicine users tended to be older than the whole sample, whereas self-treatment users and pharmacy users tended to be younger. With respect to language, we found that self-treatment users were more often German-speaking, while pharmacy users were often French-speaking. No significant differences were observed between the groups and the overall sample in terms of political orientation.

Opinions on digital-twin-related aspects

Acceptance and perceived benefits of digital twin technology

Nearly two-thirds (62%) of the participants were willing to use a digital twin (Table 6). Interestingly, a majority in all groups expressed willingness to use a digital twin, ranging from 54.4% among complementary medicine users to 66.4% among self-treatment users. Self-treatment users showed a significantly higher willingness to use digital twin services compared to the whole sample. They also saw significantly higher benefits across all presented options, underscoring their higher acceptance of this technology. Conventional medicine users were more sceptical, particularly regarding the claim that a digital twin could replace a human doctor.

Discussion

Overall, the results of this representative survey revealed a remarkably high level of openness toward the use of digital twins in healthcare. Across all healthcare utilization behaviour groups, the majority of participants expressed a willingness to use digital twin services, especially when these services are provided by trusted public healthcare institutions and demonstrated a clear benefit to personal health. This general receptivity indicates that, despite differences in values, digital literacy, and treatment utilization, digital twin technologies are broadly perceived as promising tools for improving healthcare outcomes—provided that implementation is transparent, evidence-based, and aligned with user expectations.

Differences between health care user groups

Our findings provided strong support for the first hypothesis: individuals who prioritize self-treatment methods are significantly more receptive to digital health innovations in general—and to digital twins in particular. This group, characterized by high digital literacy, frequent use of health-related apps, and strong familiarity with AI tools such as ChatGPT, also showed the highest willingness to adopt digital twin technologies. They rated the potential benefits of digital twins more highly than any other group and expressed strong interest in access to the latest medical science, health-promoting measures, and digital care services. Self-treatment users also found arguments in favour of digital twins more convincing, showed greater scepticism toward arguments against them, and displayed higher levels of trust in both public and private institutions—especially private clinics and health insurers—to use digital twins in a positive way. Moreover, they were more likely to support making anonymized health data available for digital twin research, underscoring a generally proactive and tech-affine stance toward future health technologies.

Our second hypothesis—that individuals prioritizing complementary and integrative medicine are less receptive to digital health and digital twins—was also largely confirmed. Complementary and integrative medicine users displayed the highest overall degree of scepticism toward digital twins. Complementary medicine users, in particular, showed less willingness to use such services. Both groups were also less likely to believe that digital twins could help reduce health insurance premiums, support the evaluation of medical therapy proposals, or predict the course of a disease to enable countermeasures. They were also sceptical that digital twins can enable doctors to treat patients more effectively or that individuals receive more accurate diagnoses and therapies through these services. In particular, integrative medicine users expressed distrust toward public hospitals, medical research universities, private clinics, and pharmaceutical or MedTech companies, doubting that these institutions are those who should provide the digital twin services. One possible reason for this scepticism is that complementary and integrative medicine users are more likely to perceive digital twins as being based on a flawed concept of the human being—namely, viewing humans as “machines”. They also believed more often that digital twins may create psychological stress to remain healthy and fear that individuals could be pressured into making their health data freely available for anonymized digital twin research. Nevertheless, complementary medicine users were more supportive of the idea that individuals should be allowed to use digital twins—more so than other groups, with the exception of self-treatment users.

Some of our results confirm findings of similar studies or appear plausible given the characteristics of the user groups. Complementary and integrative medicine users tended to be more often female; self-treatment users tended to be younger and higher educated [10, 11]. In contrast to findings from the 2017 Swiss Health Survey [12] and a 2022 German Survey [12, 13] which reported that individuals with higher educational level were more likely to use complementary therapies—our survey found that complementary medicine users were less likely to have completed higher education. Interestingly, our population of complementary therapy users also exhibited lower levels of digital literacy—an aspect that was not assessed in the other representative surveys.

Interestingly, complementary and integrative medicine users do not appear to be the primary “counterpart” to self-treatment users when it comes to digital innovations in medicine. Although they use tools such as ChatGPT significantly less often in their everyday lives, they value access to the latest medical science and demonstrate a differentiated use of apps—integrated medicine users tended to prefer apps that support communication with doctors, while complementary medicine users showed more scepticism toward apps for self-diagnosis.

Rather, the “counterpart” to the self-treatment users with respect to digital health utilization appears to be conventional medicine users who—despite higher digital literacy—showed clearly relying on family doctors instead over accessing the latest medical science, health-promoting measures, or digital care services. In addition, the conventional medicine users displayed indications of scepticism towards digital twins. In particular, they fear that a digital twin could replace doctors—recalling that this group places high on access to family doctors—and they express the greatest doubt that digital twins can enable early detection of health risks. Conventional medicine users also showed significantly less trust in technology companies when it comes to offering digital twins services. They appeared more sceptical for any use of digital twins for personal health management, expressing the greatest doubts about the idea that individuals can manage their own health using digital twins. They were also particularly hesitant about allowing individuals to use digital twins independently for prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

Another notable finding is that nearly half of the conventional medicine users have a private health insurance package that includes coverage for complementary medicine, despite showing the lowest overall usage of such therapies. This may be explained by the fact that many private health insurance plans in Switzerland cover a broad range of services, including preventive and health-promoting diagnostics, interventions, and complementary therapies.

An interesting group with respect to digital health utilization are the pharmacy users. In Switzerland, the range of health services offered by pharmacies is steadily increasing, now frequently including diagnostic testing and basic treatment options. This expansion enhances their role as easily accessible entry points into the healthcare system. Although pharmacy users did not show a significantly higher willingness to use digital twin services compared to the total, they were significantly more likely to believe that digital twins can help predict the course of a disease to enable countermeasures, lead to earlier detection and faster treatment of illnesses—ultimately reducing healthcare costs—and improve doctors’ ability to treat patients. They also expressed greater trust in public hospitals, medical research universities, and, interestingly, in technology companies as well as pharmaceutical and medical technology companies to use digital twins in a positive way. However, pharmacy users also feared that digital twins may create psychological stress to remain healthy. They believe digital twins should be used exclusively by healthcare professionals, and that persons should not be allowed to use them independently for prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

Influence of demographic factors

With regard to demographic factors, we observed both expected and surprising patterns. French-speaking participants in general display more scepticism towards digital twins and more distrust towards institutions using them, which is in line with previous findings regarding acceptance of digital technologies such as Big Data or AI [9, 14–16]. Women in our survey displayed significantly more scepticism toward digital twins across various dimensions, including adoption of digital twin technology and concerns that digital twins could cause psychological stress related to maintaining health—findings that are consistent with previous studies [17–24].

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into healthcare utilization and attitudes toward digital twin technologies across a representative sample of the Swiss population, several limitations should be acknowledged in questions involving digital literacy or emerging technologies. We implemented a video explainer and comprehension check to introduce the digital twin concept, as it remains a novel and complex topic. Participants’ responses may reflect limited understanding or speculative judgments rather than informed opinions, especially for those unfamiliar with digital health technologies. Although the survey was designed to be representative, the Italian-speaking population in Switzerland is relatively small and was underrepresented in the final sample. This limits the generalizability of the findings to that linguistic region and may obscure language-specific cultural or systemic differences in healthcare utilization and digital health acceptance. Furthermore, the study primarily focused on general attitudes and willingness to adopt digital twins but did not explore privacy concerns or data governance in detail, even though these are critical factors influencing public trust and acceptance of AI-based health technologies. In addition, this study does not provide indicators on how the outcomes will impact the health outcomes, support clinicians, and patients, per se. Despite these limitations, this study is unique in combining a representative population survey with a detailed analysis of healthcare utilization behaviour to explore acceptance of future digital health technologies. It provided valuable, user-centred insights that can guide the responsible development and targeted implementation of AI and digital twin solutions in healthcare.

Conclusion

This study offers novel insights into how different patterns of healthcare utilization—ranging from conventional to complementary, integrative, self-treatment, and pharmacy-based care—influence the acceptance of future-oriented digital health technologies such as AI and digital twins. While overall willingness to adopt digital twins is relatively high across all user groups, notable differences emerge. It is valuable for developers and providers of digital twins - both now and in the medium-term future—to understand that there are different groups with distinct health-related usage behaviours. Depending on whether their product is intended to address all groups or only specific ones, developers may need to target these groups differently when developing and implementing digital twins.
