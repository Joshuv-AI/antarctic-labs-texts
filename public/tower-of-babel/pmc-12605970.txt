The Effectiveness and Safety of Oral N‐Acetylcysteine in Combination With Narrow‐Band Ultraviolet B (NB‐UVB) Phototherapy Compared With NB‐UVB Phototherapy Alone in the Treatment of Vitiligo: A Pilot Study
Authors: Afsaneh Sadeghzadeh‐Bazargan, Saeideh Farahani, Azadeh Goodarzi, Roya Zeinali, Mehdi Gheisari, Najmolsadat Atefi, Mohammadreza Ghassemi, Masoumeh Roohaninasab, Abbas Dehghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsVitiligo is an autoimmune skin disease, characterized by depigmented patches over skin. Numerous studies have been conducted to identify the most effective treatment for this condition. Based on high rate of oxidative stress in pathogenesis of vitiligo, antioxidant treatments like N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) is suggested. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of oral NAC in combination with narrow‐band UVB (NB‐UVB) phototherapy, comparing it to NB‐UVB therapy alone in treating patients with generalized vitiligo.MethodsThis single‐blind, randomized controlled Phase 1 trial conducted over a period of 4 months, involving 16 participants. The patients were split into two, with one group receiving 600 mg of oral NAC twice daily, alongside NB‐UVB, while the control group received only phototherapy. The patients were reassessed after 2 and 4 months, with evaluations based on the Vitiligo Extent Tensity Index (VETI), overall patient satisfaction, treatment tolerability, and side effects.ResultsAmong the 16 patients, 68.8% were male, with an average age of 40.7 ± 9.9 years. Both treatment groups showed a significant reduction in VETI scores during the second and third assessments (p < 0.05). However, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Patient satisfaction was notably higher in the NAC + NB‐UVB group during the second visit (p = 0.01), and by the third visit, 75% of patients in the NAC + NB‐UVB group reported excellent satisfaction, compared to 25% in the control group (p = 0.07). Treatment tolerability was similar across both groups, though one patient in the NAC + NB‐UVB group experienced dizziness, which was resolved after adjusting the dose.ConclusionOral NAC appears to be a safe adjuvant therapy in the treatment of generalized vitiligo patients in combination with NB‐UVB phototherapy.Trial Register Name “Evaluation of the efficacy of NB‐UVB phototherapy with oral N‐ acetylcysteine compared with NB‐UVB phototherapy alone in the treatment of vitiligo patients: a randomized controlled clinical trial”.Clinical trial registration number: IRCT20210729052013N1.Keywords: clinical trial, NAC, N‐acetylcysteine, NB‐UVB, phototherapy, vitiligo

Introduction

Vitiligo is an autoimmune skin disease characterized by loss of melanocytes that depigmented patches are seen over any areas, including acral sites and body orifices [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. This condition affects about 0.5% to 2% of the general population, and psychological effects such as depression, anxiety and stigmatization are observed more frequently [3].

Antioxidant defenses against oxidative stress are observed in epidermal melanocytes during melanin synthesis [6, 7, 8, 9]. It is reported that oxidative stress in vitiligo was higher than in healthy subjects [10, 11, 12]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can not only trigger autoimmune responses, but also extend them to new epitopes by revealing hidden key factors that lead to melanocyte damage [13, 14]. This fact has been repeatedly studied and confirmed, so targeting this part of the pathogenesis of vitiligo with antioxidants may offer promising treatment options for patients [15, 16, 17].

Glutathione is a known antioxidant, and a decrease in the activity of the enzyme glutathione peroxidase can disrupt the balance between oxidants and antioxidants, as is the case in vitiligo [18]. The decrease in systemic glutathione levels in vitiligo patients, independent of disease activity, is a characteristic feature showing a significant association between low glutathione peroxidase levels and vitiligo [19, 20, 21]. N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) contributes to antioxidant defense primarily by enhancing glutathione production, helping to counter oxidative damage. Therefore, suppression of oxidative stress by NAC may play a therapeutic role in vitiligo patients [22, 23, 24].

Narrow‐band UVB phototherapy (NB‐UVB), first introduced in 1997, is widely regarded as one of the safest and most effective treatment options for vitiligo [8, 25]. The mechanism of action and its potential benefits is established in literature when combined with other therapies [25]. Studies have shown that this treatment improves the oxidant‐antioxidant balance in vitiligo patients by reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and increasing glutathione levels [8].

Given that both NB‐UVB and N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) contribute to increasing glutathione levels and improving antioxidant defense in vitiligo patients, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of their combined use compared to NB‐UVB alone.

Materials and Methods

Patients

This single‐blind, randomized phase 1 clinical trial was conducted over 4 months, involving 16 patients with vitiligo at dermatology clinic of Rasool Akram hospital from February 2022 to August 2022.

Participants were required to have generalized vitiligo (vulgaris, acrofacial, and universal), which were established by wood lamp, and be eligible for NB‐UVB phototherapy.

Exclusion criteria were an allergic reaction to NAC, a history of autoimmune disease or malignancy, photosensitive disorders, and receiving vitiligo treatments in 2 month, pregnancy and lactation.

The study was explained thoroughly to participants and inform consent was obtained. This trial was conducted according to Helsinki ethical principles and was approved by Ethics committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences under number IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1399.703.

The participants were randomly assigned to two groups of 8:

Group A received 600 mg of oral NAC twice daily, starting 2 weeks before beginning phototherapy, as intervention group.

Group A received 600 mg of oral NAC twice daily, starting 2 weeks before beginning phototherapy, as intervention group.

Group B served as the control group, receiving only NB‐UVB phototherapy, as control group.

Group B served as the control group, receiving only NB‐UVB phototherapy, as control group.

Both groups underwent treatment using the Dr. Honle Dermalight 3000 phototherapy device, which emits a wavelength of 311 nm, three times per week. The initial dose was set at 65 mJ/cm² and was increased by 65 mJ/cm² per session until reaching a maximum dose of 1000 mJ/cm².

Randomization and Blinding

A simple randomization technique was employed using a box containing sealed bags, each labeled with either Code A (NAC + NB‐UVB) or Code B (NB‐UVB alone). The trial was conducted as a single‐blinded study, that both the evaluating physician and the statistician remained unaware of the treatment assignments.

Assessment Methods

Patients were assessed at the beginning of the study, as well as at the second and fourth months, using 1) The Vitiligo Extent Tensity Index (VETI) scoring system: This system evaluates two key aspects of vitiligo: the extent of the disease, based on the rule of nines, and its progression stage. The stages are classified as follows: stage 0—normal pigmentation; stage 1—hypopigmentation, characterized by lighter skin and hair; stage 2—total depigmentation with black hair andperifollicular pigmentation; stage 3—total depigmentation with black hair but no perifollicular pigmentation; stage 4—total depigmentation with a mix of black and white hair, lacking perifollicular pigmentation; and stage 5—total depigmentation with considerable hair lightening. The final VETI score is determined using the following formula: (percentage of head involvement × severity) + (percentage of trunk involvement × severity) × 4 + (percentage of upper limb involvement × severity) × 2 + (percentage of lower limb involvement × severity) × 4 + (percentage of genital involvement × 1).

2) Determination of patient's overall satisfaction according to the 5 point Likert scale (none (1), little (2), moderate (3), good (4), and excellent (5)), and

3) Investigating tolerability: Patients were asked about the possible side effects of NAC, including gastrointestinal (heartburn, nausea, vomiting), as well as skin (persistent redness, itching or burning, and blistering) and other (dizziness, chest pain, fever).

Data Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 21. Variable distribution was assessed using the K‐S test. Comparisons between the two groups were conducted using the Student's t‐test and Mann‐Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi‐square test categorical variables. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic Characteristics

The mean age of groups A and B was 36.6 ± 11.1 years and 44.8 ± 6.9 years, respectively; the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.41). The mean disease duration was 10.3 ± 9.7 years in group A and 7.1 ± 4.0 years in group B, also showing no significant difference (p = 0.40).

Regarding gender distribution, 5 patients (62.5%) in the intervention group and 6 patients (75%) in the control group were male (p = 0.59). In terms of vitiligo type, acrofacial vitiligo was present in 1 patient (12.5%) from the combination group and 2 patients (25%) in the other group, while the remaining cases were classified as vulgaris type. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.54) (Table 1).

Main Outcomes after the Intervention

The initial VETI scores were 3.11 ± 3.31 in the intervention group and 3.32 ± 3.58 in the control group (p = 0.90). After 2 months, these values decreased to 1.79 ± 1.96 and 2.13 ± 2.39, respectively (p = 0.71). By the third visit, the scores further declined to 1.28 ± 1.34 in the intervention group and 1.89 ± 2.15 in the control group. Although there was no significant difference in VETI scores between groups over the 4‐month follow‐up (p = 0.71 and p = 0.57, respectively), both groups experienced a significant reduction in scores (p < 0.05), with a more pronounced decrease in the NAC + NB‐UVB group (Table 2, Figure 1).

The patient satisfaction rate was rated as excellent in 5 (62.5%) patients in the intervention group after 2 months, increasing to 6 (75.0%) after 4 months. In comparison, none of the patients in the control group reported excellent satisfaction at the second visit, which increased to 2 (25.0%) by the third visit. Table 2 illustrates the satisfaction levels in both groups, showing a higher level of satisfaction in the combination group. This trend was statistically significant at the second visit (p = 0.01).

Although satisfaction remained higher in the combination group, the difference was not statistically significant at later visits (p > 0.05).

Regarding treatment tolerance, 7 (87.5%) patients in the intervention group and all 8 (100.0%) patients in the control group tolerated the treatment well, with no significant difference between groups (p > 0.05). Side effects occurred in 1 (12.5%) patient in the NAC + NB‐UVB group and in none of the patients in the control group, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.32).

Statistical Power Analysis

Due to the small sample size (16 participants, 8 per group), a standard power calculation was conducted using a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.5), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and an independent t‐test. The calculated statistical power was approximately 32%, which is considerably below the commonly recommended threshold of 80%. This suggests that a larger sample size is required to obtain more conclusive results.

Discussion

The present study examined the efficacy and safety of oral N‐acetylcysteine (NAC), based on its antioxidant properties, in combination with phototherapy for the treatment of vitiligo. To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the use of oral NAC in this context. While the response rate was higher in the NAC + NB‐UVB group, the difference was not statistically significant compared to the phototherapy‐only group at any of the follow‐up visits.

NB‐UVB phototherapy is a well‐established treatment for vitiligo. It promotes the migration, proliferation, and differentiation of melanocytes and increases the expression of key pigmentary genes. It also reduces oxidative stress markers in vitiligo patients by decreasing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels—a marker of lipid peroxidation—and increasing glutathione peroxidase (GSH‐Px) activity, thereby restoring the oxidative‐antioxidative balance [8]. These mechanisms likely contributed to the satisfactory outcomes observed in both study groups.

Research has highlighted the potential role of antioxidant therapies in vitiligo. Studies have shown that combining antioxidants with NB‐UVB phototherapy can enhance treatment outcomes. For instance, a 2009 study by Maha et al. involving 24 patients with stable vitiligo demonstrated that combining NB‐UVB with oral alpha‐tocopherol (vitamin E) has been associated with enhanced repigmentation outcomes and reduction in plasma MDA levels compared to phototherapy alone [25]. Antioxidants neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are implicated in melanocyte destruction, thereby preserving the structural and functional integrity of melanocyte DNA, lipids, and proteins [26].

NAC, another potent antioxidant, has been studied in various dermatological conditions. For example, a topical 5% NAC gel significantly reduced comedo count in patients with acne vulgaris compared to placebo (p = 0.04) [27]. Only one prior study has investigated the use of topical NAC in vitiligo. In that study, a gel containing phenylalanine, Cucumis melo extract, and NAC, combined with phototherapy, achieved an excellent repigmentation rate of 73.5% [28].

This is the first study to investigate oral NAC in combination with NB‐UVB in vitiligo patients. NAC acts as a precursor to glutathione—a key intracellular antioxidant—helping to neutralize ROS and reduce oxidative stress, which may enhance the efficacy of phototherapy through a synergistic effect [18]. Furthermore, NAC may exert immunomodulatory effects by decreasing the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and enhancing regulatory T‐cell activity, which may help control the autoimmune response [29]. In autoimmune conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), NAC has been shown to reduce levels of inflammatory markers, including MDA and nitric oxide (NO) [30].

Although the results of the present phase 1 study showed a trend toward greater improvement in clinical parameters in the NAC group, these differences did not reach statistical significance. This may be attributed to the small sample size and relatively short duration of follow‐up.

NAC was generally well tolerated in current study, with only one case of dizziness, which resolved after dose reduction. No serious adverse events were reported. The most commonly reported side effects of NAC in the literature involve the gastrointestinal tract, such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Other reported side effects include metallic taste, drowsiness, dizziness, and wheezing [27]. Previous studies have also demonstrated the good safety profile of NAC [27, 28].

A key strength of this study is the use of the VETI scoring system for standardized clinical evaluation, along with the investigation of an oral treatment option, which may be better tolerated than topical agents for some patients.

Given the observed power calculation result (32%), the sample size in this study is indeed limited, which could affect both statistical power and generalizability. However, pilot studies or preliminary clinical trials commonly have smaller sample sizes due to constraints like recruitment limitations, the exploratory nature of the research, or resource limitations. The current study could be considered as exploratory research, aimed primarily at assessing feasibility, preliminary efficacy, and tolerability of combining NAC with phototherapy in vitiligo treatment. Results from such preliminary studies can inform larger, adequately powered randomized controlled trials.

For future studies, it is recommended to increase the sample size and a longer follow‐up period to improve statistical power and generalizability of results. Additionally, it is recommended to assess serum glutathione levels before and after treatment to better understand the mechanism of NAC in vitiligo management.

Conclusion

The results of this study could suggest that oral NAC is generally well tolerated and may offer potential benefits when combined with NB‐UVB phototherapy in patients with generalized vitiligo. Although the observed improvements were not statistically significant, the trend indicates a possible therapeutic role for NAC. Further randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up periods are warranted to validate these findings.
