Assessing postural loads and musculoskeletal disorders in saffron harvesters: a prospective repeated-measures observational study using an agricultural-specific tool
Authors: Mahdi Jalali, Seyyed Poorya Fazeli, Sayed Vahid Esmaeili, Mahdieh Niroumand, Reza Esmaeili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) represent one of the most prevalent occupational health challenges worldwide, particularly in agricultural sectors where workers are engaged in physically demanding tasks. Saffron harvesting, characterized by prolonged squatting, bending, and repetitive hand movements, imposes considerable biomechanical strain on workers, placing them at elevated risk for MSDs. This prospective repeated-measures observational study was conducted between September and November 2023 among the same cohort of 220 saffron harvesters in Razavi Khorasan Province, Iran, all of whom worked a minimum of three hours per day during the harvesting season. Data collection involved the Cornell Musculoskeletal Discomfort Questionnaire (CMDQ), researcher-developed checklists, interviews, and direct observations. Postural loads were assessed using the Agricultural Whole-Body Assessment (AWBA) method, while musculoskeletal discomfort levels were measured at two distinct time points: immediately prior to the start of the harvest season and at its conclusion (approximately 6 weeks later). Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 24. The analysis revealed mean ergonomic risk scores of 3.4 ± 1.82 (AULA), 3.1 ± 1.43 (ALLA), and 3.2 ± 1.38 (AWBA), indicating high to very high postural risk levels. Following the harvesting period, discomfort scores increased significantly across all body regions, with the knees (24.41), wrists (22.38), and lower legs (16.76) showing the highest mean values. Wilcoxon signed-rank test demonstrated significant increases in MSDs for the shoulder (P = 0.018), thigh (P < 0.001), wrist (P < 0.001), knees (P < 0.001), lower leg (P < 0.001), and foot (P = 0.010). Moreover, significant differences in MSDs scores across different working postures were observed in the following regions: neck (P = 0.015), shoulder (P = 0.009), lower back (P = 0.041), wrist (P = 0.038), thigh (P = 0.048), knees (P = 0.010), lower leg (P = 0.034), and foot (P = 0.043). Overall, the findings underscore the substantial ergonomic burden associated with saffron harvesting and highlight the urgent need for targeted ergonomic interventions. Implementing mechanization strategies, ergonomic tools, and posture training programs can play a vital role in reducing musculoskeletal risks and improving the health and productivity of workers involved in this labor-intensive agricultural activity.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Ergonomics, Agriculture, Occupational health, PostureSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are among the leading causes of occupational injury and disability in both developed and developing countries, imposing significant economic costs on national industries1,2. Studies highlight the importance of controlling and reducing these disorders, which remains a considerable challenge for ergonomists worldwide3,4. Given the significance of this issue, the World Health Organization (WHO) ranked MSDs as the second most common occupational disease after respiratory conditions in 20195.

According to the rates of illness and injury reported by the International Labour Organization (ILO), agriculture is one of the most hazardous industries6. Recent epidemiological studies also underscore this issue and reveal that the risk of MSDs, respiratory diseases, hearing loss due to noise exposure, pesticide-related illnesses, and an increased risk of cancer among agricultural workers is notably high7–10. Despite the prevalence of these diseases and numerous hazards associated with the occupation, the adverse conditions in agricultural work environments are evident when workers in this sector are medically examined11.

Due to the nature of agricultural work, which involves intense physical activities and high levels of manual labour, agricultural workers are at an increased risk of developing MSDs. The significance of risk factors contributing to MSDs and the prevalence of these disorders among farmers is such that some studies have shown that MSDs are the most common non-fatal occupational injury among farmers12,13. A study conducted in Sweden found that MSDs are the most prevalent occupational disease among farmers, with a prevalence rate of 51%, higher than that of the non-agricultural population in Sweden14. In the United States, a study indicated that over 60% of farmers suffer from MSDs in at least one area of the body15. The most recent study on the prevalence of MSDs among farmers in 2022 reported the highest prevalence in the lumbar region at 74%, shoulders at 63%, wrists at 62%, neck at 57%, and upper back at 45%. Additionally, comprehensive reviews indicate that many studies on MSDs in farmers focus on the harvest stage, emphasizing the importance of this stage compared to other work phases16,17.

According to the labour force survey conducted in the summer of 2016, the share of employment in the agricultural sector in Iran is 19.4%, reflecting a substantial number of workers in this productive sector18. Among the important agricultural products in Iran, saffron is a significant crop with a large workforce involved in its cultivation. Iran is the world’s largest producer of saffron. Besides Iran, saffron is also cultivated in Spain, Morocco, Greece, India, and Azerbaijan19. The provinces of Khorasan Razavi and Khorasan South are among the regions where many farmers rely on saffron cultivation for their livelihood20. Saffron flowers are harvested over a one-month period from mid-October to late November21,22. Due to the limited harvesting period, there have been very few studies on saffron harvesters. These few studies, however, indicate a high prevalence of MSDs among saffron harvesters20,23.

The saffron harvesting stage is the most labor-intensive and critical phase of saffron cultivation, involving the plucking of flowers and the separation of stigmas. This stage is carried out traditionally, which is often associated with various challenges. During their work, saffron harvesters experience various physical strains related to their tasks. Some of these physical risks include working in poor postures, bending over, working with bent legs, and having excessive arm extension from the body compared to a neutral position. These inadequate working conditions are also common in many agricultural occupations22,23. These risk factors contribute to the susceptibility of farmers to chronic musculoskeletal injuries, such as hip and knee osteoarthritis, lower back pain, upper body disorders and pain, and acute musculoskeletal injuries, including spinal trauma, fractures, and joint dislocations24.

Despite increasing attention to musculoskeletal risks in agricultural activities, existing studies on saffron harvesters have largely relied on general or non-specialized ergonomic assessment tools, limiting the precision and depth of their findings. Consequently, there is a lack of evidence-based, specialized data necessary for accurately analysing the physical demands and postural stressors unique to saffron harvesting. Notably, no prior studies have utilized agricultural-specific posture assessment methods to evaluate the working conditions and body postures of saffron harvesters. To address this gap, the present study applies the agricultural whole-body assessment (AWBA) method a tool specifically designed to assess ergonomic risks in agricultural settings for a systematic evaluation of postural conditions among saffron harvesters. Additionally, the study investigates and compares the prevalence and severity of MSDs before and after the one-month saffron harvesting period, aiming to quantify the physical impact of harvesting activities on workers’ musculoskeletal health.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective repeated‑measures observational study employed a longitudinal comparative approach to evaluate musculoskeletal discomforts among the same cohort of Iranian saffron harvesters from September to November 2023. Data were collected at two-time points baseline (pre‑harvest) and follow‑up (post‑harvest) and compared to assess the impact of harvesting activities on discomfort levels over an approximate six‑week period.

Participants

The study population consisted of saffron harvesters from the Razavi Khorasan province in eastern Iran, actively engaged in saffron harvesting during the harvest season. In Iran, the saffron harvesting season begins in early October and continues until the end of November. The inclusion criteria for participants were: harvesting saffron for at least 3 h per day, no pregnancy in women, no musculoskeletal injuries, and no congenital or genetic MSDs. Individuals who sustained musculoskeletal injuries unrelated to saffron harvesting during the study period were excluded. Additionally, participants who expressed a desire to withdraw from the study during the follow-up period (six-week) were also excluded. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and the study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Neyshabur University of Medical Sciences (Ethical code: IR.NUMS.REC.1401.029). All participants completed and signed the informed consent forms. The individuals gave their agreement to participate by answering the questionnaire. The researchers only had access to unidentified data.

Description of working conditions

The saffron harvesting stage is the most challenging, labor-intensive, and critical phase of saffron cultivation, involving the separation of the flowers from the saffron bulbs and the soil surface. This stage is traditionally performed manually, which is fraught with numerous difficulties. The saffron harvesting period is limited to a maximum of two months, from early October to the end of November. In traditional saffron harvesting methods, laborers assume various body postures. The most common postures are “bent over” and “semi-squatting.” In the bent trunk posture, the trunk is flexed more than 90 degrees, with straight legs and knees at an angle between 160 and 180 degrees. This posture typically exerts the most pressure on the lower back. In the semi-squatting posture, the legs are in a crouched position with knee angles less than 60 degrees. This posture generally places the most strain on the ankles and knees.

Due to the delicate and fragile nature of saffron flowers, harvesting is usually conducted in the early hours of the morning. The sooner the saffron flowers are picked, the higher their quality. Consequently, saffron harvesting is performed at a high speed, which is considered a risk factor. Overall, saffron harvesters are exposed to a wide range of risk factors, including improper posture, exertion, repetitive motions, and high work speed. Data collection: methods and tools

Data were collected through self-administered questionnaires, face-to-face interviews, and observation. The questionnaires were distributed to the saffron harvesters, and interviews were conducted to ensure the clarity and completeness of the responses. Demographic and occupational characteristics of individuals including age, weight, height, and work experience were recorded using a researcher-made check list and these characteristics were recorded by the researcher during data collection. Several validated instruments were employed to measure musculoskeletal discomfort and body postures of the participants. The data collection tools included:

Musculoskeletal Disorders Survey: The Cornell Musculoskeletal Discomfort Questionnaire (CMDQ) was utilized to evaluate the prevalence of work-related MSDs symptoms in before and after the saffron harvest period. This self-reported questionnaire gathers data on the presence and frequency of discomfort, its severity, and the extent to which discomfort interferes with work activities (see Fig. 2). Discomfort frequency is categorized into five levels: Never, 1–2 times per week, 3–4 times per week, daily, and Several times per day. Each frequency category is assigned a score as follows: Never = 0, 1–2 times per week = 1.5, 3–4 times per week = 3.5, Daily = 5, and several times per day = 10. Discomfort severity and interference are both rated on a three-point scale with scores of 1, 2, and 3. The overall score for WRMSDs symptoms is calculated by multiplying the frequency score by the severity score and the interference score. Consequently, the minimum possible score for each body part is 0, and the maximum possible score is 9025. To achieve more accurate results, the questionnaire was administered through direct interviews with the study participants, and their musculoskeletal discomfort was recorded. The discomfort levels were measured in the same group of saffron pickers at two distinct time points: at the beginning of the saffron harvest and again immediately after the harvest period ended (approximately six weeks later). Since the saffron harvesting season spans a maximum of two months and pickers work exclusively during this interval, this short-term longitudinal design allows a direct comparison of discomfort within the identical individuals across the two assessments. This approach was used to determine the impact of the saffron harvesting period on MSDs. Additionally, to evaluate the long-term effects of the two-month harvesting period on musculoskeletal discomfort, the questionnaire included the following question: “Has the two-month saffron harvesting period contributed to the development of persistent musculoskeletal discomfort in the areas under study”? The reliability and validity of the Persian version of this questionnaire have been established and reported as adequate by Afifeh Zadeh Kashani et al.26.

Body Posture Analysis: To identify the ergonomic physical risk factors contributing to musculoskeletal discomfort among the study participants, the Agriculture Whole Body Assessment (AWBA) method was employed. This method, introduced by Kong et al. in 2015, is designed to determine ergonomic risk factors for agricultural workers27. This tool was revised in 2018, and the latest version was released28. The AWBA tool assesses various body postures during work and combines them with the duration of time spent in each posture to separately evaluate risk levels for the upper body (AULA) and lower body (ALLA). The combined risk levels are then positioned in a 4 × 4 matrix to determine the overall risk level for the entire body (AWBA). The final risk levels are interpreted as follows: a final score of 1 indicates low risk, a score of 2 indicates moderate risk, a score of 3 indicates high risk, and a score of 4 indicates very high risk. The application of this method in agriculture and its comparison with other posture assessment tools has yielded favorable results11,18,29,30.

In this study, ergonomic postures were assessed through analysis of video recordings captured during saffron harvesting. In accordance with NIOSH guidelines for observation-based posture assessment, each video was meticulously reviewed to evaluate the ergonomic risks associated with worker postures. The recordings encompassed all stages of the harvesting process, enabling detailed observation of body alignment, joint angles, and movement patterns. Postures were classified and scored using the AWBA method to identify potential hazards and evaluate the risk levels associated with different postural positions31. To ensure scoring accuracy and consistency, all video analyses were performed by two trained ergonomics specialists. Both assessors had substantial prior experience in posture assessment within industrial settings and specific expertise in applying the AWBA method to agricultural tasks. To establish inter-rater reliability and reach a consensus, their initial independent assessments were reviewed and reconciled by a university professor specializing in ergonomics, who served as the final arbiter. The professor’s definitive evaluations were recorded as the final dataset for analysis. For each worker, the duration of time spent in each distinct posture was calculated. The posture in which an individual spent the greatest proportion of their total working time was subsequently identified and defined as that individual’s “dominant posture” for the purpose of subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 24. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, were calculated to summarize the data characteristics. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Since the data for MSDs scores did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were employed instead of parametric alternatives. To compare the prevalence of MSDs in different body regions between the beginning and the end of the saffron harvesting period, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was utilized. To examine the differences in discomfort levels across various working postures, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. To provide a more comprehensive interpretation of the findings and assess the practical significance of the observed differences, effect sizes were calculated in addition to statistical significance. For the paired comparisons (Wilcoxon test), the effect size (r) was derived from the standardized test statistic (Z) relative to the sample size. The magnitude of these effects was interpreted as negligible (< 0.10), small (0.10–0.29), medium (0.30–0.49), and large (≥ 0.50). A significance level of α = 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Descriptive and comparative results regarding the prevalence of MSDs among workers before and after the saffron harvesting period are presented in Table 3. Prior to the harvesting period, the highest mean discomfort score was observed in the wrist (6.15 ± 10.76), while the lowest was recorded in the hip (1.21 ± 2.97). Following the harvesting period, the mean scores increased across all body regions, with the highest severity shifting to the knees (24.41 ± 14.21) and the lowest remaining in the forearm (1.47 ± 5.19). Statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test revealed significant increases in MSD scores for the shoulder, wrist, thigh, knees, lower leg, and foot (P < 0.05). Specifically: The most substantial changes were observed in the knees (Z = −5.18, P < 0.001), which exhibited a medium-to-large effect size (r = 0.35), followed by the lower leg (r = 0.33) and wrist (r = 0.28), both showing medium effect sizes. Significant but smaller effects were noted in the thigh (r = 0.26), shoulder (r = 0.19), and foot (r = 0.17), classified as small-to-medium effects. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were found for the neck (P = 0.099), upper back (P = 0.453), lower back (P = 0.156), upper arm (P = 0.682), forearm (P = 0.727), or hip (P = 0.596). For these non-significant regions, the calculated effect sizes were either negligible (e.g., forearm r = 0.02, hip r = 0.04) or small (e.g., neck r = 0.11, lower back r = 0.10), indicating that while mean scores slightly increased, the magnitude of change was not practically significant compared to the lower extremities.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to assess the ergonomic postures and the prevalence of MSDs among saffron harvesters, with a particular focus on identifying high-risk working postures and their association with musculoskeletal discomfort in different body regions. The findings revealed notably high ergonomic risk scores for both the upper and lower limbs, with average AWBA, AULA, and ALLA values falling between the high-risk and very high-risk levels. Furthermore, there was a significant increase in musculoskeletal disorder scores in all body regions following the harvesting period compared to before, with the most pronounced discomfort observed in the knees, shoulder, and wrist. Statistically significant associations were identified between specific working postures and elevated disorder scores in the neck, shoulder, lower back, wrist, thigh, knees, lower leg, and foot, underscoring the impact of commonly adopted postures on the musculoskeletal health of saffron harvesters. These findings underscore the urgent need for ergonomic interventions and workplace modifications to protect the health and well-being of this vulnerable agricultural workforce.

The prevalence of MSDs among saffron harvesters in this study demonstrates notably higher rates compared to similar agricultural populations reported in literature. While a previous Iranian study of saffron harvesters in Gonabad reported shoulder, wrist, and ankle MSD prevalence rates of 91.7%, 90.5%, and 92.9% respectively32, the current study’s findings align with these elevated rates, particularly in the post-harvesting assessment where knee disorders reached a mean score of 24.41. Comparative analysis with other agricultural workers reveals concerning disparities - coffee harvesters in Thailand showed 81.6% prevalence of 12-month MSDs33, while oil palm harvesters demonstrated significant associations between MSDs prevalence and productivity loss34.

The significant increase in MSDs prevalence following the harvesting period observed in this study reflects the acute biomechanical stress imposed by intensive agricultural work, a phenomenon well-documented in literature. The dramatic elevation in knee disorder scores from baseline to post-harvest (reaching 24.41) parallels findings from paddy field workers in Iran, where knee injury risk was significantly elevated among those performing manual cultivation tasks35. This temporal relationship between work exposure and MSDs development could be explained by several plausible physiological mechanisms suggested in the literature: for example, prolonged static postures during harvesting may lead to tissue hypoxia and metabolic stress in supporting muscles, potentially triggering triggering inflammatory cascades that could manifest as pain and dysfunction36. According to the cumulative loading hypothesis, repetitive low-level stresses, characteristic of agricultural work, could gradually exceed tissue tolerance thresholds, possibly resulting in microstructural damage that becomes symptomatic during or immediately after peak work periods37. Studies of agricultural workers in Low- and Middle-Income Countries have consistently demonstrated this pattern, with 12-month MSDs prevalence rates reaching 61.96% in Africa and 54.16% in Asia12, which is consistent with the view that intensive seasonal agricultural work creates predictable patterns of musculoskeletal stress. The physiological adaptation-maladaptation cycle offers one explanatory framework for why previously asymptomatic workers might develop acute symptoms following intensive work periods, as it posits that tissue repair mechanisms can become overwhelmed by the rate and magnitude of mechanical stress38. The seasonal intensity of saffron harvesting, which requires workers to maintain awkward postures for extended periods during the brief harvest window, contrasts with other agricultural activities that may allow for more varied movement patterns throughout the year12.

The strong associations between specific working postures and MSD locations identified in this study provide crucial insights into the biomechanical pathways underlying occupational MSDs. The finding that posture B90-S90-E0 (bent trunk 90°, shoulder 90°, extended elbow 0°) showed the highest shoulder, lower back, and wrist disorder scores is consistent with established biomechanical principles of postural loading39. This posture creates a biomechanical disadvantage through several mechanisms: forward trunk flexion increases lumbar spine compression forces by up to 300% compared to neutral standing, while simultaneously creating excessive tension in posterior spinal musculature40. The 90° shoulder abduction position places the glenohumeral joint in a mechanically disadvantaged position, requiring sustained isometric contractions of the rotator cuff muscles to maintain arm position against gravity41. For lower body postures, the predominance of KF150 (knee flexion 150°) and its association with thigh and lower leg disorders reflects the extreme biomechanical demands of deep squatting positions. This posture creates maximal compression forces at the patellofemoral joint while simultaneously stretching posterior thigh muscles beyond their optimal length-tension relationships42. The KNL_2 kneeling posture’s association with knee disorders demonstrates how sustained kneeling creates direct pressure loading on periarticular tissues, leading to bursitis and soft tissue irritation43. These biomechanical mechanisms are supported by research showing that awkward postures create inflammatory responses at the cellular level, with sustained mechanical stress triggering cytokine release and initiating pain pathways36.

While this study provides valuable insights into the ergonomic challenges facing saffron harvesters, several limitations must be acknowledged that affect the generalizability of these findings to broader agricultural populations. The study’s focus on a specific geographic region and single crop type limits external validity, as environmental conditions, harvesting techniques, and worker demographics may vary significantly across different saffron-producing regions globally44. The prospective repeated‑measures design, while offering a detailed longitudinal snapshot of MSD prevalence, cannot establish definitive causal relationships between specific postures and disorder development. Importantly, the absence of a control group (e.g., non‑harvest agricultural workers) further restricts causal inference; without a comparator, it is not possible to disentangle the effects of saffron‑harvesting activities from other occupational or environmental factors. Moreover, the reliance on self‑reported pain scores through the CMDQ introduces potential reporting bias and cultural variations in pain perception45, which may influence comparability across populations. Finally, multiple pairwise comparisons were conducted across 12 body regions without applying strict corrections for multiple testing (e.g., Bonferroni). This approach was adopted to prioritize the detection of general trends in MSDs prevalence rather than strict hypothesis testing for each region individually, though it implies a theoretical risk of Type I error. These limitations should be explicitly recognized when interpreting the study’s conclusions. Nonetheless, a major strength of this study lies in the application of the AWBA method, a tool specifically developed for evaluating ergonomic risks in agricultural settings. Unlike general ergonomic assessment methods adapted from industrial contexts, AWBA is tailored to the unique biomechanical demands and postural dynamics of field-based agricultural tasks. Its use in this study enhances the accuracy and contextual relevance of risk assessments, thereby yielding more precise and actionable insights for improving working conditions in agriculture46. Despite its localized focus, the study’s findings contribute significantly to the growing body of evidence demonstrating that agricultural work, particularly in developing nations, creates substantial musculoskeletal health burdens12. The convergence of findings with previous literature strengthens confidence in the observed patterns, suggesting that the biomechanical challenges identified in saffron harvesting reflect broader issues in manual agricultural work. Future research should prioritize longitudinal study designs, incorporate objective biomechanical measurements, and expand geographic representation to enhance the scientific understanding of agricultural ergonomics and inform evidence-based intervention strategies for protecting worker health47.

Given the high prevalence of MSDs among saffron harvesters and the biomechanical stressors identified in this study, the implementation of targeted ergonomic interventions is imperative. One promising solution involves the use of wearable chairs or exoskeletal support systems, which allow workers to maintain semi-seated positions during harvesting activities. These devices can reduce strain on the lower back and knees by supporting the body’s weight and minimizing prolonged squatting or kneeling. Additionally, the adoption of knee-supportive devices such as padded kneeling chairs or wearable knee pads (that function as temporary seats) can alleviate direct pressure on periarticular structures and enhance postural stability during work in low-height crop environments. Moreover, the recent development of mechanized saffron harvesting machines and intelligent mechanisms in Iran presents a transformative opportunity to reduce physical workload and minimize repetitive manual tasks that contribute to MSDs48,49. Integrating such technology into traditional harvesting practices could significantly improve occupational health outcomes while maintaining or enhancing productivity. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness, feasibility, and user acceptability of these interventions under field conditions to inform widespread implementation in saffron-producing regions.

Conclusion

The results of the present study indicated that saffron harvest workers engaged in unfavorable working postures throughout their one-month harvesting period and experienced a high prevalence of MSDs within this short timeframe. The elevated risk levels observed in the upper body, lower body, and overall body highlight the poor working postures affecting various body regions, including the trunk, arms, knees, ankles, and wrists during work. High trunk flexion, knee bending, and the application of excessive force to the knees, ankles, soles of the feet, and fingers have contributed to increased MSDs among saffron harvesters. The high disorder scores in the knees, feet, and lower back regions reflect significant strain on these body parts during work. Therefore, the implementation of control measures is strongly recommended, including the use of mechanized equipment for saffron harvesting, adoption of specialized trolleys for farmers, and wearable, portable seats designed for use by saffron harvest workers.

Practical contributions

The practical contributions of this study can be articulated across four major dimensions. First, the identification of high-risk ergonomic postures among saffron harvesters—particularly deep knee flexion, prolonged trunk bending, and elevated shoulder–wrist loading—provides a scientific foundation for designing targeted ergonomic training programs. Developing visual and hands-on training modules that teach proper posture modification, micro-break techniques, and safe task alternation strategies can substantially reduce biomechanical stress during peak harvesting periods.

Second, the findings of this study highlight an urgent need to redesign ergonomic solutions and incorporate assistive technologies into the saffron harvesting process. The use of knee-support devices and wearable chairs or lightweight exoskeletal support systems can substantially reduce the biomechanical strain caused by prolonged trunk flexion, deep knee bending, and sustained awkward postures. Furthermore, the recent development of mechanized saffron harvesting machines in Iran presents a promising opportunity to minimize repetitive manual activities and lower the physical workload placed on workers50. Integrating these assistive technologies into traditional harvesting practices can significantly reduce musculoskeletal disorders and improve both worker health and productivity.

Third, this study provides evidence-based guidance for policymakers and agricultural health authorities to strengthen occupational safety regulations in small-scale farming sectors. Establishing national ergonomic guidelines specific to high-intensity seasonal agricultural activities, subsidizing the adoption of ergonomic equipment for small farmers, and implementing periodic ergonomic risk assessments can promote safer and more sustainable harvesting practices. Creating a monitoring system for agricultural MSDs, supported by regional health networks, would further enhance early detection and intervention.

Collectively, these practical contributions demonstrate that addressing the ergonomic challenges of saffron harvesting requires a comprehensive strategy combining worker training, ergonomic tool redesign, technological innovation, and supportive policy development. Implementing these recommendations can meaningfully reduce MSDs prevalence, enhance worker well-being, and improve the long-term productivity and sustainability of saffron farming communities.
