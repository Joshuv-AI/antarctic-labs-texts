Health-related quality of life among pregnant women exposed to malaria in an endemic region of Colombia
Authors: Jaiberth Antonio Cardona-Arias, Luis Felipe Higuita-Gutierrez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundScientific literature provides very limited evidence on the impact of gestational malaria (GM) on the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of pregnant women. The objective of this study was to describe the HRQoL of pregnant women exposed to malaria in an endemic region of Colombia; to compare HRQoL between women with and without GM; and to understand the meanings attributed to HRQoL among women exposed to GM.MethodsA convergent triangulation mixed-methods design (QUAN–QUAL) was used. A cross-sectional study was conducted with 400 pregnant women, and a grounded theory approach was applied to a subsample of 30. In the QUAN component, HRQoL was measured using the WHOQOL-BREF. Psychometric properties were assessed through Cronbach’s alpha, internal consistency and discriminant power using Spearman’s rho, and construct validity through exploratory factor analysis. Multivariable linear regression models were used to identify factors associated with each HRQoL domain. In the QUAL component semi-structured interviews were conducted, involving open, axial, and selective coding, following the phases of description, conceptual ordering, and theorization.ResultsAmong the 400 women, 6% reported a history of malaria during the current pregnancy, and 44% had experienced malaria at some point in their lives. The psychological health domain scored highest (median: 78), while physical health, environmental, and social domains showed lower scores—particularly physical health (median: 43). Multivariate models revealed that GM, comorbidities, and rural residence were associated with poorer physical and environmental health. The qualitative component revealed that women’s concept of HRQoL is predominantly material and objective, centred on access to health care, basic needs, and social support. GM was not perceived as a significant determinant of well-being, reflecting the normalization of the disease in endemic settings. However, emerging dimensions such as experiences of healthcare and dignified treatment by health personnel were emphasized—areas not captured by the WHOQOL-BREF.ConclusionHRQoL among pregnant women exposed to malaria in an endemic region is significantly impaired in physical, environmental, and social domains, while psychological health appears preserved, possibly reflecting adaptive coping. GM negatively affected HRQoL, though its impact was socially normalized and underrecognized by participants. The study highlights the importance of using locally grounded approaches to better capture the lived experience of health, illness, and care in vulnerable populations.Keywords: Quality of life, Health, Psychometrics, Qualitative research

Background

In the 1948 Constitution of the World Health Organization (WHO), health was defined as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease” [1]. This definition has been widely criticized by philosophers and social scientists for being overly general and utopian (in requiring completeness across several dimensions of health), for conflating health with happiness, well-being or other concepts, for failing to address the real challenges faced by health systems, and for limiting plural approaches to health by not allowing for context-specific definitions. Critics have also pointed out that it extends the boundaries of medicine into domains outside the scope of health and is unrealistic for people with disabilities, older adults, and other groups [2–5].

Despite these criticisms, the WHO has not revised its definition of health [6]. In fact, it has expanded on it by developing the concept of health-related quality of life (HRQoL), which addresses many of these concerns and serves as an extension of the original definition. In the 1980 s, the WHO convened a group of experts in medicine and the social sciences and gathered both quantitative and qualitative evidence to define HRQoL as “an individual’s perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns,” as determined by their physical and psychological health, level of independence, social relationships, environmental factors, and personal beliefs [7, 8]. This concept has been regarded as paradigmatic because it addresses shortcomings in public policy guidance, incorporates social progress into health indicators, provides a meaningful moral standard for health technology management, transcends biological dimensions, and enriches epidemiological indicators, among other benefits [9].

In research, HRQoL has experienced exponential growth over the past three decades. A PubMed search using the term “health related quality life” yields 181,914 results (528 in 1995 and 17,174 in 2024). However, when the term “malaria” is added, only three studies are found: a psychometric evaluation of the EuroQol-5 in 300 patients with uncomplicated malaria in Nigeria [10], a profile assessment of HRQoL in 1,179 individuals in Brazil [11], and a systematic review of 37 studies on the economic burden and epidemiological impact of malaria—only four of which focused on the disease’s effect on HRQoL, three using the EQ-5D and one the WHOQOL-BREF [12]. This is concerning, given that malaria can lead to severe complications, such as confusion, seizures, respiratory distress, and other outcomes that significantly affect health status and HRQoL. Infants, children under five, and pregnant women are at the highest risk of severe infection [13].

Among these, gestational malaria (GM) is a major public health concern that affects a large number of women. A recent meta-analysis of 253 studies on GM found a global prevalence of 20%. It also showed that GM increases the risk of maternal anemia (OR 2.4; 95% CI 1.87–3.06), low birth weight (OR 2.0; 95% CI 1.60–2.48), preterm birth (OR 1.65; 95% CI 1.29–2.10), and stillbirth (OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.15–1.71) [14]. Despite this robust body of evidence, no studies to date have explored the HRQoL of pregnant women exposed to malaria.

This gap reveals several limitations in the global scientific literature on malaria: little is known about the impact of malaria infection on HRQoL (GM research has predominantly been from a biomedical perspective, with outcomes focused on the parasite, morbidity or mortality); the few existing studies have primarily used the EQ-5D, which was originally designed for economic evaluations; evidence based on the WHO’s construct of HRQoL (WHOQOL-BREF) is still incipient; and in Colombia, no studies have investigated HRQoL among pregnant women exposed to malaria.

The need to carry out this study in Colombia is based on the following figures for malaria overall: in the WHO Region of the Americas, Brazil, Venezuela and Colombia accounted 76.8% of all cases; from 2022 to 2023, cases in Colombia increased by 20.4%, and Colombia reported the highest number of Plasmodium falciparum cases in America; Colombia registered an increase of malaria deaths, and risk factors of severe disease continue to persist in remote zones, such as gold-mining activities and limited access to healthcare [13].

For these reasons, the present study was conducted with the following objectives: to describe the HRQoL of pregnant women exposed to malaria in an endemic region of Colombia; to compare the HRQoL of women with and without GM; and to explore the meanings that HRQoL holds for pregnant women exposed to GM.

Methods

Study design

A convergent triangulation mixed-methods design QUAN + QUAL (Quantitative + Qualitative) was used. Each component was implemented independently, and the findings were later integrated. This design was chosen to address the complexity of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and to overcome the limitations of applying either a QUAN or QUAL approach in isolation [15]. The quantitative component consisted of a cross-sectional study, which aimed to determine the magnitude of the event and its associated factors. The qualitative component followed Corbin and Strauss’ grounded theory approach, which is useful for constructing theory based on the meanings individuals assign to everyday situations—an essential perspective for exploring HRQoL [16].

Grounded Theory is a methodological and theoretical tradition that emphasizes the inductive emergence of concepts from empirical data to develop theory through a systematic yet flexible process. Since its inception, several schools of Grounded Theory have been established, differing in their historical development, philosophical underpinnings, the role of the researcher, and data analysis procedures. These include: (i) The emergent design proposed by Glaser, which posits that theory should arise directly from the data rather than be constrained by preconceived categories; (ii) The systematic design developed by Strauss and Corbin, characterized by open and structured coding and currently the most widely applied approach; (iii) The constructivist design advanced by Charmaz, which views theory as a contextual and interpretative construction of reality; and (iv) The situational and dimensional analysis proposed by Adele Clarke, which extends Grounded Theory through postmodern and situational perspectives [17, 18].

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted in northwestern Colombia, the country’s second-highest region in terms of reported malaria cases. It included municipalities from two departments: Antioquia and Córdoba. The inclusion criteria for pregnant women were: permanent residence in the municipality, enrollment in a prenatal care programme, age over 13 years (Colombian law recognizes individuals aged 14 and older as autonomous in decisions about sexual and reproductive health), adequate health status to complete the survey or participate in an interview, voluntary participation, and signed informed consent or assent. Eligibility criteria were applied by a nurse from the prenatal care programme, who also invited potential participants to join the study.

The cross-sectional study included 400 pregnant women selected through non-probabilistic sampling to evaluate the psychometric properties of the HRQoL construct. Of these, 244 had thick blood smear results recorded during prenatal care, enabling a comparison of HRQoL between women with and without gestational malaria (GM). The grounded theory component included 30 pregnant women (10 with GM and 20 without GM). In the methods used, interpretation of data, and discussion of results, the key elements of Grounded Theory were three-phase coding (open to identify concepts and break down interviews into meaningful units; axial to establish categorical relationships that show greater conceptual complexity; and selective to identify core concepts to be discussed); theoretical sampling (data collection guided by emerging categories from the analysis of the interviews); theoretical saturation (new interviews do not generate new categories or properties); and an emphasis on the interaction between researcher and data to systematically and iteratively build the theory of HRQoL of exposed pregnant women.

Data collection and rigour criteria

The instruments of both components, QUAN QUAL, were applied by two young women (30 and 34 years old) from the community, nursing assistants known in Colombia as microscopists (health promoters with training in malaria diagnosis). They had received multiple training sessions on general aspects of fieldwork involving surveys and qualitative instruments. In addition, the researchers participated in data collection in cases where the pregnant woman granted consent.

In the quantitative component, a socioeconomic characterization survey and the WHOQOL-BREF (World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief version) instrument were administered. The WHOQOL-BREF assesses physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environmental factors through 24 items. This instrument has been validated in Colombian populations and yields a score between 0 (worst HRQoL) and 100 (best HRQoL) for each domain [19, 20].

Although previous studies used EQ5-D, in this research the WHOQOLBREF was chosen because it is multidimensional, not restricted to the physical dimension, easy to apply, aligns with the holistic of the WHO concept of health, and allows for comparing diseases and healthy individuals [19, 20]. Furthermore, regarding the EQ-5D it is important to highlight that its HRQOL concept is later than the WHOQOL (developed in the 1990 s), with a measurement closer to health states than to HRQOL, and its descriptive component (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) is used beyond its original purpose of being included in cost-utility evaluations of health technologies [21].

The qualitative component consisted of semi-structured interviews designed to explore the meanings of HRQoL. These were guided by the following predefined categories: (i) Participants’ understanding of quality of life and health (In Spanish: Dígame para usted que es tener buena salud o buena calidad de vida, qué necesita ustes para tener salud y una buena calidad de vida), and (ii) What is needed to achieve good quality of life and health during pregnancy (In Spanish: hábleme de lo que usted cree que debe tener una embarazada para disfrutar de una buena calidad de vida). Based on participants’ responses, additional questions were asked in a spontaneous, conversational format, allowing for the identification of emerging categories and theoretical saturation.

To minimize information bias in the quantitative component, validated instruments were used, thick blood smear testing followed internal and external quality control procedures, and all tools were administered by trained personnel. In the qualitative component, rigour was ensured through the saturation of predefined and emerging categories; triangulation of theories, researchers, and participants; reflexivity (including contrasting theoretical interpretations by researchers); and credibility and auditability-confirmability through prolonged participant engagement, triangulation of interpretations, and member validation of the findings.

Data analysis

In the quantitative component, descriptive statistics were used to summarize the variables. The psychometric evaluation of each WHOQOL-BREF domain was performed using the following criteria: (i) Reliability, assessed by Cronbach’s alpha, with values above 0.60 considered satisfactory; (ii) Internal consistency, assessed by Spearman’s rho correlations between each item and the score of its corresponding domain, with values above 0.30 considered acceptable; (iii) Discriminant power, assessed by comparing Spearman’s rho correlations of each item with its own domain and with the other domains—acceptable when the item’s correlation with its own domain was higher than with the others; and (iv) Construct validity, evaluated through factor loadings and the proportion of variance explained in an exploratory factor analysis.

Factors associated with each HRQoL domain, including GM as an independent variable, were identified using multivariable linear regression models. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0.

In the qualitative component, analysis of HRQoL meanings was carried out through transcription, coding, and categorization of interviews using both predefined and emerging codes. Coding process was performed manually using Microsoft Office tools, both authors coded all transcripts; discrepancies in assigning a code were resolved by consensus. The content of each category was analysed based on its properties (attributes that define the category’s content) and dimensions (the range of variation of a property). Semantic networks were established among categories to develop a theoretical model of HRQoL that complemented the quantitative findings.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Colombia’s Resolution 8430. It was classified as minimal risk and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Universidad Cooperativa de Colombia (Code BIO 452 as recorded in Minutes No. 8/2023 of September 7, 2023). Written informed consent (for adults) or assent (for participants under 18) was obtained from all participants.

In GM cases, 73% (n = 16) were Plasmodium vivax and 27% (n = 6) P. falciparum. Half lived in rural areas, 77% were adolescent or young mothers, 80% had completed secondary education, 27% were primigravidae, 93% were homemakers, and 87% reported at least one health condition. Additionally, 54% lived in nuclear households, 71% were classified as living in poverty according to the Unsatisfied Basic Needs (UBN) index, and 97% were enrolled in the subsidized healthcare system (Table 1).

Concept of quality of life

This analytical category included three subcategories. The first referred to the various definitions and synonymous terms used by pregnant women when talking about quality of life. Five key aspects emerged from their narratives: (i) Material well-being, described as having a home in good condition that offers protection from the elements, basic furniture for resting, and access to workplaces, health centres, or relatives’ homes; (ii) Monetary income, which was deemed essential to meet household needs; (iii) Survival, defined as having the necessary resources to ensure the survival of household members; (iv) Health, most often understood as the absence of illness, as a prerequisite for living a quality life; and (v) Progress, referring to the possibility of improving one’s social status and providing better opportunities for one’s children.

Overall, quality of life was mostly understood in material terms, with no subjective or perceptual dimensions emerging. No relevant differences were observed by place of residence, age, or socioeconomic status.

“Quality of life means being able to say that you have what you need to survive, that you’re not sick or hungry, that you can move around, enjoy your home, and that you have a job and the resources to meet all your needs.” (25-year-old pregnant woman, rural area)

Further exploration of the health concept led to the development of the second subcategory, highlighting the importance of health for achieving a good quality of life. The concept included both individual and environmental aspects. At the individual level, women referred to physical health, mental or cognitive capacity to perform tasks independently, and emotional well-being. At the environmental level, they emphasized the need for proper sanitation, access to water and sewage systems, clean surroundings (free of garbage or animal waste), and reliable infrastructure to move freely and perform daily activities. Health was largely reduced to the absence of disease and to environmental sanitation.

The third subcategory delved into the relationship between health and quality of life to assess whether HRQoL was a meaningful concept for the women. Initially, most interviewees perceived quality of life as broader than health or regarded health as just one of its components. This was due in part to a reductionist view of health (limited to the absence of disease and access to sanitation). However, when asked about the social determinants of health—e.g., “What do you think is needed to be healthy?”—their responses revealed a circular and reciprocal relationship between health and quality of life. They identified the same elements as necessary for both: income, housing, employment, infrastructure, access to care. They explained that when talking to health professionals, conversations tend to focus exclusively on disease and services provided by the health sector, while in their daily lives, it is impossible to conceive of good health without material well-being, just as it is impossible to imagine quality of life without good physical, mental, or emotional health and social or environmental support.

“Quality of life is having health, money, and love.” (32-year-old, urban area)

“Health means having a good house, a good hospital, good transportation, and a family that supports us.” (20-year-old, rural area)

“Good health means enjoying both the quantity and quality of life.” (35-year-old, urban area)

Dimensions of health-related quality of life (HRQoL)

In line with the above, participants defined HRQoL through several key components: their surrounding environment, social networks or support systems, physical health, emotional well-being, and the ability to live with autonomy and independence. Based on these narratives, four dimensions emerged: access to healthcare, healthcare quality, family support, and community support.

Regarding access to healthcare, pregnant women across rural and urban settings, age groups, and socioeconomic backgrounds agreed that access to quality health infrastructure was essential. They emphasized the need for well-equipped hospitals and rural health posts for emergencies and monitoring—this included both physical infrastructure and appropriate medical technology. Access to medical consultations, diagnostic testing, and medications was also considered fundamental. Finally, they expressed a desire for access to care from a variety of health professionals without needing to travel to the departmental capital or incur high expenses.

The healthcare quality subcategory was related to the availability of sufficient healthcare personnel for two main purposes: meeting local demand for services and providing more comprehensive care to prevent and treat physical, psychological, and social health issues. The need for more humane care, better patient-provider communication, and genuine concern for patients’ well-being was also emphasized.

“Since there are no gynecologists in our hospital, we have to go to Montería [departmental capital], and in those hospitals it’s confusing—appointments, service areas, billing—and on top of that, there are too many patients from different places for one specialist, so they rush the consultation, don’t listen to you, don’t ask about your symptoms.” (18-year-old, rural area)

“There’s nothing here to treat high-risk pregnancies. If something goes wrong, we not only suffer physically but also with the delays in getting authorizations.” (31-year-old, urban area)

The final two subcategories referred to family and community support. In the family persists machismo and the perception that motherhood is a woman’s sole responsibility- These patriarchal social norms detrimental role of women, they are deeply embedded beliefs and practices with unequal power dynamics, which determine risk behaviour in health, limit autonomy and decision-making over their own bodies and healthcare, emphasize a woman’s role in domestic responsibilities and childbearing, and lead to women's symptoms being dismissed or viewed as “emotional” or “hysterical,” delayed diagnoses.

Besides participants emphasized the critical role of support networks, especially grandmothers. Grandmothers were seen as protectors of maternal health and provided guidance during pregnancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding. Neighbours, community mothers, and local leaders were also recognized as important contributors to HRQoL.

Gestational health-related quality of life

When examining HRQoL specifically during pregnancy—or in the context of GM—no distinct meanings emerged compared to those already described. In other words, the concept of HRQoL remained consistent across the women’s life stages: before, during, and after pregnancy. Similarly, there were no significant differences in how women with or without GM conceptualized HRQoL. This may be explained by the low perceived risk of GM—exposure to malaria was normalized—and the fact that most GM cases were submicroscopic (i.e., undetectable by thick blood smear but positive by molecular tests) and asymptomatic, detected only through routine prenatal screening.

Despite this, two subcategories emerged: maternity and pregnancy experiences, and socioeconomic conditions. The first subcategory was characterized by the centrality of the unborn child in the women’s lives. Pregnancy was seen as redefining their identities and bringing existential meaning. Concepts of self—such as self-esteem, self-concept, self-image, and self-care—were perceived not in relation to themselves as women but in relation to the child.

“Malaria is not serious, here it is not serious, if I have a high fever that does not improve, I take the sample [referring to the thick blood sample] and that's it; if it comes out with malaria, ask the pharmacy for the drugs.” (20-year-old, rural area)

“Malaria during pregnancy is only severe if you let it progress; but that normally doesn't happen, because if the fever doesn't go down, you go to the microscopist.” (28-year-old, rural area)

“Pregnancy generates a bit of fear because of the fear of thinking about whether we'll be able to provide everything our baby needs—food, shelter, clothing, and health—but the most important thing is that the news of the pregnancy improved my life because it's something only women can do: give life, take care of another life. The news of the pregnancy made me want to take better care of myself.” (25-year-old, urban area)

The second subcategory, socioeconomic conditions, reflected the lived experiences of many women who were either abandoned by their partners or supported only in material terms. This created a context of economic and socio-emotional dependence—especially among younger women who did not live with their families or who lived in remote areas.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal a complex profile of HRQoL among pregnant women living in a malaria-endemic region. While the psychological health domain scored high (78 points), the environmental, social relationships, and physical health dimensions were considerably lower, particularly physical health, which had a poor score of 43 points. Compared to reference values in the general Colombian population with scores of 64.3–65.6 for physical health, 70.8–72.0 for psychological health, 64.3–65.9 for social relationships, and 60.5–61.6 for environment [20], the pregnant women in this study demonstrated substantially lower HRQoL in three of the four domains. The only domain exceeding the national average was psychological health, which may reflect emotional resilience or adaptation in the face of adversity. Nevertheless, the low scores in physical, environmental, and social domains reflect the structural and health-related vulnerabilities these women face, highlighting the need for comprehensive approaches that go beyond purely biomedical frameworks.

The statistical analysis revealed significant associations between nearly all WHOQOL-BREF domains, confirming the multidimensional and interdependent nature of HRQoL and supporting a biopsychosocial model of health, in which physical, emotional, social, and environmental aspects are closely interconnected [22]. However, one notable exception emerged: there was no significant association between physical health and social relationships. This finding contrasts with prior literature [23] and may be explained by the fact that, in this specific population, physical health is more heavily influenced by GM, comorbidities, and rural residence, while social relationships are shaped by family structures, gender roles, and pregnancy history. Although both domains scored low, their lack of correlation suggests that physical discomfort and social deprivation are experienced as distinct aspects of life by these women.

Physical health and environmental quality were negatively affected by GM, the presence of comorbidities, and rural residence. These results align with previous research on malaria, which has reported low physical health scores due to recurrent episodes of fever, headaches, and generalized body pain characteristic of the disease [24]. The impact on the environmental domain reinforces the notion that malaria extends beyond biological health and interacts with structural inequities, creating an accumulated burden of vulnerability—particularly in regions historically excluded from social and health development.

A central finding of the qualitative component was that pregnant women conceptualized quality of life primarily in material and objective terms. This focus on material conditions such as income, housing infrastructure, and mobility, must be interpreted within a context of structural deprivation, where poverty and extreme poverty are widespread. In this regard, Diener and Seligman [25] have argued that when material conditions are insufficient or precarious, individuals tend to evaluate their well-being based on tangible indicators, placing less emphasis on emotional or relational aspects. This hypothesis is consistent with prior studies of pregnant women in socioeconomically deprived rural areas, which have shown that perceptions of well-being are directly linked to physical surroundings, costs related to prenatal care, and barriers to healthcare access, reinforcing the centrality of material concerns in their lived experience [26].

The key findings from the qualitative component point to the significance of material factors in terms of how pregnant women perceive and experience quality of life. This is a significant finding which is consistent with current evidence from 39 mixed studies showing that malaria is an important cause of morbidity and mortality in many low- and middle-income countries, whose control depends on factors external to the health sector, that is, social and economic determinants of health that remain unaddressed and should be included in funding, implementation, and evaluation from different programmes [27].

In terms of HRQoL specifically, participants expressed a multidimensional understanding that included the importance of healthcare services and quality of care, as well as family and community support. The significance of healthcare access and quality has also been reported in previous research. For instance, a qualitative study among pregnant women in rural France found numerous perinatal care obstacles, including difficulty accessing health centres, high turnover among health professionals, short consultation times, and fear of pregnancy complications not being adequately addressed [26]. In such settings, the social and familial circle becomes particularly important due to the geographic isolation of rural areas. Emotional, economic, and logistical support from family members is essential for attending medical appointments, delivery, and postpartum care [28].

Contrary to the quantitative results, where GM was significantly associated with reduced HRQoL, women interviewed in the qualitative component did not perceive GM as a determinant of their well-being. This discrepancy may be partly explained by the social normalization of malaria in endemic regions. Similar findings have been reported in studies from sub-Saharan Africa, where malaria is seen as a routine, recurrent part of life and therefore becomes perceived as manageable or inevitable [29]. Such normalization can obscure recognition of the disease as a threat to quality of life, even when its objective effects—such as the low physical health scores observed in this study—are evident.

In this context, the presence of physical symptoms or discomfort is not necessarily interpreted as being caused by malaria if the disease has been culturally assimilated into everyday life. This does not imply a lack of impact, but rather a cultural reinterpretation of harm. This phenomenon presents a challenge for healthcare models, as low or absent perceived harm may hinder treatment-seeking behaviour. Recognizing this issue is essential for adapting health education strategies and clinical follow-up to local frameworks of meaning.

An important contribution of the qualitative component was the emergence of a conception of HRQoL in which access to healthcare services and the quality of care play a central role. Interviewed women emphasized the importance of timely access to prenatal check-ups, diagnostic tests, and treatment, as well as the need to be treated with dignity, respect, and empathy by healthcare personnel; it was not captured in QUAN component, and shows the importance of qualitative mixed studies for capturing local conceptualizations and lived realities of wellbeing. It is also important to highlight the circular and reciprocal relationship between health and quality of life found in the QUAL component. This is significant and could be related to the application of the WHOQOL-BREF, an instrument that has been developed based on the WHO definition of health. Through the collation of both quantitative and qualitative evidence, this study highlights the importance of independence, social relationships, environmental factors, and personal beliefs [6–9].

It is important to note that the relevance of qualitative designs, as discussed in the previous paragraph, can also be extended to other quantitative outcomes, such as Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs). This underscores the need to promote mixed-methods studies addressing these topics. This clarification is relevant because PROMs and PREMs are often used as complements to HRQoL, because are methodologies that can be used to improve the reporting of clinical quality indicators in pregnant women, by including little-explored areas of health care for this group [30]. Futhermore, some systematic reviews have reported the availability of several PROMs tools for assessment of the quality of healthcare for pregnant women, and others have gathered empirical evidence demonstrating their acceptance by stakeholders to improve maternal care, despite the challenges of data collection and their integration into health systems [31, 32].

Limitations

The limitations of this study include the small sample size of pregnant women with malaria, missing data from thick blood smear results, and parasitological data only apply to contexts with a predominance of Plasmodium vivax. As such, findings should not be generalized to other endemic areas of the country or to regions where Plasmodium falciparum is the dominant species, as differences in clinical presentation may lead to different impacts on HRQoL. Malaria occurs mainly in contexts of poverty and rurality where other social determinants of health are more important for those affected than GM; therefore, it is assumed that symptomatic or asymptomatic cases would not impact on the exposed results. This evidence shows that malaria control actions transcend the capacities of the health sector and require great commitment of the economic, educational, social and community.

Conclusion

HRQoL among pregnant women in a malaria-endemic area reveals significant impairments in physical, environmental, and social domains, contrasted with high scores in psychological health; this suggest emotional resilience in the face of structural vulnerability. Deterioration in physical and environmental health was associated with GM, comorbidities, and rural residence, confirming the combined impact of clinical and social factors and underscoring the need for holistic interventions beyond the biomedical model.

The qualitative component shows how GM is normalized and often rendered invisible, while highlighting emerging dimensions related to access to services and quality of healthcare during pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum. These findings underscore the importance of designing public health strategies that are aligned with local understandings of health, illness, and well-being.
