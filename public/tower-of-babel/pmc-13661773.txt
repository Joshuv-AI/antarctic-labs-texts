Comparative Efficacy and Safety of Systemic Immunomodulatory Therapies for Non‐Infectious Uveitis: A Systematic Review With Pairwise and Network Meta‐Analyses
Authors: Kai‐Yang Chen, Hoi‐Chun Chan, Chi‐Ming Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionNon‐infectious uveitis (NIU) is a chronic immune‐mediated ocular inflammatory disease that may cause irreversible visual impairment. The comparative efficacy and safety of systemic immunomodulatory therapies, including conventional agents and biologics, remain incompletely defined. This systematic review and network meta‐analysis evaluated systemic immunosuppressive therapies for NIU.MethodsPubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection were searched from inception to May 2, 2026. Randomized controlled trials and prospective comparative cohort studies evaluating systemic immunomodulatory or biologic therapies for NIU were included. The primary efficacy outcome was treatment failure. Safety outcomes included mortality, urinary tract infection, upper respiratory tract infection, injection‐site reactions, ocular hypertension, macular edema, and headache. Random‐effects pairwise meta‐analysis and frequentist network meta‐analysis were performed using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSeven studies including 992 participants were included; six were randomized controlled trials and one was a prospective cohort. In the pairwise meta‐analysis, adalimumab significantly reduced treatment failure compared with placebo (RR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.58–0.78; p < 0.0001; I² = 28.1%). The mortality estimate was inconclusive (RR, 3.80; 95% CI, 0.42–34.15), with three deaths in the adalimumab groups and none in the placebo groups. Adalimumab was associated with a higher risk of injection‐site reactions than placebo (RR, 2.67; 95% CI, 1.73–4.12). Network meta‐analysis showed no significant differences for most safety outcomes, including upper respiratory tract infection, ocular hypertension, macular edema, and headache. Infliximab showed an imprecise urinary tract infection signal versus placebo (RR, 40.98; 95% CI, 1.82–922.17), based on sparse events and one nonrandomized comparison.ConclusionAdalimumab provides the most consistent evidence for reducing treatment failure in NIU. Comparative safety estimates, particularly for infliximab, remain uncertain and hypothesis‐generating. Larger, long‐term, head‐to‐head trials are required.

Introduction

Non‐infectious uveitis (NIU) is a chronic, immune‐mediated ocular inflammation and a major cause of vision loss worldwide [1]. In the United States, the prevalence of adult NIU has been estimated at 121 per 100,000 persons, with considerable geographic variability reported globally [2, 3]. This condition disproportionately affects working‐age adults, with women showing a higher frequency than men [3]. Clinically, NIU presents with ocular pain, photophobia, floaters, and decreased vision. Anatomically, it is classified as anterior, intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis, as defined by the Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) Working Group [4].

Beyond visual symptoms, NIU has a substantial personal and societal burden. Intermittent flares, treatment toxicities, and complications such as macular edema and glaucoma contribute to reduced quality of life and productivity [5, 6, 7]. Although several studies have explored the epidemiology and burden of NIU, there remains a lack of consensus regarding the optimal therapeutic sequencing and long‐term safety of immunosuppressive regimens [1]. Most available data come from single‐center or disease‐specific cohorts, which limits generalizability. Additionally, there has been a relative lack of synthesis of the comparative efficacy and safety of conventional immunomodulators and biologic agents over the past decade [1, 7].

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of acute inflammation control; however, chronic exposure leads to serious ocular and systemic adverse effects. The integration of immunomodulatory therapies such as methotrexate, mycophenolate, and cyclosporine has improved outcomes, yet relapse rates and toxicity remain significant [8]. Biologic therapies targeting tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), particularly adalimumab, have demonstrated substantial efficacy, but evidence comparing biologics with traditional agents in a unified framework is limited [9].

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and network meta‐analysis to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of immunosuppressant therapies for NIU. Our goal was to provide an up‐to‐date, evidence‐based synthesis to support clinicians in developing individualized management strategies for patients with NIU.

Methods

We conducted and reported this systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses 2020 statement and the PRISMA extension for network meta‐analysis. Methodological decisions regarding eligibility criteria, outcome harmonization, risk‐of‐bias assessment, pairwise synthesis, network meta‐analysis, heterogeneity assessment, and sensitivity analyses were guided by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The review was registered in PROSPERO under registration number CRD420261395217. Because the review included both pairwise and network meta‐analytic components, we prespecified which outcomes were suitable for direct pairwise synthesis, which outcomes formed connected treatment networks, and which comparisons were clinically relevant but disconnected from the main network [10, 11, 12].

Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library/CENTRAL, Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection, from database inception to May 2, 2026. ScienceDirect was not used as a primary bibliographic database, although full texts available through publisher platforms were accessed when required. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free‐text terms for NIU and systemic immunomodulatory therapies. Search terms included, but were not limited to, “noninfectious uveitis,” “non‐infectious uveitis,” “autoimmune uveitis,” “idiopathic uveitis,” “intermediate uveitis,” “posterior uveitis,” “panuveitis,” “juvenile idiopathic arthritis‐associated uveitis,” “systemic immunomodulatory therapy,” “immunosuppressive therapy,” “corticosteroid‐sparing,” “methotrexate,” “mycophenolate mofetil,” “azathioprine,” “cyclosporine,” “tacrolimus,” “adalimumab,” “infliximab,” “sarilumab,” “tocilizumab,” “secukinumab,” “anti‐TNF,” “anti‐interleukin‐6,” and “anti‐interleukin‐17.” No language restriction was applied. Conference abstracts were screened to identify corresponding full‐text publications, but abstracts without complete peer‐reviewed outcome data were not included in the quantitative synthesis. The complete database‐specific search strategies, search yields, duplicate‐removal count, and final search date are provided in Table S1.

Eligibility Criteria and Selection Process

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts using prespecified eligibility criteria. Potentially eligible records were retrieved for full‐text assessment, and two reviewers independently assessed the full texts for final inclusion. Two reviewers independently extracted study characteristics, intervention details, comparator details, outcome definitions, event counts, continuous outcomes, follow‐up duration, and safety data using a pre‐piloted standardized extraction form and performed risk‐of‐bias assessment. Any disagreement during screening, full‐text assessment, data extraction, or risk‐of‐bias assessment was resolved by discussion and consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated. Screening was managed using Rayyan software [13].

The PICO framework was defined as follows. The population comprised patients with NIU, including adult and pediatric patients and anatomical subtypes of anterior, intermediate, posterior, and panuveitis. The interventions were systemic immunomodulatory or biologic therapies used for corticosteroid‐sparing control of NIU. The comparators were placebo, standard care, background corticosteroid taper, another systemic immunomodulatory therapy, or another biologic therapy, or conventional immunosuppression. The primary efficacy outcome was treatment failure or failure to achieve corticosteroid‐sparing inflammatory control, depending on the outcome definition used in each trial. Secondary outcomes included all‐cause mortality, serious adverse events, urinary tract infection (UTI), upper respiratory tract infection (URTI), injection‐site reactions, ocular hypertension, headache, corticosteroid‐sparing success, visual acuity, inflammation control, relapse or recurrence, and treatment discontinuation; macular edema was retained as a study‐reported ocular outcome. Outcomes with incompatible definitions were summarized separately rather than forced into the same pooled estimate.

Eligible studies were randomized controlled trials or prospective comparative cohort studies that enrolled patients with NIU and evaluated systemic immunomodulatory or biologic therapy. Eligible populations included adult or pediatric patients with anterior, intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis, provided that infectious uveitis was excluded or that NIU‐specific outcomes were separately extractable. Eligible interventions included systemic corticosteroid‐sparing conventional immunomodulators, such as methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, cyclosporine, and tacrolimus, and systemic biologic or targeted therapies, including adalimumab, infliximab, sarilumab, tocilizumab, secukinumab, or other systemic immune‐directed agents. Eligible comparators included placebo, standard care, corticosteroid‐taper regimens, another systemic immunomodulator, another biologic therapy, or conventional immunosuppression.

Studies were excluded if they enrolled only infectious uveitis, were animal or in vitro studies, were uncontrolled case reports or case series, were reviews or editorials, were conference abstracts without complete peer‐reviewed full‐text data, had insufficient extractable efficacy or safety data, or reported mixed populations without separable NIU outcomes. No language restriction was applied. Studies evaluating local, periocular, intravitreal, or suprachoroidal corticosteroid treatment were excluded from the primary systemic‐therapy network unless they were prespecified as part of a separate local‐therapy synthesis. Comparisons that were clinically eligible but disconnected from the main network were retained in the systematic review and summarized separately rather than artificially combined with the connected network.

Data Extraction, Outcomes, and Definitions

Using a pre‐piloted standardized extraction sheet, two reviewers independently extracted study design, country or region, recruitment period, sample size, treatment arms, intervention dose and schedule, comparator, follow‐up duration, age, sex, race or ethnicity, anatomical uveitis subtype, underlying systemic diagnosis, baseline disease activity, duration of uveitis, prior treatment exposure, concomitant corticosteroid use, concomitant methotrexate or other immunomodulatory therapy, and outcome definitions. For dichotomous outcomes, we extracted the number of participants with events and the total number of participants in each arm. For time‐to‐event outcomes, hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted when reported. For the treatment‐failure pairwise synthesis presented in Figure 2, cumulative treatment‐failure event counts from the intention‐to‐treat populations of VISUAL I [9], VISUAL II [14], and SYCAMORE [15] were used to calculate risk ratios (RRs). For continuous outcomes, we extracted means, standard deviations, and change‐from‐baseline values when available. When data were unclear or incomplete, we attempted to derive values from tables, figures, trial reports, or supporting information; if necessary, study authors were contacted for clarification. Medians and interquartile ranges were converted to means and standard deviations only when appropriate and according to established methods. Outcomes that could not be harmonized clinically or statistically were described narratively or summarized in separate evidence tables.

Treatment failure was defined according to each trial's prespecified composite end point and was extracted preferentially from intention‐to‐treat analyses. For VISUAL I, VISUAL II, and SYCAMORE, treatment failure included new active inflammatory lesions, worsening anterior chamber cell grade or vitreous haze grade, or clinically meaningful visual acuity loss, according to the original trial definitions. Because ADJUVITE used early laser flare photometry response rather than the same composite treatment‐failure end point, its efficacy outcome was not pooled with the treatment‐failure forest plot unless explicitly analyzed as a separate response outcome. Corticosteroid‐sparing success was defined as control of ocular inflammation while reducing systemic corticosteroids to the trial‐defined threshold. UTI and URTI were defined according to the original study adverse‐event reporting and included clinically diagnosed or investigator‐reported events. Ocular hypertension and macular edema were extracted using study‐reported definitions. Where definitions differed substantially across studies, the outcome was analyzed separately or described narratively.

Quality Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed separately by study design. Randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, which evaluates bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result [16]. Prospective non‐randomized cohort studies were assessed using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale, including selection, comparability, and outcome domains. Because non‐randomized evidence may be affected by confounding by indication, imbalance in concomitant therapy, and differential follow‐up, estimates derived from prospective cohort studies were interpreted as lower‐certainty evidence, particularly when they formed single‐study network links [17].

Statistical Analysis

Two complementary synthesis approaches were used. First, pairwise random‐effects meta‐analyses were performed when at least two clinically and methodologically compatible studies compared the same interventions for the same outcome. Dichotomous outcomes were summarized as RRs with 95% CIs. The treatment‐failure pairwise synthesis used the cumulative treatment‐failure event counts from the intention‐to‐treat populations of the contributing trials. Between‐study heterogeneity was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method and quantified using I2 and τ2. A two‐sided p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant for pooled intervention effects, whereas a Cochran Q test p value below 0.10 was considered suggestive of statistical heterogeneity.

Second, frequentist random‐effects network meta‐analysis was performed for outcomes in which the treatment network was connected. Network estimates were expressed as RRs with 95% CIs. Treatment nodes were defined before analysis according to systemic treatment class and individual therapy. Disconnected comparisons were not forced into the network; instead, they were summarized separately. For sparse safety outcomes, risk difference analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses. Zero‐event arms were handled using a prespecified continuity correction, and double‐zero studies were excluded from RR models but considered in risk‐difference sensitivity analyses when appropriate.

Network geometry was summarized for each outcome by reporting the contributing studies, treatment nodes, direct comparisons, number of edges, and presence or absence of closed loops. Global and local inconsistency assessment was planned using design‐by‐treatment interaction and node‐splitting methods when closed loops were available. For outcomes without closed loops, inconsistency could not be statistically assessed and was described narratively. Treatment rankings were estimated using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values, with higher SUCRA values indicating a more favorable ranking for the prespecified direction of outcome. For adverse events, higher SUCRA values were interpreted as a lower probability of harm only when the ranking was explicitly coded in that direction. SUCRA rankings were interpreted cautiously when treatment nodes were informed by single studies, sparse events, or non‐randomized evidence [18].

The certainty of evidence was assessed for all primary and secondary outcomes. Pairwise comparisons were evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and reporting bias. Network estimates and treatment rankings were evaluated using a network‐specific certainty framework, considering within‐study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Evidence derived from single studies, sparse events, very wide CIs, non‐randomized comparisons, or disconnected networks was downgraded accordingly. Certainty ratings were summarized as high, moderate, low, or very low and were used to guide the strength of interpretation.

Sensitivity analyses included leave‐one‐out analysis for treatment failure (Figure S2), exclusion of the nonrandomized prospective cohort study for UTI, URTI, injection‐site reactions, ocular hypertension, macular edema, and headache (Figures S8–S13), and risk‐difference analyses for the same sparse outcomes (Figures S14–S19). Meta‐regression was planned for clinically important effect modifiers, including follow‐up duration, age group, anatomical uveitis subtype, study design, and concomitant methotrexate use, but was interpreted as exploratory when fewer than 10 studies were available per covariate. Reporting bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger's regression only when at least 10 studies contributed to an outcome. Doi plots and LFK indices were considered exploratory when fewer than 10 studies were available. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.1, with the statistical package specified in the Supporting Methods [19] (Figures S14–S19).

Results

Literature Search

The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram summarizes the systematic study selection process from database and register searches. During identification through database searching, 1501 records were identified: 279 from PubMed/MEDLINE, 507 from Scopus, 128 from the Cochrane Library/CENTRAL, and 587 from Web of Science Core Collection. Before screening, 551 duplicate records were removed, leaving 950 records for title and abstract screening. During screening, 918 records were excluded, leaving 32 reports sought for full‐text retrieval. All 32 records were successfully retrieved. These 32 full‐text records were then assessed for eligibility. After full‐text assessment, 25 articles were excluded for specific reasons, including 10 review articles, six conference abstracts, and nine animal studies. Finally, seven articles fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and quantitative synthesis [9, 14, 15, 20, 21, 22, 23]. The PRISMA flow diagram visualizes the process of selection in addition to reasons for study exclusions in Figure 1.

Study Characteristics

Seven studies including 992 participants met the eligibility criteria for the systematic review. Six studies were randomized controlled trials, and one study was a prospective comparative cohort. The included evidence was clinically heterogeneous. Two trials evaluated adalimumab versus placebo in adult non‐infectious intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis; one enrolled patients with active NIU, and one enrolled patients with inactive corticosteroid‐dependent NIU undergoing corticosteroid withdrawal [9, 14]. Two trials evaluated adalimumab versus placebo in pediatric or early‐onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA)‐associated or idiopathic anterior uveitis [15, 23]. One phase 2 trial evaluated sarilumab versus placebo in posterior‐segment NIU [21]. One prospective cohort compared adalimumab with infliximab in JIA‐associated anterior uveitis [22]. One randomized trial compared methotrexate with mycophenolate mofetil as first‐line corticosteroid‐sparing therapy in non‐infectious intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis [20]. Because the methotrexate‐mycophenolate comparison was disconnected from the adalimumab‐placebo‐sarilumab‐infliximab network, it was summarized separately. Follow‐up ranged from 2 to 24 months, and substantial variation was present in age, anatomical subtype, disease activity, background methotrexate exposure, and outcome definition. Quality Assessment

Most randomized controlled trials were judged to have a low risk of bias across the majority of Risk of Bias 2 domains. Some concerns were identified in selected domains, particularly the randomization process (D1) and missing outcome data (D3), as shown in Figure S1A,B. The prospective cohort study [22] was judged as methodologically acceptable using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale; however, because it was nonrandomized, its estimates remain vulnerable to confounding by indication, differential treatment selection, and concomitant methotrexate exposure. Therefore, network estimates depending on this study, particularly those involving infliximab, were interpreted as lower‐certainty evidence. The Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale judged the prospective cohort study [20] as high quality with full scores in selection, comparability, and outcome domains (Table S2).

Quantitative Synthesis

Pairwise Meta‐Analysis Approach

Treatment Failure

Three randomized controlled trials [9, 14, 15], including 533 participants, contributed to the pairwise meta‐analysis of treatment failure for adalimumab versus placebo. Adalimumab significantly reduced the risk of treatment failure compared with placebo (RR = 0.67; 95% CI, 0.58–0.78; p < 0.0001), with low‐to‐moderate statistical heterogeneity (I 2 = 28.1%; τ 2 < 0.0001; Cochran Q p = 0.2489), as shown in Figure 2. Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis showed that the direction of effect remained consistent after omission of each individual study, as shown in Figure S2. The ADJUVITE trial [23] was not pooled with this outcome because its primary end point was early laser flare photometry response at month 2 rather than the composite treatment‐failure end point used in VISUAL I, VISUAL II, and SYCAMORE [9, 14, 15]. The Doi plot and Luis Furuya‐Kanamori (LFK) index are shown in Figure S3 as exploratory displays; because only three studies contributed to this analysis, these measures were not used to determine publication bias or to alter the certainty rating.

Pairwise random‐effects forest plot of treatment failure comparing adalimumab with placebo. RRs < 1 favor adalimumab. The plot incorporates the corrected treatment‐failure event counts from VISUAL I, VISUAL II, and SYCAMORE. CI, confidence interval; RR, risk ratio.

All‐Cause Mortality

Two randomized controlled trials [9, 14] including 443 participants reported all‐cause mortality. Three deaths occurred in the adalimumab groups and none occurred in the placebo groups. The pooled mortality estimate was inconclusive (RR = 3.80; 95% CI, 0.42–34.15; p = 0.23), as shown in Figure 3.

Pairwise random‐effects forest plot of all‐cause mortality comparing adalimumab with placebo. The estimate is inconclusive because only three deaths occurred, all in the adalimumab groups, with zero deaths in the placebo groups and a very wide confidence interval. CI, confidence interval; RR, risk ratio.

Network Meta‐Analysis

UTI and URTI

Four studies [9, 14, 21, 22] contributed to the UTI network, which contained adalimumab, placebo, sarilumab, and infliximab nodes. The analyzable denominator was 487 participants after restriction to treatment arms with UTI event data available for extraction. The network did not contain a closed loop; therefore, statistical inconsistency could not be assessed. Compared with placebo, neither adalimumab (RR = 1.64; 95% CI, 0.52–5.16; p = 0.40; SUCRA = 0.58) nor sarilumab (RR = 2.66; 95% CI, 0.13–52.87; p = 0.52; SUCRA = 0.53) showed a statistically significant difference in UTI risk. Infliximab showed an imprecise signal toward higher UTI risk compared with placebo (RR = 40.98; 95% CI, 1.82–922.17; p = 0.02; SUCRA = 0.03). This estimate should be interpreted as very low‐certainty and hypothesis‐generating because it was driven by sparse events, a very wide CI, and a single non‐randomized infliximab‐adalimumab comparison from the prospective cohort study [22], in which concomitant methotrexate exposure was common. Discussion

This systematic review found that adalimumab had the most consistent randomized evidence for reducing treatment failure compared with placebo in NIU. The mortality estimate was inconclusive, with three deaths in the adalimumab groups, none in the placebo groups, and a wide CI. The safety networks detected no statistically significant differences among treatments for ocular hypertension, macular edema, headache, or URTI, with wide CIs across these comparisons, whereas infliximab showed a statistically significant increase in UTI risk compared with placebo based on a sparse, non‐randomized comparison. Adalimumab was associated with a higher risk of injection‐site reactions compared with placebo, which is clinically plausible given its subcutaneous route of administration. Infliximab showed an imprecise signal toward higher UTI risk; however, this finding was driven by sparse data and a single non‐randomized cohort and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than as definitive comparative safety evidence. SUCRA rankings should be considered exploratory because several nodes were informed by single studies and wide CIs.

NIU is a major cause of ocular morbidity and pediatric uveitis may result in substantial vision loss [24]. Pediatric uveitis also presents unique diagnostic, examination, and management considerations [25]. Managing NIU remains a significant challenge for clinicians and caregivers. Multiple studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of biologic therapies in NIU [9, 14, 15, 23, 26, 27, 28]. Findings from a previous meta‐analysis indicated that immunosuppressant therapy achieved superior clinical outcomes in NIU relative to placebo and other pharmacologic options [29]. According to published guidance, systemic immunomodulatory agents that are not corticosteroids may be employed to control ongoing or markedly inflamed NIU, which can minimize the development of complications associated with structural ocular damage [30]. Guidelines further indicate that, in cases where standard treatment is insufficient, clinicians may introduce immunosuppressant medications and initiate corticosteroid dose reduction as part of ongoing management [30].

Evidence indicates that adalimumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting TNF‐α, is effective in achieving and prolonging remission in NIU, and may improve visual outcomes while reducing corticosteroid requirements [26, 27, 28]. Despite its benefits, chronic adalimumab therapy may result in side effects, particularly heightened susceptibility to infections and certain cancers [31]. Therefore, adalimumab therapy optimization in patients who achieve remission is crucial for minimizing both costs and side effects. Established guidelines, such as those from EULAR, recommend reducing the dose or extending the dosing interval of biologic agents in patients who achieve prolonged remission [32].

In our analysis, adalimumab significantly reduced treatment failure compared with placebo. This finding is supported by indirect evidence from the recently published ADVISE trial [33], which compared adalimumab with conventional immunosuppression rather than placebo and demonstrated that adalimumab achieved successful corticosteroid‐sparing in 69% of patients at 6 months versus 54% with conventional immunosuppression [33]. According to international consensus, adalimumab is the preferred biologic agent for NIU across 11 different uveitis subtypes, selected by 97.7% of surveyed uveitis specialists globally [34]. Stepwise treatment algorithms show corticosteroids used as first‐line therapy, followed by conventional immunosuppressants (methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine), with biologics like adalimumab reserved for refractory cases or when rapid steroid‐sparing is required [34, 35, 36, 37].

Approximately 25% of patients fail to respond to adalimumab, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Predictors of treatment failure include younger age, higher baseline relapse rates, and potentially immunogenicity with anti‐drug antibody formation [35]. In certain individuals with uveitis linked to JIA, the development of persistent anti‐adalimumab antibodies after treatment has been shown to compromise the therapeutic effectiveness of adalimumab. Co‐administration of additional immunosuppressive medications appears to mitigate the generation of these antibodies [36]. Although treatment failure has been observed in certain patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy [37], earlier meta‐analytic findings suggest that immunosuppressant monotherapy or combination therapy can significantly extend the duration before treatment failure relative to placebo [29].

The mortality estimate does not establish either reassurance or harm, as only three deaths occurred and the CI was wide. Clinical monitoring remains essential for serious infections, including tuberculosis and fungal infections, malignancies, and cardiovascular events during TNF‐α inhibitor therapy [38].

No statistically significant differences were detected for ocular hypertension, macular edema, headache, urinary tract infection, or URTI, and adalimumab was associated with a higher risk of injection‐site reactions than placebo. The injection‐site reaction result is consistent with the established local adverse‐event profile of subcutaneously administered biologic therapy [39, 40].

In our network‐pooled results, adalimumab was associated with a higher risk of injection‐site reactions than placebo. These reactions may include local inflammatory responses presenting with erythema, pain, swelling, or pruritus within 24 h, as well as immune‐mediated immediate or delayed reactions. Delayed reactions may occur several days after injection and may be associated with anti‐drug antibody formation and reduced therapeutic efficacy [39, 40].

A prospective cohort study [22] reported more favorable efficacy and safety outcomes with adalimumab than with infliximab. In the present network analysis, infliximab showed imprecise safety signals across several outcomes; however, these estimates were predominantly dependent on a single non‐randomized comparison with sparse events and very wide CIs. Accordingly, the infliximab estimates should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than as evidence of greater causal harm.

The SATURN trial [21] suggested the potential benefit of sarilumab for controlling posterior‐segment NIU. Although the primary end point did not reach statistical significance using validated central photographic grading of vitreous haze performed at a masked reading center, the non‐masked investigator‐based assessment showed a statistically significant advantage for sarilumab, especially in eyes with baseline vitreous haze ≥ 2, and this secondary, non‐masked finding should be interpreted with more caution than the primary reading‐center endpoint, and macular thickness, particularly in eyes with macular edema, though interpretation is limited by small sample size [21]. Also, safety outcomes were comparable to those of placebo. Overall, the SATURN trial indicates that interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) inhibition may offer clinical benefit in patients with active inflammation or macular edema, aligning with our meta‐analysis findings showing supportive evidence for sarilumab that remains limited by small sample size [21].

The ADJUVITE trial [23] strengthens the evidence that adalimumab is effective for early‐onset, JIA‐associated or idiopathic, chronic anterior uveitis unresponsive to topical steroids and methotrexate, showing that 2 months of treatment with adalimumab was effective in reducing ocular inflammation compared with placebo, and that over 12 months of therapy, adalimumab was well tolerated and associated with persistent uveitis improvement in most cases. Although the trial's small sample size means the primary end point's CI contains 1, the authors note that significance becomes clearer using the Koopman asymptotic score. These findings align closely with our pairwise meta‐analysis, which demonstrated a significant reduction in treatment failure with adalimumab, supporting its meaningful therapeutic benefit across diverse uveitis populations. The absence of treatment‐related adverse events of special interest in ADJUVITE is consistent with its reported short‐term safety findings [23]. In the present meta‐analysis, the mortality estimate remained inconclusive because only three deaths occurred and the CI was extremely wide; therefore, neither an increased mortality risk nor reassurance regarding mortality can be established from the available evidence.

This systematic review provides an updated comparative synthesis incorporating both pairwise and frequentist network meta‐analytic approaches, with particular emphasis on treatment failure and granular safety outcomes, while explicitly evaluating the influence of sparse events and observational evidence on network estimates. Most included studies were randomized controlled trials and were conducted across multiple countries. Nevertheless, the clinical heterogeneity across age groups, uveitis subtypes, underlying systemic diseases, concomitant therapies, and treatment nodes limits the extent to which network estimates can be generalized across all NIU populations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The evidence base was small, follow‐up was limited for several comparisons, and long‐term safety could not be assessed reliably. Sarilumab and infliximab were each represented by only one contributing study, substantially limiting the certainty and generalizability of comparative estimates involving these treatments. Several further limitations should be acknowledged. First, the network combines six RCTs with one prospective cohort study [22], which was the only study linking infliximab to the rest of the network through its head‐to‐head comparison with adalimumab. Indirect estimates involving infliximab, therefore, depend on a non‐randomized comparison and on the assumption of transitivity between observational and randomized evidence and should be interpreted with caution. Second, the elevated point estimate for infliximab–associated UTI (RR 40.98; 95% CI 1.82–922.17) reflects a sparse‐event comparison, where the very wide CI indicates substantial statistical fragility rather than a robust comparative safety signal; this finding should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating. In that cohort [22], the majority of patients receiving infliximab were also receiving concomitant methotrexate, so the increased infection signal cannot be attributed to infliximab in isolation and may reflect cumulative immunosuppression. Third, the included studies span clinically distinct uveitis populations (anterior, intermediate, posterior, and panuveitis) and disease entities (e.g., JIA–associated uveitis, Behçet's, sarcoidosis, Vögt–Koyanagi–Harada‐related uveitis), which introduces clinical heterogeneity that may compromise transitivity across the network. Fourth, although the inclusion criteria allowed conventional immunosuppressants, the analyzed network is dominated by adalimumab versus placebo comparisons, and methotrexate and mycophenolate mofetil from the randomized trial [20] did not connect to the rest of the network and could not be incorporated into the network meta‐analysis. Finally, concomitant ocular therapy (topical corticosteroids, peri‐ or intra‐ocular steroid injections) was not consistently reported across studies and represents a potential confounder for ocular adverse events such as ocular hypertension and macular edema, where steroid response may itself contribute to the observed outcome.

Adalimumab has the most consistent randomized evidence for reducing treatment failure and maintaining disease control in NIU. Comparative safety evidence for infliximab and sarilumab remains limited and imprecise. The infliximab safety estimates are hypothesis‐generating because they depend substantially on a single non‐randomized comparison, whereas evidence for sarilumab remains limited by the small number of participants and studies. These findings support individualized steroid‐sparing immunomodulatory treatment while emphasizing the need for stronger comparative evidence.

Larger randomized trials with extended follow‐up and direct head‐to‐head comparisons of systemic immunomodulatory therapies are needed.

This systematic review and network meta‐analysis demonstrates that adalimumab has the most consistent randomized evidence for reducing treatment failure in NIU. Comparative safety estimates for most outcomes remain imprecise, and evidence involving infliximab is particularly uncertain because it depends substantially on a single non‐randomized comparison. Evidence for sarilumab remains limited but warrants further investigation. Overall, these findings support individualized, evidence‐based selection of systemic immunomodulatory therapy while emphasizing the need for larger, long‐term, head‐to‐head randomized trials.
