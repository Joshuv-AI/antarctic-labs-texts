EASG-Bench: Video Q&A Benchmark with Egocentric Action Scene Graphs
Ivan Rodin, Tz-Ying Wu, Kyle Min, Sharath Nittur Sridhar, Antonino Furnari, Subarna Tripathi, Giovanni Maria Farinella, [1ex] University of Catania

Abstract
We introduce EASG-Bench , a question-answering benchmark for egocentric videos where the question-answering pairs are created from spatio-temporally grounded dynamic scene graphs capturing intricate relationships among actors, actions, and objects. We propose a systematic evaluation framework and evaluate several language-only and video large language models (video-LLMs) on this benchmark. We observe a performance gap in language-only and video-LLMs, especially on questions focusing on temporal ordering, thus identifying a research gap in the area of long-context video understanding. To promote the reproducibility of our findings and facilitate further research, the benchmark and accompanying code are available at the following GitHub page: https://github.com/fpv-iplab/EASG-bench .

Introduction

Recent Large Language Models have been enhanced with multimodal capabilities, equipping them with the capability to chat with long-form videos
Ren2023TimeChat
;
litahuang2024
, in addition to images and interleaved vision-language content.
While different question-answering benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate such abilities, they are usually generated from narrations
chen2023egoplan
, which makes it hard to obtain grounded question-answer pairs. In contrast, the community has recently made efforts to equip egocentric videos with more structured annotations, for instance, in the form of Egocentric Action Scene Graphs (EASG), as recently proposed in
rodin2024action
.
In this paper, we propose
EASG-Bench
, a novel question-answering benchmark where question-answer pairs are generated from the EASG annotations introduced in
rodin2024action
.
Our dataset comprises over 1,807 Q&A pairs across five categories. Each question incorporates both spatial and temporal grounding which will support grounded question-answering and language-neutral evaluations where questions are asked based on object bounding boxes rather than object names.
We provide details on the data generation pipeline, craft an evaluation method and benchmark different Large Language Models (LLMs) against the newly introduced benchmark.
Surprisingly, we observe that even the best video-LLM models struggle with temporal video understanding tasks compared to their pure language counterparts.
We investigate how to bridge the gap between Video-LLM and LLM-only performances.
We show that a chain-of-thought prompting that explicitly captures temporal ordering improves the performance.
This observation points to a future direction of research towards spatio-temporal reasoning beyond a sequence of text tokens in long-form videos.

Egocentric video Q&A benchmarks.

Several video question-answering benchmarks on egocentric videos exist in the literature each focusing on different aspects, summarized in Table
1
.
EgoSchema
mangalam2023egoschema
is designed to evaluate long-form video understanding through a challenging multiple-choice question-answering (MCQA) task.
Unlike previous benchmarks that focus solely on clip length, EgoSchema introduces temporal certificate sets to quantify the intrinsic temporal reasoning required. EgoThink
cheng2024egothink
encompasses six capabilities with twelve detailed dimensions. The benchmark is constructed using selected clips from egocentric videos, with manually annotated question-answer pairs.
QAEgo4D
patel2025advancing
, based on Ego4D-NLQ, focuses on answering questions with episodic memory using a constant-size video representation. Unlike QAEgo4D, MultiHop-EgoQA
chen2025grounded
and EgoTempo
plizzari2025omnia
require reasoning across multiple segments of a video to find the correct answer. While the questions are temporally grounded in these datasets, they are not spatially grounded. AMEGO
goletto2024amego
is a recent benchmark featuring hand-object interaction tracklets, which provides spatio-temporal grounding of the questions as the proposed
EASG-Bench
. However, the Q&As in AMEGO are not open-ended, limiting the scope of the potential responses and the ability to evaluate more complex reasoning capabilities of AI models.

Video question-answering with video-LLMs.

Past investigations also proposed different approaches for video question-answering based on video-LLMs.
TimeChat

Ren2023TimeChat
is one of the first models that follow user instructions to locate the start and end timestamps that correspond to user queries.
LITA

litahuang2024
is framed as a temporal localization assistant that leverages time tokens to encode the time-stamps relative to the video length.
Sa2VA

yuan2025sa2va
focuses on dense grounded understanding of videos, which combines SAM-2
ravi2024sam
with a vision-language model LLaVA
li2024llava
, and unifies text and video into a shared LLM token space. Recently, powerful video-LLM models such as
Qwen2.5-VL

Qwen2.5-VL
that can process multimodal inputs of various sizes and extended durations have emerged.

EASG-Bench

Benchmark creation.

We propose a novel benchmark generation approach for video question answering that leverages Egocentric Action Scene Graphs (EASGs)
rodin2024action
rather than conventional narration-based methodologies. While our work builds on the approach adopted in several previous studies, where large language models (LLMs) are employed to automatically generate question–answer pairs from narrative descriptions
plizzari2025omnia
;
patel2025advancing
, our method diverges by grounding the generation process in the structured representations provided by EASGs. The process of question-answer pairs generation is shown in Figure
1
.

EASGs capture the intricate relationships between actors, actions, and objects within the video, enabling us to generate a categorically structured set of questions that probe various aspects of the visual content. Specifically, we systematically generate questions belonging to the following categories:

•

Purpose Questions
: Inquiries that explore the underlying intent of an object or an action, e.g.,
What is the purpose of object X on a video?

•

Direct Object Questions
: Questions that focus on the primary objects manipulated during an action, e.g.
Q: What did camera wearer add to coffee? A: sugar

•

Indirect Object Questions
: Questions pertaining to secondary objects or contextual entities present during the interactions, e.g.
Where did camera wearer add sugar? A: to coffee

•

Ordering (before/after) Questions
: Temporal queries that examine the sequence of events, such as
What happened before action X?
or
What happened after action X?

Figure 2
:
Samples of questions, ground truth answers, and the answer generated by Qwen2.5-VL for each type of question in
EASG-Bench
.

By anchoring the question generation process to EASGs, we ensure that each question is categorizable, structured, temporally grounded and that objects mentioned in questions and answers are spatially grounded on video frames.

To generate the question–answer pairs from the graph sequence, we condition LLMs, specifically
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct

grattafiori2024llama
, to restrict output to only those questions that can be unambiguously answered via direct observation of the video. This constraint is critical for long, untrimmed video benchmarking for example, given a video sequence with actions such as
put cup, pour coffee, drink coffee, put cup, add sugar
, the method deliberately marks
put cup
action as not fitting for the “Ordering” QA-pair generation purpose, and avoids generating a question like “What happened after camera wearer ‘put cup’?” since such an inquiry could yield multiple valid answers. To avoid hallucinations in LLM uniqueness assessment on long sequences of action scene graphs, we implement a robust filtering strategy in which we prompt the LLM with the same graph sequence extracted from a video five times. Each iteration generates an independent assessment of whether the possible QA pair adheres to our uniqueness criterion. We then apply majority voting across these iterations to determine the suitability of the QA pair for inclusion in our dataset.

Dataset statistics.

The dataset comprises 1,807 question-answer pairs associated with 221 video clips from the Ego4D dataset. The questions are surrounded by objects grounded in EASGs. Around 1/3 of the questions are related to object functions (purpose), 1/3 about object manipulation (direct/indirect), and the remaining 1/3 about the ordering (before/after) of object manipulations. Experiments

Evaluation.

Our benchmark includes open-ended questions, which cannot be evaluated with standard metrics such as accuracy. Instead, we evaluate the question answering quality using
Llama3-8B-Instruct

grattafiori2024llama
with an LLM-as-a-judge mechanism similar to
litahuang2024
.
Given the video captions with timestamps as the context information
C
and the ground truth answer
GT
to the question
Q
, the LLM is tasked with rating the quality of a candidate answer
A
on a scale of 1 to 10, according to helpfulness, relevance, accuracy, and level of details.
It is also asked to provide an explanation of why the score is given to the input answer.
Specifically, the input to the LLM consists of 5 fields, i.e.,
{\bf P}=[C][Q][GT][A][R]
and the model produces a score
S
along with an explanation
E
, where
R
denotes the system prompt that outlines the evaluation guidelines.
Figure
3
shows an example of the evaluation.
Since the LLM is not calibrated to generate a score within the specified range, there is no guarantee that the model will give a full score to the ground truth answer. To address this, we adjust the scores by rescaling them relative to the scores given to the ground truth answer, reported as the relative scores (
Rel. Score
).

Benchmark performance.

We benchmark the proposed
EASG-Bench

TimeChat and LITA are pioneering video-LLMs designed for tasks involving temporal understanding, such as event localization, while Sa2VA is a more recent model featuring spatial grounding with the integration of SAM2 features. Qwen2.5-VL, on the other hand, has recently been released for general-purpose video comprehension, which achieves the best performance across all the categories.
We also explore the performance of SOTA LLMs on
EASG-Bench
without providing the visual input.
While the LLMs are language-only models, they excel in answering questions about object purposes and sequences (before/after), due to their strong reasoning and common-sense capability. Video-LLMs, on the contrary, tend to be less effective for ordering-related questions, possibly because their reasoning ability is diminished when the models are tuned to accommodate a new modality.
However, for questions concerning object manipulation (direct/indirect), video-LLMs significantly outperform the language-only models, as these questions necessitate video context for an accurate response.
Nonetheless,
EASG-Bench
is still challenging for all the existing models.

Chain-of-Thought prompting.

To investigate why the video-LLMs struggle with sequence-related (before/after) questions, we delve into the idea of chain-of-thought prompting with the best-performing model, Qwen2.5-VL.
Instead of directly asking “What did camera wearer do before/after action X?”, we can prompt the mode for locating action X in the first stage, and inquire about the action before/after that step in the second.
Table
3
shows a notable gain of this 2-stage prompting strategy compared to the 1-stage baseline. This largely mitigates the gap between the video-LLMs and LLMs for “after” questions. However, it still underperforms LLMs for “before” questions, which suggests that there is still room for video-LLMs to improve their
look-back
ability.

5
Conclusions

We introduced
EASG-Bench
, a novel egocentric video question-answering benchmark grounded in space and time.
A total of 1,807 questions incorporate both spatial and temporal groundings and are divided into six different categories. We evaluate different language-only and video-llm models on this benchmark.
We observe that while powerful models such as Qwen2.5-VL utilize visual signals effectively and outperform language-only baselines for most categories of questions,
they struggle with temporal video understanding tasks such as “before” and “after” kinds.
We investigate the performance gap between language-only video-llm models for such temporal reasoning tasks and see that chain-of-thought prompting can bridge the gap to some extent highlighting a need of future research on spatio-temporal reasoning beyond sequence of text tokens specially addressing long-form videos.
