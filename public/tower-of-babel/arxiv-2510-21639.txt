Introduction

With the rise of autonomous systems in
widespread application areas such as search and rescue,
warehouse management, extraplanetary exploration, handling hazardous materials, and
transportation
[
18
]
, there has been extensive work on computing a coordinated motion
plan for a team of robtots moving inside a 2D or 3D environment. In addition to avoiding
collisions with obstacles, motion plans must also avoid collisions between robots, and one thus needs to consider the problem in a higher dimensional
configuration space
. Furthermore, we wish to ensure a good quality of motion such as being short or having a small makespan.

In this paper, we consider the problem of coordinated motion planning of
k
identical robots of simple shape
(e.g., squares, disks) in a polygonal work environment. For simplicity, we focus on the case in which each
robot is modeled as a unit square. Note that if
k
is part of the input, even the problem of computing
a feasible plan is
P
SPACE-hard (see e.g.
[
1
]
). We seek an approximation
algorithm that runs in roughly
n^{O(k)}
time and returns a motion plan that aims to minimize the
total length of the paths traversed by the robots. No such algorithm is known for
k>2
.

Problem statement.

Let
\square:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid||x||_{\infty}\leq 1\}
denote the
axis-aligned square of unit radius—referred to as
a
unit square
for short—centered at the origin.
Let
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
denote a system of
k
robots, each modeled as a
unit square, that can translate inside the same closed
polygonal environment (possibly with holes)
\mathscr{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
called the shared
workspace
.
Let

be the number of vertices of
\mathscr{W}
.
A placement of one robot of
[k]
is represented by
the position of its center in the workspace
\mathscr{W}
. For such a placement
to be free of collision with the boundary
\partial\mathscr{W}
of
\mathscr{W}
,
the representing point should be at
\ell_{\infty}
-distance at least
1

from
\partial\mathscr{W}
. (Note that the robot is allowed to touch
an obstacle, since we define
\mathscr{F}
to be a closed set.)
We let

denote
the
free space
of a single robot, which is the subset of
\mathscr{W}

consisting of all collision-free placements.
A (joint)
configuration

\boldsymbol{p}
of
[k]

is represented as a
k
-tuple
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\in\mathscr{W}^{k}
,
where
p_{i}
is the placement of a robot
i\in[k]
.
The

for short,
is the set of all configurations,
and is thus represented as
\mathscr{W}^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2k}
.
A configuration
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{2k}

if
p_{i}\in\mathscr{F}
for all
i\in[k]
and
||p_{i}-p_{j}||_{\infty}\geq 2
for every pair
i\not=j
.
Let

denote the
2k

, comprising the set of all free configurations.
Clearly,
\boldsymbol{F}\subseteq\mathscr{F}^{k}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})

source configuration
and
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
a given
target configuration
.
An

for some

\boldsymbol{\pi}(T)=\boldsymbol{t}

The image of

is a (continuous) curve in the C-space, referred to

(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-path

With a slight abuse of notation, we use
\boldsymbol{\pi}
to denote its image

If
\boldsymbol{\pi}\subset\boldsymbol{F}

\boldsymbol{\pi}
is
feasible
,
and if there exists a feasible
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan we say that the

(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
is
reachable

For a plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}:[0,T]\to\mathscr{W}^{k}
, let

\pi_{i}:[0,T]\to\mathscr{W}
be the projection of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
onto the two-dimensional plane
spanned by the
2i-1

2i
coordinates specifying the motion of robot
i

that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
induces; that is,

\boldsymbol{\pi}(\lambda)=\left(\pi_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,\pi_{k}(\lambda)\right)

\lambda\in[0,T]
.
Again, with a slight abuse of notation, we also use
\pi_{i}

to denote the paths followed by robot
i
.

For a path

\mathscr{W}

denote its

We define

by
\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi}):=\sum_{i\in[k]}|\pi_{i}|

\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi})

k
robots.
If
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
is reachable, a minimum-cost feasible

(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})\in\boldsymbol{F}^{2}

(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan.
We will also study the min-sum problem when each robot is a
unit disk.
The above definitions extend verbatim to this settings, though of course the regions
\mathscr{F}

and
\boldsymbol{F}
depend on the shape of the robots.

Related work.

Algorithmic motion planning has been extensively studied in the computational geometry
and robotics communities for more than four decades: see the books and reviews
[
10
,
20
,
17
]
for a high-level overview of the most relevant results.
Multi-robot motion planning is computationally intractable even in simple settings. If the number of robots is part of the input, the feasibility problem is already
P
SPACE-hard
[
1
,
3
,
6
,
23
,
11
]
.

Notwithstanding a rich literature on multi-robot motion planning
in both continuous and discrete settings (robots moving on a graph in the latter setting), see, e.g.,
[
7
,
12
,
14
,
19
,
21
,
25
,
26
]
,
relatively little is known about algorithms producing paths with provable quality guarantees.
Approximation algorithms for minimizing the total path-length
are given in
[
3
,
22
,
24
,
1
]

for a set of unit-disk robots assuming a certain separation between the start and goal positions, as well as from the obstacles.
The separation assumption makes the problem considerably easier.
An
O(1)
-approximation algorithm was proposed in
[
8
]
for computing a plan that minimizes the makespan for a set of unit discs (or squares) in the plane without obstacles, again assuming some separation.

Computing the min-sum motion plan for two unit squares/disks even in the absence of
obstacles is non-trivial
[
9
,
16
]
.
There is some recent progress on min-sum motion planning amid obstacles
[
4
,
2
]
.
Agarwal et al.
[
2
]
give an
O(n^{4}\log n)
-time algorithm for computing a min-sum plan for two unit-square robots in a rectilinear environment when the path length is measured in the
\ell_{1}
-metric.
However, their approach neither extends to arbitrary polygonal environment nor to the
\ell_{2}
-metric,
and it is an open question whether the min-sum problem for
2
robots (under the
\ell_{2}
-metric) is in
P
.
For arbitrary polygonal environments, Agarwal et al.
[
4
]
give an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for two unit-square robots.
To accomplish the FPTAS, the algorithm in
[
4
]
strongly relies on using motion in which one robot moves at a time.
For
k>2
robots, it is easy to construct examples where the optimal motion must move many robots simultaneously, which adds considerable complexity to
the problem and the approach in
[
4
]
does not extend.
It remains an open question
whether a polynomial-time approximation algorithm exists for min-sum motion planning if
k>2
is a constant.
Note that we cannot hope for a PTAS for the min-sum problem if
k
is part of the input since even the feasibility problem
is
P
SPACE-hard .

Our results.

Our main result is a polynomial-time bicriteria approximation algorithm (PTAS) for
min-sum motion-planning with
k
congruent axis-aligned square
robots translating in a planar polygonal environment, where
k
is a constant.
For a real parameter
r\geq 1
, and for
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{F}
, let
\textsf{opt}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t},r)

denote the cost of the min-sum
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan for
k
square robots
each of radius
r
. If
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
is not reachable with respect to
k

squares of radius
r
, then
\textsf{opt}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t},r)
is undefined.
If a plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}\subset\boldsymbol{F}
is also a feasible plan with respect to
k

squares of radius
1+\rho
each, we say that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is
\rho
-robust
.
We say that
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
is
\rho
-reachable
if there exists a
\rho
-robust
plan.
We note that our use of the parameter
\rho>0
can be interpreted
as a typical robustness parameter in the sampling-based motion planning literature;
see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.
The following theorem states our main result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathscr{W}
be a closed polygonal environment in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
n
vertices.
Let
[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}
be
k
axis-aligned unit-square robots translating in
\mathscr{W}
,
let
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{F}
be a pair of free
configurations of
[k]
, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

be a parameter. If there exists a feasible
\varepsilon
-robust
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan,
then a feasible
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan of
[k]
such that

\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textsf{opt}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t},1+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon
can be
computed in
f(k,\varepsilon)n^{O(k)}
time, where
f(k,\varepsilon)=\left(k/\varepsilon\right)^{O(k^{2})}
.
If
\textsf{opt}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t},1+\varepsilon)>1
, then
\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textsf{opt}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t},1+\varepsilon)
.

Our algorithm extends to regular convex polygons in a straightforward manner. Since a disk can be approximated by regular convex polygon, we obtain the following result. See Section
5

one other robot; this was core to the argument there. We need a completely different approach here, and this is the main technical contribution
of the paper. To prove the existence of a near-optimal tame plan, we start with an arbitrary optimal plan

to eliminate all breakpoints that are far from the landmarks. To this end, we perform a

We show that

, so the sampling density needs to be
\omega(varphi^{-2})
. We prove Lemma
2.2
in Section
4
,
and it is the second main technical result of the paper.

2.2
Computing a near-optimal min-sum plan

We now describe the algorithm for computing a near-optimal
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan for

[k]
inside
\mathscr{W}
. The algorithm consists of the following steps:

1.

Computing landmarks.
Compute
\mathscr{F}^{(1)}
and
\mathscr{F}^{(2)}
using the algorithm described in
[
15
]
.
Set
\Lambda=V^{(1)}\cup V^{(2)}\cup\{s_{i},t_{i}\mid i\in[k]\}
.

2.

Sampling.
Let
\overline{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon}{f(k,\varepsilon)\cdot\sqrt{2}k}
, where
f(k,\varepsilon)
is the
function defined in Lemma
4.5
. Let
\mathbb{G}
be the set of vertices of the uniform grid
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with cells of width
\overline{\varepsilon}
. That is,
\mathbb{G}=\{(i\overline{\varepsilon},j\overline{\varepsilon})\mid i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
Compute

\mathsf{X}=\{u\in\mathbb{G}\mid u\in\mathscr{F}\text{ and $u$ is $\Delta$-close}\}.

(2)

|\mathsf{X}|=O(n/\overline{\varepsilon}^{2})=f^{\prime}(k,\varepsilon)n
, where
f^{\prime}(k,\varepsilon)=O(f^{2}(k,\varepsilon)k^{2}\varepsilon^{-2})=\left(k/\varepsilon\right)^{O(k^{2})}
.

3.

Configuration graph.
We now construct a weighted graph
\mathscr{G}=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E}),w:\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
in
\boldsymbol{F}
.
Set
\mathscr{V}=\{\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\in\mathsf{X}^{k}\mid\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{F}\}.

Set
\mathscr{E}=\{(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathscr{V}\times\mathscr{V}\mid\boldsymbol{p}\boldsymbol{q}\subset\boldsymbol{F}\}.
For an edge
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathscr{E}
, we set its weight to be

w(p,q)=\sum_{i\in[k]}||p_{i}-q_{i}||.

4.

Path computation.
If
\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}
lie in different connected components of
\mathscr{G}
, return
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
is not reachable.
Otherwise, compute a shortest path
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
from
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{t}
in
\mathscr{G}
using Dijkstra’s algorithm, and return
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
as the desired
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan.

This completes the description of the algorithm.
We now defer the analysis of its performance using Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2

to Section
7
. We show that
\mathscr{G}
contains a path from
\boldsymbol{s}
to
\boldsymbol{t}
of cost at most

(1+\varepsilon)\textsf{opt}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t},1+\varepsilon)+O(\varepsilon)
and that the time spent constructing
\mathscr{G}

and computing
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is
f(k,\varepsilon)n^{2k}
, where
f(k,\varepsilon)=(k/\varepsilon)^{O(k^{2})}
.
This implies Theorem
1.1
.

3
Near-Optimal Motion Far from Vertices

In this section we give an overview of the proof of Lemma
2.1
, which shows the existence of an
\varepsilon
-optimal

\Delta
-tame
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
-plan
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
, in which all breakpoints are
\Delta
-close
for
\Delta=O(k^{2}/\varepsilon)
.
We prove the lemma by showing that if a plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
has a breakpoint at time

such that
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\lambda)
is
\Delta
-far, we can remove the
breakpoint
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\lambda)
at a slight increase in the cost of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
The exact surgery to remove a
\Delta
-far breakpoint

requires careful consideration of the position of any robot

which is
\Delta
-far at a placement

in a neighborhood

of size

. Roughly speaking,

either lies in a “narrow”

to park all robots that come sufficiently close to

We first characterize the neighborhood of a
\Delta
-far point (Section

is in a wide area (Section

).
We present the main ideas here, and refer to the full version for all the details.

Recall that for a value
r>0
and a point

,
B(q,r)=q+r\square

denotes the square of radius
r

centered at
q
. Let

be the connected component of

that contains

(assuming

; otherwise it is undefined).
For a set

of points,

, we define
\mathscr{F}[P]=\mathscr{F}\setminus\bigcup_{p\in P}B(p,2)
, which
is the
conditional free space

\mathscr{F}
for one robot when the
|P|
robots are placed at points
in

\mathscr{F}[P]
can be computed in

[
5
, Chapter 13]
.

Topology far from vertices

The following lemma characterizes the topology of

Corridors.

Let

.
Similar to the width of

We say that a corridor

.

For a value

, let

(resp.
\sigma^{+}(r)

-distance

(resp.

(resp.

Let

(resp.

(resp.
\sigma^{+},\sigma^{+}(r)
)
and the portions of the blockers

and
\sigma^{+}(r)

Revolving areas.

We next define regions in
\mathscr{F}
in which robots can move around one another

For a placement

of a unit square robot

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose

is a

-far point that does not lie in any corridor.

Our surgery to remove

-far breakpoints not in a corridor will heavily exploit RAs,

-far points of

3.2
Surgery inside a corridor

The high-level idea of the surgery inside a corridor is to park any robots that ever travel

\Delta
-deep in the corridor, for
\Delta=\Omega(k)
,
at one of the well-behaved positions we call “parking places,” which are
O(k)
-deep in
the corridor, and
keep the robot at one of these places until it exits
T
.
We begin by stating a key property of corridors, proved in
[
4
]
,
which shows that the ordering of the robots inside a corridor
T
, with respect to its
direction vector
u
, remains the same for the duration of a continuous time interval that

T
.

Lemma 3.3

(Lemma 2.2 of
[
4
]
)
.

Let
i,j\in[k]
s.t.
i\not=j
, let
T
be a maximal corridor, and let

be its direction vector.
Let

be a time interval in a plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}

such that

.
Then the sign of

\langle\pi_{i}(\lambda)-\pi_{j}(\lambda),u\rangle
is the same for every
\lambda\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]
.

Using this lemma, we devise a
local surgery

that modifies an arbitrary min-sum plan into one only
parking the robots at well-behaved placements inside a corridor.
That is, whenever a robot enters a corridor, our surgery parks robots only
within
O(k)
distance from its portals,
at a small increase in the cost of the plan. First, we need some technical lemmas
allowing us to move robots individually to these well-behaved parking places near the portals,
and to prove the existence of sufficiently many such parking places.

3.3
Near-optimal motion in wide areas

We now describe the surgery
to remove
\Delta
-far breakpoints from wide areas.
Here we set
\Delta=Ck^{2}
, for some sufficiently large constant
C>0
.
Unlike corridors, the relative ordering of robots in a wide area may change and the robots may
cross each other,
which makes the surgery considerably more involved. We heavily rely on revolving areas, particularly WSRAs.
The following two lemmas lay the groundwork for performing the surgery and are used repeatedly.
The first lemma states that one can carve out sufficient number of WSRAs in a neighborhood of sufficiently far point, and the second lemma states that two robots can move freely within an RA.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
j\geq 0
be an integer. For any
3(24)j
-far point

that lies in a wide area,

.

Let

be a point such that
\text{RA}(p)\subseteq\mathscr{F}
, and let

such that

.
Then there exists a feasible

((p,a),(p,b))

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})

to

, and

-far point

6
Near-Optimal Motion Far from Vertices: A Complete Proof

In this section we give the full proof of Lemma
2.1
, namely the existence of an
\varepsilon
-optimal

\Delta
-tame plan, in which all breakpoints of the paths of all robots lie in
\Delta
-sized
neighborhoods of landmarks, where
\Delta=O(k^{2}/\varepsilon)
. As mentioned above, we do not need any robustness assumption here.
We prove the lemma by showing that if a plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
has a breakpoint at time

such that
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\lambda)
is
\Delta
-far, we perform a surgery on
\boldsymbol{\pi}
that removes
\boldsymbol{\pi}(\lambda)

at a slight increase in the cost. At a very high level, suppose
\pi_{i}(\lambda)

is
\Delta
-far for some

. Let
(\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+})
be the maximal interval such that

\pi_{i}(\lambda)
is
\Delta
-far for every
\lambda\in[\lambda^{-},\lambda^{+}]
. The idea is to

the robot

at time
\lambda^{-}
by parking it at a location near
\pi_{i}(\lambda)
,
and keeping it at this position until time
\lambda^{+}
. We

(unfreeze)

at time

along the shortest path in

from

to
\pi_{i}(\lambda^{+})
. When

-far point (Section

and that there were some other point

-separated, and thus it is

-separated. But again, this implies a vertical segment of length at least two contained

, a contradiction. The case where

are

-separated is similar.
We conclude that there is at most one

. So, as long as we produce a plan
such that

, it suffices only to argue that

.

There are two subcases, either (i) the portals are vertical, or (ii) the portals have slope
+1
.

Suppose (i) that the portals are vertical. This implies
(p_{i},p_{j})
is

-separated:
On the assumption that

is widening (in the

, and

. Since

, then

, implying

, contradicting that
\boldsymbol{p}\in\boldsymbol{F}
.
So,
(p_{i},p_{j})
are
x
-separated.

Let

be the intersection point between

and
the horizontal line with the same

-coordinate as

. Since

Now suppose (ii) that the portals have slope
-1

is widening, the segment

\ell

.
Moreover, since the portals have slope

,

We conclude that when

is widening, there exists a feasible
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
-plan in which robot

(p_{i},q)
-path

\pi_{i}
as defined, while every other robot

\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\leq 4\sqrt{2}+2

Case 2:

||\sigma^{-}||_{2}>||\sigma^{+}||_{2}
(

As above, we would like to simply move

via the shortest path to

We then conduct the inverse plan to the one in Case 1 to move each robot

We conclude the existence of a

The feasibility of
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}}
can be tested in
O(k^{2})
time
by testing the feasibility of linearly interpolating each robot as
in Lemma
4.4
in constant time for each of the
O(k^{2})
pairs.
If
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}}
is feasible, assign the weight
w(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u})=\mbox{\textcent}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}})
to the edge.
Altogether, constructing
\mathscr{G}
can thus be done in time
n^{2k}\varepsilon^{-O(k^{2})}k^{O(k^{2})}
.

Finally, we compute a shortest
(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t})
path in
\mathscr{G}
using Dijkstra’s algorithm,
which takes
O(|\mathscr{V}\log{\mathscr{V}}+\mathscr{E})=n^{2k}\cdot(k/\varepsilon)^{O(k^{2})}
time.
∎
