Nationwide Survey of Korean Endocrinologists on Barriers to Implementing Smart Healthcare Technologies
Authors: Han-Sang Baek, Mee Kyoung Kim, So Yoon Kwon, Kyuho Kim, Seung Shin Park, Jae Hoon Moon, Yun Mi Choi, Jae Hyoung Cho, Smart Healthcare Committee of the Korean Endocrine Society
Published: 2026

Smart healthcare technologies are increasingly being integrated into modern clinical practice. The World Health Organization defines digital health as the field of knowledge and practice related to the development and application of digital technologies for improving health outcomes [1]. This broad concept encompasses various technologies and systems, including mobile health applications, wearable devices, artificial intelligence (AI), health information systems, connected digital platforms, and telemedicine, all of which can support disease monitoring, patient engagement, and clinical decision-making. The European Union and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration offer similar definitions, describing digital health as the use of information and communication technology–based tools—including mobile health, wearable devices, and telehealth—to support healthcare delivery across prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and disease management [2,3].

Smart healthcare technologies are particularly relevant in endocrinology because many endocrine disorders require continuous monitoring, longitudinal management, and active patient participation. Currently, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), wearable monitoring systems, AI-assisted diagnostic tools, and digital therapeutic platforms are being increasingly used in diabetes and metabolic disease management [4,5]. Moreover, the growing importance of personalized medicine and remote monitoring has accelerated interest in digital health technologies within endocrine practice [6].

However, despite rapid technological advances, the real-world implementation of smart healthcare in clinical endocrinology remains uncertain. Thus, we conducted a nationwide online survey between July 11 and August 1, 2025, to evaluate perceptions, utilization patterns, and barriers to adoption among Korean endocrinologists (Appendix 1). The survey invitation was distributed by email to members of the Korean Endocrine Society whose email addresses were available in the society’s membership database. Participants were invited to complete an online questionnaire administered through Google Forms using a web link or QR code provided in the invitation. Of the 4,009 members who received the invitation, 140 completed the survey, corresponding to a response rate of 3.49%. A total of 140 endocrinologists participated; female participants accounted for 62.1% of respondents, and most participants were in their 30s or 40s (77.1%). Participating endocrinologists were distributed across primary, secondary, and tertiary healthcare institutions, with nearly half working in tertiary hospitals (47.1%). The major clinical practice areas were diabetes and metabolic diseases (73.6%), thyroid diseases (44.3%), and bone and mineral disorders (32.1%).

Most participants reported familiarity with smart healthcare technologies; 42.1% reported occasional use, 20.0% active utilization, and 1.4% indicated that they had never heard of such technologies. Regarding current clinical practice, 51.4% of the participants reported using smart healthcare technologies for selected patients, whereas 15.0% actively utilized these technologies in routine practice (Fig. 1A).

Importantly, nearly all respondents considered smart healthcare technologies necessary to improve endocrine care; 52.9% responded “very necessary” and 46.4% “necessary” (Fig. 1A). The most commonly expected benefits included improved self-management and monitoring (88.6%), increased clinical efficiency (62.1%), and optimization of treatment planning and monitoring (60.7%) (Fig. 1B).

Nevertheless, despite high awareness and perceived necessity, active implementation remained limited. The major barriers identified in structured responses were financial burden (78.6%), difficulty among elderly patients (60.0%), lack of physician education (25.7%), technological reliability concerns (22.1%), and data security issues (21.4%) (Fig. 1B). In open-ended responses, participants emphasized that structural and workflow-related barriers constrained the practical implementation of smart healthcare technologies. Many respondents reported that limited consultation time made it difficult to educate patients regarding device use and to interpret digital health data during routine outpatient visits. Several participants specifically commented that “explaining device usage requires substantial time and effort” and that “no reimbursement is provided for the additional workload associated with digital healthcare.” Interoperability between wearable devices, personal health records, and electronic medical record (EMR) systems was also frequently identified as a major limitation. Respondents noted that patient-generated health data were often difficult to integrate into existing hospital systems, and that this substantially reduced practical utility in real-world clinical settings. Additional concerns included digital accessibility among elderly patients, technological reliability, and insufficient physician training. Several participants emphasized that older patients often required repeated education to use digital devices effectively, and that this is difficult to accommodate within current clinical workflows.

Regarding future implementation, respondents repeatedly stressed the need for expanded insurance coverage, the development of clinical guidelines and standardization, physician education programs, and improved technological reliability. In open-ended responses, participants frequently requested reimbursement for CGM-related education and digital healthcare services, improved interoperability between devices and hospital systems, and simplified platforms more suitable for elderly patients. Several participants also highlighted the need for practical and policy-level support, stating that “insurance coverage expansion would substantially improve adoption” and that “education and reimbursement systems for digital healthcare are essential for real-world implementation.” Collectively, these findings suggest that the primary challenge in smart healthcare implementation is not technological innovation per se, but structural barriers within the healthcare system.

Recent Korean studies evaluating the cost-effectiveness of CGM systems have further highlighted the growing clinical and economic importance of digital health technologies in endocrine practice [7]. Along with accumulating evidence regarding the clinical utility of AI-assisted diagnostics, wearable monitoring systems, and digital therapeutics, these findings suggest that digital health technologies are poised to play an increasingly important role in chronic endocrine disease management.

Our findings demonstrate a substantial implementation gap between awareness and real-world adoption of smart healthcare technologies among Korean endocrinologists. Although most respondents recognized the need for and the potential benefits of digital healthcare, active implementation in routine clinical practice remains limited. Addressing reimbursement, workflow burden, physician and patient education, and interoperability between digital platforms and EMR systems is essential for the broader implementation of digital health technologies in endocrine practice.

In addition, further research is needed to establish the clinical utility, feasibility, and long-term clinical and socioeconomic effectiveness of digital health technologies in real-world endocrine care. Future studies should also evaluate how smart healthcare technologies can be integrated into patient monitoring, chronic disease management, and personalized endocrine care pathways. Furthermore, continued academic discussion and collaborative research efforts may help facilitate the safe and effective integration of digital health technologies into clinical endocrinology.
