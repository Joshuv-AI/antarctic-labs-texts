Social environmental risk factors for persistent self-harm in early-to-middle adolescence: data from the Tokyo Teen Cohort study
Authors: Riki Tanaka, Atsushi Nishida, Daiki Nagaoka, Rin Minami, Akito Uno, Yutaka Sawai, Ayako Okuma, Satoshi Yamaguchi, Mitsuhiro Miyashita, Syudo Yamasaki, Shuntaro Ando, Kiyoto Kasai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile most adolescent self-harm (SH) is transient, a substantial minority of adolescents repeat and continue it, requiring intensive care. Although social environmental factors play an important role in adolescent psychological development, little is known about their role in the persistence of SH. Moreover, few studies have focused specifically on early adolescence, when SH often begins, hospital presentations after SH increase sharply, and many preventive programs typically commence. We aimed to identify social environmental risk factors at age 12 that predict the persistence of SH through ages 14 and 16 in a population-based cohort.MethodsWe used data from the Tokyo Teen Cohort study (N = 1501). The experience of SH was self-reported at three timepoints (12, 14 and 16 years of age), and categorized as none, transient (one timepoint), or persistent (≥ 2 timepoints). Potential risk factors were assessed at age 12, including family related factors, school and peer related factors, sociodemographic and developmental characteristics, and psychopathological experiences. Multivariable and multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations of potential risk factors with transient and persistent SH.ResultsOf the 1501 participants 195 (13.0%) experienced transient SH and 53 (3.5%) experienced persistent SH. In fully adjusted models, maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and female sex predicted persistent SH (maternal depressive/anxious symptoms: OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.13–1.92, female: OR 4.74, 95% CI 2.18–10.29), whereas companionship with mother predicted lower likelihood (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.28–0.89). Depressive symptoms and psychotic experiences (PEs) predicted both transient (depressive symptoms: OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.50–2.10, PEs: OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.36–1.82) and persistent SH (depressive symptoms: OR 2.03, 95% CI 1.55–2.66, PEs: OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.48–2.41).ConclusionsMaternal depressive/anxious symptoms were the risk factor of persistent SH and companionship with mother was protective against persistence even after adjustment for other factors. Care for maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and support for mother-child relationships would be required to prevent the persistence of SH, in addition to the care for children’s depressive symptoms and PEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13034-026-01109-1.

Background

Self-harm (SH) is one of the major public health problems in adolescence, and around 10% of adolescents in community engage in SH [1–4]. SH is the third cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of young people [5]. Since the majority of adolescents who conduct SH do not receive medical treatment, with only 12.4% of SH episodes presenting to hospitals [1], a community-based study is required to reveal risk factors of adolescent SH for the development of a prevention strategy.

In the population level, the mean age of SH initiation is 12.8 years old (95% CI 11.78–13.84, 14 studies, 22,031 participants) [6], and the prevalence of SH decreases from mid-adolescence through young adulthood [3]. While most adolescent SH is transient, a substantial minority of adolescents repeat SH [6] and continue it into adulthood [7]. Repetition of SH increases its severity and the risk for suicide [8]. While suicide is uncommon in early adolescence and increases through adolescence [9], preceding SH before 15 years old was not uncommon among young people who die by suicide [10]. Taken together, persistence of SH in early-to-middle adolescence may be a strong predictor of suicide and may be a target for suicide prevention from middle adolescence.

Previous studies have highlighted the role of social environmental factors in adolescent SH. A hospital-based retrospective study in UK revealed that the most frequent problems preceding hospital presentation by SH under 15-years-old were difficulties in relationships with family members (77.3%) and with friends (38.9%), and school/study problems (37.9%) [11]. Community-based study also revealed social environmental risk factors were related to SH in adolescence [12]. For example, in a UK population-based cohort, maternal depressive symptoms were associated with offspring SH in mid-adolescence partly through offspring psychopathology [13]. Adverse school experiences also predicted SH in mid-adolescence adjusted for sex, socioeconomic status, maternal and their own mental health [14]. However, these studies examined the relationship between such risk factors and SH of a certain temporal point.

With respect to the risk factor for repetition of SH, previous studies revealed many factors ranging from sociodemographic, psychological to psychosocial [15]. However, little is known about the risk factors related to family and school environment for SH repetition specific to early-to-middle adolescence. Most of previous studies mixed multiple age groups in their analyses, even if their participants included early adolescents [16–22]. In a recent systematic review on the risk factors of SH repetition [15], 6 out of 27 studies investigated the risk factors in early adolescence (mean baseline age under 14 years old) [23–28]. However, these studies investigated only separation from parents and peer problems as factors related to family and school environments [26, 28]. Other factors such as the relationship with parents or school climate were not examined.

We speculated that family and school environmental factors would predict persistent SH in adolescence. These factors may contribute to persistent SH through sustained psychological distress or the prolongation of underlying psychopathology [29]. Alternatively, positive interpersonal relationships may facilitate help-seeking and early intervention, thereby contributing to the cessation of SH [30]. Adolescence is a developmental period marked by substantial changes in social relationships, highlighting the importance of examining these factors at specific stages [31]. Given that the mean age of SH initiation in the general population is 12.8 years old [6] and that many prevention programs are implemented in early adolescence [32], investigating the association between social environmental factors during this period and persistent SH may help identify potentially modifiable targets for intervention.

This study aimed to examine the social environmental risk factors in early adolescence for persistent SH in early-to-middle adolescence. We used three-timepoint data (12, 14 and 16 years of age) from a population-based birth cohort study in Tokyo, the capital and most populous city of Japan. Both SH and potential risk factors were assessed using questionnaire-based reports from adolescents and their caregivers. We selected a wide range of potential risk factors previously suggested to be related to SH of a certain temporal point in adolescence, including family related factors, school and peer related factors, sociodemographic and developmental characteristics, and psychopathological experiences.

Methods

Data and samples

Our data were derived from the Tokyo Teen Cohort (TTC) study, which is an ongoing birth cohort study of 3007 adolescents who were born between September 2002 and August 2004 [33]. The purpose of the TTC study was to reveal physiological and psychological developmental processes during adolescence and affecting or interactive factors. The study participants were randomly sampled using the resident resister in three municipalities in Tokyo: Setagaya-ku, Mitaka-shi, and Chofu-shi, which neighbor the main research institute, the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Science. The cohort profile was reported previously, and household income of the participants was slightly higher than the Tokyo average [33]. This study used three-timepoint data when the participants were 12 (time 1; T1), 14 (time 2; T2) and 16 years of age (time 3; T3). We analyzed the data of 1501 participants who answered the questionnaire on SH at all three timepoints. Trained interviewers visited the house of participants twice for each survey. At the first visit, written informed consent was obtained from the main caregiver. The adolescents and their main caregivers were asked to complete self-report questionnaires before the second visit. At the second visit, adolescents and their main caregivers were asked to complete another self-report questionnaires separately and enclose the questionnaires in the envelopes by themselves. The TTC is supported by three research institutes: the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute of Medical Science (approval number: 12–35), The University of Tokyo (approval number: 10057) and SOKENDAI (The Graduate University for Advanced Studies) (approval number: 2012002). This study was approved by the ethics committees of the three institutes mentioned above.

Outcome measures

Self-harm

SH refers to intentional self-injury or self-poisoning regardless of intent of suicide [34]. We assessed the experience of SH using self-report questionnaire. At T1, the adolescents answered the following question with yes or no: “Have you ever intentionally hurt yourself in the past year?”. At T2 and T3, they answered the same question with the following response options: never, only once, 2 to 5 times, 6 to 10 times, and more than 10 times, and they also answered the following question in free description: “Please list any body parts that have been injured within the past year” and “Please describe the ways in which you have hurt yourself within the past year”. This was a similar metric to previous studies on SH [2, 3]. As in our previous study [35], at T2 and T3, we determined whether the responses could be considered SH with the following requirements: (1) the harm was intentional or expected, (2) SH usually resulted in immediate physical injury, and (3) socially sanctioned injuries such as body piercing or tattooing were not considered SH. We referred to the definition of non-suicidal self-injury presented by the International Society for the Study of Self-Injury (https://www.itriples.org/), but we did not take the SH motivations into account since they are complex and cannot be categorized neatly [34]. At each timepoint, we divided the participants into two groups: those who self-harm and those who do not.

Family related factors

We assessed dissatisfaction with family, maternal depressive/anxious symptoms, relationships with mother and with father at T1. Maternal depressive/anxious symptoms were shown to be related to SH in previous studies [12, 13]. Since the difficulties in relationships with family members were the most frequent problems before hospital presentation with SH, we added dissatisfaction with family and relationships with mother and with father as potential risk factors [11].

Dissatisfaction with family was self-reported in the similar metrics to the UK Millennium Cohort Study [36]. Maternal depressive/anxious symptoms were assessed with the General Health Questionnaire-28 (GHQ28), a widely used self-report measurement [37] When the responder was a caregiver other than mother, we regarded it as missing value. In this study 1483 in 1501 responders were mothers, 17 were fathers and 1 was another person. Relationship with mother and father, namely companionship, reassurance of worth, and secure base, were assessed with Network of Relationships Inventory (NRI) [38]. NRI is a widely used measurement to assess the relationship between the participants and persons surrounding them such as parents or friends using the same questions. We regarded it as missing value when they answered they don’t have that person.

School and peer related factors

We assessed dissatisfaction with school, distrust in the homeroom teacher, being bullied and bullying others, relationship with friends at T1. Since feeling unconnected to school, unhappiness at school, or feeling that teachers are unfair predicted SH in adolescence in a previous study [14], we added dissatisfaction with school and distrust in the homeroom teacher as potential risk factors. Being bullied and bullying others were shown to be related to SH in a previous study [39]. Since the difficulties in relationship with friends was a frequent problem before hospital presentation with SH, we added relationship with friends as potential risk factors [11].

Dissatisfaction with school and distrust in the homeroom teacher were self-reported with the original questions. We developed the questionnaire with reference to a previous study [14]. Being bullied and bullying others were self-reported with a global scale that adopted questions from the Olweus Bully/Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ) [40]. Peer relationships were evaluated using NRI [38]. Similar to the relationship with parents, companionship, reassurance of worth, and secure base, were assessed, where we used sum of the three questions for each aspect of relationship.

Sociodemographic and developmental characteristics

We assessed sex, intelligence quotient (IQ), autistic traits (AS traits), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) symptoms, annual household income at T1, which were shown to be related to SH in previous studies [41–44].

Sex assigned at birth was answered by parents. IQ score was estimated with a short form of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children - Third Edition as in our previous study [45, 46]. AS traits were assessed with the Short Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ10) [47]. ADHD symptoms were assessed with the hyperactivity/inattention subscale from the parent-report Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [48]. Annual household income was reported with a single question.

Psychopathological experiences

Since children’s depressive symptoms, psychotic experiences (PEs), and dissociative symptoms were shown to be related to SH in the previous studies [20, 26, 35, 49–52], we assessed these symptoms at T1.

Depressive symptoms were assessed with Short Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (SMFQ) [53], a widely used self-report measurement for depressive symptoms of child and adolescents aged 8 to 18 years. PEs were self-reported with the items derived from the schizophrenia section of the Diagnostic Interview Schedule for Children (DISC) (auditory hallucinations, persecutory thoughts, thought broadcasting, and special messages from TVs/radios) [54], with an added question on visual hallucinations. Dissociative symptoms were assessed by a parent-report questionnaire using 6 items from the Child Behavior Checklist, a widely used questionnaire for identifying problematic behavior in children [55, 56]. As in our previous study [35], the dissociative symptoms score was defined as the sum of the answers to these 6 questions.

Statistical analysis

We divided the adolescents into three groups depending on SH experiences over the three timepoints: no experience group without any SH, transient group with SH at one timepoint, persistent group with SH at two or three timepoints. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to compare the effect of SH experience on potential candidates of risk factors among three groups.

We applied a multiple imputation method to handle missing values under the assumption of missing at random. In the imputation models, we included all variables used in the analyses and did not use additional auxiliary variables. By fully conditional specification, 100 imputed datasets were generated, and the analyses were conducted for these datasets. In this study all risk factor variables other than sex had missing values and were imputed. The outcome variables were included in the imputation model but had no missing values and were therefore not imputed. Sex was treated as binary variable, and other factors were treated as continuous variable and standardized into Z score.

Univariable multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine risk factors for transient or persistent SH. Subsequently, multivariable multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the effects of these factors in fully adjusted models. In multivariable regression analysis, we considered multicollinearity was small enough when the variance inflation factors (VIF) of the variables were less than 10.

As a sensitivity analysis, we conducted multivariable ordinal logistic regression analysis, in which we treated SH experiences as an ordinal outcome variable. We further conducted multivariable multinomial logistic regression analysis excluding individuals who experienced SH only at T1. We also conducted t-test and chi-square test to assess the baseline difference between the 1501 adolescents included in this analysis and 1506 adolescents who were excluded from the analysis.

As a post hoc analysis, we conducted additional multivariable multinomial logistic regression analyses with a reduced set of predictors. Given that the number of participants in the persistent SH group (n = 53) was relatively small in relation to the number of predictors, we considered the events-per-variable ratio. Specifically, variables with relatively high VIF, namely reassurance with others and secure base with others, were excluded from the model.

The significance level was set at 0.05. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, as the primary analysis addressing the main study objective was a single multivariable multinomial logistic regression model. IBM®ฎ SPSS®ฎ Statistics version 28.0 (IBM®ฎ Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

The prevalence of SH was 11.5% at T1, 4.5% at T2 and 4.9% at T3. While there was no sex difference in the prevalence of SH at T1 (female: 11.0% vs. male: 11.8%, χ²=0.23, p=.631), the prevalence was higher in female at T2 (6.5% vs. 2.6%, χ²=13.49, p<.001) and T3 (7.6% vs. 2.3%, χ²=22.58, p<.001). Discussion

This study examined the risk factors for transient or persistent SH in early-to-middle adolescence in the general population. While maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and female sex were the risk factors for persistent SH, companionship with mother was protective against persistent SH. Depressive symptoms and PEs were the risk factors both for transient and persistent SH.

This study showed maternal depressive/anxious symptoms were the risk factors for persistent SH in early-to-middle adolescence. This is in line with previous studies that revealed maternal depression predicted offspring SH in adolescence [12, 13]. This was also the first study which showed that companionship with mother was protective against SH persistence. These findings suggest the significant role of mothers in adolescent mental health. One possible explanation would be that intervention into adolescents who self-harm is likely to be initiated by the action of the mothers. Maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and poor companionship with mother could reduce the likelihood of detection of adolescent SH by mothers or help-seeking from adolescents to mothers. Another explanation could be the reduction of loneliness is effective for SH cessation. Chronic loneliness in early-to-mid adolescence was shown to predict SH [57]. A previous study showed that being alone when engaging in SH predicted SH repetition [21]. As well as poor companionship with mothers, maternal depressive/anxious symptoms would also disturb an appropriate relationship between adolescents and mothers, and increase adolescent loneliness. In Japan, women bear a greater burden of child care than men [58]. The significance in maternal factors to predict SH persistence could be exaggerated in Japanese social situations.

This study showed females have higher risk of persistent SH in early-to-middle adolescence, while they did not have higher risk of transient SH. This was consistent with the previous studies which showed females had higher risk of SH repetition in the general adolescent population [49, 59]. While the prevalence of SH is higher in female in the general adolescent population [1, 2], a recent study revealed the prevalence of suicidality such as suicidal thoughts and behaviors was higher in male at the age of 9 to 10, beginning of adolescence [60]. Higher risk of SH persistence in females may underlie the higher prevalence of SH in females in adolescence.

Depressive symptoms and PEs were the risk factors both for transient and persistent SH. Previous studies in the general adolescents showed depression predicted SH repetition [20, 26, 49], and the result of this study is consistent with those studies. Recent studies have shown PEs predict SH in the general adolescent population [50, 51]. A previous study from the TTC revealed a complex bi-directional pattern of relationships between auditory hallucinations and SH independently of depressive symptoms [52]. We firstly revealed PEs predicted SH persistence, and this result is in line with the previous studies. Specifically, bi-directional influence between PEs and SH may form a vicious circle, which would lead to SH persistence. These factors reflect current mental illness, and could be the risk factors for transient SH as well as persistent SH.

Some school and peer related factors were significantly associated with SH experience but were not significantly related to transient or persistent SH in the fully adjusted regression model. For example, dissatisfaction with school and distrust in the homeroom teacher were associated with SH experience, which is in line with a previous study [14]. In addition, the relationships with father were also significantly associated with SH experience, while did not predict transient or persistent SH in the fully adjusted regression model. It should also be noted that this study did not examine paternal depressive/anxious symptoms or relationships with grandparents and siblings. It is plausible that the risk of persistent SH may differ depending on the school environment and family structure. Factors that did not remain significant in the fully adjusted model, as well as those that were not included in the analyses, warrant further investigation.

As a clinical implication, care for maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and support for mother-child relationship would be important to prevent SH persistence, adding to treatment of adolescent’s depressive symptoms or PEs. Clinicians or carer of adolescents can involve parents in a circle of care for adolescent SH by better supporting parents [61–64]. Since adolescents’ SH itself may cause distress for mothers, attention to maternal depressive/anxious symptoms in families of adolescents who engage in self-harm should be widely considered. Although it has been unclear how parents should support adolescents with SH, we showed it protective against SH persistence that adolescents feel they spend much time and engage in various activities with mothers. It should be noted that companionship with mother was a subjective measure of adolescents, thus the amount of time with mother may not directly associate with subjective companionship with mother.

The strengths of this study include the design of the TTC study. First, this study is a three time-point longitudinal study. Three time-point study enabled us to consider the detailed trajectory of SH in early-to-middle adolescence. Second, this study was initiated at as young as 12 years old. Since the mean age of SH initiation is shown 12.8 years old [6], and we measured potential candidates of risk factors at age 12, the result of this study can be useful for early intervention against adolescent SH. Third, this study used a sample of adolescents from the general population. Since the majority of adolescents with SH do not receive medical treatment [1], this study will contribute to the development of a generalizable prevention strategy.

There are several limitations in this study. First, we used the data of the participants who answered SH measurements for three timepoints. Missing data could introduce bias, as individuals who experience SH hesitate to respond or show higher attrition rates. Second, since we used one generic question to assess SH at T1, the possibility of overreporting at T1 due to misunderstanding SH by adolescents should also be considered. While the sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the risk factors identified for persistent SH, the risk estimates for transient SH may have been affected by potential overreporting of SH at T1. Third, we used several single-item questions to assess school environment. Although these were straightforward questions and used with reference to previous studies, reliability and validity were not confirmed and caution should be paid in interpretation. Fourth, measuring predictors at the same timepoint as the first outcome (T1) may introduce temporal ambiguity. Although the sensitivity analysis excluding participants who reported SH only at T1 showed similar risk patterns for persistent SH, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be excluded.

Fifth, because most of the participants were Japanese adolescents living in Tokyo, careful consideration is necessary to generalize these study findings to other ethnic groups and countries. Especially, the risk factors related to family and school environment may be influenced by culture. Since normative family structure and division of roles within the household vary between cultures, the role of the father and other family members should also be considered in future studies.

Conclusions

While maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and being female were the risk factors of persistent SH, companionship with mother was protective against persistent SH. Depressive symptoms and PEs were the risk factors both for transient and persistent SH in early-to-middle adolescence in general population. To prevent SH persistence in adolescence, care for maternal depressive/anxious symptoms and support for mother-child relationships would be required in addition to care for adolescents’ depressive symptoms and PEs.
