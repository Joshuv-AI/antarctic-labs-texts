Measuring dissimilarity between convex cones by means of max-min angles Thanks: Submitted to the editors September 2, 2025.
Welington de Oliveira, Valentina Sessa, David Sossa

Abstract
This work introduces a novel dissimilarity measure between two convex cones, based on the max-min angle between them. We demonstrate that this measure is closely related to the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance, a well-established metric for comparing compact sets. Furthermore, we examine cone configurations where the measure admits simplified or analytic forms. For the specific case of polyhedral cones, a nonconvex cutting-plane method is deployed to compute, at least approximately, the measure between them. Our approach builds on a tailored version of Kelley’s cutting-plane algorithm, which involves solving a challenging master program per iteration. When this master program is solved locally, our method yields an angle that satisfies certain necessary optimality conditions of the underlying nonconvex optimization problem yielding the dissimilarity measure between the cones. As an application of the proposed mathematical and algorithmic framework, we address the image-set classification task under limited data conditions, a task that falls within the scope of the Few-Shot Learning paradigm. In this context, image sets belonging to the same class are modeled as polyhedral cones, and our dissimilarity measure proves useful for understanding whether two image sets belong to the same class.



keywords

convex cone, max-min angle, projection, Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance, cutting-plane, image-set classification

Introduction

Measuring dissimilarity between two mathematical objects is a fundamental problem not only for developing mathematical theory but also for practical applications. For instance, in data classification problems, one should have a mechanism to measure how alike two data objects are. In particular, in image-set classification problems, it is common to model a data image-set as an element of a Grassmann manifold (collection of all linear subspaces of fixed dimension). Thus, the problem of measuring the dissimilarity between two data image-sets can be approached by computing the distance between two linear subspaces. There are many ways of defining a distance on a Grassmann manifold, most of them are based on the principal angles between subspaces, like the Grassmann distance, projection distance, spectral distance, etc., see
[
29
]
. Principal angles are fundamental geometric tools for comparing two subspaces. Moreover, they can be efficiently computed since it reduces to the computation of the singular values of some matrix associated with the subspaces
[
20
]
.

As pointed out in
[
18
]
, a fruitful extension of the concept of linear subspace is that of convex cone. Convex cones also play an ubiquitous role in many branches of applied mathematics. For instance, in
[
27
]
, it has been reported that some classes of data image-sets can be more accurately modeled by convex cones rather than linear subspaces. Thus, measuring the dissimilarity between convex cones is a relevant issue in classification tasks, for instance. For this purpose, many distances between convex cones are known in the literature. We refer to the work of Iusem and Seeger
[
18
]
for a survey on this topic.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
denote the set of closed convex cones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
S_{n}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|u\|=1\}
be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As happens with distances between linear subspaces, it is natural to ask whether there are distances on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
based on a concept of angles between cones. Iusem and Seeger
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
have initiated the study of maximal angle on a cone. Later, Seeger and Sossa
[
23
,
24
]
have extended this study for two cones as follows: Given
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, the maximal angle between
P
and
Q
corresponds to the optimal value of the problem

\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n},\,v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\langle u,v\rangle.

(1)

Furthermore, the stationary values of the above problem give us the concept of critical angles between
P
and
Q
, which is an extension of that of principal angles between subspaces. Indeed, when
P
and
Q
are linear subspaces, both concepts coincide. However, it is not clear to us how to define a distance from critical angles since there are some computing limitations for finding all critical angles, and because problem (
1
) is NP-hard, see
[
21
,
4
]
. Even more, only the maximal angle will not be enough, since equal cones can have a nonzero maximal angle. If we consider the minimization counterpart of (
1
), we get the concept of minimal angle between cones, but different cones with nonempty intersection have a minimal angle equal to zero.

To overcome the limitations of the maximal and minimal angles between cones for defining a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we introduce a new concept of angle: for
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, the max-min angle between
P
and
Q
is defined as

\Theta(P,Q):=\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle.

(2)

We shall see that
\Theta(P,Q)
and
\Theta(Q,P)
capture distinct dissimilarity information between
P
and
Q
. Moreover, if
\Theta(P,Q)=\Theta(Q,P)=0
then
P=Q
. Thus, we shall prove that the following is a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
:

{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)=2\left\|\left(\sin\left[\frac{\Theta(P,Q)}{2}\right],\,\sin\left[\frac{\Theta(Q,P)}{2}\right]\right)\right\|_{r},

where
\|\cdot\|_{r}
is the
\ell^{r}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for
1\leq r\leq\infty
. Our construction of such a distance is inspired by the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance between compact sets.

Given
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, for a practical computation of the max-min angle
\Theta(P,Q)
it is convenient to compute
\cos[\Theta(P,Q)]
which is the optimal value of the min-max problem:

\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle.

(3)

Indeed, the above problem reduces to

\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}F_{Q}(u)\quad\mbox{where}\quad F_{Q}(u):=\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle

is convex since it is the support function of
Q\cap S_{n}
. We show in Proposition
6
below that
F_{Q}(u)
is related to the projection of
u
onto the intersection of a cone and a sphere, whose computation can be performed by means of the tools developed by Bauschke, Bui and Wang
[
5
]
.
We shall see that the minimum of
F_{Q}
over
P\cap S_{n}
can be explicitly computed for some particular instances of cones like linear subspaces and revolution cones. For the more involving case of polyhedral cones, we tackle the problem with a different approach. A specialized nonconvex version of the well-known cutting-plane method
[
1
]
is implemented to globally solve the min-max problem (
3
).
Our algorithmic framework
involves solving a challenging
master program per iteration: a nonconvex quadratically constrained optimization problem with a linear objective. When the master program is solved locally to generate iterates (as is typically required in the large-scale problems arising from image-set classification) our method produces an angle that satisfies necessary optimality conditions of the underlying problem (
3
).

To report the performance of our proposed mathematical and algorithmic framework, we address the
image-set classification task under limited data conditions. In this context, image sets belonging to the same class are modeled as
polyhedral cones. Indeed, images belonging to the same class, referred to as class
i
, can be seen as generators of a (polyhedral) cone
Q_{i}
. Different classes produce distinct cones that are constructed in a training phase. When presented with a new set of images that generates a cone
P
, the goal in the test phase is to classify this new set into one of the existing classes.
One effective method for performing this classification is by comparing the generated cones. To accomplish this, we propose using the distance
{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)
. We classify the new set of images into class
i^{*}
, which corresponds to the smallest distance. This can be expressed formally as
i^{*}\in\arg\min_{i}\,{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q_{i})
.
Our numerical results show that the proposed dissimilarity measure allows for accurate classification of the image set in the ETH-80 dataset
[
7
]
. We achieved an average
90\%
accuracy in classification, even when only a few images (limited data) were used to build the reference cones (in the training phase).

We note that image-set classification under limited data conditions falls within the scope of
Few-Shot Learning
(FSL), a framework designed to mimic the human ability to learn from few examples
[
2
]
. Unlike traditional supervised learning, which relies on large labeled datasets and extensive training, FSL is particularly valuable in domains where data is scarce, costly to label, or simply impractical in situations such as rare diseases, unique handwriting styles, or newly discovered species. In this context, providing a robust mathematical and algorithmic framework for comparing cones can significantly advance FSL applications.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the proposed dissimilarity measure between convex cones based on the concept of the max-min angle, and establishes its connection to the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance. Section
3
addresses the problem of computing this measure, focusing on the underlying optimization problems and providing a thorough analysis of their mathematical properties and optimality conditions. Special cases and numerical strategies for computing the dissimilarity are discussed in Section
4
. Section
5
is dedicated to the case of polyhedral cones, where we present an algorithmic framework based on a cutting-plane method. Section
6
reports numerical experiments using two variants of the proposed algorithm, applied to randomly generated polyhedral cones and an image-set classification task. Finally, Section
7
concludes the work with remarks and directions for future research.

\mathtt{Proj}_{X}(u):=\arg\min_{x\in X}\textstyle{\|}x-u\textstyle{\|}
,

\mathtt{d}^{2}_{X}(u):=\min_{x\in X}\textstyle{\|}x-u\textstyle{\|}^{2}
. Furthermore,
\mathtt{cl\,conv}\,X

X
, and
\mathtt{span}\,X
denotes the linear span of

. When
X

\mathtt{dim}\,X
denotes its dimension. The unit sphere and the unit ball in

are denoted, respectively, by
S_{n}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|u\|=1\}

.

2
Distance between convex cones

We start this section by proving some elementary properties of
\Theta(P,Q)
defined by problem (
2
).

2.1
Overview of the max-min angle between two cones

The following result establishes the well-definiteness of the max-min angle.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. The following statements are satisfied:

(a)

\Theta(P,Q)
is well-defined. That is, the max-min problem (
2
) has a solution.

(b)

\Theta(P,Q)
is invariant under orthogonal linear transformations. That is, for every
n
-by-
n
orthogonal matrix
V
,
\Theta(V(P),V(Q))=\Theta(P,Q)
.

Proof.

(a). Since the function
\arccos(\cdot)
is decreasing, the cosine of the optimal value of problem (
2
) coincides with the optimal value of the following min-max problem:

\cos[\Theta(P,Q)]=\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle.

The above problem has at least one solution since
P\cap S_{n}
and
Q\cap S_{n}
are compact sets, and
v\mapsto\langle u,v\rangle
and
u\mapsto\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle
are continuous (convex) functions.

(b). The equality
\Theta(V(P),V(Q))=\Theta(P,Q)
is true because

\langle Vx,Vy\rangle=\langle x,V^{\top}Vy\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\max_{u\in V(P)\cap S_{n}}\min_{v\in V(Q)\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{x\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{y\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle Vx,Vy\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{x\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{y\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle x,y\rangle.

It concludes the proof.

In order to show the intuition behind the fact that
\Theta(P,Q)
captures information about the dissimilarity between
P
and
Q
, we exemplify the max-min problem (
2
) with bi-dimensional examples. Fig.
1
(a) depicts two cones
P
(in red) and
Q
(in green).
For any given
u\in P\cap S_{n}
, the inner minimization problem in (
2
) seeks to find a vector
v_{u}\in Q\cap S_{n}
that minimizes the angle with
u
. For the example of Fig.
1
(a), such a
v_{u}
always lies in the superior boundary of
Q
regardless of the given
u\in P\cap S_{n}
. The outer maximization problem in (
2
) then seeks to find
\bar{u}\in P\cap S_{n}
yielding the maximum of minimal angles
\arccos\,\langle u,v_{u}\rangle
. Such an
\bar{u}
lies in the superior boundary of
P
. The max-min angle
\Theta(P,Q)=\arccos\,\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle
, with
\bar{v}=v_{\bar{u}}
, is thus the optimal value of (
2
).
A similar analysis by switching the roles of
P
and
Q
gives the angle
\Theta(Q,P)
marked in blue in Fig.
1
(a). Clearly, such angles are distinct. Fig.
1
(b) shows an example where
\Theta(P,Q)=0
whereas
\Theta(Q,P)>0
. Hence, looking only at a single max-min angle
\Theta(P,Q)
is not enough to assert that
P
and
Q
in Fig.
1
(b) are different cones. It is now intuitive that
\Theta(P,Q)=\Theta(Q,P)=0
implies that
P
and
Q
are equal, and vice-versa. In Theorem
5
, we prove not only that this claim holds but also that these angles induce a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. But before that, let us briefly comment on the inversion of the maximization and minimization roles in problem (
2
).

Figure 1:
Bi-dimensional illustration of the max-min problem (
2
).

Let us consider the min-max counterpart of problem (
2
). That is,

\widehat{\Theta}(P,Q):=\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle.

(4)

We point out that there is no loss of generality in focusing attention just on the max-min problem (
2
). Indeed, the next proposition shows that
\Theta(P,Q)
and
\widehat{\Theta}(P,-Q)
are supplementary angles.

Proposition 2
.

For any
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
,

\Theta(P,Q)+\widehat{\Theta}(P,-Q)=\pi.

Proof.

Observe that
\arccos(-\langle u,v\rangle)=\pi-\arccos\langle u,v\rangle
. Then,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Theta}(P,-Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,(-\langle u,v\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\pi-\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle=\pi-\Theta(P,Q),

which is equivalent to
\Theta(P,Q)+\widehat{\Theta}(P,-Q)=\pi.

For min-max problems, an important result is the celebrated minimax theorem
[
26
]
, allowing us to invert, under convexity and concavity assumptions, the order of maximization and minimization in the underlying problem. As problem (
2
) does not satisfy such assumptions, inverting the order of the maximization and minimization gives an upper bound. Indeed, the following inequality holds in generality:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Theta(P,Q)&=&\displaystyle\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\arccos\,\langle u,v\rangle=\widehat{\Theta}(Q,P).\end{array}

(5)

The angle computed by the right-hand side problem above is illustrated by
\alpha
in Fig.
1
(c). This is an example where
\Theta(P,Q)
is strictly inferior to the optimal value of the right-hand side problem above. Below, we elaborate on this example with more precision.

Example 1
.

Let
P
and
Q
be the closed convex cones in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
illustrated in Fig.
1
(c). Observe that these cones are revolution cones (see Section
4.2
). Indeed,
P
is defined by fixing the revolution (central) axis
b_{1}\in S_{2}
and the half-aperture angle
\phi_{1}
; that is,
P={\rm Rev}(\phi_{1},b_{1})
. Analogously,
Q={\rm Rev}(\phi_{2},b_{2})
. The precise elements of these cones are

\phi_{1}=\pi/12,\;b_{1}=(\cos(\pi/3),\sin(\pi/3)),\quad\phi_{2}=\pi/6,\;b_{2}=(\cos(\pi/4),\sin(\pi/4)).

Observe that
\arccos\,\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle=\pi/12
. On the other hand,
-P={\rm Rev}(\phi_{1},-b_{1})
and
\arccos\,\langle-b_{1},b_{2}\rangle=(11/12)\pi
. Thus, by using Theorem
13
and Proposition
2
we get

\Theta(P,Q)=0,\quad\widehat{\Theta}(Q,P)=\pi-\Theta(Q,-P)=\frac{\pi}{12}.

Thus, the inequality in (
5
) holds strictly.

2.2
Distances between compact sets and between convex cones

Let
\mathcal{K}_{n}
denote the set of nonempty compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The construction of our metrics on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
relies on metrics on
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. Recall that a classic distance between
C
and
D
in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance, which is defined as

{\rm Hauss}(C,D):=\max\left\{\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{D}(x),\,\max_{x\in D}\mathtt{d}_{C}(x)\right\},

(6)

where
\mathtt{d}_{C}(x):=\min_{y\in C}\|x-y\|
is the distance of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
C
, see
[
22
, Chapter 4]
. Observe that the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance is composed of the term

\Lambda(C,D):=\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{D}(x).

It is known that
\Lambda
is a quasi-metric on
\mathcal{K}_{n}
, see
[
8
,
25
]
. This is explained in the next known proposition. We give the proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3
.

Let
C,D,E\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
. The map
\Lambda:\mathcal{K}_{n}\times\mathcal{K}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies the following conditions

(a)

\Lambda(C,C)=0
;

(b)

if
\Lambda(C,D)=\Lambda(D,C)=0
, then
C=D
;

(c)

\Lambda(C,D)\leq\Lambda(C,E)+\Lambda(E,D)
.

Proof.

Item (a).
\Lambda(C,C)=0
because
\mathtt{d}_{C}(x)=0
for all
x\in C
.

Item (b). Let us prove the contrapositive statement. Suppose that
C\neq D
, then
C\not\subset D
or
D\not\subset C
. If
C\not\subset D
, then there is
\bar{x}\in C
such that
\bar{x}\notin D
. Therefore,
\mathtt{d}_{D}(\bar{x})>0
, which implies that
\Lambda(C,D)\geq\mathtt{d}_{D}(\bar{x})>0
. Analogously, if
D\not\subset C
then
\Lambda(D,C)>0
.

Item (c). Let
x\in C
, and let
k(x)\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{k\in E}\|x-k\|
. Then, from the triangle inequality of the Euclidean norm, we have that for all
y\in D
,

\|x-y\|\leq\|x-k(x)\|+\|k(x)-y\|=\min_{k\in E}\|x-k\|+\|k(x)-y\|=\mathtt{d}_{E}(x)+\|k(x)-y\|.

Then, the above inequality is preserved if we take the minimum on
D
:

\mathtt{d}_{D}(x)=\min_{y\in D}\|x-y\|\leq\mathtt{d}_{E}(x)+\min_{y\in D}\|k(x)-y\|=\mathtt{d}_{E}(x)+\mathtt{d}_{D}(k(x)).

Since
x\in C
is arbitrary, we have that

\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{D}(x)\leq\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{E}(x)+\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{D}(k(x)).

(7)

Furthermore, it is clear that
\{k(x):x\in C\}\subseteq E
. Then,
\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{D}(k(x))\leq\max_{k\in E}\mathtt{d}_{D}(k)
.
By transitivity of this inequality with (
7
), we conclude:

\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{D}(x)\leq\max_{x\in C}\mathtt{d}_{E}(x)+\max_{k\in E}\mathtt{d}_{D}(k).

Consequently, the desired triangle inequality holds.

There are other metrics that can be constructed in
\mathcal{K}_{n}
by using
\Lambda
. For instance, the Pompeiu-Eggleston distance
[
9
, Chapter 9]
is defined as

{\rm Egg}(C,D):=\Lambda(C,D)+\Lambda(D,C).

(8)

We notice that the metrics (
6
) and (
8
) are obtained by taking the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm and
\ell^{1}
-norm to the vector
(\Lambda(C,D),\,\Lambda(D,C))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The following proposition shows that we can define a distance on
\mathcal{K}_{n}
by considering any
\ell^{r}
-norm, for
1\leq r\leq\infty
, on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proposition 4
.

For
1\leq r\leq\infty
, let
{\rm dis}_{r}:\mathcal{K}_{n}\times\mathcal{K}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
defined by

{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D):=\|(\Lambda(C,D),\,\Lambda(D,C))\|_{r}.

(9)

Then,
{\rm dis}_{r}
is a distance on
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. That is, for all
C,D,E\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
, the following axioms are satisfied:

(a)

{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)\geq 0
;

(b)

{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)={\rm dis}_{r}(D,C)
;

(c)

{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)=0
if and only if
C=D
;

(d)

{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)\leq{\rm dis}_{r}(C,E)+{\rm dis}_{r}(E,D).

Proof.

Items (a) and (b) follow directly from the definition (
9
).

Let us prove (c). If
{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)=0
then
\Lambda(C,D)=0
and
\Lambda(D,C)=0
. Then, from Proposition
3
(b) we get
C=D
. Conversely, if
C=D
then
\Lambda(C,D)=\Lambda(D,C)=0
because of Proposition
3
(a). It follows that
{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)=0
.

Let us prove the triangle inequality (d). Let
C,D,E\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
. We know from Proposition
3
(c) that
\Lambda
satisfies the triangle inequality. Then,

\Lambda(C,D)\leq\Lambda(C,E)+\Lambda(E,D)\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda(D,C)\leq\Lambda(D,E)+\Lambda(E,C).

(10)

Hence,

\displaystyle{\rm dis}_{r}(C,E)+{\rm dis}_{r}(E,D)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|(\Lambda(C,E),\,\Lambda(E,C))\|_{r}+\|(\Lambda(E,D),\,\Lambda(D,E))\|_{r}

(11)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|(\Lambda(C,E)+\Lambda(E,D),\,\Lambda(E,C)+\Lambda(D,E))\|_{r},

where the inequality is because of the triangular inequality of
\|\cdot\|_{r}
. Observe that
\|\cdot\|_{r}
is isotonic in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. It means, if
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
are such that
x_{1}\leq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\leq y_{2}
, then
\|x\|_{r}\leq\|y\|_{r}
. Thus, from (
10
) we deduce

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\rm dis}_{r}(C,D)&=&\|(\Lambda(C,D),\,\Lambda(D,C))\|_{r}\\
&\leq&\|(\Lambda(C,E)+\Lambda(E,D),\,\Lambda(D,E)+\Lambda(E,C))\|_{r}.\end{array}

(12)

Therefore, by combining (
11
) and (
12
) we get the desired triangle inequality.

Now, we explain how to construct a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
(the set of closed convex cones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). Since the elements in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
are not bounded, we can not use the distance
{\rm dis}_{r}
directly because it is for compact sets. However, we can truncate the elements in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
by intersecting them with the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we can apply
{\rm dis}_{r}
on these truncated sets and it will induce a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. A similar procedure was also applied in
[
18
]
and
[
23
]
. This is specified in the next theorem. We also show the connection of such a distance with the max-min angles
\Theta(P,Q)
and
\Theta(Q,P)
.

Theorem 5
.

For
1\leq r\leq\infty
, let
{\rm Dis}_{r}:\mathcal{C}_{n}\times\mathcal{C}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be defined as

{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q):={\rm dis}_{r}(P\cap S_{n},Q\cap S_{n}).

(13)

Then,
{\rm Dis}_{r}
is a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Furthermore, it admits the formula

{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)=2\left\|\left(\sin\left[\frac{\Theta(P,Q)}{2}\right],\,\sin\left[\frac{\Theta(Q,P)}{2}\right]\right)\right\|_{r}.

(14)

Proof.

That
{\rm Dis}_{r}
is a distance on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
follows directly from Proposition
4
. Let us prove the formula (
14
). By the definitions of
\Lambda(P\cap S_{n},Q\cap S_{n})
and
\Theta(P,Q)
, and by using the identity
1-\cos\alpha=2\sin^{2}(\alpha/2)
we get,

\displaystyle\Lambda(P\cap S_{n},Q\cap S_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\|u-v\|=\left(\max_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\|u-v\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(1-\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\left(1-\cos[\Theta(P,Q)]\right)^{1/2}=2\sin[\Theta(P,Q)/2].

Thus, the desired formula follows from
(
13
)
and (
9
).

3
On computing the dissimilarity distance

In the previous section, we have seen that the max-min problem (
2
) is equivalent, in terms of solution, to the following min-max problem:

\cos\left[\Theta(P,Q)\right]=\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle.

(15)

For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we set

F_{Q}(u):=\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle,

(16)

which corresponds to the support function of
Q\cap S_{n}
. Hence,
problem (
15
) can be formulated as minimizing the support function of
Q\cap S_{n}
over
P\cap S_{n}
:

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q):=\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}F_{Q}(u).

(17)

Thus, our original problem (
2
) reduces to solve problem (
17
). Indeed, we have

\Theta(P,Q)=\arccos\left(\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)\right).

From now on, we will focus on solving problem (
17
). We start by listing some properties of the support function
F_{Q}
that will be used later.

Proposition 6
.

Let
Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, and let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The following statements hold:

(a)

The function
F_{Q}
given in (
16
) is convex and its domain is the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(b)

\displaystyle F_{Q}(u)=\frac{1+\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}^{2}-\mathtt{d}_{Q\cap S_{n}}^{2}(u)}{2}
;

(c)

\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)=\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle
.

(d)

\partial F_{Q}(u)=\mathtt{cl\,conv}\,\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
;

(e)

F_{Q}(u)=\langle u,v^{\prime}\rangle
for all
v^{\prime}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
.

(f)

If
u\notin Q^{\circ}
, then
\partial F_{Q}(u)=\{\mathtt{Proj}_{Q}(u)/\|\mathtt{Proj}_{Q}(u)\|\}
.

Proof.

Convexity of
F_{Q}
is trivial.
As
Q\cap S_{n}
is a compact set,
F_{Q}
is real-valued. This proves item (a).
Observe that for all
v\in Q\cap S_{n}
, we have that
\textstyle{\|}v-u\textstyle{\|}^{2}=1+\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}^{2}-2\langle u,v\rangle
. Thus,
\mathtt{d}_{Q\cap S_{n}}^{2}(u)=\min_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\textstyle{\|}v-u\textstyle{\|}^{2}=1+\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}^{2}-2\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle=1+\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}^{2}-2F_{Q}(u)
, giving item (b).
This last development also shows that
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)=\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle u,v\rangle
, proving items (c) and (e).
Item (d) follows, for instance, from Proposition 2.13 of
[
3
]
with the observation that
F_{Q}
is real-valued (see also
[
13
, Eq. 5]
). Item (f) is proved in
[
5
, Thm 8.1]
.

As a consequence of item (a) of Proposition
6
, we deduce that
F_{Q}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. From this fact and the compactness of
P\cap S_{n}
, the extreme value theorem ensures that (
17
) is always solvable.

We now provide a necessary optimality condition for a point
\bar{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to solve problem (
17
). Observe that (
17
) can be rewritten as

\min_{u}\;F_{Q}(u)+i_{P}(u)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\frac{1}{2}\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}^{2}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}=0,

where
i_{P}
denotes the indicator function of
P
.
We say that
\bar{u}
is a stationary point to the above problem (and thus to (
17
)) if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that the following (generalized KKT) system is satisfied:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}0&\in\partial(F_{Q}+i_{P})(\bar{u})-\lambda\bar{u}\\
\bar{u}&\in P,\;\;\textstyle{\|}\bar{u}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}\right.

(18)

Proposition 7
.

For any stationary point
\bar{u}
to problem (
17
) there exists
\bar{v}\in Q
such that the following system is satisfied:

\begin{array}[]{l}P\ni\bar{u}\perp\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\in P^{\ast},\\
\textstyle{\|}\bar{u}\textstyle{\|}=1,\;\bar{v}\in\mathtt{cl\,conv\,}\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle\bar{u},v\rangle.\end{array}

(19)

Furthermore, if
\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle\bar{u},v\rangle
is a singleton, then the above system is equivalent to

\begin{array}[]{l}P\ni\bar{u}\perp\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\in P^{\ast},\\
Q\ni\bar{v}\perp\bar{u}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{v}\in Q^{\circ},\\
\textstyle{\|}\bar{u}\textstyle{\|}=\textstyle{\|}\bar{v}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}

(20)

This is the case, for instance, if
\bar{u}\not\in Q^{\circ}
.

Proof.

We know that for the stationary point
\bar{u}
to (
17
) there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying (
18
). Observe that

\partial(F_{Q}+i_{P})(\bar{u})=\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})+\partial i_{P}(\bar{u})=\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})+N_{P}(\bar{u}),

due to the fact that
F_{Q}
is convex and real-valued and
P\neq\emptyset
is a closed convex set; see for instance
[
3
, Corollaries 2.6 and 2.7]
. Recall that
N_{P}(\bar{u})
denotes the normal cone to
P
at
\bar{u}
, and since
P
is a closed convex cone,
N_{P}(\bar{u})=\{w\in P^{\circ}:w\perp\bar{u}\}
.
The first inclusion above ensures the existence of
\bar{v}\in\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})
and
w\in N_{P}(\bar{u})
such that

0=\bar{v}+w-\lambda\bar{u}
. By multiplying this inequality by
\bar{u}
and recalling that
\langle w,\bar{u}\rangle=0
for all
w\in N_{P}(\bar{u})
, we get that
\lambda=\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle
. Thus,
-w=\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\in-N_{P}(\bar{u})\subset-P^{\circ}=P^{*}
, and we can rewrite the above system as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}P&\ni\bar{u}\perp\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\in P^{*}\\
\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})&\ni\bar{v},\;\;\textstyle{\|}\bar{u}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}\right.

Proposition
6
items (c) and (d) conclude (
19
).

Furthermore, if
\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle\bar{u},v\rangle
is a singleton, then
\bar{v}
in the stated system is the only solution to
\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle\bar{u},v\rangle
. Thus,
\bar{v}
must satisfy the following KKT system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}0&\in-\bar{u}+N_{Q}(\bar{u})-\gamma\bar{v}\\
\bar{v}&\in Q,\;\;\textstyle{\|}\bar{v}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}\right.

By proceeding as before, we get
\gamma=-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle
and thus
-\bar{u}+\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{v}\in-N_{Q}(\bar{u})\subset-Q^{\circ}
, i.e.,
\bar{u}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{v}\in Q^{\circ}
. By replacing the inclusion
\bar{v}\in\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})
above with these latter identities we get the stated system.
Proposition
6
items (f), (d) and (c) ensure that if
\bar{u}\not\in Q^{\circ}
, then
\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle\bar{u},v\rangle
is a singleton.

Remark 8
.

In general, a solution of (
17
) may not lie on the boundary of
P
. Indeed, let
Q:=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|v_{1}|\leq v_{2}\}
(the Lorentz cone in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) and let
P=-Q
. It is not difficult to see that
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\cos(3\pi/4)
, and that
\bar{u}=(0,-1)
is a solution of (
17
) since
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{2}}(\bar{u})=\{(1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2}),\;(-1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2})\}
, see (
27
). Observe that
\bar{u}
is not in the boundary of
P
. Furthermore, the vector
\bar{v}
associated with
\bar{u}
in the optimality conditions described in Proposition
7
may fail to have unit norm. Indeed, for the above example,
\bar{u}=(0,-1)
satisfies the optimality conditions (
19
) with
\bar{v}=(0,1/\sqrt{2})\in\mathtt{cl\,conv\,}\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{2}}(\bar{u})
. Observe that
\|\bar{v}\|=1/\sqrt{2}.

It is clear that
-1\leq\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)\leq 1
for every
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Below, we describe when the extremes are attained. For
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote
\mathbb{R}_{+}(w):=\{\alpha w:\alpha\geq 0\}
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Then,

(a)

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=1
if and only if
P\subseteq Q
.

(b)

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=-1
if and only if
Q=\mathbb{R}_{+}(-\bar{u})
for some
\bar{u}\in P\cap S_{n}
.

Proof.

Item (a). Suppose that
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=1
. Then,
F_{Q}(u)=1
for all
u\in P\cap S_{n}
. Hence, for all
u\in P\cap S_{n}
, there exists
v\in Q\cap S_{n}
such that
\langle u,v\rangle=1
. This last means that
u=v
, so
u\in Q
. Therefore,
P\subseteq Q
. Conversely, if
P\subseteq Q
, then for
u\in P\cap S_{n}
we can take
v=u\in Q\cap S_{n}
which implies that
\langle u,v\rangle=1
. Thus,
F_{Q}(u)=1
for all
u\in P\cap S_{n}
which means
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=1
.

Item (b). Suppose that
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=-1
. Then, there exists
\bar{u}\in P\cap S_{n}
such that
F_{Q}(\bar{u})=-1
. Thus, from the definition of
F_{Q}
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get
-1\leq\langle\bar{u},v\rangle\leq-1
for all
v\in Q\cap S_{n}
. It means that
v=-\bar{u}
for all
v\in Q\cap S_{n}
. Therefore,
Q=\mathbb{R}_{+}(-\bar{u})
. Conversely, suppose that
Q=\mathbb{R}_{+}(-\bar{u})
for some
\bar{u}\in P\cap S_{n}
. Then,
F_{Q}(u)=-\langle u,\bar{u}\rangle
for all
u\in P\cap S_{n}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=-1
.

Next, we state sufficient conditions to ensure that any stationary point of (
17
) is on the boundary of
P
relative to some subspace.

Proposition 10
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
be such that
|\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)|\neq 1
. Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the smallest linear subspace containing both
P
and
Q
. Let
\bar{u}
be a solution of the problem (
17
) such that
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(\bar{u})
is a singleton. Then,
\bar{u}
is on the boundary of
P
relative to
L
.

Proof.

Reasoning by contradiction, suppose that
\bar{u}
is in the interior of
P
relative to
L
. Then, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\bar{u}+\epsilon S_{n}\cap L\subset P
. From the stationary conditions for
\bar{u}
given in Proposition
7
, there is
\bar{v}\in Q
such that
(\bar{u},\bar{v})
satisfies the system (
19
). Moreover, since we assume that
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(\bar{u})
(
=\arg\max_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\langle\bar{u},v\rangle
) is a singleton,
(\bar{u},\bar{v})
satisfies (
20
), and we deduce that
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle
and
\|\bar{v}\|=1
. We claim that
\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\neq 0
. In fact, if
\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}=0
then,

1=\|\bar{v}\|=|\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle|\|\bar{u}\|=|\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle|,

which is not possible because of
|\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)|\neq 1
. Let
w:=(\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}-\bar{v})/\|\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}-\bar{v}\|\in S_{n}\cap L
. Then,
\bar{u}+\epsilon w\in P.
From (
20
) we have

0\leq\langle\bar{u}+\epsilon w,\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\rangle=\epsilon\langle w,\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\rangle=-\epsilon\|\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\|\leq 0,

obtaining
\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}=0
which is a contradiction.

4
Special cases

4.1
Dissimilarity between linear subspaces

Let
P
and
Q
be linear subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimensions
p
and
q
, respectively, given by

P=U(\mathbb{R}^{p})\quad\text{and}\quad Q=V(\mathbb{R}^{q}),

(21)

where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
are matrices with their columns forming orthonormal basis for
P
and
Q
, respectively.

The principal angles
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
between
P
and
Q
are defined recursively by

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{k}\,=\max_{u\in P_{k}\cap S_{n}\,,\,v\in Q_{k}\cap S_{n}}\;\langle u,v\rangle,

(22)

where
m:=\min\{{\rm dim}P,{\rm dim}Q\}
and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}P_{1}:=P,\;Q_{1}:=Q,\\[3.41432pt]
P_{k+1}:=\{w\in P_{k}:\langle u_{k},w\rangle=0\},\\[3.41432pt]
Q_{k+1}:=\{w\in Q_{k}:\langle v_{k},w\rangle=0\},\\[3.41432pt]
(u_{k},v_{k})\mbox{ solution to }(\ref{thetak}).\end{array}\right.

Observe that
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\theta_{m}\leq\pi/2
. The largest principal angle between
P
and
Q
will be denoted by
\theta_{\max}(P,Q)
. When
P
and
Q
are of the form (
21
), from
[
6
, Theorem 1]
(see also
[
23
, Theorem 6.2]
) we have that
\theta_{k}
is a principal angle of
(P,Q)
if and only if
\cos\theta_{k}
is a singular value of
V^{\top}U
. Furthermore,
\cos(\theta_{m})\leq\cos(\theta_{m-1})\leq\cdots\leq\cos(\theta_{1})
. Thus,
\cos(\theta_{\max}(P,Q))
is the smallest singular value of
V^{\top}U
.

Computing the separation between linear subspaces is a classic problem in linear algebra. For this purpose, many distances were constructed, and most of them are based on the principal angles between subspaces, see
[
29
]
. In particular,
for a pair of subspaces of the same dimension
P
and
Q
, the spectral distance between them is defined as

d_{\rm spec}(P,Q)=2\sin[\theta_{\max}(P,Q)/2].

(23)

In the next theorem, we describe the distance
{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)
when
P
and
Q
are linear subspaces.

Theorem 11
.

Let
1\leq r\leq\infty
, and let
P=U(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
Q=V(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in (
21
). Then,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)=2\left\|\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\sin\left[\frac{\theta_{\max}(P,Q)}{2}\right]\right)\right\|_{r},\quad\text{if }\mathtt{dim}P\neq\mathtt{dim}Q,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)=2\|(1,1)\|_{r}\sin\left[\frac{\theta_{\max}(P,Q)}{2}\right],\quad\text{if }\mathtt{dim}P=\mathtt{dim}Q,

where
\theta_{\max}(P,Q)\in[0,\pi/2]
is the largest principal angle between
P
and
Q
; that is,
\cos(\theta_{\max}(P,Q))
is the smallest singular value of
V^{\top}U
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
\|u\|=1
. Let us compute the projection of
u
onto
Q\cap S_{n}
:

\displaystyle\bar{v}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\bar{v}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{v\in Q\cap S_{n}}\,\langle u,v\rangle\,\Leftrightarrow\,\bar{v}=V\bar{y}\;\text{ with }\;\bar{y}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{y\in S_{q}}\,\langle y,V^{\top}u\rangle,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\bar{v}=\frac{VV^{\top}u}{\|V^{\top}u\|}\,\text{ if }\,V^{\top}u\neq 0;\,\text{otherwise}\;\bar{v}\in Q\cap S_{q}.

From Proposition
6
(e) we know that
F_{Q}(u)=\langle u,\bar{v}\rangle
with
\bar{v}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
. Therefore, from the above computation, we deduce that

F_{Q}(u)=\begin{cases}\|V^{\top}u\|,&\text{if }u\notin Q^{\perp}\\
0,&\text{if }u\in Q^{\perp}\end{cases}.

Hence, if
P\cap Q^{\perp}=\{0\}
,

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}F_{Q}(u)=\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}\|V^{\top}u\|=\min_{x\in S_{p}}\|V^{\top}Ux\|=\sigma_{\min}(V^{\top}U),

where
\sigma_{\min}(V^{\top}U)
denotes the smallest singular value of
V^{\top}U
. If
P\cap Q^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
, it is clear that
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=0
. Thus,

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{\min}(V^{\top}U),&\text{if }P\cap Q^{\perp}=\{0\},\\
0,&\text{if }P\cap Q^{\perp}\neq\{0\}.\end{cases}

Recall that
\theta_{\max}(P,Q)=\arccos(\sigma_{\min}(V^{\top}U))
. Therefore, we deduce that

\Theta(P,Q)=\arccos(\mathfrak{s}(P,Q))=\begin{cases}\theta_{\max}(P,Q),&\text{if }P\cap Q^{\perp}=\{0\},\\
\frac{\pi}{2},&\text{if }P\cap Q^{\perp}\neq\{0\}.\end{cases}

(24)

By interchanging the roles of
P
and
Q
, and recalling that
\sigma_{\min}(V^{\top}U)=\sigma_{\min}(U^{\top}V)
, we also get that

\Theta(Q,P)=\begin{cases}\theta_{\max}(P,Q),&\text{if }P^{\perp}\cap Q=\{0\},\\
\frac{\pi}{2},&\text{if }P^{\perp}\cap Q\neq\{0\}.\end{cases}

(25)

Recall that
p=\mathtt{dim}P
and
q=\mathtt{dim}Q
, and set
E:=V^{\top}U\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
. Suppose that
p>q
. The Rank-Nullity Theorem says that
\mathtt{dim}({\rm Ker}(E))+\mathtt{rank}(E)=p
. Since
\mathtt{rank}(E)\leq q<p
, we deduce that
\mathtt{dim}({\rm Ker}(E))\geq 1
. It implies that there exists a nonzero
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
V^{\top}Ux=0
. Thus,
Ux\in P\cap Q^{\perp}
, and from (
24
) we deduce that
\Theta(P,Q)=\pi/2
. Now, to compute
\Theta(Q,P)
, suppose that
P^{\perp}\cap Q\neq\{0\}
. Then, there exists a nonzero
y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that
U^{\top}Vy=0
. Hence,
\mathtt{dim}({\rm Ker}(E^{\top}))\geq 1
. The Rank-Nullity Theorem for
E^{\top}
says that
\mathtt{dim}({\rm Ker}(E^{\top}))+\mathtt{rank}(E)=q
. Then,
\mathtt{rank}(E)<q
which means that
E
is not of complete rank. Hence,
\sigma_{\min}(E)=0
, and then
\Theta(Q,P)=\theta_{\max}(P,Q)=\arccos(0)=\pi/2
. Therefore, by combining it with (
25
) we get that
\Theta(Q,P)=\theta_{\max}(P,Q)
when either
P^{\perp}\cap Q\neq\{0\}
or
P^{\perp}\cap Q=\{0\}
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)=2\left\|\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\sin\left[\frac{\theta_{\max}(P,Q)}{2}\right]\right)\right\|_{r}.

(26)

By symmetry, the above result is also valid when
p<q
. Hence, (
26
) holds whenever
p\neq q
.

Suppose that
p=q
. Then, analogous to the above reasoning, the matrix
E=V^{\top}U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is not of complete rank when
P\cap Q^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
. Thus,
\sigma_{\min}(E)=0
, and then
\Theta(P,Q)=\theta_{\max}(P,Q)=\arccos(0)=\pi/2
. By combining it with (
24
) we get that
\Theta(P,Q)=\theta_{\max}(P,Q)
in the both cases of (
24
). Analogously, we also have
\Theta(Q,P)=\theta_{\max}(P,Q)
. Hence, if
p=q
then

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm Dis}_{r}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left\|\left(\sin\left[\frac{\theta_{\max}(P,Q)}{2}\right],\sin\left[\frac{\theta_{\max}(P,Q)}{2}\right]\right)\right\|_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\|(1,1)\|_{r}\sin\left[\frac{\theta_{\max}(P,Q)}{2}\right].

The proof is complete.

Remark 12
.

We observe that
{\rm Dis}_{\infty}(P,Q)=d_{\rm spec}(P,Q)
when
P
and
Q
are linear subspaces of equal dimensions. That is, the above theorem reveals that the spectral distance (
23
) is induced by the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance.

4.2
Dissimilarity between revolution cones

A revolution cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex cone defined as

{\rm Rev}(\phi,b):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\langle b,u\rangle\geq\|u\|\cos\phi\},

where
b\in S_{n}
defines the revolution axis, and
\phi\in[0,\pi/2]
is the half-aperture angle.

Theorem 13
.

Let
P={\rm Rev}(\phi_{1},b_{1})
and
Q={\rm Rev}(\phi_{2},b_{2})
, with
b_{1},b_{2}\in S_{n}
, and
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in[0,\pi/2]
. Then,

\Theta(P,Q)=\max\left\{0,\,\min\{\pi,\arccos\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle+\phi_{1}\}-\phi_{2}\right\}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
b_{2}=e_{n}
, where
e_{n}
denotes the
n
th canonical vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Indeed, we can take an orthogonal transformation
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
so that
Vb_{2}=e_{n}
,
V(P)
and
V(Q)
remain being revolution cones of half-angle aperture
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, respectively, and
\Theta(V(P),V(Q))=\Theta(P,Q)
because of Proposition
1
(b). Thus,
Q
can be written as

Q=\{(\tilde{v},v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}:\|\tilde{v}\|\leq v_{n}\tan\phi_{2}\},

where
\tilde{v}:=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1})
for
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Indeed,

v\in Q\;\Leftrightarrow\;\langle e_{n},v\rangle\geq\|v\|\cos\phi_{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;v_{n}^{2}\geq(\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}+v_{n}^{2})\cos^{2}\phi_{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;(\tan^{2}\phi_{2})v_{n}^{2}\geq\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}.

From
[
5
, Example 9.1]
, for
u=(\tilde{u},u_{n})\in P\cap S_{n}
, we have

\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)=\begin{cases}u,&\text{if }u\in Q,\\
\left\{(\tilde{w},\cos\phi_{2}):\|\tilde{w}\|=\sin\phi_{2}\right\},&\text{if }u=(0,-1),\\
\left(\frac{\sin\phi_{2}}{\|\tilde{u}\|}\tilde{u},\cos\phi_{2}\right),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(27)

From Proposition
6
(e) we have that
F_{Q}(u)=\langle u,v^{\prime}\rangle
for any
v^{\prime}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
. Hence,

F_{Q}(u)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }u\in Q,\\
-\cos(\phi_{2}),&\text{if }u=(0,-1),\\
\|\tilde{u}\|\sin\phi_{2}+u_{n}\cos\phi_{2},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(28)

Let
\beta:=\arccos\langle b_{1},b_{2}\rangle\in[0,\pi].
We proceed to solve the problem (
17
), which becomes:

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\min_{u}F_{Q}(u)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\langle b_{1},u\rangle\geq\cos\phi_{1},\;\|u\|=1.

(29)

We consider three cases according the values of
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
and
\beta
:

Case 1.
Suppose that
\beta+\phi_{1}\leq\phi_{2}
. It means that
P\subseteq Q
. Then, from Proposition
9
we deduce
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=1
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
\pi\leq\beta+\phi_{1}
. Then
\bar{u}:=(0,-1)\in P
, and from (
28
) we have
F(\bar{u})=-\cos(\phi_{2})
. Furthermore, notice that
-\cos(\phi_{2})\leq F_{Q}(u)
for all
u\in P\cap S_{n}
. Indeed, it comes directly from (
28
) when
u\in Q
or
u=(0,-1)
. For the remaining case, it comes from

F_{Q}(u)+\cos\phi_{2}=\|\tilde{u}\|\sin\phi_{2}+(u_{n}+1)\cos\phi_{2}\geq 0,

where the inequality is because
-1\leq u_{n}\leq 1
since
\|u\|=1
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=-\cos(\phi_{2})
.

Case 3.
Suppose that
\phi_{2}<\beta+\phi_{1}<\pi
. Then,
P\not\subseteq Q
and
(0,-1)\notin P
, which imply
|\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)|\neq 1
, see Proposition
9
. Furthermore, because of (
27
), those facts also imply that
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
is a singleton for any optimal point
u
of (
29
). Now, let
L
be the smallest linear subspace containing
P
and
Q
. Observe that
L=\mathtt{span}\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
when
\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}=0
, and
L=\mathbb{R}^{n}
otherwise. Hence, from Proposition
10
we have that any solution to problem (
29
) is in the boundary of
P
relative to
L
. Thus, this problem can be written as

\min_{u}\langle u,v(u)\rangle,\quad\text{s.t. }\langle b_{1},u\rangle=\cos\phi_{1},\;\|u\|=1,

(30)

where
v(u):=\left(\frac{\sin\phi_{2}}{\|\tilde{u}\|}\tilde{u},\cos\phi_{2}\right)
is the projection of
u
onto
Q\cap S_{n}
, see (
27
) and (
28
). Suppose that
b_{1}=b_{2}
. In this case,
u=(\tilde{u},u_{n})\in S_{n}
is on the boundary of
P
if and only if
\|\tilde{u}\|=\sin\phi_{1}
and
u_{n}=\cos\phi_{1}
. Therefore,

F_{Q}(u)=\|\tilde{u}\|\sin\phi_{2}+u_{n}\cos\phi_{2}=\sin\phi_{1}\sin\phi_{2}+\cos\phi_{1}\cos\phi_{2}=\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}).

Thus,
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})
. Suppose that
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
, then
\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
is linear independent since
b_{1}\neq-b_{2}
because of
-b_{2}=(0,-1)\notin P
. The KKT optimality conditions for
u
to be solution of (
30
) say that there exits
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

v(u)=\lambda_{1}b_{1}+\lambda_{2}u.

(31)

On the other hand, since the optimal
u
is not in
Q
, otherwise
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=1
,
v(u)
is on the boundary of
Q
. Then,
v(u)
solves
\max_{v}\langle u,v\rangle
s.t.
\langle b_{2},v\rangle=\cos\phi_{2},\;\|v\|=1
. Hence, the KKT optimality conditions for
v(u)
say that there exist
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

u=\mu_{1}b_{2}+\mu_{2}v(u).

(32)

By combining (
31
) and (
32
) we deduce that
u,v(u)\in\mathtt{span}\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
. Therefore, the problem (
30
) becomes a 2-dimensional problem. There are only two unit vectors on the boundary of
P
:
\hat{u}:=(\sin(\beta-\phi_{1}),\cos(\beta-\phi_{1}))
which gives
F_{Q}(\hat{u})=\cos(\beta-\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})
, and
\bar{u}:=(\sin(\beta+\phi_{1}),\cos(\beta+\phi_{1}))
which gives
F_{Q}(\bar{u})=\cos(\beta+\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})
. This allow us to conclude that
\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\cos(\beta+\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})
.
Summarizing, we have obtained:

\mathfrak{s}(P,Q)=\begin{cases}\cos(0),&\text{if }\beta+\phi_{1}\leq\phi_{2},\\
\cos(\pi-\phi_{2}),&\text{if }\pi\leq\beta+\phi_{1},\\
\cos(\beta+\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}),&\text{if }\phi_{2}<\beta+\phi_{1}<\pi.\end{cases}

From this, we obtain the announced formula for
\Theta(P,Q)
.

5
Computing the dissimilarity measure: the polyhedral setting

In this section,
P,\,Q\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
are assumed to be polyhedral cones. That is, they are convex, closed, and finitely generated cones. Hence, without loss of generality, we assume that

P=G(\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+})=\{Gx\ |\ x\geq 0\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q=H(\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+})=\{Hy\ |\ y\geq 0\},

(33)

where
G=[g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
H=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{q}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
are matrices whose columns
are unitary, that is,
\|g_{i}\|=1
for all
i
and
\|h_{j}\|=1
for all
j
.

To compute
F_{Q}(u)
, we need to describe
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
. This is given in the next proposition, which is borrowed from
[
5
, Corollary 8.6]
. For a vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\max(w)
denotes the largest entry of
w
, and
\mathtt{cone}\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
denotes the polyhedral cone generated by the vectors
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proposition 14
.

(
[
5
, Corollary 8.6]
)
Let
Q
be a polyhedral cone as in (
33
), and let
u\in S_{n}
. Let
J(u):=\mathop{\rm arg\,max}\limits_{j=1,\ldots,q}\,\langle u,h_{j}\rangle
. Then,
\partial F_{Q}(u)=\mathtt{cl\,conv}\,\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)
, where,

\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\mathtt{Proj}_{Q}(u)}{\textstyle{\|}\mathtt{Proj}_{Q}(u)\textstyle{\|}}&\mbox{ if }\max(H^{\top}u)>0,\\
\mathtt{cone}\{h_{j}:j\in J(u)\}\cap S_{n}&\mbox{ if }\max(H^{\top}u)=0,\\
\{h_{j}:j\in J(u)\}&\mbox{ if }\max(H^{\top}u)<0.\end{array}\right.

This section deploys an optimization approach to tackle problem (
2
) by dealing with its equivalent problem of minimizing the support function given in (
17
); that is,
\min_{u\in P\cap S_{n}}F_{Q}(u)
(recall that this problem has a solution because
F_{Q}
is continuous –convex and real-valued– and
P\cap S_{n}
is compact).
Although the problem of defining
F_{Q}
is nonconvex, Propositions
6
(e) and
14
show that in the polyhedral setting, computing one of its global solutions amounts to solve a strictly convex quadratic problem yielding
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q}(u)
(and then normalizing). When solving such a subproblem, a subgradient of
F_{Q}
at
u
is readily available in Proposition
14
.

Despite this useful property, solving problem (
17
) (and thus (
2
)) remains a challenging nonsmooth, nonconvex optimization problem. In what follows, we investigate an approach to tackle this problem: a global optimization methodology that can be conveniently converted to a local-solution strategy.

5.1
Cutting-plane approach

Given that function
F_{Q}
in (
16
) is convex but nonsmooth, we may solve the problem by a cutting-plane method that, at iteration
k
, replaces
F_{Q}(u)
with the cutting-plane model:

\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u)=\max_{j=1,\ldots,k}\{F_{Q}(u^{j})+\langle v^{j},u-u^{j}\rangle\}\leq F_{Q}(u).

In this notation,
v^{j}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(u)\subseteq\partial F_{Q}(u^{j})
is as in Proposition
14
. Thus,
F_{Q}(u^{j})=\langle v^{j},u^{j}\rangle
, see Proposition
6
(e), and the cutting-model boils down to

\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u)=\max_{j=1,\ldots,k}\langle v^{j},u\rangle.

Accordingly, we get the following master program

u^{k+1}\in\arg\min_{u\in P}\;\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}=1,

which is equivalent to the following quadratic-constrained problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\displaystyle\min_{(u,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}&r\\
\mbox{s.t.}&u\in P,\;\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}^{2}=1\\
&\langle v^{j},u\rangle\leq r,\;j=1,\ldots,k.\end{array}\right.

(34)

Since
P
is polyhedral, this problem can be rewritten as
Mixed-Integer Linear Problem
(MILP) (of larger size) and globally solved by specialized algorithms. Commercial solvers such as Gurobi get as input problem (
34
), make the MILP reformulation themselves, and return a global solution.

Let
(u^{k+1},r^{k+1})
be a solution to this problem. Since the cutting-plane model is a lower approximation of the convex function
F_{Q}
, we get that
r^{k+1}
is a lower bound on
\cos[\Theta(P,Q)]
, the optimal value of (
17
). As any feasible point gives an upper bound, the difference between these bounds gives a way to terminate the cutting-plane approach presented in Algorithm
1
.

and

)

Compute

if

\langle u^{k+1},v^{k+1}\rangle-r^{k+1}\leq\mathtt{tol}

then

Stop: return the angle
\arccos{\langle u^{k+1},v^{k+1}\rangle}

7:

Note that computing

is a relatively simple task: according to Proposition

, in the worst case, it amounts to projecting

onto

(a convex programming problem) followed by normalization.
Algorithm
1
is a specialized version of the celebrated Kelley’s cutting-plane
[
1
]
, which is known to compute a global solution provided the objective function is a real-valued convex function and the feasible set is compact (not necessarily convex).
The interested reader is referred to
[
3
, Ch. 10]
for a more general cutting-plane method and its convergence analysis.

Theorem 15
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with
\mathtt{tol}=0
. Then every cluster point
\bar{u}
of the sequence
\{u^{k}\}
produced by the algorithm is a global solution to (
17
),
(\bar{u},\bar{v})
with
\bar{v}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(\bar{u})
solves (
2
) and
\lim_{k}r^{k}=\cos[\Theta(P,Q)]
.

Proof.

The fact that
\bar{u}
solves (
17
) follows from the standard analysis of the cutting-plane method; see for instance
[
3
, Th. 10.1]
.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be an index set such that
\bar{u}=\lim_{\mathcal{K}\ni k\to\infty}u^{k}
. As the projection onto a nonempty closed set is an outer-semicontinuous operator
[
22
, Example 5.23]
, we get that any cluster point
\bar{v}
of
\{v^{k}\}_{\mathcal{K}}
belongs to
\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(\bar{u})
. Hence, Proposition
6
items (c) and (e), and the equivalence of problems (
2
) and (
17
) conclude the proof.

When the solver used in Algorithm
1
(line
3
) returns a stationary point instead of solving the master problem globally, Algorithm
1
still asymptotically computes a pair of points that satisfy the necessary optimality conditions given in Proposition
7
, as demonstrated by the following result.

Theorem 16
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with
\mathtt{tol}=0
and suppose that, for all iterations
k
,
u^{k+1}
is a stationary point to

\min_{u\in P}\;\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\textstyle{\|}u\textstyle{\|}=1,

and
r^{k+1}=F_{Q}^{k}(u^{k+1})
. Let
\bar{u}
be an arbitrary cluster point of the sequence
\{u^{k}\}
produced by the algorithm. Then
(\bar{u},\bar{v})
with
\bar{v}\in\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(\bar{u})
satisfies the necessary optimality conditions given in Proposition
7
.

Proof.

By proceeding as in the first part of the proof of Proposition
7
(with
F_{Q}
replaced with
\check{F}_{Q}^{k}
), we conclude that
u^{k+1}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}P&\ni u^{k+1}\perp v^{k+1}-\langle u^{k+1},v^{k+1}\rangle u^{k+1}\in P^{*},\\
\partial\check{F}^{k}_{Q}(u^{k+1})&\ni v^{k+1},\;\;\textstyle{\|}u^{k+1}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}\right.

Since
\check{F}^{k}_{Q}\leq F_{Q}
are convex functions, we have that
any
\check{v}\in\partial\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u^{k+1})
belongs to the approximate subdifferential
\partial_{e_{k}}F_{Q}(u^{k+1}):=\{v:\,F_{Q}(u^{k+1})+\langle v,u-u^{k+1}\rangle-e_{k}\leq F_{Q}(u)\}
, with
e_{k}:=F_{Q}(u^{k+1})-\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u^{k+1})\geq 0
. Hence, the above system implies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}P&\ni u^{k+1}\perp v^{k+1}-\langle u^{k+1},v^{k+1}\rangle u^{k+1}\in P^{*},\\
\partial_{e_{k}}F_{Q}(u^{k+1})&\ni v^{k+1},\;\;\textstyle{\|}u^{k+1}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}\right.

(35)

We now prove that
\lim_{k}e_{k}=0
. To this end, observe that for any arbitrary index
i\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq e_{k}=F_{Q}(u^{k+1})-\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u^{k+1})=[F_{Q}(u^{k+1})-\check{F}^{k}_{Q}(u^{i})]+[\check{F}^{k}_{Q}(u^{i})-\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u^{k+1})]

\displaystyle=[F_{Q}(u^{k+1})-F_{Q}(u^{i})]+[\check{F}^{k}_{Q}(u^{i})-\check{F}_{Q}^{k}(u^{k+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle{\|}u^{k+1}-u^{i}\textstyle{\|}+\textstyle{\|}u^{k+1}-u^{i}\textstyle{\|},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
i<k+1
(thus
\check{F}^{k}_{Q}(u^{i})=F_{Q}(u^{i})
), and the inequality holds because both
F_{Q}
and
\check{F}^{k}_{Q}
are Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant equal to one. This last result follows from Proposition
6
(b) and the fact that, being a cutting-plane function,
\partial\check{F}^{k}_{Q}(u)
is contained in the convex hull of
\{v^{1},\ldots,v^{k}\}
.
As a result, if there would exist
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon\leq e_{k}
for all
k
large enough, then the bounded sequence
\{u^{k}\}
would not have a convergent subsequence, which is impossible in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Hence,
\lim_{k}e_{k}=0
. (Thus the algorithm terminates after finitely many iterations provided
\mathtt{tol}>0
.)

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{N}
be such that
\lim_{\mathcal{K}\ni k\to\infty}u^{k+1}=\bar{u}
.
As the approximated subdifferential is locally bounded
[
3
, Lem. 2.10]
and
\lim_{k}e_{k}=0
, there exists
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{K}
and
\bar{v}
such that
\lim_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\ni k\to\infty}v^{k+1}=\bar{v}
.
By passing to the limit as
k\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
in the system (
35
) and recalling that the approximate subdifferential, the normal cone, and the projection onto a nonempty closed set are outer-semicontinuous
[
3
, Prop 2.7, Lem. 3.2]
and
[
22
, Example 5.23]
, we conclude that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}P&\ni\bar{u}\perp\bar{v}-\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle\bar{u}\in P^{*},\\
\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})&\ni\bar{v},\;\;\textstyle{\|}\bar{u}\textstyle{\|}=1.\end{array}\right.

As
\bar{v}\in\partial F_{Q}(\bar{u})
, Proposition
6
(d) asserts that
\bar{v}\in{\tt cl\,conv}\,\mathtt{Proj}_{Q\cap S_{n}}(\bar{u})
.
By proceeding as in the final part of the proof of Proposition
7
, we conclude that
(\bar{u},\bar{v})
satisfies the necessary optimality conditions presented in Proposition
7
.

Note that the master program in the above theorem is a nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problem. To compute a stationary point
u^{k+1}
, we may consider the smooth reformulation (
34
) and apply standard NLP solvers.

7
Concluding remarks

In this work, we introduced a novel dissimilarity measure between convex cones grounded in the concept of max-min angles, offering a geometric perspective closely related to the Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance. Through a rigorous mathematical and algorithmic framework, we explored several cone configurations where the measure admits simplified or analytic forms. For cones that are linear subspaces of equal dimension, we showed that our measure coincides with the spectral distance, allowing for efficient computation via singular value decomposition. For revolution cones, we derived a closed-form expression for the measure, further illustrating its interpretability and computational tractability in structured settings.

In the more challenging case of polyhedral cones, computing the measure involves minimizing a nonsmooth convex function over a nonconvex feasible set. To face the challenge, we revisited Kelley’s cutting-plane method and proposed a local-solution variant that leverages standard nonlinear programming solvers to tackle the difficult nonconvex master program.
Our theoretical developments were complemented by a convergence analysis and extensive numerical experiments, including a practical application to image-set classification under few-shot learning conditions. The results highlight the effectiveness and practicality of our approach, with the proposed measure successfully distinguishing between image classes in data-scarce scenarios. In particular, for the considered image-set classification problem, our approach could correctly classify
7 classes out of 8 on average for all the training cone configurations.

Overall, this work contributes a robust and versatile tool for comparing convex cones, with potential implications for geometric data analysis, computer vision, and machine learning tasks where cone-based representations are relevant.
Future research may explore further global and local optimization strategies for computing the proposed dissimilarity measure in structured settings. In particular, extending the current numerical framework to handle broader classes of cones and designing specialized algorithms could enhance both accuracy and scalability. Furthermore, given the flexibility and interpretability of cone comparisons, our measure lays a solid foundation for further exploration and opens promising avenues for applications in machine learning, especially in tasks involving geometric representations, few-shot learning, and structured data analysis.
