ROS-responsive small-molecule self-assembled nanocarrier-integrated hydrogels for ulcerative colitis treatment
Authors: Chen Zhang, Shanying Han, Lei Yang, Wenya Weng, Panpan Wei, Yingping Xiao, Qingfei Zhang, Ruiyao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) has a multifactorial pathogenesis, driven primarily by excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), aberrant inflammatory activation, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and intestinal microbiota dysbiosis. Conventional therapies face challenges including poor targeting specificity, low delivery efficiency, insufficient barrier repair, and limited microbiota modulation. Therefore, developing an intelligent drug delivery system that integrates precise targeting with ROS-responsive release is critical for effective UC treatment. Here, an ROS-responsive small-molecule self-assembled nanocarrier-integrated hydrogel (HA/MPC@B) was developed for comprehensive UC treatment. HA/MPC@B was prepared by loading berberine (BBR) into D-mannose-pinacol 4-hydroxyphenylborate-1,1′-carbonyl diimidazole (MPC) self-assembled nanoparticles, followed by their incorporation into a hyaluronic acid-hyaluronic acid methacrylate (HA-HAMA) hydrogel. Pathological levels of ROS triggered the cleavage of boronic ester bonds of MPC, enabling spatiotemporally controlled BBR release, while the hydrogel prolonged drug retention at lesion sites through local physical barrier effects. In vitro assays demonstrated the biocompatibility, ROS-scavenging capacity, macrophage polarization modulation, and anti-inflammatory and barrier-repair functions of HA/MPC@B. In vivo studies using UC models confirmed its efficacy in alleviating clinical symptoms, repairing mucosal damage, and partially restoring microbiota balance. Transcriptome analysis further revealed that HA/MPC@B reversed dextran sulfate sodium-induced dysregulation in the expression of inflammation-suppressor genes and tight junction-related genes. Overall, HA/MPC@B achieved effective UC treatment through the synergistic effects of targeted delivery, ROS-responsive release, anti-inflammatory/antioxidant activities, barrier repair, and microbiota modulation.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory intestinal disease with no known cure [[1], [2], [3]]. In recent years, the global prevalence of UC has increased substantially, exceeding 400 cases per 100,000 individuals in North America [[4], [5], [6]]. The incidence rate in the UK is 12.6 per 100,000 person-years [7]. This trend is associated with abnormal immune regulation, genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and unhealthy dietary habits [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. The main symptoms of UC include bloody diarrhea or rectal bleeding, often accompanied by abdominal pain, anemia, and intestinal cramps. The disease is also characterized by recurrent episodes [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Currently, the main goal of UC treatment is to maintain long-term stable remission, thereby reducing the recurrence rate and preventing the development of colitis-related cancers [16,17]. The current drugs used to treat UC include 5-aminosalicylic acid, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and biological agents; however, their clinical remission rate is only 40% [18]. Moreover, these treatments prioritize inflammation suppression and symptom relief over restoring the intestinal microbiome composition and intestinal barrier function [19]. In addition, oral, parenteral, and rectal delivery routes suffer from low efficiency and poor targeting specificity [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Therefore, developing effective targeting delivery systems is essential for comprehensive treatment of UC.

Oxidative stress is integral to UC initiation and progression. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) degrade the gastrointestinal mucosal layer, enabling bacterial invasion and triggering intestinal inflammation [25,26]. The high local ROS concentration at inflammatory sites provides an ideal target for ROS-responsive drug delivery systems, facilitating lesion-specific, spatiotemporally controlled drug release with minimal systemic toxicity [27,28]. Recently, small-molecule self-assembled nanocarriers demonstrate the advantages of simple preparation, strong drug loading capacity, and high delivery efficiency, showing great potential in improving drug safety and efficacy in UC therapy [29,30]. Hydrogels have attracted considerable attention for the delivery of bioactive drugs owing to their superior sustained release behavior, biocompatibility, and degradability [31,32]. Particularly for UC treatment, hydrogels can provide physical barriers and prolonged drug release, thereby mitigating UC-related mucosal damage and recurrent attacks. Therefore, combining small-molecule self-assembled nanocarriers with hydrogels and introducing a ROS-responsive drug release mechanism is expected to facilitate the development of composite drug delivery systems for the sustained and efficient treatment of UC.

In this study, we prepared an ROS-responsive small-molecule self-assembled nanocarrier-integrated hydrogel (HA/MPC@B) for UC therapy (Fig. 1). First, pinacol 4-hydroxyphenylborate (PBAP) was covalently conjugated with D-mannose (MAN) using 1,1′-carbonyl diimidazole (CDI) as a coupling agent to form MPC nanoparticles, with non-toxic and removable imidazole as a by-product. Then, berberine (BBR), with anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, barrier repair, and microbiota-regulation effects, was loaded into MPC nanoparticles to obtain MAN-PBAP-CDI@BBR (MPC@B) nanoparticles. Finally, the small-molecule self-assembled nanoparticles hydrogel system (HA/MPC@B) was constructed by physically mixing the MPC@B nanoparticles with the hybrid hydrogel of hyaluronic acid (HA) and hyaluronate methacrylate (HAMA) [[33], [34], [35]]. In the system, PBAP, as a borate group derivative, can undergo oxidized borate bond cleavage in the high ROS microenvironment [[36], [37], [38]], while HA-HAMA forms an injectable, biodegradable, and biocompatible hydrogel matrix [39,40]. This small-molecule self-assembled nanoparticle-integrated hydrogel has several core effects. First, the MAN molecule in the MPC nanoparticles can specifically bind to the mannose receptor (MR) on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells to achieve targeted enrichment at the lesion sites [41,42]. Second, MPC@B nanoparticles can trigger the degradation of BBR in the high ROS microenvironment of UC lesions, enabling precise and controlled release of BBR. Third, the anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, barrier repair, and microbiota-regulation effects of BBR [43,44] are integrated with HAMA hydrogels, prolonging the retention time of the drug at the lesion sites. Results and discussions

Preparation and characterization of HA/MPC@B

To address the poor in vivo solubility of BBR and enhance its targeting capability, a three-step preparation process was developed to fabricate MAN-PBAP-CDI@BBR (MPC@B) nanoparticles. First, PBAP-CDI (PC) was synthesized via the coupling reaction of PBAP and CDI in dichloromethane. Subsequently, under the catalysis of 4-dimethylaminopyridine, PC was covalently crosslinked with MAN to generate MPC molecules, which then self-assembled in aqueous solution to form small-molecule self-assembled MPC nanoparticles. The obtained MPC featured a regular circular structure, with the outer hydrophilic layer composed of the polyhydroxy (-OH) groups of MAN and amide bonds (-CONH-) formed through its reaction with CDI, and the inner hydrophobic part composed of the phenyl ring (-C6H5) and ether linkage (-O-C(CH3)2-C(CH3)2-O-) of PBAP. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to characterize the chemical structures and functional groups of the synthesized compounds. As shown in Fig. S1a and b, during the formation of PC from PBAP and CDI, the phenolic hydroxyl O-H stretching peak of PBAP at 3300-3200 cm−1 and the carbonyl stretching peak of CDI at 1740-1720 cm−1 both disappeared. Concurrently, a new aryl imidazole carboxylate carbonyl peak emerged at 1750-1730 cm−1 in PC, accompanied by an ester bond C-O stretching peak at 1280-1250 cm−1. Characteristic peaks of boronate ester B-O (1340-1320 cm−1, 1140-1120 cm−1), ortho-disubstituted benzene ring (1610-1590 cm−1, 1510-1490 cm−1, 860-800 cm−1), and imidazole ring (1580-1560 cm−1, 1500-1480 cm−1, 1280-1260 cm−1) were preserved, confirming the successful synthesis of PC. In the subsequent synthesis of MPC from PC and MAN, the carbonyl peak of PC at 1750-1730 cm−1 diminished, and a new N-substituted carbamate carbonyl peak appeared at 1750-1720 cm−1 in MPC, verifying the progress of the reaction. Meanwhile, the characteristic peaks of boronate ester B-O, ortho-disubstituted benzene ring, imidazole ring, and glucose ring C-O-C (1150-1050 cm−1) remained intact, confirming the successful preparation of MPC.

The ultraviolet (UV)-visible normalized absorption spectra of PBAP, CDI, PC, MAN, and MPC also exhibited distinct shifts. The absorption peak of PBAP at 270-280 nm was red-shifted due to the conjugation effect, and the absorption peak of PC at 210-220 nm became stronger because of the formation of an imidazole-carbonyl conjugate system (Fig. S1c and d). In MPC, the absorption peaks at 210-220 nm and 270-280 nm of the active ester intermediates were retained in the coupling products, and the intensity of the absorption peaks varied slightly due to the change in the microenvironment of the conjugate system, which verified the successful synthesis of MPC. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) analysis indicated that PBAP, CDI, and MAN possessed both hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups after chemical bonding, providing the foundation for nanoparticle formation (Fig. S2). Compared with each starting monomer, characteristic proton signals originating from PBAP, CDI, and MAN could all be observed in the 1H NMR spectrum of the final MPC product. In the 1H NMR (DMSO-d6) spectrum of MPC, the imidazole protons originating from CDI were detected in the range of δ 7.0–8.2 ppm. The aromatic protons originating from the PBAP moiety exhibited an obvious upfield shift to δ 6.5–6.8 ppm. The α/β-anomeric doublets of D-mannose nearly disappeared, while the skeletal protons of the mannose ring overlapped with the residual water peak in DMSO, showing a broad peak at δ 3.0–3.8 ppm with no resolvable fine splitting. The disappearance of free mannose anomeric signals and the marked upfield shift of aromatic protons confirmed the successful formation of the cyclic boronate ester. Meanwhile, the well-resolved imidazole proton signals confirmed the integrity of the carbamate linkage, collectively demonstrating the successful synthesis of MPC. In addition, several minor peaks were observed in the high-field region below the DMSO solvent peak at δ 2.5 ppm. A weak residual signal was detected at δ 1.32 ppm, which was assigned to the pinacol methyl group of the starting PC, indicating the presence of trace unreacted PC. Potential by-products of the coupling reaction may exist in the final product, including multi-substituted mannose carbamate derivatives formed by non-selective reactions between multiple hydroxyl groups on mannose and PC intermediates, as well as symmetric diaryl carbonates generated from the self-coupling of PBAP. However, no corresponding peaks attributable to these by-products were observed in the spectra. The above FT-IR, UV-vis, and 1H NMR results collectively confirmed the successful synthesis of amphiphilic MPC molecules.

Subsequently, BBR was loaded into MPC nanoparticles through physical blending. The successful loading of BBR was verified by multiple characterizations. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the MPC nanoparticles had a mean diameter of 170.34 nm, and the MPC@B nanoparticles had a mean diameter of 160.41 nm (Fig. 2a and Fig. S3a). Zeta potential measurements showed that the surface charges of the MPC and MPC@B nanoparticles were −20.45 mV and −33.13 mV, respectively (Fig. 2b and Fig. S3b). In addition, MPC exhibited an average encapsulation efficiency of 77.93 ± 2.87% for BBR, with a loading capacity of 23.76 ± 0.67% (Fig. 2c). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to observe the structural characteristics of MPC@B. The SEM images indicated that the MPC and MPC@B nanoparticles were spherical with smooth and regular surfaces (Fig. 2d and e). The TEM images further confirmed the regular morphology of the MPC and MPC@B nanoparticles (Fig. 2g and h). To evaluate the stability of self-assembled MPC and MPC@B nanoparticles under gastrointestinal physiological and inflammatory pathological microenvironments, the nanoparticles were incubated in simulated gastric fluid (SGF), simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), simulated colonic fluid (SCF), and H2O2-supplemented SCF (mimicking the high-ROS microenvironment of colitis lesions). The changes in particle-size distribution and polydispersity index (PDI) were monitored by DLS (Fig. S3e–h). Obvious disassembly of MPC and MPC@B nanoparticles occurred under SGF and SCF + H2O2 conditions, as evidenced by shifted particle-size distributions and markedly elevated PDI values. In contrast, negligible variations in particle size and PDI were observed in SIF and SCF, and the nanoparticles maintained favorable stability. In addition, in vitro ROS scavenging assays demonstrated that both HA/MPC@B and MPC@B possessed broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging capacity towards ·OH, H2O2, and O2−·, suggesting their potential to alleviate intestinal oxidative stress injury and providing in vitro evidence for the application of these nanocarriers in the treatment of UC (Fig. S3i).

Subsequently, HA-HAMA hydrogel was added to the MPC@B aqueous solution, homogenized, and subjected to photocuring under UV irradiation to prepare the HA/MPC@B system. As shown in the SEM image (Fig. 2f), the HA/MPC@B hydrogel formed an interconnected 3D porous skeleton containing numerous irregular pores. This porous architecture facilitated the diffusion of MPC@B nanoparticles and strengthened their adhesion to the intestinal wall. The HA-HAMA hybrid hydrogel was prepared in an injectable hydrogel state (Fig. 2i, Fig. S3c and d). Rheological frequency scanning tests and strain scanning tests were performed on HA-HAMA and HA/MPC@B hydrogel systems (Fig. 2j and k). Both HA-HAMA and HA/MPC@B showed obvious shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing significantly as the shear rate increased, indicating good injectability. HA/MPC@B also maintained a stable gel state and favorable injectability, providing a material basis for oral delivery. In vitro BBR release assays were performed in various gastrointestinal media to validate the ROS-responsive property of the boronate ester groups (-B-O-) in MPC@B nanoparticles and their capability to mediate stimuli-triggered drug release under high-ROS conditions. In physiological media including PBS, SGF, SIF, and SCF, HA/MPC@B exhibited restrained drug release (Fig. 2l). Within 48 h, the cumulative BBR release remained at relatively low levels, with cumulative release below 20%. In contrast, in SCF + 0.1 mM H2O2, which mimics the high-ROS microenvironment of inflamed colon tissue, the cumulative drug release significantly increased to 52.02 ± 4.11% at 48 h. The release profiles demonstrated that HA/MPC@B could effectively suppress premature drug leakage during gastrointestinal transit and achieve ROS-triggered accelerated drug release in the ROS-abundant inflammatory microenvironment. Furthermore, owing to the drug preservation capacity of the hydrogel, HA/MPC@B exhibited a delayed release compared with MPC@B (Fig. S4). Overall, these results demonstrate the successful preparation of the HA/MPC@B system, which integrates ROS-responsive precise drug release, favorable rheological properties, and efficient sustained-release capabilities, supporting the feasibility and functional advantages of HA/MPC@B for UC treatment.

In vitro biosafety assay of HA/MPC@B

Biocompatibility evaluations were performed on HA/MPC@B and the nanoparticles (MPC and MPC@B) to investigate their biological safety for subsequent applications. To determine the optimal in vitro therapeutic concentration, the cytotoxicity of HA/MPC@B hydrogels against Caco-2 cells was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay at BBR concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg/mL. The cell viability in the low-concentration groups (≤1.0 mg/mL) did not show a significant difference from that in the control group (Fig. S5a). A slight decrease was detected in the high-concentration group (2.0 mg/mL). To balance the material efficacy and cytotoxicity, 1.0 mg/mL was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. Next, cell viability of the MPC, MPC@B, and HA/MPC@B groups was measured at 1.0 mg/mL. No statistically significant differences were observed compared with the control group, indicating negligible cytotoxicity (Fig. S5b). Hemocompatibility is a prerequisite for pharmaceutical agents before clinical trials. Hemolysis assays were performed on the hydrogel extracts in PBS to assess the hemocompatibility of the HA/MPC@B hydrogel. As shown in Fig. S5c, the positive control group exhibited significant hemolysis, as evidenced by the red supernatant. In contrast, the other groups did not show hemolysis. The absorbance of the supernatant at 545 nm indicated a hemolysis rate of less than 5% for the HA/MPC@B group, indicating good hemocompatibility and meeting the commonly accepted criterion for biomedical materials. To further evaluate the long-term biocompatibility of the systems, the Caco-2 cells from different groups were subjected to live/dead staining. Caco-2 cells were co-cultured with 1.0 mg/mL MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B, and then co-incubated with calcein AM and propidium iodide for staining. The live/dead cell distribution was observed at 24, 48, and 72 h. In Fig. S5d, live cells comprised the majority of cells across all groups at all time points, with green fluorescence levels comparable to those of the unstimulated control group. In summary, HA/MPC@B exhibits no significant cytotoxicity or hemolytic activity at the optimal therapeutic concentration.

In vitro macrophage targeting

MPC was synthesized by covalently coupling mannose to imidazole-modified phenylboronic acid via a cyclic boronate ester linkage. The terminal mannose moiety acts as a specific ligand for macrophage MR. To further verify the MR-mediated targeting ability of MPC toward macrophages, competitive inhibition assays were performed on RAW264.7 cells. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labelled mannose (FITC-Man) was utilized as a fluorescent tracer to monitor MR binding. Two competitive pretreatment groups were established: one group was incubated with excess free mannose to block cell-surface MR receptors, and the other group was treated with unlabeled MPC to compete for MR binding sites. The cellular internalization behavior was visualized by confocal laser-scanning microscopy and further quantified via flow cytometry. As shown in the confocal images (Fig. S6a), the FITC-Man group exhibited intense intracellular green fluorescence, demonstrating efficient MR binding and internalization of mannose probes in RAW264.7 macrophages. In contrast, the fluorescence signal was significantly attenuated in the free mannose-pretreated group, confirming that free mannose successfully occupied MR receptors and blocked FITC-Man binding. Notably, unlabeled MPC pretreatment also markedly reduced intracellular fluorescence intensity, suggesting that MPC effectively competed with FITC-Man for MR receptors. Consistent with the microscopic observations, flow cytometry quantification validated the competitive targeting behavior (Fig. S6b). The mean fluorescence intensity of the FITC-Man group was 71.9, while the MPC competitive group displayed a sharply decreased fluorescence intensity of 31.5. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MPC can specifically recognize and bind to macrophage MR, enabling precise targeting towards MR-overexpressing inflammatory macrophages.

In vitro anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects

Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are widely used in vitro inflammatory models. To investigate the in vitro anti-inflammatory potential of HA/MPC@B hydrogel, RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in LPS-containing medium to mimic an endogenous hyperinflammatory physiological state. Subsequently, the cells were co-cultured with MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B for 12 h, and the anti-inflammatory effects of HA/MPC@B were assessed via immunofluorescence staining of the CD86 and CD206 markers. In Fig. 3a, the proportion of CD86-positive cells decreased in the MPC group, although positive signals persisted. In contrast, the CD86 fluorescence signals in the MPC@B and HA/MPC@B groups were comparable to those in the control group, with a minimal number of weakly positive cells observed, indicating that MPC@B and HA/MPC@B significantly suppressed M1 pro-inflammatory polarization. In the case of CD206 staining (Fig. 3c), the MPC group showed a slight increase in the number of CD206-positive cells, and the signals remained weak. In contrast, the MPC@B and HA/MPC@B groups displayed a significant increase in CD206 fluorescence intensity, with most cells showing positive signals, indicating effective restoration of the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype. Quantitative fluorescence assays demonstrated that LPS induced M1 pro-inflammatory polarization while suppressing M2 anti-inflammatory polarization of macrophages (Fig. 3b and d). To gain further insight into the regulatory mechanism of HA/MPC@B in macrophage inflammatory polarization, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase was used as the internal control gene to ensure the reliability of gene expression detection. In RAW264.7 cells, LPS treatment significantly increased the mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and reduced the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) (Fig. 3g–j). In the presence of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B, the expression level of pro-inflammatory cytokines decreased, while the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine was restored. Compared with MPC, the restoring effects of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B were more pronounced. For example, HA/MPC@B restored IL-10 expression to 1.12 ± 0.06 times that of the control group, and MPC@B restored it to 1.24 ± 0.05 times that of the control group, showing significantly superior regulatory efficacy. To investigate the ROS-scavenging capacity of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B, H2O2-treated RAW 264.7 cells were used for experiments. The MPC@B and HA/MPC@B groups showed a significantly lower fluorescence signal than the Rosup group, confirming that HA/MPC@B effectively scavenged the intracellular ROS (Fig. 3e and f). In summary, HA/MPC@B demonstrates excellent in vitro anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects by inhibiting macrophage M1 polarization, restoring M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype, regulating inflammatory cytokine expression, and efficient ROS scavenging. These synergistic actions provide direct experimental evidence for its anti-inflammatory mechanism in UC treatment.

In vitro barrier treatment and repair effects

The structural and functional integrity of the intestinal barrier is widely recognized as a critical parameter for determining the severity of UC. Therefore, the in vitro migration-promoting activity, mucosal repairing activity, and barrier-protective function of the HA/MPC@B hydrogel were evaluated in Caco-2 intestinal epithelial cells. A classic cell scratch assay was employed to simulate intestinal mucosal injury and monitor the migration capacity of Caco-2 cells in real time. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) treatment induced cellular injury, inhibited cell migration, and significantly increased the wound area, indicating impaired physiological function of intestinal epithelial cells. The MPC group showed no significant reduction in the wound area, whereas the MPC@B and HA/MPC@B groups exhibited substantially smaller wound areas than the DSS group. These findings confirm that MPC@B and HA/MPC@B effectively enhance the migration capacity of the intestinal epithelial cells. Although the enhanced cell migration ability indicates wound recovery of Caco-2 cells, it does not represent the recovery of intestinal barrier function. To further verify the protective effect against intestinal barrier dysfunction, we performed FITC-dextran (FITC-D) permeability assays on DSS-damaged Caco-2 cell monolayers. Due to equipment limitations, only FITC-D permeability assays were conducted, and transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements were not performed on the Caco-2 monolayers. DSS injury markedly elevated the permeability of Caco-2 monolayers to FITC-D, reflecting severe disruption of epithelial tight junctions (Fig. S7a). Consistently with the scratch-wound results, the MPC group failed to reverse the enhanced monolayer permeability induced by DSS. By comparison, both MPC@B and HA/MPC@B treatments substantially reduced FITC-D permeation across cell monolayers. To further clarify the mechanism of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B in promoting intestinal barrier repair, immunofluorescence staining was carried out to assess the expression of ZO-1 and Claudin-1 in Caco-2 cells. A significant reduction in immunofluorescence area was observed in the presence of DSS for both proteins, suggesting the disruption of tight junction integrity. The MPC group showed no significant recovery in immunofluorescence area, whereas the MPC@B group exhibited a larger area, although it remained smaller than that of the control (Fig. 4c and e). In contrast, the immunofluorescence areas of Claudin-1 and ZO-1 in the HA/MPC@B group were significantly restored, approaching those of the control group, thereby confirming that HA/MPC@B can effectively repair intestinal epithelial tight junction structures. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was utilized to evaluate the expression levels of the barrier-related Occludin and ZO-1 genes. As shown in Fig. 4d and f, the control group maintained high mRNA expression of these genes. DSS stimulation led to significant downregulation of all tested genes, and no significant recovery was observed in the MPC group. The HA/MPC@B group exhibited a modest increase in gene expression, whereas the MPC@B group showed considerable upregulation of all barrier-related genes.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that HA/MPC@B can enhance in vitro intestinal mucosal repair and barrier-protection effects through promoting intestinal epithelial cell migration, repairing tight junction structures, and upregulating the expression of barrier-related genes. This assessment provides robust experimental evidence for the barrier repair mechanism in UC treatment.

Therapeutic effects on UC

Building on the in vitro anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and barrier-repairing properties of HA/MPC@B, its in vivo therapeutic efficacy and mechanism consistency were further investigated using a DSS-induced UC mouse model. Five groups were established for different experimental purposes: (1) control, (2) DSS, (3) MPC, (4) MPC@B, and (5) HA/MPC@B groups. C57 mice were treated with a 3% DSS solution on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 to induce acute UC. From days 8 to 14, mice in the MPC, MPC@B, and HA/MPC@B groups received their respective treatments by oral gavage, while the control and DSS groups received PBS. As shown in Fig. 5b–g, the DSS group showed a significant weight loss of approximately 20%. Notably, the HA/MPC@B group demonstrated the most pronounced improvement in weight recovery (Fig. 5b). In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed to explore the intestinal behaviors of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B in colitis-model mice (Fig. S8a). Colitic mice were orally administered Cy5.5-PBS solution, Cy5.5-loaded MPC@B nanoparticle solution, and Cy5.5-loaded HA/MPC@B composite hydrogel, and fluorescence signals were recorded at different time points. The fluorescence of free Cy5.5 decayed rapidly over time; only faint fluorescence remained in the gastrointestinal tract at 12 h post-administration due to rapid systemic absorption and clearance. In contrast, obvious fluorescence leakage occurred in the MPC@B group within 4 h after oral administration, resulting from partial degradation of nanoparticles under gastric-acid conditions. The fluorescence dispersed throughout the digestive tract without specific accumulation in the inflamed colon. Notably, intense and sustained fluorescence was detected in the colon of the HA/MPC@B hydrogel group from 4 h to 36 h post-treatment. Ex vivo organ imaging at 48 h did not show colon-targeted accumulation in the free Cy5.5 and MPC@B groups, likely because MPC@B nanoparticles are directly exposed to gastric acid and undergo premature decomposition in the stomach. In contrast, in the HA/MPC@B group, the cross-linked HA-HAMA hydrogel matrix forms a polymeric physical barrier that isolates encapsulated MPC@B nanoparticles from direct contact with harsh acidic gastric fluid. Benefiting from this protective effect, HA/MPC@B resists gastric degradation and successfully reaches inflamed colonic lesions. Therefore, prominent fluorescence enrichment was observed in the inflamed colon region of the HA/MPC@B group (Fig. S8b). Meanwhile, the HA-HAMA hydrogel undergoes gradual degradation within the intestinal tract to release nanoparticles, prolonging intestinal residence time and enabling effective accumulation at inflamed colonic lesions in colitis-model mice. To further clarify the therapeutic effects of HA/MPC@B in UC treatment, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed. Compared with the control group, the DSS group showed severe mucosal injury and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5h). Analogous pathological alterations were identified in the MPC group. In contrast, the MPC@B and HA/MPC@B groups showed significantly improved mucosal repair, with a more regular glandular arrangement and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. Pathological scoring confirmed that the HA/MPC@B group had the lowest score, indicating the most effective therapeutic outcome (Fig. 5i).

To assess the biosafety of HA/MPC@B at the therapeutic dosage, an additional HA/MPC@B-treated healthy control group was included in our study. As shown in Fig. S9, no obvious changes in body weight, DAI score, survival rate, spleen index, colon index, and colonic H&E staining and histological scores were observed for healthy mice after receiving HA/MPC@B oral administration, indicating the good in vivo biosafety of HA/MPC@B at the therapeutic dose. HA/MPC@B employs mannose-mediated active targeting to bind to MR on macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells, thereby significantly enhancing enrichment efficiency at inflammatory sites. Concurrently, the high-ROS microenvironment in lesions triggers the cleavage of borate ester bonds, enabling on-demand drug release that avoids systemic toxicity while increasing local drug concentration.

We further investigated the protective effects of HA/MPC@B against DSS-induced colitis via ROS-responsive mechanisms. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) immunofluorescence staining of the colonic tissues revealed that the DSS group exhibited a significantly increased proportion of MPO-positive neutrophils in the colonic mucosa and submucosa (diffuse infiltration), whereas MPC@B and HA/MPC@B considerably reduced this accumulation (Fig. 5j). Statistical analysis of the positive area ratio indicates that the MPO fluorescence signal in the HA/MPC@B group was comparable to that in the control group, indicating effective suppression of neutrophil infiltration and alleviation of local inflammation (Fig. 5k). Subsequently, qPCR was performed to detect the TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 expression levels in colonic epithelial cells. DSS stimulation markedly increased the mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, while decreasing IL-10 expression (Fig. 5l–o). In contrast, the MPC, MPC@B, and HA/MPC@B groups exhibited a gradual reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines. Among these groups, HA/MPC@B showed the most prominent inhibitory effect, restoring pro-inflammatory cytokine expression to near-control levels while significantly upregulating IL-10 expression. These results confirmed that HA/MPC@B modulated macrophage polarization from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype, thereby effectively suppressing core pro-inflammatory factors and alleviating intestinal inflammation.

In summary, HA/MPC@B specifically releases BBR at sites of colonic inflammation through its ROS-responsive mechanism to induce potent anti-colitis effects in vivo. It inhibits neutrophil infiltration while restoring inflammatory factor balance, thereby alleviating clinically characteristic lesions. These findings provide direct in vivo evidence supporting the ROS-responsive anti-inflammatory mechanism of HA/MPC@B for UC treatment.

16S rRNA sequencing analysis of gut microbiota regulated by HA/MPC@B

The progression and prognosis of UC are strongly associated with the changes in the composition and abundance of the gut microbiota [[46], [47], [48]]. The gut microbiota can modulate peripheral tissues through the systemic transport of microbial components and microbiota-derived metabolites [[49], [50], [51]]. To examine the role of gut microbiota in the treatment process of HA/MPC@B, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to analyze its regulatory effect on the gut microbiota. The microbial α-diversity indices of the DSS group, including richness (Ace) and phylogenetic diversity, showed an upward trend (Fig. S10a–d), indicating that colitis disrupts gut microbial homeostasis. In contrast, MPC, MPC@B, and HA/MPC@B did not substantially change the α-diversity of intestinal flora in mice.

DSS treatment induced remarkable disturbance of gut microbiota and intestinal homeostasis. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis revealed that pro-inflammatory genera, including Rothia, Helicobacter, Streptococcus, and Escherichia-Shigella, were markedly enriched in the DSS group, whereas beneficial taxa, such as Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella, were depleted, which confirmed severe gut dysbiosis under ulcerative colitis conditions (Fig. S10f and g). The HA/MPC@B group exhibited microbial characteristics comparable to those of the control group, thereby effectively reversing DSS-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis. At the phylum level, the control group was dominated by Bacteroidota and Firmicutes. In the DSS group, the relative abundances of pro-inflammatory bacterial genera (e.g., Helicobacter and Escherichia-Shigella) were significantly increased, which may exacerbate mucosal inflammation. Conversely, the abundance of beneficial bacterial taxa (e.g., Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136) was reduced, which may be associated with impaired intestinal barrier function. Notably, the HA/MPC@B group suppressed the pro-inflammatory bacteria and restored the beneficial bacteria (Fig. 7a and b). Samples from DSS and control groups were clearly separated in coordinate space, suggesting that the structure of the intestinal flora community had fundamentally changed under the state of UC (Fig. S10e). Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed an ROS-responsive HA/MPC@B hydrogel system that combines inflammatory targeting capability, ROS-responsive drug release, and multimodal synergistic therapy. MPC@B consisted of small-molecule self-assembled nanoparticles that could efficiently deliver BBR and integrate targeting ability, anti-inflammatory activity, and ROS-responsive drug release. The incorporation of the MPC@B into the HA-HAMA hydrogel enabled sustained drug release at inflammatory sites and mucosal protection. High-ROS levels in lesions triggered the cleavage of the borate ester bonds of MPC, thereby spatiotemporally controlling the release of BBR. HA/MPC@B showed excellent biocompatibility/hemocompatibility in vitro, scavenged intracellular ROS, modulated macrophage polarization, balanced inflammatory cytokines, and promoted intestinal barrier repair. In in vivo experiments, HA/MPC@B alleviated clinical symptoms and tissue injury, protected gut barrier integrity, and rebalanced the gut microbiota. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed the reversal of inflammation-related gene dysregulation. In summary, HA/MPC@B treats UC via a synergistic mechanism involving targeted delivery, ROS-responsive drug release, anti-inflammatory/antioxidant actions, barrier repair, and microbiota modulation, offering a promising intelligent multifunctional strategy for further clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

BBR and PBAP were procured from Macklin (Shanghai, China). CDI was obtained from Merck (Nantong, China). HAMA (EFL-HAMA-150K; molecular weight: 150 kDa, degree of substitution: 30%–40%) was sourced from EFL-Tech (Suzhou, China). MAN and DSS were provided by Macklin (Shanghai, China). The cell viability assay, cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8), and ROS assay kit (DCFH-DA) were purchased from Solarbio Life Sciences (Beijing, China) and Beyotime Biotechnology. Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Hangsi Biotechnology.

Preparation of PBAP-CDI

PBAP (4 mmol, 0.912 g) and CDI (8 mM, 1.281 g) were dissolved in 5 mL of CH2Cl2. The resulting solution was sealed and magnetically stirred for 0.5 h at 25 °C. After washing three times with ultrapure water and once with saline, the organic phase was dried using anhydrous sodium sulfate and purified by rotary evaporation (40 °C and 0.08 MPa) to obtain PBAP-CDI as a white solid.

Preparation of MPC

PBAP-CDI (6 mM, 1.836 g), MAN (1 mM, 0.180 g), and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (7.2 mM, 0.879 g) were added to 10 mL of anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide. Under nitrogen protection, the solution was magnetically stirred overnight at 25 °C. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was dialyzed against ultrapure water for 24 h in a 500 Da dialysis bag. The dialysate was refreshed every 1 h to remove unreacted small molecules and impurities. Finally, the retentate was freeze-dried at −50 °C under 0.01 MPa to recover MPC as a pale-yellow powder.

Preparation of MPC@B

A mixture of the MPC solution (50 mg in 10 mL dimethyl sulfoxide) and BBR (20 mg) was magnetically stirred at 25 °C for 2 h to allow sufficient encapsulation of BBR in the MPC nanoparticles via hydrophobic interactions. The reaction mixture was dialyzed against ultrapure water for 4 h in a 500 Da dialysis bag to remove unencapsulated BBR. Finally, the retentate in the dialysis bag was collected as the MPC@BBR (denoted as MPC@B) solution and then freeze-dried to obtain MPC@B nanoparticles.

Preparation of HA/MPC@B

HAMA (2.4 g) and HA (1 g) were added to 5 mL of the aqueous MPC@B solution (12 mM) and stirred at 25 °C for 4 h to ensure the complete dissolution of HAMA. Then, LAP photoinitiator (0.3%, w/v) was incorporated, and UV irradiation (15 mW/cm2, 30 s) was applied to obtain the homogeneous small-molecule self-assembled nanoparticle-integrated hydrogel, denoted as HA/MPC@B.

Characterization of HA/MPC@B

The size distribution and zeta potential of the MPC and MPC@B nanoparticles in aqueous solutions (25 °C, triplicate) were determined through DLS using a Zetasizer Lab instrument (Malvern Panalytical). Their stability was monitored by tracking the particle size and PDI over time at 37 °C. The morphologies and sizes of MPC, MPC@B, and HA/MPC@B were characterized using TEM and Scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Freeze-dried nanoparticles were observed directly, whereas freeze-dried hydrogels were quenched in liquid nitrogen for 60 min, crushed, and their cross-sectional microstructures were examined. The rheological properties were evaluated using a stress-controlled rheometer (HR-1, TA Instruments) with a 20 mm plate. HAMA and HA/MPC@B, supplemented with 0.3% (w/v) LAP, were photo-crosslinked (365 nm UV, 15 mW/cm2, 30 s). Their storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli were measured at 1% strain, and the shear-thinning behavior was also evaluated.

For MPC@B composite nanoparticles, the loading capacity (LC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were quantified. Briefly, 50 mg of MPC powder was mixed with 20 mg of BBR in 10 mL of anhydrous DMSO to formulate the MPC@B dispersion. The resulting mixture was transferred into a dialysis bag (MWCO 500 Da), followed by dialysis against ultrapure water to eliminate DMSO solvent and unencapsulated BBR, yielding a purified MPC@B aqueous suspension. To fully release the encapsulated BBR, the purified MPC@B suspension was incubated in 0.1 mM H2O2 for 24 h. A blank MPC nanoparticle suspension incubated with 0.1 mM H2O2 under identical conditions was set as the blank control for background absorbance subtraction. The concentration of released BBR was determined by UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 345 nm, and the total mass of encapsulated BBR within nanoparticles was calculated according to the pre-established BBR standard curve. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± SEM. The calculation formulas for LC and EE are shown below.LC(%)=mencapsulatedBBRmMPC@B×100%EE(%)=mencapsulatedBBRmtotalBBR×100%The mencapsulated BBR represents the mass of BBR encapsulated inside nanoparticles measured after H2O2-triggered cleavage; mMPC@B is the total mass of MPC@B nanoparticles; mtotal BBR is the total feeding mass of BBR added during preparation.

To investigate the ROS-responsive drug release behavior, MPC@B nanoparticles and HA/MPC@B drug-loaded hydrogel were washed, dried, sealed in dialysis bags, and incubated in five release media: PBS (pH = 7.0), simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH = 2.0, containing pepsin), simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH = 6.8, containing trypsin), simulated colon fluid (SCF, pH = 6.8, containing colon contents), and high-ROS SCF (pH = 6.8, 0.1 mM H2O2). The drug-loaded hydrogels were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm, and 2 mL samples were collected at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h (equal medium replenished) and stored at 4 °C. Drug release was quantified using an enzyme-labeling instrument at 350 nm after the sample was warmed to 25 °C.

To investigate the stability of MPC and MPC@B under intestinal environmental conditions, aqueous solutions of MPC and MPC@B were sealed in dialysis bags and incubated in five different release media: PBS (pH = 7.0), simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH = 2.0, containing pepsin), simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH = 6.8, containing trypsin), simulated colon fluid (SCF, pH = 6.8, containing colon contents), and high-ROS SCF (pH = 6.8, 0.1 mM H2O2). The incubation was performed at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. After 24 h of incubation, samples were harvested for DLS measurement to determine particle size and PDI.

To investigate the specific ROS-scavenging effect of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B, the superoxide anion scavenging assay kit (Solarbio BC1415), TMB chromogenic assay, ABTS free radical scavenging assay kit (Solarbio BC4770), and H2O2 detection kit (Beyotime S0038) were employed. The total antioxidant capacity of MPC@B and HA/MPC@B and the specific scavenging capacity of ·OH, H2O2, and ·O2− were quantitatively analyzed.

Biocompatibility assessment

For a CCK-8 assay, Caco-2 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate with 100 μL of a complete medium (control) or 1 mg/mL MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B for 24, 48, and 72 h. Then, 10 μL of the CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plate was incubated in the dark for 1 h. Cell viability was quantified using an enzyme-labeling instrument at 450 nm and 25 °C. For live/dead cell staining, the Caco-2 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and co-cultured with 500 μL of 1 mg/mL MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B for 24, 48, and 72 h. A calcein AM/PI staining solution (1:1000 v/v in PBS) was added at 300 μL/well, and the cells were incubated at 25 °C for 10 min. Cell viability was qualitatively analyzed through fluorescence microscopy and imaging at each time point. For the drug hemolysis test, a red blood cell (RBC) suspension was prepared by removing fibrinogen from healthy anticoagulated blood, followed by repeated centrifugation and washing with 0.9% NaCl or PBS until a clear solution was obtained. Subsequently, 1 mL of H2O, PBS, and an MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B solution in PBS were added to 1.5 mL EP tubes with 20 μL of the RBC suspension. After incubation at 37 °C for 4 h, the sample was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and photographed. The absorbances of the supernatants were measured using an enzyme-labeling instrument at 540 nm (3 replicates per group).

Regulation of macrophage phenotype

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and co-cultured with 500 μL of a 1 mg/mL MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B solution. Subsequently, 5 μL of LPS was added to induce polarization, followed by 12 h of additional co-culturing. The medium was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS (3 min/wash). The cells were then fixed in 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde at 25 °C for 20 min and then washed as described previously. The cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at 25 °C for 10 min and washed three times with PBS. After being blocked with 1 mL of 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at 25 °C, primary antibodies against CD86 and CD206 were added for overnight incubation at 4 °C. The cells were washed with PBS and incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (red and green) for 1 h at 25 °C in the dark. After final PBS washes, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used for nuclear staining, and the samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Gene expression (qPCR)

RAW264.7 cells and Caco-2 cells were homogenized and lysed in 1 mL Trizol reagent. A volume of 200 μL chloroform was added to the collected supernatant. The mixture was vortexed for full mixing, settled for 10 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The upper aqueous layer was collected and mixed with an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol, vortexed, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, followed by adding 80% ethanol and centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 5 min. After air-drying, the RNA pellet was dissolved in 15 μL of RNase-free water, and the mRNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Then, the reverse transcriptase was added to convert the mRNA into cDNA, and mouse gene expression was detected using qRT-PCR.

Intracellular ROS-scavenging assay

Caco-2 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate at 2 × 104 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After removing the original medium, the experimental group was treated with a medium containing MPC, MPC@B, or HA/MPC@B (the control group was treated with PBS), and the resulting sample was incubated for 12 h. All groups were then exposed to 800 μM H2O2 for 2 h to induce cell stress. Following medium replacement with serum-free DCFH-DA medium, cells were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 0.5 h, washed three times with PBS, and visualized via fluorescence microscopy. ImageJ software was used to determine the average fluorescence intensity.

Immunofluorescence co-localization experiment

RAW264.7 macrophages in logarithmic growth phase were seeded in confocal culture dishes and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Drug treatment was performed when the cells adhered to the wall and grew to 70%-80% confluence. The cells were divided into five groups: blank control, MPC, FITC-mannose, free mannose competition, and MPC/FITC-mannose competition groups. For the MPC alone treatment group, MPC nanoparticles in culture medium at a final concentration of 100 μg/mL were added to the cells. The pure FITC-mannose group was treated with the same 100 μg/mL FITC-mannose fluorescent probe. In the free mannose competition group, the cells were pretreated with 200 μg/mL free mannose, and the mannose receptor on the cell membrane was blocked; followed by adding 100 μg/mL FITC-mannose probe. In the MPC/FITC-mannose competition group, cells were incubated with 100 μg/mL MPC nanoparticle culture medium, followed by adding 100 μg/mL FITC-mannose probe. The cells in each group were incubated for 10 h at a constant temperature.

At the end of incubation, the original culture medium was discarded, and the cells were gently washed 3 times with precooled PBS buffer to remove unadsorbed and uninternalized free materials and probes. Subsequently, 4% paraformaldehyde solution was added to fix the cells at room temperature for 15 to 20 min. After fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS to remove residual fixative. To observe the cell morphology and the position of the nucleus, the cytoskeleton and nucleus were stained with phalloidin and DAPI staining solutions in the dark, respectively. After staining, the residual staining solution was removed by rinsing with PBS. Finally, laser confocal microscopy was used for fluorescence imaging, and the distribution characteristics and fluorescence intensity of FITC green fluorescence in each group were observed to qualitatively evaluate the macrophage-targeted uptake of MPC nanoparticles.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to quantitatively analyze the uptake of RAW264.7 macrophages in each group. The experimental grouping, drug concentration, and incubation conditions were completely identical to the above immunofluorescence experiments. RAW264.7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in six-well plates. After the cells had grown to 70%-80% confluence, five groups were established: blank control, MPC, FITC-mannose, free mannose competition, and MPC/FITC-mannose competition groups. The MPC group was treated with 100 μg/mL MPC nanoparticles, while the FITC-mannose group was treated with 100 μg/mL FITC-mannose. For the free mannose competition group, cells were pretreated with 200 μg/mL free mannose, followed by treatment with 100 μg/mL FITC-mannose. For the MPC/FITC-mannose competition group, cells were treated with 100 μg/mL MPC nanoparticles, followed by co-incubation with 100 μg/mL FITC-mannose. All groups were incubated for 10 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

After incubation, the supernatant medium was discarded, and the cells were thoroughly washed with pre-cooled PBS for 3 times to remove unbound free nanoparticles and fluorescent probes. An appropriate amount of trypsin was added to each well to digest the cells, and complete medium was added to terminate the digestion after cell detachment. The cell suspension of each group was collected into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to collect the cell precipitate. The supernatant was discarded, the cells were resuspended in precooled PBS and washed by centrifugation again. The process was repeated twice to obtain a uniform cell suspension. The FITC fluorescence signal intensity of each group was detected by flow cytometry.

Determination of penetrability

Caco-2 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell chamber (0.4 μm pore size), and the seeding density was 5 × 105 cells/well. The upper and lower chambers were filled with complete medium, and the intestinal epithelial barrier model was established by continuous culturing at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 21 days. The original medium in the upper and lower chambers was discarded, and HBSS buffer containing the test sample and FITC dextran (FD4, final concentration 1 mg/mL) was added to the upper chamber, while blank HBSS buffer was introduced to the lower chamber. After the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, the fluorescence intensity of FITC-dextran in the basolateral medium was measured at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm.

Establishment of UC model

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Rui'an People's Hospital (RY202508271). The 6-8-week-old mice (20-22 g) were randomly divided into the following five groups (n = 8): control (normal drinking water), DSS (3% DSS in drinking water), MPC (3% DSS + 100 μL MPC gavage), MPC@B (3% DSS + 100 μL MPC@B gavage), and HA/MPC@B (3% DSS + 100 μL HA/MPC@B gavage). A 3% DSS solution was applied as drinking water from day 1 to day 7. The drug was administered via daily gavage from days 8 to 14, followed by euthanasia on day 15. Body weight and fecal status were collected daily to calculate the DAI. All mice in the HA/MPC@B treatment group received daily oral gavage of MPC@B nanoparticles, corresponding to a precise BBR dosage of 40 mg/kg/d.

Histological analysis

Colons were dissected from the euthanized mice, and the total length and wet weight were measured immediately. Mid-colon tissues were excised, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned to evaluate intestinal mucosal damage. H&E was performed for histological evaluation. The H&E staining was scored in a double-blind manner (0-10 points) to assess mucosal damage, inflammatory infiltration, and glandular destruction. The spleens were isolated and weighed, and the spleen index was calculated as spleen weight/body weight.

AB/PAS staining analysis

For AB-PAS-stained colon tissue sections, the mucus-positive regions (blue-purple stained area) were segmented using ImageJ software by threshold adjustment. The mucus proportion was calculated as the ratio of mucus-positive area to the total epithelial tissue area within the field of view. At least three sections per mouse were analyzed. Data were presented as mean ± SEM.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

Cy5.5 was used to label the tested nanomaterials, and the AniView Kirin in vivo imaging system was used for fluorescence imaging detection. In vivo fluorescence images were collected at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h after oral administration. The anesthesia state of mice was kept consistent during imaging, and the imaging parameter settings were standardized to reduce systematic errors. The abdomen of mice was selected as the region of interest (ROI) to evaluate the biodistribution behavior of the materials in vivo after oral administration.

Intestinal permeability assay

The in vivo intestinal permeability was detected by fluorescein isothiocyanate dextran (FITC-dextran, 4 kDa; SparkJade, China, product number: SJ-MD0155). Mice were fasted for 4 h with free access to water, followed by intragastric administration of FITC-dextran dissolved in PBS at a dose of 600 mg/kg. After 4 h of gavage, mice were anesthetized, and whole blood samples were collected and placed at room temperature to allow adequate coagulation. Serum samples were obtained by centrifugation and diluted 1:10 in PBS. The diluted samples were added to a black 96-well plate, and the fluorescence signal was detected using a microplate reader. The excitation wavelength/emission wavelength was set at 485 nm/528 nm. Using blank mouse serum as a matrix to prepare a FITC-dextran standard, a linear calibration curve was obtained to calculate the content of FITC-dextran in the serum. The final results were corrected according to sample dilution.

Gut microbiota analysis

16S rDNA gene sequencing was conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech. Microbial DNA isolated from the mouse fecal samples was subjected to amplification of the V3-V4 hypervariable regions using barcode-fused specific primers. Operational taxonomic units were defined after filtering, clustering, and denoising the amplified HiFi sequences using QIIME 2. Species annotation and abundance analysis were then conducted against the SILVA 138 database. The α-diversity was analyzed with Chao1, Simpson, ACE, and Shannon indices. β-diversity was determined via PCoA. OTU Venn diagrams and evolutionary branching diagrams were obtained using R software with the VennDiagram package and LEfSe software, respectively, and the Tax4Fun2 R package was used for microbiome functional predictions.

Transcriptome sequencing

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing were conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech using the total RNA from mice colon tissues. The analysis was conducted on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with edgeR, and functional pathway enrichment of DEGs was annotated using the clusterProfiler R package against GO and KEGG databases.
