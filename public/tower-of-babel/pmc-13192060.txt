Effect of intraoperative CO₂-targeted ventilation on postoperative grip strength recovery in older patients undergoing open abdominal surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kyongsuk Son, Kentaroh Tarao, Yuki Hateruma, Yoshihiro Yamaji, Shiori Nakao, Yuina Miyata, Atsushi Nakagomi, Shiroh Isono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrip strength is a simple marker of postoperative recovery in older adults. Although mild hypercapnia during general anesthesia improves cerebral oxygenation, its impact on postoperative functional and cognitive outcomes remains unclear, particularly in older surgical patients.MethodsThis prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted at a single tertiary hospital in Japan between April 2021 and March 2023, and was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines. Patients aged ≥ 60 years undergoing elective open abdominal surgery were randomized to intraoperative normocapnia (end-tidal CO₂ 35–40 mmHg) or mild hypercapnia (45–50 mmHg). The primary outcome was grip-strength recovery rate on POD 7, expressed as a percentage of baseline, and analyzed using analysis of covariance with multiple imputation. Secondary outcomes included early postoperative delirium, perioperative cerebral oxygen saturation, and postoperative complications.ResultsA total of 120 randomized patients were included in the primary analysis using multiple imputation. Grip strength decreased on POD 1 and partially recovered by POD 7, with no significant difference in grip-strength recovery rate between the normocapnia and mild hypercapnia groups (adjusted mean difference − 0.74% points; 95% CI − 6.51 to 5.02; P = 0.798).Intraoperative regional cerebral oxygen saturation was higher in the mild hypercapnia group; however, no improvement was observed in functional or delirium-based cognitive outcomes. Lower grip strength recovery rate was independently associated with a higher incidence of early postoperative complications (risk ratio 1.22; 95% CI 1.06–1.39).ConclusionsIntraoperative mild hypercapnia increased cerebral oxygenation but did not improve early postoperative functional or cognitive recovery in older patients undergoing open abdominal surgery. Grip strength recovery rate may serve as a simple, non-invasive indicator of early postoperative risk. These findings suggest that perioperative management should consider prioritizing overall hemodynamic stability rather than modulation of end-tidal CO₂ alone.Trial registrationUMIN Clinical Trials Registry: UMIN000043793. Introduction

Grip strength has gained recognition as a robust and clinically meaningful marker of physical function in both perioperative and geriatric medicine [1, 2]. In older surgical patients, decreased grip strength has been associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including higher complication rates, prolonged hospitalization, delayed mobilization, and mortality [2–4]. Its simplicity, reproducibility, and bedside applicability make grip strength a practical and prognostically relevant endpoint for monitoring early postoperative recovery, especially in older adults.

Mild hypercapnia is a physiological approach for enhancing perfusion. Elevated end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) induces vasodilation, increasing both coronary [5, 6] and cerebral blood flow [7]. Unlike pharmacological vasodilators or propofol, hypercapnia achieves cerebrovascular vasodilation through a physiological CO₂-mediated mechanism without direct systemic cardiovascular depression or suppression of cerebral metabolic activity [8, 9]. Previous studies have demonstrated that hypercapnia improves cerebral oxygenation [7], and our previous randomized controlled trial suggested that it may also facilitate faster emergence from anesthesia [10]. In addition, several observational studies have reported associations between mild hypercapnia and reduced postoperative delirium or improved clinical outcomes [11–13].

However, the clinical significance of these changes remains unclear. Subsequent randomized trials have primarily shown improvements in regional cerebral oxygen saturation(rSO2) rather than consistent benefits in functional or cognitive recovery [14–16]. This inconsistency raises the question of whether improved cerebral oxygenation alone translates to better recovery in elderly surgical patients. In the present study, grip strength was selected as a functional outcome because, although it is not directly determined by arterial carbon dioxide levels, it reflects integrated peripheral muscle function and central motor control, which may be influenced by perioperative perfusion and hemodynamic conditions. Based on these considerations, we conducted a randomised controlled trial to evaluate whether intraoperative mild hypercapnia (ETCO₂ 45–50 mmHg), compared to normocapnia (ETCO₂ 35–40 mmHg), improves early postoperative recovery in elderly patients undergoing elective open abdominal surgery. ETCO₂ was used to guide the intervention, as it strongly correlates with PaCO₂ under controlled conditions and our study excluded patients with significant pulmonary disease [17–19]. The primary endpoint was grip strength recovery rate on postoperative day 7, and secondary outcomes included delirium, complications, and cerebral oxygenation.

Method

Ethical approval

This study received ethical approval from the Clinical Ethics Committee of Chiba University Hospital (approval number: 4017, M10783; Chairperson: Professor Iyo; Approval date: 02 February 2021) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant clinical research ethical guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. The study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) clinical trial registry (Registration number: UMIN000043793; https://center6.umin.ac.jp/cgi-open-bin/ctr/ctr_view.cgi?recptno=R000049404).

Patient selection

This study was a prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial conducted at Chiba University Hospital between May 16, 2021, and January 24, 2023. A total of 120 patients aged 60 years who underwent older undergoing elective open abdominal surgery under general anesthesia were recruited. Eligible participants were those scheduled for elective open abdominal surgery who provided written informed consent. This randomized trial was designed and in accordance with the CONSORT Statement [20]. A completed CONSORT flow diagram is provided in Fig. 1. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) undergoing laparoscopic surgery (2), diagnosed with pulmonary hypertension (3), obstructive pulmonary disease (4), congenital heart disease (5), increased intracranial pressure.

Randomization

Participants were randomized using a minimization method to achieve balance between the groups. The minimization algorithm incorporated sex and baseline grip strength as stratification variables to ensure balanced allocation of key prognostic factors.

Patients were assigned to one of two intraoperative ventilation targets:

This study used a single-blind design. Only the attending anesthesiologist was aware of the treatment allocation required implement the assigned ETCO₂ target. The participants and outcome assessors remained blinded to the group assignment to maintain objectivity throughout the study.

Data collection

Data collection included preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative assessments. Preoperatively, demographic and clinical data such as age, sex, height, weight, medical history, medications, and surgical details were recorded. Exercise tolerance was evaluated using metabolic equivalent of task (METs) [21] via a medical questionnaire.(Supplementary file1) Baseline cognitive function was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU(CAM-ICU), while grip strength was measured in a seated position using a Grip-D🄬 TKK5401 hand dynamometer (Takei Scientific Instruments, Japan) on the dominant hand.

During surgery, general anesthesia was maintained with desflurane following epidural catheter placement. The tidal volume was set at 8 mL/kg of the ideal body weight, and ETCO₂ was continuously monitored using a side-stream capnometer. Patients in the hypercapnia group had ETCO₂ maintained between 45 and 50 mmHg, while those in the normocapnia group maintained ETCO₂ between 35 and 40 mmHg. Target ETCO₂ was achieved by adjusting the respiratory rate alone, without altering tidal volume. PEEP was set at 5 cmH₂O, with minor adjustments permitted at the attending anesthesiologist’s discretion based on individual patient characteristics. FiO₂ was initiated at 0.4 and titrated upward as needed to maintain SpO₂ between 95% and 100%, typically not exceeding 0.5.

Blood pressure, heart rate, SpO₂, surgical time, estimated blood loss, and time to emergence from anesthesia were recorded. Regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) was monitored using INVOS® (Medtronic, USA) throughout anesthesia. Intraoperative hypotension was defined as a mean arterial pressure (MAP) below 65 mmHg. Body temperature was continuously monitored using a bladder temperature probe. Active warming was maintained using a forced-air warming blanket, with a target core temperature of ≥ 37.0 °C throughout the procedure.

Postoperative assessments were conducted on postoperative days (POD) 1 and 7. Grip strength was measured following the same protocol as the used for preoperative assessment. Delirium screening was performed using the CAM-ICU [22] on POD 1 and 7. Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) on POD 1, and 7. Postoperative complications were defined as those of Clavien-Dindo classification [23] Grade 2 or higher. Data on complications were collected up to one week and one month postoperatively.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study was the grip strength recovery rate on POD 7, calculated as the ratio of grip strength on POD 7 to baseline grip strength, expressed as a percentage based on an index used in previous studies [24]. Secondary outcomes included grip strength on POD 1, incidence of postoperative delirium as determined by CAM-ICU evaluations on POD1 and 7, pain intensity assessed by NRS, intraoperative rSO₂ changes, and length of postoperative hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1.9.7. Based on a two-tailed independent-samples t-test, an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.56 was used, corresponding to a between-group difference of 20% with a standard deviation of 35.5%. The standard deviation was derived from the grip-strength recovery rate reported in the non-complication group of a prior prospective observational study (100.2 ± 35.5%) [24], in which a grip-strength recovery rate below 83.92% was identified as the optimal ROC cutoff for predicting 30-day postoperative complications (AUC = 0.837). A between-group difference of 20% was defined as the minimum clinically important difference, representing an improvement sufficient to bring patients above this established risk threshold. To detect this difference with 80% power at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, 50 participants were required per group. To account for potential data loss, 60 participants per group were recruited (n = 120).

Missing outcome data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE; m = 50; predictive mean matching) under a missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model included the treatment group, age, sex, ASA physical status (ASA-PS), operative time, and MAP.

The primary analysis of the grip-strength recovery rate on POD 7 was performed using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with the treatment group (hypercapnia vs. normocapnia) as the main effect and age, sex, ASA-PS, operative time, and mean intraoperative MAP as covariates. Sensitivity analyses were performed to confirm the robustness of primary findings. Analyses using [1] POD7 absolute grip strength (with baseline adjustment) and [2] POD1 grip-strength recovery rate were conducted using the same multiple-imputation datasets as the primary analysis. In addition [3], an inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting (IPCW) analysis based on complete-case data was performed to evaluate the potential impact of dropout bias. All sensitivity analyses produced results consistent with the primary analysis.

As a secondary analysis, absolute grip strength values at three time points (baseline, postoperative day 1, and day 7) were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model. A linear mixed-effects model including time (POD1 and POD7), group, and their interaction as fixed effects and subject ID as a random intercept was also fitted to assess longitudinal changes in recovery. This model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood, and p-values were calculated using Satterthwaite’s approximation. (Table 2)

The incidence of postoperative delirium and other categorical variables was analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. (Tables 1 and 3) Continuous variables were analyzed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on the data distribution (assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test). (Tables 1 and 3)

Exploratory multivariable linear regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with POD7 grip-strength recovery rate ; explanatory variables included sex, preoperative grip strength, surgery duration, mean intraoperative MAP, preoperative albumin, and group assignment.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 120 patients were randomized to either the normocapnia group (ETCO₂ 35–40 mmHg, n = 59) or the hypercapnia group (ETCO₂ 45–50 mmHg, n = 61).

Nineteen patients (14 in the hypercapnia group and 5 in the normocapnia group) were not assessed for postoperative grip strength and were therefore excluded from the complete-case analysis (Fig. 1).

On POD 1, ten patients were not evaluated because of measurement omission or technical error (n = 8), impaired consciousness associated with systemic deterioration (n = 1), or peripheral neuropathy of the dominant hand (n = 1). On POD 7, nineteen patients were discharged before planned assessment. Accordingly, 47 patients in the hypercapnia group and 54 patients in the normocapnia group were included in the complete-case analysis. Missing outcome data were handled using multiple imputation (MICE; m = 50) for the primary analysis.

The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1, which includes all randomized participants (intention-to-treat population) regardless of missing outcome data. (Table 1). Preoperative grip strength scores did not differ significantly between groups. The target ETCO₂ range was maintained in all cases according to the protocol. As expected, mean intraoperative ETCO₂ and bilateral rSO₂ values were significantly higher in the hypercapnia group.

Primary outcome: grip strength recovery rate

The primary outcome was grip strength recovery rate, defined as the postoperative grip strength divided by the preoperative grip strength.

After multiple imputation and adjustment for age, sex, ASA-PS, operative time, and mean intraoperative MAP, there was no significant difference in POD7 grip-strength recovery rate between the groups (adjusted mean difference = − 0.74% points; 95% CI − 6.51 to 5.02; P = 0.798). The recovery rate tended to increase at postoperative week 1 compared to day 1 (estimate: +0.039, P = 0.064), although this was not statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses,—including [1] POD7 absolute grip strength with baseline grip as a covariate [2], POD1 recovery rate, and [3] an IPCW model,—yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (all P > 0.3).

A linear mixed-effects model also showed no significant group or group × time interactions (Table 2; Fig. 2-A), indicating that the recovery pattern over time was similar between the groups. These findings remained consistent even after additional adjustment for intraoperative hypotension time, age, and baseline grip strength.

Secondary outcome: absolute grip strength

In a secondary analysis combining both groups, grip strength significantly declined on postoperative day 1 compared to baseline (24.8 ± 8.1 vs. 21.5 ± 7.4 kg, P < 0.001), and remained significantly lower on day 7 (23.2 ± 8.4 kg, p = 0.0009). No significant group differences or group-by-time interactions were found at any time point (all P > 0.6). (Table 2; Fig. 2-B) After adjustment for age, sex, ASA-PS, operative time, and mean intraoperative MAP, the results were unchanged.

On POD 7, 68.3% of the patients did not returned to their baseline grip strength, and 11.9% had recovery rates of ≤ 80%.

The incidence and types of postoperative complications at one week and 30 days did not differ significantly between groups. (Table 3) However, patients who did not regain baseline grip strength by POD 7 experienced significantly more complications in the first postoperative week, with a risk ratio of 1.22 (95% CI: 1.06–1.39).

Intraoperative hemodynamic and associations of recovery

There was no significant group difference in the duration of intraoperative hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg), although the hypercapnia group showed a trend toward longer hypotension. The mean intraoperative MAP was significantly lower in the hypercapnia group, whereas norepinephrine use and total fluid volume were comparable between groups.

In an exploratory multivariable linear regression analysis, the overall model was not statistically significant (P = 0.189; adjusted R² = 0.029). However, male sex was positively associated with grip strength recovery rate at postoperative day 7, whereas longer duration of intraoperative hypotension was associated with reduced recovery rate. Other factors, including preoperative grip strength, surgery time, preoperative albumin level, and hypercapnia intervention, were not significantly associated with the recovery rate. (Table 4)

Discussion

Our randomized controlled trial demonstrated that intraoperative ETCO₂-targeted ventilation at higher levels increased regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) but did not improve postoperative grip strength recovery rate or cognitive outcomes. This dissociation between physiological changes and clinical recovery suggests that modulation of ETCO₂ alone is insufficient to enhance early postoperative functional or cognitive outcomes.

These findings align with several previous studies that reported increased cerebral blood flow or rSO₂ under hypercapnia [25–28], without corresponding improvements in neurocognitive performance. Mild hypercapnia increases coronary and cerebral blood flow through vasodilation [5, 25], but the same vasodilatory effect may lower MAP [11, 16] and compromise peripheral tissue perfusion. This physiological trade-off may explain why an increased rSO₂ did not lead to improved functional recovery. In our trial, patients with significant pulmonary disease were excluded and ventilatory settings were standardized, supporting the validity of ETCO₂ as a surrogate for PaCO₂. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that in older patients, increased closing capacity and ventilation-perfusion mismatch may widen the PaCO₂–ETCO₂ gradient, potentially causing ETCO₂ to underestimate true arterial CO₂. The use of ETCO₂ rather than PaCO₂ was an intentional design choice, reflecting our aim to evaluate a ventilation strategy feasible in routine clinical settings where invasive arterial monitoring is not universally employed. Although arterial blood gas data were available in a subset of patients, the frequency of missing values precluded a reliable gradient analysis across the full cohort.

Our exploratory analysis showed that the hypercapnia group exhibited a lower average intraoperative mean arterial pressure (MAP), suggesting that vasodilation may offset any increase in cardiac output [16]. Since tissue perfusion depends not only on oxygen content but also on perfusion pressure, suboptimal MAP may have limited oxygen delivery to peripheral tissues, including the skeletal muscle. Age-related changes in skeletal muscle microcirculation may theoretically make older adults more susceptible to decreases in perfusion pressure. Although hypercapnia has been shown to augment peripheral tissue perfusion and cardiac output in healthy volunteers [29], peripheral muscle blood flow was not directly assessed in the present study, and whether this mechanism contributed to the observed outcomes remains to be determined in future studies [30]. We also found a trend suggesting that prolonged intraoperative hypotension (MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) was associated with poorer grip strength recovery rate and supports the notion that perfusion pressure may be more influential than CO₂ levels alone in determining functional recovery [31].

Importantly, patients who failed to recover their baseline grip strength by postoperative day 7 experienced significantly more complications in the early postoperative period, consistent with prior evidence linking grip strength to frailty and adverse outcomes [2–4]. Our results extend this concept by demonstrating that early postoperative grip-strength recovery rate may serve as a simple, non-invasive surrogate marker to guide postoperative risk stratification and individualized care.

No significant differences were observed between the groups in the incidence of postoperative delirium. Although postoperative cognitive dysfunction was not directly assessed, our findings are consistent with previous studies [15, 28] that reported no cognitive benefit from intraoperative hypercapnia.

Limitation

This study had several limitations. First, cognitive outcomes were evaluated only through delirium screening, and the study was not powered to detect subtle cognitive changes; formal neuropsychological assessment of POCD was not performed.

Second, follow-up was limited to the early postoperative phase, and longer-term outcomes were not assessed. Although METs were recorded preoperatively, the narrow distribution across the cohort provided insufficient statistical power to detect a meaningful correlation with postoperative grip-strength recovery rate, and a post-hoc correlation analysis was therefore not performed.

Third, several potentially influential factors were not systematically assessed, including electrolyte imbalances, temperature dysregulation, metabolic acidosis, and nutritional status. While no formal prehabilitation program was implemented, all patients received standard postoperative rehabilitation care including early mobilization. Time to emergence was also not prospectively evaluated; whether hypercapnia-facilitated earlier emergence contributes to functional recovery warrants investigation in future studies.

Fourth, the multivariable regression analysis was exploratory and did not reach statistical significance, despite suggesting a potential association between intraoperative hypotension and impaired grip-strength recovery.

Finally, although more patients in the hypercapnia group were excluded from the complete-case analysis (15 vs. 4), most exclusions resulted from early discharge or technical omissions rather than adverse events. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation, all sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary findings, and baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups, suggesting that attrition did not introduce meaningful selection bias.

Conclusion

Intraoperative mild hypercapnia improved cerebral oxygenation but did not enhance postoperative grip strength—regardless of recovery rate, absolute values—or cognitive recovery. However, the grip strength recovery rate was associated with early postoperative complications, supporting its potential as a simple, non-invasive clinical marker of recovery capacity. These findings suggest that perioperative strategies to enhance postoperative outcomes should prioritize hemodynamic optimization and individualized perfusion management rather than relying solely on modulation of ETCO₂.
