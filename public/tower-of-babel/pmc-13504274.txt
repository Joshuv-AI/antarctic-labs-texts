Hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia in renal glucosuria in the presence of a novel sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 variant
Authors: Sinéad Cadogan, Amanda Madden, Olivia O’Dwyer, Karl Kavanagh, James J O’Byrne, Maria M Byrne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal glucosuria (OMIM #233100) is a rare inherited condition caused by pathogenic variants in SLC5A2, which encodes sodium–glucose cotransporter 2. Although typically considered asymptomatic, renal glucosuria has recently been associated with hypoglycemia, with only 4 cases reported to date. We describe an 18-year-old woman with persistent glucosuria and recurrent postprandial hypoglycemia, presenting with lightheadedness, fatigue, and hunger. A 24-hour urine collection showed elevated glucose excretion of 4 g/1.73 m2/day (22.2 mmol/1.73 m2/day; reference range: 0-0.32 g/1.73 m2/day [0-1.8 mmol/1.73 m2/day]). A 5-hour oral glucose tolerance test confirmed symptomatic hypoglycemia with an exaggerated insulin and C-peptide response, consistent with reactive hyperinsulinemia. Genetic testing identified a novel heterozygous potentially significant SLC5A2 variant which may affect a functionally important amino acid. The patient’s symptoms improved substantially following dietary counseling and adherence to a low-glycemic index diet. This case suggests that hypoglycemia in SLC5A2-related renal glucosuria may result from a hyperinsulinemic response rather than urinary glucose loss alone, a mechanism that has received limited attention in the literature. It supports considering renal glucosuria in patients with unexplained hypoglycemia and highlights the value of prolonged glucose tolerance testing and potentially genetic analysis.

Introduction

The SLC5A2 gene encodes sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2), the transporter responsible for reabsorbing ∼90% of filtered glucose in the renal proximal tubules [1]. Loss-of-function variants in SLC5A2 impair glucose reabsorption, leading to renal glucosuria—a rare condition characterized by urinary glucose loss despite normal blood glucose levels [2]. To date, >110 SLC5A2 variants have been described, most of which remain classified as variants of uncertain significance [3]. This reflects the marked allelic heterogeneity of renal glucosuria, in which the majority of reported variants are private [3]. The condition typically follows a codominant inheritance pattern and exhibits variable penetrance [2, 3]. Significant heterozygous variants often result in mild glucosuria, whereas homozygous or compound heterozygous variants may cause more substantial glucose excretion [3].

Renal glucosuria is generally considered benign and asymptomatic. However, rare cases of hypoglycemia have been reported, with only 4 documented to date [4-7]. Of these, only 2 were symptomatic. Syed et al described a young woman with postprandial hypoglycemia occurring 2 to 3 hours after meals and marked glucosuria (>1000 mg/dL [55.5 mmol/L]) [4]. Ottosson-Laakso et al reported a compound heterozygous female with recurrent postprandial hypoglycemia, chronic urinary tract infections, and urinary glucose excretion of 55.2 g/day (3063 mmol/day) [5]. Sada et al identified frequent hypoglycemic excursions on continuous glucose monitoring in a patient identified through cascade screening [6], and Papadimitriou et al reported a 2-year-old with failure to thrive, renal glucosuria and asymptomatic hypoglycemia, successfully managed with uncooked cornstarch [7]. These reports underscore the phenotypic variability of SLC5A2 variants and suggest that symptomatic hypoglycemia may occur only in a subset of individuals, possibly influenced by genotype or other modifying factors.

Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, such cases offer insight into the systemic effects of chronic glucosuria and possible compensatory adaptations in glucose regulation. We describe a case of renal glucosuria potentially due to a novel SLC5A2 variant in an adolescent female presenting with symptomatic postprandial hypoglycemia. This case challenges the assumption that hypoglycemia in renal glucosuria results solely from urinary glucose loss and instead points to a broader systemic role for SGLT2 in glucose homeostasis.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old woman was referred for evaluation of persistent glucosuria and recurrent episodes of hypoglycemia. Glucosuria was first identified at age 14 during investigation for recurrent urinary tract infections, which continued intermittently over the following 4 years. From age 16, she began experiencing frequent episodes of lightheadedness, fatigue, hunger, sweating, and headaches. These episodes typically occurred a few hours after meals, resolved with food intake, and significantly impacted her daily functioning, including school performance. Capillary glucose monitoring during symptomatic episodes confirmed hypoglycemia, with values as low as 38 mg/dL (2.1 mmol/L). She denied having any episodes whilst fasting.

She had no significant medical history and was not taking any regular medications. There was no family history of diabetes, renal disease, or hypoglycemia. Her parents were nonconsanguineous. On examination, height was 1.765 m, weight was 72 kg, and body mass index was 23.1 kg/m2. Cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

Diagnostic assessment

Baseline biochemical evaluation, including renal, hepatic, and electrolyte profiles, was unremarkable. Glycemic markers were within normal limits, with a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 5.4% (35 mmol/mol; reference range: 4.0%-6.0% [20-42 mmol/mol]) and a fasting glucose concentration of 77 mg/dL (4.3 mmol/L; reference range: 70-99 mg/dL [3.9-5.5 mmol/L]). The fasting C-peptide level was 2.4 ng/mL (797 pmol/L; reference range: 0.8-5.2 ng/mL [260-1730 pmol/L]).

Endocrine evaluation showed a morning serum cortisol concentration of 18.7 µg/dL (515 nmol/L; reference range: 5.4-16.5 µg/dL [150-455 nmol/L]), a thyroid-stimulating hormone concentration of 2.02 mIU/L (reference range: 0.35-4.94 mIU/L), and a free thyroxine concentration of 0.85 ng/dL (11.0 pmol/L; reference range: 0.7-1.5 ng/dL [9.0-20.0 pmol/L]). The insulin-like growth factor 1 level was 206 ng/mL (26.9 nmol/L; reference range: 123-546 ng/mL [16.1-71.4 nmol/L]). A 24-hour urine collection revealed elevated urinary glucose excretion of 4 g/1.73 m2/day (22.2 mmol/1.73 m2/day). There was no biochemical evidence of generalized proximal tubule dysfunction. Urinary phosphate excretion was 17 mmol/24 hours (reference range: 16-48 mmol/24 hours), urinary urate was 2.3 mmol/24 hours (reference range: 1.5-4.4 mmol/24 hours), and amino acid excretion was within normal limits. The urine protein:creatinine ratio was 7.7 mg/mmol (0.0077 g/mol; reference range: 0-15 mg/mmol [0-0.015 g/mol]). These findings excluded Fanconi syndrome and supported a diagnosis of isolated renal glucosuria.

In the evaluation of recurrent hypoglycemia, a detailed history excluded exogenous, metabolic, and behavioral causes. There was no history of weight loss, disordered eating, alcohol excess, bariatric or gastrointestinal surgery, or exposure to insulin or insulin secretagogues. Weight was stable, and there were no clinical features suggestive of an eating disorder. Hypoglycemia occurred exclusively in the postprandial state, with no fasting episodes, making insulinoma very unlikely.

To evaluate her hypoglycemic symptoms, a prolonged 5-hour oral glucose tolerance test was performed following an overnight fast of at least 8 hours, using a 75 g oral glucose load. Serial measurements of plasma glucose, insulin, and C-peptide were obtained every 30 minutes (Table 1). The test demonstrated an exaggerated early insulin and C-peptide response, peaking at 30 minutes. This was followed by progressive hypoglycemia with a glucose nadir of 50 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) at 180 minutes. At this time, the patient reported nausea, hunger, lightheadedness, and fatigue—symptoms consistent with her typical episodes. These findings are consistent with reactive hyperinsulinemia as a driver of postprandial hypoglycemia. The insulin:C-peptide molar ratio remained <1 throughout testing, supporting endogenous insulin secretion.

Given the persistent glucosuria in the absence of hyperglycemia or evidence of renal dysfunction, genetic testing was performed. Sequencing of a glycogen storage disorder gene panel identified a heterozygous missense variant in the SLC5A2 gene: c.394C>A;p.Arg132Ser [8]. This variant has not been previously reported in disease-specific variant databases or the published literature and is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance. Notably, 2 other pathogenic variants involving the same codon have been reported in association with renal glucosuria, supporting the functional importance of this residue and increasing the suspicion that the identified variant may play a role in pathogenesis.

Treatment

No pharmacologic intervention was required. The patient was reviewed by a dietitian and provided with dietary counseling on following a low-glycemic index diet.

Outcome and follow-up

Following dietary education and lifestyle modifications, the patient reported a marked reduction in the frequency and severity of hypoglycemic episodes. Over a 12-month follow-up period, she remained symptomatically improved with no documented episodes of severe hypoglycemia requiring assistance. She remains under outpatient endocrinology follow-up.

Family screening was limited. The patient's mother and sister, the only available relatives, did not demonstrate glucosuria on urinalysis, with urinary glucose concentrations of 3.6 mg/dL (0.2 mmol/L) and 1.8 mg/dL (0.1 mmol/L), respectively (reference range: <18 mg/dL [<1.0 mmol/L]). No additional family members were accessible for genetic testing to assess segregation of the SLC5A2 variant.

Discussion

This case demonstrates that postprandial hypoglycemia occurring in renal glucosuria in the presence of an SLC5A2 variant may be associated with an exaggerated hyperinsulinemic response, a mechanism that has received limited attention in the literature. This finding challenges the conventional assumption that hypoglycemia in renal glucosuria results solely from excessive urinary glucose loss and suggests a more complex systemic role for SGLT2 in glucose homeostasis [9].

Although the identified SLC5A2 variant remains classified as a variant of uncertain significance and segregation analysis was not feasible, this is not entirely unexpected as renal glucosuria is characterized by substantial allelic diversity and a predominance of private variants [3, 10]. The marked phenotypic concordance in our patient supports consideration of the variant as a plausible contributor to the observed phenotype.

In our patient, a 5-hour oral glucose tolerance test revealed symptomatic hypoglycemia accompanied by an exaggerated insulin and C-peptide response, consistent with reactive hyperinsulinemia. Notably, urinary glucose excretion was relatively modest, suggesting that the extent of glucosuria may not directly correlate with hypoglycemic risk.

One potential explanation for the unexpected hyperinsulinemic response observed in our patient lies in the emerging evidence that SGLT2 may play a role beyond the kidney—specifically, within pancreatic islet cells. While SGLT2 is well-established in renal proximal tubules and has been confirmed in pancreatic α cells, its expression in β cells remains under investigation [11]. Ottosson-Laakso et al analyzed pancreatic islets from 131 human donors using RNA sequencing [5]. They found that SLC5A2 is expressed in islet cells, albeit at lower levels than SLC5A1. Importantly, SLC5A2 expression demonstrated a strong positive correlation with INS (the insulin gene) but not with the glucagon gene or glucagon secretion, suggesting possible co-expression with β-cell–specific genes. While the functional significance of this correlation remains uncertain, it suggests a potential role for SGLT2 in β-cell physiology. A pathogenic SLC5A2 variant therefore could plausibly alter β-cell glucose sensing or intracellular glucose handling, leading to dysregulated insulin secretion and reactive hypoglycemia.

A second possible mechanism involves compensatory metabolic adaptations to chronic glucosuria. Persistent urinary glucose loss may induce a subtle energy deficit, increasing β-cell sensitivity to postprandial glucose elevations as the body attempts to preserve energy balance, and may contribute to the exaggerated insulin response observed during the oral glucose tolerance test.

Historically, individuals with familial renal glucosuria have been considered asymptomatic and not at risk of hypoglycemia—a view that supported the development of SGLT2 inhibitors as safe therapies with minimal hypoglycemic risk [12, 13]. However, our findings suggest that this assumption may not hold true for all SLC5A2 variants, highlighting the need to better understand the systemic effects of lifelong SGLT2 disruption. Beyond its genetic implications, this has broader clinical relevance given the widespread use of SGLT2 inhibitors in type 2 diabetes, where they offer substantial glycemic, cardiovascular, and renal benefits [14, 15]. Studying individuals with lifelong SGLT2 dysfunction may provide insights into the long-term physiological effects of chronic glucosuria and inform therapeutic safety considerations.

This case highlights the potential for symptomatic hypoglycemia in a condition generally considered benign. Clinicians managing patients with renal glucosuria should maintain a high index of suspicion for hypoglycemic symptoms and consider extended glucose tolerance testing or continuous glucose monitoring in such cases. Genetic analysis may aid diagnosis and risk stratification, support variant interpretation, and guide management.

Further research is needed to determine whether hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia is unique to specific SLC5A2 variants or reflects a broader physiological consequence of SGLT2 dysfunction. Longitudinal studies of individuals with SLC5A2-related renal glucosuria, including detailed metabolic profiling and genotype–phenotype correlation, are needed to clarify the systemic effects of chronic glucosuria and the risk of associated complications such as hypoglycemia.

In summary, this case illustrates a potential association between SLC5A2-related renal glucosuria and hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, suggesting a possible expanded physiological role for SGLT2 beyond the kidney.
