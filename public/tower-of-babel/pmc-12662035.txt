Omalizumab to dupilumab switch in chronic spontaneous urticaria☆
Authors: Öner Özdemir
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

I have read the article titled "Effectiveness of transitioning from omalizumab to dupilumab in chronic spontaneous urticaria patients with inadequate response to omalizumab" by Hayama et al with great interest.1 Nevertheless, a couple of concerns were raised in my mind about their brief research report.1 And clarifying these concerns of this study will help understand the study better.

In the first paragraph of article,1 these 2 sentences already contradict the study's logic, which states that “omalizumab showed efficacy after 6 months” and “clinical studies mention that no significant improvement after 24 weeks of dupilumab“. These sentences do not reflect the actual literature.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 They say that the success of this kind of work is not well documented in the literature. Both articles conclude that omalizumab must be used for at least 6 months, and even when used for 24 weeks (6 months), dupilumab remains ineffective in cases of non-response. In fact, the literature already stated that this study would not be successful in this situation, but the authors decided to try it anyway. Moreover, the authors' methods were not designed from the outset in accordance with the literature.1 The duration of use for omalizumab and dupilumab is less than 6 months. Ultimately, I believe the results did not differ from what the literature had previously stated.

I can list my other 3 concerns regarding the study method as follows: To accurately assess the effectiveness of omalizumab, the standard application should have been administered every 4 weeks, but in some patients, the intervals were extended to 6–8 weeks. As can be seen from Table 1 and it appears that in 2 patients (patients 2 and 7), it was administered within less than 4 weeks.1 Second-generation antihistamines should have been increased up to 4 times before omalizumab, but here it is said that they were increased up to 2 times.1 Five of the 12 enrolled patients have AD and asthma.1 In these cases, it is complex and problematic even to say that their urticaria is chronic spontaneous urticaria.

Some interpretations and conclusions of the study results in the article1 are incorrectly stated. This sentence, right at the beginning (the dupilumab arm showed a numerically higher disease control rate), is also misleading or exaggerated. The article's following statement ("4 out of 12 patients showed decreased UCT scores compared to baseline before starting dupilumab.") is entirely incorrect, and only 2 patients had decreased UCT scores. The results obtained do not match the title of the study.1 It is stated that the transition to dupilumab is effective, but this is not true for most patients. We are also having difficulty understanding where and how the "3 UCT points for the minimum significant difference" mentioned in the article1 were derived and decided. In the abstract and methods, receiving at least 4 doses of omalizumab is required for study participation. However, as seen in Table 1, patient 6 has only used omalizumab for 3 months.

In Table 1 of Article,1 UCT scores improved only partially in 4/12 patients. Only 2 of these are ≥12 and are well controlled. The average score even decreased after discontinuing dupilumab after 4 months. Even though the omalizumab response was not good, the score at least increased from an average of 6.5 ± 2.2 to an average of 8.4 ± 3.0. In omalizumab treatment, 2 people's UCT scores decreased, 2 remained stable, and 8 increased. With dupilumab, 3 people's scores decreased, 2 remained stable, and 7 increased. Dupilumab seemed to work in the 2 people who remained stable with omalizumab and the 1 who decreased. Only 3 people scored ≥12 after 1 month and 4 months with dupilumab.1

Figure 1a1 shows that the mean UCT scores before and after dupilumab treatments are significant, but the effect of both drugs (omalizumab + dupilumab) is present here. It is incorrect to attribute this cumulative effect to only dupilumab. The accurate and independent impact of dupilumab is better seen in the comparison after 1 and 4 months, and a decrease in the mean effect is observed. Perhaps combining these drugs, as reported in a recent study,2 could be more effective. Compared to before omalizumab, the UCT scores of 12 out of 14 patients had increased after dupilumab, except for patients 1 and 3. Figure 1b1 shows that the effect of the drugs is not clearly positive in one direction, but rather a mixed situation.

I disagree with the authors that dupilumab may improve disease control in a subset of omalizumab-resistant CSU patients. When examining the effect of dupilumab at 1 and 4 months, without the cumulative effect of omalizumab, it is seen that the average score decreased rather than increased. Only 3 people had a score of ≥12 after dupilumab. Interestingly, in 3 patients who returned to omalizumab after an inadequate response to dupilumab, the UCT score reached ≥12.

In conclusion, I would like to thank the authors for this excellent, high-quality study and its results, which have increased our awareness of omalizumab, dupilumab, and the interchangeability and efficacy of these 2 drugs.
