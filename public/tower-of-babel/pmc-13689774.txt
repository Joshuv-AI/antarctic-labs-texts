Directional Coronary Atherectomy as a Diagnostic Adjunct in Suspected IgG4-Related Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Ayaka Ujita, Shunsuke Maruta, Kazuya Shinouchi, Daiki Akiyama, Hideaki Aihara, Hidetaka Nishina, Yuichi Noguchi, Masayoshi Ozawa, Tomoko Ishizu, Akimune Kuwayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunoglobulin (Ig) G4-related disease can involve the cardiovascular system, and isolated vessel involvement is diagnostically challenging.Case SummaryA 79-year-old Japanese man with a history of percutaneous coronary intervention presented with exertional angina. History of Presentation

A 79-year-old Japanese man with exertional angina was referred to our hospital. He had undergone percutaneous coronary intervention to the left anterior descending artery (LAD) more than 20 years earlier. Coronary computed tomography (CT) angiography demonstrated in-stent chronic total occlusion (CTO) of the LAD and severe stenosis in the distal left circumflex artery (LCX), along with marked adventitial thickening involving both arteries (Figure 1). Whole-body contrast-enhanced CT also showed diffuse adventitial/perivascular thickening extending from the ascending aorta to the terminal aorta and common iliac arteries, most prominently around the terminal aorta. Although calcified atherosclerotic changes were present, these findings suggested possible immunoglobulin (Ig) G4-related vascular involvement superimposed on concomitant atherosclerotic disease. No organomegaly, lacrimal or salivary gland enlargement, retroperitoneal fibrosis, lymphadenopathy, or other inflammatory lesions were observed on CT (Video 1). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography showed no findings suggestive of IgG4-related pancreaticobiliary disease. After consultation with rheumatology, the patient was considered to have IgG4-related disease predominantly involving the coronary arteries and aorta. Serum IgG and IgG4 levels were 4,759 mg/dL and 3,260 mg/dL, respectively.

Past Medical History

The patient’s medical history included dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and hypergammaglobulinemia. His long-term medications included aspirin and pitavastatin.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included systemic vasculitis, infectious aortitis, malignant disorders such as lymphoma or multiple myeloma, and other autoimmune diseases. Serological test results and blood cultures were negative, and immunoelectrophoresis showed polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia without monoclonal gammopathy. Together with markedly elevated serum IgG4 levels and characteristic imaging findings, IgG4-related disease was considered the most likely diagnosis.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved myocardial wall thickness in the LAD territory. Coronary angiography revealed an in-stent CTO of the LAD, with a blunt-type entry. The distal LCX exhibited 90% stenosis (Figure 2, Video 2-1 and 2-2). Based on the contrast-enhanced CT findings showing marked adventitial thickening around the distal LCX, the lesion was suspected to have a substantial inflammatory component and to be potentially responsive to steroid therapy. Because the lesion was located in a distal branch and was not considered an appropriate target for percutaneous coronary intervention, revascularization and intravascular imaging were deferred. By contrast, the in-stent CTO of the LAD represented a fixed mechanical obstruction unlikely to respond to medical therapy alone. Given the patient’s exertional angina and preserved myocardial wall thickness in the LAD territory, revascularization was considered appropriate. Because the LAD lesion was located within a previously implanted stent, an antegrade approach under biplane fluoroscopic guidance was considered feasible and safe. The J-CTO score was 1. Directional coronary atherectomy (DCA) was planned to obtain tissue samples, if feasible.

Management

An antegrade approach was successfully employed (Video 3). DCA of the intimal lesion was performed under intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance using tip-detection guidance to confirm catheter orientation, followed by drug-coated balloon angioplasty (Videos 4 to 6). Multiple intimal tissue specimens excised via DCA were obtained for histopathological evaluation. Although the specimens did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for IgG4-related disease, they showed lymphocytic and plasmacytic infiltration with partially organized fibrosis. Reliable quantification of IgG4-positive plasma cells and the IgG4/IgG-positive plasma cell ratio was not feasible because of specimen artifacts. However, immunohistochemistry demonstrated a predominance of IgG4-positive plasma cells relative to IgG-positive plasma cells (Figure 3). Based on the patient’s overall clinical presentation, characteristic imaging findings, markedly elevated serum IgG4 levels, and supportive histopathological features, a clinical diagnosis of IgG4-related disease was established. Treatment with oral prednisolone was initiated at a dose of 30 mg/d, corresponding to approximately 0.5 mg/kg/d, after consultation with rheumatology. The dose was tapered by 5 mg every week to 15 mg/d and then by 2.5 mg every 2 weeks to 7.5 mg/d. Further gradual tapering was planned with close monitoring of symptoms, serum IgG4 levels, and follow-up imaging findings, with consideration of steroid-sparing therapy to avoid prolonged steroid monotherapy.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient’s serum IgG4 levels decreased after initiation of prednisolone therapy (Figure 4). Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT performed 1 month after treatment initiation, when the prednisolone dose was 15 mg/d, and 3 months after treatment initiation, when the dose was 7.5 mg/d, demonstrated marked regression of coronary adventitial thickening, with similar resolution of the aortic adventitial/perivascular thickening (Figure 5). At 3 months, follow-up coronary angiography showed satisfactory patency of the revascularized LAD-CTO, whereas the distal LCX lesion exhibited a decrease in stenosis with steroid therapy alone, supporting the possibility of an inflammation-related mechanism. IVUS was performed only for the LAD lesion and demonstrated regression of the intima–media complex, and follow-up CT showed a reduction in adventitial/perivascular thickening in both the LAD and distal LCX lesions (Figure 6). During steroid therapy, pitavastatin 1 mg/d was switched to rosuvastatin 5 mg/d plus ezetimibe 10 mg/d after LDL-C increased from approximately 65 mg/dL to 110 mg/dL, and LDL-C was subsequently maintained below 55 mg/dL. Teneligliptin and imeglimin were added for mild worsening of glycemic control. Although intensified lipid-lowering therapy may have contributed to stabilization of concomitant atherosclerotic disease, regression of adventitial/perivascular thickening was evident after initiation of corticosteroid therapy and is difficult to explain solely by intensification of lipid-lowering therapy.

Discussion

IgG4-related disease is a systemic inflammatory and fibrotic disorder characterized by infiltration of IgG4-positive plasma cells, which can involve the cardiovascular system.1 Although coronary artery involvement has been reported in this disease, isolated vascular lesions remain diagnostically challenging because of limited access to tissue for histopathological confirmation.2,3 In this case, the lesions were confined to the coronary arteries and aorta without involvement of other organs, rendering histopathological evaluation crucial for establishing a diagnosis. DCA was introduced in the early 1990s but fell out of favor in the stent era because of its technical complexity and limited efficacy in preventing restenosis.4,5 In recent years, however, DCA has been reappraised in Japan owing to advances in intravascular imaging, which enable safe and precise lesion debulking.6 In addition, the use of drug-coated balloons has been associated with reduced restenosis rates.7 In the present case, DCA was performed to debulk the fibrotic intimal lesion and achieve adequate luminal expansion before drug-coated balloon angioplasty, while simultaneously allowing the retrieval of intimal tissue for histopathological evaluation.

IgG4-related vascular disease predominantly involves the adventitia; however, previous reports have shown that inflammatory cell infiltration may extend into the intima in selected cases.8 In addition, serial IVUS studies have demonstrated regression of not only adventitial but also intimal lesions after steroid therapy, suggesting inflammatory involvement of the intimal layer.9 On this basis, we histopathologically evaluated the intimal tissue obtained via DCA. Although the specimens did not demonstrate abundant IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration comparable to that typically observed in adventitial lesions, we observed IgG4-positive cells in a pattern not characteristic of conventional atherosclerotic changes.

Taken together with the characteristic findings on contrast-enhanced CT and IVUS, as well as the markedly elevated serum IgG4 levels, the histopathological findings supported a clinical diagnosis of IgG4-related disease in this case, and steroid therapy was initiated. Following treatment, serial imaging demonstrated regression of both adventitial and intimal lesions, and the severe stenosis in the distal LCX was reduced with medical therapy alone, supporting the appropriateness of the diagnostic assessment and therapeutic strategy.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first report to demonstrate that tissue sampling via DCA can provide supportive histopathological information in IgG4-related coronary artery involvement. This case shows that DCA-derived intimal biopsy may serve not only as a therapeutic strategy but also as a valuable adjunctive diagnostic tool in selected cases of IgG4-related coronary artery involvement in which conventional biopsy is not feasible.
