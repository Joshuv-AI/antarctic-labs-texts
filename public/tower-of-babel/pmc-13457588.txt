Integrative fragmentomic and mutational signature profile of plasma cfDNA for early lung cancer detection
Authors: Jianxing He, Huiting Wang, Yi Feng, Jianfu Li, Peiling Chen, Xin Zheng, Wenhai Fu, Caichen Li, Hua Bao, Song Wang, Shuang Chang, Dongqin Zhu, Shanshan Yang, Yang Shao, Wen Zhong, Weisheng Guo, Rong Yin, Wenhua Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting lung cancer effectively in the general population is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes and improving the 5-year survival rate. While low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) is the current standard, it has limitations in broader populations. We developed a blood-based multi-omics model using whole-genome cell-free DNA (cfDNA) features to distinguish lung cancer from non-cancer individuals. This study included 1600 patients and an equal number of non-cancer controls, divided into training and validation cohorts. The model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 95.59% for the training cohort and 95.74% for the validation cohort. The model consistently performed well across various cancer stages and histological subtypes. To further validate the performance of the model, an external validation cohort was utilized. Notably, it also effectively differentiated non-cancer samples from cancer samples in the external validation cohort, with 85.9% sensitivity and 94.78% specificity. Importantly, in simulated population screenings, our ctDNA assay outperformed both LDCT and a previously established method. This suggests its potential utility in wider lung cancer screening programs, possibly complementing the LDCT approach. In conclusion, our ctDNA assay emerges as a promising and highly sensitive tool for the early detection and categorization of lung cancer.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most lethal cancer worldwide, with approximately 2.48 million new cases and 1.82 million deaths, according to GLOBOCAN 20221. The high lethality of this disease is primarily attributed to the majority of diagnoses occurring at advanced stages, when the treatments are significantly less effective. According to SEER, patients with localized lung cancer, in which the tumor is confined to the lung, have a 5-year relative survival rate is 65%. For patients with regional disease, where the malignancy has spread to nearly all lymph nodes, the 5-year relative survival drops to around 37%. For patients with metastatic lung cancer, the 5-year relative survival falls sharply to 9%. These statistics underscore the pivotal role early detection plays in enhancing therapeutic efficacy and improving survival rates.

Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) serves as the standard screening approach for high-risk individuals, which has demonstrated a 20% reduction in lung cancer mortality in previous trials2. However, LDCT has limited applicability for population-wide screening due to limitations such as cost, accessibility, radiation exposure, and a high false-positive rate, which can lead to unnecessary follow-up imaging and invasive procedures3,4. Definitive diagnosis ultimately relies on tissue biopsy, which is invasive and may not be feasible for small or difficult-to-reach lesions, with risks such as bleeding and pneumothorax. Additionally, traditional serum biomarkers, such as CEA, CYFRA21-1, and SCC, are frequently used in clinical practice, but their sensitivity and specificity are limited, particularly for early-stage disease5. Radiomics and artificial intelligence-based imaging analysis have also been explored, yet these approaches often require high-quality imaging data and lack broad external validation6. These challenges highlight the urgent need to develop non-invasive approaches to enable broader population screening beyond high-risk groups.

Current methodologies for early detection of cancer using cell-free DNA (cfDNA) focus on identifying genetic and epigenetic aberrations, including methylation patterns, copy number alterations, abnormal fragmentation, and specific mutations. However, these approaches often confront challenges such as biomarker instability, limited scope, and high development costs. In response to these limitations, our study employs a multi-omics approach using low-coverage whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to develop a robust model for early lung cancer screening. This model leverages fragmentomic patterns and mutational signature profiles, enhancing the potential for early detection. The WGS method significantly boosts the sensitivity of detection by identifying large-scale epigenetic differences in cfDNA patterns across the entire genome. Additionally, mutation-based sequencing techniques, such as panels targeting commonly mutated genes, have required deep sequencing due to the rarity of mutations, rendering the process costly and time-consuming. For instance, CAPP-seq, a targeted deep sequencing technique, achieved a Stage I AUC of only 0.697. Recognizing these limitations, recent advances have shifted focus to extracting somatic mutation profiles from cfDNA using WGS. Studies such as those by Bruhm et al.8, Bae et al.9, and Wan et al.10 have introduced innovative features like GEMINI scores, local variant density, and mutational contexts. However, these methods are often limited in their ability to differentiate germline mutations from background noise accurately. Our model addresses these issues by incorporating a WGS-based mutational signature (MS), capturing characteristic combinations of mutations from specific mutagenesis processes. This approach not only sidesteps the need for matched controls but also significantly enhances detection sensitivity at low coverage, paving the way for a more sensitive and effective lung cancer screening tool.

Our study presents an enhanced lung cancer detection assay leveraging WGS-derived ctDNA profiles. By integrating fragmentomic and mutational profiling using low-depth WGS, we aim to better distinguish individuals with and without malignant lung cancer. We further validate our model using an external cohort previously employed in Wang et al.11 and explore the broader implications of our assay through a hypothetical population analysis by Monte Carlo simulations12. Our ultimate ambition is to pioneer an ultra-sensitive screening test that complements the current LDCT methodology, enabling a more comprehensive population screening approach. We aim to position our test as a precursor to LDCT for high-risk indications.

Results

Participant disposition and characteristics

A total of 1600 participants were enrolled in this study across two distinct centers, all of whom underwent WGS and feature extraction (Fig. 1A). A training cohort was derived, consisting of a “cancer group” and a “non-cancer group”. The “cancer group” encompassed 400 participants who were clinically diagnosed with lung cancer. The “non-cancer group” included 240 individuals who either had a prior diagnosis of benign nodules or did not undergo biopsy due to low radiographic suspicion based on LDCT results. Additionally, this group included 160 clinically healthy subjects. Similarly, 800 subsequently enrolled participants were assigned to the validation cohort, which consisted of 400 lung cancer patients and 400 non-cancer controls. The non-cancer control group included 160 healthy subjects and 240 subjects with benign nodules. The training cohort was used to develop the model and determine a cut-off for classification. The independent validation dataset was subsequently utilized to assess the model’s performance.

Regarding histological categorization, lung adenocarcinoma (ADC) was the predominant subtype, accounting for 83.0% (332/400) of the cases in the training cohort and 86.2% (345/400) in the validation cohort.

cfDNA profiles

Three cfDNA features were extracted from low-pass WGS data and used for modeling (Fig. 1B). At the chromosomal level, copy number variation (CNV) profiles resembled previously reported genomic landscapes in East Asian-ancestry LUAD13. Specifically, elevated cfDNA contributions were observed from regions 1p, 5p, 5q, 7p, 7q, 8q, and 16p, whereas regions 4q, 11q, 13q, 17p, and 17q showed reduced cfDNA levels (Fig. 2A), reflecting genome-wide chromosomal alterations associated with tumor-derived cfDNA.

Analysis of fragment size distribution (FSD) revealed a leftward shift in cancer samples compared with healthy and nodule groups (Fig. 2B), indicating that cancer-derived cfDNA fragments are, on average, shorter. This observation aligns with fragmentomic profiles reported in lung cancer by Cristiano et al.14, and likely reflects tumor-specific processes, such as nucleosome positioning, apoptotic DNA cleavage, and altered chromatin organization. The concordance between these fragmentomic patterns and established lung cancer biology reinforces the plausibility of our assay in capturing tumor-derived cfDNA signals and supports its potential utility for early detection.

Mutational signature (MS) analysis showed notable differences between cancer and non-cancer samples, consistent with findings reported by Wan et al. 10. In particular, the smoking-associated SBS4 signature was significantly enriched in cancer samples (Fig. 2C), in line with prior studies highlighting its prevalence in LUAD and association with patient prognosis10,15,16. In addition, cancer samples showed relatively enrichment of the apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic polypeptide-like (APOBEC) mutational process (Supplementary Fig. 1). Consistent with these single-feature observations, SHAP analysis of the stacked ensemble model indicated that the APOBEC-related SBS2 exhibited a clear directional contribution, with higher values consistently increasing the cancer score (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, the aging-associated signatures SBS1 and SBS5 displayed relatively symmetric SHAP distributions, which is biologically consistent with their clock-like and ubiquitous nature. The smoking-associated SBS4 also showed strong positive contributions, supporting its well-established biological relevance in lung cancer.

Performance of the model in the training cohort

We constructed a multi-omics lung cancer predictive model using the training cohort. The model construction process is detailed in Fig. 1 and the “Methods” section. The performance of individual features in the training and validation cohorts is presented in Supplementary Tables 1–3, respectively.

As illustrated in Fig. 3A, our study achieved superior area under the curve (AUC) values in various classifications. An AUC value of 95.59% (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 94.1%–97.01%) was achieved in differentiating between the cancer and non-cancer groups. Furthermore, an AUC value of 99.85% (95% CI: 99.7%–99.99%) was observed in classifying healthy individuals versus cancer patients. An AUC of 92.75% (95% CI: 90.34%–95.15%) was achieved in distinguishing individuals with nodules from cancer patients.

Based on the sensitivity of 95.75% (95% CI: 93.3%–97.33%) observed in the training dataset, we selected a cancer score of 0.60 as the cutoff. This threshold yielded a specificity of 90% (95% CI: 86.67%–92.57%) in distinguishing cancer and non-cancer groups. For distinguishing cancer from nodules, the specificity reached 84.17% (95% CI: 79.15%–88.24%). A specificity of 98.75% (95% CI: 95.57%–99.65%) was obtained in classifying cancer patients and healthy individuals (Fig. 3B).

Our model demonstrated high sensitivity in detecting cancers at varying stages, as depicted in Fig. 3C. We observed an upward trend in cancer scores from early to late stages, reflecting the increased challenge of detecting early-stage disease. The lower cancer scores in stage IV are likely due to the small sample size. Moreover, no statistically significant difference was observed in cancer scores among various disease subgroups (Wilcox test, all P > 0.05), which were defined by clinical characteristics such as tumor size, sub-stage at stage I, ADC subgroup, and histology (Supplementary Fig. 3). The model maintained consistently high sensitivity across different subgroups of patients with lung cancer (Supplementary Table 4).

Performance of the model in the separate validation cohort

To validate the performance of our cfDNA-based assay, we collected two validation cohorts. The separate validation cohort was collected at the same site (Guangzhou Medical University First Hospital) as the training cohort but during a separate time frame. The external independent validation cohort, on the other hand, was collected from a different site (Jiangsu Cancer Hospital; Supplementary Data 1).

In the separate validation cohort, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) mirrored that of the training cohort, achieving 95.74% in distinguishing cancer from non-cancer cases (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 4). Within the non-cancer group, healthy individuals achieved a specificity of 99.38% (95% CI: 96.55%–99.89%), while those with benign nodules had 85% (95% CI:79.93%–88.96%) (Fig. 3E). The model’s sensitivity for detecting the cancer group stood at 94.75% (95% CI: 92.11%–96.54%). The sensitivities and specificities at various stages of cancer and among different subgroups in the validation cohort demonstrated similar trends to those observed in the training dataset, as shown in Fig. 3F. Reflecting the inherent challenge of early-stage cancer detection, the sensitivity for Stage I was 94.21% and for Stage II was 94.12%, while both Stage III and Stage IV demonstrated an optimal performance with a sensitivity of 100% (Supplementary Table 5). This trend was further echoed in the sub-stages of Stage I, with sensitivity increasing from 91.89% in Stage IA1 to 100% in Stage IB. Furthermore, tumors of a larger size demonstrated higher detection rates compared to smaller tumors. Analysis of different histological subtypes of lung cancer revealed that small cell lung carcinomas (SCLCs) were detected more readily than lung adenocarcinomas (ADCs), with respective sensitivities of 100% and 94.49%.

Performance of the model in the external validation cohort

The external validation cohort was sourced from a different hospital and had previously been used in a study focused on early-stage lung cancer detection. This cohort comprised both healthy controls and NSCLC patients. Notably, our model achieved an overall sensitivity of 85.9% (95%CI: 81.60% - 89.33%) and a specificity of 94.78% (95% CI: 91.10%–96.99%) within this external validation cohort (Fig. 4A). The sensitivity slightly decreased but remained at a comparable level. A notable observation across all cohorts—including the training cohort and the independent validation cohort—was the progressive increase in cancer scores from early to advanced lung cancer stages. The sensitivities for Stages I through IV were 83.76%, 89.19%, 89.19%, and 100%, respectively (Fig. 4B). Table 2 compares more details of the performance in three cohorts.

Evaluation of pre-analytical variables on the model outcome

To ascertain the influence of pre-analytical variables on the robustness of our diagnostic model, we conducted an analysis involving an additional 12 healthy participants. This evaluation focused on transport and plasma preservation conditions. Regardless of variations in temperature and interval before processing, all samples were consistently classified as non-cancer (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Similarly, plasma samples preserved for varying durations consistently yielded scores below the model’s cutoff threshold, demonstrating their stability (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

Furthermore, we explored the potential impact of varying sequencers, operators, and runs to ensure that operational variations did not influence our results. Comprehensive statistical tests and performance evaluations were carried out under these varying conditions. The analyses revealed no significant differences in output scores (P values are detailed in Supplementary Tables 6–8), confirming that variations in sequencers, runs, and operators do not significantly affect the model’s performance. The consistent sensitivity and specificity metrics across various sequencers, runs, and operators further affirm our model’s reliability and robustness.

Monte Carlo simulation

In order to assess the potential performance of our ctDNA blood test assay in detecting lung cancer at a population level, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation on a hypothetical population of 100,000 individuals. This simulation used an estimated lung cancer prevalence rate of 1.79%, derived from the study “Community-based mass screening with low dose CT for lung cancer in Guangzhou”17. Current adherence rates to LDCT screening in the US stand at 5.9%, while estimated adherence to the previous published assay (the combination of blood test and LDCT is 60%)18. Given the improved accessibility and compliance of blood-based tests, we conservatively estimated an adherence rate of 70% for our ctDNA assay.

The estimated sensitivity and specificity of LDCT alone, the previously published method, and our ctDNA blood assay were modeled within this hypothetical population. Uncertainties associated with these parameters were addressed using probability distributions centered on empirical estimates. These were sourced from the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) cohorts for LDCT, the LUCAS cohort for the previously published assay, and our independent validation cohorts for our ctDNA blood assay.

In our independent validation cohort, our assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 94.75% and a specificity of 90.75%. According to the NLST study, the estimated sensitivity and specificity of LDCT are 93.9% and 73.4%, respectively2. Meanwhile, the LUCAS study reported a sensitivity and specificity of 90% and 80%, respectively, for the previously published assay.

In the simulated population, LDCT alone would, on average, identify 98 cases of lung cancer. In contrast, our ctDNA assay would detect an estimated 1,187 cases of lung cancer, representing a 12-fold increase over LDCT and a 1.3-fold increase over the previous published assay. The statistical robustness of our assay is further supported by the calculated predictive values. The Positive Predictive Value (PPV) of our ctDNA assay in this hypothetical population is 0.169, meaning that approximately 16.9% of individuals identified as high-risk by the test are indeed lung cancer patients. Notably, the Negative Predictive Value (NPV) of our assay is as high as 0.999, indicating near-perfect accuracy in identifying individuals without lung cancer. Compared to existing strategies, our ctDNA assay significantly improves both PPV and NPV (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we developed an ultrasensitive blood-based assay integrating multi-omics cfDNA features derived from low-depth WGS to improve the early detection of lung cancer. By integrating cfDNA profiles of copy number variation, fragment size distribution, and mutational signatures, our model can distinguish individuals with and without lung cancer with high accuracy. The constructed ensemble model demonstrated excellent predictive power, achieving high AUC values in both the training and independent validation cohorts. Notably, the model maintained robust performance in an external independent validation cohort, with a sensitivity of 85.90%. Together, these results indicate that integrating multiple complementary cfDNA features provides a comprehensive representation of tumor-derived signals and supports reliable lung cancer detection in a noninvasive setting.

Within the rapidly evolving landscape of cfDNA-based cancer detection, several recent studies have explored multi-omics integration strategies. For instance, Bruhm et al.8 employed single-molecule, genome-wide mutation profiling using ultra-deep sequencing to sensitively capture rare somatic variants in cfDNA, while Bae et al.9 integrated genomic and epigenomic features through multi-modal modeling to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Compared with these approaches, our study emphasizes a balance between biological information content, technical robustness, and clinical scalability. The majority of lung cancer cases were Stage I (83% in training and 81.2% in validation), with most samples exhibiting tumor fraction below 3%, reflecting the low ctDNA abundance typical of early-stage disease. Rather than relying on ultra-deep sequencing or extensive epigenomic profiling, we focus on low-depth WGS, extracting complementary genome-wide signals that remain robust under low-coverage conditions. Feature selection is guided by biological relevance and signal stability, with mutational signatures further refined through stringent SNP filtering and background noise reduction. At the modeling level, a stacked ensemble framework is used to integrate heterogeneous cfDNA features, allowing each omic layer to contribute complementary predictive information while maintaining robustness and interpretability. Importantly, the model’s performance is consistently supported by independent external validation, underscoring its generalizability across cohorts and sequencing settings.

Moreover, the robustness of our findings is further supported by a large sample size, which increases statistical power and minimizes the likelihood of random chance findings. This large cohort enables us to draw more reliable inferences and provides a solid foundation for the general applicability of our findings across diverse populations. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our external validation cohort, while independent, originates from a single geographic and ethnic population, and the model’s generalizability to other populations has yet to be established. Future multi-center international studies are needed to evaluate and optimize the model across diverse ethnicities and clinical settings, ensuring broader applicability of the model.

The proposed model carries significant clinical value. Detecting lung cancer in low-risk or asymptomatic populations remains challenging, and current screening strategies are often limited by cost, accessibility, and radiation exposure. Our model’s high accuracy and non-invasiveness make it a promising pre-screening tool that could potentially extend screening beyond high-risk groups and improve early detection and treatment outcomes. Clinically, the model is intended to complement standard-of-care LDCT screening. A positive result would prompt LDCT and further diagnostic evaluation, while a negative result would lead to routine follow-up and monitoring. By prioritizing high sensitivity, the model aims to reduce false-negative results, thereby minimizing missed early-stage cancers. As a triage tool, it may also optimize LDCT utilization by reducing unnecessary scans and radiation exposure in individuals with a low likelihood of malignancy. In addition, the model shows strong performance in distinguishing benign nodules, which may further decrease false-positive findings and reduce unnecessary invasive procedures. However, implementing this test in asymptomatic populations requires careful consideration of false positives, as even with high specificity, screening large populations may inevitably generate some false-positive results, which may lead to additional imaging, invasive procedures, and psychological stress. Therefore, in real-world practice, the assay should be used as a triage tool, and positive results should be confirmed by LDCT and clinical evaluation. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal follow-up intervals and referral pathways to minimize unnecessary workups and avoid overdiagnosis. We are planning a large-scale prospective clinical trial to further validate and refine our model. This trial will rigorously evaluate the model’s effectiveness and its integration into existing lung cancer screening protocols.

Although low-coverage WGS is more economical than ultra-deep sequencing, the cost of implementing this assay for large-scale screening remains a practical consideration. Compared with standard LDCT screening, our approach is non-invasive and avoids radiation exposure, which may reduce the need for follow-up imaging and invasive procedures. These features could help offset some of the upfront costs associated with sequencing. Future studies are planned to perform formal cost-effectiveness analyses to evaluate the feasibility and potential economic benefits of integrating this assay into broader lung cancer screening programs.

In summary, our study presents a promising noninvasive, liquid biopsy-based approach to cancer detection. By integrating multi-omics cfDNA features from WGS into a machine learning model, we demonstrate its potential for lung cancer detection. Further research is needed to enhance our model’s utility, but our findings highlight the transformative potential of multi-omics data and advanced computational modeling in cancer detection.

Methods

Participant enrollment

A total of 800 non-cancer participants and cancer patients from Guangzhou Medical University First Hospital, following exclusions for post-treatment samples, other cancers, acute inflammation, loss to follow-up, and QC failures, were enrolled to form our training cohorts. The non-cancer group consisted of both healthy individuals (n = 160) and those with pulmonary nodules indicated by LDCT (n = 240). The cancer group (n = 400) comprised individuals diagnosed with cancer at various stages of the disease. A validation cohort was subsequently collected, consisting of another set of 800 participants, similarly divided into non-cancer (160 healthy individuals and 240 with pulmonary nodules) and cancer groups (n = 400). Participant enrollment and study design are shown in Fig. 1.

Both the cancer and non-cancer groups from two cohorts encompassed a wide range of clinical characteristics. The cancer samples spanned different TNM staging, tumor sizes, ADC subgroups, and nodule counts. Likewise, the pulmonary nodules in the non-cancer cohorts exhibited diverse characteristics such as histology, inflammatory status, infection status, diameter, and nodule counts. Table 1 presents the detailed clinical information of the participants from both cohorts. Notably, all participants were age- and sex-matched to ensure consistency in the comparative analysis. This study was approved by the ethics committee at Guangzhou Medical University First Hospital (approval no. 2021-95) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

We further validated our final model using an external validation cohort obtained from the Jiangsu Cancer Hospital, China. This cohort was previously employed in a study focused on the detection of early-stage lung cancer11. This cohort included 542 participants, consisting of 312 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients across stages I to IV, and 230 healthy controls. This study was approved by the ethics committee at Jiangsu Cancer Hospital (approval no. JSLMTCR-2017-002) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample collection and clinical evaluation

For healthy subjects in the non-cancer group, the venous blood samples were collected during routine physical examinations. For subjects with nodules and cancer patients, blood samples were collected before LDCT and surgery. Sample collection, shipment, and processing were standardized and uniformly conducted across all participants. Staging was determined by the treating physician based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system (8th edition)19. Samples were collected using EDTA blood collection tubes. Samples were kept at 4 °C for no more than 2 h before centrifugation, and blood samples were centrifuged at 1800 × g for 10 min. Plasma samples were centrifuged a second time at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris.

Library preparation and sequencing

cfDNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed according to previously reported protocols20, conducted at the Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc., a testing laboratory certified by both the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) and the College of American Pathologists (CAP). cfDNA extraction was performed using the Hamilton Microlab STAR automated liquid handling platform (Hamilton Company, USA) in conjunction with the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, USA). The concentration of the extracted cfDNA was subsequently measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The PCR-free WGS library construction was automated using the Biomek (Beckman Coulter, USA) and involved 5-10 ng of the cfDNA sample along with the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA Biosystems, USA). Constructed libraries were quantified using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems, USA) and sequenced using paired-end reads on NovaSeq platforms (Illumina, USA). The analysis was conducted in a strictly blinded manner. Samples were processed using two distinct sequencers, handled by three different technicians, across nine separate runs.

Multi-omic cfDNA feature generation

Raw reads for the WGS data were first trimmed with Trimmomatic, followed by PCR duplicates removal of PCR duplicates using the Picard toolkit21. Reads were subsequently mapped onto the human reference genome (GRCh37/UCSC hg19) using the sequence aligner Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA, v0.7.12)22. To mitigate the potential impact on the predictive power of the different coverages among the WGS data, we down-sampled the coverages to a unified 5X.

We extracted three types of cfDNA features from the WGS data for model construction: copy number variation (CNV), fragment size distribution (FSD), and mutational signature (MS).

Copy number variations (CNVs) refer to genetic alterations involving changes in the number of copies of a particular gene in the genome23. In this study, CNV profiles were generated using ichorCNA16,24. The genome for each sample was segmented into 1 Mb bins, followed by GC correction of each bin, ensuring that the observed variations in read depth across the genome were not due to sequencing bias. A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) analyzed the sequencing depth of each genomic bin, comparing it to a baseline derived from a WGS dataset of a healthy cohort, as described by Adalsteinsson et al.24. This comparison generated a log2 ratio for each bin, quantifying the relative copy number variations. Log2 ratios for each bin were computed to serve as features indicative of CNVs. This analysis generated 2475 variables, providing a comprehensive overview of the CNV landscape in each sample20,25. In addition to generating CNV profiles, ichorCNA was also used to estimate the tumor fraction (TF) for each sample. TF estimates were primarily used for descriptive and subgroup analyses to contextualize model performance.

Fragment size distribution (FSD) profiles focused on the detailed length patterns of cfDNA fragments, categorizing them in 5 bp increments within the range of 100 bp to 220 bp (e.g., 100–104 bp, 105–109 bp)20,25. Fragment ratios in each bin were calculated at the arm level of human autosomes. Raw coverage scores for the fragment size distribution profiles were scaled to z-scores by comparing each bin’s value to the overall mean value of the corresponding chromosome arm. A total of 936 variables were generated for the fragment size distribution features.

Mutational signatures rely on the somatic mutations in cancer genomes caused by multiple mutational processes26. We employed an advanced SNP and noise filtering method developed by Wan et al.10, specifically designed for low-coverage WGS of cfDNA. First, we performed SNP and background noise filtering at the mutational level right after variant calling. SNP filtering integrated data from the dbSNP database, the East Asian cohort of the 1000 Genome Project27, and an independent reference cohort of 1000 healthy controls. Variants were filtered to exclude sites identified by these databases, minimizing the influence of germline mutations and background noise. Variants appearing more than three times in the same run were also excluded. Following initial filtering, we applied a deep learning-driven fragment-level filter for ctDNA signal-to-noise enrichment, which was adapted from the MRD-EDGE tool developed by28. The nucleotide context surrounding single-base substitutions (SBSs) extracted from WGS was analyzed29. Each SBS was classified into one of six types (C > A, C > G, C > T, T > A, T > C, and T > G) based on the pyrimidine context of the base pair. Additionally, one base flanking the SBS on the 5’ and 3’ sides was considered, resulting in 96 distinct SBS mutation contexts (6 × 4 × 4). In this analysis, the context profile represented mutation counts extracted from WGS across the 96 context bins, following GC correction. The context profile, representing mutation counts extracted from WGS of a plasma sample, was corrected for GC content and further refined by subtracting a mutational context baseline established from independent 3000 healthy samples. This baseline was generated using the same filtering processes and established the mean value of these controls as the baseline for each of the 96 SBS bins. Mutational signatures (MS) were fitted to the 96 context profiles using the “fit_to_signature()” function from MutationalPattern (version 1.10.0)30, which applies non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to identify signature profiles and their contributions. Additionally, mutational signatures associated with oxidative damage and sequencing errors, as annotated by the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutation in Cancer (COSMIC), were filtered out31,32. Notably, the reference databases used for SNP removal and the baseline control group for mutational contexts were both independent of the cohorts involved in this study.

Machine learning modeling

During the development phase of our machine learning model, we employed a two-layer architecture integrating three multi-omics cfDNA features. Crucially, the validation cohort and the external validation remained untouched until the model’s finalization, ensuring the prevention of data leakage and guaranteeing a reliable evaluation of the model’s capabilities.

For the first layer, each cfDNA feature was utilized to create its specific base models. This was achieved using five distinct algorithms: Random Forest (RF), Generalized Linear Model (GLM), XGBoost, Deep Learning (DL), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM). Each algorithm underwent hyperparameter tuning complemented by fivefold cross-validation. The efficiency of individual base models was gauged using the AUC metric. Based on AUC performance, the top five models for each feature were selected to constitute the base models of this layer. This selection process resulted in a total of 15 models, derived from the combination of three features and their respective five top-performing models. Consequently, each feature generated a distinct cancer score matrix for each participant in the study. These matrices served as the foundational input for the second layer of our modeling architecture. This approach ensured equitable contributions from each feature to the final analysis, irrespective of the frequency with which any algorithm was chosen across different features. The generation of these unique matrices for each feature standardizes the influence of each feature on the model’s outcomes. By integrating the outputs from all five selected algorithms into a single matrix for each feature, we ensure that the contribution of each feature is harmonized, guaranteeing a balanced and equitable representation in the model’s second layer, where the composite cancer score is calculated. The second layer of our model employed a meta-model to integrate outputs from the diverse first-layer models. This ensured that no single feature or model disproportionately influences the outcomes.

A notable difference from the framework developed by Wang et al.11 was our selection criteria for the base models. Models were picked solely based on performance, irrespective of the algorithmic type. This means that, potentially, the five base models for a specific feature could all employ the same algorithm but differ in hyperparameters, as opposed to being a mix of five diverse algorithms.

The final outputs of our stacked model were cancer probability scores ranging from 0 to 1 for all participants. A higher cancer score output denoted a higher probability of lung cancer. We determined a score cutoff for classifying individuals into cancer and non-cancer groups based on achieving 95% sensitivity in the training cohort. Participants with cancer scores above the cutoff were classified as a high-risk population group, whereas those with cancer scores below the cutoff were identified as a low-risk population. The performance of this classification was then evaluated in an independent validation cohort and an external validation cohort.

Pre-analytical variables analysis

To evaluate the potential effects of various pre-analytical variables on the model’s robustness and reproducibility, we conducted a small cohort study involving an additional 12 healthy participants from Guangzhou Medical University First Hospital, evaluating the impact of varying transport conditions and plasma preservation durations. For each healthy participant, two tubes of blood were collected during each session. Blood collection sessions were scheduled one month apart, with each participant providing a total of four tubes of blood for the analysis. The impact of different transportation times (24, 48, and 72 h post-collection) and storage conditions at room temperature was examined, with the reference condition defined as the state of the sample stored at 4° within 2 h of collection. The effects of freezing durations of plasma (3 days, 7 days, 1 month, and 6 months) were also studied, with the reference condition defined as the plasma sample that was not frozen and processed immediately. For each set of conditions, the output score and specificity were utilized to gauge the robustness and reliability of the model.

Additionally, we performed statistical tests and evaluated the performance metrics for our cohort results processed using varying sequencers, operators, and runs. Our cohort samples were analyzed using two distinct sequencers, handled by three different operators, across a total of nine separate runs. The Wilcoxon test was used for comparisons between cancer and non-cancer groups across sequencers. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess differences across various runs and operators. The sensitivity and specificity metrics across different sequencers, runs, and operators were used to evaluate the consistency and reliability of our test.

Monte Carlo simulations

In this study, we applied Monte Carlo simulations to create a hypothetical cohort of 100,000 individuals, reflecting the potential screening population18. This method is particularly beneficial in our context, where even though the sample size of our cohorts is relatively large, the real-world variability of factors such as test sensitivity and specificity, patient adherence to testing protocols, and the prevalence of lung cancer introduces significant uncertainty. These factors are not constants but variables that follow specific distributions, adding layers of complexity that normal forecasting models cannot easily capture.

Monte Carlo simulations are invaluable for understanding how the distributions of these factors impact the outcomes of our study. These simulations use repeated random sampling to generate numerical results, effectively accounting for the potential variability in these parameters. For each run of the simulation, random values were drawn from probability distributions centered around empirical estimates of these parameters.

The empirical estimates applied within these simulations were extracted from various established sources: the sensitivity and specificity values for LDCT were ascertained from the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST)2; the estimates for a previously published assay were produced from the LUCAS cohort18; and for our novel ctDNA blood assay, the parameters were sourced from our independent validation cohorts. Additionally, data on the prevalence of lung cancer in the general populace17 and adherence rates to screening processes were incorporated into the simulations. These parameters were modeled using beta distributions.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, the receiver operating characteristic curves were generated using the pROC package. Based on true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) of cancer detection, we calculated sensitivity (TP/[TP + FN]), specificity (TN/[TN + FP]), accuracy ([TP + TN]/[TP + FP + TN + FN]), PPV (TP/[TP + FP]) and NPV (TN/[TN + FN]), as well as the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), using binom.confit packages in R. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Kruskal–Wallis test, and ANOVA test were performed using R (version 4.1.2). The normality assumptions test for ANOVA was tested.
