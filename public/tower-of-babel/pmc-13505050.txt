Efficiency and biomechanics of a novel CAD/CAM individual distalizer versus cervical headgear: a preliminary prospective cohort study and exploratory finite element analysis
Authors: Yichen Luo, Limin Fan, Mao Liu, Yunxi Zhu, Ye Cao, Xin She, Luwei Liu, Bin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaxillary molar distalization is one of the standard non-extraction strategies for correcting Class II malocclusion. However, conventional modalities are frequently hindered by profound compliance dependence, inadvertent anchorage loss, and the necessity for invasive surgical interventions. To address these limitations, a fully customized, non-invasive, intraorally cemented individual distalizer (ID) was engineered. The clinical efficiency of the ID was compared with that of the cervical headgear (CHG), and a finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted to investigate the differences in mechanical mechanisms.MethodsFifteen patients (24 molars) treated with the ID and 13 patients (26 molars) treated with CHG were enrolled. Molar and incisor three-dimensional movements were measured on superimposed digital models using the palatal rugae as a reference. Treatment duration and monthly distalization rates were calculated. FEA was conducted on a representative patient to simulate the stress distribution and displacement patterns under loading conditions of 200 g (ID) and 450 g (CHG). Stabilized inverse probability weighting (SIPW) was applied for potential baseline imbalances. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was used to analyze the data, accounting for the clustering of bilateral molars. The significance level was set at 5% (ɑ=0.05).ResultsThe distalization duration of the ID group (64.51 days, 95%CI [53.25, 75.77]) was significantly shorter than that of the CHG group (181.32 days, 95%CI [158.81, 203.83]) with similar distalization distance (P < 0.001). The ID group (1.97 mm/month, 95%CI [1.60, 2.34]) showed higher distalization efficiency compared with the CHG group (0.57 mm/month, 95%CI [0.18, 0.96]; P < 0.001). The ID group demonstrated a trend of molar intrusion (-0.03 mm, 95%CI [−0.61, 0.55]), while the CHG group showed the opposite (0.95 mm, 95CI [0.35, 1.56]; P = 0.033). Tooth movement of the ID and CHG groups in FEA corroborated these clinical findings. No significant differences were observed regarding molar inclination and torque, or anterior inclination and extrusion between the two groups.ConclusionsThe ID provides a highly efficient, compliance-independent alternative to cervical headgear, with the potential advantage of vertical molar control, making it particularly suitable for hyperdivergent patients. Nevertheless, careful monitoring of anterior anchorage is recommended during the distalization.Trial registrationThis study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100048402) on July 6, 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08760-2.

Background

Maxillary molar distalization is a classic non-extraction strategy used to correct a Class Ⅱ molar relationship, or expand the arch to obtain space for mild to moderate dental crowding [1, 2]. Several orthodontic appliances have been applied for molar distalization clinically. Cervical headgear (CHG) is used with a facebow to push the maxillary molars distally [3–5]. It exerts a strong distal force on the buccal surface of the molar without affecting the anchorage of the other teeth. However, the forces are intermittent and heavily dependent on the patient’s compliance [3]; the extrusion of the molar was also reported [4, 6], which is unfavorable to high-angle patients. Clear aligner therapy, favored for its superior aesthetics, demonstrates a predictability rate of approximately 88% in executing maxillary molar distalization [7]. However, a concern is that the enamel demineralization might happen during the treatment [8], and the achievement of clear aligners also requires patient’s strict cooperation as well. Besides, the high cost of treatment makes it unsuitable for patients with financial constraints.

In order to avoid patients’ compliance, fixed appliances are also usually used for the maxillary molar distalization. Pendulum appliance is cemented to the patient’s teeth with a Nance button for anchorage and uses palatal forces to achieve molar distalization. Extensive literature confirms that such mechanics often induce adverse labial tipping of the maxillary incisors secondary to anchorage loss. Furthermore, the voluminous acrylic Nance button resting on the palatal vault frequently impedes phonetics and precipitates mucosal discomfort [9, 10]. The resin base on the palatal rugae would affect the pronunciation and cause discomfort. Recently, the temporary anchorage devices (TADs) are proposed to provide a strong anchorage for the molar distalization [11]; however, they require invasive implant surgery and might cause implant mobility or peri-implantitis especially planted close to the adjacent root [12]. It could be inferred that a non-invasive, compliance-free and handy appliance for molar distalization would be a valuable addition to the available treatment options.

Here, we developed an intraorally cemented and non-invasive metal individual distalizer (ID) fabricated by computer aided design and computer aided manufacture (CAD/CAM). The ID was composed of band or occlusal rest retainers, a pair of traction hooks, a Nance button and minor connectors, which could distalize the maxillary molar with the use of a pair of buttons by elastomeric chains. Authorization as a patent of the People’s Republic of China has been granted for this appliance (CN110680526B). The design and fabrication was mostly based on digital workflow which ensured the fit between the appliance and the oral tissue as possible, and less manual operations were required compared with conventional appliances. A narrative review demonstrated improved fit and reduced intraoperative adjustments when CAD/CAM workflows are used in maxillofacial reconstruction, supporting the rationale for a digital workflow in appliance fabrication [13]. Also, the ID was patient-specifically designed rather than prefabricated, which could promisingly improve the function and patient’s satisfaction [14]. The distal force exerted by ID could continuously act on both buccal and palatal surfaces of the molar and it didn’t strictly require the patient to insert or remove the appliance regularly. Moreover, the force was applied as a pulling force from the distal end rather than a pushing force from the mesial side exerted by conventional appliances.

Studies have shown that the distalization efficiency is corresponded to the acting point of the force [15], force magnitude [5, 16] and the eruption stage of the maxillary second and third molars [17]. Of these factors, the orthodontic force mainly influenced the progress of the distalization. Finite element analysis (FEA) has been used to investigate the mechanical mechanism in orthodontic tooth movement [18–20]. The displacement trend of the tooth is regarded as the prediction of the movement by an applied force [21]. The stress on the periodontal ligament (PDL) is possibly related to whether the orthodontic force is appropriate for a tooth. However, a few study investigated the correlation between the FEA results and the clinical observations [22].

Therefore, the aim of this prospective cohort study was to evaluate the molar distalization efficiency of ID in comparison with that of CHG. And the differences in mechanical mechanism of the two appliances were investigated by FEA.

Methods

Patients’ enrollment

This prospective cohort study was carried out in the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, Nanjing, China with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (PJ-2022-224-001), and was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100048402). This study followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cohort studies.

The sample size evaluation was conducted for two-tailed two-sample t-tests assuming equal variance in PASS 2021 (NCSS, Kaysville, Utah, USA) according to the study of Mossaz et al. [6] and a pilot case based on molar’s distal displacement. A minimum of 23 molars in each group was required to achieve a power of 0.95 and an alpha level of 0.05, assuming that the means of molar’s distal displacement were 1.66 mm and 4.17 mm at 3 months, respectively, with a common standard deviation of 2.3 mm.

The inclusion criteria for the patients were: unilateral or bilateral class Ⅱ molar relationship; unerupted maxillary second molar or adequate space distal to maxillary first molar; hypodivergent or normodivergent (FMA < 32 degree); no severe defect or malposition of maxillary central incisor and good compliance. The exclusion criteria included uncontrolled periodontic or endodontic diseases, systemic diseases concerning craniofacial development or previous orthodontic treatment. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of each participant upon the enrollment. Patients were subsequently allocated to either the ID or CHG cohort based on individual preference. It must be acknowledged that this non-randomized allocation paradigm introduces an inherent selection bias, as individuals with historically poor compliance indices may naturally gravitate toward the compliance-free ID system. The baseline characteristics including age, gender and cephalometrics (SNA, SNB, ANB, FMA) were recorded for each patient. The treatment plans for the both groups were designed by the same orthodontist (LW. L, deputy director) and checked by the supervisor (B. Y, director).

Distalization treatment

In the ID group, all patients were introduced to the ID for molar distalization. The appliance was designed on the digital impression of the cast with 3Shape Appliance Designer (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark). A pair of traction hooks was set at over 6 mm distal to the maxillary first molar and 0.5 mm above the mucosa. The band or occlusal rest retainers were designed on the premolars or the contralateral molar (for unilateral patients). A modified Nance appliance was intimately adapted to the anterior palatal rugae to robustly reinforce anterior anchorage. Each part of the appliance was connected by bar-like minor connectors and occlusal interferences were eliminated. Checked by the orthodontist, the ID was fabricated by selective laser melting 3D printer (Profeta, Nanjing, China) of cobalt-chrome alloy. Then it was kept in heat treatment furnace (AHS200, Chamlion Laser Technology Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) with temperature elevated to 1000℃ within one hour and maintained for one hour followed by furnace cooling, eventually finished by manual polishing. After try-in and fit examination, the appliance was cemented to the dentition by 3 M RelyX™ Luting (3 M, Saint Paul, Minnesota, USA). Two buttons were bonded to the buccal and palatal surfaces of the molar to be distalized respectively (Fig. 1). Approximately 100 g of distal tensile force, calibrated by Correx tension gauge (Haag-Streit, Köniz, Switzerland), was applied on each side of the molar by hanging the elastomeric chains (Ormco, Brea, California, USA) from the traction hook to the button. Therefore, a total force of 200 g was applied on the molar. The force magnitude was determined in accordance to that of using Pendulum appliance (200–250 g) [9], which is regarded as a typical intraoral fixed appliance for molar distalization. A stringent recall protocol was instituted, requiring patients to attend clinical evaluations biweekly, with the progressive elastomeric chains systematically renewed at 4-week intervals. There might be a few days’ deviation due to schedule adjustments. The appliance was removed on the achievement of Class Ⅰ molar relationship.

In the CHG group, metal molar bands with bow tube were cemented to the bilateral maxillary first molars by 3 M RelyX™ Luting. The facebow (Shinye medical technology, Hangzhou, China) was adjusted to match the curve of the dentition and a U-loop was prepared just mesial to the bow tube. The pulling force on each side was calibrated to be approximately 450 g [10]. The patients were instructed to wear the CHG appliance no less than 12 h a day and revisit the clinician every 4 weeks to evaluate and adjust the appliance.

When the molar distalization was accomplished, the patients would continue their comprehensive orthodontic treatment according to their treatment plans. Complications including excessive root resorption, periodontal compromise and alveolar bone loss during the following treatment would be reported.

Tooth movement analysis

For each patient, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images were acquired by NewTom 5G XL scanner (NewTom, Bologna, Italy) before the orthodontic treatment. Digital impressions were taken before (PRE) and after (POST) molar distalization process. The duration of appliance utility was recorded in days as treatment time.

Considering the adherence to the As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) principle, only one single CBCT scan was acquired per patient before the treatment. This scan was part of the comprehensive orthodontic diagnostic records, not exclusively for research purposes. Meanwhile, CBCT was indispensable for extracting the 3D models of the molars and incisors, which could not be accomplished by using 2D alternative imaging. To eliminate any additional radiation exposure during the investigation, the outcomes were evaluated on non-radiologic intraoral digital impressions.

Quantitative assessment of three-dimensional spatio-dental displacement was executed via the rigorous superimposition of digitized post-treatment dental models against baseline CBCT volumetric reconstructions [3, 23–25]. The distalized molar and ipsilateral central incisor were extracted from CBCT slices to generate Stereo lithography (STL) models in Amira 2019 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The teeth were masked through magic wand tool and separated by limit line drawing, and smoothed at an extent of 3. Then they were imported into Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, Rock Hill, South Carolina, USA) along with the PRE and POST maxillary models. A local coordinate system was established for each tooth model as follows: the origin (the centroid of the tooth), the X-axis (+ palatal, - buccal), the Y-axis (+ distal, - mesial) and the Z-axis (+ apical, - occlusal). For a molar, the tips of mesial-buccal cusp (MB), mesial-palatal cusp (MB) and palatal root (PR), and the centroid (C) were determined as landmarks; the midpoint of incisal ridge (IR), the tip of root (R) and the centroid (C) were determined for an incisor. The centroid was determined by creating feature tool while the other landmarks were determined manually. The marked molar and incisor were aligned to the corresponding position on the PRE model through superimposing the coronal section using N-point alignment and global registration. Then, the POST model was aligned to the PRE model guided by palatal rugae superimposition using best fit alignment, where the region of the first, second and third rugae was included as the reference. A copy of molar and incisor models with landmarks were aligned to the POST model similar to the PRE model (Fig. 2, S1, S2).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). No data were missing in this cohort. The normality of the data was examined by Shapiro-Wilk test. The quantitative baseline characteristics were compared by student-t tests, while Fisher’s precision probability tests were applied for categorical variables. The ICC for each variable was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement for repeated measurements performed by the same observer. The ME for each variable was calculated by Dahlberg’s error formula [27]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{ME}\mathrm{=}\sqrt{\sum_{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{=1}}^{\mathrm{N}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{2}}}{\mathrm{2}\mathrm{N}}}$$\end{document}

ME is the measurement error; di is the difference between the first and the second measure and N is the sample size.

To ensure the robustness of the estimates against potential baseline imbalances inherent in observational designs, stabilized inverse probability weighting (SIPW) was employed. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) followed by an estimated marginal means (EM Means) comparison was used to analyze normally distributed data; while for non-normally distributed data, the empirical robust covariance estimators were applied in the GLMM, which could further account for the non-normality and potential heteroscedasticity. The appliance was set as the fixed effect, while the patient was set as the random effect. Morevoer, relevant baseline characteristics (age, gender, SNA, SNB, FMA) were included as covariates in the GLMM for a doubly robust estimation. This approach would avoid the inflation of type I error rates as possible associated with treating bilateral teeth as independent samples and balance the potentional selection bias from non-randomized allocation. The significance level was set at 5% (ɑ=0.05).

Finite element analysis

To further evaluate the biomechanical differences between the ID and the CHG, FEA was carried out as an exploratory study on a single representative patient in ID group, whose maxillary right first molar was distalized and second molar was unerupted. The ID was designed to be cemented on bilateral abutment teeth. The geometries of the maxillary dentition, maxillary cortical bone and cancellous bone were extracted from the CBCT slices. Then the extracted models were smoothed and polished in Geomagic Wrap to eliminate minor spikes or folded surfaces. The PDL models were constructed through thickening the root surfaces of the teeth uniformly by 0.2 mm [28], and the palatal mucosa model was similarly constructed at a thickness of 2 mm [29] to contact with the Nance button. The ID model was exported from 3Shape Appliance Designer. The buttons, facebow and molar band models were created in Solidworks 2023 (Dassault Systemes, Concord, Massachusetts, USA) based on the dentition model. The buttons, 3 mm in diameter and 1 mm in height [18], were created on the buccal surface and the palatal surface of the molar, respectively. In the model of the CHG, only the right side of the facebow was established considering the symmetry of the appliance. The band was positioned on the maxillary right first molar with a thickness of 0.8 mm and a tube on the buccal surface with an inner diameter of 1.1 mm [30]. The facebow with a diameter of 1.1 mm, was parallel to the dentition and a U-shaped curve was formed mesial to the band’s tube.

The FEA was carried out in ANSYS Workbench 2023 R1 (ANSYS, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA) using static structural module (Fig. 4). Material properties were listed in Table 1 and all the materials in this study were assumed to be linearly elastic, homogeneous and isotropic. Although the PDL exhibits viscoelastic and non-linear properties, the simplification was considered sufficient for comparing the relative stress distribution patterns and initial displacements between two appliances, consistent with established methodologies [18]. The friction coefficient between the ID and the mucosa, and the inner bow and the band tube was set to 0.01 [31]; the contacts between other structures were defined as bonded. All the structures were meshed by non-linear mechanical tetrahedron element. The mesh size for PDL was 0.2 mm [32], and 1.0 mm for the other structures verified by a convergence test, where the variation of the maximum von Mises stress on PDL was less than 5%. The numbers of nodes and elements for the ID models were 1,440,811 and 792,345, and for the CHG models they were 1,462,198 and 778,750. In regard to the boundary conditions, the top surface of the maxillary bone was fixed in all degrees of freedom. For the ID, two contraction forces of 1 Newtons (100 g) were applied respectively between the buttons and the traction hooks by adding a preload to the spring contact with a longitudinal stiffness of 0.29 Newtons/mm [33]; while for the CHG, a force of 4.5 Newtons (450 g) was applied on the facebow along the distal direction.

Results

Twenty-eight patients from 2022 to 2024 were enrolled in this study according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The enrolled patients were divided into 2 groups; totally 24 molars from 15 patients were distalized by IDs while 26 molars from 13 patients were distalized by CHGs. The baseline characteristics for each group are illustrated in Table 2. No statistical significances were detected between the two groups. No clinical or radiographic signs of unacceptable root resorption, periodontal compromise, or alveolar bone loss were observed during the orthodontic treatment.

The normality test showed that the treatment time (P = 0.035) and the molar’s torque (P = 0.019) for the ID group, and the incisor’s inclination (P = 0.002 and 0.013) and extrusion (P = 0.018 and 0.009) for the both groups exhibited non-normal distribution, while the other data were normally distributed.

Tooth movement

The ICC and ME for the re-measured variables are displayed in Table 3. The intra‑observer reliability was excellent for all variables, with ICC values above 0.951, indicating the consistency of the repeated landmark identification and measurement procedures. The observations on treatment time, molar’s movement and incisor’s movement for each group are shown in Table 4. The ME for molar’s distal movement (0.229 mm) accounted for less than 10% of the observed results, while the angular MEs were below 1 degree. It reveals that ID abbreviated the treatment time for the molar to distalize to the target position than CHG (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d=-3.58), so the efficiency of ID was notably higher (P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.87). Moreover, the ID seemed to intrude the molar slightly while the CHG did the opposite (P = 0.033, Cohen’s d=-0.84). For other metrics, no statistical significance was found between the two groups. However, the effect size was calculated as Cohen’s d = 0.47 and 0.43 for incisor’s inclination and extrusion respectively, indicating a minor-to-medium effect. Moreover, patient age exhibited a negative correlation with incisor’s extrusion (P = 0.004; Table S3).

Discussion

Maxillary molar distalization is used for correcting Class II malocclusion. Yet few appliances could meet the need for compliance-free, non-invasive and custom design. The current study compared the molar distalization efficiency using non-invasive, cemented ID with that using conventional CHG. We found that the ID offered a notably faster distalization efficiency, and had a potential effect of molar intrusion which was corresponded with the results of the FEA. Distal inclination and torque of the molars were comparable for the ID and the CHG, as well as the incisor’s movement trends.

As a prospective cohort study, there were no statistical differences in the baseline characteristics of patients enrolled in the ID and CHG groups (Table 2). Although the groups were divided according to the patients’ willingness, the baseline data showed that the patients were comparable and SIPW was applied to further compensate for the risk of bias. In this study, the individual tooth was used as the sample unit for the tooth movement analysis. This method was applied in a previous study evaluating molar distalization and eruption, where 74 molars from 42 patients were included for the analysis [17]. Here we referred to this method to maximize the data utilization for a limited sample size. Furthermore, the introduction of GLMM could account for the clustering effect of bilateral teeth nested within the same patient. This allowed for the use of tooth as the sample unit while statistically adjusting for the non-independence of bilateral teeth. However, the variables still showed scattered distributions in Fig. 5, which indicated the individual variations concerning tooth movement among the participants such as alveolar bone density, bite force and dietary habit.

The PDL was defined as non-linear hyperelastic material in some studies involving FEA [31, 32]. According to these studies, the Young’s modulus of PDL was consistent while the strain was smaller than 7.5%. In this study, the maximum strain of PDL was 0.3048%; so the linear elastic material property was sufficient for the analysis. However, it should be noted that the von Mises stress on PDL could be greater in non-linear analysis than in linear analysis with the same loaded force in a comparative FEA study [35]. Therefore, higher von Mises stress would be observed if non-linear material property was applied for the PDL.

The analysis of tooth movement was based on CBCT reconstruction and digital impressions alignment, which considered the three-dimensional displacements of the tooth. Simulating the root position by aligning the extracted tooth model with digital impression was proved to have a high accuracy with an average error of 0.054 mm [25]. The palatal rugae was regarded as a stable reference during the growth and was used for superimposing digital impressions of different periods in previous studies on orthodontic tooth movement monitoring [23, 24]. In the current study, molar’s movements along different spacial directions were calculated respectively by the changes of the landmarks’ coordinates rather than cephalometric measurements, where only sagittal plane was investigated.

Though CHG applied a higher force on the molar and induced a greater distal movement trend illustrated by FEA, it took a significantly longer period to distalize the molar to the target position (64.51 vs. 181.32 days). Also, the distalization efficiency using the ID was 1.97 mm/month while it was 0.57 mm/month for using the CHG. The efficiency of using clear aligners for maxillary molar distalization in early permanent dentition was estimated to be 0.28 mm/month according to the study of Li et al. [36]. Pendulum supported by mini-screws was also a typical strategy, and the efficacy of molar distalization was about 0.37 mm/month in the study of Çakmak et al. [37]. The ID was found to be competitively more efficient than other contemporary appliances. The rate orthodontic tooth movement could vary from 0.47 mm/month to 3.11 mm/month [38–40], which covers the findings in this study. Considering the high efficiency of ID, concerns regarding potential periodontal compromises, including lagging alveolar bone apposition and risks of external apical root resorption as well as patient-reported outcomes should be noted in the future to evaluate the ID’s clinical performance more generally [41, 42]. It should be acknowledged that the distal inclination of the molar partly increased the coronal distal displacement; since the molar’s inclination was similar between the ID and the CHG group (P = 0.926), the efficiency advantage of the ID was reliable. The efficacy of CHG is related to the cumulative hours of wearing, which heavily depends on the patient’s compliance [3]. Despite explicit clinical directives mandating CHG wear for a minimum of 12 h daily, complex psychosocial factors and substantial physical discomfort frequently converge to compromise the actual cumulative wear time. While for ID, the appliance and the elastomeric chains were equipped all day to provide orthodontic force continuously. It was reported that the force exerted by the elastomeric chain would decay to approximately 79.8% of the initial force [43], which could still meet the minimum orthodontic force of 70 g for a tooth’s bodily movement [44]. The main reason for the high distalization efficiency of the ID was probably the persistence of the force. However, it seems controversial whether force magnitude or force duration contributes more to orthodontic tooth movement. The study of Talvitie et al. showed that heavier force magnitude could result in greater maxillary first molar and second molar distalization [16]; however, their another study indicated that little differences were observed between the heavy force and light force on the measurement level of the dental arch [5]. An in vivo study on beagle dogs revealed that a continuous light force could induce faster tooth movement than an intermittent heavy force [45]. The outcomes in this study agreed with the perspective that force regime affected the rate of tooth movement more. Additionally, the friction system could also influence the effectiveness of orthodontic tooth movement indicated by a systematic review [46]. For CHG, the distal force was conducted to the molar through the face bow and the molar band which involved the friction between the bow and the tube. Whilst the distal force was directly applied on the molar by the elastomeric chains hanging on the buttons while using ID. So the difference in the friction involved between ID and CHG would partially account for the higher efficiency of ID.

The shortened molar distalization duration provided by ID could consequently contribute to a shorter orthodontic duration, which would be beneficial to the patient in several aspects. The excessive orthodontic duration might increase the risk of root resorption [47] and demineralization [48]. Also, the patient’s oral health-related quality of life and compliance could be decreased by an extended duration of orthodontic treatment [49].

We found that the ID had a potential effect of molar intrusion compared with the CHG. Although the difference in molar extrusion between the two appliances was statistically significant, it turned out that two-thirds of the molars distalized by the ID were intruded while the other one-third were not. Unfortunately, the exploratory analyses did not reveal any factors which might be correlated with molar’s extrusion. Therefore, the effectiveness of vertical control by the ID requires further validation. The clinically observed intrusive effect is theoretically attributable to the spatial geometric relationship of the force vectors; specifically, engineering the traction hooks apical to the bonded buttons would generate a continuous, apically directed force component as hypothesized (Fig. 7). The molar movement along the Z-axis calculated in the FEA might support this explanation (Table 5). It will be worth optimizing the design of ID to enhance the effect of molar’s vertical control. In this study, the CHG treatment extruded the molar by 0.95 mm in average. Previous studies based on cephalometric evaluations have revealed that CHG would cause molar extrusion during treatment. Mossaz et al. [6] reported molar crown movement of 5.05 mm away from the horizontal plane, while Sambataro et al. [4] reported 1.14 mm (high-pull) and 2.7 mm (cervical-pull) taking the palatal plane as the reference. The measurements of molar extrusion in our study was smaller than in previous studies. One of the most important reasons might be that the evaluation in our study was conducted by palatal rugae superimposition and the landmark was the centroid of the molar. Therefore, the vertical changes caused by maxilla growth and molar inclination were probably excluded. Increased vertical dimension is one of the outcomes of molar extrusion, which is unfavorable for hyperdivergent patients with Class Ⅱ malocclusion [24, 50]. Considering this aspect, ID could be a better choice for these patients.

Molar’s distal inclination and counterclockwise torque were observed in both groups, and the differences between the two groups were of no statistical significance within the limits of the sample size. The distal inclination had been reported as a side effect of molar distalization by various appliances or strategies [24, 51, 52]. In our study, the distal forces were mainly applied to the molar’s crown in both groups. For a lack of an opposite force to achieve a couple moment, the distal force was unable to reach an ideal moment-to-force ratio for molar’s bodily movement. Therefore, further modifications like positioning the buttons more apically or adding attachments for mesial protraction might address this problem. Molar’s torque was also a common side effect of the ID or the CHG. Interestingly, the torque trend in FEA was in line with molars in the CHG group, however it was a contrary for the ID group. This deviation could be partly explained by the difference in sensitivity to mechanical force between the buccal and palatal tissues of the molar [15]. Besides, marginal geometric discrepancies between the idealized button coordinates in the FEA mesh environment and their actual clinical bonding topography may account for this slight biomechanical variance. The unerupted maxillary second molar probably blocked the counterclockwise torque of maxillary first molar in FEA. Additionally, this FEA was focused on the condition that the orthodontic force was initially applied, however the long-term tooth movement might not completely follow the initial trend. Considering the flexibility of force application for the ID, adjusting the buttons’ position or the force proportion on the two sides of the molar might control the torque more intentionally.

The incisor’s movement was analyzed to evaluate the influence on the anchorage. It showed that the incisors had a similar movement for each group. That is to say, the use of the ID would not aggravate the anchorage loss for the anterior teeth compared with the use of the CHG. The CHG took the extraoral structures as the anchorage, which should have little interference with the anterior teeth. The anchorage for the ID was provided by the dentition (except the distalized molar) and the palatal rugae, which might be sufficient for the molar distalization by a force of 200 g. The von Mises stresses on PDLs of the abutment teeth turned out to be notably lower than the molars (Table 5). Therefore, the inclination and extrusion of the incisors in this study was inferred to be the result of maxilla growth. The negative correlation between patient age and incisor’s extrusion partially supported this inference. In the study of Christou et al. [53], the incisors of adolescents showed labial inclination and slight extrusion by measuring the cephalograms and the casts over a 4-year-long observation. Although the observation period in our study was shorter, the incisor’s movement trend was consistent with theirs. Nevertheless, we still noticed that 2 patients in the ID group showed prominent incisor’s inclination of more than 15 degree, and the Cohen’s d value of 0.47 represents a potentially incisor’s inclination effect by the ID, where the insignificance was possibly attributable to limited statistical power due to the small sample size. So it would be worthwhile to enhance the anchorage like ligating the anterior teeth to prevent potential anchorage loss. If excessive anterior teeth inclination was observed during the wear of ID, it would be better to remove the elastomeric chains immediately to avoid severer anchorage loss.

Here, we designed a CAD/CAM, non-invasive and compliance-free appliance for maxillary molar distalization and preliminarily proved its high efficiency. However, the limitations of this study were as follows. The group division was determined by patients’ willingness, which could contribute to selection bias that patients with good compliance might tend to choose CHG. However, relevant factors that could influence compliance or prior compliance history data were not collected. The decay of elastomeric chain force was not specifically recorded, so the actual effective wearing time of ID might be overestimated. The analysis of the molars and the incisors was conducted on the level of an individual tooth not a patient within a small sample size and only the distalization period was included rather than the entire treatment. Hence the generalizability was limited, and a multi-center long-term follow-up with a larger sample size is required to assess the stability and root absorption of the distalized molars. The study mainly focused on the efficiency of the appliance, however patient-reported outcomes as pain, discomfort, speech disturbance or mucosal irritation were not recorded. These would be worthy to be investigated in further studies. The FEA was established on a single patient as an exploratory analysis thus the individual heterogeneity was ignored, which should limit the external validity. Besides, the material properties were simplified and the models were idealized in the FEA. So the FEA results should be regarded as a trend of movement rather than exact physiological quantification.

Conclusions

A new appliance for maxillary molar distalization was developed and its clinical efficacy was investigated. Within the limitations of this study, we can draw the conclusion that the ID had a faster efficiency for molar distalization and might achieve better vertical control compared with conventional CHG. The molar’s distal inclination and torque were potential side effects of the ID and careful monitoring of anterior anchorage is recommended while using the ID.
