Liter-Scale Precision Synthesis and Measurement of Nano-to-Micrometer Scale PSNPs with Graphene Liquid Cells
Authors: Chanho Um, Joonwoo Seo, Yong Duk Kim, Inbo Park, Dong-Kwon Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monodisperse polystyrene nanoparticles (PSNPs) have great potential as standard materials in various fields, including environmental analysis, biological research, and instrumental calibration. However, reproducible liter-scale synthesis of PSNPs with controlled sizes across the nano-to-micrometer size range remains challenging because particle nucleation, growth, and colloidal stability are highly sensitive to polymerization conditions. In addition, particle size measurements often vary depending on the analytical method used. In this study, we report a robust liter-scale synthetic protocol for highly monodisperse PSNPs with controlled diameters of 50, 100, 200, and 1000 nm by optimizing key synthetic parameters. The synthesized PSNPs were benchmarked against commercial PSNP standards using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering, scanning mobility particle sizing, and static light scattering. Conventional dry-grid TEM yielded smaller apparent particle sizes than the particle size analyzers, indicating that drying and electron-beam exposure can influence the measured dimensions of soft polymer nanoparticles. To further evaluate the PSNP morphology under liquid-confined conditions, graphene liquid cell (GLC)-based solution-phase TEM was employed as a complementary characterization method.

Introduction

Monodisperse polystyrene nano-to-micro particles have demonstrated broad potential for applications in fields such as drug delivery, , nanostructure template, − and chromatography matrix. Recently, the potential applications have expanded as standard materials for simulating and comparing the physical behavior of particle-based pollutants such as fine dust or microplastics in the environment, and calibrating analytical instruments has been increasingly recognized. − Polystyrene nanoparticles (PSNPs) are widely utilized due to their ease of synthesis via radical polymerization into highly spherical shapes with narrow size distributions, excellent physical and chemical stability, and facile surface modification. , Therefore, the demand for strategies capable of large-scale, high-yield synthesis has become increasingly important in industrial applications.

Emulsion polymerization is a representative method for synthesizing monodisperse PSNPs. , In this method, particle dispersion and the size of nanoparticles are primarily determined by the concentration of the surfactant. Excessively high or low surfactant concentrations can negatively affect nucleation, thereby influencing the uniformity of the product. However, a major drawback of conventional emulsion polymerization is the requirement for a large quantity of surfactant, which complicates postsynthesis purification and may leave residual surfactant, limiting potential applications. To address this limitation, Zhenxing et al. reported that highly monodisperse PSNPs smaller than 200 nm could be synthesized by adding a small amount of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) below its critical micelle concentration, effectively achieving emulsion polymerization under quasiemulsifier-free conditions. Considering that the concentration of added surfactant did not form micelles, only a homogeneous nucleation process occurs in this polymerization system, which is called a quasiemulsifier-free emulsion polymerization. However, even with this method, systematic studies of optimizing the reaction parameters to target specific particle sizes remain limited. Furthermore, emulsion polymerization is suitable for the synthesis of relatively small particles ,, by generating numerous nucleation sites through micellar nucleation or mixed nucleation mechanisms, thereby maximizing the particle number and minimizing the monomer consumption per individual particle, , and therefore, other types of polymerization methods are required for the synthesis of larger particles.

Dispersion polymerization is another effective technique for producing polymer nanoparticles. The amount of micelles is extremely small, so nuclei generated by oligomer precipitation are limited, and as a result, the number of nuclei generated is smaller than that in emulsion polymerization, resulting in higher monomer consumption in the growth stage. , Additionally, continuous precipitation prevents nucleation oversaturation and instantaneous nucleation bursts, while the stabilizer rapidly adsorbs onto the surface of preparticles, suppressing secondary precipitation. Consequently, only a small number of nuclei grow throughout the polymerization process, yielding relatively large particles. ,, At this time, the concentration of stabilizer and initiator is closely related to the polymerization rate, significantly affecting particle size and uniformity. However, precisely controlling the polymerization rate is difficult, requiring additional methods to achieve the desired particle characteristics. , One of them is a method using a comonomer. Gorsd et al. reported that adding a comonomer such as allylamine can improve particle growth and dispersion by controlling the characteristics of the particle surface, which is effective in controlling the overall particle size and improving uniformity.

In addition, accurate characterization of particle morphology, size, and dispersion is essential for evaluating the quality of the product. Common analytical techniques include particle size analyzers, which determine particle size and distribution in the solution or dried state. However, these ensemble-averaged methods cannot resolve the solution state morphology and size of individual particles. While electron microscopy can provide direct visualization of particle morphology, size, and uniformity. , However, it has long been suggested that the process of drying solution samples for electron microscopy measurements may differ from the actual state and size of the PSNPs in the solution. − From this perspective, it was necessary to observe the PSNPs using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) in the solution phase. Previous studies have proposed cryo-TEM as an alternative approach for preserving the solution-state morphology of nanoparticles during TEM analysis. Cryo-TEM is particularly effective for minimizing structural deformation through vitrification under cryogenic conditions, but it generally requires complex sample preparation and specialized instrumentation. In contrast, GLC-TEM enables direct observation of nanoparticles in a liquid environment and allows time-dependent analysis under relatively accessible experimental conditions.

To complement conventional particle-size characterization methods and to better evaluate the morphology of PSNPs under liquid-phase conditions, we additionally employed a graphene liquid cell (GLC) technique. Recently, GLC has emerged as a powerful method for high-resolution analysis of nanomaterials in liquid environments. Graphene, a single layer of carbon atoms, offers several advantages for electron microscopy. It minimizes background electron scattering, allows efficient energy dissipation due to its high thermal and electrical conductivity, exhibits excellent flexibility and mechanical strength, and is impermeable to small molecules. These properties enable graphene membranes to encapsulate gaseous, liquid, or solid substances for imaging under both ambient and vacuum conditions. Accordingly, GLC-TEM can provide complementary information for evaluating nanomaterials’ morphology under liquid-phase conditions. −

Numerous polymerization methods have been developed to produce monodisperse PSNPs across a wide range of size ranges. However, scaling these polymerization systems from laboratory scale to liter-scale synthesis remains challenging because particle nucleation, growth kinetics, and colloidal stability are highly sensitive to reaction environments such as mixing efficiency, heat transfer, and local monomer or surfactant concentration gradients. As the reaction volume increases, these factors can alter nucleation behavior and particle growth dynamics, potentially resulting in broader particle size distributions, reduced reproducibility, and aggregation, even under nominally identical synthetic conditions. Despite the increasing industrial demand for the large-scale production of monodisperse PSNPs, systematic studies on scalable parameter optimization across broad particle-size ranges remain limited. Given the increasing importance of understanding and optimizing synthesis conditions based on particle size, this study systematically investigates and establishes the optimal reaction parameters for PSNP synthesis at a liter scale. Accordingly, this study focuses on the scalable optimization of established emulsion and dispersion polymerization methods to produce spherical and monodisperse PSNPs of 50, 100, 200, and 1000 nm. PSNPs of various sizes were synthesized on a large scale by using optimized synthetic conditions, and their precisely controlled size distributions were analyzed using various analytical tools. Because polymeric nanoparticles can undergo deformation during conventional dry-state TEM analysis, additional GLC-TEM characterization was performed to complement conventional particle size measurements and to better evaluate the morphology of PSNPs under liquid-phase conditions.

Experimental Section

Materials

The reagents used in the study are as follows. Styrene (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99.0%), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (≥98.5%), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) (M w ∼ 29,000 g/mol), potassium persulfate (KPS) (≥99.0%), 4,4′-azobis­(4-cyanovaleric acid) (ACVA) (≥98.0%), and 1-pentanol (≥99.0), ammonium persulfate (APS) (≥98.0%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and allylamine (≥99.0%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). NaOH (≥97.0%) and MgSO4 (≥99.0%) for styrene purification were purchased from Daejung Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd. (Shiheung, South Korea). The graphene used in the fabrication of GLC was CVD single-layer graphene grown on Cu foil 4″ × 4″ (Graphene Platform, Japan), the TEM grids were purchased from TED PELLA (Quantifoil Cu Orthogonal Array). The 50, 100, 200, and 1000 nm commercial PSNP standard were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA).

Characterization

TEM images were obtained using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (H-7100, Hitachi) operating at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV. The hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of the particles were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (ELSZneo, Otsuka Electronics), and analyses were performed in distilled water at room temperature. A scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS) (Electrostatic Classifier 3082, TSI) was used to measure the average size and size distribution of 50, 100, and 200 nm PSNPs in the aerosol. Measurements were performed under controlled flow rates to obtain accurate number-based size distributions. Static light scattering (SLS) (LA-960 V2, Horiba) was used to further measure the average size and size distribution of 1000 nm PSNPs. The scattered light intensity was measured at multiple angles. Raman spectra were collected using an inverted Raman microscope (Weve, South Korea) with a 100× objective (N.A. 1.3) (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the signal was obtained by a ND filter 10%, exposure time 1 s, and 532 nm laser condition.

Purification of the Styrene Monomer

Since the purchased styrene monomer contains 4-tert-butylcatechol, a polymerization inhibitor, a procedure to remove it is necessary. 200 mL of styrene and 200 mL of 10% NaOH solution were mixed, stirred for 5 min, transferred to a 1 L separatory funnel, allowed to stand for 5 min, and the aqueous layer (lower layer) was discarded. This process was repeated 3 times. This allowed the polymerization inhibitor to be removed. 200 mL of DW was added to the supernatant obtained after this repetition, stirred for 5 min, transferred to a 1 L separatory funnel, and allowed to stand for 5 min, and the aqueous layer (lower layer) was discarded. This process was repeated 3 times. This allowed the remaining NaOH to be removed. After that, the supernatant was transferred to a 250 mL beaker, 15 g of MgSO4 was added, mixed for 1–2 min, and filtered under reduced pressure to obtain yellow styrene. This removes any remaining distilled water in the styrene solution. 200 mL of styrene and a magnetic stirrer are placed in a 500 mL round flask and combined with a distillation apparatus connected to a vacuum pump, followed by vacuum distillation. Finally, a transparent styrene monomer that has been purified and distilled can be obtained and is used for polymerization immediately after distillation.

50, 100, and 200 nm PSNP Synthesis

The procedures described below correspond to laboratory-scale optimization experiments. The liter-scale synthesis conditions discussed in the main text were obtained by scaling up the optimized laboratory-scale formulations while maintaining identical reagent ratios and reaction conditions. Polystyrene nanoparticles with sizes of 50, 100, and 200 nm were synthesized through emulsion polymerization. A 500 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a mechanical stirrer and a reflux condenser was charged with 90 mL of DW, 5 g of styrene as a monomer, 0.1 g of 1-pentanol as a cosurfactant, and SDS as a surfactant and heated while stirring at 300 rpm. To obtain PSNPs with sizes of 50, 100, and 200 nm, 160, 130, and 80 mg of SDS were added, respectively. The solution was stirred and purged with nitrogen to remove dissolved oxygen, followed by heating to 70 ± 1 °C. A solution containing 0.2 g of the initiator KPS dissolved in 10 mL of distilled water was prepared and quickly added to the solution at 70 ± 1 °C. The reaction was carried out for 24 h. After the reaction was completed, the solution was washed by centrifugation (14,000 rpm, 30 min) and redispersed in DW. When experimenting with a liter scale, the same procedure was used, and only the amount of chemical was doubled.

1000 nm PSNP Synthesis

The procedures described below correspond to laboratory-scale optimization experiments. The liter-scale synthesis conditions discussed in the main text were obtained by scaling up the optimized laboratory-scale formulations while maintaining identical reagent ratios and reaction conditions. Polystyrene nanoparticles measuring 1000 nm were synthesized via dispersion polymerization. A 500 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a mechanical stirrer and a reflux condenser was mixed with 250 g of ethanol as a solvent, 6.00 g of PVP as a stabilizer, 25 g of styrene as a monomer, and 1.2 mL of allylamine as a comonomer. The mixture was stirred at 200 rpm and heated. Oxygen was removed by bubbling nitrogen, while the temperature reached 72 ± 1 °C. Once the temperature reached 72 ± 1 °C, 0.17 g of ACVA, the initiator, was added, and the reaction was carried out for 24 h. After the reaction was completed, the solution was washed by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 1 min), redispersed in 300 mL DW, and the particle size was confirmed by TEM analysis. When experimenting with a liter scale, the same procedure was used, and only the amount of chemical was doubled. The optimized laboratory-scale synthesis condition consisted of 6.0 g PVP and 0.17 g ACVA. These conditions were subsequently used for the particle characterization presented in Table and for proportional scale-up experiments described in the main text.

Graphene Liquid Cell Fabrication

A thick aluminum foil is drilled with a 4 mm diameter circular hole, placed on top of the chemically vapor-deposited single-layer graphene grown on Cu foil, and flattened with two slide glasses to make it thin. The Cu layer was etched with 0.1 M APS for 4–5 h. The Al/graphene was washed in 2 L of DW for 2 h. The graphene was transferred onto the TEM grid by lifting the graphene upward from the bottom. A reverse tweezer was used to pick up the graphene-transferred TEM grid, and it was dried.

A 0.01% PSNP solution and a 1.25% uranyl acetate solution were mixed at a 50:1 ratio. Uranyl acetate is a representative heavy-metal staining agent used for low-contrast organic materials because of its high electron density. 0.5 μL of the solution was dropped onto the graphene-transferred TEM grid. The etched and washed Al/graphene is picked up with a tweezer and transferred onto the TEM grid to form a graphene liquid cell.

Result and Discussion

We first investigated the synthetic parameters influencing the particle size and uniformity of the synthesized PSNPs using a normal TEM. To synthesize nanosized PSNPs, a quasi-emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization method was used, in which a smaller amount of SDS was added as a surfactant than in a conventional emulsion polymerization, resulting in the synthesis of smooth, spherical PSNPs. The polymerization temperature was set to 70 ± 1 °C based on previously reported procedures, and the particle size was controlled exclusively by adjusting the amount of SDS under otherwise identical conditions. In brief, 50.0 g of styrene monomer and 1.0 g of 1-pentanol were added to 900 mL of distilled water (DW), and the temperature of the mixture was set at 70 ± 1 °C. At this temperature, varying amounts of SDS were added according to the target size. 2.0 g of potassium persulfate (KPS) dissolved in 100 mL of DW was heated up to 70 ± 1 °C, then quickly added to the solution mixture. Since temperature can affect uniform nucleation and particle dispersion in polymer polymerization, the process of prewarming the initiator solution to a temperature close to the solvent temperature is important to prevent abrupt temperature changes. ,

The mixed solution was stirred for 24 h after the addition of KPS. After cooling down the solution at room temperature, it was centrifuged to obtain a final product in a concentration of 1.0 w/v %. As shown in Figure A, it showed a clearly decreased PSNP size from over 200 nm to below 50 nm with the increasing amount of SDS (Figure A). Maintaining the temperature at 70 ± 1 °C is another single key parameter to obtain monodisperse PSNPs, which is well matched with the previous results. In this relationship, we found that the optimal SDS amounts were determined to be 160, 130, and 80 mg for synthesizing PSNPs of 50, 100, and 200 nm, respectively. TEM analysis (measurement of more than 300 particles in TEM images) revealed that PSNPs were close to the target size and possessed high monodispersity, exhibiting sizes of 51.1 ± 4.1 nm, 96.4 ± 2.5 nm, and 196.5 ± 5.4 nm, respectively, with high precision (Figure S1, Table ).

Key parameters on the synthesis of PSNPs. (A) Amount of SDS for 50, 100, and 200 nm PSNPs, (B) temperature, (C) amount of PVP, and (D) amount of ACVA for 1000 nm PSNP solution (n = 100 particles).

The correlation between the amount of SDS and the particle size is similar to the behavior expected in general emulsion polymerization, as confirmed in previous studies. ,, First, in a quasi-emulsifier-free emulsion polymerization, when a water-insoluble monomer such as styrene is used, initiation begins, with the monomer forming an oligomeric free radical (Scheme A). When the radical grows to a certain length, its solubility in the solvent decreases, causing it to precipitate. Unlike typical emulsion polymerization, where the surfactant simply forms micelles, the precipitated oligomer forms mixed micelles with a portion of the surfactant SDS. This increases the concentration of micelles that form nuclei compared with conventional methods. Consequently, the greater the amount of SDS, the greater the concentration of micelles that grow, leading to a progressively smaller particle size. Within these formed micelles, monomer polymerization is initiated to form primary particles. SDS, which was present in the solution without forming micelles, adsorbed onto the particle surface, and interacted with the initially formed particle surface, providing steric and electrostatic repulsion to prevent particle aggregation, ensure stability, and facilitate particle growth.

Schematic Diagram of Polymerization. (A) Emulsion Polymerization and (B) Dispersion Polymerization

Next, PSNPs with the target size of 1000 nm, corresponding to the micrometer-size range, were synthesized via dispersion polymerization. The effects of reaction temperature, polymer stabilizer PVP, and initiator ACVA on the particle size were systematically investigated. Typically, a 1 L scale reaction for targeting 1000 nm PSNPs was conducted in 900 mL of ethanol. 12.0 g of PVP, 50.0 g of styrene, 2.4 mL of allylamine, and 0.3 g of ACVA were quickly added and stirred at 72 ± 1 °C for 24 h. Finally, it was centrifuged to obtain 1 L PSNP solution in a concentration of 0.75 w/v %. Although there was a large variation in the size of PSNPs by changing the reaction temperature, the deviation of PSNP size was the smallest when polymerization was performed at 72 ± 1 °C, and PSNPs closest to the target size of 1000 nm were synthesized. Therefore, 72 ± 1 °C appears to be the most optimal condition (Figure B). As the amount of PVP increased from 1.0 to 6.0 g, the particle size decreased, and no substantial reduction in particle diameter was observed above 3.5 g of PVP (Figures C and S2). However, additional optimization experiments revealed that particle size distributions became less uniform when the ACVA concentration was varied under the 3.5 g PVP condition. In contrast, particles synthesized with 6.0 g of PVP maintained a narrow size distribution over the tested ACVA range, indicating improved colloidal stability and reproducibility during dispersion polymerization. Therefore, 6.0 g of PVP was selected for subsequent optimization and scale-up experiments. Dispersion polymerization differs from emulsion polymerization in that, rather than proceeding through nucleation initiated by micelles spontaneously formed by surfactants above a critical concentration, it proceeds via the growth of oligomers generated in the solution during the early stage of the reaction, which subsequently lose solubility and precipitate to form particles (Scheme B). As the oligomers formed early in the reaction grow, their solubility decreases and they precipitate, forming primary particles. PVP adsorbs to the surface of the primary particles, preventing aggregation and maintaining the particles in a separated state. As the amount of PVP increases, the number of separated and stable particles increases, resulting in smaller particle size and higher uniformity.

Meanwhile, the relationship between the amount of initiator (ACVA) and size was critical, as shown in Figures D and S3. As the amount of ACVA increased, the number of free radicals generated in the solution increased, and accordingly, more oligomers with short-chain lengths with high solubility were formed. As a result, the amount of monomer consumed for each primary particle increased. Therefore, the average size of the ultimately growing particles increased, and the size distribution also tended to be relatively broadened. , Based on the ACVA optimization results, 0.17 g of ACVA was selected as the final synthesis condition because it produced PSNPs closest to the target diameter of 1000 nm while maintaining high monodispersity and reproducibility (Figure D). Finally, PSNPs of size 1000 nm were synthesized by using 6.0 g of PVP and 0.17 g of ACVA under optimized dispersion polymerization conditions.

The scale-up was carried out from the existing 500 mL scale to the liter scale. The synthesized PSNPs were further characterized to verify that particle size and monodispersity were maintained after scale-up to the liter scale. To obtain a more specific interpretation of the size data of the synthesized PSNPs, additional analysis was performed in addition to TEM analysis, as shown in Figure . For TEM analysis, the particle sizes were measured to be 51.1 ± 4.1 nm, 96.4 ± 2.5 nm, 196.5 ± 5.4 nm, and 966.1 ± 31.1 nm, and the corresponding dispersities were 8.0, 2.6, 2.7, and 3.2%, which are comparable to those of the commercial PSNP standards (48.0 ± 6.3, 100.7 ± 8.1, 194.8 ± 7.2, and 987.7 ± 18.8 nm size, and 13.1, 8.0, 3.7, and 1.9% dispersity, respectively), indicating a similar level of size uniformity (Figure S4). However, conventional TEM analysis methods, which essentially require drying the sample before measurement, struggle to accurately represent the state of particles in solution. Furthermore, polymeric nanoparticles can undergo partial deformation or shrinkage under prolonged electron-beam exposure, which may influence the measured particle size during TEM analysis. However, such effects can be reduced through strategies such as low-dose imaging, minimized exposure time, and time-dependent evaluation of particle-size changes, allowing TEM to remain a reliable and widely used technique for nanoparticle characterization.

Therefore, three different particle size analyzers (i.e., dynamic light scattering (DLS), scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS), and static light scattering (SLS)) were used to compare the results. DLS measures fluctuations in scattered light intensity caused by the Brownian motion of particles suspended in a fluid, and it is primarily employed for particle size analysis due to its suitability for small particle systems. , The size distributions of synthesized PSNPs obtained from DLS were 56.9 ± 8.1, 112.1 ± 8.2, 213.6 ± 15.4, and 1050.3 ± 64.0 nm (Table ), confirming that the synthesized PSNPs closely matched the target sizes, but a broader size distribution was observed compared with that obtained by TEM analysis. Since DLS analyzes the hydrodynamic diameter, which reflects not only the solution-state constrained size but also contributions from the surfactant layer and ionic layer, therefore, a relative discrepancy from the solution-state constrained size can arise.

Unlike DLS, the SMPS is an instrument that analyzes the size distribution of particles in gases such as atmospheric aerosols and can measure the size of dried state particles with high accuracy. However, since this SMPS analyzes based on electrical mobility, its use is limited when the particles are too large. , Therefore, only particles with sizes of 50 nm, 100 nm, and 200 nm could be analyzed with SMPS. The analysis results of synthesized PSNPs using SMPS were measured as 52.9 ± 1.2, 108.9 ± 1.1, and 204.4 ± 1.0 nm (Table ), respectively, confirming a very low standard deviation. SLS is an instrument that analyzes the size distribution of particles by measuring the intensity of fixed scattered light generated when light is irradiated on the particles from various angles. Since the intensity of scattered light is proportional to the particle size, it is suitable for analyzing relatively large particles. When PSNPs with a size of 1000 nm synthesized through SLS were analyzed, the result was 1026.6 ± 53.7 nm (Table ).

When the commercial PSNP standards were measured with a DLS, they were measured to be 55.4 ± 6.4, 109.3 ± 9.7, 211.1 ± 15.9, and 1064.2 ± 29.7 nm. SMPS and SLS results of commercial PSNP standards were 47.8 ± 0.5, 102.9 ± 1.3, 205.4 ± 0.9, and 1014.4 ± 68.8 nm (Table , Figure S5), respectively. Comparative analysis of various analytical methods confirmed that PSNPs were synthesized precisely at the target size and verified monodispersity of the particles. Each analytical method showed different trends in the measurement results. TEM measurements generally yielded smaller particle sizes than other analytical methods. This result is expected to the shrinking caused by sample drying in the high vacuum and electron beam irradiation during analysis. DLS showed the greatest variation in the PSNPs’ size distribution because it reflects the hydrodynamic diameter, including the surfactant and ionic layer. In contrast, SMPS allows direct particle counting through separation based on electrical mobility, and SLS performs well on monodisperse samples through intensity-based light scattering analysis. Therefore, PSNP analysis using SMPS and SLS confirmed results close to the solution-state constrained size and with minimal variation in the size distribution. These results demonstrate that the complementary use of various analytical methods successfully determined the precise size of synthesized PSNPs, almost the same quality as that of commercial PSNP standards.

In spite of the usefulness of DLS, SMPS, and SLS as particle size analyzers, they provide ensemble-averaged results that cannot provide detailed size variation in the product. In this regard, TEM is an alternative analytical method. It would be greatly beneficial, if possible, to measure the PSNPs in solution with TEM. To achieve this goal, we employed GLC on a TEM grid. The GLC on the TEM grid was fabricated using a sandwiched method, which encases graphene sheets above and below the solution to trap the liquid. , First, the graphene-coated Cu foil is chemically vapor-deposited (CVD) and etched in ammonium persulfate (APS) to leave only the graphene bilayer. After transferring the graphene bilayer to a DW solvent, the TEM grid is lifted from below to transfer the graphene layer (Figure A­(a)). Next, the PSNP solution was pipetted onto the TEM grid with the transferred graphene, and another graphene layer was transferred from top to bottom, trapping the solution between the two graphene layers to fabricate the GLC (Figure A­(b)). At this time, the solution can stably exist between the layers because the van der Waals force between the graphene layers is relatively strong. When the fabricated GLC was analyzed by Raman analysis, the G peak of typical pure graphene was observed at 1580 cm–1 and the 2D peak was observed at 2655 cm–1, representing successful formation of graphene on the TEM grid (Figure S6). This analysis confirmed that particle boundaries remained sufficiently circular during observation, enabling reliable diameter tracking throughout the irradiation.

Particle analysis of PSNPs using GLC was performed at 100 s intervals for up to 800 s, while the electron beam was continuously irradiated onto the sample (Figure B). The method used at this time was to measure the length from the center of the PSNPs to the surface a total of 72 times at 5° intervals (Figure S7). As shown in Figure B­(a), PSNPs (50 nm) showed a significant size variation in the center-to-surface distance compared to other sizes. This behavior is attributed to the relatively larger measurement uncertainty associated with small particles, where slight variations in edge determination can produce comparatively large relative errors in the center-to-surface distance analysis. In contrast, PSNPs with sizes of 100, 200, and 1000 nm showed more reproducible and consistent size measurements because the relative influence of edge-detection uncertainty decreased with increasing particle size. Interestingly, PSNPs (a) 50 nm, (b) 100 nm, (c) 200 nm, and (d) 1000 nm all showed the gradual size decrease over time, reaching almost no change after 600 s. The rates of average size changes for 50, 100, 200, and 1000 nm PSNPs were 5.1, 20.2, 9.8, and 6.3%, respectively, and it was confirmed that the largest size reduction occurred at 100 nm.

The size changes of synthesized PSNPs were further studied using GLC and normal grid TEM. TEM images of PSNPs in the GLC with sizes of 50, 100, 200, and 1000 nm are shown in Figure A (Figure A), and TEM images of PSNPs on the normal TEM grid are shown in Figure B (Figure B). No significant damage or deformation of the particle surface was observed in the GLC analysis. However, the analysis using a typical TEM grid showed that the 100 and 200 nm PSNP surfaces were deformed, resulting in a different contrast ratio of the particle surface in the TEM image and rough deformation of the smooth PSNP surface. , These results suggest that the surface structure is altered by damage caused by the very strong electron beam of the TEM to the polymer with relatively weak intermolecular bonds. Additionally, the particle sizes measured using a normal TEM grid were observed to be up to 10% smaller than those obtained using a GLC grid (Figure C). PSNPs (50 nm) showed the size of 56–57 nm in the GLC TEM grid, while it showed 51.1 ± 4.1 nm on the normal TEM grid. Interestingly, PSNPs (100 nm) showed a 22.4% size reduction starting from 102.9 ± 5.2 nm on the GLC TEM grid until 700 s, after which there was almost no change in size. On the normal TEM grid, there was a 6.9% size reduction starting from 96.4 ± 2.5 nm until 400 s, after which there was almost no change in size. PSNPs (200 nm) showed similar results to PSNPs (100 nm), showing a 9.4% size reduction from 205.9 ± 3.5 nm on the GLC TEM grid until 700 s and a rapid size reduction from 196.5 ± 5.4 nm on the normal TEM grid until 200 s, with a 10.1% size reduction until 400 s. After that, there was almost no change in size like PSNPs (100 nm). For PSNPs (1000 nm), both GLC and normal TEM grids showed a rapid size reduction up to 200 s, after which the rate of change gradually decreased. The GLC TEM grid starting at 1026.4 ± 5.3 nm ultimately showed a size reduction of 7.7%, while the normal TEM grid starting at 966.1 ± 8.8 nm showed a size reduction of 13.3%. To further compare the effects of imaging conditions on particle-size evolution, the initial shrinkage slope during the first 200 s of irradiation (Figure A) and the retention ratio after 800 s were quantified (Figure B). The initial shrinkage slope reflects the susceptibility of PSNPs to early-stage beam-induced dimensional changes, whereas the retention ratio represents the extent to which the original particle size is preserved after prolonged irradiation.

As summarized in Figure , significant differences between GLC-TEM and conventional TEM grid measurements were observed for most of the particle sizes. In particular, 100 nm PSNPs exhibited a significantly steeper initial shrinkage slope and a lower retention ratio in GLC-TEM, indicating an enhanced beam-induced compaction under solution-phase conditions. In contrast, the retention ratio of approximately 200 nm PSNPs was not significantly different between the two imaging methods (p = 0.331), despite noticeable differences in their temporal size evolution profiles observed in Figure . This indicates that end point measurements alone may mask intermediate beam-induced structural evolution occurring during TEM observation.

This apparent discrepancy arises because the retention ratio reflects only the final particle size after irradiation, whereas the temporal evolution profiles include the entire size-change process. Because the initial shrinkage slope and retention ratio represent end point-derived metrics, statistical comparisons in Figure were performed using Welch’s t-test. In contrast, the complete time-course data sets shown in Figure were evaluated using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA to assess the effects of irradiation time, imaging technique, and their interaction. Accordingly, analysis of the complete time-course data set using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant time × technique interaction effects, demonstrating that the particle-size evolution behavior differed significantly between GLC-TEM and conventional TEM grid measurements.

These results suggest that the observed size reduction is governed by multiple processes rather than by a single drying-related effect. The discrepancy in the initial particle size between conventional dry-grid TEM and GLC is likely due to solvent loss during drying, which causes partial collapse or densification of the PSNPs before imaging and therefore results in a smaller apparent particle diameter on conventional TEM grids. In contrast, the temporal decrease in particle size observed during TEM imaging is primarily attributed to electron-beam-induced effects. Continuous electron-beam exposure can densify or compact PSNPs through local heating, radiolysis-mediated reactions, and beam-induced rearrangement of polymer chains. , In addition, chain cleavage and relaxation can soften the particle structure and allow partial decay, whereas competing cross-linking reactions can progressively stabilize the structure, leading to the reduced rate of size change observed after prolonged irradiation, which is also commonly reported for other polymers. , While both dry-grid TEM and GLC exhibited beam-induced shrinkage over time, the effect was particularly pronounced for approximately 100 nm PSNPs in GLC. This behavior may reflect a size-dependent balance between beam sensitivity and sufficient internal free volume or solvent content, making these particles more susceptible to beam-induced compaction than smaller 50 nm particles or larger 200–1000 nm particles. Interestingly, the 50 nm PSNPs showed a slight increase in particle size during TEM observation. Previous studies have reported electron-beam-induced expansion of polymer latex particles, suggesting that irradiation can induce transient swelling depending on particle composition and internal solvent distribution. Therefore, the initial size increase observed for the 50 nm PSNPs may reflect temporary structural relaxation or redistribution of residual solvent within the polymer matrix before beam-induced densification becomes dominant. Overall, most PSNPs became smaller during TEM observation and then reached a plateau, indicating that the initial beam-induced structural relaxation and densification were followed by a relatively stabilized polymer morphology. Therefore, GLC analysis provides a more reliable representation of PSNP morphology in solution than conventional dry-grid TEM, while also revealing time-dependent beam effects that should be considered when interpreting polymer nanoparticle dimensions.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically optimized and standardized liter-scale synthesis conditions for PSNPs with precisely controlled sizes by using established emulsion and dispersion polymerization methods. By adjustment of the amount of SDS in emulsion polymerization, PSNPs of 50, 100, and 200 nm in size were successfully synthesized. In dispersion polymerization, monodisperse PSNPs of 1000 nm in size were obtained by controlling the reaction temperature and concentrations of stabilizers and initiators. These conditions reflected the nucleation and growth mechanisms of the particles, resulting in a consistent target size and narrow distribution. Furthermore, analyses using particle size analyzers such as DLS, SMPS, and SLS provided a robust characterization platform to verify the solution-state-constrained size and size distribution of the synthesized PSNPs. The synthesized PSNPs exhibited similar size distribution and dispersity to the commercial PSNP standards, confirming that the developed protocol produces well-defined and reproducible particles suitable for use as standard materials.

To address the particle morphology and size deformation that typically occurs during TEM analysis, which involves drying a solution on a grid, we fabricated GLCs and used them to observe the solution state of PSNPs. TEM analysis using GLC confirmed the solution-state-constrained morphology and size of PSNPs in solution. Compared to conventional TEM analysis, particle deformation or shrinkage, which can occur during measurement for TEM, was minimal. This demonstrates that GLC-based TEM analysis is an effective complementary analytical tool for directly observing PSNPs in solution and monitoring their time-dependent structural evolution.

Consequently, this study establishes practical guidelines for the scalable synthesis and characterization of monodisperse PSNPs while also proposing an analytical method for directly observing solution-phase nanoparticle behavior using GLC-based TEM analysis. The methodology presented here may facilitate the scalable production of well-defined polymer nanoparticle standards and improve the interpretation of solution-phase nanoparticle characterization by electron microscopy.
