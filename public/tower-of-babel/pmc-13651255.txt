Genetic diversity and morphology of South Korean Aedes koreicus (Diptera: Culicidae) provide insights into the origin of European populations
Authors: Junyoung Lee, Jungyoon Lee, Changseob Lim, Ji Hyoun Kang, Sangwoo Seok, Hee Il Lee, Yeon Jae Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAedes koreicus (Edwards, 1917) is native to Korea, northern China, and the Russian Far East, and has gained attention following its first detection in Europe in 2008 and subsequent spread across Europe. The Korean Peninsula, which includes the type locality of Ae. koreicus, has been regarded as a potential source region for invasive populations of this species, along with other parts of its native range. However, little is known about the genetic and morphological attributes of Korean populations.MethodsWe obtained DNA from 172 specimens of Ae. koreicus from 19 populations in South Korea, including Jeju Island. Genetic diversity and population structure were assessed using the mitochondrial COI marker, with 147 European sequences and one northern Chinese sequence available from GenBank included in the comparison. We also examined 520 specimens from 40 South Korean localities to evaluate variation in morphological characters previously reported in European studies.ResultsA total of 23 COI haplotypes were identified among 172 specimens from 19 sites in South Korea. H2 was the most frequent and widely distributed haplotype (116/172, 67.4%), occurring at 17 sites. Among the 147 European sequences, comprised 35 haplotypes, only one of which, H2, was shared between South Korean samples and German sequences. Pairwise FST comparisons showed significant differentiation among Korean Peninsula, Jeju Island, and most European regions except Germany, while AMOVA further supported genetic structuring among these three groups. Morphological characters of South Korean populations, including those from Jeju Island, were similar to those reported from Germany and fell within the range of variation previously documented from Korean specimens. However, the characters differed from variants reported from Belgium, Italy, and other European populations.ConclusionsSouth Korean populations showed genetic and morphological differences from several European populations of Ae. koreicus. Although additional genetic data and broader sampling in both the native and invaded ranges are required for validation, our results suggest that European Ae. koreicus populations are unlikely to have originated solely from Korea, indicating the possibility of additional source regions within the native range outside Korea.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13071-026-07633-5.

Background

Within the family Culicidae Meigen, several species in the genus Aedes Meigen have become notorious invasive mosquitoes, with Ae. aegypti (Linnaeus), Ae. albopictus (Skuse), and Ae. japonicus (Theobald), representing prominent examples [1–3]. More recently, Ae. koreicus (Edwards), a species native to the East Palearctic including Korean Peninsula, northern China, and the Russian Far East [4], has emerged as another invasive species of concern, following its successful establishment and expansion across European countries. Since its first detection in Belgium in 2008 [5], the species has subsequently been recorded from 11 European countries and one Central Asian country: Italy in 2011 [6], European Russia in 2013 [7], Slovenia in 2013 [8], Switzerland in 2013 [9], Germany in 2015 [10], Hungary in 2016 [11], the Crimea region of Ukraine in 2016 [12], Kazakhstan in 2018 [13], Austria in 2018 [14], the Czech Republic in 2021 [15], the Netherlands in 2021 [16], and Slovakia in 2024 [17].

The rapid expansion of hematophagous Ae. koreicus across the pan-European regions has increased its public and veterinary health relevance, given that this species has been suggested as a potential vector of arboviruses and filarial nematodes. Several experimental studies have shown its competence for chikungunya, Sindbis, and Zika viruses [18–20], and Dirofilaria repens-positive individuals have been detected in Hungary [21]. In addition, blood-meal analyses from northern Italy also indicated mainly mammalophilic feeding, including human blood meals [22], although no contribution to arboviral outbreaks in Europe has yet been demonstrated. In its native range in Korea including Jeju Island, the southernmost part of Korea, Ae. koreicus is a common mosquito, with larvae frequently occurring in aquatic containers in wooded and vegetated habitats [23–25]. However, the biology and genetic data of Korean populations remain scarce. As a result, both their epidemiological relevance in the native range and their possible role as source populations for the invaded range remain insufficiently evaluated.

Population genetic studies have investigated the geographic origin and dispersal scenarios of European Ae. koreicus populations. Mitochondrial analyses suggested a major dispersal route along the Belgium–Italy axis and revealed substantial haplotype diversity and genetic structure among European populations [26]. Microsatellite-based analyses further supported a connected invasion history between northern Italy and Slovenia, likely derived from a common invasive source initially detected in the Belluno district in Italy [27]. Yet the same study found that mainland South Korean individuals were genetically unrelated to the Italian and Slovenian populations, leaving the relationship between European invasive populations and Korean native-range populations unresolved.

One possible explanation for the uncertain link between the invasive and native populations lies in the putative morphological types previously proposed within Korean Ae. koreicus [5, 28]. Belgium, Italy, and Slovenian populations have been reported to show a banded hind tarsomere V, a feature regarded as characteristics of Jeju Island specimens based on morphological examination and have therefore been interpreted as possibly originating from Jeju Island rather than the Korean Peninsula [5, 26, 27, 29].

Morphological differences between Jeju Island and Korean Peninsula populations of Ae. koreicus were first noted by Tanaka et al. [28] with respect to the number of pale erect forked scales on the vertex and the composition of broad and crescent-shaped scales on the antepronotal lobe. This distinction was expanded during the description of the Belgian invasive population, known as the first introduced population in Europe, leading to the proposal of two morphotypes, a “Korean Peninsula morphotype” and a “Jeju Island morphotype” [5]. In their classification, the Belgian specimens were assigned to the “Jeju Island morphotype” based mainly on the presence of a clear basal pale band on hind tarsomere V, together with several other characters. The invasive German population later reported by Pfitzner et al. [30] lacked this basal pale band and morphologically differed from Belgian population including pedicel, vertex, and the antepronotal lobe, suggesting a different possible invasion origin.

Together with genetic differentiation among European populations, these morphological differences provide valuable information on possible invasion and dispersal scenarios between the native and invaded ranges. However, genetic diversity and morphological variation have not been comprehensively examined across the native range, particularly in the Korean Peninsula and Jeju Island, which include the type locality of Ae. koreicus and have repeatedly been invoked as possible source regions for European invasive populations. Accordingly, this study aims to explore the genetic diversity, population structure, and morphological characters of native Ae. koreicus populations in South Korea, including Jeju Island.

Methods

Mosquito samples

Mosquitoes were collected from 2023 to 2024 at 27 localities in South Korea covering mainland Korea and Jeju Island (Fig. 1). Adults were collected using BG-sentinel traps, human landing catches, and sweeping, and were preserved as dried specimens with silica gel. Larvae of Ae. koreicus were collected from various containers, including rock pools, tires, and black plastic bottles. To avoid cohort-biased sampling, larval sampling within each site covered multiple containers. Collected larvae were transferred to the laboratory and reared by site in 14 cm × 21 cm × 5 cm plastic trays filled with tap water. Trays were checked daily, and when pupae appeared, they were transferred using a plastic dropping pipette to a separate plastic tray placed inside an adult cage measuring 32 cm × 32 cm × 32 cm. One day after adult emergence, when the wings had dried, adults were collected using aspirators and preserved with silica gel at − 20 °C for DNA extraction or at room temperature for morphological examination. In this study, Ae. koreicus specimens from the following two collections were used: KDCA, Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (Cheongju, South Korea) and KUEM (Seoul, South Korea). Among the deposited specimens, only adult Ae. koreicus specimen that were in good condition for identification and morphological comparison were included in the genetic analyses and morphological examination. Detailed metadata included in this study are provided in Additional file 1.

DNA extraction and COI gene amplification

DNA extraction of adult mosquitoes was performed using one foreleg of each adult mosquito specimen. The DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for extraction, following the instructions of manufacturer. A 658-bp fragment of cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) was then amplified using the modified universal primers, LCO1490mod (5′-TCTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3′) and HCO2198mod (5′-TAAACTTCTGGGTGTCCGAAGAATCA-3′), which were specifically developed for Aedes koreicus by Hohmeister et al. [31]. Amplification was conducted using a T100 Thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following thermal conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, extension process at 72 °C for 30 s; and a final extension step of 72 °C for 5 min. Successfully amplified PCR products were purified and sequenced by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). All obtained sequences were aligned and manually edited in BioEdit v. 7.2 [32] and uploaded to GenBank (accession numbers: PZ359250–PZ359398 and PZ384356–PZ384378).

Genetic data analyses

To assess COI genetic structure across South Korea and the invaded European range, we included 147 European sequences and one Chinese sequence from Beijing (accession number: OP482488) after filtering GenBank sequences. All included sequences have at least country-level locality information and sufficient length (>> 600 bp) for alignment. Sequences lacking locality data (e.g., OP704246) or containing missing or ambiguous sites (e.g., OL379398) were excluded during the filtering process. Together with our newly generated South Korean sequences, the final dataset comprised 320 COI sequences (603 bp) from South Korea, China, and nine invaded European countries (Austria, Belgium, Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Italy, the Netherlands, Slovakia, and Slovenia). For each site, the number of haplotypes (Nh), haplotype diversity (h), nucleotide diversity (π), Tajima’s D, and Fu’s Fs were calculated in Arlequin v.3.5 [33]. COI haplotype network was constructed using TCS under the 95% statistical parsimony connection limit [34]. Network visualization was performed using tcsBU [35]. Pairwise p-distances among haplotypes were calculated using MEGA v.10.2.6 [36]. Population structure was assessed by calculating pairwise FSTvalues using Arlequin v.3.5 [33]. The resulting FST values were then used to generate a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plot in GenAlEx v6.5 [37]. Regional population structure was further examined by analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) in Arlequin v.3.5[34], significance tested using 1,000 permutations under the locus-by-locus option. In AMOVA, three grouping schemes were used. First, South Korean populations were divided into two groups: non-Jeju localities, including the Korean Peninsula (KP) and Ulleung Island (UL), and Jeju Island (JJ). Second, all populations were divided into two groups: South Korea (KR) and Europe (EU). Third, all populations were assigned to three groups: non-Jeju localities in South Korea (Korea Peninsula and Ulleung Island), Jeju Island (JJ), and Europe (EU). Except for haplotype network analysis, all population-level calculations and analyses were restricted to populations represented by five or more individuals. Accordingly, the final dataset for population-level analyses included 14 South Korean populations comprising 155 individuals and 135 European sequences from Belgium, Germany, Hungary, and Italy.

Morphological examination

We examined a total of 520 Ae. koreicus specimens from 40 localities in South Korea, including Jeju Island and Ulleung Island, deposited in KDCA and KUEM. Morphological examination was conducted using SZX7 stereomicroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Image of the characters were taken using a Canon 6D Mark II camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan) mounted with a Nikon CFI Plan 10× objective lens (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and image stacking was performed using Zerene Stacker (Zerene System, Richland, WA, USA). The examined morphological characters followed those mentioned in previous studies [5, 28, 30]: the color of pedicel scales and erect forked scales on the vertex, the shape of scales on antepronotal lobe, basolateral pale patches on abdominal tergites, and the basal pale bands on hind tarsomere V.

Results

Genetic diversity of South Korean Aedes koreicus

A total of 23 COI haplotypes were detected among 172 specimens from 19 localities in South Korea. The number of haplotypes per population ranged from one to seven, and haplotype diversity ranged from 0.000 to 0.833. The highest haplotype diversity was observed at ST17 on Jeju Island (0.833), followed by ST14 at Tongdo Temple, Yangsan, in the southeastern part of the Korean Peninsula (0.801). The overall genetic diversity of the South Korean populations was characterized by moderate haplotype diversity (0.539) and low nucleotide diversity (0.002), both of which were lower than the haplotype diversity (0.903) and nucleotide diversity (0.007) observed in European invasive populations. Haplotype network of Ae. koreicus

A TCS network constructed from 320 Ae. koreicus COI sequences included 59 haplotypes (H1–H59; Fig. 2). H2 was the most common haplotype in South Korea, occurring at 17 sites (116/172, 67.4%). This haplotype was also shared with 14 sequences from Hessen, Germany [25], and was the only one shared between South Korean and European populations. Some European haplotypes showed relatively high divergence from the main haplotype network. The long branches leading to several Europe-specific haplotypes suggest the presence of distinct maternal lineages in Europe. In particular, H25, represented by sequences from Austria (MN103399) and Belgium (KM258298), and H30 from the Czech Republic (OM307662) were highly divergent and were not connected to the remaining haplotypes in the TCS network, with pairwise p-distances ranging from 4.31 to 6.47% (Additional file 3). In contrast, excluding Jeju Island populations, South Korean haplotypes showed a star-like pattern with short internal branches, consistent with shallow mitochondrial differentiation and a narrow range of pairwise p-distance (0.166–0.663%; Additional file 3).

Jeju Island haplotypes were positioned apart from the main South Korean cluster in the network. Among the Jeju Island haplotypes, H18 (11/32) and H20 (7/32) were the most common haplotypes and occurred exclusively in Jeju Island populations. However, neither Jeju Island nor non-Jeju South Korean haplotypes matched European haplotypes, except for the 14 German sequences assigned to H2.

Because morphological information was unavailable for most GenBank sequences, only six European COI sequences could be directly linked to hind tarsomere morphology, the most representative diagnostic character of the “Jeju Island morphotype”, as defined by Versteirt et al. [5]. These sequences were from Belgium (JF430393), Italy (MW365453), and Slovakia (PX472713, PX472714, PX472716, PX472718), all corresponding to specimens with a complete basal pale band on hind tarsomere V. The Belgian sequence (JF430393) from the earliest recorded population in Maasmechelen was assigned to H26, while the Italian sequence (MW365453) from Lombardy was assigned to H51; both differed from all South Korean haplotypes, showing pairwise p-distance of 0.663–1.493% and 0.995–1.824%, respectively (Additional file 3). The sequences of Slovakian specimens showing the same complete pale band were assigned to H29 (PX472716), H43 (PX472714), and H57 (PX472713, PX472718) and none of them matched South Korean haplotypes, although they were positioned close to the H2-centered cluster in the network.

Population structure

The populations of Ae. koreicus in South Korea generally exhibited low genetic differentiation (FST = − 0.066 to 0.177), except for some Jeju Island populations (Table 2). ST16 and ST17 on Jeju Island showed significant differentiation from all other sites, with FST values ranging from 0.508 to 0.784, but were not significantly differentiated from each other (FST = − 0.0416). Morphological variation

Among 520 Ae. koreicus specimens examined from 40 localities in South Korea, including 110 individuals from seven localities on Jeju Island, the ‘Jeju Island morphotype’ defined by Versteirt et al. [5] was not detected (Table 4). A complete basal pale band on hind tarsomere V was not observed in any South Korean specimen. Most Jeju Island specimens exhibited only dark scales, and very few had scattered pale scales or incomplete pale bands, corresponding to the variation described for Korean specimens by Tanaka et al. [28]. Scattered pale scales or incomplete pale band on hind tarsomere V occurred in both Jeju Island and non-Jeju localities, with no notable difference in its frequency between Jeju Island (5/110, 4.55%) and other localities (32/410, 7.80%) in South Korea. We also re-examined the KUEM specimen from Korean Peninsula (Seoku-dong, Hwaseong-si, Gyeonggi-do), which was reported by Seok et al. [24], as the “Jeju Island morphotype” having a complete pale band on hind tarsomere V, and found that it had been misidentified as Ae. koreicus and was actually Ae. vexans (Additional file 4). Discussion

Our survey across 27 localities in South Korea, including Jeju and Ulleung Islands, confirmed the broad distribution of Aedes koreicus in its native range in Korea. The frequent occurrence of Ae. koreicus during our larval collections from various containers is consistent with previous studies that identified the species as a dominant species in forest aquatic containers in South Korea [23, 39]. The use of a wide range of artificial containers, such as cement drainage channels, rubber buckets, small plastic cups, and tires, may facilitate the successful establishment of Ae. koreicus in urban and peri-urban environments across temperate European regions, as observed for its sibling species Ae. japonicus [3, 40]. Further studies on the natural history of this species in its native range, including habitat preference, seasonal phenology, and biotic interactions within mosquito communities, would be valuable. With the ongoing expansion of Ae. koreicus in Europe and its predicted establishment potential in other temperate regions, including North America [24, 41], such native-range bionomic knowledge will provide increasingly useful context for modeling future expansion and guiding surveillance and control in invaded regions.

Assessing the genetic diversity of invasive species in both native and invaded ranges can provide insights into invasion pathways and potential source regions. In our mt-COI dataset covering 19 localities in South Korea, haplotypes from Jeju Island were structured apart from those from non-Jeju localities, including the Korean Peninsula and Ulleung Island, and did not match any sequences from European countries. This pattern does not support previous morphology-based interpretations that linked the early Belgian [5, 40] and later expanded Italian populations [6, 29, 38] to a Jeju Island source. Rather, their divergence from both Jeju- and non-Jeju haplotypes from South Korea, together with their position in the haplotype network, suggests possible origins from other parts of the native range outside Korea. Consistently, FST values and AMOVA results together revealed genetic differentiation between South Korean populations and European populations, including Belgian and Italian populations, further supporting this interpretation. A recent microsatellite-based population genetic study also provided molecular evidence that mainland South Korean individuals are unlikely to be the source of Italian and Slovenian populations [27].

In contrast, the German invasive population showed a closer genetic affinity to South Korean populations from non-Jeju localities, as it contained the shared haplotype H2 and other haplotypes closely connected to H2 in the network. This was also reflected in the pairwise FSTcomparisons, in which the German population was not significantly differentiated from most mainland South Korean populations, unlike the other European populations from Belgium, Hungary, and Italy. Morphological evidence also supports this affinity, as our South Korean specimens mostly corresponded to the characters reported for German populations by Pfitzner et al. [30], particularly the absence of a complete basal pale band on hind tarsomere V. By contrast, the banded form has been reported from other European populations in Belgium, Italy, Slovakia, Slovenia, and Switzerland [5, 6, 9, 27, 29, 38]. Although Jeju Island specimens were also morphologically similar to the German population, COI haplotypes separated Jeju Island populations from both German and non-Jeju South Korean populations, indicating a possible non-Jeju South Korean source for the German population rather than a Jeju Island origin. Previous European studies [26, 42] and our dataset revealed high divergence among some European Ae. koreicus haplotypes. The inter- and intra-country mitochondrial divergence and the distinct genetic and morphological differences between the German populations and Belgian and Italian populations collectively suggest that European Ae. koreicus may have been established through multiple introductions from different source regions, possibly including South Korea. Furthermore, ecological niche analyses comparing native and European populations of Ae. koreicus have suggested potential invasive source populations outside South Korea [43].

Although Jeju Island populations were genetically distinct from other populations in South Korea, our examination of a considerable number of specimens did not detect a geographically distinct morphological form corresponding to Jeju Island, contrary to previous distinction between two morphotypes from Jeju Island and Korean Peninsula [5, 28]. The earlier recognition of a geographically distinct form may have been influenced by sampling bias, as Tanaka et al. [28] examined only nine Jeju Island specimens, while Versteirt et al. [5] examined ten specimens without including Korean Peninsula populations for comparison.

Specifically, the concept of the “Jeju Island morphotype” proposed by Versteirt et al. [5] appears to have conflated distinct character states, particularly regarding the basal pale band on hind tarsomere V, which was treated as an aberrant character distinguishing the “Jeju Island morphotype” from the “Korean Peninsula morphotype”. Tanaka et al. [28] recognized the variation in hind tarsomere V, sometimes including pale dorsobasal scales or incomplete pale banding (Fig. 4d), but did not establish this character as Jeju-associated variation. Versteirt et al. [5] also acknowledged this variation and distinguished the small, interrupted band in Jeju Island specimens from the clear and complete basal band observed in Belgian specimens, but nevertheless treated these two distinct character states as part of the same “Jeju Island morphotype”. Moreover, our examination of a large number of specimens did not support the interpretation by Versteirt et al. [5] that incomplete banding represents only a Jeju-specific variant (Table 4). In our examined material, this variation was detected at low frequencies in both Jeju Island and the Korean Peninsula, and no complete basal band was detected in any South Korean specimen. This agrees with earlier diagnoses of Korean specimens by Miyagi [44] and Tanaka et al. [28], which described partial or irregular pale scaling in a small proportion of Korean specimens rather than the clear, complete band reported as a characteristic feature in several European invasive populations, including Belgian, Italian, and Slovenian invasive populations [27, 29, 38, 40].Fig. 4Morphological characters of South Korean Aedes koreicus. a Head, dorsal view. b Thorax, lateral view. c Hind tarsomere V without a basal pale band. d Hind tarsomere V with an incomplete basal pale band. e Antepronotal lobe composed only of broad pale scales. f Pedicel covered with pale scales g Numerous dark erect forked scales on vertex (dorsal view). h Numerous dark erect forked scales on vertex (lateral view). A similar ambiguity applies to the pronotal scale character. Tanaka et al. [28] noted a relative increase of crescent-shaped scales on the antepronotal lobe in Jeju Island specimens while broad scales still predominated. However, Versteirt et al. [5] only noted the postpronotum character and simplified it as “numerous falcate scales” without specifying their abundance relative to broad scales, and this pronotal character was later reduced to the presence of falcate scales on the antepronotum in a subsequent summary [30]. Meanwhile, in our material, all South Korean specimens from both Jeju Island and non-Jeju localities had only broad pale scales on the antepronotal lobe (Table 4), suggesting closer similarity to the German specimens reported by Pfitzner et al. [30].

Consequently, our morphological examination did not support a distinct Jeju Island-associated morphotype within South Korea. Instead, the European specimens previously identified as the “Jeju Island morphotype” differed morphologically from both Jeju Island and non-Jeju South Korean specimens. The genetic separation of Jeju Island haplotypes from both non-Jeju South Korean and European haplotypes, together with the absence of shared haplotypes with Europe, further argues against Jeju Island as a source of any European invasive population in the current dataset. Therefore, discrete characters of the Belgian population described by Versteirt et al. [5] should not be interpreted as a “Jeju Island morphotype” but rather as a distinct European invasive variant, or provisionally as a Belgian-associated form. However, morphological characters remain unexamined for some European invasive populations, including those from Austria, the Czech Republic, European Russia, and Hungary [7, 11, 14, 15]. In addition, most available genetic data included in our dataset lack corresponding adult morphological information, even though all Belgian and Italian populations are generally known for their banded morphology of hind tarsomere V [5, 27, 41]. This highlights the need for broader morphology-linked genetic data across the native and invaded ranges to evaluate whether the observed morphological variation represents discrete forms or clinal variation. Comparable data from other parts of the native range, particularly from northern China and the Russian Far East, remain extremely limited: in our data set, only a single sequence from China was available, and it did not match any South Korean or European haplotype, further emphasizing the importance of genetic and morphological data from a broader geographic range within the native distribution.

Based on the morphological and genetic evidence, we hypothesize that European Ae. koreicus populations were established through multiple introductions from different source regions. The German population may have a closer link to mainland South Korea, whereas other European populations, including those from Belgium and Italy, are more likely to involve other native-range sources. However, this interpretation should remain tentative because of the limited genetic and morphological data currently available. Broader sampling from the native range, including northern China and the Russian Far East, together with high-resolution population genomic data, is needed to test this hypothesis.

Conclusions

As climate change and global trade continue to promote the spread of container-breeding mosquitoes, comparisons between native and invaded populations are essential for understanding the ecological and evolutionary processes underlying invasion success. By integrating genetic and morphological data from South Korean native-range populations with data from European populations, this study shows that European Ae. koreicus populations are unlikely to reflect a single South Korean source and may instead involve multiple introductions from different parts of the native range. Population genomic data covering broader native and invaded ranges will be needed to clarify source regions and invasion histories.
