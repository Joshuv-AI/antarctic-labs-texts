Building capacity for patient advocacy in liver health: a cross-country qualitative study
Authors: Serena Barello, Michela Monaci, Beatrice Credi, Marko Korenjak, Sarah Bigi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore how patient advocates active in liver health understand and enact meaningful advocacy across diverse cultural and health-system contexts and to identify empirically grounded competencies that can inform evidence-based training and capacity building for patient advocacy.DesignCross-country qualitative study using a focused ethnographic approach, based on in-depth semi-structured interviews and reflexive thematic analysis.SettingPatient advocacy organisations active in liver health at local, national and international levels across Europe and selected non-European contexts.Participants23 experienced patient advocates affiliated with liver disease patient organisations in 17 countries.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted via videoconferencing. Data were analysed following a reflexive thematic analysis approach, with maximum-variation purposive sampling to capture diversity in advocacy roles, experience and health-system contexts.ResultsFive interrelated themes were identified, conceptualising patient advocacy as a relational and value-driven practice enacted across multiple levels of action. Findings highlighted a set of communicative, ethical, strategic, emotional and contextual competencies that advocates progressively develop to translate lived experience into system-relevant action. Advocacy was described as shaped by structural constraints, cultural norms and emotional burden, requiring adaptive strategies and sustained reflexive learning.ConclusionsPatient advocacy in liver health relies on a constellation of competencies that extend beyond lived experience alone and can be explicitly supported through structured training. By articulating these competencies, this study provides an empirical foundation for developing evidence-based educational tools for patient advocates and for strengthening mechanisms that integrate experiential knowledge into health policy and governance, including “Listening-Informed” Policy approaches.

Introduction

Patient advocacy has become an increasingly visible and influential component of contemporary health systems, particularly in contexts characterised by chronic conditions, complex care pathways and persistent health inequities. Across clinical, policy and public health domains, advocates are recognised as key actors in representing patient perspectives, influencing decision-making and promoting more equitable and responsive systems of care. Advocacy has been conceptualised not merely as representation but as a relational, ethical and political practice that links lived experience with institutional processes and governance structures.13

In parallel, health systems across Europe and beyond are undergoing transformations that place growing emphasis on patient engagement, co-production and participatory governance. While these developments have expanded opportunities for patient involvement, they have also exposed tensions related to legitimacy, representativeness, power asymmetries and the professionalisation of advocacy roles.46 Recent scholarship has highlighted the risk of tokenistic participation when patient voices are included without adequate support, training or recognition of the competencies required to engage meaningfully with complex institutional environments.7 8

Within this broader landscape, liver health represents a particularly salient and underexplored context for examining patient advocacy. Chronic liver diseases—including viral hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD/MASH), cirrhosis and liver cancer—are marked by fragmented care trajectories, prognostic uncertainty and substantial informational and emotional burdens for patients and families.911 Liver conditions are also heavily stigmatised in many settings, further complicating patient–provider communication, public awareness and policy prioritisation.12 13

Patient organisations and advocates have played an increasingly central role in addressing these challenges. In recent years, advocacy efforts have contributed to viral hepatitis elimination strategies, the reframing of MASLD/MASH nomenclature and communication and the co-production of early detection and population-level screening initiatives.1416 These developments illustrate how advocacy intersects with clinical practice, public health and policy, positioning advocates as key intermediaries between scientific communities, decision-makers and people living with liver disease.

Despite this growing relevance, empirical research examining how patient advocates in liver health understand and enact their role remains limited. Existing studies have often focused on advocacy outcomes or organisational activities, with less attention to advocates’ lived experiences, developmental trajectories and the competencies required to navigate diverse cultural and health-system contexts. More broadly, while conceptual frameworks for patient advocacy have been proposed, there is a lack of qualitative, cross-country evidence grounding these models in advocates’ own accounts.17 18

Addressing this gap, the present study aims to explore how patient advocates active in liver health conceptualise and practise meaningful advocacy across different national and organisational settings. By examining advocates’ experiences, values, strategies and perceived challenges, the study seeks to identify empirically grounded competency domains that can inform evidence-based training and capacity-building initiatives. In doing so, it contributes to ongoing debates on the professionalisation of patient advocacy and the integration of experiential knowledge into health policy and governance.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a focused ethnographic design. In line with contemporary understandings of focused ethnography in health research, we used this approach to investigate a clearly bounded and specialised community of patient advocates through a specific applied research question and a time-limited but intensive qualitative design. Focused ethnography has been described as particularly suited to health research when the aim is to generate context-sensitive insights into the practices, meanings and experiences of a defined social group within pragmatic study timeframes.19 20 In our study, this orientation was operationalised through in-depth semi-structured interviews with experienced patient advocates from multiple countries, with attention to the cultural, organisational and health-system contexts shaping advocacy practice. The study is part of the ICONIC project on meaningful advocacy in liver health promoted by the European Liver Patients’ Association (ELPA). Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).20

Study participant recruitment

Adults aged 18 years or older were eligible to participate if they were actively engaged in patient advocacy within an ELPA affiliated organisation and were either persons living with a chronic liver condition, informal caregivers of a person with liver disease or advocates involved in liver health advocacy without direct lived experience as patients or caregivers. Participants were required to be involved in advocacy activities at the local, national or international level and to be able to communicate in English or in their native language, with translation support provided when necessary. However, the specific scope of advocacy engagement was used as an eligibility criterion rather than as a participant characteristic systematically recorded for comparative analysis.

Participants were recruited through ELPA, which contacted affiliated associations across Europe and invited patient advocates to participate voluntarily after review of the study information sheet and provision of written informed consent. This recruitment strategy enabled inclusion of advocates from 17 countries across six macro-regions represented within the ELPA network.

Sampling followed a maximum-variation purposive strategy to capture heterogeneity in gender, age, years of advocacy experience, educational background and clinical focus areas.21

Recruitment continued until sufficient information power and thematic saturation were achieved, defined as the point at which no substantially new themes emerged across interviews.

Data collection

Data were collected between September and October 2025 through semi-structured interviews conducted remotely via secure videoconferencing platforms. Interviews lasted 30–45 min. Consistent with a focused ethnographic orientation, interviews were designed to elicit situated accounts of advocacy practice, role enactment and meaning-making within participants’ organisational, cultural and health-system contexts. The interview guide was developed collaboratively with ELPA and explored: (1) experiences of patient advocacy; (2) definitions of “meaningful advocacy”; (3) competencies perceived as essential to the advocate role; (4) perceived barriers, facilitators and cultural/contextual factors; and (5) suggestions for future advocate training.

Interviews were conducted by two researchers who are psychologists with training and experience in qualitative health research. No prior relationship existed between interviewers and participants. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and anonymised. Interviews were conducted in English or in participants’ native languages with translation support when necessary. Quotations reported in languages other than English were translated into English for publication. No participants withdrew.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach.22 23 The two researchers who conducted the interviews (MM and SB) independently coded an initial subset of transcripts to develop a shared coding framework, which was iteratively refined through team discussion. Coding was conducted in the original language of each transcript to preserve nuance. Particular attention was paid to how meanings and advocacy practices were shaped by contextual features, including national, organisational and health-system differences. Analysis was supported by MAXQDA software.

Cross-case comparisons were conducted across participant role (patient, caregiver, advocate), geographic region and years of advocacy experience, in order to explore both shared patterns and contextual variation in how advocacy was understood and enacted. Member checking was conducted with four participants, who reviewed thematic summaries and provided feedback that was incorporated into the final analysis. Analytic rigour was supported through researcher triangulation, reflexive memo writing and maintenance of an audit trail.

The research team comprised psychologists and communication researchers with expertise in patient engagement and qualitative methods. Two co-authors (BC; MK) were affiliated with the ELPA, whereas the remaining authors were affiliated with academic institutions. Reflexive discussions were held throughout the analytic process to examine how researchers’ disciplinary backgrounds, institutional positions and prior engagement with patient organisations might influence interpretation.

Patient and public involvement

Patient advocates and representatives of patient organisations contributed to the development of the study design and interview guide. Two researchers, MK and BC, representatives of the ELPA, contributed to refining study objectives, interview topics and recruitment procedures. Four participants contributed to member checking by reviewing preliminary thematic summaries.

Theme 1. Advocacy as lived commitment and identity

Participants described advocacy as a long-term personal commitment closely connected to their lived experience with liver disease, either as patients or caregivers. Advocacy was often portrayed as extending beyond discrete activities to become integrated into personal identity, shaping priorities, relationships and everyday life. Engagement typically developed over time and was described as requiring continuity rather than episodic involvement.

Advocacy was frequently framed as a responsibility toward others living with liver disease, particularly individuals perceived as less visible or less able to represent themselves.

At some point it stops being something you do occasionally. It becomes part of who you are. (Participant 7)

You feel responsible not only for yourself, but for everyone who cannot speak or is not heard. (Participant 15)

Theme 2. Translating lived experience into collective voice

Participants described advocacy as a process of transforming individual experiences into shared messages that could be communicated to institutions, professionals and policymakers. This translation was described as deliberate and reflective, requiring advocates to move beyond personal narratives toward collective concerns.

Listening to other patients and caregivers was described as essential to ensuring that advocacy positions reflected broader experiences rather than individual perspectives, particularly when engaging in formal decision-making spaces.

You can’t just speak for yourself. You have to listen first, then bring something that represents many voices. (Participant 12)

My story matters, but it only becomes useful when it connects with the experiences of others. (Participant 4)

Theme 3. Navigating skills, strain and growth

Participants described advocacy as involving a range of skills developed primarily through experience, including understanding health systems, communicating with professionals, participating in meetings and engaging with policy processes. Skill development was described as gradual and shaped by opportunities for participation and exposure to institutional environments.

At the same time, participants reported emotional and practical strain associated with advocacy work, particularly in relation to workload, emotional burden and balancing advocacy with personal health or caregiving responsibilities. Learning and growth were often described as emerging alongside difficulty.

You learn by doing, but sometimes you learn the hard way. (Participant 19)

There are moments when it becomes overwhelming, especially when you are also managing your own illness. (Participant 8)

Theme 4. Working within and against structural constraints

Participants described advocacy as taking place within complex organisational, cultural and health-system contexts that shaped how advocacy could be enacted. Participants reported navigating differences in national health systems, institutional cultures and levels of openness to patient involvement.

Some participants described supportive relationships with clinicians and policymakers, while others reported barriers such as limited recognition, unclear roles or tokenistic inclusion. Advocacy strategies were frequently adapted in response to these conditions, with adjustments made to language, positioning and expectations depending on context.

Sometimes you are welcomed as a partner, sometimes you are invited just to tick a box. (Participant 6)

You learn quickly that what works in one country or setting doesn’t work in another. (Participant 3)

Theme 5. Learning advocacy through experience and exchange

Participants consistently described advocacy learning as primarily experiential, occurring through practice, peer exchange and informal mentorship rather than through structured educational pathways. Networks of advocates were described as key spaces for sharing knowledge, reflecting on experiences and providing mutual support.

While some participants reported access to formal training opportunities, these were described as unevenly available and variable in relevance. Several participants expressed a need for training that better integrated experiential knowledge with practical guidance for navigating institutions and sustaining advocacy work over time.

Most of what I know, I learned from other advocates. (Participant 21)

Training helps, but without experience and exchange with peers, it remains abstract. (Participant 10)

Across themes, patient advocacy in liver health was described as an evolving practice grounded in lived experience, shaped by relational and structural contexts and developed primarily through sustained engagement and peer exchange. While common patterns were evident across participants and countries, participants’ accounts also highlighted contextual variation in how advocacy is enacted. In particular, advocates with longer experience and broader exposure to institutional or policy settings tended to describe advocacy in more strategic and system-oriented terms, whereas participants with less experience or more limited formal training more often foregrounded the challenges of learning advocacy through practice and navigating uncertainty. Variations were also shaped by differences in national health-system structures and in the degree of openness to patient involvement.

Discussion

This study examined how patient advocates in hepatology understand and enact meaningful advocacy across diverse cultural and organisational contexts. Advocates consistently described advocacy as a relational and value-driven practice anchored in solidarity, justice and human rights—an understanding aligned with broader conceptualisations of advocacy as an ethical and equity-oriented endeavour.13 Their accounts echo prior analyses framing advocacy as a relational, communicative and political practice that connects lived experiences with institutional decision-making.4

Our findings reinforce the argument that advocacy is not merely a set of techniques but a form of civic professionalism, grounded in normative commitments and sustained emotional and relational labour.6 7 This perspective resonates with emerging frameworks for equity-oriented advocacy, which emphasise normative grounding, multilevel orientation, relational communication, critical and systemic literacy, reflexivity and accountability.8 The experiences of hepatology advocates in this study provide empirical grounding for these components, as participants described operating across micro-, meso- and macro-levels; adapting communication to patients, clinicians and policymakers; and navigating complex organisational and political ecosystems.24

This competency-based and developmental understanding of advocacy is also consistent with earlier patient engagement frameworks, which emphasise the need to equip individuals and health systems with practical tools and relational capabilities to enable meaningful participation across clinical and organisational settings, including through digital and hybrid modalities.25

A central contribution of this study is the attention to hepatology-specific dynamics shaping advocacy. Chronic liver diseases are characterised by fragmented care trajectories, prognostic uncertainty and substantial informational and emotional burdens. These features mirror communicative and relational vulnerabilities described in prior qualitative research in hepatology, including difficulties in understanding clinical documentation, navigating uncertainty and sustaining trust in care relationships.9 Within this context, advocates frequently acted as boundary actors, supporting patients in interpreting information, counteracting stigma and mediating between bureaucratic systems and lived experience. This aligns with earlier work emphasising the relational and emotional labour required to support patient participation in liver care.10 11 26

Beyond the clinical encounter, our findings align with growing evidence that advocacy plays an increasingly central role in liver-health initiatives at system and population levels. Patient organisations have contributed to viral hepatitis elimination strategies, the reframing of MASLD/MASH nomenclature and communication and the co-production of early detection and population-level screening initiatives.1214 The advocates’ accounts presented here provide qualitative insight into the everyday relational and organisational work that sustains such initiatives and enables their translation into practice.

The developmental nature of advocacy emerged strongly across interviews. Participants described advocacy as a craft learnt over time through experience, mentorship and iterative reflexive practice, consistent with conceptualisations of advocacy as a dynamic process.15 Although the core themes were broadly shared across the sample, cross-case comparison suggested that advocacy experience, access to training and exposure to institutional settings influenced how explicitly participants articulated the strategic, relational and adaptive dimensions of advocacy. This aligns with evidence identifying experiential and tacit knowledge as core dimensions of advocacy competence,8 and with profiles previously described in health advocacy roles, which include empathy, diplomacy, organisational management, policy literacy and strategic communication.16 17

At the same time, structural constraints were pervasive. Limited funding, bureaucratic rigidity, political instability and—in some contexts—legal restrictions on civil society participation reflect patterns described in global health governance literature, where fragile civic spaces undermine advocacy capacity.18 Emotional burden and risk of burnout were also recurrent, echoing research on the psychological load associated with patient-facing advocacy roles. These challenges appear particularly salient in hepatology, a field marked by persistent stigma, low public awareness and uneven national investment in liver health.

Participants’ reflections on training needs closely overlapped with existing evidence on patient engagement and communication in hepatology. Clear, accessible communication and emotional attunement—also central in recent qualitative work on clinical summaries—were described as foundational to effective advocacy.9 Advocates consistently called for structured training approaches that integrate experiential knowledge with disease literacy, ethical reflection, patient engagement skills and policy navigation, aligning with recent European efforts to professionalise patient advocacy.8 27

Taken together, this study contributes to a more integrated understanding of patient advocacy in hepatology as both a civic practice and a professional competency—anchored in human rights, enacted through relational and organisational labour, and shaped by structural constraints within health systems. By situating these findings within the rapidly evolving landscape of liver health—including MASLD/MASH recognition, hepatitis elimination initiatives, and population-level screening programmes—this study highlights the strategic and irreplaceable role of patient advocates as connectors across clinical, scientific and policy domains.

Implications for policy and practice

The identified competency domains provide a practical framework for developing structured, evidence-based training for patient advocates in liver health. At policy level, the findings support the formal inclusion of trained advocates in advisory bodies, guideline development and health-system redesign processes, particularly in contexts characterised by stigma, fragmented care pathways and limited patient representation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The focused ethnographic design enabled in-depth exploration of meanings, practices and relational dynamics among experienced patient advocates in hepatology. Inclusion of advocates from 17 countries and multiple macro-regions enhanced analytical richness and allowed examination of advocacy across diverse cultural, organisational and health-system contexts. Methodological rigour was supported through maximum-variation purposive sampling, researcher triangulation, reflexive thematic analysis and an explicit audit trail. Collaboration with patient organisations in developing the interview guide and the use of member checking further strengthened credibility and relevance.

Several limitations should be considered. Despite the cross-country scope, findings may not be fully transferable to other disease areas or advocacy contexts, where structural conditions, stigma dynamics and care pathways differ. Recruitment through a European umbrella organisation may have favoured advocates with stronger organisational capacity, potentially under-representing more informal or grassroots advocacy experiences. The high educational level and extensive experience of participants may further limit transferability to novice advocates or resource-constrained settings. In addition, although advocacy at local, national or international level formed part of the inclusion criteria, the specific scope of advocacy engagement was not systematically recorded at individual level, limiting more detailed comparison across participants.

Additional limitations relate to study design. Data were collected through cross-sectional, self-reported online interviews, limiting observation of advocacy practices in situ and examination of change over time. As with all qualitative self-report research, social desirability bias cannot be excluded, particularly when discussing sensitive organisational or political issues.

Conclusion

This study offers one of the first in-depth qualitative examinations of patient advocacy in hepatology. Advocates described advocacy as a lived, relational and ethically grounded practice enacted across clinical, organisational and policy domains. Their narratives reveal advocacy as skilled work requiring emotional intelligence, communicative expertise, organisational competence and political literacy, often carried out under structurally constrained conditions. These findings extend existing conceptual and empirical work on patient advocacy, situating hepatology advocacy within broader debates on equity, civic participation, patient engagement and the institutionalisation of patient roles in healthcare.4 15

By integrating these insights with emerging frameworks for equity-oriented advocacy and prior research on communication and patient experience in liver care, this study provides a robust empirical foundation for co-designing culturally sensitive, evidence-based training for patient advocates. Strengthening structural support, formal recognition and interprofessional collaboration will be essential to ensure that advocacy can fulfil its transformative potential within hepatology and beyond.
