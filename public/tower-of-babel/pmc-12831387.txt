Fentanyl, Amphetamine-type Stimulants, Heroin, and xylazine in drug residues in two Northern border Mexican cities
Authors: Clara Fleiz Bautista, María Elena Medina-Mora, Silvia L. Cruz, Miguel Angel López-Brambila, Valentín Miranda-Soto, Daniel Chávez, Jorge Mata, Alfonso Chávez, Luis Segovia, Lourdes Angulo, Said Slim, Lilia Pacheco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Illicitly manufactured fentanyl (IMF) and amphetamine-type stimulants (ATS) are driving the fourth wave of the opioid crisis in the United States and Canada. Accordingly, the aim of this paper was to determine the presence of heroin, fentanyl, ATS, xylazine, and other substances in Tijuana and Mexicali, Baja California, two cities on the US-Mexico border. We also tested the positive predictive value of fentanyl and ATS test strips. To do this, we randomly selected 300 drug residues from syringe plungers provided by two non-governmental organizations (NGOs) with harm reduction programs (one in each city) and from community samples obtained at shooting galleries and meeting places for people who inject drugs. We then analyzed them with GC–MS and BTNX test strips for amphetamine-type stimulants (ATS) and fentanyl and its analogs. Test strips showed a high positive predictive value of 81% when compared with GC–MS. Most residues had fentanyl, followed by methamphetamine, heroin, and xylazine. Fentanyl and methamphetamine co-occurrence were the norm rather than the exception in drug residues. Xylazine was more prevalent in fentanyl-containing samples in Mexicali, whereas the combination of fentanyl and methamphetamine was more common in Tijuana. Benzodiazepines were not detected in any of our samples. In conclusion, in two key Mexican northern border cities, most heroin samples contained fentanyl. Opioid and ATS use was common in Tijuana and Mexicali, while xylazine emerged as a significant adulterant in Mexicali and was subsequently detected in Tijuana. The evolving drug market demands continuous drug testing while public health policies are required to address the challenges raised by this scenario.

Introduction

Illicitly manufactured fentanyl (IMF) use is a major public health problem for many countries, including Mexico [1]. Its increasing production, distribution, and consumption have led to an unprecedented rise in overdose deaths, particularly in the United States and Canada [2–4], Other serious health risks include blood-borne infections and skin lesions in people who inject drugs (PWID), especially opioids adulterated with xylazine [5].

IMF is synthesized in laboratories without quality control, and therefore, has chemical impurities and varying amounts of the active substance. IMF is gradually replacing heroin in Canada and the United States due to its high profitability, low cost, and high potency [6, 7]. Fentanyl use has played a key role in the opioid crisis in the United States, with over 80,000 deaths reported in 2024 [8].

In Canada, IMF was identified in over 75,000 samples collected from 2012 to 2022. The results showed that, up to 2019, IMF was combined mainly with heroin, cocaine, and methamphetamine. From 2020 onwards, there was a significant local expansion of synthetic drugs and an increase in fentanyl mixed with sedatives [9]. In Mexico, IMF use has primarily been reported along the border with the United States, particularly among people who inject heroin, who are often unknowingly exposed to this adulterant [1]. Due to their proximity to the United States, Tijuana and Mexicali have binational labor markets and a constant flow of people and illicit drugs. IMF use began in these cities, significantly impacting communities with high social and health vulnerability [10].

In 2018, a rapid assessment study was conducted along the US-Mexico border of 750 people who use heroin in Tijuana (Baja California), San Luis Río Colorado (Sonora), and Ciudad Juárez (Chihuahua), focusing on treatment centers and injection sites. This population reported an increase in the availability of white powder heroin, known as “china white”, and a rise in overdoses. At the time, although the population was unaware of fentanyl, it had noticed that this form of heroin had a stronger effect [11].

In 2020, a drug-checking study using fentanyl test strips found fentanyl in 93% of 55 china white samples collected in Tijuana [1]. Another study documented a significant increase in the availability of china white, while 53% of 652 analyzed residues collected from various injection sites in Tijuana were found to contain fentanyl [12]. At the same time, there was a significant increase in the demand for fentanyl-related treatment. The National Mental Health and Addictions Commission (CONASAMA) documented an increase from 10 officially confirmed cases in 2018 to 430 in 2023, concentrated in the northern states of Baja California, Chihuahua, Sinaloa, and Sonora [13].

National overdose death rates peaked in 2021, primarily in eight states along the US-Mexico border [14]. The Baja California Forensic Medical Service (SEMEFO-BC) tested for the presence of various drugs in all the corpses received at its premises in 2022. Of the 1,775 bodies examined, 896 (50.5%) of the decedents tested positive for multiple drugs. Among them, 351 (39%) were positive for fentanyl identified by specific test strips [13]. These studies have identified a rapid spread of fentanyl at Mexico`s northern border. However, it is crucial to continue undertaking studies to detect changes in drug supply and effectively address the emerging challenges of a market with an increasingly large share of synthetic drugs. Considering that most studies use test strips [1, 15–17], the aim of this study was twofold: a) to determine the presence of heroin, fentanyl, methamphetamine, xylazine, and other substances in samples of paraphernalia (used syringes) in Tijuana and Mexicali, Baja California; and b) to compare the results obtained with using fentanyl and ATS test strips and gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to analyze the positive predictive value of these strips.

Methods

Drug residue collection

Two sites were selected for the collection of 4,411 drug residues (used syringes): the civil organizations’ harm reduction sites (50% of the samples), one in each city, and the shooting galleries and meeting places of people who inject drugs (50%). We used both sites because people who use drugs often live on the streets and congregate at these places. Each city was mapped to locate the gathering places of people who inject drugs through information obtained by the harm reduction programs of the Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) PrevenCasa in Tijuana and Verter in Mexicali. These places perform outreach work, and the community trusts them. Paraphernalia to obtain all samples (including those for confirmatory tests) was collected at these points from September 2022 to August 2023. At the harm reduction sites, people who use drugs deposited their used syringes in rigid containers and were given a harm reduction kit with sterile syringes, cookers, cotton filters, and injectable water in exchange. Additionally, used syringes were collected from the mapped areas. All samples were placed in labeled containers for tracking throughout the study. Our research team handled and extracted the residues from the syringes according to the protocols established by the Official Mexican Standard on Infectious Bio-Hazardous Residue Management.

Syringe selection

To ensure safe handling and select the samples for analysis with test strips, each container was opened separately, with syringes being placed in a stainless-steel tray. We checked the syringes to identify their general condition and discard those that were broken, lacked safety caps, or contained obvious contaminants (such as soil) that could affect the analysis. Unusable syringes were deposited in Hazardous Biological-Infectious Contagious Waste containers. We selected complete syringes, with safety caps and no visible contaminants. All the selected syringes were organized on the tray and uniquely coded for traceability.

A random and systematic selection of syringes was subsequently conducted for the (GC–MS) analysis: To establish the selection frequency for each sample, the selection interval was determined by dividing the total number of samples expected to be collected (approximately 4000) at the end of the study for strip analysis by the number of samples set as the target to be analyzed by GC–MS (n = 300; this sample size was selected considering expediency and operational and financial constraints). A random number was generated between one and the selection interval to determine the first syringe to be selected. The value of the selection interval was added to the number of the selected syringes; and this process was repeated with the remaining syringes on the tray. If the value of this sum was greater than the total number of syringes on the tray, the difference between the value of this sum and the total number of syringes in that tray was calculated to determine the first syringe to be selected from the tray of the following container. Of the selected samples, 193 were from Tijuana and 107 from Mexicali. The results presented in this paper correspond to these 300 samples.

Sample preparation for strips and GC–MS testing

We removed the plungers from their syringes, placing them in individual 4-cm diameter and 5-cm deep containers with 15 ml of purified water to dilute the substances in each plunger. After briefly stirring the solution, we placed the testing strips in it, waited for 15 min and read the results. For fentanyl and fentanyl analogs, we used BTNX rapid response test strips with a detection limit of 130 ng/ml with a referred sensitivity and specificity close to 100%. Although specific sensitivity and specificity varied for ATS, depending on the substance tested, the approximate detection limit was 1 μg/ml.

Insulin syringes without plungers were used to recover samples for GC–MS analysis. We placed the syringes vertically with the needles covered with plastic caps, added 1 ml of methanol and left them untouched for 30 min to one hour, depending on whether the needle was clogged, to ensure that the solids remaining in the syringes and needles had dissolved. The syringe contents were then placed in 2-ml glass vials and sent to the lab for analysis. The GC–MS system was an Agilent 7890 gas chromatographer attached to a 5975 Agilent mass spectrometer (Santa Clara, CA, US) equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 mm × 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm). An Agilent Technologies 7693 auto sampler was used to inject 1 μL of a solution sample. The ionization energy was 70 eV, with a mass range of 30 to 800 m/z. The initial temperature of the column was set at 70 °C, maintained for two minutes, and then ramped up from 40 °C/min to 250 °C. The temperature of the injector was set at 250 °C and that of the detector at 230 °C. The flow rate of the carrier gas (He) was 1.0 mL/min injected with a gas dilution of 1:4. Individual compounds present in each sample were identified using the mass spectra library of the National Institute of Standards and Technology.

GC–MS analysis allows the identification and relative quantification of volatile compounds present in a sample. In this study, the compound identified was classified as positive in the sample, while the relative amount of the compound present in the sample was interpreted as a percentage.

Data analysis

The substances reported in this study were detected at percentages above 0.1% by GC–MS. Values below this percentage were considered negative. For comparisons between proportions of residues containing specific substances in Mexicali and Tijuana, we used the z test followed by the Bonferroni test. An alpha value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We used 2 × 2 contingency tables, the squared Chi test to compare main substances found in drug residues in Tijuana and Mexicali, and the Fisher’s exact test to determine whether there was an association between the results obtained with test strips and GC–MS. In addition, to measure the accuracy of the test strips as diagnostic tests, we calculated the sensitivity (fraction of samples containing a drug identified by fentanyl or ATS test strips: True Positive), and specificity (fraction of samples without a drug, yielding a negative result with these test strips: True Negatives).

Sensitivity refers to the ability of a test (or instrument) to yield positive results for an individual (or sample) who has the condition tested. In our case, sensitivity indicates the proportion of samples that tested positive with immunoreactive strips out of all samples that were positive by GC-MS. Specificity refers to the ability of a test (or instrument) to detect negative results for an individual (or sample) who does not have the condition tested [18]. In 2 x 2 contingency tables, the first cell (A; first row, first column) corresponds to the cases that were positive to a specific drug using both detection methods (true positives), the second cell (B; first row, second column), includes those that were positive using the immunoreactive strips but not GC-MS (false positives). Similarly, the third cell (C; first column, second row) corresponds to false negatives, and the fourth cell (D; second column, second row), to true negatives.

Sensitivity = True positives (A)/[True positives (A) + False negatives (C)]

Specificity = True negatives (D)/[True negatives (D) + False positives (B)]

Statistical analyses were performed with the IBM SPSS Statistics v.21 program (New York, US). To calculate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and confidence limits (95%), we used the Diagt module of Stata V.13 Statistics /Data Analysis Program (Texas USA), taking GC–MS analysis as the reference standard.

Results

Main substances found in drug residues

Fentanyl and its analogs were the most prevalent substances found in residues (n = 229, 76.3% of the samples), followed by methamphetamine and its analogs (n = 176, 58.7%). Heroin dropped to third place (n = 102, 34%), and xylazine was the next most prevalent drug (n = 61, 20.3%). Of the 300 residues analyzed, 11 did not contain any active drug.

Opioids

The most abundant substance found in positive opioid samples was fentanyl (n = 228; 99.6%), followed by despropionyl fentanyl (n = 104; 45.4%) and p-fluorofentanyl (n = 71; 31%). In addition, 65 samples (28.4%) contained acetyl analogs of fentanyl. Although the relative percentage of fentanyl content varied from less than 0.6% to a maximum of 100%, most residues (n = 148) contained 20% or more. Of the 104 samples containing despropionyl fentanyl (n = 103) or N-phenylpropionamide, (n = 1, a typical component of fentanyl to which it breaks down) most of them (n = 65) had low content (5% or less). Similarly, 55 out of 71 residues with p-fluorofentanyl contained less than 5% (Fig. 1A). The presence of heroin was confirmed in 102 residues with morphine being found in 40. Other compounds naturally occurring in opium were also detected, such as codeine (n = 20), hydrocotarnine (n = 7), papaverine (n = 3), and noscapine (n = 5), mostly at percentages of under 2%. Among the semisynthetic opioids, 6-monoacetyl morphine (6-MAM) was the most abundant (n = 123), followed by 6-acetyl codeine (n = 104), thebacon (n = 6), and 3-monoacetyl morphine (3-MAM or Mor3Ac) (n = 1). Together, these results suggest that some opioids remained from incomplete morphine purification of heroin synthesis (Fig. 1B). ATS

Methamphetamine was the most abundant ATS (n = 176, 58.7%). Eight of the 300 samples (2.7%) contained N-acetylmethamphetamine (NacMeth) while the percentage of NacMeth ranged from 0.2 to 1.7%.

Benzodiazepines

We did not detect benzodiazepines in any of the drug residues analyzed.

Discussion

The main findings of this study are (a) the confirmed presence of fentanyl in the opioid supply in Tijuana and Mexicali, (b) the emergence of xylazine as an opioid adulterant, and (c) the increase in methamphetamine use among PWID.

Fentanyl is replacing heroin, while xylazine emerged as an adulterant on the northern border of Mexico

Our results show that fentanyl is displacing heroin in the opioid supply in Tijuana and Mexicali. In 2018, we reported that black tar was the most prevalent form of heroin and that people who use drugs were unaware of either the presence or the effects of fentanyl [11]. However, PWID are now aware that fentanyl is present in the “china white” they purchase. The reason they use it may either be that it is what they can afford, or because some PWID specifically seek the effects of “china white”.

Our results are in line with reports from other countries. Several researchers believe that powerful synthetic drugs, such as fentanyl, will increasingly challenge the long-standing dominance of heroin in opioid black markets because they are easier to produce and more profitable [19]. In keeping with this idea, an analysis of drug seizures and overdoses in certain US states (such as New Hampshire), British Columbia in Canada, and certain European regions indicates that fentanyl has virtually replaced heroin [20]. The increased prevalence of fentanyl in the drug market poses specific challenges due to the higher risk of overdoses and greater difficulty in reversing them compared to heroin [21]. The present results contribute to raising awareness of this situation.

Our study is the first confirmatory report of the presence of xylazine in Mexico, documenting 61 positive samples in Mexicali and seven in Tijuana. Our group suspected that this drug had become available on the Mexican market because of reports from the NGOs of PWID complaining about severe skin lesions. Xylazine is an alpha-2-adrenergic receptor agonist used as a veterinary sedative not approved for human use [22]. Known as “tranq” or “tranq dope,” xylazine was first detected in Puerto Rico, usually in combination with heroin or cocaine [23]. After the initial identification of xylazine in Philadelphia in the 2010s, [24] it rapidly spread to most of the other states and by 2021, 91% of the heroin/fentanyl samples analyzed in Philadelphia contained xylazine [22]. Adverse effects associated with xylazine include dry mouth, bradycardia, hypotension, extreme sedation (immobility), and death [5]. Skin lesions are common in those who are repeatedly exposed to xylazine, whether injected or smoked. Lesions can appear at the injection site or elsewhere and can progress to skin ulcers, severe ulcerations, and necrosis [5, 25]. Both xylazine and opioids produce sedation and respiratory depression, increasing the risk of fatal overdoses [26] partly due to a more pronounced and longer-lasting decrease of brain oxygen levels than that produced by opioids alone [27]. Due to the life-threatening effects of the fentanyl-xylazine mixture, the Center for Forensic Science Research and Education (CFSRE) issued a public alert in the US in 2020 [28]. In 2022, the Canadian Community Epidemiology Network on Drug Use (CCENDU) did the same to raise awareness among service providers and PWIDs [29]. Based on preliminary data from the present study, the Mexican Health Authorities (CONASAMA) also issued a National Public Health alert on May 7, 2024. [30]

Fentanyl and methamphetamine co-occurrence in residues is the norm, rather than the exception in Tijuana and Mexicali

Our data show that most PWID use fentanyl and amphetamines. However, the design of our study does not allow us to determine whether these substances were already present in what they bought, whether they were intentionally mixed by the person who used them, or whether several substances were injected in various sessions using the same syringe. Despite these limitations, service providers and social workers should be aware of the simultaneous or subsequent use of these drugs. In a parallel study conducted with the same population that provided the syringes, PWID complained of itching, paranoia, and more frequent opioid overdoses. Moreover, some reports have shown that people who use drugs intentionally combine these substances, attempting to counteract the risk of opioid overdose with a stimulant and the paranoia and excessive stimulation produced by stimulants with opioids [31]. In addition, both substances are widely available at low cost.

The use of fentanyl and ATS by PWID also reflects an international trend. According to recent data, the United States is currently experiencing the fourth wave of the opioid crisis, driven by supply rather than demand, and is characterized by ATS and opioid availability and use. [32, 33]

Lack of benzodiazepine detection in drug residues

We did not detect benzodiazepines in any of the residues. This finding was somewhat unexpected because benzodiazepine use is common among PWID [34]. Our data should be seen within a broader context because legal benzodiazepines in oral form are easy to obtain without a prescription in certain drug stores in Tijuana and Mexicali [35]. This makes it easier to take them orally, precluding the need to inject them.

Drug residues have various cutting agents and pharmacologically active adulterants in drug residues

Although the heroin samples mainly contained fentanyl, we also found fentanyl analogs, and the fentanyl precursor 4NPP. All of these have been described as common heroin adulterants in the United States and Canada [36, 37]. In addition, heroin samples contained several alkaloids, some with psychoactive effects (such as morphine, codeine, and 6MAM) and some without (such as noscapine, papaverine, and hydrocotarnine) [38, 39]. Thebacon is a semisynthetic opiate structurally related to the natural alkaloid thebaine. This mixture reflects the coexistence of plant-derived and synthetic opioids in the northern Mexican drug market. By contrast, amphetamine samples were quite pure. The small amounts of N-acetylamphetamine and dimethyl amphetamine detected are byproducts of amphetamine synthesis. [40]

Regional differences

Tijuana and Mexicali are in the east of Baja California. Mexicali is the state capital, with a population of just over one million. Tijuana has over twice as many inhabitants and is the site of over 50 million annual border crossings [41]. Due to their strategic geographical location, these cities have been key areas for smuggling fentanyl and methamphetamine between the two countries. Demand for these drugs comes mainly from the United States. However, some of the substances are used in Mexico by vulnerable populations, including people who are homeless or deportees from the United States lacking local support networks [11]. The evolving market has already introduced xylazine to Mexicali, and it is logical to assume it will spread to other areas. Xylazine produces significant adverse health effects, increasing the risk of overdose and the occurrence of skin lesions. [5] The emergence of this drug in Mexico replicates what happened with fentanyl a few years ago, when this opioid was mixed with heroin and PWID unwittingly began to use it. This situation requires a coordinated effort to make naloxone available to people who use drugs and service providers and expand drug-monitoring services to other cities to address the challenges raised by the presence of adulterants in more traditional drugs.

The test strips used in this study have a high positive predictive value

As mentioned earlier, this study is part of a larger initiative in which we tested 4,411 drug residues with test strips and conducted a confirmatory study in 300. Researchers have benefited from validation for different testing methods such as test strips and GC–MS and Fourier-Transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) [15, 17, 42–45]. Considering that GC–MS studies are difficult to perform due to economical constraints and the scarcity or absence of analytical laboratories in many regions of the country, we were interested in determining the predictive value of these strips for future analysis. We found a high association between BTNX test strips and GC–MS results with positive predictive values of 81% in both cases. These strips are a convenient detection method for field drug checking and places lacking spectrometry equipment. We could not compare the accuracy of xylazine strips to detect this substance because they were unavailable when this study was conducted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in two crucial Mexican northern border cities, most heroin samples were found to contain fentanyl. Opioid and ATS use were common in Tijuana and Mexicali, while xylazine emerged as a significant adulterant in Mexicali.

Study limitations

We obtained the substances from used syringes provided by people seeking help at the main harm reduction programs coordinated by the civil society that offered care to highly vulnerable populations in each city, as well as the meeting points mapped by the NGOs.

Although the population on the northern border exposed to heroin, methamphetamine-type stimulants, and fentanyl use shares similar consumption practices, such as injecting almost every day and several times a day, sharing syringes, and unfavorable social conditions, such as not having access to health and education services and living on the streets. Our results are limited to Tijuana and Mexicali and therefore cannot be generalized to the rest of the border region, as drug supply varies among cities.

For example, in San Luis Río Colorado, there is a wide supply and distribution of methamphetamine with opioids on the streets, while in Ciudad Juárez, the distribution is highly controlled in certain areas of the city, so people who use opioid choose not to mix it with methamphetamine and instead mix it with cocaine, known as a “speed ball.” [11] It is essential to continue epidemiological surveillance in this region and the rest of the country, strengthen warning systems, and provide community-based harm reduction strategies to people exposed to an increasingly adulterated drug market.

The GC–MS results are expressed as the relative percentage of the substances found in each sample rather than in actual amounts. Future studies should use calibration curves to provide quantitative results and incorporate other places in northern Mexico.

Finally, drug-residue analysis of paraphernalia has its limitations compared to the analysis of biological samples (such as urine, hair, and blood). Some studies have documented cross-contamination when analyzing waste, false positives due to the type of rapid test used, or detection problems arising from the sample preparation process [16]. Aware of this limitation, but also of the context surrounding the drug-using population in this border region, we proposed working with doses from syringes and not associating any samples with individuals, for ethical and biosafety reasons.
