Chytridiomycosis in a colony of hellbenders Cryptobranchus alleganiensis
Authors: L. Novotny, C. M. Powers, S. M. Royal, A. K. Preston, G. K. Hendrix, M. F. Sola
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe hellbender Cryptobranchus alleganiensis is a fully aquatic, long‐lived, and state endangered salamander endemic to the eastern and central United States. Chytridiomycosis is a fungal skin disease of salamanders that can be caused by either Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) or by recently described B. salamandrivorans. B. salamandrivorans is responsible for massive mortality of salamanders in Europe but, to date, has not been reported in the United States, in contrast to Bd. Three of the deceased animals were autopsied, and samples for histopathology, microbiology, and Taqman quantitative polymerase chain reaction were taken.ResultAutopsy revealed an abundant, thick, yellow to green mucoid material on the surface of the skin, mainly on the tail and flanks. Histopathology of the skin samples revealed infection with chytrid fungus consistent with chytridiomycosis. Taqman quantitative polymerase chain reaction confirmed presence of Bd , and bacterial culture confirmed co‐infection with Aeromonas hydrophila. Infected hellbenders were treated with ciprofloxacin, itraconazole, and ceftazidime.ConclusionThe chytridiomycosis outbreak was most likely caused by sudden environmental stress due to filtration failure and secondary bacterial infection. The surviving animals have not shown any clinical signs of chytridiomycosis for more than 1 year after the treatment and were released the wild river system.

INTRODUCTION

The hellbender Cryptobranchus alleganiensis is a fully aquatic, long‐lived, and state endangered salamander that is closely related to the Japanese giant salamander Andrias japonicus and the Chinese giant salamander Andrias davidianus. Hellbenders are endemic to the eastern and central United States and inhabit cool, highly oxygenated streams (Taber et al. 1975). Two subspecies of hellbender have been described: the eastern hellbender C. alleganiensis alleganiensis, which is found around the north‐flowing streams draining to the Missouri and Mississippi rivers; and the Ozark hellbender C. alleganiensis bishop, which occurs in the Black and White River drainages of southern Missouri and northern Arkansas (Sabatino and Routman 2009).

Chytridiomycosis is a fungal skin disease of salamanders that can be caused by either Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) or the recently described B. salamandrivorans (Bsal). Both Bd and Bsal are believed to have their origin in East Asia or Southeast Asia (Kilpatrick et al. 2010; Sun et al. 2023). Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans is linked to a significant decline in wild populations of European amphibians of the order Urodela (newts and salamanders), especially fire salamanders Salamandra salamandra (Martel et al. 2013, 2014). The chytrid fungus Bsal is responsible for massive mortality of salamanders in Europe, but to date, it has not been reported in the United States (Bales et al. 2015; Waddle et al. 2020).

Infection with Bd has been an emerging disease for the past two decades, with potentially lethal effects on postmetamorphic life stages of wild amphibians over a broad geographic area (Van Rooij et al. 2015), and it may cause chytridiomycosis in captive individuals (Junge 2012). Common signs of Bd infection in salamanders are excessive skin shedding; cutaneous discoloration, mainly in the ventral parts of the abdomen and thighs; and eventually death as a result of osmotic imbalance (Berger et al. 2000; Borteiro et al. 2019).

Both Bd and Bsal infect the keratinocytes, subsequently causing epidermal hyperplasia, hyperkeratosis, and mucus hypersecretion as a reaction to the pathogens. Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans can infect deeper layers of skin, resulting in skin necrosis, whereas Bd infections mostly cause skin proliferation (Martel et al. 2013, 2014). Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans chytridiomycosis resembles Bd chytridiomycosis at the early stages of infection.

Salamander skin has features of constitutive defense due to its physical, chemical, and biological properties (Colombo et al. 2015). One of these mechanisms is skin shedding (also termed "sloughing"), which is a physiologic strategy for the clearing of skin infections (Meyer et al. 2012; Ohmer et al. 2017). Skin shedding is not the only natural antimicrobial mechanism of defense in salamanders. The skin mucus provides a physical and chemical barrier and contains a symbiotic microbiome, which itself may act as an antimycotic agent by producing antimicrobial substances (e.g., Janthinobacterium lividum and its secreted metabolite, violacein; Woodhams et al. 2015, 2018) or simply by competing with pathogens for space and nutrients (Lam et al. 2011; Kearns et al. 2017; Rebollar et al. 2020).

Hellbenders have working lungs, which can be used for respiration in water with low levels of oxygen (Ultsch and Duke 1990), but in well‐oxygenated waters, oxygen absorption occurs through the capillaries of the lateral folds, making the skin a main means of respiration (Boutilier and Toews 1981). Any skin disease in this area has a profound effect on respiratory function. Moreover, the amphibian skin is a central organ for maintaining water and electrolyte equilibrium (Boutilier et al. 1992).

In vitro, Bd has an optimal growth rate at temperatures of 17–25°C (Piotrowski et al. 2004). The life cycle of Bd is composed of a free‐living, infectious zoospore, which is chemoattracted to the amphibian skin using its motile flagellum. Within the skin, the zoospores encyst into a thallus, absorb the flagellum, and develop rhizoids. They then produce a germ tube through which they inject their contents into deeper keratinocytes. Within 1–2 days postexposure, the developing sporangium undergoes division of its nucleus and then its cytoplasm, yielding individual zoospores that develop inside the sporangial capsule. These zoospores are then released through a discharge tube and seek another amphibian to infect (Van Rooij et al. 2012; Grogan et al. 2018, 2020).

Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis has been isolated from wild hellbenders since 1969 (Bodinof et al. 2011) but usually with no clinical disease (Bales et al. 2015). However, Bd can cause mortality in hellbenders within rear‐and‐release programs (Dean et al. 2016; Dusick et al. 2017). The causes of recrudescence of clinical chytridiomycosis are unclear.

METHODS AND RESULTS

Eastern hellbenders in the Aquaculture Research Laboratory at Purdue University experienced mortality in December 2022 (33%; 49 of 152 individuals died), with reported skin lesions. Hellbenders were kept in artificial streams with a water temperature of 21°C and were fed shrimp Mysis spp. and bloodworms Glycera spp. three times per week. The facility uses filtered well water, which is pumped out to the building with additional filtration (bag filters) and ultraviolet sanitizers before entering the streams. The main facility experienced a filtration failure in mid‐December, which resulted in increased iron deposits entering the stream, thus increasing the iron concentration to levels as high as 6 mg/L (usual levels = 0.5–1.0 mg/L). Dissolved oxygen was maintained between 8.9 and 9.4 mg/L (i.e., between 90% and 95%).

Clinical signs and mortalities occurred after the filtration failure. The hellbenders were reported as lethargic, with excessive shedding of the skin. Skin lesions appeared 3–4 days after the filtration failure, with the onset described as rapid and aggressive. The first mortality occurred on December 16, 2022, and the final mortality occurred on January 3, 2023. Two females and one male (2.5 years old) were submitted for an autopsy 10 days after filtration failure. Weights of the hellbenders ranged from 110 to 140 g, and nose‐to‐tail length was 26–28 cm.

Autopsy revealed an abundant, thick, yellow to green mucoid material on the surface of the skin, mainly on the tail and flanks, in all three animals (Figure 1). The spleen was mildly enlarged. The entire gastrointestinal tract contained mild to moderate amounts of loose, yellow ingesta. Skin swabs and samples of skin for freezing were collected. Samples of skin, spleen, and liver were collected for bacteriology. Samples of skin, brain, heart, kidneys, stomach, intestine, and skeletal muscle were collected for histopathology and stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The tissues were processed routinely and embedded in paraffin wax (formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissues). Sections (4 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, periodic acid–Schiff, Grocott methenamine silver, and Perls' Prussian blue.

An eastern hellbender exhibiting abundant yellow to green mucoid material on the surface of the skin along with excessive skin shedding.

Histologically, there was multifocal, moderate to marked epidermal hyperplasia, with keratinocytes piling up to 18 layers thick, increased mitotic figures, and multifocal, moderate to marked orthokeratotic hyperkeratosis, hypersecretion of mucus, and multifocal epidermal erosions (Figure 2). The epidermis was multifocally infiltrated by low numbers of granulocytes. Within the hyperkeratotic stratum corneum, there were numerous round chytrid thalli, approximately 5–7 μm in diameter, with one central septum (Figure 3). Thalli positively stained with periodic acid–Schiff and Grocott methenamine silver. There were also cyst‐like zoosporangia containing multiple discrete, basophilic zoospores in the viable epidermis (Figure 2). Within the hyperkeratotic layers and stratum corneum, there were numerous empty thalli. Within the hyperkeratotic layers were pools of mucus, some necrotic debris, and numerous short bacilli. The superficial dermis multifocally contained low to moderate numbers of lymphocytes and granulocytes. Granular dermal glands contained a large amount of eosinophilic secretion. No lesions were noted in other tissues.

Bacteriology

Duplicate 5% sheep's blood agar (Remel) and MacConkey agar (Remel) plates were inoculated with skin swabs, liver, and spleen collected during autopsy. Blood agar plates were incubated at 35 ± 2°C with 5% CO2 and at room temperature (25 ± 2°C). MacConkey agar plates were incubated at 35 ± 2°C and at room temperature. Growth was observed at 18–24 and 48 h. Colonies were identified via matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry using the direct transfer method. Bacterial species were reported with scores over 2.0 using Biotyper software (Bruker). The isolate from skin swabs, liver, and spleen was identified as Aeromonas hydrophila.

Molecular biology

The DNA was extracted from swabs and fifty microliters of PrepMan Ultra Sample Preparation Reagent (Life Technologies) were added to each sample. Samples were vigorously vortexed for 40 s, incubated for 10 min at 100°C, allowed to cool to room temperature, and centrifuged for 3 min at 24,104 × g. Ten microliters of each supernatant were transferred to 90 μL of ultrapure water, respectively. The DNA was stored at 4°C prior to use.

The TaqMan quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) Bd/Bsal multiplex assay used the primers, probes, and PCR program as previously described (Boyle et al. 2004; Hyatt et al. 2007; Blooi et al. 2013). Samples were run in triplicate on a 384‐well plate in a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An internal positive control (TaqMan Exogenous Internal Positive Control; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was run on each sample to detect PCR inhibitors. Each plate contained two wells with plasmid positive control containing Bd and Bsal fragments (Molecular Pisces) and one well with water as a negative control. Each reaction contained 10 μL of 2× TaqMan Environmental Master Mix 2.0 (Thermo Scientific), 0.3‐μM each of Bsal primers SterF and SterR, 0.1‐μM probe SterC, 0.9‐μM each of Bd primers ITSi‐3 Chytr and 5.85 Chytr, 0.25‐μM chytrid probe Chytr MGB2, 0.2‐mg/mL bovine serum albumin, 5 μL of the DNA template as prepared above, and 2.99 μL of nuclease‐free water to a total volume of 20 μL. The TaqMan program was as follows: one cycle of 56°C for 2 min, followed by 95°C for 10 min; and then 55 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Samples that amplified at a cycle threshold (C t ) of 50 or higher were considered not detected. Samples amplifying at a C t less than 50 in at least two wells were considered positive. Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis was detected in all individuals, with a C t less than 30 (in all three tested animals); Bsal was not detected.

Treatment

Infected hellbenders were isolated from the cohort and other animals during the duration of the disease event. However, all hellbenders from the stream were treated due to potential exposure. We treated hellbenders with a ciprofloxacin (500 mg per 56.8 L of water) bath for 12 days and conducted 50% water changes, adding new ciprofloxacin, every 3 days. We also treated all hellbenders with an itraconazole (0.01% solution) bath (5 min/day) for 12 days. Itraconazole baths were changed every 3 days or if the bath became contaminated with fecal matter. Heavily infected hellbenders were described as individuals with skin lesions and a thickened epidermis layer covering 50% or more of the body. Heavily infected individuals were additionally treated with a ceftazidime submuscular injection (25 mg/kg) once daily for 3 days. Despite medical intervention, all heavily infected hellbenders succumbed to the disease.

DISCUSSION

The morphology of fungal organisms in this case was consistent with Bd infection, which was confirmed by PCR examination. The morphology of the thalli may help to discriminate between Bd and Bsal (Bd possesses only one septum, whereas Bsal has multiple septa; DiRenzo et al. 2021), but PCR or in situ hybridization methods are the only definitive ways of distinguishing between Bd and Bsal.

The source of Bd in this case is unknown. The well water tested negative for Bd historically, which does not exclude the possibility of a recent introduction of Bd into the water system. No new hellbenders were introduced into the system recently. The technicians and caregivers change gloves and wash hands prior to going into a system. Each system has its own equipment that is not shared with other systems. All equipment was sanitized after use as well. It is known that wild hellbenders can maintain subclinical Bd infections for long periods of time (Bodinof et al. 2011; Souza et al. 2012; Kaganer et al. 2023); hence, it is possible that the hellbenders carried low numbers of Bd and that this subclinical infection became clinical after the filtration failure. Anthropogenically induced stress, such as an increased pathogen burden, altered microclimates, or captive handling, can lead to immunosuppression and increased susceptibility to disease (Voyles et al. 2009; Collins 2010; Rollins‐Smith 2017).

Histopathology confirmed a marked thickening of the skin, with extensive hyperkeratosis creating a thick barrier to gas exchange. In addition to these skin lesions, Bd produces cytotoxic mycotoxins, which may further damage the skin tissue (Fites et al. 2013), including intraepidermal capillaries, the presence of which is a unique feature of hellbender skin (Pessier 2018). We stained the skin sections with Perls' Prussian blue for iron, but there were no obvious deposits of iron in the skin tissue; thus, skin damage from iron deposits due to the increased iron concentration in the water was unlikely.

Loss or reduction of critical skin functions increases the susceptibility of hellbenders to Bd infection (Voyles et al. 2009). Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis itself causes skin damage, which may aggravate the disease by facilitating further infection with Bd spores as well as infection by opportunistic bacteria from the aquatic environment.

Bacteriology isolated A. hydrophila from the skin, liver, and spleen. This infection was most likely secondary but probably contributed to the detrimental course of the disease. Previous studies suggest that host skin bacterial communities may not be a predictor of disease resistance but rather that chytrid infection is associated with community‐level microbiome disruption and subsequent negative health effects (Jani and Briggs 2014).

In summary, this chytridiomycosis outbreak at the Purdue University Aquaculture Research Laboratory was most likely caused by sudden environmental stress due to filtration failure and secondary bacterial infection. The surviving animals have not shown any clinical signs of chytridiomycosis for more than 1 year after the treatment and were released into the wild river system.
