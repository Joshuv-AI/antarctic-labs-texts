Perspectives of parents/caregivers prior to a pilot intervention trial to improve transition to adult care for adolescents with type 1 diabetes: A qualitative study
Authors: Kenzie Tapp, Elise Mok, Dawn Davis, Sylvia Guérin-Marion, Jennifer Frei, Michele Gibbon, Melanie Henderson, Melissa Miljanovski, Deborah Da Costa, Rayzel Shulman, Meranda Nakhla, Jessica Kichler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo help mitigate potential complications often experienced by adolescents with type 1 diabetes (T1D) during the transition to adult healthcare, transition care programs aim to provide developmentally appropriate healthcare, promote adolescent decision-making and self-care skills, and prepare adolescents for adult diabetes care. However, there is limited research considering parent/caregiver needs during the transition preparation process for adolescents with T1D.ObjectiveTo identify parent/caregiver perspectives prior to taking part in a transition care intervention involving group education sessions and peer support for parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D.Design and SettingPre-intervention, semi-structured interviews were conducted with parents/caregivers of adolescents (14–16 years) with T1D followed in a tertiary care paediatric diabetes clinic and participating in a pilot, randomised controlled trial: The Group Education Trial to Improve Transition for Parents/Caregivers of Adolescents with T1D (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05445284). We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis to identify key aspects of the intervention that may help support parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D during their transition to adult care.ParticipantsOf the 17 parents/caregivers randomized to the intervention group, 13 completed pre-intervention interviews.ResultsFour themes were generated from the parents/caregivers, including (1) Creating a Community of Learners (i.e., importance of fostering a bidirectional and supportive environment for parents/caregivers), (2) Psychological Perspectives of Parenting (i.e., acknowledgement of shifting parental/caregiver roles during adolescents’ transition), (3) Diabetes Management During Adolescence (i.e., identification of relevant diabetes education topics to be covered), and (4) Ideal Format and Delivery of the Program (i.e., practical recommendations for the logistics of the intervention).ConclusionsThe findings provide practical and pragmatic suggestions to refine the parent/caregiver-based transition of care intervention for a future, full-scale trial, including addressing parental/caregiver needs as they learn how to best support their adolescent with T1D during transition to adulthood. Future transition programmes for adolescents with T1D may also benefit by incorporating aspects of the current transition readiness program for parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D.

Adolescence is marked by an increase in identity exploration, reliance on peers and a desire for independence from their family of origin.1 2 For adolescents with type 1 diabetes (T1D), this shift in social support and autonomy can complicate disease management, especially within the context of other hormonal changes during puberty.2 Specifically, this multifaceted developmental stage has been found to be associated with a decrease in diabetes self-management behaviours and routine medical follow-up, as well as an increase in emergency department visits and hospitalisations.35 Similarly, there is an increase in glycaemic instability during this period, such that only 17% of adolescents currently meet recommended glycaemic targets.6 During the transition process, parents/caregivers also face challenges as they navigate their shifting involvement in their adolescent’s T1D care, often facing conflict with their adolescent over T1D self-management.7 8 This shift in responsibility can create parental/caregiver distress and uncertainty about appropriate involvement and potential adolescent health consequences.7 9

To prevent complications in T1D management during adolescence and to support the successful transition into adulthood, transition care programs have been recommended through many clinical practice guidelines from academic societies.1012 Transition care is defined as the intended movement of adolescents with chronic health conditions from child-focused healthcare systems to more individual, adult-focused healthcare systems, which fosters a purposeful and gradual care process.13 Overall, the goal of transition care programs is to provide developmentally appropriate healthcare (e.g., one that fosters independence and autonomy) and to develop adolescent decision-making and self-care skills, thereby helping prepare the adolescent for adulthood.14 As parents/caregivers attempt to support their adolescent during this transition process, they can face the paradox of wanting to support the development of their adolescent’s autonomy, while still being involved enough in their daily care to oversee T1D management.7 However, limited research has considered the additional needs of parents/caregivers throughout this transition process.9

Difficulties with the shift in responsibility (e.g., youths’ lower level of memory skills and self-reliance related to diabetes) for diabetes care between parents/caregivers and their adolescents contribute to complications.15 16 Conversely, parental/caregiver support and encouragement can help to promote improved self-efficacy and T1D knowledge in adolescents, which can bolster autonomy in young adulthood.17 Despite these known findings, current transition programs often do not provide specific support or targeted guidance for parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D.9 18 This can lead to increased parental/caregiver stress and confusion, as well as parent/caregiver-youth conflict, which can have negative health implications for the parent/caregiver and/or adolescent, disrupt the overall functioning of the family, and lead to difficulties with T1D management.18

Furthermore, parents/caregivers often report that the transition is difficult due, in part, to a changing role in helping their adolescent manage their T1D and a lack of parental/caregiver preparation for transition care.1922 As such, parents/caregivers have expressed a need for structured education on how to effectively transition the responsibility of diabetes care from parent/caregiver to adolescent, as well as the need for parent/caregiver peer support during this process.7 9 18 23 Parent/caregiver group education sessions are a potential complement to adolescent-based transition interventions, as they can address parents’/caregivers’ educational and psychosocial needs in a group setting.82427

Purpose of the Study

A pilot randomised controlled trial (RCT) examining the feasibility of parent/caregiver group education sessions and peer psychosocial support in augmenting transition care in adolescents with T1D (the Group Education Trial to Improve Transition (GET-IT) for Parents/Caregivers of Adolescents with T1D) is currently underway. As part of the pilot RCT, the purpose of the current qualitative study was to determine parent/caregiver perspectives of their anticipated needs in supporting their adolescent with T1D before starting the pilot GET-IT intervention. Specifically, the current study aimed to identify key aspects of the upcoming pilot intervention that parents/caregivers believe may be beneficial to them, as well as other aspects of the GET-IT intervention for parents/caregivers that may require refinement for the full-scale RCT. As such, the following research questions guided this qualitative inquiry: (a) What do parents’/caregivers’ pre-intervention expectations (e.g., perceptions of the anticipated content and format, acceptance and usefulness of the intervention, preconceived accessibility and logistical factors) reveal about what they hope to gain by participating in the parent/caregiver GET-IT pilot group intervention sessions and (b) In what ways, if at all, could this information be used to refine the pilot GET-IT parent/caregiver group intervention sessions for the future, full-scale RCT?

Methodology

Study Design

The GET-IT for Parents pilot RCT intervention arm consists of parent/caregiver group education and peer support sessions related to adolescents’ transition to adulthood with T1D. The pilot RCT parent study aims to both assess the feasibility and refinement of the intervention to inform a full-scale multicentre RCT (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05445284). Participants were parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D, ages 14–16 years, who were at least 6 months post-diagnosis and received their care at a tertiary care paediatric diabetes clinic in Montreal, Canada (i.e., Montreal Children’s Hospital of the McGill University Health Centre [MUHC]). Parent/caregiver was defined as a mother, father, or ‘other’ primary caregiver. Participants with severe neurocognitive disabilities or who were non-fluent in English or French were excluded. For this qualitative study, all parent/caregiver participants who were randomized to the active arm of the RCT (i.e., intervention) were eligible and were invited to participate in the semi-structured interview before starting the intervention (i.e., at baseline).

Data Collection Procedures

Parents/caregivers and adolescents provided both verbal and written consent, where parents/caregivers were subsequently interviewed by study personnel trained in qualitative interviewing (KT, MM, JF and MG) in the participants’ preferred language of English or French from January 2024 to March 2024. Sociodemographic and clinical information was also collected from medical records and/or self-report. The baseline interview consisted of a semi-structured interview guide (see online supplemental appendix A) that focused on assessing parents’/caregivers’ expectations and needs concerning T1D transition readiness prior to participating in the parent/caregiver group education and peer support program (e.g., perceptions of the anticipated content and format, acceptance and usefulness of the intervention, preconceived accessibility and logistical factors). This semistructured interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive review of the literature on transition readiness and parental involvement in T1D management, where all authors collaboratively reviewed and refined the questions to ensure clarity and relevance. No formal changes were made to the interview guide after the interviews began. The semi-structured format allowed for flexibility in follow-up questions and participant-led discussions to capture diverse perspectives and experiences. Two patient partners (one parent of an adolescent with T1D and one parent of a young adult with T1D) also provided feedback on the design, data collection and reporting of the study, helping to ensure that the study methods were relevant to the target population. All the interviews were completed and audio/video recorded through Microsoft Teams in a single 20 to 45 minute session. They were then orthographically transcribed in their original language and assigned a unique identifier code, where any identifying information was removed.

Participants

In total, 13 out of 17 parents/caregivers who were randomized to the active arm of the pilot RCT study completed the semi-structured interviews prior to starting the intervention. Parent/caregiver participants within this sample consisted of nine self-identified females and four self-identified males. Additionally, 69.2% (n=9) of participants identified as White, followed by Black (n=1; 7.6%), Chinese (n=1; 7.6%), Latinx (n=1; 7.6%), and other (n=1; 7.6%). Most participants were married or in a civil union (n=11; 84.6%) and had completed a university certificate, diploma, or degree (n=8; 61.5%). The participants’ adolescent children had a length of diabetes diagnosis range between 8 months to 11.1 years.

Theoretical Positioning of the Current Qualitative Study

Recent research has highlighted the need for qualitative researchers to explicitly reflect on the underpinnings and theoretical frameworks that guide their methodology, especially studies that do not employ qualitative methodologies that already have philosophies grounding them.28 29 As such, we were grounded by a relativist ontological approach and an interpretive/constructivist epistemological approach. Relativism posits that research constructs various views of reality, as multiple views of reality are inherently possible and subjective since they are based on differing combinations of social, cultural, and historical backgrounds.30 Similarly, and building from this, an interpretive/constructivist approach is concerned with subjectivity and a co-construction of meaning, wherein there are multiple realities, schemas, and representations of knowledge.31 This study was not concerned with the accuracy or a universal ‘truth’ of parents’/caregivers’ preconceptions of the group education and peer support sessions or their experiences with adolescents with T1D. Rather, this study was interested in the construction of participants’ experiences based on their own unique perspectives and perceptions as a parent/caregiver to appreciate their individual viewpoints.

Approach to Data Analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis (RTA), as outlined by Braun and Clarke,29 32 33 was implemented to identify, organize, and interpret repeated patterns of meaning across the dataset. Throughout this analytical process, themes, or shared patterns of considerable meaning, are actively constructed by the researcher(s) and their subjective lenses.29 Although there are six phases of RTA, including (1) familiarization, (2) generating initial codes, (3) generating themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report, the process is non-linear and bends back on itself.32

RTA was undertaken by two primary coders, KT and JK, as well as a secondary coder, MM. First, the coding team familiarized themselves with the dataset by reading and re-reading the transcripts numerous times, noting any initial ideas within separate research journals. Second, KT and JK began to identify segments of text that represented single meanings (i.e., codes), reviewing these in debriefing meetings with one another and with MM. Third, the coding team amalgamated the codes generated previously to construct themes and/or subthemes. Phases four and five consisted of meetings among the coding team members where they created definitions and corresponding names to interpret the themes further and compared these conceptions to the dataset by re-coding the transcripts. These potential themes were also brought to the larger author group (i.e., MN, EM, JF, MG, DD, SGM and DDC) to determine consensus and evaluate each theme based on its relevance to the research questions and the narratives told by participants. The sixth phase simultaneously occurred throughout the analytical process and involved input from members of the larger group.

Trustworthiness and Reflexivity

Establishing trustworthiness, or the supposed rigor of a study, is paramount within qualitative research to ensure overall confidence within the methods, data, and interpretations presented.34 35 Nowell et al36 further describe and define trustworthiness as reflecting the quality of the research findings, which is based on principles originally outlined by Lincoln and Guba,37 including credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability, and authenticity. We upheld these standards by keeping detailed logs and research journals, conducting debriefing and audit sessions, involving multiple coders, selecting thick and representative descriptions of the data, and providing sufficient information regarding methodological and analytical processes. We also considered standards of reflexivity, as outlined by Tracy,38 to acknowledge that our findings were created through a co-construction of meaning between our research team and participants, thus representing one possible interpretation of the data based on our own identities.

Results

Four main interconnected themes were co-constructed by the researchers using the parent/caregiver perspectives from the qualitative interviews, which highlighted their readiness, wants, needs, and expectations for the parent/caregiver group education and peer support program. These themes included: (1) Creating a Community of Learners, (2) Psychological Perspectives of Parenting, (3) Diabetes Management During Adolescence, and (4) Ideal Format and Delivery of the Program (see online supplemental appendices B and C).

(1) Creating a Community of Learners

Participants first highlighted the importance of having a learning environment that fostered a safe and meaningful bidirectional learning experience for the program, and its content, to be impactful. They expressed how creating a feeling of community with other parents/caregivers could result in further engagement through dialogue and self-reflection, as well as increase ownership of their learning and motivation to develop. This was further exhibited in two subthemes, including (1.1) Connection and Reciprocal Support and (1.2) Open-minded Approach.

(1.1) Connection and Reciprocal Support

Participants expressed that it was going to be important to share and listen to lived experiences within the group education and peer support sessions to allow for a give and take of ideas and to aid in the generation of new knowledge. They felt that by having other group members share their stories with them, they could learn about different ways to navigate situations with their adolescent and potentially foster a more developed, informative, or altered perspective on the transition process. Participants reflected on how they “always question [themselves] if [they’re] doing good” (011P); therefore, they want to “talk with other parents who have teenagers in a similar situation, just to see if [they’re] on the right track” (020P) and recognize that “people’s experiences, sometimes they mirror yours, sometimes they foreshadow yours” (028P). Participant 024P expanded further on how this receipt of stories from the other parents/caregivers could influence their experience within the group education sessions:

I think, too, that what has often happened to me, to talk, to exchange with other parents of adolescents with diabetes, because it’s like there’s one extra issue to their adolescence… And in the end, when it will all be over, I hope that the whole experience has made me think, has allowed me to analyze, to see things differently when I am going through things with my adolescent. To address things, well, maybe a bit differently. Especially things that are a bit more difficult, where we may feel a bit powerless, let’s say.

Additionally, participants explained how this sharing of stories with other parents/caregivers could help to foster connection among group members to normalize their experiences, reduce stigma and isolation, provide reassurance, and a sense of belonging and validation. They reflected on the need to “create a support network for [them] as peers and as parents” (010P) to not only rely on one another for advice and comradery, but to foster a mutual understanding among “others that are facing the same, similar situation” (014P). Participants added that this would provide them with the reassurance that they are not alone and “are not the only ones living through this same thing” (024P). They felt that “knowing that if [they] have questions, there’s people [they] can reach out to” (028P) was one of the most important aspects that the program could provide as “[they] have to support one another in all this” (034P) as “sharing with others gives [them] strength” (010P).

Participants also commented on how they hoped to share their own experiences within the group education and peer support sessions to impart their own wisdom on their peers, contributing to a reciprocal learning environment. For example, participant 027P stated, “If I can help other parents… that would be great through this process. Because it’s definitely not an easy thing that we’re going through,” while participant 014P said, “I think if some parts are familiar [to me], I will share my experience.” Participant 034P reflected on this further by explaining that the idea of contributing to others’ experiences and learning was the main factor that drew them to participate in this program:

We sometimes use our experience a bit to share, too, with other people who we, unfortunately, learn that they too are diagnosed with type 1 diabetes… [Name of Teen] wants to use [their] experience as well to be able to teach, to share with others and to be able to help others. So, some type of cooperation in all this, a solidarity, between the members of T1D. So, it’s a bit like that – our desire to participate in this research study.

Overall, participants expressed how important the dynamic aspects of the group interactions would be to the effectiveness of the intervention and their overall learning experiences. With T1D being so isolating, not only for their adolescents with T1D, but for parents/caregivers, they wanted to have a supportive space to engage in reciprocal dialogue about their lived experiences. Not only would this benefit themselves, but it would benefit all the group members to create a true “community of learners” (024P).

(1.2) Open-minded Approach

Overall, an open-minded attitude towards the upcoming pilot intervention was expressed by many participants, where they communicated an overarching flexibility and willingness to receive information and potentially integrate new perspectives into their daily lives. This was reflected through a growth mindset that many participants articulated, where they highlighted the importance of opportunities for self-reflection and feedback within their own development as parents/caregivers supporting an adolescent with T1D. For instance, participant 020P expressed, “It was just kind of looking forward to meeting some other parents and getting some feedback from them,” while participant 024P reflected on the importance of making progress and “growing as a [parent] who is supporting [their teen] with diabetes.” Participant 006P reflected on how the group education and peer support sessions will hopefully provide participants with the opportunity to take time to consciously refine their parenting/caregiving skills:

Sometimes it goes by fast, and just to be part of a program like this, for me, well, it forces me to think, you know? And because life sometimes goes so fast—but now, to have these meetings, these sessions, well, whether I like it or not, it’s time for me to think about it and to take the time to work on it… And to be able to take the time to take care of it.

Participants also acknowledged that not all the information presented in the upcoming group education and peer support sessions may be useful or applicable to them, but that it is still beneficial to “have a review” (020P) and to “learn what [they] want, what [they] don’t want, and what [they] find good” (010P). They ultimately recognized and accepted that “not all tools fit in [their] toolbox” (028P); therefore, the intervention would be a unique learning experience for all involved. Participant 028P commented further on this: “At this point, I’m there to glean whatever information I can and whatever is useful, it’s useful. Whatever is not useful, we chalk up to trivia.”

When participants discussed potentially learning new information from the upcoming group education and peer support sessions, they described a desire to apply what they had learned through a trial-and-error experimental approach. They expressed wanting to integrate the information that they anticipated being taught within these sessions into their day-to-day lives to not only increase their learning and understanding, but to evaluate their own effectiveness and make modifications if needed. Participant 006P explained this process further:

Well, probably, I would have to say, to get tips, and to be able to, as in after each session, to see what it’s like… And it’s to get tips and maybe to try to put them into practice. To have tricks, advice and to put it into practice and to be able to, over the course of the sessions, see some progress too. Ideally, for me, I would like that, to be able to apply what I learned and to see if it works, you know?

Participant 030P reflected this sentiment as well:

I will try what I have learned. If I can integrate it, if I can use it, I will try. It could be that it does not work. I will try again one more time, and if it does not work, then maybe that particular trick is not appropriate for me either. So, I will set it aside, maybe to try it again at a later time, or even just put it on pause.

However, participants recognized that this self-evaluation process might take a significant amount of time to digest the information they would be presented with during the upcoming educational and peer support sessions. Specifically, they highlighted the importance of giving themselves the physical time and space to process the information to not only better their understanding of the material, but to also not pressure themselves to be perfect. They recognized that it would be a ‘learning experience’, a ‘learning curve,’ and a ‘trial’. For example, participant 020P communicated this gradual and accepting approach when discussing what they hoped to gain from the group education and peer support sessions:

And you can have the information, but whether you’re able to actually, in the moment, use the information the way you’re supposed to, you know, we’ll see. And only time will tell. I think that’s—I think it’ll be amazing to have.

(2) Psychological Perspectives of Parenting

Within this second theme, despite not even starting the intervention yet, participants already recognized and accepted the need for a shift and renegotiation of the parental/caregiver role to aid in their adolescents’ transition. Although the participants explained that in the past this was sometimes difficult, they stated that they would try to actively face the changes that would be required of them as part of the upcoming intervention in a direct and purposeful manner. This was further represented within two subthemes, including (2.1) mindfully encouraging independence and autonomy and (2.2) prioritizing a teamwork approach.

(2.1) Mindfully Encouraging Independence and Autonomy

While discussing their anticipated role changes as parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D transitioning into adult care and adulthood, participants exhibited a thoughtful approach regarding how to best facilitate the growth of their adolescent as they progressed through this developmental phase. They grappled with the difficulties of trying to find a balance between complete independence for their adolescent with T1D and complete parental/caregiver involvement in the medical regimen. They highlighted that they wanted to honour both the role of themselves as parents/caregivers, but also support the ever-changing role of their adolescent, thus necessitating a gradual shift within this continuum of autonomy and control. Participants expressed wanting tools as part of the upcoming intervention to help them and their adolescent “navigate through” this period of adolescence (033P) and to “become more independent” and not use the parents/caregivers as a “crutch” (006P). Simultaneously, they highlighted the reality of “growing apart” throughout this transition (011P).

But as participant 001P expressed, this increase in independence and responsibility placed on the adolescent “doesn’t prevent [the parent/caregiver] from always being there to help [their teen] all the same.” Participant 024P reflected on this further:

Well, that adult care, it’s to be able to slowly erase ourselves, up to a certain point. As for the rest, we do not stop being parents. But to let our adolescents—to take the reins a bit more and to develop their sense of confidence or competence as it relates to their diabetes. And to stop panicking all the time [laughs]. But that it’s to gain more confidence in [my teen].

Participant 027P also commented on this need to discuss the increasing need for independence and autonomy as part of the upcoming intervention:

So, I would love to learn how to—I’m trying to be as easy on [them] as possible and open and, you know, and maybe talk about that. About how to leave [them] their space. But at the same time, be able to know where [they] are at every moment, you know? So, it’s that, but also not to make the kids feel like we’re breathing down their necks.

Participants expressed how important the intervention could be in helping them with this shift in parent/caregiver and adolescent roles to lay the groundwork for adulthood. They noted that the developmental period of adolescence was the ideal time for their adolescents to “experiment” (011P) with the various elements of adulthood as they could do so in a safe and stable environment first. This would allow the adolescent, as they near adulthood, “to have taken control of [their] diabetes, and be able to take care of it and to take care of it properly” (006P). As participant 028P acknowledged, most of the time, adulthood and its transition is “sink or swim” where “[the teens] are left to fend on their own.” Instead, this program could provide parents/caregivers with an opportunity to mitigate this, and as 027P said, “give us as many tools or things to think about now instead of having to think about them when it’s the day where [they’re] 18 and [they] move out.” Participant 020P discussed this further:

Because right now it’s, as you know, teenagers, they think they’re immortal and they think nothing phases them. But as parents, we’re thinking about the future and [the teen’s] future. And [them] being in [their] 20s and 30s and being out and being at college. And being able to create good habits now so it becomes second nature to [them] when [they are] in [their] 20s.

Overall, participants were already accepting of the fact that their adolescent needed to experiment with more independence in the management of their T1D in the hopes of creating a good set of habits for them as they progressed into adulthood. Despite this being uncharted territory for most participants, they recognized the need for their teen “to fly with [their] own wings too” (001P), and thus, wanted to learn and discuss different methods for how to support this in a healthy and understanding way as part of the upcoming education and peer support sessions. They understood that although these shifts in their parenting/caregiving approaches would change their own role and level of involvement as parents/caregivers, this did not halt their parenting/caregiving role entirely. Rather, an intervention that provided participants with the opportunity to grow and change alongside their adolescent in their journey with T1D was what many participants articulated they wanted.

(2.2) Prioritizing a Teamwork Approach

Throughout this role transition, participants also noted that a change in the parent/caregiver and adolescent dynamic would allow for further collaboration and mutual respect. Participants reflected on the need for an intervention that prioritized building trust between themselves and their adolescent to explore new ideas and ways of interacting throughout this transition to adulthood. Many participants already understood and accepted that their adolescents would sometimes have “slip-ups” (010P) during this experimentation phase, but that the parents/caregivers needed to approach this with emotional support and acceptance. The parent/caregiver participants often stated that they wanted to “keep the lines of communication open” (034P) to facilitate this trust and openness and encourage their adolescents to learn from any missteps by continuing to push forward. Participant 024P discussed the need to learn how to build mutual trust to encourage this parent/caregiver-adolescent dynamic:

It’s learning to trust [them], that [they are] able to face these situations… It’s in my own attitudes, to let [them] feel that I trust [them] because I think that [they have] to go through that for [them] to develop [their] own proper behaviours.

Being able to build this mutual trust between parents/caregivers and adolescents through the upcoming intervention could lead to a mutual understanding between the parents’/caregivers’ and adolescents’ intentions behind their behaviours and, subsequently, improve their collaboration in T1D management. Participants expressed a desire for their adolescents to understand their intentions and see that “it’s not easy for the parent” (011P). However, they also recognized that it was necessary for them to also understand their adolescents’ intentions as “[Parents] don’t know how it feels. [They] don’t know how T1D is in [their adolescent’s body]” (011P). Participants wanted to be seen as “teammates” in their adolescent’s T1D management and to be on “the same page” as one another (020P). Participant 020P explained this further:

That would be something that I would love as a takeaway. I’d really love it if, at the end of the sessions, I come to a place in my understanding of the disease. And how I need to be there for [my teen]. And that [they] see me as someone who’s on the same team. Not just someone who’s cracking a whip trying to get [them] to eat [their] broccoli… that I’m just rooting for [them], not trying to control anything.

Overall, participants wanted to learn how to facilitate and communicate a mutual trust and understanding to their adolescents to strengthen their relationships and maintain the management of T1D as a part of the upcoming intervention. They recognized the reciprocal and bidirectional nature of the parent/caregiver-adolescent relationship, in which change would be required on the part of the parent/caregiver as well as the adolescent. They were already accepting of the idea of an equal or balanced partnership with their adolescent even prior to the intervention. They hoped that changes resulting from the time spent dedicated to the intervention would potentially allow for a reduction in conflict, frustration, and setbacks, while also fostering growth and development in their teamwork approach.

(3) Diabetes Management During Adolescence

Participants also discussed the diabetes-related content that they felt would be important to learn throughout the upcoming group education and peer support sessions. They felt that these topics would benefit all parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D, as they would provide a comprehensive understanding of T1D management and transition processes. More specifically, participants described wanting to learn about the following content during the intervention: how to manage T1D within the context of adolescent developmental milestones, future and long-term health concerns of T1D, day-to-day tips and tricks for T1D management, general or scientific knowledge about T1D, general information about adolescence and development, how to communicate with their adolescent effectively, how to provide emotional support to their adolescent, and coping and co-regulation skills for participants themselves.

As mentioned above, participants often discussed wanting to engage in an intervention that helped them support their adolescents as they navigated their T1D throughout the context of adolescent milestones. These milestones included, but were not limited to, being more involved in sports, consuming alcohol, engaging in smoking or vaping, learning to drive, going on longer trips away from the family, and navigating social relationships with peers and romantic interests. For example, participant 033P mentioned how drinking “has been a big conversation” with their adolescent, while 011P questioned how to navigate this: “How [are they] gonna do later on in case that [they go] to a party and drink alcohol?” Participants wanted to discuss “what is reasonable for someone with T1D” (001P) within these situations during the upcoming intervention, as well as “how to integrate those things so that they’re not so scary or difficult or complicated” (027P).

In relation to this, participants also wanted to discuss the future, long-term, chronic complications often associated with T1D and the potential acute complications of T1D that can occur during the transition to adulthood during the education and peer support sessions. They wanted to be provided with information about “all the side effects that [T1D] could bring” (001P) and to not only be presented with information for the “short-term” but to also discuss “later on and long-term” concerns (011P). They added that having “concrete examples” or even “statistics” (006P) to support this information would be helpful not only for their understanding as parents/caregivers, but also to share this information with their adolescents in a more meaningful and impactful way.

Along with these discussions about long-term complications, though, participants also reported wanting more short-term solutions or easy-to-apply day-to-day tips and tricks to help their adolescent manage their T1D. Participants wanted ‘concrete’, ‘practical’, ‘everyday’ or ‘manageable’ tools or strategies that they could easily implement. They explained wanting such material as this is their “everyday life, [their] day-to-day” (010P) and that they need to know “how to manage situations that always happen to [their] adolescents” (010P). Participant 006P expressed how important the translation of practical tips would be to them:

But the ideal program, it’s that after having had these meetings, for us to feel that, “Well, okay. I now have some good tricks. I know how I will be better prepared to support my child.” So that then I will be able to say, “Good, mission accomplished.”

Additionally, participants expressed interest in having more detailed, evidence-based information from the scientific literature about T1D, the developmental period of adolescence, and the interconnection between these two topics. Participants wanted to “slip into the specifics” (010P) regarding the “scientific information” (014P) about T1D, as many reported that although they had been taught this information about the pathophysiology of T1D when their adolescent was initially diagnosed, they were unable to understand or absorb this information as it was “overwhelming” (027P) and they were “new” to the concept of T1D (014P). However, they wanted this to be presented “alongside the concept of adolescence” (024P) as T1D and adolescence “all comes together, and then adolescence, it adds a whole other dimension” (024P). They added that it would be beneficial to discuss this within the group as many participants expressed trying to source this information themselves but “[they’re] never sure that [they’re] getting the right information or if [they’re] understanding it in the right way” (027P) where they needed assistance trying to navigate “this ocean of information” (034P).

Participants also highlighted the need for material that conveyed communication skills so that they could connect with their adolescents about T1D effectively. They wanted to learn how to “really develop [their] talking about [T1D]” (011P), as well as how to present information “not necessarily in a positive manner, but at least not so negative” (006P) to reduce the stigma and shame surrounding T1D and to encourage their adolescents to persevere. Participant 020P and their co-parent/caregiver explained this further:

Co-Parent/Caregiver 020P: …We do have a teenager who is a little reckless about it. And so, being able to communicate with [them] in a positive –

020P: Productive.

Co-Parent/Caregiver 020P: Yeah, nurturing way so [they] can take something away from it too, you know? Not just us nagging parents who are on [their] back all the time.

Similarly, participants also wanted to learn about how to best provide emotional support to their adolescents throughout their journey with T1D. They highlighted the increased need for adolescents to receive emotional support from parents/caregivers as they are going through a difficult time during development, but also how this is intensified for their adolescents due to the complications with T1D. For example, Participant 008P stated:

On my part, I’m considering getting psychological support for my adolescent… I think that on that level, we have to kind of support them more, because they are kind of fed up with diabetes… I think that that’s what they need, support that is a little more on an emotional level.

Lastly, participants discussed not only wanting information for how to emotionally support their adolescents, but they also wanted to discuss different coping and co-regulation skills that parents/caregivers could use to help manage their own worries and stresses. Participants continually mentioned that “there’s a lot of worry” (020P) that they hold about their adolescents’ T1D and that they wanted to learn how to manage this to be a better support for their adolescent and to model positive coping strategies. For example, Participant 027P explained:

But just the stress that I’ve been going through… And so hopefully, yeah, maybe just to see how other parents are reacting or how they [are] trying to get through each day, you know? And figure out how to stay calm and process everything in a healthy way.

Although participants were open to the content that would be presented within the group education and peer support sessions, as highlighted in theme one, they also articulated specific diabetes-related topics that they felt would be beneficial for all parents/caregivers to learn and even re-learn about T1D. They discussed how important these topics were as it had been difficult to find this information on their own, where a lot of this information had “gone over [their] heads” (028P) when initially presented to them at the point of diagnosis. Participants generated many topics concerning T1D that they felt they were lacking in and wanted to explore this foundational diabetes knowledge to better themselves as parents/caregivers and to ease the potential difficulties of transition to adult care for their adolescents with T1D.

(4) Ideal Format and Delivery of the Program

Although interviewed prior to partaking in the group education and peer support program, participants provided various structural components that they felt they would need to be present when they ultimately began the program. First, participants provided recommendations regarding how they would like the program to be scheduled or offered that would make it easier for them to attend the sessions, as well as factors that would make it more difficult for them to attend the sessions. Specifically, participants highlighted the benefits of having the sessions coincide with their adolescent’s T1D diabetes clinic appointment so that they only had to travel to the location of the sessions once, as well as only requiring at least one parent/caregiver to be in attendance and considering the time of day the sessions were offered to prevent participants from missing work. Conversely, participants mentioned that if the sessions were not spaced out over time, were longer than one hour, and were scheduled during busy times of the year (e.g., summer holidays, during exam times for their adolescent), it would impede their attendance to future sessions.

One of the most common recommendations among participants was the ability to offer the sessions through an online or hybrid option. Participants thought that this would be a barrier-reducing solution if they truly could not attend an in-person session (i.e., due to sickness, not having time to commute, etc.). However, they still preferred the sessions to be offered primarily in person where the “whole online thing would be for an emergency” (027P) or be a “plan B” (024P).

Participants also discussed how important it was that “experts” be involved in designing and facilitating the sessions as they could provide “full clarification” (033P) on the subject matters they wanted to discuss. They wanted these experts to be from varying backgrounds and professions to support a multidisciplinary approach to better serve their diverse needs (e.g., mental health discussions with a psychologist or social worker, physical health discussions with a diabetes healthcare provider). Participants then felt that they could be assured that the information they were receiving was from a reputable and accurate source and that it was the most up-to-date and effective information they could receive.

Additionally, participants also mentioned how they would like the program to offer “check-ins once in a while” (011P) between the formal education and peer support sessions to see how they were processing the information and to address any other questions they may have in a one-on-one setting. Participants wanted to know that they not only had a support network of other parents/caregivers that they could rely on for further support, as mentioned in theme one above, but that the experts or session personnel were also there as more formal sources of support. Participant 034P explained how meaningful this type of support would be to them: “So, the fact that a parent can be independently followed up as well in this support process—Well, I think that’s why we decided to register to this research study.”

Lastly, participants also wanted to ensure that the information that was going to be presented during the educational and peer support sessions was done so in a “basic way that [they] can actually grasp” (020P). This would further prevent participants from becoming overwhelmed during the sessions, where they could “learn how to crawl before [they] walk” (020P). Participant 028P explained this further:

What you gotta do, is you gotta make it fun. Because that will stick. But if you make it dry and academic and scientific, that will appeal to a certain group of science-minded individuals. But for the most part, people will just glaze over.

Participants added that this assurance of their understanding could be facilitated through providing take-home materials that summarize the topics discussed in each session. They thought this could be provided through “short videos or links” (034P), “a short abstract,” (010P) or other “online resources” (001P). Despite some participants wanting some general diabetes topics covered universally, they also wanted to ensure that these upcoming sessions were still tailored enough to meet their own individual needs to allow them to benefit as much as possible.

Discussion

The themes from the current study (i.e., Creating a Community of Learners, Psychological Perspectives of Parenting, Diabetes Management During Adolescence, and Ideal Format and Delivery of the Program) reflect the parental/caregiver reports of readiness, wants, needs, expectations, and wishes prior to participating in a group education and peer support program to enhance their supportive role for adolescents with T1D transitioning to adult care. These themes highlighted the unique perspectives of the parents/caregivers who are an integral part of the transition process alongside their adolescent with T1D. Most qualitative studies on transition readiness from paediatric to adult diabetes care have primarily focused on the viewpoints of adolescents and young adults (AYA) with T1D.39 Although the perspective of the AYA with T1D is still the most crucial to the design of transition readiness interventions, parental support has long been associated with influencing the AYA’s ability to demonstrate increased responsibility for diabetes self-management across the developmental trajectory.40 Despite this, very few parents of AYAs with T1D are included in studies that describe the barriers and facilitators to the transition to adult diabetes care,9 39 41 42 much less be a focus of the transition program interventions themselves,4345 such as in the current study.

In addition to the generated themes and subthemes from the parent/caregiver perspectives in this study, there were some additional insights from the semi-structured interviews, which are noteworthy. For example, some parents/caregivers wanted to know more about the logistics of the specific adult diabetes care team in which their adolescent with T1D would be receiving future care (e.g., knowing the communication processes, on-call logistics, emergent care response expectations, etc.). These expressed needs by the parents/caregivers are important to explore as additional areas for future transition interventions that can be implemented within adult clinic systems who ‘receive’ the young adults with T1D for diabetes care (i.e., receivership models), as this is beyond what the paediatric diabetes clinics alone can do to prepare them for transition to adult care.4649

Parents/caregivers also provided additional advice for future transition intervention endeavours, which were particularly expressed within themes three (Diabetes Management During Adolescence) and four (Ideal Format and Delivery of the Program). Specifically, parents/caregivers highlighted more nuanced recommendations in how a future parent/caregiver education and support group could be conducted, based more on the diabetes management needs of the adolescent with T1D. For example, the current design of the parent/caregiver education and peer support group in the parent study is more of a psychosocial and developmentally oriented transition program, yet some parents/caregivers expressed wanting more of a focus on diabetes knowledge/education and concrete ‘tips and tricks’ for managing T1D rather than more psychosocial support. Therefore, transition programming in both paediatric and adult clinics must be designed in a way that is flexible enough to be tailored to the differing needs of both the adolescents with T1D and their parents/caregivers. For example, the current intervention was semi-structured by design, allowing parents to take the lead on whether they preferred more or less support within each session or between sessions through optional ‘check-ins’. Future transition program could also provide different ‘streams’ or ‘tracks’ for families who may want different types of transition support (e.g., more diabetes management focused vs more psychosocially focused).

Strengths and Implications

The purpose of this study was to determine parents’/caregivers’ pre-intervention expectations (e.g., perceptions of the anticipated content and format, acceptance and usefulness of the intervention, preconceived accessibility and logistical factors), which can help to establish an unbiased and varied account of current wants and needs pertaining to their transition readiness. The semi-structured interview methodology allows for the information to be authentically drawn from the parent/caregiver themselves and include a diverse range of perspectives. It is important to be able to establish this myriad of perspectives prior to the intervention, so actual intervention experiences (e.g., appraisal of the actual content and format, suitability and usefulness of the intervention, realized accessibility and logistical factors) can then be compared with post-intervention interviews, which are currently underway. As such, we will integrate the pre-intervention and post-intervention qualitative interview findings as a next step in our research, allowing us to determine in what ways, if at all, the current pilot intervention should be refined for a future, full-scale RCT.

Above and beyond helping to shape the forthcoming full-scale RCT transition intervention for parents/caregivers, the findings of the present study highlight the importance of attending to the parental/caregiver role to complement interventions for adolescents with T1D during transition to adult care. Specifically, the parents/caregivers highlighted educational information they would like to receive about diabetes management during adolescence to streamline and augment the knowledge they already have, as well as suggestions to make the format and delivery of the program most effective for this population. Even beyond these practical and pragmatic suggestions, parents/caregivers verbalized their strong desire to create a community of learners where reciprocal learning and mutual peer support were central to the intervention. Therefore, the results from this study align with other research that suggests it is crucial for transition programs to incorporate interventions that also address parental/caregivers’ needs,394245 as well as an opportunity to build support networks, so they can further explore how to best support their adolescents with T1D during this process.

Limitations and Future Directions

The current study has limitations for transferability of the findings to other subgroups of parents/caregivers and adolescents with T1D experiencing the transition process, as the current study outcomes only represent the experiences of a small subsample of parents/caregivers at one paediatric diabetes site in Montreal, Canada. Similarly, there were unique features of certain subgroups within this cohort that may not be fully addressed in the current group education and peer support program as it is currently designed. First, most of our participants were highly educated and a part of two-parent families, so single parents or those with lower educational attainment may present with different needs. Second, our sample included some families who recently immigrated to Canada and were managing additional considerations as they were adjusting to a new country (e.g., language barriers, cultural differences and shifts in diabetes-related medical care practices from their home country), in conjunction with the transition from paediatric to adult diabetes care. As well, those parents/caregivers whose adolescents were more recently diagnosed with T1D (i.e., within the past one to two years) often articulated how unprepared they felt for the next stage of adult care as they felt that they were still not fully in command of paediatric diabetes management. Therefore, subgroups such as these may require a different transition intervention approach, which may include additional diabetes knowledge/education and concrete diabetes self-management support beyond what a standard transition program may offer. Although our intervention focuses on the parent within the wider family dynamics, the overarching trial included both parent and adolescent-reported outcomes related to family conflict, diabetes distress, diabetes responsibility, diabetes self-compassion, and resilience.

Another future direction raised by some parents/caregivers was the need for researchers to design and implement other, larger diabetes psychoeducational programs that could include: (1) educating the public more about T1D overall and (2) informing larger social support networks (i.e., post-secondary education settings) about what is involved in helping an adolescent with T1D transition to adulthood, in general. As well, some parents/caregivers expressed a desire to incorporate their adolescent with T1D in parallel, peer-based educational groups about transition to adulthood with T1D so they could also potentially benefit from this intervention together. As such, building future transition programs that incorporate both adolescent and parent/caregiver-based transition intervention modalities may be able to more holistically prepare the adolescent with T1D as they transition to adult care and adulthood. Overall, future transition programs could benefit from incorporating the above suggestions and perspectives from parents/caregivers of adolescents with T1D to refine other parent and caregiver-based transition care interventions.
