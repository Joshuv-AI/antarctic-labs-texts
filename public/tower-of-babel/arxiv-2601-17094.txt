Boltzmann-GPT: Bridging Energy-Based World Models and Language Generation
Junichiro Niimi

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) generate fluent text, yet whether they truly understand the world or merely produce plausible language about it remains contested. We propose an architectural principle, the mouth is not the brain, that explicitly separates world models from language models. Our architecture comprises three components: a Deep Boltzmann Machine (DBM) that captures domain structure as an energy-based world model, an adapter that projects latent belief states into embedding space, and a frozen GPT-2 that provides linguistic competence without domain knowledge. We instantiate this framework in the consumer review domain using Amazon smartphone reviews. Experiments demonstrate that (1) conditioning through the world model yields significantly higher sentiment correlation, lower perplexity, and greater semantic similarity compared to prompt-based generation alone; (2) the DBM’s energy function distinguishes coherent from incoherent market configurations, assigning higher energy to implausible brand-price combinations; and (3) interventions on specific attributes propagate causally to generated text with intervened outputs exhibiting distributions statistically consistent with naturally occurring samples sharing the target configuration. These findings suggest that even small-scale language models can achieve consistent, controllable generation when connected to an appropriate world model, providing empirical support for separating linguistic competence from world understanding.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as remarkably capable language generators. Diverse architectures, including masked language models such as bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)
[
1
]
, autoregressive models such as generative pretrained transformer (GPT)
[
2
,
3
,
4

, and encoder-decoder models such as text-to-text transfer transformer (T5)
[
5
]
, have demonstrated unprecedented fluency across tasks including dialogue, summarization, translation, and code generation. The rapid adoption of such language models, particularly GPT, has extended beyond research into commercial applications
[
6
]
.

However, a fundamental question remains: do these models understand the world, or do they merely generate plausible language about it? Modern LLMs incorporate sophisticated reasoning techniques, such as chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
[
7
]
, self-consistency
[
8
]
, and multi-agent deliberation
[
9
,
10
]
, that extend far beyond simple next-token prediction. Still, the underlying representations emerge from language: they are trained on language and optimized for language.

This perspective has gained further momentum in late 2025, with LeCun describing autoregressive LLMs as ”doomed” due to their inability to model causality
1
1

1

NYU Seminar, September 2025.
, and Hassabis emphasizing that AGI requires world models capable of understanding physical reality
2
2

2

Google DeepMind Podcast, December 2025.
.

The world itself, namely its entities, their relationships, and their constraints, is never directly observed. LLMs learn to talk about the world, but whether they have formed a coherent model of it is far from clear. The mouth speaks fluently; whether the brain comprehends is another matter. This motivates our architectural principle:
the mouth is not the brain
.

This entanglement creates practical difficulties as well. In current LLMs, language generation capabilities and factual knowledge are inseparable. Updating the model’s knowledge thus requires retraining the entire system, incurring substantial computational cost and risking degradation of linguistic competence.

This paper therefore proposes an architectural principle: separate the world model from the language model and assign distinct roles to each. The world model learns the latent structure of a domain: what entities exist, how they relate, and which configurations are coherent, while the language model receives this structured representation and renders it as fluent text. We liken this division to the relationship between cognition and language production in the brain: the world model functions as a belief system that determines what to say, while the language model functions as a linguistic faculty that determines how to say it. The mouth is not the brain—but neither is the language model a passive conduit. It serves as a language area that shapes expression while remaining agnostic to content.

We instantiate this principle in the domain of consumer product reviews, where structured behavioral data (purchases, ratings, brand choices) coexists with rich textual expressions of opinion. We evaluate our approach on Amazon product reviews
[
11
,
12
]
, demonstrating that sentiment interventions produce coherent, appropriately shifted text while preserving other attributes. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes the proposed model, Sections 4–6 present experiments, and Section 7 concludes with implications and limitations.

1.2
Contributions

Our contributions are summarized as follows.

Separating world models from language models.

By delegating content determination to the world model while using GPT-2 as a language generator, we demonstrate that consistent language generation based on world understanding is possible.

Energy-based coherence verification.

We demonstrate that even the world model alone can detect anomalies by assigning high energy to samples that differ from the learned structure. In addition, energy-based world model possesses high generalization performance against the input.

Efficient conditioning for small-scale LLMs.

We show that conditioning through the world model enables even small language models like GPT-2 to generate text with significantly improved sentiment correlation, fluency, and semantic coherence compared to prompting alone.

Interventional Generation.

We show that this architecture supports interventional generation with discriminant validity: manipulating ratings alters sentiment while price and brand interventions do not.

Related Work

2.1
Language Model

The development of language models has progressed rapidly over the past decade. Early neural approaches employed recurrent architectures such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs)
[
13
,
14
]
, long short-term memory (LSTM)
[
15
]
, and gated recurrent units (GRUs)
[
16
]
, which captured sequential dependencies but struggled with long-range context. The introduction of the attention mechanism
[
17
]
and Transformer architecture
[
18
]
marked a turning point, enabling efficient parallel processing through self-attention. Building on this foundation, large-scale autoregressive models have achieved remarkable performance. In particular, LLMs such as GPT demonstrated that unsupervised pretraining on large corpora yields powerful representations, and subsequent scaling efforts have shown consistent improvements with increased parameters and data.

Recent advances have extended LLM capabilities further. For instance, CoT prompting elicits step-by-step reasoning, while reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
[
19
,
20
]
aligns model outputs with human preferences. Multi-agent frameworks
[
21
,
22
]
and tool-augmented systems
[
23
,
24
]
have pushed the boundaries of what LLMs can accomplish.

However, purely text-based learning faces fundamental hurdles. As LeCun famously noted, ”the world is not text.” He has argued that scaling autoregressive models alone cannot yield human-level intelligence
[
25
]
. Similar concerns have been raised about the gap between fluent generation and genuine understanding
[
26
]
. Studies on hallucination further support this critique, identifying it as a structural limitation of LLMs
[
27
]
. The reliance on next-token prediction provides no direct incentive to model causal structure or support counterfactual reasoning. These concerns motivate the exploration of complementary approaches—specifically, world models that represent domain structure independently of language.

2.2
World Model

World models have gained attention as a complementary approach to LLMs. Two major trends can be identified.

The first originates in the context of reinforcement learning. Ha & Schmidhuber
[
28
,
29
]
introduced an RNN-based world model comprising Vision (
V
), Memory (
M
), and Controller (
C
) modules. This architecture learns environmental dynamics by predicting the state at time
t+1
from the current state and action. A key design principle is the asymmetry between components:
C
(867 parameters) is orders of magnitude smaller than
V
(4.3M) or
M
(0.4M), and
C
operates only on
Z
, an abstract representation produced by
V
. This demonstrates that if a world model captures sufficient structure, the downstream controller need not be large.

The second trend, advocated by LeCun, defines a world model as an internal representation capturing the structure of the world
[
25
]
. Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)
[
30
]
instantiates this vision by predicting abstract representations rather than raw sensory inputs, avoiding pixel-level reconstruction while capturing semantically meaningful structure. I-JEPA
[
30
]
and V-JEPA
[
31
]
have demonstrated strong performance with improved sample efficiency. LeCun has also positioned Energy-based Models (EBMs) as a natural framework for world models
[
25
]
, where an energy function assigns low values to plausible configurations and high values to implausible ones.

Our approach draws on both traditions. Like Ha & Schmidhuber, we separate the world model from the output generator and route information through an abstract bottleneck representation. Like LeCun, we adopt an energy-based formulation—specifically, a Deep Boltzmann Machine (DBM)
[
32
]
—that explicitly represents the coherence of configurations. However, our world model differs from both in a key respect: rather than modeling temporal dynamics (state transitions over time), it captures the co-occurrence structure of a domain (which attribute configurations are coherent). This design choice reflects our application to consumer behavior, where the “structure of the world” is better characterized by market regularities than by sequential state evolution.

2.3
Boltzmann Machine

We now provide the technical background on the specific architecture we employ. EBMs define a probability distribution over configurations by assigning low energy to plausible states and high energy to implausible ones
[
33
]
. Among EBMs, Boltzmann Machines (BMs) hold a foundational position as one of the earliest neural network architectures capable of learning complex probability distributions over binary variables
[
34
]
.

Figure 1:
Boltzmann Machine

A Boltzmann Machine consists of visible units representing observed data and hidden units capturing latent structure. The energy of a configuration is defined as:

\displaystyle E(v,h)=-v^{\top}Wh-b^{\top}v-c^{\top}h

(1)

where
W
denotes the weight matrix between visible and hidden units, and
b
,
c
are bias terms. The probability of a visible configuration is given by the Boltzmann distribution:
P(v)\propto\sum_{h}\exp(-E(v,h))
.

Training BMs requires estimating the gradient of the log-likelihood, which involves computing expectations over the model distribution—a computationally intractable problem for general architectures. The Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM)
[
35
,
36
]
addresses this by eliminating intra-layer connections, yielding a bipartite graph between visible and hidden units. This restriction enables efficient inference: given visible units, hidden units are conditionally independent, and vice versa. Contrastive Divergence (CD) provides an efficient approximation to the gradient, making RBM training practical
[
36
]
.

Deep Boltzmann Machines (DBMs)
[
32
]
extend RBMs by stacking multiple hidden layers, allowing the model to capture hierarchical structure in data. Unlike Deep Belief Networks (DBNs)
[
37
]
, which stack RBMs in a directed manner, DBMs maintain undirected connections throughout, enabling top-down and bottom-up inference. Training DBMs typically proceeds in two phases: layer-wise pretraining using RBMs, followed by joint fine-tuning across all layers.

A key property of BMs for our purposes is their unsupervised training objective. The model learns to capture statistical regularities without any task-specific supervision, constructing a stable representation of “how the world is”—what configurations are coherent, what entities tend to co-occur, and what patterns are plausible. This aligns naturally with the notion of a world model.

We use the term “belief” in two senses: the technical sense from Boltzmann Machines (the posterior over hidden units) and the cognitive sense (propositional attitudes that shape behavior). In our architecture, the DBM’s hidden layers encode latent states that capture the structure of consumer behavior, serving as a bottleneck representation that determines what the language model will say.

DBMs have previously been applied to multimodal learning
[
38
]
, jointly representing text and images for tasks such as tag classification
[
39
]
. However, this prior work does not address text generation or connection to external language models. Our contribution lies in using the DBM as an explicit, inspectable world model that conditions a frozen LLM through learned soft prompts.

2.4
Combination of LLMs and World Models

Several studies have explored combinations of LLMs and world models. A recent survey
[
40
]
identifies social simulacra and urban design systems
[
22
,
41
,
42
]
as instances of this combination. These systems utilize LLMs as agents, observing micro-level individual actions and macro-level interactions within simulated environments. However, in such systems the world model is implicit—it emerges within the LLM through memory streams and reflection mechanisms, with the LLM serving simultaneously as both world model and output generator.

A related line of work incorporates world model concepts into LLM training. LLM-JEPA
[
43
]
introduces a JEPA-style auxiliary loss for LLM finetuning, treating pairs of related inputs (e.g., natural language and corresponding code) as different views of the same underlying knowledge. This approach improves downstream performance while maintaining generative capabilities, but treats the world model as an implicit regularizer within the LLM architecture rather than as a separate component.

In contrast, our architecture employs an explicit, separately trained world model that conditions a frozen LLM through learned adapters, maintaining clear separation between domain understanding and language generation.

2.5
Consumer Review Generation

This study applies the proposed architecture to consumer review generation. Text generation for product reviews has attracted attention as a means to provide explanations in recommender systems and to augment training data. Early approaches employed recurrent neural networks with attention mechanisms to generate reviews conditioned on user and item representations
[
44
,
45
]
. More recent work has utilized LLMs to construct prompts from user behavioral history—past reviews, item titles, and ratings—and finetunes models to generate personalized reviews reflecting individual writing styles and sentiment tendencies
[
46
]
.

A fundamental challenge underlying such personalization efforts is consumer heterogeneity: consumers differ systematically in preferences, needs, and decision-making processes
[
47
,
48
]
. These differences span not only observable demographics but also latent dimensions such as price sensitivity, brand loyalty, and lifestyle preferences
[
49
]
. A recent survey on persona in LLMs
[
50
]
distinguishes role-playing (assigning personas to LLMs) from personalization (adapting LLMs to user personas), yet both paradigms struggle with abstract dimensions—cultural background, lifestyle, and idiosyncratic preferences—that are difficult to articulate explicitly
[
51
]
. Moreover, LLM-generated personas exhibit systematic biases that fail to reflect real population distributions
[
52
]
.

These difficulties motivate our approach: learning latent representations of consumer heterogeneity directly from behavioral data through an energy-based world model, rather than relying on explicit textual descriptions of personas.

Proposed Model

Our proposed model consists of three components (Fig.
2
): a world model that captures domain structure, an adapter that projects latent representations into language space, and a language model that generates text. We first conduct DBM’s layer-wise pretraining and joint fine-tuning, followed by training of the adapter. The language model is frozen through training and inference.

3.1
World Model: Deep Boltzmann Machine

3.1.1
Architecture

The world model is implemented as a DBM with one visible layer and
L
hidden layers. As in this study, consumer behavior, where the field dealing with discrete events, the co-occurrence structure between attributes (i.e., the occurrence relationships among brand preferences, price ranges, and evaluations by perspective) constitutes a more fundamental “structure of the world” than the temporal transitions observed in prior research.

The visible layer has
J
units, representing consumer behavior as a one-hot encoded binary vector (e.g., brand name, price tier, user rating, and other contextual signals such as repeated purchases). The
l
-th hidden layer contains
H_{l}
units for
l=1,\ldots,L
. For notational convenience, we define
h^{(0)}\equiv v
and
H_{0}\equiv J
. The energy function of the DBM is defined as:

\displaystyle E(v,h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(L)})=

(2)

\displaystyle-\sum_{l=1}^{L}h^{(l-1)\top}W^{(l)}h^{(l)}-b^{\top}v-\sum_{l=1}^{L}c^{(l)\top}h^{(l)}

(3)

where
W^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{l-1}\times H_{l}}
is the weight matrix connecting layer
l-1
to layer
l
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
,
c^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{l}}
are bias terms for the visible and
l
-th hidden layer respectively. The probability distribution over visible configurations is given by marginalizing over all hidden states:

\displaystyle P(v)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(L)}}\exp(-E(v,h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(L)}))

(4)

The bipartite structure—no intra-layer connections—ensures that hidden units within each layer are conditionally independent given the adjacent layers, enabling efficient inference via mean-field approximation.

3.1.2
Layer-wise Pretraining

Training a DBM directly is challenging due to the intractability of the partition function. Following Salakhutdinov & Hinton (2009), we employ a greedy layer-wise pretraining strategy using RBMs.

For each layer
l=1,\ldots,L
, we train an RBM treating
h^{(l-1)}
(or
v
when
l=1
) as visible units and
h^{(l)}
as hidden units. Contrastive Divergence (CD) is used to approximate the gradient of the log-likelihood. After training each RBM, we compute the posterior mean activations
\mathbb{E}[h^{(l)}|h^{(l-1)}]
over the training data, which serve as input for training the next layer.

After pretraining all
L
layers, the weights are used to initialize the full DBM. To account for the difference in input statistics between RBMs (single-directional input) and DBMs (bidirectional input), the weights of intermediate layers are scaled by a factor of 2, following the standard correction procedure.

3.1.3
Joint Fine-tuning

After pretraining, we fine-tune the entire DBM jointly to maximize the log-likelihood of the training data. Inference of the posterior
P(h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(L)}|v)
is performed using mean-field variational approximation, iterating until convergence. Let
\mu^{(l)}
denote the mean-field parameter for layer
l
. The update equations are:

\displaystyle\mu^{(l)}\leftarrow\sigma\left(W^{(l)\top}\mu^{(l-1)}+W^{(l+1)}\mu^{(l+1)}+c^{(l)}\right)

(5)

for
l=1,\ldots,L
, where we define
\mu^{(0)}\equiv v
and
W^{(L+1)}\mu^{(L+1)}\equiv 0
(i.e., the top layer receives no top-down input). The function
\sigma
denotes the element-wise sigmoid.

The gradient of the log-likelihood involves a positive phase (clamping the visible units to data) and a negative phase (sampling from the model distribution). We approximate the negative phase using Persistent Contrastive Divergence (PCD), maintaining a set of persistent Markov chains across gradient updates.

The trained DBM serves as an unsupervised world model: it learns the joint distribution over consumer behavior patterns without any task-specific supervision. The hidden layer activations
[\mu^{(1)};\mu^{(2)};\ldots;\mu^{(L)}]
—concatenated into a single vector of dimension
\sum_{l=1}^{L}H_{l}
—constitute the latent belief representation passed to the adapter.

3.2
Language Model: Frozen GPT-2

We employ GPT-2 as the language model, with all parameters frozen throughout training and inference. The role of GPT-2 in our architecture is analogous to that of a language faculty: it does not determine the content of generation but provides the grammatical and stylistic competence necessary to articulate beliefs as fluent text.

This competence derives entirely from GPT-2’s pretraining on large-scale corpora. The model has internalized the statistical regularities of natural language—syntax, word co-occurrence patterns, discourse structure—and can deploy this knowledge given appropriate conditioning. In our framework, this conditioning comes not from a textual prompt describing the desired content, but from soft prompt embeddings projected from the world model’s latent state.

By keeping GPT-2 frozen, we enforce a strict separation: the world model learns domain structure, while the language model contributes only linguistic competence acquired during pretraining. The language model never observes the raw tabular features; it receives only the adapter’s output. This design ensures that any content-level variation in generated text must originate from the world model, not from the language model’s internal representations.

3.3
Adapter: Projecting Beliefs to Language Space

The adapter bridges the world model and the language model by projecting latent belief states into the embedding space of GPT-2. Specifically, we concatenate the mean-field activations from all hidden layers of the DBM,
[\mu^{(1)};\ldots;\mu^{(L)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}H_{l}}
, and pass this vector through a MLP.

The MLP outputs a sequence of
K
soft prompt embeddings, each of dimension
D
matching GPT-2’s embedding size. Formally:

\displaystyle S=\text{MLP}([\mu^{(1)};\ldots;\mu^{(L)}])\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}

(6)

These soft prompts are prepended to the text prompt embeddings (e.g., ”Write a product review:”) and fed to GPT-2 as conditioning context. The soft prompts carry the semantic content derived from the world model, while the text prompt specifies the output format and task framing.

During training, only the adapter parameters are updated since the DBM is finetuned and GPT-2 remains frozen. The training objective is to minimize the cross-entropy loss (CE Loss)
[
53
]
between the generated text and the ground-truth review, given the corresponding consumer profile. This setup follows the soft prompt tuning paradigm
[
54
]
, but differs in that our soft prompts are derived from an external world model rather than learned end-to-end from text alone. CE Loss is optimized using AdaMax
[
55
]
.

Inference

We now describe the inference procedure from a given consumer profile to generated review text.

Input encoding.

A consumer profile is represented as a binary visible vector

v
is 160.

Belief Inference.

Given
v

\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(L)}
are initialized and iteratively updated until convergence:

\displaystyle\mu^{(l)}\leftarrow\sigma\left(W^{(l)\top}\mu^{(l-1)}+W^{(l+1)}\mu^{(l+1)}+c^{(l)}\right)

for
l=1,\ldots,L

\mu^{(0)}\equiv v
and
W^{(L+1)}\mu^{(L+1)}\equiv 0

The converged values

3.5
Generation Examples

To validate the text generation of the proposed model, we establish two baselines. In Baseline 1, GPT-2 receives only the text prompt (Fig.
3
) containing the instruction, one-shot example, and target product attributes (product name, price, rating). This baseline represents the standard prompting approach where the language model must generate appropriate content based solely on textual instructions without any learned representation of consumer behavior.

In Baseline 2, GPT-2 receives an extended text prompt that includes detailed behavioral context that would otherwise be encoded by the DBM. Specifically, we augment the prompt with rich descriptions of the consumer profile: purchase history patterns, brand preferences, price sensitivity indicators, topic interests extracted from past reviews, and other contextual signals.

Finally, in the proposed model, as full architecture, the DBM encodes the visible vector
v
into latent belief states through mean-field inference, the MLP adapter projects these beliefs into
K
soft prompt embeddings, and the frozen GPT-2 generates text conditioned on both soft prompts and the text prompt. This design compresses rich behavioral information into a fixed-dimensional representation that fits within the language model’s context window.

The results of generating text using three models based on the same sample are shown in Table
1
.
First, in Baseline 1, the rating was correctly reflected as input information, but the generation ended without any elaboration.
Next, Baseline 2, which includes detailed purchase information within the prompt, failed in a different manner. While this approach provides comprehensive information, the resulting prompt length far exceeds GPT-2’s effective context window, leading to degraded attention over distant tokens. As shown in Table

Finally, the proposed model produced content that included descriptions of the used item’s condition, such as the battery and screen, alongside high-rating content.

These results confirm that the proposed model’s output coherently reflects internal representations derived from customer evaluations and preferences (e.g., rating, price, topics). Conversely, the baselines exhibit missing information and unintended content. This demonstrates that even small-scale language models can control their output when connected to an appropriate world model.

4
Experiment I: Generation Quality

4.1
Motivation

Section 3 introduced the structure of the proposed model and demonstrated that the proposed model incorporated more information from reviews than GPT-2 alone. However, quantitative evaluations of the proposed model, such as the fluency of the generated text and its relationship to the input, have not yet been conducted. Thus, we propose following hypothesis:

H1: Conditioning through the world model produces text more consistent with ground-truth reviews than prompt-based conditioning alone.

4.2
Method

This experiment generates review texts using both the proposed model and the baseline (GPT-2 alone) on 500 randomly selected samples, and quantitatively evaluate the results using multiple metrics. Note that Baseline 2 (verbose context) is excluded from this comparison as it failed to produce coherent output due to context length limitations (see Section 3.5).

Sentiment correlation.

To examine the relationship between the generated and actual reviews, we use correlation coefficients between the sentiment. To calculate sentiment, we use Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER)
[
58
]
, a widely-adopted rule-based polarity analyzer
[
59
,
60
]
.

Perplexity.

For fluency, we use perplexity of the generated texts. Lower perplexity indicates that the generated text conforms to natural language statistics, reflecting grammatical coherence and naturalness.

Cosine similarity.

For the semantic similarity, we use cosine similarity between the generated and actual review texts. Higher similarity indicates that the generated content captures the semantic essence of what a real consumer would write given the same behavioral profile.

.001), indicating more natural language generation. Cosine similarity with ground-truth reviews showed a modest but significant improvement (0.49 vs 0.40, p
<
.001). Importantly, for synthetic data generation, excessively high similarity would indicate memorization rather than generalization. The moderate similarity observed here suggests that the model generates novel text while preserving the semantic characteristics associated with the input conditions.

These results support H1: conditioning through the world model produces text more consistent with
ground-truth reviews.

5
Experiment II: World Model Coherence

5.1
Motivation

Experiment I evaluated the integrated model, but did not reveal whether the world model itself has learned meaningful structure. Two questions arise: first, does the DBM generalize beyond training data, or does it merely memorize sample-specific patterns? Second, if the DBM has captured co-occurrence structure (e.g., brand–price relationships), does it assign higher energy to incoherent configurations?

In EBMs such as DBMs, the energy function assigns low energy to probable configurations and high energy to improbable ones. This property enables both generalization testing (comparing energy changes across train/test splits) and coherence validation (examining energy under counterfactual interventions). We thus propose:

H2: Energy changes under intervention are consistent between training and held-out test samples, indicating that the DBM captures generalizable structure rather than memorizing individual patterns.

H3: The DBM assigns higher energy to input configurations that violate learned market structure than to plausible configurations.

5.2
Method

Given a visible configuration
v
, we compute the free energy by marginalizing over all hidden states:

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(v)=-\log\sum_{h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(L)}}\exp(-E(v,h^{(1)},\ldots,h^{(L)}))\end{split}

(8)

For computational tractability, we approximate this using the variational free energy based on the mean-field approximation.

\begin{split}\tilde{F}(v)=&-\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mu^{(l-1)\top}W^{(l)}\mu^{(l)}-b^{\top}v\\
&-\sum_{l=1}^{L}c^{(l)\top}\mu^{(l)}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}H(\mu^{(l)})\end{split}

(9)

where
\mu^{(0)}\equiv v
and
H(\mu^{(l)})
are entropy of variational distributions in each layer.

5.3
Evaluation.

To test whether the DBM has learned meaningful market structure, we measure the change in free energy when input configurations are intervened to create implausible combinations. Specifically, we focus on brand–price relationships: premium brands such as Apple rarely appear in the lowest price tier, while other brands span a wider price range.

Brand Categories.

We compare Apple against “Others,” where Others refers to products not belonging to any of the 13 major brands encoded in the visible layer (Apple, Samsung, Google, Motorola, OnePlus, Sony, LG, Nokia, Oppo, Xiaomi, Huawei, HTC, and BlackBerry). These minor brands exhibit more heterogeneous pricing strategies and are less strongly associated with specific price tiers.

Intervention Design.

We select samples from three price tiers (Mid, High, Premium) and intervene by changing the price attribute to the Entry (lowest) tier while holding all other attributes constant. This intervention is performed separately for Apple-branded products and for Others, yielding six intervention conditions.

Energy Change Measurement.

For each sample, we compute the variational free energy
\tilde{F}(v)
before and after intervention. We report the mean percentage change in free energy:

\displaystyle\Delta F=\frac{\tilde{F}(v_{\text{intervened}})-\tilde{F}(v_{\text{original}})}{\tilde{F}(v_{\text{original}})}\times 100

(10)

A positive
\Delta F
indicates that the intervened configuration is less probable under the learned distribution, suggesting that the DBM recognizes it as inconsistent with observed market structure.

Statistical Testing.

We test whether the mean energy change differs significantly from zero using a paired
t
-test across samples within each intervention condition. This allows us to determine whether the observed energy increases reflect systematic patterns learned by the DBM rather than random fluctuations.

6
Experiment III: Causal Specificity of Intervention

6.1
Motivation

Experiments I and II establish that the proposed model generates quality text
and that the DBM encodes market structure. However, a critical question remains:
do interventions on the world model propagate to generated text in a
causally specific manner? That is, manipulating a variable should affect
semantically related aspects of the output while leaving unrelated aspects
unchanged. We thus propose:

H4: Intervention on a specific input variable (e.g., rating) alters the corresponding semantic dimension of generated text, while intervention on causally unrelated variables (e.g., price, brand) does not.

Even if this hypothesis H4 is supported, the serious concerns remain. Did the model merely lower sentiment through intervention, or did it create reviews to align with the evaluation methods of existing low-rated samples? Therefore, we derive another hypothesis as follows:

H5: Intervened outputs are distributionally consistent with naturally occurring samples that share the target attribute configuration.

6.2
Method

To demonstrate that intervened input vectors for DBM correctly affect the generated text, we create the following experimental design. Changing the Rating within the input vector should directly impact the sentiment level of the output text, whereas price range and brand do not necessarily have a clear correlation with sentiment.

Therefore, we randomly select 500 samples each from existing data: i.) samples with the highest Rating, ii.) with the highest Price, and iii.) samples where the Brand is Apple. For these samples, we measure the average change in sentiment before and after altering Rating and Price from highest to lowest, and changing Brand from Apple to Samsung.

The sentiment before and after the intervention is evaluated with VADER. Finally, the statistical significance of the difference is tested with paired
t
-test.

It is clear that lower prices do not necessarily lead to lower evaluations; in some cases, high cost performance compared to popular brands can result in higher ratings. Similarly, the fact that switching from Apple to Samsung does not affect the sentiment indicates that no bias toward specific brands (e.g., Apple always scoring high) has been introduced into the model. These results suggest that the model accurately capture such consumer behaviors. Thus, H4 is supported.

7
Conclusion

7.1
Key Findings

Key findings of this study is summarized as follows:

World model conditioning enhances generation quality.

In Experiment I, the proposed model demonstrated significant improvements over GPT-2 alone in all metrics: sentiment correlation (
r=0.383
and
-0.037
), perplexity (
11.977
and
20.897
), and cosine similarity (
0.491
and
0.402
) through conditioning via DBM. This demonstrates that even small-scale LLMs can achieve consistent generation when connected to an appropriate world model. This provides empirical evidence demonstrating that “the form/meaning separation”
[
26
]
is implementable at the architectural level.

DBM robustly captures semantic structure

Experiment II demonstrated that DBM learned the co-occurrence structure between brand and price relationship. Similar results was obtained between train and test sets, indicating strong robustness of the EBM. By the intervention of the price range to entry price, free energy significantly increased for Apple brand while decreased for minor brands.

Discriminant validity is confirmed for interventions

Interventions to factor which is expected to affect sentiment scores (i.e., ratings) resulted in significant fluctuations of sentiment, whereas no significant changes were observed for other factors (i.e., price, brand). This demonstrates discriminant validity, proving that the world model satisfies the causal independence among attributes.

Counterfactual outputs are distributionally valid

Experiment 3 demonstrated that the polarity distributions between samples subjected to low-rating intervention and naturally occurring low-rating samples were statistically consistent. This indicates that the model does not simply invert sentiment but reproduces the complex emotional structure observed in human negative reviews.

7.2
Implications

This study has both theoretical and practical implications.

7.2.1
Theoretical Implications

First, LLMs have been criticized for their black-box nature, particularly the absence of mechanisms to assess whether an input configuration is coherent with respect to learned domain structure. In our architecture, the energy function of the world model externalizes this coherence as a scalar value, enabling quantification of the model’s confidence in the plausibility of a given configuration. This interpretability arises precisely because language generation is separated from world understanding—a core principle of our approach.

Second, our experiments demonstrate that the DBM-based world model enables independent manipulation of each input attribute. Consequently, once domain structure has been sufficiently learned, generation conditioned on novel attribute combinations becomes possible without retraining.

Third, the fact that sufficient linguistic quality was achieved with GPT-2, which is relatively small by contemporary standards, suggests that the language generation capacity of LLMs has already reached a saturation regime, while world understanding remains the bottleneck. This finding aligns with LeCun’s claim that autoregressive LLMs are “doomed,” and contributes empirical evidence to the ongoing debate on the limits of scaling laws.

Fourth, by separating the language model from the world model, the proposed architecture maintains modularity. Since each component exists independently, replacing either the language model (e.g., GPT-2 to Llama) or the world model (e.g., adding hidden layers to DBM) requires only retraining the Adapter, leaving other components intact.

An unexpected benefit of the separation principle may be implicit regularization. Because the DBM compresses inputs into belief states that capture structural relationships rather than sample-specific details, the adapter learns to map abstract representations to soft prompts rather than memorizing input-output pairs directly. This hypothesis is consistent with our observation that training and test metrics remain similar, though a formal ablation comparing between DBM-mediated model and direct feature projection would be needed to confirm this effect.

Finally, although this study focused on text generation by connecting the world model to an LLM, the applicability of world model-based generation extends beyond language. A single trained DBM can, in principle, be connected to different output modalities, such as classification heads, image generators, or text decoders, via modality-specific adapters. This flexibility is precisely what the separation principle affords: the “brain” captures domain structure once, and different output systems can express it in their respective forms.

7.2.2
Practical Implications

In this study, even DBM that merely captures co-occurrence structure is able to generate sentences close to the original distribution when generating reviews under counterfactual conditions. This implies that the proposed model is applicable not only for data augmentations, but also in diverse scenarios such as data anonymization and sample size inflation. This enables quality assurance for synthetic data, as the DBM’s energy function allows for pre-filtering of abnormal combinations that should not be generated.

Text generation leveraging domain-specific knowledge has traditionally required combining frontier LLMs with RAG, involving not only large-scale models such as GPT-5 but also cloud-based retrieval infrastructure. Beyond construction and maintenance costs, this approach raises concerns including data privacy risks inherent in cloud usage. Our findings demonstrate that, when the world model has sufficiently captured domain structure, even a modest language model such as GPT-2 can generate high-quality text. This enables lightweight architectures suitable for low-cost, on-premises deployment, making domain-specific generation accessible without reliance on external APIs or cloud services.

More broadly, our architecture provides a general framework for bridging tabular behavioral data and unstructured outputs. For marketing practitioners, this means that a single world model trained on purchase histories could drive multiple downstream applications, such as generating personalized review text, creating targeted advertising imagery, and segmenting customers, while maintaining consistency across all outputs.

7.3
Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, in this study, to verify that replacing the model clearly improves language capabilities, we used GPT-2 as the language generator. However, more modern and large-scale LLMs, such as GPT-5 and Gemini 3, might include such world inside the model. Yet, whether frontier LLMs have internalized world models remains an open question. Our architecture sidesteps this epistemological impasse by making the world model explicit and inspectable.

Second, for the world model, this study employed DBM. However, multiple options are available for a world model, such as variational autoencoder (VAE)
[
61
]
and Modern Hopfield Networks
[
62
]
. In particular, since VAE may enable more stable training, it is necessary to continue refining the model architectures.

Third, many world models aim to capture “the temporal structure,” the sequential interaction between the model’s behavior and the environment. However, since our model focused on market structure in the form of consumer behavior, it only captured the co-occurrence structure between attributes. Therefore, future work must expand this into a true world model by utilizing time-series data.

Fourth, our experiments are limited to a single domain (smartphone reviews on Amazon). Whether the proposed architecture generalizes to other product categories, languages, or entirely different domains such as medical records or financial reports remains to be validated.
