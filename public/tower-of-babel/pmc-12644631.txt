Multi-frame fusion enhances analytical and diagnostic efficiency in corneal confocal microscopy
Authors: Ying Zou, Juan Cao, Jiamu Chen, Li Chen, Qincheng Qiao, Xinguo Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To propose a low-cost and effective image enhancement strategy based on multi-frame fusion for corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) that improves image quality without requiring additional hardware or changes to clinical workflows. The method involves aligning and integrating consecutive frames of the same region. Its performance was systematically evaluated across image alignment accuracy, noise reduction, morphological nerve feature extraction, and disease classification. Quantitative experiments showed that the proposed approach significantly enhances structural clarity and measurement reliability. Key parameters such as corneal nerve fiber length (CNFL), corneal nerve fiber density (CNFD), and corneal nerve branch density (CNBD) showed substantial improvements, especially in diabetic patients. Enhanced images consistently improved both traditional metrics-based discrimination and deep learning classification models across multiple architectures, demonstrating the method’s generalizability and clinical value. The proposed multi-frame fusion strategy effectively enhances CCM images with minimal additional acquisition time and without burdening patients or operators, making it highly suitable for real-world clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25458-7.

Introduction

Corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) is a clinical technique enabling in vivo, real-time, and noninvasive visualization of corneal nerve fibers, demonstrating significant potential in diagnosing various diseases1–3. By processing and analyzing CCM images, researchers can extract clinically relevant morphological features that serve as potential biomarkers for early disease diagnosis and prognosis monitoring4–6. However, CCM remains primarily confined to research settings and has yet to be widely adopted in routine clinical practice, highlighting the need for further studies to standardize and optimize its clinical utility7,8.

During CCM examinations, subjects undergo image acquisition, followed by a series of digital image processing steps to convert raw images into quantitative parameters reflecting corneal microstructure. 9When imaging equipment captures images, it inevitably introduces random noise onto the images. This noise usually originates from the random fluctuations of the sensor, interference during the transmission process, or environmental factors10,11. Such degradation is nearly inevitable and directly impacts downstream tasks—including image analysis, parameter extraction, and disease classification12. Thus, effective preprocessing to enhance image quality is critical for ensuring the accuracy of subsequent analyses12,13.

Multi-frame fusion is an image enhancement technique that integrates information from multiple consecutive frames to generate higher-quality images with reduced noise14. Its core premise is that each frame represents a superposition of the true signal and independent noise15. During CCM acquisition, multiple overlapping images are typically captured at the same location. By leveraging inter-frame redundancy, aligning structural features, and performing pixel-level fusion, this method effectively suppresses noise and improves overall clarity15. Specifically, it achieves this by precisely aligning identical structures across frames, ensuring spatial coherence of corresponding pixels, and synthesizing them into an optimized composite image.

This study proposes a dedicated multi-frame fusion pipeline for CCM images and systematically evaluates its efficacy across multiple downstream analytical and diagnostic tasks. Specifically, we first benchmark the performance of mainstream image registration algorithms on CCM data to identify the optimal alignment approach for fusion workflow construction. Subsequently, we conduct tripartite comparative analyses between original and fused images, evaluating: (1) noise levels and image quality metrics, (2) corneal nerve morphometric parameter extraction, and (3) disease classification performance. These validations aim to demonstrate the method’s practical utility in both research and clinical settings. Ultimately, we present an easily implementable yet potent image enhancement strategy to improve CCM image quality and interpretability, facilitating its clinical translation.

Methods

Participants

This study incorporated data from three independent clinical research centers: (1)Center 1 (Jinan): 121 healthy volunteers and 111 diabetic patients. (2)Center 2 (Shanghai): 100 healthy volunteers and 152 diabetic patients. (3)Center 3 (Qingdao): 48 healthy volunteers and 186 diabetic patients. In total, 718 participants were enrolled, comprising 269 healthy volunteers and 449 diabetic patients. Diabetes diagnosis followed the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) criteria16. All participants were aged 18–80 years and provided written informed consent. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

All participants underwent bilateral corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) examinations using the Heidelberg Retina Tomograph III (HRT-III) system (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Germany) equipped with the Rostock Corneal Module. The device was configured with a 670 nm helium-neon laser source, providing 1 μm horizontal and vertical resolution at 800× magnification, with a 400 μm × 400 μm field of view. All images were acquired and stored through a fully digital imaging system. During the examination procedure, carbomer eye gel (Berlin, Germany) was applied as a coupling agent between the microscope lens and corneal cap. Prior to imaging, topical anesthesia was administered using 0.4% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops. Participants were instructed to maintain stable head positioning while fixating on a central target. The objective lens was carefully advanced until contacting the central cornea, followed by precise focal adjustments to optimize visualization of the subbasal nerve plexus layer. Multiple high-quality images were acquired and stored for each participant to ensure adequate sampling for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Each participant had multiple images collected and stored, with a mean of 440.58 and a standard deviation of 195.11. The initially acquired images varied greatly in quality and required selection. The criteria for selecting high-quality images were defined as follows:

(1) High contrast between corneal nerves and the background: Corneal nerves should exhibit a clear gray-scale contrast relative to the surrounding basal epithelial tissue. (2) Continuous nerve fiber morphology: The image should contain uninterrupted nerve fibers and no breaks caused by imaging artifacts, motion blur, or other non-pathological distortions. (3) Comprehensive coverage of the sub-basal nerve plexus (SBP): The image should focus on the SBP layer and be free from interference from other corneal layers such as the epithelium or stroma.

High-quality images with two or more substantially overlapping fields were selected for subsequent parameter quantification and diagnostic experiments.

This study obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Approval No.: KYLL-2017–231) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Image alignment

In the multi-frame fusion process, the CCM images requiring alignment are typically acquired consecutively within a short time interval, exhibiting only minor in-plane motion. The corneal nerve structures in these images possess distinct and stable features, making them well-suited for global alignment strategies to achieve optimal registration performance.

Global alignment aims to identify key point correspondences between images and estimate the optimal geometric transformation model, with homography being the most commonly used approach17. This transformation maps points from one plane to another and is represented by a 3 × 3 homogeneous matrix, requiring at least four corresponding point pairs for unique determination18. Once the homography matrix is estimated, perspective transformation and registration of the source image to the reference image can be performed18. Thus, the CCM image alignment problem can be reduced to the precise estimation of the homography matrix.

To systematically evaluate the performance of different estimation algorithms for CCM image registration, we designed a comprehensive benchmarking experiment using the CORN-1 public dataset19, which contains 1,516 CCM images of varying imaging positions and quality levels. Synthetic image pairs were generated by applying random perspective transformations to each original image, simulating the slight geometric variations encountered during real-world acquisitions. The specific procedure consists of two stages, described as follows:

First, at the geometric level, random perspective transformations were introduced. Three representative algorithm classes were benchmarked:

1. Feature-based methods.

Feature-based methods rely on matching distinctive image features to solve for the homography matrix. Traditional approaches typically involve separate feature extraction using detectors like FAST20or Harris21 followed by descriptor generation with methods such as BRIEF22. Integrated approaches like ORB23and SIFT24 combine both steps in a unified framework. The field has seen significant advances through deep learning-based methods including SuperPoint25, DISK26, DoGHardNet27, and ALIKED28, which demonstrate improved robustness for complex structures and low-quality images. Feature matching was performed using either brute-force matching(BF-Match)29, Fast Library for Approximate Nearest Neighbors (FLANN)30, or the deep learning-based LightGlue31, with RANSAC employed to filter outlier matches before final homography estimation.

2. Correlation-based methods.

Correlation-based methods were represented by the Enhanced Correlation Coefficient (ECC) algorithm32,33, which directly optimizes transformation parameters by maximizing inter-image correlation.

3. Optical flow-based methods.

Optical flow-based methods estimate pixel motion trajectories for alignment, with sparse methods like Lucas-Kanade34 and dense approaches including Farneback35 and TV-L136 representing traditional techniques. Recent deep learning architectures such as PWC-Net37 and RAFT38 have achieved state-of-the-art performance in global optical flow estimation. For fair comparison with other approaches, sparse points derived from flow fields were paired and processed through RANSAC for homography estimation.

Alignment accuracy was quantified using four metrics: (1) Mean Absolute Error (MAE) (2) Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) (3) Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) (4) Structural Similarity Index (SSIM). MAE and RMSE measure pixel-level registration errors, PSNR reflects overall image fidelity, and SSIM aligns with human perception of structural consistency.

In addition, for feature-based methods, we also calculated the reprojection error. Specifically, the CORN-1 dataset provides centerline-annotated images corresponding to each original CCM image. During the random perspective transformation and subsequent alignment of the original images, the homography matrices were recorded and applied to the corresponding annotated images as well. After undergoing two transformations, the centerline-annotated images were re-binarized using Otsu’s method39.

The reprojection error between skeletons was quantified using the following metrics: (1) Chamfer distance: the average of the mean squared distances from skeleton A to skeleton B and vice versa, sensitive to global shape differences.(2) Hausdorff distance: the maximum mismatch distance between two point sets, reflecting the farthest point discrepancy and highly sensitive to noise and outliers.(3) IoU (Intersection over Union): the ratio of the intersection size to the union size of two skeleton sets. In skeleton evaluation, this equals the number of overlapping skeleton pixels divided by the total number of combined pixels. IoU ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect overlap.(4) Dice coefficient: a similarity measure equal to twice the number of intersecting pixels divided by the sum of the pixels in both skeletons. The Dice coefficient also ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect overlap.

The evaluation metrics were computed only within the valid region of the aligned images. The top-performing algorithm was selected for multi-frame fusion based on quantitative results.

Image fusion

Following image alignment, a mean fusion strategy was applied to integrate the multi-frame CCM images. As a canonical pixel-level fusion method, mean fusion operates by averaging pixel intensities across temporally acquired images at corresponding spatial locations, thereby generating a composite image with enhanced signal-to-noise characteristics40. Although residual motion may exist, the structural scale of the target CCM features is much larger than such potential misalignments, substantially reducing the risk of blurring in the fused image.

Critically, mean fusion avoids the introduction of fusion-induced artifacts that may arise from nonlinear processing or adaptive weighting schemes41. This preservation of anatomical fidelity is particularly vital for medical imaging applications where structural integrity directly impacts diagnostic interpretation42. The method’s inherent linearity ensures unbiased representation of tissue morphology without introducing spurious features that could confound subsequent quantitative analysis.

Validation and experiments

To comprehensively evaluate the benefits of multi-frame fusion in image quality enhancement and downstream analytical tasks, we conducted a three-tiered experimental validation using clinically acquired CCM images:

1. Image quality and noise level assessment.

Image sequences with ≥ 6 overlapping frames were selected from all participants to ensure adequate fusion conditions. Registered images were fused incrementally (1 to 6 frames) to generate enhanced versions (Fig. 2).

Noise characteristics were objectively measured using an advanced unsupervised estimation method grounded in wavelet theory. Building upon Donoho’s noise estimation framework43,44, we implemented a multi-basis approach that computes the median absolute deviation of high-frequency wavelet coefficients across six distinct wavelet families (Haar, Daubechies-2/4, Symlets-4, and Coiflets-1).45

2. Corneal nerve morphometric analysis.

To further analyze the performance of the fused images in corneal nerve feature extraction, high-quality image sets with at least two overlapping fields of view were screened from the dataset, and corneal nerve analyses were performed on the pre- and post-fusion images, respectively. Two automated analysis tools, ACCMetrics9 and AiCCMetrics46, were used to extract key neuromorphological metrics, including corneal nerve fiber total length density (CNFL), fiber number density (CNFD) and nerve branching density (CNBD). The morphological parameters before and after fusion were compared and analysed to assess the effect of image enhancement on the accuracy of neural structure identification and quantitative assessment.

3. Disease classification performance.

The ultimate clinical utility was evaluated through diagnostic classification experiments comparing healthy volunteers and diabetic patients. Two complementary approaches were implemented:

First, Utilizing the extracted morphometric features (CNFL, CNFD, CNBD) to build logistic regression model classifiers, with performance quantified through receiver operating characteristic(ROC) analysis and area under the curve(AUC) calculations.

Second, we conducted extensive deep learning experiments with five representative convolutional neural network architectures (ResNet18, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet121, VGG16, ResNeXt50), all initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights. The training protocol incorporated several best practices: AdamW optimization with differential learning rates (1e-4 for backbone, 1e-3 for classifier), cosine annealing scheduling with minimum learning rate of 1e-6, early stopping based on validation loss, and balanced data augmentation (random flips and scaled crops during training, center crops for validation). Crucially, all experiments maintained strict reproducibility through fixed random seeds (42) and deterministic algorithms, with performance evaluated via five-fold cross-validation reporting mean and standard deviation of accuracy, F1-score, and AUC across folds.

Evaluation of the morphological analysis results of corneal nerves

A total of 479 CCM image sequence sets with sufficient overlapping regions were included, comprising 322 images from healthy participants and 157 from diabetic patients. The mean fusion frame rate was 2.84, with a standard deviation of 1.11 and a range of 2 to 7. For each image sequence set, the mean displacement amplitude in the horizontal direction was 67.87 pixels (SD = 51.96), while in the vertical direction it was 52.10 pixels (SD = 44.64).

The results showed that all morphological parameters were significantly increased after image enhancement compared with the original images in both the healthy and diabetic groups (paired t-test, P < 0.001). In the ACCMetrics analysis, for example, CNFL increased from 13.38 to 15.95, CNFD from 21.19 to 24.63, and CNBD from 24.69 to 39.79 in the healthy group, and the diabetic group showed a similar trend, with an increase in CNFL from 10.97 to 12.88, and an increase in CNBD from 21.02 to 30.33. Although the standard deviations increased after image enhancement, the relative standard deviations (RSD) decreased across all parameters and groups, indicating a slight reduction in relative variability. This suggests that image enhancement improved the consistency of the morphological parameter measurements. The results of AiCCMetrics analyses further confirmed the trend, with all parameters significantly increased after enhancement. (Fig. 4; Table 4)

Discussion

This study systematically evaluates the utility of a multi-frame fusion enhancement strategy in CCM image processing, validating its efficacy and robustness across three dimensions: image quality, nerve structure analysis, and disease classification. Results demonstrate that the mean fusion method not only excels in noise reduction and image quality improvement but also significantly enhances downstream task performance, underscoring its practical value.

For image alignment, we compared multiple mainstream algorithms. The SIFT feature extraction combined with LightGlue matching achieved optimal performance. Post-enhancement via mean fusion, overall noise levels decreased markedly with increasing frame counts. Image quality metrics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM) also improved, confirming the strategy’s effectiveness in mitigating random perturbations and enhancing clarity and stability. Further nerve feature analysis revealed that fused images significantly improved measurements of key parameters (CNFL, CNFD, CNBD). Additionally, ROC curve analysis based on morphological parameters demonstrated superior disease-discriminative capability with fused images. Across multiple convolutional neural network architectures, fused images consistently elevated model classification performance, with notable gains in sensitivity metrics (F1-score, AUC) compared to raw images. These findings indicate that enhanced images preserve original structural information while improving nerve fiber discernibility, reducing misjudgment risks from poor image quality (Fig. 6).

Preprocessing CCM images aims to improve quality (e.g., noise reduction), which is critical for all subsequent analytical steps12. Prior studies employed diverse methods—morphological operations, local histogram equalization, and various filters—often as ancillary steps rather than standalone solutions, leaving their impact on downstream tasks unexplored47–50. Some works specifically targeted CCM denoising or enhancement: Han et al. proposed a Retinex-based noise suppression algorithm, outperforming four competing methods11. Ma et al. introduced StillGAN, a bidirectional GAN that maps low-to-high-quality image domains, improving nerve segmentation and diagnostic performance51. However, these approaches operate on single images, relying on morphological or deep learning techniques, which risk amplifying noise or generating artificial structures due to limited information51. In contrast, preprocessing multi-frame CCM images leverages richer data, enhancing robustness and minimizing artifacts.

Notably, our proposed strategy incurs negligible additional costs in clinical practice. Capturing multiple frames at the same location takes only seconds, imposing no extra burden on operators or discomfort on subjects. Unlike costly hardware upgrades or new equipment, this method requires no protocol modifications yet delivers significant gains in image quality and analytical efficacy. Multicenter validation further supports its suitability for resource-limited settings or large-scale screenings.

This study has some limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the current approach relies on global homography-based registration, which may not fully account for non-rigid deformations or localized structural variations in some images. Consequently, the present implementation is limited to datasets in which images with stronger motion artifacts have potentially been excluded, and the reported results should be interpreted within this context. Future research should explore more sophisticated alignment techniques, such as local or pixel-level registration, to better handle complex distortions. Second, the validation of the image alignment step was performed on synthetically distorted images generated by random perspective changes. While this provides a controlled means of assessment, such distortions do not fully reflect the non-linear motion artifacts observed in real CCM data. Therefore, the alignment accuracy reported here may not be directly transferable to real-world scenarios. Future studies should incorporate validation on larger and more diverse clinical datasets to establish robustness under realistic conditions. Third, although rare, occasional misalignments during registration could negatively impact fusion quality and subsequent analysis. To mitigate this risk, we recommend implementing manual verification steps in clinical workflows to prevent potential diagnostic errors. Finally, the generalizability of this method to other clinically relevant conditions—such as dry eye disease or diabetic neuropathy—remains to be validated. Expanding the scope of disease cohorts in future studies will be essential to confirm the broader utility of this image enhancement strategy.

Conclusion

This study presents a multi-frame fusion-based image enhancement strategy that effectively improves the quality and analytical performance of CCM images without requiring additional hardware support. Through systematic evaluation of image alignment accuracy, noise reduction, nerve morphology parameter extraction, and disease classification performance, we demonstrate that this method not only enhances image clarity and structural integrity but also significantly improves the robustness and discriminative capability of automated nerve metric quantification and disease diagnosis. By preserving the simplicity of clinical workflows, multi-frame fusion enhances the reliability and clinical utility of CCM image analysis.
