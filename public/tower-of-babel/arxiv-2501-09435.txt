A cluster theory approach from mutation invariants to Diophantine equations
Zhichao Chen, Zixu Li

Abstract
. In this paper, we define and classify the sign-equivalent exchange matrices. We give a Diophantine explanation for the differences between rank 2 cluster algebras of finite type and affine type based on [ CL24 ] . We classify the positive integer points of the Markov mutation invariant and its variant. As an application, several classes of Diophantine equations with cluster algebraic structures are exhibited. Keywords: Sign-equivalent, Diophantine explanation, Cluster algebras, Integer points. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 13F60, 11D09, 11D25.

2.1.
Seeds and cluster algebras

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
\mathcal{F}
be a rational function field of
n
variables over
\mathbb{Q}
. A
seed
is a pair
(\mathbf{x},B)
, such that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is an
n
-tuple of algebraically independent and generating elements of
\mathcal{F}
and
B=(b_{ij})_{n\times n}
is a skew-symmetrizable matrix. The
n
-tuple
\mathbf{x}
is the
cluster
, elements
x_{i}\in\mathbf{x}
are
cluster variables
and
B
is the
exchange matrix
.

Given a seed
(\mathbf{x},B)
and
1\leq k\leq n
, we define
\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x},B)=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
such that
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{n}^{\prime})
, where

\displaystyle\ x_{i}^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x_{k}^{-1}(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{[b_{jk}]_{+}}+\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{[-b_{jk}]_{+}}),&\text{if}\ i=k,\\
x_{i},&\text{if}\ i\neq k,\end{array}\right.

and
B^{\prime}=(b_{ij}^{\prime})_{n\times n}
is given by

\displaystyle b_{ij}^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-b_{ij},&\text{if}\ i=k\;\;\mbox{or}\;\;j=k,\\
b_{ij}+[b_{ik}]_{+}b_{kj}+b_{ik}[-b_{kj}]_{+},&\text{if}\ i\neq k\;\;\mbox{and}\;j\neq k.\end{array}\right.

In fact,
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is still a seed and
\mu_{k}
is involutive, that is
\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(\mathbf{x},B)
, see
[
Nak23
]
. Then,
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is called the
k-direction mutation
of
(\mathbf{x},B)
. Two seeds (exchange matrices) are said to be
mutation-equivalent
if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of mutations. The mutation equivalence class of
B
is denoted by
[B]
. A
cluster pattern

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{t},B_{t})|\ t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\}
is a collection of seeds which are labeled by the
n
-regular tree
\mathbb{T}_{n}
, such that
(\mathbf{x}_{t^{\prime}},B_{t^{\prime}})=\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{t},B_{t})
for any
t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}t^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{T}_{n}
.

In the following, we use the notations that
\mathbf{x}_{t}=(x_{1;t},\dots,x_{n;t})
and
B_{t}=(b_{ij;t})_{n\times n}
. Note that for an arbitrary fixed vertex
t_{0}\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
, we call the seed
(\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}},B_{t_{0}})

initial seed
and denote by
\mathbf{x}_{t_{0}}=\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
the
initial cluster
and
B_{t_{0}}=B=(b_{ij})_{n\times n}
the
initial exchange matrix
.

Definition 2.1

(
Cluster algebra
)
.

For a cluster pattern
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, the
cluster algebra

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
is the
\mathbb{Q}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{F}
generated by all the cluster variables
\{x_{i;t}|\ i=1,\dots,n;t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}\}
. Here,
n
is the
rank
of
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 2.2

(
Finite type
)
.

A cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
is called of
finite type
if it contains only finite distinct seeds. Otherwise, it is called of
infinite type
.

In particular, the cluster algebras of rank 2 as follows are simple but important.

Definition 2.5

(
Sign-equivalent

An exchange matrix

2.2.
Permutation matrices and irreducible matrices

We recall some notions and properties about permutation matrices and irreducible matrices according to
[
Gan98
, Chapter XIII]
.

Definition 2.6

(
Permutation matrix
)
.

A permutation matrix
P
is a square matrix that has exactly one entry of 1 in each row and each column with all other entries 0.

Definition 2.7

(
Reducible matrix
)
.

Let
B\in\text{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})
. It is said to be
reducible
if there is a permutation matrix
P
, such that

\displaystyle PBP^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}{B_{1}}&{B_{2}}\\
{O}&{B_{3}}\end{array}\right),

where
B_{1}\in\text{Mat}_{k\times k}(\mathbb{R})
and
B_{3}\in\text{Mat}_{(n-k)\times(n-k)}(\mathbb{R})
with
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Otherwise, it is said to be
irreducible
.

In particular, assume that
B
is an exchange matrix. Since
B
is sign-skew-symmetric, it is reducible if and only if there is a permutation matrix
P
, such that

\displaystyle PBP^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}{B_{1}}&{O}\\
{O}&{B_{3}}\end{array}\right),

where
B_{1}\in\text{Mat}_{k\times k}(\mathbb{Z})
and
B_{3}\in\text{Mat}_{(n-k)\times(n-k)}(\mathbb{Z})
. Under this case, it is also called the
direct sum
of the square matrices
B_{1}
and
B_{3}
. Furthermore, for a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we define the (right) action of
\sigma
on
B
by
\sigma(B)=B^{\prime}=(b_{ij}^{\prime})
, where

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{ij}=b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}.

It is easy to check that
\sigma(B)
is still an exchange matrix. Moreover, there is a permutation matrix
P
corresponding to
\sigma
, such that

\displaystyle\sigma(B)=PBP^{T}.

Indeed, there is an equivalence relation for exchange matrices:
B^{\prime}\sim B
if there is some permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, such that
B^{\prime}=\sigma(B)
. We call
B
and
B^{\prime}

permutation-equivalent
.

2.3.
Mutation invariants of cluster algebras

Given a cluster algebra
\mathcal{A}
of rank
n
, we recall the definition of mutation invariants based on
[
CL24
, Definition 1.1]
.
By Laurent phenomenon
[
FZ02
, Theorem 3.1]
and the positivity theorem
[
GHKK18
, Theorem 4.10]
, all the clusters labeled by
t
can be written as

\mathbf{x}_{t}=(c_{1;t}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}),\cdots,c_{n;t}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})),

where each
c_{i,t}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
is a Laurent polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients.

Definition 2.8

(
Mutation invariant
)
.

A non-constant and reduced rational function

if for any

\mathcal{T}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\mathcal{T}(x_{1;t},\cdots,x_{n;t}).

In particular, if

.

In fact, if
\mathcal{T}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a mutation invariant, then so does

is a non-constant polynomial. For example, according to

\displaystyle F\left(\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+1}{x_{1}x_{2}}\right),\ F\left(\dfrac{x_{2}^{4}+x_{1}^{2}+2x_{1}+1}{x_{1}x_{2}^{2}}\right).

First of all, we exhibit the compatibility between permutations and cluster mutations as follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and

. Then, the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}(\sigma(B))=\sigma(\mu_{k}(B)).

Proof.

Let
B=(b_{ij})_{n\times n}
,
B^{\prime}=\mu_{k}(B)=(b^{\prime}_{ij})_{n\times n}
and
\sigma(\mu_{k}(B))=(c_{ij})_{n\times n}
. Then, on the right hand side, by the mutation rules (
2.1
) and the definition of permutation
\sigma
, we have
c_{ij}=b^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}
, where

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)},\ \text{if}\ i=\sigma^{-1}(k)\;\;\mbox{or}\;\;j=\sigma^{-1}(k),\\
b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}+[b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(k)}]_{+}b_{\sigma(k)\sigma(j)}+b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(k)}[-b_{\sigma(k)\sigma(j)}]_{+},\ \text{if}\ i\neq\sigma^{-1}(k)\\
\mbox{and}\;j\neq\sigma^{-1}(k).\end{array}\right.

On the left hand side, we assume that
\sigma(B)=(d_{ij})_{n\times n}
and
\mu_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}(\sigma(B))=(d^{\prime}_{ij})_{n\times n}
. Then, it implies that
d_{ij}=b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle d_{ij}^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-d_{ij},&\text{if}\ i=\sigma^{-1}(k)\;\;\mbox{or}\;\;j=\sigma^{-1}(k),\\
d_{ij}+[d_{ik}]_{+}d_{kj}+d_{ik}[-d_{kj}]_{+},&\text{if}\ i\neq\sigma^{-1}(k)\;\mbox{and}\;j\neq\sigma^{-1}(k).\end{array}\right.

Hence, we get

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)},\ \text{if}\ i=\sigma^{-1}(k)\;\;\mbox{or}\;\;j=\sigma^{-1}(k),\\
b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}+[b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(k)}]_{+}b_{\sigma(k)\sigma(j)}+b_{\sigma(i)\sigma(k)}[-b_{\sigma(k)\sigma(j)}]_{+},\ \text{if}\ i\neq\sigma^{-1}(k)\\
\mbox{and}\;j\neq\sigma^{-1}(k),\end{array}\right.

which implies that

for any

and
\mu_{\sigma(k)}(\sigma(B))=\sigma(\mu_{k}(B))
.

can be generated by the initial solution
(1,1,1)
through finite cluster mutations
\mu_{i}\ (i=1,2,3)
.

Hence, we only need to prove that when
k\neq 1
and
k\neq 3
, there is no positive integer solution. There are two cases for the values of
k
to be discussed as follows.

(1)

If
k=2
, we assume that
(a,b,c)
is a solution. We claim that
\min(a,b,c)\geq 2
. Otherwise, we might assume that
a=1
. Then, we get

\displaystyle b^{2}+c^{2}+1=2bc,

which contradicts with
b^{2}+c^{2}\geq 2bc
. Hence, without loss of generality, we assume that
a\geq b\geq c\geq 2
. Let
f(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-2bc\lambda+b^{2}+c^{2}
and

\displaystyle(a^{\prime},b,c)=\mu_{1}(a,b,c)=\left(\dfrac{b^{2}+c^{2}}{a},b,c\right).

Then, it implies that
a
and
a^{\prime}
are the two zeros of
f(\lambda)
and
a^{\prime}=2bc-a\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Note that

\displaystyle f(b)=2b^{2}+c^{2}-2b^{2}c=b^{2}(2-c)+c(c-b^{2})<0.

Hence, we conclude that
a>b>a^{\prime}
, which implies that
\max(a,b,c)>\max(a^{\prime},b,c)
.
If
b\geq c\geq a^{\prime}
, take

\displaystyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)=\mu_{2}(a^{\prime},b,c)=\left(a^{\prime},\frac{{a^{\prime}}^{2}+c^{2}}{b},c\right).

Similarly, we get
b>c>b^{\prime}
, which implies that
\max(a^{\prime},b,c)>\max(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)
.
If
b>a^{\prime}\geq c
, take

\displaystyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)=\mu_{2}(a^{\prime},b,c)=\left(a^{\prime},\frac{{a^{\prime}}^{2}+c^{2}}{b},c\right).

Then, we have
b>a^{\prime}>b^{\prime}
, which implies that
\max(a^{\prime},b,c)>\max(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)
.
By repeating the process above, that is cluster-mutating a solution at the direction where the number is maximal, we get a sequence of solutions whose maximal numbers are strictly decreasing. However, it will end up with a solution
(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})
, such that

\displaystyle\min(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})<2.

Hence, it contradicts with the fact that
\min(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})\geq 2
.

(2)

If
k\geq 4
, we assume that
(a,b,c)
is a solution. We claim that
\min(a,b,c)\geq 2
. Otherwise, we might assume that
a=1
. Then we get

\displaystyle b^{2}+c^{2}+1=kbc,

which contradicts with
Lemma

5.1
. Hence, without loss of generality, we assume that
a\geq b\geq c\geq 2
. Let
g(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-kbc\lambda+b^{2}+c^{2}
and

\displaystyle(a^{\prime},b,c)=\mu_{1}(a,b,c)=\left(\dfrac{b^{2}+c^{2}}{a},b,c\right).

Then, we have
a
and
a^{\prime}
are the two zeros of
g(\lambda)
and
a^{\prime}=kbc-a\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Since

\displaystyle g(b)=2b^{2}+c^{2}-kb^{2}c=2b^{2}(1-c)+c(c-b^{2})-(k-3)b^{2}c<0,

we conclude that
b
must lie between
a
and
a^{\prime}
, that is
a>b>a^{\prime}
. Then, we get

\displaystyle\max(a,b,c)>\max(a^{\prime},b,c).

Similar to the process of
(1)
, we can always mutate a solution at the direction where the number is maximal and get a sequence of solutions whose maximal numbers are strictly decreasing. However, it will end up with a solution
(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})
, such that

\displaystyle\min(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})<2,

which is a contradiction.

Consequently, by cases
(1)
and
(2)
, the theorem holds.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume that
(a,b)

(1)

\displaystyle a^{\prime}=\dfrac{b^{4}+1}{a}>\dfrac{b^{4}+1}{b^{2}}>b^{2},

Lemma 5.6
.
