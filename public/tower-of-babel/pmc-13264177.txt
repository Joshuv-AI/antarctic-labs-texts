Exposure to calcium stearyl lactylate induces hepatointestinal toxicity and gut microbiota dysbiosis in mice
Authors: Chunjing Qiu, Chenxu Guo, Xia Xue, Pengya Feng, Qiang Zhang, Ya Li, Simeng Liu, Yingying Li, Ihtisham Bukhari, Feifei Ren, Yuexiao Zhang, Pengyuan Zheng, Yang Mi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcium stearyl lactylate (CSL) is a widely used food emulsifier, particularly in pasta products. In this study, we investigated the potential toxicological effects of CSL exposure on organ health and gut microbiota in mice. Over a 12-week period, mice were administered CSL at 50, 500, and 5000 mg/kg·bw. Our study found that CSL exposure induced liver and colon inflammation, significantly elevating hepatic injury markers (ALT and AST). In parallel, key intestinal functional markers (CXCL-1, CXCL-2, IL-1β, TNF-α, ZO-1, and Occludin) were markedly altered, indicating compromised gut barrier integrity. 16S rRNA sequencing revealed that CSL administration disrupted gut microbial diversity, characterized by decreased beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) and increased potentially harmful genera, including Anaerotruncus, Desulfovibrio, and Helicobacter. These findings indicate that long-term CSL intake can induce hepatointestinal damage and provoke significant dysbiosis of the gut microbiome.

Introduction

The gut microbiota regulates host physiology and maintains intestinal homeostasis (Yu et al., 2021; Hills Jr. et al., 2019; Panyod et al., 2023). Dysbiosis, an imbalance in microbial composition, can promote disease through microbiota-derived metabolites and host metabolic pathways, contributing to obesity, metabolic syndrome, and inflammatory bowel disease (Vijay and Valdes, 2022; Jin et al., 2017). Diet is a key environmental factor shaping the gut microbial community (Bevilacqua et al., 2024). Emerging evidence indicates that the gut microbiota metabolizes food additives, thereby modulating their biological effects (Seto et al., 2025). Certain food additives have been shown to alter microbial composition, disrupt epithelial integrity, as well as impair gut barrier function, changes that are closely linked to the pathogenesis of various intestinal disorders (Whelan et al., 2024; Bishehsari et al., 2020). Therefore, specific dietary components and additives may damage the intestinal mucosa and facilitate the development of gut inflammation-related diseases (Cox et al., 2021). However, the interactive relationships between food additives, microbiota dysbiosis, and host pathology remain poorly understood. To reveal the interaction behind, we investigated the toxicological effects of calcium stearyl lactylate (CSL) on hepatointestinal health and gut microbiota homeostasis in mice.

CSL is a widely used emulsifier in flour-based and pasta products (Berton-Carabin et al., 2018). By substantially reducing the interfacial tension between oil and water, CSL facilitates the formation of stable emulsions from otherwise immiscible hydrophobic and hydrophilic phases (Berton-Carabin et al., 2018). In addition to its emulsifying properties, CSL functions as a defoaming, thickening, and lubricating agent, and is commonly added to flour to improve dough elasticity and stability (Cox et al., 2021). CSL is present in a wide range of daily consumer products, including bread, noodles, and instant noodles (Shakhman et al., 2025). Mounting evidence indicates that chronic exposure to certain food additives can lead to severe organ dysfunction in mice (Yu et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2019). For instance, long-term intake of dioctyl phthalate induces liver damage, disrupts lipid metabolism, and impairs reproductive function (Sultana et al., 2023; Schmidt, 2024). Similarly, prolonged consumption of phosphorus-containing food additives alters mineral metabolism and adversely affects bone health (Gutierrez et al., 2015). Furthermore, increasing evidence has linked food emulsifiers to the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and metabolic disorders (Halmos et al., 2019; Chassaing et al., 2022). Despite the widespread use of CSL, it remains unknown whether long-term or excessive intake of this emulsifier adversely affects human health, even potentially.

Although approved food additives are required to undergo acute and subacute toxicity testing prior to market approval, these conventional safety assessments often fail to capture low-grade intestinal inflammation, subtle barrier dysfunction, or persistent gut dysbiosis, pathological changes that are closely associated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and organ-specific toxicity (Yu et al., 2021; Bevilacqua et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2019). As an amphiphilic emulsifier, CSL has the potential to directly disrupt epithelial tight junctions, disturb microbial homeostasis, and trigger sustained low-grade inflammation via the gut-liver axis and the gut-immune axis (Bevilacqua et al., 2024; Halmos et al., 2019; Chassaing et al., 2022). However, whether CSL exerts such effects remains largely unexplored. Elucidating these potential mechanisms represents a critical knowledge gap that directly informs the rationale for the present study.

To date, only limited evidence is available regarding the potential organ damage associated with long-term CSL exposure in adults (Sultana et al., 2023). To address this knowledge gap, the present study was designed to investigate the toxicity of CSL and its impact on digestive and metabolic organs. Four-week-old mice were administered CSL by gavage at three doses (50, 500, and 5000 mg/kg·bw) for 12 weeks. Body weight changes and histopathological alterations in the stomach, liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon were systematically monitored. In addition, intestinal barrier function and inflammatory status were evaluated. 16S rRNA sequencing was also performed to characterize CSL-induced changes in gut microbiota composition and diversity. We hypothesized that CSL exposure induces dose-dependent intestinal barrier dysfunction, gut dysbiosis, and intestinal inflammation, which in turn promote liver injury and systemic organ damage (Yu et al., 2021; Halmos et al., 2019; Chassaing et al., 2022). The present study was designed to elucidate the organ-specific toxicity of CSL and its underlying mechanisms, thereby providing mechanistic insights for evaluating the potential health risks associated with long-term dietary exposure to this widely used emulsifier, particularly in the context of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and Main reagents/supplies

Four-week-old SPF-grade C57BL/6 J male mice weighing 18–21 g were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. [License No.: SCXK (Zhejiang) 2019–0001]. Mice were selected as the experimental model because they represent the most widely used and well-accepted mammalian system for food additive toxicity assessment, intestinal inflammation, and gut microbiota research (Yu et al., 2021; Bevilacqua et al., 2024; Bishehsari et al., 2020). The C57BL/6 J strain was chosen due to its genetic stability, standardized background, and high sensitivity to intestinal barrier damage and low-grade inflammation induced by emulsifiers (Halmos et al., 2019; Chassaing et al., 2022). Four-week-old young mice were used to simulate exposure from adolescence to early adulthood, a life stage with high intake of processed foods containing emulsifiers and relatively developing intestinal and immune functions (Cox et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2019). Only male mice were included in this study to avoid interference from the estrous cycle and hormonal fluctuations, which can significantly affect gut microbiota, intestinal permeability, and inflammatory responses (Seto et al., 2025; Whelan et al., 2024). This approach is a common strategy in preliminary toxicological studies to reduce confounding factors and ensure stable dose–response results. Although male-only data cannot be directly extrapolated to females, the core mechanisms of intestinal barrier injury, liver toxicity, and dysbiosis are highly conserved between sexes, thus, the present findings can provide a mechanistic reference for both genders, and further studies using female mice will be conducted in the future.

Corn oil and calcium stearoyl lactylate (CSL) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sterilized standard diet and poplar wood bedding were purchased from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [Su Feed License (2019) 01008]. Alkaline phosphatase detection kits were provided by Shenzhen Cubic Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (License No: KY-D-2025045) and performed in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Experimental methods

C57BL/6 J young mice (4 weeks old, 19–21 g)

Were housed in a SPF-grade environment and acclimated for 7 days prior to experimentation. The study design was performed in compliance with general principles of chronic toxicity studies recommended by OECD guidelines

Dose selection was based on EFSA exposure assessments (high adult exposure: 19–53 mg/kg bw/day), the acute oral LD₅₀ of CSL (10.985 g/kg), and the acceptable daily intake (ADI: 0–20 mg/kg bw/day) (Jandhyala et al., 2015; Prior et al., 2024). Three dose levels were applied:50 mg/kg bw: to mimic human-relevant dietary exposure, 500 mg/kg bw: to evaluate dose–response relationships, 5000 mg/kg bw: as an extreme high-dose group for safety boundary evaluation and maximum tolerated dose assessment, consistent with approaches used in food additive chronic toxicity studies to define the upper threshold of potential effects (Jandhyala et al., 2015). Mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 10 per group, 10 mice per cage):Control group (Con): 0 mg/kg bw CSL, Low-dose group (L): 50 mg/kg bw CSL, Medium-dose group (M): 500 mg/kg bw CSL, High-dose group (H): 5000 mg/kg bw CSL.

CSL was dissolved in corn oil and administered daily by oral gavage (intragastric administration) for 12 consecutive weeks. To standardize handling procedures and reduce animal stress, all mice first received an equal volume of normal saline by gavage, followed by vehicle (corn oil) or CSL treatment 1 h later. This pretreatment step served to standardize the gavage operation procedure and minimize stress-related physiological variation across groups.

General health status (coat condition, stool consistency, diarrhea, visible blood in feces) was monitored daily. Food and water were available ad libitum. Body weight was measured weekly throughout the 12-week treatment period.

At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized with pentobarbital sodium. Blood samples were immediately collected via orbital enucleation to minimize stress and hemolysis, ensuring adequate quality for serum biochemical and inflammatory analyses. Stomach, liver, kidney, spleen, and colon tissues were rapidly dissected for histopathological and molecular analyses.

Determination of serum indicators

Hepatic function parameters including, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), along with renal function indicators, namely creatinine (Cr) and urea, were assessed using an automatic biochemical analyzer (iMagic-V7, Shenzhen Kubeier Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The levels of diamine oxidase (DAO) and D-lactic acid (LA) were quantified via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and colorimetric assay, respectively, using commercial kits from Wuhan Ilarite Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All biochemical assays were conducted strictly according to the manufacturers' protocols.

2.2.3 HE Staining: Mouse stomach, liver, spleen, and colon fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were subjected to dehydration, embedding, sectioning, spreading, fishing, and baking. Subsequently, the slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Structural changes and inflammatory conditions in the stomach, liver, kidneys, spleen, and colon were observed under a microscope (Leica Microsystems Shanghai Co., Ltd.). The liver, kidney, spleen, stomach, and colon, were collected for histopathological observation. Liver and kidney were analyzed as vital metabolic and excretory organs frequently affected by food additives (Cox et al., 2021). Spleen was included as a key immune organ associated with intestinal inflammation (Halmos et al., 2019). Stomach was examined because emulsifiers directly contact the gastric mucosa after oral administration (Halmos et al., 2019). The colon was both selected because they represent the most common sites of inflammation in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Shakhman et al., 2025).

Analysis of intestinal flora composition

One week before sacrifice, mouse feces were collected daily using sterile EP tubes and stored in a − 80 °C refrigerator. The feces were sealed with dry ice and sent to Frasergen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Fecal genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Shanghai, China). The primer pair 27F-1492R (forward: 5’-AGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′, reverse: 5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) was used for full-length 16S rDNA high-throughput sequencing. The reads obtained from PacBio Sequel II sequencing were assembled, and the raw sequences were quality-controlled and filtered using QIIME2 (2022.2). Samples with 16S rDNA gene sequence similarity greater than 97% were defined as an operational taxonomic unit (OTU). Subsequently, OTU clustering and species annotation were performed. Based on the OTU clustering results (Lin et al., 2025), RStudio (4.1.3) was used for statistical and visual analyses of intestinal flora at the phylum, genus, and species levels. In the LDA analysis, non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used, and significantly different flora were identified based on the LDA values (LDA > 2). Correlation analysis was conducted with biochemical indicators.

Statistical Methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0, with a one-way ANOVA. Herein, * indicates P < 0.05, ** indicates P < 0.01, and *** indicates P < 0.001. Graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Results

CSL induces liver function damage

After 12 weeks of CSL intervention, increasing CSL doses were associated with a gradual slowing of weight gain (P > 0.05, Fig. 1B). The liver is the body's largest detoxifying organ and is the primary target organ for CSL exposure (Barouki et al., 2023). Observations on the liver appearance in each group revealed that as the concentration of CSL increased in the M and H groups, the liver enlarged and its colour deepened (Fig. S1C). HE analysis of the liver in group C showed normal cells and tissue structure. However, exposure to CSL exhibited a range of pathological changes. In the M and H groups, the liver showed a greater number of small fat vacuoles (Fig. 1A). In addition, CSL exposure significantly increased the levels of ALT and ALP enzymes in the H group (PALT < 0.001, PAST < 0.01) (Fig. 1C), indicating that high-dose CSL exposure has a significant impact on the liver. CSL disrupted the homeostasis of gut microbiota in mice

A total of 1,082,049 valid CCS sequences were obtained from sequencing 40 samples. Non-redundant sequences were extracted and subjected to OTU clustering, yielding 3034 OTU levels. Excluding unclassified species, there were 13 phyla, 18 classes, 55 orders, 81 families, 149 genera, and 110 species. The Venn diagram showed that there were many unique OTUs among the groups. The Shannon and Simpson indices of alpha diversity showed no statistically significant differences between the four groups (Fig. 3C, D). It should be clarified that alpha diversity mainly reflects the richness (number of microbial species) and evenness (distribution of different microbial species) within a single sample, thus, this result indicates that CSL exposure did not significantly affect the overall richness or evenness of gut microbes in individual samples. However, beta diversity analysis (using Adonis combined with PCoA results) and UniFrac hierarchical clustering showed clear differences, the phylogenetic community structures of CSL-exposed samples were significantly different from those of the control group, and the CSL group was clearly separated from the control group along Axis 1 (16.72%) (Fig. 3E and F). This difference in beta diversity reflects changes in the composition of gut microbiota communities between groups (i.e., the types and proportions of microbes in the CSL-exposed groups were different from those in the control group), suggesting that CSL exposure significantly altered gut microbiota composition.

An analysis of OTUs was performed to further clarify the changes in gut microbiota composition at the phylum, genus, and species levels. Firmicutes was the most predominant phylum, contributing 42.59%, 39.87%, 34.77% and 35.19% of the gut microbiota in the C, L, M, and H groups, respectively, followed by Bacteroidetes, contributing 38.96%, 37.91%, 35.55% and 22.17% of the gut microbiota, respectively, in these four groups (Fig. 3G). The Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, a marker of obesity, decreased after CSL exposure, which may explain the CSL-induced reduction in body weight (Fig. 3H).

The structural composition of microbiota in each group at the genus level was also analyzed. Muribaculaceae and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group were the dominant microbiota in all groups. At the genus level, compared with the C group, the abundances of Gastranaerophilales, Dubosiella, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium in the L, M, and H groups gradually decreased with increasing CSL exposure dose (Fig. 3I). Observing the abundance at the species level, compared with the C group, it was found that with the increase of CSL exposure dose, the abundance of Helicobacter sp. in the L, M, and H groups gradually increased, while the abundances of Burkholderiales bacterium, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Lactobacillus_murinus, and Lactobacillus reuteri gradually decreased (Fig. 3J).

At the genus level, CSL exposure significantly altered the gut microbial landscape, characterized by a notable reduction in beneficial bacteria, predominantly represented by Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus (indicated in green). Discussion

Food additives are widely applied in food systems globally, among which calcium stearoyl lactylate (CSL) serves as a common emulsifier used in bread, bakery products, desserts, and infant formulas. Although short-term toxicity is considered low, the long-term effects of CSL exposure on intestinal homeostasis, liver function, and gut microbiota remain largely unclear. A previous study showed that 14C-labeled CSL could be distributed to the liver, gastrointestinal tract, and kidneys within 48 h (Barouki et al., 2023), yet whether long-term, high-dose intake impacts intestinal health and microbial ecology remains unknown. In the present study, we confirmed that long-term intragastric administration of CSL disrupted intestinal barrier integrity, induced low-grade colon inflammation, altered gut microbial composition, and caused liver injury in C57 mice, with effects most pronounced at high doses. These findings provide new evidence that exposure to food emulsifiers may threaten intestinal and systemic health.

A prominent effect of CSL observed in this study was impaired intestinal barrier function, as indicated by downregulated mRNA expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin, accompanied by elevated expression of pro-inflammatory mediators including CXCL-1, CXCL-2, IL-1β, and TNF-α. These changes are characteristic of low-grade colon inflammation and mucosal disruption, which represent early events in the pathogenesis of enterocolitis. Impaired intestinal permeability allows luminal antigens, bacterial components, and toxins to translocate across the mucosa, activating local and systemic inflammatory responses. The intestinal barrier damage has been reported for other dietary emulsifiers, which are widely believed to promote colon inflammation by disrupting epithelial integrity (Phillips et al., 1981; Panyod et al., 2024; Harusato et al., 2022). Our findings extend these observations to CSL and confirm that this emulsifier can directly trigger mucosal dysfunction and inflammation in the colon. Consistent with previous reports, gut microbiota disorder also disrupts the integrity of tight junctions in the intestinal epithelium, leading to intestinal barrier dysfunction (Viennois et al., 2020).

The intestinal mucosal mechanical barrier relies on tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and Occludin, whose high expression helps repair mucosal integrity and reduce inflammatory responses (Viennois et al., 2020; Neurath et al., 2025). Goblet cell-derived MUC2 mucin forms a protective mucus layer that prevents direct contact between epithelial cells and luminal bacteria (Dodiya et al., 2020). Several emulsifiers have been reported to disrupt tight junctions and increase intestinal permeability, including sucrose monoesters, fatty acids, lauric acid, and sodium taurocholate (Huang et al., 2024; Vital et al., 2014; Blaak et al., 2020). We found high-dose CSL significantly reduced ZO-1 and Occludin expression, thereby increasing intestinal permeability and enabling translocation of lipopolysaccharides and bacterial toxins into the portal circulation (Mooser et al., 2025). These observations collectively indicate that food emulsifiers, including CSL, can directly damage intestinal barrier function and initiate low-grade colon inflammation.

CSL exposure also induced profound gut dysbiosis, characterized by distinct changes in beneficial and harmful microbial taxa, which further amplified colon inflammation. Under physiological conditions, gut microbiota maintain homeostatic structure and function, environmental disturbances such as unhealthy diets or chemical exposure can lead to dysbiosis characterized by depleted beneficial bacteria and enriched harmful taxa (Gomaa, 2020; Chandrasekaran et al., 2024). The liver constitutes a critical detoxification organ that prevents bacterial metabolites from entering systemic circulation, and liver injury can further alter microbiota via the gut-liver axis (Hu et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2021). CSL-induced liver injury was accompanied by significant dysregulation of microbial diversity and structure in our finding.

At the phylum level, CSL decreased the abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria while increasing Proteobacteria, a phylum commonly associated with microbial instability and intestinal dyshomeostasis (Sivaprasadan et al., 2024; Furuhashi et al., 2020). High-fat, low-fiber diets have been linked to elevated Proteobacteria, which also promote colitis in susceptible hosts (Rousta et al., 2021; Erturk-Hasdemir et al., 2021). These changes are consistent with the increased colon inflammation observed in our study. At the genus level, CSL significantly reduced beneficial genera including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus (Chiu et al., 2021), which maintain intestinal homeostasis, produce anti-inflammatory metabolites, and protect against pathogen invasion. In parallel, CSL enriched harmful or pro-inflammatory genera including Mucispirillum, Anaerotruncus, Enterorhabdus, Desulfovibrio, Muribaculaceae, Helicobacter, and Pseudomonas (Hu et al., 2016; Zhai et al., 2018; Xiang et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2021; Du et al., 2021; Robinson and Lehours, 2020). Many of these taxa are closely associated with colon inflammation, liver injury, and metabolic disorders. At the species level, CSL decreased beneficial species including Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Lactobacillus murinus, Bacteroides acidifaciens, Lachnospiraceae bacterium, and Parabacteroides goldsteinii (Goldberg, 2000; Song et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022), which support barrier function, produce butyrate, regulate bile acid metabolism, and suppress inflammation. In contrast, CSL enriched pro-inflammatory or pathogenic species including Mucispirillum schaedleri, Helicobacter hepaticus, Desulfovibrio fairfieldensis, Alistipes inops, Bacteroides dorei, and Butyricimonas virosa (Li et al., 2024; Herp et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021; Nie et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2023), which are associated with colitis, liver disease, metabolic dysfunction, and systemic inflammation.

Correlation analysis confirmed strong functional links between dysbiosis, inflammation, and organ injury. Bifidobacterium was negatively correlated with ALT, AST, and ALP, whereas Helicobacter was positively correlated with these liver injury markers. Desulfovibrio fairfieldensis was positively correlated with CXCL-2, while Bacteroides dorei, Alistipes inops, and Butyricimonas virosa were negatively correlated with ZO-1 and Occludin. Meanwhile, Bacteroides dorei was positively correlated with ALT, AST, ALP, CXCL-1, CXCL-2, IL-1β, and TNF-α. These relationships are consistent with reports that dysbiotic microbiota contribute to colon inflammation and liver injury (Li et al., 2024; Whitehill et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2020; Surewaard et al., 2018; Menghini et al., 2019; Daniel et al., 2017) and support a mechanistic model in which CSL-induced dysbiosis promotes barrier dysfunction and colon inflammation, further leading to liver injury via the gut–liver axis.

Notably, CSL is widely used in infant formulas, a population characterized by immature intestinal barriers and rapidly developing microbiota (Lin et al., 2025; Mooser et al., 2025). Premature and newborn infants are highly vulnerable to necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), a severe intestinal inflammatory disease closely linked to barrier dysfunction and dysbiosis (Harusato et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). Although this study was conducted in adult mice, our results raise concern that early-life CSL exposure may disrupt intestinal mucosal development and increase NEC susceptibility in neonates. In addition, sex, diet, and life-stage are critical factors influencing microbiota composition and inflammatory sensitivity (Gomaa, 2020; Chandrasekaran et al., 2024; Furuhashi et al., 2020), these factors were not explored in the present study and warrant further investigation.

The exposure to CSL induces intestinal barrier damage, low-grade colon inflammation, and gut dysbiosis characterized by reduced beneficial microbes and enriched pathobionts, further leading to systemic inflammation and liver injury via the gut–liver axis. This study clarifies the organ-specific toxicity and intestinal inflammatory mechanism of CSL, highlighting potential health risks of long-term dietary exposure to emulsifiers, especially for vulnerable populations such as infants.

Conclusion

As a commonly used emulsifier, long-term and high-dose intake of CSL can lead to increased serum liver enzyme indices in mice, causing certain damage to liver function, it promotes the expression of colonic inflammatory chemokines and inflammatory factors, reduces the mRNA expression levels of genes related to the intestinal mucosal barrier, alters intestinal mucosal permeability, and disrupts the homeostasis of intestinal microbiota. Subsequent experiments can conduct intestinal flora metabolomics studies to investigate the extent of multi-organ damage and the homeostasis of intestinal flora in mice at different time periods under long-term CSL exposure, providing a more experimental basis for clinical practice.
