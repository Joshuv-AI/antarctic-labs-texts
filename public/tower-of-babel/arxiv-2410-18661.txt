Recognizing Sumsets is NP-Complete
Amir Abboud, Nick Fischer, Ron Safier, Nathan Wallheimer

Abstract
Sumsets are central objects in additive combinatorics. In 2007, Granville asked whether one can efficiently recognize whether a given set S S is a sumset, i.e. whether there is a set A A such that A + A = S A+A=S . Granville suggested an algorithm that takes exponential time in the size of the given set, but can we do polynomial or even linear time? This basic computational question is indirectly asking a fundamental structural question: do the special characteristics of sumsets allow them to be efficiently recognizable? In this paper, we answer this question negatively by proving that the problem is NP-complete. Specifically, our results hold for integer sets and over any finite field. Assuming the Exponential Time Hypothesis, our lower bound becomes 2 Ω ⁡ ( n 1 / 4 ) 2^{\Omega(n^{1/4})} .

1.1
Results

This paper gives a negative answer to Granville’s question, proving that the Sumset Recognition problem is NP-hard. This gives formal support for the intuition of Alon, Granville, and Ubis that sumsets are not structured enough to allow for fast identification.

1.2

1.3
Technical Overview

In this section, we give an intuitive overview of the new ideas leading to our results. We will mainly focus on the NP-hardness of the
integer
Sumset Recognition problem, which already requires many of our insights. As may be expected, we prove NP-hardness by designing a polynomial-time reduction from the classical
3
-SAT
4
4

4

We quickly recall the 3-SAT problem: The input consists of a 3-CNF formula
\phi
on
n
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. That is,
\phi
consists of
m

clauses
, each of which consists of three
literals
taking form
x_{i}
or
\overline{x}_{i}
. An assignment
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfies a positive literal
x_{i}
if
\alpha_{i}=1
and a negative literal
\overline{x}_{i}
if
\alpha_{i}=0
. The assignment satisfies a clause if it satisfies at least one of its literals, and it satisfies
\phi
if it satisfies all clauses. The 3-SAT problem is to decide whether there is a satisfying assignment of
\phi
.
problem to Sumset Recognition. That is, given a
3
-SAT formula
\phi
on
n
variables, our task is to create a set
X_{\phi}\subseteq[\poly(n)]
such that we can express
X_{\phi}=A+A
, for some set
A
, if and only if
\phi
is satisfiable.

We will present a conceptually natural approach that, as we will see later, requires significant technical work to work out. The baseline idea is to construct a set
X_{\phi}
such that each feasible set
A
, in the sense that
A+A=X_{\phi}
, encodes an assignment. Then, in a second step, we will modify this construction in accordance with the input formula, such that only the sets
A
corresponding to
satisfying
assignments remain.

Step 1: Encoding Assignments

Following this general idea, the first step is to construct a
variable gadget,
which is a constant-sized set
V
that can be expressed as the sumset of exactly two sets
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
(i.e.,
V=V_{0}+V_{0}=V_{1}+V_{1}
). We will interpret
V_{0}
as the
0
-assignment and
V_{1}
as the
1
-assignment of a variable. For technical reasons that will become clear later, we also require that there are two elements
v_{0}\in V_{0}\setminus V_{1}
and
v_{1}\in V_{1}\setminus V_{0}
.
5
5

5

Using a computer search, we have identified the smallest gadget that fits these requirements:
V=\{0,\dots,18\}\setminus\{5\}
,
V_{0}=\{0,1,2,6,7,8,9\}
,
V_{1}=\{0,1,3,6,7,8,9\}
,
v_{0}=2
and
v_{1}=3
. However, equipped with the “positioning” and “masking” tools that we develop soon, we can find a significantly simpler and
understandable
gadget. See
Section

5
for more details.
Then, we assemble
X_{\phi}
to be a set of
n
appropriately shifted copies of
V
:

X_{\phi}=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(V+2s_{i}).

The hope is that the sumset roots of
X_{\phi}
are in one-to-one corresponds with assignments
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and have the form

A_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(V_{\alpha_{i}}+s_{i}).

This looks promising, as indeed the shifted set
V+2s_{i}
has two unique sumset roots, namely
V_{0}+s_{i}
and
V_{1}+s_{i}
. However, a major problem with this approach is that
A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}
also contains
cross terms
of the form
s_{i}+s_{j}+V_{\alpha_{i}}+V_{\alpha_{j}}
, for
i\neq j
. These cross terms also have to be included into
X_{\phi}
—but of course, we cannot “hardcode” any variable assignment
V_{\alpha_{i}}
. That is, for each
i,j
we need to add
s_{i}+s_{j}+V_{\alpha_{i}}+V_{\alpha_{j}}
to
X_{\phi}
without knowing whether each of
V_{\alpha_{i}}
and
V_{\alpha_{j}}
is
V_{0}
or
V_{1}
. This puts us in a precarious situation: In order to build
X_{\phi}
oblivious to the assignment, we would have to find a variable gadget
V
with sumset roots
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
, i.e.
V=V_{0}+V_{0}=V_{1}+V_{1}
, which additionally satisfies that
V=V_{0}+V_{1}
. If we could do this, we could simply add
s_{i}+s_{j}+V
to
X_{\phi}
for all
i,j
. Unfortunately, this seems too much to ask for, and we could not find such a gadget. Our actual solution for the cross terms is significantly more involved and uses non-trivial constructions from additive combinatorics. We will describe it towards the end of this overview; for now, let us ignore the problematic cross terms and continue with the general picture.

Step 2: Enforcing Satisfying Assignments

The second step is to include the
m
clauses into the construction of
X_{\phi}
to make sure that only the roots
A_{\alpha}
corresponding to
satisfying
assignments
\alpha
survive. Our approach is as follows: Let
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
denote a fresh set of shifts. To encode the clauses, we would like to force
A_{\alpha}
to also include the sets
C_{k}
defined as follows:

C_{k}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\text{ appears}\\
\text{positively in}\\
\text{the $k$-th clause.}\end{subarray}}\{t_{k}-s_{i}-v_{1}\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\text{ appears}\\
\text{negatively in}\\
\text{the $k$-th clause.}\end{subarray}}\{t_{k}-s_{i}-v_{0}\}

Note that these sets depend on the formula
\phi
but not on the assignment
\alpha
.
While in the reduction we control
X_{\phi}
but not
A_{\alpha}
, we would like to add certain elements to
X_{\phi}
that will force any feasible
A_{\alpha}
to include all
C_{k}
’s.
At the very least, we have to add all elements in
\bigcup_{k,\ell}(C_{k}+C_{\ell})
to
X_{\phi}
. In addition, and this is key, we insert all elements
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
into
X_{\phi}
. Our intention is to force that
t_{k}\in A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}
if and only if
\alpha
satisfies the
k
-th clause. Indeed, if the
k
-th clause is satisfied by a positive literal
x_{i}
(i.e.,
\alpha_{i}=1
), then, recalling that
s_{i}+v_{1}\in A_{\alpha}
, we have
t_{k}=(v_{1}+s_{i})+(t_{k}-s_{i}-v_{1})\in A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}
. And similarly, if it is satisfied by a negative literal
\overline{x}_{i}
(i.e.,
\alpha_{i}=0
) then since
s_{i}+v_{0}\in A_{\alpha}
we have that
t_{k}=(v_{0}+s_{i})+(t_{k}-s_{i}-v_{0})\in A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}
.

Ideally, this should complete the description of our reduction—however, due to various interesting challenges, the story does not end here. Specifically, we face two major problems, which we will describe, along with our solutions, in the following paragraphs. For both of these problems, we managed to find surprisingly clean, modular solutions in terms of the upcoming
Lemmas

1.4
and
1.5
.

Problem 1: Positioning

The first issue in this outline is how to prove the “completeness” direction. I.e., given a set
A
with
X_{\phi}=A+A
, can we guarantee that
\phi
is satisfiable? If the set
A
takes the form
A_{\alpha}
from before, for some assignment
\alpha
, then this is plausible: We merely have to ensure that we can never spuriously express
t_{j}
as the sum of two unrelated elements from
A_{\alpha}
. This can be achieved by choosing the set of shifts
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
to be a (slightly modified)
Sidon set
, i.e., a set without non-trivial solutions to the equation
a+b=c+d
. See
Section

5
for more details.

However, the more serious issue is that it is a priori not clear that
A
looks like
A_{\alpha}
. Could it not happen that
A
looks entirely obscure? To rule this out, we conceptually would like to enforce that
A
includes only certain elements by requiring that
A\subseteq U
for some prespecified set
U
; we loosely refer to this constraint as “positioning
A
”. Then we could strategically choose
U
to be in line with the sets
A_{\alpha}
—and only these sets—by

U=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}((V_{0}\cup V_{1})+s_{i})\cup\bigcup_{k\in[m]}C_{k}.

And indeed, we show that the superset condition
A\subseteq U
can be enforced by suffering only
constant
blow-up in the universe size. Formally, we prove the following lemma:

1.4
Positioning for
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
X,U\subseteq\{0,\dots,n\}
. There is a set
X^{\prime}\subseteq\{0,\dots,2^{24}n\}
that can be constructed in time
\poly(n)
and satisfying that:

\exists A\subseteq U:X=A+A\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}:X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}.

The idea behind the proof of
Lemma

1.4
is as follows: We first design a
skeleton set

I\subseteq\{0,\dots,c\}
over some constant-size universe (in the lemma,
c=2^{24}
). We require that (i)
I
is
primitive
, i.e. that its sumset
I+I
cannot be expressed as
J+J
for some other set
J
. And (ii),
I
shall contain a specific constellation of integers tailored towards the following approach.

In the reduction, we will partition
\{0,\dots,cn\}
into
c
successive length-
n
intervals and use the skeleton set
I
for determining which of these intervals should be non-empty in the set
X^{\prime}
. Let
X^{\prime}_{i}
denote the part of
X^{\prime}
that falls into the
i
-th interval, and similarly define
A^{\prime}_{i}
. Then (i) implies that by choosing
X^{\prime}
as a subset of the intervals associated to
I+I
(i.e.,
X^{\prime}_{i}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
i\in I+I
), we can ensure that any set
A^{\prime}
with
X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}
is a subset of the intervals associated to
I
(i.e.,
A^{\prime}_{i}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
i\in I
).

Next, in order to enforce that
A\subseteq U
in addition to
A+A=X
, we will place copies of
X
,
U
,
\{0\}
, and
[2n]
at appropriately chosen intervals in
X^{\prime}
that are informed by the property (ii) of the skeleton set
I
. This, in turn, will force us to appropriately place copies of
A,U,\{0\}
, and
[n]
in
A^{\prime}
. In a bit more detail about property (ii), our skeleton set
I
contains the constellation
a,u,s-a,s-u
for some integers
a,u,s
that act independently (i.e., that satisfy no non-trivial equations, e.g. their pairwise sums are distinct) and that cannot be represented as the pairwise sum of elements from
I
. The insight is that by setting
X^{\prime}_{2a}=X
and
X^{\prime}_{2s-2a}=\{0\}
and
X^{\prime}_{s}=U
, we in turn force that
A^{\prime}_{a}+A^{\prime}_{a}=X
and
A^{\prime}_{s-a}=\{0\}
and

(A^{\prime}_{a}+A^{\prime}_{s-a})\cup(A^{\prime}_{u}+A^{\prime}_{s-u})=A^{\prime}_{a}\cup(A^{\prime}_{u}+A^{\prime}_{s-u})=U.

The latter effectively enforces that
A^{\prime}_{a}\subseteq U
; to get the equality the solution separately ensures that
(A^{\prime}_{u}+A^{\prime}_{s-u})=U
.
In particular, the set
A=A^{\prime}_{a}
satisfies exactly the two conditions
X=A+A
and
A\subseteq U
. We omit further details here, and instead refer to
Section

3
. Let us finally emphasize that throughout, we have not optimized most constants and that the specific constant
2^{24}
can likely be dramatically lowered.

Problem 2: Masking (And Stopping the Infinite Game)

The second major problem we have to deal with is the problematic cross terms we encountered before. Recall that the issue was that the set
A_{\alpha}
as constructed before consists of several copies of variable and clause gadgets. In the sumset
A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}
, our intention is that certain variables and clause gadgets interact (in the sense that the sums they cause are meaningful). However, the set
A_{\alpha}+A_{\alpha}
also contains the cross terms caused by variable-variable, variable-clause, and clause-clause combinations. And even for the relevant variable-clause combinations, we have to cover the elements
t_{k}\pm 1
, which might or might not be present. For concreteness, focus again on the cross term
V_{\alpha_{i}}+V_{\alpha_{j}}+s_{i}+s_{j}
from before, caused by two variable gadgets. As already argued, we cannot simply include this set into
X_{\phi}
as this would force fixed values for the variables
i
and
j
.

Instead, we follow a different idea, which we call “masking”: We will add to
A_{\alpha}
even more sets
M_{1},\dots,M_{s}
(so-called “masks”). The goal is that each cross term, say,
V_{\alpha_{i}}+V_{\alpha_{j}}+s_{i}+s_{j}
, is contained in a sumset
M_{a}+M_{b}
. In the simplest form, we choose the masks to be singleton sets and hit all unwanted cross term elements one by one. This requires some bookkeeping to make sure that the masks do not interfere with the relevant positions, like
t_{j}
.

Disregarding these details, there is a fundamental conceptual flaw in this idea: By including masks into
A_{\alpha}
, we also introduce
new
cross terms involving these masks, e.g.,
V_{\alpha_{i}}+s_{i}+M_{a}
. To cover these new cross terms, we would have to introduce even more masks, which in turn cause more cross terms—and this game continues infinitely. Is this inherent, or is there any hope to salvage this idea and stop the infinite game?

Perhaps surprisingly, it turns out that by choosing the masks appropriately—singletons in the first iteration of the game and intervals in the second iteration—we can stop the game after two iterations. The insight is that, with some extra work, after the first iteration all cross terms become predictable in the sense that we can determine their minimum and maximum elements. Then, using that for any set
A\subseteq\{0,\dots,n\}
we have
A+\{0,\dots,n\}=\{\min(A),\dots,\max(A)+n\}
, after the second iteration we can simply include all cross terms into
X_{\phi}
without introducing new masks. The details of this idea turn out to be extremely technical.

In summary, we prove the following lemma:

1.5
Masking for
\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
X,Y,U\subseteq\{0,\dots,n\}
. There are sets
X^{\prime},U^{\prime}\subseteq\{0,\dots,2^{17}n^{2}\}
that can be constructed in time
\poly(n)
and satisfying that:

\exists A\subseteq U:X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}:X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}.

This lemma should be understood as follows: The Sumset Recognition problem is equivalent to the problem of detecting the existence of a set
A
such that
A+A
does not have to precisely match
X
. Rather, there are positions
Y\setminus X
that we don’t care about (and that are masked in the reduction). See
Section

4
for more details and the proof of
Lemma

1.5
.

Additional Challenges for
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}

In contrast to many other settings in additive combinatorics, in our context, dealing with integers is simpler than dealing with, say,
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
or
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{d}
. Even seemingly simple tasks like the construction of primitive sets are significantly more involved for
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
and
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{d}
(see
Section

3.2
). This is mainly, but not exclusively, due to a special role that
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
takes here: In contrast to the integers and finite fields of odd order, there are order-
2
elements
a
(i.e., satisfying that
2a=0
); in fact all nonzero elements have order
2
. This has several complicating consequences: For instance, there are no sets with a unique sumset representation, as whenever
X=A+A
then also
X=(A+a)+(A+a)
. Another complication is that in the positioning step we relied on the fact that
2a\neq 0
; over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{d}
we have to take a different route which leads to weaker positioning and masking constructions (see
Lemmas

3.6
and
4.2
) where we replace the conditions that
X=A+A
and
X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
by respectively
X=A+B
and
X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y
. Nevertheless, even these weaker versions are ultimately sufficient to prove NP-hardness.

1.4
Conclusions and Open Questions

In this paper, we have proven the NP-completeness of what may be considered the most basic computational question related to additive combinatorics. This opens up the door to investigating the time complexity of many other problems related to sumsets. Let us conclude with three specific questions that we find interesting.

First, while we have shown that it is not possible to recognize sumsets
quickly
, it is still open to determine the extent to which non-trivial algorithmic strategies are helpful. In other words, we would like to know the fine-grained complexity of the Sumset Recognition problem. Our reduction takes a
3
-SAT instance on
n
variables and produces a set of integers in an interval of size
O(n^{4})
. This leads to a conditional
2^{\Omega(n^{1/4})}
lower bound; could there be a sub-exponential
2^{O(n^{0.99})}
algorithm?

1.6
ETH-Hardness over
\mathbb{Z}
.

The Sumset Recognition problem over
\mathbb{Z}
cannot be solved in time
2^{o(n^{1/4})}
, unless the Exponential Time Hypothesis fails.

A second question concerns the average case complexity of Sumset Recognition (and factoring). Can we generate hard instances by taking a random set
A
, and asking the algorithm to retrieve
A
from the set
A+A
?

Finally, it would be interesting to study the approximability of the problem: given a set
S
, find the largest
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
such that
S^{\prime}
is a sumset.

Condition (ii) enforces specific multiplicities for exactly the elements in

. For any such element

, it can easily be verified that

(since all elements in

have a nontrivial component

, but
j_{e}\perp I^{\prime}
) and that
x\not\in I^{\prime\prime}+I^{\prime\prime}
(by construction we can only express the elements in

in this way). It follows that
r_{I+I}(x)=r_{I^{\prime}+I^{\prime}}(x)
. Having this in mind, we can verify condition (ii) by hand: The sums

and

are unique,
s
appears exactly two times as

and similarly

appears exactly two times as

(iii)

This condition is trivial by construction: We can express

for all elements

. ∎

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in I+I^{\prime\prime}$}).

In this section, we implement the “masking” step, which transforms the problem of testing the exact sumset condition
X=A+A
to a more relaxed version testing whether
X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
. Specifically, for the integers, we prove the following reduction:

See
1.5

In
Section

4.1
, we provide a proof of
Lemma

1.5
, and in the following
Section

4.2
, we design the appropriate analogue for finite field vector spaces.

4.1
Masking for
\mathbb{Z}

Unfortunately, the proof of
Lemma

1.5
turns out to be quite technical. While in the positioning step working with integers had several advantages, in the masking step, we have to deal with several drawbacks. One particular drawback is as follows: In order to “mask” a set
A
, we want to find another set
M
such that
A+M
is predictable without knowing
A
. Over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
this easily works by choosing
M=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
, in which case
A+M=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
without having any knowledge about
A
(other than the trivial condition that
A
be nonempty). Over the integers, the natural analogue is to pick an interval
M=[0,N]
. However, then
A+M=[\min(A),\max(A)+N]
, even if we presuppose that
A\subseteq[0,N]
, which is inconveniently not independent of
A
. Throughout the proof, this issue will introduce several less important side cases. The first step in dealing with it is the following lemma:

4.1
.

Let
X,Y,U\subseteq[0,n]
. We can compute sets
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},U^{\prime}\subseteq[0,32n]
in time
\poly(n)
such that:

(i)

\exists A\subseteq U:X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.

(ii)

All elements in
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},U^{\prime}
are even.

(iii)

\min(X^{\prime})=\min(Y^{\prime})=\min(U^{\prime})=0
,
\max(X^{\prime})=\max(Y^{\prime})=32n
,
\max(U^{\prime})=16n
.

We write
2X=\{2x:x\in X\}
. The construction is simple (emphasizing again that our goal is not to optimize the constants but rather to achieve simple proofs):

\displaystyle X^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{0\}\cup(2X+8n)\cup\{32n\},

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2[0,3n]\cup(2Y+8n)\cup 2[8n,16n],

\displaystyle U^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{0\}\cup(2U+4n)\cup\{16n\}.

For the soundness, let
A\subseteq U
satisfy that
X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
. We choose
A^{\prime}=\{0\}\cup(2A+4n)\cup\{16n\}
.
Noting that

A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}=\{0\}\cup(2A+4n)\cup(2A+2A+8n)\cup\{16n\}\cup(2A+20n)\cup\{32n\},

it is straightforward to verify that
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
and that
X^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime}
.

For the converse direction, suppose that
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
satisfies that
X^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\subseteq Y^{\prime}
. Let
A
be the part of
A^{\prime}
that falls in the interval
[4n,6n]
where we divide each number by
2
(here we use that
A^{\prime}
contains only even numbers since
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
). Using again that
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
we can express
A^{\prime}
as

. It follows easily that

.
∎

By first applying the transformation from

4.1
, we assume throughout that
X,Y,U\subseteq[0,32n]
with
\min(X)=\min(Y)=\min(U)=0

. Furthermore, if

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $k\in I+I$}),

Soundness

Suppose that there is some set

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{1,i}

\displaystyle=[0,N]

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in I$}),

A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}

A\subseteq U

X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
. Let, in analogy to before,

that falls into the length-

In comparison to the previous subsection, the masking proof over

4.2
Masking for
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
.

Let
p

X,Y,U,V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}

\poly(p^{d})
we can construct sets
X^{\prime},U^{\prime},V^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d^{\prime}}
, where
d^{\prime}=2d+3
, such that:

\exists A\subseteq U,B\subseteq V:X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime},B^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime}:X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}.

If
X\not\subseteq Y
then the reduction is trivial, so assume that
X\subseteq Y
and let
Z=Y\setminus X
. Consider the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d+3}
; by embedding
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
into the first
d
coordinates, say, we view
Z
also as a subset of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d+3}
. We exploit the remaining three dimensions by letting
a,b,w\perp Z
denote nonzero pairwise orthogonal vectors; let
W\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
denote the subspace orthogonal to
w
. We define
X^{\prime}=\{(i,x):i\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d+3},x\in X^{\prime}_{i}\}
and similarly
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
, where
X^{\prime}_{i},U^{\prime}_{i},V^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
are the sets defined as follows:

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{a+b}

\displaystyle=Y,

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in W\setminus\{a+b\}$}),

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{i+w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in W$}),

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{i-w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in Z\cup\{a\}$, only relevant if $p>2$})

and

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{a}

\displaystyle=U,

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{z}

\displaystyle=\{z\}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $z\in Z$}),

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{i+w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in W\setminus\{a+b\}$}),

and

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{b}

\displaystyle=V,

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{a+b-z}

\displaystyle=\{0\}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $z\in Z$}),

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{-w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}.

Moreover, all sets
X^{\prime}_{i},U^{\prime}_{i},V^{\prime}_{i}
, which we have not specified here, are set to be empty.

Soundness

For the soundness, assume that there is a pair
A\subseteq U,B\subseteq V
with
X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y
. We construct sets
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime}
with
X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
. Write
A^{\prime}=\{(i,x):i\in I,x\in A^{\prime}_{i}\}
and similarly for
B^{\prime}
where the sets
A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{a}

\displaystyle=A,

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{z}

\displaystyle=\{z\}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $z\in Z$}),

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{i+w}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in W\setminus\{a+b\}$}),

and

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{b}

\displaystyle=B,

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{a+b-z}

\displaystyle=\{0\}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $z\in Z$}),

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{-w}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}.

Again, all sets
A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}
that we have not specified here are assumed to be empty. From the construction, it is immediate that
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
and that
B^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime}
.

Next, we show that
A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}
. To this end, we verify that
A^{\prime}_{i}+B^{\prime}_{j}\subseteq X^{\prime}_{i+j}
for all
i,j\in I
. This is trivial whenever
X^{\prime}_{i+j}=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
which covers almost all cases. The only other relevant cases are as follows:

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{a}+B^{\prime}_{b}

\displaystyle=A+B\subseteq Y=X^{\prime}_{a+b},

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{z}+B^{\prime}_{a+b-z}

\displaystyle=\{z\}+\{0\}\subseteq Z\subseteq Y=X^{\prime}_{a+b}.

Let us finally check that
X^{\prime}\subseteq A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
. That is, we check that for all
k
,
X^{\prime}_{k}\subseteq\bigcup_{i+j=k}(A^{\prime}_{i}+B^{\prime}_{j})
. The most interesting case is

X^{\prime}_{a+b}=Y=X\cup Z\subseteq(A+B)\cup\bigcup_{z\in Z}\{z\}=(A^{\prime}_{a}+B^{\prime}_{b})\cup\bigcup_{z\in Z}(A^{\prime}_{z}+B^{\prime}_{a+b-z}).

The less interesting cases are

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}+\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{i+w}+B^{\prime}_{-w}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in W\setminus\{a+b\}$}),

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{i+w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}+B

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{i+w-b}+B^{\prime}_{b}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $i\in W$}),

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{z-w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=\{z\}+\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{z}+B^{\prime}_{-w}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $z\in Z$}),

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{a-w}

\displaystyle=\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=A+\smash{\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}}

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}_{a}+B^{\prime}_{-w}.

(Strictly speaking, here we assumed that
A,B\neq\emptyset
. This can easily be enforced by a trivial corner case.)

Completeness

Suppose that there are sets
A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime},B^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime}
with
X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
; we show that there are sets
A\subseteq U,B\subseteq V
with
X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y
. We again write
A^{\prime}_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}:(i,x)\in A^{\prime}\}
for
i\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d+3}
, and similarly for
B^{\prime}
. From our construction, it is clear that

X^{\prime}_{k}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d+3}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}(A^{\prime}_{i}+B^{\prime}_{j})\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d+3}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}(U^{\prime}_{i}+V^{\prime}_{j}).

In particular, consider the case
k=a+b
. By carefully checking all combinations of
i
and
j
above, we in fact have that

X^{\prime}_{a+b}=(A^{\prime}_{a}+B^{\prime}_{b})\cup\bigcup_{z\in Z}((A^{\prime}_{z}+B^{\prime}_{a+b-z})\cup(A^{\prime}_{a+b-z}+B^{\prime}_{z})).

By the construction of
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
, the big union on the right becomes exactly a subset
Z^{\prime}\subseteq Z
. Therefore, and using that
X^{\prime}_{a+b}=Y
, we have that
Z^{\prime}\cup(A^{\prime}_{a}+B^{\prime}_{b})=Y
. In particular,
X\subseteq A^{\prime}_{a}+B^{\prime}_{b}
, and we have thus witnessed sets
A=A^{\prime}_{a}
and
B=B^{\prime}_{b}
as desired.
∎

5
Reduction from 3-SAT

In this section, we employ the technical lemmas from Sections
3
and
4
to show a polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to Sumset Recognition. We start with the integer case in
Section

5.1
and then deal with finite fields in
Section

5.2
.

5.1
Reduction for
\mathbb{Z}

Given a 3-SAT instance, a formula
\phi
on
n
variables and
m
clauses, the reduction outputs a Sumset Recognition instance; a set
X_{\phi}\subseteq[0,2^{57}(n+m)^{4}]
,
such that
\phi
is satisfiable if an only if there exists
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
such that
X_{\phi}=A+A
.
The main technical part in this section is a reduction for a related problem where given
\phi
, we output three sets
X,Y,U
such that
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if
exists
A\subseteq U
such that
X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
. In this reduction, we will employ the following Sidon set construction. Recall that we call a set
S

Sidon
if it does not contain nontrivial solutions to the equation
i+j=k+\ell
, or equivalently, if
|S+S|=\binom{|S|+1}{2}
.

5.1
Sidon Sets over
\mathbb{Z}
.

In time
\poly(n)
we can compute a Sidon set
S\subseteq[0,4n^{2}]
of size
n
.

This result is classic, but we include a quick proof based on Erdős and Turán’s construction
[
27
]
for the sake of completeness. By Bertrand’s postulate, there is a prime number
n\leq p<2n
, and we can find
p
by, say, Eratosthenes’ sieve in deterministic time
O(n\log\log n)
. As Erdős and Turán proved
[
27
]
, the set

S^{\prime}=\{(x,x^{2}):x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\}

is a Sidon set over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
. Indeed, suppose that
(i,i^{2})+(j,j^{2})=(k,k^{2})+(\ell,\ell^{2})
. Equivalently, we can write
i-k=\ell-j
and
i^{2}-k^{2}=\ell^{2}-j^{2}
. Thus, either
i-k=\ell-j=0
or
i+k=\ell+j
. In the latter case, in combination with the initial assumption, we find that
i=\ell
and
j=k
. This set
S^{\prime}
can be lifted to the integers by embedding
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
into the integers via
(x,y)\mapsto x\cdot 2p+y
(viewing
0\leq x,y<p
as integers). The resulting set
S
satisfies that
S\subseteq[0,2p(p-1)+1]\subseteq[0,4n^{2}]
and can clearly be constructed in time
\poly(n)
.
∎

5.2
.

In time
\poly(n,m)
we can compute integers
s_{1},\dots,s_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
such that:

(i)

0\leq s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\leq N
and
4N\leq t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\leq 5N
, where
N=16(n+m)^{2}
.

(ii)

Whenever
|2s_{i}-s_{j}-s_{k}|<4
, then
i=j=k
.

(iii)

Whenever
|s_{i}-s_{j}+t_{k}-t_{\ell}|<4
, then
i=j
and
k=\ell
.

The previous lemma yields a Sidon set
\{s_{1}^{\prime},\dots,s_{n}^{\prime},t_{1}^{\prime},\dots,t_{m}^{\prime}\}\subseteq[0,4(n+m)^{2}]
; we then choose
s_{i}:=4s_{i}^{\prime}
and
t_{i}:=4t_{i}^{\prime}+64(n+m)^{2}
. Property (i) is fulfilled by construction. For property (ii), note that
|2s_{i}-s_{j}-s_{k}|<4
if and only if
2s_{i}^{\prime}-s_{j}^{\prime}-s_{k}^{\prime}=0
. The Sidon assumption guarantees that this happens if and only if
i=j=k
. Property (iii) follows by a similar argument.
∎

5.3
Reduction from 3-SAT for
\mathbb{Z}
.

There is an algorithm that, given a 3-SAT formula
\phi
on
n
variables and
m
clauses, outputs sets
X,Y,U\subseteq[0,160(n+m)^{2}]
,
such that

\text{$\phi$ is satisfiable}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A\subseteq U:X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y.

The algorithm runs in time
O(n^{2}+m^{2})
.

We denote the variables of
\phi
by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Let
s_{1},\dots,s_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
denote the integers as constructed by
Corollary

5.2
.
We then define the three sets as follows. Let
X:=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}
, and let

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\text{ appears}\\
\text{positively in}\\
\text{the $k$-th clause.}\end{subarray}}\{t_{k}-s_{i}-1\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\text{ appears}\\
\text{negatively in}\\
\text{the $k$-th clause.}\end{subarray}}\{t_{k}-s_{i}\}

\displaystyle\qquad(\text{for $k\in[m]$}),

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(s_{i}+\{0,1\})\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[m]\end{subarray}}C_{k},

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(2s_{i}+\{0,2\})\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[n]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}(s_{i}+s_{j}+\{0,1,2\})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
k\in[m]\end{subarray}}(s_{i}+\{0,1\}+C_{k})\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\in[m]\end{subarray}}(C_{k}+C_{\ell}).

Corollary

5.2
(i) guarantees that
0\leq s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\leq N
and
4N\leq t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\leq 5N
where
N=16(n+m)^{2}
. Hence, all sets contain only nonnegative integers of size at most
10N\leq 160(n+m)^{2}
.

Soundness.

Suppose that
\phi
is satisfiable and let
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denote a satisfying assignment of
\phi
. We show that there is a set
A\subseteq U
satisfying that
X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
. We choose

A=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\{s_{i}+\alpha_{i}\}\cup\bigcup_{k\in[m]}C_{k}.

Observe that
A\subseteq U
. In addition, it is not too difficult to see that
A+A\subseteq Y
, since:

\displaystyle A+A

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i,j\in[n]}\{s_{i}+s_{j}+\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
k\in[m]\end{subarray}}(s_{i}+\alpha_{i}+C_{k})\cup\bigcup_{k,\ell\in[m]}(C_{k}+C_{\ell}).

The second and third terms are clearly included in the third and fourth terms of
Y
. For the first term we distinguish two cases: If
i=j
, then
\{s_{i}+s_{j}+\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\}\subseteq 2s_{i}+\{0,2\}
which is included in the first term of
Y
. Otherwise, if
i\neq j
, then
\{s_{i}+s_{j}+\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}\}\subseteq s_{i}+s_{j}+\{0,1,2\}
is clearly included in the second term of
Y
.

Let us finally prove that
X\subseteq A+A
. As
X=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\}
we need to show, for each
k\in[m]
, that
t_{k}\in A+A
.
Since
\alpha
satisfies
\phi
, it satisfies some literal of some variable
x_{i}
in the
k
-th clause.
Consider the term
s_{i}+\alpha_{i}+C_{k}\subseteq A+A
. Assume, on the one hand, that
x_{i}
appears positively in this clause, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}=1
.
Then by the definition of
C_{k}
we have
t_{k}=s_{i}+1+(t_{k}-s_{i}-1)\in A+A
. On the other hand, if
x_{i}
appears negatively in
C_{k}
(and thus
\alpha_{0}=0
), then
t_{k}=s_{i}+0+(t_{k}-s_{i})\in A+A
.

Completeness

Before we start with the actual completeness proof, let us first assert that our construction admits the following properties:

•

2s_{i}+1\not\in Y
. Of course
2s_{i}+1
does not appear in
2s_{i}+\{0,2\}
, but it requires an argument that
2s_{1}+1
does not spuriously appear in the other terms in
Y
: Since
Y\subseteq U+U
, any other expression would lead to a nontrivial solution of
2s_{i}=s_{j}+s_{k}\pm 3
, or
2s_{i}=s_{j}+t_{k}-s_{\ell}\pm 3
or
2s_{i}=t_{k}-s_{j}+t_{\ell}-s_{h}\pm 3
. The first equation is ruled out by
Corollary

5.2
(ii), and the latter two are ruled out by
Corollary

5.2
(i).

•

The only possible representations of
t_{k}
in
U+U
are
t_{k}=(s_{i}+0)+(t_{k}-s_{i})
and
t_{k}=(s_{i}+1)+(t_{k}-s_{i}-1)
(whenever the variable
x_{i}
appears in the
k
-th clause). Indeed, any other representation would take one of the forms
t_{k}=s_{i}+s_{j}\pm 3
,
t_{k}=s_{i}+(t_{\ell}-s_{j})\pm 3
(for
i\neq j
or
k\neq\ell
), or
t_{k}=(t_{\ell}-s_{i})+(t_{h}-s_{j})\pm 3
. The first and last equations are ruled out by
Corollary

5.2
(i), and the second one is ruled out by
Corollary

5.2
(iii).

Equipped with these insights, we continue with the completeness proof. So, assume that there is a set
A\subseteq U
satisfying that
X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y
; we construct a satisfying assignment of
\phi
. The first claim implies that it cannot simultaneously happen that
s_{i}+0\in A
and that
s_{i}+1\in A
(as otherwise
A+A\not\subseteq Y
). In light of this, we let
\alpha_{i}=1
if and only if
s_{i}+1\in A
. To prove that
\alpha
is satisfying, consider any clause
k
. Since
X\subseteq A+A
, we in particular have that
t_{k}\in A+A\subseteq U+U
. By the second claim,
t_{k}
must be represented either in the form of
t_{k}=(s_{i}+0)+(t_{k}-s_{i})
or
t_{k}=(s_{i}+1)+(t_{j}-s_{i}-1)
. In the former case we have that
s_{i}+0\in A
(and thus
\alpha_{i}=0
) and
t_{k}-s_{i}\in U
(and thus
x_{i}
appears negatively in the
k
-th clause). In the latter case we have that
s_{i}+1\in A
(and thus
\alpha_{i}=1
) and
t_{k}-s_{i}-1\in U
(and thus
x_{i}
appears positively in the
k
-th clause). In both cases, the clause is satisfied.
∎

Let us now show a polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to the Sumset Recognition problem. Since the problem is clearly in NP, this will prove that the problem is NP-complete.
Given a 3-SAT instance; a formula
\phi
on
n
variables and
m
clauses, apply the algorithm from
Lemma

5.3
to obtain three sets
X,Y,U\subseteq[0,160(n+m)^{2}]\subseteq[0,2^{8}(n+m)^{2}]
, such that:

\text{$\phi$ is satisfiable}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A\subseteq U:X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y.

Then, apply the algorithm from
Lemma

1.5
on
X,Y,U
to obtain two sets
U^{\prime},X^{\prime}\subseteq[0,2^{17}\cdot(2^{8}(n+m)^{2})^{2}]=[0,2^{33}(n+m)^{4}]
, such that:

\exists A\subseteq U:X\subseteq A+A\subseteq Y\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}:X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}.

As a corollary,
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if
\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}
such that
X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}
.
Finally, apply the algorithm from
Lemma

1.4
on
X^{\prime},U^{\prime}
to obtain a Sumset Recognition instance
X_{\phi}\subseteq[0,2^{24}\cdot 2^{33}(n+m)^{4}]=[0,2^{57}(n+m)^{4}]
, such that:

\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}:X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}:X_{\phi}=A^{\prime\prime}+A^{\prime\prime}.

Thus,
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if
\exists A^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
such that
A^{\prime\prime}+A^{\prime\prime}=X_{\phi}
.

The running times of the above algorithms are all
\poly(n,m)
. Hence, we showed a polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to Sumset Recognition.
∎

5.2
Reduction for
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}

In this section, we rework the reduction for finite fields. We start with a similar construction of Sidon sets over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
.

5.4
Sidon Sets over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
.

Let
p
be a prime, and let
d=2\lceil\log_{p}(n)\rceil
. In time
\poly(n)
we can construct a Sidon set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
of size
n
.

We follow a classic construction dating back to Bose and Ray-Chaudhuri
[
10
]
; see also
[
21
]
. Let
q=p^{d}
. By representing the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
, it suffices to construct a Sidon set in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
. For odd
p
, such a set is for instance
S=\{(x,x^{2}):x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
; the proof is identical to the argument in
Lemma

5.1
. We take
S=\{(x,x^{3}):x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
if instead
p=2

[
10
]
. Polynomial arithmetic can be implemented in time
\poly(q)
, and so the construction time of
S
is also bounded by
\poly(n)
.
∎

5.5
Reduction from 3-SAT for
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
.

There is an algorithm that, given a 3-SAT formula
\phi
on
n
variables and
m
clauses, outputs sets
X,Y,U,V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
, where
d=2\lceil\log_{p}(n+m)\rceil+5
, such that

\text{$\phi$ is satisfiable}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists A\subseteq U,B\subseteq V:X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y.

The algorithm runs in time
\poly(n,m)
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
denote the variables in
\phi
. Further, let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
, where
d=2\lceil\log_{p}(2n+m)\rceil+3\leq 2\lceil\log_{p}(n+m)\rceil+5
, denote the elements of a Sidon set of size
2n+m
as constructed by
Lemma

5.4
. We use the three remaining dimensions to obtain pairwise orthogonal nonzero vectors
v_{0},v_{1},w
that are also orthogonal to
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
. We then define:

\displaystyle C_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\text{ appears}\\
\text{positively in}\\
\text{the $k$-th clause.}\end{subarray}}\{t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{1}\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\text{ appears}\\
\text{negatively in}\\
\text{the $k$-th clause.}\end{subarray}}\{t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{0}\},

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(a_{i}+\{v_{0},v_{1}\}),

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}(b_{i}-\{v_{0},v_{1}\})\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[m]\end{subarray}}(w+C_{k}),

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\{a_{i}+b_{i}\}\cup\bigcup_{k\in[m]}\{w+t_{k}\},

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\{a_{i}+b_{i}\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[n]\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}(a_{i}+b_{j}+\{v_{0},v_{1}\}-\{v_{0},v_{1}\})\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
k\in[m]\end{subarray}}(a_{i}+\{v_{0},v_{1}\}+w+C_{k}).

Soundness.

Suppose that
\phi
is satisfiable and let
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{n}
denote a satisfying assignment of
\phi
. We show that there are sets
A\subseteq U
and
B\subseteq V
satisfying that
X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y
. To this end, we pick

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\{a_{i}+v_{\alpha_{i}}\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}\{b_{i}-v_{\alpha_{i}}\}\cup\bigcup_{k\in[m]}(w+C_{k}).

Observe that
A\subseteq U
and
B\subseteq V
, and that

A+B=\bigcup_{i,j\in[n]}\{a_{i}+b_{j}+v_{\alpha_{i}}-v_{\alpha_{j}}\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]\\
k\in[m]\end{subarray}}(a_{i}+v_{\alpha_{i}}+w+C_{k})\subseteq Y.

We finally show that
X\subseteq A+A
. On the one hand, it is clear that
a_{i}+b_{i}\in A+B
for all
i\in[n]
. On the other hand, focus on an arbitrary clause
k\in[m]
. Since
\alpha
is a satisfying assignment, there must be a variable
x_{i}
which satisfies the
k
-th clause. If the variable appears positively (and thus
\alpha_{i}=1
), then
w+t_{k}=(a_{i}+v_{1})+(w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{1})\in A+B
. Otherwise, if the variable appears negatively (and thus
\alpha_{i}=0
), we find
w+t_{k}=(a_{i}+v_{0})+(w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{0})\in A+B
.

Completeness

For the completeness proof, we again first gather some structural observations. Specifically:

•

a_{i}+b_{i}+v_{0}-v_{1}\not\in Y
and
a_{i}+b_{i}+v_{1}-v_{0}\not\in Y
. We check that neither of these elements appears spuriously in the second and third terms in
Y
. For the third term, this is clear as it contains the shift
+w
, which is orthogonal to
a_{i}+b_{i}\pm v_{0}\pm v_{1}
. And if the second term would contain, say,
a_{i}+b_{i}+v_{0}-v_{1}
, then we would also obtain a solution to the equation
a_{i}+b_{i}=a_{j}+b_{h}
(where
j\neq h
). This is ruled out since
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
stem from a common Sidon set. The same argument shows that
a_{i}+b_{i}
can be represented in exactly two ways in
U+V
, namely as
a_{i}+b_{i}=(a_{i}+v_{0})+(b_{i}-v_{0})
and
a_{i}+b_{i}=(a_{i}+v_{1})+(b_{1}-v_{1})
.

•

The only possible representations of
w+t_{k}
in
U+V
are
t_{k}=(a_{i}+v_{0})+(w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{0})
and
t_{k}=(a_{i}+v_{1})+(w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{1})
(whenever the variable
x_{i}
appears in the
k
-th clause). Indeed, any other representation would necessarily take the form
w+t_{k}=a_{i}+b_{j}
or
w+t_{k}=a_{i}+(w+t_{\ell}-a_{j})
(where
i\neq j
or
k\neq\ell
). The first equation is ruled out since
w
is orthogonal to all other terms in the equation, and the second out is ruled out since
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
stem from a common Sidon set.

We are ready to complete the proof. So assume that there are sets
A\subseteq U
and
B\subseteq V
satisfying that
X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y
; we construct a satisfying assignment of
\phi
. The first claim implies that either
a_{i}+v_{0}\in A
or that
a_{i}+v_{1}\in A
. Indeed, if both elements were missing we would violate the condition that
a_{i}+b_{i}\in X\subseteq A+B
(which requires at least one of these two elements to be present). Suppose instead that both elements are present in
A
. Then the condition
a_{i}+b_{i}\in X\subseteq A+B
entails that
b_{i}-v_{0}\in B
or
b_{i}-v_{1}\in B
. Either case leads to a contradiction as then also
a_{i}+b_{i}+v_{1}-v_{0}\in A+B
or
a_{i}+b_{i}+v_{0}-v_{1}\in A+B
, respectively, both of which contradict
A+B\subseteq Y
by the first claim.

Therefore, let
\alpha_{i}=1
if and only if
a_{i}+v_{1}\in A
. To prove that
\alpha
is satisfying, consider any clause
k
. As
X\subseteq A+B
, in particular we have that
w+t_{k}\in A+B\subseteq U+V
. By the second claim,
t_{k}
must be represented either in the form of
t_{k}=(a_{i}+v_{0})+(w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{0})
or
t_{k}=(a_{i}+v_{1})+(w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{1})
. In the former case we have that
a_{i}+v_{0}\in A
(and thus
\alpha_{i}=0
) and
w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{0}\in V
(and thus
x_{i}
appears negatively in the
k
-th clause). In the latter case we have that
a_{i}+v_{1}\in A
(and thus
\alpha_{i}=1
) and
w+t_{k}-a_{i}-v_{1}\in V
(and thus
x_{i}
appears positively in the
k
-th clause). In both cases, the clause is satisfied.
∎

This proof is analogous to the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, and for this reason, we will be brief. We concatenate the three reductions in
Lemmas

5.5
,
4.2
and
3.6
. Specifically, by
Lemma

5.5
we transform a given 3-SAT instance
\phi
on
n
variables and
\poly(n)
clauses into sets
X,Y,U,V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
, where
d=O(\log_{p}n)
, which is then transformed by
Lemma

4.2
into sets
X^{\prime},U^{\prime},V^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d^{\prime}}
, where
d^{\prime}=O(\log_{p}n)
, which are finally transformed by
Lemma

3.6
into a set
X^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, where
d^{\prime\prime}=O(\log_{p}n)
.
Lemmas

5.5
,
4.2
and
3.6
guarantee that
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if
\exists A\subseteq U,B\subseteq V
with
X\subseteq A+B\subseteq Y
if and only if
\exists A^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime},B^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime}
with
X^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
if and only if
\exists A^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
with
X^{\prime\prime}=A^{\prime\prime}+A^{\prime\prime}
. We have thereby transformed the initial 3-SUM instance into an equivalent instance of the Sumset Recognition problem over
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d^{\prime\prime}}
, where
|\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d^{\prime\prime}}|=p^{d^{\prime\prime}}=\poly(n)
. The total running time is
\poly(n)
.
∎

5.3
ETH-Hardness

Finally, we analyze our hardness reduction through a more fine-grained lens and derive exact conditional lower bounds for the Sumset Recognition problem based on the
Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)
. This hypothesis, as introduced by Impagliazzo and Paturi
[
30
]
, postulates that the 3-SAT problem cannot be solved in time
2^{\epsilon n}\poly(n)
for all
\epsilon>0
. We rely on the well-established Sparsification Lemma to better control the number of clauses in a 3-SAT instance:

5.6
Sparsification Lemma
[
31
]
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
c_{\varepsilon}>0
and an algorithm that, given a 3-SAT formula
\phi
on
n
variables, computes in
2^{\varepsilon n}\poly(n)
time 3-SAT instances
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}
with
k\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
such that
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if
\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{i}
is satisfiable. Moreover, the number of clauses in each formula is at most
c_{\varepsilon}n
.

We show that if there is an algorithm for Sumset Recognition running in time
2^{o(n^{1/4})}
, then there is also an algorithm for 3-SAT running in time
2^{o(n)}
, thus refuting the Exponential Time Hypothesis.
More formally, assume, for the sake of contradiction, that for any
\delta>0
, there exists an algorithm for Sumset Recognition running in time
2^{\delta N^{1/4}}\poly(N)

on instances taken from
[0,N]
. Let us show that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is an algorithm for 3-SAT whose running time is
2^{\varepsilon n}\poly(n)
.

Let
\phi
be a 3-SAT formula on
n
variables and let
\varepsilon>0
.
Apply the algorithm from
Lemma

5.6
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\varepsilon/2
to obtain
k\leq 2^{\varepsilon^{\prime}n}
formulas
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}
such that each formula has
at most
c_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}n
clauses, and
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if
\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{i}
is satisfiable. Its running time is
2^{\varepsilon^{\prime}n}\poly(n)
.
Now, apply the reduction from 3-SAT to Sumset Recognition, given in the proof of
Theorem

1.2
, to each of the formulas.
The result of this step is
k
instances of Sumset Recognition
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\subseteq[0,N]
, where
N\leq 2^{60}(n+c_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}n)^{4}=2^{60}(1+c_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{4}n^{4}
.
Moreover,
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if one of the Sumset Recognition instances has a sumset root.
Hence, to decide if
\phi
is satisfiable, apply the algorithm for Sumset Recognition on each of those instances. The total running time is then

2^{\varepsilon^{\prime}n}\cdot\poly(n)+k\cdot 2^{\delta(2^{60}(1+c_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})^{4}n^{4})^{1/4}}\cdot\poly(N)=2^{\varepsilon^{\prime}n}\cdot 2^{\delta 2^{15}(1+c_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})n}\cdot\poly(n)
.
By setting
\delta\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/(2^{15}(1+c_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}))
, the running time becomes
2^{2\varepsilon^{\prime}n}\cdot\poly(n)=2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot\poly(n)
.
∎

By exactly the same proof, we can also establish that it is ETH-hard to solve the Sumset Recognition problem over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
in time
2^{o(|\mathbb{F}|^{1/4})}
.
