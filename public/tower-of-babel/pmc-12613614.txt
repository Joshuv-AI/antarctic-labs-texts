Background

Salinity stress is a primary environmental factor limiting the survival, secondary metabolism, and productivity of plant species [1]. Under salinity stress, plants undergo several biochemical and physiological changes, including osmotic stress, ion toxicity, oxidative damage, and disruption of nutrient balance, which eventually lead to reduced cell turgor, inhibition of cell division and expansion, decreased photosynthesis, and impaired nutrient uptake [2]. Plants have evolved diverse adaptation strategies to cope with salt stress, ranging from morphological adjustments to sophisticated molecular mechanisms, including growth restriction [3], transcriptional reprogramming [4], reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging and osmolyte accumulation [5].

The molecular mechanisms underlying salt tolerance involve two major categories of stress-inducible proteins: functional proteins that directly protect cellular structures, and regulatory proteins that modulate gene expression networks [6]. Regulatory proteins, including various protein kinases and transcription factors (TFs), play crucial roles in orchestrating complex stress response pathways [7]. For instance, in Arabidopsis thaliana, heterotrimeric G-proteins regulate lateral root development and stem cell niche expansion under stress conditions [8]. Similarly, in rice, ethylene signaling through the ETR1-CTR1-EIN2-EIN3/EIL1 cascade activates AP2/ERF transcription factors and enhances antioxidant gene expression to mitigate oxidative damage under salt stress [9].

Haloxylon ammodendron, a xerophytic chenopod shrub native to the deserts of Central Asia, exhibits remarkable tolerance to extreme environmental conditions, including high salinity, drought, and temperature fluctuations. Its exceptional adaptability has made it a cornerstone species in desertification control projects across northwestern China, where it has demonstrated significant ecological success [10]. Unlike many glycophytes that employ avoidance strategies, H. ammodendron grows in salt-affected soils. This adaptation relies on specialized physiological and molecular mechanisms that remain largely unexplored. The few existing studies have primarily focused on its drought responses [11], leaving its salt adaptation strategies, particularly the temporal dynamics of its molecular responses across different salinity levels, largely uncharacterized. This knowledge gap severely limits our ability to optimize desert restoration efforts and develop stress-resilient plant resources for arid regions.

Transcriptomic approaches have enhanced our understanding of plant responses to environmental stresses by enabling comprehensive profiling of gene expression dynamics. Recent advances in RNA sequencing technologies have facilitated the elucidation of molecular mechanisms underlying salt adaptation in various plant species. Salt tolerance mechanisms have been extensively investigated in species such as Sophora alopecuroides [12], Panicum virgatum L. [13], and Betula platyphylla [14]. These studies have revealed species-specific adaptations, including enhanced phenylpropanoid biosynthesis in S. alopecuroides [12], chromosome doubling-mediated tolerance in switchgrass [13], and PP2C-mediated signaling in B. platyphylla [14]. So far, there remains little understanding of how H. ammodendron responds to high-salinity.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of H. ammodendron's salt tolerance mechanisms through an integrated temporal transcriptomic approach. Our objectives were to: (1) characterize the temporal dynamics of gene expression under varying salinity levels (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mmol/L) at three critical time points (7, 21, and 30 days); (2) identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with salt stress responses; (3) construct gene co-expression networks using WGCNA to identify key modules and hub genes; and (4) elucidate the biological pathways underlying H. ammodendron's salt adaptation. This represents the first comprehensive temporal transcriptomic analysis of H. ammodendron under varying salinity concentrations, addressing a significant gap in our understanding of extremophile adaptation strategies and providing valuable insights for ecological restoration in salt-affected regions.

Methods

Experimental plant cultivation

The study was conducted in the nursery garden of Wuwei City, Gansu Province, located in the northwestern region of China (37°9′N, 102°6′E), an important implementation base for the Three-North Shelter Forest Program. A total of 54 healthy H. ammodendron seedlings were cultivated in sandy loam soil characterized by loose structure, excellent aeration and drainage properties, non-cohesive texture, and favorable tillability. However, this soil type exhibited relatively poor nutrient and water retention capacity. While suitable for early seedling establishment and juvenile growth, it demonstrates limited capacity for sustained mature plant development. The seedlings were initially established through seeds under standardized nursery conditions and received two flood irrigation treatments post-germination to ensure proper establishment. After three months of cultivation, the four-month-old seedlings were subjected to salt stress treatments, representing the optimal developmental stage for stress response analysis while maintaining enough plant vigor for experimental manipulation. To prepare the salt solutions, we used two neutral salts (NaCl and Na2SO4) and two alkaline salts (Na2CO3 and NaHCO3). The salts were mixed in a molar ratio of NaCl:Na2SO4:NaHCO3:Na2CO3 = 1:18:18:1. This follows the methodology established for saline-alkali stress simulation studies [15]. This approach allowed us to systematically assess H. ammodendron's response to varying levels of mixed salt stress that combines both neutral and alkaline components. The total salt concentration was set at 100 mmol/L, and the amount of each salt was calculated based on this concentration and the molar ratio. The salts were dissolved in deionized water to prepare six different salt concentrations (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mmol/L), establishing different concentration gradients (Y0, Y100, Y200, Y300, Y400, Y500). Y0 represents the treatment with a 0 mmol/L salt solution, Y100 represents irrigation with a 100 mmol/L salt solution, and so on. Sampling for sequencing was performed at 7, 21, and 30 days under the six distinct salinity stress conditions. Each treatment group comprised three biological replicates, resulting in a total of 18 experimental groups (6 concentrations × 3 time points). A standardized sample nomenclature was adopted. For instance, Y100071 identifies the first biological replicate exposed to 100 mmol/L salinity stress for 7 days. Y200212 denotes the second biological replicate subjected to 200 mmol/L salinity stress for 21 days. This experimental design with 54 H. ammodendron plants (18 groups × 3 replicates) facilitated comprehensive analysis of concentration-dependent and temporal responses to salinity stress. After preparing the salt solutions, all plants received a single irrigation with their respective treatment solutions on day 1, with no subsequent watering throughout the 30-day experiment. This protocol was designed to simulate the episodic water availability typical of H. ammodendron's natural desert habitat, creating combined salt-drought stress conditions that reflect realistic field environments, though this approach limits our ability to distinguish salt-specific from drought-specific molecular responses.

EC analysis

Soil electrical conductivity (EC) was measured using a calibrated conductivity tester (HM-WSY, Shandong Hengmei Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) at 7 and 21 days post-treatment. Measurements were not performed on day 30 due to logistical constraints. For each sample, measurement points were selected to represent soil conditions around plant roots while avoiding stones and major root masses. The electrode probes were inserted vertically into the soil to a standardized depth of 10–15 cm, ensuring complete soil contact. After instrument stabilization (approximately 30 s), EC values and soil temperature were recorded. Temperature compensation was applied when necessary to ensure accuracy. To minimize spatial variation, measurements were taken at three locations adjacent to the root zone of each plant. This soil-based measurement approach reflects the actual ionic environment experienced by plant roots following salt solution irrigation, distinguishing our methodology from hydroponic culture systems.

pH evaluation of soil samples

Soil pH was determined using a standardized slurry method. Fresh soil samples were processed immediately after collection to avoid alterations in chemical properties. For each sample, 10 g of soil with large particles removed was placed in a beaker containing 25 mL of distilled water (1:2.5 soil-to-water ratio). The suspension was thoroughly agitated for 1 min to ensure homogenization and then allowed to equilibrate for 30 min. Subsequently, pH 5–9 precision test strips were immersed in the clarified supernatant for 30 s, and readings were taken by comparing the resulting color against a standardized color chart. The pH values were classified according to established soil science criteria: neutral (pH 7.0), slightly acidic (pH 6.5–7.0), weakly acidic (pH 6.0–6.5), acidic (pH 5.5–6.0), strongly acidic (pH < 5.5), slightly alkaline (pH 7.0–7.5), weakly alkaline (pH 7.5–8.0), alkaline (pH 8.0–8.5), and strongly alkaline (pH ≥ 8.5).

Transcriptome sequencing

Young apical stem tissues were selected for analysis due to their established role as central hubs for ion transport and salt tolerance coordination, with recent research demonstrating that key transporters limit Na + accumulation specifically in stem tissues to protect sensitive aerial parts from ionic toxicity [16]. These samples were subsequently sent with dry ice to Majorbio company (Beijing, China) for high-throughput Illumina sequencing (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). After processing the sequencing data with fastp v0.18.0, the high-throughput output yielded a large volume of high-quality raw data, with the majority of bases achieving a quality score of Q30 or higher. To ensure that the raw reads are of sufficiently high quality, low-quality reads, such as reads containing adapters, were all removed to obtain clean reads. Additionally, the obtained clean reads were mapped to the H. ammodendron reference genome (https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/The_genome_of_Haloxylon_ammodendron/17128424/1 (6 December 2021)) using HISAT 2 software. Then, the mapped reads were assembled and quantified using FeatureCount and StringTie to obtain the counts, Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), and Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values.

ClusterGVis analysis of all genes

To systematically investigate concentration-dependent molecular responses, the 54 samples were categorized into four groups: control (Y0), low-concentration (Y100, Y200), medium-concentration (Y300, Y400), and high-concentration (Y500). Temporal analysis was performed using the ClusterGVis package in R, based on average TPM values of all 41,647 genes across the three time points for each concentration group. This analysis stratified each concentration group into eight distinct temporal expression clusters (C1-C8), representing genes with similar expression dynamics over time. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were subsequently performed for all genes within each cluster to identify associated biological functions and pathways. Given that each cluster typically yielded hundreds of enriched terms, we selected the top 5 most significant terms (lowest q-values) from each cluster as representative of that cluster's primary biological functions, employing a systematic "representative subset" approach. This strategy yielded 40 representative terms per concentration group (8 clusters × 5 terms), which were then subjected to comparative analysis to identify concentration-specific biological responses.

Differential expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (v1.34.0) in R (v4.1.0), which employs negative binomial generalized linear models with empirical Bayes shrinkage for dispersion estimation. Prior to analysis, genes with zero counts across all samples were filtered to ensure robust statistical inference. DEGs were identified through pairwise comparisons between control (Y0) and each salt treatment (Y100, Y200, Y300, Y400, Y500) at three time points (7, 21, and 30 days), resulting in 15 comparisons per time point. The DESeq() function performed Wald tests for significance testing, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied for multiple testing correction. DEGs were defined using stringent thresholds: |log2(Fold Change)|≥ 1.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. The total number of DEGs across all 15 treatment comparisons was calculated for each time point (7, 21, and 30 days) to identify the most critical period of transcriptional response to salt stress.

WGCNA analysis

WGCNA was employed to identify gene modules correlated with physiological traits (pH and EC), enabling detection of coordinated gene networks and hub genes beyond traditional differential expression analysis [17]. After establishing that the 21 st day was the critical stress duration, we identified 19,399 stress-responsive genes by filtering those with fold-change > 1 between control and treatment groups across all salt concentrations. Then, we constructed the co-expression networks using the WGCNA package in R, which is a scale-free network construction method that identifies gene clusters with highly correlated expression profiles. Genes exhibiting similar expression profiles were grouped into the same module afterwards. Based on the expression profiles of all genes and the similarity of module eigengenes (ME), the biological modules were subsequently categorized. The parameters for module classification were set as follows: minimum power value at 3, mergeCutHeight threshold at 0.25, and a minimum module size of 50. The Pearson correlation coefficients between modules and sample traits were calculated based on the ME values. Modules exhibiting a Pearson correlation coefficient less than −0.5 and a p-value below 0.05 were deemed to be significantly associated with EC and pH. The biological roles of genes within the modules were elucidated through GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Gene connectivity within modules and correlations between modules were assessed using the WGCNA package. Following the identification of genes exhibiting robust correlation and statistical significance (p < 0.05) within each key module, these genes were integrated and compared against the reference genome of Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. (taxid: 3702) using blast in NCBI (BLAST: Basic Local Alignment Search Tool). Subsequently, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was reconstructed utilizing STRING (STRING: functional protein association networks) and Cytoscape version 3.10.0. Within this network, hub genes or functionally significant candidate genes were pinpointed based on their maximal node connectivity.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation of mRNA expression patterns

To validate the RNA-seq results, qRT-PCR was performed on four hub genes identified through WGCNA analysis (SS00930/ATPD, SS39716/TIG1, SS38705/RPL13, and SS19480/RPL5). Total RNA was extracted from stem tissues of H. ammodendron seedlings treated with 300 mmol/L salt solution at 7, 21, and 30 days, with three biological replicates per time point. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 μg total RNA using HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Gene-specific primers were designed and synthesized by Genewiz (Suzhou, China), and the housekeeping gene SS00067, which showed stable expression across all conditions with a coefficient of variation < 15% in our RNA-seq data, was used as an internal reference. qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 40 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method with the 7-day samples serving as calibrators. All reactions were performed in technical duplicates, and the specificity of amplification was verified by melting curve analysis.

Results

Changes in EC values and pH in H. ammodendron under various salinity stresses

To elucidate the effects of salinity on H. ammodendron, we measured EC and pH of the soil surrounding plants irrigated with saline solutions at concentrations of 0, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mmol/L for 7 and 21 days. As shown in Fig. 1 and Table S1, both the pH and EC of the soil increased proportionally with the concentration of the applied saline solution. EC showed highly significant concentration-dependent increases (P < 0.001). At 7 days, EC rose dramatically from control levels (114.4 ± 24.3 μS/cm) to maximum stress conditions (1,552.4 ± 481.8 μS/cm). At 21 days, EC increased from 26.7 ± 4.6 μS/cm to 243.4 ± 169.1 μS/cm under 500 mmol/L treatment (Fig. 1A). While soil pH showed an increasing trend from 6.5 ± 0.0 to 8.8 ± 1.4 at 7 days and from 5.8 ± 0.6 to 8.8 ± 0.6 at 21 days across the concentration gradient, differences between treatments were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), likely reflecting the buffering capacity of the desert soil matrix (Fig. 1B). The pronounced EC responses across all salt concentrations, coupled with the pH gradient effects, demonstrate that H. ammodendron was successfully exposed to a well-characterized spectrum of ionic and alkaline stress intensities, establishing a robust experimental foundation for subsequent transcriptomic analyses.

Sequencing quality and annotation results

A total of 2,376,490,434 raw reads were generated by transcriptome sequencing of 54 samples. After removing low-quality data, 38,875,706 to 58,739,714 clean reads were retained for each sample, and the Q20 in each sample was higher than 98%, the Q30 in each sample was higher than 95%, which indicated the good quality after cleaning. In addition, the average GC content of all samples was 42.29%. Overall, 41,647 genes were obtained by Trinity assembly. The mapping ratio of each sample in the reference transcriptome was 80.51%−85.51% (Table S2). The transcript detection analysis revealed an average detection rate of 79.8% with sequencing depths ranging from 7.34 × to 10.84 ×, and approximately 69% of detected transcripts achieved coverage depths ≥ 5 × (Table S3). Furthermore, A total of 27,850 genes with functional annotations were identified through Non-Redundant Protein Database (Nr), Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Pfam annotation, representing 66.87% of all obtained genes. Then, most of the clean reads could be mapped to the reference genome of H. ammodendron to gain the counts, FPKM and TPM (Table S4). These results showed that high-quality transcriptome data were obtained for further analyses.

Temporal analysis of all genes during salinity stress

As determined using the ClusterGVis package in R, the low-, medium-, and high-concentration groups were each successfully classified into eight significant clusters from C1 to C8. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, B, and C, the temporal expression profiles of genes in the low, medium, and high concentration groups exhibited distinct trends across the time gradient. Specifically, in the low-concentration group, gene expression levels initially increased at 7 days, decreased at 21 days, and then rebounded at 30 days (Fig. 2A). In the medium-concentration group, the temporal trend was characterized by a decrease at 7 days, an increase at 21 days, and stabilization at 30 days (Fig. 2B). In the high-concentration group, gene expression levels consistently increased from 7 to 21 days, followed by a decline at 30 days (Fig. 2C). Through comparative analysis of the time-series differences among the low, medium, and high concentration groups, it was revealed that the regulatory mechanisms underlying stress responses may vary depending on the salinity stress concentration.

Differential expression and enrichment analysis of genes

In total, 2,630 DEGs (1,264 up-regulated and 1,366 down-regulated) were identified at 7 days, 4,533 DEGs (3,005 up-regulated and 1,528 down-regulated) were identified at 21 days, 2,581 DEGs (1,182 up-regulated and 1,399 down-regulated) were identified at 30 days. By comparing the number of DEGs at 7, 21, and 30 days, it was found that the total number of DEGs in H. ammodendron was the highest at 21 days (Fig. 4). This suggests that 21 days may be the most critical time point for H. ammodendron to respond to salinity stress.

Validation of gene expression patterns by qRT-PCR

To confirm the reliability of our RNA-seq data, we performed qRT-PCR analysis on four hub genes identified through WGCNA. The qRT-PCR results showed strong concordance with the RNA-seq findings across all tested genes (Fig. 10, Table S7). All four hub genes exhibited a consistent temporal expression pattern characterized by high expression at 7 days, significant downregulation at 21 days, and partial recovery at 30 days post-treatment. Specifically, ATPD showed a 75% reduction in expression at 21 days compared to 7 days (from 0.88 ± 0.14 to 0.25 ± 0.15), followed by partial recovery at 30 days (0.25 ± 0.02). Similar patterns were observed for TIG1 (78% reduction), RPL13 (59% reduction), and RPL5 (73% reduction) at 21 days relative to 7 days. These results confirm that day 21 represents a critical transition point in H. ammodendron's molecular response to salt stress, characterized by coordinated downregulation of key regulatory genes involved in energy metabolism, protein synthesis, and transcriptional control.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying salt adaptation in H. ammodendron, an ecologically important desert plant with exceptional salt tolerance capabilities. Through comprehensive transcriptomic analyses, including time-series analysis, differential expression profiling, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis, we have revealed the complex dynamics of gene expression patterns and identified key regulatory hubs involved in salt stress responses. Our findings provide novel insights into the remarkable salt tolerance of H. ammodendron and its ecological significance in desert ecosystems.

Concentration-dependent molecular strategies reveal adaptive plasticity

Our analysis revealed remarkable plasticity in H. ammodendron's molecular responses across different salt concentrations, demonstrating finely tuned adaptation strategies specific to the severity of salt stress. Unlike model plants where salt responses typically involve early (minutes to hours) to intermediate (hours to days) phases [18], H. ammodendron exhibited distinct long-term patterns characteristic of extremophile adaptation strategies.

At low salt concentrations (100–200 mmol/L), gene expression initially increased at 7 days, decreased at 21 days, and rebounded at 30 days. This "increase–decrease-rebound" pattern primarily involved pathways related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis—particularly benzoxazinoid and isoflavonoid pathways—along with enhanced fatty acid oxidation and RNA processing. These metabolic adjustments reflect energy optimization strategies similar to those reported in other plants under mild stress conditions [19], but the temporal dynamics appear unique to H. ammodendron.

As salt concentrations increased to moderate levels (300–400 mmol/L), the expression pattern shifted to a "decrease-increase-stabilize" trend, with enhanced emphasis on ribosome biogenesis, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and cation transmembrane transport. This shift from metabolic adjustments to protein synthesis and ionic homeostasis likely represents a transition from growth maintenance under mild stress to cellular protection under moderate stress. Similar concentration-dependent shifts in stress response strategies have been observed in S. alopecuroides, another desert plant, though through different molecular pathways [20], suggesting convergent evolution of stress response plasticity in desert species.

Under high salt concentrations (500 mmol/L), H. ammodendron exhibited a third pattern of "consistent increase then decline" in gene expression, with unique enrichment in pathways involved in nuclear speck organization, vesicle-mediated transport, protein modification, and alternative metabolic pathways such as utanoate and arachidonic acid metabolism. This concentration-dependent activation of multiple stress response layers demonstrates a sophisticated hierarchical stress management system.

Temporal dynamics of salt stress responses in H. ammodendron

The time-course analysis of gene expression patterns revealed distinct temporal dynamics across different salt concentration treatments, highlighting a sophisticated time-dependent adaptive strategy in H. ammodendron. Our differential expression analysis identified 21 days as a critical time point, with 4,533 DEGs—significantly higher than at 7 days (2,630) or 30 days (2,581). This temporal pattern suggests that H. ammodendron undergoes a transition from initial stress perception to a coordinated adaptive response at approximately three weeks after salt exposure.

This timing differs from that observed in other halophytes such as S. alopecuroides, where major transcriptional reprogramming occurs earlier [12], suggesting that H. ammodendron may prioritize long-term adaptation over immediate responses. The delayed peak in transcriptional changes may represent an energy-conserving strategy characteristic of plants adapted to resource-limited desert environments, where premature or excessive stress responses could deplete valuable resources. This temporal signature may be a distinctive feature of H. ammodendron's adaptation strategy, contributing to its exceptional success in harsh desert conditions where salt stress is often chronic rather than acute.

Salt-responsive gene modules and functional coordination

By integrating our physiological measurements with transcriptomic data through WGCNA, we identified three key modules (ME4-yellow, ME6-red, and ME9-magenta) significantly associated with EC and pH traits at the critical 21-day time point. Functional enrichment analysis of these modules revealed coordinated regulation of six major functional categories: photosynthesis, metabolic processes, stress responses, gene expression and protein synthesis, cellular structure, and energy metabolism—forming a comprehensive salt adaptation network.

The enrichment of photosynthesis-related pathways such as photosynthesis, light reaction, photosystem II assembly, photosynthesis proteins, chloroplast thylakoid and chloroplast stroma suggests that maintaining photosynthetic efficiency is crucial for H. ammodendron's salt tolerance. This aligns with findings in other halophytes that photosynthetic adjustments serve as a primary adaptation mechanism under salt stress, highlighting a specialized photosynthetic adaptation mechanism [21]. Metabolic pathways involving amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and stress response pathways related to photoprotection and vitamin B6 metabolism collectively indicate a multifaceted approach to maintaining cellular homeostasis. In particular, the significant enrichment of ATP-dependent processes across all three key modules suggests that energy metabolism serves as a central hub in H. ammodendron's salt stress response network, coordinating various adaptation mechanisms.

Core metabolic pathways underlying salt adaptation

Detailed pathway analysis revealed sophisticated carbon fixation mechanisms underlying H. ammodendron's photosynthetic adaptation to salt stress. The carbon fixation pathway demonstrated coordinated regulation of both Calvin-Benson cycle and C4 dicarboxylic acid cycle components within ME4 and ME6 modules. The carbon fixation process initiates with atmospheric CO2 assimilation via the C4 dicarboxylic acid cycle in mesophyll cells. Here, phosphoenolpyruvate serves as the primary CO2 acceptor to form oxaloacetate, which is subsequently converted to malate and aspartate through malate dehydrogenase (SS23515/SS34938/SS14854, SS29039) and alanine transaminase (SS10535). These four-carbon intermediates are then transported to bundle-sheath cells, where CO2 is decarboxylated and concentrated for efficient refixation through the Calvin-Benson cycle. Within bundle-sheath cells, the Calvin-Benson cycle operates through coordinated expression of key carboxylating enzymes, including multiple ribulose-bisphosphate carboxylase large chain subunits (SS41127/SS41082/SS41073/SS41029/SS34263/SS34264, among others), phosphoglycerate kinase (SS29827), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (SS27039/SS38023), while carbon skeletons are regenerated via transketolase (SS24547), fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (SS40277), and phosphoribulokinase (SS07133) to ensure continuous ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate availability. The pathway integration with glycolysis/gluconeogenesis through fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (SS21365/SS16398) and sedoheptulose-1,7-bisphosphatase (SS28364) facilitates efficient carbon partitioning toward carbohydrate synthesis and energy metabolism, demonstrating that this dual-pathway carbon fixation strategy represents a critical metabolic adaptation enabling H. ammodendron to maintain photosynthetic performance while minimizing photorespiratory losses under salt stress conditions.

The alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism pathway revealed critical amino acid metabolic adjustments essential for H. ammodendron's salt tolerance. Within the ME6 module, nitrogen assimilation was enhanced through upregulated glutamine synthetase (SS21576) and asparagine synthase (SS34443), facilitating efficient incorporation of inorganic nitrogen into organic compounds. Subsequently, aspartate aminotransferase (SS08636/SS28732) and alanine transaminase (SS10535) coordinated amino acid interconversion, maintaining metabolic flexibility and enabling dynamic nitrogen redistribution among different amino acid pools. These enzymatic activities supported the synthesis of osmotically active amino acids crucial for cellular osmoregulation, providing essential osmolytes for water retention and ion balance under salt stress conditions. The pathway demonstrated extensive metabolic integration through connections to glycolysis and the citrate cycle, supplying carbon skeletons for energy metabolism, while adenylosuccinate lyase (SS10142) linked amino acid metabolism to purine biosynthesis, supporting nucleotide synthesis essential for maintaining cellular function. Furthermore, the pathway's coordination with multiple biosynthetic routes, including arginine biosynthesis, lysine biosynthesis, and pantothenate-CoA biosynthesis, demonstrates comprehensive metabolic reprogramming that supplies precursors for stress-protective compounds. This integrated nitrogen metabolism strategy highlights the central role of amino acid metabolic networks in H. ammodendron's multifaceted approach to salt stress adaptation and cellular homeostasis maintenance.

The citrate cycle analysis within the ME9 module confirmed energy metabolism as the central hub coordinating H. ammodendron's salt stress responses. Key TCA cycle enzymes, including citrate synthase (SS04583) and isocitrate dehydrogenase (SS01467), showed significant enrichment, ensuring sustained ATP production and maintaining metabolic flux under salt stress conditions. The pathway's strategic position at the intersection of carbohydrate catabolism and biosynthetic processes enables efficient energy generation while providing essential metabolic intermediates for stress adaptation. Notably, 2-oxoglutarate serves as a critical branch point connecting energy metabolism to amino acid biosynthesis pathways, particularly arginine and glutamate metabolism, facilitating both nitrogen assimilation and osmolyte production. The integration with ascorbate metabolism through oxaloacetate highlights the TCA cycle's role in supporting antioxidant defense systems essential for managing salt-induced oxidative stress. Furthermore, the connection to glycolysis/gluconeogenesis through phosphoenolpyruvate demonstrates metabolic flexibility, allowing H. ammodendron to efficiently switch between catabolic and anabolic processes as energy demands fluctuate under varying salt concentrations. This central positioning of the TCA cycle validates our earlier finding that ATP-dependent processes serve as the core regulatory network, orchestrating diverse physiological adaptations and maintaining cellular energy homeostasis crucial for H. ammodendron's exceptional salt tolerance.

Hub gene network architecture and regulatory coordination

Network analysis identified ATPD (ATP synthase δ subunit) as the most connected hub gene, along with five sub-hub genes (TIG1, RPL13, RPL5, RPS13, and Q9LK47_ARATH). ATPD plays a critical role in energy production, with its overexpression shown to enhance photosynthetic efficiency in rice [22] and maintain ATP synthase integrity in Arabidopsis [23]. In the context of H. ammodendron, ATPD likely facilitates efficient energy production under salt stress, supporting energy-intensive processes such as ion transport, osmotic adjustment, and antioxidant defense. The centrality of ATPD in the network suggests that H. ammodendron may have evolved specialized energy metabolism strategies to support its exceptional salt tolerance, distinguishing it from other salt-adapted plants.

The five sub-hub genes collectively expand the regulatory network through roles in transcriptional control (TIG1) and protein synthesis (RPL13, RPL5, RPS13, Q9LK47_ARATH). TIG1, a TCP transcription factor that regulates cell elongation [24], may help maintain growth under salt stress, while the ribosomal proteins likely ensure proper protein synthesis and turnover. RPL5's involvement in telomere regulation [25] further suggests a role in maintaining cellular integrity during prolonged stress exposure, which may be particularly relevant for a long-lived perennial species like H. ammodendron.

Together, these hub genes form an integrated regulatory network that coordinates energy production, gene expression, and protein synthesis to maintain cellular function under salt stress. Unlike simpler, more linear stress response pathways reported in many glycophytes, H. ammodendron appears to employ a complex, highly interconnected network that enables precise coordination across multiple cellular systems. This integrated approach likely underpins H. ammodendron's exceptional salt tolerance capacity and ecological success in extreme environments.

Study limitations and future directions

Several aspects of our experimental design warrant discussion. The single irrigation protocol simulated realistic desert conditions where H. ammodendron experiences combined salt-drought stress, but this approach prevented separation of salt-specific and drought-specific responses. Our transcriptomic data therefore reflect adaptation to multiple concurrent stresses rather than isolated salinity tolerance. Additionally, focusing on stem tissues may have missed tissue-specific responses in roots and leaves, while our three-timepoint analysis could have overlooked early or prolonged adaptive responses. Furthermore, while we successfully measured soil parameters (EC and pH) to confirm stress establishment and provide environmental context for transcriptomic analysis, we did not include direct measurements of plant physiological parameters such as chlorophyll content, osmolyte accumulation, or ion homeostasis, which would have provided valuable validation of our molecular findings and enhanced understanding of phenotype-genotype relationships. Future studies should include drought-only controls and regular irrigation to isolate individual stress contributions, examine multiple tissue types, employ finer temporal resolution, and integrate comprehensive physiological characterization with transcriptomic analysis to provide more complete mechanistic insights. Nonetheless, this represents the first comprehensive temporal transcriptomic analysis of H. ammodendron and identifies key molecular targets for understanding stress tolerance in desert plants.

Conclusions

Informed by the salt tolerance traits of H. ammodendron and our preliminary experimental findings, we established six different concentration levels as the stress condition to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying its salt adaptation. Temporal analysis using ClusterGVis revealed distinct gene expression trends across low, medium, and high concentration groups, with unique patterns of increase, decrease, and stabilization over time, suggesting varied regulatory mechanisms under different salinity stress levels. Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses identified unique biological functions and pathways for each group, with the medium-concentration group showing the most diverse enrichment terms and categories. Then, through DEseq2 analysis in R, we identified 2,630 DEGs at 7 days, 4,533 DEGs at 21 days, and 2,581 DEGs at 30 days, and three modules (ME4, ME6 and ME9) related to salt response in the H. ammodendron after WGCNA analysis. Enrichment analyses highlighted key biological pathways involved in salinity stress. Detailed pathway analysis further revealed three core metabolic networks underlying H. ammodendron's salt adaptation: carbon fixation pathways demonstrating dual Calvin-Benson and C4 cycle regulation for enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, alanine-aspartate-glutamate metabolism facilitating nitrogen assimilation and osmotic regulation, and the citrate cycle serving as the central energy hub coordinating ATP production and metabolic flexibility. These interconnected pathways collectively demonstrate H. ammodendron's sophisticated metabolic reprogramming strategy under salt stress conditions. Ultimately, our comprehensive network analysis identified 91 hub genes associated with salt stress responses at the critical 21-day time point, with ATPD emerging as the central hub gene, supported by five key regulatory genes (TIG, RPL13, RPL5, RPS13, and Q9LK47_ARATH). These findings provide novel insights into the molecular regulatory network underlying salt adaptation in H. ammodendron, highlighting the coordinated role of energy metabolism and protein synthesis in mediating salt tolerance at the transcriptomic level.
