Multidisciplinary approach to implementing ferric carboxymaltose for IV iron replacement in heart failure with iron deficiency
Authors: Marat Fudim, Francesco Ferrante, Cassandra M. Vorgang, Carolyn M. Rosner, Robert J. Mentz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIron deficiency (ID) is a common comorbidity in patients with heart failure (HF). Treatment with intravenous (IV) iron is recommended by the ACC/AHA/HFSA and ESC guidelines to improve HF symptoms, exercise capacity, and quality of life, and to reduce the risk of hospitalization. Ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) is indicated for ID in adults with HF (NYHA class II/III) to improve exercise capacity. There are challenges in ensuring that eligible patients with HF and ID achieve iron repletion and associated optimal outcomes in routine practice. These challenges include inpatient formulary restrictions which only allow for low-dose IV irons and care transitions when the course of IV iron treatment spans the inpatient and outpatient setting. Despite the ease of administration as a single rapid infusion of up to 1000 mg of iron, inpatient and outpatient formularies do not always include FCM. This guide shares a general workflow used at university-based and private hospital systems, to facilitate routine iron assessment and IV iron repletion in a typical HF care pathway. Perspectives from the cardiologist, nurse, advanced practice provider (APP), and pharmacist roles provide a comprehensive view and emphasize the importance of a multidisciplinary, collaborative approach to optimize outcomes for patients with HF and ID.

Introduction

Iron deficiency (ID) is a common co-morbidity in patients with heart failure (HF) and is associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [1,2]. Treatment with intravenous (IV) iron is recommended by the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/ Heart Failure Society of America (ACC/AHA/HFSA) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for patients with HF and ID [3–5]. However, achieving iron repletion and optimal outcomes remains challenging among these patients.

This guide outlines the epidemiology of ID in HF and treatment approaches, and shares real-world workflows used at academic and non-academic hospital systems, to enable routine iron assessment and IV iron repletion in a typical HF care pathway. Perspectives from the cardiologist, nurse, advanced practice provider (APP), and pharmacist roles are included for a holistic view of this process, highlighting the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to optimize outcomes for patients with HF and ID.

Iron deficiency in heart failure: pathophysiology and prevalence

In HF, iron homeostasis is often disrupted due to chronic inflammation, reduced dietary intake, malabsorption, and increased blood loss causing depleted iron stores or reduced iron availability [6,7]. Iron is essential for oxygen storage, transport, use in cells, and for mitochondrial functioning and aerobic respiration [8]. Cardiac myocytes are highly energy-dependent and are particularly susceptible to the negative effects of ID [8]. ID is associated with cardiac remodeling, leading to HF progression [6,8]. Whereas fatigue, weakness, impaired mental function, and reduced exercise capacity are features of both ID and HF [9,10], ID is an independent risk factor for increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [1,2,11].

Across left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) categories, more than 40% of patients with HF have co-morbid ID [2,12]. A study of 4,422 patients with HF demonstrated that 68% had ID (serum ferritin <100 ng/mL, or serum ferritin 100–299 ng/mL with transferrin saturation [TSAT] <20%), with ID being more common in patients with more severe HF symptoms, those with anemia or preserved ejection fraction (as compared to HF with reduced ejection fraction [HFrEF]), and among women [2,4].

Treatment guidelines and clinical trial data

The AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines recommend obtaining iron studies for all patients with HF (Table S1) [3]. IV iron replacement is recommended to improve functional status and quality of life (QoL) in patients with HFrEF and ID with or without anemia (see Table S2 for details of trials supporting these recommendations) [3].

The ESC guidelines recommend periodic screening for ID for all patients with HF (ID defined as ferritin <100 ng/mL or 100–299 ng/mL if TSAT is <20%) [4,5]. Therapy with ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) is recommended in patients with ID and symptomatic HF with LVEF <45% to alleviate HF symptoms and improve exercise capacity and QoL (Table S1); IV iron supplementation is recommended in patients with ID and symptomatic HFrEF or HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction to alleviate HF symptoms and improve QoL [4,5]. In patients recently hospitalized for HF, with LVEF <50% and ID, IV FCM is recommended to reduce the risk of HF re-hospitalization [4].

In a meta-analysis of IV iron therapy for patients with HF and ID, FCM versus placebo significantly reduced the composite outcome of HF re-hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality [13]. Patients on FCM showed improvement in exercise capacity compared with those on placebo [14,15]. Additionally, in the Phase II IRONOUT trial, high-dose oral iron did not improve exercise capacity over 16 weeks in patients with HFrEF and ID [16]. These clinical trial data support the use of IV iron over oral iron in patients with ID and HF.

A recent network meta-analysis of 13 clinical trials evaluated the effect of IV irons (FCM, ferric derisomaltose, and iron sucrose), oral irons (ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, sucrosomial iron, ferric hydroxide polymaltose complex, and iron polysaccharide), and placebo on functional and clinical outcomes in patients with HF [17]. IV iron versus placebo resulted in significant improvements in 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), ferritin levels, and HF hospitalizations with moderate to high certainty [17]. Oral iron resulted in comparable but not statistically significant improvements in 6MWD and inconsistent improvements in ferritin levels [17]. Although neither oral nor IV irons were associated with significant reductions in all-cause or cardiovascular mortality, IV irons showed a trend toward benefit [17].

In a separate network meta-analysis that compared the efficacy of FCM versus those of ferric derisomaltose and iron sucrose in patients with HFrEF and ID, all three IV irons improved functional capacity, ferritin, and TSAT levels, with FCM providing the most consistent increases in TSAT levels and ferric derisomaltose providing the largest improvement in point estimates for ferritin levels [18]. FCM also significantly reduced the risk of HF hospitalization compared with placebo (risk ratio [95% confidence interval]: 0.80 [0.69–0.92]), whereas ferric derisomaltose (0.80 [0.61–1.04]) and iron sucrose (0.41 [0.15–1.07]) showed similar but nonsignificant reductions in HF hospitalizations [18].

Although it has historically been more difficult to identify effective therapies for patients who have HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) than for those with HFrEF [19], in recent years there have been important advances in HFpEF as well (e.g., sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) [20,21]. Thus, although the majority of clinical trials of IV iron to date have focused on patients with below-normal LVEF, the emerging data on functional status benefits with IV iron in HFpEF (such as those seen in FAIR-HFpEF [22] and in a retrospective study [23]) suggest the potential for benefit in clinical outcomes as well. Nonetheless, further recommendations will require clear evidence of efficacy and safety in adequately powered randomized trials.

FCM for the treatment of ID in HF

FCM is a U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved treatment for ID in adults with HF and New York Heart Association class II/III, to improve exercise capacity [24]. FCM provides a dose of up to 1,000 mg of iron administered as a single rapid infusion without a test dose, as compared with other low-dose IV irons that may require multiple doses and clinic visits. This is particularly beneficial for patients with HF, who often have a high visit burden, thereby potentially reducing healthcare resource utilization [25].

Inpatient screening and diagnosis

The inpatient team screens for ID during hospitalization, following the ACC/AHA/HFSA and ESC guidelines for the diagnosis and management of anemia and ID in patients with HF [3,4]. According to these guidelines, ID is defined as ferritin <100 μg/L, or 100–300 μg/L (by ACC/AHA/HFSA) or 100–299 ng/mL (by ESC) if TSAT <20% [3,4]. Additionally, newer data suggest that TSAT <20%, rather than ferritin, may be a better way to define ID in HF [26].

Recommended timing for ID screening is upon admission to the inpatient setting, when laboratory evaluation including cardiac biomarkers (where appropriate) and basic metabolic panel are conducted. Including iron studies is a simple additional task that supports appropriate evaluation and management. The integrated HF care path includes iron studies as a checkbox in the order set of the electronic health record. The order set is executed at HF admission and 1–3 months after receiving IV iron. The rounding clinician and/or APP team documents the type of IV iron and the total dose received to ensure administration of a complete replacement dose. APPs and other clinical team members should collaborate with pharmacists for proper IV iron dosing.

Transitions of care

Each institution’s multidisciplinary team should develop a standardized transition protocol to address variations in inpatient iron repletion protocols, formulations, and dosing strategies.

To facilitate transitions of care, all patient information including previous lab work and iron dosing recommendations should be shared with the new provider by the referring individual. Patients may need to be referred elsewhere due to insurance coverage and/or travel constraints – an infusion center locator website may be a useful resource in this situation. Depending on the institution, either the nurse, pharmacist, or APP may initiate the transition process; the appropriate referring provider should be identified to ensure a streamlined workflow.

Patients are seen in the HF clinic at 7 days post-discharge, then return to their HF clinician. A protocolized approach to post-discharge care is strongly emphasized, with heavy reliance on Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT) and remote monitoring clinics. For patients with barriers to adherence or missed follow-up appointments, support through additional outreach (phone/e-mail) should be incorporated into the protocol.

Transition from inpatient to outpatient setting

A challenge in transitioning from inpatient to outpatient care arises when hospital formularies utilize low-dose IV irons, which often require multiple administrations. Patients may be discharged with incomplete iron repletion, which requires continuation of treatment in the outpatient setting. Therefore, it may be beneficial for patients with HF in the inpatient setting to receive a loading dose of up to 1,000 mg (based on weight and hemoglobin levels) prior to discharge [24]. This will allow for considerable iron repletion before their follow-up visits for the remaining infusions [27].

Patients are seen in the outpatient advanced HF clinic for hospital follow-up and GDMT titration. TSAT/ferritin levels are assessed around 7-days post-discharge (if not assessed during their inpatient stay) or at the first outpatient appointment, then every 3–6 months, or at least annually after iron repletion (Figure 2). Iron status is also evaluated if there is a change in clinical status, e.g., decompensated or worsening HF and hospitalization. The most recent iron studies are noted under the current HF GDMT status, to highlight TSAT and ferritin levels with indication of repletion or need for repletion. Although follow-up plans are tailored to patients, a common goal is to schedule three visits within 30 days post-discharge, which include the HF/cardiology team at least twice and a primary care provider or transitional clinic once. If GDMT needs to be up-titrated after hospital discharge, which is commonly the case, the patient is seen every 2 weeks. If iron repletion is needed, a clinician assigns a care plan and completes the IV iron order set, alerting the infusion center that a patient should be scheduled for treatment. The infusion clinic completes the pre-authorization and notifies the ordering provider of any coverage gaps. Having multiple options for infusion services across the system can improve access to therapy, with HF GDMT clinics providing both IV iron prescription and implementation. Outpatient screening and diagnosis

Following the AHA/ACC/HFSA guidelines and ESC guidelines, routine iron studies are encouraged in the HF clinic at least 3–12 months post-IV iron treatment. Physicians, APPs, clinicians, and GDMT or HF pharmacists screen patients for ID along with their GDMT titration when they visit the outpatient setting for their routine low- to moderate-risk HF appointment every 3–6 months or annually. If iron repletion is needed, treatment is ordered through a care plan at the same institution or referred to an infusion center if travel constraints exist.

Treatment initiation and administration

Dosing and infusion

FCM can be administered intravenously via infusion or as a slow IV push at approximately 100 mg (2 mL) per minute for a dose of 500 mg or 750 mg of FCM, or over 15 minutes for a 1,000 mg dose of FCM. For patients with ID and HF, the dosage of FCM depends on body weight and hemoglobin levels (see Table 1 for details). FCM is supplied in a 750 mg iron/15 mL vial or 100 mg iron/2 mL vial. In the first year of the HEART-FID trial, participants receiving FCM were given an average of 1,808.9 mg of iron, exceeding the typical course of 1,500 mg [29]. Individuals followed through the 3 years received an overall cumulative dose of 2,316.9 mg of FCM [29]. In the trial, study investigators implemented FCM dosing as per the label [28]. Monitoring, adverse reactions, and contraindications

In clinical practice, IV iron products are well tolerated. FCM does not require a pre-medication test dose, but patients receiving a dose should be monitored for hypersensitivity reactions for at least 30 minutes after administration and until clinically stable following completion of each administration [24]. Serious anaphylactic/anaphylactoid reactions have been reported in clinical trials in 0.1% of patients receiving FCM [24]. Other serious adverse reactions related to hypersensitivity reactions may include rash, pruritus, urticaria, wheezing, or hypotension, which have been reported in 1.5% of patients receiving FCM [24]. Additionally, for patients at risk for low serum phosphate and in those who require a repeat course of treatment within 3 months, serum phosphate levels should be monitored for symptomatic hypophosphatemia [24]. Possible risk factors for hypophosphatemia include a history of gastrointestinal disorders related to the malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins or phosphate, inflammatory bowel disease, hyperparathyroidism, concurrent or prior use of medications affecting proximal renal tubular function, vitamin D deficiency, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, and malnutrition [24]. Most cases of hypophosphatemia occurred after repeated exposure to FCM in patients with no reported history of renal impairment, and were resolved in 3 months [24].

From the pharmacist’s perspective, contraindications and exclusion criteria used in clinical trials should be considered, e.g., recent acute coronary syndrome or stroke. Additionally, pharmacists should account for elemental iron in blood products received prior to IV iron. If the patient has received any IV iron products in the prior 90 days, any documented allergies to IV iron products need to be reconciled. Fishbane-type reactions and mild hypersensitivity reactions need to be differentiated from moderate or severe hypersensitivity reactions, as management strategies and ability to rechallenge or resume IV infusion depends on the severity of the reaction [30].

Reimbursement

Cost-effectiveness

In the outpatient setting, treatment considerations include insurance approval and overall cost of care (CoC) for patients. FCM was deemed to be highly cost-effective versus placebo in the US [31] and resulted in fewer IV iron infusions vs. low-dose irons, lesser outpatient visits/hospital admissions, decreases in inpatient days, reduced HF rehospitalizations, lowest unadjusted mean monthly total costs, and lowest adjusted total costs [13,25]. These findings suggest FCM’s potential decreases in overall CoC for patients.

Authorization

Coverage for FCM should be confirmed with payers, at times requiring prior authorization, before scheduling patients for outpatient administration. Occasionally, payers may require peer-to-peer review prior to approval or switching to a different payer-approved iron formulation. Accessible resources are essential to ensuring patients receive timely treatment.

Refusal of prior authorization is often due to incorrect diagnosis codes. Accurate International Classification of Diseases codes can help avoid this: Iron deficiency E61.1, Chronic IDA D50.9 and Chronic Systolic heart failure I50.22, acute on chronic systolic heart failure I50.23, HFrEF I50.2. Accurate codes also ensure that outpatient doses are adjusted for patients with ID alone (1000 mg and 500 mg) versus those with ID with anemia (750 mg × 2).

Barriers and solutions to improving access to FCM

The lack of timely iron studies and follow-up on results are barriers to identifying ID in patients with HF. Cost is a barrier for patients to receive FCM, prevents positioning on formularies, and requires additional time for the healthcare team to complete prior authorization or patient assistance program forms. Besides obtaining insurance authorization, increasing access to FCM requires addressing knowledge gaps regarding the use of IV iron for ID in the HF population, with many clinicians still prescribing oral iron as first-line treatment despite its ineffectiveness [16]. Clinical and economic benefits to the use of FCM in this patient population remain underrecognized. Therefore, to increase access to FCM on inpatient and outpatient formularies, the value of selecting FCM for the treatment of ID in patients with HF should be communicated to stakeholders by key contributors on Pharmacy & Therapy (P&T) Committees and Formulary Development teams.

The IV route of administration, which typically requires visiting an infusion clinic, is also a significant barrier. With FCM, fewer infusion appointments may improve adherence [32], and the decreased visit burden with potential reduced total chair time for treatment may be desirable to patients.

Tools that prompt testing for ID in HF, based on electronic medical records, are a promising solution. These include standardized checklists, documentation, selective alerts or advisories, and the ability to place serial orders. They may be used to create order sets and implement workflows that minimize healthcare provider time and fit into standard work types.

Multidisciplinary approach

In the inpatient setting, doctors, APPs, and HF pharmacists collaborate during screening, diagnosis, and inpatient therapy (Figure 3). A key point of collaboration is the transition to the outpatient setting, where treatment continuity must be maintained. For recently discharged patients, and those seen in outpatient clinics, the multidisciplinary team collaborates with the primary care physician, the GDMT clinic, and infusion clinic to ensure seamless progression of care. Conclusions

Despite guidelines recommending regular screening and treatment of ID in HF, significant barriers remain to optimal therapy. These include screening, ensuring optimal/payer-approved IV iron therapies are on inpatient and outpatient formularies, and managing transitions of care.

In patients with ID in HF, FCM increases functionality and QoL, reduces CoC and the composite outcome of re-hospitalizations and cardiovascular death [13,31]. Starting doses of up to 1,000 mg should be considered in the inpatient setting prior to discharge, to provide clinical and potential economic benefits to the patient.

As outlined, institutions should develop and implement a multidisciplinary approach to the diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring of ID in patients with HF, in inpatient and outpatient settings. The standardized workflows described here have been successfully implemented, ensuring access to guideline-recommended IV iron replacement in patients with HF, and ID assessment throughout the patient journey.

Future perspective

Given the prevalence of ID among patients with HF and the effects of iron repletion on exercise capacity, functional status, and QoL in these patients, it is critical that all patients with HF have access to appropriate screening, diagnosis, and treatment for ID. Raising awareness of the indications and clinical benefits of FCM and providing practical ways to incorporate it into standard HF workflows may ensure that patients with HF receive standard of care and achieve iron repletion. Care teams can adapt the workflows included in this article to existing processes in their institutions to coordinate transitions of care and provide treatment continuity for their patients.
