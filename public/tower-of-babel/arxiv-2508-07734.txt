Decorrelation phenomena in mixed moments for orthogonal families of L L -Functions and their applications: automorphic periods, Fourier coefficients of half-integral weight forms, and isotropy subgroups of Tate–Shafarevich groups
Shenghao Hua

Abstract
. The Keating–Snaith conjecture for orthogonal families may be viewed as analogous to a Gaussian distribution with a negative mean, and the possibility that mixed moments resemble a composition of independent moments, these two insights were combined and applied in Lester and Radziwiłł’s proof of quantum unique ergodicity for half-integral weight automorphic forms, via Soundararajan’s method under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH). This observation also yields a crucial and nontrivial saving in the resolution of certain arithmetic problems. Inspired by this, we select a series of typical mixed orthogonal families of L L -functions: GL 2 \mathrm{GL}_{2} quadratic twisted families. Under the assumptions of the GRH and the Generalized Ramanujan Conjecture, we prove upper bound estimates for their moments and present the following three arithmetic applications: i) The decorrelation of automorphic periods averaged over imaginary quadratic fields. ii) The decorrelation of Fourier coefficients of half-integral weight modular forms. iii) The decorrelation of the analytic orders of isotropy subgroups of Tate–Shafarevich groups of elliptic curves under quadratic twists.

1.
Introduction

Selberg
[
65
]
demonstrated that on the critical line, the real and imaginary parts of the logarithm of the Riemann zeta function are distributed like real Gaussian variables with mean zero and variance
\frac{1}{2}\log\log T
, as the imaginary part ranges from
T
to
2T

Montgomery
[
57
]
provided a prediction regarding the pair correlation of zeros of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line, describing the expected distribution of their spacings, then Dyson observed that this predicted distribution matches the pair correlation statistics of eigenvalues from the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble in random matrix theory.

Katz and Sarnak
[
42
]
extended this framework to families of
L
-functions over function fields, demonstrating that the distribution of their low-lying zeros corresponds to orthogonal, unitary, or symplectic symmetry types, depending on the family. Building on these ideas, Keating and Snaith
[
43
,
44
]
used the Circular Ensemble from random matrix theory to conjecture that the logarithm of the central values of
L
-functions in various families follows different Gaussian distributions, each with explicit leading constants.
This significantly generalized prior results by Conrey–Ghosh
[
13
,
14
]
and Balasubramanian–Conrey–Heath-Brown
[
1
]
.
Their framework was subsequently refined and extended by Conrey–Farmer
[
11
]
, Diaconu–Goldfeld–Hoffstein
[
16
]
, Conrey–Farmer–Keating–Rubinstein–Snaith
[
12
]
.
A corrected and clarified version in the function field setting was later given by Sawin
[
64
]
.

Current research on the central values of
L
-functions focuses primarily on three fronts: deriving asymptotic formulas for low-order moments, establishing lower bounds
[
63
]
, and obtaining upper bounds. For the latter, Soundararajan
[
70
]
developed a method based on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) that achieves the predicted order (up to a small power of
\log T
), while Harper’s refinement
[
25
]
removes the extraneous
\varepsilon
-power entirely.

In particular, for orthogonal families of
L
-functions, the moments of central values in the range between order
0
and
1
(excluding the endpoints) contribute a negative power of
\log
in their leading-order asymptotics.
This negative logarithmic contribution has concrete applications in arithmetic problems, as it leads to nontrivial savings in analytic estimates.

Traditionally, studies have focused on twisting a single automorphic form.
Building on Chandee’s work
[
7
]
on shifted moments of the Riemann zeta function, and later the work of Milinovich and Turnage-Butterbaugh
[
56
]
on integral moments of product of
L
-functions,
it has been observed that when multiple automorphic forms share the same symmetry type under a common twist, their mixed moments, i.e., products of their
L
-values, exhibit statistical independence under suitable conditions.

The combination of the decorrelation phenomenon observed in mixed moments and the negative logarithmic power bounds predicted by the Keating–Snaith conjecture for low-order moments (
0<k<1
) has emerged as a powerful framework in modern analytic number theory.
This interplay plays a central role in several recent breakthroughs.
For instance, Lester and Radziwiłł
[
51
]
proved quantum unique ergodicity for half-integral weight automorphic forms, Huang and Lester
[
35
]
investigated the quantum variance of dihedral Maass forms, while Blomer, Brumley, and Khayutin
[
3
]
proved the joint equidistribution conjecture proposed by Michel and Venkatesh in their 2006 ICM proceedings article
[
55
]
.
Blomer and Brumley
[
4
]
subsequently proved the joint equidistribution of orbits in arithmetic quotients.
Jääsaari, Lester, and Saha
[
38
]
established sign changes for coefficients of Siegel cusp forms of degree 2, and showed that the mass of Saito–Kurokawa lifted holomorphic cuspidal Hecke eigenforms for
\operatorname{Sp}_{4}(\mathbb{Z})
equidistributes on the Siegel modular variety as the weight tends to infinity
[
39
]
.
Hua, Huang, and Li
[
33
]
established a case of their joint Gaussian moment conjecture (with the holomorphic version discussed in Huang
[
36
]
). More recently, Chatzakos, Cherubini, Lester, and Risager
[
9
]
obtained a logarithmic improvement on Selberg’s longstanding bound for the error term in the hyperbolic circle problem over Heegner points with varying discriminants. Hua
[
34

demonstrated that for
0<p<2

\ell^{p}
-norm of some quadratic forms in holomorphic Hecke cusp forms tends to zero asymptotically with respect to expansion in a given orthonormal basis of Hecke eigenforms.

In this paper, we illustrate this phenomenon using quadratic twists of

forms as an example. The family of quadratic twists of a single

1.2.
Automorphic periods

Let
q
be a non-degenerate integral ternary quadratic form, and let
\mathrm{SO}_{q}
be its special orthogonal group.
There exists a quaternion algebra
B/\mathbb{Q}
with reduced norm
\mathrm{Nr}_{B}
and a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
such that

(\mathbb{Q}^{3},q)\simeq(B_{0},\lambda\mathrm{Nr}_{B}),

where
B_{0}
is the subspace of trace-zero quaternions. This identification allows us to replace
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
by a lattice
L\subset B_{0}
with quadratic form
q=\lambda\mathrm{Nr}_{B}
.

Under this isometry,
\mathrm{SO}_{q}\simeq G:=\mathrm{PB}^{\times}=B^{\times}/\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
, acting on
B_{0}
by conjugation. The genus classes correspond to

G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}_{f})/K_{f},

where
K_{f}
stabilizes
L\otimes\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For each positive integer
d
,
Representations
(a,b,c)
of
d
by
q
correspond to maximal tori

T\simeq\mathrm{Res}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{G}_{m}/\mathbb{G}_{m},

where
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d/\lambda})
. These tori give rise to adelic torus orbits

[Tg]:=T(\mathbb{Q})\backslash T(\mathbb{A})\cdot g\subset[G]:=G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash G(\mathbb{A}),

which encode arithmetic information on representations of
d
by
q
.

The equidistribution of these orbits comes from Duke’s theorem
[
18
]
and reduces to studying Weyl sums (automorphic periods)

P_{d}(\varphi):=\int_{[T]}\varphi(tg)\,dt,

for automorphic forms
\varphi
on
[G]
.

Waldspurger’s formula relates the square of these toric periods twisted by characters
\chi
to central values of automorphic
L
-functions:

\frac{\left|\int_{[T]}\varphi(tg)\chi(t)dt\right|^{2}}{\prod_{v}\langle\varphi_{v},\varphi_{v}\rangle}=c|d|^{-\frac{1}{2}}L\bigl(\frac{1}{2},\pi^{\mathrm{JL}}_{K}\otimes\chi,\frac{1}{2}\bigr)L\bigl(1,\pi^{\mathrm{JL}}_{K}\otimes\chi\bigr)^{-1}\prod_{v}\mathcal{P}_{v}(\varphi_{v},\chi_{v}),

where
\pi^{\mathrm{JL}}
is the Jacquet–Langlands transfer of
\pi
to
\mathrm{PGL}_{2}
,
\pi^{\mathrm{JL}}_{K}
its base change to
K
, and
\mathcal{P}_{v}
are local period integrals.

Without loss of generality, let
\lambda=1
.
For quadratic characters
\chi_{d}
associated to
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
, these periods encode the central values of quadratic twist
L
-functions

L\bigl(s,\pi^{\mathrm{JL}}\times\chi_{-d}\bigr).

Thus, the geometric automorphic periods over special torus orbits parametrized by discriminants
d
are intimately linked to the analytic behavior of quadratic twist
L
-functions for
\mathrm{GL}_{2}
automorphic forms.

Using Theorem
1.1
, we have the following decorrelation for the automorphic periods at special points.

1.3.
Fourier coefficients of half-integral weight modular forms

Let

In the theory of modular forms,

Among these, the Kohnen plus space

S^{+}_{k+1/2}(\Gamma_{0}(4N))

It consists of modular forms

This subspace is not only technically

Shimura’s correspondence between

Kohnen
[
46

of level
N

2k

As a result, each Hecke eigenform

The Fourier coefficients

A remarkable theorem of Waldspurger,

connects these Fourier coefficients

-functions.
Specifically, for a fundamental discriminant

(1.3)

|c(|d|)|^{2}=\frac{(k-1)!}{\pi^{k}}\frac{\langle g,g\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}L\left(\tfrac{1}{2},f\otimes\chi_{d}\right),

where

is the quadratic Dirichlet
character associated to

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

denotes the Petersson inner product.
This formula shows that the size of

Here, we consider the product of the Fourier coefficients of several half-integral weight modular forms and study the sum of their absolute values over a range of fundamental discriminants.

1.4.
Isotropy subgroups of Tate–Shafarevich groups of elliptic curves

For an elliptic curve
E/\mathbb{Q}
, we normalize its
L
-function so that its central value is at
s=\frac{1}{2}
.
The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture relates the first non-zero term in the Taylor expansion of the
L
-function at the central point to various arithmetic quantities, including the order of the Shafarevich–Tate group, the Tamagawa numbers, and the regulator.

Conjecture 1.7

(BSD conjecture)
.

For
E
over
\mathbb{Q}
,

\lim_{s\to\frac{1}{2}}\frac{L(s,E)}{(s-\frac{1}{2})^{r}}=\frac{\#\Sh(E)\Reg(E)\Omega_{E}\prod_{p\text{ prime}}c_{p}(E)}{\left(\#E_{\text{tors}}\right)^{2}},

where:

•

r=r(E)=\text{ord}_{s=\frac{1}{2}}L(E,s)
is the vanishing order of
L
-function at the central point, i.e., the analytic rank of
E
,

•

\Sh(E)
is the Shafarevich–Tate group,

•

\Reg(E)
is the regulator which is defined as the determinant of the Néron–Tate height pairing on the free part of
E(\mathbb{Q})
,

•

\Omega_{E}
is the real period,

•

c_{p}(E)
are the Tamagawa numbers at the finite places
p
of
\mathbb{Q}
,

•

E_{\text{tors}}
is the torsion subgroup of
E(\mathbb{Q})
.

For an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
, when the analytic rank, is at most 1, then the results of Gross–Zagier
[
23
]
and Kolyvagin
[
49
]
imply that the analytic rank coincides with the algebraic rank.
Therefore, for
L(\frac{1}{2},E)\neq 0
, we have
r=0
and
\Reg(E)=1
.
According to Goldfeld’s conjecture
[
21
]
,
50\%
of the quadratic twists
E^{(d)}
of
E
satisfy this condition.

Cassels constructed an alternating bilinear pairing

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\Sh(E)\times\Sh(E)\to\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z},

on the Tate–Shafarevich group
\Sh(E)
, which is non-degenerate assuming
\Sh(E)
is finite.
This non-degeneracy implies that
\Sh(E)
is a finite self-dual group under the pairing.
Consequently, the order of
\Sh(E)
must be a perfect square, since a non-degenerate alternating pairing can only exist on a finite abelian group whose order is a perfect square (or on a vector space of even dimension). Moreover, there exists an isotropic subgroup

H(E)\subseteq\Sh(E),

meaning that
\langle x,y\rangle=0
for all
x,y\in H(E)
, with
\#H(E)^{2}=\#\Sh(E)
, and such subgroups generate
\Sh(E)
.

Mazur
[
54
]
showed that
1\leq|E_{\mathrm{tors}}|\leq 12
.
From Pal
[
59
, Proposition 2.5]
, we know that for square-free integers
d
satisfying
\gcd(|d|,2N)=1
, the real period
\Omega(E^{(d)})
satisfies

\Omega(E^{(d)})=\tilde{u}\sqrt{|d|}\,\Omega(E),

where
\tilde{u}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
is a constant depending only on
E
and
d
.
Under the assumptions of the BSD conjecture and the GRH, one can observe a decorrelation between the isotropic subgroups of the Tate–Shafarevich groups of different elliptic curves when considering their quadratic twists of the same rank zero.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\frac{1}{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\leq\sigma d\leq 2D\\
\mu(|d|)^{2}=1\\
d\equiv a\pmod{N_{0}}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\delta_{r(E_{i}^{(d)}=0)}\#H(E_{i}^{(d)})}{|d|^{1/4}}\ll_{E_{1},\dots,E_{m},\varepsilon}(\log D)^{-\frac{m}{8}+\varepsilon}.

Remark 1.9
.

Using sieve methods as in Hua and Huang
[
32
]
, for integers
d
with a bounded number of prime factors, the Tamagawa numbers of

are bounded. In this case, for most twists, the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group can grow as large as
\sqrt{|d|}
. Therefore, it is reasonable to use
|d|^{1/4}
as a comparison scale for

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
1\leq i\leq m
, and suppose each
f_{i}
is either an even-weight Hecke eigenform or a Hecke–Maass form satisfying the GRC.
Assume that the forms
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are pairwise distinct.
Then for any
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{m}>0
and for
2\leq y\leq x
, we have

(2.3)

\sum_{y<p\leq x}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\right)^{2}\delta_{p\nmid d}}{p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}^{2}\right)\log\frac{\log x}{\log y}+O_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}}(\log\log\log|d|).

Proof.

Assuming the GRH for
L(s,f_{i}\times f_{j})
with
i\neq j
, the function
\log L(s,f_{i}\times f_{j})
is analytic for
\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x}
.
By a classical argument of Littlewood
[
73
, (14.2.2)]
, in this region we have

(2.4)

|\log L(s,f_{i}\times f_{j})|\ll\left(\operatorname{Re}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\log|\operatorname{Im}(s)|.

For
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
, we have

\sum_{n}\frac{|\Lambda_{f_{i}}(n)\Lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|}{n^{1+s}}\ll 1,

and Deligne’s bound yields

\sum_{a\geq 2}\sum_{p^{a}\leq x}\frac{|\Lambda_{f_{i}}(p^{a})\Lambda_{f_{j}}(p^{a})|}{p^{a}}\ll 1.

Applying Perron’s formula for
x\geq 2
gives

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\lambda_{f_{j}}(p)}{p}=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\Lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\Lambda_{f_{j}}(p)}{p}\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1-ix\log x}^{1+ix\log x}\log L(s+1,f_{i}\times f_{j})x^{s}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{s}\\
+O\left(\frac{x\sum_{p\text{ prime}}\frac{|\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\lambda_{f_{j}}(p)|}{p^{2}}}{x\log x}\right)+O(1).

Shifting the contour to
\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x}
, we encounter a simple pole at
s=0
with residue
\log L(1,f_{i}\times f_{j})
. The upper horizontal contour is bounded by

\ll\frac{1}{x\log x}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x}+ix\log x}^{1+ix\log x}|\log L(s+1,f_{i}\times f_{j})||x^{s}||\mathrm{d}s|\ll\frac{\log x\log(x\log x)}{x\log x}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{1}x^{u}\mathrm{d}u\ll 1,

and similarly for the lower horizontal contour.

From (
2.4
), we obtain for
x\geq 2
:

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\lambda_{f_{j}}(p)}{p}=\log L(1,f_{i}\times f_{j})+O\left(1+\frac{\log x}{\sqrt{x}}\int_{-x\log x}^{x\log x}\frac{\log u}{1+|u|}\mathrm{d}u\right).

Applying this estimate twice yields for
z\geq 3
:

\left|\sum_{2<p\leq z}\frac{\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\lambda_{f_{j}}(p)}{p}\right|\ll 1.

From
[
20
]
, each
\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{i}
is a self-dual cuspidal form,
we have similar result for
L(s,\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{i})
.

Then using the GRC and the bound
\sum_{p\mid d}\frac{1}{p}\ll\log\log\log d
, we have (
2.3
).

Before stating the next lemma, we introduce the following notation.
For parameters
2\leq y\leq x

\mathcal{P}(d;x,y)=\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\right)\chi_{d}(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x}}}\left(1-\frac{\log p}{\log x}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(V;x)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{D\leq\sigma d\leq 2D:\mu(|d|)^{2}=1,\ d\equiv a\pmod{N_{0}},\ \mathcal{P}(d;x,x)>V\right\},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(D)

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume the setup above.
Let
C\geq 1
be fixed and let
\varepsilon>0
be sufficiently small.
Then, for all

\mathcal{A}\left(V;D^{\frac{1}{\varepsilon V}}\right)\ll_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}}D\left(e^{-\frac{(1-2\varepsilon)V^{2}}{2\sigma(D)^{2}}}(\log\log D)^{3}+e^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{11}V\log V}\right).

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we assume that
\varepsilon>0

\varepsilon V
remains small.
We restrict to the range

\sqrt{\log\log D}\leq V\leq C\frac{\log D}{\log\log D}.

Following the optimization method of Soundararajan, we set the length of the Dirichlet polynomial to be

We decompose

This choice ensures

Using Lemma

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We establish Theorem
1.1
by applying Soundararajan’s method
[
70
]
.

Proof.

Using the relation (
2.2
) and bounding the contribution from terms with
n\geq 3
, we obtain the decomposition

(3.1)

\sum_{p^{n}\leq x}\frac{\Lambda_{f_{i}\times\chi_{d}}(p^{n})}{np^{n(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x})}}\frac{\log\frac{x}{p^{n}}}{\log x}=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\lambda_{f_{i}}(p)\chi_{d}(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{\log x}}}\frac{\log\frac{x}{p}}{\log x}\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\leq\sqrt{x}}\frac{(\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{i}}(p)-1)\delta_{p\nmid d}}{p^{1+\frac{2}{\log x}}}\frac{\log\frac{x}{p^{2}}}{\log x}+O_{f_{i}}(1).

Applying Lemma
2.3
to the second sum in (
3.1
) yields

(3.2)

-\frac{1}{2}\log\log x+O_{f_{i}}(\log\log\log D).

Define the key quantities

(3.3)

\eta(D):=\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}\right)\log\log D,

and

(3.4)

\mathcal{L}(d):=\prod_{i=1}^{m}L(1/2,f_{i}\times\chi_{d})^{\ell_{i}},

with the counting function

(3.5)

\mathcal{B}(V):=\#\left\{D\leq\sigma d\leq 2D:\mu(|d|)^{2}=1,~d\equiv a\!\!\!\!\pmod{N_{0}},~\log\mathcal{L}(d)>V\right\}.

By integration by parts, we have

(3.6)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\leq\sigma d\leq 2D\\
\mu(|d|)^{2}=1\\
d\equiv a\!\!\!\!\pmod{N_{0}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}(d)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{V}\,d\mathcal{B}(V)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{V}\mathcal{B}(V)\,dV=e^{\eta(D)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{V}\mathcal{B}(V+\eta(D))\,dV.

Under GRH and GRC, a Littlewood-type bound (see
[
6
, Corollary 1.1]
or
[
8
, §4]
) implies

(3.7)

\log\mathcal{L}(d)\leq C\frac{\log D}{\log\log D}

for some constant
C>1
. Therefore, in the above integral, we may restrict to the range

(3.8)

\sqrt{\log\log D}\leq V\leq C\frac{\log D}{\log\log D},

while for smaller
V
we trivially bound the contribution by
D+1
.

Set

(3.9)

x:=D^{1/(\varepsilon V)}.

Then, for

(3.10)

\sqrt{\log\log D}\leq V\leq(\log\log D)^{4},

we have

-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}\right)\log\log D+O_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}}(\log\log\log D)\leq\eta(D).

From Lemma
2.2
and (
3.2
), we deduce

\mathcal{B}(V+\eta(D))\leq\mathcal{A}(V(1-2\varepsilon);x),

which holds for
\sqrt{\log\log D}\leq V\leq(\log\log D)^{4}
, and remains valid for
V\geq(\log\log D)^{4}
since in that range
V+\eta(D)=V(1+o(1))
.

Combining these estimates with Lemma
2.4
, we obtain for some absolute constant
C>0
:

(3.11)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\leq\sigma d\leq 2D\\
\mu(|d|)^{2}=1\\
d\equiv a\!\!\!\!\pmod{N_{0}}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}(d)\ll De^{\eta(D)}\int_{\sqrt{\log\log D}}^{C\frac{\log D}{\log\log D}}e^{V}\left(e^{-\frac{(1-\varepsilon)V^{2}}{2\sigma(D)^{2}}}(\log\log D)^{3}+e^{-\varepsilon V\log V}\right)dV\\
\ll D(\log D)^{\varepsilon}\cdot e^{\eta(D)+\frac{\sigma(D)^{2}}{2}}\ll D(\log D)^{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}(\ell_{i}-1)}{2}+\varepsilon},

where in the last step we use the Gaussian integral identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+x}\,dx=\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\,e^{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}}.

This completes the proof.
∎
