Perceptions surrounding intranasal naloxone use and access among a population experiencing homelessness
Authors: Julena Maurer, Sharon Connor, Martha Ndung'u, Brittany Solosky, Judith Okwamba, Linh Huynh, Jessica Abbott, Ian Foo, Anna Marie White, Anita Leon-Jhong, Max Hurwitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug overdose is the leading cause of unintentional death in the United States. Studies suggest people experiencing homelessness (PEH) are at higher risk of overdose. Naloxone, trademarked Narcan®, is a medication that can reverse the effects of an opioid overdose. Despite steps taken to increase the availability of naloxone, opioid overdose deaths continue to occur at alarming rates. Few studies have explored naloxone use among PEH, and to the knowledge of the authors, none conducted in the United States have focused on the perspective of this population. The purpose of this study is to describe perceptions of naloxone use and access among PEH.MethodsThis was a qualitative study with semi-structured interviews conducted between January 2024 and March 2024. Subjects included adults who met the definition of homelessness per the McKinney-Vento Act and were recruited at a medical clinic within a low barrier shelter. Interview questions were developed using Health Belief Frameworks, and interviews were audio recorded, transcribed, then coded using principles of Grounded Theory to identify themes.ResultsTwelve interviews were conducted. Analysis of the interview transcripts identified themes including distress and misperceptions surrounding substance use, misconceptions about supportive measures following opioid overdose, and wide availability but limited accessibility of naloxone due to physical and social barriers. Participants expressed a desire for training on naloxone and other opioid overdose response measures.ConclusionsPEH report varying access to naloxone and are conflicted about using the medication. Results of this study will be used to develop programs addressing barriers and promoting facilitators to naloxone use among populations at higher risk for opioid overdose.Keywords: Homeless, Narcan®, Naloxone, Substance use, Barrier(s), Facilitator(s), Access

Introduction

The opioid epidemic continues to devastate communities across the United States (U.S.), with overdose deaths disproportionately affecting people experiencing homelessness (PEH). According to the Centers for Disease Control (CDC), there were 105,007 documented drug overdose deaths in 2023; an overall age-adjusted rate of 31.3 deaths per 100,000 standard population.1 This was a 4% decrease compared to the 32.6 deaths per 100,000 standard population in 2022.1 Yet, despite the downtrend, this follows a quadrupling of drug overdose deaths between 2002 and 2022.1 Drug overdose remains the leading cause of unintentional death in the United States (U·S) and death from drug overdose remains a significant public health emergency in the U.S.1 Additionally, studies have found drug overdose mortality rates among PEH to be ten to twelve times higher than the general population, making drug overdose one of the leading causes of death overall for this group of people.2

Naloxone, when administered promptly, is a medication that can rapidly reverse the effects of an opioid overdose. In March of 2023, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first over-the-counter (OTC) naloxone product. This intranasally administered medication was introduced to the market in addition to the already approved prescription-only products. The FDA's intention for this approval was to combat the concerningly high rates of opioid overdose death by increasing access to this lifesaving medication.3 A recent study using the U.S. National Emergency Medical Services Database found overall trends of naloxone administration, by individuals other than emergency medical personnel, had increased from 2020 to 2022 by 43.1%. However, the authors of this study did not differentiate these rates specifically among PEH.4 Despite naloxone's proven effectiveness, current efforts to increase its access, and seemingly increased administration rates by non-medical personnel, opioid overdose deaths continue to occur. Simultaneously, naloxone use among PEH remains limited, hindered by factors such as social stigma and an insufficient support system.5

The pharmacological development of naloxone has been a significant step in opioid overdose death prevention. Yet, addressing the social determinants of health (SDOH) that affect naloxone administration in an overdose situation is equally important. For example, housing and education are two SDOH factors associated with the opioid crisis and addressing them could provide insight into addressing opioid overdose death rates.5 Prior literature on naloxone use among PEH suggests educational interventions may increase naloxone prescribing among PEH.6, 7, 8. One study specifically evaluated the prevalence of carrying naloxone among people who inject drugs, finding a positive correlation between homelessness and the likelihood of carrying naloxone.9 This study also noted the need for larger studies to examine this relationship further.9 As early as 2006, a qualitative study in the United Kingdom (U.K.) examined awareness and perceptions of peer take home naloxone use among PEH who used drugs.10 However, to our knowledge, there has not yet been a U.S. based study focused on the perspectives of naloxone among PEH, regardless of drug use history and following the OTC naloxone product approval.

Understanding perceptions of naloxone among PEH is crucial to improving recent efforts to reduce barriers to its access. These perceptions can provide insight into developing effective community-informed interventions that could reduce current rates of opioid overdose deaths further. This study seeks to address this gap in knowledge by directly evaluating the perceptions and accessibility of intranasal naloxone among PEH.

Methods

Setting

Second Avenue Commons is a 95-bed low-barrier shelter located in Pittsburgh, PA, that serves adults who are without permanent, stable housing. The health center within the shelter is affiliated with a large academic medical center and is open Monday through Friday 9 AM to 5 PM. An interdisciplinary team at this walk-in clinic provides health services for acute illness, minor injuries, and chronic conditions to those staying at the shelter and other PEH. Take-home naloxone is readily available for free in the exam rooms and at the front desk. The health center nurse also regularly stocks naloxone in the bathrooms on various floors of the shelter.

Participant recruitment

All persons at least 18 years of age who could speak English and were able to provide consent to participate in the interview were eligible for this study. All participants also had to meet the definition of homelessness according to category 1 of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act. This act defines homelessness as, “lacking a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence, including residence in a publicly or privately operated shelter designated to provide temporary living arrangements.”11 Team members from the health center recruited patients at the end of their appointments, and a general invitation to interview was announced to those sitting in a common recreation area just outside the health center doors. The latter recruitment method was employed to decrease selection bias, as those sitting in the common area included individuals who were not necessarily patients of the health center. The clinic provides care to an estimated 20 to 30 people every day for various reasons and the common area entertains 100 to 150 people daily depending on the weather. If interest was expressed, the subject was led to a private room inside the clinic for the interview. They were then read a prewritten consent statement informing them of the study's intentions, assuring them they could decline to respond at any time and that their responses would have no impact on their relationship with Second Avenue Commons. Once verbal consent was provided by the interviewee, the interview was immediately conducted. Participants were also aware they could stop the interview or withdraw from the study at any time without questioning. Monetary incentives were not provided.

Data collection

This project was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board as exempt due to minimal risk providing a waiver of written consent. An interview guide was developed to promote consistency among interviewers as displayed in Fig. 1. The interview guide was developed using the Health Belief Model and Integrated Behavior Model as frameworks.12, 13 These frameworks emphasize understanding key human behaviors around decision making, specifically motivations, intentions, perceived benefits and perceived threats.12, 13 Questions addressed six categories: (1) Attitude towards naloxone use (2) Perceived norms around naloxone use (3) Perceived benefits to using naloxone (4) Perceived severity of substance use disorder prevalence (5) Perceived barriers to performing behavior (naloxone administration) (6) Perceived self-efficacy to administering naloxone in a situation involving opioid overdose. Supplementary material provides theoretical construct mapping of the interview guide. All questions were determined to be pertinent to the objective. Questions that would collect private information unrelated to the study's objectives, such as asking about personal substance use were not included intentionally to assist with interviewee privacy and comfortability. All interviews were conducted by the primary investigator, a pharmacy resident (JM), or one of the designated co-investigators, pharmacy students (JO, BS, JA, IF). All investigators were trained on how to conduct a qualitative interview prior to subject recruitment.

Interview guide used for reference by interviewers during each interview.

Data analysis

All interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private room at the health center and lasted between 20 min and 60 min. Each interview was audio recorded using a secure Microsoft Teams© meeting. Recordings were transcribed verbatim into a Microsoft Word© document and reviewed by two independent investigators (JM, JO, BS, JA, IF). No other software was used for data analysis. A codebook was developed using the process of open coding, wherein codes were developed using the data from a review of an initial sample of 2 transcripts. For example, identification of barriers to accessing naloxone was a common theme among the first two transcripts. Each time a barrier to accessing naloxone was identified by the first coder, the quote was highlighted, and “BARRIER_ACCESS” was placed as a linked comment into the Microsoft Word© document. A second coder followed the same method for identifying themes on a separate copy of the respective word document without prior knowledge of the first coder's identified themes.

Afterwards, six of the investigators (JM, SC, JO, BS, JA, IF) met over Microsoft Teams© to review the two transcripts with their uniquely identified themes. If there was a discrepancy between themes or codes for those themes, the investigators discussed until a mutual theme and/or code could be agreed upon. This preliminary codebook was then used by all coders as a guide for each subsequent interview. Each interview was analyzed and discussed by two independent coders in the same fashion that the codebook was developed. In cases where agreement could not be reached, a third coder (SC) who had not participated in the interviews was designated as a tiebreaker; this step was never needed. Codebook development was an iterative process; additional concepts or ideas identified in subsequent interviews that were not already defined in the codebook led to the development of new codes following discussion and agreement among the coders. All audio files and documents related to the research were stored on password-protected laptops on a secure server requiring two factor authentication limited to approved study personnel to meet research security standards.

Once codes for each interview were agreed upon, a theme development meeting of six investigators was held to discuss and finalize the overall themes (JM, SC, JO, BS, JA, IF). Data was analyzed using thematic analysis.14 Coding and analysis occurred concurrently with data collection. Following development of the preliminary codebook, each subsequent interview was examined for the presence of existing codes as well as the emergence of new concepts. Thematic saturation was determined through ongoing comparison of codes across interviews, such that no new codes or themes were identified in the final interviews. The investigators defined saturation as the point at which subsequent interviews yielded no additional themes or conceptual categories but rather reinforced previously identified ones, indicating that sufficient thematic coverage had been achieved. All identified themes were incorporated into an integrated understanding and coherent narrative.

Results

Twelve interviews were conducted between January 2024 and March 2024. Seven of the participants identified as male and five as female. The mean age of participants was 49 years but ranged from 29 years old to 69 years old. The majority were Caucasian (92%) and were staying at the Second Avenue Commons Shelter at the time of their interview (75%). Theme

1: Fear and distress related to substance use and overdose influence perceptions of naloxone use.

Participants described a large prevalence of substance use happening in their community. When recounting personal experiences of witnessing substance use or drug overdose, many expressed feelings of fear using words such as “scary” and described feelings of distress.

“The chances [of being in a situation involving overdose] are a lot larger than they used to be…Homeless people like to get high because if you're homeless there's not much to look forward to.” (I1).

“…I've seen a lot of [drug use] here at [the shelter] you know, in the past couple years that I've been in here…” (I2).

“I'd never seen [an overdose] happen before. It was scary.” (I3).

“I know there are many people here who are in various stages of recovery [from substance use disorder] and given that I'm sure there are many more who are still using.” (I10).

Theme

2: Limited confidence in overdose response skills contributes to low perceived self-efficacy in administering naloxone.

Many participants stated they wouldn't be comfortable administering naloxone because they are not trained healthcare professionals or do not know how to perform other lifesaving measures such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Some expressed concerns about not knowing how to respond after the naloxone is administered, especially if the person overdosing were to have a violent reaction.

“…[S]ince I'm not a certified first responder or I don't have CPR first aid. I don't feel comfortable [administering naloxone] …Well, if you don't have licenses, you can be sued for helping somebody and it's just ridiculous.” (I4).

“I won't [administer naloxone] …because I don't know how to do CPR. I don't know how to save a life…” (I5).

“Umm, I don't think [administering naloxone] is that difficult. It's just the fact of maybe not wanting to get hurt by the person that may thrash and fight [after receiving the dose].” (I4).

“I wouldn't try to get involved because…if anybody's OD'ing I don't wanna get cuts all over me. I'm not getting my hands all scraped… If I could help them, I would, but I'm just gonna call a professional.” (I9).

Theme

3: Misperception of substance use disorder leads to conflicting feelings about administering naloxone in some situations.

Support for naloxone as a tool to reverse opioid overdose was abundant among participants, but some were conflicted because of what they thought those using substances would want. A common concern among interviewees was the misperception that those using substances do not want naloxone administered because it will ruin their high or that they want to end their lives.

“See, I would like to think that [they] would be receptive and grateful [to be administered Narcan®], but I have also heard stories of people who have been brought back [from overdose] …you hear them tell you like “ohhh, you ruined my high” (I11).

“You have to give up on [people who use drugs] …you can't save somebody that don't want to save themself.” (I8).

“[T]hey wanna do hard [drugs]. They wanna take the chance of risking their own lives. If they want to do that, that's on their own.” (I5).

“… [naloxone] could save a life, [but] this junkie is trying to kill themselves and they just don't care about themselves…you give them [naloxone] and they just wanna overdose.” (I6).

Theme

4: Naloxone is widely available but not always readily accessible within the community.

Most participants described naloxone as being widely available in the community. Many named multiple places where take-home naloxone was available but expressed concerns with not having access to the medication depending on the time of day or day of the week.

“[Naloxone is] everywhere…Yeah, they put it every restaurant, bathrooms, everywhere…it's everywhere. It's all on the shelves out there.” (I7).

“I think [Narcan® is] really easy [to obtain], especially around here. What I've seen at [shelter] it's easy if you wanted Narcan®, you can have it, have access. I mean then again, there's some days where it's not easy access because, umm, the doctor's office or [the health center at the shelter] it's not open on the weekends so it's not easy to get to [the Narcan®]. I mean, you could go to the staff, but over the weekends…that's not easy to access it and I really don't see too many shelters around here giving [Narcan®] out, I'll be honest.” (I6).

“I know [naloxone] is available at many different places around the shelter… but I've never noticed it going missing, like people taking them.” (I11).

Describing a previous experience involving opioid overdose: “[T]here wasn't any [Narcan®] at hand up in the bathroom on the third floor…so they [the staff] had to run and go to the office of whatever and get Narcan®. But they're trying to work on that it's always there at hand, but there's not…There's sometimes where it's not there.” (I5).

Theme

5: Perceived barriers to carrying naloxone on a regular basis include associated stigma and perceived cost.

When asked what barriers they could identify that might make it difficult for people to carry naloxone, many expressed concerns for being stereotyped by others if they had naloxone on them. They expressed the belief that naloxone's association with drug use could cause others to perceive them as a drug user if it was on their person. The cost of naloxone in pharmacies and stores was reported to be unaffordable.

“I think I'd get stereotyped…Well they would see the Narcan® and think you must be a junkie. You get stereotyped.” (I9).

“If I'm carrying Narcan® with me everywhere and I'm not getting paid for it. Then I'm probably doing a bunch of sh*t that I don't need to be doing.” (I1).

“I think that Narcan®, when people see Narcan®, they automatically assume even the carrier is a user.” (I4).

“You can purchase it at the store for a lot of money…and I do mean a lot of money. ‘Cause I already priced it, it's over fifty dollars.” (I3).

Theme

6: There is a willingness to learn more about naloxone and a desire for training.

Participants expressed limited knowledge of naloxone or confusion about its mechanism of action. Participants suggested educational sessions could make it easier for people to obtain, carry, or use the medication. Many cited hands-on training sessions in a controlled environment would be beneficial for increasing comfort in acting during an opioid overdose.

“I just know… [naloxone is] a drug to come out of when you're going into overdose…and so mainly it's for meth…if I'm not mistaken, or heroin.” (I6).

“You can just go and tell the people [experiencing homelessness] you know, what they use [naloxone] for. Maybe some people don't know what it's for, you know…” (I2).

“I would feel more comfortable if somebody would actually start teaching about [naloxone] more often around here and not just what's on the package you know, maybe you could give a lesson about it…what would make me feel comfortable giving it is knowing that I could save a life. That would make me feel comfortable.” (I6).

“Informational seminar. Let [people experiencing homelessness] learn what [Narcan®] can do and what it can't do. Give them all the info from the internet. It's not there to hurt you, it's there to help.” (I9).

“[G]et someone a class. Make it accessible. Teach them how to use it, how to store it, administer it. I'm sure [the people who are offering the naloxone] say, ‘hey, you want to keep this? You can save someone's life.’ [and the reply is] ‘Yeah, sure.’ but [the people receiving the naloxone] think what am I gonna do with this?” (I10).

The use of opioids remains a significant ongoing problem as perceived by members of this population. Fear and distress surrounding opioid use and overdose were commonly expressed feelings among study participants. Most stated they would not administer naloxone in a situation involving opioid overdose due to a limited knowledge of supportive measures or the perceived risk of being harmed by the person receiving the naloxone. When asked about accessing naloxone in the community, participants described the availability of the medication as widespread. Yet, access to naloxone was reported to be limited by barriers such as financial cost, business hours, and fear of stigmatization. Suggestions for increasing naloxone use among PEH included educational sessions about substance use disorder and hands-on training with naloxone products.

Discussion

Given the disproportionately high risk of death from opioid overdose in populations of PEH, understanding how to increase the accessibility of naloxone and improve the use of the medication in lifesaving situations is essential. Although there have been several studies that have examined naloxone within a population of PEH,6, 7, 8., 9, 10. this, to our knowledge, is the first study to explore the perceptions of naloxone use in the context of its accessibility among members of this population in the U.S following the 2023 FDA approval of OTC naloxone product.

Many study participants recalled feelings of fear or distress when witnessing past situations of opioid overdose, which led to hesitancy when making the decision to administer naloxone. Several studies have shown opioid overdose to have a traumatic impact on witnesses, termed “overdose trauma,” sometimes resulting in long-term psychological symptoms.15., 16 This highlights the need to acknowledge possible trauma when discussing naloxone, especially with members of a population at higher risk for experiencing or witnessing opioid overdoses. That being said, no studies have directly examined the correlation between witnessing an opioid overdose and hesitancy to administering naloxone. Future research exploring the specific impact of “overdose trauma” on naloxone administration could help determine if feelings of fear and distress, as described by participants of this study, represent a significant barrier to timely administration of naloxone.

Another barrier to administering naloxone during situations of opioid overdose expressed by study participants was low perceived self-efficacy resulting from a lack of first aid and CPR training. Although the authors did not identify any studies specifically measuring the impact of CPR knowledge on naloxone administration, there have been many studies focused on the impact of CPR training on bystander response during cardiac arrest. For example, a 2022 review article from the American Heart Association states that studies have found a significantly higher percentage of people (72%) who received CPR training felt confident performing CPR compared to those who had not received training (23%).17 It is plausible that similar trends in confidence could apply to naloxone administration, but further research is needed to determine whether CPR or other first aid training improves readiness to respond to opioid overdose, particularly among PEH. To prevent overlooking the importance of naloxone administration and other lifesaving supportive measures, such training would need to emphasize the appropriate indications for CPR in the context of opioid overdose.

Participants expressed concern for potential harm to themselves by the person receiving naloxone. This perception is aligned with a long-standing association between naloxone administration and aggressive behavior. The statement “In some patients, there may be aggressive behavior upon abrupt reversal of an opioid overdose” was added to the manufacturer package insert in 2020 as an update to the original 2015 version and remains there today.18., 19. However, evidence surrounding the potential for aggression due to rapid opioid overdose reversal is conflicting. One recent study terms “rage” as an outcome following successful opioid overdose reversal.20 Other studies conclude anger following naloxone administration to be unrelated to medication administration and rather a product of responder administration method and communication style.21, 22. Considering these new findings regarding the potential for aggressive behaviors following rapid reversal of an opioid overdose, members of this study's target population may be more likely to administer naloxone if the fear of possible aggression can be minimized and they feel more prepared for how to approach someone who has overdosed. It is possible that these concerns may be mitigated through preparation with intentional strategies during an educational session or hands-on training that may increase confidence and willingness to act.

In addition to safety concerns, some recalled past experiences that affirmed misperceptions around the wishes of the person who had overdosed. These include the incorrect assumption that the person who overdosed did not want to live or would be upset that their high was interrupted; beliefs that discouraged some from intervening. This finding is similar to that of the 2006 U.K. study that found subjects to be less willing to administer naloxone if they were aware of an active suicide attempt.10 Substance use has been well established as a medical disorder, and myths about addiction and suicide have been repeatedly dispelled by multiple credible organizations including the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) and the National Institute on Drug Administration (NIDA).23., 24. Yet, these misperceptions persist among members of this study's target population. It is important that conversations about naloxone and substance use with members of a population of PEH incorporate strategies to dispel these myths and dismantle harmful narratives to improve naloxone use which may ultimately reduce the number of deaths from opioid overdose.

Having naloxone readily available is essential to being able to administer it at the soonest possible time. Participants identified several barriers to accessing naloxone, including limited health center hours and financial cost at pharmacies. Local and state-level organizations have attempted to address these barriers through free naloxone distribution programs, including online request systems, fixed-site distribution (such as vending machines), and emerging alternative delivery models.25., 26., 27. The health center nurse at the study site also stocks the bathrooms of Second Avenue Commons with naloxone on a weekly basis. Usage tracking, especially availability at times of opioid overdose, remains unfeasible due to the anonymous nature of distribution. Despite these efforts, perceived accessibility among PEH remains low. It is possible that placing the medication in areas where opioid use is high may help. The impact of newer interventions, like vending machines, will take time as it is early in its implementation. Strategies may focus on increasing utilization of current distribution programs to encourage more people to carry the medication when it is available, especially those who are members of a population at high risk for opioid overdose. Additionally, consideration should be made to simplify logistics as strategies for distribution may not be feasible for the target population due to logistics such as advanced planning, reliable access to the internet or transportation and a mailing address needed for online requests.These findings highlight the importance of placing naloxone in locations where opioid use is most likely to occur and ensuring distribution methods are low-barrier, visible, and easily accessible.

Even when naloxone is available and accessible, stigma plays a significant role in the willingness to carry it and use it. Study participants reported concerns about being negatively perceived as drug users, which prevented them from accepting take-home naloxone in the past. Stigma has been previously recognized as a factor undermining the distribution of naloxone.28 There have been some studies that have explored attempts to reduce stigma. A 2022 study by Drexel University medical students found that their opioid overdose prevention training program (OOTP) improved participants' understanding of the challenges faced by individuals with opioid use disorder including the nuances and potential harm.29 This study concluded that efforts should be made to incorporate biopsychosocial components into future OOTPs.29 Although this study included participants from the general population, it is possible that educational sessions tailored to PEH could benefit from interventions targeting the stigmatization of naloxone. It is also possible that OOTPs conducted for those in the broader community that are not experiencing homelessness could decrease the stigmatizing actions that PEH are faced with. Both factors would lead to an improvement of attitudes about regularly carrying naloxone and foster a supportive environment for naloxone use.

Many of the perceived barriers to naloxone use and accessibility discussed can likely be addressed during intentional educational sessions as previously conducted in various other populations. The participants of this study shared a strong willingness to learn more about naloxone and partake in training sessions. Increased education was widely supported among participants to address misinformation and build confidence in administering the medication. Numerous studies have demonstrated benefit in educational interventions to improve naloxone use. A 2021 umbrella review of six systematic reviews, encompassing 87 studies, found that opioid overdose education and naloxone distribution (ONED) programs “produce long-term knowledge improvement regarding opioid overdose.”30 These ONED programs improved participants' attitudes towards naloxone, provided sufficient training for participants to safely and effectively manage opioid overdoses and effectively reduced opioid-related mortality.30 A few other studies, focused on populations of PEH, have shown similar rates of acceptability of educational sessions with improvements in knowledge of naloxone and increases in its distribution following these interventions.6, 7, 8.

Ultimately, educational training sessions should focus on explaining how naloxone works, what to expect after its administration, and how to respond with other lifesaving measures if needed during an opioid overdose. Empowering individuals with both knowledge and practical skills may reduce the fear and hesitation associated with opioid overdose response.31 Educating PEH about naloxone and demystifying the process following an opioid overdose may be beneficial in encouraging them to use naloxone when needed. Open discussions in a safe, inclusive environment with community representation could be an effective way to deliver these messages. Efforts should be made to specifically address concerns about aggression or negative reactions from individuals after naloxone administration. Hands-on training programs that are practical and scenario-based could help prepare potential responders to act confidently in these traumatic scenarios and emphasize the importance of administering naloxone even under uncertain circumstances.

Limitations

The study window was relatively small, which could have limited the number of interviews conducted, however saturation was ultimately believed to be reached by investigators. While the chosen site for participant recruitment and conducting interviews serves people from multiple areas of Pittsburgh, PA and some participants anecdotally mentioned recently migrating from other areas of the U.S, geographical bias remains a likely factor. The investigators acknowledge this limitation along with the understanding that varying geographical populations have unique barriers to facilitators. When developing this study's methods, factors to increase replicability for future application in other communities were specifically accounted for through transparency in presenting the methods, as well as including the interview guide and coding strategy in this manuscript.

The majority of participants identified as Caucasian, and the primary language for all participants was English. According to the Allegheny Department of Human Services, during the time interviews were conducted, only 42% of the population experiencing homelessness in Allegheny County was Caucasian.32 Although data about participation denials was not officially collected, the study recruiters determined no person who expressed interest in participation was excluded due to a language barrier. Recruiters also anecdotally recalled multiple potential subjects declined to participate after expressing discomfort with discussing the subject manner. Those who declined participation were not asked any further questions to maintain respect for their privacy given the nature of the study's subject matter. The investigators recognize although these demographics were representative of the area surrounding the chosen site, this sample is not necessarily demographically representative of the overall target population and likely differs from other communities as well. Future studies that offer translation services and have increased minority participation are important and encouraged to ensure accurate representation of themes across various populations of PEH.

This study was conducted out of the health center where investigators worked, as clinicians or trainees, and participants were likely patients of the health center at some point in time, if not at the time of the interview. There is potential for sample bias as those who felt most comfortable with the interviewer or clinic staff might have been more likely to participate. An interviewer-interviewee power dynamic and a social desirability influence in interviewee answers could also be possible. To mitigate these influences, interviewers emphasized confidentiality and anonymity and clearly stated that participation and responses would have no impact on current or future care or relationships with clinic staff. On the other hand, an already established relationship and rapport between subjects and the interviewers could have contributed to increased comfort during interviews resulting in increased transparency in responses. Notably, there is also potential for recall bias from participants during the interviews when recounting past experiences of witnessing and opioid overdose or accessing naloxone. Reflexivity was actively maintained throughout data analysis through regular team discussions during coding and theme development, during which investigators reflected on how their professional identities, assumptions related to naloxone access, and experiences in clinical and harm reduction settings could influence data interpretation. Discrepancies in coding and interpretation were discussed until consensus was reached, helping to ensure that themes remained grounded in participants' accounts rather than researcher preconceptions.

Finally, investigators were healthcare workers, most of whom were a part of the healthcare team at Second Avenue Commons during the study. Their prior knowledge and experiences with naloxone may have introduced variability in interview conduct and interpretation of responses, despite standardized training. To mitigate this, team meetings involving interviewers and coders were held before and throughout the project to address potential investigator bias and reinforce techniques to limit investigator influence on data collection and analysis. An interview guide was established, and transcripts were also interpreted by multiple investigators to minimize this limitation.

Recommendations/next steps

The results of this study suggest several opportunities for intervention, including targeted education and training programs that address both the barriers and misconceptions surrounding naloxone use. These findings point to actionable strategies such as peer-informed training approaches, trauma-informed communication during overdose response, and thoughtful naloxone distribution practices that promote accessibility while minimizing stigma. These programs could be integrated into existing services for PEH to empower individuals to act quickly in opioid overdose situations. Future research should focus on expanding upon this study's findings, incorporating the perspectives of a wider range of stakeholders, such as shelter staff and healthcare providers, and extending the methods used in this investigation to other communities with people at high risk for an opioid overdose including counties with more income inequality and minority groups.33

Conclusions

Substance use and opioid overdose deaths continue to disproportionately affect PEH. This study highlights significant barriers to naloxone use, such as lack of knowledge and stigma surrounding both naloxone and substance use, while also revealing a strong desire for more education and support within the target population. Addressing these barriers through appropriately designed interventions and expanding accessibility may lead to greater usage of naloxone by non-medical personnel within this population and ultimately save lives. Further research is necessary to deepen our understanding of these issues and to develop effective strategies for increasing naloxone use among populations at higher risk for opioid overdose.
