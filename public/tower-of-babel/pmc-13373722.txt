CIDEB and CGI-58 differentially regulate liver lipid-droplet cholesterol to modulate metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis severity
Authors: Ikki Sakuma, Rafael C. Gaspar, Henrique N. Morgan, Jie Zheng, Ali R. Nasiri, Sylvie Dufour, Mario Kahn, Mateus T. Guerra, Yuki Taki, Yusuke Kawashima, Dean Yimlamai, Mark Perelis, Daniel F. Vatner, Kitt Falk Petersen, Varman T. Samuel, Tomoaki Tanaka, Gerald I. Shulman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) increases liver-related mortality, and new therapies targeting its underlying mechanisms are warranted. We examined whether two lipid-droplet proteins, CIDEB and CGI-58, exert opposing control over MASH by altering cholesterol in liver lipid droplets. Using antisense oligonucleotides, we silenced CIDEB or CGI-58 in the livers of C57BL/6J mice fed a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined high-fat diet. CIDEB silencing decreased both triglyceride and cholesterol levels in liver lipid droplets and lowered plasma transaminases and the number of crown-like structures. These protective effects were abrogated by cholesterol supplementation. Conversely, CGI-58 knockdown raised triglyceride and cholesterol levels and exacerbated MASH; bempedoic acid, a cholesterol-synthesis inhibitor, reversed these changes. Dual CIDEB/CGI-58 silencing confirmed that CGI-58 loss abrogated the protective effects of CIDEB knockdown. Our data establish liver lipid-droplet cholesterol as a critical determinant in MASH mediated by CIDEB and CGI-58 and demonstrate that CIDEB knockdown confers protection by enhancing CGI-58-dependent lipolysis.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most common chronic liver disorder worldwide, and its progressive inflammatory form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), is now a leading cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality.1

Excessive lipid droplet accumulation in the liver can be detrimental to hepatocyte function and exacerbate MASH progression.2 Among hepatic lipid-droplet proteins, the disease-promoting PNPLA3 I148M variant (rs738409) is common worldwide, with a global minor-allele frequency of roughly 26%, and consistently exacerbates steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis.3 By contrast, the splice-site loss-of-function variant HSD17B13 rs72613567 has a global minor-allele frequency of about 18% and acts as a protective allele that attenuates liver injury.4

Beyond these common alleles, rare variants in two additional lipid-droplet proteins—CIDEB (cell-death-inducing DNA fragmentation factor-like effector B) and CGI-58 (comparative gene identification 58/ABHD5)—have emerged as potent genetic modifiers of MASH.5 CIDEB promotes hepatic lipid-droplet fusion, and rare germline loss-of-function variants (global minor-allele frequency (MAF) ≈0.37%; predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) ≈0.07%) protect against steatohepatitis and cirrhosis6; employing N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-conjugated small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) against CIDEB has shown therapeutic promise in recent mouse studies.7,8 Conversely, CGI-58 is the obligatory co-activator of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL).2 Heterozygous loss-of-function or deleterious missense variants in CGI-58 predispose carriers to inherited MASLD that can progress to steatohepatitis; such variants are rare (aggregate minor-allele frequency ≈0.09%, corresponding to ≈1 carrier per 1,131 individuals).9 CGI-58 binds directly to PNPLA3 I148M on liver lipid-droplet surfaces10,11 and binds ATGL and prevents CGI-58-ATGL complex formation.12 In this manner, CGI-58 serves as a central node in MASH pathogenesis related to both PNPLA3 and HSD17B13.

Despite these insights, how CIDEB and CGI-58 alter the lipid composition of liver lipid droplets—particularly cholesterol content—and the consequences of those changes for MASH progression remain unknown. Furthermore, any crosstalk between the CIDEB-mediated lipid droplet fusion pathway and the CGI-58-ATGL lipolytic cascade has yet to be elucidated.

Here, we employed 2-O-methoxyethyl antisense oligonucleotides (MOE ASO) to generate CIDEB- and CGI-58-knockdown mouse models, which we challenged with a choline-deficient, L-amino acid-defined high-fat diet (CDAHFD). We show that CIDEB and CGI-58 exert mirror-image effects on liver lipid-droplet cholesterol content and, consequently, on MASH progression. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the hepatoprotective effect of CIDEB knockdown is abolished by concomitant CGI-58 silencing, pinpointing a CGI-58-dependent lipolytic mechanism as the downstream effector of CIDEB inhibition.

RESULTS

CIDEB knockdown alleviates MASH due to CDAHFD via decreasing cholesterol in liver lipid droplets

To evaluate whether CIDEB knockdown decreases cholesterol in liver lipid droplets and alleviates MASH in the 1-week CDAHFD model, C57BL/6J mice were treated with either MOE control ASO or MOE Cideb ASO for 3 weeks, followed by CDAHFD for 1 week (Figure 1A). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (real-time qPCR) analysis of liver tissues confirmed the knockdown efficiency of Cideb ASO (Figure 1B). Plasma ALT and AST levels were lower in Cideb ASO-treated mice than in controls (Figure 1C). H&E staining and BODIPY demonstrated that CIDEB knockdown decreased liver lipid droplet size (Figures 1D and 1E). The number of crown-like structures also decreased in the Cideb ASO-treated mice’s livers (Figures 1D and 1F). BODIPY and filipin staining demonstrated decreased free cholesterol in liver lipid droplets of Cideb ASO-treated mice (Figure 1D). In line with these staining results, triglycerides, free cholesterol, cholesterol ester, and total cholesterol in liver lipid droplets decreased in Cideb ASO-treated mice (Figure 1G). The level of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine in liver lipid droplets did not differ between control and Cideb ASO-treated mice (Figure 1G). Consistent with pathological findings, real-time qPCR demonstrated a reduced mRNA expression of MASH-associated macrophage markers (Gpnmb, Trem2, and Lgals3) and fibrosis marker (Col1a1) in Cideb ASO-treated mice (Figure 1H).

We recently demonstrated that cholesterol in liver lipid droplets is a key determinant of MASH.13 In the previous study, we found that atorvastatin or Hsd17b13 ASO alleviated liver inflammation and fibrosis induced by CDAHFD, accompanied by a reduction in cholesterol content in hepatic lipid droplets. In contrast, cholesterol supplementation in the CDAHFD model exacerbated MASH, accompanied by an increase in cholesterol content in hepatic lipid droplets.

In the current study, knockdown of CIDEB lowered both triglycerides and cholesterol in liver lipid droplets. To evaluate whether reducing fatty acid flux or triglyceride storage alone is sufficient for protection, we knocked down the major hepatic fatty acid transporter CD36 with a hepatocyte-targeted GalNAc-ASO. Although CD36 knockdown decreased hepatic fatty acids and triglycerides, it left lipid-droplet cholesterol levels and the number of crown-like structures unchanged and failed to improve markers of MASH (Figures S1A-S1G). Because saturated fatty acids have been proposed as endogenous TLR4 ligands, we assessed whether eliminating TLR4 signaling itself mitigates the 1-week CDAHFD phenotype. TLR4-knockout mice showed no benefit in the same 1-week CDAHFD model (Figures S2A-S2F). In contrast, we knocked down SOAT2, the major hepatic cholesterol esterification enzyme, using a hepatocyte-targeted GalNAc-ASO. SOAT2 knockdown attenuated the CDAHFD-induced elevations in serum ALT and AST (Figures S3A-S3C).

Together, these results suggest that lipid-droplet cholesterol is a more critical driver than fatty acid flux or triglyceride burden, whereas changes in fatty acids and triglycerides alone are not sufficient to account for protection in this model.

Accordingly, we next tested whether increases in cholesterol per se could negate the protective effect of CIDEB knockdown. Cideb ASO-treated C57BL/6J mice were divided into mice fed CDAHFD alone and CDAHFD with 2% cholesterol (CDAHFD + Chol) (Figure 1I). CDAHFD + Chol exacerbated the MASH phenotype with higher transaminase concentrations (Figure 1J) and increased number of crown-like structures (Figures 1K and 1L). Consistent with these findings, free cholesterol, cholesterol ester, and total cholesterol in liver lipid droplets increased in cholesterol-supplemented mice (Figure 1M). There was no change in lipid-droplet triglycerides, phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine in CDAHFD + Chol-fed mice (Figure 1M). Thus, cholesterol supplementation abrogated the protective effects of Cideb ASO in CDAHFD-fed mice.

CGI-58 knockdown aggravates MASH due to CDAHFD via increasing cholesterol in liver lipid droplets

We next investigated the function of CGI-58, a co-activator of ATGL. To assess whether CGI-58 knockdown increases cholesterol in liver lipid droplets and exacerbates MASH in the 1-week CDAHFD model, we fed C57BL/6J mice CDAHFD for 1 week and treated them with either MOE control ASO or MOE CGI-58 ASO (Figure 2A). Real-time qPCR confirmed efficient hepatic knockdown of CGI-58 (Figure 2B). Plasma ALT and AST levels were higher in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice than in controls (Figure 2C). H&E staining and BODIPY demonstrated that CGI-58 knockdown increased liver lipid droplet size (Figures 2D and 2E). The number of crown-like structures increased in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice’s livers (Figures 2D and 2F). BODIPY and filipin staining demonstrated increased free cholesterol in liver lipid droplets of CGI-58 ASO-treated mice (Figure 2D). In line with these staining results, triglycerides, free cholesterol, cholesterol ester, and total cholesterol in liver lipid droplets were higher in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice (Figure 2G). The level of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine in liver lipid droplets did not differ between control and CGI-58 ASO-treated mice (Figure 2G). Real-time qPCR further showed increased expression of MASH-associated macrophage markers (Gpnmb, Trem2, and Lgals3) and fibrogenic markers (αSMA and Col1a1) (Figure 2H).

We next tested whether bempedoic acid—which inhibits ATP-citrate lyase and reduces cholesterol synthesis—could reverse this phenotype (Figure 2I). Bempedoic acid lowered plasma ALT and AST (Figure 2J), reduced hepatic steatosis (Figure 2K), and decreased the number of crown-like structures (Figures 2K and 2L). It also lowered triglyceride, free cholesterol, cholesterol ester, and total cholesterol in lipid droplets, while phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine remained unchanged (Figure 2M). Thus, inhibiting hepatic cholesterol synthesis attenuates MASH in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice.

Because bempedoic acid reduced both droplet cholesterol and triglycerides, we next evaluated the specific contribution of triglyceride accumulation. We silenced glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAM), which converts glycerol-3-phosphate to lysophosphatidic acid and drives de novo triglyceride synthesis, without directly affecting cholesterol metabolism. C57BL/6J mice receiving MOE CGI-58 ASO under 1 week of CDAHFD feeding were divided into MOE control ASO and MOE Gpam ASO groups (Figure S4A). GPAM silencing significantly lowered plasma AST, and plasma ALT trended downward without reaching significance (Figures S4B and S4C). H&E staining showed reduced steatosis (Figure S4D), and crown-like structures were modestly decreased, although not significantly (Figures S4D and S4E). While triglyceride in liver lipid droplets decreased, total cholesterol was unchanged (Figure S4F). Collectively, these findings suggest that lipid-droplet cholesterol, rather than triglyceride accumulation per se, is the principal driver of liver inflammation and fibrosis in CGI-58 deficiency.

Knockdown of CIDEB or CGI-58 alters protein expression related to lipolysis/fatty acid oxidation and lipid-droplet proteins

RNA sequencing and comprehensive proteome analysis were performed using the liver samples from Cideb ASO-treated mice study in Figure 1A and CGI-58 ASO-treated mice study in Figure 2A.

In Cideb-ASO livers, analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from RNA sequencing identified 60 upregulated and 8 downregulated genes compared to the control ASO group (Figure 3A). Gene set enrichment analysis revealed significant suppression of inflammatory pathways—TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, interferon-γ, and interferon-α responses—as well as the profibrotic TGF-β signaling cascade (Figure 3B). In CGI-58 ASO livers, analysis of DEGs from RNA sequencing identified 30 upregulated and 34 downregulated genes compared to the control ASO group (Figure 3C). Gene set enrichment showed positive enrichment of TNF-α signaling via NF-κB and interferon-γ/α response (Figure 3D). Consistently, the expression of core enrichment genes in the TNFα signaling via NF-κB gene set decreased in the Cideb ASO group but increased in the CGI-58 ASO group (Figure 3E). We also calculated a zonation index (pericentral score – periportal score) based on the expression levels of pericentral and periportal marker genes. In the Cideb ASO study, the Cideb ASO group showed a lower zonation index. In contrast, no difference in zonation index was observed between the CGI-58 ASO and control ASO groups (Figure 3F).

Because both CIDEB and CGI-58 localize to liver lipid droplets, we next evaluated protein expression related to lipolysis/fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and lipid-droplet protein with cholesterol-ester-handling factors (e.g., Soat1/2) by comprehensive proteome analysis. CGI-58 ASO livers demonstrated lower levels of the lipolytic enzymes Lipe and Mgll, together with reduced cholesterol-ester hydrolase Ces1d (Figure 3G). Cideb-ASO livers showed higher levels of Atgl and CGI-58, while the cholesterol-esterifying enzyme Soat1 was decreased (Figure 3G). Among perilipins, which are key components of the lipid-droplet coat, Cideb ASO increased Plin5 expression, whereas CGI-58 ASO decreased Plin5 expression. Regarding FAO, CGI-58 ASO reduced the abundance of multiple β-oxidation proteins, including Cpt1a, Acadvl, Hadha, Hadhb, Acadm, Etfa, Etfb, Etfdh, and Acaa2, consistent with suppressed FAO. In contrast, Cideb ASO modestly increased several of these proteins (e.g., Acadvl, Hadhb, Acadm, Etfa/Etfb, and Acaa2). CGI-58 ASO decreased the abundance of representative OXPHOS proteins spanning complexes I–V (e.g., Ndufs1, Sdha, Cyc1, Cox4i1, and Atp5f1b). In contrast, Cideb ASO modestly increased several OXPHOS proteins (Ndufs1, Sdha, Cyc1, and Atp5f1b), whereas Cox4i1 was not increased (Figure 3G). In addition, we examined markers of mitochondrial content, including TOMM20, TOMM40, VDAC1, and citrate synthase and did not observe a significant change in either the CGI-58 ASO or Cideb ASO studies (Figure 3G). This proteomics dataset did not reveal coordinated changes in canonical SREBP2 targets, or in LXR target proteins (Figure 3G).

CGI-58 or ATGL knockdown abrogates the protective effect of CIDEB knockdown

To determine if CGI-58 knockdown can overcome the protective effect of Cideb ASO, C57BL/6J mice treated with MOE Cideb ASO under CDAHFD 1-week feeding were divided into two subgroups: MOE control ASO group and MOE CGI-58 ASO group (Figure 4A). Real-time qPCR analysis of liver tissues confirmed efficient knockdown effect of CGI-58 ASO in Cideb ASO-treated mice under CDAHFD 1-week feeding (Figure 4B). Plasma ALT and AST in CGI-58 ASO group were higher than in the control group (Figure 4C). Knockdown of CGI-58 led to enlargement of the livers in Cideb ASO-treated mice (Figure 4D). Consistently, H&E staining showed that CGI-58 knockdown increased liver lipid droplet accumulation. The number of crown-like structures increased in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice’s livers (Figures 4D and 4E). In liver lipid droplets, triglycerides, cholesterol ester and total cholesterol, phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine were higher in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice, while free cholesterol did not change (Figure 4F). Real-time qPCR demonstrated an increased mRNA expression of MASH-associated macrophage markers (Gpnmb, Trem2, and Lgals3), and fibrosis markers (αSMA, and Col1a1) in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice (Figure 4G). Thus, CGI-58 knockdown abrogated the protective effects of Cideb ASO in CDAHFD-fed mice accompanied by increasing cholesterol in liver lipid droplets.

CGI-58 is known to be a co-activator of ATGL. Next, we directly tested whether ATGL depletion affects the benefits of CIDEB knockdown by using an Atgl-targeting ASO. C57BL/6J mice treated with an MOE Cideb ASO and fed a CDAHFD for 1 week were divided into two subgroups and co-treated with either a GalNAc control ASO or a GalNAc Atgl ASO (Figure 4H). Real-time qPCR analysis of liver tissue confirmed efficient Atgl knockdown in Cideb ASO-treated mice (Figure 4I). Plasma ALT and AST levels were higher in the Atgl ASO group than in the control group (Figure 4J). Consistently, the number of crown-like structures increased in the livers of Atgl ASO-treated mice (Figures 4K and 4L). Lipid droplet lipidomics showed that triglycerides, free cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, and total cholesterol were increased in the Atgl ASO group (Figure 4M). In addition, real-time qPCR demonstrated increased hepatic mRNA expression of Trem2, Lgals3, and Col1a1 in Atgl ASO-treated mice (Figure 4N). Collectively, ATGL knockdown abrogated the protective effects of Cideb ASO in CDAHFD-fed mice and was accompanied by increased lipid-droplet cholesterol, phenocopying the effects observed with CGI-58 knockdown.

DISCUSSION

We show that CIDEB knockdown, using MOE-ASOs, markedly reduced liver lipid droplet size, lowered both triglyceride and cholesterol content within lipid droplets, and protected against CDAHFD-induced steatohepatitis (Figure 1). This protection was accompanied by increased protein expression of lipolytic machinery—including ATGL and its co-activator CGI-58—and decreased levels of the cholesterol-esterifying enzyme SOAT1, suggesting that enhanced triglyceride hydrolysis and impaired cholesterol esterification contributed to the observed benefit. Conversely, dietary cholesterol supplementation in Cideb ASO-treated mice increased liver lipid-droplet cholesterol and aggravated liver injury and macrophage crown-like structure formation.

By contrast, knockdown of CGI-58 exacerbated CDAHFD-induced steatohepatitis (Figure 2). Lipid-droplet size and both cholesterol and triglyceride content within droplets were increased, and expression of MASH-associated macrophage and fibrogenic markers was elevated. Proteomic analysis revealed downregulation of key lipolytic enzymes—Lipe and Mgll—and of the cholesterol-ester hydrolase Ces1d, suggesting that impaired droplet lipolysis led to cholesterol-ester retention. Importantly, pharmacologic inhibition of liver cholesterol synthesis with bempedoic acid alleviated steatohepatitis in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice, underscoring the central pathogenic role of lipid-droplet cholesterol.

Perilipins are key components of the lipid-droplet coat that regulate the accessibility and functional interactions of the ATGL lipase complex. PLIN2 limits ATGL access to lipid droplets,14 and recent siRNA-mediated PLIN2 silencing ameliorated MASH,15 suggesting that lipid-droplet coat proteins can modulate MASH severity through mechanisms distinct from the direct lipolytic activation mediated by CGI-58/ATGL. Prior work has shown that PLIN5 is enriched on smaller lipid droplets and is often associated with mitochondria.16 In our study, the opposite regulation of PLIN5 by CIDEB vs. CGI-58 knockdown (Figure 3G) suggests that these interventions remodel the lipid droplet surface in divergent ways, which is consistent with the reciprocal changes in lipid droplet size observed in our model.

Finally, simultaneous silencing of CGI-58 in CIDEB-knockdown mice abrogated the protective effects of CIDEB inhibition: lipid-droplet cholesterol and triglyceride levels rose, crown-like structure formation increased, and plasma transaminases and fibrogenic gene expression were elevated. Together, these findings position CIDEB and CGI-58 as counter-regulatory modulators of liver lipid-droplet cholesterol homeostasis and identify CGI-58-dependent lipolysis as the critical downstream effector through which CIDEB inhibition confers protection.

For CIDEB, two recent studies demonstrated that CIDEB knockdown by GalNAc-siRNA reduced liver inflammation and fibrosis in mouse models of both high-fat diet and CDAHFD.7,8 These findings were consistent with our observations. However, neither of these reports investigated the mechanistic basis by which decreased lipid droplet size due to CIDEB depletion ameliorated steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis.

Previous studies have demonstrated that liver-specific CGI-58-knockout mice fed standard chow developed only simple steatosis by 16 weeks of age, with inflammation, fibrosis, and hepatic cholesterol accumulation appearing by 42 weeks.17 Under Western diet (40% fat, 0.2% cholesterol) feeding for 14 weeks induced both triglyceride and cholesterol deposition in the liver, accompanied by steatohepatitis and fibrosis.18 Two studies employing MOE-ASO to silence CGI-58 in the liver (and adipose tissue) under high-fat feeding observed increased hepatic triglyceride accumulation but did not address progression to inflammation or fibrosis.19,20 Moreover, CGI-58 ASO-treated mice exhibited decreased plasma NEFA concentrations, indicating that CGI-58 knockdown suppressed lipolysis in both liver and adipose tissues.

Our data demonstrate that the hepatoprotective effect of CIDEB knockdown is mediated specifically through a reduction in liver lipid-droplet cholesterol. By integrating proteomic data showing upregulation of ATGL and CGI-58 with our genetic and dietary manipulations, we establish a causal pathway in which CIDEB depletion enhances ATGL/CGI-58-dependent lipolysis, lowers both liver lipid-droplet triglyceride and cholesterol content, and thereby ameliorates CDAHFD-induced steatohepatitis.

In addition, Cideb ASO modestly increased the abundance of several proteins involved in mitochondrial β-oxidation, suggesting a partial upregulation of FAO capacity. In our prior study on high-fat-diet-fed mice, CIDEB knockdown robustly upregulated hepatic FAO.21 Consistent with this, a recent independent study reported that Cideb deletion increases FAO and that Pparα deletion abrogated the protective effects conferred by Cideb deletion.22 As a future direction, pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of CPT1A could provide a rigorous test of whether enhanced FAO is strictly required for the protective effects of CIDEB knockdown.

This mechanistic insight identifies the ATGL-CGI-58 axis as a key downstream target of CIDEB inhibition. Whereas previous CGI-58-knockout or ASO models had not been evaluated in a 1-week CDAHFD context, our work demonstrates that CGI-58 silencing under this acute MASH model leads to marked accumulation of cholesterol as well as triglycerides in liver lipid droplets and progression of MASH. The limited efficacy of GPAM knockdown in improving MASH in CGI-58 ASO-treated mice despite lowering triglycerides contrasts sharply with the robust benefit of bempedoic acid, which concurrently suppresses liver lipid-droplet cholesterol and triglyceride deposition. Together, these results pinpoint liver lipid-droplet cholesterol accumulation, in addition to triglyceride storage per se, as the principal driver of MASH progression in CGI-58-deficient livers.

In the present study, our proteomics dataset did not reveal coordinated changes in canonical SREBP2 targets, or in LXR target proteins (Figure 3G), arguing against increased cholesterol production or a major LXR-driven transcriptional disposal/efflux program as primary drivers of the reciprocal lipid-droplet cholesterol/MASH phenotypes of CIDEB knockdown/CGI-58 knockdown. Instead, these data suggest that the dominant mechanism is more likely related to altered cholesterol redistribution/retention within lipid droplets. Lipid-droplet triglycerides and cholesterol are known to be tightly coupled.23 In the setting of CIDEB knockdown, enhanced lipolysis via the ATGL-CGI-58 pathway appears to primarily reduce lipid-droplet triglycerides, with a subsequent decline in lipid-droplet cholesterol.

Although CIDEB and CGI-58 have not been previously connected, it is known that CIDEC can bind to both CGI-58 and ATGL, inhibiting lipolysis.24,25 When mutated, CIDEC can lead to partial lipodystrophy.26 Given the similarity between CIDEB and CIDEC, it is plausible that CIDEB may also interact with CGI-58 and ATGL to regulate lipolysis in hepatocytes. Further biochemical and structural investigation is needed to evaluate this hypothesis.

There is accumulating evidence that implicates cholesterol in MASH progression. Loss-of-function mutations in LXR alpha provoke hepatic cholesterol accumulation and exacerbate inflammation and fibrosis,27,28 while FGF21 has been shown to ameliorate MASH by upregulating hepatocellular ABCG5/8 via the KLB, thereby enhancing cholesterol excretion from hepatocytes to the bile duct.29 EHBP1 promotes PCSK9 secretion, reducing hepatic cholesterol load and downregulating TAZ to improve MASH.30 Together, these findings suggest that liver lipid-droplet proteins could serve as therapeutic targets for reducing liver lipid-droplet cholesterol deposition and attenuating MASH progression.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that hepatic lipid-droplet cholesterol is a critical determinant in both CIDEB- and CGI-58-driven models of MASH. CIDEB knockdown conferred protection by enhancing CGI-58-dependent lipolysis, thereby lowering both cholesterol and triglyceride content within liver lipid droplets. These findings strongly support CIDEB as a promising therapeutic target for MASH.

Limitations of the study

We used the MOE ASO platform, which not only is highly effective in the liver but also elicits knockdown in adipose tissue. Because CIDEB expression is predominant in the liver and minimal in adipose,26 with CIDEC predominating in adipose, we infer that the protective effects we observed stem primarily from hepatic CIDEB silencing. Nevertheless, the adipose CIDEB knockdown effect on MASH cannot be fully excluded.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

The Yale University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all mouse studies. Male C57BL/6J mice, 9 weeks of age (Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were group housed at the animal care facility at Yale University Animal Research Center and maintained under controlled temperature and lighting (12:12 h light/dark cycle, lights on at 7 a.m.) with ad lib access to water and food. Mice received weekly intraperitoneal injections of N-acetylgalactosamine-modified antisense oligonucleotides (GalNAc-ASO) against Atgl, Soat2, Cd36 or nontargeting control ASO at a dose of 5 mg/kg per week for three weeks or 2′-O-methoxyethylribose modified antisense oligonucleotides (MOE-ASO) against Cideb, CGI-58, Gpam or nontargeting control ASO at a dose of 50 mg/kg per week for three weeks. In C57BL/6J mice, a choline-deficient, amino acid-defined high-fat diet (CDAHFD) (60% fat, 0.1% methionine, and no choline, A06071302, Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) was used to induce MASH for one day to eight days. CDAHFD with 2% cholesterol (L-Amino Acid Diet With 60 kcal% Fat With 0.1% Methionine, No Choline, & 2% Cholesterol A06071315, Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) was used in cholesterol supplementation study. Mice were treated daily with bempedoic acid (10 mg/kg) mixed in a small amount (100 mg) of peanut butter or with peanut butter alone as control. Only male mice were used in this study. Therefore, the influence of sex on the observed phenotypes could not be assessed, and whether similar results would be observed in female mice remains to be determined.

METHOD DETAILS

Liver lipid droplet fractionation

The subcellular fractionation protocol has been previously described.31 In brief, for the lipid droplet fractionation, 20 mg of fresh liver tissue was homogenized in 700 μL of homogenization buffer A (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 1mM EDTA, 0.25 mM EGTA, 250 mM sucrose), then 300 μL of 3% sucrose was layered on top of the homogenate. Samples were centrifuged 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The lipid cake was completely collected with a needle.

Measurement of lipid metabolites in liver lipid droplet extracts

Tissue lipid metabolites analyses were performed as previously described.32 For measurements of total cholesterol, triglycerides, phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine, liver lipid droplet extracts from approximately 20 mg of liver tissue were homogenized in 3 mL of 2:1 chloroform/methanol with 10 nmol cholesterol D7, 5 nmol C17 cholesterol ester, 4 nmol C17-phosphatidylcholine and 4 nmol C17-phosphatidylethanolamine as internal standards. After phase separation with 1 mL of water plus 1 mL of chloroform, samples were homogenized and centrifuged, and the chloroform phase was collected. This supernatant was applied to free cholesterol, cholesterol ester, total cholesterol, triglycerides, phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine measurements.

For measurement of total cholesterol, 1 mL supernatant was dried down by nitrogen gas and then diluted by 200 μL 1X Assay Diluent buffer (Total Cholesterol Assay Kits, Cell Biolabs), including 0.25% NP-40. The supernatant was applied to Total Cholesterol Assay (Total Cholesterol Assay Kits, Cell Biolabs) in Figures S1F and S4F.

For measurement of triglycerides, 22.5 μL supernatant is dried down by nitrogen gas and then applied to Sekisui’s Triglyceride-SL Reagent Assay (Sekisui Diagnostics, USA).

For measurement of free cholesterol and cholesterol esters, the organic layer was dried and resuspended in 100 μL of chloroform. The sample was analyzed by LC/MS/MS. An ESI source in positive mode was interfaced with an API 6500 QTRAP tandem mass spectrometer (SCIEX) in conjunction with a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system (Shimadzu). The cholesterol esters and free cholesterol were separated with a C18 betasil HPLC column (Thermo) at a gradient with buffer A (50% methanol, 50% Water, 0.1% formic acid, 10 mM ammonium formate) and buffer B (80% isopropanol, 20% methanol, 0.1% formic acid, 10 mM ammonium formate).

For measurement of phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, the organic layer was dried and resuspended in 3 mL of chloroform. The sample was analyzed by LC/MS/MS. An ESI source in positive mode was interfaced with an API 6500 QTRAP tandem mass spectrometer (SCIEX) in conjunction with a Shimadzu Prominence HPLC system (Shimadzu). The phospholipids were separated with a Luna Silica HPLC column (Phenomenex) at a gradient with buffer A (90% chloroform, 9.5% methanol, 0.5% water, 0.2% ammonium hydroxide) and buffer B (30% chloroform, 30% acetonitrile, 35% methanol, 5% water, 0.2% ammonium hydroxide).

Liver triglycerides and fatty acids measurement

Approximately 50 mg of liver tissue was homogenized in chloroform/methanol (2:1 vol/vol). Samples were mixed with sulfuric acid (1 mol/L) and centrifuged for phase separation. The organic phase was measured using Sekisui’s Triglyceride-SL Reagent (Sekisui Diagnostics, USA). Hepatic fatty acid content was determined by GC/MS.

Plasma biochemical measurement

Plasma biochemical measurement analyses were performed as previously described.32 Plasma triglyceride was measured using Sekisui’s Triglyceride-SL Reagent (Sekisui Diagnostics, USA). Plasma ALT, AST, and total cholesterol levels were determined by using a COBAS Mira Plus analyzer (Roche, Switzerland).

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (real-time qPCR) analysis

Real-time qPCR experiments were performed as previously described.33 The gene-specific mRNA expression values were determined and normalized to those of Arbp as an internal control. Briefly, total RNA (1 μg) was extracted using an RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and reverse-transcribed using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). The cDNA products were subjected to real-time qPCR using a Step One Plus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). All primer information is provided in Table S1.

Histological analysis

Yale Pathology Tissue Services performed hematoxylin, eosin (HE), and CD68 immunostaining. The number of crown-like structures of CD68 immunostaining were counted using the NIH’s ImageJ. Frozen liver sections were stained with Bodipy and Filipin, identifying lipid droplets and free cholesterol. The slides were subjected to a fluorescence microscope with Bodipy (Excitation 488 nm and Emission >505 nm) and Filipin (Excitation 735 nm – Two-photon and Emission 420–480 nm). The lipid droplets size of Bodipy staining was assessed using the NIH’s ImageJ.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from frozen mouse liver tissues using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry, and RNA integrity was evaluated using a TapeStation system. RNA-seq libraries were prepared from total RNA using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Raw sequencing reads were uploaded to RNAseqChef (https://imeg-ku.shinyapps.io/RNAseqChef/), a cloud-based pipeline for automated RNA-seq data processing and analysis.34 Differential gene expression analysis was performed using RNAseqChef with EBSeq as the DEG caller. Genes with a fold change ≥2 or ≤0.5 and a Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Hallmark gene sets to identify biological pathways enriched. Visualization and data interpretation were performed using RNAseqChef integrated analysis tools.

We examined the expression of periportal (PP) and pericentral (PC) marker genes using the RNA-seq data and calculated a zonation index (PC score – PP score). PP markers were Cps1, Sds, Cyp2f2, Hal, Gls2, Ass1, Arg1, Pck1, Aldob, and Fbp1, and PC markers were Glul, Cyp2e1, Cyp1a2, Oat, Slc1a2, Ahr, Gck, Aldh3a2, Cyp2a5, and Cyp4a14. For each sample, PC and PP scores were computed from log2(TPM +1)-transformed expression values by averaging the z-scored expression of the predefined PC and PP marker gene sets, and the zonation index was defined as the difference between these scores.

Non-target proteome analysis

Frozen mice liver tissues were redissolved in 100 mM Tris pH 8.0, 4% SDS, 20mM NaCl and 10% acetonitrile using a sonicator (BIORUPTOR II, CosmoBio, Tokyo, Japan). The protein content was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and adjusted to 150 ng/μL with 100 mM Tris pH 8.0, 4% SDS, 20mM NaCl and 10% acetonitrile. Then 200 μL of protein extract was subjected to clean up and digestion with the SP3-LASP method as described by Konno35 with minor modifications. Briefly, two types of Sera-Mag SpeedBead carboxylate-modified magnetic particles (hydrophilic particles, cat# 45152105050250; hydrophobic particles, cat# 65152105050250; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) were used. These beads were combined at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio, washed twice with distilled water, and reconstituted in distilled water at a concentration of 10 μg solids/μL. 20 μL of reconstituted beads was then added to the protein extract followed by 1-propanol to bring the final concentration to 75%, with mixing for 20 min. Beads were collected and washed twice with 80% 1-propanol and once with ethanol. Subsequently, the beads were resuspended in 80 μL of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 10 mM CaCl2 containing 0.02% lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol, and 2 μL of 500 ng/μL Trypsin Platinum (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Thereafter, to digest the protein, the sample was gently mixed at 37°C for 14 h. The digested sample was reduced and alkylated with the addition of 8 μL of 110 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine and 440 mM 2-chloroacetamide at 80°C for 15 min, and then acidified with 16 μL of 5% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The sample was desalted using a GL-Tip SDB (GL Sciences, Tokyo, Japan), which was washed with 25 μL of 80% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% TFA, followed by equilibration with 50 μL of 3% ACN in 0.1% TFA. The sample was loaded onto the tip, washed with 80 μL 3% ACN in 0.1% TFA, and eluted with 50 μL of 36% ACN in 0.1% TFA. The eluate was dried in a centrifugal evaporator (miVac Duo Concentrator; Genevac, Ipswich, UK). The dried sample was then redissolved in 0.02% decyl maltose neopentyl glycol (DMNG) containing 0.1% TFA. The peptide concentration in the digest sample was measured using a Pierce Quantitative Fluorescent Peptide Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and adjusted to 200 ng/μL with 0.02% DMNG containing 0.1% TFA.

The peptides were directly injected onto a Global Peptide Separation column (C18, 75 μm ID, 30 cm length, 1.7 μm beads; CoAnn Technologies, Richland, WA, USA) at 50°C and then separated with a 100-min gradient (A = 0.1% formic acid in water, B = 0.1% formic acid in 80% ACN) consisting of 0 min 7% B, 88 min 37% B, 94 min 60% B, 100 min 70% B at a flow rate of 150 nL/min using an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides eluted from the column were analyzed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an InSpIon system (AMR, Tokyo, Japan) for DIA-MS. MS1 spectra were collected in the range of m/z 495–745 at a 60,000 resolution to set an AGC target of 3 × 106 and a maximum injection time of “Auto”. MS2 spectra were collected at m/z 200–1,800 at a 45,000 resolution to set an AGC target of 3 × 106, a maximum injection time of “Auto”, and a normalized collision energy of 26%. The isolation width for MS2 was set to 4 Th, window patterns at m/z 500–740 were used for window placements optimized via Scaffold DIA v3.0.1 (Proteome Software, Portland, OR, USA). Six biological replicates were prepared for each of the 16 groups, including one control group, for a total of 96 samples analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

The DIA-MS data were queried against the in silico mouse spectral library, using DIA-NN v. 1.8.1.36 Initially, the spectral library was generated from the mouse UniProt protein sequence database (Proteome ID UP000000589, 21949 entries, downloaded on March 7, 2023) using DIA-NN. The parameters for spectral library generation included trypsin as the digestion enzyme, allowance for 1 missed cleavage, a peptide length range of 7–45, precursor charge ranging from 2 to 4, precursor m/z ranging from 490 to 750, and fragment ion m/z ranging from 200 to 1800. Additionally, “FASTA digest for library-free search/library generation”, “deep learning-based spectra, RTs, and IM prediction”, “n-term M excision”, and “C carbamidomethylation” were enabled. For the DIA-NN search, the following parameters were applied: a mass accuracy of 10 ppm, MS1 accuracy of 10 ppm, protein inference based on genes, utilization of neural network classifiers in single-pass mode, quantification strategy using robust LC (high precision), cross-run normalization set to “off”. Furthermore, “unrelated runs”, “use isotopologues”, “heuristic protein inference”, and “no shared spectra” were enabled, while the use of match-between-run was deactivated. The threshold for protein identification was set at 1% or less for both precursor and protein false-discovery rate. Protein quantification values were aggregated over the quantification values of unique peptides as calculated by DIA-NN.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

The results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed by unpaired one-sided Student’s t test. Significance is indicated by asterisks, as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.
