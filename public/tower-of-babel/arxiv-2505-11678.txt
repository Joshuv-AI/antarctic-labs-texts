Fairness-Utility Trade-off via Wasserstein Projection
Yan Chen, Zheng Tan, Jose Blanchet, Hanzhang Qin

Abstract
Ensuring fairness in data-driven decision-making is a critical concern, but existing fairness constraints often involve trade-offs with overall utility. We propose a fairness framework that enforces strong demographic parity-related fairness criteria (with ϵ \epsilon -tolerance) in propensity score allocation while guaranteeing a minimum total utility. This approach balances equity and utility by calibrating propensity scores to satisfy fairness criteria and optimizing outcomes without incurring unacceptable losses in performance. Grounded in a binary treatment and sensitive attribute setting under causal fairness setup, our method provides a principled mechanism to address fairness while transparently managing associated economic and social costs, offering a practical approach for designing equitable policies in diverse decision-making contexts. Building on this, we provide theoretical guarantee for our proposed utility-constrained fairness evaluation framework, and we formalize a hypothesis testing framework to help practitioners assess whether the desired fairness-utility trade-off is achieved.

Introduction

Over the past decade, ensuring fairness in data-driven decision-making has become a critical concern across many domains, including personalized marketing, lending, and healthcare
(
Kallus and Zhou 2021
;
Richards et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Kumar et al. 2022
;
Ahmad et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Giovanola and Tiribelli 2023
;
Bertsimas et al. 2012
;
Manski et al. 2023
;
Qi 2017
)
. A substantial body of research has sought to formalize fairness through constraints on predictive models
(
Gardner et al. 2019
;
Alikhademi et al. 2022
;
Pleiss et al. 2017
;
Jacobs and Wallach 2021
;
Taskesen et al. 2021
;
Navarro et al. 2021
)
, aimed at safeguarding individuals or groups from discriminatory treatment
(
Chouldechova 2017
;
Imai and Jiang 2023
;
Kizilcec and Lee 2022
)
. However, imposing such constraints often introduces trade-offs with respect to overall utility or utility
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Mehrotra et al. 2018
;
Ge et al. 2022
;
Mashiat et al. 2022
;
Kallus and Zhou 2021
)
. In practice, fairness cannot be addressed by metrics in isolation; responsible frameworks must account for the potential economic or social costs that arise when sensitive features are disregarded.

Recent empirical evidence underscores the complexity of these trade-offs. Using only non-sensitive covariates in recommendation systems or learning classifiers to boost revenue or utility may seem like a way to prevent algorithmic discrimination. However, real-world data structures are complex and difficult to fully understand. For instance, the study
Sun et al. 2024
highlights how excluding sensitive personal information can lead to significant decreases in revenue or total utility in advertising and personalization applications.
Sun et al. 2024
demonstrates that excluding personal data significantly reduces customer engagement and market transactions, disproportionately impacting niche merchants and underserved customers, such as women and rural residents who rely more heavily on e-commerce due to limited access to physical stores. Another example comes from the accuracy-fairness tradeoff in image classification and representation learning. The authors of
Dehdashtian et al. 2024
propose a method to numerically quantify this tradeoff for specific prediction tasks and group fairness definitions, enabling a new evaluation scheme for representations in computer vision. Their analysis of multiple image classification tasks reveals that pre-trained models often fall significantly short of the optimal balance between accuracy and fairness.

These observations motivate a more nuanced approach to fairness: one that ensures equitable outcomes for protected groups while explicitly maintaining a sufficient level of overall utility. Particularly, we aim to answer the following questions:

(i)

How can the trade-offs be quantified from data under customized fairness and utility criteria based on demographic attributes?

(ii)

How can hypothesis testing be developed for utility-constrained fairness given the underlying data distribution?

To answer the first question, we propose a general utility-constrained fairness evaluation framework that formalizes strong demographic parity
(
Jiang et al. 2020
)
in propensity score allocation while ensuring a guaranteed lower bound on total utility. Unlike many existing fairness approaches that sacrifice utility for equity, our method explicitly enforces a minimum total utility. Grounded in a causal inference setting with binary treatment (e.g., “treat” vs. “do not treat”) and binary sensitive attributes (e.g., membership in a protected group), our framework balances fairness and utility by constraining propensity scores across sensitive attributes with a small tolerance level for practical variability. Simultaneously, it imposes a utility constraint to maintain the total potential outcome above a specified threshold under the chosen treatment policy.

For fairness, we address discrimination in binary treatment assignment by imposing constraints on propensity scores, aligning with classification tasks in machine learning. While the most widely used fairness concept in classification — demographic parity enforces statistical independence between class predictions and sensitive attributes, it has practical limitations, as noted by
Jiang et al. 2020
, including sensitivity to threshold choices and difficulty in imposing independence constraints. To overcome these issues,
Jiang et al. 2020
introduces
strong demographic parity
, which imposes independence directly on model outputs (or treatment assignments in our case) by minimizing Wasserstein distances between treatment distributions across sensitive groups. Building on this, our framework adopts an
\epsilon
-approximate strong demographic parity criterion to ensure fair treatment assignment, providing a direct mechanism for analyzing the tension between fairness constraints and total utility. This mechanism ensures that, while we address fairness in the treatment assignment, we do not incur unacceptable losses in overall performance or revenue, thereby achieving a principled balance between equity and practical efficacy.

To address the second question, we formalize a hypothesis testing framework using our evaluation mechanism and derive the limiting distribution of a term that stochastically dominates the test statistic. This approach enables practitioners to assess whether the desired fairness-utility tradeoff is achieved.

In the remainder of the paper, we formalize our utility-constrained fairness framework and provide guidance for its application in empirical evaluations. We illustrate our hypothesis-testing approach through synthetic data and empirical analyses. Our results demonstrate that it is possible to satisfy a strong notion of fairness while maintaining a desired level of utility, offering new insights into designing balanced policies in general data-sensitive settings.

1.1
Related Work

The field of algorithmic fairness has rapidly expanded in recent years, producing a wide array of fairness definitions and modeling techniques. Early research focused on notions, such as demographic parity
(
Calders et al. 2013
;
Feldman et al. 2015
;
Zafar et al. 2017
)
— which seeks to match decision probabilities across protected groups, equalized odds
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
— which requires that false positive and false negative rates remain independent of group membership, equal opportunity
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
and their probabilistic counterparts
(
Pleiss et al. 2017
)
. More broadly, group fairness notions aim to reduce disparities in the proportions of favorable outcomes across different sensitive groups, with additional examples including equality of opportunity
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
and disparate impact
(
Feldman et al. 2015
)
. Despite the abundance of available definitions, there is no consensus on a single industry-standard measure. Furthermore, aside from trivial cases, it is generally impossible for a machine learning algorithm to satisfy multiple fairness notions simultaneously. For a comprehensive overview of these and other fairness notions, see
(
Pessach and Shmueli 2022
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
.

Subsequent studies explored the fairness–utility trade-off, revealing that imposing fairness constraints can degrade performance if not carefully managed
(
Dehdashtian et al. 2024
;
Rychener et al. 2022
;
Si et al. 2021
;
Gölz et al. 2019
;
Pessach and Shmueli 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Dutta et al. 2020
;
Small et al. 2024
)
. Several methods have been proposed to mitigate these trade-offs, including adversarial debiasing
(
Aïvodji et al. 2021
;
Zhang and Sang 2020
)
, regularization-based approaches
(
Radovanović et al. 2020
;
Jiang et al. 2020
)
, and post-processing techniques
(
Lohia et al. 2019
;
Petersen et al. 2021
)
that adjust outcomes or probabilities after an unconstrained model is trained.

A growing body of work also examines causal fairness, leveraging principles from causal inference to identify and mitigate discriminatory effects rooted in underlying data-generating processes. In this view, fairness is formulated in terms of interventions on sensitive attributes and their causal pathways to outcomes. For instance, counterfactual fairness
(
Kusner et al. 2017
)
explicitly requires that an individual’s outcome would remain the same if we “counterfactually” change their sensitive characteristic. Other efforts focus on path-specific fairness, where fairness constraints are imposed on select causal pathways deemed illegitimate for decision-making, while allowing legitimate pathways to influence the outcome. These causal approaches are particularly relevant in domains such as hiring, lending, and medical treatment, where understanding the structural relationships between sensitive features, confounders, and outcomes can be critical for reducing discrimination while maintaining predictive accuracy.

Moreover, recent empirical work highlights the unintended consequences of not using sensitive or granular personal data. For instance, the large-scale field experiment reported in
Sun et al. 2024
demonstrates that overly restrictive data regulations can significantly reduce revenue and overall user satisfaction. This evidence underscores the importance of considering total utility or utility alongside fairness, rather than seeking fairness in isolation. In parallel, fairness constraints on classifiers or propensity scores have become a key tool in fair machine learning
(
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Pereira Barata et al. 2024
)
, especially for decision policies like treatment assignment
(
Fang et al. 2023
)
or recommendation systems
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
. Existing work on adjusting propensity scores to reduce group disparities often relies on simplified fairness notions, lacks explicit utility guarantees, or employs trade-off evaluation frameworks that may be overly task-specific
(
Sacharidis et al. 2019
;
Dehdashtian et al. 2024
)
.

Against this backdrop, our work seeks to fill a critical gap by incorporating both fairness—via a strong demographic parity
(
Jiang et al. 2020
)
constraint in propensity scores—and a guaranteed minimum utility requirement. By jointly enforcing these criteria, we aim to demonstrate that it is possible to uphold meaningful fairness standards without sacrificing the economic or societal benefits that organizations rely on.

In terms of methodology, the approach that we utilize is related to
Taskesen et al. 2021
,
Si et al. 2021
, which in turn casts fairness problems as hypothesis testing using the Robust Wasserstein Profile Inference projection method introduced in
Blanchet et al. 2019
(which focuses on asymptotic statistics for projections defined via equalities, not in terms of inequalities as we do here). Our focus is different, we aim at studying (i) and (ii), where as
Taskesen et al. 2021
;
Si et al. 2021
focus on studying specific notions of fairness based on hard constraints (no parameters are used to quantify the trade-offs) as we do here.

2.1
Optimal Transport and Wasserstein Distance

We first introduce the notion of optimal transport costs via Wasserstein distance. Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
denote the set of all probability distributions on
\mathcal{Z}\overset{\Delta}{=}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

Definition 1

(Optimal transport costs and Wasserstein Distance)
.

Given a lower semiconinuous function
c:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, the type-2 Wasserstein optimal transport cost
\mathcal{W}_{c}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})
for any
\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
is defined as

\mathcal{W}_{c}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})=\min_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(Z,Z^{\prime})^{2}]},

where
\Gamma(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})
is the set of all joint distributions
(Z,Z^{\prime})
such that the law of
Z=(X,A,Y)
is
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and the law of
Z^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},A^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
.

When
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is a metric on
\mathcal{Z}
, and
W_{c}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the 2-Wasserstein distance
Villani et al. 2009
. For the rest of the paper we set

c((x,a,y),(x^{\prime},a^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\infty\cdot|a-a^{\prime}|+\infty\cdot|y-y^{\prime}|,

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This choice is common in applications such as distribution alignment, generative modeling, and fairness constraints. Conceptually, the 2-Wasserstein distance accounts not only for the pointwise distance between distributions but also for how the distributions’ mass must be “rearranged”. Consequently, the Wasserstein framework offers a powerful method to compare complex distributions while retaining meaningful geometric information about
\mathcal{X}
. This perspective is especially useful in fairness settings, where aligning distributions across groups is often a goal, and the optimal transport interpretation allows for a direct assessment of how different populations overlap or diverge in feature space.

Strong Demographic Parity

We are interested in the scenario where the action doesn’t depend on the sensitive information
A
. Specifically, we want
Y\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}A
. Let

Q_{a,\tau}=\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a}(X)>\tau|A=a)

for sensitivity attribute group
A=a
. Note that
\pi_{a}(X)\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}A
implies
W\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}A

(SDP) fairness criterion proposed by

Jiang et al. 2020
showed that this criterion is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a}(X)>\tau)-\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a^{\prime}}(X)>\tau)\right|=0,\ \forall a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}.

(1)

Note that (
1
) holds if and only if
\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a}(X)>\tau)=\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a^{\prime}}(X)>\tau)
for any
\tau\in[0,1]
.

The SDP criterion requires that

\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbf{1}\{\pi_{1}(X)>\tau\}-\mathbf{1}\{\pi_{0}(X)>\tau\}]\right|\right]=0,

i.e. the
1
-Wasserstein distance between the probability measure of
\pi_{1}(X)
and
\pi_{0}(X)
under
\mathbb{Q}
is
0
. Note that

\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}[\left|\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{1}(X)>\tau)-\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{0}(X)>\tau)\right|]=0

holds if and only if
p_{\pi_{1}(X)}^{\mathbb{Q}}=p_{\pi_{0}(X)}^{\mathbb{Q}}
almost surely, where
p_{\pi_{a}(X)}^{\mathbb{Q}}
is the probability density function (pdf) of
\pi_{a}(X)
for
a\in\{0,1\}
with
X\sim\mathbb{Q}
.

In our fairness evaluation, we adopt
\epsilon
-approximate strong demographic parity for propensity scores across different sensitive groups, allowing an
\epsilon
-tolerance for SDP as follows:

\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\left|\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a}(X)>\tau)-\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{a^{\prime}}(X)>\tau)\right|\leq\epsilon,\ \forall a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}.

We consider this
\epsilon
-relaxed fairness criterion since in practice, The fairness in treatment assignment, or the use of propensity scores, may conflict with overarching utility constraints. Additionally, imposing a strict strong demographic parity constraint can prove overly restrictive or even unattainable. To address this, practitioners have the flexibility to adjust the parameter
\epsilon
to suit the specific requirements of their applications.

3
Proposed Causal Fairness Evaluation Framework

We impose the following assumption on the data-generating process:

Assumption 1
.

The joint distribution
(Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0),X_{i},A_{i},W_{i})
satisfies

1)

Unconfoundedness:
W_{i}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}\{Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)\}|X_{i},A_{i}
.

2)

Overlapping: There exists some
\eta\in(0,1)
such that
\eta\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{i}=1|X_{i}=x,A_{i}=a)\leq 1-\eta
for any
x\in\mathcal{X},a\in\mathcal{A}
.

3)

Boundedness:
0\leq Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)\leq B
for some bounded constant
B>0
.

Assumption
1
is standard in causal inference literature. By definition, the expected utility under probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
is equal to

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[Y(W)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[WY(1)+(1-W)Y(0)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[Y(1)W+(1-W)Y(0)|X,A\right]\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[m_{1}(X,A)\pi_{a}(X)+m_{0}(X,A)(1-\pi_{a}(X))]\\
&\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{a}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[m_{1}(X,a)\pi_{a}(X)+m_{0}(X,a)(1-\pi_{a}(X))\right]\end{array}

(2)

The utility-constrained fairness evaluation criterion
\mathcal{R}_{\epsilon}^{r}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})
under
\epsilon
-tolerance for strong demographic parity (SDP) may be formulated as

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\quad\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})}\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{c}(\mathbb{Q},\hat{\mathbb{P}})^{2}\\
\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\quad\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbf{1}\{\pi_{1}(X)>\tau\}-\mathbf{1}\{\pi_{0}(X)>\tau\}]\right|\right]\leq\epsilon\\
\\
&\quad\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{a}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[m_{1}(X,a)\pi_{a}(X)+m_{0}(X,a)(1-\pi_{a}(X))]\geq r\end{array}

(P)

The final constraint addresses the reservation level
r
for overall expected utility. We employ expected utility in the second constraint of (

7
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a general utility-constrained fairness evaluation framework that addresses the trade-off between utility and fairness under customizable fairness and utility criteria. Existing fairness evaluation frameworks are often highly task-specific and lack generalizability. Building on these limitations, we introduce a flexible and adaptable framework that can be applied across a wide range of applications.

Our framework specifically incorporates a Wasserstein projection from a reference probability measure to a target probability distribution that satisfies strong demographic parity with
\epsilon
-tolerance while ensuring a utility level of
r
. Practitioners can adjust the parameters
\epsilon
and
r
to align with their specific application requirements. Additionally, we provide numerical results and empirical illustrations on using this framework to test hypotheses regarding whether a given probability distribution satisfies the utility-fairness trade-off criteria. While the limiting distribution derived in this work may be somewhat conservative, we leave further refinement of this aspect for future research. Moreover, we extend the framework to incorporate alternative metrics for quantifying fairness in Appendix
9
, with a detailed analysis of this generalization also deferred to future work.

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\quad L(\lambda,\alpha;\nu)\\

where

Note that

(11)

and we already know that

A More General Causal Fairness Evaluation Framework

The proposed utility-constrained fairness criterion built upon strong demographic parity motivate us to propose a more generalized utility-constrained fairness evaluation framework via wasserstein projection (
UC-WPF
) for group fairness. Let
\hat{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be a reference probability measure,
F(\cdot)
be a convex functional defined on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})

R(\cdot,a)

\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}^{r}(\hat{\mathbb{P}})=\begin{cases}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}&\mathcal{W}_{c}(\mathbb{Q},\hat{\mathbb{P}})^{2}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&F(\mathbb{Q})\leq\epsilon\\
&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{a}\mu(X,a)]\geq r.\end{cases}

(UC-WPF)

Suppose
\mathbb{Q}_{1}\overset{d}{=}\pi_{1}(X)
,
\mathbb{Q}_{0}\overset{d}{=}\pi_{0}(X)
,
X\sim\mathbb{Q}
. Our previously proposed fairness evaluation framework corresponds to the case where

8.2

related to the fairness constraints

Example 1

\mathrm{D}_{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}||\mathbb{Q}_{0})\leq\epsilon
, where
\mathrm{D}_{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}||\mathbb{Q}_{0}):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\pi_{1}(x)\log(\pi_{1}(x)/\pi_{0}(x))d\mathbb{Q}(x)
, which is linear in
\mathbb{Q}
, so
\mathrm{D}_{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{1}||\mathbb{Q}_{0})
is convex in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Example 2

(Total-variation fairness constraint)
.

For the total-variation fairness constraint

\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{0})=\sup_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])}|\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{1}(X)\in\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{0}(X)\in\mathcal{A})|\leq\epsilon.

Note that

\begin{array}[]{rl}&\quad|\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{1}(X)\in\mathcal{A})-\mathbb{Q}(\pi_{0}(X)\in\mathcal{A})|\\
&=|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbf{1}\{\pi_{1}(X)\in\mathcal{A}\}-\mathbf{1}\{\pi_{0}(X)\in\mathcal{A}\}]|,\end{array}

which is convex in
\mathbb{Q}
. Since the supremum of a family of convex function is still convex, the total-variation fairness constraint is convex in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Example 3

(Integral Probability Metrics fairness constraint)
.

For a set of real valued functions
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Integral Probability Metrics (IPM) is defined as

\mathrm{IPM}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fd\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fd\nu.

One example is
\mathcal{F}=\{f:\|f\|_{H}\leq 1\}
where
H
is a reproducing kernel hilbert space (RKHS), which gives the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD). So

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{IPM}(\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{0}(X))&\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[f(\pi_{1}(x))-f(\pi_{0}(x))]d\mathbb{Q}(x)\\
&\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[f(\pi_{1}(X))-f(\pi_{0}(X))],\end{array}

which is the supremum of a family of linear functions in
\mathbb{Q}
, thus
\mathrm{IPM}(\pi_{1}(X),\pi_{0}(X))
is convex in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Following this evaluation framework, we can extend the approach outlined above to derive strong duality results, determine the limiting distribution of test statistics, and implement hypothesis tests for utility-constrained fairness hypotheses.
