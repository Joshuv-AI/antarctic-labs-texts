New Tuberculosis Infection and Reactivation of Tuberculosis in Patients with Psoriasis or Psoriatic Arthritis Receiving IL-23 Inhibitors: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Gang Wang, Sang Woong Youn, Chia-Ling Chang, Hidehisa Saeki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biologic agents, including the interleukin-23 (IL-23) inhibitors (IL-23is) guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab, are effective therapies for the treatment of psoriasis (PsO) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA). However, the risk of latent TB infection (LTBI) reactivation or new tuberculosis (TB) infection is of question, given documented evidence of occurrence of cases in patients with PsO and PsA treated with biologics with other mechanisms of action, and the relatively high prevalence of TB in some Asian Pacific (APAC) countries. We conducted a systematic literature review to summarize published evidence related to reactivation of LTBI or new TB infections among IL-23i-treated patients with PsO or PsA, with a focus on the APAC region. We searched the EMBASE, MEDLINE, MEDLINE In-Process, CNKI, Wanfang, ICHUSHI, KOREAMED, and AIRITI databases and gray literature sources for reports on randomized controlled trials and real-world observational studies reporting reactivation of LTBI or new TB infections among IL-23i-treated patients with PsO or PsA. A total of 58 publications from 41 studies met prespecified inclusion criteria, including 9 publications from 8 studies in APAC countries. No LTBI reactivation events or new TB cases were observed in 33 of 34 studies that enrolled IL-23i-treated patients with PsO, including patients with LTBI who did not complete chemoprophylaxis. In one observational study, LTBI reactivation was reported in a single risankizumab-treated patient. Similarly, no cases of LTBI reactivation or new TB cases were observed in 8 studies enrolling IL-23i-treated patients with PsA. The results summarized here suggest that the risks of new TB infections or LTBI reactivation are minimal among IL-23i-treated patients with PsO or PsA. Routine testing for LTBI in patients initiating IL-23i treatment should not be required, particularly in regions with low TB prevalence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01809-8.

Key Summary Points

IL-23 inhibitors (IL-23is; guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab) offer effective treatment for psoriasis (PsO) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and, compared to tumor necrosis factor alpha inhibitors, may carry a lower risk of tuberculosis (TB) infection or latent TB infection (LTBI) reactivation; however, guidelines still recommend screening for LTBI before initiating IL-23i therapy.Across 34 studies that included IL-23i-treated patients with PsO, no new active TB cases were observed, and a single case of LTBI reactivation was reported in an observational study after 3 years of follow-up.Across 8 studies that enrolled IL-23i-treated patients with PsA, no new TB infections were reported, and no studies documenting LTBI reactivation events were identified.No cases of reactivation of LTBI were identified in patients with PsO or PsA who did not receive TB prophylaxis.The data summarized here suggest an absence of evidence that IL-23is present a risk of TB infection or LTBI reactivation, whether or not patients received TB prophylaxis, and support that routine TB testing for patients initiating IL-23i treatment should not be required.

Introduction

Psoriasis (PsO) is a chronic, systemic, immune-mediated inflammatory disorder primarily characterized by erythematous, scaly plaques occurring on the skin [1]. PsO is associated with a spectrum of systemic comorbidities, most notably psoriatic arthritis (PsA), an inflammatory arthropathy that occurs in up to 30% of those with PsO and can lead to joint damage and functional impairment if not adequately managed [2]. PsO directly affects approximately 2–3% of the global population [1]. Although PsO prevalence rates in the Asia–Pacific (APAC) region, ranging from 0.5% to 1.0% in countries such as China, Japan, and South Korea [3], are lower than those in Western populations, the absolute numbers of patients in APAC are substantial because of the region’s large population, underscoring the public health significance of PsO and PsA in these countries.

Current therapeutic options for PsO include topical treatments, phototherapy, and systemic treatments, often given in combination. Topical agents such as corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues, and retinoids may be sufficient to control mild to moderate disease [4]. For more moderate-to-severe disease, systemic treatments, including traditional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (e.g., methotrexate and cyclosporine), as well as biologic therapies (e.g., tumor necrosis factor [TNF] alpha inhibitors [TNFis]), are typically used [4]. Interleukin (IL)−23 inhibitors (IL-23is), including guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab, represent an available class of biologic therapies. IL-23 is a cytokine crucial for the differentiation and maintenance of pathogenic T helper 17 (Th17) cells, which are implicated in the inflammatory processes of both PsO and PsA [5]. Clinical trials have demonstrated that IL-23is can lead to significant improvements in skin clearance and joint symptoms, offering a highly effective option for patients [5].

Clinical guidelines from different regions recommend that all patients initiating biologics for PsO, regardless of their mechanism of action, be screened for latent Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) infection (LTBI), because of warnings for other biologics used for treating PsO and PsA, namely TNFis, given their association with an impaired ability to contain TB infection and increase risk of associated complications [6–8]. However, IL-23is may carry lower risks of TB infection and LTBI reactivation compared to TNFis [9] because the IL-23/Th17 pathway, while critical for mucosal and skin immunity, does not contribute to the formation and maintenance of granulomas, which is mediated by TNFα signaling [10, 11].

One potential complicating factor for the assessment of TB outcomes in patients receiving biologics is geoepidemiological patterns of TB incidence and prevalence. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies TB incidence rates below 10 cases per 100,000 population-year, as seen in regions such as Europe, North America, and some APAC countries (e.g., Japan, Australia, and New Zealand), as “low”, whereas rates ≥ 40 per 100,000 population-year are considered “high” [12, 13]. Given global variation in TB incidence and prevalence, the aim of this systematic literature review (SLR) is to summarize worldwide reported events of LTBI reactivation and new TB infections in IL-23i-treated patients with PsO or PsA, with particular attention to the APAC region.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram

Methods

This SLR is presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement guidelines [14]. The review was designed to address the question, “What are the reported cases of LTBI reactivation and new TB infections in patients diagnosed with PsO or PsA receiving IL-23is?” The Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes and Study Type (PICOS) framework was used to guide development of the research question and study inclusion criteria (Table 1).

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Data Sources and Search Strategy

The EMBASE, MEDLINE, MEDLINE In-Process, CNKI, Wanfang, ICHUSHI, KOREAMED, and AIRITI databases were searched on April 16, 2025. Search strings comprised indexed and free-text terms (Tables S1–S7). Gray literature sources and conference proceedings were searched using free-text terms for material published from January 1, 2022, onward (Table S8).

Study Eligibility Criteria

Phase 2 or 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and real-world observational studies that assessed adults who were ≥ 18 years of age, diagnosed with PsO or PsA, and treated with IL-23i therapy (guselkumab, risankizumab, or tildrakizumab), irrespective of assessment of latent TB infection prior to initiating treatment, and reported on LTBI reactivation or new TB infections were eligible for inclusion in this review. Case studies, notes, news articles, editorials, and letters were excluded from this review.

Selection Procedure

Zotero reference management software (Corporation for Digital Scholarship) was used to remove duplicate records obtained from electronic database searches. Titles and abstracts of database records were first screened against pre-defined inclusion criteria by a single human reviewer and a Large Language Model (LLM) integrated via a secure application programming interface (API) and provided with a detailed prompt (see “Study selection” in Supplementary materials file). Any discrepancies between the single human reviewer and LLM screenings were resolved by a third, independent reviewer. As well, the independent human reviewer verified an arbitrarily selected 20% sample of records for which the human reviewer and LLM were in agreement. Full texts of records not excluded during title and abstract screening were then retrieved and screened by two human reviewers working in parallel, and discrepancies were resolved by an independent third reviewer.

Data Extraction

Data from articles for eligible studies were extracted by a single reviewer and entered into a pre-designed table developed in Microsoft Excel®. Data extracted included publication details (title, authors, publication year, sponsor, citation information), study characteristics (study objective, study design, sample size, inclusion/exclusion criteria, follow-up duration, study setting, data source, reported limitations and conclusions), baseline characteristics (prior TB test during screening, active or LTBI at baseline, baseline TB management), and outcomes (LTBI reactivation or new TB cases). All extracted data were subsequently verified by a second, independent human reviewer.

Multiple reports of the same study (including long-term extensions, pooled or secondary analyses, and conference abstracts) were identified through assessment of study identifiers, trial characteristics, and participant cohorts and were collated as a single study in accordance with PRISMA guidance for handling multiple reports. Data were synthesized at the level of the unique study, and participant numbers were derived from the primary parent study only, ensuring that each cohort contributed once to the total and that no participants were double counted.

Quality Assessment

The quality of studies included in the SLR was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies and the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB2) tool for RCTs [15, 16]. Briefly, these assessments addressed the quality of randomization and blinding, the balancing of treatment groups, whether study outcomes had been fully reported, and whether the type of analysis reported was appropriate and adequately implemented.

Results

Study Overview

Of the 1245 records screened, 41 studies described in 58 articles published between 2015 and 2025 reported on the reactivation of LTBI or new TB cases among patients with PsO or PsA who were treated with IL-23is (Fig. 1 and Table 2). Twenty-five studies (40 publications) were RCTs, and 16 (18 publications) were real-world evidence or observational studies. A total of 23 studies included patients treated with guselkumab, 12 with risankizumab, 2 with tildrakizumab, 2 with guselkumab and risankizumab, 1 with guselkumab and tildrakizumab, and 1 with all three IL-23is. These studies most frequently included multinational cohorts (n = 18), or patients from Europe (n = 10), APAC (n = 8), or the Americas (n = 4). Of the 8 studies (9 publications) focused exclusively on APAC populations, 2 were RCTs from Japan and 6 were observational studies, including 4 studies from Mainland China, 1 from Taiwan, and 1 (2 publications) from Japan. Among these 8 studies, 5 included patients treated with guselkumab, 2 with risankizumab, 1 with tildrakizumab, and 1 with guselkumab and risankizumab. Quality Assessment

The 25 RCTs included in this analysis were evaluated for methodological quality using the RoB2 tool [16]. Across all RCTs, the risk of bias was consistently judged to be low in all domains, including randomization procedures, adherence to intended interventions, management of missing data, selection of measurement, and reported results (Table S9). The risk of bias arising from period and crossover effects was also considered for the 9 crossover trials, and was shown to be low as well. The quality of the real-world and observational cohort studies considered was assessed using the NOS tool [15]. Across the 16 studies included in the analysis, most were assessed as being of moderate to high methodological quality (Table S10). Strengths included the clear selection of cohorts and reliable outcome assessments. However, some studies had limitations regarding comparability due to limited interpretation or lack of adjustment for confounders and follow-up reporting.

Overall TB Outcomes

LTBI Reactivation Among Patients with PsO

Four RCTs (8 publications) and 14 real-world observational studies (16 publications) reported on LTBI reactivation in IL-23i-treated patients with PsO (Table S11) [18, 20, 23, 25, 34–36, 39–43, 45–51, 53–55, 75]. In the VOYAGE 1 and 2 trials (N = 1721), 122 patients with LTBI and receiving guselkumab were monitored for up to 5 years without a single documented event of LTBI reactivation [34–37]. The IMMhance trial for risankizumab (N = 407) included 55 patients with LTBI, none of whom developed reactivation [25]. Similarly, the GUIDE study (N = 822) included 6 patients with LTBI who had completed TB prophylaxis at baseline, and no cases of LTBI reactivation were reported over a 68-week follow-up period [18].

Similar results were reported in the real-world and observational studies, among which 4 included patients with LTBI with or without TB prophylaxis (n = 2–189; not reported in 1 study) [39, 41–43, 49], and 4 required prophylactic treatment for all included patients with LTBI (n = 1–2; not reported in 2 studies) [45, 54, 55, 75]. No cases of LTBI reactivation were reported in these 8 studies.

In the other 6 observational studies, the baseline prophylaxis status for patients with LTBI was not reported (n = 2–21; not reported in 1 study) [40, 46–48, 50, 51, 53]. Only 1 study (Demirbas et al. 2025) reported an event of LTBI reactivation in a patient enrolled in a cohort of risankizumab-treated patients with PsO followed for 156 weeks [40]. However, the authors did not provide details related to this patient, such as comorbidities, concomitant immunosuppressant use, or the timing of LTBI reactivation relative to risankizumab initiation.

New TB Cases Among Patients with PsO

In total, 25 publications from 22 included studies reported new TB cases among IL-23i-treated patients with PsO (Table S12). Patient eligibility based on TB status at enrollment varied across studies, with most (n = 15) explicitly excluding individuals with active TB at baseline [17, 19–22, 25–34]. Among the 6614 patients collectively enrolled in these 22 studies, which included long-term follow-up periods of up to 264 weeks, no new cases of active TB were reported, regardless of the specific IL-23i used, study duration, or geographic region.

LTBI Reactivation Among Patients with PsA

One RCT (1 publication) and 1 observational study (2 publications) reported LTBI reactivation events in patients with PsA (Table S13) [42, 43, 57]. In the COSMOS trial for guselkumab, all patients with LTBI (number of patients was not reported) at baseline received TB prophylaxis, and no cases of LTBI reactivation occurred during the 56-week follow-up period [57]. In the observational study conducted by Tada et al. (2024, 2025), patients with LTBI (number of patients was not reported) were treated with guselkumab with or without prophylactic anti-TB therapy, and no cases of reactivation were reported during the 52-week follow-up period [42, 43].

New TB Cases Among Patients with PsA

Eight RCTs (18 publications) that included IL-23i-treated patients with PsA (N = 111–1508) reported new TB cases (Table S14). Six of the 8 studies explicitly excluded patients with active TB at baseline [57–59, 65–68, 71]. Across all studies, including those with unreported baseline TB status, no cases of active TB were identified among IL-23i-treated patients with PsA over 24–112 weeks of follow-up. Notably, the DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2 trials (guselkumab), which together enrolled over 1000 TNFi-experienced and TNFi-naïve patients, respectively, reported no new active TB cases over up to 112 weeks of follow-up [66–73]. Similarly, the KEEPsAKE 1 (N = 946) and KEEPsAKE 2 (N = 419) trials for risankizumab in PsA also reported no new TB cases through up to 100 weeks of follow-up [59–64].

Patients with LTBI Who Did Not Receive Prophylaxis

Among the studies reporting data on LTBI reactivation, 5 studies (6 publications) included patients who did not receive TB prophylaxis, including 1 RCT and 4 observational studies. In the IMMhance trial, 31 of the 55 patients with LTBI received no prophylaxis, and none experienced reactivation [25]. In the EARLY study reported by Mortato et al. (2025), 35 patients with PsO with LTBI at baseline who did not receive TB prophylaxis were included, and no cases of LTBI reactivation were reported during a mean follow-up period of 121.7 (SD 82.4) weeks [41]. In the report by Torres et al. (2024), IL-23i (guselkumab, risankizumab, or tildrakizumab)-treated patients with PsO and LTBI (n = 189) had completed or received either incomplete or no TB prophylaxis before and during IL-23i treatment [49]. No cases of LTBI reactivation were observed during the follow-up period (mean 32.87 [SD 20.95] months) [49]. Similarly, in the PERSIST study, 1 of 2 patients with LTBI at baseline did not receive TB prophylaxis, and neither experienced LTBI reactivation during the 52 weeks following initiation of guselkumab [39]. In the study by Tada et al. (2024 and 2025), among patients in the overall PsO and PsA cohort, 6 had a positive interferon-γ (IFNγ) release assay and 8 had a positive tuberculin skin test at baseline, with 5 patients receiving TB prophylaxis [42, 43]. During 52 weeks of follow-up, no cases of LTBI reactivation were observed.

TB Outcomes for APAC Cohorts

LTBI Reactivation Among Patients with PsO or PsA in APAC Cohorts

In total, 6 studies (7 publications) reported LTBI reactivation among patients with PsO or PsA receiving IL-23is; all were observational studies. Five studies were conducted in Mainland China and Taiwan and included patients with PsO treated with IL-23is (guselkumab, risankizumab, or tildrakizumab). Among these 5 studies, patients with LTBI at baseline received TB prophylaxis in 4, while there was no mention of TB prophylaxis status in the other study. No cases of LTBI reactivation were reported during follow-up ranging from 16 weeks to 92 months [44, 45, 53–55]. In the study from Japan for guselkumab (Tada et al. 2024 and 2025) in PsO or PsA, patients with LTBI at baseline who either did or did not receive TB prophylaxis were included. Similarly, no cases of LTBI reactivation were observed during 52 weeks of follow-up [42, 43].

New TB Cases Among Patients with PsO in APAC Cohorts

In total, 3 studies (3 publications) reported the number of new cases of TB among IL-23i-treated patients with PsO, including 2 RCTs from Japan and 1 observational study from China. Both RCTs evaluated guselkumab (NCT02325219, N = 164) and risankizumab (SustaIMM, NCT03000075, N = 180) over 52 weeks [30, 32], while the observational study (Zheng et al. 2023) included patients receiving guselkumab over a 92-week period (N = 37) [53]. Both RCTs excluded patients with active TB at baseline before randomization, and the observational study included 2 patients with a positive T-SPOT study. No new cases of TB were observed across these 3 studies.

Discussion

This SLR provides a comprehensive summary of outcomes from studies reporting on the number of LTBI reactivation events and new TB cases in IL-23i-treated patients with PsO or PsA. Across 41 RCTs and real-world evidence studies (58 publications) published between 2015 and 2025, in which 13,423 patients with PsO or PsA (including more than 500 patients reported with LTBI at baseline) received guselkumab, risankizumab, or tildrakizumab for 12–264 weeks, 1 case of LTBI reactivation and no new cases of active TB were reported. However, in the study reporting the single case of LTBI reactivation (Demirbas et al. 2025) [40], clinical details regarding this case, including potentially confounding factors, such as status and management of TB before and during IL-23i treatment, comorbidities, concomitant use of immunosuppressants, timing of reactivation relative to IL-23i initiation, and local TB epidemiology, were not reported, thus limiting interpretation of this case. Notably, some studies included patients with LTBI who received IL-23is without TB prophylaxis. In these studies, no cases of LTBI reactivation were observed [25, 39, 41–43, 49]. Further, findings were consistent across APAC and other regions, study designs, and patient populations. Collectively, these results underscore the absence of evidence that IL-23is present a risk for LTBI reactivation and new cases of TB in these patient groups.

These findings align with two previously published SLRs. Li et al. (2025) evaluated IL-23i safety in psoriatic patients with LTBI and, across 7 studies, identified 1 case of LTBI reactivation reported in a research letter, a publication type excluded from the present review per prespecified eligibility criteria [76]. Wu et al. (2023) conducted an SLR and meta-analysis of RCTs assessing short- and long-term infection risk in IL-23i-treated patients with PsO and PsA and identified no cases of LTBI reactivation or incident TB across 14 short-term and 6 long-term studies [44]. The present review extends this body of evidence in two key respects: it incorporates both RCT and real-world evidence study designs across a larger and more recent evidence base (41 studies; 2015–2025), and it is the first to systematically examine TB risk with an explicit focus on the APAC region, a population at elevated baseline TB risk.

According to the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis (GRAPPA 2022) [77] and the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD 2019) [7], IL-23is are strongly recommended for patients with PsO or PsA, especially in cases where conventional systemic therapies are inadequate or contraindicated; yet these guidelines acknowledge that TB screening is recommended prior to initiation of biologics, including IL-23is. However, more recent expert consensus statements support an alternative approach regarding TB testing and IL-23i treatment. The National Psoriasis Foundation Medical Board and International Psoriasis Council issued a joint position statement in 2025 based on a comprehensive review of clinical evidence, stating that “routine testing for latent tuberculosis infection is not required prior to or during treatment of psoriasis patients with IL-17 or IL-23 inhibitors,” but citing exceptions for patients in TB endemic areas and for patients using concomitant immunosuppressants that may increase TB infection or LTBI activation [78]. Similarly, a Skin Inflammation & Psoriasis International Network expert consensus statement supports this sentiment, with panelists calling for a critical reassessment of guidelines and suggesting that the necessity of TB prophylaxis for all patients with LTBI who initiate IL-23i treatments should be reconsidered [79].

The findings from our SLR suggest that there is no evidence for elevated TB reactivation risk with IL-23is in patients with PsO or PsA and LTBI, including those who do not receive TB prophylaxis. Consequently, they support consensus statements cited above indicating that TB testing for patients with PsO or PsA prior to initiating IL-23i treatment should not be required, and that clinical practice guidelines should be updated. This is particularly important as TB prophylaxis may be associated with hepatic or neurologic side effects that can lead to undue harm to patients at low risk for reactivation of LTBI. This is especially noteworthy given the relatively high rates of false-positive screening results [80], particularly in low-TB-prevalence regions [81], such as Europe, the USA, Japan, and Australia [13].

Study Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this SLR is the inclusion of a broad range of study designs, encompassing both RCTs and real-world evidence, which enhances the generalizability of our findings. The large number of studies with diverse patient populations, including those with LTBI and prior biologic exposure, provides a robust evidence base for evaluating TB risk with IL-23i treatment. Additionally, inclusion of more recent studies ensures that our conclusions reflect the current state of clinical practice.

The quality assessments we performed indicate that the included RCTs were well designed, with robust randomization procedures, adherence to intended interventions, complete and reliable outcome data, objective and appropriate outcome measurements, and transparent reporting. Further, the assessed real-world observation studies were generally well conducted, though some risk of bias may limit interpretation of results because of incomplete adjustment for confounders.

Another strength is that this SLR, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to leverage generative AI for assistance with title and abstract screening of the reports retrieved by our search. We consider it unlikely that this unduly influenced the studies that were ultimately included in the review because screening was performed in parallel with a human reviewer, and generative AI could not independently exclude records. A quality check of 20% of the screening decisions was performed by an independent reviewer to validate the AI performance. This innovative approach may serve as a model for future systematic reviews, potentially improving efficiency and consistency in the screening process when combined with human oversight.

Our study also has notable limitations. First, most studies included in this review only included a subset of enrolled patients with LTBI; therefore, risks for reactivation of LTBI could be underestimated in higher-risk populations. Second, although some information on TB outcomes in patients with LTBI who did not receive TB prophylaxis was identified, the number of studies reporting data on this subgroup was limited. Thirdly, long-term data beyond 5 years of follow-up remain limited, and ongoing surveillance is warranted to further characterize the TB-related safety profiles of IL-23is. Fourthly, although AI-assisted screening was implemented with independent human verification, the possibility of missed or misclassified records due to model limitations cannot be fully excluded, and relevant studies may have been omitted. Finally, this review did not systematically include national pharmacovigilance data of patients with PsO or PsA treated with IL-23is from Asian countries, so underreporting of TB events cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

This SLR summarizes evidence on the TB-related safety profile of IL-23is in PsO and PsA, with a focused analysis of data from the APAC region to address an area of previously limited representation in the literature. The findings are consistent with a growing body of literature supporting IL-23is as safe therapeutic options in patients with LTBI, even without TB prophylaxis. Notably, IL-23is have not shown signals indicative of an increased risk of progression from latent to active TB infection, a finding that aligns with recent international consensus statements. Taken together, this review supports recent consensus recommendations that routine TB testing prior to initiating IL23is is likely unwarranted in patients with PsO or PsA.
