Factors associated with the scope of practice of primary health care nurses
Authors: Ewa Molka, Klaudia Krybus, Izabella Uchmanowicz, Gosha Colquhoun, Ercole Vellone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Poland, primary health care (PHC) is delivered through publicly funded, first-contact community practices, in which nurses play a key role in providing comprehensive services. However, empirical evidence describing how organizational and professional factors relate to their legally defined scope of practice remains limited. This study examined bivariate associations between selected organizational and professional characteristics and the scope of activities delivered by PHC nurses in Poland.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 157 PHC nurses in Poland. The scope of health services was assessed using an inventory based on the Polish Minister of Health regulation covering preventive, educational, diagnostic, therapeutic, and organizational activities. Associations between the scope of services and form of employment, education level, workplace location, and age were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsSignificant differences in the scope of health services were observed across all analyzed factors. Form of employment showed the greatest variation, with self-employed nurses and practice owners more frequently reporting advanced clinical, diagnostic, and decision-making activities compared with nurses employed under salaried employment contracts within primary care entities (e.g., patient self-care preparation, p = 0.008; assessment of recovery process, p = 0.007). Education level was associated with advanced services, including physical examinations (p = 0.045), point-of-care diagnostic testing (p < 0.001), issuing referrals for diagnostic tests (p = 0.014), and suture removal (p < 0.001). Differences by workplace location were observed for preventive, educational, and family-oriented activities, which were reported more often in small towns and rural areas (e.g., pain assessment, p = 0.016; family caregiving capacity assessment, p = 0.035). Age was also associated with service scope of practice, with older nurses more frequently implementing health programs (p = 0.011) and delivering comprehensive care activities (p = 0.032).ConclusionThe scope of health services reported by PHC nurses in Poland differed across selected organizational, professional, and demographic characteristics. In bivariate analyses, variations were observed according to form of employment, education level, workplace location, and age. As these findings are based on unadjusted associations, further research using multivariable approaches is needed to clarify whether these relationships represent independent effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04649-1.

Introduction

Primary health care (PHC) is a key pillar of the health care system and represents the first point of contact and entry into the health system for most patients. In the context of dynamic demographic changes, the growing number of chronic diseases, and an increasing workforce deficit, the role of PHC is becoming increasingly important for maintaining the sustainability and efficiency of the health care system [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization report Primary Health Care: A Framework for the Future, the future of health care should be based on decentralized, interdisciplinary, and patient-centered models, in which nurses play a key role in both clinical and organizational decision-making [1].

The European Commission emphasizes that the appropriate use of nurses’ professional competencies is a key structural solution to address the uneven distribution of medical resources and the overload of the physician-based systems [3]. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) reports that developing independent professional roles for nurses increases the availability of health services, particularly in underserved regions [4]. Examples from France, Canada, and Australia demonstrate that extending nurses’ professional autonomy leads to greater care efficiency, improved patient satisfaction, and enhanced system stability [5, 6].

In Poland, PHC nurses may provide services either as employees of primary care providers or as self-employed professionals running individual or group practices contracted with the National Health Fund (NFZ). Although self-employment is legally permitted, the predominant model remains employment within primary care entities. The choice of employment model is typically determined by organizational arrangements between the provider and the nurse, as well as by contractual conditions with the NFZ, rather than by local health authorities. Thus, both institutional and individual factors influence the form of practice [7–9].

Organizational efficiency in health care is a multidimensional concept, referring to the ability of institutions or medical personnel to achieve desired health outcomes while optimally using available resources. Three dimensions of efficiency are commonly distinguished: technical (the relationship between inputs and outputs), economic (the cost of services), and social (impact on patient and population well-being) [10–12]. For PHC nursing, organizational efficiency encompasses not only delivering services according to clinical guidelines but also adapting to a changing environment, managing work time and clinical priorities, and facilitating interdisciplinary communication.

The PHC sector in Poland faces numerous challenges, including work overload, staff shortages, limited access to postgraduate training, and uneven distribution of resources [8, 9]. These factors significantly affect nurses’ job satisfaction, engagement and overall efficiency [13–15]. In response, many countries are developing flexible work models that increase professional autonomy and decision-making capacity.

Contemporary nursing management emphasizes that professional autonomy, clinical leadership, and health entrepreneurship are key drivers of organizational efficiency in primary health care. Nurses who independently organize their practice perform managerial, care coordination, and educational functions, requiring high levels of clinical and managerial competence [16]. The professional autonomy of nurses in Poland has been progressively defined in legislation over several decades. Earlier legal frameworks recognized nurses as independent health professionals. The Act of 27 August 2004 on Health Care Services Financed from Public Funds enabled nurses to contract services within the publicly funded system. Subsequently, the Act of 15 July 2011 on the Professions of Nurse and Midwife consolidated and codified the scope of professional practice and responsibilities [17]. In many contemporary health systems, care for patients with chronic conditions remains predominantly organized around biomedical models focused on diagnosis, treatment, and curative interventions. However, primary health care nursing is traditionally grounded in holistic, preventive, and community-oriented approaches that integrate health education, long-term care coordination, and support for self-management. This conceptual distinction highlights the potential added value of expanding nursing scope of practice within PHC settings, particularly in the context of aging populations and increasing multimorbidity [18–20].

From an organizational perspective, self-employed nurses can be considered social micro-enterprises that integrate clinical and managerial functions [21]. This requires the ability to analyze population health needs, plan services, manage patient relationships, and evaluate outcomes, competencies that are essential in modern health care systems [21]. These regulatory and organizational characteristics provide an important contextual background for interpreting differences in the reported scope of practice among PHC nurses in Poland.

Despite statutory professional autonomy, Poland still lacks empirical studies describing how the scope of activities performed by PHC nurses varies according to employment form, education level, and work context (urban vs. rural). In particular, it remains unclear whether and how these organizational and professional characteristics are associated with the actual performance of legally defined PHC activities in routine practice. Exploring these patterns may contribute to a better understanding of how nursing roles are operationalized in different organizational settings. Although international studies describe expanding nursing roles in primary care, limited empirical evidence exists regarding how legally defined PHC nursing activities are distributed across different employment models, educational levels, and organizational contexts within the Polish system.

The aim of this study was to examine bivariate associations between selected organizational and professional characteristics (form of employment, education level, workplace location, and age) and the scope of activities reported by PHC nurses in Poland. We hypothesized that these characteristics may be associated with variation in the reported scope of practice. In this study, scope of practice was operationalized as the performance of legally defined primary care activities.

Materials and methods

Participants and settings

The study was conducted from January to March 2024 among PHC nurses employed in public and non-public medical facilities in Poland. A total of 157 eligible nurses completed the survey and were included in the analysis; at the time of the study, all were actively providing guaranteed services within the scope of PHC. Participants were invited through primary health care facilities via direct contact with managers or coordinators. Invitations, together with access to the questionnaire, were distributed to 71 primary health care facilities in the Częstochowa region, Poland, where a total of 254 nurses were employed. Reminder emails were also sent to the facilities to encourage participation. In addition, information about the study was posted on a professional online forum dedicated to primary health care nurses in the region. A total of 157 completed questionnaires were returned and included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Participant recruitment and inclusion flow

All participants were registered nurses authorized to practice in Poland; differences in education level reflect historical and postgraduate educational pathways within the Polish nursing system rather than access to the profession itself. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach through primary health care facilities. As participation was voluntary and anonymous, the exact response rate could not be calculated. Eligibility criteria included active employment in primary health care and a minimum of one year of professional experience in this setting. The study included both nurses who independently received patient declarations of choice and those collaborating with other providers. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All participants were informed about the aim of the study and the principles of its implementation. Completion of the survey was considered as providing informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Research instrument

A cross-sectional survey design was chosen to enable structured comparison of the frequency of legally defined PHC activities across multiple organizational subgroups within a single national sample. A proprietary questionnaire was used, developed based on the Regulation of the Minister of Health of 24 September 2013 on health care services guaranteed within primary health care [22]. The questionnaire comprised 50 closed questions and a sociodemographic section (Supplementary Table S1). The questions addressed the delivery of health services, professional autonomy, work organization, and the scope of preventive, diagnostic, and educational activities. Responses were measured using a five-point Likert scale (1–5), where 1 indicated “definitely not” and 5 “definitely yes.” For the purpose of the study, activities were categorized according to the structure of the Polish primary health care regulation and included preventive (e.g., health promotion and risk group screening), educational (e.g., patient counselling and vaccination education), diagnostic (e.g., physical examination and point-of-care testing), therapeutic (e.g., implementation of physician orders and selected procedures), and organizational activities (e.g., care coordination and documentation). Each activity was assessed as a self-reported frequency of performance using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “definitely not” to “definitely yes.”. This scale enabled assessment of the frequency with which specific professional activities were performed in different organizational contexts.

Statistical analysis

Responses to the questionnaire items were analyzed by calculating absolute and relative frequencies for all possible response categories. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when the assumptions of the chi-square test regarding expected cell counts were not met. The analyses were limited to bivariate comparisons. Multivariable modeling was not performed due to the exploratory nature of the study and the relatively small and uneven subgroup sizes. Age was included as a demographic variable reflecting potential cohort-related differences in education pathways and professional development within the Polish nursing system. Years of professional experience were reported descriptively but were not included in comparative analyses to avoid potential collinearity with age. A significance level of 0.05 was adopted; p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.4.1.

Scope of health services delivered by workplace location

Workplace location was associated with differences in the scope of health services delivered by PHC nurses. Nurses working in small towns and rural areas more frequently provided preventive activities, counselling on healthy lifestyles, pain assessment and monitoring, and evaluation of the nursing and caregiving capacity of the family. Selected procedural activities, including rectal enemas and adjustment of feeding techniques to the patient’s condition, were also reported more often outside large urban settings. Scope of health services delivered by form of employment

Form of employment was associated with differences in the scope of selected health services delivered by PHC nurses. Self-employed nurses or practice owners more frequently performed activities related to patient counselling, preparation for self-care and self-nursing, assessment of the patient’s general condition and recovery process, and advanced diagnostic and therapeutic procedures. Differences were also observed for selected procedural activities and nurse-led clinical decisions. Discussion

This cross-sectional exploratory study aimed to examine bivariate associations between selected organizational, professional, and demographic characteristics including form of employment, education level, workplace location, and age and the scope of activities reported by PHC nurses in Poland. In summary, the study identified significant differences in the reported scope of legally defined PHC activities across all analyzed factors in bivariate analyses. Form of employment showed the most pronounced variation, with self-employed nurses more frequently reporting activities involving independent clinical assessment, point-of-care diagnostic testing, and selected nurse-prescribing decisions permitted under Polish regulations. Higher education level was associated with broader engagement in diagnostic and preventive tasks, while workplace location and age were related to differences in community-oriented and preventive activities. These findings were consistent with the exploratory hypothesis that selected organizational and demographic characteristics would be associated with variations in reported scope of practice.

The results showed that nurses running individual businesses reported performing a broader range of health services than those employed on a salaried basis. This pattern may be associated with differences in organizational arrangements and role expectations among self-employed nurses. Similar findings have been reported in the United Kingdom, where nurses running their own practices exhibit higher levels of independence, motivation, and job satisfaction [23–25]. These observations may be interpreted in light of the concept of professional governance, in which nurses’ professional and decision-making autonomy is emphasized. From an organizational perspective, self-employment may be linked to differences in how professional responsibilities are structured and distributed. In Poland, however, the development of individual nursing practices faces administrative and financial constraints, including difficulties in obtaining National Health Fund contracts, lack of infrastructural support, and bureaucratic burdens [7, 9, 25, 26]. Despite these barriers, self-employed nurses reported performing a broader range of activities, although these findings represent unadjusted associations and should be interpreted with caution.

Nurses with a Master’s degree more frequently reported performing diagnostic, preventive, and educational activities in bivariate analyses. These findings may be discussed in relation to the concept of Advanced Nursing Practice (ANP), which describes expanded competencies among nurses with higher levels of education [5, 27, 28]. Similar associations between higher educational attainment and expanded clinical roles have been reported in American and Scandinavian studies [29, 30]. In Poland, the potential of master-educated nursing staff may not be fully reflected in the current organization of PHC services.

Differences in the nature of professional activities were observed across workplace locations in bivariate analyses. Nurses from small towns and rural areas more frequently reported providing comprehensive services, including health assessment, education, and family support [31]. These observations may be discussed in relation to community-oriented primary care models, in which nurses often assume multiple clinical and community roles. Similar patterns have been described in studies from Finland, Norway, and Canada, where nurses working in rural settings were reported to assume broader roles compared with their urban counterparts [32–34]. In the Polish context, these differences may also be partially explained by geographic and organizational factors. In rural areas and small towns, access to specialized outpatient and hospital services may be more limited, which can require PHC nurses to address a broader biopsychosocial spectrum of patient needs within the local setting. This may include greater involvement in preventive care, family support, and coordination with community resources. Such geographic realities can contribute to expanded practical responsibilities in rural primary care.

Age-related differences were observed, with older nurses more frequently implementing preventive and educational programs. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as age may reflect overlapping effects of professional experience, cohort-specific education and training pathways, and organizational positioning within primary health care. Due to the cross-sectional design, these factors could not be disentangled. Similar patterns have been reported internationally, where more experienced or senior PHC nurses tend to assume broader roles in health promotion, chronic disease management, and preventive activities, particularly in community-based and nurse-led care models [35]. This suggests that age-related differences observed in the present study may reflect cumulative professional capital rather than age per se, reinforcing the need to interpret demographic effects within broader organizational and role-based contexts [36].

Practical and organizational implications

The findings may have practical and policy implications for primary health care organization. Professional autonomy, forms of employment, and educational attainment were associated in bivariate analyses with differences in reported scope of practice. The observed differences across employment forms warrant further investigation before drawing conclusions regarding organizational strategies. These findings may support workforce planning, role development, and the implementation of nurse-led models of care in primary health services. The potential role of nurse-led models of care in primary health services remains an important area for future research. From a health policy perspective, the observed variation in reported activities may inform discussions on organizational arrangements in primary health care. Further research using direct measures of cost, quality, and health outcomes is needed to better understand the efficiency of primary health care nursing practice.

Study limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inference, so observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of cause-and-effect relationships. Second, the scope of health services was assessed using self-reported data, which may be subject to reporting bias and social desirability effects. Third, organizational efficiency was operationalized indirectly through the scope of practice delivered, rather than through direct measures of health outcomes, resource utilization, or care quality. Fourth, age was analyzed as an independent determinant; however, it may reflect a combination of professional experience, cohort-related differences in education and training, and organizational seniority, which could not be disentangled. An additional limitation is that the analyses were restricted to bivariate comparisons. As multivariable modeling was not performed, the observed associations may be influenced by interrelationships between age, education level, form of employment, and workplace location. Therefore, it was not possible to determine whether the reported associations represent independent effects. Finally, although the study included nurses from diverse workplace settings and employment forms, the findings may not be fully generalizable to health systems with different regulatory, organizational, or professional frameworks. Additionally, some comparison subgroups, particularly self-employed nurses and selected age categories, were relatively small, which may have affected the statistical stability of the observed differences. The study also did not include detailed data on functional role delineation, specific competencies, or scope-of-duty regulations within subgroups, limiting the ability to determine whether the observed differences reflect structural role variation or individual practice patterns. Given the number of statistical comparisons performed, no formal multiplicity correction was applied. This decision was consistent with the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of the study, and therefore statistically significant findings should be interpreted cautiously as descriptive signals rather than confirmatory evidence. The use of a convenience sampling strategy and the lack of data on non-respondents limit the ability to assess potential selection bias. Systematic differences between respondents and non-respondents cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

Organizational, professional, and demographic characteristics were associated with differences in the reported scope of activities delivered by primary health care nurses in Poland. Form of employment showed the strongest variation, with self-employed nurses more frequently reporting performance of a broader range of legally defined activities. Higher educational attainment was associated with greater engagement in diagnostic, preventive, and educational tasks. Workplace location and age were also related to differences in community-oriented and preventive activities.

These findings suggest that employment model and educational pathway may be associated with differences in how nursing competencies are utilized within primary health care. Further research using multivariable designs and outcome-based measures is needed to determine whether these associations represent independent effects and to assess their implications for health system performance.
