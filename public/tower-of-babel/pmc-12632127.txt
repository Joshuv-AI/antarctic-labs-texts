Drought stress differentially suppresses growth and triggers antioxidant and anthocyanin pathways in Brassica oleracea (C3) and Echinochloa crusgallii (C4)
Authors: Galal Khamis, Emad A. Alsherif, Maha H. Khalaf, Shereen Magdy Korany, Danyah A. Aldailami, Abeer S. Aloufi, Maria Gabriela Maridueña-Zavala, Soad K. Al Jaouni, Seham M. Hamed, Samy Selim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although drought responses of C3 and C4 plants have been widely investigated, direct comparative analyses of their physiological and detailed metabolic biosynthetic adaptations remain limited. This study provided a detailed pathway-level analysis of drought stress responses in a Brassica oleracea (kale, C3) versus Echinochloa crus-galli (barnyard grass, C4) moving beyond previous comparisons that typically focus on broad physiological differences. Our study integrates multi-level physiological, molecular, biochemical, and metabolic data, emphasizing specific drought-adaptive biosynthetic pathways such as anthocyanin and glucosinolate synthesis. Drought stress significantly reduced biomass and photosynthetic efficiency (p < 0.05) in both species, with kale exhibiting greater declines. Kale also accumulated higher oxidative stress indicators, with H₂O₂ and lipid peroxidation (MDA) increasing by 21.81% and 113%, respectively, relative to barnyard grass, indicating stronger oxidative damage. Both species significantly upregulated antioxidants metabolites and enzymes, reflecting activation of protective mechanisms. Anthocyanin content rose by 83% in barnyard grass and 113% in kale under drought. Detailed pathway analysis revealed elevated precursor levels (phenylalanine and p-coumaroyl-CoA) and increased activities and gene expression of key enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway, including cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H), chalcone synthase (CHS), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL), highlighting the specific C3-C4 photosynthetic-type biochemical strategies for drought adaptation. The strong activation of defense pathways such as anthocyanin and glucosinolate biosynthesis suggests that kale compensates for its lower drought tolerance by increasing metabolic flexibility. Molecular evidence further supports induced expression of anthocyanin related biosynthetic genes. The detailed, multi-level metabolic analysis uncover the -specific C3-C4 photosynthetic-type biochemical strategies for drought adaptation. This work advances plant physiology knowledge by revealing how C3 and C4 plants differ in metabolic drought adaptations, offering practical relevance for both crop improvement and weed management. This reveals key physiological and biochemical differences, with kale (C3) needing more metabolic adjustment to handle drought.

Introduction

Climate change severely restricts the amount of water available to plants and increases the frequency of drought conditions [1]. Drought stress is one of the most important environmental factors that influences plant development and productivity. This disrupts several essential photosynthetic elements. Drought stress reduces the intracellular CO2 content through stomatal closure, which suppresses the Rubisco-catalyzed carboxylation pathway and increases photorespiration [2]. Decreases in the photosynthetic fixation of CO2 result in lower Calvin cycle ATP and NADPH consumption, which leads to over-reduction of photosynthetic electron transport, and production of superoxide radical O2•− is formed via the Mehler reaction [3].

Plant species and their photosynthetic pathways significantly influence drought stress responses, with variations in tolerance observed despite shared mechanisms [4]. C3 plants, adapted to temperate climates, contrast with C4 plants, which thrive in warm, arid environments and typically exhibit greater drought tolerance [5]. C4 plants benefit from higher photosynthetic efficiency, CO₂ fixation rates, and water-use efficiency (WUE), providing an advantage under water-limited conditions [4]. Studies show drought impacts gas exchange less severely in C4 species than in C3 plants, supporting their suitability for dry environments [2]. However, C4 plants may be more sensitive to soil moisture levels for carbon assimilation compared to C3 plants [6], highlighting nuanced differences in their drought resilience strategies. Studies have explored differential gene expression in C3 (Oryza sativa) and C4 (Zea mays) plants, revealing higher photosynthesis levels in C4 species [5, 7]. In C4 Cleome gynandra, chlorophyll fluorescence, photoinhibition, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and antioxidant enzyme induction were elevated compared to C3 Cleome spinosa [2]. Additionally, Opoku et al. [8] reported that C4 sorghum exhibits higher photosynthetic efficiency than C3 barley, reflecting differences in carbon fixation and stomatal regulation. While physiological differences between C3 and C4 plants under drought are established, detailed biochemical and metabolic analyses, especially those focusing on key biosynthetic pathways like anthocyanin and glucosinolate synthesis, are less studied. By integrating multi-parameter assessments of antioxidant systems, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and enzymatic activities, our study reveals the specific C3-C4 photosynthetic-type metabolic strategies underlying distinct drought resilience mechanisms.

Under drought stress, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), induces oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules including proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids, initiating programmed cell death. Nevertheless, as secondary messengers in intracellular signaling and regulation, ROS have a significant physiological role under steady-state settings [9]. To mitigate drought stress, plants activate their antioxidant defense systems, including the ascorbate (ASC)–glutathione (GSH) cycle [1–3, 9]. The plant’s primary defense against abiotic stress is a sophisticated antioxidant system that works to manage toxic Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) levels and maintain cellular homeostasis. ASC is a key metabolite in plant stress responses, with increased endogenous ASC and proline enhancing antioxidant activity and stress tolerance [10, 11]. ASC supports ROS detoxification, photosynthetic efficiency via the xanthophyll cycle, and stress hormone biosynthesis (e.g., ABA, ethylene) [11–13]. Plants activate enzymatic antioxidants, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), ASC peroxidase (APX), and glutathione reductase (GR), to combat ROS [14]. SOD converts superoxide to H₂O₂, which POX, CAT, and APX (via the Asada–Halliwell pathway) scavenge in cellular compartments [15, 16]. Anthocyanins, recognized biomarkers of abiotic stress, act as signaling molecules and ROS scavengers, protecting photosynthetic machinery from photooxidation under stressors like light, cold, salt, or drought, as observed in Arabidopsis [5, 17].

This study establishes a mechanistic baseline of drought response differences between C3 and C4 plants that can inform future development of drought-resilient crops. Thus we selected kale (Brassica oleracea, C3) and barnyard grass (Echinochloa crus-galli (L.) Beauv., C4) as representative species with contrasting photosynthetic pathways and drought tolerance profiles. Barnyard grass is a C4 species that naturally thrives in warm, arid environments and reflects inherent physiological drought tolerance, serving as a comparative model for C4 plants. It is considered one of the most widespread and problematic weeds in rice fields. It has a high level of ecological flexibility. For instance, it can generate many seeds, germinate, mature quickly, and grow in anaerobic environments [18]. These traits make it a highly competitive weed but also a relevant model for studying stress tolerance in C4 plants. It significantly reduces rice yield and quality by competing for water, light, and soil nutrients [19]. In contrast, Kale is an agronomically important C3 crop with known sensitivity to drought but significant nutritional value as it is rich in ts well-characterized antioxidant and anthocyanin-rich profile [20], making it a valuable model for studying drought responses and breeding improvement. It is a leafy, non-headed cabbage, are often featured on lists of “healthiest foods” or “superfoods [21]. Compared to cereals such as rice or wheat and model plants such as Arabidopsis, kale offers a valuable balance of experimental practicality and metabolic complexity [22]. The remarkable drought tolerance of kale is mainly attributable to its combined hormonal and physiological responses. The upregulation of stress-related transcription factors such as DREB2A, HSFA2, and NAC041/084, along with increased activity of antioxidant enzymes like CAT and APX, mediates these responses by reducing ROS-induced damage [23].

Drought strongly affects crop growth and metabolism, with notable differences between C3 and C4 species. Although physiological effects are documented, detailed biochemical and molecular analyses are scarce, especially on enzyme activities, gene expression, and metabolites. Understanding these pathways is crucial for revealing the specific C3-C4 photosynthetic-type stress tolerance mechanisms. This study compares kale (C3) and barnyard grass (C4), highlighting distinct drought adaptation strategies through integrated physiological and biochemical data, mainly reflecting C3-C4 photosynthetic type differences rather than species-specific traits. These insights can inform improved crop resilience under climate change. Kale’s drought response offers targets for breeding stress-tolerant, nutrient-rich crops, while knowledge of barnyard grass’s resilience supports weed management under drought. More broadly, the comparison advances plant physiology and metabolism by elucidating how C3-C4 photosynthetic-type differ in metabolic drought adaptations, with practical relevance for agriculture.

Materials and methods

Growth conditions and experimental setup

Seeds of Echinochloa crus-galli (L.) Beauv., (barnyard grass, C4) Brassica oleracea var. acephala and (kale, C3) were obtained from Agricultural Research Center, Giza, Egypt. After being immersed in sodium hypochlorite (15%, v/v) for 20 min, a homogenous seed of barnyard grass (C4) and kale (C3) was surface sterilized. The seeds were then propagated in Petri dishes and grown in 25 × 15 cm pots filled with 650 g of synthetic soil (40% dry weight sand, 25% dry clay, and 35% silt). A growth chamber with regulated circumstances (16 h day/8 hours night, 25 °C/18°C Day/night, and 400 µE m− 2 s− 1 photosynthetically active sunlight) was used to develop the plants. Drought stress was applied by gradually lowering soil moisture to 30% of its maximum water-holding capacity. This level was consistently maintained through daily pot weighing and precise water adjustments. At 30% soil water content, plants exhibited visible wilting during the day, reflecting severe yet non-lethal drought stress. Control pots were watered daily to maintain 60% soil water content (SWC), measured by weighing soil before and after drying at 105 °C, with moisture calculated as a percentage of dry soil weight. Drought treatment involved withholding irrigation post-sowing, reducing SWC to ~ 30% over 2 weeks, causing wilting and > 50% growth reduction to simulate natural drought. Plants were harvested at the early to mid-vegetative stage and stored at −80 °C for biochemical analyses. Five independent experiments used at least 20 plants per condition. Post-experiment, soil was dried at 110 °C for 24 h to assess water content. Leaf water potential was measured with a tensiometer (DIK-PC-40, Daiki Rika Kogyo Co., Ltd.) using the pressure chamber method [Scholander et al., 1965]. Relative water content (RWC) was calculated as RWC = (FW-DW)/(TW-DW) × 100, where FW, DW, and TW are fresh, dry, and turgid weights, respectively.

Measuring the net rate of photosynthesis

According to Sulpice et al. [24] RuBisCO activity was measured as the glycerate kinase (3-PGA) conversion rate of extracted leaf samples (µmol 3-PGA m− 2 min− 1). The activity of rubisco was determined directly (“direct rubisco”), without incubation of the extract in the presence of 10 mM HCO3 − and 20 mM Mg2+ to convert the non-carbamylated rubisco into the carbamylated form.

The LI-COR LI-6400 was used to measure the net photosynthetic rate (Asat, µmol CO2 m− 2 s− 1). The measurements were conducted under controlled chamber conditions set according to the climate treatment, specifically at 420 ppm CO2, 23.5 °C (block temperature), saturating light intensity at 1500 µmol m− 2 s− 1, and ambient relative humidity. Measurements were taken on the abaxial (lower) surface of fully expanded leaves, selected at the mid-vegetative stage, and normalized per unit leaf area to allow direct comparison across treatments.

Determining the oxidative damage markers

Using a mortar and pestle, 200 mg of frozen shoot tissues were homogenized in 2 mL of 80% ethanol to measure the amount of lipid peroxidation (MDA). The supernatant was incubated with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) to generate thiobarbituric acid-malondialdehyde (TBA-MDA) following centrifugation at 10,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C. The absorbance was measured in a 96-well plate reader at 440, 532, and 600 nm [25]. Using the FOX1 technique based on xylenol orange, the concentration of H2O2 was determined [26]. The tissue was homogenized in 1.0 mL of ice-cold 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA), which rapidly inactivates enzymes and precipitates proteins to stabilize the H₂O₂ in the extract. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for H₂O₂ quantification. The peroxidemediated oxidation of Fe2+ and the subsequent reaction of Fe3+ with xylenol orang. Catalase was used to remove H2O2 from the reaction mixture to assess the specificity for H2O2. the Fe3+ xylenol orange complex at a wavelength of 560 nm.

Evaluation non-enzymatic antioxidants

Total antioxidants were extracted in 2 mL of ice-cold 80% ethanol after frozen tissues (200 mg FW) were crushed using a MagNALyser in liquid nitrogen. The extract was combined with an equal volume of FRAP (ferric reducing/antioxidant power assay) reagent (0.3 M acetate buffer (pH3.6), 0.01 mM TPTZ in 0.04 mM HCl, 0.02 M FeCl3.6H2O) and measured at 600 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy Mx, Biotek Instruments Inc., Winooski, Vermont, USA) and Trolox as standard [27]. The MagNALyser was used to extract tocopherols from 100 mg of frozen tissues in 6 mL of hexane, and the mixture was centrifuged for 15 min at 14,000× g. The extracts were dried using a CentriVap concentrator (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA), reconstituted in hexane, and the tocopherols were separated and measured using HPLC (Shimadzu, Hertogenbosch, The Netherlands) under normal phase conditions using a Particil Pac 5 μm column material, length 250 mm, i.d. 4.6 mm. The internal standard was dimethyl tocol (DMT) at 5 ppm. Shimadzu Class VP 6.14 software was used to analyse the data.

To extract phenols and flavonoids, 200 mg of FW were homogenized in 2 mL of 80% ethanol (v/v), centrifuged, and the phenols and flavonoids content was estimated using the clear supernatant. According to Zhang et al., [28], a Folin–Ciocalteu test was used to measure the amounts of phenols. Chlorogenic acid was used as the reference. The modified aluminum chloride calorimetric technique [29] was used to quantify the flavonoid concentration, using quercetin as a reference.

Measuring the enzymatic antioxidants

Glutathione (GSH) and reduced ascorbate (ASC) were measured by HPLC analysis according to Potters and Horemans [30]. For the HPLC run, known concentrations of glutathione and reduced ascorbate were used as standards. Total ASC (tASC) and total GSH (tGSH) were measured following reduction with 0.04 M dithiothreitol DTT for 10 min at room temperature. APX, dehydroASC reductase (DHAR), GR, peroxidase (POX), SOD, and CAT activities were measured using a microplate assay according to Murshed et al. [31]. 100 mg of frozen shoot tissue was extracted using MagNALyser and 1 mL of extraction buffer, including 50 mM MES/KOH (pH 6.0). The POX activity was measured using pyrogallol oxidation (ε430 = 2.47 mM− 1cm− 1). SOD activity was determined [32], by measuring the inhibition of NBT degradation at 560 nm. By measuring the rate of H2O2 breakdown at 240 nm (ε240 = 43.6 mM− 1 cm− 1), as established by Aebi [33], catalase activity was determined. To achieve linear time and protein-concentration dependency, all reactions buffers measures were scaled down for semi-high throughput using a microplate reader (SynergyMx, Biotek Instruments Inc., Winooski, Vermont, USA). Using the Bradford test (BioRad), the protein content of the enzyme extracts was determined [34].

Evaluation of anthocyanin metabolism

Anthocyanins were extracted by blending 0.1 g of frozen plant powder in 10 mL of acidified methanol (methanol: HCl, 99:1 v/v). The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 24 h in the dark, followed by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 5 min. The anthocyanin content in the supernatant was quantified by measuring absorbance at 550 nm, using an extinction coefficient of 33,000 M⁻¹cm⁻¹ to calculate concentration [35]. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity was determined by extracting fresh tissue in 0.2 M sodium borate buffer (pH 8.8). Enzyme activity was measured spectrophotometrically based on the formation of trans-cinnamic acid at 290 nm.

The activity of 4-coumarate: coenzyme A ligase (4CL) was assessed by estimating the formation of p-coumarate at 333 nm. Fresh samples were extracted in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.9), and the reaction was monitored by measuring the increase in 4-hydroxy-trans-cinnamic acid at 340 nm. One unit of 4CL activity was defined as the formation of 1.0 nmol of hydroxy-trans-cinnamic acid per minute [36]. Chalcone synthase (CHS) activity in shoot samples was determined using a colorimetric assay that quantifies chalcone production from malonyl-CoA and p-coumaroyl-CoA, serving as a proxy for flavonoid biosynthesis [37]). Enzyme activity was expressed in terms of the nmol of chalcone produced per minute per mg of protein.

RNA extraction and quantitative (Q)-PCR

For total RNA extraction, shoot tissues of kale (B. oleracea var. acephala) and barnyard grass (E. crusgalli) were homogenized, and RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA quantity and quality were assessed on a QIAxcel platform. One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA (Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, Fermentas). Relative mRNA expression for PAL, CHS, 4CL, and C4H was measured by quantitative PCR using gene-specific primers. A SuperMix-UDG (Invitrogen) was used to perform QPCR analysis (denaturation: 10 min at 95 °C; amplification and quantification: 40 times, 40 s at 95 °C, 20 s at 55 °C, 30 s at 72 °C). QPCR was performed on the Mx3000P System (Agilent) with melt curve analysis to confirm specificity, and appropriate controls were included to ensure accuracy.

Glucosinolates levels

Glucosinolates were extracted following a modified protocol. To inhibit myrosinase activity, samples were steamed for 2 min before extraction. One gram of plant tissue was ground in 3 mL of 70% aqueous methanol containing 1.5 g/L trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). The mixture was incubated at 70 °C with agitation for 30 min, then cooled and centrifuged at 8000×g for 20 min. The supernatant was filtered and evaporated to dryness at 40 °C. The residue was re-dissolved in 0.2 mM HEPES–KOH buffer (pH 7.0).

For enzymatic activity assessment, 10 µL of glucosinolate extract was incubated with 0.12 U thioglucosidase at 37 °C for 24 h. The reaction was stopped with 18 mM perchloric acid. Controls were prepared by replacing extracts with buffer. Released glucose, equimolar to glucosinolate content, was measured using a glucose oxidase/peroxidase assay kit (GAGO20-1KT, Sigma). Allyl-glucosinolate served as positive control and sinigrin as calibrant.

Results and discussion

A plant’s ability to survive and remain productive under drought stress hinges on its specific physiological and metabolic strategies [38]– [39]. Drought-tolerant species achieve this by optimizing key functions, such as photosynthesis and water use, while activating protective pathways, including the production of antioxidants and osmoprotectants, to minimize cellular damage and sustain internal balance [38]– [39]. These adaptive responses—which encompass changes in growth, metabolism, and even root/leaf structures, are intrinsically tailored to the genetic background of the species. Understanding these diverse, specific C3-C4 photosynthetic type defense mechanisms is vital for breeding more drought-resilient crops and for developing effective agricultural and ecological strategies to cope with increasing climate variability [38]– [39].

Drought-Induced reductions in growth and photosynthesis: a comparative analysis

Both kale (C3​) and barnyard grass (C4​) suffered significant negative impacts from drought, showing reduced fresh weight (Fig 1A), dry weight (Fig. 1B), and plant height (Fig. 1C) and (Table 1). However, the degree of impact was markedly different, which may reflect the distinct photosynthetic strategies of these species. Specifically, dry biomass in kale decreased by 59.37%, a substantially greater reduction than the 40.99% decrease observed in barnyard grass (Fig. 1B). This result aligns with previous findings where C3​ species (e.g., Cleome spinosa or wheat) typically experience more severe growth inhibition under water stress compared to C4​ counterparts (e.g., Cleome gynandra or maize) [2, 4, 40]. Divergent photosynthetic limitations and stress mechanisms

To understand this differential reduction in growth, we investigated key photosynthesis-related parameters, including the photosynthetic rate (Anet​), leaf pigment content, and RuBisCO activity (Fig. 2; Table 1). Drought stress significantly impaired photosynthesis (Fig. 2A) in both species, evidenced by declines in RuBisCO activity and Anet​ (Fig. 2A-C). This contrasting severity highlights the core difference in their adaptive mechanisms, including C3​ Vulnerability: Kale, an inherently less water-efficient C3​ plant, likely suffered a more severe stomatal limitation that quickly constrained the supply of CO2​ to the RuBisCO enzyme. The lack of a CO2​-concentrating mechanism (CCM) means the RuBisCO active sites were more exposed to O2​, accelerating the wasteful process of photorespiration and causing a faster decline in Anet​ [43–45]. This rapid functional decline directly correlates with the greater overall growth reduction observed in kale. C4​ Resilience and Metabolic Constraint: Barnyard grass, with its CCM, maintains higher CO2​ concentrations around RuBisCO even under partial stomatal closure, ensuring superior Water Use Efficiency (WUE) [4, 4, 4]. While this protects Anet​ initially (allowing it to outperform kale) [46], prolonged or severe water deficit shifts the constraint from stomatal to metabolic limitation. This is reflected by the significant, though less severe, decline in RuBisCO activity and the likely reduction in the electron transport rate (ETR), which is necessary to fuel the CCM [47, 48]. The continued decline in Anet​ and pigment content (Fig. 2A, B) suggests that the C4​ system eventually exhausts its metabolic capacity to sustain high photosynthetic flux. Novel Mechanistic Insight (FD-mediated Defense): The differential response may also involve ROS scavenging pathways linked to carbon fixation. Studies show that the expression of Ferredoxin (FD), an electron transfer protein that also supports ROS scavenging, increases in C4​ plants under drought but decreases in C3​ plants [49, 50]. This suggests that the C4​ pathway’s biochemical machinery may be co-opted to enhance antioxidant defense, a strategy unavailable to C3​ plants and potentially explaining the relatively lower oxidative damage (and the lower need for secondary metabolites) observed in barnyard grass.

In summary, the observed difference in growth and photosynthetic decline is not merely a quantitative reduction but a qualitative reflection of divergent survival mechanisms: C4​ relies on innate photosynthetic efficiency to delay the onset of severe damage, while C3​ rapidly collapses at the functional level, necessitating a greater metabolic investment elsewhere to cope with the resulting stress (as detailed in the subsequent discussion on antioxidants).

Drought-Induced oxidative stress and redox status: contrasting C3​ and C4​ defense mechanisms

Drought stress triggered marked but distinctly different changes in the oxidative and redox status of kale (C3​) and barnyard grass (C4​), directly reflecting the inherent differences in their physiological resilience and antioxidative capacities. Oxidative damage, assessed by measuring MDA, reliable biomarker of lipid peroxidation and membrane damage [2, 51], was significantly amplified in both species (P ≤ 0.05). However, the degree of damage was fundamentally different: MDA increased by a pronounced 113% in kale compared to 63.25% in barnyard grass (Fig. 3B). The C3​ Strategy: Aggressive Inducible Defense

To overcome this heightened oxidative challenge, kale mounted an aggressive and disproportionately large inducible defense across its low-molecular-weight antioxidant pool. Core redox-regulating compounds, ASC, GSH, and glutaredoxins, rose 2.06-, 2.12-, and 2.32-fold, respectively, in kale—significantly higher increases than the 1.3- to 1.6-fold rises seen in barnyard grass (Table 2). The logic for this disparity lies in the photosynthetic mechanism: the lack of the C4​ CO2​-concentrating mechanism means the C3​ plant must rely on this massive synthesis of secondary metabolites (such as phenolics and flavonoids [38]) and redox metabolites to detoxify the surge of ROS generated by photorespiration under stomatal closure. This is further evidenced by kale’s sharp induction of generalized non-enzymatic antioxidants: phenols (96.29% higher), total antioxidant capacity (TAC, 61.7% higher), flavonoids (69.76% higher), and tocopherols (87.7% higher) compared to its control (Table 2). The C4​ Strategy: High Constitutive Baseline

In stark contrast, the C4​ plant, due to its superior inherent Water Use Efficiency (WUE) and minimal photorespiration, appears to rely on a higher constitutive (baseline) antioxidant capacity. Despite kale’s dramatic relative induction, the absolute levels of its generalized non-enzymatic antioxidants remained lower than those in barnyard grass under the same stress: kale’s levels corresponded to only 65.6% to 86.7% of the values measured in the C4​ species (Table 2). This lower magnitude of change but higher absolute baseline in C4​ suggests its defense system is primed for stress, requiring less emergency induction.

Antioxidant Defense Focus

While most measured enzymatic antioxidants (including POX, CAT, SOD, DHAR, MDHAR, GR, GPX, thioredoxin, and peroxiredoxins) showed non-significant activity increases in both species (Table 2), the enzymatic focus appears to shift depending on the photosynthetic type. The pronounced induction of ASC and GSH in kale strongly suggests the APX-driven ASC–GSH cycle was the dominant, rapidly activated enzymatic pathway for H2​O2clearance [2]. APX, with its higher affinity for H2​O2​ [2], is crucial, especially in chloroplasts, in conjunction with the accumulated ASC. This contrasts with the tendency of C3​ plants like wheat to rely comparatively more on CAT for removing photorespiratory H2​O2​ [4], indicating that while both utilize similar enzyme families, their specific pathway reliance and metabolite pools (like ASC and GSH being higher in C4​ maize [4]) are tailored to their unique stress liabilities. This coordinated activation of metabolites and specific enzymes is thus identified as a critical component of plant adaptation to drought [38].

Anthocyanin accumulation: a key highlight of divergent metabolic reprogramming

A central finding of this study is that kale (C3​) initiates a far more extensive metabolic reprogramming than barnyard grass (C4​), highlighted by amplified anthocyanin accumulation and a superior resulting antioxidant capacity under drought stress. Anthocyanins, key secondary metabolites synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway, are crucial for protecting plants against drought-induced ROS [6].

Differential activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway

The data strongly support this differential response at the precursor level. In kale (C3​), phenylalanine, the pathway’s initial precursor, increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) by 132.9% compared to control kale, and by 48.8% and 34.99% compared to control and drought-treated barnyard grass, respectively (Fig. 4A). This massive upstream induction fuels the heightened C3​ defense. In barnyard grass, drought elicited a 64.2% increase in cinnamic acid versus control, and 48.6% and 5.6% more than control and drought-treated kale, respectively, compared to control barnyard grass, control kale, and drought-treated kale, respectively (Fig. 4B). These results align with reports of enhanced anthocyanin metabolism under stress, where C3​ plants preferentially accumulate anthocyanins [6]. The activation profiles of downstream biosynthetic enzymes further confirm the enhanced metabolic reprogramming in kale. The conversion steps, starting with PAL to form cinnamic acid, leading through C4H to p-coumaric acid, and then via 4CL and CHS to form naringenin, were robustly activated in both species. However, kale consistently showed greater induction: PAL increased by 58.4% in kale and 26.7% in barnyard grass; p-coumaric acid increased by 105.7% vs. 29.3%; CHS increased by 71.9% vs. 30.8%; 4CL increased by 53.3% vs. 17.85%; and C4H increased by 56.4% vs. 48.7% compared to their respective controls (p ≤ 0.05 for all significant changes) (Figs. 4E-I). Anthocyanin accumulation and adaptive mechanism

The consequence of this differential enzyme activation is a critical divergence in defense strategy: anthocyanin accumulation rose by a dramatic 113.6% in kale compared to 83.9% in barnyard grass relative to their controls, resulting in superior absolute accumulation and antioxidant capacity in the C3​ plant (Fig. 4D). This strongly suggests that the C3​ plant (kale) relies more heavily on anthocyanins as a primary defense strategy against the severe oxidative stress generated by drought. To understand changes in anthocyanin metabolism under drought, we analyzed the expression of key biosynthetic enzymes at the molecular level (Fig. 5). Both C3 kale and C4 barnyard grass significantly upregulated genes in the phenylpropanoid pathway. Barnyard grass showed increases in PAL (4.08-fold) (Fig. 5A), CHS (1.63-fold) (Fig. 5B), C4H (1.98-fold) (Fig. 5C), and 4CL (1.77-fold) (Fig. 5D) compared to controls. Kale exhibited even higher induction: PAL (5.71-fold), CHS (1.78-fold), C4H (3.20-fold), and 4CL (1.86-fold). When both C3​ and C4​ plants close their stomata to conserve water, the inherent nature of the C3​ pathway is far more prone to photorespiration and the resultant accumulation of damaging ROS than the C4​ pathway, which uses a CO2​-concentrating mechanism to suppress photorespiration. The C3​ plant must compensate for this physiological vulnerability by synthesizing large quantities of these pigments, which scavenge ROS, prevent lipid peroxidation, and stabilize membranes [52–55]. In contrast, the C4​ plant (barnyard grass), with its superior physiological resilience, is less metabolically obligated to synthesize high levels of these pigments. This robust anthocyanin-mediated defense confirms that C3​ plants employ a more dynamic and intensive metabolic adjustment to survive water deficit, aligning with studies that show higher anthocyanin content enhances drought resistance in various crops [56–59, 59]. Drought induced glucosinolate accumulation in kale (C3)

Although glucosinolates (GSLs) are specific to kale (Brassicaceae) and absent in barnyard grass (Poaceae), measuring them provides valuable specific C3-C4 photosynthetic type into kale’s unique metabolic response to drought stress. Since our study compares two physiologically and biochemically distinct species, including GSL analysis highlights unique adaptive mechanisms in kale that cannot be assessed in barnyard grass, further emphasizing the C3​ plant’s intensive metabolic reprogramming strategy.

GSLs are specialized secondary metabolites that play a vital role in defense against herbivores, pathogens, and environmental stressors []. Under drought conditions, kale activates the GSL biosynthetic pathway as a crucial component of its metabolic stress response, resulting in increased accumulation of these compounds. Our findings revealed that drought stress led to a 17.21% increase in total glucosinolate content compared to control plants. Notably, glucoraphanin levels were significantly elevated (P ≤ 0.05), showing a substantial 75.13% increase relative to the control (Table 2). This demonstrates that kale directs significant metabolic flux toward specialized sulfur-containing compounds under water deficit.

Consistent with these results, drought appears to trigger a profound biochemical defense response in kale characterized by the upregulation of aliphatic alkenyl GSLs and enhanced expression of key biosynthetic and catabolic enzymes, including AOP2, TGG1, TGG2, and PEN2. These GSL-mediated responses serve a dual purpose: they not only contribute to maintaining redox homeostasis and protecting cells against oxidative damage (complementing the anthocyanin and ASC/GSH systems) but also facilitate stomatal regulation and osmotic adjustment, thereby reducing water loss and contributing to water use efficiency [61, 61, 62]– [63]. Therefore, the drought-induced glucosinolate accumulation in kale acts as a multifaceted protective mechanism, essential for the C3​ plant’s survival where innate physiological tolerance is lacking.

Conclusion

This study reveals that under drought stress, kale (C3) shows greater sensitivity than barnyard grass (C4), with more marked reductions in growth and photosynthesis, and higher oxidative damage. Kale compensates through stronger activation of anthocyanin and glucosinolates pathways. Furthermore, the molecular evidence provides mechanistic support for our findings by confirming drought-induced upregulation of expression of genes involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis. These metabolic adjustments not only aid drought tolerance but may also improve nutritional quality. The detailed pathway insights provided can inform breeding programs aiming to enhance drought resilience by selecting or engineering the metabolic flexibility traits identified here. Moreover, understanding these mechanisms supports the development of crop management practices that optimize antioxidant and secondary metabolite profiles to mitigate drought effects and improve crop performance under water-limited conditions. Overall, integrating the potential implications for drought-resilient crop development, agricultural management strategies, or biotechnological applications would substantially enhance the impact of the study.
