Abstract
Interstellar objects (ISOs) can become gravitationally bound to the solar system, enabling multiple observations. If these bound ISOs exist, they provide valuable opportunities to generalize models of planetesimal formation, composition, and ejection beyond our solar system. We explore two mechanisms for ISO capture: gravitational braking (primarily with Jupiter) and the Hills mechanism with the Sun. Using N-body simulations, we investigate ISO capture rates, ejection probabilities, and the resulting orbital elements. Our simulations suggest that approximately 10 ISOs may currently be bound to the solar system, with the vast majority captured through gravitational braking by Jupiter. These objects are bound by a Tisserand parameter with respect to Jupiter less than three, tend to have high eccentricities, semi-major axes larger than Jupiter’s, and inclinations modestly biased toward the prograde direction. This region is sparsely populated by observed solar system objects. By comparing known solar system objects to the most probable orbital elements of our simulated ISOs, we identify 18 candidate objects in the current solar system population that may be bound ISOs captured by Jupiter. For the Hills mechanism, we find it to be a viable but less common capture pathway, typically producing isotropic inclinations and perihelia less than one au. Based on these characteristics, we identify seven additional candidate objects for ISOs captured through the Hills mechanism.

I
Introduction

Interstellar objects (ISOs) are planetesimals no longer gravitationally bound to a star
(
D. Z. Seligman & A. Moro-Martín 2022
;
A. Siraj & A. Loeb 2022
)
. ISOs that pass through our solar system are called interstellar interlopers
(
D. Jewitt & D. Z. Seligman 2023
)
. Although few interlopers have been detected, significant effort has gone into their modeling and observation, with hundreds of papers published to date and future space missions advocated
(
A. M. Hein et al. 2022
)
. Since the discoveries of 1I/‘Oumuamua in 2017, 2I/Borisov in 2019, and 3I/ATLAS in 2025, the astronomical community has actively pursued their study
(
A. Siraj & A. Loeb 2022
;
D. Z. Seligman & A. Moro-Martín 2022
;
D. Jewitt 2024
;
D. Jewitt & D. Z. Seligman 2023
;
A. Fitzsimmons et al. 2024
;
D. Z. Seligman et al. 2025
)
. The first two objects exhibited non-gravitational accelerations, and all three have compositions distinct from most objects within our solar system
(
M. A. Cordiner et al. 2025
)
. These interlopers can inform us about orbital dynamics, planetary formation and ejection, and galactic composition. Traditional models of planetary formation, composition, and ejection relied solely on observations within our solar system. Interlopers may now allow us to extend these models to a broader astrophysical context and widen our scope in the search for evidence of life beyond Earth.

To access this frontier of astronomy, we require larger sample sizes and a broader approach to studying these objects. We already have upcoming ground- and space-based missions, such as the Rubin Observatory Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) and Near-Earth Object Surveyor (NEO Surveyor), both of which may detect interstellar interlopers
(
D. Jewitt & D. Z. Seligman 2023
)
. The best-case scenario would be to send probes to intercept an interloper and gather data in situ
(
D. Seligman & G. Laughlin 2018
;
K. Moore et al. 2021
;
S. A. Stern et al. 2024
)
. However, given the expected high speeds and low number density of interlopers, even if we detect an ISO, it is difficult to plan a mission before it escapes our solar system. Instead, we consider the possibility that the interloper does not escape our solar system. Although rare, the three-body system that large planets such as Jupiter create with our Sun can cause perturbations of the interloper that cause it to lose energy and become (at least temporarily) bound to our solar system
(
W. Dehnen et al. 2021
)
. We will refer to such ISOs as Type 8 ISOs, following the taxonomy of
A. M. Hein et al. 2022
. Specifically, Type 8 ISOs are recently (not during the formation of our solar system) captured interlopers.

Given the possibility of Type 8 ISOs currently in our solar system, we ask how to identify or constrain which solar system objects are of interstellar origin. We expect that they will have unique properties, specifically including their orbital elements, due to the strict criteria for capture. Previous papers have investigated this via simulations, with varying results
(
W. Dehnen et al. 2021
;
K. J. Napier et al. 2021a
;
K. J. Napier et al. 2021b
;
A. Siraj & A. Loeb 2019
;
T. O. Hands & W. Dehnen 2020
;
D. Mukherjee et al. 2023
)
. However, many previous studies do not include non-gravitational acceleration or stability in their models despite the first two known interstellar objects exhibiting significant non-gravitational acceleration. Nor do some include subsequent planetary interactions. High eccentricities, which imply close solar encounters, may necessitate modeling additional effects such as radiation forces, outgassing, and general relativity for the long-term characterization of bound ISO orbits
(
A. Sekhar et al. 2023
;
A. Sekhar et al. 2017
;
D. Jewitt 2024
)
. In addition to the gravitational slingshot of single interstellar objects, binary interstellar objects (ISBOs), which are two ISOs that are gravitationally bound to each other, may also provide a mechanism for capture that has not yet been explored in this context. Specifically, the Hills mechanism can cause an incoming ISBO to break apart, with one of its components becoming bound
(
J. G. Hills 1988
)
.

We will address these limitations by designing new models and simulations that incorporate long-term effects on orbital elements and interstellar binary objects (ISBOs). The paper is as follows: First, we analytically describe the capture from a gravitational slingshot for single ISOs, and similarly for the capture of ISBOs via the Hills mechanism. We then describe the N-body simulations we use to investigate the capture rate and orbital element distributions of Type 8 ISOs. Next, we present the results of the N-body simulations and compare them to our analytical results and known solar system objects. Finally, we discuss the significance of our results and future avenues of research.

II
Capture from Gravitational Slingshot

We assume that all of the incoming ISOs are close enough to Jupiter during capture such that we can neglect the Sun’s gravity, and thus model the capture as a gravitational slingshot (see Figure
1
).

The final velocity relative to the heliocentric frame is:

v_{f}=\sqrt{v^{2}+v_{Jupiter}^{2}+2v\cdot v_{Jupiter}\cos(\theta+\delta)}

(1)

where
\delta
is the angle of deflection from gravity in the planet’s frame,
v
is the initial velocity of the ISO near Jupiter (in Jupiter’s frame),
v_{Jupiter}
is Jupiter’s velocity in the heliocentric frame, and
\theta
is the angle between the initial incoming velocity of the ISO and Jupiter’s velocity. We assume that the capture timescale is small enough that
v_{Jupiter}
is effectively constant. To solve for the probability of capture with our initial assumptions, we solve for when: 1) the initial velocity is greater than the escape velocity, and 2) the final velocity is less than the escape velocity. A reduction in heliocentric velocity requires
\cos(\theta+\delta)<\cos(\theta)
. Due to high deflections being unlikely and the assumption that the distribution of incoming ISOs is isotropic, we expect that incoming ISOs moving along Jupiter’s prograde direction will be favored, since any deflection is more likely to result in a reduction in velocity.

To solve this, we need the deflection angle
\delta
, which is given by:

\delta=2\tan^{-1}(\frac{GM_{Jupiter}}{bv^{2}})

(2)

Where
b
is the ISO impact parameter. This is only an approximation valid for a two-body system. In our three-body system, we expect perturbations from the Sun, but we consider these negligible at the semi-major axis of Jupiter and for the short timescales for capture.

Using the deflection to find the change in velocity, we can semi-analytically solve for the capture rate and the orbital parameters right after capture by using our synthetic population of ISOs (see Section
IV.1
).

The semi-major axis is defined by:

a=-\frac{GM_{\odot}m}{2E_{f}}

(3)

where
E_{f}
is the final orbital energy:

E_{f}=\frac{1}{2}mv_{f}^{2}-G\frac{M_{\odot}m}{r}-\frac{GM_{Jupiter}m}{r_{J}}

(4)

and
r_{J}
is the separation of the ISO and Jupiter immediately after deflection;
r
is the distance between the ISO and the Sun. We assume
r\approx a_{Jupiter}
and
r_{J}
is the same as the distance from Jupiter during initialization. With this final energy, we can count the number of synthetic ISOs that become bound (final energy is negative). This roughly gives the probability of capture. Then the capture rate, or the number of bound ISOs per unit time, can be found as the probability of capture convoluted with the flux of incoming ISOs into our sample space (see Section
IV.1
, Equation
10
)

For eccentricity, it not only depends on the change of energy but also on the change of angular momentum:

e^{2}=1+\frac{2E_{f}L_{f}^{2}}{G^{2}M^{2}m^{3}}

(5)

We assume that the deflection occurs solely in a single plane, which is defined by a vector orthogonal to the separation vector between the ISO and Jupiter at initialization and the initial velocity vector of the ISO. From this, we can derive the final velocity vector as a rotation in this plane by
\delta
, and thus the final inclination is given by:

\cos i=\frac{(\textbf{L}_{f})_{z}}{|\textbf{L}_{f}|}

(6)

where
\hat{z}
defines the ecliptic.

We expect the change in energy or angular momentum to be small, but large enough to turn
E_{f}
negative. Assuming that
v_{f}
is of the same order of magnitude as
v_{Jupiter}
, the final energy is comparable to that of Jupiter, and so we would expect semi-major axes to be around that of Jupiter or higher, due to the additional interaction term with Jupiter in Equation
4
. Since our initial distribution is isotropic, we might expect our inclinations to be uniform in
\sin(i)
. However, particles are more likely to be bound if they move parallel to Jupiter. Since small deflections are more likely, the ISO will remain roughly parallel to Jupiter post-capture. Thus, we expect the captured objects to show a bias towards prograde inclinations along the ecliptic. This assumption breaks down for high deflections, but such cases are rare.

III
Capture from the Hills Mechanism

In the Hills mechanism, an ISBO passes sufficiently close to a massive body (e.g., the Sun) such that tidal forces can unbind the binary, which can cause one component to lose enough energy to become gravitationally bound (see Figure
2
). This process is a restricted three-body problem, unlike the single-object captures where gravitational slingshot effects dominate. The simplest method is to approximate the final energy by considering the initial energy and a characteristic energy scale during tidal disruption.

Figure 2
:
Diagram of the Hills mechanism.
1
is the trajectory of the incoming ISBO. As it reaches the tidal disruption radius
R_{T}
, it disrupts, causing one of the binary components to escape into a hyperbolic trajectory (
2
), and the other component to become bound into an elliptical orbit (
3
). Note that vectors and distances are not drawn to scale.

The tidal force experienced by the binary is approximately given by:

f\approx\frac{2GM_{\odot}m_{tot}r_{b}}{r^{3}}

(7)

Where
m_{tot}
is the total mass of the binary,
r_{b}
is the radius of the binary orbit around its barycenter, and
r
is the distance from the Sun at the moment of tidal disruption. This equation is valid when
r\gg r_{b}
. We assume the binary is circular around a common barycenter, and the masses of the two binary components are equal. By treating this force as a smooth function of
r
, we can associate an energy:

\Delta E\approx\frac{GM_{\odot}m_{tot}(\textbf{r}_{b}\cdot\textbf{r})}{r^{3}}\,

(8)

This represents the change in energy for the binary components once they are tidally disrupted
(
B. Sersante et al. 2025
)
. However, this equation assumes that the binary is unaffected by the Sun’s gravity until it reaches the tidal disruption radius. After it reaches the tidal disruption radius, it becomes dominated by tidal forces. This assumption is more accurate for deeper encounters or low impact parameters. The vector
r
is approximately the tidal disruption radius during disruption:

|\textbf{r}|\approx R_{T}=r_{b}(\frac{32M_{\odot}}{m_{tot}})^{\frac{1}{3}}

(9)

Using our initial conditions, we can calculate the final energy of the closer binary component as
E_{f}=E_{0}+\Delta E
. If this energy becomes negative, then the ISBO component becomes bound. The dot product is dependent on the orientation of the binary plane to the separation vector at the tidal disruption radius. Since our distribution is isotropic, and there are two binary components, we may assume an average value of the absolute value of the dot product. However, this will most likely end in an underestimate. Although the initial distribution (at infinity) is isotropic, tidal forces will produce a torque that shifts the distribution of vectors towards the Sun. The phase of the binary orbit also matters, and may cause the binary to disrupt slightly closer to the Sun than the tidal disruption radius. For simplicity, we instead assume a maximum value of the dot product.

We also semi-analytically check for collision by checking that the minimum separation from gravitational focusing of the center of mass does not coincide with the radius of the Sun. However, this model excludes post-disruption collisions. For a small tidal radius, the center of mass of the binary may avoid collision with the Sun, but the disrupted component that loses energy can still collide.

Using this characteristic energy change (Equation
8
), we can semi-analytically estimate the probability of capture of an ISBO via the Hills mechanism. It is important to note when this model breaks down. For small impact parameters, a collision of the disrupted binary component becomes likely, leading to an overestimate of the actual number bound. For high impact parameters, our assumption that the binary hasn’t deformed before reaching the tidal radius is weaker. Most likely, the binary will begin to widen slightly as it approaches, making it easier to disrupt. Therefore, we expect that at high impact parameters, our model will underestimate the actual number bound. At all scales, we make the simplifying assumption that the dot product in Equation
8
is maximal and precisely at the tidal disruption radius during disruption. In reality, the dot product and actual distance from the Sun during disruption vary slightly.

IV
N-body Simulations

We run N-body simulations using the REBOUND and REBOUNDx Python packages
(
H. Rein & S.-F. Liu 2012
;
D. Tamayo et al. 2019
)
. The general set-up of these simulations is as follows: We first initialize a population of our ISOs or ISBOs in a shell around the main perturbing body. We then check that the object is initially unbound to our system. If so, we simulate forward until the particle has escaped or become bound. For those that have become bound, we continue simulating for
\sim 2
Myr to characterize ejection rates and orbital parameters. We have two main simulations: ISOs captured by the Jupiter-Sun system and ISBOs captured by the Sun via the Hills mechanism.

IV.1
Single Interstellar Objects

For the single ISOs, the main perturbing body is Jupiter. We take a spherical shell at the Hill radius of Jupiter (53 million kilometers) and populate the shell with test (massless) particles. We assume that the distribution of ISOs is isotropic. We set the position on the shell uniformly throughout the entire sphere. We then orient the velocity vector randomly within a hemisphere with its pole centered towards Jupiter. The velocity distribution is assumed to be the same as the local stellar velocity distribution. We approximate this as a log-normal distribution, where the velocity is the velocity at infinity (see Figure
3
)
(
T. M. Eubanks et al. 2021
)
. Using energy conservation, we can calculate the actual velocity at initialization. For collision, we assume each test particle is spherical with a diameter of one kilometer. If the ISO collides with Jupiter or the Sun, we remove the particle from the simulation and do not count it towards the number bound.

Sampled velocity distribution of ISOs at infinity.

f(v)=\frac{1}{v\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{(-\frac{(\ln{v}-\mu^{2})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})}
, with parameters

and

. For more information on the velocity distribution, see

After initialization, we integrate backward for 10 years, checking that the particle is interstellar. Our criterion for interstellar or not is that the orbital energy of the particle is positive. For particles that meet this criterion, we label them as ISOs and integrate them forward for 20 years. This time, we check that the particle is bound (i.e., orbital energy is negative). We then count the number bound and save the initial parameters for each. We calculate the rate of capture as:

\text{Bound ISOs per unit time}=n_{iso}\int vf(v)p(v)\text{dv}\int dS\int d\hat{v}

(10)

where
f(v)
is the probability density function for our log-normal velocity distribution,
p(v)
is some unknown probability distribution for capture into a bound orbit, and
n_{iso}
is the number density of ISOs, which we assume to be
0.1

\text{au}^{-3}

(
W. Dehnen et al. 2021
;
A. Siraj & A. Loeb 2019
;
A. Do et al. 2018
)
. The integral over
vf(v)
is the average velocity of the incoming particles. The integral over
S
is the surface of the sphere we initialize our ISOs in:
\int dS=4\pi r_{max}^{2}
, where
r_{max}=53
million kilometers. The integral over
\hat{v}
is the perpendicular velocity over the hemisphere oriented towards the center of our larger sphere:
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta d\theta d\phi=\pi
.

Since
p(v)
is unknown, we use a Monte-Carlo method to numerically determine the capture rate. For each value
v_{i}
, we simulate and save whether the object is bound by a number
I_{i}
, where
I_{bound}=1
and
I_{unbound}=0
. We can then estimate the capture rate as:

\text{Bound ISOs per unit time}\approx 4\pi^{2}r_{max}^{2}n_{iso}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\cos\theta_{i}I_{i}

(11)

where the first part is just the geometric factor from before, and we’ve done a weighted average of convolution between the incoming flux and the probability of capture in Equation
10
. Note that
\theta_{i}
is the angle between the position vector from Jupiter and the velocity vector for the ISO labeled
i
, and
N
is the total number of ISOs sampled.

Next, we investigate the long-term stability of the captured objects. Using the initial parameters saved from the bound ISOs, we initialize a new simulation identical to the previous, except with added higher-order effects of the three other giant planets and non-gravitational acceleration, and integrate forward for up to a maximum of 50 times the inverse capture rate (the average number of years until one ISO becomes bound). Each year, we check whether the particle has collided or escaped using the same criterion described for the first simulation. If the particle collides or escapes during that year, we record its lifetime. If not, we continue simulating. For particles that survive for long enough to see another bound ISO (i.e., the inverse of the capture rate), we consider them stable enough to be observed and record their orbital elements. Given the ejection rate, we model the current population of Type 8 ISOs and analyze their orbital elements.

IV.2
Binary Interstellar Objects

For the binary objects, the main perturbing body is the Sun. We take a spherical shell at 3 au from the Sun and populate the shell with ISBOs. We initialize each binary as two equal-mass objects in circular orbits about their mutual barycenter. The distribution of positions and directions is assumed to be isotropic. Each point on the shell, each bulk velocity vector, each binary orbital plane orientation, and each phase of the binary orbit have equal weight. The bulk velocity (the velocity of the common barycenter of the binary) at infinity is the same as the log-normal distribution for ISOs (see Figure
3
). As before, we calculate the actual velocity at initialization with energy conservation.

We also have to consider the distribution of binary separation and the total mass of the binaries. We assume that the distribution of binary separation follows that of outer solar system binaries due to the possibility of ISBOs produced by stellar flybys. We infer the binary distribution from Kuiper belt binaries, which is approximately a log-normal, as shown in Figure
4

(
K. S. Noll et al. 2008
)
.

IV.3
Radiation effects, Outgassing, and General Relativity

We include radiation pressure and Poynting-Robertson drag from REBOUNDx in our simulations
(
D. Tamayo et al. 2019
;
J. A. Burns et al. 1979
)
. We also model the Yarkovsky effect in its simplified form via REBOUNDx
(
D. Tamayo et al. 2019
;
D. Veras et al. 2019
)
. Since we have no preference for spin direction, we randomize the possibility of inward migration or outward migration with equal probabilities. We include a simple gravitational potential from REBOUNDx with a general relativity correction
(
D. Tamayo et al. 2019
;
A. Nobili & I. W. Roxburgh 1986
)
. This correction accurately models the precession but not the mean motion. However, since the masses of our ISOs are small relative to the Sun, this error is negligible. We also consider outgassing, for which we use the empirical model from
T. Kramer & M. Läuter 2019
. See the
GitHub
for the C code used to reproduce outgassing in our simulations
1
1

1

https://github.com/rainermd/ISO-ISBOCapture
. All particles receive the same parameters for all of the higher-order effects; see the Appendix
A
for the exact parameters used. Values are estimated from ’Oumuamua when data is available, or Jupiter-family comets if not. Of course, physical ISOs will have a wide range of these parameters, particularly for the Yarkovsky effect, since it is dependent on rotation. However, these are higher-order perturbations, and changes in their parameters do not significantly affect our results on the ejection rates or the distribution of orbital parameters.

V
Results

V.1
Capture of Single Interstellar Objects

After simulating 50 million single ISOs around Jupiter, we find that 1393 ISOs became captured, or a capture rate of 1 bound ISO every 48500 years. Our semi-analytical result concluded that 1011 single ISOs should become bound via gravitational slingshot, or 72% of the numerical result. The reason for this underestimate most likely stems from excluding the Sun in the capture process, and the relative velocity near Jupiter is slightly different from that at initialization at the Hill radius.

After continuing to simulate for 1.9 million years after each capture, we found that the number of ejected ISOs follows a Weibull distribution (see Figure
5
). This maximum simulation time was chosen to accurately depict the stability of bound ISOs while reducing run time. The probability that an ISO was captured at some time
t
years ago and is still bound is given by:

P_{iso}(t)=2.79\cdot 10^{-5}\exp(-(\frac{t}{1.9\cdot 10^{5}\text{ years}})^{0.46})

(15)

This is the capture rate multiplied by the rate of ejection. Although in general the capture rate and the ejection rate are not independent, given the long timescales after capture, we approximate the two rates (or probabilities) as independent.

Figure 5

The shape parameters of the Weibull function show a broad distribution of lifetimes. Most eject quickly, lasting less than 200000 years, but there is a long tail of metastable ISOs. Assuming that the conditions of the solar system are approximately the same throughout the majority of the solar system’s lifetime, we integrate
P(t)
for 4.5 billion years to find the number of bound ISOs currently. Doing so yields a current population of eight bound ISOs in the solar system from single ISOs captured by interacting with Jupiter. Without higher-order effects (see Section
IV.3
), bound ISOs are only slightly more stable, with around 9 bound ISOs currently. We also repeated this simulation for each of the three other giant planets. We found that there should be two bound ISOs from Saturn, and zero from Uranus and Neptune.

There is a similar trend in the semi-major axis, where the analytical population is, on average, farther out (and shows less spread) than the simulated population. Over the 48500 years of simulation, further perturbations caused some ISOs to migrate inward. The analytical semi-major axis distribution peaks near Saturn’s semi-major axis. However, as the simulation progresses, the distribution flattens out significantly due to repeated encounters with the giant planets, causing ejections. V.1.2
Comparison of ISOs to Solar System Objects

Now we consider whether there exist objects in our solar system that could originally be ISOs, and the potential for observing such objects. To do so, we search for objects that fall within the densest regions of our histograms, satisfy the constraint
-3<T_{J}<3
, but are also atypical of most objects that have well-defined orbital parameters. The most convincing constraint is the dense region in Figure
8
near
T_{J}=3
(see also Figure
9
). To quantitatively describe this region, we map the density of this region using a Gaussian kernel density estimation with a bound of
T_{J}\leq 3
. We plot contours corresponding to the highest density regions (HDR) that contain
10\%,25\%,50\%
, and
90\%
of the total probability. If ISOs do exist, they will almost always lie within the
90\%
HDR contour, but will tend to be closer to the lower contours. To identify ISO candidates, we search through the JPL small-body database, filtering for objects that lie within the
25\%
HDR region of the 3-dimensional parameter space (semi-major axis, eccentricity and, inclination). Doing so, we find
18
ISO candidates (see Appendix
C
). Although this contour accounts only for
25\%
of the total possible population, its boundary is sharp and lies in a unique area of orbital parameter phase space relative to known solar system populations (see Figure

Given our simulation results, we expect there to exist eight bound ISOs, and so we estimate that around two of them lie within this 25% HDR contour. Many of these objects have not been studied well and represent excellent candidates for future surveys in search of bound ISOs. Specifically, many of them lack data on physical characteristics and composition, which is required to confidently identify these objects as interstellar. Most notably, the 10% HDR contour is nearly empty of observed solar system objects except for one, the trans-Neptunian object 2024 XS15. One object also happens to be the number of ISOs we would predict in this region, so 2024 XS15 is one of the strongest ISO candidates. Other objects may be bound ISOs but do not lie within the

First, we notice the discrepancy between our semi-analytical calculation and simulated data. At large

, which corresponds to a small tidal disruption radius, our analytical calculation greatly overestimates the amount bound. This behavior is consistent with the limitations outlined in Section

f
, and therefore larger tidal disruption radii, we notice the opposite: our calculation greatly underestimates the actual value. This underestimate also matches the limitations of our calculation, as we neglect the deformation of the binary as it approaches the tidal disruption radius. This error increases as we go to higher impact parameters. However, for middle values of
f

Other important quantities to note are the capture rate
C
and the Weibull parameters
t_{0}
and
k
. As expected, large values of
f
, which means more tightly bound binaries, are the least likely to be captured, averaging millions of years until the capture of a single ISBO. As
f
decreases, the period between captures decreases, making these wider binaries (wider in terms of the ratio to their size) easier to capture. This trend plateaus for smaller values of
f
. As you approach smaller values of
f
past
1/200
, the capture rate approaches a constant. At small values of
f
, the effects of collision are negligible. Then the capture rate scales by the inverse probability of a large energy change (roughly
\propto R_{T}^{2}
, see Equation
8
) times the inverse probability of encountering the tidal radius (roughly
\propto R_{T}^{-2}
), which is constant. For the higher values of
f
, we find that
C\propto f^{4}
. For these values, we must consider collision. For high
f
, the energy change at the tidal radius will nearly always be enough to cause one ISBO component to be bound, so we lose a factor of
R_{T}^{2}
. Then we have the inverse probability of encountering the tidal radius (
R_{T}^{-2}
) times the inverse probability of collision, which also roughly scales as
R_{T}^{-2}
. Then
C
scales by
R_{T}^{-4}
, or
f^{4}
(see Equation
12
). In the middle regime, the scaling is more complicated and yields a minimum around
f=1/20
.

V.2.2
Comparison of ISBOs to Solar System Objects

Two key features of the orbital elements of bound ISBOs are low perihelion distances and isotropic inclinations. The low perihelion distance (
q<1
au) means that the orbit of such a bound ISBO (if it exists) is most likely Earth-crossing. If this is true, it suggests that if these objects exist, they should be observable. Given the period distribution of ISBOs, such an object may have already been observed. Similar to the single ISO case, we construct the most likely orbital parameter space using a Gaussian kernel density estimation. Figure
11
shows the density and HDR contours in perihelion and inclination parameter space. Searching for objects that lie within the 25% contour, we identify 7 bound ISBO candidates. Relative to the single ISO case, this region is not as unique from other solar system objects, and unless our assumptions are off by an order of magnitude, we would only expect to see one bound ISBO in this region.

In both cases of bound ISBOs and ISOs, orbital parameters alone do not suffice to determine interstellar origin. Orbital parameters can help narrow down the search, but only when combined with physical characteristics, such as anomalous composition, can any confidence be put in their origin. For example, although not listed in the appendix, the asteroid (467372) 2004 LG is located within the 90% HDR. Most ISOs should exhibit behavior closer to that of comets than asteroids
(
D. Jewitt 2024
)
. However, (467372) 2004 LG may be an inactive comet
(
P. Wiegert et al. 2020
)
. It is most notable in that its perihelion distance matches well with our predicted tidal disruption distance (
q=0.213
au). At such perihelion and stability, it is expected that the object will be subject to “supercatastrophic disruption”, or disintegrate into millimeter-scale particles, by the Sun
(
P. Wiegert et al. 2020
)
. One possible explanation for its survival may be that (467372) 2004 LG is a recently captured ISBO, and so has not survived as long as backwards simulations suggest. Another object which is not within the 25% HDR, but lies within the larger 90% HDR and is anomalous is the comet 96P/Machholz. Although this comet currently only has a moderate inclination (
i=58.14^{\circ}
), its three-body interactions with the Sun and Jupiter cause its inclination to oscillate on millennial timescales and it is predicted to spend most of its past and future at
i>70^{\circ}

(
A. Sekhar et al. 2017
)
; in addition, it is a strong candidate for a captured ISBO due to its unusually tiny perihelion distance (
q=0.124
au). It has also been previously hypothesized to have an extrasolar origin due to its unusual composition
(
D. G. Schleicher 2008
;
N. L. Eisner et al. 2019
)
. However, it is possible that 96P and similar objects in this region can be misidentified as ISBO candidates if they previously underwent fragmentation from a larger object, which is common for near-Sun perihelia.

VI
Discussion

Our results suggest that there are currently
\sim 10
Type 8 ISOs in the Solar System, with the majority originating from gravitational braking with Jupiter. A smaller fraction may result from encounters with the other giant planets, with two bound ISOs expected from Saturn. While our semi-analytical model is sufficient for the capture phase, it does not include ejection, which is necessary to characterize stability. As expected, most bound ISOs undergo multiple Jupiter or other giant planet encounters, leading to swift ejection. This process follows a Weibull distribution in the lifetime of the object in our solar system. Contributions from interstellar binary objects (ISBOs) and the Hills mechanism are unclear due to a lack of data on ISBOs, namely the distribution of
f
and their number density. However, we show that this mechanism does provide a viable method for capturing a small number of ISBOs into our solar system.

There are notable uncertainties in our simulations, particularly in the assumed parameters. The number density of ISOs is not well constrained; recent observations of confirmed interstellar interlopers suggest it may be higher than previously thought, which would raise the expected number of bound ISOs linearly. For example, the number density has been estimated to be as high as
0.2\text{ au}^{-3}

(
A. Do et al. 2018
)
. If this is the case, then our expected number of bound ISOs should be
\sim 20
. The number density of ISBOs is even less determined, but current evidence implies it is a small or negligible fraction of the total ISO population. Further research on the ejection of ISBOs from their parent systems is needed to understand their capture probability. For example, stellar flybys, such as those in
S. Pfalzner et al. 2024
,
N. W. H. Moore et al. 2020
, and
S. J. Kenyon & B. C. Bromley 2004
, may eject ISBOs if the binary resides in the outer regions of its parent system and the ejection event preserves the binary structure. Other possibilities in addition to stellar flybys include scattering with giant planets, but research suggests that such a mechanism would not preserve binaries
(
A. H. Parker & J. J. Kavelaars 2010
)
. However, more detailed simulations and observations of ISBOs are necessary to assess whether either mechanism introduces a substantial population. These simulations and observations could also better constrain ISBO characteristics, such as separation and orbital properties, as we currently assume circular binaries with separations similar to Kuiper Belt binaries. We also assume equal masses, whereas in reality, it is more likely that one component dominates the mass of the ISBO. The mass density of ISBOs may also be an order of magnitude less than what we assume here, if they follow the average comet mass density. If this is the case, then tidal disruption radii will be larger for all values of
f
, possibly leading to easier capture. By constraining these values, the results of our simulations may be better interpreted. We also do not account for fragmentation of ISOs, which may occur due to tidal forces or solar heating during a close encounter with the Sun. While the Hills mechanism approximates such captures, N-body simulations are poorly suited to model fragmentation in detail.

For most captured single ISOs, we find that their orbital elements are somewhat consistent with Halley-type comets, with lighter overlap with Centaurs, Jupiter-family comets, and Trans-Neptunian objects. However, a distinct region of simulated ISOs, given by the
<50\%
HDR contour in Figure
9
, is not well populated by numbered objects. Going to the
10\%
HDR, we only have one observed solar system object: (2024) XS15, which makes it a strong Type 8 ISO candidate. This region of orbital parameter space may serve as a promising target for future ISO surveys. As the observed population of Halley-type comets significantly exceeds our predicted eight bound ISOs, the Oort cloud origin theory remains the dominant explanation for Halley-type comets
(
J. H. Wang & R. Brasser 2014
)
. Still, it may be possible that a small subset of Halley-type comets is interstellar in origin. Interestingly, the prograde inclination bias we observe in captured ISOs could help explain the small prograde excess in Halley-type comets, despite the expected isotropy of the Oort cloud theory
(
J. H. Wang & R. Brasser 2014
)
. However, the region of interest for ISOs only loosely overlaps with Halley-type comets. We would only expect a substantial subset of bound ISOs in the known Halley-type comets if the number of bound ISOs greatly exceeds the predicted
\sim 10
bound ISOs from our simulations.

The captured binary ISOs roughly follow small perihelion distances and isotropic inclinations, given by the HDR contours in Figure
12
. These parameters overlap with the Apollo asteroid group, although captured ISBOs are expected to resemble comets more than asteroids, and generally have higher inclinations. Thus, it is unlikely that any given Apollo asteroid is a captured ISBO. One exception is (467372) 2004 LG, which has a perihelion close to our expected tidal disruption radius (
q=0.213
au), a high inclination (
71.17^{\circ}
), and is an exception to the disruption hypothesis of Sun-grazing asteroids
(
P. Wiegert et al. 2020
)
. Despite lying past the 25% HDR due to its moderate inclination, the comet 96P/Machholz is also a strong ISBO candidate with its unusual perihelion distance and composition
(
P. Wiegert et al. 2020
)
.

In Appendix
C
and Appendix
B
, we provide a list of 18 candidate ISOs and seven candidate ISBOs with orbital elements most consistent with interstellar origins. This is only for the most dense population of our simulated orbital parameter space, and thus, there may exist ISO candidates outside of these regions. For this reason, we have included two ISBO candidates that lie outside of the
25\%
contour, but within the
90\%
contour and display anomalous properties. Our simulations thus predict that around three of the 18 ISO candidates are actually interstellar, and one of the seven ISBO candidates is interstellar. Most of the single candidates are unnumbered and require better observations to refine their orbital elements and confirm they fall within our region of interest. However, orbital properties alone are not definitive proof. Future studies of compositional, dynamical, and physical characteristics of these candidates will be essential to strengthen confidence in their classification. For example, it is suspected from current observations that ISOs will typically resemble comets more so than asteroids (or ‘dark’ comets, such as ’Oumuamua)
(
D. Jewitt 2024
)
. However, the line between asteroids and comets is blurred, especially in the context of ISOs. Continued discoveries, such as with 3I/ATLAS, combined with refined simulations, may help us better define the characteristics of Type 8 ISOs and build an accurate picture of the bound interstellar population. If confirmed, these objects present a rare opportunity to study interstellar material in a controlled and repeatable manner, offering profound insights into the broader galactic environment.
