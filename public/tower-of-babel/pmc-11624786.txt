Association of pre-existing conditions with major driver mutations and PD-L1 expression in NSCLC
Authors: Peiling Chen, Xin Zheng, Caichen Li, Jianfu Li, Chen Yang, Yi Feng, Bo Cheng, Hengrui Liang, Zhichao Liu, Yi Zhao, Shan Xiong, Feng Li, Ran Zhong, Shuting Zhan, Huiting Wang, Yang Xiang, Wenhai Fu, Wenjun Ye, Bo’ao Jiang, Xianzhe Fan, Jun Liu, Jianxing He, Wenhua Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to explore how pre-existing conditions such as blood types, family history of cancer and comorbid diseases correlate with the genetic and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression that contributes to the heterogeneous biological behaviours of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).Materials and methodsA cohort of 5507 NSCLC patients who underwent surgical resection between January 2014 and July 2018 was studied. Targeted next-generation sequencing was used to detect mutations in nine pivotal cancer-related genes, and immunohistochemical staining was applied to assess PD-L1 expression. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify significant correlations.ResultsAll patients underwent NGS, with 1839 were also evaluated for PD-L1 expression. Several significant findings were found: ROS1 mutations were closely associated with a family history of lung cancer (OR 7.499, 95% CI 1.094 to 30.940, p=0.013). Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) L858R mutations were common among patients with a family history of non-lung cancers and those with hypertension (OR 2.089, 95% CI 1.029 to 4.135, p=0.037 and OR 1.252, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.562, p=0.048, respectively). Pre-existing conditions such as diabetes and hepatitis B surface antigen positivity (OR 1.468, 95% CI 1.042 to 2.047, p=0.026 and OR 1.373, 95% CI 1.012 to 1.847, p=0.038, respectively) were correlated with EGFR exon 19 deletions. RhD negativity showed potential ties to BRAF mutations (OR 0.010, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.252, p=0.001). A history of tuberculosis linked to increased PD-L1 expression in immune cells (OR 3.597, 95% CI 1.295 to 14.957, p=0.034).ConclusionThis large-scale, cross-sectional study reveals a complex interplay between genetic mutations, immunological features and pre-existing conditions in NSCLC patients, offering insights that could inform personalised treatment strategies.

Introduction

Recent advances in understanding genetic profiles and the immune landscape have revolutionised the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), leading to the development of highly effective targeted therapies and immunotherapies.1 2 Key driver mutations, such as those in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1), have enabled personalised treatment approaches.35 Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), including osimertinib, crizotinib, entrectinib and lorlatinib, have demonstrated remarkable success in treating patients with these mutations.68 Additionally, the therapeutic arsenal has expanded to include drugs targeting mutations such as B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) V600E, Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) G12C and Ret proto-oncogene (RET) rearrangements.911 These advancements, along with the rise of immunotherapies targeting tumours expressing programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), have significantly improved patient outcomes.

Comprehensive genomic analysis has revealed that driver mutations are present in about 50% of lung adenocarcinomas, including alterations in EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS, phosphoinositide-3-kinase catalytic alpha (PIK3CA), MET proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase (MET), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and RET.12 This growing body of genetic data allows for more precise, individualised treatment strategies, enhancing the effectiveness of NSCLC management.

It is crucial to explore how patient-specific factors, particularly pre-existing conditions, influence the genetic and immunological profiles of NSCLC. Age and smoking status are well-established risk factors for lung cancer and are closely linked to the development of comorbidities.13 Pulmonary disorders, including interstitial lung disease, emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), have been investigated for potential associations with genetic mutations in lung cancer. Some studies report a lower incidence of EGFR mutations and ALK rearrangements in patients with these conditions, though the evidence is mixed.1416 In contrast, the presence of pulmonary diseases seems to have minimal impact on the prevalence of KRAS mutations.14 17 Furthermore, comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity may affect lung cancer prognosis, but their relationship with genetic alterations in NSCLC remains underexplored.13 18 19

Recent research has increasingly focused on how pre-existing conditions, such as blood type and family history of cancer, affect the likelihood of NSCLC driver mutations. For example, individuals with AB blood type exhibit a lower frequency of EGFR mutations.20 A strong correlation between a family history of cancer—especially lung cancer—and an increased incidence of EGFR mutations highlights the genetic underpinnings of cancer susceptibility.21 22 While the role of PD-L1 expression in guiding the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors is well recognised, its association with patient demographics and pre-existing health conditions requires further investigation.23 24

These insights have prompted a comprehensive exploration into how driver mutations in nine key cancer-related genes, along with PD-L1 expression, correlate with pre-existing conditions, such as blood type, family cancer history and prevalent comorbidities, within a large Chinese population. This deeper understanding of genetic and immunological profiles, tailored to patient and tumour characteristics, holds the potential to transform clinical approaches to lung cancer care.

Material and Methods

Study design and patients

This single-centre, cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the relationship between pre-existing conditions—such as blood type, family history of cancer and comorbidities—and driver mutations, as well as PD-L1 expression, in patients with NSCLC. We retrospectively reviewed all NSCLC patients who underwent surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Guangzhou, China) from January 2014 to July 2018. Eligible participants were those with documented tumour genotypes or sufficient tissue for molecular analysis. Patients were excluded if they lacked data on family cancer history, comorbidities or genotypes, or if they had received neoadjuvant therapy. Histological sections from all patients were re-reviewed to confirm diagnoses.

Molecular analysis

Genomic and immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses were conducted using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumour samples, with a minimum requirement of 20% tumour nuclei. Targeted next-generation sequencing was employed to analyse somatic mutations in nine cancer-related genes: EGFR, ALK, RAS, ROS1, RET, BRAF, PIK3CA, MET and HER2. DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and quantified with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Library preparation involved hybridisation capture of critical introns and exons, followed by sequencing with an average coverage of >500× on an Ion Proton Sequencer (Thermo Fisher). A customised bioinformatics pipeline (Otype) was used to process the data, identifying single nucleotide variations, short insertions or deletions, copy number variations and gene rearrangements.25

IHC staining for PD-L1 was performed on tumour cells and tumour-infiltrating immune cells using the VENTANA PD-L1 (SP142) Assay (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The OptiView Amplification Kit and OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit were employed as detection systems. PD-L1 positivity was defined as membranous staining in ≥1% of tumour cells.

Data collection and definitions

Pre-existing conditions analysed included blood type (ABO and Rhesus groups), family history of cancer (including lung and other cancers) and comorbidities such as prior tumour history, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, pulmonary diseases (eg, COPD, tuberculosis) and HBsAg positivity. A family history of cancer was defined as any cancer diagnosis in first-degree relatives. Comorbidities were identified by reviewing patients’ medical records for clinical history and relevant treatments. Additional clinical data, including age at surgery, gender, smoking status, tumour stage and histology, were obtained from electronic medical records via the LinkDoc database (LinkDoc Technology, Beijing, China). Smoking status was recorded as a binary variable (presence or absence).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by using R Studio V.19.0 (R Studio, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics V.22.0 (SPSS). Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate associations between driver mutations or PD-L1 expression and patient characteristics, including pre-existing conditions. A forward stepwise logistic regression model was applied for multivariate analysis (entry criteria: p<0.05, removal criteria: p>0.10). All statistical tests were two sided, with p values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To ensure the robustness of our multivariate logistic regression analysis, we evaluated multicollinearity among the independent variables by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF values for all variables were low, with most close to 1, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. This suggests that the independent variables were not highly correlated, supporting the validity of our statistical results.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in this study.

Results

Baseline characteristics and pre-existing conditions

This study analysed data from 5507 patients diagnosed between January 2014 and July 2018, as summarised in table 1. The cohort was predominantly male (55.11%, n=3035) with a median age of 60 years (IQR: 52–67). Nearly half of the participants were non-smokers (47.83%). Most patients were diagnosed at early stages (I–II, 59.74%), and adenocarcinoma was the most common histological type (82.44%).

Blood type distribution showed type O as the most frequent (39.28%, n=2163), followed by type B (27.02%, n=1488), type A (25.86%, n=1424) and type AB (6.77%, n=373). The RhD-positive trait was present in 95.61% (n=5265) of the cohort. A family history of cancer was reported by 2.71% (n=149) of patients, including lung cancer (1.82%, n=100) and other cancers (1.23%, n=68).

Pre-existing conditions documented included hypertension (23.90%, n=1316), diabetes mellitus (9.68%, n=533), prior tumour history (4.39%, n=242), coronary heart disease (4.03%, n=222) and pulmonary diseases (6.77%, n=373). Pulmonary conditions specifically included COPD (1.78%, n=98), tuberculosis (4.01%, n=221) and other pulmonary disorders (1.20%, n=66). HBsAg positivity was found in 6.59% (n=363) of patients.

Driver mutations were identified in 63.83% of patients (n=3515), as shown in figure 1. EGFR mutations were the most common, occurring in 46.79% of patients (n=2694), including exon 19 deletions (n=1107), L858R mutations (n=1244), T790M mutations (n=74) and other rare mutations (n=269). RAS mutations were observed in 7.50% (n=413) and ALK fusions in 2.45% (n=135). Less frequent mutations included PIK3CA, MET, HER2, RET, ROS1 and BRAF.

PD-L1 IHC staining was performed on tumour samples from 1839 patients (33.39%). Due to the limited availability of tissue samples after routine pathological evaluation in clinical practice, and the prioritisation of PD-L1 testing in advanced cases being considered for immunotherapy, only a subset of patients underwent staining. Among these patients, 33.55% (n=617) showed positive PD-L1 expression in tumour cells, while 76.29% (n=1403) demonstrated PD-L1 positivity in tumour-infiltrating immune cells.

This section examines the relationships between specific EGFR mutations and patient characteristics as well as pre-existing conditions. Univariate analysis revealed significant associations between EGFR exon 19 deletions and factors such as female gender, age under 50, never-smoking status, early-stage tumours (I–II), adenocarcinoma histology and a personal history of cancer (online supplemental table 1). EGFR L858R mutations were significantly associated with female gender, age 50 and above, never-smoking status, early-stage tumours, adenocarcinoma histology, a family history of lung cancer, hypertension and COPD. Additionally, blood type AB (compared with type O, 8.0% vs 6.4%, p=0.058) and a family history of other cancers (1.8% vs 1.1%, p=0.055) showed borderline significance. EGFR T790M mutations were linked to late-stage tumours (III–IV) and adenocarcinoma histology, with no significant associations with pre-existing conditions. However, there was a marginal association with never-smoking status (50.0% vs 47.8%, p=0.050). EGFR rare mutations were associated with female gender, never-smoking status and adenocarcinoma histology, with younger age (<50 years) showing borderline significance (22.7% vs 18.1%, p=0.059)

Multivariate logistic regression analysis (figure 3) identified key predictors for specific EGFR mutation subtypes. Exon 19 deletions were strongly associated with female gender (OR 1.761, 95% CI 1.388 to 2.243, p<0.001), never-smoking status (OR 0.659, 95% CI 0.494 to 0.876, p=0.004), adenocarcinoma histology (OR 7.314, 95% CI 4.394 to 13.244, p<0.001), history of diabetes (OR 1.468, 95% CI 1.042 to 2.047, p=0.026) and HBsAg positivity (OR 1.373, 95% CI 1.012 to 1.847, p=0.038). Predictors for L858R mutations included female gender (OR 1.319, 95% CI 1.057 to 1.650, p=0.015), age 50 and older (OR 1.859, 95% CI 1.447 to 2.407, p<0.001), never-smoking status (OR 0.589, 95% CI 0.454 to 0.763, p<0.001), adenocarcinoma histology (OR 3.419, 95% CI 2.403 to 5.012, p<0.001), a family history of other cancers (OR 2.089, 95% CI 1.029 to 4.135, p=0.037) and hypertension (OR 1.252, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.562, p=0.048), with early-stage tumours nearing significance (p=0.059). For rare EGFR mutations, adenocarcinoma histology (OR 20.730, 95% CI 4.579 to 366.406, p=0.003) and never-smoking status (OR 0.554, 95% CI 0.321 to 0.938, p=0.030) were the strongest predictors. No significant predictors were identified for T790M mutations in the multivariate analysis.

Associations of other driver mutations with patient characteristics and pre-existing conditions

Univariate analysis revealed significant correlations between various driver mutations and patient characteristics or pre-existing conditions. ALK mutations (online supplemental table 2) were strongly associated with younger age (under 50 years), advanced-stage tumours (III–IV) and adenocarcinoma histology. ROS1 rearrangements were more frequent among never-smokers and those with advanced-stage tumours. RAS mutations were linked to male gender, age over 50, smoking history, adenocarcinoma histology, blood type A and histories of hypertension, diabetes and coronary heart disease. BRAF mutations were associated with advanced-stage tumours and Rh antigen negativity. The only significant predictor for PIK3CA mutations was non-adenocarcinoma histology (online supplemental table 3). MET mutations were correlated with advanced-stage tumours, and blood type A showed borderline significance compared with blood type O (37.7% vs 25.7%, p=0.054). HER2 mutations were linked to younger age, never-smoking status, adenocarcinoma histology and the absence of hypertension. In contrast, RET mutations showed no significant associations with patient characteristics or pre-existing conditions.

Multivariate analysis further refined these findings. RAS mutations (figure 3) were significantly associated with male gender (OR 0.239, 95% CI 0.150 to 0.372, p<0.001), age over 50 (OR 3.980, 95% CI 2.171 to 8.183, p<0.001), smoking history (OR 1.452, 95% CI 1.023 to 2.079, p=0.039) and adenocarcinoma histology (OR 3.275, 95% CI 1.997 to 5.715, p<0.001). For ALK mutations, only younger age (under 50 years, OR 0.480, 95% CI 0.272 to 0.863, p=0.012) remained significant in the multivariate model. ROS1 fusions were strongly associated with advanced-stage tumours (III–IV, OR 4.317, 95% CI 1.739 to 11.331, p=0.002) and a family history of lung cancer (OR 7.499, 95% CI 1.094 to 30.940, p=0.013). BRAF mutations were significantly linked to advanced-stage tumours (III–IV, OR 7.106, 95% CI 1.354 to 60.658, p=0.033) and RhD antigen negativity (OR 0.010, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.252, p=0.001). No significant predictors were identified for PIK3CA, MET, HER2 or RET mutations (figure 3).

Associations of PD-L1 expressions with patient characteristics and pre-existing conditions

Univariate analysis revealed significant associations between PD-L1 positivity in tumour cells and several patient characteristics (online supplemental table 4). These included male gender, smoking status, advanced-stage tumours (stages III–IV), non-adenocarcinoma histology and a personal history of tumours. Blood type B also showed borderline significance compared with blood type O (41.3% vs 38.1%, p=0.054). No significant correlations were found with other demographics or medical histories, such as different blood types or a family history of cancer.

In multivariate analysis, independent factors associated with PD-L1 positivity in tumour cells included smoking (OR 1.517, 95% CI 1.088 to 2.119, p=0.014), advanced-stage tumours (III–IV, OR 1.690, 95% CI 1.256 to 2.269, p=0.001) and non-adenocarcinoma histology (OR 0.269, 95% CI 0.188 to 0.382, p<0.001) (figure 4).

For PD-L1 expression in tumour-infiltrating immune cells, significant associations were found with non-adenocarcinoma histology and a history of tuberculosis. These associations were confirmed in the multivariate analysis, where non-adenocarcinoma histology (OR 0.405, 95% CI 0.220 to 0.698, p=0.002) and a history of tuberculosis (OR 3.597, 95% CI 1.295 to 14.957, p=0.034) were significantly linked to PD-L1 expression in immune cells.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore differences between lung cancer subtypes, using both univariate and multivariate logistic regression. These analyses separately examined adenocarcinoma and non-adenocarcinoma groups, with detailed results provided in online supplemental tables 5–14.

Discussion

Key phenotypic indicators such as sex, smoking status and tumour histology are strongly associated with driver mutations in NSCLC. Our study, consistent with previous research, confirms that EGFR mutations are more common in women, non-smokers and patients with adenocarcinoma, while RAS mutations are more prevalent in men, smokers and also linked to adenocarcinoma.26 27 Despite some differences among mutation subtypes, these patterns emphasise the importance of these factors in guiding treatment decisions.

Age also significantly influences the distribution of mutations. Previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of ALK, ROS1, RET fusions and HER2 exon 20 insertions in younger patients, whereas EGFR L858R mutations are more frequent in older individuals.28 29 Our findings align with these observations, particularly noting ALK mutations in younger patients and EGFR L858R and RAS mutations in older individuals, highlighting the need for age-specific treatment strategies in NSCLC.

Furthermore, our study confirms the association between ROS1 fusions and advanced-stage disease, consistent with earlier research.5 30 However, a study from China found no significant correlation, likely due to the rarity of these mutations and differences in cohort demographics.31 Additionally, we observed that BRAF mutations are predominantly found in the later stages of NSCLC, suggesting their potential role in tumour progression, which warrants further investigation.

Beyond well-known patient characteristics, our large-scale study highlights the diverse mutation profiles associated with pre-existing conditions in NSCLC. Notably, ROS1 mutations were more prevalent in individuals with a family history of lung cancer, and EGFR L858R mutations were significantly linked to a family history of other cancers. These findings suggest complex interactions between mutation types and patient characteristics. For instance, EGFR exon 19 deletions were associated with diabetes and hepatitis B positivity, while EGFR L858R mutations were linked to hypertension. Additionally, BRAF mutations were associated with Rh-negative blood types, suggesting the involvement of distinct genetic networks or independent biological processes that require further exploration.

Logistic regression analysis revealed that the initial correlations between RAS mutations and conditions such as hypertension, diabetes and coronary heart disease were not sustained in multivariate analysis. Moreover, we found minimal evidence of significant impacts of pre-existing conditions on other driver mutations, including EGFR T790M, rare EGFR mutations, ALK, PIK3CA, RET, MET and HER2.

Previous studies have noted a higher frequency of EGFR mutations among patients with a family history of cancer, particularly linking familial lung cancer to susceptibility genes like EGFR T790M.32 33 However, our analysis did not find a significant overall correlation between a family history of lung cancer and EGFR mutations. While the EGFR L858R mutation was significantly linked to a family history of other cancers, no substantial association was found for other subtypes, including T790M. Notably, primary T790M mutations, unlike those acquired after TKI resistance, are rare, occurring in less than 1% of lung cancer cases and about 2% of those with EGFR mutations.34 Individuals with inherited T790M mutations may have an increased risk of acquiring additional EGFR mutations, such as L858R or 19del, which aligns with our observation of T790M mutation frequency—1.34% (74/5507) across all NSCLC cases and 2.87% (74/2577) within activated EGFR mutations, predominantly alongside other EGFR variants. Only two cases had a documented family history of cancer, suggesting that the limited sample size and uneven distribution of EGFR subtypes might not accurately reflect the true relationship between T790M mutations and family cancer history. Similar to Oxnard et al’s findings on germline EGFR T790M mutations, this could be due to varying penetrance of these inherited mutations.35 Further studies with larger cohorts are essential to clarify the familial occurrence, penetrance and precise correlation with lung cancer susceptibility for T790M mutations.

Lung tumours with RAS mutations exhibit distinct metabolic traits. Previous research has shown that, both in vitro and in vivo, KRAS inhibition can induce insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake, while biguanides like metformin and phenformin can suppress tumour growth and angiogenesis.3639 However, despite initial correlations between RAS mutations and pre-existing conditions such as hypertension, diabetes and coronary heart disease, our multivariate analysis did not confirm these findings, which is in line with other studies.40 This highlights the complexity of the relationship between RAS mutations and comorbidities, underscoring the need for more advanced models to fully understand these interactions.

The association between pre-existing pulmonary diseases and lung cancer is well established, with molecular studies showing that patients with conditions like interstitial lung disease and COPD tend to have lower rates of EGFR and ALK mutations.141741 Our results are consistent with this, finding no significant association between these diseases and EGFR, KRAS or ALK mutations. Contrary to previous reports of higher PIK3CA mutation rates in COPD-related NSCLC,42 our data did not support this, suggesting that further research is needed to explore PIK3CA mutations in patients with pulmonary conditions.

Extensive research has also explored PD-L1 expression in NSCLC. Early studies downplayed its clinicopathological significance, but more recent meta-analyses have identified strong correlations with sex, smoking status, histology, differentiation and TNM stage.4345 Our study confirms these findings, particularly the association of PD-L1 expression in tumour cells with smoking, advanced tumour stages and non-adenocarcinoma histology. We also observed higher PD-L1 expression in tumour cells among male patients. Additionally, PD-L1 expression in immune cells was significantly associated with non-adenocarcinoma histology and a history of tuberculosis, reflecting the complex nature of immune responses in NSCLC. Variations in study populations and the variables analysed, such as adenocarcinoma prevalence, should be considered when interpreting these findings.

This study offers one of the most comprehensive analyses of gene mutations and PD-L1 expression in NSCLC patients, examining the impact of clinical characteristics and pre-existing conditions. The results provide important insights that can enhance personalised treatment strategies for these patients. However, there are several limitations to consider. While comorbidities were documented based on clinical diagnoses, reducing the likelihood of under-reporting or misclassification, the retrospective nature of the study may still affect the accuracy of the data. Reliance on existing medical records can lead to incomplete or inconsistent information. Additionally, PD-L1 staining was only conducted on a portion of the samples (1839 out of 5507), and the relatively low prevalence of some driver mutations may limit the broader applicability of our findings. Moreover, although PD-L1 expression was assessed using IHC, we were only able to distinguish between tumour and immune cells without identifying specific immune cell types. Future research should employ more advanced methods, such as flow cytometry or single-cell RNA sequencing, to better characterise immune cell subtypes and further investigate the connections between driver mutations, PD-L1 expression and patient characteristics.

In conclusion, this comprehensive, large-scale observational study reveals that the spectrum of driver mutations and PD-L1 expression in NSCLC patients is associated with pre-existing conditions. Specifically, ROS1 mutations strongly correlate with a family history of lung cancer. EGFR L858R mutations are notably linked to a history of other types of cancers and are more prevalent among patients with hypertension. Additionally, EGFR 19del mutations are clearly associated with diabetes and hepatitis B positivity. Independently, BRAF mutations correlate with Rh-negative blood types and elevated PD-L1 expression in immune cells is observed in patients with a history of tuberculosis.
