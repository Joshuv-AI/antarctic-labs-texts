The TPR family protein VPA1365 regulates biofilm matrix to promote biofilm formation in Vibrio parahaemolyticus
Authors: Wenliang Yin, Jiaxin Lu, Zhiming Pan, Xinan Jiao, Dan Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVibrio parahaemolyticus is a notorious foodborne opportunistic pathogen capable of sensing external environmental signals to regulate its survival and virulence. In recent years, this pathogen has been increasingly detected in freshwater foods, indicating a distribution shift from its original marine reservoirs. Biofilm formation plays a crucial role in its adaptation ability from high-salt to low-salt environments. Here, we showed that VPA1365, a TPR family regulator, significantly promotes biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus. The Δvpa1365 mutant exhibited impaired biofilm formation under either low-salt (0.1 M NaCl) or high-salt (0.5 M NaCl) condition compared to that of the wild-type (WT) strain. The deletion of vpa1365 did not alter the flagella-mediated motility, however, it significantly reduced the metabolic activity of biofilm cells and production of key biofilm matrix components (exopolysaccharides, extracellular DNA, and extracellular proteins). Besides, the Δvpa1365 mutant exhibited lower expression levels of biofilm-related genes than that of the WT strain. All observed phenotypes were largely restored to WT levels in the complemented strain Δvpa1365-vpa1365. Therefore, our findings identify VPA1365 as a key regulator that enhances bacterial fitness via the positive regulation of biofilm formation. These insights deeply advance our comprehension of its environmental survival mechanisms and lay the groundwork for interventions to inhibit biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05045-0.Keywords: Vibrio parahaemolyticus, VPA1365, NaCl, Biofilm formation

Introduction

Vibrio parahaemolyticus, a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen widely distributed in fluvial and estuarine ecosystems, is the leading cause of foodborne gastroenteritis and diarrhea in humans [1, 2]. The pathogen was first isolated following a large food poisoning outbreak in Osaka, Japan, in 1950 [3]. It has since been detected in diverse seafood and marinated products, especially in coastal areas [4]. Notably, many studies have reported that it can be frequently isolated from freshwater foods such as crayfish and fish [5, 6]. Recent research indicates that the isolation rate from freshwater fish (42%) is approaching that from marine fish (58%), pointing to the potential for the pathogen to persist in freshwater aquaculture environments [7–9]. It is evident that salinity serves as the key distinguishing factor between marine and freshwater habitats. Similarly, the bacterium overcome osmotic stress within the host for colonization, a physiological challenge analogous to low-salt stress [10]. Under low-salt conditions, Vibrio species often employ multiple adaptive strategies, including biofilm formation to mitigate osmotic fluctuations, activation of efflux pump systems to expel compatible solutes, and adjustment of morphology to reduce membrane tension [11, 12]. Our previous study also revealed a high prevalence of V. parahaemolyticus in freshwater foods in Jiangsu Province, China [13]. Multilocus sequence typing analysis (MLST) further indicated that many freshwater isolates were phylogenetically closer to human-derived strains, and some isolates exhibited stronger biofilm formation, although their overall biofilm-forming capacity remained lower than that of marine and clinical strains. These findings imply that enhanced biofilm formation may be a pivotal determinant for the survival and proliferation of V. parahaemolyticus under dynamic environmental conditions or during host infection.

Biofilms are major contributors to chronic contamination and pathogen persistence in food processing settings, and pose a serious challenge to clinical treatment [14]. Structurally, biofilms are complex microbial communities in which surface-adherent bacteria embed themselves within a self-produced extracellular matrix [15]. This group behavior serves as a key survival strategy for bacterial persistence under hostile conditions and for stable colonization within host tissues [16]. In V. parahaemolyticus, the biofilm development often relies on the motility mediated by flagellar or pili, and controlled regulatory circuits [17]. Following successful adhesion, bacteria secrete abundant extracellular polymeric substances, including extracellular polysaccharides (EPS), proteins, and eDNA, which consolidate the biofilm architecture and enhance its resilience to environmental fluctuations [17–19]. Notably, in V. parahaemolyticus, EPS biosynthesis is tightly governed by cpsA-K and scvA-O gene clusters [20]. The production of EPS directly determines the mechanical stability and structural integrity of biofilms [21]. Such changes in matrix components often significantly compromise the biofilm development, thereby affecting bacterial fitness in different environments.

In any case, environmental stress remains a primary trigger for biofilm formation in bacteria. In V. parahaemolyticus, sublethal doses of chloramphenicol suppress multiple physiological processes including growth rate, biofilm formation, motility, and cytotoxicity [22]. Low concentrations of ethanol enhance the motility and biofilm formation of V. parahaemolyticus [23]. Environmental magnesium availability also broadly influences motility, biofilm development, and virulence in this pathogen [24]. Our previous study showed that low salinity (0.5% NaCl) alters gene expression linked to bacterial fitness and the type III secretion system-mediated cytotoxicity [25]. It is widely recognized that biofilm formation is controlled by many transcription factors that respond to various signals [26, 27]. For instance, the EnvZ/OmpR system in V. parahaemolyticus serves as a ferric iron sensor upregulating biofilm formation to enhance intestinal colonization [28]. Similarly, the TetR-family regulator AphA senses autoinducers, directly induces transcription of the c-di-GMP-related gene eapA, and thereby promotes biofilm assembly at low cell density [29]. We previously identified VPA1365 as a sensor of low-osmolarity signals that regulates virulence of V. parahaemolyticus, including biofilm formation [30]. Notably, VPA1365 belongs to the tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) protein family, whose members are involved in various physiological processes and virulence regulation [31]. In V. vulnificus, a bicistronic operon rcbAB encoding TPR domain-containing proteins mediates calcium-induced biofilm development [32], suggesting TPR family proteins may play conserved roles in biofilm regulation.

In this study, we characterized VPA1365 as a positive regulator of biofilm formation under both high NaCl and low NaCl conditions, based on analyses of biofilm cell viability and matrix components (eDNA, proteins and EPS). Expression levels of biofilm-related genes were further assessed in Δvpa1365 mutant. Collectively, these findings expanded the functional characterization of VPA1365 and provided deeper insights into the regulatory network of biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The Vibrio parahaemolyticus RIMD2210633 and its derivative mutants used in this study were cultured at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium. The ∆vpa1365 in-frame deletion mutant and complemented strain ∆vpa1365-vpa1365 were generated by Yin et al. [30]. We supplemented the medium with 100 µg/mL carbenicillin (Carb) and 24 µg/mL chloramphenicol (Cm) when needed. For the complementation assay, we induced the expression of VPA1365 in the complemented Δvpa1365-vpa1365 strain with 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Biofilm-related phenotypes were assessed in WT, ∆vpa1365, and ∆vpa1365-vpa1365 strains using LB medium with 0.1 M NaCl (low-salt condition) or 0.5 M NaCl (high-salt condition).

Growth curve and colony counting analysis

For growth curve analysis, overnight cultures of V. parahaemolyticus and derivative strains were normalized to an OD600 of 1.0 and then diluted 1:100 in LB medium containing 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl. After thorough vortexing, 200 µL aliquots of each culture were transferred into a 96-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 180 rpm in a microplate reader (Epoch 2, BioTek), and the OD600 was measured automatically at 1-hour intervals to monitor bacterial growth. For colony counting analysis, bacterial counts were determined by collecting 100µL samples, performing 10-fold serial dilutions, and plating for colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration. The total number of colonies was calculated and expressed as CFU/mL.

Swimming and swarming motility assay

Overnight cultures of the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL. Furthermore, 10 µL aliquots of each suspension were spotted onto LB plates with 0.3% agar (for swimming motility) and LB plates with 1.5% agar (for swarming motility), each supplemented with 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. The diameters of the migration circles were measured to determine motility.

Biofilm formation and colony morphology assay

Overnight cultures of the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 strains were adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0, and then diluted 1:100 into 200 µL of fresh LB medium containing 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl in a 96-well polystyrene microplate. These microplates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 6, 18, and 31 h. After incubation, non-adherent cells were discarded, and the surface-adhered biofilms were subjected to two PBS washes. The biofilms were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (200 µL) for 30 min. Following staining, the biofilm was solubilized by adding 220 µL of 95% ethanol and incubating for 1 h. The absorbance at 570 nm (OD₅₇₀) was measured using a microplate reader (Epoch 2, BioTek) to quantify biofilm formation. Colony morphology was assessed as previously described [32]. Briefly, overnight cultures of the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 strains were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL. Afterwards, 10 µL aliquots of each suspension were spotted onto LB agar plates containing 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 34 h prior to examination.

Measurement of biofilm matrix components

Biofilm matrix components, including extracellular proteins, eDNA, and EPS, were quantified using the established methods [33]. For component analysis, biofilms were mechanically harvested from 6-well plates, washed twice with pre-chilled PBS, and resuspended in pre-chilled 0.01 M KCl with sonication. Afterwards, bacterial counts were determined by collecting 100 µL samples, performing 10-fold serial dilutions, and plating for CFU enumeration. The total number of colonies was calculated. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 6 min) and filtration with 0.45 μm membrane filter, the remaining supernatant was collected and used to determine protein (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit) and eDNA (M5 PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit) concentrations. For EPS quantification, the samples were analyzed by the established phenol-sulfuric acid method [34], with absorbance read at 490 nm (OD490). Results were expressed after normalization to colony counts.

Assessment of metabolic activity in biofilms

To evaluate metabolic activity of biofilm cells from each group, the 2,3-bis(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)-2 H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide (XTT) reduction assay was performed as described [35]. In short, overnight cultures of the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 were adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0, diluted 1:100 in 200 µL of fresh LB medium containing 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl, and allowed to form biofilms statically in a 96-well polystyrene plate at 37 °C for 31 h. Following removal of planktonic cells, biofilms were mechanically harvested, washed twice with PBS and resuspended in PBS. Afterwards, bacterial counts were determined by collecting 100 µL samples, performing 10-fold serial dilutions, and plating for CFU enumeration. Other wells were washed twice with PBS and air-dried. A reaction mixture containing 100 µL of sterile PBS, 100 µL of XTT (1.0 mg/mL), and 8 µL of menadione was added to each well, which was then incubated in the dark at 37 °C for at least 30 min. Metabolic activity was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm (OD490) using a microplate reader. Results were expressed after normalization to colony counts.

Quantitative Real-Time Reverse Transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed as previously described [36], with all primers listed in Table 1. Briefly, overnight cultures of the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 were grown in LB medium containing 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl to stationary phase. The total RNA was extracted using a Bacterial RNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), and the RNA quality of each sample was verified on a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (IMPLEN NP80, Germany). After normalization, RNA samples were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III All-in-One RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) under the following conditions: 50 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 15 s. qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master MiX. The following target genes were analyzed: fliE and lafA (flagellar proteins), scvA, mfpA, and scrA (biofilm regulatory proteins), and cpsE (capsular polysaccharide protein). The gyrB gene was used as an internal reference, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [37]. All experiments were performed in triplicate using three independent biological samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistics were analyzed with GraphPad Prism version 8.0 and the results were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD; n = 3). Differences between groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post-test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. The p values ≤ 0.05 and ≤ 0.01 were considered significant (*) and extremely significant (**), respectively. The p value > 0.05 was regarded as non-significant (ns).

Results

Deletion of vpa1365 does not affect the growth of V. parahaemolyticus

To determine whether the deletion of vpa1365 influences the growth rate of Vibrio parahaemolyticus, we measured the growth curves of the wild-type (WT), Δvpa1365, and complemented strain (Δvpa1365-vpa1365) in LB medium supplemented with 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl. As shown in Fig. 1A, the Δvpa1365 displayed growth comparable to the WT and complemented strain under shaking conditions, with all reaching the growth stationary phase by almost 7 h (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 1.2). Interestingly, growth in high-salt medium (Fig. 1B) was characterized by both an increased rate and a higher stationary-phase density (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 1.5) relative to growth in low-salt medium. Under static growth conditions, all tested strains exhibited lower growth rates than those observed under shaking conditions (Fig. 1C and D). However, the deletion of vpa1365 had no observable effect on bacterial growth under 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl conditions. Besides, we performed colony counting for all strains to compensate for the limitation of measuring OD600 using a plate reader. As shown in Fig. S1, no significant differences in colony numbers were observed among the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 under 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl conditions. Similarly, the bacterial growth rate under shaking conditions at 6 h was approximately twice that observed under static conditions. Colony counts were consistently higher under 0.5 M NaCl than 0.1 M NaCl conditions, regardless of static or shaking culture conditions. These findings demonstrate that V. parahaemolyticus is capable of growth in low-salt or high-salt environments and that vpa1365 is dispensable for growth under these conditions.

The growth curves of WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 under shaking (180 rpm) (A, B) and static culture conditions (C, D) in LB medium with 0.1 M NaCl or 0.5 M NaCl at 37 °C. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3) from three independent experiments

Deletion of vpa1365 reduces the biofilm formation of V. parahaemolyticus

Our previous study has shown that VPA1365 is involved in the regulation of biofilm formation [30]. In this study, to investigate the role of VPA1365 in biofilm formation of V. parahaemolyticus under different NaCl concentrations, we first examined a common biofilm phenotype (colony morphology). As shown in Fig. 2A, both the WT and the complemented strain Δvpa1365-vpa1365 formed rugose colonies on LB agar plates with low NaCl, whereas the Δvpa1365 mutant exhibited smooth colonies. Under high-salt conditions, the WT, Δvpa1365 and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 strains formed smooth rather than rugose colonies (Fig. 2B). To further confirm the positive role of vpa1365 in biofilm formation, we additionally constructed a vpa1365-overexpressing strain (WT-vpa1365) and found that overexpression of vpa1365 promoted rugose colony formation under low-salt conditions but had no significant effect on colony morphology under high-salt conditions (Fig. 2). These results indicate that VPA1365 positively regulates the rugose colony formation in V. parahaemolyticus specially under low-salt conditions.

Under 0.1 M NaCl conditions, the WT, Δvpa1365-vpa1365, and WT-vpa1365 strains exhibited rugose colonies, while others showed smooth colonies. Representative images from three independent experiments are shown. Scale bars: 0.5 cm

To comprehensively validate the contribution of vpa1365 to biofilm formation, we evaluated biofilm biomass at different time points using crystal violet staining. As presented in Fig. 3A, the WT strain showed a time-dependent increase in biofilm formation under low-salt conditions, with the OD₅₇₀ value increasing from 0.3 at 6 h to 0.7 at 31 h. However, the Δvpa1365 displayed significantly attenuated biofilm accumulation, as evidenced by the OD₅₇₀ value reaching only 0.27 at 31 h (Fig. 3A). Genetic complementation of VPA1365 in Δvpa1365 significantly restored biofilm formation to levels similar to those of the WT (Fig. 3A). Under high-salt conditions, the WT and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 showed an early peak in biofilm biomass at 6 h (OD570 ≈ 2.5), followed by a gradual decline over time (Fig. 3B). However, the Δvpa1365 sustained a markedly lower and steady level of biofilm (OD570 ≈ 0.3 ~ 0.8) compared with those of WT and Δvpa1365-vpa1365. Together, these results indicate that VPA1365 is required for biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus and contributes to biofilm formation in different salinity environments.

Lacking of vpa1365 reduces the metabolic activity of biofilm cells

To further characterize the physiological state of biofilm cells, the metabolic activity of biofilm cells was assessed using an XTT assay. Under low-salt conditions, the Δvpa1365 showed a significant decrease in metabolic activity in biofilm cells compared to the WT, and the Δvpa1365-vpa1365 partially restored this activity (Fig. 4A). Under high-salt conditions, a similar trend was observed, with overall metabolic activity levels comparable to those measured under low-salt conditions (Fig. 4B). Quantitatively, the metabolic activity of biofilm cells in the Δvpa1365 was reduced by 34% and 41% relative to the WT under 0.1 M NaCl and 0.5 M NaCl, respectively. These results demonstrate that deletion of vpa1365 not only impairs biofilm formation but also affects the metabolic activity of biofilm-embedded cells.

Lacking of vpa1365 has no effect on motility of V. parahaemolyticus

Given that bacterial motility contributes to initial surface attachment and subsequent biofilm development, we assessed whether VPA1365 influences biofilm formation by affecting the swimming and swarming motility under 0.1 M NaCl and 0.5 M NaCl conditions. As shown in Fig. 5, V. parahaemolyticus exhibited enhanced swimming and swarming motility under high-salt conditions compared with low-salt conditions, which may partially account for its enhanced biofilm formation under high salinity. Nevertheless, the Δvpa1365 mutant exhibited similar swimming and swarming capacities to those of the WT and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 on both LB plates with 0.3% and 1.5% agar, indicating that the deletion of vpa1365 did not impact bacterial motility (Fig. 5A and B). The motility-deficient ΔrpoN mutant strain was used as a negative control. Under all tested culture conditions, the ΔrpoN exhibited impaired swimming and swarming abilities compared to the WT, Δvpa1365 and Δvpa1365-vpa1365, as evidenced by relatively smaller motility rings (Fig. 5). These results demonstrate that the regulation of biofilm formation by VPA1365 is independent of flagella-mediated motility.

Disruption of vpa1365 reduces the contents of the biofilm matrix

Given that VPA1365 does not affect bacterial flagella-mediated motility, we next investigated whether it regulates biofilm formation by modulating key biofilm matrix components like EPS, extracellular proteins, and eDNA, which collectively contribute to biofilm formation. As depicted in Fig. 6A, the level of eDNA in the Δvpa1365 was significantly reduced compared with that of the WT under low-salt conditions, and this defect was largely restored in the complemented strain Δvpa1365-vpa1365. Similarly, extracellular protein levels in the Δvpa1365 were significantly reduced to approximately 80% of those observed in the WT and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 (Fig. 6B). Moreover, deletion of vpa1365 led to a significant decrease in EPS production, the primary structural component of the biofilm, to approximately 49% of the WT level, whereas complementation of vpa1365 partially restored EPS production (Fig. 6C). Under 0.5 M NaCl condition, the Δvpa1365 mutant similarly exhibited significantly reduced levels of eDNA, extracellular proteins and EPS relative to those of the WT, and the complemented strain Δvpa1365-vpa1365 restored levels of these components in the biofilm (Fig. 6). Notably, the levels of eDNA and extracellular protein were 1.5-fold and 2-fold higher, respectively, under high-salt conditions than those under low-salt conditions. However, under high-salt conditions, the EPS content in bacteria was 4-fold higher than that under low-salt conditions, which supports a greater potential for biofilm formation. This result is consistent with the higher biofilm formation under high-salt conditions than under low-salt conditions, as detected by crystal violet staining at 6 h (Fig. 3). Collectively, these results demonstrate that VPA1365 positively regulates biofilm formation by promoting the production of essential biofilm matrix constituents.

VPA1365 regulates the expression of biofilm formation-related genes

To further define the regulatory role of VPA1365 in biofilm formation, the expression levels of several biofilm-associated genes were analyzed. A previous study indicated that VPA1365 promotes the pili-mediated adhesion ability by directly binding to the promoters of pilA and mshA to positively regulate their expression [30]. Consistent with the unchanged swimming and swarming motility phenotypes, no significant differences were observed in the expression of the flagellar genes fliE and lafA among the WT, Δvpa1365, and Δvpa1365-vpa1365 under low-salt conditions (Fig. 7A). In contrast, genes involved in EPS biosynthesis, including cpsE, scvA, and scrA, were significantly downregulated in Δvpa1365, showing an approximate 2-fold decrease compared to those of WT, and their expression was restored in Δvpa1365-vpa1365 (Fig. 7A). Moreover, the expression of mfpA, a gene associated with DNA metabolism, was markedly lower in the Δvpa1365 than that in the WT and Δvpa1365-vpa1365. A similar expression pattern was observed under high-salt conditions, with biofilm matrix-related genes remaining significantly downregulated in the Δvpa1365, despite unchanged expression of flagellar-related genes (Fig. 7B). Altogether, these findings indicate that VPA1365 positively regulates the expression of genes critical for extracellular protein, EPS, and eDNA accumulation, thereby contributing to biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus.

Discussion

The TPR family proteins contain tandem repeat motifs to form a distinctive “horseshoe-shaped” or “propeller-like” structure. This enables specific protein-protein or protein-DNA interactions and facilitates their participation in diverse cellular processes, including gene expression, protein degradation, and cell cycle regulation [38–40]. Our previous work identified a TPR family protein, VPA1365, located within the type III secretion system 2 (T3SS2) gene cluster of V. parahaemolyticus, which directly binds to the promoters of virulence-related genes and positively regulates the function of T3SS2 [30]. The lack of vpa1365 significantly impaired the expression and secretion of the T3SS2 translocon protein VopD2 under low-salt conditions [30]. In this study, we reveal that lack of vpa1365 led to a significant biofilm defect under both low-salt and high-salt liquid medium conditions (Fig. 3). VPA1365 enhances biofilm formation primarily by coordinately upregulating the expression of many genes involved in biofilm matrix components, including eDNA, extracellular proteins and EPS (Figs. 6 and 7). Given its established role in T3SS2-mediated virulence and the high prevalence of T3SS2-related genes among many clinical isolates [30, 41], we propose that VPA1365 may play a dual role in coordinating virulence and biofilm-mediated environmental adaptation.

Biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus is governed by an integrated regulatory network, including environmental stress responses, EPS biosynthesis, pilus-mediated adhesion, and motility [42, 43]. Flagellum-driven swimming and swarming motility enhance the initial attachment of bacterial cells to surfaces and the biofilm dispersion [43, 44]. However, deletion of vpa1365 affected neither motility nor the expression of flagellar genes (e.g., fliE and lafA), indicating that the biofilm defects observed in the Δvpa1365 mutant are independent of flagellar-mediated motility. Given the critical role of pili in bacterial adhesion and biofilm initiation [45], together with the evidence that TPR-containing proteins participate in type IV pilus assembly of Francisella [46], we hypothesized that the TPR family protein VPA1365 promotes biofilm formation by influencing pilus-mediated processes. Our previous study revealed that the TPR family protein VPA1365 directly binds to the promoters of pilus-associated genes such as pilA and mshA, suggesting it promotes early-stage biofilm formation through pili-mediated initial adhesion [30]. Furthermore, bacterial subfamilies of secretion systems such as T3SS, also contribute to the assembly of pilus structures [47]. Our previous observation that deletion of vpa1365 abrogates T3SS function indirectly supports a possible role for VPA1365 in pilus-related processes. The present study showed that the Δvpa1365 mutant exhibited a marked reduction in early biofilm formation at 6 h compared to the WT under both at low-salt and high-salt conditions (Fig. 3). Therefore, we propose VPA1365 might promote early biofilm development by enhancing the pilus-dependent adhesion.

Following successful surface attachment, bacteria produce a protective extracellular matrix comprising EPS, eDNA, and proteins to form biofilm [48]. In this study, we systematically demonstrate that VPA1365 positively regulates the production of these key matrix components. EPS, which account for 50%-90% of the biofilm matrix, are the primary architectural component conferring biofilm integrity [49]. We found that VPA1365 is essential for the expression of many EPS biosynthesis genes, similar to the LysR-family regulator AcsS, which activates cpsA [50]. The downregulation of these genes in the Δvpa1365 correlated with reduced EPS production. In addition to the downregulation of EPS-related genes, we also observed a decreased expression of mfpA, which is associated with eDNA, in the Δvpa1365 relative to the WT (Fig. 7). Therefore, the impaired production of key matrix components provides a direct mechanistic explanation for the defective biofilm formation in the Δvpa1365. Besides, the impairment of critical virulence determinants such as the T3SS or type VI secretion system (T6SS) also leads to substantial defects in biofilm formation [51, 52]. As deletion of vpa1365 impairs T3SS2 function, it may indirectly reduce biofilm formation by disrupting the secretion of biofilm-promoting factors.

Under liquid culture conditions, bacteria primarily adhere at the air-liquid interface of the culture dish, where EPS facilitate bacterial adhesion and thereby promote biofilm formation. We found that the WT produced significantly higher levels of EPS and other extracellular matrix components under high-salt conditions than those under low-salt conditions, consistent with the increased biofilm formation detected by crystal violet staining at 6 h. In contrast, on solid media, the WT formed rugose colonies under low-salt conditions but smooth colonies under high-salt conditions. This discrepancy likely arises because the transition between rugose and smooth colonies is influenced not only by the abundance of EPS but also by the interplay of mechanical forces and swelling pressure [17]. Under low-salt conditions, which impose osmotic stress, V. parahaemolyticus often produce abundant EPS and form rugose colonies that enhance structural stability and adaptation ability. Under high-salt conditions, the native environment for V. parahaemolyticus, bacteria may not need to produce large amounts of EPS for rugose colony formation. Instead, they likely utilize other regulatory mechanisms (e.g., surfactants, oxygen sensing, or quorum sensing) to maintain a smooth colony that supports rapid expansion and dispersal [53]. A similar pattern was observed for V. parahaemolyticus on Heart Infusion plates: colonies are rugose at 0.5% NaCl but smooth at 4.0% NaCl [54]. Consistently, overexpression of vpa1365 (WT-vpa1365) promoted rugose colony formation under low-salt conditions, but had no significant effect on colony morphology under high-salt conditions, further supporting its role in biofilm formation. Collectively, these findings suggest that VPA1365 orchestrates biofilm matrix production through a comprehensive regulatory network, providing a mechanistic explanation for the biofilm defects in the Δvpa1365 mutant.

Recent studies highlight the risk of V. parahaemolyticus cross-contamination across aquatic products [55], and biofilm formation often constitutes a critical strategy for its survival and pathogenicity [56]. Lowering salinity from 3% to 0.25% significantly enhances bacterial biofilm formation [11], which strongly suggests the importance of biofilm formation in bacterial adaptation to low-salinity conditions. Our previous transcriptomic analysis showed upregulation of biofilm-associated genes in M9G medium with 0.5% NaCl [25]. Although vpa1365 expression was not significantly altered in the prior RNA-seq data [25], the Δvpa1365 exhibits significantly reduced biofilm formation compared to the WT and complementation strain (Fig. 3). These findings imply that VPA1365 is a previously overlooked but important positive regulator of biofilm development in V. parahaemolyticus. Besides, when bacteria are cultured under low-salt or high-salt conditions, they may require several hours to activate osmotic compensatory mechanisms (e.g., synthesis of compatible solutes) before entering exponential growth, which may result in a diauxic-like growth pattern (Fig. 1A). Similarly, V. parahaemolyticus exhibited a significant prolongation of the lag phase under 0.25% NaCl conditions [11], and RNA-seq analysis revealed that the bacterium adapts to the low-salt environment by regulating cellular metabolism and enhancing oxidative stress resistance [11]. Interestingly, our results show that biofilm formation of the WT gradually increases over time under low-salt conditions but decreases under high-salt conditions (Fig. 3). This trend is consistent with previous reports describing similar patterns among four different V. parahaemolyticus genotypes under 0.25% and 3% salinity [11]. These observations suggest that V. parahaemolyticus might adopt distinct biofilm strategies in response to environmental salinity. Under low-salt conditions, the time-dependent accumulation of biofilm indicates that V. parahaemolyticus might require a gradual adaptation to osmotic stress (Fig. 3A), with biofilm formation functioning as a key protective mechanism. Under high-salt conditions, which are closer to the native marine habitat, the bacteria quickly formed biofilms during the early phase to adapt to the environment (Fig. 3B). Following this initial adaptation phase, biofilm levels gradually decrease, promoting dispersal and planktonic growth. In this context, biofilms likely function as transient structures that support early-stage survival and adaptation of V. parahaemolyticus. Nevertheless, the absence of vpa1365 results in lower biofilm formation in V. parahaemolyticus, highlighting its potential as a target for controlling biofilm formation under both low-salt and high-salt conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies VPA1365 as a key positive regulator of biofilm formation by promoting the production of essential matrix components, including EPS, eDNA, and extracellular protein (Fig. 8). Moreover, the dynamic changes in biofilm formation under different salinities suggest a conditional survival strategy, with sustained biofilms forming under low-salt conditions for protection and transient biofilms under high-salt conditions to facilitate dispersal, in both of which VPA1365 contributes to biofilm regulation. Collectively, these findings expand our insights into the biofilm regulatory network and establish VPA1365 as a potential target for controlling biofilm-mediated persistence and pathogenicity in V. parahaemolyticus.
