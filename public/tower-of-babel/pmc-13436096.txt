Glenoid reconstruction with screw-type implant versus iliac crest bone grafting for anterior shoulder instability: study protocol for a prospective, multicentre, non-inferiority, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yuxuan Wang, Peng Pi, Junhao Feng, Wei Huang, Zhenglin Zhu, Hong Chen
Published: 2026

For patients with critical glenoid bone defects (≥15%), Bankart repair has limited effectiveness and bony augmentation surgery is often required. Introduction

Anterior shoulder instability is a common shoulder disease predominantly affecting young, active individuals. Bankart repair, with or without remplissage, represents the most frequently performed surgical intervention for this disorder. However, extensive evidence indicates an inverse relationship between glenoid defect size and shoulder stability, suggesting that a glenoid defect of 15% or more constitutes a critical bone loss beyond which soft tissue repair alone cannot restore normal glenohumeral translation.1–3 For patients with critical glenoid bone loss, bony augmentation procedures, including Latarjet, iliac crest bone graft (ICBG) and distal tibial allograft, are commonly employed.4 While the effectiveness of these techniques is well established,5 postoperative graft resorption is a frequent phenomenon that can lead to anterior shoulder instability.6

The patient-specific titanium implant has been developed to overcome the issue of graft resorption. Cadaveric studies have shown that such 3D-printed implants, which replicate the morphology of the contralateral healthy glenoid, are comparable to the bony augmentation procedure in restoring glenohumeral stability and kinematics.7–9 Recent clinical data also support the reliability and safety of such an implant over a 2-year follow-up period.10 Nevertheless, given the non-weight-bearing and low-congruency nature of the glenohumeral joint, the advantage of anatomical glenoid restoration offered by 3D-printed implants must be weighed against inherent limitations, including limited availability, high cost and lengthy manufacturing time. In fact, established bony augmentation techniques using the coracoid or free bone block yield satisfactory clinical outcomes, despite their inability to fully reproduce native glenoid geometry.11 12

The authors have developed a screw-type glenoid reconstruction implant to address the aforementioned issues. This implant features a cylindrical head available in incremental lengths (2 mm increments) to accommodate glenoid defects of various sizes. It is designed to be placed extracapsularly and flush with the bone surface, thereby compensating for the glenoid defect without attempting to fully restore the native glenoid anatomy (see figure 1).

This research protocol will investigate the effectiveness and safety of the screw-type glenoid reconstruction implant for the management of anterior shoulder instability patients with critical glenoid bone loss through a randomised controlled trial (RCT).

Research questions

In anterior shoulder instability patients with critical glenoid bone loss, does glenoid reconstruction with the screw-type implant yield comparable clinical outcomes to ICBG at 1 year postoperatively?

Methods and analysis

Study design

Multicentre, two-arm, non-inferiority RCT.

Outcome measures

Outcome measures will be collected at baseline (prerandomisation) and then at 3, 6 and 12 months.

Primary outcome

Rowe score at 1 year postoperatively: a 3-item physician-completed instrument, which has been designed to assess shoulder stability after Bankart repair.13 Its questions address the categories of shoulder stability, motion and function, with scores ranging from 0 to 100. A score of 90–100 points indicates an excellent evaluation, and 0–50 points indicates a poor evaluation.

Secondary outcomes

ASES score

The American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score is a patient-reported outcome measure designed to assess shoulder function and pain. It consists of two domains: a pain visual analogue scale (VAS) and a 10-item functional activities scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better shoulder function and less pain. It is widely used to evaluate outcomes in patients with various shoulder pathologies.14

SANE score

The Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) score is a patient-reported, global rating scale of shoulder function. Patients are asked to rate the overall function of their affected shoulder as a percentage of normal (0% to 100%), where 100% represents a completely normal shoulder. It provides a simple, quick and valid assessment of overall shoulder status.15

VAS score

The VAS for pain is a self-completed, unidimensional measure of pain intensity. It typically consists of a 10 cm horizontal line anchored by word descriptors at each end (eg, ‘no pain’ and ‘worst pain imaginable’). Patients mark on the line to indicate their current pain level, and the score is determined by measuring the distance in centimetres from the ‘no pain’ anchor. It provides a continuous scale for assessing subjective pain intensity.16

Resource use questionnaires

Primary health economic analysis will concentrate on direct intervention and healthcare/personal social services costs, while wider impact (societal) costs will be included within the sensitivity analyses.

EQ-5D-5L

Health-related quality of life measure consisting of five dimensions, each with 5 levels of response. Each combination of answers is converted into a health utility score. Gives a single preference-based index value for health status that can be used for broader cost-effectiveness comparative purposes.17

The shoulder redislocation rate

The shoulder redislocation rate is a primary, objective clinical outcome measure used to evaluate the success of surgical or non-surgical interventions for anterior shoulder instability. It is defined as the proportion or percentage of patients who experience a recurrent, clinically confirmed anterior shoulder instability of the glenohumeral joint following an initial treatment episode within a specified follow-up period. This measure directly assesses the restoration of structural stability and is a critical endpoint in trials comparing the effectiveness of different stabilisation techniques.

The return to sports rate

The return to sports rate is a functional outcome measure assessing an athlete’s ability to resume preinjury sports activities following shoulder stabilisation surgery. It is typically evaluated through patient-reported questionnaires or clinical interviews, documenting the time to return to sport, the level of sports participation regained (eg, same level, reduced level or unable to return), and the perceived stability and confidence in the affected shoulder during sports.

X-ray

Evaluate glenohumeral osteoarthritis using X-rays and assess according to the Samilson & Prieto grading system.18

Sample size

Based on the primary outcome measure, shoulder stability assessed using the Rowe score, the sample size was estimated. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the Rowe score is 9.7 points, with an estimated SD of 13.4 points.21 22 We set the non-inferiority margin at 6 points, corresponding to 60% of the MCID to ensure the clinical relevance of the non-inferiority test. Using a statistical power of 90% and a significance level of 5%, 84 patients per group are required to detect a statistically significant difference. Accounting for an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up, the final planned total sample size is 186 patients, with 93 patients allocated to each group.

If they are eligible and willing to join the study, they will sign an informed consent form (see online supplemental material), after which baseline demographic data, preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes will be collected.

Randomisation

RCT randomisation

After completing initial screening during hospital admission and providing informed consent, patients will be randomised to the two treatment groups via a centralised web-based system. Allocation concealment will be maintained by an independent randomisation team at Chongqing Medical University, which will be responsible for generating the randomisation sequence, with no involvement in participant allocation. Group allocation will be performed by computer using a minimisation algorithm incorporating a random element, to ensure baseline balance across groups. Stratification will be based on patient age, physical activity or sports level, and recruiting site.

Postrandomisation withdrawals

Participants may withdraw from the trial at any time without penalty. Unless a participant explicitly withdraws consent for continued participation, data collection will proceed until the trial concludes. All data collected prior to withdrawal will be retained in the analysis. Participants may decline trial-specific questionnaires while still providing routine clinical data. Withdrawn participants will not be replaced. The investigators or Trial Steering Committee may withdraw participants for safety reasons.

Interventions

Both surgical interventions—glenoid reconstruction with a screw-type implant and ICBG—will be performed under general anaesthesia. Patients will be positioned in the lateral decubitus position with the affected side up. The shoulder will be placed in 45° of abduction and 30° of forward flexion with 5 kg of traction under arm suspension. The affected shoulder and entire upper limb will be prepared and draped in a sterile fashion. A standard posterior arthroscopic portal will be established for visualisation to confirm the extent of the labral injury and glenoid bone defect.

In the screw-type implant group, a guidewire will be drilled from posterior to anterior under the guidance of a targeting device. After confirming its position, the bone tunnel will be reamed and its length measured. Through a cannula, two screw-type glenoid implants of appropriate size will be inserted via the anterior portal. Finally, standard labral and capsular repair and plication will be performed.

In the ICBG group, an incision will be made approximately 2 cm posterior to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). A tricortical iliac bone graft of appropriate size matching the glenoid defect will be harvested using an osteotome. The graft will be contoured, drilled, and passed with high-strength sutures pre-attached to a suspensory fixation device. A guidewire will be drilled from posterior to anterior under the guidance of a targeting device. After confirming its position and reaming the tunnel, the fixation device and bone graft will be introduced into the joint via the anterior portal. The graft position will be adjusted and fixed by tying the sutures. Finally, standard labral and capsular repair and plication will be performed, as described for the screw-type implant group.

All patients will undergo rehabilitation supervised by a physical therapist, and both groups will follow the same rehabilitation protocol:

Weeks 0–6: the operated shoulder will be immobilised with a sling. Participants will be instructed to perform passive range of motion (ROM) exercises and may additionally perform wrist and elbow flexion-extension exercises as well as isometric contraction training of the periscapular muscles.

Blinding

This study uses a partially blinded design: patients and operating surgeons will not be blinded to group allocation, whereas outcome assessors, imaging evaluators, physiotherapists and statisticians will remain blinded to treatment allocation for all study procedures and analyses.

Adverse event management

An adverse event (AE) is defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a trial participant, including any unfavourable and unintended sign, symptom or disease, which is temporally associated with the study intervention (screw-type implant or ICBG) and related to the index shoulder (the treated shoulder) or the study procedure.

Expected AEs associated with the injury and its surgical treatments include: surgical site infection, recurrent dislocation or instability, haematoma, neurovascular injury (including axillary nerve palsy), pain (including complex regional pain syndrome), delayed wound healing or wound dehiscence, intraoperative fracture, implant loosening or failure, and humeral bone loss. Additionally, AEs related to anaesthesia or the perioperative period, such as urinary retention, respiratory tract infection, venous thromboembolism and cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events, may also occur in this patient population.

Patients experiencing any of the above events will be closely monitored by the study team until their condition stabilises. If the condition remains unstable at the end of the scheduled follow-up period, the study team will continue to provide safety follow-up until improvement is achieved, while routine clinical care will be managed by the treating physicians.

All serious AEs (SAEs) must be reported to the principal investigator (PI) within 24 hours of occurrence. The PI will assess the causality and severity of the event, formulate a management plan and report the SAE to the local Ethics Committee and the trial sponsor within 24 hours of assessment. In the event of a participant death, the PI must submit a written report to the Ethics Committee within 7 days.

End of trial

The trial will end when all participants have completed their 12-month follow-up. The trial may be stopped prematurely if required by the Ethics Committee, following recommendations from the Data and Safety Monitoring Board, in the event of unexpected serious safety concerns, or if funding ceases.

Trial oversight

The Trial Management Group will meet monthly and report to the independent Trial Steering Committee (TSC) and Data Monitoring and Safety Committee (DMSC). The TSC and DMSC will hold annual meetings and will be responsible for protocol oversight, monitoring trial progress and supervising overall study conduct.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were consulted during the development of the trial protocol, and a dedicated patient and public involvement (PPI) reference group was established to represent the target population. Recruitment will be conducted via outpatient clinics. PPI representatives hold seats on the trial management team, have reviewed all study materials and will oversee trial progress throughout the study period. They will also contribute to result dissemination post-trial. Participants will receive no incentives beyond standard clinical care for their participation.

Analysis

RCT statistical analysis

All data will be processed using two-sided statistical tests in R software V.4.0.3, with a p value ≤0.05 defined as statistically significant. Following data collection, three analysis sets will be created: a full analysis set (FAS), a per-protocol set and a safety set.

Stringent quality control procedures will be implemented to ensure data consistency and reliability. All clinical recordings and outcome scoring at each participating centre will be performed by designated, fixed personnel. Imaging data will be acquired via each site’s Picture Archiving and Communication System and undergo centralised, blinded evaluation by a dedicated core research team member. All data will be prospectively entered into standardised electronic case report forms. The final statistical analysis will be performed uniformly by a designated statistician within the research team.

All primary analyses will be conducted following both the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle and per-protocol principles. The primary outcome (Rowe score at 12 months postrandomisation) will be compared between the two treatment groups using unadjusted and adjusted regression analyses. Adjusted analyses will account for stratification variables (age, activity level, recruiting site), baseline Rowe scores and other clinically important covariates. For non-inferiority inference, between-group differences will be estimated with 95% CIs; non-inferiority will be concluded if the upper bound of the 95% CI falls below the prespecified 4-point non-inferiority margin.

Descriptive statistics for functional outcomes (ASES score, SANE score, EQ-5D-5L) will be reported at each time point. Between-group comparisons for secondary outcomes will follow the same regression framework as the primary analysis. Categorical outcomes (eg, dislocation, complications) will be compared using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. For time-to-event outcomes (eg, recurrent dislocation), Kaplan-Meier curves will be constructed and Cox proportional hazards models will be used to compare event rates between groups if sufficient events are observed. Longitudinal recovery patterns across all follow-up time points will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models to account for temporal effects and repeated measures.

Missing data and treatment crossovers will be monitored throughout the trial. Multiple imputation will be used to handle missing data if appropriate, with all imputation assumptions explicitly reported. Sensitivity analyses using complete case analysis will be performed to validate imputation results. If a large number of crossovers are observed, complier-average causal effect (CACE) models will be used for supplementary analysis.

RCT health economic analysis

A trial-based cost-utility analysis will be conducted to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of screw-type glenoid reconstruction vs traditional autologous ICBG. Reporting will strictly adhere to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) guidelines.23

The primary analysis will adopt a healthcare system perspective, focusing on direct medical costs. A societal perspective will be included in sensitivity analyses. Health outcomes will be measured in QALYs derived from the EQ-5D-5L administered at baseline and all postoperative time points, with utility scores calculated using a Chinese population value set.24

Direct medical costs will be prospectively collected via a microcosting approach, including surgical consumables, hospitalisation, rehabilitation and complication management. Indirect costs (eg, productivity loss) for the societal perspective will be collected via patient questionnaires. In addition to the within-trial analysis over the 12-month follow-up period, a decision-analytic model will be developed to extrapolate costs and QALYs to a 10-year time horizon, using trial data and published long-term evidence. A 5% annual discount rate will be applied to costs and QALYs for the extrapolation. The methodological framework will follow NICE Technology Appraisal guidance and economic evaluations of similar orthopaedic procedures.25

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio will be calculated as (Cost_screw - Cost_ICBG)/(QALY_screw - QALY_ICBG). Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (using Monte Carlo simulation) will be performed, with results presented as a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve, showing the probability of the screw-type implant being cost-effective across a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds. Extensive one-way sensitivity analyses will also be conducted to test the robustness of findings.

All economic analyses will be based on the ITT population, consistent with the primary statistical analysis. Missing data will be handled per the prespecified statistical analysis plan. A full Health Economic Analysis Plan will be finalised and signed off before database lock.

Main strengths and limitations

This is a non-inferiority, multicentrer, randomised controlled study for the treatment of anterior shoulder instability with a new screw-type implant. The main limitations are that the intervention could not be blinded and that the follow-up period was short.
