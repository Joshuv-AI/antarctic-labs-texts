Fluctuation exponents of the open KPZ equation in the maximal current phase
Andres A. Contreras Hip, Sayan Das, Antonios Zitridis

Abstract
. We consider the open KPZ equation ℋ ⁡ ( x , t ) \mathcal{H}(x,t) on the interval [ 0 , L ] [0,L] with Neumann boundary conditions depending on parameters u , v ≥ 0 u,v\geq 0 (the so-called maximal current phase). For L ∼ t α L\sim t^{\alpha} and stationary initial conditions, we obtain matching upper and lower bounds on the variance of the height function ℋ ⁡ ( 0 , t ) \mathcal{H}(0,t) for α ∈ [ 0 , 2 3 ] \alpha\in[0,\frac{2}{3}] . Our proof combines techniques from [ DGK23 ] , which treated the periodic KPZ equation, with Gibbsian line ensemble methods based on the probabilistic structure of the stationary measures developed in [ CK24 , BLD22 , BKWW23 , BCY24 , Him24 ] .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The model and main results

The Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation is a singular stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{H}=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}\mathcal{H}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\mathcal{H})^{2}+\xi,

where
\xi
denotes 1+1 dimensional space-time white noise. Introduced in
[
KPZ86
]
as a model for stochastic interface growth, the KPZ equation has since become a central object of study in both mathematics and physics due to its deep connections with directed polymers in random environments, last passage percolation, interacting particle systems, and random matrix theory. We refer the reader to the surveys
[
Cor12
,
HHT15
,
QS15
,
Gan22
]
for comprehensive overviews of the KPZ equation and its universality class.

In this paper, we study the open KPZ equation, where (
1.1
) is restricted to a finite interval
x\in[0,L]
for
L>0
and is subject to Neumann boundary conditions

(1.2)

\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}(0,t)=u,\quad\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}(L,t)=-v,

for parameters
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
. To emphasize the dependence on the domain length, we denote the solution by
\mathcal{H}_{L}=\mathcal{H}
. The solution admits a natural interpretation as the log-partition function of the continuum directed random polymer (CDRP) in a strip, with attractive or repulsive interactions at the boundaries
x=0
and
x=L
.

Due to the nonlinearity and the singular nature of the noise, the equation with these boundary conditions is ill-posed in the classical sense. A standard way to make sense of this setup is via the Cole–Hopf transformation, defining
\mathcal{Z}_{L}:=e^{\mathcal{H}_{L}}
. Then
\mathcal{Z}_{L}
formally satisfies the stochastic heat equation (SHE)

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{Z}_{L}=\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{xx}\mathcal{Z}_{L}+\mathcal{Z}_{L}\xi,

with Robin boundary conditions

(1.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{Z}_{L}(0,t)=(u-\tfrac{1}{2})\mathcal{Z}_{L}(0,t),\qquad\partial_{x}\mathcal{Z}_{L}(L,t)=-(v-\tfrac{1}{2})\mathcal{Z}_{L}(L,t).

The inclusion of this 1/2 factor is just a convention that ensures that the point
u=v=0
is special in terms of the phase diagram for the open KPZ equation (see Figure
1
). It is known that this equation admits a mild solution that is unique, almost surely positive (for a certain class of initial data), and adapted to the natural filtration of the noise; see
[
CS18
,
Par19
]
. The solution to the open KPZ equation is then defined via
\mathcal{H}_{L}:=\log\mathcal{Z}_{L}
.

A fundamental question in the study of the open KPZ equation is to understand the nature of its stationary state. We say that a probability measure
\Lambda(\cdot)
on
C[0,L]
is stationary if the solution
\mathcal{H}_{L}
to (
1.1
) with boundary conditions (
1.2
) and initial condition
\Lambda
satisfies the invariance relation

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}_{L}(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{H}_{L}(0,t)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Lambda(\cdot)\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0.

Recently, there has been significant progress in characterizing these stationary measures
[
CK24
,
BLD23
,
BLD22
,
BKWW23
,
BCY24
,
Him24
]
(see also the review
[
Cor22
]
). It is now known that for every
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique stationary measure
\Lambda=\Lambda_{u,v}
, given in terms of a suitably reweighted Brownian motion (see Section
2.1
for precise description).

In this work, we focus on the fluctuation of the solution itself. Our first result provides an asymptotic formula for the variance.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix any
t,L>0
. Assume
u,v\geq 0
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{L}(0,t)
be the stationary solution of the open KPZ equation (
1.1
) on
[0,L]
with boundary data (
1.2
). There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}_{L}(0,t))}-\sqrt{t}\sigma_{L}\right|\leq C\sqrt{L}.

Here,
\sigma_{L}^{2}
is the asymptotic variance which has the following functional formula:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+2\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)}dx}{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)}dx\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)}dx}\right]

where
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}
are three independent copies of the stationary solution
\Lambda
.

We remark that the recent work
[
BLD25
]
expresses cumulants of the KPZ equation in
terms of a functional equation involving an integral operator. It would be interesting to match the formulas in
[
BLD25
]
with ours described above in (
1.5
).

As for the exact nature of the fluctuations, when the domain length
L
is fixed and
t\to\infty
, due to known ergodicity results (see, e.g.,
[
Par22
,
KM22
]
), we expect Gaussian fluctuations for the height function
\mathcal{H}_{L}(0,t)
. A more intriguing regime arises when both
L
and
t
go to infinity
jointly
. For concreteness, we take
L=\lambda t^{\alpha}
for
\lambda,\alpha>0
. Naturally, the fluctuation behavior in this regime depends sensitively on the boundary parameters
u,v
as they influence the large-scale geometry of the polymer paths and the structure of the stationary measure (Figure
1
).

When
u<0,v>u
(the
low-density phase
), or when
v<0,u>v
(the
high-density phase
), it is predicted that the polymer paths are localized near
x=0
or
x=L
, respectively. In the symmetric case
u=v<0
, localization occurs near either boundary with probability
1/2
. Because of this predicted localization near the boundaries, one expects the height function to exhibit Gaussian fluctuations of order
O(\sqrt{t})
regardless of the value of
\alpha
in these phases.

Figure 1
.
Phase diagram of the open KPZ equation.

In contrast, when
u,v\geq 0
(the so-called
maximal current phase
), the polymer paths are expected to be delocalized in the bulk. In this phase, the fluctuation exponents are predicted to depend nontrivially on the scaling parameter
\alpha
in this phase. The model is expected to interpolate between the Gaussian class
(\alpha=0)
and the KPZ class
(\alpha=\infty)
, where one recovers the familiar KPZ scaling exponents:
1/3
for height fluctuations and
2/3
for transversal fluctuations – the exponents observed in full-space and supercritical half-space KPZ settings
[
Wu23a
,
DS25
]
.

Our second result stated below captures the precise fluctuation exponent for
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda t^{\alpha}}(0,t)
when
\alpha\in(0,2/3]
in the maximal current phase. We achieve this by computing exact decay rate of the asymptotic variance
\sigma_{L}^{2}
defined in (
1.5
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix any
t\geq 1
,
\lambda>0
, and
\alpha\in(0,2/3)
. For each
L\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{H}_{L}
be the solution to the open KPZ equation (
1.1
) on
[0,L]
subjected to boundary conditions (
1.2
) started from stationary initial data. Assume
u,v\geq 0
. There exists constant
C(u,v)>1
such that for all
L\geq 1

C^{-1}L^{-1/2}\leq\sigma_{L}^{2}\leq CL^{-1/2}

where
\sigma_{L}^{2}
is the asymptotic variance defined in (
1.5
). Consequently, by Theorem
1.1
,
there exist constants
C_{1}(u,v,\lambda,\alpha)
,
C_{2}(u,v,\lambda,\alpha)>1
such that

\displaystyle C_{1}^{-1}\cdot t^{1-\alpha/2}\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda t^{\alpha}}(0,t))\leq C_{1}\cdot t^{1-\alpha/2},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda t^{2/3}}(0,t))\leq C_{2}\cdot t^{2/3},

and there exists
\delta>0
and
C_{3}(u,v,\delta)>0
such that for all
\lambda\in(0,\delta)

\displaystyle C_{3}^{-1}\cdot t^{2/3}\leq\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda t^{2/3}}(0,t)).\phantom{\leq C\cdot t^{2/3}.}

As the transversal fluctuations of the KPZ equation in full space are of order
O(t^{2/3})
, the scale
L=O(t^{2/3})
represents a critical regime in which the height function exhibits nontrivial spatial correlations across the domain. This is often referred to as the relaxation regime. The cases
\alpha<2/3
and
\alpha>2/3
are known as the super-relaxation and sub-relaxation regimes, respectively. Theorem
1.2
captures the fluctuation exponent in the super-relaxation regime and part of the relaxation regime. We expect that
\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}_{L}(0,t))
should be of order
t^{2/3}
when
L=\lambda t^{2/3}
for large
\lambda
, and more generally when
L\gg t^{2/3}
; however, our current methods are not sufficient to establish this rigorously.

1.2.
Context

Our main theorems contribute to a growing line of work aimed at understanding phase transition behavior in KPZ-type models with boundaries or finite-size effects, where the nature of fluctuations changes as the system size or boundary parameters are varied. This has been studied extensively in the periodic and half-space settings, which we now briefly review.

Periodic setting

The long-time behavior of finite-size KPZ systems, where the system size also grows with time, was first systematically studied in the mathematics literature in the context of the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP) on the torus. In this setting,
[
DEM93
]
was the first to derive that the asymptotic variance decays as
L^{-1/2}
and explained how this decay relates to the
2/3
KPZ exponent. In the setting of the periodic KPZ equation, the work of Dunlap, Gu, and Komorowski
[
DGK23
]
established analogous variance bounds to those in Theorem
1.2
, covering both the super-relaxation regime and part of the sub-relaxation regime. Their techniques serve as an important point of comparison for the present work (see Section
1.3
for details).

Subsequently, by exploiting the integrable structure of the model, a series of works
[
Pro16
,
BL16
,
BL18
,
BL19
,
BL21
,
BLS22
,
Liu18
]
obtained exact formulas, fluctuation exponents, and limiting distributions for TASEP on the torus. In particular, it has been shown that the rescaled height fluctuations in the relaxation regime interpolate between Gaussian and Tracy–Widom statistics. For first-passage percolation and longest increasing subsequences, precise fluctuation exponents and Gaussian fluctuations have been rigorously established in the super-relaxation regime
[
CD13
,
DJP18
]
.

Half-space setting

In the setting where
L=\infty
and
v=0
, one obtains the half-space KPZ equation. A phase transition from Gaussian statistics to Tracy–Widom-type statistics is observed in this model by setting
u=ct^{-1/3}

and varying the constant
c
. This transition was first identified in a series of works by Baik and Rains
[
BR01a
,
BR01b
,
BR01c
]
in the context of last passage percolation. Multi-point fluctuations were subsequently analyzed in
[
SI04
]
, and similar results were later proven for exponential LPP in
[
BBCS18a
,
BBCS18b
]
using the framework of Pfaffian Schur processes. For the half-space KPZ equation and its integrable discretization—the half-space log-gamma polymer—analogous phase transitions and fluctuation exponents have been studied in mathematics works
[
BBC20
,
BW22
,
IMS22
,
BCD24
,
DZ24a
,
DY25
,
Gin24
,
DS25
]
and in physics works

[
GLD12
,
BBC16
,
IT18
,
DNKLDT20
,
KLD18
,
BKLD20
,
BLD21
,
BKLD22
]
. In a recent work
[
GT24
]
, the authors study the stationary half-space KPZ equation with boundary parameter
u=ct^{-\beta}
for
\beta\in[0,1/3]
and
c<0
, and establish variance bounds for the height function in a spirit similar to ours. We note, however, that their proof relies on entirely different techniques. In addition to the above models, the Baik–Rains phase transition has recently been rigorously proven for half-space ASEP and six-vertex models in the works
[
He25
,
He24
]
.

Finally, we note that all of the aforementioned results pertain to the spatial dimension
d=1
. For
d\geq 2
, we refer to
[
CSZ23
,
DG22
,
GHL25
,
GHL24
,
KN24
,
Tao24
,
CKNP21
,
CH19
,
CK19
,
GY23
]
, where various important properties of the SHE and/or the KPZ equation such as fluctuations, spatial ergodicity, and intermittency have been investigated.

1.3.
Proof ideas

In this section, we sketch the key ideas behind the proofs of our main results. As mentioned earlier, our approach is inspired by the proof techniques developed in
[
DGK23
]
. We begin by outlining the main ideas from their work and then highlight the key similarities and differences in our setting.

1.3.1.
Key ideas in
[
DGK23
]

In
[
DGK23
]
, the authors consider the KPZ equation in the periodic setting and prove analogous variance results. The stationary measure in this case is given by
\mathfrak{B}
, a Brownian bridge on
[0,L]
from
0
to
0
. Using the Clark–Ocone formula, they derive bounds on the variance for each fixed
t
and
L
, and obtain the following expression for the asymptotic variance
\sigma_{L,\mathrm{per}}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{L,\mathrm{per}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{B_{1}(x)+2B_{2}(x)+B_{3}(x)}dx}{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{B_{1}(x)+B_{2}(x)}dx\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{B_{2}(x)+B_{3}(x)}dx}\right]

where
B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
are independent copies of
\mathfrak{B}
. The core idea in their analysis is to show that
\sigma_{L,\mathrm{per}}^{2}\sim L^{-1/2}
by analyzing this expression. This analysis is nontrivial and relies crucially on the structure of the Brownian bridge. Exploiting its properties, they reformulate the variance as

(1.6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{L,\mathrm{per}}^{2}=L\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{B_{1}(x)+B_{2}(x)}dx\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{B_{2}(x)+B_{3}(x)}dx}\right]=L\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{L}e^{v_{k}\cdot V(x)}dx\right)^{-1}\right]

where
V
is a
2
-dimensional Brownian bridge and
v_{k}=-2^{-1/2}(\sqrt{3},(-1)^{k})
,
k=1,2
. The first reduction above is via circular translation invariance of Brownian bridges and the second reduction is by writing correlated Brownian bridges
(B_{1}+B_{2},B_{2}+B_{3})
in terms of a
2D
Brownian bridge from the origin to origin. The key insight here is that the dominant contribution to the above expectation comes from paths for which

\omega(V):=\sup_{x\in[0,L]}\max_{k=1,2}v_{k}\cdot V(x)

is bounded above. Their proof then proceeds by estimating the probability that
\omega(V)\leq a
which involves understanding Brownian bridges killed upon exiting the wedge
\omega^{-1}((-\infty,a])
. This is made possible by explicit transition density formulas for such killed bridges in terms of Bessel functions, which play a central role in their technical arguments.

\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
law for
u,v>0
. The term
\int_{0}^{L}e^{-(\Lambda(s)-\Lambda^{\prime}(s))}ds
in the exponent imposes a strong penalty whenever
\Lambda<\Lambda^{\prime}
, effectively pushing
\Lambda
above
\Lambda^{\prime}
. On the other hand, the boundary terms
-u(\Lambda(0)-\Lambda^{\prime}(0))
and
-v(\Lambda(L)-\Lambda^{\prime}(L))
encourage the two processes to be close at
x=0
and
x=L
.

1.3.2.
Our proof

We now turn to the proof idea of our theorems. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is also an application of the Clark–Ocone formula and largely follows the same structure as in
[
DGK23
]
, with necessary modifications to account for the presence of boundary conditions. We therefore focus on explaining the proof strategy for Theorem
1.2
. While our goal is likewise to show that
\sigma_{L}^{2}
, as defined in (
1.5
), exhibits the same
L^{-1/2}
decay rate, neither a reduction analogous to (
1.6
) nor explicit formulas – such as those involving Bessel functions used in
[
DGK23
]
– are available in our setting. This is due to the more intricate structure of the stationary measure
\Lambda
, which we now explain.

Stationary measure description

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{free}}
denote the law of a pair
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})
of independent Brownian motions, with
\Lambda
started from
0
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
started from a point distributed according to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. This defines an infinite measure. We define a new probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
on the pair
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}):[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}
via the Radon–Nikodym derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{free}}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})\propto\exp\left(-u(\Lambda(0)-\Lambda^{\prime}(0))-v(\Lambda(L)-\Lambda^{\prime}(L))-\int_{0}^{L}e^{-(\Lambda(s)-\Lambda^{\prime}(s))}\,ds\right).

A recent sequence of works
[
CK24
,
BLD22
,
BKWW23
,
BCY24
,
Him24
]
has shown that the law of
\Lambda(x)
under this measure yields the stationary measure for the open KPZ equation (
1.2
) on
[0,L]
when
u+v>0
. Figure
2
describes how a sample from the measure
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
typically looks like (for
u,v>0
).

[y-L\delta,y+L\delta]

Variance decay rate

Returning to the expression for
\sigma_{L}^{2}
in (
1.5
), we note that unlike in the periodic setting, there is no circular translation invariance for the stationary measure. To proceed, we rewrite:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+2\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)}\,dx}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)}\,dx\cdot\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)}\,dx}\right]=\sum_{y=0}^{L-1}\mathfrak{F}(y),

where

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(y):=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\int_{y}^{y+1}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+2\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-2\Lambda_{2}(y)-\Lambda_{3}(y)}\,dx}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}\,dx\cdot\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}\,dx}\right].

To get a sense of how
\mathfrak{F}(y)
behaves, it is helpful to study the decay of the following simpler quantity:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}(y):=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}\,dx\cdot\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}\,dx}\right].

At an intuitive level,
\mathfrak{F}(y)
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}(y)
should have the same decay rate. This is because the numerator in (
1.8
) is an integral over a unit interval and hence is expected to be of constant order
O(1)
. So to understand the behavior of
\sigma_{L}^{2}
, it suffices to focus on estimating the typical size of
\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}(y)
. We do this in two steps.

Step 1. Reduction to Brownian bridges with random endpoints.

To study the decay rate of (
1.9
), we reduce the analysis to computations involving Brownian bridges with random endpoints. The key idea is that when
y
lies in the bulk (say, in the interval
[L/4,3L/4]
), there is sufficient separation between the three curves
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
for
i=1,2,3
. As a result, the processes
\Lambda_{i}
near
y
should behave approximately like Brownian motions.

To make this precise, we show that conditional on the sigma-field
\sigma(\Lambda_{i}(y\pm L\delta),\Lambda_{i}(y)\mid i=1,2,3)
, for sufficiently small
\delta>0
, the Radon–Nikodym derivatives in the interval
[y-L\delta,y+L\delta]
are bounded from below. This means that for the purpose of obtaining a lower bound, we may effectively ignore the RN derivatives and instead work with Brownian bridges having random endpoints
\Lambda_{i}(y\pm L\delta)
and
\Lambda_{i}(y)
, see Figure
3
(A).

For the upper bound, we use a stochastic monotonicity argument, which says that the process
(\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}(y))
, conditional on
\sigma(\Lambda_{i}(0),\Lambda_{i}(y),\Lambda_{i}(L))
, is stochastically larger than a Brownian bridge connecting
\Lambda_{i}(0)
,
\Lambda_{i}(y)
, and
\Lambda_{i}(L)
, see Figure
3
(B). This provides a comparison framework that allows us to control the fluctuations from above.

Step 2. Estimating functionals under Brownian bridges with varying endpoints.

Once reduced to the Brownian bridge setting, we apply a linear transformation (as in
[
DGK23
]
) to express the functionals as those of 2D Brownian bridge from the origin to a random endpoint. The key difference in our case is the presence of the random endpoint, which requires extending the estimates in
[
DGK23
]
to ones that depend on the endpoints. This extension is relatively straightforward for the lower bound, but significantly more challenging for the upper bound. To keep the exposition light, we defer a detailed discussion of the lower bound to Section
5.1
, noting here that the main difficulty arises from the fact that the argument in
[
DGK23
]
relies on stopping times for 2D Brownian bridges from the origin to the origin, for which precise tail estimates are available via a conformal transformation – a tool not available in our setting.

Other technical aspects

We emphasize that our actual proof works with
\mathfrak{F}(y)
, not
\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}(y)
. While the idea of estimating
\mathfrak{F}(y)
is morally similar to that for
\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}(y)
, there are several technical differences that must be addressed. We chose to present the simplified version involving
\widehat{\mathfrak{F}}(y)
above, as it captures the core intuition while avoiding the more delicate technicalities required for
\mathfrak{F}(y)
. In particular, to show that the numerator in
\mathfrak{F}(y)
is of order
O(1)
, and to control the random endpoints appearing in the steps above, one needs exponential moment estimates for the stationary measures. A significant portion of our work is devoted to understanding the diffusive scaling limits of the two layer Gibbs representation of the stationary measures and establishing various exponential moments for the underlying process. This is done in Section
2
of the paper.

1.4.
Extensions to other models and phases

Our proof strategy offers a general framework for establishing variance bounds in open KPZ-type models that admit a similar two-layer representation of the stationary measure – such as geometric last passage percolation and the log-gamma polymer on a strip
[
BCY24
]
.

In this work, however, we do not address the low and high density phases. In these regimes, we expect that
\sigma_{L}^{2}
does not decay with
L
. Let us briefly outline the main challenges involved in analyzing this setting.

First, while the two-layer description of the stationary measure is conjectured to hold for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
(see Theorem 1.8 in
[
BCY24
]
), a rigorous proof is currently available only when
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
with
u+v>0
. Suppose we restrict attention to the low density phase (
u<0
,
v>u
) under the assumption that
u+v>0
so that the existing theory applies. In this case, it is known that
\Lambda(x)
converges to
\mathfrak{B}(x)+ux
, where
\mathfrak{B}
is a Brownian motion
[
HY04
]
. Heuristically, then, we should have the following weak convergence:

\displaystyle\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+2\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)}dx}{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)}dx\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)}dx}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{1}(x)+2\mathfrak{B}_{2}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{3}(x)+4ux}\,dx}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{1}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{2}(x)+2ux}\,dx\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{2}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{3}(x)+2ux}\,dx},

where
\mathfrak{B}_{1},\mathfrak{B}_{2},\mathfrak{B}_{3}
are independent standard Brownian motions. Since
\mathfrak{B}_{i}(x)=O(\sqrt{x})
and
u<0
, the above improper integrals converge almost surely, and the entire ratio on the right-hand side is almost surely finite. However, it appears nontrivial to prove that this limiting random variable lies in
L^{1}
, particularly for small values of
|u|
. Extending the above convergence to
L^{1}
would require a delicate analysis of the two-layer Gibbs structure in this regime – one that is qualitatively different from the maximal current phase studied in this paper. A rigorous treatment of these low and high density phases remains an interesting direction for future work.

Outline

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we investigate the diffusive properties of the stationary measure. In Section
3
, we prove our main theorems, assuming upper and lower bounds on the asymptotic variance. The matching upper and lower bounds on the variance are established in Sections
4
and
5
, respectively. Appendix
A
collects moment bounds for the open SHE.

2.
Diffusive properties of stationary measures

2.1.
Gibbsian line ensembles and stationary measures

In this section, we state the description of the stationary measure and relate it with Gibbs measures.

[
CK24
]
proved the existence of a stationary measure for the open KPZ equation on
[0,1]
for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
and characterized it via a multi-point Laplace transform under the condition
u+v>0
. Later,
[
BLD22
,
BKWW23
]
provided a probabilistic description of this measure by inverting the multi-point Laplace transform, valid for
L=1
,
u+v>0
. Most recently,
[
Him24
]
extended the validity of this description to all
L>0
and
u+v>0
. We state this description below.

Let
U
be a Brownian motion started from
0
with diffusion coefficient
2
. Let
V:[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}
be independent from
U
. The law of
V
is
absolutely continuous with respect to the free Brownian (infinite) measure with Lebesgue measure for
V(0)

and Brownian law from there with diffusion coefficient
2
, with Radon-Nikodym derivative proportional to

\displaystyle\exp\left(-uV(0)-vV(L)-\int_{0}^{L}e^{-V(s)}ds\right).

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 1.4 in
[
Him24
]
)
.

Assume
u+v>0
. With the above definitions,

\frac{1}{2}(U(x)+V(x)-V(0))
is the stationary measure for the open KPZ equation (
1.2
) on
[0,L]
.

Uniqueness of the above stationary measure follows from the work of
[
KM22
,
Par22
]
.
We now give an alternative description of the stationary measure.

Definition 2.2

(Open-KPZ Gibbs measure)
.

Assume
u+v>0
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{free}}
denotes the law of two independent Brownian motions started from
0
and a value that is distributed according to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
respectively. This is an infinite measure. Suppose
u,v\geq 0
and
L\geq 1
. We define the Open-KPZ Gibbs measure to be the law
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
on
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}:[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}
given in terms of the following Radon-Nikodym derivative

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{free}}}(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})\propto W_{u,v}:=\exp\left(-u(\Lambda(0)-\Lambda^{\prime}(0))-v(\Lambda(L)-\Lambda^{\prime}(L))-\int_{0}^{L}e^{-(\Lambda(s)-\Lambda^{\prime}(s))}ds\right).

Using properties of Brownian motions (see the discussions in Section 1.6.1 of
[
BCY24
]
) and a change of variables, one can check that the law of
\Lambda(\cdot)
under
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
(defined in Definition
2.2
) is the stationary measure for the open KPZ equation (
1.2
) in
[0,L]
. We remark that a slightly different Gibbsian formulation is given in
[
BCY24
]
, which is valid for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
, modulo a polymer convergence result (see Theorem 1.8 therein). For the purposes of the present work, however, it is more advantageous to use the formulation in Definition
2.2
,since it can be directly related to the
H
-Brownian Gibbs measure studied in
[
CH16
]
.

Definition 2.3

(
H
-Brownian Gibbs measure)
.

Fix
k\geq 1
,
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
f,g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
. Let
H:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a convex continuous function.
Let
\mathbb{P}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a};\vec{b}}
denotes the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
on
[t_{1},t_{2}]
from
\vec{a}
to
\vec{b}
. Define a measure
\mathbb{P}_{W;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a};\vec{b};f,g}
absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a};\vec{b};f,g}
with the following Radon-Nikodym derivative:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{H;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a};\vec{b};f,g}}{d\mathbb{P}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a};\vec{b}}}(B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{k})=W_{H}:=\prod_{i=0}^{k}\exp\bigg(-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}H(B_{i+1}(s)-B_{i}(s))ds\bigg)

where
B_{0}:=f
and
B_{k+1}:=g
. We call
\mathbb{P}_{H;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a};\vec{b};f,g}
as the
H
-Brownian Gibbs measure.

The above measure was first introduced in
[
CH16
]
. They showed that the full-space KPZ equation with narrow wedge initial data can be viewed as the top curve of a line ensemble whose conditional distribution restricted to finite region is a
H
-Brownian Gibbs measure with
H(x)=e^{x}
. Using this line ensemble structure, various properties of the full-space KPZ equation are proved in the literature. We refer to
[
CGH21
,
DG23
,
DZ24b
,
DZ24c
,
Wu23a
,
Wu23b
,
DS25
]
for references. and it arises naturally in the context of full-space and half-space KPZ line ensembles
[
CH16
,
DS25
]
.

In our context, the above measures are related to stationary measures as follows. Suppose
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})\sim\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
according to Definition
2.2
. Take any
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq L
. From Gibbsian description, it is clear that conditional distributions can be expressed as
H
-Brownian Gibbs measures defined in Definition
2.3
with
H(x)=e^{x}
. We have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Law}\bigg((\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\mbox{ conditioned on }(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})|_{(t_{1},t_{2})^{c}}\bigg)=\mathbb{P}_{W;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{2;(a,a^{\prime});(b,b^{\prime}),+\infty,-\infty},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Law}\bigg(\Lambda|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\mbox{ conditioned on }\Lambda|_{(t_{1},t_{2})^{c}},\Lambda^{\prime}\bigg)=\mathbb{P}_{W;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{1;a;b,+\infty,\Lambda^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Law}\bigg(\Lambda^{\prime}|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\mbox{ conditioned on }\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}|_{(t_{1},t_{2})^{c}}\bigg)=\mathbb{P}_{W;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{1;a^{\prime};b^{\prime};\Lambda,-\infty},

where
a=\Lambda(t_{1})
,
a^{\prime}=\Lambda^{\prime}(t_{1})
,
b=\Lambda(t_{2})
, and
b^{\prime}=\Lambda^{\prime}(t_{2})
.
H
-Brownian Gibbs measure enjoys certain stochastic monotonicity, which we record below.

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 2.6 in
[
CH16
]
)
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and
t_{1}<t_{2}
. Suppose
\vec{a}^{(1)},\vec{a}^{(2)},\vec{b}^{(1)},\vec{b}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\vec{a}_{k}^{(1)}\geq\vec{a}_{k}^{(2)}
and
\vec{b}_{k}^{(1)}\geq\vec{b}_{k}^{(2)}
. Suppose
f^{(1)},f^{(2)},g^{(1)},g^{(2)}:[t_{1},t_{2}]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f^{(1)}\geq f^{(2)}
and
g^{(1)}\geq g^{(2)}
. There exists a coupling of
\vec{\mathcal{Q}}^{(1)}\sim\mathbb{P}_{W;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a}^{(1)};\vec{b}^{(1)};f^{(1)};g^{(1)}}
and
\vec{\mathcal{Q}}^{(2)}\sim\mathbb{P}_{W;[t_{1},t_{2}]}^{k;\vec{a}^{(2)};\vec{b}^{(2)};f^{(2)};g^{(2)}}
such that almost surely
\mathcal{Q}_{j}^{(1)}(s)\geq\mathcal{Q}_{j}^{(2)}(s)
for all
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
, and
s\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
.

2.2.
Diffusive limits of the Open-KPZ Gibbs measure

In this section, we study diffusive limits of
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
measure defined in Definition
2.2
. Throughout this subsection we assume
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})
is distributed as
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
and consider the following processes on
[0,1]
:

\displaystyle B_{L}(x):=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\Lambda(xL),\quad B_{L}^{\prime}(x):=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\Lambda^{\prime}(xL),

(2.3)

\displaystyle U_{L}(x):=B_{L}(x)+B_{L}^{\prime}(x),\quad V_{L}(x):=B_{L}(x)-B_{L}^{\prime}(x).

Due to the diffusive scaling we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}((B_{L},B_{L}^{\prime})\in A):=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{free}}[W_{u,v}^{L}\mathbf{1}_{(B_{L},B_{L}^{\prime})\in A}]}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{free}}[W_{u,v}^{L}]}.

where

\displaystyle W_{u,v}^{L}=\exp\left(-u\sqrt{L}V_{L}(0)-v\sqrt{L}V_{L}(1)-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)

is same as
W_{u,v}
defined in (
2.1
). The main result of this subsection is the following.

Theorem 2.5

(Diffusive limits)
.

We have the following.

(a)

If
u,v>0
,
B_{L},B_{L}^{\prime}
converge to non-intersecting Brownian motions that start from zero and end at the same point.

(b)

If
u>0,v=0
,
B_{L},B_{L}^{\prime}
converges to non-intersecting Brownian motions that start from zero.

(c)

If
u=0,v>0
,
B_{L},B_{L}^{\prime}
converge to
B,B^{\prime}
where
B(x)
and
B^{\prime}(x)-B(1)
are independent standard Brownian motions that are conditioned to be
B(x)\geq B^{\prime}(x)
on
[0,1]
.

Furthermore, we have pointwise
L^{2}
convergence.

Figure 4
.
Limiting measures when
u,v>0
(Left),
u>0,v=0
(Middle), and
u=0,v>0
(Right).

We refer to Figure
4
for depiction of the limiting measures in the three cases. We require a couple of preliminary lemmas before proving Theorem
2.5
. Our first lemma provides a lower bound for the denominator of (
2.4
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
u+v>0
.

(a)

Suppose
u,v>0
. There exists a constant
c>0
depending on
u,v
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{free}}[W_{u,v}^{L}]\geq cL^{-3/2}
for all
L\geq 1
.

(b)

Suppose
u=0
or
v=0
. There exists a constant
\widetilde{c}>0
depending on
u,v
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{free}}[W_{u,v}^{L}]\geq\widetilde{c}L^{-1/2}
for all
L\geq 1
.

Proof.

Clearly it suffices to prove the lemma for all large enough
L
. We shall drop the subscript
\mathsf{free}
from the notation for simplicity. Suppose
u,v>0
. Consider the event

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}:=\left\{V_{L}(0),V_{L}(1)\in[0,1/\sqrt{L}],\ V_{L}(x)>0\mbox{ for all }x\in[0,1]\right\}.

In words, the
\mathsf{A}
event requires the boundary points to be within certain
O(1/\sqrt{L})
window and
B_{L}
always stay above
B_{L}^{\prime}
. Note that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}]\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}]}{\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-|u|-|v|}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-L\int\limits_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})}.

The ratio on the r.h.s. of the above equation can be viewed as the conditional expectation of
\exp\left(-L\int\limits_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)
conditioned on the event
\mathsf{A}
. By stochastic monotonicity, this conditional expectation is increasing w.r.t. the endpoints of
V_{L}(\cdot)
. Thus sending
V_{L}(0),V_{L}(1)\downarrow 0
, we see that

\displaystyle\mbox{r.h.s.~of \eqref{inder}}\geq e^{-|u|-|v|}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\exp\left(-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)\right]

where
\mathbb{P}_{+}
is the law of a Brownian excursion.
We now claim that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})\geq c_{2}L^{-3/2},\quad\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\exp\left(-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)\right]\geq c_{1}

holds for all large enough
L
. Part
(a)
of the lemma follows by plugging this bound back in (
2.5
). We thus focus on proving (
2.6
). Towards this end, note that conditioned on
V_{L}(0),V_{L}(1)
,
V_{L}
is a Brownian bridge from
V_{L}(0)
to
V_{L}(1)
with diffusion coefficient
2
. By the reflection principle, the probability of a Brownian bridge from
x>0
to
y>0
with diffusion coefficient
2
stays positive is
(1-e^{-xy})
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})=\sqrt{L}\int_{0}^{1/\sqrt{L}}\int_{0}^{1/\sqrt{L}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-(x-y)^{2}/4}(1-e^{-xy})dxdy.

which is bounded from below by
c_{2}L^{-3/2}
for some constant
c_{2}>0
. This verifies the first part of the claim.

Using the one point density of Brownian excursion from
[
DIM77
]
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right]

\displaystyle=L\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{+}[e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}]ds

\displaystyle=L\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{L}y}\cdot\frac{2y^{2}e^{-y^{2}/2s(1-s)}}{\sqrt{2\pi s^{3}(1-s)^{3}}}dyds

\displaystyle=L\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z\sqrt{Ls(1-s)}}\cdot\frac{2z^{2}e^{-u^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}dzds

\displaystyle=2L\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2z^{2}e^{-z^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{1/2}e^{-z\sqrt{Ls(1-s)}}dsdz.

For the inner integral we note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1/2}e^{-z\sqrt{Ls(1-s)}}ds\leq\int_{0}^{1}e^{-z\sqrt{Ls/2}}ds\leq\frac{4}{z^{2}L}.

Thus
\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right]\leq c_{3}
for some
c_{3}>0
. This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{+}\bigg(L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\leq 2c_{3}\bigg)\geq 1/2

which forces

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\exp\left(-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)\right]\geq e^{-2c_{3}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{+}\bigg(L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\leq 2c_{3}\bigg)\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-2c_{3}}.

This verifies the second part of (
2.6
) completing the proof of part
(a)
.

For part
(b)
, without loss of generality, assume
u>0
and
v=0
. We consider the following event instead:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}:=\left\{V_{L}(0)\in[0,1/\sqrt{L}],\ V_{L}(1)\in[0,1],\ V_{L}(x)>0\mbox{ for all }x\in[0,1]\right\}.

By the same arguments as part
(a)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}]\geq e^{-|u|}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mathsf{A}})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{+}\left[\exp\left(-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right)\right].

We have already noted that the above expectation is at least
c_{2}
. By the same argument used to bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A})
, we may similarly bound
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mathsf{A}})
as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mathsf{A}})=\sqrt{L}\int_{0}^{1/\sqrt{L}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{-(x-y)^{2}/4}(1-e^{-xy})dxdy\geq\widetilde{c}_{1}L^{-1/2}.

This verifies part
(b)
.
∎

Having established a lower bound on the partition function
\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}]
, we may now deduce various probability statements under
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
by estimating the numerator. Our first lemma in this direction shows that
u
and
v
weight parameters has a pinning effect at starting and ending points.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
u>0
, then
\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}[V_{L}(0)^{2}]\xrightarrow{L\rightarrow+\infty}0
. If
v>0
, then
\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}[V_{L}(1)^{2}]\xrightarrow{L\rightarrow+\infty}0
.

Proof.

Can’t be too low.
Suppose
u>0
. Fix any
\varepsilon>0
. We first show that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}[V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\leq-\varepsilon\}}]\to 0\mbox{ if }u>0,\quad\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}[V_{L}(1)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(1)\leq-\varepsilon\}}]\to 0\mbox{ if }v>0.

Let us rewrite
W_{u,v}^{L}
as

\displaystyle W_{u,v}^{L}=\exp\left(-(u+v)\sqrt{L}V_{L}(0)-v\sqrt{L}(V_{L}(1)-V_{L}(0))-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}(V_{L}(s))}ds\right).

Note that under the free law
V_{L}(0)
is distributed as Lebesgue measure and
V_{L}(s)-V_{L}(0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}B(s)
where
B
is a Brownian motion started from
0
with diffusion coefficient
2
. Thus by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\leq-\varepsilon\}}]=\int_{-\infty}^{-\varepsilon}x^{2}e^{-(u+v)x\sqrt{L}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[e^{-2Y_{1}}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[e^{-2Y_{2}(x)}]}\,dx

where
Y_{1}:=\exp(v\sqrt{2L}B(0))
and
Y_{2}(x):=L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}(\sqrt{2}B(s)+x)}ds
. Let us define the event

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}:=\left\{\sup_{s\in[0,L^{-8}]}B(s)\leq\frac{|x|}{2}\right\}.

We have
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{A}^{c})\leq\exp(-x^{2}L^{8}/16)
and on
\mathsf{A}
,

Y_{2}(x)\geq L\int_{0}^{L^{-8}}e^{-\sqrt{L}(|x|/2+x)}ds=L^{-7}e^{-\sqrt{L}(|x|/2+x)}.

When
x\leq 0
, we have
|x|/2+x=-|x|/2
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{-2Y_{2}(x)}]\leq\exp(-x^{2}L^{8}/16)+\exp\big(-2L^{-8}e^{\sqrt{L}|x|/2}\big).

Plugging this estimate back in (
2.8
) along with the fact that
\mathbb{E}[e^{2Y_{1}}]=e^{v^{2}L}
, we see that the integral in (
2.8
) is going to zero exponentially fast as
L\to\infty
for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}]=\int_{-\infty}^{-\varepsilon}e^{-(u+v)x\sqrt{L}}e^{v^{2}L/2}[e^{-L^{-7}e^{-\sqrt{L}|x|/2}}+e^{-cx^{2}L^{8}}]\,dx.

Thanks to the polynomial lower bound from Lemma
2.6
, we can conclude the first part of (
2.7
).
An analogous argument shows that if
u>0
, we also have
\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}\left[V_{L}(1)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\leq-\varepsilon\}}\right]\to 0.
and if
v>0
we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}\left[V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(1)\leq-\varepsilon\}}\right]\to 0,\quad\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}\left[V_{L}(1)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(1)\leq-\varepsilon\}}\right]\to 0.

This verifies the second part of (
2.7
).

Can’t be too high.
We now claim that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}[V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\geq\varepsilon\}}]\to 0\mbox{ if }u>0,\quad\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}[V_{L}(1)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(1)\geq\varepsilon\}}]\to 0\mbox{ if }v>0.

Suppose
u,v>0
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W_{u,v}^{L}\cdot V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\geq\varepsilon,V_{L}(1)\geq-u\varepsilon/2v\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-u\sqrt{L}V_{L}(0)-v\sqrt{L}V_{L}(1)}\cdot V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\geq\varepsilon,V_{L}(1)\geq u\varepsilon/2v\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-u\sqrt{L}V_{L}(0)+u\sqrt{L}\varepsilon/2}\cdot V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\geq\varepsilon\}}\right]\leq e^{u\sqrt{L}\varepsilon/2}\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}x^{2}e^{-ux\sqrt{L}}dx.

The last integral is of the order
O(e^{-c\varepsilon\sqrt{L}})
which decays faster than polynomial. Hence again thanks to the polynomial lower bound from Lemma
2.6
, we can conclude

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}\left[V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\geq\varepsilon,V_{L}(1)\geq-u\varepsilon/2v\}}\right]\to 0.

However, by (
2.9
) we also have

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v}^{L}\left[V_{L}(0)^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(1)\leq-u\varepsilon/2v\}}\right]\to 0.

Thus combining (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) we arrive at the first part of (
2.10
) when both
u,v>0
. If
v=0
, then considering an additional event is not necessary. Using
W_{u,v}^{L}\leq e^{-u\sqrt{L}V_{L}(0)}
, we can directly verify that
\mathbb{E}[W_{u,v}^{L}\mathbf{1}_{\{V_{L}(0)\geq\varepsilon\}}]=O(e^{-c\varepsilon\sqrt{L}})
which implies the first part of (
2.10
) (again via Lemma
2.6
). An analogous argument leads to the second part of (
2.10
) as well. This completes the proof.
∎

With the control on the end-points, we may now establish Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Note that the parameters
u,v
can be swapped by inverting the time. Indeed, if
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})
is distributed as
\mathbb{P}_{u,v}^{L}
, then
(\widetilde{\Lambda},\widetilde{\Lambda}^{\prime})
is distributed as
\mathbb{P}_{v,u}^{L}
where

\widetilde{\Lambda}(x):=\Lambda(L-x)-\Lambda(L)+\Lambda(0)\mbox{ and }\widetilde{\Lambda}^{\prime}(x):=\Lambda^{\prime}(L-x)-\Lambda^{\prime}(L)+\Lambda^{\prime}(0).

Hence it suffices to consider the case when
u>0
and
v\geq 0
. Recall the sum and difference processes,
U_{L}
and
V_{L}
, from (
2.3
). As the Radon-Nikodym derivative depends only on the difference, conditioned on
B_{L}^{\prime}(0)
, the following three things hold simultaneously:

•

U_{L}
and
V_{L}
are independent,

•

U_{L}
is Brownian motion with diffusion coefficient
2
started from
B_{L}^{\prime}(0),

•

V_{L}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. a Brownian motion with diffusion coefficient
2
started from
-B_{L}^{\prime}(0)
with a Radon-Nikodym derivative proportional to:

\exp\left(-v\sqrt{L}\cdot V_{L}(1)-L\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\sqrt{L}V_{L}(s)}ds\right),

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
assuming bounds on the asymptotic variance. As explained in the introduction, the proof of Theorem
1.1
follows similar ideas to those in Section 2 of
[
DGK23
]
, where an analogous statement was proved for the periodic KPZ case.

To outline the proof, we first introduce some notation. Let
\mathcal{Z}(x,s;y,t)
be the time-
t
solution to the SHE (
1.3
) with boundary conditions (
1.4
) started from Dirac delta initial data
\delta(x)
at time
s
. These are often called propagators of the SHE from
(x,s)
to
(y,t)
. Let
C_{+}([0,L])
be the space of all non-negative continuous functions on
[0,L]
that are not identical to zero.
For
f,g\in C_{+}([0,L])
, using the propagators, we may define generalizations of partition functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(g,s;y,t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{L}\mathcal{Z}(x,s;y,t)g(x)dx,\quad\mathcal{Z}(x,s;f,t):=\int_{0}^{L}\mathcal{Z}(x,s;y,t)f(y)dy,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(g,s;f,t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathcal{Z}(x,s;y,t)g(x)f(y)dxdy.

Here,
\mathcal{Z}(g,s;x,t)
is the solution to the SHE (
1.3
) with boundary conditions (
1.4
) started from initial data
g(x)
at time
s
and

\mathcal{Z}(g,s;f,t)
can be viewed as the partition function of the point-to-point
directed polymer on the strip of length
t-s
, with the two endpoints sampled from
g
and
f
respectively. When the
g\equiv 1
or
f\equiv 1
, we simply write
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{1},s;y,t)
or
\mathcal{Z}(y,s;\mathbf{1},t)
for
\mathcal{Z}(g,s;y,t)
or
\mathcal{Z}(x,s;f,t)
respectively. For the log-partition functions, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(g,s;y,t):=\log\mathcal{Z}(g,s;y,t),\quad\mathcal{H}(x,s;f,t):=\log\mathcal{Z}(x,s;f,t),\quad\mathcal{H}(g,s;f,t):=\log\mathcal{Z}(g,s;f,t).

and for the normalized partition function we write

(3.1)

\displaystyle\rho(g,s;y,t):=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(g,s;y,t)}{\mathcal{Z}(g,s;\mathbf{1},t)},\quad\rho(x,s;f,t):=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(x,s;f,t)}{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{1},s;f,t)}.

We will allow
g,f
to be random. Since allowing random initial and ending data allows several sources of randomness, to avoid confusion, we use
\mathbf{E}
to denote the expectation only with respect to the noise
\xi
. The expectations with respect to
f,g
, will
be denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{f},\mathbb{E}_{g}
respectively. When there is no possibility of
confusion, we use
\mathbb{E}
as the expectation with respect to all possible randomnesses. When
g
is equal in distribution to
e^{\Lambda(x)}
, where
\Lambda(x)
is the stationary measure,
\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda},0;y,t)
is the height function corresponding to the stationary open KPZ equation. In this case, we drop
(e^{\Lambda},0)
from the notation and simply write
\mathcal{H}(y,t)
and
\mathcal{H}(f,t)
.

The key idea behind Theorem
1.1
is the following decomposition formula for the centered height function (the analogous decomposition for the periodic KPZ appears in Proposition 3.1 in
[
DGK23
]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix any
t>0
and adopt the above notations and conventions. Let
\mathcal{H}(0,t)
be the stationary solution of the open KPZ equation (
1.1
) on
[0,L]
with boundary data (
1.2
). We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(0,t)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{H}(0,t)]=I_{L}(t)+Y_{L}(0)-Y_{L}(t)

where

(3.2)

\displaystyle I_{L}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)\right],\qquad Y_{L}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s)\right]-\mathcal{H}(0,s).

where
\Lambda_{2}
is distributed according to the stationary measure independent of the noise and the initial data.

Given the above decomposition, we observe that

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}(0,t))}-\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(I_{L}(t))}\right|\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Y_{L}(0)-Y_{L}(t))}.

Theorem
1.1
then follows from the following two propositions.

Proposition 3.2

(Leading order)
.

For each
t>0
we have
\operatorname{Var}(I_{L}(t))=t\sigma_{L}^{2}
where
\sigma_{L}^{2}
is defined in (
1.5
).

Proposition 3.3

(Subleading term)
.

For any
t\geq 0
, then we have as
L\to\infty

\frac{1}{L}\operatorname{Var}[Y_{L}(t)]\rightarrow\operatorname{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}_{B_{2}}\max_{x\in[0,1]}\{B_{1}(x)+B_{2}(x)\}\right],

where
B_{1},B_{2}
are independent copies of
B
coming from Theorem
2.5
.

In the following subsection, Section
3.1
, we prove the decomposition formula (Proposition
3.1
) and the asymptotics for the subleading term (Proposition
3.3
). The proof of Proposition
3.2
, on the other hand, requires a few additional tools. We defer its proof to Section
3.2
.

3.1.
Proof of Propositions
3.1
and
3.3

In this section, we prove Propositions
3.1
and
3.3
. We continue with the same notation introduced before Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let us denote
e^{\Lambda_{1}}
to denote the initial data for
\mathcal{H}
so that
\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)=\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)
. Given the definitions of
I_{L}(t)
and
Y_{L}(s)
from (
3.2
), we have that

\displaystyle I_{L}(t)+Y_{L}(0)-Y_{L}(t)=\mathcal{H}(0,t)+Y_{L}(0)-\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)]

Thus it suffices to show

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{H}(0,t)]=Y_{L}(0).

Towards this end, we first claim that the initial and final data for
\mathcal{H}
can be swapped, i.e., for any given
f,g\in C_{+}([0,L])
we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathcal{Z}(y,0;x,t)g(y)f(x)dxdy

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\mathcal{Z}(y,0;x,t)g(x)f(y)dxdy=\mathcal{H}(f,0;g,t).

Indeed, (
3.4
) follows as at the level of propagators we have the following invariance in swapping:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{Z}(y,0;x,t)\}_{x,y\in[0,L]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\mathcal{Z}(x,0;y,t)\}_{x,y\in[0,L]}.

The above claim is standard in the SHE setting and can be proved by approximation to the smooth noise case, which in turn can be shown using the Feynman–Kac formula
[
Par22
, Proposition 2.2]
. We refer to Appendix B of
[
DGK23
]
for details related to periodic KPZ.

Thanks to (
3.4
) we have that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{H}(0,t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},0;e^{\Lambda_{1}},t)]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{H}(0,t)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\log\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},0;x,t)e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)}dx}{\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},0;0,t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\log\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)}dx\right]

where the last equality is due to the stationarity:
\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},0;\cdot,t)/\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},0;0,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}e^{\Lambda_{2}(\cdot)}
.
On the other hand, as
\mathcal{H}(0,s)=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}[\log\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;0,s)]
, for any
s\geq 0
we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle Y_{L}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)]-\mathcal{H}(0,s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\log\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;x,s)e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}dx}{\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;0,s)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}dx\right]

where the last equality is again via stationarity:
\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;\cdot,t)/\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;0,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}e^{\Lambda_{1}(\cdot)}
. In view of (
3.6
), this verifies (
3.3
), completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Thanks to (
3.7
), applying a diffusive scaling yields

\displaystyle\frac{Y_{L}(s)-\log L}{\sqrt{L}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\mathbb{E}_{B_{L,2}}\left[\log\int_{0}^{1}e^{\sqrt{L}(B_{L,1}(x)+B_{L,2}(x))}dx\right],

where
B_{L,i}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\Lambda_{i}(xL)
. In Theorem
2.5
, we studied diffusive limits of
B_{L,i}
and showed that
B_{L,i}\to B_{i}
(in distribution and in
L^{2}
) where
B_{i}
’s are independent copies of
B
coming from Theorem
2.5
. In particular, by Skorohod theorem and Laplace principle, we may get a common probability space where

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\log\int_{0}^{1}e^{\sqrt{L}(B_{L,1}(x)+B_{L,2}(x))}dx\to\max_{x\in[0,1]}(B_{1}(x)+B_{2}(x)).

almost surely as
L\to\infty
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\min_{x\in[0,1]}(B_{L,1}(x)+B_{L,2}(x))\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\log\int_{0}^{1}e^{\sqrt{L}(B_{L,1}(x)+B_{L,2}(x))}dx\leq\max_{x\in[0,1]}(B_{L,1}(x)+B_{L,2}(x)).

By Theorem
2.5
, both upper and lower bounds in above equation are in
L^{2}
. Thus the desired convergence follows by an application of dominated convergence theorem.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
3.2

We prove Proposition
3.2
in this section. As before, we assume
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
are two independent copies of the stationary measure. By definition (
3.2
),

(3.8)

\displaystyle I_{L}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)-\mathbf{E}\left[\mathcal{H}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)\right]\right].

We divide the proof into three steps. In
Step 1
, we derive a suitable formula for the random variable inside the outer expectation, assuming the square integrability of a certain random variable. In
Step 2
, we use this expression to prove the proposition. In
Step 3
, we verify the square integrability of the aforementioned random variable.

Step 1.
In this step, we derive a formula for
\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)-\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)]
for any
f,g\in C_{+}([0,L])
. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
be the filtration generated by
\xi
and
D
denote the Malliavin derivative w.r.t.
\xi
. For any square integrable random variable
F
measurable w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, the Clark-Ocone formula
[
CKNP21
]
states that

\displaystyle F-\mathbf{E}[F]=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbf{E}\left[D_{y,s}F\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\xi(y,s)dyds

provided
D_{y,s}F
is square integrable. We apply the above formula with
F:=\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)
where
g,f\in C_{+}([0,L])
to get

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)-\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)]=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbf{E}\left[D_{s,y}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\xi(y,s)dyds.

We postpone the verification of square integrability of
D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)
to
Step 3
. Let us simplify the expression for the Malliavin derivative.
Observe that

\displaystyle D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)=D_{y,s}\log\mathcal{Z}(g,0;f,t)=\frac{D_{y,s}\mathcal{Z}(g,0;f,t)}{\mathcal{Z}(g,0;f,t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{Z}(g,0;y,s)\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)}{\int_{0}^{L}\mathcal{Z}(g,0;y^{\prime},s)\mathcal{Z}(y^{\prime},s;f,t)dy^{\prime}}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho(g,0;y,s)\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)}{\int_{0}^{L}\rho(g,0;y^{\prime},s)\mathcal{Z}(y^{\prime},s;f,t)dy^{\prime}}

where the second equality follows by known formula for the Malliavin derivative of the SHE
[
CKNP23
]
and the last equality follows from the definition of normalized partition function (
3.1
).
Note that
\rho(g,0;\cdot,s)
is measurable w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{s}
and
\mathcal{Z}(\cdot,s;\cdot,t)
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Thus, if we define

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{s,t}(h,y;f):=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)}{\mathcal{Z}(h,s;f,t)}\right]

we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=\mathscr{G}_{s,t}(\rho(g;\cdot,s),y;f)\cdot\rho(g,0;y,s).

This leads us to the following formula:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)-\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)]=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}\mathscr{G}_{s,t}(\rho(g,0;\cdot,s),y;f)\rho(g,0;y,s)\xi(y,s)dyds.

Step 2.
If we take
f=e^{\Lambda_{2}}
in (
3.10
) and then take expectation w.r.t.
\Lambda_{2}
. we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathscr{G}_{s,t}(h,y;f)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\mathcal{Z}(y,s;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)/\mathcal{Z}(0,s;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)}{\mathcal{Z}(h,s;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)/\mathcal{Z}(0,s;e^{\Lambda_{2}},t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s;y,t)/\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s;0,t)}{\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s;h,t)/\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s;0,t)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{\Lambda_{2}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}h(x)dx}\right].

The first equality above is by definition. The second equality is via the swap invariance stated in (
3.5
). The third equality follows via stationarity:
\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s;\cdot,t)/\mathcal{Z}(e^{\Lambda_{2}},s;0,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}e^{\Lambda_{2}(\cdot)}
.
Thus in view of (
3.8
) and (
3.11
) we have

\displaystyle I_{L}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{\Lambda_{2}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}};x,s)dx}\right]\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;y,s)\xi(y,s)dyds.

By Ito’s isometry we thus have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(I_{L}(t))=\mathbb{E}[I_{L}(t)^{2}]=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\frac{e^{\Lambda_{2}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}};x,s)dx}\right]\right)^{2}\rho^{2}(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;y,s)\right]dyds.

Assuming
\Lambda_{3}
to be another copy of the stationary measure we may write the above integrand as

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2}}\left[\frac{e^{\Lambda_{2}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}};x,s)dx}\right]\right)^{2}\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;y,s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}}\left[\frac{e^{\Lambda_{2}(y)+\Lambda_{3}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)}\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}};x,s)dx\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{3}(x)}\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}};x,s)dx}\right]\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;y,s)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}}\left[\frac{e^{\Lambda_{2}(y)+\Lambda_{3}(y)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{1}(x)}dx\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{3}(x)+\Lambda_{1}(x)}dx}\right]\cdot e^{2\Lambda_{1}(y)}

where the last equality in distribution follows via stationarity:
\rho(e^{\Lambda_{1}},0;\cdot,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{e^{\Lambda_{1}(\cdot)}}{\int_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}}(x)dx}
. Taking an expectation w.r.t.
\Lambda_{1}
in the above formula, in view of (
3.12
), yields Proposition
3.2
.

Step 3.
In this step, we verify that
D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)
is indeed square integrable, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left|D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)\right|^{2}dyds<\infty.

The proof essentially relies on moment estimates for open SHE which we collect in Appendix
A
. Without loss of generality assume
\int_{0}^{L}f(x)dx=\int_{0}^{L}g(x)dx=1
. We shall show

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t/2}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left|D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)\right|^{2}dyds<\infty.

The proof for the boundedness of the integral over
s\in[t/2,t]
is analogous.
From (
3.9
) we have

\displaystyle|D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho(g,0;y,s)^{2}\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)^{2}}{\left(\int_{0}^{L}\rho(g,0;y^{\prime},s)\mathcal{Z}(y^{\prime},s;f,t)dy^{\prime}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho(g,0;y,s)^{2}\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\rho(g,0;y^{\prime},s)\mathcal{Z}(y^{\prime},s;f,t)^{-2}dy^{\prime}

where the above inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality. As
\mathcal{Z}(\cdot,s;\cdot,t)
and
\rho(g,0;\cdot,s)
are independent, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(g,0;y,s)^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\rho(g,0;y^{\prime},s)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)^{2}\mathcal{Z}(y^{\prime},s;f,t)^{-2}\right]dy^{\prime}\right]

Thanks to Proposition
A.6
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}(y,s;f,t)^{2}\mathcal{Z}(y^{\prime},s;f,t)^{-2}\right]\leq C
. As
\rho(g,0;\cdot,s)
is a density, we arrive at

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)|^{2}\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\rho(g,0;y,s)^{2}\right]

Recall the expression for
\rho(g,0;y,s)
from (
3.1
). By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\rho(g,0;y,s)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(g,0;y,s)^{2}\mathcal{Z}(g,0;\mathbf{1},s)^{-2}]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(g,0;\mathbf{1},s)^{-4}]\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(g,0;y,s)^{4}]}.

By Proposition
A.5
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(g,0;\mathbf{1},s)^{-4}]\leq\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(x,0;\mathbf{1},s)^{-4}]g(x)dx\leq C,

whereas Proposition
A.4
and Jensen’s inequality lead to

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(g,0;y,s)^{4}]}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(x,0;y,s)^{4}]^{1/4}g(x)dx\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{L}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}(x,0;y,s)^{4}]}g(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{L}s^{-1}e^{-|x-y|/\sqrt{s}}g(x)dx.

Plugging the above two bounds back in (
3.15
), in view of (
3.14
), yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t/2}\int_{0}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left|D_{y,s}\mathcal{H}(g,0;f,t)\right|^{2}dyds\leq C\int_{0}^{t/2}\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}s^{-1}e^{-|x-y|/\sqrt{s}}g(x)dxdyds

which can be seen to be finite by performing the integration over
y
first and then over
s
followed by
x
. This verifies (
3.13
).

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Given Theorem
1.1
, the proof of Theorem
1.2
follows by computing the asymtotics of
\sigma_{L}^{2}
as
L\to\infty
. Recall
\mathfrak{F}(y)
from (
1.8
). The following two proposition gives uniform upper and lower bounds for each
\mathfrak{F}(y)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists
c>0
such that for all
L\geq 2
,

\mathfrak{F}(y)\geq cL^{-3/2}\mbox{ for all }y\in[L/4,3L/4].

Proposition 3.5
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
L\geq 2

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(y)

\displaystyle\leq Cy^{-3/4}(L-y)^{-3/4}\mbox{ for all }y\in[L^{1/4},L-L^{1/4}-1],

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(y)

\displaystyle\leq CL^{-3/4}\mbox{ for all }y\in[0,L^{1/4}]\cup[L-L^{1/4},L-1].

Proposition
3.4
and
3.5
are the core technical parts of the paper and are proven in Sections
4
and
5
respectively. Let us prove Theorem
1.2
assuming them.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Thanks to Proposition
3.4
and (
1.7
) we have

\sigma_{L}^{2}=\sum_{y=0}^{L-1}\mathfrak{F}(y)\geq\sum_{y=\lceil L/4\rceil}^{\lfloor 3L/4\rfloor}\mathcal{F}(y)\geq cL^{-1/2}/4.

On the other hand, using (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{L}^{2}=\sum_{y=0}^{L-1}\mathfrak{F}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in[0,L^{1/4}]\cup[L-L^{1/4},L-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{F}(y)+\sum_{y\in[L^{1/4},L-L^{1/4}-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{F}(y)

\displaystyle\leq CL^{-1}+C\sum_{y=1}^{L-1}y^{-3/4}(L-y)^{3/4}\leq C^{\prime}\left(1+\int_{0}^{1}x^{-3/4}(1-x)^{-3/4}dx\right)\leq C^{\prime\prime}L^{-1/2}.

Thus, there exists
C>0
such that for all large enough
L
we have
C^{-1}L^{-1/2}\leq\sigma_{L}^{2}\leq CL^{-1/2}
. This verifies the first claim of the theorem.
When
L=\lambda t^{\alpha}
, using Theorem
1.1
we get that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}(0,t))}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{t}L^{-1/4}+C\sqrt{L}=C\lambda^{1/4}t^{1/2-\alpha/4}+C\sqrt{\lambda}t^{\alpha/2},

\displaystyle\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{H}(0,t))}

\displaystyle\geq C^{-1}\sqrt{t}L^{-1/4}-C\sqrt{L}\geq C^{-1}\lambda^{1/4}t^{1/2-\alpha/4}-C\lambda^{1/2}t^{\alpha/2}.

When
\alpha\in(0,2/3)
, or when
\lambda\in(0,\delta)
and
\alpha=2/3
for sufficiently small
\delta
, we have
C^{-1}\lambda^{1/4}t^{1/2-\alpha/4}\geq 1/2C\lambda^{1/2}t^{\alpha/2}
. Theorem
1.2
then follows from the above matching upper and lower bounds (up to constants).
∎

4.
Lower Bound for the asymptotic variance

In this section, we prove Proposition
3.4
. The key idea, as explained in the introduction, is to exploit the stochastic monotonicity inherent in the Gibbsian line ensemble structure of the stationary measures. This allows us to reduce the problem to a computation involving Brownian bridges. In Section
4.1
, we detail the monotonicity argument, and in Section
4.2
, we carry out the computation in the Brownian bridge setting.

4.1.
Local Brownianity in the bulk

We split the proof of Proposition
3.4
into four steps for clarity. We work under the assumptions
u,v\geq 0
with
u+v>0
in first three steps. In Step 4, we will explain how the proof can be modified to handle the simpler case
u=v=0
.

Step 1: Outer and Inner events.
In this step define a bunch of events that are necessary for our proof. Fix
M,\delta>0
and define

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{Loc}_{i}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{|\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}(y)|\leq M\mbox{ for all }x\in[y,y+1]\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Loc}_{i}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{|\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y)|\leq M\mbox{ for all }x\in[y,y+1]\big\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{Sep}_{i}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Lambda_{i}(y)\in[\sqrt{L},2\sqrt{L}],\ \Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y)\in[-2\sqrt{L},-\sqrt{L}]\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\Lambda_{i}(y\pm L\delta)-\Lambda_{i}(y)\in[-2\sqrt{\delta L},-\sqrt{\delta L}]\big\}.

\displaystyle\mathsf{MLD}_{i}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y\pm L\delta)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y)\in[-2\sqrt{\delta L},2\sqrt{\delta L}]\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{NMax}_{i}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}(y)\leq-\sqrt{\delta L}/2\mbox{ for all }x\in[0,y-L\delta]\cup[y+L\delta,L]\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}\big(\mathsf{Loc}_{i}(y)\cap\mathsf{Loc}_{i}^{\prime}(y)\mathsf{Sep}_{i}(y)\cap\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y)\cap\mathsf{MLD}_{i}^{\prime}(y)\cap\mathsf{NMax}_{i}(y)\big).

Let us briefly describe what do the above events mean. In words, the event
\mathsf{Loc}_{i}(y),\mathsf{Loc}_{i}^{\prime}(y)
controls the process
\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
on a
local scale
, namely, on an interval of size
1
around
y
. The event
\mathsf{Sep}_{i}(y)
fixes the location of
\Lambda_{i}(y)
and
\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y)
on certain
O(\sqrt{L})

O(\sqrt{L})

separation
between
\Lambda_{i}(y)

\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y)
. This is crucial and hence derives the name of the event. The
\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y),\mathsf{MLD}_{i}^{\prime}(y)
events are
macroscopic local difference
events that controls the difference of
\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
on two points that are separated by a small window of macroscopic length (i.e., of order
O(L)
). Finally the event
\mathsf{NMax}_{i}(y)
(
near-max
) essentially states that
\Lambda_{i}(y)
is close to the maximum of
\Lambda_{i}
in a certain quantitative sense.
Let us define the sigma field:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(y):=\sigma\big\{(\Lambda_{i}(x),\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x))\mid x\in[0,y-L\delta]\cup[y,y+1]\cup[y+L\delta,L],i=1,2,3\big\}.

\mathcal{F}(y)

Lemma 4.1
.

For each
\delta>0
, there exists
c_{\delta}>0
and
M=M(\delta)>0
such that
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)\big)\geq c_{\delta}.

We fix the above choice of
M
(depending on
\delta
) for the rest of the proof.

We now define a bunch of ‘inner’ events (‘inner’ in the sense that they will be measurable w.r.t. the processes restricted to
[y-L\delta,y]\cup[y+1,y+L\delta]
). First we define a cut-off function. Let
\chi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function such that
\chi|_{[-1,1]}=0
and
\chi|_{[-2,2]^{c}}=1
. For every
r>0
, we consider the function
q_{r}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined via

(4.2)

\displaystyle q_{r}(y)=\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{y}\chi(x)\chi\left(\frac{4}{r+2}x+\frac{2-3r}{r+2}\right)x^{-2/3}dx.

We notice that
q_{r}
is smooth,
q_{r}
is of order at
|y|^{1/3}
and
q^{\prime}_{r}(y)=\frac{1}{3}\chi(y)\chi\left(\frac{4}{r+2}y+\frac{2-3r}{r+2}\right)y^{-2/3}
, so
q_{r}^{\prime}(y)=0
when
y\in[0,1]\cap[\frac{r}{2}-1,r]
. A plot of the
q_{r}|_{y\geq 1}
function is given in Figure
6
.

We set
q:=q_{L\delta}
and define the following events:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y+1)-\Lambda_{2}(y+1)\leq 1-q(|x-y-1|)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{2}(y+1)-\Lambda_{3}(y+1)\leq 1-q(|x-y-1|)

\displaystyle\mbox{ for all }x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)\leq 1-q(|x-y|)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{2}(y)-\Lambda_{3}(y)\leq 1-q(|x-y|)\mbox{ for all }x\in[y-L\delta,y]\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x)\geq\sqrt{L}\mbox{ for all }x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]\mbox{ and }i=1,2,3\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x)\geq\sqrt{L}\mbox{ for all }x\in[y-L\delta,y]\mbox{ and }i=1,2,3\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)\cap\mathsf{B}_{+}(y).

The importance of these events is that on the event
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)\cap\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)
, the random variable appearing in (
1.8
) is bounded below uniformly. We will prove this in the next step.

Step 2: Reduction to Brownian bridges.
We claim that there exists a deterministic constant
\widetilde{c}_{\delta}>0
such that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\int\limits_{y}^{y+1}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+2\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-2\Lambda_{2}(y)-\Lambda_{3}(y)}dx}{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}dx\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}dx}\geq\widetilde{c}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)\cap\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}

holds almost surely for all
y\in[L/4,3L/4]
and
L
large enough. This follows easily by noticing that

•

The
\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}\mathsf{Loc}_{i}(y)
event controls the integrals on the range
[y,y+1]
.

•

The
\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)
event controls the integrals on the range
[y-L\delta,y]
and
[y+1,y+L\delta]
.

•

The
\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}(\mathsf{NMax}_{i}(y)\cap\mathsf{Loc}_{i}(y))
event controls the integrals on the range
[0,y-L\delta]
,
[y+L\delta,L]
.

Given (
4.4
), we thus have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(y)\geq\widetilde{c}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)\cap\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\big]=\widetilde{c}_{\delta}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\mid\mathcal{F}(y)\big]\big].

Note that the law of
(\Lambda_{i}(x),\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x))_{x\in[y-L\delta,y]\cup[y+1,y+L\delta]}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}(y)
are absolutely continuous w.r.t. two independent copies of pair of Brownian bridges (with endpoints that are in
\mathcal{F}(y)
, i.e., they are random) with a Radon-Nikodym derivative of the form:

\displaystyle W_{i,+}

\displaystyle:=\exp\bigg(-\int_{y+1}^{y+L\delta}e^{-(\Lambda_{i}(s)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(s))}ds\bigg),\quad W_{i,-}:=\exp\bigg(-\int_{y-L\delta}^{y}e^{-(\Lambda_{i}(s)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(s))}ds\bigg).

More precisely, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\mid\mathcal{F}(y)\big]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{B}\bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}W_{i,+}W_{i,-}\bigg]}{\mathbb{E}_{B}\bigg[\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}W_{i,+}W_{i,-}\bigg]}\geq\mathbb{E}_{B}\bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}W_{i,+}W_{i,-}\bigg],

where we write
\mathbb{E}_{B}
to denote that the expectation is computed under Brownian bridge laws. The above inequality follows from the fact that the
W_{i,\pm}
s are all less than
1
. Recall the
\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)
and
\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)
events from (
4.3
) (
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)
is contained in both of them). Note that under
\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)\cap\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)
,
W_{i,\pm}\geq\gamma_{\delta}
for some
\gamma_{\delta}>0
for all
L\geq 1
. Thus,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{B}\bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}W_{i,+}W_{i,-}\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{\delta}^{6}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma_{\delta}^{6}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)\big)\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)).

Since the conditional processes on
[y-L\delta,y]
and
[y+1,y+L\delta]
are independent, we have
\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)\big)=\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)\big)
. We have the following estimate on
\mathbb{P}_{B}(\mathsf{A}_{\pm}(y))
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Recall the
\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y)
event from (
4.1
). There exists
c_{\delta}^{\prime}>0
such that for all
y\in[L/4,3L/4]
we have

\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{i=1}^{3}\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y)}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{B}(\mathsf{A}_{\pm}(y))\geq c_{\delta}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{i=1}^{3}\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y)}\cdot L^{-3/4}.

The proof of the above lemma follows from exact calculations of Brownian motion probability and is deferred to next subsection.
Similarly we also have
\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)\big)=\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{-}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)\big)
. We claim that there exists
\delta>0
small enough such that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{\pm}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{\pm}(y)\big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}.

We shall prove (
4.8
) via a stochastic monotonicity argument in the next step. Note that combining (
4.6
), (
4.7
), Lemma
4.2
, and the claim in (
4.8
), we get that there exists
c_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}>0
such that for all
y\in[L/4,3L/4]
and
L
large enough the following deterministic bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{in}}(y)}\mid\mathcal{F}(y)\big]\geq c_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}\cdot L^{-3/2}.

holds for some
c_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}>0
. Plugging this back in (

) and in view of Lemma

we have
\mathfrak{F}(y)\geq c_{\delta}c_{\delta}^{\prime}c_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}L^{-3/2}
. This completes the proof of the proposition modulo (
4.8
) (and Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
).

Step 3: Stochastic monotonicity argument.

In this step we prove (
4.8
). We shall demonstrate the bound only for the quantity
\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)
, the other part being analogous. Set
\overline{\Lambda_{i}}(x):=\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}(y+1)
and
\overline{\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}}(x):=\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x)-\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(y+1)
. and define events

\displaystyle\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\overline{\Lambda_{i}}(x)\leq\sqrt{L}/8\mbox{ for all }x\in[y+1,y-L\delta]\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\overline{\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}}(x)\leq\sqrt{L}/8\mbox{ for all }x\in[y+1,y-L\delta]\big\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{Fall}_{i,+}(y)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\overline{\Lambda_{i}}(x)\geq-\sqrt{L}/2\mbox{ for all }x\in[y+1,y-L\delta]\big\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)\cap\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)\supset\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)\cap\mathsf{Fall}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)

which implies

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{B}_{+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y))\geq\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{out}}(y)}\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)\cap\mathsf{Fall}_{i,+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)).

So, if we can control
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y))
and
\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Fall}_{i,+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y))
, we are done. We shall demonstrate this control using stochastic monotonicity.
Fix an
\varepsilon>0
. We claim that
we can choose
\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y))\geq 1-\varepsilon,\quad\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y))\geq 1-\varepsilon.

We shall prove this claim only for the
\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)
event. Rest cases are analogous. Let us define the
\sigma
-field

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\sigma\{\mathcal{F}(y)\cup\sigma\big\{(\Lambda_{i}(x),\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}(x))\mid x\in[0,L],i=2,3\big\}\big\}

Conditioned on
\mathcal{G}
, under
\mathbb{P}_{B}
law,
\Lambda_{1}(\cdot)
is still a Brownian bridge on
[y+1,y+L\delta]
from
\Lambda_{1}(y+1)
to
\Lambda_{1}(y+L\delta)
. Let us denote this conditional law as
\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}
. Note that
\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)
and
\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)
are measurable events w.r.t.
\sigma\{\Lambda_{1}(x)\mid x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]\}\cup\mathcal{G}
. Let us set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{+}(y):=\bigcap_{x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]}\big\{\overline{\Lambda_{1}}(x)\leq 1-q(|x-y-1|)-\overline{\Lambda_{2}}(x)\big\}.

\overline{\Lambda_{2}}
are measurable w.r.t.
\mathcal{G}
. Thus the above event can be interpreted as an event which requires
\overline{\Lambda_{1}}(x)
to be less than a given barrier
f(x):=1-q(|x-y-1|)-\overline{\Lambda_{2}}(x)
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\mid\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{+}(y)\big)=\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{f}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\big)

where
\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{f}
is the law of the Brownian bridge
B
conditioned to be less than the barrier
f
. Now
\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)
event is decreasing as we increase the boundaries. So by stochastic monotonicity, taking
f\to\infty
pointwise we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{f}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\big)\geq\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\big).

The latter can be assumed to be larger than
1-\varepsilon
by taking
\delta
small enough depending only on
\varepsilon>0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{E}}{\mathbb{E}_{B}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\cap\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{+}(y)}\mid\mathcal{G}\big]}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{E}}{\mathbb{E}_{B}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{+}(y)}\mid\mathcal{G}\big]}.

where
\mathsf{E}:=\bigcap_{x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]}\{\overline{\Lambda_{2}}(x)\leq 1-q(|x-y-1|)-\overline{\Lambda_{3}}(x)\}
. Taking expectation w.r.t.
\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}
on both sides, we get that

\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)

which is equivalent to
\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{1,+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)\geq 1-\varepsilon
. This verifies (
4.10
).

Next we claim that by taking
\delta
a bit smaller we can ensure

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{Fall}_{i,+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y))\geq 1-3\varepsilon.

We shall again prove this only for
i=1
. In fact we shall prove something stronger:

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)\geq 1-3\varepsilon.

holds for all small enough
\delta
. Continuing with the notations of the previous claim, let us consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{f}\big(\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)\big).

The
\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)
is decreasing as we decrease the boundaries. Note that in presence of
\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)
event, we have
f(x)\geq R:=1-L^{1/3}-\sqrt{L}/8
. We decrease the barrier to
R
and also decrease the boundaries of the Brownian bridge: from
\Lambda_{1}(y+1)
to
\Lambda_{1}(y+1)-\sqrt{L}/4
and from
\Lambda_{1}(y+L\delta)
to
\Lambda_{1}(y+L\delta)-\sqrt{L}/4
to get a new law which we denote by
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{R}
. Let us denote the Brownian bridge under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{R}
as
\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(\cdot)
. Note that under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{R}
both
\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)
and
\sup_{x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]}\{\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y+1)\leq R\}
events are likely. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)}\mathbb{P}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{f}\big(\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{B|\mathcal{G}}^{R}\big(\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\ \mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{B|\mathcal{G}}\bigg(\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)\cap\sup_{x\in[y+1,y+L\delta]}\left\{\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y+1)\leq R\right\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)}.

where the last inequality follows by taking
\delta
small enough. Multiplying by
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{E}}
and then taking expectation w.r.t.
\Lambda_{2},\Lambda_{3}
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Fall}_{1,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\mid\mathcal{F}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{Rise}_{2,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{Rise}_{3,+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\mid\mathcal{F}(y)\big)\geq(1-3\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\mid\mathcal{F}(y)\big).

where the last inequality follow from (
4.10
). This proves (
4.11
). Thus by union bound we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{B}\big(\bigcap_{i=1}^{3}\mathsf{Rise}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)\cap\mathsf{Fall}_{i,+}^{\prime}(y)\mid\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\big)\geq 1-12\varepsilon.

Taking
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{24}
fixes our choice of
\delta
and in view of (
4.9
) completes the proof of (
4.8
).

Step 4.
u=v=0
case.
Let us explain the modification needed for
u=v=0
. First note that the following slightly stronger version of (
4.4
) holds:

\displaystyle\frac{\int\limits_{y}^{y+1}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+2\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-2\Lambda_{2}(y)-\Lambda_{3}(y)}dx}{\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{1}(x)+\Lambda_{2}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}dx\int\limits_{0}^{L}e^{\Lambda_{2}(x)+\Lambda_{3}(x)-\Lambda_{1}(y)-\Lambda_{2}(y)}dx}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\geq\widetilde{c}_{\delta}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{i=1}^{3}(\mathsf{Loc}_{i}(y)\cap\mathsf{MLD}_{i}(y)\cap\mathsf{NMax}_{i}(y))\cap\mathsf{A}_{+}(y)\cap\mathsf{A}_{-}(y)}.

Indeed the events on the right hand side of the above equation are include the events that are used to control the integrals appearing on the left hand side (as noted after (
4.4
)). Taking expectations, this leads to the following analog of (
4.5

to
0
. And similarly,
(\Lambda_{i}(x)-\Lambda_{i}(y+1))_{x\in[y+1,L]}
conditioned on
\mathcal{G}(y)
is stochastically larger than a Brownian bridge
(\mathfrak{B}_{i}^{(2)}(x))_{x\in[y+1,L]}
from
0
to
-\sqrt{L-y}\cdot U_{i}^{(2)}
. Thus, the inner expectation in (
5.4
) is upper bounded by Brownian bridge analog of the same expression. The core technical result that completes the upper bound argument is the following estimate on this Brownian bridge analog.

Proposition 5.2
.

Fix
L\geq 1
,
L_{1},L_{2}\in[L^{1/4},L/2]
. and
b\geq 1
. Suppose
(\mathfrak{B}_{i}^{1})_{i=1,2,3,j=1,2}
are independent Brownian bridges on
[0,L_{j}]
with
\mathfrak{B}_{i}^{j}(0)=0
and
\mathfrak{B}_{i}^{j}(L_{j})=-b\sqrt{L_{j}}
. There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{L_{j}}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{j}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}^{j}(x)}dx\right)^{-1}\right]\leq C\cdot e^{Cb^{3/2}}L_{j}^{-3/4},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{L_{1}}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{1}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}^{1}(x)}dx+\int_{0}^{L_{2}}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{2}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}^{2}(x)}dx\right)^{-1}\right]\leq C\cdot e^{Cb^{3/2}}L_{1}^{-3/4}L_{2}^{-3/4}.

Let us assume Proposition
5.2
for a moment. Set
U:=\max_{i,j}U_{i}^{(j)}
.
Thanks to (
5.6
), and the stochastic monotonicity argument explained earlier, for
y\in[L^{1/4},L-L^{1/4}]
we have

r.h.s. of (
5.3
)

\displaystyle\leq Cy^{-3/4}(L-y)^{-3/4}\mathbb{E}\bigg[A(y)R(y)^{-2}\exp(CU^{3/2})\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq Cy^{-3/4}(L-y)^{-3/4}\mathbb{E}\big[A(y)^{-3}\big]\mathbb{E}\big[R(y)^{-6}\big]\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp(3CU^{3/2})\bigg],

where the last inequality follows via Hölder inequality. By Lemma
5.1
, the above three expectation are uniformly bounded. Thus we have (
3.16
). For
y\in[0,L^{1/4}]\cup[L-L^{1/4},L-1]
, we rely on (
5.5
) instead to derive (
3.17
). This completes the proof of the upper bound when

with
u+v>0
.

When

, the stationary measure is simply a Brownian motion. In this case, Lemma
5.1
still holds by direct estimates for Brownian motion. The stochastic monotonicity argument used earlier is not required here, as the underlying laws are already those of Brownian bridges. One can then directly apply Proposition
5.2
to the inner expectation in (

) to obtain the desired upper bound.

5.2.
Framework for the proof of Proposition
5.2

We will prove (
5.6
) only, as the proof of (
5.5
) is similar and simpler. The case

is relatively straightforward. Indeed, by Jensen inequality and Brownian motion moment computation we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{L_{j}}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{j}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}^{j}(x)}dx\right)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=1}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{k}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}^{k}(x)}dx\right)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathfrak{B}_{k}^{j}(x)+\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}^{j}(x)}]dx\right)^{-1}\leq Ce^{Cb/\sqrt{L_{1}}+Cb/\sqrt{L_{2}}}.

Note that if
b\geq\log L
, we have

\displaystyle Ce^{Cb/\sqrt{L_{1}}+Cb/\sqrt{L_{2}}}\leq C^{\prime}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime}b^{3/2}}\leq C^{\prime}\cdot e^{C^{\prime}b^{3/2}}L_{1}^{-3/4}L_{2}^{-3/4}

for some

. This verifies (
5.6
). So, we may assume
b\leq\log L
for all

for the rest of the proof.

As in Section
4.2.2
, we begin by rewriting the expression in (
5.6
) in terms of two-dimensional Brownian bridges.
Set
\mathbf{w}_{j}:=\bigg(b\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}L_{j}},0\bigg)
. Let
V^{i}
be independent planar Brownian bridges of length
L_{i}
from the origin to
\mathbf{w}_{i}
. We have

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mbox{r.h.s.~of~\eqref{twol1l2bd}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{L_{1}}e^{v_{k}\cdot V^{1}(x)}dx+\int_{0}^{L_{2}}e^{v_{k}\cdot V^{2}(x)}dx\right)^{-1}\right].

We remark that in
[
DGK23
]
, the authors analyze expressions of the form

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{L}e^{v_{k}\cdot V(x)}\,dx\right)^{-1}\right],

where
V
is a two-dimensional Brownian bridge of length
L
from the origin to origin. They show that this quantity is of order
L^{-3/2}
. The main contribution to the expectation arises when the path
V
remains confined to the wedge
N_{a}=\omega^{-1}((-\infty,a])
. Since the Brownian bridge starts and ends at the origin—which lies close to the boundary of the wedge—maintaining this constraint incurs a probability of
L^{-3/4}
for each endpoint, leading to an overall contribution of
L^{-3/4}\cdot L^{-3/4}=L^{-3/2}
. In our case, the Brownian bridges

start at the origin but terminate at points in the bulk, specifically at
\mathbf{w}_{j}=\left(b\sqrt{2L_{j}/3},0\right)
. As a result, the probability that
V^{j}
remains confined to the wedge

is of order
L_{j}^{-3/4}
.

To show this rigorously, we define few constants, events, and stopping times.

Appendix A
Moment bounds of Open SHE

In this section, we collect moment bounds for the solution of the open SHE (
1.3
) with Robin boundary conditions (
1.4
). Most of the results in this appendix are either proved in the literature or follow by straightforward adaptations of standard arguments.

Set
A=(u-\frac{1}{2})
and
B=-(v-\frac{1}{2})
for simplicity.
We first discuss properties of the solution of the heat equation with Robin boundary conditions:

(A.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial z^{2}},\quad z\in[0,L],\;t>0

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0}=Av(t,0),\;\left.\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right|_{z=L}=Bv(t,L).

The following lemma provides the description of the fundamental solution to the above equation.

Lemma A.1
.

For each
n\geq 0
, let
\kappa_{n}\in((n-\frac{1}{2})\pi,(n+\frac{1}{2})\pi)
be the unique solution to

\tan(L\kappa_{n})=\frac{\kappa_{n}(A-B)}{\kappa_{n}^{2}+AB}

in the interval
((n-\frac{1}{2})\pi,(n+\frac{1}{2})\pi)
.
Set
\psi_{n}(z)=a_{n}\cos\left(\kappa_{n}z\right)+a_{n}\frac{A}{\kappa_{n}}\sin\left(\kappa_{n}z\right)
, where

a_{n}^{-2}:=\int_{0}^{L}\bigg(\cos(\kappa_{n}x)+\frac{A}{\kappa_{n}}\sin(\kappa_{n}x)\bigg)^{2}dx.

The kernel

(A.2)

K_{t}^{A,B}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\kappa_{n}^{2}}\psi_{n}(x)\psi_{n}(y),

solves (
A.1
) with Dirac delta initial data
\delta_{0}
.

Remark A.2
.

It is a straightforward calculation to verify that
\lim_{n}a_{n}^{-2}=\frac{L}{2}
, therefore
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a bounded sequence.

Proof.

We first note that there exists a unique weak solution to (
A.1
). Consider the space
\mathcal{A}=\{u\in H^{2}([0,L]):u_{x}(0)=Au(0),\;u_{x}(L)=Bu(L)\}
, where the equalities are taken in the sense of traces. We observe that the second derivative operator (i.e the Laplacian)
u\mapsto u_{xx}
is selfadjoint in
\mathcal{A}
. Indeed, after integration by parts we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}u_{xx}(x)v(x)dx-\int_{0}^{L}u(x)v_{xx}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\bigg[u_{x}(x)v(x)\bigg]_{0}^{L}-\bigg[u(x)v_{x}(x)\bigg]_{0}^{L}

\displaystyle=Bu(L)v(L)-Au(0)v(0)-(Bu(L)v(L)-Au(0)v(0))=0.

Since the Laplacian is selfadjoint, we deduce that
\Delta^{-1}
is also selfadjoint and, by standard theory of elliptic equations, it is also compact. Therefore the spectral theorem can be applied and hence we can consider
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the eigenvalues of the problem

(A.3)

\begin{cases}(\psi(x))_{xx}=\lambda\psi(x),\;\;x\in[0,L],\\
\psi_{x}(0)=A\psi(0),\;\psi_{x}(L)=B\psi(L).\end{cases}

We call
\psi_{n}(x)
the eigenvector that corresponds to the eigenvalue

because
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of

Bv(t,L),&\text{ if }x=L.\end{cases}

In addition, for

such that

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt+C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{L}(K_{t-s}(x,w)-K_{t-s}(y,w))\mathcal{Z}(w,s)\xi(s,w)dwds\bigg|^{p}\bigg].

(
