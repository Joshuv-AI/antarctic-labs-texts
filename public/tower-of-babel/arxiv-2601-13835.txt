The Role of Prosodic and Lexical Cues in Turn-Taking with Self-Supervised Speech Representations
Sam O’Connor Russell, Delphine Charuau, Naomi Harte

Abstract
Fluid turn-taking remains a key challenge in human-robot interaction. Self-supervised speech representations have driven many advances, but it remains unclear whether self-supervised speech representation-based turn-taking models rely on prosodic cues, lexical cues or both. We introduce a vocoder-based approach to control prosody and lexical cues in speech more cleanly than prior work. This allows us to probe the voice-activity projection model, an self-supervised speech representation-based turn-taking model. We find that prediction on prosody-matched, unintelligible noise is similar to accuracy on clean speech. This reveals both prosodic and lexical cues support turn-taking, but either can be used in isolation. Hence, future models may only require prosody, providing privacy and potential performance benefits. When either prosodic or lexical information is disrupted, the model exploits the other without further training, indicating they are encoded in self-supervised speech representations with limited interdependence. Results are consistent in CPC-based and wav2vec2.0 self-supervised speech representations. We discuss our findings and highlight a number of directions for future work. All code is available to support future research.

Introduction

Turn-taking is fundamental to human interaction, allowing conversations to flow smoothly
[
10
]
. As speech planning takes longer than transitions between speakers, listeners predict when to speak ahead of time, supported by lexical and prosodic cues
[
10
]
. Achieving fluid turn-taking is critical for human-robot interaction
[
21
]
. However, most systems detect end-of-turn silence, leading to unnatural delays
[
21
]
.

Predictive turn-taking models address this by predicting speaker changes ahead of time
[
7
,
19
,
11
]
. Feature engineering in earlier models, e.g. GeMAPS
[
19
]
, has since been replaced by self-supervised speech representations
[
7
,
11
]
. self-supervised speech representations encode rich phonetic, lexical and prosodic cues
[
14
,
8
,
23
]
, highly relevant to turn-taking
[
10
,
5
]
. Despite their efficacy, the richness of speech is collapsed into a single representation, making it challenging to establish what drives prediction. Prior work has shown self-supervised speech representation-based turn-taking is quite robust to the flattening of pitch or intensity
[
7
]
. Lexical information may therefore support prediction. Alternatively, as only pitch or intensity was flattened, the model may rely on the unmodified cue. Furthermore, we recently found background noise severely impacts turn-taking prediction
[
20
]
. As noise did not fully degrade lexical content, prosodic and lexical cues in self-supervised speech representation may be partially interdependent.

These challenges reflect the wider issues around self-supervised speech representation use in speech processing
[
8
]
. Their black-box nature hinders interpretability, limiting their ability to comply with ethics standards when decisions need to be explainable
[
8
]
. This has motivated a number of methods for studying self-supervised speech representations. Probing provides a layer-wise analysis, but labels are required as small classifiers are trained with supervised learning
[
14
]
. Input corruption can test sensitivity to a specific cue by removing it, without the need for labels
[
6
]
. A downside is common manipulations like low-pass filtering disrupt both intelligibility and prosody
[
15
]
. Neuroscience routinely uses vocoders to cleanly isolate prosody while removing intelligibility, or vice versa
[
3
,
13
]
. To our knowledge, these techniques have not been applied to turn-taking or self-supervised speech representation interpretability.

Motivated by the need for greater self-supervised speech representation interpretability and recent work in turn-taking, we ask: (i) do self-supervised speech representations support turn-taking primarily via prosodic cues, lexical cues, or both?; and (ii) are prosodic and lexical cues in self-supervised speech representations interdependent? i.e., can one be disrupted whilst the other remains useful?

Our contributions are threefold. Firstly, we introduce a vocoder method to cleanly control the prosodic and lexical information in speech. Secondly, we apply this method to turn-taking, showing that self-supervised speech representation encodes both cues independently: when one is removed, the other is exploited without further training. Finally, we show that unintelligible noise matched to speech prosody achieves similar performance to clean speech. This motivates future prosody-only models, which have performance and privacy benefits. We conclude with a discussion of our findings for both self-supervised speech representation and turn-taking.

Self-supervised speech representations and turn-taking

Self-supervised speech representations have driven advances in speech processing as they capture rich representations of phonetics, prosody and semantics
[
14
,
8
]
; all of which are relevant to turn-taking
[
10
]
. The voice-activity projection
[
7
]
model is a state-of-the-art transformer-based turn-taking model leveraging self-supervised speech representation.
The voice-activity projection model is widely studied
[
20
,
6
,
9
]
, though questions concerning its operation remain. Firstly, which cues drive prediction? An earlier study showed that when either pitch or intensity was flattened, performance fell but remained above baseline
[
6
]
. Thus, lexical cues might dominate prediction, but as only pitch or intensity was flattened, further work is needed. Secondly, it is unclear whether cues from speech captured by self-supervised speech representations are interdependent. Our recent study suggests interdependence: light noise, which disrupts prosody but leaves speech mostly intelligible, leads to poor prediction
[
20
]
.

Several methods to better understand self-supervised speech representation models exist. Probing provides a layer-wise analysis
[
14
]
. Input corruption directly manipulates speech to understand how a cue is captured by self-supervised speech representations. Common methods such as low-pass filtering impact both intelligibility and prosody, limiting insights
[
7
,
15
]
. Methods which more cleanly isolate cues are used in neuroscience to study language processing
[
3
,
13
]
akin to the highly entangled nature of self-supervised speech representations. Vocoders decompose speech into spectral and amplitude components, enabling isolation of prosodic and lexical cues
[
3
]
. Yet to our knowledge, they have not been applied to turn-taking with self-supervised speech representation.

5
Discussion of Key Findings

Turn-taking remains a key unsolved problem in human-robot interaction. Self-supervised speech representations have transformed turn-taking and speech processing, though interpretability remains challenging. In this paper, we take a new approach to analysis, isolating prosodic and lexical information in speech.
We showed a VAP turn-taking model trained on noise matched to original prosody retains 91% of the accuracy of a model trained and tested on clean speech. Thus prosody alone can function as a turn-taking cue. Similarly, speech with flat prosody and just lexical cues supports turn-taking with high accuracy. Performance is still better when both prosodic and lexical cues are present (clean speech). To answer of our first research question, both prosody and lexical cues can function as independent turn-taking predictors, and both support prediction.

Our second question explored whether prosodic and lexical cues are interdependent in self-supervised speech representations. Performance is remarkably similar in unintelligible speech matched to the original prosody (
\approx 70\%
), even when the model was trained on clean speech (
\approx 66\%
). Hence, an self-supervised speech representation-based turn-taking model can rely on prosody when lexical information is degraded and vice-versa. These cues are therefore not interdependent in self-supervised speech representation. We showed this was also true for a wav2vec2.0 model.

Our work has revealed much about self-supervised speech representation-based turn-taking and self-supervised speech representations, raising a number of directions for future work. Our method isolates these cues more cleanly than prior work
[
15
]
. It should therefore be used to clarify the extent to which self-supervised speech representations exploit lexical information in outperforming engineered prosodic features, not addressed in prior work
[
22
]
. Secondly, based on our finding that prosody alone supports turn-taking, lightweight prosody-based models should be considered. Another advantage is that as we have shown, isolating prosody removes useful lexical information. This has significant privacy advantages e.g. sensitive topics removed. Furthermore, as both the spectral envelope and pitch contours display individual speaker differences
[
1
]
, using the prosodic contour reduces, but does not fully eliminate, identifiable information. Finally, future work should explore cross-linguistic generalisation of self-supervised speech representations with our method, building on prior work
[
9
]
.

A limitation is the potential for residual phonetic information to persist in prosody-matched noise, though this is unlikely, as all spectral information is removed. Furthermore, the WER value confirms no useful information persists. Finally, we only consider English in the CANDOR corpus and future work should consider other corpora. However, this corpus is large (850 hr) and representative, containing many speakers across
\approx
1600 dyads of casual talk.

6
Conclusion

We revealed that self-supervised speech representation independently encode prosodic and lexical cues: when one is disrupted, self-supervised speech representation-based turn-taking models reliably use the other without additional training. We further showed that the prosodic contour is sufficient for turn-taking, with performance on noise matched to prosody nearly matching clean speech performance. Our findings enhance self-supervised speech representation interpretability, motivating future work in prosody-only turn-taking models, which have important advantages including privacy.
