Predicting intracranial hypertension as a secondary insult using the pathological peptidome of brain injured patients
Authors: Kelly E. Platfoot, Alexander Satoski, Anil K. Roy, Alex B. Valadka, Andrew K. Ottens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracranial hypertension (IHT) is a common secondary insult that worsens morbidity and death after acute brain injury from traumatic and hemorrhagic events. Suspicion of IHT is determined with a combination of neurological examination and imaging but is often hampered by sedation and limited by cost and radiation exposure for repeated assessment. Given the uncertainty, many at risk for IHT may not have intracranial pressure (ICP) monitors inserted when weighed against the risks of this invasive surgical procedure. Thus, the availability of blood-based biomarkers diagnostic of IHT would represent a major advance in neurointensive care. Pathological processes of brain injury, such as IHT-causing vasogenic edema, involve intra- and extra-cellular proteolytic activity, producing small-sized peptide byproducts cleared through waste streams. Thus, novel peptide content introduced to the blood with brain injury was assessed in this early-phase hypothesis generating study for its biomarker potential to prognose IHT using samples from thirteen patients that either had acute brain injury and ICP monitors inserted or had exclusively extra-cranial injuries as hospitalized control cases. Patients were then classified into high ICP (n = 6) or normotensive (n = 7) populations. Mass spectrometry was performed to build a peptide panel prognostic of high ICP (> 20 mmHg) events within 72 h of injury. Seventy-nine peptides were identified that differentiated patients with high ICP events through an increase in serum concentrations that preceded ICP elevation within the 72-hour window. Seven top-performing peptides were selected for the final model, which were derived from COL5A2, MICAL2, MRTFA, PCSK1, PRDM16, PRSS56, and SMAD6 proteins. Receiver operating characteristic curves showed that the top-ranked peptides achieved an area under the curve of 0.97. Functionally, these peptides were associated with perivascular dynamics relevant to vasogenic edema. The assay remained prognostic throughout the 72-h window, supporting its potential longitudinal use in the intensive care setting. In conclusion, peptides produced during brain injury pathobiology promise a new biomarker source with the potential to inform on secondary insults such as IHT, supporting further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46313-3.

Introduction

Intracranial hypertension (IHT) is a common secondary insult that worsens outcome after acute brain injuries from causes such as trauma or intracerebral hemorrhage1. While estimates vary, close to half of those hospitalized for acute brain injury exhibit further complication due to IHT2,3. Cerebral edema can develop if abnormal extracellular ion concentrations and extravasated plasma factors cause an increase in interstitial fluid volume and subsequent tissue swelling. Excess fluid within the confines of the skull results in IHT as intracranial pressure (ICP) is elevated, resulting in ischemia and secondary injury4,5. Indeed, cerebral edema related IHT accounts for nearly 50% of mortality after severe traumatic brain injury (TBI)6. Yet, despite the known relationship between high ICP and worsened patient outcome7,8, it remains difficult to diagnose IHT during acute neurointensive care9. Many patients at risk for developing IHT are comatose, often precluding the use of neurological evaluations to screen for high ICP. Further concerns about repeated radiation exposure, the risks of patient transport, and cost for imaging studies, further challenge IHT diagnosis, which can happen sporadically within hours to days after injury. While those at risk may undergo invasive ICP monitoring, the uncertainty in estimating risk for IHT has to be weighed against the inherent risk of the invasive procedure10,11. Thus, alternative means to prognose those at risk of, or currently experiencing, high IHT is warranted.

Blood-based biomarkers are recognized for their potential to characterize brain injury and guide patient care. Most investigations have used protein biomarkers, such as the FDA-approved GFAP and UCH-L1 biomarkers used to guide head CT (computed tomography) orders in patients with mild TBI within 12 h of injury12–15. However, no blood-based biomarkers are approved to guide ongoing brain injury intensive care and to assess secondary insults such as IHT. This may be in part due to the difficulty in detecting brain-originating protein macromolecules in peripheral fluids16. Protein macromolecules are size-restricted in their diffusion away from their cellular source out into waste clearance streams, which is complicated further by the compromise of clearance pathways over time, in part because of IHT causing cerebral edema17–19. Tissue remodeling and cellular responses in the wake of injury involves substantially elevated proteolytic activity, leaving protein biomarkers also susceptible to degradation within the brain as well as in the periphery20. However, peptide products of such proteolytic activity are several-fold smaller in size compared with their originating proteins and thus more readily efflux from the brain, with less likelihood of further degradation or binding. Thus, endogenously produced peptides represent a promising and largely untapped potential source of brain injury biomarkers. Previous work from our lab described the utility of the TBI-responsive urinary peptidome in predicting patient recovery during inpatient rehabilitation21,22. Work from others since has investigated cleaved tau, neurogranin, vimentin, and myelin basic protein (MBP) as peptide biomarkers of primary injury from TBI23–25. The early-phase hypothesis generating study presented here assesses a peptidomic biomarker discovery approach for its potential to develop a prognostic model of IHT as a secondary outcome in acute brain injured patients, differentiated from those who remain normotensive during the first 72 h of intensive care treatment.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment

Adult patients admitted to VCU Health were enrolled with informed consent within 18 h of injury under the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board protocol HM20010330 (approved 07/07/2025). The study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki’s principles. Informed consent was obtained by study staff within 24 h of injury, which included consent to publish findings from study data. Consent was obtained from a legally authorized representative in cases where the patient was incapacitated or otherwise unable to give consent. Patients of both sexes between the ages 16 to 80 who presented to the emergency department with a prehospital injury consistent with trauma or a presumptive diagnosis of stroke were considered for enrollment. A total of 13 patients between the ages of 26 and 64 with no prior history of neurological disease could be used in this study. All subjects had to have remained in the intensive care unit for at least 72 h (Table 1). Subjects having acute brain injury after traumatic or hemorrhagic events, confirmed by positive head CT abnormalities, had to have undergone surgical placement of an external ventricular drain (EVD) for ICP monitoring. Additional normotensive subjects were selected from hospitalized polytrauma patients having had no brain injury events and being negative for head CT findings. Extra-cranial traumatic injuries were balanced between the two groups as noted for all subjects in Table 1, and included injuries to the chest, abdomen, and/or musculoskeletal system. Exclusion criteria were anemia or significant blood loss (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL), massive transfusion (> 4 units packed red blood cells in 1 h and ongoing need for transfusion), positive pregnancy test, or being a ward of the state. Patient data were deidentified and transferred to a study database on the university’s REDCap research platform. A summary of subject-specific information is provided in Table 1.

ICP recording

EVDs were surgically placed in brain injured patients in accordance with current guidelines from the Brain Trauma Foundation. Qualifying criteria included GCS score ≤ 8 and evidence of structural brain damage on initial head CT scan, or GCS score > 8 and evidence of structural brain damage on CT scan with high risk for progression. ICP measurements were recorded following EVD placement using the Moberg Component Neuromonitoring System on a Philips IntelliVue bedside device. Patients that exhibited an elevated ICP within 72 h of injury, defined as ICP ≥ 20 mm Hg for more than 60 min using a two-hour rolling average, were counted in the high ICP group, while the remaining subjects were placed in the normal ICP group.

Sample collection, preparation, and analysis

Blood samples (5 mL) were drawn and processed in SST™ serum separation tubes (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) with serum collected into low protein binding storage tubes kept at -80 °C. Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor (added to 1X concentration, Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL) was used to limit ex vivo proteolytic degradation of the samples. Serum samples were then prepared for peptidomic mass spectrometry analysis as previously described26. Briefly, 40 µL serum aliquots (volume balanced) were five-fold diluted in mass spectrometry-grade water, clarified by ultrafiltration (0.22 μm), reduced in 18 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine, alkylated using 30 mM iodoacetamide, and denatured with 20% acetonitrile and 0.4% formic acid. Processed samples were stored in Waters Total Recovery autosampler vials at -80 °C.

Peptidomic serum samples (8 µL injected) were separated and analyzed as previously described26. Solid phase lipid extraction and desalting were performed on a Waters Symmetry C18 trapping column. Peptide content was gradient separated (6% to 35% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid modified, 55 °C) on a Waters NanoAcquity (Milford, MA) ultra performance liquid chromatography system with a 150 mm X 75 μm HSS T3 capillary column. Eluted peptides were detected on a Waters Synapt G2-Si HDMS tandem mass spectrometer, with ion mobility enabled, and operated in data-independent acquisition mode with tandem mass spectra collected between 50 and 1950 m/z. Spectral peak picking and precursor-to-product binning were performed using PLGS v3.03 with peptide ions aligned across biological replicates with EndogeSeq by their retention time (± 1.5 min), drift time (± 2 bins), and charged-reduced mass (MH+, ± 8 ppm). The resultant data were filtered to retain reproducible peptides detected in at least 6 of 13 patients with imputation performed in cases of an ICP group-selective, non-random drop below the limit of detection using a value set centered at the limit of quantification with variance set to the median coefficient for that group. Ion intensity values were log2 transformed to be normally distributed.

Summary statistics

The high ICP and normotensive patients were assessed for group differences important to the study. Student’s t-tests were performed to compare the ages and GCS scores between high and normal ICP groups. A chi-squared test was also performed to verify the distribution of both sexes was similar between both groups.

Model building and refinement

A biomarker model was developed with serum peptides measures to discriminate those brain injury patients who will experience high ICP events within 24 h to 72 h post injury. The subject population was divided into model building (n = 7) and validation (n = 6) cohorts with equal loading of subjects with known high ICP events (Table 1). Modeling was performed using partial-least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) with R functions through the Metaboanalyst toolset v5.027. Analysis was first performed against peptides measured at 24 h post-injury to develop an initial prognostic model that discriminated subjects that had or went on to develop high ICP within the first 72 h. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were used to rank peptide performance. The list of peptides with above-average discriminant performance (VIP > 1) was then refined for forward-prognostic performance by assessing whether measures remained elevated in serum in tandem with high ICP recordings across the full 24–72 h observation period. Model performance was assessed by leave-n-out cross-validation using the Monte Carlo method suitable with a small samples size.

Peptide sequence identification, enrichment analysis, and model validation

Tandem mass spectra corresponding to the selected peptide features (unique by their retention time, drift time, and mass) were searched against a human Swissprot database (Uniprot.org, March 2022) using Waters PLGS v3.3 and EndogeSeq with no enzyme specificity to identify endogenous sequences as previously described26. Precursor and product mass tolerances were set to 8 and 12 ppm, respectively. Ubiquitination, phosphorylation, sulfonation, and acetylation post-translational modifications were considered. Matched sequences were adjusted to a 5% false discovery rate when compared against random sequences. Site-selective protease prediction in forming n- and c-termini of biomarker peptides was performed using ProCleave28. Identified sequences were assessed for their origins from brain-enriched proteins, determined using the Human Protein Atlas29. Functional associations among preliminary biomarker peptides were assessed by enrichment analysis using the ToppGene Suite30. Peptides sources from brain-enriched proteins and associated with brain-injury relevance-enriched biological processes were prioritized in final biomarker selection. Models built using top-ranked peptides (by VIP score) were assessed in Metaboanalyst using receiver operator characteristic and class prediction probability analyses.

Results

Serum peptide model building for prognosticating acute ICP events in brain injured patients

To build a biomarker model that identifies subjects with brain injury who will develop elevated ICP, a study population of critical care patients was stratified into two cohorts: High ICP – brain injured subjects (p1-6) with ICP exceeding 20 mmHg for over 1 h within 72 h of injury; Normal ICP – a control population (p7-13) with brain injured subjects having normal ICP over the initial 72 h postinjury and polytrauma patients without brain injury (Table 1). There were no differences in age (t stat=-0.204; p = 0.842) or sex (df = 1, N = 13, x2 = 0.660) distributions between groups. GCS scores differed between groups (t stat=-3.204; p = 0.008) as expected given inclusion of polytrauma patients without head injuries in the control group.

An initial 24-h postinjury assay point was targeted for acute elevations in ICP. By 24 h, most patients have completed initial resuscitation and stabilization, allowing ready and actionable use of the assay; however, once developed, the assay could be used for testing at earlier timepoints. Mass spectrometric analysis of 24-h serum peptidomic samples produced 5,735 replicating detections, filtered to those observed across six or more subjects in the study. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to broadly assess any latent discriminant capacity within the dataset (Fig. 1A). The predominant covariance (36.2%) within the data, displayed by principal component 1 (PC 1), separated all High ICP subjects from Normal ICP subjects. A 2.3-fold smaller principal component (PC 2) resolved the Normal ICP group into its two subpopulations without further separation among the High ICP group. These unsupervised results demonstrated latent discriminant capacity within the serum peptidome for brain injury patients with and without high ICP events. However, to maximize model robustness, the dataset was filtered further to retain 1,453 detections that were measured in all subjects of the High ICP group.

Study subjects were then randomly apportioned into building and validation cohorts with equal sampling from the High and Normal ICP groups (Table 1). Using the building cohort, supervised discriminant modeling was performed by PLS-DA on the 24-h serum peptide measures (Fig. 1B). PLS-DA maximizes group separation and assigns each peptide a variable’s importance in the projection (VIP) score. All told, 567 detections (39%) exceeded a VIP > 1 cutoff, indicating their above-average contribution to the model31. Peptide-wise, the 567 were broadly distributed in terms of their mass, hydrophobicity, and cross-section as measured by their m/z, retention time, and mobility time, respectively (Fig. 1C), supporting that their discriminant capacity was not an artifact of experimental bias.

Next, to refine the model’s forward-prognostic relevance, the 567 peptides were assessed for whether they remained discriminant of high ICP across the first 72 h post injury, which considered that peptide measures indicative of future high ICP events should remain elevated. Measures of the 567 peptides were assayed between 24 h and 72 h at 6-h intervals, with samples stratified by whether they preceded an elevated ICP event or not (yes or no classification, respectively, Fig. 1D). Applying PLS-DA, 97% of samples were correctly clustered within 95% confidence limits (demarked by red and green ovals) based on the predominant covariance in the data (component 1, Fig. 1D). Following the refinement, 227 had a VIP > 1, as illustrated for the top 25 scoring prognostic peptides in Fig. 1E, demarked by their unique numeric identifier. Cross validation by a Monte Carlo leave-n-out method simulated subsampling of the data to test prognostic performance. Results showed high accuracy (0.9661) using component 1 factors with the predictive correlation remaining high for left-out samples across cross-validation folds (Q2 of 0.75289 ≈ R2 of 0.77723).

Sequence identification of prognostic serum peptides

Moving forward, tandem mass spectra for the 227 peptides were searched with 79 sequences (34.8%) identified within a 5% false discovery rate, derived from 72 different proteins (Supplemental Table 1). Adding the validation cohort, we see that the 79 peptides retained discriminatory capacity in an unsupervised PCA analysis, with 86.4% of covariance separating samples along the first principal component (Fig. 2A). These 79 peptides were detected in all High ICP subjects. In contrast, 63 (79.7%) of these peptides could not be detected in the Normal ICP control subjects, indicating that the identified model peptides were highly specific to brain injured subjects with acute high ICP events.

To finalize peptide selection, functional relevance to brain injury pathobiology was considered. First, peptides were selected to have originated from brain-enriched proteins, with 28 peptides meeting this criterion (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table 1). Then, enrichment in biological processes relevant to brain injury and recovery was assessed. Significant enrichment was found for organ morphogenesis (18 peptides, GO:0009887; p = 1.19E-05), response to wounding (19 peptides, GO:0009611; p = 8.49E-06), and CNS development (19 peptides, GO:0007417; p = 2.44E-05). Peptides that overlapped brain enrichment with these pathological processes (dashed outline, Fig. 2B) were then ranked by their VIP score, selecting those with highest prognostic performance (Fig. 2C). Accounting for the overlap among the top three ranked peptides in each biological process, seven have been targeted in the final model, derived from these proteins in ranked order: PCSK1, MICAL2, PRDM16, SMAD6, COL5A2, PRSS56, and MRTFA. Table 2 summarizes how each marker mechanistically associates with ICP elevation and what proteases are predicted to form the n- and c-termini of the biomarker peptides. Matrix metalloproteases (MMP’s 2,3,7,8,9,12,13; ADAMTS-4; Meprin β) and cathepsins (cathepsin B, E,L) were repeatedly observed; these are involved in extracellular remodeling and secretory proteolytic processing and are known to be activated in response to brain injury.

Performance of the serum peptidomic prognostic model

The top-performing peptides were predominantly from proteins involved in regulating the neurovascular unit, with their originating proteins known to regulate blood flow, angiogenesis, and vascular integrity in the brain (Fig. 3). Moreover, the predicted MMP and other matrix-related protease involvement is consistent with the progressive dysfunction in the endothelium and perivascular Virchow-Robin space known to occur in vasogenic edema. The prognostic performance of the top peptides was assessed using the validation cohort. In unsupervised PCA analysis, patients with high ICP events were fully resolved from subjects without ICP events along PC 1, with 92.1% covariance among these peptides contributing to this discrimination (Fig. 4A). Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed an area under the curve of 97% in a model of the top two ranked peptides from proteins PCSK1 and MICAL2, demonstrating high model sensitivity and specificity (Fig. 4B). As expected, performance increases with addition of peptides, with 100% accurate High and Normal ICP group prediction across building and validation subjects after adding a third peptide from PRDM16 (Fig. 4C). Predicted probability values were significantly different for patients with high ICP events than for those without such events (t stat=-8.257; p = 4.83E-06). Moreover, there was no difference in probability values between building and validation cohorts (t stat = 0.590; p = 0.567), denoting consistent model performance. Lastly, we can see that serum quantities for all seven peptides were at least 6-fold higher in the High ICP group than in control subjects (Fig. 4D), demonstrating assay sensitivity across a broad dynamic range across these novel biomarkers.

Discussion

To date, there are no blood-based biomarkers or other minimally invasive tools capable of predicting or accurately detecting IHT after brain injury. GFAP and UCH-L1, the two FDA-approved blood-based biomarkers for TBI, are used for triage purposes within the first hours after injury, while no blood-based biomarkers have been approved for stroke15,32. GFAP has exhibited signs of greater clinical utility, with promising peripheral diagnostic applications in TBI and stroke. However, UCH-L1 has been found to be less consistent in its diffusion to the periphery and is said to be impacted more by blood-brain barrier dynamics15,33,16,32,34. This divergence is likely due to the origin of GFAP in astrocytes. These cells are in close interact with perivascular drainage channels, making GFAP entry into blood and its peripheral detection more feasible. However, this brings to attention the fact that larger molecules, such as whole proteins, are more restricted in their efflux through the interstitial space between cells, with diffusion into bulk interstitial fluid streams being a limited factor in their reliability as diagnostic or prognostic markers18. Heightened proteolytic activity following brain injury, however, has been well documented and produces a multitude of peptide byproducts35–37,20,38. Endogenously produced peptide fragments can be much smaller and thus can diffuse more readily from their cellular source in the brain to enter bulk drainage streams and later appear in collected fluid samples such as serum. Thus, there is a small, albeit growing, body of work in the literature investigating these breakdown products (e.g., c-tau, spectrin, MBP, etc.), while the broader brain injury peptidome remains a largely untapped source for diagnostic tools22–25.

This hypothesis generating study sought to identify peptide biomarkers that may predict clinically relevant elevations in ICP among brain injured patients. Clinical samples from a brain injured population, comprised of TBI patients with hemorrhagic burdens and hemorrhagic stroke patients, with implanted EVDs were collected while ICP was continuously monitored. In model building, 79 endogenous peptides were found to have forward-predictive capabilities in differentiating patients that experienced clinically relevant elevations in ICP within 72 h of injury from those that did not. From these 79, the model was refined to seven high-ranked discriminatory peptides from brain-enriched proteins with pathobiological relevance to organ morphogenesis, CNS remodeling or response to wounding. Notably, the seven model peptides were between 0.56% and 2.6% as massive as their parent proteins, ranging in length from 11 to 31 amino acids. Each was a predicted proteolytic product of caspase, cathepsin, or MMP proteases, all well known to exhibit heightened activity in the wake of brain injury.

Importantly, the identified peptides were cleaved from proteins important to vascular development, function, and maintenance relevant to vasogenic edema following brain injury. Pcsk1, for example, is responsible for processing the vasoactive prohormones vasopressin and oxytocin, which play opposing roles in blood pressure regulation (Fig. 3;39. Thus, selective proteolytic degradation of Pcsk1 as evidenced by elevations of its peptide products in the blood of patients fits with dysregulated cerebral perfusion that might be expected in patients with IHT. MICAL2 is involved in VEGF signaling both by mediating Semaphorin3A-NRP2 responses and its involvement in the p130Cas interactome, ultimately contributing to inflammation-induced angiogenesis (Fig. 3;40. Degradation of MICAL-2 impedes its role in the SRF pathway to regulate nuclear MRTF-A trafficking, a cofactor needed to mediate reactive angiogenesis41,42. The SRF pathway is also involved in the astrocyte response to hemorrhagic stroke43,44. Astrocytes, through their role in the blood-brain barrier, are intimately involved in the pathological increase in fluid during vasogenic edema17,45,19. SMAD6 is regulated in the notch angiogenic signaling cascade activated following brain injury (Fig. 3;46,47 and is involved in glial inflammatory responses to wounding48. PRDM16 regulates arterial development and stabilization in part by modulating expression of Angpt2 (Fig. 3;49, which when degraded can in turn impact recruitment of vascular smooth muscle cells and contribute to dysfunctional cerebral pressure autoregulation. Col5a2 has been found to be impacted following TBI and influences TGF-ß and Wnt/ß-catenin signaling pathways involved in angiogenesis (Fig. 3;50,51. Lastly, loss of the serine protease PRSS56, while not well studied in the brain, has been shown to impair fluid drainage structures within the eye and plays a causal role in open angle glaucoma (Fig. 3;52. Here, we found its peptide to be highly discriminatory of subjects with high ICP events, suggesting a role in fluid balance within the CNS as well. Thus, these peptides appear well suited to inform on brain-injury altered vascular permeability, impaired vascular remodeling, and the fluid imbalance involved in IHT following brain injury.

The final panel of seven endogenous peptides was able to accurately differentiate between high and normal ICP patient groups, supporting the proof-of-concept that pathologically generated peptides can serve as biomarkers applied to secondary sequelae of brain injury. The biomarker development approach here was strengthened by use of a set-aside validation subject cohort. However, further studies are needed across a larger subject population to validate the prognostic utility of these peptides. Sample size was limited in this initial hypothesis generating study given that only a fraction of patients hospitalized for brain injury have an EVD implanted to provide the ICP data needed to develop the biomarker model. Future studies would also benefit from more frequent ICP recording than the 1-hour sampling rate used here, which would allow for more accurate assessment of ICP spikes that occur on short time scales. Although this effort focused on prognostic biomarkers in acute brain injury patients, it is possible that IHT after other disease processes may have similar molecular origins53.

Conclusions

A peptide biomarker panel was developed to readily discriminate between patients with and without subsequent clinically relevant elevations of ICP. The identified peptides were associated with vascular dysfunction, blood pressure autoregulation, and fluid drainage structures, all of which may be relevant to intracranial pressure dynamics. The pathological processes tied to these peptides may also be present in a variety of neurological conditions that can lead to intracranial hypertension. Long-term, these peptides may become useful tools for proactively identifying and treating IHT as a secondary insult in acute brain injury.
