Prime editing models the MTARC1 A165T variant in human liver organoids, demonstrating reduced steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis
Authors: Amel Ben Saad, Nahid Arghiani, Arden D. Weilheimer, Stefan D. Gentile, Benjamin J. Toles, Sudipta Tripathi, Seher Mohsin Sayed, Anil Chandraker, Alan C. Mullen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent cause of chronic liver disease worldwide. This progressive, multifactorial condition is characterized by hepatic steatosis, which can evolve into steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer. Genetic factors influence the development, progression, and complications in MASLD, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with altered risk. Mitochondrial amidoxime reducing component 1 (MTARC1) rs2642438 (p.A165T) variant has been identified as protective, but the role of MTARC1 and the impact of this variant in hepatocytes remains poorly understood.Methods:We applied prime editing to create the rs2642438 variant in human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) before differentiation into human liver organoids (HLOs) to investigate the effect of the variant under conditions of steatotic and fibrotic injury.Results:Compared with HLOs formed from hESCs containing the MTARC1 reference sequence, HLOs with the rs2642438 variant show lower levels of MTARC1 protein and triglycerides and are protected from steatotic and fibrotic injury, as predicted by the phenotype observed in patients carrying the variant. The observed decrease in triglyceride levels with the variant appears to be driven more by suppression of de novo lipogenesis than stimulation of β-oxidation in the HLO model. While resmetirom, the thyroid hormone receptor-beta (THRB) agonist approved to treat patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), was effective in reducing triglyceride levels in the setting of steatotic injury in HLOs with the reference sequence, HLOs containing the variant did not show further reduction in triglyceride levels with exposure to resmetirom.Conclusions:Collectively, these findings establish a platform to model disease-associated SNPs in HLOs, provide insight into the activity of the MTARC1 variant, and suggest that SNP profiling may help identify patients more likely to respond to MASLD therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic liver disease (CLD) remains a major global health burden, causing more than 2 million deaths annually 1 and affecting ~4.5 million adults in the United States. 2 Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), the leading cause of CLD, impacts over 30% of the adult population and is predicted to increase in prevalence. 3 Furthermore, a concerning rise in MASLD prevalence has been observed among young children and adolescents, with current estimates indicating nearly 1 in 10 children in the United States are affected. 4 MASLD is a progressive disease, starting with steatosis, which can progress to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver cancer. 5 Therapeutic options for patients with MASLD are limited. Lifestyle modifications, including weight loss, Mediterranean diet, limiting alcohol consumption, and physical exercise, are first-line therapies. 6 Recently, resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor beta (THRB) agonist, was approved for adults with MASH and moderate fibrosis, 7 and the GLP-1 receptor agonist semiglutide has now been shown to reduce fibrosis in MASH. 8 While these treatments are significant advances, only 25% of patients receiving resmetirom show improved fibrosis, 9 and about a third of patients show improvement in the combined endpoints of steatohepatitis or fibrosis with semiglutide. 8 A better understanding of the pathogenesis and progression of MASLD is still needed to identify more effective therapies.

MASLD is a complex and multifactorial disease, influenced by diverse determinants, including genetic variation, that shape its development, progression, and complications. 10 Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified susceptibility loci or single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with an altered risk in developing MASLD, including SNPs in patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 3 (PNPLA3), transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2), glucokinase regulatory protein (GCKR), membrane-bound O-acyltransferase domain-containing 7 (MBOAT7), hydroxysteroid 17 beta dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13), and mitochondrial amidoxime reducing component 1 (MTARC1). 11 PNPLA3 rs738409 (p.I148M) is the most common and replicated variant among different populations, and is strongly associated with increased hepatic triglyceride levels, liver fibrosis, and cirrhosis. 12 Similarly, TM6SF2 rs58542926, GCKR rs780094, and MBOAT7 rs641738 variants are associated with a higher risk for chronic liver disease.13–15 In contrast, HSD17B13 rs72613567 and MTARC1 rs2642438 are reported as protective against MASLD and its progression.16–18

Initial studies linked the MTARC1 rs2642438 G>A (p.A165T) variant to decreased markers of liver injury.17,19 Additional analyses have shown that carriers have lower levels of circulating transaminases (ALT and AST) and serum cholesterol, decreased hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and fibrosis, and reduced severity and risk of MASH, autoimmune hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, and all causes of cirrhosis, as well as a lower rate of liver-related mortality.17,19–22 The MTARC1 gene (known also as MARC1 and MOSC1) encodes a molybdenum enzyme located in the outer mitochondrial membrane. It reduces N-oxygenated compounds and plays a role in drug metabolism and cellular detoxification.23,24 The protective effect of the MTARC1 p.A165T variant is linked to reduced MTARC1 protein levels in the liver.25,26 Studies have explored the potential of MTARC1 depletion to phenocopy the protective effects of the variant, and N-acetylgalactosamine-conjugated siRNAs (GalNac-siRNA) targeting MTARC1 in primary human hepatocytes and diet-induced MASH mouse models attenuate hepatic steatosis and inhibit disease progression.27–29 These findings support the therapeutic potential of targeting MTARC1 to treat MASLD, and patient filings suggest active development of oligonucleotide-based approaches directed against MTARC1 mRNA. 23

While there is strong evidence supporting the hepatoprotective effect of MTARC1 p.A165T variant and its potential as a therapeutic candidate, the molecular mechanisms by which MTARC1 variants influence the trajectory of disease remain poorly understood. Thus, in vitro human models are needed to explore and understand the implications of the MTARC1 rs2642438 p.A165T variant in the liver.

In this study, we created a model of the MTARC1 rs2642438 p.A165T variant using liver organoids (HLOs) derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs). 30 We edited hESCs with prime editing before differentiating hESCs with the MTARC1 reference or variant sequence into HLOs, containing hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), and cholangiocytes, to study their phenotype and response to liver injury in conditions that model MASH and fibrotic injury.

METHODS

hESC culture, differentiation into HLOs, and liver injury induction

H1 human embryonic stem cells (WiCell, WA01—NIH registration number 0043) were cultured and differentiated into HLOs as previously described.30,31 In brief, hESCs were maintained in mTeSR plus medium (StemCell Technologies, 100-0276) in plates pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, 354230). After reaching confluency, hESCs were differentiated to definitive endoderm, then to foregut spheroids. On day 7 of differentiation, cells were embedded in Matrigel and treated for 4 days with retinoic acid (Tocris, 60695) before switching to hepatocyte culture medium (Lonza, CC-3198). 32 On day 21, we obtained self-organized 3D HLOs containing hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, and HSCs. 30 HLOs were extracted from Matrigel on day 21 and cultured on an orbital shaker. To induce liver injury, isolated HLOs were treated for 4 days with palmitic acid (PA, 500 μM) or transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1, 10 ng/mL). 30 To control for variability across HLO experiments, we analyzed each MTARC1 genotype using 4 independent hESC clones and performed differentiations in parallel when comparing MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T clones. Each clone was differentiated in 3 wells, so that a data point for one hESC line in one experiment is the mean of 3 technical replicates. The number of independent replicates (separate rounds of HLO differentiation) for each experiment is indicated in the figure legends.

Prime editing

To generate the MTARC1-wildtype (WT) and MTARC1-A165T clones, hESCs were edited using prime editing strategy 5 (PE5) as described in Doman et al. 33 Briefly, hESCs were detached using accutase (StemCell Technologies, 07920) and nucleofected with 1 μg of in vitro transcribed Cas9-nickase fused to reverse transcriptase (nCAS9-RT) mRNA, 1 μg of in vitro transcribed engineered dominant-negative MLH1 mutant (MLH1dn) mRNA, 100 pmoL of chemically modified synthetic engineered pegRNA (epegRNA) and 60 pmoL of synthetic nicking single guide RNA (sgRNA) using the P3 Primary cell 4D Nucleofector X kit S (Lonza, V4XP-3032). After 72 hours from the first nucleofection, hESCs were nucleofected a second time using the same reagents. Edited hESCs were cultured for an additional 72 hours to recover, and then single clones were picked, cultured in 96-well plates until confluency, and screened for the targeted edit. Genomic DNA from single-cell-derived colonies was isolated using the Arcturus PicoPure DNA Extraction kit (Applied Biosystems, 11815-00), PCR amplified using primers designed to amplify around the target edit (primer sequences are attached in Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447), and amplicons were subjected to Sanger sequencing. In vitro transcribed nCAS9-RT and MLH1dn mRNAs were prepared as described in Doman et al. 33 epegRNAs and sgRNA (sequences are included in Supplemental Table S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447) were designed using the pegFinder website. 34

Reverse transcriptase, quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026), reverse transcribed using the iScript gDNA clear cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, 1725035) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green universal master mix (Applied Biosystems, 4309155). Actin Beta (ACTB) was used as an internal control for all the experiments. All primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447.

Pluripotent marker staining

hESCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, J61899-AK) for 10 minutes, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton, blocked for 1 hour in 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) solution (Sigma, A4737), and then stained with antibodies recognizing OCT4 and NANOG (Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, cells were incubated with Donkey anti-Goat IgG Cross-Adsorbed Secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488 and 59, and then were visualized and imaged with the Leica DMi8 automated microscope (Leica Systems) using the 10× objective.

Western blots

HLOs were lysed in RIPA Buffer (Boston BioProducts, BP-115D) supplemented with Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific, 78438). After homogenization, the lysates were centrifuged at 20,000g at 4 °C for 10 minutes. Supernatants were then collected. Protein concentrations were quantified using Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, 23227), and equal protein quantities supplemented with Bolt LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen, cat# B0007) and Bolt Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen, cat# B0009) were loaded in Bolt 4%–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, NW04120BOX). After protein transfer using the iBlot 2 Dry Blotting system, membranes were blocked with 3% milk (Lab Scientific, M0841) in Tris-Buffered Saline Tween (TBST) (Boston BioProducts, IBB-180X) for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. The following day, membranes were washed with TBST, incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour, washed again, incubated with the Super Signal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrates (Thermo Scientific, 34580), and scanned using a LI-COR Odyssey M imaging machine. All antibodies used are listed in Supplemental Table S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447. Tubulin was used as a loading control. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ-Fiji software.

Bodipy staining

HLOs cultured in control or PA conditions were collected and washed with PBS. Lipid droplets were stained with Bodipy 493/503 and nuclei with DAPI for 40 minutes at 37 °C. The stained HLOs were then visualized and imaged with the Leica DMi8 automated Microscope (Leica Systems) using the 10× objective. Image analysis and fluorescence intensity quantification were performed using the ImageJ-Fiji software.

Triglyceride assay

HLOs were homogenized in 4% NP-40 solution (Fisher Scientific, PI85124). Triglyceride levels were then measured using a fluorometric triglyceride quantification assay kit (Abcam, Ab65336) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cytokines and chemokines quantification

HLOs were cultured in control or PA conditions. Media were collected, and secreted cytokines and chemokines were quantified using the customized Milliplex human cytokine/chemokine multiplex assay (Millipore, HCYTA-60K) following the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Statistics

All graphs and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Unpaired two-tailed Student test and two-way ANOVA test were used for statistical analyses as indicated. Error bars indicate standard deviation (SD).

RESULTS

Generation of MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T hESCs and differentiation into HLOs

The MTARC1 variation rs2642438 G>A (p.A165T) is a single nucleotide change of guanine (G) to adenine (A), substituting the amino acid alanine (A) for threonine (T) at amino acid position 165. hESCs used in this study were initially heterozygous (A/G) (Figure 1A). We used prime editing to create hESCs with the homozygous MTARC1 reference sequence (MTARC1-WT, G/G) and hESCs with the homozygous MTARC1 A165T variant (MTARC1-A165T, A/A). For each MTARC1 genotype (G/G and A/A), we generated 4 independent single-cell-derived clones with the desired homozygous modification. Successful editing was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1B). Sanger sequencing also showed that the hESCs are heterozygous for PNPLA3 rs738409 SNP, homozygous for TM6SF2 and GCKR WT alleles, and homozygous for HSD17B13 rs72613567 (Supplemental Figure S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447).

After gene editing, we assessed pluripotency. We found similar expression of the pluripotency markers OCT4/POU5F1 and NANOG in all edited hESCs, at levels comparable to non-edited hESCs, as quantified by RT-qPCR (Figure 1C) and immunostaining (Figure 1D). In addition, hESC clones all exhibited typical embryonic stem cell colony morphology (Figure 2A), confirming further that prime editing did not interfere with pluripotency features. We then differentiated all MTARC1-WT (G/G) and MTARC1-A165T (A/A) hESC clones to HLOs. 30 Regardless of their genotype, all hESCs clones were successfully differentiated into HLOs when evaluated on day 21 (Figure 2A).

MTARC1 A165T variant induces protein loss and reduces lipid accumulation

Following the differentiation of MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T hESC clones to HLOs, we determined the impact of their genotype on MTARC1 transcript and protein levels. MTARC1 mRNA levels were not significantly different between the 2 genotypes (Figure 2B). However, MTARC1 protein levels were significantly reduced in HLOs with the MTARC1-A165T (A/A) genotype compared with HLOs expressing MTARC1-WT (G/G) as revealed by Western blot (Figures 2C, D).26,35 The original hESC line was heterozygous for the MTARC1 variant (Figure 1A), and MTARC1 expression was maintained at similar levels in HLOs with the MTARC1-WT (G/G) genotype and the heterozygous (A/G) genotype compared with the A165T (A/A) genotype (Supplemental Figure S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447).

Hepatic lipid accumulation is a key hallmark of MASLD development and progression. 6 Previously, we defined a free fatty acid–induced steatosis/steatohepatitis HLO model by challenging HLOs with palmitic acid (PA). 30 We treated MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T HLOs with PA and quantified steatosis using Bodipy staining (Figure 3A). Following the PA challenge, HLOs carrying the MTARC1-A165T variant exhibited a reduced Bodipy signal (right panel) in comparison to MTARC1-WT HLOs (left panel), with consistent lipid phenotypes across all clones with the same genotype (Figure 3A). Quantification of Bodipy intensities confirmed these observations (Figure 3B). Under control conditions, a slight but non-significant decrease in Bodipy staining of MATRC1-A165T HLOs was also observed. To confirm the reduction of steatosis in HLOs carrying the protective variant, we next quantified intracellular triglyceride levels (Figure 3C), which were also reduced in HLOs expressing the MTARC1-A165T variant under PA treatment. These findings show that MTARC1-A165T variant protects against the development of steatosis in the HLO model.

MTARC1 A165T variant reduces the expression of genes in the de novo lipogenesis pathway

To further investigate how the MTARC1-A165T variant attenuated lipid accumulation in HLOs, we quantified the impact of the MTARC1-A165T variant on the expression of genes that regulate lipid metabolism. ACC1 and FASN are 2 key genes whose products regulate de novo lipogenesis by catalyzing the first 2 steps of fatty acid synthesis, respectively. 36 Expression of ACC1 was reduced in the MTARC1-A165T variant compared with MTARC1-WT HLOs in both standard culture conditions and with the addition of PA (Figure 4A). FASN expression significantly decreased under standard conditions, but the addition of PA suppressed FASN to similar levels in both MTARC1-A165T variant and MTARC1-WT HLOs (Figure 4B). We next quantified the expression of genes that regulate β-oxidation: CPT1A, CPT2, and ACADAM. CPT1A showed a trend toward reduced expression in the MTARC1-A165T variant compared with MTARC1-WT HLOs (Figure 4C), while CPT2 and ACADAM expressions did not change (Figures 4D, 3E). Overall, these results suggest that the presence of the MTARC1-A165T variant may suppress steatosis in the HLO model by reducing de novo lipogenesis rather than increasing β-oxidation.

MTARC1 A165T variant attenuates the impact of resmetirom

The THRB agonist, resmetirom, was recently approved for patients with MASH and can reduce steatosis in the setting of MASLD, 8 and we wanted to understand how the MTARC1-A165T variant affects responses to resmetirom. We found that treatment of MTARC1-WT HLOs with resmetirom in the presence of PA suppressed triglyceride levels, while resmetirom did not further suppress triglyceride levels in HLOs expressing the MTARC1-A165T variant in the presence of PA (Figure 4F and Supplemental Figure S3, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447). While the MTARC1-A165T did not show a change in triglyceride levels in response to resmetirom, THRB and CPT1A expression were induced in both MTARC1-WT and -A165T HLOs, consistent with resmetirom activity in both conditions (Figure 4G and Supplemental Figure S4, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447). These results show that while both MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T HLOs respond to resmetirom, resmetirom does not appear to provide any further benefit in lowering triglyceride levels in HLOs for the MTARC1-A165T variant.

MTARC1 A165T decreases inflammation in HLOs

Inflammation is a key feature in MASLD progression from steatosis to steatohepatitis. 37 To evaluate the impact of the MTARC1-A165T variant on the transition from steatosis to steatohepatitis, we quantified cytokine and chemokine expression in response to PA. We found that MTARC1-A165T variant HLOs secreted less IL-6, IL-1α, TNF-α, and RANTES compared with WT HLOs (Figures 5A–D). These results suggest that the A165T variant produces a muted inflammatory response, which could reduce the rate of progression from MASLD to MASH.

MTARC1 A165T protects from liver fibrosis

In MASLD, HSCs play a central role in the development of fibrosis and disease progression. Once activated, HSCs produce the extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins that accumulate in fibrosis. 38 The presence of HSCs in our HLOs (Supplemental Figure S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C447) enables liver fibrosis modeling using TGF-β1 treatment. 31 We evaluated the fibrotic response of HLOs carrying MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T variants to treatment with TGF-β1. ACTA2 (α-SMA) expression, which marks HSC activation, 38 was reduced in HLOs expressing the MTARC1-A165T variant (Figures 6A–E). In addition, a significant reduction in COL1A1 and COL1A2 mRNA levels was observed in HLOs carrying the MTARC1-165T variant compared with MTARC1-WT HLOs (Figures 6F, G), and type 1 collagen protein levels were also reduced in MTARC1-165T HLOs (Figures 6H–K). These results show that the MTARC1-165T variant suppresses HSC activation and is associated with reduced expression of type I collagen, a key component of the ECM contributing to the fibrotic scar.

DISCUSSION

The human MTARC1 gene was discovered in 2006 and has been linked to drug metabolism and detoxification. 23 With the discovery of the protective MTARC1 rs2642438 (p.A165T) variant, 17 there has been growing interest directed toward understanding the role of MTARC1 and how protective variants modulate the development and progression of chronic liver disease.

Current models have yielded limited insights into the functions of MTARC1 and its variants. In vivo mouse studies have reported contradictory results regarding Mtarc1 function, with some studies showing that loss of Mtarc1 did not affect liver steatosis or fibrosis and others showing a protective effect in MASH models.29,35 Furthermore, immortalized and primary hepatocyte models present limitations and fail to replicate the complex functions of the liver and have relied on ectopic expression or depletion of MTARC1.25,39 In recent years, organoids derived from pluripotent cells have emerged as a promising and powerful alternative to study human liver diseases in culture. 40 With their 3D multicellular organization, HLOs can better approximate the cellular heterogeneity, cell crosstalk, and functions of the liver. In this study, we generated an HLO model by performing prime editing in a heterozygous hESC line to derive isogenic MTARC1-A165T and MTARC1-WT homozygous lines. This approach enables interrogation of the MTARC1 variant within a shared genetic background in a multicellular human organoid system. While this design isolates a single variant, additional donor-derived variants may influence the observed phenotypes, and this platform establishes a foundation for future studies examining MTARC1 activity across diverse genetic backgrounds.

The MTARC1-165T variant is linked to decreased protein levels, as observed in hepatocarcinoma cell lines and mouse livers overexpressing the human MTARC1 A165T variant.25,29,35,41 These findings are supported by studies showing that the MTARC1 A165T protein exhibits reduced stability, greater ubiquitination, and accelerated degradation by the proteasome compared with the MTARC1 WT protein.26,42 Data to address protein stability from human samples have been more limited, with studies from deceased donors showing reduced MTARC1 protein levels in the presence of the MTARC1 A165T variant, 35 but studies from pediatric liver biopsies did not showsignificant differences. 43 Our findings in HLOs further support the conclusion that the MTARC1-A165T is associated with reduced MTARC1 protein levels despite similar levels of MTARC1 transcripts.

The MTARC1-165T variant is also linked to reduced steatosis in patients,17,41 and these observations have been extended to human primary hepatocytes and mouse models through depletion of MTARC1.27,29 Consistent with these findings, we observed that HLOs carrying the MTARC1-A165T variant were protected from lipid/triglyceride accumulation when challenged with PA. We also found that the reduction of lipids in MTARC1-A165T variant HLOs is associated with lower expression of genes that regulate de novo lipogenesis (ACC1 and FASN). While increased β-oxidation has been linked to the A165T variant in primary hepatocytes, 39 CPT2 and ACADAM levels did not change significantly in the A165T variant HLOs, and CPT1A even showed a trend toward decreased expression in the variant, all pointing away from a role for increased β-oxidation as a path to reduce lipid accumulation in this HLO model. These findings suggest a central role of MTARC1 in lipid handling, but further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify its function.

Consistent with clinical observations, 22 we found that HLOs expressing the MTARC1-A165T variant produce lower levels of cytokines and chemokines in response to PA treatment. MTARC1 expression is primarily restricted to hepatocytes in the liver, 24 and our HLO model does not contain macrophages, 30 suggesting that inflammatory signals produced by hepatocytes to recruit macrophages 44 are suppressed in the A165T variant. In addition, IL-6, IL-1α, and TNF-α can promote HSC activation and collagen production, 45 and decreased expression of these ligands may also be the path by which the A165T variant reduces fibrogenesis, even in the presence of TGF-β1.

Resmetirom provides a significant advance in treatment options for patients with MASH by promoting THRB activity;9,46 however, there are no studies that evaluate how SNPs linked to disease progression may impact responses to resmetirom therapy in patients. Our data show that resmetirom also increases THRB expression in HLOs, consistent with the autoregulation of THRB through THRB-binding sites in its own promoter.47–49 This activation of THRB expression is observed in both MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T HLOs. While THRB activity is increased in both MTARC1-WT and MTARC1-A165T HLOs, as evidenced from the induction of THRB and CPT1A, triglyceride levels in the MTARC1-A165T variant HLOs are not further reduced with resmetirom treatment, raising the possibility that patients with the MTARC1-A165T variant may benefit less from this therapy. One possible explanation is that reduced de novo lipogenesis in the MTARC1-A165T variant limits the benefits of β-oxidation induced by resmetirom. Similar limitations in resmetirom activity were observed in human hepatocytes containing the I148M (disease) variant of PNPLA3 compared with human hepatocytes containing the reference sequence, in a microphysiology system. 50 These findings suggest that understanding the impact of disease-related SNPs may help identifypatients who are more likely to benefit from resmetirom or future drugs to treat MASH.

Establishing HLOs from hESCs engineered to contain disease-relevant SNPs provides a robust platform for investigating genetic mechanisms that shape liver disease. In this study, we modeled the MTARC1-A165T variant and found it to be associated with reduced MTARC1 protein expression and protection against lipid accumulation, linked to suppression of de novo lipogenesis. We also observed decreased secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which may contribute to the attenuated fibrotic response observed following TGF-β1 stimulation. Notably, the variant lowers triglyceride levels, and resmetirom treatment did not confer additional benefit, suggesting that genetic background may influence therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these findings underscore the utility of engineered HLOs as a platform for dissecting disease-relevant pathways and advance our understanding of how the MTARC1-A165T variant influences the progression of chronic liver disease.
