CanRisk-DB: an artificial intelligence-driven comprehensive database of cancer risk factors
Authors: Yongjie Xu, Changfa Xia, Shiyuan Tong, Hui Yu, Fang Liu, Shiqing Chen, Jing Liu, Yujie Wu, Jiachen Wang, Qianru Li, Sibo Zhu, Wanqing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer development is influenced by a complex interplay of diverse risk factors, but synthesizing this fragmented research remains challenging. To address this, we developed CanRisk-DB, an Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven database that systematically aggregates and standardizes published evidence on cancer-associated risk factors. Using a multi-stage AI pipeline based on the PICOS framework, we analyzed 435,975 publications from PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane (2000-2024), employing a Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation framework to extract cancer types, risk factors, and quantitative estimates (e.g., relative risk [RR], hazard ratio [HR], standardized incidence ratio [SIR]). In the literature screening stage, the system demonstrated high accuracy and efficiency. From 9550 relevant articles, CanRisk-DB compiled 445,646 standardized records covering 76 risk factor groups and 42 cancer types across 80 countries over 50 years. Validated against benchmark datasets, this publicly accessible resource constitutes a comprehensive knowledge base of cancer risk factors, supporting etiological research, risk analyses, and the development of evidence-informed prevention strategies. The CanRisk-DB is available at http://www.canrisk-ai.com.

Introduction

Cancer is the leading cause of death before the age of 70 years in worldwide1,2. The development of cancer is shaped by a wide range of risk factors, encompassing both non-modifiable determinants, such as genetic predisposition and modifiable influences including environmental exposures, lifestyle, and infections2. Significantly, it has been reported that 30–50% of cancer cases are preventable through appropriate behavioral modifications3. A comprehensive understanding of the relationship between cancers and their risk factors is crucial for elucidating their etiology, optimizing prevention strategies, and ultimately reducing cancer-related morbidity and mortality.

Despite significant advancements in cancer research, current knowledge on risk factors remains fragmented, with limited integration across diverse cancer types and exposures. While meta-analyses have provided valuable insights into specific risk factor-cancer relationships, synthesizing broader patterns across multiple cancers and risk categories presents a formidable challenge due to the sheer volume and heterogeneity of published data. Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), offers a transformative approach to this problem by enabling efficient extraction, organization, and synthesis of risk factor data from large-scale biomedical literature (e.g., PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases). AI automates the conversion of unstructured textual data into structured, analyzable formats, thereby enabling high-throughput meta-analyses, integrative risk assessments, and temporal trend analyses at an unprecedented scale4,5.

In this study, we developed CanRisk-DB, the most comprehensive database of cancer-risk associations to date. Leveraging an LLM-based multi-agent system (CanRisk-AI), our platform systematically aggregates, standardizes, and analyzes risk factor data from the global literature. This resource provides a foundational framework for generating hypotheses that could inform future cancer prevention strategies, thereby supporting evidence-based research and guiding subsequent mechanistic studies.

Performance verification of dataset extraction by CanRisk-AI

To assess the performance of CanRisk-DB, a random sample of 1500 abstracts was selected from the pool of 291,603 preliminarily filtered abstracts. Evaluation was conducted against a gold standard, with comparisons made between the outputs of CanRisk-AI and human researchers across three critical stages.

In the abstract screening stage, human researchers retained on average 109 abstracts (standard error of the mean [SEM]: 27), while CanRisk-AI retained 99 (SEM: 20). The sensitivity of human screeners was 97.7% (SEM: 2.3%), compared to 99.4% (SEM: 0.6%) for CanRisk-AI. Specificity reached 96.3% (SEM: 1.8%) for human screening and 97.0% (SEM: 1.4%) for the AI system (Fig. 2B, Table S1).

Following full-text screening, human researchers retained 67 articles (SEM: 4), and CanRisk-AI retained 66 (SEM: 3). Human sensitivity was 97.7% (SEM: 2.3%), while the AI achieved 98.8% (SEM: 1.2%). Cumulative specificity improved to 99.2% (SEM: 0.4%) for human evaluators and 99.4% (SEM: 0.2%) for AI screening (Fig. 2B, Table S2).

During data extraction, CanRisk-AI processed 57 gold-standard full-text articles and identified 1530 data entities, comprising 22,950 individual data points related to cohorts, cancer types, and risk factors. Precision was 95.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 94.5–96.6%) for effect size extraction and 96.3% (95% CI: 94.3–97.8%) for cohort information. Sensitivity was 88.3% (95% CI: 86.7–89.8%) for effect size extraction and 99.6% (95% CI: 98.6–100%) for cohort information. (Fig. 2B, Table S3).

Processing speed analysis demonstrated a substantial efficiency advantage for CanRisk-AI. The AI system required an average of 0.4 s (SEM: 0.1) per abstract (1013 tokens) and 1.5 s (SEM: 0.3) per full text (10,211 tokens). In contrast, manual screening took 39.8 s (SEM: 5.5) per abstract and 131.7 s (SEM: 9.1) per full text (Fig. 2B, Table S2). For full-text data extraction, CanRisk-AI averaged 14.5 min (SEM: 2.4) per article (41,344 tokens), whereas human researchers required 92 min (SEM: 11.7).

Application of CanRisk-DB

CanRisk-DB serves as a comprehensive knowledge base for investigating cancer risk associations, enabling researchers to systematically query risk factor profiles for specific malignancies and perform subsequent meta-analyses. To demonstrate its utility, we present two representative case studies.

From CanRisk-DB, a total of 13 studies on oropharyngeal cancer6–15 using pre-defined search strategies (Table S4) were identified. Case study 2: type 2 diabetes and gastric cancer risk in Asian populations

Given the escalating prevalence of type 2 diabetes in Asia17–19 and the high burden of gastric cancer (ranked fifth globally, with a particularly high prevalence in East Asia)2, we utilized CanRisk-DB to explore the association between these two conditions. The platform enabled rapid identification of relevant studies, from which we extracted complete cohort data from 11 publications. Our pooled analysis yielded a significant relative risk of 1.52 (95% CI: 1.19–1.95) (Fig. 4B), which was consistent with the earlier findings by Shimoyama and colleagues20.

Web server implementation

CanRisk-DB is freely accessible at http://www.canrisk-ai.com with no registration required. The website provides an intuitive interface that allows users to select cancers and risk factors via dropdown menus to retrieve relevant records, with an option to download results in multiple formats.

Discussion

In this study, we present CanRisk-DB, a comprehensive cancer risk factor database developed through systematic aggregation of published research using AI-assisted methodologies. Our platform represents a significant advancement in cancer epidemiology by providing standardized, multidimensional annotations of risk associations, raw quantitative data for advanced analyses, and efficient retrieval capabilities through an intuitive web interface.

The development of CanRisk-DB represents a transformative advancement in cancer risk assessment, building upon existing resources to enable new dimensions of research. While established databases, such as Global Burden of Disease (GBD) and Global Cancer Observatory (GCO) provide foundational population-level data, CanRisk-DB significantly enhances etiological investigation through its unprecedented integration of multidimensional evidence spanning 80 countries and four decades of research, systematically curated using a PICOS-based framework. This approach delivers comprehensive coverage of 42 cancer types paired with 76 modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors, while incorporating critical granular details often absent in conventional datasets. By including population characteristics, such as genetic subtypes and socioeconomic strata alongside raw quantitative indicators like hazard ratio (HR)/RR with confidence intervals and exposure ranges, CanRisk-DB provides the necessary resolution to advance precision prevention research beyond what current databases can support. Importantly, its structured, model-ready data architecture facilitates advanced analytical applications, such as multifactorial interaction studies and dynamic risk modeling, thereby supporting investigations into cancer etiology and the development of targeted prevention strategies.

Our validation studies suggest that the AI-assisted pipeline can substantially improve efficiency, processing literature considerably faster than manual methods while maintaining high precision (~96%) in data extraction within the evaluated dataset. In our case study, the system identified 27% more relevant studies than conventional search strategies, including studies addressing multiple cancer types or risk factors that may be underrepresented in keyword-based searches. These findings highlight the potential advantages of the approach, although broader validation will be needed to fully establish its performance across diverse contexts.

The practical applications of CanRisk-DB extend across multiple research domains. Epidemiologists can leverage the database’s standardized effect estimates for meta-analyses, as demonstrated by our pooled analysis of type 2 diabetes and gastric cancer risk in Asian populations. Public health researchers may use the comprehensive risk factor data to identify high-risk populations for targeted screening programs. Furthermore, the availability of raw data enables methodological innovations in causal inference and risk prediction modeling that were previously constrained by data accessibility. In addition, CanRisk-DB can serve as a valuable guide for mapping the research landscape across cancer types, allowing investigators to identify which risk factors are well studied and which remain underexplored. This capability is particularly important for prioritizing future studies and addressing existing knowledge gaps. Additionally, CanRisk-DB offers a robust foundation for systematic reviews and meta-analyses, helping researchers verify literature search completeness and minimize evidence oversight.

Several limitations of the current version should be acknowledged. First, the exclusion of literature published before 2000, which accounts for approximately 21.8% of the retrieved records, may have led to the omission of some seminal early studies. Second, while the AI system demonstrated high accuracy in validation tests, inherent challenges in natural language processing may lead to occasional misclassifications, particularly when dealing with semantically ambiguous study descriptions. Third, the current implementation of the platform is based on commercial cloud-based LLM services, meaning that inference speed, computational resource availability, and overall efficiency are inherently constrained by the infrastructure. This will introduce variability in processing time when invoking different models across platforms. In addition, quantifying token consumption and processing time in a standardized manner is challenging because these metrics are highly dependent on model architecture, prompt complexity, and vendor-specific service configurations. Finally, the current platform lacks integrated analytical modules for direct meta-analysis and dose-response modeling—a limitation we are actively addressing through ongoing development of in-platform statistical tools.

In conclusion, CanRisk-DB provides a structured and accessible resource that integrates systematic evidence synthesis with AI-assisted curation. By combining data breadth, granularity, and retrieval flexibility, CanRisk-DB offers a foundation that can be further developed to support research on cancer etiology and to inform prevention strategies. While further expansion and validation are ongoing, the database already demonstrates considerable potential to improve the efficiency of evidence synthesis and broaden the scope of cancer risk factor research. With continued refinement and development, we anticipate that CanRisk-DB will become an invaluable resource for researchers and policymakers dedicated to mitigating the global burden of cancer.

Methods

Framework of CanRisk-DB establishment

The establishment of the CanRisk-DB framework comprises three main stages: literature collection, literature screening and data extraction, and database construction (Fig. 1).

For literature collection, the retrieval process followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines21,22. Using the PICOS framework23, abstracts were systematically sourced from PubMed, Cochrane, and Embase databases (Table S5). The specific search strategy and results pertaining to risk factors associated with oropharyngeal cancer are provided in the Table S6.

For literature screening and data extraction, the CanRisk-AI framework facilitates two core procedures: screening of literature and extraction of risk-related information. During the screening phase, manuscripts in PDF format underwent a two-tier evaluation—first at the abstract level, followed by full-text assessment—leveraging prompt-engineered queries via the DeepSeek R1 and Doubao-1.5-pro-32k models.

Literature selection was guided by the PICOS framework. To further enhance the AI’s ability to recognize PICOS-related information, additional exclusion criteria were applied during the screening stage. During the screening process, independent assessments were conducted by two large language models (DeepSeek R1 and Doubao-1.5-pro-32k). A study was retained only if both models independently judged it to meet all predefined eligibility criteria.

For risk information extraction, a multi-agent collaborative system was employed. Multiple agents independently extracted data concerning study subjects, outcomes, risk factors, and risk effect sizes. The outputs were integrated to form preliminary groupings organized by cohort, outcome, risk factor, and effect sizes.

Specifically, the system is organized into a three-layered multi-agent framework. The first layer, the primary information extraction agents, consists of four independent modules, each dedicated to a specific category of information extraction. The cohort information agent captures characteristics of study cohorts, including the number of cohorts, study location, study period, cohort size, age distribution, sex distribution, and other demographic attributes. The outcome information agent identifies the cancer types considered as study endpoints and records their operational definitions. The exposure risk agent extracts risk factors specified as exposures, along with their definitions. Finally, the effect size agent collects details on effect size measures, encompassing their type, definitions, and adjustment parameters.

The second layer, the information integration and task allocation agent, integrates cohort, exposure, outcome, and effect size information into a unified structure. Exposures and outcomes are systematically cross-mapped, while cohort characteristics are combined with effect size data. Based on this integrated information, a range of predefined grouping scenarios is established, and extraction tasks are organized and distributed accordingly.

The third layer, the grouped information extraction agents, executes the extraction tasks in parallel. Multiple agents employ GRAG to retrieve relevant text segments and generate initial groupings. The outputs are subsequently subjected to further GRAG-based retrieval and agent-driven validation, ensuring that the final grouped information is optimized for accuracy, consistency, and reproducibility. All results were exported in the JSON format.

For database construction, data extracted through CanRisk-AI were further refined to develop CanRisk-DB. First, duplicate entries within each study were identified and removed. Next, records with incomplete information regarding exposure groups, non-exposure groups, or outcomes were excluded during data cleaning. Effect sizes were filtered to retain only those corresponding to RR, HR, or standardized incidence ratio (SIR). Finally, each retained record was evaluated for validity; only entries containing at least one effect size or complete sample size information, including event and non-event counts across exposure groups were preserved.

Implementation of LLMs

The LLMs employed in this study were hosted on the Volcengine platform (https://www.volcengine.com/). Model inference was conducted programmatically using the volcengine-python-sdk, which provides structured access to API endpoints supporting multimodal input and structured output generation.

File parsing

Manuscripts were obtained in PDF format for full-text processing. Text, figures, and tables were extracted separately using MinerU (v1.1.0)24. Tables, initially captured as images, underwent orientation correction via Tesseract OCR (v5.5.0, https://github.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract). Table content recognition was subsequently performed with TableStructureRec (v1.1.10, https://github.com/RapidAI/TableStructureRec), converting tables into both markdown and HTML formats. Final validation of table extractions was carried out using the multimodal capabilities of Doubao-1.5-vision-pro to ensure accuracy and structural consistency.

Multimodal chunk construction

Text segmentation was guided by the inherent document structure (e.g., “Methods”, “Results”). Within each section, paragraphs were dynamically partitioned into chunks ranging between 500 and 1200 words to maintain semantic coherence. For non-text elements (figures and tables), the corresponding image data were integrated with surrounding textual context—specifically, the two preceding and two subsequent paragraphs. This composite information was processed via Doubao-1.5-vision-pro to produce a summarized textual representation, which was then treated as an independent multimodal chunk.

RAG construction

For each manuscript, a GRAG framework was constructed using LightRAG (v1.2.7)25. The Doubao-embedding-large model was utilized for embedding generation, while the Doubao-1.5-lite-32k model was employed for entity extraction. A predefined entity list, including “Cancers”, “Risk Factors”, “Risk Values”, “Cohort Information”, “Adjustment Methods”, “Timeline”, “Groups”, “Demographics”, “Geography”, “Data Sources”, “Risk Groups”, “Control Groups”, “Population”, “Events”, “References”, “Methods”, “Results”, “Discussion”, “Conclusion”, “Acknowledgments”, and “Author Information”, guided the extraction process.

Verification of the performance of CanRisk-AI

A random sample of 1500 abstracts was selected for validation. Three domain experts, serving as the gold standard, independently annotated the abstracts according to predefined inclusion criteria. Discrepancies in labeling were resolved through majority voting, wherein agreement between any two experts was considered sufficient for final adjudication. Finally, 57 abstracts were identified as meeting the thematic requirements and were subsequently subjected to information extraction.

Three trained researchers and CanRisk-AI were employed to filter literature and extract risk values from full-text articles independently. Performance was evaluated based on sensitivity, specificity, and average processing time per article.

Classification of cancers and risks

Cancer classification was performed based on the framework outlined in Cancer Today, with specific modifications to anatomical and histological categories2. The categories were expanded to include eye, small intestine, bone, and sarcomas excluding Kaposi sarcoma. Specifically, Colon and Rectum were combined and labeled as Colonrectum, while gallbladder and biliary tract were grouped under Gallbladder and Biliary Tract. All cancer categories and their corresponding ICD codes are detailed in Table S7. During statistical processing, ambiguous cases that could not be classified based on literature content were labeled as “unclassified”. Records involving multiple cancer sites (e.g., “head and neck cancer”) were designated as “multiple”, and non-organ-specific cancers (e.g., “childhood tumors”) were categorized under “Others”. These adjustments resulted in a total of 42 major cancer categories.

Our research on risk factor statistics was grounded in the Level 2 risk factors from the GBD database26. After a comprehensive review of risk factors reported in the literature, we visualized only those documented in at least ten separate publications, resulting in 76 risk factors being presented in the figure.

Methodological application of CanRisk-DB

For data retrieval from CanRisk-DB, in Case 1, we identified all studies investigating risk factors for oropharyngeal cancer and further filtered those that reported SIRs with complete cohort information. In Case 2, following the procedure described previously, we retrieved studies from CanRisk-DB by specifying “Stomach” as the cancer type and “Type 2 Diabetes” as the risk factor, with the study population restricted to Asian countries. Only studies providing complete sample size information—including the number of events and non-events across exposure groups—were retained. In addition, we restricted the control group to those defined as the general population. The extracted data were organized into 2 × 2 contingency tables.

Meta-analytic procedures were implemented using the R packages meta and metafor27,28. Specifically, the metabin function from the meta package was applied to pool effect estimates using 2 × 2 data under a fixed and random-effects model. To evaluate heterogeneity, we calculated both I² and τ² statistics within the metabin function framework.

Quality assessment checklist for AI studies

To ensure transparency, reproducibility, and reliability of this AI study, we applied a quality assessment checklist. The checklist covers key domains including data quality, reference standards, performance evaluation, potential bias and limitations, and reproducibility (Table S8).

Data source

All primary articles were downloaded manually either from open-access publications or accessed through institutional subscriptions provided by SinoMed database (www.sinomed.ac.cn).

Statistical analysis

The collected data are presented as mean ± SEM. The sample estimation using confidence intervals for proportions was calculated with the Clopper–Pearson method. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using Welch’s t-test. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical and computational analyses were conducted using the Python software environment (version 3.10).
