Reproductive concerns and fertility intention among reproductive-age Chinese women with breast cancer: A multiple mediation analysis
Authors: Shan Li, Qingchun Geng, Yaqin Zhou, Lili Wang, Yanfei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated the relationship between reproductive concerns and fertility intentions among reproductive-age Chinese women with breast cancer, examining the mediating roles of fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from April 2025 to September 2025 among 463 married reproductive-age women with breast cancer. Participants completed validated questionnaires assessing fertility intentions, reproductive concerns, fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy. Structural equation modeling was employed to test a parallel multiple mediation model, with place of residence and number of children included as covariates.ResultsThe mean fertility intention score was 42.77 ± 5.49. Reproductive concerns demonstrated a significant negative direct effect on fertility intentions (β = −0.278, 95% CI [−0.353, −0.179]), accounting for 58.4% of the total effect. All three hypothesized mediators showed significant indirect effects: perceived social support (β = −0.089, 95% CI [−0.133, −0.050], 18.7% of total effect), marital intimacy (β = −0.077, 95% CI [−0.119, −0.042], 16.2% of total effect), and fertility information needs (β = −0.032, 95% CI [−0.060, −0.011], 6.7% of total effect). The total indirect effect through all three mediators was significant (β = −0.198, 95% CI [−0.268, −0.128]), accounting for 41.6% of the total effect.ConclusionsReproductive concerns were associated with lower fertility intention, partly through their associations with lower fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy. Health care providers should provide proactive, needs-responsive fertility counseling, strengthen social support, and promote couple-centered communication to support informed reproductive decision-making.Keywords: Breast cancer, Fertility intention, Reproductive concerns, Mediation analysis, Reproductive-age women

Introduction

Breast cancer has emerged as the most prevalent malignancy among women globally, with incidence rates demonstrating a sustained upward trajectory. This trend is particularly pronounced in China, where breast cancer accounts for approximately 15.6% of all newly diagnosed female cancers, with an estimated 357,161 new cases annually.1 A concerning epidemiological shift toward younger age at diagnosis has been observed in China, where patients with breast cancer are diagnosed at a median age of 48–50 years, nearly a decade younger than their Western counterparts, with a higher proportion of cases occurring in women under 40 years.2,3 Concurrently, advances in multimodal treatment approaches and early detection strategies have yielded remarkable improvements in survival outcomes, with 5-year relative survival rates reaching 82% overall and approaching 90.6% among women aged ≤ 35 years.4 This convergence of earlier age at diagnosis and improved survival has created a growing population of reproductive-age breast cancer survivors who face complex fertility-related decisions.5

The unique sociocultural and policy context in China adds additional layers of complexity to fertility decision-making among young breast cancer survivors. Traditional Chinese culture, deeply rooted in Confucian philosophy, places paramount importance on childbearing and family lineage continuation, embodied in the ancient adage of three unfilial acts, having no descendants is the gravest. This cultural imperative creates substantial psychological pressure on women to fulfill reproductive expectations.6 Combined with China's dramatic family planning policy evolution from the restrictive one-child policy to the current three-child policy, these cultural and policy factors have profoundly influenced reproductive attitudes.7 The delayed childbearing trend among urban Chinese women, coupled with supportive national fertility policies, has resulted in many patients with breast cancer maintaining fertility desires post-diagnosis despite facing cancer-related reproductive challenges.8

Fertility intention refers to an individual's motivation and plan to pursue pregnancy, and is a key component of reproductive decision-making.9 Within social cognitive theory, behavioral intention is regarded as a motivational construct that is shaped by prior cognitive appraisal and perceived contextual resources. Although reciprocal influences are theoretically possible, intention formation is generally viewed as being influenced by preceding psychological and environmental determinants. In breast cancer survivorship, fertility intention is clinically meaningful because it is closely linked to patients' information seeking, engagement with fertility counseling, and preferences regarding fertility preservation and family-building options. Importantly, the present study focuses on fertility intention as the primary outcome and does not assess subsequent fertility-related behaviors or pregnancy outcomes.10 Accordingly, this study aims to identify psychosocial determinants of fertility intention among reproductive-age women with breast cancer , which can inform early assessment and targeted supportive care.

Reproductive concerns are defined as multidimensional worries and psychological preoccupations about fertility and parenthood after cancer, including concerns about infertility risk, pregnancy safety, cancer recurrence, offspring health, financial burden, social stigma, and role expectations.11,12 From a stress–appraisal perspective, reproductive concerns can be conceptualized as a threat-related cognitive–emotional evaluation triggered by cancer diagnosis and treatment-related fertility risks. According to stress appraisal theory, such threat evaluations typically precede downstream motivational or behavioral responses. Therefore, reproductive concerns may function as a proximal psychological antecedent in fertility-related decision-making processes. Previous research conducted primarily in Western populations has documented that 40%–70% of young patients with breast cancer report moderate to high levels of reproductive concerns, which are associated with diminished quality of life and impaired psychological well-being.12,13 However, the manifestation and impact of reproductive concerns may differ in the Chinese context, where collectivistic values, filial piety obligations, and concerns about “face” (mianzi) may intensify fertility-related anxieties and shape their content in culture-specific ways.14,15

In the present study, fertility information needs, social support, and marital intimacy are conceptualized as parallel mediators. These constructs represent distinct domains within the social cognitive framework: cognitive appraisal engagement (information needs), environmental resources (social support), and relational context (marital intimacy). Although interrelations among them are possible, current empirical evidence does not establish a consistent temporal hierarchy. Modeling them as parallel mediators allows examination of their unique indirect contributions while maintaining theoretical parsimony. Although these psychosocial constructs may interact in real-life contexts, they represent conceptually distinct domains and are therefore modeled as parallel mediators rather than sequential variables.

Bandura's triadic reciprocal determinism suggests that behavioral intentions are shaped by dynamic interactions among personal, environmental, and cognitive factors. Within this framework, reproductive concerns may function as an upstream psychological appraisal triggered by cancer diagnosis and treatment-related fertility risks. From a stress appraisal perspective, reproductive concerns represent an individual's cognitive–emotional evaluation of potential threats to future fertility and family-building goals.16,17 Such concerns may subsequently be associated with how individuals perceive available resources and relational contexts, including their perceived social support, marital intimacy, and perceived need for fertility-related information. In turn, these psychosocial resources may shape patients' motivation and readiness to consider future childbearing, thereby influencing fertility intentions. Based on this theoretical rationale and prior empirical evidence, the present study conceptualizes reproductive concerns as the independent variable and fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy as parallel mediators linking reproductive concerns to fertility intention.16

Fertility information needs refer to patients’ perceived demand for knowledge regarding the impact of cancer treatment on fertility, fertility preservation options, and pregnancy-related safety. A high level of information needs reflects the extent to which patients perceive gaps in fertility-related knowledge. However, it does not necessarily imply that individuals actively seek information or engage in fertility-related decision-making behaviors.18 Access to clear and needs-responsive fertility-related information—including Disease Impact Information, Treatment Impact Information, Psychosocial Information, Reproductive Management Information, Sexual Health Information, and Fertility Preservation Information—may support patients in developing more realistic expectations regarding fertility and future family-building, thereby supporting informed reproductive decision-making.19, 20, 21 However, oncofertility services remain underdeveloped in China, and fertility-related discussions are not consistently integrated into oncology care.22

Social support represents an individual's appraisal of emotional, instrumental, and informational support from family, friends, and significant others.23 In the cancer context, support may be relevant to fertility intention by buffering distress, enabling coping, and facilitating practical problem-solving.24,25 At the same time, structural and clinical barriers—such as limited access to specialized oncofertility services, cost, time-sensitive treatment schedules, and clinician discomfort or limited training in fertility counseling—may constrain reproductive planning regardless of support levels.26 Research has documented positive associations between social support and fertility intentions, suggesting that robust support networks facilitate adaptive coping with reproductive concerns.27 However, in the Chinese context, family involvement may be double-edged: while supportive relationships may buffer against concerns, excessive family pressure to reproduce may exacerbate psychological burden.28

Marital intimacy represents emotional closeness, open communication, and mutual trust between spouses, which can enable couples to discuss fertility-related worries and engage in shared decision-making.29 High-quality marital relationships provide a secure foundation for discussing fertility worries and engaging in collaborative decision-making.30 In Chinese marriages, where traditional gender roles may constrain open discussion of reproductive concerns, marital intimacy may facilitate communication and joint decision-making between partners, and is associated with clearer fertility intentions.31

While existing literature has confirmed bivariate associations among these variables in reproductive-age cancer patients, the specific mediating pathways remain unexplored, particularly within the Chinese cultural context. Most previous research has been conducted in Western populations and examined these variables in isolation rather than within an integrated model. No previous study has simultaneously investigated whether and how fertility information needs, social support, and marital intimacy mediate the relationship between reproductive concerns and fertility intention among Chinese breast cancer survivors. Understanding these culture-specific mechanisms is essential for developing theoretically grounded, culturally appropriate interventions that address not only individual psychological factors but also relational and social contextual factors.

It is acknowledged that reciprocal or sequential relationships among these variables are theoretically plausible, particularly given Bandura's emphasis on dynamic interactions. However, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, the proposed model represents one theory-driven and conceptually coherent directional configuration suitable for hypothesis testing. Future longitudinal research is warranted to further explore potential bidirectional or sequential mechanisms. Therefore, this study employed structural equation modeling to investigate the multiple mediating pathways linking reproductive concerns to fertility intention among reproductive-age Chinese women diagnosed with breast cancer. We hypothesized that: (1) reproductive concerns would be negatively associated with fertility intention both directly and indirectly; (2) fertility information needs, social support, and marital intimacy would serve as parallel mediators; (3) higher reproductive concerns would be negatively associated with fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy, while these three factors would be positively associated with fertility intentions, thereby creating negative indirect associations. By elucidating these mechanisms within the Chinese context, this research aims to provide empirical evidence for developing culturally tailored oncofertility interventions and inform health care policies addressing the reproductive needs of young breast cancer survivors in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2025 and September 2025 using convenience sampling. Participants were reproductive-age women with breast cancer receiving treatment at the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University in Jiangsu Province, China. Inclusion criteria included: (1) women with pathologically confirmed primary breast cancer who had undergone surgery or neoadjuvant chemotherapy; (2) married women aged 18–45 years; (3) normal cognitive function with adequate reading comprehension ability; and (4) estimated survival time > 2 years with willingness to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) history of psychological disorders or mental illness; (2) severe comorbidities in other systems; and (3) pre-existing diagnosis of infertility prior to cancer diagnosis.

Sample size

Sample size determination was based on Fritz and MacKinnon's32 recommendations for detecting mediation effects in structural equation modeling. For a multiple mediation model with three parallel mediators, assuming small-to-medium effect sizes for the indirect paths (standardized path coefficients of approximately 0.20–0.30 for the a paths (independent variable to mediators) and b paths (mediators to dependent variable), and considering the need to detect effects across all three indirect pathways simultaneously, a minimum sample size of approximately 300–350 participants is required to achieve 80% statistical power at α = 0.05 using bias-corrected bootstrap methods. Given the complexity of our multiple mediation model with three parallel mediators and the need to control for multiple covariates, we aimed for a more conservative sample size of at least 400 participants to ensure adequate power for detecting small-to-medium indirect effects across all pathways. Accounting for an anticipated 10% rate of incomplete or invalid responses, the target recruitment goal was set at 444 participants. Ultimately, 470 questionnaires were distributed, of which 463 were validly completed and returned, yielding an effective response rate of 98.51%.

Measures

Demographic and clinical information

Based on a literature review and research team discussion, a self-designed questionnaire collected information on age, parity, number and gender of children, surgical approach, adjuvant treatment regimen, tumor stage, monthly household income, education level, employment status, medical insurance type, use of ovarian protection agents, and fertility-related knowledge.

Fertility intention scale (FIS)

The FIS was originally developed by Li et al. 33 to assess fertility intentions among breast cancer survivors and was subsequently adapted and validated for mainland Chinese populations by Zhang et al.34 The scale measures the strength and certainty of women's intentions to pursue future pregnancy following cancer diagnosis and treatment. The instrument comprises 15 items distributed across four dimensions: desire for (additional) children, perceived behavioral control over fertility decisions, subjective norms regarding childbearing, and pregnancy planning attitudes. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating stronger fertility intentions. The original scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.880), and the Chinese version showed good reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.803). In the present study, the Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.926, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Reproductive concerns after cancer scale (RCAC)

The RCAC was developed by Gorman et al.35 to comprehensively assess fertility-related concerns among female cancer survivors and was translated into Chinese and culturally adapted by Qiao et al.36 The scale evaluates multiple dimensions of reproductive concerns that may arise following cancer diagnosis and treatment. The instrument contains 18 items across six domains: concerns about fertility potential, partner disclosure and relationship issues, child's health, acceptance by others, personal health, and pregnancy timing. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 18 to 90. Higher total scores reflect greater levels of fertility-related concerns. The Chinese version demonstrated satisfactory reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.792). In the current study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.933).

Relationship assessment scale (RAS)

The RAS, developed by Hendrick,29 is a brief measure of global relationship satisfaction widely used to assess marital quality and intimacy. It consists of seven items evaluating overall satisfaction with the relationship, perception of relationship quality compared to others' relationships, fulfillment of relationship needs, frequency of regrets about the relationship, expectations met, love for partner, and overall problem frequency. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale, with response options varying by item but consistently scaled such that higher scores indicate greater relationship satisfaction and intimacy. Total scores range from seven to 35. The Chinese version has demonstrated good psychometric properties (Cronbach's α = 0.850).37 In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.866.

Fertility information needs assessment questionnaire for reproductive-age breast cancer patients

This questionnaire was developed by Wang et al.18 specifically to evaluate the information needs regarding fertility-related issues among reproductive-age Chinese patients with breast cancer. The instrument was developed through a rigorous process including literature review, qualitative interviews with patients and health care providers, expert panel review, and psychometric validation. The questionnaire comprises 32 items across six dimensions: cancer treatment impact on fertility, fertility preservation options and procedures, post-treatment conception timing and safety, pregnancy-related health risks, offspring health concerns, and health care system navigation for fertility services. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not needed at all) to 5 (extremely needed), with total scores ranging from 32 to 160. Higher scores indicate greater fertility information needs. The original questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.951). In the present study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.962).

Perceived social support scale (PSSS)

The PSSS was originally developed by Zimet et al.38 and translated into Chinese by Jiang.39 This widely used instrument measures individuals' subjective appraisal of social support adequacy from multiple sources. It has been extensively validated in Chinese cancer patient populations. The scale consists of 12 items distributed equally across three dimensions: support from family (4 items), support from friends (4 items), and support from significant others (4 items). Each item is rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). Total scores range from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating higher levels of perceived social support. Scores can be categorized as low support (12–36), moderate support (37–60), and high support (61–84). The Chinese version has demonstrated strong psychometric properties with test-retest reliability of 0.880. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.932, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Data collection

The research team consisted of the principal investigator, one faculty advisor, and three registered nurses from the oncology department who underwent standardized training in research ethics, informed consent procedures, and questionnaire administration protocols. All team members passed competency assessments prior to participant recruitment.

Eligible participants were identified through review of medical records and consultation with treating physicians. Potential participants were approached during their outpatient clinic visits or inpatient hospital stays. After confirming eligibility and explaining the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality protections, and right to withdraw without consequences, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection was conducted in private consultation rooms to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. Participants with normal literacy and no visual impairment were given the questionnaire packet and asked to complete it independently using pen and paper. Trained investigators remained available in the room to answer procedural questions or clarify item wording if requested, but did not influence participants’ responses. For participants who experienced difficulty reading or comprehending items due to limited literacy, visual impairment, or other barriers, investigators read items and response options verbatim in a neutral tone without providing interpretative cues or suggestions. Participants verbally indicated their responses, which investigators recorded accurately. The typical completion time was 20–30 minutes.

Upon completion, questionnaires were immediately collected by investigators who conducted on-site quality checks to identify potential issues including patterned or inconsistent responding, missing data, and multiple responses to single items. When problems were identified, investigators respectfully requested clarification or completion while the participant was still present. All questionnaires were assigned unique identification codes to protect participant confidentiality, and personal identifying information was stored separately from survey responses in locked filing cabinets accessible only to authorized research personnel.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26 and Mplus Version 8.3. All SEM analyses were conducted under the supervision of a senior methodologist (Y.Z.) with expertise in structural equation modeling and mediation analysis to ensure methodological rigor and appropriate model specification and interpretation. Data entry was conducted independently by two trained research assistants using Microsoft Excel, and entries were cross-validated to identify and correct discrepancies, ensuring data accuracy and integrity. Data were first screened for missing values, revealing less than 5% missing data per variable, randomly distributed (MCAR). Therefore, missing data were handled using expectation-maximization (EM) imputation.

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data, all of which followed normal distribution, were expressed as means and standard deviations (Mean ± SD). Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to compare fertility intention scores across different demographic and clinical characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis examined relationships between fertility intention and reproductive concerns, marital intimacy, fertility information needs, and social support.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to test the hypothesized multiple mediation model. The model specified reproductive concerns as the independent variable, fertility intention as the dependent variable, and fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy as parallel mediators. Based on prior literature and theoretical relevance, several sociodemographic and clinical variables (e.g., age, cancer stage, treatment modality, ovarian protection use, and socioeconomic indicators) were initially considered as potential covariates. 40, 41, 42 To maintain model parsimony and avoid over-adjustment in the structural equation model, preliminary analyses were conducted to examine their associations with fertility intention. Place of residence and number of children were the only variables that showed meaningful associations with fertility intention and were therefore retained as covariates in the final model.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices with the following criteria:43 the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ2/df < 3), comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) ≥ 0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08. Path coefficients were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors. These indices were selected because CFI/TLI and RMSEA/SRMR jointly capture comparative and absolute fit, are widely recommended in SEM reporting, and provide complementary sensitivity to model misspecification beyond the χ2 test.43

The significance of direct and indirect effects was assessed using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals with 5000 resamples. Mediation effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not contain zero. For each mediator, we calculated the indirect effect (product of path coefficients from the independent variable to the mediator and from the mediator to the dependent variable) and the proportion of the total effect mediated. The total indirect effect through all three mediators combined was also estimated. Statistical significance for all analyses was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Effect sizes were reported alongside statistical significance to facilitate interpretation of practical importance.

Results

Participant characteristics and univariate analysis

A total of 463 reproductive-age women with breast cancer were included in the final analysis. Most participants (67.82%) had college-level education or above, and 63.50% were currently employed. Regarding clinical characteristics, 53.35% were diagnosed at stage II, and 88.34% received two or more types of adjuvant therapy. Only 39.31% used ovarian protection agents during chemotherapy. The majority (84.02%) had not actively consulted health care providers about fertility issues, though 63.93% were aware that cancer treatment could impair ovarian function and fertility. Regarding reproductive history, 16.85% had no children, 45.14% had one child, and 38.01% had two or more children.

Univariate analysis revealed that fertility intention scores differed significantly by place of residence and number of children (Table 1). Participants from urban areas reported significantly higher fertility intention scores compared to those from rural areas (43.04 ± 5.36 vs. 41.52 ± 5.94, t = 2.156, P = 0.033). Significant differences in fertility intention were also found across groups with different numbers of children (F = 3.195, P = 0.042), with childless participants showing the highest fertility intention scores (43.97 ± 4.97), followed by those with one child (42.87 ± 5.50) and those with two or more children (42.11 ± 5.63). No significant differences in fertility intention were observed for other demographic or clinical variables, including age, education level, tumor stage, surgical approach, adjuvant treatment regimen, use of ovarian protection agents, monthly household income, employment status, medical insurance type, prior receipt of fertility counseling, knowledge of treatment effects on fertility, or awareness of assisted reproductive technology (all P > 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant bivariate associations among all study variables. Reproductive concerns after cancer were significantly negatively correlated with fertility intention (r = −0.412, P < 0.001), marital intimacy (r = −0.236, P < 0.001), perceived social support (r = −0.266, P < 0.001), and fertility information needs (r = −0.213, P < 0.001). These correlation patterns were broadly consistent with the hypothesized associations and served as descriptive context for the subsequent multiple mediation analyses.

Multiple mediation model testing

A parallel multiple mediation model was tested using structural equation modeling, with fertility intention as the dependent variable, reproductive concerns as the independent variable, and fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy as parallel mediators. Place of residence and number of children were included as covariates based on theoretical considerations and their significant associations with fertility intention in preliminary analyses (see Methods section).

The hypothesized model demonstrated excellent fit to the data: χ2 = 448.443, df = 369, χ2/df = 1.215, CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.989, RMSEA = 0.022, SRMR = 0.067. All fit indices exceeded the recommended thresholds, indicating that the proposed model adequately represented the relationships among the observed variables.

Path coefficients for the structural model are presented in Fig. 1. Reproductive concerns showed significant negative effects on all three mediators: fertility information needs (β = −0.246, P < 0.001), perceived social support (β = −0.310, P < 0.001), and marital intimacy (β = −0.281, P < 0.001). In turn, all three mediators demonstrated significant positive effects on fertility intention: fertility information needs (β = 0.132, P < 0.05), perceived social support (β = 0.286, P < 0.001), and marital intimacy (β = 0.273, P < 0.001).

The significance of direct and indirect effects was assessed using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5000 resamples (Table 3). Results revealed that reproductive concerns had a significant negative direct effect on fertility intention (β = −0.278, 95% CI [−0.353, −0.179]), indicating that higher levels of fertility-related concerns were associated with lower fertility intentions, even after accounting for the mediating variables.

Second, the indirect effect through marital intimacy was significant (β = −0.077, 95% CI [−0.119, −0.042]), indicating that reproductive concerns decreased fertility intention in part by diminishing marital intimacy. Third, the indirect effect through perceived social support was significant (β = −0.089, 95% CI [−0.133, −0.050]), demonstrating that reproductive concerns reduced fertility intention partially through their negative impact on perceived social support.

The total indirect effect of all three mediators combined was significant (β = −0.198, 95% CI [−0.268, −0.128]), accounting for approximately 41.6% of the total effect. Among the three mediators, perceived social support demonstrated the strongest indirect effect, followed by marital intimacy and fertility information needs.

Discussion

Main findings

This study explored the relationship between reproductive concerns and fertility intentions among reproductive-age women with breast cancer, examining the mediating roles of fertility information needs, social support, and marital intimacy. Our findings provide novel insights into the complex psychological and social factors influencing fertility decision-making in this population.

The present study found that reproductive-age women with breast cancer demonstrated moderate levels of fertility intention, with a mean total score of 42.77 ± 5.49 on the FIS. This finding is comparable to results reported by Huang et al.44 among Taiwanese breast cancer survivors (41.18 ± 12.62) using the same validated instrument, suggesting that fertility preservation remains a relevant concern for Chinese women diagnosed with breast cancer during their reproductive years. The similarity in FIS scores between mainland Chinese and Taiwanese populations may reflect shared cultural values regarding motherhood and family continuity within Chinese societies. Although direct numerical comparisons are limited by differences in measurement approaches, our findings are generally consistent with studies conducted in Western populations. For instance, Kopeika et al.45 reported that 41% of breast cancer survivors in the United Kingdom desired future pregnancy, which suggests that sustained fertility intentions are not unique to Chinese cultural contexts. However, systematic reviews have also noted that fertility desire may decline over time due to fear of recurrence and uncertainty regarding offspring health,46 indicating that fertility intention may fluctuate depending on survivorship stage and perceived medical risk.

Cross-cultural comparisons with Western populations are complicated by methodological differences in assessing fertility intentions. While studies from the United Kingdom, Europe, and the Middle East have primarily reported the proportion of patients expressing fertility desires rather than standardized scale scores, the available data suggest substantial interest in fertility preservation across diverse cultural contexts. For instance, studies from Hong Kong reported that 46.1% of young patients with breast cancer expressed fertility-related concerns that influenced their treatment decision-making.47 Although these proportion-based findings cannot be directly compared to FIS scores, they collectively indicate that fertility intentions represent a significant psychosocial concern for reproductive-age patients with breast cancer internationally, irrespective of the measurement approach employed.

Notably, women without children expressed significantly higher fertility intentions than those with children, consistent with previous research.48 Within the context of traditional Chinese culture, childbearing represents not only a biological function but also an important dimension of women's social identity and a means of fulfilling emotional needs related to nurturing and family continuity. Among women who have already achieved their desired family size, the motivation for additional childbearing naturally diminishes. The significant difference in fertility intentions between urban (43.04 ± 5.36) and rural (41.52 ± 5.94) residents aligns with previous findings.49 This disparity likely stems from the greater financial burden that childbearing represents for rural families already strained by cancer treatment costs. As breast cancer increasingly affects younger women who may not have completed family building, health care providers should routinely assess fertility intentions before initiating treatment. Early identification of patients desiring future fertility allows for timely referral to reproductive specialists for fertility preservation, while also ensuring appropriate contraceptive counseling during treatment to prevent unintended pregnancies that could further compromise fertility.

Our findings demonstrated a significant negative association between reproductive concerns and fertility intentions (β = −0.278, 95% CI [−0.353, −0.179]), accounting for 58.4% of the total effect. This negative association is consistent with prior literature demonstrating that higher reproductive concerns are linked to poorer psychological well-being and increased decisional conflict among young cancer survivors.12,13 The scoping review by Xie et al.12 highlighted that reproductive concerns are frequently associated with reduced confidence in family-building decisions, while Canzona et al.13 conceptualized fertility concerns as a central component influencing future-oriented planning after cancer. Together, these findings support the pattern of a direct negative association between reproductive concerns and fertility intention observed in our model.

Previous research indicates that reproductive concerns among reproductive-age patients with breast cancer primarily revolve around fears regarding personal health and potential offspring health.46 Many patients harbor misconceptions about post-cancer pregnancy, believing it endangers both maternal and child health. However, evidence suggests that pregnancy following breast cancer treatment does not adversely affect survival outcomes; indeed, the “healthy mother effect” suggests that breast cancer survivors who achieve pregnancy may experience more favorable disease-free survival and overall survival compared to those who do not.50, 51 Additionally, studies show no significant increase in birth defects or obstetric complications among children born to breast cancer survivors compared to the general population.51

These findings highlight the critical need for health care providers to address reproductive concerns through accurate education, correcting misconceptions, and providing comprehensive fertility-related information. For patients with high levels of reproductive concerns, psychological support should be offered, with referrals to mental health specialists when necessary to alleviate reproductive anxiety and improve overall quality of life.

The present study found that fertility information needs partially mediated the association between reproductive concerns and fertility intention (β = −0.032 95% CI [−0.060, −0.011]), accounting for 6.7% of the total effect. Specifically, higher reproductive concerns were associated with lower fertility information needs, and lower fertility information needs were subsequently associated with weaker fertility intention. This finding suggests that women experiencing greater reproductive concerns may not necessarily report greater informational demands. One possible explanation is that fertility-related topics may impose emotional burden or fear for some patients, making them less willing to engage with fertility-related information. Such reduced perceived informational demand may limit opportunities to obtain accurate knowledge about fertility preservation, pregnancy safety, and reproductive options after breast cancer, thereby weakening confidence and readiness to consider future childbearing. However, fertility information needs in this study reflect perceived informational demand rather than actual information-seeking behavior. Therefore, this interpretation should be considered tentative, as avoidance coping and information-seeking behavior were not directly measured. Nonetheless, regardless of the underlying mechanism, reduced engagement with fertility-related information, whether stemming from emotional avoidance or lower perceived informational demand, may similarly constrain patients' access to knowledge about fertility preservation and reproductive options, a pattern that aligns with prior findings linking information insufficiency to adverse reproductive decision-making outcomes. Previous research has shown that insufficient or unmet fertility-related information may be associated with greater decisional conflict among young adult female cancer survivors.52 Decision-support literature further indicates that providing fertility-related decision support may help reduce decisional conflict and support value-consistent reproductive decision-making.53 Consistent with clinical guidance emphasizing standardized and timely fertility counseling, health care providers should not wait for patients with high reproductive concerns to actively request fertility-related information, but should proactively offer sensitive, individualized, and needs-responsive fertility counseling.54

Our findings revealed that perceived social support exerted the most substantial mediating effect in the relationship between reproductive concerns and fertility intentions (β = −0.089, 95% CI [−0.133, −0.050]), accounting for 18.7% of the total effect. This aligns with prior work suggesting that stronger perceived support is associated with higher fertility intention among reproductive-age women with breast cancer.44 Importantly, intention does not necessarily translate into pregnancy. Pregnancy rates among breast cancer survivors remain low in the literature, reflecting a combination of medical considerations (e.g., recommended timing after treatment), treatment urgency, fear of recurrence, and structural barriers such as limited access to fertility preservation counseling, high costs, and uneven availability of specialized services.26,55 Within these constraints, social support may be relevant to intention by enhancing coping resources and hope, facilitating information exchange, and enabling practical problem-solving. Peer support from fellow cancer survivors who have successfully achieved pregnancy following treatment appears particularly influential, as contact with such peers not only bolsters patients' confidence but also instills hope regarding their own reproductive prospects.8 Health care providers should therefore actively strengthen patients’ support networks through specific strategies including establishing structured peer support programs offering diverse engagement modalities (in-person meetings, online forums, educational workshops), encouraging sustained emotional support from family and friends, and implementing interventions to reduce reproductive anxieties while enhancing decision-making confidence.

The present study confirmed that marital intimacy served as a significant mediator in the relationship between reproductive concerns and fertility intentions (β = −0.077, 95% CI [−0.119, −0.042]), accounting for 16.2% of the total effect. Fertility decisions fundamentally involve both partners, and patients consistently identify spousal support as crucial to their reproductive decision-making processes. High-quality marital intimacy facilitates self-disclosure, enabling patients to share their feelings, needs, and thoughts regarding fertility decisions with their partners, thereby gaining spousal trust and support that enhances confidence in confronting fertility-related challenges.56 A Japanese intervention study documented that psychoeducational interventions targeting both patients and their spouses effectively improved marital intimacy, increased oncofertility knowledge, and facilitated fertility decision-making.57 Clinical practice should therefore extend beyond individual patient counseling to actively engage spouses in fertility-related discussions, encouraging couples to engage in open dialogue about their perspectives, concerns, and expectations regarding fertility, collaboratively identify solutions, and work toward shared decision-making to enhance fertility intentions.

A key added value of the multiple-mediator model is that it quantifies the relative leverage points through which reproductive concerns relate to fertility intention. In our model, the indirect pathway through perceived social support was strongest, followed by marital intimacy and fertility information needs. Clinically, this pattern suggests that interventions should be prioritized and tailored rather than uniformly applied. For women with high reproductive concerns and low perceived support, the first-line focus may be rapid linkage to supportive resources (e.g., family engagement, peer connection, and practical assistance) to restore confidence and coping capacity. When couple dynamics are strained, early couple-based communication support may provide greater incremental benefit than information delivery alone. Targeted, needs-responsive fertility information remains essential, but its potential impact may be maximized when delivered alongside strengthened support and relationship resources.

Clinical implications

These findings have important clinical implications for oncology and reproductive health professionals caring for reproductive-age women with breast cancer. Health care providers should systematically integrate fertility assessment into routine oncology care, proactively initiating fertility-related discussions before treatment commencement to assess patients' reproductive goals and concerns, and providing timely referrals to reproductive specialists for fertility preservation consultations. Given the significant mediating roles of fertility information needs, perceived social support, and marital intimacy, clinical interventions should adopt a multi-faceted approach that includes: (1) proactively delivering standardized, culturally appropriate educational resources addressing common misconceptions about pregnancy safety after cancer treatment and available fertility preservation options, particularly targeting patients with elevated reproductive concerns who may avoid seeking information independently; (2) establishing peer support programs and encouraging family involvement to strengthen patients’ social support networks; and (3) implementing couple-centered counseling that invites spouses to participate in fertility consultations and facilitates collaborative reproductive decision-making. Interventions should prioritize addressing reproductive concerns as the upstream factor, recognizing that reducing fertility-related anxieties may help patients engage more openly with fertility-related counseling, strengthen perceived social support, and improve marital intimacy. Multidisciplinary oncofertility care teams comprising oncologists, reproductive endocrinologists, nurses, social workers, and mental health professionals are ideally positioned to deliver such integrated care and address the complex psychosocial needs of this vulnerable population.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the directionality of observed relationships. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal precedence among study variables, examine how reproductive concerns, psychosocial resources, and fertility intentions evolve across the cancer survivorship trajectory, and identify critical windows for intervention. Second, recruitment from a single tertiary care institution in China may limit generalizability to other health care settings, patient populations, and cultural contexts. Multi-center studies involving diverse geographic regions and health care systems would enhance external validity and permit examination of regional or institutional variations in fertility-related care and outcomes. Third, all measures were based on self-report, which may introduce response bias. Future studies could incorporate objective measures such as medical records and fertility preservation utilization data to complement self-reported outcomes and strengthen the validity of findings. Fourth, our study did not assess important potential moderators such as cultural values, religious beliefs, and health care provider communication quality that may influence the strength of observed relationships. Investigation of these moderators could identify subgroups of patients who may benefit most from targeted interventions and inform the development of culturally sensitive, patient-centered care strategies. Finally, although reproductive concerns were assessed using a validated multidimensional measure, our questionnaire did not include body image–related fertility concerns (e.g., distress arising from treatment-related weight changes or hair loss), which may represent an important psychosocial dimension in this population. Future studies should incorporate body image-related reproductive concerns to provide a more comprehensive understanding of fertility-related experiences and decision-making among breast cancer survivors.

Conclusions

This study found that reproductive concerns among reproductive-age patients with breast cancer were associated with fertility intentions both directly and indirectly through three key mediators: fertility information needs, social support, and marital intimacy. These findings highlight the multifaceted nature of fertility-related decision-making in this population and underscore the need for comprehensive, multi-component interventions. Health care providers should systematically assess fertility intentions, deliver evidence-based fertility counseling to address misconceptions and reduce reproductive concerns, facilitate access to fertility preservation services, strengthen patients’ social support networks through peer support programs and family engagement, and promote couple-based communication to enhance marital intimacy. By addressing these interconnected psychosocial factors, clinicians may better support reproductive-age patients with breast cancer in making informed, autonomous fertility decisions that align with their personal values and life goals.
