GFPT2 upregulation predicts poor prognosis and is associated with TGF-β/Smad activation in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Zixu Wang, Jiang Fu, Shengjie Tang, Gang Qiu, Shoujun Tang, Tao Liu, Haiyang Hu, Haiyang Guo, Li Yang, Haining Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlutamine–fructose-6-phosphate amidotransferase 2 (GFPT2) is a rate-limiting enzyme of the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, but its protein-level expression and functional relevance in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) remain unclear.MethodsGFPT2 protein abundance was evaluated in Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) LUAD proteomics and validated by Western blotting in paired LUAD and adjacent tissues. The Cancer Genome Atlas Lung Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-LUAD) transcriptomes were analyzed for differential expression and pathway enrichment, with survival assessed by Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression. Stable GFPT2 knockdown/overexpression in A549 cells was used to examine proliferation, migration, invasion, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/Smad2/3 signaling by Western blotting.ResultsGFPT2 protein was significantly upregulated in LUAD compared with adjacent tissues. TCGA-based analyses showed distinct transcriptional profiles between GFPT2 mRNA-high and GFPT2-low tumors, with enrichment of extracellular matrix organization, cell adhesion, and TGF-β–related signaling. High GFPT2 mRNA expression was associated with poorer overall survival and remained an independent adverse prognostic factor in multivariable Cox models. In A549 cells, GFPT2 overexpression was accompanied by enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas GFPT2 knockdown showed the opposite effects. Consistently, GFPT2 upregulation was associated with increased TGF-β1 levels and Smad2/3 phosphorylation, suggesting a positive association with TGF-β/Smad signaling activity.ConclusionGFPT2 is upregulated at the protein level in LUAD and is associates with aggressive cellular phenotypes and unfavorable prognosis. Integrated multi-omics analyses and cell-based experiments support a link between GFPT2 and TGF-β/Smad2/3 pathway activation, indicating GFPT2 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05342-0.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1, 2]. Despite advances in surgical resection, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, a substantial proportion of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, and the overall prognosis of LUAD remains unsatisfactory [3–5]. These challenges underscore the need to identify novel molecular determinants involved in LUAD progression and to explore their potential clinical and therapeutic relevance.

Glutamine–fructose-6-phosphate amidotransferase 2 (GFPT2) encodes one of the rate-limiting enzymes of the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP), catalyzing the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate and glutamine into glucosamine-6-phosphate and glutamate, a key precursor for protein O-GlcNAcylation [6–9]. Increasing evidence indicates that GFPT2 is aberrantly upregulated in multiple malignancies and is associated with enhanced tumor aggressiveness and poor clinical outcomes [10]. Functionally, GFPT2 has been reported to promote proliferation, migration, and invasion in colorectal, ovarian, and breast cancers [11–14]. Notably, in lung cancer, GFPT2 is highly expressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts and has been implicated in metabolic reprogramming and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related processes, suggesting a potential role in shaping a pro-invasive tumor microenvironment [15]. However, the expression characteristics and mechanistic relevance of GFPT2 in LUAD, particularly at the protein level, remain incompletely defined.

Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), a member of the TGF-β superfamily, is stored in the extracellular matrix (ECM) as latent complexes and signals through cell-surface receptors upon activation [16–18]. Among its isoforms, TGF-β1 regulates cellular behaviors and ECM production and is a key driver of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibrogenesis [19, 20]. In the canonical pathway, TGF-β activates Smad2/3-dependent transcriptional programs that contribute to migration, invasion, and ECM remodeling [21, 22]. Aberrant TGF-β/Smad signaling is implicated in cancer progression, and in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) it promotes EMT and metastatic phenotypes [23–25].

In this study, we integrated proteomic and transcriptomic analyses with clinical LUAD tissue validation and in vitro functional experiments to systematically investigate GFPT2 expression in LUAD and its association with malignant phenotypes and TGF-β/Smad signaling activity. Our findings provide multi-level evidence supporting the clinical and biological relevance of GFPT2 in LUAD.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Differential protein expression analysis was performed using LUAD proteomic data obtained from Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC), including 109 tumor samples and 102 adjacent non-tumorous tissues. Relative GFPT2 protein abundance between tumor and adjacent tissues was compared.

Transcriptomic analyses were conducted using the Cancer Genome Atlas Lung Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-LUAD) dataset downloaded from the UCSC Xena platform. HTSeq-Counts expression matrices and corresponding clinical information were obtained, and Ensembl gene identifiers were converted to HGNC gene symbols according to GENCODE v22 annotation. Only primary tumor samples were retained for subsequent analyses. Patients were stratified into GFPT2 mRNA high- and low-expression groups based on the median GFPT2 mRNA expression level.

Differential gene expression analysis between the two groups was performed using DESeq2, with thresholds of |log2 fold change| > 0.585 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are listed in ESM 1.

To explore signaling pathways associated with GFPT2-related transcriptional alterations, all genes were ranked according to DESeq2-derived statistics, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using KEGG pathway gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Gene sets with sizes of 15–500 genes and with nominal P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 were considered statistically significant.

To further assess the involvement of TGF-β signaling, core genes from the KEGG TGF-β signaling pathway were examined for differential expression between GFPT2 high- and low-expression groups. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed using the STRING database (version 12.0, Homo sapiens) with a confidence score threshold of 0.4 to identify interaction networks linking GFPT2 with TGF-β–related genes.

Using clinical follow-up data from the TCGA-LUAD cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing and Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate the association between GFPT2 mRNA expression and overall survival.

Clinical sample collection

Paired tumor and adjacent non-tumorous tissue samples were collected from patients with LUAD who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Suining Central Hospital, between December 2023 and December 2024. Eligible patients had postoperative pathological confirmation of LUAD, had not received radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy prior to surgery, and had sufficient fresh tumor tissue with matched adjacent non-tumorous tissue located at least 3 cm away from the tumor margin, together with complete clinicopathological data. Patients with autoimmune diseases, human immunodeficiency virus infection, or a history of other malignancies were excluded.

Fresh tumor and matched adjacent tissues were collected in pairs and processed within 15 min after surgical resection. Tissue specimens were rinsed with sterile saline and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analyses. Clinicopathological characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in ESM 2.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from LUAD tissues or cultured cells using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (YaMei Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., China). Approximately 100 mg of frozen tissue or harvested cells were lysed on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min (tissue samples) or 20 min (cell samples) at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected as total protein extracts.

Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Biosharp, China). Membranes were blocked with a protein-free blocking solution (YaMei Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.) at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, protein bands were detected using an ECL detection kit (GenMol Biotechnology, China) and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon Science & Technology, China).

Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and target protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH. Detailed information on antibodies and dilution ratios is provided in ESM 3.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human LUAD cell line A549 was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentiviral vectors for GFPT2 knockdown and overexpression were constructed and packaged by GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For GFPT2 silencing, three short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting GFPT2 were used, along with a non-targeting shRNA as a negative control (sh-NC). For overexpression, a GFPT2-expressing lentiviral vector and the corresponding empty vector control were employed.

A549 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and infected with the indicated lentiviruses in the presence of an infection enhancer (HiTransG P; GeneChem Co., Ltd.). After incubation for 16 h at 37 °C, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. At 72 h post-infection, cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 24 h to establish stable GFPT2 knockdown or overexpression cell lines.

Reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA concentration and purity were assessed prior to reverse transcription. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the All-in-One Script RT Premix (with dsDNase) (ABM Biotech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using 2× SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Universal) (ABM Biotech Co., Ltd.) in a 96-well plate on a real-time PCR system, with a total of 40 amplification cycles. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for RT–qPCR are listed in ESM 4.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 (Yakon Biotech, China). Briefly, stable A549 cell lines were digested, resuspended in complete medium, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL (100 µL per well), with three technical replicates per group. Peripheral wells were filled with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to minimize evaporation effects.

At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times.

Wound healing assay

Stably transduced cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until approximately 100% confluence was reached. A linear wound was generated using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by washing twice with PBS, and cells were subsequently cultured in serum-free medium to minimize proliferation-related interference and to better assess cell migratory capacity. Images of the same fields were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h under a light microscope. Each experiment was performed independently three times. Wound width and wound closure rate were quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers with 8-µm pore size polycarbonate membranes (Corning, USA). Matrigel (Corning) was thawed at 4 °C, diluted 1:8 with serum-free medium, and 50 µL of the diluted Matrigel was applied to the upper surface of each insert and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C.

Stably transduced A549 cells were harvested and resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL. A total of 200 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber, while 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant.

After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h, non-invading cells were removed from the upper surface of the membrane. Invading cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with crystal violet, and counted under an inverted microscope. Images were captured from three randomly selected fields per insert. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

All cell-based experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test.

Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences assessed by the log-rank test. The prognostic significance of GFPT2 expression was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), and bioinformatic analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2).

Results

GFPT2 protein is upregulated in LUAD tissues

To investigate GFPT2 protein expression in LUAD, we first analyzed proteomic data from the CPTAC cohort, which included 109 LUAD tumor samples and 102 adjacent non-tumorous tissues. Unpaired analysis showed that GFPT2 protein abundance was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent non-tumorous tissues (Fig. 1A).

Paired-sample analysis further confirmed this increase, with the majority of cases exhibiting positive log2 fold changes of GFPT2 protein expression in tumor tissues relative to matched adjacent tissues (Fig. 1B).

To validate these proteomic findings, Western blot analysis was performed using 18 paired LUAD and adjacent non-tumorous tissue samples. Densitometric quantification normalized to GAPDH demonstrated that GFPT2 protein expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared with paired adjacent tissues (Fig. 1C).

Transcriptomic data from the TCGA-LUAD cohort were subsequently analyzed. Patients were stratified into GFPT2 high- and low-expression groups based on the median GFPT2 expression level. Differential expression analysis identified a large number of significantly altered genes between the two groups, as illustrated by the volcano plot (Fig. 1D).

Hierarchical clustering analysis of the top 50 most significantly differentially expressed genes (25 upregulated and 25 downregulated) revealed a clear separation between GFPT2 high- and low-expression groups, indicating distinct global transcriptional profiles associated with GFPT2 expression status (Fig. 1E).

GFPT2 mRNA expression is associated with TGF-β signaling alterations, interaction networks, and clinical prognosis in LUAD

To explore the biological pathways associated with high GFPT2 mRNA expression, pathway enrichment analyses were performed based on the TCGA-LUAD cohort. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the GFPT2 mRNA high-expression group was significantly enriched in multiple pathways related to extracellular matrix organization, cell adhesion, cytokine signaling, and immune regulation, including the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 2A).

Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis further confirmed significant enrichment of the TGF-β signaling pathway in the GFPT2 mRNA high-expression group (normalized enrichment score = 1.803, false discovery rate = 1.36 × 10⁻³). The enrichment plot demonstrated that TGF-β pathway–related genes were preferentially distributed toward the top of the ranked gene list, indicating positive enrichment in samples with high GFPT2 mRNA expression (Fig. 2B).

To further characterize the expression pattern of TGF-β signaling components, core genes from the KEGG TGF-β pathway were extracted to construct a functional module. Heatmap visualization showed distinct expression patterns between GFPT2 high- and low-expression groups, with TGF-β pathway–related genes exhibiting relatively higher expression levels in the GFPT2 mRNA high-expression group, consistent with the GSEA results (Fig. 2C).

To investigate the functional relationships between differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways, a gene–pathway association network was constructed based on significantly enriched KEGG pathways. The network revealed extensive interconnections among immune regulation, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix–related pathways, with several key genes, including THBS1, COL3A1, and TNFAIP6, occupying hub positions and linking multiple pathways (Fig. 2D).

Furthermore, protein–protein interaction analysis demonstrated that GFPT2 was indirectly connected to TGF-β family members, such as TGFB3, INHBA, and GDF6, through proteins involved in hyaluronan metabolism, extracellular matrix regulation, and inflammatory signaling, including HAS1, HAS2, HAPLN1, TNFAIP6, and IL6 (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that GFPT2 may be associated with modulation of TGF-β signaling at the interaction network level.

Clinically, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with high GFPT2 mRNA expression had significantly poorer overall survival compared with those in the low-expression group (log-rank P = 0.042) (Fig. 2F). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis indicated that high GFPT2 mRNA expression remained an independent adverse prognostic factor after adjustment for age and clinical stage (hazard ratio = 1.40, 95% confidence interval: 1.017–2.10) (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results indicate that GFPT2 expression is closely associated with TGF-β–related signaling alterations, molecular interaction network remodeling, and unfavorable clinical outcomes in LUAD.

GFPT2 expression is associated with proliferative, migratory, and invasive phenotypes in A549 LUAD cells

To investigate the functional role of GFPT2 in the malignant behaviors of LUAD cells, stable GFPT2 knockdown and overexpression models were established in A549 cells. Transfection efficiency was validated at both the protein and mRNA levels. Western blotting and RT–qPCR analyses confirmed that GFPT2 expression was significantly reduced in shGFPT2-1 and shGFPT2-2 cells, whereas GFPT2 expression was markedly increased in GFPT2-overexpressing cells compared with corresponding control groups (Fig. 3A, B).

CCK-8 assays showed that GFPT2 knockdown was associated with reduced proliferative capacity of A549 cells, whereas GFPT2 overexpression was accompanied by increased cell proliferation over time (Fig. 3C).

Wound healing assays further showed that GFPT2 knockdown was associated with a reduced wound closure rate, indicating impaired migratory ability, while GFPT2 overexpression was accompanied by accelerated wound closure compared with control cells (Fig. 3D). Representative images of wound closure at indicated time points are shown.

Consistent with these findings, Transwell invasion assays showed that GFPT2 knockdown was associated with fewer invading cells, whereas GFPT2 overexpression was accompanied by increased invasive capacity in A549 cells (Fig. 3E).

Collectively, these results indicate that GFPT2 expression is associated with enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUAD cells, suggesting a functional association between GFPT2 and malignant cellular behaviors.

GFPT2 expression is positively associated with TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling activity in LUAD cells

To further explore the association between GFPT2 expression and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling activity in LUAD cells, key components of this pathway were examined.

In A549 cells with GFPT2 knockdown, the protein level of TGF-β1 was markedly reduced, accompanied by a significant decrease in Smad2/3 phosphorylation, as indicated by reduced p-Smad2/3 to total Smad2/3 ratios (Fig. 4A). In contrast, GFPT2 overexpression was accompanied by increased TGF-β1 expression and enhanced phosphorylation of Smad2/3 (Fig. 4B).

These results indicate a positive association between GFPT2 expression and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling activity, suggesting that this pathway may be involved in GFPT2-associated malignant behaviors in A549 cells.

Discussion

LUAD is the most common histological subtype of NSCLC, with persistently high incidence and mortality rates that pose a serious threat to human health [26]. Although recent advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy have markedly promoted precision treatment for LUAD, the emergence of therapeutic resistance and pronounced molecular heterogeneity continue to substantially limit clinical efficacy [27]. Therefore, systematic elucidation of key molecular abnormalities and their associated signaling pathways in LUAD is of great importance for the identification of potential therapeutic targets. In the present study, we integrated proteomic data, bioinformatic analyses, and in vitro functional experiments to demonstrate aberrant protein-level upregulation of GFPT2 in LUAD and its close association with malignant cellular phenotypes and activation of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby providing multi-level evidence supporting a potential biological role of GFPT2 in LUAD progression.

GFPT2 is one of the rate-limiting enzymes of the HBP and participates in protein O-GlcNAcylation by regulating the production of UDP-GlcNAc, thereby playing an important role in cellular metabolic regulation and signal transduction [28, 29]. Previous studies have shown that GFPT2 is highly expressed in multiple malignancies and is closely associated with tumor invasion, metastasis, and unfavorable prognosis [30–32]. Szymura et al. further reported that GFPT2 is involved in EMT–associated cell migration in NSCLC and that its mRNA high expression is significantly correlated with poor patient survival [33]. Consistent with these findings, survival analyses in the present study revealed a stable association between elevated GFPT2 mRNA expression and adverse outcomes in patients with LUAD, and multivariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed GFPT2 as an independent prognostic factor. Notably, publicly available TCGA-LUAD transcriptomic data do not show a consistent tumor-associated increase in GFPT2 mRNA compared with normal lung tissues, which was not fully consistent with the protein-level upregulation observed in CPTAC and our paired-tissue Western blotting, as well as prior immunohistochemical findings. This discrepancy may be attributable to post-transcriptional regulation, differences in protein stability, or tumor microenvironment–related regulatory mechanisms. Accordingly, by integrating CPTAC proteomic data with Western blot analysis of paired LUAD tissues, we validated the significant upregulation of GFPT2 at the protein level, providing more direct evidence for its aberrant expression in LUAD.

At the functional level, we employed GFPT2 knockdown and overexpression models to evaluate the association between GFPT2 expression and malignant phenotypes of LUAD cells. Our results showed that GFPT2 silencing was associated with reduced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas GFPT2 overexpression showed the opposite pattern, indicating that GFPT2 expression is functionally associated with multiple malignant behaviors in LUAD cells. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing the pro-tumorigenic role of GFPT2 in colorectal, ovarian, and breast cancers [11–14], and further extend its oncogenic function to LUAD. Taken together, our data support the notion that GFPT2 may serve as a potential prognostic biomarker in LUAD and may be associated with tumor aggressiveness and invasive cellular behaviors during disease progression.

Based on differential expression and pathway enrichment analyses of the TCGA-LUAD cohort, we identified a significant association between high GFPT2 mRNA expression and the TGF-β signaling pathway, suggesting a potential link between GFPT2 and TGF-β–related signaling networks. Accumulating evidence indicates that the TGF-β/Smad pathway plays a pivotal role in LUAD progression by inducing EMT, enhancing invasive and metastatic capacities, and shaping an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [34–37]. Multiple studies have highlighted the importance of TGF-β/Smad signaling in regulating malignant phenotypes of LUAD cells. For example, Cai et al. reported that TGF-β/Smad signaling participates in LUAD cell migration and invasion [38]; Liu et al. demonstrated that AHNAK2 promotes LUAD cell migration, invasion, and EMT via activation of the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway [39]; and Li et al. showed that DAB2 interacts with LASP1 to regulate proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cells through modulation of TGF-β/Smad signaling [40]. Collectively, these studies underscore the central role of the TGF-β/Smad pathway in LUAD cell behavior.

In line with these findings, construction of a TGF-β pathway core gene module in the present study revealed overall upregulation of pathway-related genes in GFPT2 high-expression samples, consistent with GSEA results. Furthermore, a KEGG gene–pathway association network based on DEGs demonstrated extensive interconnections among pathways related to ECM remodeling, cell adhesion, and immune regulation, with the TGF-β signaling pathway occupying a central position. At the protein interaction level, STRING analysis indicated that GFPT2 does not directly interact with TGF-β family members but may be linked to the TGF-β signaling network through multiple indirect interaction routes involving hyaluronan metabolism, ECM regulation, and inflammation-related factors. These observations suggest that GFPT2 may be indirectly associated with TGF-β signaling activity through tumor microenvironment– and matrix-associated processes. In line with this notion, Zhang et al. reported that GFPT2 is highly expressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts and promotes lung adenocarcinoma progression through tumor microenvironment–related metabolic reprogramming [15]. Consistently, Western blot analyses showed that GFPT2 upregulation was accompanied by increased TGF-β1 expression and enhanced Smad2/3 phosphorylation, whereas GFPT2 knockdown produced the opposite effects. Collectively, these multi-level data support a positive association between GFPT2 expression and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling activity, which may be related to malignant phenotypes of LUAD cells.

Despite these findings, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the functional experiments were primarily conducted in vitro and relied on a single LUAD cell line, A549, which may not fully capture the molecular heterogeneity of LUAD. Although both GFPT2 knockdown and overexpression in A549 cells provided bidirectional functional evidence, validation in additional LUAD cell lines is required to further confirm the generalizability of these findings. Notably, previous studies using additional NSCLC models, including H1299 cells, have also supported the involvement of GFPT2 in cell motility [33]. Second, the lack of in vivo animal models precludes further validation of the tumor-associated role of GFPT2 under physiological conditions. Third, although our bioinformatic analyses and cell-based experiments suggest a positive association between GFPT2 expression and TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling activity, the present study does not establish a direct causal relationship between GFPT2 and this pathway. Further pathway inhibition or rescue experiments are required to clarify whether TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling mediates GFPT2-associated malignant phenotypes.

From a translational perspective, GFPT2 has emerged as a potential therapeutic target and prognostic biomarker. Previous studies have shown that GFPT2 depletion significantly suppresses growth and invasion of KL-mutant NSCLC cells, highlighting its therapeutic potential in specific molecular contexts [41]. In addition, the HBP mediated by GFPT2 has been proposed as a novel intervention target in KRAS-mutant lung cancer [42]. Moreover, GFPT2 expression has been associated with increased aggressiveness and poor prognosis in various solid tumors, including ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, and NSCLC, through distinct oncogenic signaling axes such as β-catenin and NF-κB [11, 13, 33]. Combined with our findings showing aberrant GFPT2 protein expression, functional associations with malignant phenotypes, and close association with TGF-β/Smad signaling in LUAD, GFPT2 represents a promising candidate for prognostic evaluation and further therapeutic investigation. Future studies may explore strategies targeting GFPT2 alone or in combination with TGF-β pathway inhibitors to improve precision treatment for LUAD.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that GFPT2 is significantly upregulated at the protein level in LUAD tissues and is closely associated with TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling activation. Our findings suggest that TGF-β/Smad signaling may be involved in GFPT2-associated malignant phenotypes, although the direct causal relationship requires further validation. Together with its unfavorable prognostic significance, these findings support GFPT2 as a potential diagnostic and therapeutic target in LUAD.
