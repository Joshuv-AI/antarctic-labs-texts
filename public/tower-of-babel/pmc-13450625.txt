Molecular Epidemiology and Multilevel Drug Resistance Mechanisms of Escherichia coli in the Context of One Health: A Comprehensive Narrative Review
Authors: Meicheng Liu, Ling Liu, Hui Fu, Xiaoyan Li, Yue Wang, Benjin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscherichia coli (E. coli) is an important pathogen responsible for foodborne disease outbreaks and can be transmitted through the food chain via the fecal-oral route, causing both intestinal and extraintestinal infections in warm-blooded animals. Against the backdrop of improper use of antibiotics in medicine, agriculture, and animal husbandry, together with the adaptive evolutionary capacity of E. coli, the increasing detection rate of multidrug-resistant E. coli and the shifting of dominant clones among regions have posed a great challenge to the treatment and control of infections. Against this background, this comprehensive narrative review takes the molecular epidemiological characteristics of E. coli as an entry point to systematically summarize its biological features and epidemiological patterns and then explains the mechanisms of antibiotic resistance from the molecular level, including intrinsic resistance conferred by its inherent structural features, acquired resistance (acquisition of drug resistance genes, alteration of drug targets, production of target protection proteins, enzymatic modification and inactivation of antibiotics, changes in cell membrane permeability, and upregulation of active efflux pump systems) and biofilm-mediated adaptive resistance. Finally, this review summarizes current antibiotic treatment strategies and emerging therapeutic approaches for E. coli infections. Within a One Health framework, this review highlights the resistance characteristics and transmission dynamics of E. coli to inform prevention strategies and antimicrobial development.

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli), first isolated in the late 19th century, is a common Gram-negative bacillus. In humans and other vertebrates, E. coli can persist as a commensal member of the intestinal microbiota, but under conditions such as impaired host immunity or disruption of the gut microecological balance, it may also act as a major opportunistic pathogen, causing intestinal or extraintestinal infections, including diarrhea,1 bloodstream infections,2 urinary tract infections (UTIs),3 and central nervous system infections.4 In recent years, infections caused by E. coli have remained highly prevalent worldwide and have emerged as a major public health concern. According to data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), nearly 60,000 E. coli-associated outbreak reports and approximately two million E. coli infection cases were recorded in the United States between 1971 and 2023, resulting in more than 2000 deaths.5 In China, according to data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET), the clinical isolation rate of E. coli has consistently remained above 18% over the past decade, highlighting its persistent clinical prevalence.6 With the massive use and even abuse of antibiotics, an increasing number of bacterial pathogens have evolved into multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs). In the bacterial priority pathogens list published by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2024, E. coli was classified into the Critical Priority Group, underscoring its significance to global public health.7 In addition, E. coli exhibits remarkable adaptive evolutionary capacity and can acquire and maintain antimicrobial resistance (AMR) through multiple pathways under antibiotic selective pressure, which further contributes to a high rate of recurrent infections.8

The One Health concept recognizes the close ecological interconnectedness of microbiomes across humans, animals, and the environment, including the interspecies transmission of bacterial strains.9 In the context of the increasing burden of infectious diseases and the worsening threat of AMR, the One Health framework emphasizes a holistic research perspective integrating humans, animals, and the environment while highlighting their ecological interdependence.10 As a commensal and opportunistic pathogen with remarkable genomic plasticity, capable of colonizing humans, animals, and diverse environmental niches, E. coli acts as both a major clinical pathogen and an important reservoir of AMR across human, animal, and environmental settings, making it an ideal indicator organism for investigating the cross-sector transmission of AMR. In addition, the molecular epidemiological evolution and multilayered resistance mechanisms of E. coli collectively contribute to the persistence and spread of AMR, underscoring the urgent need for an integrated understanding of its biology, epidemiology, and resistance determinants to support infection control and antimicrobial therapy. Against this background, this comprehensive narrative review summarizes the molecular epidemiology, biological characteristics, and resistance mechanisms of E. coli and discusses current and emerging therapeutic strategies from a One Health perspective.

A comprehensive narrative literature search was performed using the PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases. The search strategy incorporated keywords such as “Escherichia coli”, “molecular epidemiology”, “biological characteristics”, “molecular typing”, “antimicrobial resistance”, “multidrug resistance”, “resistance mechanisms”, “treatment”, and “One Health”, combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR) to improve search precision and relevance. Furthermore, the reference lists of relevant publications were manually screened to identify additional studies of interest.

Molecular Epidemiology of E. coli

Basic Properties of E. coli

E. coli is an opportunistic pathogen that causes a wide range of infectious diseases in humans and animals and is classified as a facultative anaerobic Gram-negative short bacillus. As an important component of the normal intestinal microbiota, E. coli is typically one of the earliest microorganisms to colonize the neonatal gut. It predominantly resides in the mucus layer of the colon and cecum and is subsequently shed in feces along with epithelial turnover and bacterial detachment. During commensal colonization, E. coli maintains a mutually beneficial relationship with the host and does not cause disease; however, its pathogenic potential becomes evident when host immune function is compromised, intestinal microecological balance is disrupted, or the mucosal barrier is breached. Moreover, certain strains have acquired specific virulence attributes through long-term adaptive evolution, enabling them to cause a broad spectrum of endogenous or exogenous infections, such as diarrhea, cystitis, and meningitis.11 The pathogenicity of E. coli relies on the coordinated action of multiple virulence factors, including adhesins, iron acquisition systems, enterotoxins, invasins,12 hemolysins,13 and transporter proteins.14 Different combinations of these virulence factors confer highly diverse pathogenic mechanisms on E. coli, leading to the continuous emergence of novel bacterial pathotypes. In addition, as a foodborne pathogen, outbreaks of infections caused by E. coli have become a major public health problem.

Molecular Typing of E. coli

A variety of typing methods have been developed for E. coli, among which serotyping based on cell surface antigens represents the most traditional and widely used approach. In addition, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) has developed rapidly in recent years and has become a core tool in molecular epidemiological studies and global surveillance systems.

Multiple antigens are present on the surface of E. coli, including the somatic (O) antigen, flagellar (H) antigen, capsular (K) antigen, fimbrial (F) antigen, and the enterobacterial common antigen (ECA).15,16 The O antigen, located in the outermost layer of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on the outer membrane (OM), exhibits high structural diversity and genetic plasticity and serves as the central determinant for serotyping. E. coli harbors over 100 O antigens, including multiple subtypes, with specific O types associated with defined virulence phenotypes.11 The H antigen is composed of flagellin proteins, and variations in flagellin structural domains contribute to H antigen diversity. Currently, 53 H antigens have been identified in E. coli. Notably, some strains have been reported to express two distinct H antigen phenotypes, which may be associated with structural alterations in mobile genetic elements (MGEs) carrying H-antigen-encoding genes.17 The K antigen, which is predominantly polysaccharide in nature, represents a surface-associated virulence factor. Nearly 100 K antigens are known for E. coli.18 Accumulating evidence indicates correlations among O, H, and K antigens. Accordingly, E. coli serotyping is primarily based on the combined profiles of these three phenotypes. The F antigen is mainly associated with bacterial adhesion. ECA exists in three structural forms: cyclic ECA (ECACYC), lipopolysaccharide-linked ECA (ECALPS), and phosphoglyceride-linked bound ECA (ECAPG), all of which are conservatively distributed in the OM of Enterobacteriaceae.16,19 The characteristics of these five surface-associated antigens are summarized in Table 1. The MLST typing technique is based on the determination of the nucleotide sequences of the seven housekeeping genes, assigning allele numbers to each distinct locus sequence, and arranging the allele numbers in a specific order to obtain the sequence type (ST) of the strain, so as to identify the unique genotype of the strain. Closely related strains typically share the same ST or differ at only a few loci. Owing to its high reproducibility and standardized nomenclature, MLST has become a standard approach for investigating the clonal dissemination and evolutionary relationships of E. coli across diverse hosts, regions, and AMR backgrounds.15 Using MLST, Vanstokstraeten et al20 classified 234 pathogenic E. coli bloodstream isolates from a medical center in Belgium into 82 STs, among which ST131 and ST88 were associated with nosocomial infections. Similarly, Xia et al21 applied MLST to globally distributed blaNDM-carrying E. coli strains and identified 232 STs, with ST167 predominating in China and India and ST361 in Europe, highlighting the regional distribution patterns of AMR clones. Nonetheless, conventional seven-locus MLST provides limited discriminatory power for closely related strains. With the rapid advancement of whole-genome sequencing technologies, higher-resolution methods have been developed based on MLST, namely core genome MLST (cgMLST) and whole genome MLST (wgMLST). cgMLST targets the core genes shared by strains, whereas wgMLST incorporates both core and accessory genomic loci, thereby providing substantially improved resolution for phylogenetic and epidemiological investigations.22 Currently, cgMLST and wgMLST have been widely applied to investigate globally disseminated E. coli lineages from clinical, animal, and environmental sources. An Australian study23 analyzed 5471 E. coli isolates collected from human, animal, environmental, and food sources over a 36-year period using cgMLST and demonstrated that 10 STs, including ST131 and ST1193, were shared across different ecological niches, suggesting extensive cross-sector transmission. In addition, a large-scale genomic dataset comprising 1999 E. coli isolates revealed substantial phylogenetic and lineage diversity across multiple cg/wgMLST profiles, further emphasizing the value of high-resolution typing schemes for global surveillance and source-tracking investigations.24 Collectively, these studies demonstrate that MLST-based approaches provide an effective framework for investigating the evolutionary dynamics and dissemination of high-risk E. coli clones.

Evolution of Drug Resistance in E. coli

E. coli is mainly transmitted through the fecal-oral pathway, with a broad and complex transmission chain. Fruits, vegetables, and meat products are frequently contaminated due to unhygienic conditions such as fecal contact and cross-contamination. Humans and animals may become infected through the consumption of contaminated food or drinking water, resulting in sporadic cases or even outbreak events. In addition, a wide variety of animals are thought to be reservoir hosts for E. coli, so human-to-human, animal-to-animal, and human-to-animal contacts can further facilitate its dissemination across different hosts. Previous studies25 have shown that livestock products seem to be more susceptible to colonization by AMR bacteria, whereas vegetables, fruits, and herbs are occasionally contaminated; however, consumption of raw or undercooked foods–particularly in the presence of cross-contamination–significantly increases the risk of bacterial infection.

With the widespread use of antibiotics in medicine, agriculture, and animal husbandry, global antibiotic selection pressure has continued to intensify, driving the rapid emergence and spread of MDROs. As one of the most prevalent members of the clinical Enterobacteriaceae bacteria, E. coli exhibits remarkable evolutionary plasticity and adaptability in acquiring AMR.8 Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBLs) were first reported in 1983, and ESBL-producing E. coli (ESBL-EC) subsequently became globally prevalent.26 In 1988, plasmid-mediated cephalosporinases (pAmpC enzymes) were discovered in E. coli, which can spread across bacterial species and geographic regions via transformation and conjugation, further exacerbating resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. Carbapenem antibiotics are considered to be the last line of defense in the clinical treatment of bacterial infections with Gram-negative bacteria, especially Enterobacteriaceae, and are widely used against ESBL-EC infections. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) were first reported in the United States in 2001, and the production of carbapenemase is a major determinant of carbapenem resistance. CRE has since undergone rapid global spread, leading to its inclusion among MDROs.27 Fluoroquinolone antibiotics (FQs) have long been used for the treatment of E. coli infections and were once regarded as last-resort agents.28 Mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining regions (QRDRs) of target genes and the presence of plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes are common resistance mechanisms in E. coli. In 1998, PMQR genes, such as qnr, were first identified, after which resistance rates of E. coli to FQs steadily increased and now rank among the highest worldwide.28,29 Although the clinical use of aminoglycoside antibiotics (AGAs) has declined due to potential toxicity and the widespread availability of alternative drugs, they remain of critical importance in the treatment of MDRO infections.30 Mutations at the A site on 16S rRNA (A1408G) and specific methylation modifications (m7G1405 or m1A1408) leading to AGAs resistance were found in Enterobacteriaceae in 1987, followed by the discovery of plasmid-mediated 16S rRNA methyltransferases, and in addition, the production of aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme (AME) is another important mechanism for the development of resistance, all of which have led to high levels of AGAs resistance. A study31 successfully validated the gar gene that produces high levels of resistance to AGAs in E. coli. Sulfonamide antibiotics (SAs) were first used clinically in 1935 for the treatment of bacterial infections, and resistance to SAs associated with Gram-negative enteric bacteria is mostly mediated by plasmids. The acquired SAs resistance genes sul1, sul2, and sul3 were identified in E. coli in 1975, 1980, and 2003. It has been shown that expression of a single sul gene can lead to high levels of resistance to SAs in E. coli without additional AMR genes.32 Tigecycline belongs to the third generation of tetracyclines with ultra-broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity for the treatment of Gram-positive or Gram-negative MDRO infections. In 2019, the plasmid-mediated high-level tigecycline resistance gene tet(X4) was found in E. coli isolates to inactivate all tetracycline families, which poses a serious threat to clinical therapy.33 Colistin is mostly used in agriculture and veterinary medicine due to its toxicity, and with the increasing AMR in bacteria of animal origin and the emergence of CRE, it is considered to be the last line of defense for the treatment of Gram-negative MDRO infections. In 2016, the plasmid-mediated mobile colistin resistance gene mcr in E. coli was first reported in China, and its dissemination across bacterial species and host populations has further highlighted the complexity of AMR spread within the One Health framework.34 In response to the above phenomenon, some older antibiotics such as fosfomycin (FOS) have been reevaluated in terms of clinical applications due to their broad-spectrum activity and unique mechanisms of bacterial inhibition.35 Nevertheless, the emergence of resistant strains continues to compromise their therapeutic efficacy. The ongoing evolution of AMR has raised serious global public health concerns, underscoring the urgent need for the development of novel antimicrobial agents. Epidemiologic Characteristics of E. coli

E. coli is one of the most common pathogens in hospital- and community-acquired infections. According to data from the CHINET in China, the clinical isolation rate of E. coli remains high, although an overall downward trend was observed from 2014 to 2024 (Figure 2A).6 Bacterial foodborne outbreaks are one of the major public health risks. The CDC National Outbreak Reporting System provides statistics on annual E. coli foodborne outbreaks in the United States (Figure 2B).5 In addition, according to the CDC National Outbreak Reporting System, two foodborne outbreaks of E. coli were reported in the United States in October 2024, which were caused by E. coli O157:H7-contaminated onions and E. coli O121-contaminated organic carrots, respectively, with one fatality in each outbreak. Among the known E. coli STs, several high-risk clones have attracted widespread attention due to their strong AMR, virulence, and transmission potential. ST131 is the most extensively studied global multidrug-resistant high-risk clone, closely associated with UTIs and bloodstream infections, and is considered a representative human-associated MDRO clone.36 ST167, an international multidrug-resistant high-risk clone producing New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM), particularly NDM-5, has played a critical role in the dissemination of the blaNDM-5 gene in China.37 A study38 from France further demonstrated that ST361 is also emerging as a key contributor to the spread of NDM-5 carbapenemase, suggesting an expanding host spectrum of this AMR determinant. ST410 is a globally distributed MDRO clone with the capacity to produce both ESBLs and carbapenemases. It is widely disseminated across animals, humans, and the environment and usually belongs to the second or third most frequent ST, following ST131 and ST167.39 ST1193 is an emerging high-risk E. coli clone and is considered one of the major globally disseminating lineages following ST131.40 In contrast to human-associated high-risk clones, ST95 is a common ST among avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC) with significant population and genetic diversity, and can be transmitted between animals and humans, raising the risk of zoonotic diseases.41 Furthermore, a newly identified multidrug-resistant ST2797 isolate from wastewater, which may belong to ESBL-EC, has the potential to colonize the human gut and shares similar phenotypic characteristics with ST131, indicating that environmentally derived strains may play an important role in the evolution and dissemination of AMR clones.42

β-Lactamase production is a common mechanism by which Gram-negative bacteria develop resistance to β-lactam antibiotics in clinical settings. At present, there are two classification systems for β-lactamase. The molecular biology classification (Ambler classification) based on differences in specific amino acid sequences and hydrolysis mechanisms divides them into four major classes, A, B, C, and D. The three major classes, A, C, and D, are serine enzymes, whereas class B consists of metalloenzymes. Representative enzyme families include TEM, SHV, CTX-M, and KPC in class A; NDM and VIM in class B; AmpC enzymes in class C, with major families such as CMY, ADC, FOX, and DHA; and class D enzymes, collectively referred to as OXA-type β-lactamases.43 The functional classification (Bush classification), based on differences in substrates, biochemical properties, and inhibitors, is divided into three groups. Group 1 consists of AmpC enzymes, which belong to class C; Group 2 is the largest and most heterogeneous group of β-lactamases, containing many subgroups belonging to classes A and D; Group 3 consists of metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), which contain zinc ions at their active sites. A small number of penicillinases included in group 4 of the 1995 Bush classification were omitted from the updated taxonomy because they were more likely to be included in the other functional groups.44

ESBLs are plasmid-mediated enzymes that can hydrolyze penicillins, cephalosporins, and monocyclic amide antibiotics but have weak hydrolysis ability for cephamycins and carbapenems, and most of them belong to classes A and D. SHV-ESBLs and TEM-ESBLs were the most general ESBLs in the 1990s, however, with the massive use of third-generation cephalosporins, CTX-M-ESBLs were discovered and led to worldwide dissemination, while gradually replacing the dominance of SHV-ESBLs and TEM-ESBLs, and the speedy global spread of the E. coli ST131 clone which produced CTX-M-15 was involved in this process.45,46 Notably, distinct CTX-M variants exhibit marked geographic heterogeneity. CTX-M-14 and CTX-M-55 are the most prevalent types in Asia and are also the chief genotypes in China; CTX-M-1 is mainly widespread in Europe, accounting for dominance in France and Canada; CTX-M-15 is widely distributed in most global regions including the United States, South Korea, and Spain; among the MGEs involved, the plasmid CTX-M-1_IncI1-I(Alpha)_Escherichia has been identified as a key vehicle facilitating the inter-host and inter-regional transmission of CTX-M enzymes on a global scale.47 Intestinal colonization of ESBL-EC in healthy populations is closely related to community-acquired diseases, and the overall intestinal carriage rate of ESBL-EC in the global community is estimated to be 16.5%, with the United States being the lowest (3.5%) and China being the fourth (58.5%). Importantly, the intestinal carriage of multidrug-resistant ESBL-EC in healthy populations is increasing worldwide, highlighting the expanding community reservoir of clinically relevant AMR determinants.48

Carbapenemases mostly belong to class A, B and D, which are a class of β-lactamases specialized in hydrolysis of carbapenem antibiotics, and NDM, KPC, VIM, IMP, OXA-48 are the most common types.49 Epidemiological data indicate that the overall global prevalence of CRE, covering livestock, humans and the environment, was estimated to be 43.06%, with Carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli (CREC) being the second most prevalent CRE bacterial species (26.42%).50 E. coli is one of the major carriers of the blaNDM gene, and its prevalence exhibits marked geographical heterogeneity, with Asia accounting for the highest proportion (77.60%).21 Epidemiological investigations have shown that ST167, ST410, and ST361 are the most prevalent STs among blaNDM-carrying E. coli worldwide, with ST167, the dominant lineage, being particularly prevalent in Asia and North America. In addition, among the various blaNDM variants, blaNDM-5 is the most common and predominates in Asia, Europe, Africa, and North America.21 Notably, multidrug-resistant CREC strains harboring multiple carbapenemases have already emerged, such as E. coli ST8346 co-carrying blaNDM-5, blaKPC-2, and blaOXA-181.51 The emergence of these high-risk clones underscores the evolutionary adaptability of CREC and their potential to accelerate the interspecies dissemination of carbapenem resistance genes through MGEs.

The emergence of colistin resistance has raised serious concerns. A global analysis52 of clinical colistin-resistant E. coli isolates reported an overall prevalence of 1.26%, with mcr-1 being the most frequently detected colistin resistance gene, followed by mcr-3; among mcr-carrying strains, the E. coli ST10 clone is the most widely disseminated lineage. Collectively, these findings indicate that the parallel dissemination of multiple AMR genes is progressively narrowing therapeutic options for E. coli infections and underscore the urgent need to strengthen cross-host and cross-environment AMR surveillance and control within a One Health framework.

Many resistance genes in E. coli are disseminated through plasmid-mediated horizontal gene transfer (HGT). The extensive diversity, structural plasticity, and modular organization of plasmids have substantially accelerated the emergence and spread of MDROs in both hospital and community settings. Accumulating evidence indicates that clinically relevant AMR genes are frequently co-localized on the same plasmids, facilitating the simultaneous acquisition and stable maintenance of multiple AMR phenotypes. In most of the above-mentioned studies, multidrug resistance plasmids were found, for example, plasmids carrying the blaCTX-M gene showed a high frequency of synergy with AGAs, FQs and cephalosporin AMR genes, thereby promoting the stepwise accumulation of multidrug resistance under antibiotic selection pressure.47 Similarly, plasmid replicons carrying the qnr gene have been reported to co-carry mcr-1.1, emphasizing the potential of single MGEs to facilitate the concurrent spread of AMR.28

Classification of E. coli

Classification According to Virulence-Associated Factors and Pathogenic Characteristics

Based on virulence-associated factors (VAFs) and pathogenic characteristics, E. coli can be classified into three major groups: commensal E. coli, diarrheagenic E. coli (DECs), and extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPECs). DECs include seven categories: enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC), enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC), enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC), diffusely adherent E. coli (DAEC), and adherent-invasive E. coli (AIEC).11 ExPECs of human origin include uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), newborn meningitis E. coli (NMEC), sepsis-associated E. coli (SEPEC). In addition, there are also APEC, mammary pathogenic E. coli (MPEC), and porcine ExPEC.3,4,41

DECs play an important role in the development of intestinal diarrhea. EPEC is a human enteric pathogen, which mostly colonizes the small intestine and usually causes acute diarrhea in children in developing countries, with high morbidity and mortality. EHEC is generally regarded as an evolutionary derivative of EPEC and represents the most crucial foodborne pathogen causing human gastrointestinal infections. EHEC typically colonizes the large intestine and exhibits a pronounced zoonotic nature, with O157:H7 being the most typical serotype. EHEC can produce Shiga toxins (Stxs), which can lead to a spectrum of clinical manifestations, including diarrhea, hemorrhagic colitis, and hemolytic uremic syndrome. EPEC and EHEC can utilize the type III secretion system (T3SS) to inject effector proteins into host cells to facilitate their colonization and pathogenicity. When they adhere closely to the surface of intestinal epithelial cells, the microvilli on the brush border side of the intestinal epithelial cells are gradually shed and disappear, the cytoskeleton is rearranged, and pseudopods are formed by the intestinal epithelial cells to wrap the bacteria. This process is known as the attaching-and-effacing lesion (A/E).53,54 ETEC is a general pathogen of bacterial diarrhea in children, travelers, and livestock in low- and middle-income countries and can colonize the small intestine of both humans and animals. ETEC has two types of key virulence factors: colonization factors (CFs) responsible for adhesion and enterotoxins, which are classified into heat-stable toxin (ST) and heat-labile toxin (LT).1,55 EAEC often causes acute persistent diarrhea and traveler’s diarrhea and affects the growth of children in low-income and industrialized countries. EAEC adheres to jejunal, ileal, and colonic epithelial cells, and is named for its distinctive “aggregation-like” or “bricks-and-mortar” adherence to Hep-2 epithelial cells. EAEC produces an enteroaggregative heat-stable toxin and aggR is recognized as a central transcriptional regulator.56 EIEC is phylogenetically closely related to Shigella, which has a strong pathogenicity and can cause symptoms similar to bacillary dysentery such as diarrhea and mucopurulent blood stools. EIEC primarily invades colonic epithelial cells and is considered an obligate intracellular pathogen, with its pathogenic process largely mediated by the T3SS.57 DAEC represents a heterogeneous group of strains that share certain phylogenetic similarities with EAEC. DAEC displays a diffuse adherence pattern on Hep-2 and HeLa cells and predominantly colonizes the small intestine. It has been implicated in childhood diarrhea and UTIs in adults, with pathogenesis mainly mediated by Afa/Dr family adhesins.58 AIEC is a key microbial factor in the pathogenesis of Crohn’s disease (CD). AIEC is capable of invading intestinal epithelial cells, particularly in the ileum, and surviving and replicating within macrophages. ExPECs can colonize the intestines of humans or animals for long periods of time without causing gastrointestinal symptoms. However, once they breach the intestinal barrier and disseminate to extraintestinal sites, they may give rise to a wide range of severe and potentially life-threatening infections. ExPEC infections predominantly affect neonates, women, the elderly, and immunocompromised populations and are most often transmitted through fecal-oral, domestic, sexual, and foodborne routes. Among the globally disseminated ExPEC lineages, ST131 is the most prevalent lineage across different geographic regions.61 UPEC represents the leading causative agent of both uncomplicated and complicated UTIs. UPEC possesses a high degree of adaptability, enabling coordinated regulation of multiple virulence factors to evade host immune defenses and establish persistent colonization, thereby contributing to recurrent and difficult-to-treat infections.3 NMEC often leads to meningitis in preterm infants and full-term neonates, with high morbidity, mortality, and disability. Survivors frequently suffer from permanent neurological impairments, posing a substantial burden on affected families and public health systems.62 SEPEC is the most frequent E. coli in bacterial sepsis and is capable of disseminating via the bloodstream to invade multiple internal organs. Although the pathogenic mechanisms of SEPEC have not yet been fully elucidated, its invasive capacity, environmental adaptability, and immune evasion strategies are widely considered to play pivotal roles in the development and progression of disease.63,64 APEC constitutes an important heterogeneous subgroup within ExPECs and is primarily associated with respiratory and systemic infections in poultry, causing avian E. coli disease.65 Accumulating evidence indicates that APEC shares substantial phylogenetic and virulence similarities with human ExPECs, supporting its classification as a potential foodborne zoonotic pathogen.41 Therefore, APEC is recognized as a reservoir of virulence and AMR genes relevant to human ExPEC infections. MPEC is the most usual pathogen causing mastitis in dairy cows and has an affinity for mammary epithelial cells, often forming biofilms that cause persistent infection in the mammary gland.66,67 Porcine ExPEC often colonizes the genitourinary tract of pigs and can cause uremia, pneumonia, and arthritis.68 Studies69 have found that porcine ExPEC is genetically correlated with and potentially transmissible to ExPEC of human origin, suggesting that pigs may serve as an important potential source of ExPEC infections in humans. Classification According to Gene Structure

Based on differences in gene substructure, E. coli can be classified into multiple phylogenetic groups, primarily including A, B1, B2, C, D, E, F, and several less prevalent lineages. In general, non-pathogenic E. coli strains are predominantly assigned to groups A and B1, with group A strains mainly associated with human hosts, whereas group B1 strains are widely distributed among poultry and wildlife. In contrast, strains belonging to group B2 typically harbor a broader repertoire of virulence-associated genes and exhibit the highest pathogenic potential, followed by those in group D; these two groups are the most frequently implicated in ExPECs. In addition, group E is commonly considered to be closely associated with EHEC.2,62,69,72,73

Mechanisms of Drug Resistance in E. coli

Current Status of Drug Resistance in E. coli

AMR is one of the most serious public health crises of the 21st century, and with the rapid progression of AMR, an estimated 10 million deaths will be attributed to AMR globally by 2050. A study74 on the global burden of AMR reports that in 2019, E. coli was the leading pathogen causing death from AMR infection. The 2024 surveillance data from the China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CARSS) revealed that the resistance rates of E. coli to ampicillin, cefazolin, ciprofloxacin, cefuroxime, and co-trimoxazole were all greater than 50%, highlighting the severe resistance of E. coli to β-lactam antibiotics, FQs, and SAs in China.75 In October 2025, “The Global Antibiotic Resistance Surveillance Report” published by the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) indicated that the global resistance rate of E. coli to third-generation cephalosporins reached 44.8%.76 It also highlighted the severe resistance landscape across different infection type-antibiotic combinations. For instance, E. coli causing UTIs exhibited a resistance rate of 53.2% to co-trimoxazole. Resistance to colistin, a last-resort therapy for the treatment of Gram-negative MDRO infections, varies in different regions. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MICs) greater than 4 mg/L was detected in mcr-1 colistin-resistant E. coli (CORE) in Egypt, and in Turkey, the MIC of mcr-1 CORE was detected to be greater than 8 mg/L, and in Cambodia, the MIC of different mcr CORE was located between 4 μg/mL and 16 μg/mL.77,78 Tigecycline is used to treat carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli infections, and Asia has a higher detection rate of tet(X4)-positive E.coli than Europe and Africa during 2016–2021. Clinical isolates of E. coli originating from China have an MIC of greater than 0.5 mg/L for tigecycline, suggesting an increasing tigecycline resistance situation in China.79 Although the return of older antibiotics to the clinic and antibiotic combinations can be effective in addressing AMR, acquired mutations in bacteria and the selective proliferation of mutants remain a threat, and treatment failure with aztreonam in combination with ceftazidime-avibactam or cefiderocol alone for NDM-5-producing E. coli infections has been reported in a previous study.80 As the infection rate of E. coli increases year by year, the problems of MDR and pandrugresistant (PDR) are becoming more and more prominent, which seriously plague the sustained and healthy development of medicine, agriculture and animal husbandry. The root cause of the occurrence of AMR is the presence of AMR genes, and E. coli is both a donor and a recipient of resistance genes; therefore, AMR in E. coli is considered one of the global threats to humans and animals.81

Intrinsic Resistance

Intrinsic resistance refers to the natural resistance of bacteria to certain antibiotics, mediated by their unique structural or functional characteristics and exhibiting stability.82,83 LPS located in the asymmetric OM of Gram-negative bacteria mediates intrinsic resistance to different classes of antibiotics. The intrinsic resistance of E. coli to vancomycin arises from differences in the permeability of the OM to glycopeptide antibiotics. The resistance of E. coli to macrolide antibiotics (MAs) is believed to be associated with the Tol-Pal inner membrane (IM) system, low OM permeability, the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump system, and proteolytic systems regulating peptidoglycan metabolism.84 Inactivating specific proteins or employing synergistic actions between cationic bacteriocins, peptidomimetics, and corresponding antibiotics to disrupt cell membrane integrity has emerged as an effective strategy against intrinsic resistance.85,86

Acquired Resistance

The acquired resistance mechanisms of E. coli primarily encompass the following: acquisition of drug resistance genes, alteration of drug targets, production of target protection proteins, enzymatic modification and inactivation of antibiotics, changes in cell membrane permeability, and upregulation of active efflux pump systems.

Acquisition of Drug Resistance Genes

E. coli can frequently exchange genes between different species or genera through HGT, driving the transfer of AMR genes.87 Plasmids, bacteriophages, insertion sequences, transposons, integrons and other MGEs can move between DNA molecules and participate in HGT. Conjugation, transduction, and transformation play crucial roles in the process of HGT.88

Plasmids are important MGEs for HGT of AMR genes, driving the emergence and spread of AMR.89 The resistance genes carried by plasmids can be transferred to other bacteria via HGT or vertically transmitted to offspring bacteria.82 Research87 has found gene exchange between E. coli plasmids isolated from food sources and those isolated from clinical settings, further demonstrating the significant role of HGT in the spread of AMR. Researchers have identified multiple resistance gene-carrying plasmids in E. coli. The clinical isolate E. coli RJ749, which exhibits high resistance to FQs, harbors a novel multi-resistance plasmid coexisting with three PMQR genes.90 Plasmids IncX4 and IncHI2 carry the colistin resistance gene mcr-1.91 blaNDM-5 tends to be transmitted via plasmid-mediated HGT, facilitating the rapid dissemination of carbapenem resistance genes in E. coli. Specifically, both plasmids IncX3 and IncFIB can carry blaNDM-5.92,93 The β-lactamase genes blaCTX and blaOXA coexist in plasmid IncF, mediating resistance to β-lactam antibiotics.94 Plasmid IncQ1 carrying the tigecycline resistance gene tet(X4) is remarkably transferable, enabling E. coli to evade the effects of all tetracyclines.33 At present, four plasmid-mediated SAs resistance genes have been identified: sul1, sul2, sul3, and sul4. The first three are predominantly present in E. coli, encoding dihydropteroate synthases insensitive to SAs. Notably, sul1 is also recognized as a marker for class Ι integrons.32,95 It is noteworthy that while conferring resistance to bacteria, resistance plasmids also induce compensatory mutations in bacteria to maintain the persistence and stability of the plasmid.

IS26 cannot move independently and is predominantly found in resistance plasmids of Gram-negative bacteria, playing a crucial role in the HGT. In the novel multi-resistant plasmid conferring resistance to FQs in E. coli mentioned above, the IS26 insertion was identified flanking the qnrS2 resistance gene.90 IS26-mediated amplification of the blaCTX‑M‑65 gene on plasmids enhances cephalosporin resistance in E. coli by increasing gene copy number and expression.96 When IS26 is present, plasmids carrying the blaNDM gene in E. coli undergo moderate fusion, reflecting the plasmid’s ongoing evolutionary capacity and the dynamic process of plasmid fusion.97 IS1294 not only participates in plasmid internal rearrangements but also mediates the fusion of plasmids with different replicon types in E. coli, thereby promoting the prevalence of multidrug-resistant plasmids.89

Phage-mediated HGT is one of the primary pathways for bacteria to acquire AMR and a key mechanism for the widespread dissemination of resistance genes.82 Metagenomic sequencing reveals that phage genomes harbor various resistance genes, with both virulent and temperate phages capable of HGT. Phage transduction plays a crucial role in this process.98 Enterobacterial phage P1 is a temperate phage capable of high-frequency transduction across different genera. It exhibits a high prevalence in E. coli and is associated with AMR in the bacterium.99 In 2014, researchers reported a P1-like phage RCS47 carrying blaSHV-2 from group B1, which conferred resistance to β-lactams in E. coli.100 Phage-plasmids (P-Ps) combine the characteristics of both phages and plasmids, carrying numerous resistance genes located within class Ι integrons. These can confer resistance to β-lactams, carbapenem antibiotics, AGAs, FQs, and colistin.101 P1-like P-Ps are commonly found in E. coli and possess multiple highly variable AMR gene insertion sites within their sequences.98,102 In 2022, researchers isolated E. coli P1-like P-Ps JL22 carrying blaCTX-M-55 from a duck farm in China, revealing yet another pathway for the dissemination of ESBL genes.102

E. coli utilizes two types of transpeptidases to catalyze the formation of amide bonds in cell wall peptidoglycan molecules: D-D transpeptidases/Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which are the primary targets of β-lactams, and L-D transpeptidases, which are largely insensitive to most β-lactams but remain susceptible to carbapenems.103 β-lactams exert their antibacterial effects by inhibiting peptidoglycan synthesis and triggering bacterial autolysin activity to disrupt peptidoglycan. PBPs are their primary targets. Studies104 have shown that insertion of a four-amino acid sequence (YRVP, YRIN, or YRIK) after residue 333 of the E. coli PBP3 protein confers high-level resistance to β-lactams, including aztreonam and cephalosporins. Guanosine penta- and tetraphosphate [(p)ppGpp] is a key intracellular signaling molecule and has been implicated in AMR. YcbB encodes a functional L-D transpeptidase. Recent studies103,105 have demonstrated that elevated levels of the alarmone ppGpp, together with YcbB overexpression, enhance β-lactam resistance in E. coli, including resistance to ampicillin. This resistance is mediated by a ppGpp-dependent reprogramming of cell wall synthesis, whereby L-D transpeptidase activity functionally replaces PBP-mediated D-D transpeptidation. A proteomics study106 of ampicillin-resistant E. coli revealed that, under ampicillin stress, the abundances of aldehyde-alcohol dehydrogenase (AdhE) and 3-oxoacyl-acyl carrier protein reductase (FabG) were significantly altered and both were identified as ampicillin-binding proteins, providing clues from the perspective of energy metabolism for understanding ampicillin resistance mechanisms.

AGAs are potent antibiotics widely used for the treatment of E. coli infections, targeting the 16S rRNA of the 30S ribosomal subunit. Methylation of 16S rRNA represents an important mechanism of AGAs resistance and can be mediated by both plasmid-encoded methyltransferases (eg, ArmA, RmtA-RmtH, and NpmA) and endogenous enzymes in E. coli, including RsmE, RsmF, RsmH, and RsmI.107 Studies108 have shown that rmtB and rmtC are closely associated with resistance to amikacin and kanamycin. Notably, elevated expression of rsmI has been proposed as a potential biomarker for AGAs resistance, particularly in response to kanamycin exposure.107 The translation elongation factor EF-G, encoded by fusA, is a key mediator of ribosomal translocation and is not involved in decoding, nor is it considered a classical target of AGAs. Recent studies109 have shown that fusA mutations give rise to altered EF-G variants that, in the presence of AGAs, selectively silence antibiotic-bound ribosomes, thereby alleviating AGAs-induced reductions in translation rates and the accumulation of aberrant proteins that lead to cellular toxicity. These findings reveal a non-canonical form of target alteration-mediated acquired resistance and highlight the remarkable plasticity of bacterial AMR evolution.

Globally, FQs are commonly prescribed for UTIs and intestinal infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria. Bacterial type II topoisomerases, represented by DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, are the primary targets of FQs. DNA gyrase, encoded by gyrA and gyrB, is the principal drug target in Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, whereas topoisomerase IV, encoded by parC and parE, serves as the main target in Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus.110,111 Mutations in these target genes are the major cause of FQs resistance. These mutations are predominantly clustered within a specific region known as the QRDR, with mutations in gyrA and parC occurring more frequently than those in gyrB and parE.112 Studies113,114 have shown that even a single minor mutation in gyrA can lead to widespread FQs resistance in E. coli. Point mutations within the QRDR of gyrA, including S83L, D87N, and K154R, confer resistance to ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin in E. coli. In a study113 investigating FQs-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and E. coli, the highest detection frequency was observed for the S80I mutation in the QRDR of parC, while additional point mutations such as V141A and E84A were also identified.

Colistin exerts its antibacterial activity by interacting with the lipid A moiety of LPS in the OM of Gram-negative bacteria. Alterations in the LPS structure can therefore lead to colistin resistance. In Gram-negative bacteria, colistin resistance is mainly attributed to specific modifications of lipid A, including the addition of phosphoethanolamine (PEtN) and 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L-Ara4N), catalyzed by lipid A phosphoethanolamine transferase (EptA) and 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose transferase (ArnT), respectively. The expression of these two enzymes is regulated by the two-component regulatory systems PhoPQ and PmrAB.115 Studies116 have demonstrated that the R81H substitution in pmrA is an independent determinant of the colistin-resistant phenotype in mcr-negative E. coli. The study117 further reported that fragment deletions in mgrB and phoP, as well as multiple point mutations in pmrB (including G206R, Y222H, M1I, L14P, P178S, and T235N), were associated with colistin resistance in mcr-negative E. coli. The EptB enzyme can also catalyze the addition of PEtN to LPS by modifying the Kdo residues of the LPS core region. In mcr-1-positive E. coli, the expression levels of phoP, phoQ, and eptB are elevated, suggesting that these genes may serve as potential molecular indicators for monitoring colistin resistance in E. coli.115,118 In addition to the canonical PhoPQ and PmrAB systems, recent studies119 have revealed that QseBC can modulate colistin resistance-associated pathways via cross-talk with PmrAB, further expanding the regulatory network governing lipid A modification in Gram-negative bacteria.

Production of Target Protection Proteins

Based on their modes of action, target protection proteins can be broadly categorized into three types. The first type functions by sterically occluding binding sites, thereby preventing drug-target interactions. The second type acts by inducing conformational changes in the target that reduce antibiotic binding affinity. The third type remodels the target in a way that allows it to remain functional despite the presence of bound antibiotic. This mechanistic diversity highlights target protection proteins as a versatile and distinct resistance strategy.82,120

PMQR represents an important mechanism of resistance to FQs. Among PMQR determinants, qnr genes encode Qnr proteins, which act as target protection proteins that protect DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV from inhibition by FQs, thereby conferring low-level resistance in E. coli. To date, seven qnr genes have been identified, including qnrA, qnrB, qnrC, qnrD, qnrE, qnrS and qnrVC.121 A study122 on FQs-resistant E. coli found that qnrB1-positive E. coli exhibited a significant upregulation of qnrB1 expression under FQs selective pressure, accompanied by an increase in plasmid copy number, which was associated with the development of FQs resistance. Tetracyclines inhibit protein synthesis by binding to the A-site region of the 30S ribosomal subunit, thereby preventing the binding of aminoacyl-tRNA. Tetracycline ribosome protection proteins (TRPPs) interact with the ribosome and induce conformational changes that reduce tetracycline binding, thus protecting the ribosome from the inhibitory effects of tetracyclines.82 tet(M) is the predominant TRPPs gene in E. coli, and the Tet(M) protein confers resistance to doxycycline and minocycline.123,124 Although tet(Q) has not been detected in naturally isolated E. coli strains, expression of an exogenous tet(Q) gene under experimental conditions can confer high-level resistance to tetracyclines in E. coli.124

Enzymatic Modification and Inactivation of Antibiotics

Bacteria can produce modifying enzymes and inactivating enzymes that degrade antibiotics or render them inactive. Inactivating enzymes disrupt the intrinsic structure of antibiotics, whereas modifying enzymes inactivate antibiotics through chemical modifications such as acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, nucleotidylation, and hydroxylation, ultimately leading to AMR.

β-Lactamases are the most common antibiotic-inactivating enzymes in E. coli, functioning primarily by hydrolyzing the β-lactam ring to inactivate the antibiotic and by binding to β-lactams to prevent their interaction with the target.82 The tet(X) gene encodes a tetracycline-inactivating monooxygenase, which hydroxylates tetracyclines and thereby reduces their antibacterial activity.123 This gene has been detected in multiple E. coli strains and mediates resistance to tetracyclines. Studies125 have also shown that tet(X) can be harbored on broad-host-range IncW plasmids in E. coli, indicating its potential for HGT. Based on the type of chemical modification, AMEs can be classified into three categories: AG N-acetyltransferases (AACs), AG O-nucleotidyltransferases (ANTs), and AG O-phosphotransferases (APHs). In E. coli, the most prevalent AME-encoding gene is aac(3)-II, followed by aac(6’)-I. Furthermore, aac(6’)-I and aph(3’)-IV confer resistance to AGAs such as gentamicin.126,127

FOS inhibits bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis by targeting UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvyl transferase (MurA), a key enzyme involved in the early stage of peptidoglycan biosynthesis. Fos enzymes are bacterial resistance determinants that inactivate FOS through chemical modification and are mainly classified into three groups: FosA (FosA1-FosA11), FosC2, and FosL (FosL1-FosL2). FosA proteins belong to the glutathione S-transferase family and inactivate FOS by catalyzing a glutathione-dependent opening of the epoxide ring. FosC2 is an ATP-dependent phosphotransferase that confers resistance through phosphorylation of FOS, whereas FosL1 is a recently identified glutathione S-transferase-like protein.35,128,129 Studies130 have shown that FosA3 is the most prevalent FOS-modifying enzyme in E. coli, and its encoding gene fosA3 frequently coexists with other AMR genes, such as blaCTX-M and mcr-1, on the same plasmid, indicating a high potential for co-selection and dissemination. In addition, fosL1 was first identified in E. coli and is located on IncX1 plasmids, representing a novel plasmid-mediated FOS resistance gene.128

Changes in Cell Membrane Permeability

The OM of Gram-negative bacteria is composed of phospholipids, LPS, lipoproteins, and outer membrane proteins (OMPs), serving as the first line of defense against adverse environmental conditions and being closely associated with AMR and virulence. Antibiotics enter Gram-negative bacteria mainly through two routes: diffusion through hydrophilic channels formed by OMPs and lipid-mediated penetration of the OM. Hydrophilic antibiotics, such as β-lactams, tetracyclines, and FQs, predominantly rely on the former pathway to gain access to the bacterial interior; therefore, alterations in OMPs can significantly affect bacterial susceptibility to hydrophilic antibiotics. In E. coli, several major OMPs have been identified, including OmpF, OmpC, OmpA, and PhoE. Among them, OmpF and OmpC form non-specific porin channels that allow the passage of small hydrophilic molecules. Compared with OmpC, OmpF possesses a larger pore size and higher permeability and is generally considered to facilitate antibiotic influx, whereas OmpC partially restricts this process. In Gram-negative bacteria, reduced expression of OmpF accompanied by increased expression of OmpC is commonly associated with decreased susceptibility to antibiotics.82

A study131 showed that under cefmetazole exposure, E. coli can reduce OM permeability by downregulating major OMPs, particularly OmpF and OmpC, thereby mediating AMR; alterations in PhoE expression have also been suggested to be involved in this process. In β-lactamase-negative E. coli, reduced expression or loss of OmpF has been demonstrated to be an important mechanism conferring resistance to cefalexin.132 In Enterobacterales, decreased OM permeability often acts synergistically with increased β-lactamase expression and represents a fundamental basis for the development of carbapenem resistance. Moreover, in CREC, regardless of the presence of carbapenemase genes, loss, truncation, or mutation in ompC or ompF can serve as important synergistic resistance mechanisms, further enhancing resistance to carbapenem antibiotics.133,134 The EnvZ-OmpR two-component system is a key regulator of OmpC and OmpF expression. A mutation in envZ (T402M) has been shown to alter EnvZ-OmpR signaling, resulting in marked downregulation or loss of OmpF accompanied by increased OmpC expression. These changes reduce OM permeability and contribute to resistance to β-lactams.135 Studies136,137 have shown that mutations in genes involved in LPS biosynthesis and modification pathways in E. coli, such as lpcA, rfaC, rfaD, rfaE, waaQ, and eptB, are associated with altered OM structure and barrier function, thereby contributing to decreased susceptibility to antibiotics including tigecycline and ciprofloxacin. These LPS-related mutations are thought to promote resistance primarily by modifying OM permeability and may act as synergistic mechanisms that influence porin function.

Upregulation of Active Efflux Pump Systems

Efflux pump systems are self-protective mechanisms that have evolved in bacteria under external selective pressures. These systems prevent the intracellular accumulation of toxic compounds and antibiotics and actively expel molecules that have already entered the cell and are therefore regarded as a rapid and efficient mechanism of AMR.82 The efflux transporter proteins in Gram-negative bacteria primarily belong to the following families: resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND), major facilitator superfamily (MFS), small multidrug resistance (SMR), multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE), ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, and proteobacterial antimicrobial compound efflux (PACE) (Figure 4).83,138 Figure 4Six families of efflux transporter proteins in Gram-negative bacteria. The major efflux pump systems identified in E. coli include the ABC family MacAB-TolC; MFS family, such as EmrAB-TolC, MdfA, MdtM, QepA, and Tet(A); RND family AcrAB-TolC and OqxAB; and SMR family EmrE.138 Among these systems, the RND family AcrAB-TolC multidrug efflux pump represents the most important efflux system in E. coli. This tripartite complex consists of the inner-membrane transporter AcrB, periplasmic adaptor protein AcrA, and the outer-membrane canal protein TolC (Figure 5A). In E. coli, expression of the AcrAB-TolC multidrug efflux pump is tightly controlled by a multilevel transcriptional regulatory network, in which the local repressor AcrR and the global transcriptional activators Rob, SoxS, and MarA act coordinately (Figure 5B).83 AcrR directly represses transcription of the acrAB operon; however, nonsense mutations in acrR result in marked overexpression of AcrA and AcrB, thereby conferring resistance to multiple antibiotics, including ampicillin and tigecycline.123,135 Studies139 have shown that antidepressants can act as non-antibiotic selection pressures and promote efflux-dependent antibiotic cross-resistance in E. coli, primarily via the AcrAB-TolC efflux pump. EmrAB-TolC is an efflux pump belonging to the MFS family and has been well characterized in E. coli. Previous studies140 have demonstrated that overexpression of EmrAB-TolC confers resistance to compounds such as nalidixic acid and thiolactomycin. Recent structural characterization of the EmrAB-TolC tripartite complex has further provided a molecular basis for its efflux activity. Plasmid-mediated Tet(A) variants, which belong to the MFS family of efflux pumps, have been demonstrated to confer tigecycline resistance in E. coli. Adaptive Resistance

Adaptive resistance represents a bacterial stress response triggered by environmental stressors. Factors such as pH, ion concentration, nutrient availability, and exposure to antibiotics can induce this process. Upon removal of the external inducing signals, this resistance is lost, rendering it rapid, transient, and reversible compared with other resistance mechanisms. Adaptive resistance may, to some extent, establish the prerequisite conditions for the subsequent development of stable, persistent resistance.82

A study141 comparing the antibacterial activity and resistance evolution of delafloxacin and ciprofloxacin against E. coli demonstrated that the patterns of resistance development differed under varying pH conditions. Specifically, ciprofloxacin-resistant E. coli exhibited a more rapid progression of resistance at pH 6.0 than at pH 7.3, whereas the opposite trend was observed for delafloxacin-resistant E. coli, with more pronounced resistance evolution occurring at pH 7.3. These findings highlight the role of environmental adaptation in AMR development and provide insight into the treatment of E. coli infections in acidic microenvironments. Alterations in bacterial basic metabolic pathways can contribute to the development of adaptive resistance. Studies106 have shown that under ampicillin-induced stress, E. coli undergoes a range of metabolic responses, including the downregulation of energy metabolism-related pathways and a reduction in cellular energy-generating capacity. These metabolic adaptations modulate the physiological state of bacterial cells and play an important role in the development of AMR.

Cryptic resistance can be considered a form of adaptive resistance and is primarily mediated by latent, previously unclassified AMR genes that become activated under antibiotic stress. Multiple cryptic AMR genes have been identified in E. coli. For example, the aminotransferase- and ligase-encoding genes alaA and ddlA can confer resistance to D-cycloserine; amplification of nadD, which is involved in NAD⁺ and NADPH biosynthesis and salvage pathways, is associated with polymyxin B tolerance; and genes related to peptidoglycan recycling, including rlpA, mepS, and mltC, can confer resistance to β-lactams.142 Repeated exposure to high concentrations of ciprofloxacin was shown to select E. coli mutants carrying a gyrB L422P substitution outside the QRDR, which does not increase the MIC but confers a FQ-specific hyperpersistence phenotype.143

Biofilms are highly organized microbial communities formed as an adaptive survival strategy under adverse environmental conditions and are closely associated with AMR and persistent infections. Biofilm development is generally described as a four-step process comprising bacterial attachment, microcolony formation, biofilm maturation, and biofilm dispersion. Biofilms can mediate AMR through multiple mechanisms, such as the barrier effect of the biofilm matrix that limits antibiotic penetration (Figure 6A), phenotypic alterations (Figure 6B), reduced metabolic activity and growth rates of biofilm-resident cells (Figure 6C), activation of stress response pathways (Figure 6D), HGT of AMR genes within the biofilm microenvironment (Figure 6E), and quorum sensing-mediated regulation of gene expression (Figure 6F).144 Glucose is an important carbon source for E. coli, and its metabolic activity profoundly influences bacterial physiology during antibiotic exposure. In E. coli biofilm models, antibiotics such as tetracycline exhibit pronounced spatial heterogeneity, with higher accumulation at the community periphery than in the interior. This phenomenon is closely associated with the spatial organization of glucose metabolism: due to the dense cellular architecture of the biofilm, glucose availability decreases from the periphery toward the interior, establishing a stable metabolic gradient accompanied by differences in membrane potential. This metabolism-driven modulation of membrane potential slows antibiotic accumulation within the biofilm center, providing a temporal window for the development of adaptive resistance in E. coli.145 In the study by Hassuna et al,146 biofilm-forming UPEC demonstrated significantly higher resistance to levofloxacin, nitrofurantoin, and meropenem compared with non-biofilm-forming UPEC. Strategies for Anti-Infective Therapy

E. coli can develop resistance to multiple classes of antibiotics, including β-lactams, AGAs, FQs, and tetracyclines. The resistance mechanisms employed by E. coli vary across different antibiotic classes, and this mechanistic heterogeneity contributes to the therapeutic challenges associated with E. coli infections. Moreover, the increasing diversity of resistance phenotypes in E. coli further complicates clinical antimicrobial selection and targeted antimicrobial therapy. As shown in Figure 7, substantial differences exist in antibiotic selection for infections caused by E. coli with different resistance profiles.27,147 Third-generation cephalosporins remain important therapeutic agents for Enterobacterales infections. However, with the global dissemination of third-generation cephalosporin-resistant E. coli (3GCR-EC) and ESBL-EC, and because ESBLs can hydrolyze third-generation cephalosporins, the clinical efficacy of these antibiotics has become increasingly limited. Consequently, carbapenems are widely used for the treatment of infections caused by these resistant strains, particularly for severe systemic infections, including bloodstream infections, intraperitoneal infections, and urinary tract infections. The widespread use of carbapenems has contributed to the increasing prevalence of CREC, posing major challenges for clinical management. Compared with infections caused by 3GCR-EC and ESBL-EC, therapeutic options for CREC infections are considerably more limited, and severe infections often require combination therapy. Carbapenem resistance in E. coli is commonly associated with carbapenemase production, porin loss, and efflux pump overexpression, all of which substantially compromise carbapenem efficacy and further restrict available treatment options. Currently available agents for CREC treatment include FQs, AGAs, fosfomycin, polymyxins, tigecycline, and β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. Notably, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations are also commonly used for infections caused by 3GCR-EC and ESBL-EC, whereas AGAs are recommended as alternative therapeutic agents for complicated urinary tract infections caused by 3GCR-EC and CREC. In addition, multidrug-resistant E. coli strains commonly display co-resistance to multiple antimicrobial classes, particularly FQs and β-lactams, thereby further limiting options for empiric antimicrobial therapy. These phenotype-specific therapeutic differences reflect the close relationship between drug resistance mechanisms and clinical treatment strategies. Therefore, clinical decision-making should comprehensively consider factors including infection site, disease severity, and resistance profile in order to optimize antimicrobial therapy. Although some conventional antibiotics have been reevaluated and reintroduced into clinical practice, the escalating challenge of AMR in Gram-negative bacteria underscores the urgent need for novel anti-infective strategies. Recent studies have reported several antibacterial molecules with novel targets or mechanisms of action. For instance, lolamycin has been developed as a narrow-spectrum antibiotic that selectively targets the lipoprotein transport system of Gram-negative bacteria.148 In addition, antimicrobial peptides such as Api137 and Api88 exert antibacterial effects by multimodally binding to the bacterial ribosome and disrupting protein translation,149 while the newly identified lariocidin binds to the small ribosomal subunit and disrupts protein synthesis.150 Collectively, these studies highlight promising directions for the development of innovative antibiotics targeting Gram-negative pathogens.

In addition to the development of novel antibacterial molecules, alternative therapeutic strategies for multidrug-resistant E. coli infections are also being actively developed. Phage therapy has emerged as a promising antibacterial approach owing to its high host specificity and potent antibacterial activity. A previous study151 demonstrated that bacteriophage vB_Eco_ZCEC08 exhibited strong lytic activity against multidrug-resistant UPEC isolates. Furthermore, combination therapy with vB_Eco_ZCEC08 and gentamicin significantly inhibited bacterial regrowth both in vitro (in culture medium and human urine) and in vivo (in a rat urinary tract infection model), suggesting that phage-antibiotic combination therapy may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for UPEC infections.152 Moreover, engineered phages incorporating CRISPR-Cas systems, such as SNIPR001, can selectively and efficiently eliminate pathogenic E. coli strains. Importantly, these engineered phages substantially reduce intestinal E. coli burdens in mice while causing minimal disruption to the commensal gut microbiota, highlighting their potential as a precision therapeutic approach for pathogenic E. coli infections.153

Overall, despite the development of multiple alternative therapeutic strategies, the control of E. coli infections still faces substantial challenges, including limited treatment options, the rapid dissemination of high-risk clones, the heterogeneity of resistance mechanisms, and the continuous emergence of MDROs. Therefore, optimizing antimicrobial stewardship and advancing resistance phenotype-guided therapeutic approaches will be critical for improving clinical outcomes and mitigating the continued spread of AMR.

Conclusions and Future Directions

As an important MDRO, E. coli poses a persistent and serious threat to global public health. E. coli is not only a major pathogen responsible for human clinical infections but also a key constituent of microbial communities in animal hosts and environmental reservoirs. High-risk E. coli clones that have acquired enhanced virulence and AMR through adaptive evolution can disseminate widely across different hosts and ecological niches and rapidly expand under antibiotic selective pressure. Some lineages even exhibit the potential to cause regional or global outbreaks, leading to a marked increase in difficult-to-treat infections and further exacerbating the challenges of the post-antibiotic era. From a One Health perspective, the rapid dissemination of multidrug-resistant E. coli across humans, animals, food systems, and environmental reservoirs underscores the importance of comprehensively understanding its molecular epidemiology, resistance mechanisms, and therapeutic challenges.

In this review, we systematically summarize the molecular epidemiological characteristics of E. coli from the perspectives of biological features, classification and typing, and epidemiological patterns. The diverse and complex resistance mechanisms of E. coli are comprehensively discussed and categorized into intrinsic, acquired, and adaptive resistance. In addition, current therapeutic strategies for E. coli infections are reviewed in light of recent clinical practice and research advances. Together, these discussions provide a theoretical basis for a deeper understanding of E.coli resistance characteristics, pathogenic mechanisms, transmission dynamics, and control strategies from a One Health perspective.

Given the strong evolutionary plasticity of E. coli and the widespread dissemination of AMR mediated by HGT across different hosts and environmental interfaces, interventions confined to a single sector are insufficient to curb the spread of AMR. Guided by the One Health concept, integrated human healthcare, animal production systems, environmental monitoring, and continuous cross-sectoral surveillance of molecular epidemiology and resistance mechanisms have become critical for addressing this challenge. In parallel, strengthening clinical antimicrobial stewardship, improving infection prevention and control strategies, and reducing non-essential antibiotic use in agriculture and animal husbandry are all critical measures for limiting the emergence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant E. coli strains. In this context, the development and translational application of novel antibiotics targeting Gram-negative bacteria, as well as alternative therapeutic strategies, will represent important avenues for mitigating the threat posed by E. coli.

Looking forward, future studies integrating molecular epidemiology, whole-genome sequencing, and comparative genomics will be critical for elucidating the evolutionary dynamics and cross-sector transmission pathways of multidrug-resistant E. coli clones. The establishment of high-resolution genomic surveillance and source-tracking networks across human, animal, and environmental reservoirs will further facilitate the real-time monitoring of high-risk E. coli lineages and AMR dissemination, thereby providing essential technical support for precision prevention and control strategies within the One Health framework.
