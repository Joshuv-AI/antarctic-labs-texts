Crop establishment-irrigation interactions driven rice (Oryza sativa L.) performance and water productivity
Authors: Moneesa Bashir, Mohammad Anwar Bhat, Tauseef Ahmad Bhat, Eajaz Ahmad Dar, Tahir Ahmad Sheikh, Aijaz Nazir, Umer Yousuf Parray, Arun Karnwal, Ahmed Almoayyad, Abdel Razzaq Al-Tawaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcessive water use in flooded irrigation systems poses a significant challenge in rice cultivation. Therefore, it is essential to adopt irrigation management techniques that conserve water, enhance water productivity, and address water scarcity. This experiment aimed to evaluate the yield and water productivity of various crop establishment methods combined with different irrigation management techniques. The experiment was designed using a split-plot layout with three replications. The main plots comprised three crop establishment techniques: the System of Rice Intensification (SRI), Direct Seeding (DSR), and Transplanting (TPR). The five irrigation regimes were randomized to the subplots implemented, including: continuous submergence (flooding) up to a depth of 3 cm (I1); irrigation application of 24 L m− 2 four days after the ponded water had disappeared during crop growth (I2); irrigation application of 24 L m− 2 four days after the ponded water disappeared until panicle initiation, followed by submergence until the dough stage (I3); irrigation application of 24 L m− 2 four days after the disappearance of ponded water until flowering, then submergence until the dough stage (I4); and saturation throughout until the dough stage (I5). The Shalimar Rice-4 variety was utilized for the study. The results indicated that the SRI method of crop establishment yielded the highest grain yields of 7.72 t ha^-1 and 7.93 t ha^-1, alongside straw yields of 9.64 t ha^-1 and 9.98 t ha^-1, in the years 2021 and 2022, respectively. Furthermore, the highest total crop water productivity was observed in the saturation treatment compared with continuous flooding. Consequently, the System of Rice Intensification with saturation emerged as the most efficient irrigation strategy, as it retained the highest total crop water productivity under the temperate conditions of Kashmir.Keywords: Crop establishment methods, Rice, System of rice intensification, Water management, Water productivity, YieldSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Food security is anticipated to further decline as the global population is projected to reach 9.7 billion by 2050 and 11 billion by 21001. This situation is exacerbated by factors such as land degradation, acute water scarcity, and ecological imbalance. Rice (Oryza sativa L.), a staple crop in Asia, holds significant economic and nutritional value. Among the 114 countries that cultivate rice, over 50 produce more than 100,000 tons annually2. Approximately 146.5 million hectares of agricultural land are allocated to rice cultivation globally3,4. Therefore, to meet the increasing food demands of a growing population, it will be imperative to augment rice production by 70–90% by 2050 relative to 2010 levels5,6. However, crops are facing challenges adapting to diminishing water resources7,8, making reductions in water availability a critical concern.

The predominant role of traditional practices within the farming community, particularly in the Kashmir Valley, is evident in the cultivation of rice, which has been passed down through generations. In traditional rice cultivation, a standing water level of 5–20 cm above the soil surface is maintained throughout the crop cycle through flooding techniques9,10. While these practices contribute to rice yield, they also result in substantial water consumption. Furthermore, inefficiencies in water use, coupled with significant water resource waste, lead to detrimental effects on the environment through leakage, runoff, and drainage11,12. A significant challenge in agriculture that requires urgent attention is maintaining high, consistent crop yields while minimizing water usage. Rice, a critical staple, is particularly noted for its substantial water requirements, with irrigation accounting for approximately 80% of total water consumption in Asian agriculture13,14. This situation underscores the need to implement a range of water conservation technologies to meet the increasing demand for rice production and sustenance amid limited water resources15–19. Consequently, agricultural researchers and technological experts have developed various water-saving irrigation technologies (WSI), including dry cultivation20, alternate wetting and drying irrigation (AWD)21,22, shallow wet irrigation, intermittent irrigation, and controlled irrigation23,24. These water conservation methods effectively reduce water consumption by minimizing evapotranspiration and other losses, thereby promoting efficient water usage. Among these methods, AWD is particularly prevalent. AWD involves maintaining conventional irrigation practices or retaining water at a specified depth for a designated period, followed by a drying phase and subsequent re-watering20. In comparison to traditional flooding methods, alternate wetting and drying (AWD) not only significantly reduces the volume of irrigation water utilized in paddy fields25–27, but also enhances the aesthetic qualities and overall quality of rice, as well as improving rice milling outcomes28,29. Moreover, AWD increases water-use efficiency20 and ultimately leads to higher rice yields30. While the majority of the cited studies have concentrated on rice cultivation, there remains a paucity of comprehensive research examining the effects of AWD on agricultural productivity and resource-use efficiency31–34. Another critical aspect of rice farming that requires reduced water input is dry cultivation. This method refers to the practice of sowing rice directly into dry soil, conserving water by predominantly relying on rainwater during the growing season, supplemented by artificial irrigation as necessary35. This technique has facilitated rice production in numerous regions characterized by water scarcity, thereby enhancing food security in those areas. However, a significant challenge with this approach is weed invasion, which can lead to crop failure and substantial yield losses. The System of Rice Intensification (SRI) has also attracted research attention for its potential to enhance rice production while improving nutrient uptake and utilization efficiency36,37. Key elements of SRI include transplanting widely spaced single seedlings, mechanical weeding with a revolving push weeder to aerate the soil, and using young seedlings less than 15 days old38. Studies have indicated that SRI can increase yields by 50 to 100%39. This study aims to facilitate an integrated evaluation of irrigation management across various rice establishment methods, thereby providing a comparative analysis of water-use efficiency and productivity within diverse cultivation systems. It is imperative to test the hypothesis that water conservation strategies can synergistically enhance crop productivity and water-use efficiency when implemented in conjunction with Alternate Wetting and Drying (AWD) treatment. Despite significant advancements in water-saving irrigation technologies and alternative establishment methods, a comprehensive comparative evaluation of their interactions within the specific climatic and edaphic conditions of the Kashmir Valley is conspicuously lacking in the existing literature. This study addresses this critical gap by systematically assessing the combined effects of three crop establishment techniques—System of Rice Intensification (SRI), Direct Seeded Rice (DSR), and Transplanting Rice (TPR)—in conjunction with five distinct irrigation regimes on rice yield, water productivity, and leaf water status over two consecutive growing seasons.

Materials and methods

Study site and experimental conditions

A field experiment was conducted at the Research Farm of the Agronomy Division, Faculty of Agriculture, Sher-e-Kashmir University of Agricultural Sciences and Technology of Kashmir (SKUAST-K), India. This study examines the 2021–2022 agricultural season. The experiment was carried out at an elevation of 1590 meters above sea level, with geographic coordinates of 34°21’ N and 74°23’ E.

Soil physicochemical properties

The soil had a clay loam-like consistency. Soil analysis results showed nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium at 322.5, 19.95, and 173.2 kg ha− 1, respectively. pH was neutral, and the amount of organic carbon was medium (0.67%).

Climatic conditions

Meteorological data pertinent to the experimental period were sourced from the SKUAST-K Agrometeorological Observatory, located near the experimental site, during the crop growth phases of 2021 and 2022. The climatic conditions during this period were characterized as generally favourable. Specifically, the total precipitation recorded was 476.3 mm and 420.8 mm in 2021 and 2022, respectively. The maximum temperatures reached were 35.5 °C and 32.6 °C, while the minimum temperatures recorded were 6.7 °C and 4.6 °C. Relative humidity exhibited maximum values of 89.6% and 92.9% and minimum values of 33.7% and 37.6% for the respective years. Additionally, the total sunshine hours were documented as 8.6 and 9.6 h for 2021 and 2022, respectively, throughout the crop growth period.

Experimental design and treatment details

The experimental design was split plot design encompassing three main plot treatments viz. System of Rice Intensification (SRI), Direct Seeded Rice (DSR) and Transplanted Rice (TPR) each of which were laid in 5 subplots viz. I1: Continuous submergence (flooding) up to 3 cm depth; I2: Application of irrigation (24 L m− 2) 4 days after disappearance of ponded water throughout crop growth; I3: Application of irrigation (24 L m− 2) 4 days after disappearance of ponded water up to panicle initiation and then submergence up to dough stage; I4: Application of irrigation (24 L m− 2) 4 days after disappearance of ponded water up to flowering and then submergence up to dough stage; I5: Saturation (1 cm). Thus, there were 15 treatment combinations, each replicated thrice, for a total of 45 plots. Establishment methods were randomized to main plots, and irrigation regimes to subplot treatments as per the proper randomization rule. Each plot was 6.0 m by 3.0 m. The rice variety used was Shalimar Rice-4 (SR-4). For nutrient management in rice, Nitrogen (N) was given in three splits: one at the time of sowing/transplanting, along with complete doses of phosphorus (P) and potassium (K), and 2nd split was given during maximum tillering, and 3rd split was given during Panicle Initiation. Weeds were managed by applying bensulsuron methyl + pretilachlor (60 g + 600 g a.i. ha-1) 4 days after sowing/transplanting under the trade name Erase at 10 kg ha− 1. Manual weeding was performed 15 days after herbicide application.

Soil moisture monitoring and water balance estimation

To ensure consistent irrigation, a surface drip irrigation system was installed between 5 and 8 days prior to sowing. A polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipeline was positioned adjacent to the experimental plots, featuring a plot inlet located centrally within each plot to facilitate outflow. To ensure that only one plot within each replication receives irrigation at any given time, a watertight butterfly valve was installed at each plot inlet. Irrigation water was administered for each treatment until the desired quantity was attained.

Soil water balance

Volumetric soil moisture was assessed to a depth of 100 cm using an access tube in conjunction with a Delta-T Devices PR2 soil moisture profile probe (Delta-T Devices, UK). This profile probe estimates soil moisture content based on its permittivity or refractive index. The operational principle of the probe involves generating a 100 MHz signal upon the application of power. An electromagnetic field is transmitted through the walls of the access tube via pairs of stainless-steel rings integrated into the probe. The permittivity of the soil, which is primarily influenced by the soil water content, produces a constant-voltage output—a simple yet sensitive indicator of soil moisture levels as measured by the HH2 meter. Soil moisture levels were tested at six distinct depths (0–10, 10–20, 20–30, 30–40, 40–60, and 60–100 cm) every three to six days. Additionally, a water meter installed on the submain line was utilized to calculate the irrigation requirements for each plot. Field capacity

Utilizing the field approach, this study defined field capacity. A small area (3 m × 3 m) was delineated by earthen dikes, weeds, and stubble. To achieve soil saturation, water was introduced into the dike region. A black polyethylene sheet was employed to mitigate evaporation within the designated area. Soil samples were systematically collected using an auger at consistent intervals across the wetted zone following the initial day of saturation, with depths categorized as 0 to 15 cm, 15 to 30 cm, 30 to 45 cm, 45 to 60 cm, and 60 to 75 cm. The resulting data were subsequently analysed to determine the field capacity.

Biometric crop observations

Readings were always taken from the net plots, leaving the border and penultimate rows on all sides of each plot. Crops were harvested as soon as they reached physiological maturity, or when the grain moisture content reached 20%. Following a 72-hour sun-drying period in the field, the harvested crop was tied into plot-wise bundles, and the weight of each plot was measured using a spring balance. The crop was threshed by pounding it against wooden logs, and the grain yield was calculated and expressed in t ha− 1.

Discussions

Grain yield, a critical economic metric for crops, serves as a comprehensive indicator of the cumulative effects of various crop growth factors and yield characteristics. The results indicated that the yield of rice cultivated using the SRI was significantly higher than that of rice produced through traditional transplanting or direct seeding methods. The enhanced grain yield associated with the SRI approach may be attributed to transplanting seedlings at an earlier developmental stage, which facilitates increased tillering and root growth. Additionally, the implementation of a square planting arrangement with wider spacing (25 cm x 25 cm) promotes greater capacity for both canopy and root development, as well as improved conditions for subsequent grain filling. Notably, in contrast to the actual yield of the SR-4 variety observed in the valley, its production of both grain and straw was reduced in the experimental setting. This decline in yield may be linked to the experiment’s reliance on deficit irrigation, which substantially diminished overall output across SRI, DSR, and TPR methods. Additionally, the soil properties of the trial location, which was not initially a rice-growing region, differed somewhat from those of the valley’s rice-growing areas. The yield curtailment was impacted by this as well. Under the SRI technique, the increase in grain output was attributed to larger root volumes, more vigorous tillers with longer panicles, and more well-filled spikelets with higher grain weight. Comparable rice yields have been reported by Mankotia et al. (2009)52 and Mahajan et al. (2012)53 using alternative crop establishment procedures other than transplanting. During the study, it was also demonstrated that, compared with other establishment methods, the harvest index and straw yield both improved substantially under SRI, due to improved growth and yield attributes that led to higher biomass. The results are consistent with the findings of Sowmya and Ramana’s (2012)54 research.

Grain yield and total biological yield (grain plus straw) determine the harvest index. Different planting methods significantly influenced the harvest index, with the System of Rice Intensification (SRI) method achieving the highest index. Similar findings were reported by Aijaz et al. (2020)55 and Nazir et al. (2022)56. The study revealed that treatment I1 produced higher yields across various irrigation management regimes than the other treatments. The comparable grain yields of I5 plants to those of I1 and I3 may be attributed to similar dry matter accumulation, panicle spikelet counts, and percentages of filled grains. In contrast, I2 plants yielded the least grain because they were irrigated with 24 L per square meter 4 days after the ponded water evaporated during crop growth. This yield was similar to that of I4, which received the same irrigation amount for 4 days after the ponded water receded, until flowering, followed by continuous submersion until the dough stage. These results align with the findings of Anning et al. (2018)57.

Plants from I2 and I4 yielded the lowest grain output, likely due to reduced soil moisture availability during the flowering season. Water stress at this stage hindered the partitioning of assimilates during grain filling, leading to a significant decrease in grain yield. Sarvestani et al. (2008)58 reported that water stress during flowering significantly reduced grain yield. This finding aligns with recent studies (Yang & Zhang, 201059; de Varies et al., 201060, which indicated that applying alternate wetting and drying (AWD) at any growth stage considerably lowers grain yield by decreasing soil moisture. Anning et al. (2018)57 noted that regularly submerging fields after implementing AWD up to the booting stage had no significant impact on rice grain yield. Conversely, Akram (2013)61 found that the grain yield experienced a more significant reduction when water stress was applied during the panicle initiation stage compared to the flowering stage. Variations in soil type, stress levels, and cultivars may account for these discrepancies in results.

SRI utilized the highest amount of water among all crop establishment methods, which led to greater water productivity compared to the others. Direct-seeded and transplanted rice produced fewer tillers than SRI, resulting in reduced canopy formation and lower transpiration rates. Plants with larger canopy structures tend to transpire more water than those with smaller canopies. In the I1 treatment, plants consumed the most water because the plots remained continuously submerged from transplanting to harvest. Permanent submersion accelerates seepage and percolation, thereby increasing overall water consumption (Abdul-Ganiyu et al., 2015)62.

Plants from the I2 treatment recorded the highest real and irrigation water productivity, as well as the highest percentage of water saved, despite having the lowest water use, whereas plants from the I5 treatment reported the best overall water productivity. Since water was not always retained above the soil surface, continuous submergence treatment uses more water and loses less water through evaporation, seepage, and percolation than AWD and saturation situations. This outcome is consistent with research by Abdul-Ganiyu et al.62, Chu et al. (2015)63, and Nandini et al. (2025)64, which found that extended submersion increased water consumption and reduced water productivity. Plants from I1 consumed the most water due to their higher rate of vegetative development (canopy formation) and the standing water layer present in the plots from transplanting to harvest.

Conclusion

The study highlighted that, within the temperate climatic conditions of Kashmir, the system of rice intensification demonstrated superior performance compared to both direct-seeded and transplanted rice, as evidenced by grain yields of 7.72 and 7.93 t ha⁻¹, respectively, and a total water productivity of 0.41 kg m⁻³. Nonetheless, in low-lying areas with uncontrolled irrigation due to a shallow water table, transplanting is the more advantageous option compared to the system of rice intensification. Among the various irrigation regimes examined, saturation was identified as the most effective method for enhancing total crop water productivity in rice. Consequently, it is recommended that the system of rice intensification, in conjunction with maintaining soil moisture near saturation, be adopted as an efficient and sustainable agricultural practice in temperate agroecological contexts.
