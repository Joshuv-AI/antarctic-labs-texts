Invariant Subspaces for Generalized Differentiation and Volterra Operators
Alexandru Aleman, Alex Bergman

Abstract
In this paper we provide a far-reaching generalization of the existent results about invariant subspaces of the differentiation operator D = ∂ ∂ t D=\frac{\partial}{\partial t} on C ∞ ​ ( 0 , 1 ) C^{\infty}(0,1) and the Volterra operator V ​ f ​ ( t ) = ∫ 0 t f ⁡ ( s ) ​ 𝑑 s Vf(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)ds , on L 2 ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L^{2}(0,1) . We use an abstract approach to study invariant subspaces of pairs D , V D,V with D ​ V = I DV=I , where V V is compact and quasi-nilpotent and D D is unbounded densely defined and closed on the same Hilbert space. Our results cover many differential operators, like Schrödinger operators and a large class of other canonical systems, as well as the so-called compact self-adjoint operators with removable spectrum recently studied in [ 6 ] . Our methods are based on a model for such pairs which involves de Branges spaces of entire functions and plays a crucial role in the development. However, a number of difficulties arise from the fact that our abstract operators do not necessarily identify with the usual operators on such spaces, but with rank one perturbations of those, which, in terms of invariant subspaces creates a number of challenging problems.



1
Introduction

The motivation for the study initiated in this paper goes back to the invariant subspaces of the Volterra operator
V
defined on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
by

Vf(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(s)ds,

as well as the invariant subspaces of its unbounded left inverse

Df(x)=f^{\prime}(x),

when restricted to
C^{\infty}(0,1)
.
In 1938 Gelfand
[
16
]
posed the problem of determining the invariant subspaces of
V
. This problem has a rich history and has been studied by many authors.
The, by now well known result, that each such subspace consists of functions which vanish a.e. on a fixed interval
(0,a)
, has been originally proved by Agmon,
[
1
]
and independently by Brodskii
[
12
]
and Donoghue
[
15
]
. Later, among others, Sarason
[
38
]
gave a different proof based on properties of the backward shift operator on the Hardy space on the upper half plane.

The study of invariant subspaces of
D
on
C^{\infty}(0,1)
has been initiated much later by the first author and B. Korenblum (
[
3
]
, 2008). The structure of these subspaces is much more complicated. They can be classified according to the spectrum of the restriction of
D
which can be either void, or a discrete set in
{\mathbb{C}}
, or the entire complex plane. The first category, also called
residual subspaces
is fully described in
[
3
]
and are of primary interest for this work, It turns out, that a residual subspace consists of functions in
C^{\infty}(0,1)
that vanish on a compact subinterval. This interval may reduce to a point in which case we have that the functions together with all their derivatives vanish at that point. This is clearly related to the invariant subspaces of right inverses of
D
, that is, Volterra operators of the type

V_{a}f(t)=\int_{a}^{t}f(s)ds,\quad a\in[0,1].

The case of differentiation invariant subspaces where the spectrum of the restriction is discrete is related to the appropriate version of
spectral synthesis
in this context. This subtle problem was investigated by the first author, A. Baranov and Y. Belov in

[
2
]
and completely solved subsequently by A. Baranov and Y. Belov in
[
5
]
. The structure of the remaining type of
D-
invariant subspaces is not understood.

The ideas related to invariant subspaces of the Volterra operator have been extended to other classes of integral operators. The closest to the subject of this paper is the direction suggested by C. Remling in his book
[
36
]
, namely to describe the invariant subspaces of Green’s operators associated to canonical systems, for example, Schrödinger operators. We shall provide a description of these objects in Section
5
. The reason for our particular interest is the interplay between invariant subspaces of these operators and the so-called residual subspaces of their left inverses which, in this case, are differential operators.

It is the aim of this paper to study such situations in a very general context. More precisely, we are interested in invariant subspaces for pairs of Hilbert space operators
D,V
satisfying
DV=I
together with certain abstract conditions which mimic the situations described above and cover a large class of examples of interest.
The formal definition is as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
H
be a separable Hilbert space. A pair of operators
D
and
V
will be called
admissible
if

(i)

D:\mathscr{D}(D)\subset H\to H
is a densely defined closed operator,

(ii)

D
has a one-dimensional kernel

(iii)

D
has a self-adjoint restriction with compact resolvent,

(iv)

V:H\to H
is compact and quasi-nilpotent, that is,
\sigma(V)=\left\{0\right\}
,

(v)

\text{Ran}(V)\subset\mathscr{D}(D)
and
DV=I
.

The assumption that
V
is compact is actually redundant, since it follows from the compactness of the resolvent of the self-adjoint operator in (iii).

In this setup the operator
V
plays the role of the Volterra operator and
D
of the derivative multiplied by appropriate constants to ensure (iii). Other typical examples of pairs of admissible operators are given by second order differential operators with one fixed boundary condition and their Green’s operators (solution operators). We give a large class of examples including Schrödinger operators and canonical systems of differential equations in Section
2
. Finally, these conditions cover also the class of self-adjoint operators with removable spectrum introduced in
[
6
]
. These are compact self-adjoint operators having a rank one perturbation which is quasi-nilpotent. We shall discuss in detail these examples in Section
2
.

The key tool in our investigation is a functional model which is obtained in a similar way to the approaches in spectral theory. More precisely, it turns out that
D
possesses a family
\{\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\}
of eigenvectors such that the function
\lambda\mapsto\phi_{\lambda}
is an entire
H-
valued function, so that the
generalized Fourier transform

{\mathcal{W}}
defined on
H
by

{\mathcal{W}}x(\lambda)=\langle x,\phi_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle,\quad x\in H

induces a unitary map onto a Hilbert space of entire functions. Our first main theorem identifies this space as a shifted de Branges space, that is,

{\mathcal{W}}H=e^{-i\alpha z}{\mathcal{H}}(E),

(1)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
E
is a regular Hermite-Biehler function and
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
is the corresponding de Branges space of entire functions (see Section
3
for details). Here and throughout by the usual abuse of notation we write
z
for both a point in
{\mathbb{C}}
as well as for the identity function on
{\mathbb{C}}
.
While in spectral theory of certain differential operators such models yield a ”diagonalization”, in our case the situation is more complicated;
{\mathcal{W}}D{\mathcal{W}}^{-1}
is just an extension of
M_{z}^{*}
, where
M_{z}
is multiplication by the independent variable and
{\mathcal{W}}V^{*}{\mathcal{W}}^{-1}
is a rank one perturbation of the backward shift
f\mapsto\frac{f-f(0)}{z}
.

De Branges spaces have attracted renewed attention is recent years and have been an instrumental tool in resolving many open problems in operator theory, complex analysis, harmonic analysis, and spectral theory, see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
11
,
19
,
23
,
25
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
31
,
33
,
34
]
for some examples of this.

The fact that the lattice of invariant subspaces of the Volterra operator is totally ordered by inclusion has been extended in many ways. Such operators are called
unicellular
. Kalisch
[
20
]
and Kisilevskiĭ
[
21
]
proved unicellularity of a large class of integral operators whose kernels do not vanish on the diagonal. Another interesting class consists of operators with positive imaginary part.
Kisilevskiĭ
[
21
]
proved that a cyclic irreducible operator
V
with positive and nuclear imaginary part is unicellular. A very good account for results of this type is the book of Gohberg and Krein
[
17
]
. We should point out that none of the conditions above apply to the operators
V
considered in this paper.

Clearly,
D
has many right inverses which are all compact since by assumption (ii) above they differ by a rank one operator. In Section
5
we shall show that among these, the quasi-nilpotent ones form a one-parameter family
V_{\beta},~|\beta+\alpha|\leq\tau(E)
. They generalize the Volterra-type operators

f\mapsto\int_{\beta}^{t}f(s)ds,\quad\beta\in[0,1],

which are unicellular if and only if
\beta=0
, or
\beta=1
.
It turns out that a similar result holds in the general context as well. This is due to the fact that the invariant subspaces of these operators can be described in terms of the model space. More precisely, their ”Fourier transforms” have the form

{\mathcal{W}}M=(e^{i\gamma z}{\mathcal{H}}(F))^{\perp},\quad\gamma\in\mathbb{R},

where the de Branges space
{\mathcal{H}}(F)
is a closed subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
. These subspaces always form a chain. Moreover for fixed
\beta
as above,
\gamma
must satisfy

|\gamma+\alpha|\leq\tau(E)-\tau(F),\quad|\beta-\gamma|\leq\tau(F).

(2)

This leads to a general unicellularity theorem. For an entire function
f
we denote by
\tau(f)
its exponential type.

Theorem 1
.

Let
D
and
V
be an admissible pair. Then
D
has at most two (and at least one) unicellular quasi-nilpotent right-inverses given by

V_{+}f=Vf+\langle f,\mathcal{W}^{-1}s_{+}\rangle\phi_{0},

and

V_{-}f=Vf+\langle f,\mathcal{W}^{-1}s_{-}\rangle\phi_{0},

where

s_{\pm}=\frac{1-e^{i\beta_{\pm}z}}{z}\in\mathcal{W}H,

and

\beta_{+}=\tau(E)-\alpha\text{, and }\beta_{-}=-(\tau(E)+\alpha).

These operators coincide if and only if
\tau(E)=\alpha=0
.

In particular, the original operator
V
is unicellular if and only if
|\alpha|=\tau(E)
.

These general results have a number of applications
which are presented in §
5.1
.
These include the result mentioned above regarding invariant subspaces of Green’s operators associated to canonical systems, as suggested by C. Remling in
[
36
]
.
It turns out, that for singular Hamiltonians these are again given by functions that vanish on a.e. on interval
(0,c)
. The non-singular case is more involved. Here we show that every such Green’s operator has a rank one perturbation which is a Green’s operator as well and is unicellular.

The unicellularity result applies to compact self-adjoint operators with removable spectrum as defined above. We show that under some natural assumptions, such operators possess a quasi-nilpotent rank one perturbation which is also
unicellular. This completes the recent work of Baranov and Belov
[
6
]
on the subject.

The more difficult problem within this circle of ideas is to describe the invariant subspaces of
D
. The first step is to restrict
D
to an appropriate space where it acts as a continuous operator. This is the natural analogue of
C^{\infty}
defined by

C^{\infty}(D)=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\mathscr{D}(D^{n})

which becomes a Fréchet space with respect to the translation invariant metric

d(f,g)=\sum_{j}2^{-j}\frac{\lVert D^{j}f-D^{j}g\rVert_{H}}{1+\lVert D^{j}f-D^{j}g\rVert_{H}}.

As in the classical case, we prove that the invariant subspaces of
D
split naturally into three categories according to the spectrum of the restriction of
D
. Theorem
10
shows that if
{\mathcal{J}}\subset C^{\infty}(D)
is closed and
D-
invariant then
\sigma(D|{\mathcal{J}})
is either void, or a discrete set of eigenvalues, or the whole complex plane.

In this paper we focus on closed
D-
invariant subspaces
{\mathcal{J}}\subset C^{\infty}(D)
with
\sigma(D|{\mathcal{J}})=\emptyset
. These subspaces are called
residual
. Our approach differs essentially from the approach in
[
3
]
, which built heavily on the self-adjoint operator of multiplication by independent variable,
Mf(x)=xf(x)

on
L^{2}(a,b)
. A possible analogue in the general context would be a
solution of the Heisenberg commutation relation

DM-MD=I.

Unfortunately, it turns out (see for example
[
39
,
40
]
or Chapter 12 of
[
10
]
)
that the admissible pairs for which the commutator equation has a self-adjoint solution are essentially unitarily equivalent to the classical one. Thus this technique is not available to us and instead our approach is based on the theory of nearly invariant subspaces of de Branges spaces discussed in Section
3
.

Intuitively, the description of residual
D-
invariant subspaces is related to finding the appropriate analogue of the space of
C^{\infty}-
functions vanishing on a nontrivial interval, or vanish at one point together with all their derivatives. The idea developed in this paper is to describe such subspaces using Volterra-invariance. Thus:

1)
C^{\infty}-
functions vanishing on an interval can be interpreted as

{\mathcal{J}}_{M}=\{x\in C^{\infty}(D):~D^{j}x\in M\},

where
M\subset H
is invariant for a quasi-nilpotent right inverse of
D
, In this case, by (
2
) there exists a compact interval
J\subset[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
, such that
M
is invariant for all operators
V_{\beta},\beta\in J
.

2)
C^{\infty}-
functions vanishing together with all their derivatives at a given point can be interpreted as

{\mathcal{J}}_{M}=\{x\in C^{\infty}(D):~D^{j}x\in M\},

where this time
M=V_{\beta}C^{\infty}(D)
for some fixed
\beta\in[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
.

Remarkably enough this leads to a complete description of residual subspaces in this quite general context.

Theorem 2
.

Every
D-
invariant residual subspace
{\mathcal{J}}
of
C^{\infty}(D)
is of the form
{\mathcal{J}}_{M}
. The subspace
{\mathcal{J}}
has the form 1) above if and only if
{\mathcal{J}}
is not dense in
H
. In this case, the subspace
M\subset H
is the closure of
{\mathcal{J}}
in
H
.

A very natural and basic question which arises is whether subspaces of the form 1) are intersections of subspaces of form 2). More precisely, if
{\mathcal{J}}_{M}
is of the form 1) with corresponding interval
J
, it is true that

{\mathcal{J}}_{M}=\bigcap_{\beta\in J}V_{\beta}C^{\infty}(D)\,?

The answer is negative in this generality, and the reason is that the representation
{\mathcal{J}}={\mathcal{J}}_{M}
is not unique despite the fact that the interval
J
in 1) is uniquely determined by
{\mathcal{J}}
.
This is a quite subtle phenomenon which we discuss is discussed at the end of §
6.3
.
However, in Theorem
13
we prove that given an interval
J\subset[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
, with nonvoid interior,

{\mathcal{J}}_{0}=\bigcap_{\beta\in J}V_{\beta}C^{\infty}(D),

is the largest residual subspace corresponding to this interval. In the standard case this is also the unique such subspace and it turns out that this continues to hold for other cases as well, more precisely, when the chain of de Branges subspaces of
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
is
thin
in the sense of Belov and Borichev
[
7
]
.

The opposite situation occurs when the Hermite-Biehler function
E
in our model has exponential type zero. In this case all intervals above reduce to
\{0\}
and in Corollary
8

we prove that the set of residual subspaces is totally ordered.
For example (see Corollary
9
), this implies that for any regular Schrödinger operator
D
on
[a,b]
with a separated boundary condition at
a
, a residual subspace consists of functions that vanish on
[a,c],~a<c<b
, or when
c=a
, it has the form described in 2) with
\beta=a
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains a list of examples of admissible pairs which will be used to illustrate the general results. Section
3

contains preliminary material regarding de Branges spaces of entire functions and their connection to canonical systems. Section
4
regards the model associated to an admissible pair. Unicellularity of generalized Volterra operators and their invariant subspaces are considered in Section
5
. Section

6
regards the invariant subspaces of
D
, especially the structure of residual subspaces.

2
Examples

We begin by providing a large class of examples of admissible pairs
D
and
V
.

2.1
The classical case

Let
D=-i\frac{d}{dx}
be defined on the absolutely continuous functions on
(0,1)
,

\mathscr{D}(D)=\left\{f\in AC(0,1):f,Df\in L^{2}(0,1)\right\}.

It is well known that
D
has simple eigenvalues at each point in the plane with eigenfunction
e^{i\lambda x}
and self-adjoint restrictions with compact resolvent. Also it is clear that the classical Volterra operator

Vf(x)=i\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt,

is a quasi-nilpotent right inverse of
D
.

2.2
Regular Schrödinger operators

Let
-\infty<a<b<\infty
and consider the Schrödinger operator
D=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+q
, where
q\in L^{1}(a,b)
is real-valued. For the facts to come we refer the reader to any one of the references
[
30
,
42
,
41
]
. The papers
[
35
,
24
,
26
]
also treat Schrödinger equations with a similar flavor to our work. For
f\in L^{2}(a,b)
consider the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}(D-\lambda)u=f\\
u(a)=0\\
\dot{u}(a)=0\end{cases}.

(3)

By a solution to (
3
) we shall mean a function
u
belonging to the maximal domain of
D

\mathscr{D}(D)=\left\{u\in\text{AC}(a,b):\dot{u}\in\text{AC}(a,b)\text{ and }u,-\ddot{u}+qu\in L^{2}(a,b)\right\},

satisfying
(D-\lambda)u=f
pointwise a.e. on
(a,b)
and
u(a)=\dot{u}(a)=0
. It is well known that the Cauchy problem has a unique solution
u_{\lambda}\in\mathscr{D}(D)
for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
f\in L^{2}(a,b)
and
\lVert u_{\lambda}\rVert_{2}\leq C_{\lambda}\lVert f\rVert_{2}
, where
C_{\lambda}>0
is a constant not depending on
f
. Also the map
\lambda\mapsto u_{\lambda}(t)
is entire for each fixed
t
. In particular the map
V_{\lambda}f=u_{\lambda}
defines a bounded linear operator, such that
(D-\lambda)V_{\lambda}f=f
for all
f\in L^{2}(a,b)
. We let
V=V_{0}
. The operator
V_{\lambda}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator. Indeed if
v_{\lambda}
and
u_{\lambda}
are any two linear independent solutions and
W(u_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})
is their Wronskian, then

V_{\lambda}f(x)=\frac{1}{W(u_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})}\int_{a}^{x}\left(u_{\lambda}(x)v_{\lambda}(t)-u_{\lambda}(t)v_{\lambda}(x)\right)f(t)dt.

(4)

Note that the integral kernel vanishes on the diagonal. It is not difficult to see that
V
is quasi-nilpotent. By separated boundary condition
(\alpha)
,
0\leq\alpha<\pi
, at
a
we mean the functions in the maximal domain
\mathscr{D}(D)
satisfying

u(a)\cos(\alpha)-\dot{u}(a)\sin(\alpha)=0.

We define separated boundary conditions at
b
analogously. Selecting any separated boundary condition at
a
gives an admissible pair
D
,
V
.

2.3
Canonical systems

The following class of examples provides a far-reaching generalization of the regular Schrödinger operators from above.

Fix
0<\ell<\infty
. Let
{\bf H}:(0,\ell)\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be a positive,
{\bf H}\geq 0
, real-matrix valued function on
(0,\ell)
. To simplify the exposition we assume that
{\bf H}
has constant trace equal to
1
on
(0,\ell)
. A canonical system is a differential equation of the form

\Omega\partial_{t}X(t)=\lambda{\bf H}(t)X(t),\;\;\;\;\;\Omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.

(5)

The matrix-valued function
{\bf H}
is called the
Hamiltonian
of the canonical system.

Let
L^{2}((0,\ell);{\bf H})
be the weighted
L^{2}-
space on
(0,\ell)
with norm

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}((0,\ell);H)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\ell}\langle H(x)f(x),f(x)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}dx<\infty,

where we identify elements
f,g
with with
\lVert f-g\rVert_{L^{2}((0,\ell);{\bf H})}=0
.

In order to construct admissible pairs, the simplest case is when
\det({\bf H})\neq 0
a.e.. In this case, the unbounded operator
D
given by

D={\bf H}^{-1}\Omega\frac{d}{dt}

with maximal domain of definition is closed. Self-adjoint restrictions with compact resolvent can be easily found using boundary conditions.

Now define
V
by

Vf(x)=-\Omega\int_{0}^{x}{\bf H}(t)f(t)dt,f\in\mathscr{H},

(6)

and note that it satisfies
DV=I
.

Proposition 1
.

The operator
V
defined by (
6
) is compact and quasi-nilpotent on
L^{2}((0,\ell);{\bf H})
.

Proof.

Since
\text{trace}({\bf H})=1
we have
{\bf H}(x)\leq I
and

\|Vf\|\leq\int_{0}^{x}\sqrt{\langle{\bf H}f(t),f(t)\rangle}dt.

With a standard argument we conclude for any bounded sequence
(f_{n})
in
L^{2}((0,\ell);{\bf H})
, that
(Vf_{n})
is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous, hence by the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, it has a uniformly subsequence. This implies the compactness of
V
.

To see that
V
is quasi-nilpotent it suffices to check that
V
has no eigenvalues. Obviously,
0
is not an eigenvalue. If
(I-\lambda V)f=0
, note that both
0
and
f
are solutions to the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\Omega X^{\prime}=\lambda{\bf H}X\\
X(0)=0\end{cases}

which gives
f=0
a.e. and completes the proof.∎

The case when
{\bf H}
is not invertible a.e. is more involved because it requires an appropriate modification of the definition of
D
, which will then lead to a modified definition of the underlying Hilbert space. Here we shall only give a brief overview of the necessary steps and refer the reader to Section 2 of
[
37
]
or the first few chapters of
[
36
]
for a detailed account. Let us note from the beginning that if
{\bf H}
is singular on a set of positive measure any element
f\in L^{2}((0,\ell);{\bf H})
is identified with its projection on the range of
{\bf H}
.

We start with the following technical definition which plays an important role.

Definition 2
.

A singular interval is an interval
I=(a,b)\subset(0,\ell)
, such that
{\bf H}(x)=\langle\cdot,e_{\alpha}\rangle e_{\alpha}
, a.e. for some
e_{\alpha}=(\cos(\alpha),\sin(\alpha))^{T}
and
(a,b)
is contained in no larger interval where the same is true. A point
t\in(0,\ell)
is called singular if it belongs to the interior of a singular interval, otherwise it is called regular.

Let us consider the closed subspace
\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
of
L^{2}((0,\ell);H)
consisting of functions which are constant a.e. on singular intervals, that is,

\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}=\bigcap_{I\text{ singular}}\left\{f\in L^{2}((0,\ell);{\bf H}):{\bf H}f|I\text{ is constant a.e.}\right\}.

Next we define the domain of
D
as the subspace
\mathscr{D}(D)\subset\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
consisting of
f\in\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
with

(i)

f
is absolutely continuous on
(0,\ell)
,

(ii)

there exists
g\in\mathscr{H}
with
\Omega f^{\prime}=Hg
,

(iii)

\langle f(0),(0,1)^{T}\rangle=0.

The main problem which arises here is that this domain in not necessarily dense in
\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
. However, one can address this difficulty with help of some natural assumptions. A straightforward modification of the arguments in the proofs of Corollary 2, Theorem 2 in Section 2 of
[
37
]
(by just omitting one boundary condition) leads to the following result.

Theorem 3
.

If
{\bf H}
satisfies

(i)

There exists no
\epsilon>0
, such that
H(x)=\langle\cdot,e_{\pi/2}\rangle e_{\pi/2}
a.e on
(0,\epsilon)
, with
e_{\pi/2}=(0,1)^{T}
.

(ii)

There exists no
\epsilon>0
, such that
(\ell-\epsilon,\ell)
is a singular interval,

then
\mathscr{D}(D)
is dense in
\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
and the operator
D:\mathscr{D}\mapsto\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
by
Df=g
, with
g
given by (ii) is well-defined, closed and possesses a self-adjoint restriction.

Finally, note that
\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
is invariant for
V
. Indeed, let
I
be a singular interval and let
f\in\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
. Then
since
\Omega e_{\alpha}\in\ker{\bf H}(x),~x\in I
, it is easily seen that
Vf\in\mathscr{H}_{\bf H}
. Then Proposition
1
shows that
D,V
is an admissible pair.

2.4
Self-adjoint operators with removable spectrum

Let
A
be an injective compact self-adjoint operator with simple removable spectrum, i.e. there exists
x,y\in H
, such that the rank one perturbation

Vu=Au+\langle u,x\rangle y,\quad u\in H,

(7)

is quasi-nilpotent. Such operators were studied in
[
6
]
. Let us assume that
y\notin\text{Ran}(A)
and define

\mathscr{D}(D)=\text{Ran}(A)+{\mathbb{C}}y
and

D:\mathscr{D}(D)\subset H\to H,\;\;D(Au+cy)=u.

(8)

Proposition 2
.

Let
A
be be an injective compact self-adjoint operator with simple removable spectrum, let
V
be the quasi-nilpotent operator given by (
7
) and assume that
y\notin\text{Ran}(A)
. If
D
is given by (
8
), then
D,V
is an admissible pair.

Proof.

To verify condition (i) note that
\mathscr{D}(D)
is dense in
H
, since
\text{Ran}(A)
is. To see that
D
is closed, assume that
u_{n}=Av_{n}+c_{n}y\in\mathscr{D}
and
v_{n}=Du_{n}
converge to
u
respectively to
v
. Then
(c_{n})
converges to
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.
u=Av+cy\in\mathscr{D}
and
Du=v
. (ii) is obvious, as well as
(iv) and (v). To see (iii), define the unbounded operator
B
on
H
by
\mathscr{D}(B)=\text{Ran}(A)
and
BAu=u
.
Recall that
0\notin\sigma(A)
and let
\{u_{\lambda},~\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}
be an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
A
. Then each
u_{\lambda}\in\mathscr{D}(B)
and
Bu_{\lambda}=\lambda^{-1}u_{\lambda}
, which easily implies that

is self-adjoint, and obviously, a restriction of

.∎

3
Preliminaries

We shall use standard notations for the usual function spaces on the upper half-plane
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
. Thus
H^{2}=H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is the Hardy space and equals the (inverse) Fourier transform of
L^{2}(0,\infty)
. Equivalently
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
consists of analytic functions
f
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
with

\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sup_{y>0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert f(x+iy)\rvert^{2}dx<\infty.

H^{\infty}=H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
denotes the space of bounded analytic functions in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
N(\mathbb{C}_{+})
denote the functions of bounded type, or the Nevanlinna class in the upper-half plane, i.e. analytic functions of the form
f=g/h
, with
g,h\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
. All three spaces have identical definitions in the lower half-plane
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
.

The mean-type of functions
h
analytic in
\mathbb{C}_{\pm}
is defined as

\text{m.t.}(h)=\limsup_{y\to\pm\infty}\frac{\log\lvert h(iy)\rvert}{y}.

For example, the mean-type of a function,
f
, of bounded type is given by minus the exponent of
e^{i\alpha z}
appearing in the inner-outer factorization of
f
.

For entire functions
f
one defines the (exponential) type type
\tau(f)
as

\tau(f)=\limsup_{\lvert z\rvert\to\infty}\frac{\log\lvert f(z)\rvert}{\lvert z\rvert}.

These notions are connected by a famous theorem of Krein
(see
[
22
]
, p. 115, or
[
18
]
, Part 2).

Theorem 4
.

Let
f
be an entire function of bounded type in both half planes,
\mathbb{C}_{\pm}
. Then
f
is of finite exponential type and its exponential type is equal to the maximum of its mean-type in the upper and lower half-planes.

We now turn to de Branges spaces of entire functions and start with Hermite-Biehler, briefly HB functions.
These are entire functions
E:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, such that
\lvert E(z)\rvert>\lvert E(\bar{z})\rvert
, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

To any HB function
E
we associate the de Branges
\mathcal{H}(E)
consisting of entire functions
f
satisfying

(i)

f/E\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
,

(ii)

f^{\#}/E\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
,

where, as usual, for entire functions
f
we denote
f^{\#}(z)=\overline{f(\overline{z})}
. The norm on
\mathcal{H}(E)
is the one inherited from
L^{2}(\lvert E(t)\rvert^{-2}dt)
,

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}(E)}=\lVert f/E\rVert_{2}=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert f(t)E(t)^{-1}\rvert^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}.

For the theory of de Branges spaces we refer to de Branges’ book
[
14
]
. Other good references for the subject include
[
36
,
37
]
. From property
(i)
we see that
\mathcal{H}(E)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space where the kernel is given by

k_{\lambda}^{E}(z)=\frac{E(z)\overline{E(\lambda)}-E^{\#}(z)\overline{E^{\#}(\lambda)}}{2\pi i(\bar{\lambda}-z)}.

Of course, de Branges spaces are characterized by kernels of this form. The most classical examples are the Paley-Wiener spaces which are the Fourier transforms of
L^{2}(-a,a)
. In this particular case,
E(z)=e^{-iaz}
,
a>0
.

It is important to note that de Branges spaces can also be introduced in an axiomatic way (see
[
14
]
).

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space consisting of of entire functions satisfying

(i)

For
w\in\mathbb{C}
the linear functional
f\mapsto f(w)
is bounded.

(ii)

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, such that
f(\lambda)=0
we have
\frac{z-\bar{\lambda}}{z-\lambda}f\in\mathcal{H}
and
\lVert\frac{z-\bar{\lambda}}{z-\lambda}f\rVert=\lVert f\rVert
.

(iii)

f^{\#}(z)=\bar{f}(\bar{z})\in\mathcal{H}
, and
\lVert f\rVert=\lVert f^{\#}\rVert
.

Then there exists a HB function
E
, such that
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(E)
as Hilbert spaces.

A subspace,
X
of
\mathcal{H}(E)
is called a
de Branges subspace
if it is a de Branges space with respect to the norm inherited from
\mathcal{H}(E)
. Equivalently, in view of the previous theorem,
X
satisfies (i)-(iii) in that statement, hence it has the form
{\mathcal{H}}(F)
for some HB function
F
.

When
E
has no real zeros the de Branges subspaces of
\mathcal{H}(E)
have the following remarkable ordering property due to de Branges (see
[
36
,
37
,
14
]
).

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
E
is a HB function. If
\mathcal{H}(F)
and
\mathcal{H}(W)
are two de Branges subspaces of
\mathcal{H}(E)
, and the corresponding HB functions
F
and
W
have no real zeros, then either
\mathcal{H}(F)\subset\mathcal{H}(W)
or
\mathcal{H}(W)\subset\mathcal{H}(F)
.

For a HB function
E
with no real zeros we define
\text{Chain}(\mathcal{H}(E))
to be the collection of de Branges subspaces, whose HB functions have no real zeros. In general we call a collection of de Branges spaces a de Branges chain if it is totally ordered with respect to isometric inclusion.

A de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E)
is called
regular
(or short) if it is invariant under the backward shift operator,
Lf(z)=z^{-1}(f(z)-f(0))
. This is equivalent to the condition

\frac{1}{(z+i)E(z)}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+}).

By extension we call
E
a regular HB function.

An important property of such spaces is the following result
(
[
37
]
, Theorem 9, see also
[
36
,
14
]
).

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}(E)
be a regular de Branges space. If
\mathcal{H}(F)
is a de Branges subspace and
F
has no real zeros, then
\mathcal{H}(F)
is regular.

It follows that if
E
is a regular HB function then it is of finite exponential type
\tau(E)
which equals its mean-type in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
defined above.

Regular HB functions are related to canonical systems in the following way.
Let
{\bf H}
be a Hamiltonian as in §
2.3
and
\Theta=(\Theta_{+},\Theta_{-})^{T}
be the unique solution to the canonical system

\Omega\partial_{t}X(t)=z{\bf H}(t)X(t),\;t\in(0,\ell),

satisfying
\Theta(0)=(1,0)^{T}
. Then
E_{t}(z)=\Theta_{+}(t,z)+i\Theta_{-}(t,z)
is a regular HB function for each
t\in(0,\ell]
, except in the degenerate case

{\bf H}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\text{ for almost every }s\in(0,t).

In addition,

\text{Chain}(\mathcal{H}(E_{t}))=\left\{\mathcal{H}(E_{s}):s\in(0,t],s\text{ is regular}\right\},

Conversely, it is a deep theorem due to de Branges that to every regular de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E)
with
E(0)=1
there exists a (essentially unique) Hamiltonian
{\bf H}
with constant trace equal to
1
and an interval
(0,t]
, such that
E=E_{t}
and

\text{Chain}(\mathcal{H}(E))=\left\{\mathcal{H}(E_{s}):s\in(0,t],s\text{ is regular}\right\}.

For each regular HB function
E
with no real zeros,
E(0)=1
, the type of
E
may also be calculated from the associated canonical system using the following Krein-de Branges formula

\tau(E)=\int_{0}^{\ell}\sqrt{\det{{\bf H}(x)}}dx,

(9)

see
[
36
,
13
,
37
,
14
]
.

Remark 1
.

Any
f\in\mathcal{H}(E)
satisfies
\tau(f)\leq\tau(E)
. In particular, if the exponential type of
E
is zero then the exponential type of all elements in
\mathcal{H}(E)
is zero.

This connection to canonical systems can be used to establish the existence of de Branges subspaces of a given type. We include a proof of the result for the sake of completeness. A variant can be found in de Branges’ book
[
14
]
as well.

Lemma 1
.

Let
E
be a regular HB function with
\tau(E)>0
. Then for each
0<h<\tau(E)
there exists a HB function
F
zero free on
\mathbb{R}
with
\tau(F)=h
and
\mathcal{H}(F)
is a de Branges subspace of
\mathcal{H}(E)
.

Proof.

We may suppose
E(0)=1
. Let
{\bf H}
be a canonical system associated with
E
. Then

\tau(E)=\int_{0}^{\ell}\sqrt{\det{{\bf H}(x)}}dx.

The right-hand side is continuous in
\ell
and therefore it is possible to find
\delta>0
, such that

\tau(E)-\epsilon=\int_{0}^{\ell-\delta}\sqrt{\det{{\bf H}(x)}}dx.

It is also possible to choose
\delta
, such that
\ell-\delta
does not belong to a singular interval, since
\det({\bf H}(x))=0
, almost everywhere on singular intervals. Then the canonical system
{\bf H}
on
(0,\ell-\delta)
gives a de Branges subspace,
\mathcal{H}(E_{\epsilon})
of
\mathcal{H}(E)
with
\tau(E_{\epsilon})=\tau(E)-\epsilon
.
∎

Finally, we discuss the concept of
associated functions
to a given de Branges space.

Definition 3
.

An entire function
S
is said to be associated with a de Branges spaces
\mathcal{H}(E)
if whenever
S(\lambda)\neq 0
and
f\in\mathcal{H}(E)
, we have

\frac{f(z)S(\lambda)-f(\lambda)S(z)}{z-\lambda}\in\mathcal{H}(E).

Examples of associated functions include
E
,
A=2^{-1}(E+E^{\#})
, and
B=-i2^{-1}(E-E^{\#})
. An entire function
S
is associate with the de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E)
if and only if
S/E
,
S^{\#}/E
are of bounded type, non-positive mean-type in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+t^{2}}\frac{\lvert S(t)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert E(t)\rvert^{2}}dt<\infty.

This characterization can be reformulated as follows.

Proposition 3
.

The entire function
S
is associated to
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
if and only if
S\in{\mathcal{H}}((z+i)E)
. In particular, if
E
is regular then for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the exponential function
z\mapsto e^{i\beta z}
is associated to
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
if and only if
|\beta|\leq\tau(E)
.

4
The model

We now turn to the construction of the analytic model for an admissible pair
D
and
V
. The idea is fairly straightforward and reminiscent from the spectral theory of linear differential operators. It is based on the simple observation given in the lemma below. The more subtle part is to identify the objects resulting from the constructions.

Throughout in what follows we shall denote by
A
the densely defined self-adjoint restriction of
D
given in assumption (iii) and by
\Lambda=\sigma(A)
. Moreover, let
\phi_{0}
a non-zero vector in
\ker D
. Recall that by assumption (ii)
\phi_{0}
spans
\ker(D)
.

Lemma 2
.

For each
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},~\ker(D-\lambda I)
is spanned by

\phi_{\lambda}=(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\phi_{0}.

Moreover, the set

\{u_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\|\phi_{\lambda}\|}\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in\Lambda\},

is an orthonormal basis of
H
.

Proof.

The statement is almost self-explanatory. We have that

\phi_{\lambda}=(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\phi_{0}=\phi_{0}+\lambda V(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\phi_{0}=\phi_{0}+\lambda V\phi_{\lambda},

so that
\phi_{\lambda}\in\mathscr{D}(D)
and
D\phi_{\lambda}=\lambda DV\phi_{l}a=\lambda\phi_{\lambda}
, i.e.
\phi_{\lambda}\in\ker(D-\lambda I)
. Conversely, if
Du=\lambda u
, it follows that
D(I-\lambda V)u=0
, i.e.
u
is a multiple of
\phi_{\lambda}
. The second part follows from the first, the fact that the resolvent of
A
is compact, and the spectral theorem.

∎

With the lemma in hand we can proceed to construct our model.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the space of entire functions

\mathcal{H}=\left\{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C}):\hat{f}(\lambda)=\langle f,\phi_{\bar{\lambda}}\rangle_{H}\text{, for some }f\in H\right\}.

(10)

The second part of Lemma
2
shows that
\hat{f}
is uniquely determined by
f
, as well as by the values
\hat{f}(\lambda),~\lambda\in\Lambda
. In particular, we can define a norm on
\mathcal{H}
by

\|\hat{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|f\|_{H},

(11)

which makes the map
\mathcal{W}:H\to{\mathcal{H}},~{\mathcal{W}}f=\hat{f}
unitary.

The unitary map
\mathcal{W}
can be seen as a generalized Fourier transform associated to the operator
D
. If
D=-id/dx
, then
\mathcal{W}
is the classical (inverse) Fourier transform and if
D
is a regular Schrödinger operator it is nothing but the classical Weyl-Titmarsch transform.
The usual gain from considering such analytic models is a much more tractable form of the operators involved in the process.

We shall relate the operators
D,V
and the self-adjoint restriction
A
of
D
acting on
H
, to the forward and backward shifts
M_{z},L
acting on
{\mathcal{H}}
, More precisely, we consider the operator
M_{z}
of multiplication by the independent variable acting on
{\mathcal{H}}
(
M_{z}f=zf
), with the maximal domain of definition

{\mathscr{D}}(M_{z})=\{f\in{\mathcal{H}}:~zf\in{\mathcal{H}}\},

and the backward shift
L
on
{\mathcal{H}}
formally defined by

Lg(z)=\frac{g(z)-g(0)}{z},\quad g\in{\mathcal{H}},\,z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(12)

Proposition 4
.

With the above notations we have:

(i)
{\mathcal{W}}V^{*}{\mathcal{W}}^{-1}=L
,

(ii) If
x\in{\mathscr{D}}(D^{*})
then
{\mathcal{W}}x\in{\mathscr{D}}(M_{z})
and
{\mathcal{W}}D^{*}x=M_{z}Wx
.

(iii)
M_{z}
is a closed symmetric operator with

(M_{z}-\lambda I){\mathscr{D}}(M_{z})=\{f\in{\mathcal{H}}:~f(\lambda)=0\},\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},

Proof.

(i) If
x\in H,~\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}V^{*}x(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\langle x,V\phi_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle=\langle x,V(1-\overline{\lambda}V)^{-1}\phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\langle x,(1-\overline{\lambda}V)^{-1}\phi_{0}\rangle-\langle x,\phi_{0}\rangle)=L{\mathcal{W}}x(\lambda).

(ii) If
x\in{\mathscr{D}}(D^{*})
then

{\mathcal{W}}D^{*}x(\lambda)=\langle x,D\phi_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle=\lambda{\mathcal{W}}x(\lambda),\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}},

which gives the assertion.

(iii) If
(f_{n})
is a sequence in
{\mathscr{D}}(M_{z})
with
f_{n}\to f,~M_{z}f_{n}\to g
in
{\mathcal{H}}
we use the continuity of point evaluations on
{\mathcal{H}}
to obtain

g(\lambda)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda f_{n}(\lambda)=\lambda\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(\lambda)=\lambda f(\lambda),

which shows that the graph of
M_{z}
is closed. To see that
M_{z}
is symmetric we use the following claim:

If
x,y\in H
satisfy
\langle x,\phi_{0}\rangle=\langle y,\phi_{0}\rangle=0
then
\langle V^{*}x,y\rangle=\langle x,V^{*}y\rangle
.
By (i), an equivalent formulation is: If
u,v\in{\mathcal{H}}
satisfy
u(0)=v(0)=0
then

\langle Lu,v\rangle=\langle u,Lv\rangle

(13)

With (
13
) in hand, for
f,g\in{\mathscr{D}}(M_{z})
write
f=Lu,~g=Lv
with
u=M_{z}f,~v=M_{z}g
and since
u(0)=v(0)=0
, an application of (
13
) gives the symmetry of
M_{z}
.

To verify the claim, recall that
H
has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
A
which have the form

e_{\lambda}=c_{\lambda}(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\phi_{0},\quad\lambda\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}.

We have

(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\phi_{0}=\phi_{0}+\lambda V(I-\lambda V)^{-1}\phi_{0},

and from
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\langle x,\phi_{0}\rangle=\langle y,\phi_{0}\rangle=0
we obtain

\langle V^{*}x,y\rangle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\langle x,Ve_{\lambda}\rangle\langle e_{\lambda},y\rangle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\lambda}\langle x,e_{\lambda}\rangle\lambda\langle Ve_{\lambda},y\rangle=\langle x,V^{*}y\rangle

which gives the claim and concludes the proof.
∎

Another piece of information of crucial
importance regards the structure of the underlying model space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
This is described in the main result of this section. Recall that the HB function
E
is called regular if
[(z+i)E]^{-1}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
.

Theorem 8
.

There exists a regular HB function,
E
, with no real zeros, and a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
e^{i\alpha z}\mathcal{W}H=\mathcal{H}(E)
as Hilbert spaces.

The result can be seen as a theorem related to the axiomatic definition of de Branges spaces of entire functions described in Theorem
5
. More precisely, Theorem
8
can be deduced from the description of Hilbert spaces of entire functions which only satisfy the first two conditions characterizing de Branges spaces but not necessarily the third.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a Hilbert space of entire functions without common zeros and satisfying

(i)

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
the linear functional
f\mapsto f(\lambda)
is bounded,

(ii)

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, such that
f(\lambda)=0
we have
\frac{z-\bar{\lambda}}{z-\lambda}f\in\mathcal{N}
and
\lVert\frac{z-\bar{\lambda}}{z-\lambda}f\rVert=\lVert f\rVert
.

Then there exists a zero free entire function
u
, satisfying
uu^{\#}\equiv 1
and a HB function
E
with no real zeros, such that

u\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{H}(E),

and multiplication by
u
is a unitary map from

\mathcal{N}
onto
\mathcal{H}(E)
. If, in addition, one of the following conditions holds

1.

\mathcal{N}
is a subspace of a de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(W)
,

2.

\mathcal{N}
is invariant for the backward shift
L
and
\sigma(L|\mathcal{N})=\{0\}
,

then
u(z)=e^{i\alpha z}
, for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover, if the second assumption holds then
E
is regular and
|\alpha|\leq\tau(E)
.

Proof.

By assumption (i),
\mathcal{N}
has a reproducing kernel
k^{\mathcal{N}}
.
By Theorem 4.1 in
[
4
]
, or by the proof of Theorem 23 in
[
14
]
it follows that
k^{\mathcal{N}}
has the form

k^{\mathcal{N}}(z,\lambda)=k^{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{F(z)\overline{F(\lambda)}-G(z)\overline{G(\lambda)}}{2\pi i(\bar{\lambda}-z)},

(14)

where
F
and
G
are the entire functions given by

\begin{cases}F(z)=\sqrt{\pi}k_{i}^{\mathcal{N}}(z)k_{i}^{\mathcal{N}}(i)^{-1/2}(z+i)\\
G(z)=\sqrt{\pi}k_{-i}^{\mathcal{N}}(z)k_{-i}^{\mathcal{N}}(-i)^{-1/2}(z-i)\end{cases}.

(15)

Note that (
14
) together with the assumption that
\mathcal{N}
has no common zeros implies that
F
and
G
have no common zeros in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Indeed, by (
14
)

F(\lambda)=G(\lambda)=0
implies
k^{\mathcal{N}}_{\lambda}\equiv 0
which contradicts this assumption on
\mathcal{N}
.

Now set
\lambda=\overline{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}
in (
14
) and note that since the denominator in (
14
) vanishes, the numerator must vanish as well, i.e.

F(\bar{z})\overline{F(z)}=G(\bar{z})\overline{G(z)}\Leftrightarrow F^{\#}(z)F(z)=G^{\#}(z)G(z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(16)

In particular, neither
F
nor
G
can have a real zero
x
, otherwise from
|F(x)|^{2}=|G(x)|^{2}
we would conclude that
x
is a common zero of these functions which cannot hold by the above argument. Moreover, from above if
F(\lambda)=0
, then
G(\lambda)\neq 0
, hence by (
16
) it follows that
G^{\#}(\lambda)=0
and the order of the zero
\lambda
for
G^{\#}
is at least as large at the order of the zero
\lambda
for
F
. Thus the function
v=G^{\#}/F
is entire and similarly,
F^{\#}/G=1/v^{\#}
is entire, i.e.
v
has no zeros in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Finally, from the equality
|F(x)|^{2}=|G(x)|^{2},~x\in\mathbb{R}
we see that
|v|=1
on
\mathbb{R}
. Equivalently,
vv^{\#}=1
.

Set
u=v^{1/2}
and
E=uF
. Then
E
is zero-free on
\mathbb{R}
,
E^{\#}=u^{\#}F^{\#}=\frac{F^{\#}}{u}
and the inequality
|E(z)|>|E^{\#}(z)|,z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
is equivalent to

|u(z)|^{2}|F(z)|=|G^{\#}(z)|>|F^{\#}(z)|,\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},

which follows from

0<|k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{N}}(\lambda)|=\frac{|F(\lambda)|^{2}-|G(\lambda)|^{2}}{4\text{Im }\lambda},\quad\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}_{-}.

Thus,
E
is a HB function and the equalities

u(z)\overline{u(\lambda)}k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{N}}(z)=k_{\lambda}^{E}(z),\quad k_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{N}}(z)=\frac{1}{u(z)\overline{u(\lambda)}}k_{\lambda}^{E}(z),

give that multiplication by

is unitary.

To prove the second part, we shall show that both conditions imply that

is of bounded type in
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
. Since
|u|=1
on the real line, it will follow that
u(z)=e^{i\alpha z}
, for some real number

and all

.

1. If
\mathcal{N}
is a closed subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}(W)
then from the formulas defining

in (

) it follows that

u^{2}(z)=\frac{(k^{\mathcal{N}}_{-i})^{\#}(z)(k^{\mathcal{N}}_{-i}(-i))^{-1/2}}{k^{\mathcal{N}}_{i}(z)(k^{\mathcal{N}}_{i}(i))^{-1/2}}.

Since
k^{\mathcal{N}}_{i}
and
k^{\mathcal{N}}_{-i}
belong to
\mathcal{H}(W)
, it follows that
k^{\mathcal{N}}_{i}/W,(k^{\mathcal{N}}_{-i})^{\#}/W\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
and so as a quotient of two

functions,

is of bounded type and obviously so is

.

2. Assume that
\mathcal{N}
is backward-shift invariant denote this operator by

and assume that
\sigma(L)=\{0\}
. A simple computation reveals that if
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the operator

is given by

L(I-\lambda L)^{-1}h(z)=\frac{h(z)-h(\lambda)}{z-\lambda},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

If
u\mathcal{N}={\mathcal{H}}(E)
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
f\in{\mathcal{H}}(E)
, then from
uL(I-\lambda L)^{-1}u^{\#}f\in{\mathcal{H}}(E)
we obtain after a direct computation using

5
Generalized Volterra operators

The aim of this section is to explore invariant subspaces of right inverses of the generalized differentiation operator
D

{\mathcal{H}}
depends essentially on the choice of the right inverse
V
of the unbounded operator
D
considered in this paper. Of course,
D
has a collection of right inverses which are all rank-one perturbations of the chosen

V_{x}=V+\langle\cdot,x\rangle\phi_{0},

(19)

where

is arbitrary but fixed. All of these operators are compact, but they are not necessarily quasi-nilpotent, and not necessarily unicellular. For example, if
D
is the classical differentiation operator with maximal domain in

and

Vf(t)=\int_{a}^{t}f(s)ds,\quad f\in L^{2}(a,b),

if and only if

V_{x}f(t)=\int_{x}^{t}f(s)ds,\quad f\in L^{2}(a,b),

for some
c\in[a,b]
and
V_{x}
is unicellular if and only if
x=a
, or
x=b
.
It turns out that an analogous result continues to hold in the very general context as well. For this purpose it will be more convenient to represent
V_{x}
as operators acting on the model space. Since

V_{x}^{*}=V^{*}+\langle\cdot,\phi_{0}\rangle x,

we obtain from Proposition
4

{\mathcal{W}}V_{x}^{*}{\mathcal{W}}^{-1}f(z)=\frac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}+f(0){\mathcal{W}}x(z)=\frac{f(z)-f(0)(1-z{\mathcal{W}}x(z))}{z}.

(20)

To establish the analogy to the standard example described above, we start by describing the quasi-nilpotent right inverses of

.

Proposition 5
.

The compact operator

has spectrum
\{0\}

Proof.

Since

8

6.1

We now turn our attention to the operator

\mathscr{D}(D^{n})=\{x\in\mathscr{D}(D^{n-1}):~D^{n-1}x\in\mathscr{D}(D)\}.

Proposition 7
.

We have

\mathscr{D}(D^{n})=\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}V^{k}\phi_{0}+V^{n}x;~a_{k}\in\mathbb{C},0\leq k\leq n-1,\,x\in H\right\}.

In particular,
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})\subset\mathscr{D}(D^{n-1})
. Moreover, each
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
is a Hilbert space with respect to the norm

\|x\|_{n}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|D^{k}x\|_{H}^{2},

(24)

and the inclusion map from
\mathscr{D}(D^{n+1})
into
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
is compact.

Proof.

Since
DV=I
,
Dx=y
implies

x=Vy+a_{0}\phi_{0},

which gives the result for
n=1
. If we assume the assertion holds true for
n-1
then any

has the form

y=p(V)\phi_{0}+V^{n-1}x
with

a polynomial of degree at most

. Then
D^{n-1}y\in\mathscr{D}(D)
, is equivalent to

x=c\phi_{0}+Vz\,\Rightarrow y=c\phi_{0}+Vp(V)\phi_{0}+V^{n}z,

which concludes the proof of the first part. The remaining part is straightforward. For example, the compactness assertion follows directly from the compactness of
V
on
H
.
∎

Remark 3
.

1)
The argument actually shows that
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
is the maximal domain of

.

2) Obviously,

is invariant for any right inverse of

Recall that the eigenvectors of

Proof.

The first part follows directly from the simple observation that

belongs to the closed linear span of these vectors, hence by a repeated application of
V
, there exists a polynomial
q
such that

V^{n}x+q(V)\phi_{0}\in\overline{\bigvee\left\{\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in\Lambda\right\}}\subset\overline{\bigvee\left\{\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\right\}}.

Now by the definition of the derivative,

\frac{1}{k!}\phi^{(k)}(0)=V^{k}\phi_{0}\in\overline{\bigvee\left\{\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\right\}},\quad k\geq 0,

hence the set on the right contains
p(V)\phi_{0}
for any polynomial
p
and Proposition
7
gives the desired result.
∎

Let us now turn to our analogue of
C^{\infty}
defined as

C^{\infty}(D)=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathscr{D}(D^{n}).

(25)

The space appears naturally as a projective limit of the Hilbert spaces
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
(see
[
32
]
, Chapter 3). Alternatively, the topology on
C^{\infty}(D)
is given by the translation-invariant metric

d(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-j}\frac{\lVert D^{j}x-D^{j}y\rVert_{H}}{1+\lVert D^{j}x-D^{j}y\rVert_{H}}.

and
C^{\infty}(D)
is complete and separable with respect to this metric.
The operator
D
together with all its right inverses act continuously on
C^{\infty}(D)
. Moreover,
C^{\infty}(D)
is a dense subspace of
H
, since it contains all eigenvectors
\phi_{\lambda},~\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
of
D
.

Recall from
[
32
]
that any continuous
linear functional
\varphi
on
C^{\infty}(D)
can be written in the form

\varphi(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\langle D^{j}x,y_{j}\rangle_{H},

(26)

with
y_{j}\in H\setminus\{0\},~0\leq j\leq n
fixed. The representation is not unique and the smallest
n
for which such a representation holds will be called the
order
of
\varphi
.
Obviously,
\varphi
has order at most
n
if and only if it extends to a continuous linear functional on
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
.
The space of these continuous functionals
will be denoted by
(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}
.

As an application we prove the following proposition which collects basic density results that will be needed in the sequel.

Proposition 8
.

We have

(i)

\left\{\phi_{z}\right\}_{z\in\mathbb{C}}
has dense linear span in
C^{\infty}(D)
,

(ii)

For any

\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, the set
\left\{[V(I-\lambda_{0}V)^{-1}]^{j}\phi_{\lambda_{0}}:~j\geq 0\right\}
has dense linear span in
C^{\infty}(D)
.

Proof.

(i) is a direct application of Corollary
6
. (ii) If
\varphi
is a continuous linear functional on
C^{\infty}(D)
which annihilates the set in the statement, by (
26
) and another application of Corollary
6
it follows that all derivatives of the entire function
\lambda\mapsto\varphi(\phi_{\lambda})
vanish at
\lambda_{0}
. Then the function vanishes identically and the result follows by (i).
∎

We shall extend our generalized Fourier transform
\mathcal{W}
from Section
4
to the dual of
C^{\infty}(D)
by

\mathcal{W}\varphi(z)=\varphi(\phi_{z})^{\#}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\langle y_{j},D^{j}\phi_{\overline{z}}\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}z^{j}\langle y_{j},\phi_{\overline{z}}\rangle.

(27)

The dual space
(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}
can be endowed with the weak-star topology, i.e. the smallest topology such that all maps
\varphi\mapsto\varphi(x),~x\in C^{\infty}(D)
are continuous.

We shall use repeatedly the following results. The first is an appropriate version of the Krein-Smulian theorem and can be found in
[
32
]
, Chapter 6.

Proposition 9
.

For
r>0
let

B(0,r)^{\circ}=\{\varphi\in(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}:~|\varphi(x)|\leq 1~\text{whenever }d(x,0)\leq r)\}.

A convex set
C\subset(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}

is
w^{*}-
closed if and only if
C\cap B(0,r)^{\circ}
is
w^{*}-
closed for all
r>0
.

Note that the separability of
C^{\infty}(D)
implies that the
w^{*}-
topology on each polar set
B(0,r)^{\circ}
is metrizable. For this reason, it is sufficient to prove the sequential closedness of the sets involved in the above proposition. The following observation is very useful in this sense.

Proposition 10
.

If
(\varphi_{n})
converges weak-star to
\varphi
in
(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}
then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that the order of
\varphi_{n}
is at most
N
for all
n\geq 1
. In addition, the extensions of
\varphi_{n}
to
\mathscr{D}(D^{N})
converge weak-star as well.

Proof.

By uniform boundedness (or Baire’s theorem) there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\varphi_{n}(x)|\leq 1
whenever
d(x,0)\leq\delta
and
n\geq 1
. Then if
N\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies
\sum_{j>N}2^{-j}<\frac{\delta}{2}
, we have

d(x,0)<\sum_{j=0}^{N}2^{-j}\frac{\lVert D^{j}x\rVert_{H}}{1+\lVert D^{j}x\rVert_{H}}+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{N}\|D^{j}x\|+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\sqrt{N}\|x\|_{N}+\frac{\delta}{2}.

This implies
|\varphi_{n}(x)|\leq 1
whenever
\|x\|_{N}\leq\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{N}}
and
n\geq 1
, i.e. the order of
\varphi_{n}
is at most
N
for all
n\geq 1
. The sequence of restrictions of
\varphi_{n}
to
\mathscr{D}(D^{N})
is uniformly bounded and converges on a dense subspace of
\mathscr{D}(D^{N})
.
∎

6.2
Spectra of restrictions and annihilators of invariant subspaces

The main purpose of this section is to consider closed subspaces of
C^{\infty}(D)
which are invariant under
D
. We shall denote by
\mathscr{J}
the collection of all such subspaces.

As in the case of standard differentiation on intervals contained in the real line, the structure of such subspaces is related to the spectrum of the restriction of the operator (see
[
3
]
).
We begin with some typical examples of invariant subspaces for
D

and spectra of the corresponding restriction. To this end, it is useful to recall (see, for example, the proof of Proposition
7
) that the solutions of
(D-\lambda I)x=y
have the form

x=c\phi_{\lambda}+V(1-\lambda V)^{-1}y,\quad c\in\mathbb{C}.

(28)

Examples 2
.

1) Let
K
be one of the spaces
H,~\mathscr{D}(D^{n}),~n\geq 1
, or
C^{\infty}(D)
, and let
M\subset K
be a proper closed
V-
invariant subspace. Then

\mathcal{J}_{M}=\left\{x\in C^{\infty}(D):D^{j}x\in M\text{, for all }j\geq 0\right\}.

belongs to
\mathscr{J}
and satisfies

\sigma(D|\mathcal{J}_{M})=\emptyset
. To see this, note first that
\sigma(V|\mathcal{J}_{M})=\{0\}
.
This is obvious when
K=H
, or when
K=\mathscr{D}(D^{n}),~n\geq 1
. In the case
K=C^{\infty}(D)
apply the previous observation to the
closure of
M
in
\mathscr{D}(D^{n}),~n\geq 1
and use the fact that if
y\in C^{\infty}(D)
the solution
x
of
(\lambda V-I)x=y
belongs to
C^{\infty}(D)
as well. Thus,

M
is invariant for
(V-\mu)(1-\lambda V)^{-1},~\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, in particular, it cannot contain any
\phi_{\lambda},~\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Otherwise, since
\phi_{z}=(I-\lambda V)(I-zV)^{-1}\phi_{\lambda}
,
M
would contain all such elements and equal
H
. This shows that and if
y\in\mathcal{J}_{M}
, the unique solution
x\in\mathcal{J}_{M}
of the equation
(D-\lambda I)x=y
is given by

x=V(1-\lambda V)^{-1}y\in\mathcal{J}_{M},

and the claim follows.

We also note that these assertions continue to if hold
V
is replaced by any other quasi-nilpotent right inverse of
D
.

2) The spectrum of
D
on the whole space
C^{\infty}(D)
equals
\mathbb{C}
. In the classical case, i.e.
D=-i\frac{d}{dx}
on
C^{\infty}(a,b)
, there are also smaller
D-
invariant subspaces with this property, for example

\mathcal{J}=\{f\in C^{\infty}(a,b):~f|I\cup J=0\},

where
I,J
are two disjoint closed subintervals of
(a,b)
.

3) Let
A
be a self-adjoint restriction of
D
with compact resolvent. Then there exists an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors
\left\{\phi_{\lambda}\right\}_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}
, where
\sigma(A)\subset\mathbb{R}
is discrete.

For
\emptyset\neq\Lambda\subset\sigma(A)
, let
\mathcal{J}_{\Lambda}
be the closed span of
\{\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in\Lambda\}
in
C^{\infty}(D)
. This space is
D-
invariant since
D
is continuous on
C^{\infty}(D)
, and for every finite linear combination

x=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\phi_{\lambda_{j}},\quad a_{j}\in\mathbb{C},\,\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda,

we have
Dx\in\mathcal{J}_{\Lambda}
.

We claim that
\sigma(D|\mathcal{J}_{\Lambda})=\Lambda.
Indeed, it is easy to verify that for
\mu\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Lambda
, the linear map
R_{\mu}
defined on the span of
\{\phi_{\lambda}:~\lambda\in\Lambda\}
by

R_{\mu}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\phi_{\lambda_{j}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{a_{j}}{\lambda_{j}-\mu}\phi_{\lambda_{j}}\quad a_{j}\in\mathbb{C},\,\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda,

extends continuously to
\mathcal{J}_{\Lambda}
and is an inverse of
(D-\mu I)|\mathcal{J}_{\Lambda}
.

In the classical case when
D=-i\frac{d}{dx}
on some interval on the real line, the three examples above describe completely the types of spectra that can occur for the restriction to an invariant subspace;
\mathbb{C},~\emptyset
or a discrete subset of a complex plane (see
[
3
]
). It turns out that this result continues to hold in the general case.

Theorem 10
.

For each
\mathcal{J}\in\mathscr{J}
the spectrum
\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})
is either the whole complex plane,
\mathbb{C}
, or the discrete (possibly void) set

\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\phi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}\right\}.

If
\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})\neq\mathbb{C},\emptyset
then each
\lambda\in\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})
has spectral multiplicity one.

Proof.

By Proposition
8
the set
\{\lambda:~\phi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}\}
must be discrete. Therefore, it will be sufficient to prove that if
\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})\neq\mathbb{C}
, then for each
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
,
(D-\mu I)|\mathcal{J}
is bijective whenever it is injective. The continuity of the inverse is automatic since
\mathcal{J}
is Fréchet. To this end, assume
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C},~\lambda\neq\mu
, are such that
(D-\lambda I)|\mathcal{J}
has the continuous inverse
R:\mathcal{J}\mapsto\mathcal{J}
, and
(D-\mu I)|\mathcal{J}
is injective, i.e.
\phi_{\mu}\notin\mathcal{J}
. We need to show that

(D-\mu I)|\mathcal{J}
is surjective. This is immediate in the case when
I+(\lambda-\mu)R
is invertible on
\mathcal{J}
since
(I+(\lambda-\mu)R)^{-1}x
is a solution of
(D-\mu I)x=y
.

The remainder of the proof consists in removing the above assumption on
I+(\lambda-\mu)R
. Observe that from
(D-\lambda I)R=I
it follows that

Rx=\ell(x)\phi_{\lambda}+V(I-\lambda V)^{-1}x,\quad x\in\mathcal{J},

(29)

where
\ell
extends to a continuous linear functional on
C^{\infty}(D)
.
Use (
26
) to choose
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\ell
is a continuous linear functional on
\mathscr{D}(D^{n_{0}})
, and for
n\geq n_{0}
and let
\mathcal{J}_{n}
be the closure of
\mathcal{J}
in
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
. By Proposition
7
(see also 2) of Remark
3
) it follows that
R
extends to a compact operator on
\mathcal{J}_{n},~n\geq n_{0}
. Moreover, if
n>1
this extension satisfies

(D-\lambda I)Rx=x,\quad x\in\mathcal{J}_{n}.

Indeed, if
(x_{k})
is a sequence in
\mathcal{J}
converging to
x\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
, then
Rx_{k}\to Rx
in
\mathcal{J}_{n}
, and since
D
is continuous from
\mathscr{D}(D^{n})
to
\mathscr{D}(D^{n-1})
we obtain

(D-\lambda I)Rx=\lim_{k\to\infty}(D-\lambda I)Rx_{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=x,

where the above limits are considered in
\mathscr{D}(D^{n-1})
. Now use the assumption that
\phi_{\mu}\notin\mathcal{J}
to find

n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\phi_{\mu}\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}
if
n\geq n_{1}
. Then from above
I+(\lambda-\mu)R|\mathcal{J}_{n}
is injective if
n>n_{1}
, since for
x\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
,
I+(\lambda-\mu)Rx=0
implies

0=(D-\lambda I)(I+(\lambda-\mu)R)x=(D-\mu I)x,

which implies
x=0
. But then, using the compactness of
R
, we conclude that
I+(\lambda-\mu)R|\mathcal{J}_{n}
is invertible. Thus, for such
n
we obtain exactly as above that for each
y\in\mathcal{J}
, the equation
(D-\mu I)x=y
has the solution

x_{n}=((I+(\lambda-\mu)R)|\mathcal{J}_{n})^{-1}y.

Finally, we observe that the solutions
x_{n},x_{m}
differ only by a multiple of
\phi_{m}u
and since
m,n>n_{1}
we conclude that
x_{n}=x_{m}=x\in\mathcal{J}
.

∎

Let us now turn to annihilators of nontrivial
D-
invariant subspaces.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{J}\in\mathscr{J}
and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{J}^{\perp}
have order
N\geq 0
.

(i)

If
\overline{\lambda}\notin\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})
and
\mathcal{W}\varphi(\lambda)=0
, then the continuous linear functional given by

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\varphi(V(I-\overline{\lambda}V)^{-1}fx),

annihilates
\mathcal{J}
, if
N>0
it has order
N-1
, and satisfies

\frac{\mathcal{W}\varphi(z)}{z-\lambda}=\mathcal{W}\varphi_{\lambda}(z).

(ii)

If
\mathcal{J}^{\perp}
contains a nonzero functional
\varphi
such that
\mathcal{W}\varphi
has infinitely many zeros in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})}
. Then there exists a nonzero
y\in H
which annihilates
\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

If
\overline{\lambda}\notin\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})

the resolvent
R=(D-\overline{\lambda}I|\mathcal{J})^{-1}
has the form

Rx=\ell(x)\phi_{\overline{\lambda}}+V(I-\overline{\lambda}V)^{-1}x,

with
\ell
a continuous linear functional on
C^{\infty}(D)
.
Since
\mathcal{W}\varphi(\lambda)=0
it follows that

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\varphi(V(I-\overline{\lambda}V)^{-1}x)=\varphi(Rx)=0,

for all
x\in\mathcal{J}
. By the form of
\varphi
it follows easily that
\varphi_{\lambda}
has order
N-1
if
N>0
. Moreover,

\mathcal{W}\varphi_{\lambda}(z)=\varphi(V(I-\overline{\lambda}V)^{-1}\phi_{z})^{\#}=\varphi((z-\overline{\lambda})^{-1}(\phi_{z}-\phi_{\overline{\lambda}}))^{\#}=\frac{\mathcal{W}\varphi(z)}{z-\lambda}.

(ii) follows by a repeated application of (i). ∎

6.3
Residual subspaces and their annihilators

As pointed out before, we are especially interested in
D-
invariant subspaces
\mathcal{J}
with
\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})=\emptyset
. Such subspaces will be called
residual
. They possess an alternative characterization based on the following simple observation.

Lemma 3
.

If
\mathcal{J}\in\mathscr{J}
and
p
is a polynomial then
p(D)\mathcal{J}
is closed.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
(D-\lambda I)\mathcal{J},~\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is closed. Let
(x_{k})
be a sequence in
\mathcal{J}
such that
(D-\lambda I)x_{k}\to y\in H
. Then there is a sequence
(c_{k})
in
\mathbb{C}
such that

V(I-\lambda V)^{-1}(D-\lambda I)x_{k}=x_{k}-c_{k}\phi_{\lambda}\to V(I-\lambda V)^{-1}y,

(30)

when
k\to\infty
. If
\phi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{J}
, it follows that
(x_{k})
converges to
x\in\mathcal{J}
and
y=(D-\lambda I)x\in(D-\lambda I)\mathcal{J}
. If
\phi_{\lambda}\notin\mathcal{J}
, let
\varphi
be a continuous linear functional in
\mathcal{J}^{\perp}
with
\varphi(\phi_{\lambda})=1
. Then applying
\varphi
to both sides of (
30
) we obtain that
(c_{k})
converges and as above, we obtain that
(x_{k})
converges to
x\in\mathcal{J}
and
y=(D-\lambda I)x\in(D-\lambda I)\mathcal{J}
.
∎

With the lemma in hand, the characterization of residual subspaces is as follows.

Proposition 12
.

A subspace
\mathcal{J}\in\mathscr{J}
is residual if and only if

\mathcal{J}=\bigcap\{p(D)\mathcal{J}:~p\text{ polynomial}\}.

Proof.

If
\sigma(D|\mathcal{J})=\emptyset
we have
(D-\lambda)\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}
for all
\lambda
and the equality in the statement holds. Conversely, if the equality holds, then
(D-\lambda I)\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}
for each
\lambda
. Suppose for a contradiction that
D-\lambda_{0}I
is not injective for some
\lambda_{0}
. Then for each
j\geq 0
there exists a nonzero
x_{j}\in\mathcal{J}
, such that
(D-\lambda_{0})^{j}x_{j}=\phi_{\lambda_{0}}
. From this it follows that
V_{\lambda_{0}}^{j}\phi_{\lambda_{0}}\in\mathcal{J}
for all
j\geq 0
, and
\mathcal{J}=C^{\infty}(D)
by Proposition
8
(ii).
∎

Note that for every subspace
\mathcal{J}\in\mathscr{J}
we can consider its
residual part

\mathcal{J}_{res}=\bigcap\{p(D)\mathcal{J}:~p\text{ polynomial}\}.

By the above proposition this is a closed residual subspace of
\mathcal{J}

Let us now turn to annihilators of residual subspaces. It turns out that these spaces can be described using the generalized Fourier transform
{\mathcal{W}}
.

To state our result we use the same notation as in Theorem
8
, that is

\mathcal{W}H=e^{-i\alpha z}\mathcal{H}(E)
, with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{J}\in\mathscr{J}
be residual. Then:

(i)

If for all
0\neq\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
,
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi
has finitely many zeros in
{\mathbb{C}}
, there exists

\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lvert\beta+\alpha\rvert\leq\tau(E)

such that

\mathcal{W}\mathcal{J}^{\perp}=\left\{pe^{i\beta z}:~p\text{ polynomial}\right\},

(ii)

If there exists
0\neq\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
such that
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi
has infinitely many zeros in
{\mathbb{C}}
then there exists a HB function
F
such that
\mathcal{H}(F)\in
Chain
(\mathcal{H}(E))
and a compact interval
J\subset[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
, of length
2\tau(F)
and with midpoint
\sigma

such that

\mathcal{{\mathcal{W}}}\mathcal{J}^{\perp}=\left\{pg:~g\in e^{i\sigma z}\mathcal{H}(F),\,p\text{ polynomial}\right\}\cup\left\{qe^{i\beta z}:\beta\in J,\,q\text{ polynomial}\right\}.

(iii)

The real number
\beta
from (i), the interval
J
as well as the set
\left\{pg:~g\in e^{i\sigma z}\mathcal{H}(F),\,p\text{ polynomial}\right\}

from (ii) are uniquely determined by the residual subspace
{\mathcal{J}}
.

(iv)

Every subspace of
(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}
of the form described in (i) or (ii) is the annihilator of a residual subspace of
C^{\infty}(D)
.

Proof.

We begin with some simple observations regarding functionals
\varphi_{\beta}\in(C^{\infty}(D))^{\prime}
with
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{\beta}(z)=e^{i\beta z}
for some fixed
\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

a) The parameter
\beta
must be real and satisfy
\beta\in[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
, since by (
26
)

{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{\beta}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}z^{j}{\mathcal{W}}x_{j}(z),

with
{\mathcal{W}}x_{j}\in{\mathcal{W}}H=e^{i\alpha z}{\mathcal{H}}(E)
.

b) For every
\beta\in[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
,
\varphi_{\beta}
has order at most one. Indeed, by a) and Proposition
5
the operator
V_{\beta}
is a bounded right inverse of
D
on
H
. If we fix
x_{0}\in H
with
{\mathcal{W}}x_{0}(0)=1
we have

{\mathcal{W}}x_{0}-e^{i\beta z}=\langle V^{*}_{\beta}x_{0},D\phi_{\overline{z}}\rangle.

Then by the density of
\{\phi_{z}:z\in{\mathbb{C}}\}
in
C^{\infty}(D)
we obtain

\varphi_{\beta}(x)=\langle x,x_{0}\rangle-\langle Dx,V^{*}_{\beta}x_{0}\rangle,\quad x\in C^{\infty}(D).

Even if this will only be used later, note that since
\|V_{\beta}\|
are uniformly bounded in
\beta
, the same holds true for the norms of
\varphi_{\beta}
as functionals on
\mathscr{D}(D)
. Now let
{\mathcal{J}}
be residual and
\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
be such that the entire function of finite exponential type
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi
has finitely many zeros in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Then

\mathcal{W}\varphi(z)=p(z)e^{i\beta z},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

where
p
is a polynomial and
\beta\in\mathbb{C}.
By a repeated application of Proposition
11
(i) together with observation a) above, it follows that

\beta\in[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
,
\varphi_{\beta}\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
and
\varphi=\varphi_{\beta}\circ p(D)
.

If all

\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
satisfy this condition, that is, under the assumption in (i),
\beta
is uniquely determined by
{\mathcal{J}}
. Indeed, if
\varphi_{\beta},\varphi\gamma\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
then
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{\beta}-{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{\gamma}
either has infinitely many zeros in
{\mathbb{C}}
, or
\beta=\gamma
. This proves (i) and the corresponding uniqueness assertion in (iii).

Let us now assume that there exists
0\neq\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
such that
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi
has infinitely many zeros. Another repeated application of Proposition
11
(i) shows that
{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
contains nonzero functionals of order zero, i.e. the closure
{\mathcal{J}}_{H}
of
{\mathcal{J}}
in
H
is strictly contained in
H
. Then

\mathcal{N}=\{{\mathcal{W}}y:~y\in\mathcal{J}_{H}^{\perp}\},

is a nonzero nearly invariant subspace of
{\mathcal{W}}H
without common zeros. By Corollary
1
we have

\mathcal{N}=e^{i\sigma z}{\mathcal{H}}(F),

with
\mathcal{H}(F)\in
Chain
(\mathcal{H}(E))
and

|\sigma+\alpha|\leq\tau(E)-\tau(F),

Thus, the first set displayed in (ii) coincides with the set of
\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
such that
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi
has infinitely many zeros and as such, it is uniquely determined by
{\mathcal{J}}
. The remaining set of functionals in
{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
is, of course, uniquely determined by
{\mathcal{J}}
as well. It consists of functionals
\varphi\in{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}
such that
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi
has finitely many zeros. By the discussion at the beginning of the proof
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi=qe^{i\beta z}
, where
q
is a polynomial and
\beta\in[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
. Note that since

is associated to

.
The proof of (i)-(iii) is now complete.

To see (iv) we need to show that any subspace described in (i) or (ii) is weak-star closed. By Proposition

we need to show that if
(\varphi_{n})
is a weak-star convergent sequence in such a subspace and also in

Assume first that for infinitely many

with

polynomials and either
\beta_{n}=\beta

is pointwise convergent to a polynomial of degree at most

The remaining case is when there exists

{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{n}
has infinitely many zeros for all

and converges weak-star to
\varphi

F
and some interval

.
Again by Proposition
10
, there exists

such that each

hence for

{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{n}(z)=f_{n}(z)\sum_{k=0}^{N}p_{kn}z^{k},

\varphi_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}p_{kn}\langle D^{k}x,y_{n}\rangle_{H},

Since

A somewhat surprising aspect is that the first set displayed in (ii) does not necessarily determine the de Branges space

uniquely. This is a more subtle matter which we now address. The issue is related to the well known fact that (see
[

]
) in de Branges spaces,
\mathscr{D}(M_{z})
, the domain of the operator

of multiplication by the independent variable may fail to be dense in the space. In order to state our result we recall the correspondence between de Branges spaces and canonical systems, respectively Hamiltonians described in Section

. Let us denote by
{\bf H}_{E}
the Hamiltonian associated to
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
that way.

Proposition 13
.

Let
E
be a regular HB function. The following are equivalent:

(i) There exist HB functions
F,G
with
{\mathcal{H}}(F),{\mathcal{H}}(G)\in\text{\rm Chain}({\mathcal{H}}(E))
with

\left\{pg:g\in\mathcal{H}(F),p\text{ polynomial}\right\}=\left\{pg:g\in\mathcal{H}(G),p\text{ polynomial}\right\},

(ii) There exists a HB function
G
with
{\mathcal{H}}(G)\in\text{\rm Chain}({\mathcal{H}}(E))
such that
\mathscr{D}(M_{z}|{\mathcal{H}}(G))
is not dense in
{\mathcal{H}}(G)
,

(iii)
{\bf H}_{E}
has singular intervals.

Proof.

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) is Lemma 7 in
[
7
]
. If (ii) holds use the axiomatic definition of de Branges spaces to conclude that
\overline{\mathscr{D}(M_{z}|{\mathcal{H}}(G))}
is a de Branges subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}(G)
, hence it belongs to
\text{Chain}({\mathcal{H}}(E))

and the equality in (i) holds with
{\mathcal{H}}(F)=\overline{\mathscr{D}(M_{z}|{\mathcal{H}}(G))}
.
Indeed, since

\frac{f-(f/g)(\lambda)g}{z-\lambda}\in{\mathcal{H}}(F),\quad f,g\in{\mathcal{H}}(G),\,g(\lambda)\neq 0,

it follows that
f,g\in\left\{pg:g\in\mathcal{H}(F),p\text{ polynomial}\right\}
which proves the claim. Conversely, if (i) holds we can assume without loss that
{\mathcal{H}}(F)\subset{\mathcal{H}}(G)
.
Let
f\in\mathcal{H}(G)
with
f\notin\mathcal{H}(F)
. By assumption there exists a polynomial
p
and
g\in\mathcal{H}(F)
, such that
f=gp
. Hence
f/p=g\in\mathcal{H}(F)
. If we choose the polynomial
p
to have minimal degree, then
zf/p\notin\mathcal{H}(F)
. Thus, if
L
denotes the backward shift on
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
,

\mathcal{H}(F)\subsetneq\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}(G):Lf\in\mathcal{H}(F)\right\}.

Using again the axiomatic characterization of de Branges spaces it follows easily that the right hand side is a de Branges subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}(E)
which we denote by
{\mathcal{H}}(G_{1})
. If
g_{0}{\mathcal{H}}(G_{1})\cap{\mathcal{H}}(F)^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
and
h\in\mathscr{D}(M_{z}|{\mathcal{H}}(G_{1}))
, then

\langle h,g_{0}\rangle=\langle Lzh,g_{0}\rangle=0,

because
Lzh\in{\mathcal{H}}(F)
. This shows that
\mathscr{D}(M_{z}|{\mathcal{H}}(G_{1}))
is not dense in
{\mathcal{H}}(G_{1})
and the proof is complete. ∎

6.5
Some special cases

It is interesting to compare the description of residual subspaces in this general context with
the classical case when
D=-i\frac{d}{dx}
on
C^{\infty}(a,b)
. Recall from
[
3
]

(see also the Introduction) that in this case the residual subspaces consist of functions which vanish on a fixed compact subinterval of
(a,b)
. The interval may reduce to a point in which case we require that all derivatives of the functions in the subspace vanish at that point as well. In order to give an interpretation of this result in the general case considered here, we need to find natural analogues to zeros of
C^{\infty}-
functions.
Theorem
12
suggest the following two alternatives:

1)
x\in C^{\infty}(D)
”vanishes” at
\beta\in[-\alpha-\tau(E),-\alpha+\tau(E)]
if
\varphi_{\beta}(x)=0
, or equivalently, by Lemma
4
,

. Recall that
\varphi_{\beta}
is defined by
{\mathcal{W}}\varphi_{\beta}(z)=e^{i\beta z}
.

2)

”vanishes” on the compact interval

if for all

,
D^{n}x
belongs to the
V_{\beta}-
invariant subspace

, where
\sigma
is the midpoint of

,
2\tau(F)
is its length and
\beta
is an arbitrary point in

The second alternative is not ambiguous by the uniqueness statement in Theorem

(iii). However, the condition is
less intuitive than, for example, the obvious generalization of 1):

2’)

”vanishes” on the compact interval

if

The next result clarifies completely the condition 2’) in the most general case.

Theorem 13
.

Let

{\mathcal{J}}_{0}=\bigcap_{\beta\in J}V_{\beta}C^{\infty}(D),

Proof.

It will be sufficient to prove that

\beta\in I

By Theorem
11

is contained in the annihilator of any residual

with residual interval

Let us now turn to the claims. Since
