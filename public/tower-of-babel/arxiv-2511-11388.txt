Robust inverse material design with physical guarantees using the Voigt–Reuss Net Publication type: Original article
Sanath Keshav, Felix Fritzen, Keshav S, Fritzen F

Abstract
We propose a spectrally normalized surrogate model for forward and inverse mechanical homogenization that guarantees physical admissibility of effective stiffness tensors throughout training, inference, and optimization. Building on classical Voigt-Reuss bounds, we construct a universal reparameterization of symmetric positive definite constitutive tensors: the difference between the Voigt and Reuss bounds is factorized into a Cholesky-like operator, and each effective tensor is represented by a dimensionless, symmetric positive semi-definite matrix with spectrum confined to the unit interval. Learning is performed in this bounded spectral space, and the inverse mapping ensures that all predictions are symmetric positive definite and lie between the Voigt and Reuss bounds in the Löwner sense. We first apply this framework to 3D linear elasticity using an open dataset of stochastic biphasic microstructures. A fully connected Voigt-Reuss net is trained on over 750 750 K FFT-based homogenization labels using 236 isotropic morphological descriptors and three non-dimensional elastic contrast parameters. Because the inputs are rotationally invariant, the surrogate recovers the isotropic projection of the effective stiffness tensor with near-perfect fidelity: isotropy-related components achieve R 2 > 0.99 R^{2}>0.99 , while anisotropy-revealing entries are correctly identified as unpredictable. At the tensor level, the relative Frobenius error has a median of ≈ 1.7 % \approx 1.7\% and mean ≈ 3.4 % \approx 3.4\% across train/validation/test, operating close to the irreducible error floor set by isotropic projection and far below classical Hill-type estimates. We then couple spectral normalization with a differentiable microstructure renderer and a convolutional network for 2D plane-strain elasticity of highly anisotropic, high-contrast biphasic composites generated by thresholded trigonometric fields. The resulting end-to-end differentiable convolutional Voigt-Reuss net maps amplitude matrices and thresholds to effective stiffness tensors, achieving R 2 > 0.99 R^{2}>0.99 on all components and subpercent Voigt-Reuss normalized losses, while remaining strictly within the bounds. The surrogate captures percolation-induced eigenvalue jumps along threshold sweeps and generalizes robustly to out-of-distribution, diffusion-generated microstructures. Treating the parametric microstructure as design variables, we solve inverse problems via batched, first-order optimization using a single surrogate, matching target tensors with relative errors of only a few percent and producing diverse near-optimal designs. Overall, the Voigt-Reuss net provides a physically grounded surrogate for multiscale elasticity that unifies accurate forward prediction with large-batch, constraint-consistent inverse materials design. The approach is generic to elliptic problems and readily extensible to other transport and coupled-physics settings where rigorous bounds are available.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

The mechanical response of heterogeneous materials is governed by a subtle interplay between phase properties, volume fractions, and microstructural geometry across multiple scales.
From fiber-reinforced polymers to architected lattices and metamaterials, designers are increasingly seeking to engineer microstructures to tailor effective stiffness, anisotropy, and load transfer pathways for specific applications.
Classical homogenization theory provides a rigorous framework for relating microstructure and local constitutive laws to effective properties, e.g., via asymptotic expansions, variational principles, and empirical estimates and bounds
[
42
,
28
,
47
,
30
]
.
Yet, the corresponding
G-closure

[
11
,
2
]
, i.e., the set of all effective tensors realizable by mixing given phases in all admissible microgeometries, is typically high-dimensional, non-convex, and analytically intractable.

Computational homogenization and direct numerical simulation have become the workhorses to explore the material design space in practice.
Finite element (FE) and FFT-based schemes
[
29
,
24
,
40
,
19
]
allow one to solve corrector problems on intricate unit cells and extract homogenized tensors.
These methods are robust and systematically improvable, but they are also computationally expensive:
A single 3D simulation on a
192^{3}
voxel grid with strong phase contrast involves
\approx
21 million degrees of freedom. Therefore, it can already be costly, and design tasks that require exploring tens of thousands of candidate microstructures or solving nested optimization problems are often prohibitive if each evaluation relies on high-fidelity simulation. More strikingly, differentiating these results with respect to design parameters is challenging, if not impossible, in many situations.
These bottlenecks have motivated a surge of interest in surrogate models that learn microstructure property relationships from data and can be queried at negligible cost once trained.

1.2
Machine learned effective property forecasting

Machine-learning-based surrogates for homogenization are now widely explored.
Approaches range from regression on hand-crafted descriptors (e.g., morphological statistics, correlation measures, or spectral features)
[
26
,
35
,
27
]
to deep convolutional networks ingesting microstructure images or volumes directly
[
1
,
32
,
17
]
.
They have been used to predict scalar effective properties
[
35
]
, full elasticity tensors
[
12
]
, and even local fields
[
7
,
31
]
.
In parallel, data-driven paradigms in computational mechanics
[
4
,
25
,
22
]
have demonstrated that learned constitutive maps can replace or augment traditional phenomenological models, enabling faster simulations and design. Furthermore, these works have demonstrated the need to incorporate physical constraints into the surrogates to leverage data efficiency, robustness, and out-of-data prediction accuracy.
On the design side, several works now combine surrogates with generative models
[
5
]
, optimization
[
37
,
33
,
39
]
, learned inverse mappings
[
23
]
, or autoencoders
[
46
]
to tackle inverse problems, where one seeks microstructures or process parameters that achieve targeted effective responses.

Despite these advances, most existing surrogates for effective tensors suffer from a critical limitation: they treat the components of the effective tensor as generic regression targets, with physical constraints imposed (if at all) through post-processing or soft penalization.
In linear elasticity, the effective stiffness tensor must be symmetric, positive definite, and satisfy basic variational bounds, most notably the first-order Voigt and Reuss bounds, which constrain the admissible tensors in the Löwner sense
[
30
]
.
Naive regression does not respect such structural constraints: predicted tensors can be indefinite, violate ellipticity, or lie outside the Voigt-Reuss envelope, especially when queried far from the training distribution.
Such violations are more than just aesthetic flaws: they can destabilize downstream FE simulations, break thermodynamic consistency, and render inverse-design loops unreliable.
Common ad hoc remedies include projecting predictions onto the cone of symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices, learning a Cholesky decomposition
[
45
]
, clamping eigenvalues, or adding penalty terms to the loss in the spirit of physics-infused neural networks (PINNs
[
18
]
). None of these approaches guarantees adherence to Voigt-Reuss bounds, and often this leads to ill-conditioned optimization landscapes.

At the same time, homogenization theory provides a much richer structure than simple SPD constraints.
For a given set of phase tensors and volume fractions, the effective tensor must lie in the intersection of the SPD cone with a set of variational bounds such as the classical Voigt and Reuss bounds
[
43
,
38
,
44
]
. Under some assumptions, the Hashin-Shtrikman bounds
[
14
]
, and more refined and problem-specific estimates
[
10
,
41
]
can apply, as well.
In Löwner order, the basic Voigt-Reuss inequality states that the effective tensor is bounded above and below by those of the weighted arithmetic and harmonic averages, respectively.
Geometrically, this defines a compact spectral simplex in the space of SPD matrices where the difference between the Voigt and Reuss bounds is positive semi-definite and encodes the admissible variation of effective responses.
Any surrogate used for forward prediction or inverse design
must
remain in the thereby defined feasible set. In particular, any configurations explored along optimization trajectories must be inside this envelope.

Another source of difficulty is the intrinsic complexity of the G-closure, especially for high-contrast and anisotropic composites.
For strongly heterogeneous materials, small changes in geometry can cause large, non-smooth changes in effective response, particularly near percolation thresholds or when load-bearing paths reconfigure
[
42
,
8
]
.
Effective stiffness eigenvalues can exhibit jumps as microstructure parameters (e.g., volume fraction, connectivity, or orientation) cross critical values.
Mean-field approaches, such as e.g., the Mori-Tanaka scheme
[
30
]
and self-consistent schemes, which implicitly assume dilute or weakly interacting inclusions, cannot capture these transitions.
Standard neural surrogates can approximate them, but when unconstrained, they may also generate unphysical responses, e.g., in poorly sampled regions of the design space.
Robust inverse design thus demands surrogates that are both expressive enough to capture non-smooth, geometry-driven behavior and structured enough to enforce physical admissibility by construction.

In a recent work
[
20
]
, we proposed a spectral normalization framework for learning effective conductivities resulting from the solutions of elliptic problems.
The core idea is to exploit the Voigt-Reuss gap as a natural scaling for the admissible response and to reparameterize the effective tensor via a normalized SPD matrix with spectrum confined to the unit interval.
Learning is then carried out in the bounded unit-cube space of eigenvalues and orthogonal factors of the normalized tensor. The reconstruction of the constitutive tensor based on the normalized representation automatically enforces symmetry, positive definiteness, and Voigt-Reuss admissibility.
That prior study focused on scalar-valued elliptic problems such as thermal conduction.

1.3
Objectives and scope.

In this paper, we generalize the Voigt-Reuss net framework to linear elasticity and to demonstrate how a spectrally normalized surrogate can be used not only for fast, physically admissible forward prediction of effective stiffness tensors, but also as a robust engine for gradient-based inverse material design. Building on our earlier study for scalar elliptic problems
[
20
]
, we first formalize a spectral normalization for generic symmetric positive definite constitutive tensors (of arbitrary order), including 3D small-strain elasticity as a special case. Any effective tensor is mapped through a Cholesky-like factor of the Voigt-Reuss difference into a dimensionless matrix with spectrum in
[0,1]
, yielding a task-agnostic target space with scale-free, well-conditioned losses and the guarantee that, after inverse mapping, all surrogate predictions are symmetric positive definite and confined to the Voigt-Reuss envelope.

Within this general framework, we consider two representative elasticity settings. First, we construct a Voigt-Reuss net for 3D biphasic composites based on the open microstructure dataset
[
36
,
35
]
, where each sample is described by isotropic averaged morphology descriptors and non-dimensional phase contrast parameters. This case probes the performance of spectral normalization in a purely descriptor-based, rotationally invariant forward problem. Second, we couple spectral normalization with a differentiable microstructure renderer and a convolutional neural network for 2D plane-strain composites generated from thresholded trigonometric fields
[
9
,
8
]
. In this setting, the surrogate operates directly on parametric microstructure representations, enabling end-to-end differentiability of the map from design parameters to effective stiffness and, consequently, first-order inverse design with respect to full target tensors or tensor-valued functionals. Throughout, we restrict attention to linear elastostatics, but emphasize that the proposed spectral normalization is generic and applicable to other elliptic operators (e.g., thermal conductivity, diffusion, poroelasticity) and further.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
we recall the Voigt-Reuss bounds for elliptic problems and derive the associated spectral normalization, including its matrix formulation and numerical implementation.
Section

3.3
discusses how this normalization can be combined with generic machine-learning models.
Section

4
presents the 3D mechanical homogenization study based on descriptor inputs, while
Section

5
introduces the differentiable microstructure renderer, the convolutional Voigt-Reuss net, and a series of forward and inverse design experiments for highly anisotropic, high-contrast 2D composites. The conclusions summarize the main findings and outline opportunities for extending Voigt-Reuss net to richer G-closure problems and multi-physics design.

Crucially, because the network was trained on a large and diverse dataset that includes real-world composite parameter ranges, it is well positioned to handle practical engineering scenarios where bulk and shear moduli vary within similar bounds.

5
Application to highly anisotropic and high contrast 2D composites

In this section, we describe the application of the Voigt-Reuss net to a 2D mechanical problem, specifically the prediction of the effective elasticity tensor
\underline{\underline{\overline{C}}}\in Sym_{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
of periodic, biphasic composites under plane strain.
The data was generated and obtained from a prior study
[
8
]
, which constructed a large database of unit cells
\Omega=[-0.5,0.5]\times[-0.5,0.5]\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and their homogenized elastic tensors, particularly targeting structured materials with extreme elastic anisotropy.
The data are generated with a previously documented procedure to sample large families of periodic, single-scale unit cells composed of two phases that can realize a wide range of anisotropic effective elastic tensors
[
9
]
.
The construction is based on thresholded trigonometric fields with few spatial modes, which (i) guarantees periodicity by design, (ii) offers direct control over geometric symmetry through simple algebraic constraints on amplitudes, and (iii) yields a monotonic handle on the volume fraction over a wide range (1–99% volume fraction of phase 1; see below for details) through the threshold parameter
\tau\in[0,1]
.

Figure 7
:
Schematic of the procedure to generate periodic biphasic unit cells from thresholded trigonometric fields
[
8
]

Let
\{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the orthonormal basis vectors of the 2D unit cell (
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\cdot{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
).
For two odd integers
M_{1},M_{2}\in\{1,3,5,\dots\}
(the numbers of modes in the
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}
directions, respectively), define the finite index sets

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{1}=\Big\{-\tfrac{M_{1}-1}{2},\ldots,0,\ldots,\tfrac{M_{1}-1}{2}\Big\},\qquad{\cal I}_{2}=\Big\{-\tfrac{M_{2}-1}{2},\ldots,0,\ldots,\tfrac{M_{2}-1}{2}\Big\}.

(24)

Given an amplitude matrix
\underline{\underline{A}}=\big(A_{mn}\big)_{m\in{\cal I}_{1},\;n\in{\cal I}_{2}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M_{1}\times M_{2}}
with entries sampled independently and identically from a uniform distribution
A_{mn}\sim{\cal U}[-1,1]
, we form the periodic field
\psi({\boldsymbol{x}};A)
,

\displaystyle\psi({\boldsymbol{x}};A)=\sum_{m\in{\cal I}_{1}}\sum_{n\in{\cal I}_{2}}A_{mn}\;\cos\bigl(2\pi\;(m\;x_{1}+n\;x_{2})\bigr),\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega:=\bigl\{\;x_{1}\;{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}+x_{2}\;{\boldsymbol{e}}_{2}\;\big|\;x_{1},x_{2}\in\big[-\frac{1}{2},\;\frac{1}{2}\big)\;\bigr\}.

(25)

We then apply range normalization on
\psi({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}})
such that the rescaled field
\widetilde{\psi}({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}})\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega}\psi({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}}),\quad\psi_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega}\psi({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}}),\quad\widetilde{\psi}({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}})=\frac{\psi({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}})-\psi_{\mathrm{min}}}{\psi_{\mathrm{max}}-\psi_{\mathrm{min}}}\in[0,1].

(26)

For a threshold
\tau\in[0,1]
, the binary indicator
\chi
of the microstructure is

\displaystyle\chi({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)\;=\;1_{\{\widetilde{\psi}({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}})\geq\tau\}},\qquad c_{1}(\tau;\underline{\underline{A}})\;=\;\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\chi({\boldsymbol{x}};\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)\;\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},\qquad c_{0}(\tau;\underline{\underline{A}})\;=\;1-c_{1}(\tau;\underline{\underline{A}}).

(27)

By construction
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
vary monotonically with
\tau
, that is,
c_{0}(\tau)\leq c_{0}(\tau+\delta)
for
\delta>0
, and the inverse inequality for
c_{1}
holds by construction.
Symmetry classes of the microstructure can be enforced by simple constraints on
\underline{\underline{A}}
(see
fig.

8
).

\underline{\overline{\sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\sigma}_{11}\\
\overline{\sigma}_{22}\\
\sqrt{2}\;\overline{\sigma}_{12}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{C}_{1111}&\overline{C}_{1122}&\sqrt{2}\;\overline{C}_{1112}\\
\overline{C}_{2211}&\overline{C}_{2222}&\sqrt{2}\;\overline{C}_{2212}\\
\sqrt{2}\;\overline{C}_{1211}&\sqrt{2}\;\overline{C}_{1222}&2\;\overline{C}_{1212}\\
\end{bmatrix}:\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\varepsilon}_{11}\\
\overline{\varepsilon}_{22}\\
\sqrt{2}\;\overline{\varepsilon}_{12}\end{bmatrix}=\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}:\underline{\overline{\varepsilon}}.

(28)

The homogenized elasticity tensor
\underline{\underline{\overline{C}}}
is obtained by a standard two-scale first-order computational homogenization, i.e., by solving the corrector problems for three orthonormal macroscopic loadings.
Thermodynamic stability requires
\overline{C}_{1111},\overline{C}_{2222},\overline{C}_{1212}>0

\overline{C}_{1122},\overline{C}_{1112},\overline{C}_{2212}
may be negative. Symmetry and positive definiteness of the plane strain stiffness
\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}
(
28

3.2
; also
[
20
]
) automatically.
In the study
[

]

\underline{\underline{A}}
for different values of

and by sweeping the threshold
\tau
; see
[
8
]
for details.
In summary, the dataset construction involves:

•

Mode grids.
Five sets of spatial modes
(M_{1}\times M_{2})\in{\cal P}
, with
{\cal P}=\big\{\;3\times 3,\;5\times 5,\;7\times 7,\;9\times 9,\;11\times 11\;\big\}
.

•

For each mode set

,

\underline{\underline{A}}^{(p,i)}
with entries

The prediction of effective elastic properties for periodic composites is fundamentally constrained by the classical G-closure problem. The G-closure describes the set of all possible effective elasticity tensors that can be realized by mixing two given phases in all possible microgeometries, subject to prescribed volume fractions and symmetry constraints. For high-contrast composites, where the elastic moduli of the two phases differ by several orders of magnitude, the G-closure set becomes extremely large and nontrivial. The variability of the microstructures in this dataset is substantial: the use of random trigonometric fields with varying numbers of modes and amplitude matrices generates a vast diversity of geometries, ranging from nearly isotropic to highly anisotropic and from simple to complex topologies. This diversity is further amplified by sweeping the threshold parameter
\tau
, which tunes the volume fraction and can induce topological transitions in the microstructure (see
fig.

9
). These factors render the learning problem particularly challenging. First, the mapping from microstructure to effective tensor is highly nonlinear and nonlocal: small changes in geometry can lead to large changes in the effective response, especially near percolation thresholds or in the high-contrast regime. Second, the admissible set of effective tensors (the G-closure) is a highly complicated set of symmetric positive definite matrices, strictly bounded by the upper Voigt and lower Reuss bounds. Third, the presence of extreme anisotropy and high contrast means that the model must generalize across a wide range of physical behaviors, including cases where subtle geometric features dominate the effective response. Consequently, accurately predicting the effective elasticity tensor in this space requires a surrogate model that is highly expressive and physically constrained, motivating the use of spectral normalization and the proposed generalization of the Voigt-Reuss net
[
20
]
for mechanical problems.

5.1
Neural surrogate architecture

Next, we describe the end-to-end differentiable surrogate used to map the parametric unit cell specification
(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)
to the effective plane-strain elasticity tensor
\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}
.
The model has three stages:

(i)

A fixed-shape embedding
{\cal T}
that pads amplitude grids of various mode sets in
\mathcal{P}
into a common
K\times K
hyper mode set;
Let the mode grid of a sample be
\underline{\underline{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, where
m,n\in\{3,5,7,9,11\}
.
We define a centered embedding into
\underline{\underline{A}}^{\urcorner}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
with

chosen such that

for all possible
m,n
(here,
K=11

\displaystyle{\cal T}:\ \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{K\times K},\quad\underline{\underline{A}}^{\urcorner}_{ij}\;=\;\begin{cases}\underline{\underline{A}}_{\;i-i_{0},\;j-j_{0}},&i_{0}\leq i<i_{0}+m,\ \ j_{0}\leq j<j_{0}+n,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\text{where }\begin{aligned} i_{0}=\frac{(K-m)}{2},\\[2.0pt]
j_{0}=\frac{(K-n)}{2}.\end{aligned}

(29)

Thus, every sample is represented by a fixed
K\times K
matrix
\underline{\underline{A}}^{\urcorner}
, enabling batched GPU training while preserving the spectral layout (modes remain centered).

(ii)

A differentiable microstructure renderer

that synthesizes a soft microstructure from
\underline{\mu}=(\underline{\underline{A}}^{\urcorner},\tau)
;
Given the grid resolution
(N_{x_{1}},N_{x_{2}})=(100,100)

\underline{\underline{\psi}}(x_{1},x_{2};\underline{\underline{A}}^{\urcorner})\;=\;\sum_{p=-(K-1)/2}^{(K-1)/2}\sum_{q=-(K-1)/2}^{(K-1)/2}{A}^{\urcorner}_{pq}\;\cos\big(2\pi(px_{1}+qx_{2})\big),\qquad(x_{1},x_{2})\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]^{2}\cap\Pi_{N_{x_{1}},N_{x_{2}}},

(30)

where

26

soft

(iii)

A regressor predicting the normalized degrees of freedom
\widehat{\underline{\xi}}
(see
section

3.2
).
Let the rendered microstructure be a monochrome image
\underline{\underline{\chi}}\in[0,1]^{N_{x_{1}}\times N_{x_{2}}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

\underline{s}=[c_{0},\tau]^{\top}\in[0,1]^{2}

\underline{\underline{\chi}}
and
\tau
is the threshold used.
A convolutional trunk
{\cal C}_{\theta}
extracts an image descriptor
\underline{h}={\cal C}_{\theta}(\underline{\underline{\chi}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) with a sigmoid head

then regresses the normalized target from the concatenation

\underline{\widehat{\xi}}={\cal M}_{\theta}\big([\underline{h};\underline{s}]\big)=[\widehat{\underline{\xi}}_{\lambda},\widehat{\underline{\xi}}_{\mathrm{q}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Here,
\underline{\xi}_{\lambda}\in(0,1)^{3}
are the normalized eigenvalues and
\underline{\xi}_{\mathrm{q}}\in(0,1)^{3}

This is followed by the unique and deterministic spectral inverse transform

to

The final surrogate can be written as the composition,

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\theta}\;\circ\;{\cal C}_{\theta}\;\circ\;{\cal R}\;\circ\;{\cal T}:\ (\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)\mapsto\widehat{\underline{\xi}}\in(0,1)^{6},

(32)

where subscript

This construction ensures automatic feature detection from microstructure images, as well as end-to-end differentiability in

Furthermore, this construction can be straightforwardly extended to 3D by embedding 3D amplitude grids, rendering volumetric microstructures via trigonometric fields, and applying a 3D convolutional regressor, enabling end-to-end differentiable surrogates for 3D systems. However, the computational requirement for the 3D setting will increase considerably compared to the 2D setting.

5.2
Numerical experiments and results

The convolutional trunk
{\cal C}_{\theta}
is a deep residual CNN with periodic convolutions and four stages. Each stage consists of a ResidualBlock with two periodic convolutional layers with increasing kernel sizes (
k_{1}=3
,
k_{2}=5
,
k_{3}=7
,
k_{4}=9
), batch normalization, and ReLU activations, followed by
2\times 2

increases as
18\to 24\to 30\to 36
across the four blocks, which implies

. After the final block, the feature map is flattened and concatenated with the auxiliary scalars
[c_{0},\tau]
. This is passed to a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with five hidden layers of sizes 256, 128, 64, 64, and 64, each with batch normalization and a mixed activation (
[SELU, Tanh, Sigmoid, Identity]
), and a final sigmoid-activated output layer of dimension 6. The model uses periodic convolutions throughout to preserve microstructure periodicity. Training uses the
AdamW
optimizer with an initial learning rate of
10^{-1}
, and a
ReduceLROnPlateau
scheduler (factor 0.5, patience 50 epochs).
The loss function is defined as the mean Voigt-Reuss normalized error
\phi
(
eq.

18
) over each batch,

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{batch}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{batch}}}\phi(\underline{\underline{\widetilde{{C}}}}^{(i)},\widehat{\underline{\underline{\widetilde{{C}}}}}^{(i)}),

where

is the batch size,
\underline{\underline{\widetilde{{C}}}}^{(i)}
is the true normalized stiffness tensor, and
\widehat{\underline{\underline{\widetilde{{C}}}}}^{(i)}
is the predicted tensor for sample

Overall, Voigt-Reuss net accurately captures the geometry-controlled locally non-smooth dependence of the effective stiffness on the threshold, including (multiple) percolation-induced spectral jumps, while remaining strictly admissible. In particular, the number of such spectral jumps varies among the microstructures studied; that is, it is not explicitly considered in the trained Voigt-Reuss net, but rather implicitly through the data.
In contrast, Mori-Tanaka estimates, which hinge on dilute, non-interacting inclusion assumptions and spherical Eshelby kernels, cannot track topology-driven anisotropy and fail as connectivity grows.
The differentiable chain
(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)\to\widehat{\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}}
provides reliable gradients, except for genuine topological events, which appear as informative spikes that could be exploited in inverse design (e.g., to seek or avoid connectivity transitions).

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\widehat{\overline{C}}}}\;(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)={\cal S}^{-1}\left({\cal M}_{\theta}\big({\cal C}_{\theta}({\cal R}({\cal T}(\underline{\underline{A}}),\tau))\big)\right),

\displaystyle{\cal J}(\underline{\underline{\widehat{\overline{C}}}})=\min_{\underline{\underline{A}},\;\tau\in(0,1)}{\cal E}_{\mathsf{F}}\big(\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}^{\star},\widehat{\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}}\;(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)\big).

6
Conclusions

6.1
Summary

Across all settings, and in both 2D and 3D, we demonstrated that spectral normalization realized as the major building block of the Voigt-Reuss net provides a principled scaffold for learning effective constitutive tensors that are guaranteed to be symmetric positive definite and to lie between Voigt and Reuss bounds in the Löwner sense. By mapping every sample into a bounded, unit-cube target space, our surrogates possess robust training dynamics, well-conditioned, scale-free losses, and return physically admissible predictions by construction.

We formalized a universal reparameterization introduced in
[
20
]
and extended it to mechanical homogenization problems.
The learning objective leads to a task-agnostic loss
\phi(\underline{\underline{\widetilde{Y}}},\widehat{\underline{\underline{\widetilde{Y}}}})\in[0,1]
that directly measures errors relative to the admissible Voigt-Reuss gap, decoupling learning from units and improving numerical stability.

In the case of 3D mechanical homogenization as a forward problem, we trained a Voigt-Reuss net surrogate using 236 isotropic microstructure descriptors from
[
36
,
35
]
along with three non-dimensional phase parameters
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{3})
, on
>1.18
Mio high-fidelity DNS labels of effective elasticity tensors.
Because the inputs are rotationally invariant, the surrogate recovers the isotropic projection of
\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}
nearly perfectly, while anisotropy-revealing entries remain unpredictable:
the components related to isotropy attain
R^{2}\geq 0.998
, and the remaining components show
R^{2}\approx 0
.
At the tensor level, histograms of the relative Frobenius error of the training, validation, and testing datasets overlap tightly (mean
\approx 3.4\%
, median
\approx 1.7\%
), indicating strong generalization across microstructures and contrasts.
ECDFs reveal that the isotropic projection forms a hard lower floor (median
1.03\%
), and Voigt-Reuss net sits close to the floor (median
1.71\%
), outperforming the Hill average (median
29.12\%
) by far.
Real composite pairs (steel-Al, Al-SiC, epoxy-glass, etc.) lie squarely in the low-error regime spanned by our extensive sampling.

In the context of the 2D mechanical homogenization (forward and inverse), we introduced an end-to-end differentiable renderer for parameterized thresholded trigonometric fields, enabling a hybrid convolutional and multi-layer perceptron surrogate that ingests
(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)
and outputs a spectrally admissible
\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}
.
Forward prediction reaches
R^{2}>0.99
across all components of the elasticity tensor and subpercent Voigt-Reuss normalized losses, and the model remains accurate with modest errors across volume fractions and respects the Voigt-Reuss bounds inherently.
Crucially, the differentiable chain
(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)\mapsto\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}
exposes extremely informative gradients and resolves non-smooth percolation events (eigenvalue jumps) along threshold sweeps seamlessly, while remaining strictly admissible.
Leveraging these properties, we performed inverse design to target full tensors and functionals (e.g., maximizing shear-normal coupling), recovering classical optima (e.g.,
45^{\circ}
laminates at
c_{0}\!\approx\!0.5
) and diverse near-optimal candidates in a single batched optimization run.
This capability is particularly remarkable: by specifying a single target effective stiffness tensor
\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}^{\star}
, the optimizer can generate a multitude of structurally distinct microstructures that achieve nearly identical mechanical properties, with relative Frobenius errors as low as a few percent.
This many-to-one mapping underscores the richness of the G-closure set, where multiple geometric realizations can produce equivalent effective responses, offering designers flexibility in selecting structures based on additional criteria such as manufacturability, weight, or connectivity.
Moreover, the batched optimization approach enables simultaneous exploration of hundreds of candidates, ensuring diversity while guaranteeing that every proposed design lies within the physically admissible Voigt-Reuss bounds, thus avoiding infeasible or unstable configurations. It should also be noted that the same machinery supports inverse tasks without auxiliary networks, enabling large-batch, first-order exploration of G-closure subsets while remaining inside the Voigt-Reuss bounds at every iterate.

Overall, Voigt-Reuss net delivers accurate, admissible, and data-efficient surrogates for multiscale materials, unifying forward prediction and gradient-based design under a single, physically grounded normalization.

6.2
Discussion

The results in 3D and 2D together highlight two complementary roles of the Voigt-Reuss net framework.
First, when coupled with hand-crafted descriptors in 3D, it acts as a physically constrained surrogate for effective stiffness that is robust to large contrast variation but fundamentally limited by the information content of the input features (here: isotropic descriptors).
Second, in the 2D setting with a differentiable renderer, it becomes a fully image-based, end-to-end differentiable map from parametric microstructure generators
(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)
to effective tensors, opening the door to gradient-based inverse design.
Both use cases share the same spectral normalization backbone and loss, suggesting that the methodology is not tied to any particular architecture but rather to the Löwner-bounded reparameterization of constitutive tensors.

The 3D study illustrates a key structural limitation that is often implicit in data-driven homogenization: when the feature map is invariant under
SO(3)
, no surrogate can recover orientation-dependent anisotropy.
In our case, the descriptors
[
35
]
encode only isotropy-averaged information, so the model can learn at best the isotropic projection
\overline{\underline{\underline{C}}}_{\mathrm{iso}}
of the effective stiffness.
The sharp split in the
R^{2}
-matrix, near-perfect prediction for isotropic components, and
R^{2}\approx 0
for anisotropy-revealing entries is not a failure of the network but a faithful reflection of the available input information.
The ECDF analysis makes this explicit: the isotropic projection error forms a hard lower floor for any surrogate using these descriptors, and Voigt-Reuss net operates close to that floor.
This has two practical consequences.
First, it provides a diagnostic for feature sufficiency: if anisotropic components remain unpredictable while isotropic ones are accurate, one should enrich the descriptor set (e.g., directional correlations, band features, Minkowski tensors, or full CNN-based encoders).
Second, even in such limited regimes, spectral normalization still guarantees admissible outputs and stable training, making it a safe drop-in replacement for unconstrained regressors.

The 2D experiments, by contrast, operate with a much richer generative parameterization of microstructures via thresholded trigonometric fields.
Here, the bottleneck is no longer missing directional information but the size and diversity of the G-closure subset explored.
Embedding the amplitude matrix into a fixed hyper-grid, rendering via a differentiable trigonometric field, and regressing the normalized spectral degrees of freedom together yield a surrogate that is sensitive to connectivity, topology changes, and extreme anisotropy, yet remains numerically stable.
The threshold-sweep study is particularly interesting: Voigt-Reuss net tracks sharp eigenvalue jumps at percolation events and exposes these transitions through spikes in
\partial\|\underline{\underline{\widehat{\overline{C}}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}/\partial\tau
, while classical Mori-Tanaka estimates deteriorate as their dilute, non-interacting assumptions are violated.
This combination of geometric sensitivity and spectral admissibility is precisely what is needed for reliable inverse design in high-contrast media. Also, the straightforward gradient evaluation facilitates the detection of such jumps.

Against this backdrop, the inverse-design studies highlight the distinctive benefits of a spectrally normalized surrogate:
(i)
Robust gradients.
All outputs live in
[0,1]^{m}
, and the renderer
{\cal R}
is smooth in
(\underline{\underline{A}},\tau)
; hence, gradients remain well behaved even at extreme phase contrasts, enabling stable first-order optimization without manual rescaling or ad-hoc clipping.
(ii)
Batched optimization.
Optimizing hundreds of candidates in one GPU pass provides a diverse family of designs (useful for screening, multi-criteria trade-offs, or robustness analysis) essentially at the cost of a ”single run”.
(iii)
Single model, forward and backward.
Unlike frameworks that pair a forward surrogate with a separate inverse network or require differentiable simulators, our pipeline uses one spectrally normalized surrogate both for prediction and for backpropagating arbitrary differentiable objectives (tensor matching, eigenvalue ratios, directional moduli, compliance measures, volume constraints, etc.).
(iv)
Guaranteed admissibility.
Every iterate and every final design respects Voigt-Reuss bounds in the Löwner sense; the optimizer never leaves the feasible spectral simplex, avoiding unphysical attractors that commonly plague unconstrained neural inverse design.
(v)
Empirical coverage of G-closure.
The cosine-threshold parameterization spans a rich, multimodal subset of the G-closure.
Empirically, we recover both classical optima (e.g.,
45^{\circ}
laminates at
c_{0}\approx 0.5
for maximal shear-normal coupling) and less obvious patterns that realize nearly identical effective property tensors, directly reflecting the intrinsic non-uniqueness of microstructure-to-property mappings.

At a higher level, the framework can be viewed as a “spectral layer” that decouples physical admissibility from architectural choice.
Any regressor that produces normalized spectral parameters
\underline{\widehat{\xi}}
can be wrapped by
{\cal S}^{-1}
to yield outputs that are symmetric positive definite and automatically bounded by Voigt and Reuss.
This separation of concerns should generalize beyond small-strain elasticity to other linear elliptic operators, and to multi-property surrogates where several coupled tensors must remain jointly admissible.
Similarly, the 2D image-based architecture is not unique: one could replace the cosine renderer with voxelized tomography, graph-based generators, or diffusion models, while still leveraging the same spectral normalization for the output layer.

There are, however, clear limitations and avenues for improvement.
In 3D, the present results are intentionally constrained by isotropic descriptors; to capture full anisotropy, one must either (i) augment the feature set with orientation-sensitive statistics or (ii) transition to 3D CNNs operating directly on voxelized microstructures, at a much higher computational cost.
In 2D, the cosine-threshold family, while expressive, samples only a subset of all possible microstructures; enriching the generator (e.g., multi-scale fields, hierarchical patterns, or manufacturing-aware constraints) would expand the reachable portion of the G-closure.
Moreover, the current formulation is deterministic: incorporating uncertainty quantification (e.g., Bayesian surrogates or ensemble methods) would allow one to propagate microstructural variability and quantify confidence in inverse designs, which is essential for safety-critical applications.

Finally, the combination of spectral normalization and end-to-end differentiability makes Voigt-Reuss net compatible with existing structural and topology-optimization workflows.
Because the surrogate provides fast, differentiable, and physically admissible predictions, it can act as a “constitutive oracle” inside larger multi-scale loops, enabling gradient-based co-design of architecture and material.
The same machinery supports inverse tasks without auxiliary networks, enabling large-batch, first-order exploration of G-closure subsets while staying inside Voigt-Reuss bounds at every iterate.
In this sense, Voigt-Reuss net offers a unifying, physics-grounded interface between classical homogenization theory and modern deep-learning-based materials design.
