The effects of mechanical ventilation during v-a ecmo support: a systematic review
Authors: Ilaria Protti, Nora Di Tomasso, Paolo Meani, Maarten Ter Horst, Giacomo Grasselli, Nicole P. Juffermans, Annemijn H. Jonkman, Christiaan L. Meuwese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMechanical ventilation is routinely used during veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO), but its role in influencing hemodynamics and supporting cardiac recovery remains poorly understood. This systematic review aims to summarize and describe studies that investigated the effects of mechanical ventilation settings and adjunctive respiratory interventions on cardiac function, recovery, and clinical outcomes in V-A ECMO patients.MethodsA systematic search in literature up to June 6th 2025 was conducted to identify studies assessing the effects of ventilation strategies or hypoxemia-rescue therapies on physiological and clinical outcomes in adults supported by V-A ECMO or in animal V-A ECMO models. Because of anticipated heterogeneity across studies, we refrained from pooling of results.ResultsOut of 5,750 records screened, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria (10 clinical, 1 experimental, 1 computational). Four studies in humans observed that lower driving and peak inspiratory pressures, along with reduced respiratory rates, were associated with improved survival in V-A ECMO patients. One study found that moderate positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) conveyed protective effects on the lungs without compromising cardiac function, whereas excessive PEEP impaired systolic function without causing systemic hypotension. A computational study suggested that higher intrathoracic pressures may have cardioprotective effects by unloading the left ventricle and reducing myocardial oxygen demand. Very limited studies have explored adjunctive ventilation strategies, including inhaled nitric oxide and defined oxygenation targets during V-A ECMO, exhibiting at least a moderate risk of bias.ConclusionsEvidence on the effects of mechanical ventilation during V-A ECMO on cardiac function, recovery and clinical outcomes is limited, heterogeneous, and at considerable risk of bias. Well-designed mechanistic and interventional studies are needed to determine whether ventilation strategies can support myocardial recovery and facilitate successful V-A ECMO weaning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06111-9.

Introduction

Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-A ECMO) is a rescue therapy for patients with refractory cardiopulmonary collapse [1]. Despite advances in ECMO technology and the growing expertise of multidisciplinary cardiogenic shock teams, which have led to a wider use of V-A ECMO over recent decades, its use remains associated with high rates of weaning failure and mortality [2]. Although ventricular assist device implantation or heart transplantation offer an alternative exit strategy in selected candidates [3], V-A ECMO most often serves as a time-limited bridge to recovery. Restoration of biventricular function is therefore a key determinant of successful weaning and patient survival [4].

Multiple approaches are used to promote myocardial recovery, including inodilators [5] and left ventricular (LV) unloading techniques [6]. Interestingly, mechanical ventilation can also profoundly influence hemodynamics: positive intrathoracic pressure may impair both right ventricular (RV) [7] and LV preload [8], while simultaneously modulating RV afterload through lung recruitment and overdistension [9, 10], and reducing LV afterload by lowering transmural pressure [11]. The net effect of positive intrathoracic pressure on cardiac output is therefore largely determined by lung and chest wall mechanics and LV function itself [12, 13]. These effects may be further amplified during V-A ECMO, where partial bypass of the pulmonary circulation further modifies ventilation–perfusion matching and cardiac loading conditions [14, 15].

Current Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) guidelines recommend a “lung-rest” ventilation strategy irrespective of ECMO mode or underlying pathology [16, 17]. This guidance is primarily based on data from patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) supported with veno-venous (V-V) ECMO [18, 19]. Hereby, also the potentially beneficial effects of mechanical ventilation on cardiac loading and function are ignored. The impact of mechanical ventilation strategies during V-A ECMO are poorly characterized and have not been summarized, underscoring the need for a systematic search of literature.

In this systematic search and review, we aimed to describe studies that addressed the effects of mechanical ventilation settings on physiological and clinical outcomes in V-A ECMO-supported patients.

Methods

We conducted a systematic search on the effects of mechanical ventilation settings on physiological and clinical outcomes during V-A ECMO support. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420251077344). This systematic review was conducted and reported in full compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement, and all PRISMA 2020 checklist items were addressed [20].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion when they were based on original data from adult (≥ 18 years) patients or preclinical models in whom/which V-A or veno-arterial–venous (V-AV) ECMO was used for refractory cardiac arrest (CA) or cardiogenic shock (CS). Exposures of interest were controlled mechanical ventilation settings and hypoxemia-rescue therapies (details in Supplementary Table S1). Studies were deemed eligible regardless of whether comparators were included. Primary outcomes were clinical endpoints (V-A ECMO duration, V-A ECMO weaning success, intensive care unit [ICU] length of stay, mortality) and physiological measures (RV and LV function assessed by invasive pressure–volume [P–V] loop indices or echocardiography). Secondary outcomes included vasoactive drug requirements. We excluded abstract-only and non-English publications, case reports, and studies in solely pediatric populations, those involving V-V ECMO or other circulatory support devices, and studies lacking V-A ECMO-specific controlled ventilation interventions or relevant outcomes.

Search strategy and data extraction

We systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Google Scholar from inception to June 6th, 2025. The strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH, Emtree) and free-text terms for V-A ECMO, mechanical ventilation settings, rescue therapies for refractory hypoxemia, and outcomes related to RV and LV function. No date or language limits were applied at the search stage. Full search strategies for each database are reported in Supplementary Tables S2.

Two reviewers (IP, NDT) independently screened all studies on basis of titles/abstracts and subsequently, in full text in EndNote (v21.2). Conflicts were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (CM). The same reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized, PRISMA-aligned form. Extracted items included study identifiers and design, population characteristics, V-A ECMO indication, ventilation settings, hypoxemia-rescue therapies, timing of assessment, physiologic and clinical outcomes, vasoactive agents requirements, and key findings (details in Supplementary Table 3).

Risk of bias was assessed by the same reviewer pair, applying validated tools according to the study design, and excluding preclinical studies from this assessment (as per the PRISMA guidelines). Each domain was rated as low, moderate, or high risk. Specifically, for RCTs, we evaluated five domains using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool [21]: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. A study was considered to have a low risk of bias if no concerns were identified in any of the five domains; moderate risk was assigned if concerns were present in one or more domains but did not significantly impact the overall validity; and high risk was assigned when serious concerns were identified in one or more domains. For non-randomized studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [22] was used, which evaluates study quality across three domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and outcome assessment. Each item within these domains contributes one point, for a maximum score of 9 points. Because the NOS does not provide universal cut-offs for classifying studies, the overall risk-of-bias judgement was based on both the total score and the specific domains in which points were lost, with the selection and comparability domains carrying more weight in the overall assessment. In general, higher scores reflect stronger methodological design and group comparability, whereas moderate or high risk of bias indicates increasing limitations in study design and control of confounding.

Data synthesis and analysis

Given the small number of eligible studies and the expected heterogeneity across designs, populations, interventions, and outcomes, we decided apriori not to conduct pooling of results. Instead, we conducted a structured narrative synthesis, grouping the results from the included records in two main domains: (1) clinical outcomes, and (2) physiological outcomes, both further grouped by intervention that was studied.

Continuous outcomes are presented as means or medians and categorical outcomes as counts and percentages, as reported in the original studies. Effect estimates (e.g., odds ratios, hazard ratios) are presented when available.

Results

The search yielded 5,750 records. After de-duplication, 5,616 manuscripts were screened on title and abstract, and subsequently, 186 studies were evaluated in full text (Fig. 1). Clinical outcomes

Among the studies reporting clinical outcomes, four specifically investigated ventilation strategies aimed at minimizing lung pressures and volumes. Two large ELSO registry-based studies found that lower peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak), lower dynamic driving pressure (DDP), and reduced RR were independently associated with improved survival in both ECPR [survival with any Ppeak > 20 cmH20 (adjusted OR 0.69, per 1 SD [0.64, 0.75]; p < 0.001), with higher DDP (adjusted OR 0.72, per 1 SD increase [0.65, 0.79]; p < 0.001) and with increasing RR (adjusted OR 0.82 per 1 SD change [0.75 to 0.89]; p < 0.001)] [23] and CS [survival to discharge with low intrapulmonary pressure ventilation (adjusted OR 1.69 [1.21–2.37]; p = 0.0021); median Ppeak (22 vs 24 cm H2O; p < 0.001) and median DDP (14.5 vs 16 cm H2O; p < 0.001) significantly lower in those surviving to discharge] [24]. In the latter study, higher PEEP was associated with a modestly increased survival, although the association did not reach statistical significance [continuous PEEP (adjusted OR 1.08 [0.98, 1.20]; p = 0.14] [24]. Findings align with a prospective study [25], which implemented a multimodal therapeutic bundle—including optimized mechanical ventilation with low TV (4–6 ml/kg), optimal high-PEEP, and minimized FiO₂—and reported a marked reduction in mortality compared with baseline practice (57.1% vs 21.8%, p < 0.05, 18 months after the bundle implementation). Among patients supported with V-A ECMO for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), survivors showed progressive recovery of respiratory system compliance and consistently lower airway and driving pressures [26], whereas another study reported significantly higher native lung minute ventilation (7.04 vs 5.32 L/min, p = 0.01) with a trend towards larger TV in hospital survivors compared to non-survivors, although multivariable logistic regression analysis found that minute ventilation was not independently associated with outcomes, likely reflecting improving compliance in those with better prognosis [27].

Only one case-series directly reported the effect of the ventilation mode [28], describing five ECPR patients in whom transition to HFPV markedly improved oxygenation, enabled successful V-A ECMO weaning and yielded 80% survival.

A limited number of studies addressed oxygenation management, and in particular, the impact of native-lung FiO₂ on outcomes. In a multicenter randomized clinical trial in patients on V-A ECMO, Burrell et al. [29] compared conservative (SpO₂ 92–96%, PaO₂ 60–90 mmHg) versus liberal (SpO₂ 97–100%, PaO₂ 100–150 mmHg) oxygenation targets, achieved by titrating both sweep gas FiO₂ and native-lung FiO₂. While the conservative approach effectively reduced hyperoxia, it did not improve any clinical outcome, including ICU-free days, survival to six months, or V-A ECMO duration and ICU-LOS. In contrast, two large ELSO registry analyses [23, 30] linked oxygen exposure to survival. Early arterial hyperoxia within 24 h of V-A ECMO initiation was independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality in a dose-related fashion (adjusted OR, 1.14 per PaO2 50 mmHg higher [1.12–1.16]; p < 0.001). Importantly, higher native-lung FiO₂, independent of sweep gas, was also weakly but significantly associated with mortality (adjusted OR 1.02 [1.00–1.05]; p = 0.04), with the highest risk observed with FiO₂ > 60% [30]. Similarly, decreased native-lung FiO₂ was associated with improved survival (adjusted OR 0.75 for each 1 SD change [0.69 to 0.82]; p < 0.001), with mortality risk rising once FiO2 exceeded 40% [23].

In an observational study in patients supported by V-A ECMO and Impella (ECPELLA), inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) was associated with improved survival and higher V-A ECMO weaning rates [31].

Physiological outcomes

Among the studies reporting physiological outcomes, Soule’ et al. [32] prospectively investigated the effects of individualized PEEP titration during V-A ECMO, combining electrical impedance tomography (EIT) with echocardiographic monitoring. EIT-guided PEEP titration improved lung homogeneity and compliance, and resulted in an average individualized PEEP level of 10 cmH₂O. While macro-hemodynamics remained stable, echocardiography showed that excessive PEEP impaired biventricular systolic function, whereas intermediate levels favored both lung protection and cardiac performance. Specifically, RV outflow tract velocity–time index and Tricuspid Annular Plane Systolic Excursion both peaked at a PEEP of 10 cmH₂O and declined with PEEP of 20 cmH₂O, RV S′ velocity further improved at 5 cmH₂O PEEP, and LV outflow tract velocity–time index was highest at 10 cmH₂O PEEP but decreased at higher pressures, whereas LV end-diastolic diameter remained unchanged.

De Lazzari et al. [33] used a computational cardiovascular model to examine intrathoracic pressure manipulation. Increasing intrathoracic pressure caused LV P–V loops [34] to narrow and shift downward and leftward, with reduced end-diastolic volume (EDV), slightly higher end-diastolic pressure (EDP), lower end-systolic pressure (ESP), and decreased stroke volume (SV), accompanied by a rightward shift of the end-systolic pressure–volume relationship (ESPVR). RV P–V loops [34] demonstrated a similar downward and leftward shift, with reductions in ESP, SV, and overall loop area. These geometric changes were associated with lower stroke work (SW), potential energy (PE), and pressure–volume area (PVA) in both ventricles, while ventriculo-arterial coupling remained stable for the LV but declined modestly for the RV.

Edinger et al. [35] explored oxygenation management in a rat model of V-A ECMO by varying native-lung FiO₂ (0.5, 0.21, or 0) while keeping oxygenator FiO₂ fixed at 1.0. Anoxic ventilation precipitated a cardiogenic shock phenotype, leading to significant declines in arterial pressures, cardiac output, stroke volume, and LV ejection fraction, despite unchanged LV end-diastolic volume, accompanied by severe hypoxemia, hypercapnia, reduced ScvO₂, metabolic acidosis, and rising LDH levels. In contrast, FiO₂ 0.21 and 0.5 produced comparable hemodynamics.

Finally, the previously described observational study on the effects of iNO during ECPELLA support [31] observed an increase in pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) as a marker of improved RV function, together with a parallel rise in Impella flow within 24 h of initiation.

None of the screened and included articles investigated the prespecified secondary outcome related to the requirement for vasoactive drugs.

Risk of bias assessment

We assessed a total of 10 studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB2) tool for the single RCT (n = 1) and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for the nine observational studies (n = 9) (Table 2). The only RCT (n = 300) [29], demonstrated a moderate risk of bias, driven predominantly by a 29.5% protocol deviation rate in the conservative oxygen arm vs 1.3% in the liberal arm, despite adequate randomization and blinded outcome assessment. Among the observational studies, NOS scores ranged from 4/9 to 9/9. Three large registry studies (Tonna et al. 2022 [23], n = 7,488; Rali et al. 2023 [24], n = 2,226; Jentzer et al. 2023 [30], n = 9,959) were assessed to have a moderate or moderate-to-high risk of bias, respectively. These scores reflect a substantial potential for residual and unmeasured confounding intrinsic to registry data, even with extensive multivariable adjustment and methods such as generalized estimating equations. Four single-center studies [26, 27, 31, 32] (NOS scores 7–8/9, n = 15–138) showed a moderate or moderate-to-high risk of bias, with limitations including small sample sizes, confounding by indication, and inadequate covariate adjustment. One multi-center prospective study with a retrospective control group (Jing et al. 2022 [25], n = 297, NOS 9/9) exhibited a moderate risk of bias reflecting temporal bias and absence of a concurrent control despite achieving full NOS scoring. Discussion

Mechanical ventilation is nearly universally applied in V-A ECMO patients and may offer unique therapeutic opportunities. Yet, our systematic review highlights a striking lack of high-quality evidence in this setting. As a result, current data does not allow robust conclusions on how mechanical ventilation affects cardiac function, recovery, or clinical outcomes in this population. This gap underscores the urgent need for mechanistic studies that move beyond descriptive observations and can directly inform evidence-based clinical practice.

Our systemic search identified studies that largely described the effects of lung-protective ventilation strategies—including low TV [25, 36], limited driving and inspiratory pressures [23, 24], and reduced RR [23, 37]—on clinical outcomes in V-A ECMO-supported patients. The observed benefits align with the well-established benefits observed in ARDS patients [38], suggesting that strategies proven effective in general mechanically ventilated populations may also be relevant for V-A ECMO-supported patients. This observation seems biologically plausible, as these patients are frequently at high risk of acute lung injury due to factors such as pulmonary contusion due to chest compressions during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) [39], transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) and transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO) [40], aspiration [41], atelectasis, and prolonged mechanical ventilation leading to ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) [42] or ventilation-induced lung injury (VILI) [43–45]. Moreover, in the context of LV failure, elevated LV end-diastolic pressure (EDP) and LV-aortic uncoupling may exacerbate the risk of acute lung injury by raising left atrial pressures, promoting pulmonary edema, and further reducing lung compliance [46, 47].

It is noteworthy that only a minority of the included studies directly addressed the hemodynamic effects of mechanical ventilation in the pathophysiological context of V-A ECMO. Evidence from a small physiological study suggests that higher PEEP may modestly impair load-dependent echocardiographic indices for both the RV and LV without affecting overall hemodynamics [32]. This pattern seems interpretable on basis of computational PV-loop models, showing more favorable bi-ventricular mechanoenergetics at higher intrathoracic pressures [33]. From a clinical perspective, it is plausible that an individual-tailored PEEP exists: a value that optimizes ventriculo-arterial coupling and cardiac mechanical efficiency while preserving lung protection, as suggested by invasive RV PV-loop assessments [48]. These effects may be unique to V-A ECMO, where the parallel extracorporeal circulation mitigates macrohemodynamic risks by unloading the RV and maintaining organ perfusion. In contrast, in patients without V-A ECMO support, elevated intrathoracic pressure may more readily reduce cardiac output and compromise perfusion [49, 50]. Nonetheless, the limited signals available in the literature preclude any inferences regarding the hemodynamic effects of mechanical ventilation during V-A ECMO, highlighting a major knowledge gap.

The inferences from the studies identified in our systematic search are limited as a consequence of the small number of included studies, their heterogeneity in design and domains, and inherent risk of bias. Additionally, the observational nature of many studies and variability in sample sizes, methodologies, and outcome measures prevented us from pooling findings. Given the current lack of robust data, future research should primarily focus on physiological studies to thoroughly investigate the pathophysiology of heart–lung interaction during V-A ECMO and gain a deeper understanding of the hemodynamic effects of mechanical ventilation. Once sufficient physiological data are available, RCTs and interventional studies will become more feasible and informative.

Conclusion

Mechanical ventilation is a cornerstone therapy for patients supported with V-A ECMO. However, the available literature on its effects on hemodynamics, cardiac recovery, or clinical outcomes is sparse, of low quality, and highly heterogeneous, preventing any reliable inferences. This evidence gap underscores the urgent need for well-designed, high-quality studies to provide mechanistic insights and guide evidence-based clinical practice.

Few studies with low quality and high heterogeneity have investigated mechanical ventilation strategies and their effects on cardiac function, recovery and clinical outcomes in patients receiving V-A ECMO support.Current evidence does not permit any inference regarding the effects of mechanical ventilation on cardiac function and recovery, or clinical outcomes in patients supported with V-A ECMO.High-quality physiological and interventional studies are urgently needed to explore the effects of ventilation strategies in V-A ECMO, as part of a multimodal treatment approach aimed at improving outcomes in a patient population still burdened by high mortality.
