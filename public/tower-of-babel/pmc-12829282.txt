The Childbearing Motivation Scale (C-CMS): translation, reliability and validity testing
Authors: Jiaxin Cheng, Xiaoting Wang, Jia Liu, Jinrui Fei, Chuang Li, Ge Tian, Hongxia Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a result of the changes in fertility patterns in the present era, the trend in fertility levels and demographic development has become a focus of attention for all sectors of society. Previous studies have mostly measured childbearing levels in terms of people’s external manifestations of fertility intentions, and there is a relative lack of research on childbearing motivation, the antecedent of individual fertility intentions. At the same time, there is a lack of validated scales of childbearing motivation with a complete evaluation system. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the reliability and validity of the Chinese version of the Childbearing Motivation Scale, which provides a reference for improving fertility intentions and overall childbearing levels in the reproductive age group.MethodsSelected 666 people in the reproductive age group in three cities in Liaoning Province. The reliability of the scale was validated using internal consistency, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability. The validity of the scale was verified using face validity, content validity, construct validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. The age variable was tested for measurement invariance using a multi-group validated factor analysis.ResultsThe Chinese version of the Childbearing Motivation Scale consists of 2 subscales, positive childbearing motivation subscales, and negative childbearing motivation subscales, with 9 dimensions and 37 entries. Cronbach’s alpha values for the total scale and dimensions ranged from 0.864 to 0.954. The I-CVI for the scale ranged from 0.833 to 1.000 and the S-CVI was 0.933. Exploratory factor analysis extracted nine metrics explaining 66.453% of the total variance. The results of the validation factor analysis showed that the fit of the model’s indicators was within an acceptable range.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the Childbearing Motivation Scale has good psychometric properties and can be used as a valid measurement tool for evaluating childbearing motivation in reproductive age groups. At the same time, it will help to develop suitable education and intervention strategies for healthy reproduction for the reproductive age group.

Introduction

Given that the modern notion of life has been greatly influenced by numerous external environmental elements, including the rapid socioeconomic development and the popularization of the media [1, 2], The age group of childbearing subjects has transitioned from the post-70s and 80 s, when people grew up in the atmosphere of family and collectivism, to the post-90s and 00 s, when people pay more attention to personal experience and autonomous expression. The population of this era is experiencing changes in reproductive autonomy and choice due to educational expansion, changes in marriage and parenthood, economic fluctuations, uncertain expectations, and constraints on the cost of childbearing [3, 4]. Changes in fertility patterns, such as more and more people of childbearing age choosing to " be able to have children but not to have them " and delaying the age of first marriage and childbearing until after 30 years of age [5–7]. Ultimately, this results in a decline in overall fertility levels [8]. Analysis of socio-demographics and data from macro-models shows that childbearing levels in East Asia have been gradually declining since the 1960 s, and the downward trend has further accelerated into the twenty-first century, with fertility ratios dropping to around 1.5 in most countries and regions. In the United States, the fertility rate fell from 2.12 to 1.73 between 2007 and 2018 [9]. In China, the fertility ratio has dropped to 1.0 for many years, and the country’s population distribution has been in a state of “fertility depression” for 30 years or more, leading to increased pressure from the aging of the population, a decrease in the young workforce and uneven population development [10, 11]. The consequences and impacts of this low childbearing status cannot be ignored, and raising childbearing levels is one of the major practical problems that need to be solved urgently [12]. Given this, China’s central government has issued a series of documents to adjust the population development strategy, but the effect is not obvious. It can be seen that external policies are no longer the main cause of the low fertility state, and the key issue is no longer whether the policy “allows or disallows births”, but rather whether individuals are internally driven by their “willingness to give birth” [13]. Some scholars, predicting fertility perceptions in terms of fertility intentions, focus on whether they want to have children, how many they want to have, whether they want to have a boy or a girl, and when they want to have children [14–16]. However, the exact tendency of the subject’s procreative activity is not indicated, and only the external form of childbearing motivation is described. If we want to identify the main reasons for childbearing behavior, we should dig deeper into the concept of fertility directly through the childbearing motivation [17, 18].

Childbearing motivation is the primordial driving force behind an individual’s attitude towards parenthood and childbearing behavior, defined as “traits that drive action”, and is the answer to the question “Why do we want to have children” [19–21]. In the micro-model, themed on the individual’s motivation in the reproductive decision-making process, the intrinsic significance of childbearing motivation can drive individuals towards or away from reproductive behavior. Specifically, facilitating forces may be reflected in the value of the individual in the economy and the social environment, such as the pursuit of fulfilling one’s moral obligations and increasing economic support [22]. It is manifested in the fulfillment of personal feelings, such as the pursuit of the meaning of life and the experience of the process of conceiving life. It is embodied in the continuation of family relationships and the maintenance of the relationship between husband and wife, such as the inheritance of the bloodline and the possession of the fruit of love. At the same time, along with that comes the effort required in parenting [23]. Young children cannot grow up without parental care, including help with food, clothing, housing, transportation, and attention to the child’s physical and mental health and development, all of which can bring unknown parenting pressures to the childbearing age group. At this stage in China’s history, society is paying more attention to children’s development than in the past, and the high expectations for children’s education in Confucianism over the centuries have increased the pressure on parents to raise children. On the economic side, work is necessary for an individual’s survival, and when under the double pressure of work and childcare, the pessimism of being overwhelmed is even more pronounced [24]. However, the combination of these negative pressures may prevent individuals from thinking and acting on their fertility. Therefore, in order to understand the reasons for changes in individual fertility perceptions, reproductive behavioral decisions, and even macro fertility levels, it is crucial to introduce a universal scale that can more scientifically assess the motivation of people of childbearing age to have children [25].

Miller scholars have proposed that childbearing motivation consists of both positive and negative aspects, which are not complete opposites but are interconnected through multiple sub-dimensions [26–28]. While reliable and valid self-reported childbearing motivation instruments are available, they are missing to varying degrees in assessing orientation and group characteristics. For example, the Parenthood Motivation Scale, while differentiating between dimensions reporting childbearing motivation, only assesses their intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, and most of the analyses target the positive sub-dimensions [29]. In the population selection, the main subjects explored were only women undergoing IVF treatment and pregnant women of childbearing potential, which is lacking in terms of generalizability. The Parenthood-Motivation list encompasses the relationship between a couple’s desire and motivation to have children, focusing on the fact that both partners play a role in choosing to have children [30, 31]. However, the subject population for this scale is long-term involuntary childless couples and does not apply to men and women in the general population. There is also a tendency for most of the measured motives to be positive in the assessment of motivation, and negative motives are not taken into account. The Female Reproductive Reasons Scale, designed by Chinese scholars, provides a new interpretation of fertility from a female perspective, focusing not only on “birth” but also taking into account the process of “parenting” [32]. However, the scale remains vague in terms of positive and negative assessments and does not manage to differentiate between the two aspects. The Childbearing Motivation Scale, produced by Chinese scholar Tianli Liu, attempts to design a questionnaire on childbearing motivation that is universally applicable to groups of childbearing age and distinguishes between positive and negative in the form of subscales [33]. However, the small number of sub-dimensions in their eventual establishment may limit the range of measurements. At the same time, the scale does not specify whether the motivation for the loss of youth mentioned on the negative subscale is physical or psychological, ignoring the issue of outward appearance that will be valued by the contemporary reproductive age group. While all of the above instruments for measuring childbearing motivation are useful, there are omissions in terms of question content or subjects. In addition, studies of reproductive age groups generally define the maximum age of childbearing as 40 and do not take into account the increase in childbearing among women aged 40 and over [34]. New measures should overcome these sampling limitations in order to better understand current childbearing patterns. In light of this, this study will assess the psychometric characteristics of the Childbearing Motivation Scale (CMS), developed by Guedes et al. in 2015, in the Chinese cultural context, and translate it into Chinese [35]. The scale introduces a rich set of dimensions for assessing childbearing motivation, which can reveal the spiritual and socio-cultural dimensions of fertility perceptions in greater depth, and help to better understand the childbearing motivation of the reproductive age group.

The dimensionality of the CMS can be analyzed in terms of the Self Determination Theory (SDT) of motivational cognition, which, unlike other theories that view motivation as a single concept that varies only quantitatively, suggests that the childbearing motivation is a continuum from controlled motivation to autonomous motivation, and is more revealing of the role of an individual’s autonomous thoughts and his or her surroundings as drivers of procreation [36, 37]. According to the SDT, childbearing motivation can be categorized into internal childbearing motivation, external childbearing motivation, and no childbearing motivation. External motivations for childbearing can be further categorized as integrative regulation, identity regulation, introspective regulation, and external regulation. Based on individual motivation and experienced perceptions of control points, SDT further classifies external and introspective regulation in external motivation as controlled motivation, and identificational and integrative regulation in external motivation along with internal motivation as autonomous motivation. Controlled childbearing motivation can be defined as the state in which an individual’s motivational state for childbearing behavior is controlled, and the individual is forced to produce or refuse to produce childbearing behaviors more often due to the views and perceptions of external environments or pressures generated by his or her thoughts. The belief that reproductive behaviors are chosen to satisfy external goals, to gain rewards, recognition, or to avoid punishment, e.g., I fear that I will not be able to protect my child in the face of societal dangers, is a motivation that suggests that negative tendencies may be exhibited in the choice of reproductive behaviors due to the consideration of the dangers of the external environment in which they live. Autonomous childbearing motivation refers to a state in which an individual’s motivation for childbearing behavior is based on his or her own autonomous choices, and the individual’s behavior under autonomous motivation is consistent with the individual’s core self, reflecting his or her preferred and accepted values and the behavior under this state of motivation is self-satisfying, reflecting the individual’s intrinsic tendency to seek new things and challenges. Inherent tendencies. Autonomous childbearing motivation reflects the identity-regulated and integration-regulated states of individual childbearing motivation. When an individual chooses to procreate or refuses to procreate on his or her own because of perceived value or defects of procreation, or when an individual absorbs society’s life philosophy, values, and morals about procreation, making them part of his or her self and using them to guide his or her procreation behavior. The idea of having children to have the intimacy of one’s blood, for example, is one that individuals pursue spontaneously and wholeheartedly. The establishment of questions and integration of dimensions in CMS can be explained by the perspective of autonomous and controlled fertility motives in SDT theory, and the theoretical framework based on SDT is shown in Fig. 1.

Methods

Introduction to source scales

The CMS entries are developed from in-depth interviews and literature reviews, revised based on expert content validity testing, and validated for reliability and validity with 614 participants aged 19–49 years old drawn from the general population. The results confirm good reliability and validity and widespread use [38, 39]. The Likert 5-point scale is employed, with subscale scores ranging from 1 to 5 for the mean score of each entry and scores ranging from 1 to 5 on a scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. Structural and convergent validity are good, with good internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients range from 0.85 to 0.89, with all scale dimensions having values larger than 0.70. The scale consists of two subscales, Positive Childbearing Motivation (PCM: F6-F9) and Negative Childbearing Motivation (NCM: F1-F5), with 9 dimensions and 47 entries. The descriptions and titles of the potential emotions, and meanings represented by each dimension are listed below:

F1-Childrearing burden and immaturity (1, 2, 8, 10, 15, 18): Parental responsibility for the child is not only the smooth birth of the child but also the long-term process of upbringing. For example, the lack of patience and care in the child’s upbringing and education, as well as dealing with the child’s problems, makes the mood irritable. When facing the rigors of parenting and taking on the role of a parent, internal autonomous concerns inevitably arise. This dimension reflects the fact that negative emotions such as fear and worry hold individuals back from the idea of becoming a parent [40].

F2-Social and ecological worry (6, 9, 13, 19): this dimension is concerned with worries about the impact of external social and environmental risks on the uncertainty of children’s future. For example, there is the fear of being unable to bring up healthy children in this deteriorating general environment and social context. This suggests that social insecurity may have a broader impact on current fertility patterns [41].

F3-Marital stress (4, 12, 14, 21): Marital stress refers to constraints on a partner’s autonomous life, activity style, and intimacy. For example, the presence of a child causes my partner and me to lose time together, and it causes me to lose closeness with my partner. The emotional balance that is essential to a marriage is the foundation of relationship stability, and instability in the relationship can upset this balance and affect current fertility patterns [5].

F4-Financial problems and economic constraints (5, 7, 17, 20): Amid the challenges of the external socio-economic landscape, the prevalence of unemployment and job insecurity fuels anxiety among contemporary young and middle-aged individuals. The birth of a child exacerbates financial strains under the dual pressures of work and life [42]. The financial crisis exerts a dramatic impact on individuals, leading them to approach reproductive behavior negatively.

F5-Physical suffering and body image worry (3, 11, 16): This dimension reflects concerns about the threats childbirth poses to an individual’s physical health and body image. It represents a contemporary issue that discourages or delays childbearing, encompassing fears about maternal and fetal health, as well as challenges in postnatal body image recovery. The paramount importance of life safety constitutes a significant factor influencing fertility decisions for both genders [43].

F6-Socioeconomic aspects (6, 8, 10, 13, 17, 20, 23): This dimension examines motivational connotations stemming from socioeconomic factors and falls under controlled motivation. It refers to the extrinsic rewards attainable through parenthood, such as meeting family expectations, fulfilling moral duties, and enacting adult roles. This dimension highlights social recognition and responsibility-taking as drivers of childbearing [44].

F7-Personal fulfillment (3, 4, 7, 14, 16, 18, 19, 25): In addition to motivation from external control, childbearing increasingly serves internal autonomous motivation at the level of self-actualization and personal psychological enjoyment. It covers emotional connections and life values such as wanting to have a loved one with whom you have a blood connection, experiencing pregnancy, and making your life meaningful. The above emotional needs have a significant impact on fertility decisions [45].

F8-Continuity (2, 5, 9, 21, 24, 26): this dimension reflects the intrinsic autonomous role of family tradition factors on childbearing motivation. It suggests a traditional sense of parenthood, a desire for the permanent presence of family values, and an inherent notion of family inheritance. Such as the transmission of family traditions and the continuation of the bloodline [46, 47].

F9-The couple relationship (1, 12, 15, 22): this dimension refers to the process of married life, where the presence of children is considered one of the driving forces to achieve the goals of marriage. Strengthening the couple’s relationship, increasing marriage completion, or propelling the marriage to the next stage as some of these sub-dimensions are potentially autonomous motivations. This view expresses the motivation to expand self-development or procreation to strengthen the relationship with a partner [48].

Translation of the scale

Through email, permission is acquired from the original scale’s creators to translate the scale by the Brislin translation approach [49]. Direct translation: To create the Chinese version of Scale A1, two master’s degree nursing students who have completed CET-6 translate the English version of the scale into Chinese Versions 1 and 2. The research team then discusses the results of the two translations and makes necessary modifications and summaries. Back translation: The Chinese version of Scale A1 is back-translated into English by two medical doctors who have no contact with the original scale and have studied abroad in English-speaking countries for more than two years. They compare the translated version and the original scale with the research team members and repeatedly discuss the differences. After the translated version is formed, an online meeting is held to organize all participants in the scale translation to compare and discuss the original scale, Chinese Version A1, and the back-translated version according to the principle of equivalence, forming Back-translated Version B1. Expert comparison: A bilingual nursing expert with 20 years of international work experience compares Back-translated Version B1 with the original scale. Any entries that differ are discussed and revised by the expert and the researcher until over 90% agreement is reached, completing Back-translated Version B2. Feedback from the original authors: The back-translated Version B2 is sent to the original authors for comparison with the original scale. They provide feedback on discrepancies, which are then adapted and modified by the researcher and bilingual experts until the original authors confirm that the consistency exceeds 95%, resulting in the initial Chinese version of the scale, A2.

Cross‑cultural adaptation

The scale must be completely comprehended in light of the Chinese context and way of thinking because of the linguistic, cultural, and other variations among nations. In order to make the scale more appropriate for the Chinese target audience, the current study team cross-culturally modified it with the original authors’ permission. In this study, six experts are invited to evaluate the initial Chinese version of Scale A2 as a whole and the individual entries. Basic information of experts: 3 undergraduates, 2 masters, 1 doctorate; 5 associate senior titles, 1 full senior title; Years of service 10–20 years 2, 21–30 years 2, 31–40 years 2; age 31–40 years 2 41–50 years 2, 51–60 years 2. Experts are invited to evaluate and advise on the linguistic appropriateness, content relevance, and scale structure of each item of the scale in the context of Chinese culture. The scale’s content validity was assessed using the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) and the entry-level content validity index (I-CVI). When the former is > 0.78 and the latter > 0.8, the scale is considered to have good content validity. Based on expert recommendations, three items with I-CVI < 0.78 were deleted, and four pairs of entries were merged due to similarity in sentence meaning (Table 1). The modified entries were reordered to form the Chinese version of the 40-item Chinese version of the Childbearing Motivation Scale (C-CMS) scale A3.

Pre-survey

In August 2024, 30 Chinese individuals of reproductive age were chosen by convenience sampling to participate in a pre-survey utilizing the Chinese version of the A3 scale, aiming to explore the reproductive age group’s understanding of the questionnaire content. Inclusion criteria: Age between 19 and 49 years old [35]. Able to communicate in Chinese and independently read and understand the content of the questionnaire as well as fill out the questionnaire. Informed consent to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria: people with psychiatric disorders or cognitive disorders. The opinions obtained in the pre-survey are summarized with expert opinions and modified into a scale suitable for Chinese cultural expression.

Formal survey

Our university’s Ethics Committee grants its clearance to the study (Ethics approval number: JZMULL2025031). The surveys include the C-CMS and the researcher’s general information questionnaire. The general information questionnaire includes gender, age, only child status, ethnicity, literacy, marital status, occupation, and personal monthly income. A combination of on-site surveys and Questionnaire Star is used to collect information, and the on-site surveys are all implemented by the researcher, who completes and checks the completeness of the questionnaires on the spot. All options of the questionnaire star distribution are set as mandatory, and a uniform guide is used to introduce the purpose of this study, precautions, and the principle of confidentiality to the respondents.

Using the same inclusion criteria as the pre-survey, reproductive age groups were chosen using the convenience sample method in three cities in Liaoning Province, China, between September 2024 and January 2025. The sample size should be 5–10 times the number of items in the questionnaire, according to Kendall’s sample size calculation method. In this study, there are 48 items, and considering the 20% invalidity rate, the required sample size is 288 ~ 576 cases [50]. With a higher-than-expected recruitment rate, we obtained 690 participants (not including the 30 individuals from the pre-survey), which meets the sample size requirements. 666 completed the C-CMS, with a response rate of 96.52%.

Item analysis

Discrimination Analysis: The total scale scores are sorted in descending order into high and low subgroups (top 27% high and bottom 27% low), and the differences between the high and low subgroups are compared using independent samples t-tests. If the decision value (t-value) is >3 and P < 0.05, it represents a strong identification of the entry and is retained [51].

Homogeneity test: Pearson correlation analysis is used to calculate the correlation coefficients between each entry and the total score, with correlation coefficients r > 0.4 indicating a good correlation of the scale entries, and entries with correlation coefficients < 0.4 or those that do not reach the level of significance being considered for deletion. Commonality and factor loadings are tested, and entries with commonality < 0.2 (or factor loadings < 0.45) are considered for deletion.

Reliability testing

Reliability analysis is an estimate of the consistency of the scale measurements; the higher the consistency of the measurements, the higher the reliability of the instrument [52]. The internal reliability of the scale is assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and split-half reliability. When Cronbach’s alpha >0.800, it indicates good reliability of the scale, and when Cronbach’s alpha >0.700, it indicates that the reliability of the scale meets the criteria. Spearman-Brown is used to assess the split-half reliability of the scale. The external reliability of the scale is assessed by sampling 40 females to fill out the questionnaire again after 2 to 3 weeks, and the test-retest reliability is calculated using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to ensure the stability of the scale over time [53].

Validity testing

Validity analysis is the degree to which a measurement tool can accurately measure what needs to be measured [54]. The validity of this study was examined in terms of face validity, content validity, construct validity, discriminant validity, and convergent validity. In this study, 10 childbearing age groups (with the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as before) were invited to measure face validity by rating the impact of the scale entries based on their experiences. Each entry was scored on a 5-point Likert scale of importance, ranging from “not important” to “most important” on a scale of 1 to 5, with entry importance assigned a value of = Frequency % × Importance, and if the score was ≥ 1.5, the entry was retained. Experts (consistent with those involved in cross-cultural debugging) were invited to conduct 2 rounds of correspondence (same methodology as the first round) to validate the content validity of the C-CMS scale.

Exploratory Factor Analysis(EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis(CFA) were used to evaluate the construct validity of the Chinese version of the scale. The 333 samples collected in September and October 2024 were used as EFA (N1 = 333) and the 333 samples collected in November and December 2024 and January 2025 (N2 = 333) were used as CFA. Harman’s one-way analysis of variance is used to determine whether there is significant common method bias in this study. When the difference in Bartlett’s test of sphericity reaches the significant level (0.001) and the KMO is >0.800, it indicates that the data are suitable for factor analysis by principal component analysis and maximum variance orthogonal rotation. The data are calculated and tested using the Harman one-way test, which yields a maximum factor variance explained rate of < 40%. This tentatively indicates that the common method bias does not explain most of the variance among the variables, suggesting that the structural validity of the scale is good. To further validate the degree of fit between the theoretical conceptualization and the actual data, this study conducts a validation factor analysis by constructing a structural equation model with positive childbearing motivation and negative childbearing motivation as exogenous latent variables, and their respective sub-dimensions as endogenous latent variables. Model estimation is performed using the great likelihood method through AMOS28.0 software. Acceptable ranges of goodness of fit for the main fit indices were (χ2/df) < 3.00, comparative fit index (CFI) >0.9, incremental fit index (IFI) >0.9, Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) >0.9, and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08 [55]. A common method bias test is conducted using Harman’s one-way validation factor analysis. If the fit index of the one-way model is poor, it indicates that the scale does not have serious common method bias and that its construct validity is good.

The discriminant and convergent validity are tested by calculating the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). If CR >0.80 and AVE >0.50, the convergent validity is acceptable. In order to test whether C-CMS has the same significance between women and men and whether its measurements are equivalent, the present study uses a multi-group validated factor analysis to test the invariance of the measurements. Measurement invariance includes: Configural Invariance to test whether the structure of latent variables is the same across groups. Metric Invariance/Weak Invariance to test for equal factor loadings between groups. Scalar Invariance/Strong Invariance to test whether the intercepts of the observed variables are equal across groups. Error Variance Invariance/Strict Invariance to test for equality of error variance across groups. If the above equivalence model holds, and the amount of change in both CFI and RMSEA does not exceed the suggested thresholds, i.e., ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, it can be assumed that the scale represented by the model is invariant across clusters [56, 57].

Statistical methods

SPSS 27.0 software is used for descriptive statistical analysis and the reliability and validity of the scale are analyzed. Measurement data are described by mean ± SD, and count data are described by frequency (n) and constitutive ratio (%). Two independent samples t-tests are used to analyze entry discrimination, Pearson correlation coefficients to analyze the homogeneity of the entries concerning the total score, and factor analyses for commonality and factor loading tests. Data conform to a normal distribution and are analyzed using t-tests and one-way ANOVA. CFA tests are performed using AMOS28.0 software. The measurement invariance test is performed using Mplus8.3 software.

Results

Item analysis

The t-test showed that the absolute values of the critical ratio values of the high and low groups were in the range of 4.602 to 21.517 (P < 0.01), and all the entries had a good degree of discrimination. The correlation coefficients between the entries showed that three of the entries with r < 0.4 were deleted, and the r values of the other items were in the range of 0.451 to 0.607 (P < 0.01). Tests of commonality and factor loadings showed that the four items had a commonality of <0.2 (or a factor loading of <0.45). After adjustment, this scale was recognized as 37 entries.

Reliability analysis

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total C-CMS scale was 0.929, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the positive childbearing motivation subscale was 0.913. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the economic, individual, family, and couple dimensions were 0.925, 0.902, 0.864, and 0.954, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the negative childbearing motivation subscale was 0.902. The childcare, social, marital, economic, and physical dimensions were 0.888, 0.904, 0.908, 0.925, and 0.874, respectively. The split-half reliability of the total scale was 0.725 and the test-retest reliability was 0.885. The test-retest reliability of the positive childbearing motivation subscale was 0.898 (P < 0.01), and the test-retest reliability of the dimensions were 0.929, 0.869, 0.797, and 0.909 for economy, individual, family, and couple, respectively. The test-retest reliability of the negative childbearing motivation subscale was 0.899 (P < 0.01), and the test-retest reliability of the dimensions childcare, social, marital, economic, and physical were: 0.889, 0.799, 0.863, 0.803, and 0.899, respectively.

Validity analysis

The results showed that the importance assignment scores for the scale entries ranged from 3.7 to 4.8, which met the requirement of a score of ≥ 1.5, indicating good face validity of the scale. After two rounds of expert review of the scale, the I-CVI of each entry ranged from 0.833 to 1.000, and the S-CVI/Ave was 0.933, which all met the score requirements indicating good content validity. The results showed KMO = 0.882 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity with a χ2 value of 17044.4, P < 0.001, indicating that the data could be factor analyzed. The EFA was performed using principal component analysis with maximum variance rotation, and nine factors were extracted, explaining 66.453% of the total variance, with the eigenvalues, total variance explained, and the loadings of each entry on the factor to which it belongs shown in Table 2. The Harman one-way test yielded a maximum factor variance explained of 13.187%. Discussion

Under the severe low fertility environment, solving the childbearing problem will be a long-term and arduous task [58]. To solve this problem, more attention needs to be paid to the current mainstay of the new generation of childbearing. Childbearing motivation is an important part of the concept of reproduction and is at the forefront of the reproductive chain, and the examination of motivation cannot be neglected [46]. When we are confused about low fertility as an outcome, we should go back to the beginning of the chain of cause and effect, not just to some intermediate link [59]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore a suitable scale of childbearing motivation to understand the fertility tendency of the Chinese population. The present study attempts to overcome the limitations of the current instruments used to assess childbearing motivation. Translation and revision of the Childbearing Motivation Scale, a specific instrument for childbearing motivation in reproductive age groups, which has been widely used abroad and has both positive and negative aspects, independently and simultaneously [39]. The C-CMS assesses the motivation to have children in the reproductive age group along multiple dimensions, and this data can help the health sector to better assess, develop individualized intervention plans and improve the propensity to have children in the reproductive age group.

In this study, a team of experts with excellent characteristics and qualifications, including experts in linguistics, and methodology, and familiar with research in the field of reproduction, were invited to review the scale in terms of entries and dimensions, and in the first round of expert review, three items of the source scale were given deletion for failing to reach 0.78 on the I-CVI. The reason for this is that it is not in line with Chinese culture and language, and the problem is not clear and easy to measure. Four pairs of entries with similar sentences were merged. Combining the three methods of finding the decision value, correlating the entries with the total score, and factor analysis as the judgment criteria for item analysis, the three items with weak correlations and low effect values for the closeness of influence between the topic and the variable were deleted. In the reliability test of the scale, the C-CMS scale showed good internal and external reliability. The C-CMS was finalized to contain 2 subscales, namely, positive childbearing motivation and negative childbearing motivation. The former contains 4 dimensions and 20 entries. The latter contains 5 dimensions and 17 entries with clear and easy-to-understand items. It can be used to assess the motivation for childbearing among the Chinese population of childbearing age, as shown in Table 8. In addition, C-CMS can also provide a comprehensive picture of the nature of positive and negative childbearing motivation through the scores obtained on the subdimensions of each subscale, with face validity scores meeting the requirements in validity tests [37]. The results of the secondary review of the initial Chinese version of the scale, I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave by the expert review team judged the content validity of this scale to be good, and the positive/negative motivation for childbearing subscale to have good discriminant validity. The EFA extracted a total of 9 male factors, explaining the total variables to meet the requirements, with each entry having moderate or higher factor loadings, and the dimensions in which the entries were located were all consistent with the original scales. The results of the multi-group validation factor analysis showed that C-CMS’s configural invariance, metric invariance, scalar invariance, and strict invariance were all supported by the data. With the gradual increase in the constancy restriction, the model did not change significantly and did not lose its main psychometric property, i.e., the C-CMS is cross-group stable and applicable across genders. Configural invariance was established to show that measures within different gender groups in C-CMS can reflect the same psychological constructs. After additional restrictions, the fit did not deteriorate or mildly deteriorate, the assumption of equivalence was considered reasonable, and the assumption of metric invariance was acceptable in this study. The metric invariance was established to show that C-CMS has a reciprocal relationship between each item and the corresponding latent variable in the male and female groups, and also has reciprocal units, representing the same meaning in the change of units in their scores across genders. Test scores for males and females can be directly compared and interpreted; scores are the same and have the same meaning. The scalar invariance was established to indicate that the C-CMS has the same intercept across genders, indicating that the scale has the same reference point for each item across genders. Strict invariance indicates that the magnitude of measurement error due to random factors is the same across genders. The present study considers that the determination of “motivation for childbearing” should be attributed to both spouses and not to the man or woman alone. The measurement invariance should be verified first, and the interpretation made on this basis will be reliable. In summary, the factor structure of C-CMS with the Chinese reproductive age group as the sample has cross-sex equivalence, and the observed differences in C-CMS by sex can be explained by the latent variables.

The results of the study showed that women’s positive childbearing motivation scores were lower than men’s positive childbearing motivation scores, i.e., men showed a stronger motivation for childbearing compared to women. The reasons for this are, on the one hand, the fact that childbearing is a long-term, ongoing process for women, which causes inevitable changes in their daily lives and work, and, in particular, the passive restriction of their physical activities during the late stages of pregnancy and the puerperium, which creates fear and boredom and results in the manifestation of weaker motivation for active childbearing. On the other hand, women are the main bearers of childbearing, and in the values of the traditional Chinese marriage system and cultural practices, returning to the family and childcare after childbearing seems to be a mandatory option for women, requiring them to invest more time and energy. However, economic and social development and the influx of new ideas have transformed their concepts of marriage, childbearing, and the family, which have to a certain extent reduced or influenced women’s motivation to have children. Consistent with the findings of existing studies, but contrary to what was found in Kenya, no significant change in fertility intentions was found among women who had experienced the recession, for reasons that may be related to differences in measurement methods [61, 62]. In terms of education level, individuals with higher levels of education showed higher positive childbearing motivation scores. This result is consistent with existing research findings that higher levels of education are associated with greater childbearing positivity [63]. The reason for this may be that less educated individuals have less knowledge and a single source of information. Individuals with higher education can improve their human capital, have higher cultural quality and professional skills are more likely to have better job opportunities and higher personal labor income, and have a higher opportunity cost of childbearing, which makes them more inclined to be active in childbearing.

Limitations

First, this study utilized a convenience sampling method, and although the sample size met the study criteria, the sampling was limited to the reproductive age group within Liaoning Province, China. Considering the more similar upbringing in a province, there are biases in the participants’ educational base, psychological dispositions, and other factors that can limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies could consider exploring whether construct validity varies with age, region, or parenthood, i.e., adding tests of measurement invariance for a larger number of groups. General information such as the addition of subgroups of age groups, expansion to different cultural areas, and investigation of parenthood and tests of measurement invariance were conducted. In addition, consideration should be given to the use of qualitative methods to explore how participants interpret the programs, which would enhance the process of cross-cultural adaptation. Second, this study utilized a cross-sectional design and self-reported data collection, which somewhat limited our in-depth understanding of the causal relationships between variables. Future research may consider using longitudinal studies to more precisely examine how motivations for childbearing evolve. Finally, this study was conducted using a relatively simple survey instrument, focusing only on the positive and negative tendencies of fertility motivation in the reproductive age group in comparison with the groups of general demographic information. There has been no in-depth study of the specific factors affecting the motivation of reproductive age groups to have children (e.g., family and social support, psychological state, etc.). In future studies, additional assessment tools could be considered to explore the relationship between positive or negative childbearing motivation and related influences, providing empirical evidence for the development of more effective interventions. Therefore, it is recommended that the relevant authorities take a gender perspective in promoting childbearing and adopt targeted measures for groups with strong correlations to improve the overall tendency of childbearing motivation. At the same time, the implementation of fertility policies should be individualized, and attention should be paid to the degree of knowledge of fertility-related information among people with lower literacy levels, so as to avoid the problem of poor information affecting the motivation and decision-making on childbearing.

Conclusions

After cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation, the C-CMS has good reliability and validity and has been successfully introduced in China. The Chinese version of the C-CMS will be used as a measurement tool to assess the propensity for positive and negative childbearing motivation in the reproductive age group. It will help to develop the most appropriate education and intervention strategies for healthy reproduction for reproductive age groups.
